# Natural Farm Establishment Site Selection and Wood Preserve Design

## Overview

Fukuoka provides detailed guidance on establishing a natural farm from scratch, covering site selection, wood preserve creation, shelterbelt planting, [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-establishment-bare-hillside]] on hillsides, garden terracing, and rice paddy construction. These practical instructions form the physical foundation upon which the philosophical [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] farming are built.

## Site Selection

The ideal location for a natural farm is at the foot of a hill or mountain. Fukuoka identifies several reasons for this preference over flat plains or isolated mountain forests:

- **Pleasant weather**: Slightly elevated sites typically have more moderate temperatures and better air drainage than valley bottoms or exposed ridges.
- **Abundant resources**: Hillsides provide firewood, vegetables, bamboo, and other materials required for food, clothing, and shelter.
- **Water access**: Having a stream nearby makes irrigation straightforward and supports diverse plant and animal life.
- **Deep, fertile soil**: The ideal location features enormous trees towering above deep, rich black or brown soil with clear water.

The natural farm must be able to supply all materials essential for food, clothing, and shelter. In addition to fields for growing crops, a complete natural farm should include a bordering wood that serves as both a natural preserve and a source of organic fertility.

## The Wood Preserve

The woods surrounding a natural farm serve as a natural preserve and a direct or indirect source of [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]]. Fukuoka describes three strategies for creating deep, fertile soil:

1. **Direct burial of coarse organic matter** deep in the ground.
2. **Gradual soil improvement** by planting grasses and trees that send roots deep into the soil.
3. **Nutrient transport** from the humus of upland woods or forest downhill via rainwater or other means.

When no uphill wood exists, a new wood or bamboo grove can be developed for this purpose. The preserve should include several types of trees:

- **Companion trees that enrich the soil**, such as leguminous species and deep-rooted trees that bring nutrients from subsoil layers.
- **Timber trees** for [[natural-building-materials-guide]] and firewood.
- **Trees that supply food for birds and animals**, supporting the predator-prey balance that controls pests.
- **Trees that provide habitat for natural enemies of insect pests**, creating biological pest control infrastructure.

### Establishing a Wood on Bare Hilltop

Hill and mountain tops are generally infertile and dry, making them highly susceptible to denudation. Fukuoka recommends a specific succession for establishing woody cover:

1. **Vine planting first**: Plant kudzu to prevent soil erosion. The vine rapidly covers bare ground and stabilizes the soil surface.
2. **Low conifers**: Sow seeds of a low conifer such as moss cypress to create an evergreen mountain cover.
3. **Pioneer vegetation**: Cogon grass, bracken ferns, and low bushes such as lespedeza, eurya, and moss cypress grow thickly at first.
4. **Successional transition**: This initial vegetation gradually gives way to urajiro fern, kudzu, and a mix of trees that further enrich the soil.

### Tree Selection for Hillsides and Valleys

Fukuoka specifies different tree species for different positions on the slope:

**Hillside plantings** should include:
- Evergreens: Japanese cypress and camphor tree
- Deciduous trees: Chinese hackberry, zelkova, paulownia, cherry, maple, and eucalyptus

**Valley and foot-of-hill plantings** should include:
- Oaks and evergreens such as cryptomeria and live oak
- Interplanted with walnut and ginkgo for food production

### Bamboo as a Reserve

A bamboo grove can serve equally well as the wood preserve. Bamboo offers several advantages:

- **Rapid growth**: A bamboo shoot reaches full size in one year, producing more vegetative material annually than ordinary trees.
- **Coarse organic material**: The woody culms provide excellent material for burying in the ground to improve soil structure.
- **Structural benefits**: Bamboo is hollow with a large void ratio, and it decomposes slowly, helping soil retain air and water.
- **Edible shoots**: Certain species produce edible bamboo shoots that can be harvested as vegetables.
- **Light and portable**: Dried bamboo is light and easy to carry for construction and craft purposes.

## Shelterbelts

Shelterbelts and windbreak trees provide multiple functions beyond wind protection:

- **Wind damage prevention**: The most obvious function, protecting crops and soil from desiccating winds.
- **Soil fertility maintenance**: Leaf litter from shelterbelt trees contributes organic matter to adjacent fields.
- **Environmental improvement**: Shelterbelts moderate temperature extremes, reduce evaporation, and create microclimates favorable to [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]].

### Species Selection for Shelterbelts

Fast-growing species commonly used:
- Cedar, cypress, acacia, and camphor tree

Slower-growing but durable species:
- Camellia, umbrella tree, wax myrtle, and Chinese anise
