# Foliar Fungi — Sooty Molds, Black Mildews, and Downy Mildews

## Overview

Living plant leaves are colonized by a large and diverse group of fungi distributed widely across different climates and plant hosts. This page covers several major groups of foliar fungi: sooty molds, black mildews, downy mildews, Taphrinales, and other common leaf colonizers.

## Sooty Molds

Sooty molds are **saprobic ascomycetes** belonging to several families within the Dothideales. They form distinctive black mats on living leaves and stems and are particularly abundant in the tropics. They use honeydew secreted by plants or sap-sucking insects such as aphids and scale insects as their nutrient source. Unlike parasitic fungi, sooty molds show no host preference and colonies may consist of mixed populations of eight or more species.

### Growth Forms

Reynolds (1975) recognized two growth forms for sooty molds:

**Deciduous growth form:** Characterized by a thin hyphal layer that peels away from its substratum in drier, cooler months. Found predominantly in lowland tropical regions but absent from rain forests. Also occurs along the coast of the southeastern United States, commonly on citrus trees. Representative genera include *Limacinula* and *Trichomerium*.

**Permanent growth form:** Occurs mainly on twigs and stems where it forms thick, cushionlike black hyphal mats. Predominantly found in neotropical highlands and temperate lowlands such as north coastal regions of North America. Species of *Limacinia* are representative.

### Diversity and Distribution

Sooty molds may form a thin network of hyphae, a velvety growth, or a dark crust on leaves and smaller twigs. On trunks and larger branches, mold growth is usually more robust. They predominate along continental margins of North and South America, in Central America, and on Western Hemisphere islands.

In Nothofagus forests of New Zealand, trunks and branches can be covered with sooty molds up to 9 meters or more in height, with growth so extensive that only small areas of bark remain visible. They occur in some form in most vegetation types of the world but are most prevalent around margins of rain forest communities. Hughes (1976) noted that the trunks and branches of trees in these forests can be covered up to 9 m or more with sooty molds. Some species of sooty molds on leaves are found in close association with glandular trichomes (Hughes 1976).

### Morphology and Reproduction

Some species produce as many as **three different asexual forms** (Hughes 1976). The mycelium is composed of a weft of dark hyphae, sometimes several millimeters thick, with conidiogenous cells and various types of conidia formed within. In some species the weft of hyphae is apparently unorganized, whereas in others the mycelium is highly organized with a characteristic appearance. The conidia vary in size, shape, septation, and pigmentation.

The *Metacapnodiaceae* produce distinctive tapering moniliform hyphae with rigid smooth-walled cells, forming dense, lustrous colonies (Parbery and Brown 1986). The *Seuratiaceae* produce dark, gelatinous, lobed thalli with reproductive structures quite different from other sooty molds.

### Collection Techniques

Sooty molds on leaves are easier to collect and identify than those on tree trunks. A drop of 2% collodion/parlodion solution (in [[diethyl-ether]] or absolute ethanol) or cellulose acetate is spread gently on the leaf colony using a glass rod or similar tool. The solution dries in minutes to a transparent film in which the fungus is embedded. When the film is peeled from the leaf surface (the "leaf peel" technique), it removes the intact colony. The peel is placed on a microscope slide and the collodion removed with acetone for examination under a compound light microscope. Water-soluble mucilage glue can also be used in the same way.

Keys for genera can be found in Luttrell (1973), and species descriptions in Hughes (1976) and Batista and Ciferri (1963). Reynolds (1971, 1978a, 1978b, 1979, 1982, 1983, 1985) reported on collections from the Neotropics.

## Black Mildews

Black mildews are **obligate plant parasites** belonging to the order **Meliolales** (Ascomycota). The name derives from the scattered, dark, superficial, circular colonies produced on plant host surfaces. The order contains more than 1,580 species (Hawksworth et al. 1995), with approximately 1,400 in the genus *Meliola* (Parbery and Brown 1986).

### Hosts and Distribution

More than 90% of plant hosts are dicotyledonous angiosperms, though black mildews also occur on monocotyledons, gymnosperms, and pteridophytes. Reports of infections on species in some large plant families common in rain forests, including Orchidaceae, Gesneriaceae, and Araceae, are limited. They are especially abundant in the tropics but range from Chile to southern United States, South Africa to central Europe and Scotland, and Japan to Tasmania.

They are most diverse in tropical scrub or open parkland, relatively rare in dense tropical rain forest undergrowth, but may be abundant in the upper canopy. The greatest infections in rain forests occur in naturally or anthropogenically disturbed areas. They are absent from arid subtropics but common in some areas of South Africa with dry seasons up to 6 months.

### Morphology

Each colony normally represents a single species but can become confluent with heavy infections. Colonies are found mostly on mature leaves but also on leaf petioles and young twigs; fruit is rarely infected. Colonies are characterized by very dark, superficial, **hyphopodiate hyphae** radiating from a central mass of globose ascomata.

The most distinctive feature is the presence of short, two-celled lateral branches called **capitate hyphopodia** (or mucronate hyphopodia), distributed in opposite, alternate, or mixed arrangements at the ends of hyphal cells. A fine hyphal filament beneath the hyphopodium penetrates the host cell wall to produce a haustorium within the host cell. Species of *Meliola* are further characterized by mycelial setae that arise laterally from hyphal cells and immediately bend to assume a perpendicular position. These setose colonies tend to collect debris such as pollen and insect frass that absorbs water.

The ascomata are either cleistothecia or perithecia, darkly pigmented, and may have appendages. Ascospores are dark brown, usually 4–5 celled, rarely exceeding 10 mm long. Black mildews lack asexual states and produce hyphae of uniform 6–10 mm diameter with a characteristic branching pattern.

Keys for genera can be found in Müller and von Arx (1973); species descriptions and illustrations are in monographic treatments by Hansford (1961, 1963). Collection techniques for black mildews are similar to those for sooty molds.

## Downy Mildews

Downy mildews are a group of more than **300 species** of plant-parasitic Straminipiles belonging to the **Peronosporales** and **Sclerosporales** in the Oomycota. Although traditionally studied by mycologists and included in mycological treatments, they are now recognized as belonging to the Straminipila (chromistan algae and their relatives) rather than the true Fungi. They are characterized by microscopic, erect, treelike, determinate sporangiophores protruding singly or in tufts through stomata on the leaves of flowering plant hosts, giving the macroscopic appearance of a white to gray "down" on affected tissues (Moore-Landecker 1990). Many downy mildews are associated with serious plant diseases.

Fifty-four plant families of dicotyledons and two families of monocotyledons serve as hosts. Several genera of Sclerosporales occur on monocots (e.g., *Verrucalvus*, *Sclerophthora*, *Sclerospora*, *Peronosclerospora*), whereas genera of Peronosporales occur on dicots. *Peronospora* is the largest genus with 260 species.

They are obligate parasites producing intercellular hyphae with intracellular haustoria. Sporangia form zoospores upon germination, whereas conidia germinate by germ tube formation. Genera are delimited based on the characteristic branching of the sporangiophores and sporangia. Sexual reproduction (oospores) is infrequent (Moore-Landecker 1990). All species require free moisture for sporulation, which is restricted to periods of high relative humidity and darkness. With the exception of *Verrucalvus*, they cannot be grown in pure culture. They are able to sporulate repeatedly for several successive nights from the same lesions, and depending on the species and host, the sporulation period for systemic infections can extend for several months or more under favorable conditions.

The downlike appearance on leaves is frequently accompanied by chlorosis or necrosis of host tissue. Keys for identification can be found in Waterhouse (1973).

## Taphrinales and Protomycetales

**Taphrina** (Taphrinales, Ascomycota) includes about 95 species of biotrophic microfungi causing hyperplasia, galls, or witches' brooms on ferns and higher plants. Some species are associated with deformed fruit, leaf spot, or leaf curl, and discolor infected tissues yellow or pink, often with a whitish bloom indicating sporulation. Asci are produced in a palisade on or from within the epidermis; there are no specialized fruiting bodies. The common leaf curl of peach is caused by *[[taphrina-deformans]]*. Species are fairly host-specific and occur almost exclusively in temperate regions. Foliar damage is most visible in spring. They can be cultured but behave like slow-growing pale pink yeasts.

**Protomyces** (Protomycetales) includes about 10 species causing galls in leaf and stem tissues of Apiaceae and Asteraceae, containing large thick-walled overwintering chlamydospores. Many species are readily cultured. Little has been published on tropical species, although *P. andinus* has been reported from Asteraceae in Ecuador and Brazil.

## Other Leaf Colonizers

Among the earliest colonizers of angiosperm leaves are yeasts: red yeasts in the genera *Sporobolomyces* and *Rhodotorula*, and white yeasts including *Cryptococcus* species. *[[aureobasidium-pullulans]]* is a ubiquitous and cosmopolitan filamentous fungus on leaf surfaces in early summer and autumn in temperate regions. In culture it produces copious slimy conidial masses ranging from creamy white to pink to dark brown or black. *[[sclerophoma-pythiophila]]* (anamorph of *Sydowia polyspora*) resembles Aureobasidium and is common on conifers.

Leaf-colonizing fungi in temperate regions tend to be more numerous on lower leaves and on more peripheral leaves in the canopy. Fungal growth is often better on the abaxial (lower) than the adaxial (upper) leaf surface because the former is more sheltered and has a higher number of stomata.

Common leaf-spotting fungi in tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions include *Alternaria*, *Ascochyta*, *Cercospora*, *Cladosporium*, *Corynespora*, *Phyllosticta*, *Pestalotia*, and *Pestalotiopsis*. On temperate broadleaf trees and shrubs, common genera include *Coccomyces*, *Discula*, *Gnomonia*, *Mycosphaerella*, *Rhytisma*, and *Venturia*. The life cycle of a typical foliage parasite involves ascomata production on infected leaves during fall or early spring, ascospore discharge as new foliage appears, and overwintering as saprobic mycelium or ascomata.

Needle-cast fungi on conifers include genera such as *Lophodermium*, *Lophodermella*, *Elytroderma*, *Hypodermella*, and *Lirula*. They often produce symptoms in the first growing season but fruiting bodies may not sporulate until foliage is a year old or older. Symptoms such as chlorotic, discolored foliage and repeated defoliation may be readily observed, but it may be necessary to collect older foliage or previous year's cast needles for species determination.

## Foliar Fungi on Grasses

Parasitic fungi occurring on grasses in temperate regions include ascomycetes such as *Gaeumannomyces*, *Gibberella*, *Monographella*, *Phyllachora*, and *Pleospora* (with *Stemphylium* anamorphs), as well as specialized pathogen groups previously noted. Common hyphomycetous fungi on grasses include *Alternaria*, *Cladosporium*, *Drechslera*, *Fusarium*, *Pseudocercosporella*, *Rhynchosporium*, and *Ulocladium*. Common coelomycetes include *Ascochyta* and *Colletotrichum*.

Common foliar parasites of grasses in tropical regions include species of *Cercospora*, *Drechslera*, *Magnaporthe* (anamorph *Pyricularia*), *Rhynchosporium*, and *Sphaerulina*. *Pyricularia* species are particularly important as parasites of rice, other cereals, and grasses. *Pyricularia grisea* (synonym *P. oryzae*; teleomorph *[[magnaporthe-grisea]]*), the causal agent of rice blast, is an important pathogen in many rice-growing countries. The fungus also parasitizes more than 50 other grass species.

Species of *Cercospora* on grasses and sedges cause lesions on leaves, nodes, panicles, and grain. The leaf spots are elliptical with more or less pointed ends and grayish centers with dark margins. They begin as small, water-soaked areas and develop into lesions up to 1.5 × 0.5 cm. Heavy spotting or infection at the tillering stage can kill the host.

Few parasitic fungi on living rushes and sedges in temperate areas have been reported other than rusts, smuts, and members of the Clavicipitaceae. Included among those fungi are the ascomycete *Didymella*, the hyphomycete *Pseudocercosporella*, and the coelomycetes *Ascochyta* and *Colletotrichum*.

## Collection and Identification of Foliar Fungi

The presence of foliar pathogens is recognized macroscopically by water-soaked areas, distortion, or more commonly, chlorotic and/or necrotic lesions on leaves. Occasionally, the fungi will cause total necrosis or shriveling. To identify a fungus, it is usually necessary to examine reproductive structures such as acervuli, pycnidia, perithecia, or apothecia under a compound light microscope.

If fungal reproductive structures are not present or not mature, it may be necessary to incubate the leaves in a moist chamber to encourage sporulation. Keys and descriptions for genera and species of fungi on temperate trees, shrubs, and woody climbers can be found in Ellis and Ellis (1985), and those for western North America in Funk (1985). Because many foliar fungi are host-specific, correct host identification is essential, and healthy foliage and reproductive structures should be collected along with infected material.

## Fly-Speck Fungi

The name "fly-speck" fungi refers to genera of the Microthyriaceae (Microthyriales sensu Batista 1959) of the Ascomycota. The term name was derived from the dark, flattened, dimidiate-scutate (shield-shaped) ascomata, which often appear as small, dark, superficial dots on a leaf surface. In the sole monograph of this group, Batista (1959) recognized 447 species in 45 genera. The largest genus, *Micropeltis*, includes more than 100 species.

Microthyriaceae fruit primarily on living leaves but also on young stems and occasionally on fruits, mostly in tropical and subtropical regions. The ascospores are uniseptate to multiseptate, and the mycelium is usually entirely superficial with haustoria forming in the host epidermal cells. The mycelium can be hyaline and inconspicuous, form dark networks of anastomosing hyphae, or form plates of radiating hyphae. Some members cause necrosis and produce ascomata on dead host tissue. For keys to families and genera, see Luttrell (1973, as Hemisphaeriales). For species descriptions and illustrations, see Batista (1959).
