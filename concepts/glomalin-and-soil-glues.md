## Glomalin and Soil Glues

## Overview

Glomalin refers to the sticky, glue-like substances produced by a wide variety of soil organisms that play a critical role in soil aggregation, water retention, and overall soil health. These materials have long been underappreciated, but as [[elaine-ingham]] emphasizes:

> "Glomalin is the tip of the ice-berg, but it is great that finally the sticky materials in soil are being recognized for their importance!"

## What Is Glomalin?

Glomalin is a glycoprotein-like substance found in soil that acts as a biological glue, binding soil particles together into stable aggregates. While it was initially identified as a product of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi, research has since shown that many other soil organisms also produce similar sticky materials.

> "VAM are not the only organisms making glomalin. Many other organisms in soil make this material."

The production of glomalin and related soil glues is a fundamental function of a healthy [[soil-food-web]]. These substances:

- Bind soil particles into micro- and macro-aggregates
- Improve soil structure and porosity
- Enhance water infiltration and retention
- Protect soil organic matter from rapid decomposition
- Create [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] microorganisms

## R.C. Foster's Research

R.C. Foster, working in Australia, published significant findings on this topic in *The Ultrastructure of the Rhizosphere*. Foster's electron microscopy work revealed the extensive network of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and glues that surround plant roots and soil particles in the rhizosphere. His research documented how bacteria and fungi coat soil particles with adhesive layers, fundamentally shaping the physical architecture of the soil environment immediately adjacent to roots.

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Glues

### Bacterial Glues

Bacteria produce glues primarily to anchor themselves to soil particles and root surfaces. These adhesive substances allow bacteria to maintain position in the soil matrix, access nutrients at the root-soil interface, and resist being washed away by water movement. Bacterial glues tend to operate within a narrower pH range and contribute to the initial stages of soil aggregation, particularly microaggregate formation.

### Fungal Glues

Fungal glues, including glomalin from VAM fungi, are generally more robust and persistent than bacterial glues. [[fungal-hyphae]] extend through soil particles, secreting sticky materials that bind larger aggregates together. The pH environment preferred by fungi differs from that of bacteria, and the glues they produce reflect these different biochemical conditions. Fungal-dominated systems tend to produce more stable, longer-lasting soil aggregates.

### pH Differences

The pH of the microenvironment influences which organisms dominate and consequently which types of glues are produced. Bacterial glues tend to form in more neutral to slightly alkaline conditions, while fungal glues are associated with slightly more acidic environments. Understanding these pH relationships helps guide management practices aimed at promoting desired soil structure.

## Manure Antibiotics and De-Wormers

A significant practical concern addressed by Ingham is the presence of antibiotics and de-worming compounds in manure from treated livestock. These pharmaceutical residues can persist in soil and harm beneficial soil organisms if not properly broken down.

### Breakdown Strategies

The key strategy for decomposing antibiotics, wormers, and herbicides in manure or compost is to actively feed the appropriate groups of decomposer organisms:

- **Bacterial foods** feed bacteria that can begin breaking down simpler chemical compounds
- **Fungal foods** support fungi capable of decomposing more complex molecular structures
- **Molasses** serves as a readily available energy source that stimulates both groups

By ensuring the compost pile has adequate food sources for decomposers, the soil foodweb can metabolize and neutralize pharmaceutical residues before they reach fields or gardens.

### Temperature Monitoring

Temperature is a critical indicator of compost health and microbial activity. Monitoring compost temperatures helps identify problems:

- Properly heating compost indicates active decomposition, while sudden temperature drops may signal exhausted food supplies or inhibited microbial activity from toxic compounds like antibiotics. Temperatures that fail to rise suggest insufficient microbial populations or inadequate food sources.

Persistent temperature anomalies should prompt investigation into potential contamination from pharmaceutical residues or other inhibitors.

## Persistent Contaminants

### Clopyralid

Clopyralid is a herbicide notorious for its extreme persistence in soil and compost. The critical problem with clopyralid is that **no known decomposer organisms** can break it down. This means that clopyralid-contaminated materials can persist through the entire composting process and remain phytotoxic when the finished compost is applied. Prevention — keeping clopyralid-contaminated materials out of compost — is the only effective management strategy.

### Chlordane

Chlordane, a formerly common pesticide, is also highly persistent but differs from clopyralid in an important way: **some decomposer organisms do exist** that can break it down, though the process is slow. A healthy and diverse soil foodweb can gradually reduce chlordane residues over time, underscoring the importance of maintaining robust microbial communities in contaminated soils.

## The Bacteria-Then-Fungi Decomposition Cycle

Decomposition in soil and compost follows a characteristic succession:

1. **Bacteria dominate first** — They rapidly colonize fresh organic matter, consuming simple sugars and readily available compounds. Their activity drives initial heating in compost.

2. **Fungi follow** — As bacterial activity depletes simple compounds, fungi move in to decompose more complex materials like cellulose, lignin, and other recalcitrant organic molecules.

This succession is fundamental to proper composting and [[soil-organic-matter-dynamics-and-fungal-decomposition-interactions]].

## Humus, Humic Acid, and Fulvic Acid

> "FUNGI finish the process of making humus, not bacteria."

### Humus

Humus is the fully decomposed, stable end-product of organic matter breakdown. It is dark, colloidal, and resistant to further decomposition. Humus provides long-term soil fertility benefits including [[cation-exchange-capacity]], water retention, and nutrient buffering.

### Humic Acid

[[humic-acid]] is a major component of humus, consisting of large, complex organic molecules. It is soluble in alkaline conditions but precipitates in acid. Humic acids are the primary products of fungal decomposition and contribute significantly to soil structure through their adhesive properties.

### Fulvic Acid

Fulvic acid is a smaller, more biologically active fraction of humus. It remains soluble across a wider pH range and plays a key role in nutrient chelation and transport, making minerals more available to plant roots.

## Molasses for Fungal Dominance

Molasses is a practical tool for managing the microbial balance in compost:

> "The addition of molasses to the pile will favor fungi over bacteria and can speed up the whole process dramatically. 500 ml per 4 kg waste works good."

The recommended rate of **500 ml of molasses per 4 kg of waste material** shifts the fungal-to-bacterial ratio in favor of fungi. This is particularly useful when composting woody or lignin-rich materials that require fungal decomposition, or when the goal is to produce compost with a fungal-dominated biology suitable for perennial plants and woody crops.

## Brix, Plant Quality, and Protein

### Brix as a Quality Measure

Brix is a measurement of dissolved solids (primarily sugars) in plant sap, typically measured with a refractometer. Higher brix readings generally indicate:

- Greater sugar content in plant tissue
- Better mineral nutrition
- Healthier plants with more complete protein synthesis
- Improved flavor, shelf life, and pest resistance

### Protein Synthesis and Pest Attraction

When plants have adequate nutrients and a [[establishing-healthy-soil-foodweb]], they synthesize complete proteins efficiently. Plants grown with healthy foodwebs contain **3 to 10 times more protein** than those grown in biologically depleted soils.

When protein synthesis is incomplete due to nutrient deficiencies, plants accumulate **free amino acids** in their tissues. These free amino acids are highly attractive to insect pests and foliar diseases. Essentially, pest problems are often symptoms of incomplete protein synthesis rather than the primary problem.

### Nutrient Timing During Flowering

During the flowering stage, plants have specific nutrient demands:

- **Nitrate** availability should be managed carefully during flowering, as excess nitrate can interfere with reproductive development
- **Amino sugars** play an important role during flowering and fruit set, supporting proper pollination and seed/fruit development

Understanding these timing relationships allows growers to support plants through critical developmental stages.

## Key Takeaways

- Glomalin and soil glues are produced by many organisms, not just VAM fungi
- Bacterial and fungal glues differ in pH preferences, persistence, and role in aggregation
- Antibiotics and de-wormers in manure can be decomposed by feeding appropriate foods to microbes
- Clopyralid has no known decomposers — prevention is the only strategy
- Chlordane can be broken down by specific decomposers in a healthy foodweb
- Decomposition progresses from bacteria to fungi, with fungi completing humus formation
- Molasses (500 ml per 4 kg) promotes fungal dominance and accelerates composting
- Healthy soil foodwebs enable complete protein synthesis, reducing pest pressure
- Brix measurements provide a practical indicator of plant quality and nutrient sufficiency
