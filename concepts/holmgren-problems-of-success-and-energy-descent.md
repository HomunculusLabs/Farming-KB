# Holmgren Problems of Success and Energy Descent

One of Holmgren's most original contributions to sustainability thinking is his analysis of the "problems of success" — the structural dilemmas that arise when effective environmental action creates its own contradictions. This concept, developed primarily in Principle 3 (Obtain a Yield), reveals why well-intentioned sustainability efforts often fail to produce systemic change and points toward more effective strategies.

## The Rebound Effect

In systems modelling of sustainable transition, the phenomenon of "rebound" describes how design and behaviour changes that result in energy and resource savings tend to be immediately spent or invested in other sectors of the economy, which further stimulates demand for energy and resources.

Holmgren's colleague observed this firsthand after decades of helping friends reduce energy consumption through efficient technology. His friends now drove their energy-efficient cars further, and his sister's savings on electricity and gas for her new passive solar house gave her the money for an airfare to Europe.

This is not hypocrisy — it is a structural property of all self-organising systems. Maximum power is an energy law: systems that yield the most will continue to prevail. The pattern of successful systems must shift from dependence on high and opportunistic yields from non-renewable resources to using low and sustained yields from renewable resources.

## The Structural Dilemma of Transition

Obtaining a yield represents a structural dilemma for a transition to [[energy-descent-action-planning]]:

- It is tricky to determine how we can obtain a yield and reproduce our successes without creating more demand than is sustainable
- Systems under pressure to shift from high to low energy sources tend to do so by crashing and rebuilding
- Bypassing this destructive path requires integrating the apparent paradox between the abundance and the limits of nature

## The Co-Optation Trap

The transition from permaculture self-reliance and small business to large organisational roles presents similar structural dilemmas. Creative, ethically driven people in large organisations tend to be co-opted and corrupted by the large-system forces within which they must work:

- The roles allocated to even the most powerful allow far less holistic and integrated action than is possible at the personal level
- Quality of change is traded for quantity of influence
- When thinking and activity are highly focused around specific, limited and conventionally measured outcomes, degradation in quality is greatest

## The Prosperous Road Down

Howard and Elizabeth Odum's concept of "the prosperous road down" is Holmgren's proposed solution. We must transform ourselves so that we are happy, healthy and comfortable without the need to consume the planet and the future. This is not austerity — it is a richer form of prosperity based on diverse, local, and renewable wealth.

## Solutions Through Networked Action

These problems can be partially addressed by the progressively more holistic approach embodied in the holistic learning loop. Where success in meeting one environmental objective leads to stimulation of other networked and progressively more integrated low-energy alternatives, the rebound from those successes can reconfigure the economy and society while decreasing total resource demand.

**Example:** When money saved from an energy-efficient car or house is put into ethical investment, spent buying local organic produce, or used to employ a neighbour, adverse rebound effects are reduced.

**Second-hand goods:** When purchasing technology from large-scale systems, second-hand goods are often better value and dilute the feedback of demand for new goods.

## The Built Environment as Problematic Energy Storage

The transformation of energy into progressively more elaborate and valuable forms extends beyond food and fuel to the things we make — tools, buildings, and infrastructure. Modern society has been very active in "catching and storing energy" in all of its stuff. Unfortunately:

- Most stores are useless without the rest of the industrial complex
- They mostly encourage more energy consumption and waste
- Energy-consuming buildings and freeways rely on constant flow of new fossil energy
- Even computers may depend on continuous redundancy and replacement to stave off accumulating software corruption

## Disaster Vulnerability

The vulnerability of modern society to natural and man-made disasters is so great that a series of natural disasters could lead to global depression or even the collapse of modern civilisation. The 1923 Tokyo earthquake scenario, imagined at the peak of the Japanese economic boom when eight of the ten biggest banks were Japanese, illustrates the cascading risk.

The global insurance industry is already struggling with the run of natural disasters in rich countries attributed to global warming. As material assets proliferate and dependence on centralised systems intensifies, large-scale vulnerability compounds. Modern industrial society is setting itself up to play out the story of Atlantis.

## Peasant Resilience

The peasants in the world's poorest countries remain amazingly resilient in the face of natural disasters. Despite massive international disaster relief, self-reliant response remains the predominant factor in restoration of normality. In the 1989 San Francisco earthquake, community response in poor black neighbourhoods was more effective than in affluent white suburbs.

This suggests that poverty and self-reliance are not the same thing. Communities that have maintained skills of mutual aid, improvisation, and local production are more resilient than affluent communities dependent on centralised systems — even when the affluent communities have more resources in theory.

## Design Criteria for Effective Action

Holmgren's analysis implies several design criteria for sustainability action:

1. **Network effects:** Ensure that successes in one area stimulate further low-energy alternatives rather than high-energy consumption
2. **Avoid co-optation:** Maintain holistic, integrated action at human scale rather than being absorbed into large institutional systems
3. **Second-hand strategy:** Dilute demand for new industrial production by maximising reuse of existing goods
4. **Ethical reinvestment:** Redirect savings from efficiency gains into local, cooperative, and renewable systems
5. **Build household resilience:** Rebuild the informal fallback strategies and back-up systems at every level of society
6. **Embrace complexity:** Accept that the design of sustainable culture is beyond any mortal's capability — the process must be iterative and organic
