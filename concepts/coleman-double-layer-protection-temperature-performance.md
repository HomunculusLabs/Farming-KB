# Double-Layer Protection and Temperature Performance

The core innovation of Eliot Coleman's winter-harvest system is the use of two layers of protection — an outer greenhouse structure and an inner lightweight row-cover fabric suspended over the crops — to create a dramatically warmer growing environment without any supplemental heat.
This passive thermal protection system is what makes cold-house winter production possible in Zone 5 Maine,
where outdoor temperatures regularly drop to -10°F (-23°C) or lower.
Understanding the temperature performance of each layer,
and how they combine,
is fundamental to the entire winter-harvest approach and the key to its economic viability.

## The Two-Layer Principle

Coleman's cold house uses a simple but powerful principle: each additional layer of protection raises the minimum temperature by approximately one USDA hardiness zone.
A single layer of greenhouse poly provides roughly one zone of protection,
and the inner row-cover fabric provides a second zone. In practical terms,
this means that crops inside the double-layer system experience conditions equivalent to USDA Zone 7,
even though the outdoor environment may be Zone 5.

This "zone shifting" is not exact — it varies with solar radiation,
wind speed,  cloud cover,
and other factors — but it provides a useful rule of thumb for understanding the system's capabilities.
A grower in Zone 4 could expect Zone 6 conditions,
and a Zone 6 grower would enjoy Zone 8 conditions,
expanding the range of winter crops accordingly.
The concept of zone shifting through multiple protective layers can theoretically be extended further,
but practical management difficulties increase with each additional layer and diminishing returns rarely justify the added complexity and reduced light transmission that additional layers impose on the system.

## The Outer Layer: Greenhouse Poly

The outer layer of protection is a conventional hoop-house or Gothic-arch greenhouse covered with greenhouse-grade polyethylene film.
This layer provides the primary shelter from wind and precipitation,
and it captures solar radiation during the day.
The key temperature effects include daytime solar gain,
nighttime insulation,  and wind protection.

On sunny winter days,  even when outside temperatures are below freezing,
the greenhouse interior can warm to 60°F to 80°F (16°C to 27°C) or higher due to the greenhouse effect.
This daytime warmth is critical because it heats the soil mass,
which then releases stored heat slowly through the night.
The amount of solar gain depends on the angle of the sun,  cloud cover,
and the cleanliness and transparency of the poly film covering the structure.

At night,  the poly film provides a modest insulating effect,
typically raising the interior temperature 3°F to 5°F (2°C to 3°C) above outside temperature through radiant heat retention and wind sheltering alone.
The greenhouse poly used at Four Season Farm is typically UV-stabilized,
IR-blocking (infrared) film.
The IR-blocking additive reduces radiative heat loss from the interior to the night sky,
improving nighttime temperature retention by perhaps 2°F to 3°F (1°C to 2°C).
This modest improvement accumulates over the course of a winter and is particularly valuable during clear,
calm nights when radiative cooling is most intense.

Some growers use double-inflated poly (two layers of film with air inflated between them) for the outer greenhouse covering.
This provides additional insulation — roughly equivalent to another half zone of protection — but reduces light transmission by approximately 10-15%.
Coleman has generally preferred single-layer poly for maximum light transmission,
relying on the inner row cover for the second layer of protection and keeping the overall system as simple and light-efficient as possible.

## The Inner Layer: Row Cover Fabric

The inner layer is a lightweight,
spun-bonded polypropylene row cover fabric (such as Agribon or Reemay) suspended over wire hoops above the crop canopy.
On cold nights,
the temperature under the row cover is typically 5°F to 10°F (3°C to 6°C) warmer than the ambient greenhouse air above the fabric.
The trapped air layer between the row cover and the crop canopy acts much like the air gap in a double-pane window,
reducing conductive and convective heat loss from the plant microclimate.

The row cover also traps radiant heat from the soil,
provides a physical barrier that prevents frost formation directly on leaf surfaces,
and reduces moisture loss from the crop canopy.
This moisture management effect is a mixed blessing — higher humidity reduces transpiration stress but can promote fungal diseases if ventilation is inadequate within the greenhouse structure.

The row cover is not permanently installed.
It is placed over the crops in late fall as temperatures drop and is managed throughout the winter.
During sunny days when the greenhouse interior warms significantly,
the row cover must be pulled back or removed entirely to prevent excessive heat buildup that can damage or kill the crops beneath it.
Direct contact between the row cover and plant leaves reduces its insulating effect and can cause physical damage where frost or condensation freezes the fabric to the leaves overnight.
Proper suspension maintaining at least 6 to 12 inches of clearance above the crop canopy is essential for optimal performance of the inner layer.

## Combined Temperature Performance

The two layers working together produce a cumulative effect greater than either alone.
Coleman's temperature monitoring at Four Season Farm has documented the following performance characteristics across different winter weather conditions.

On sunny winter days,
the greenhouse interior (above the row cover) may reach 60°F to 80°F.
Under the row cover,
temperatures may be even higher — sometimes exceeding 90°F — which is why row cover management during sunny periods is essential.
On cloudy winter days,
the combined protection yields approximately 10°F to 20°F above outside temperature.
On cold winter nights — the critical period for crop survival — the total protection is approximately 8°F to 12°F above outside temperature at the crop level.

In Coleman's Zone 5 Maine location,
the all-time record low temperature under the double-layer system has been approximately -5°F (-21°C),
when the outdoor temperature was approximately -15°F (-26°C).
This confirms the approximately 10°F differential on the coldest nights.
Most cold-hardy winter vegetables can survive -5°F without significant damage,
especially when they have been gradually hardened by progressively colder conditions through the fall months.

## The Role of Soil Thermal Mass

The soil inside the greenhouse acts as a thermal battery,
absorbing solar energy during the day and releasing it slowly at night.
This thermal mass effect is a significant contributor to nighttime temperature maintenance and is greatest when the greenhouse floor is bare soil,
when the soil is moist,  and when the soil is dark-colored.
Coleman's raised beds with dark,
compost-rich soil are ideal for maximizing thermal mass storage capacity.

During extended cloudy periods of 3-5 consecutive days,
the soil thermal mass is gradually depleted without being recharged by daytime solar gain,
and the system's nighttime protection diminishes.
This is the most challenging weather pattern for cold-house growers and the primary condition that determines the minimum survival temperature for overwintered crops.

## Crop Selection Implications

Crops must survive the expected minimum temperature under the double-layer system (~-5°F in Zone 5).
This limits the winter palette to cold-hardy species: spinach,
certain lettuce varieties,  Asian greens,  kale,  scallions,  leeks,
radishes,  baby turnips,  and carrots.
Crops that cannot survive this threshold require the heated warm house.

## Comparison with Heated Systems

The cold house achieves temperature gains through passive solar energy and insulation with zero heating energy input.
Coleman's economic analysis shows the cold house is dramatically more profitable than heated winter production because heating costs of conventional greenhouses typically exceed the value of crops produced.
He also experimented with a "cool house" maintaining approximately 35°F with minimal heat,
but economics were marginal.
