# Ustilaginomycetes: Smut Fungi Classification and Diversity

The Ustilaginomycetes (smut fungi) are a major class within the
phylum [[basidiomycota-classification-major-clades]], characterized by the production of
teliospores (diploid overwintering spores) and dimorphic life
histories that include a saprobic yeast state and a pathogenic
filamentous state. Smut fungi are among the most important plant
pathogens globally, causing devastating diseases of cereal crops
including wheat, corn, barley, and sugarcane. The class includes
approximately 1,500 described species distributed across
numerous genera.

## Classification and Major Clades

Ultrastructural studies and molecular phylogenetic analyses
support the hypothesis that the Ustilaginomycetes includes three
major clades, or subclasses, each containing one to several
orders:

- **Entorrhizomycetidae**: Contains the single order
  Entorrhizales, which are root parasites of plants in the
  family Juncaceae (rushes) and Cyperaceae (sedges). These fungi
  are unusual among smuts in that they infect below-ground
  tissues rather than above-ground reproductive structures.
- **Ustilaginiomycetidae**: Includes the orders Ustilaginales
  (the classic smut fungi) and Urocystales. The Ustilaginales
  contains the majority of described smut species, including
  important pathogens such as Ustilago maydis (corn smut) and
  Ustilago tritici (loose smut of wheat).
- **Exobasidiomycetidae**: The largest subclass, encompassing
  the orders Doassansiales, Entylomatales, Exobasidiales,
  Georgefisherales, Graphiolales, Malasseziales,
  Microstromatales, and Tilletiales. This diverse group includes
  both plant pathogens and yeasts associated with animal hosts.

## Teliospores and Life Cycle

Teliospores are the defining feature of the Ustilaginomycetes.
These diploid resting spores are typically darkly pigmented,
thick-walled, and capable of surviving adverse environmental
conditions for extended periods. Upon germination, teliospores
undergo meiosis to produce a promycelium (basidium) bearing
haploid basidiospores (sporidia). The sporidia may germinate
to produce a yeastlike saprobic phase or, upon encountering a
suitable host plant, fuse to form a dikaryotic mycelium that
invades host tissues.

The dimorphic nature of smut fungi -- switching between a yeast
phase and a filamentous pathogenic phase -- is shared with
certain other basidiomycetes, including members of the
[[urediniomycetes-rust-fungi-yeastlike-relatives]]. Because of certain morphological
similarities, members of these two groups often have been
confused with one another, leading to controversial and
conflicting classifications in the literature.

## Relationship to Urediniomycetes

The relationship between the Ustilaginomycetes and the
[[urediniomycetes-rust-fungi-yeastlike-relatives]] has been a longstanding source of taxonomic
confusion. Traditional classifications placed both groups in the
Teliomycetes, based on possession of the teliospore, or in the
Phragmobasidiomycetes, based on the presence of septate basidia.
However, phylogenetic analyses of molecular characters do not
support monophyly of either grouping. The teliospore and the
overall "smut" morphology represent convergent evolution in
morphology among fungi.

Numerous taxa that once were assigned to the Ustilaginales (e.g.,
Microbotryum species) now have been demonstrated convincingly to
be derived members of the Urediniomycetes rather than true smut
fungi. This reclassification reflects the power of molecular
phylogenetic methods to resolve relationships that were
intractable based on morphology alone.

## Ecological and Economic Significance

Smut fungi are primarily parasites of flowering plants
(Angiosperms), particularly members of the grass family
(Poaceae). They typically infect the reproductive structures of
their hosts, replacing seeds or grains with masses of dark,
powdery teliospores. This mode of parasitism can cause
catastrophic yield losses in cereal crops. The smut fungus
Ustilago scitaminea, for example, can cause yield losses of up
to 100% in susceptible sugarcane varieties.

Some smut fungi have been used as model organisms for studying
plant-pathogen interactions. Ustilago maydis has become
particularly important as a genetic and molecular model, owing
to its well-characterized life cycle, ease of cultivation, and
the availability of molecular tools for genetic manipulation.

The Exobasidiomycetidae also includes the order
Malasseziales, which contains [[malassezia-commensal-pathogenic-yeasts]] species -- lipophilic
yeasts that are obligate commensals of mammalian skin. While not
plant pathogens, these organisms represent a remarkable ecological
transition within the Ustilaginomycetes from plant parasitism to
animal association.

## Research Methods and Challenges

Many smut fungi are obligate biotrophs that cannot be cultured
on artificial media, making them difficult to study in the
laboratory. Molecular phylogenetic methods have been
essential for understanding relationships within the group,
though the obligate nature of many species limits the
availability of DNA for sequencing. Teliospore morphology,
host specificity, and disease symptoms remain important
characters for species identification, even as molecular
data continue to refine our understanding of species
boundaries and generic circumscriptions.
