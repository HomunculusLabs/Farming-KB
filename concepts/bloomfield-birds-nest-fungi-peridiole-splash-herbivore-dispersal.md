## Overview

Bird's nest fungi (Nidulariaceae, primarily *Cyathus* species) produce
tiny [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] shaped like champagne flutes that contain packets
of spores called peridioles. These fungi employ a two-stage dispersal
strategy: raindrops splash peridioles onto surrounding vegetation, and
herbivores grazing nearby consume the peridioles, passing the spores
through their digestive systems before depositing them in nutrient-rich
dung — the ideal substrate for the [[next-generation-sequencing-hidden-fungal-diversity]] to colonize.

## Fruiting Body Structure

The fruiting body of a bird's nest fungus consists of:

- A cup-shaped receptacle (peridium) typically 5–15 mm tall,
  resembling a miniature wine glass or champagne flute
- Multiple peridioles — lens-shaped spore packets — resting inside
  the cup, each 1–2 mm in diameter
- A funiculus, a coiled cord attaching each peridiole to the inner
  wall of the cup, which unfurls upon impact

The cups are typically found growing on [[microfungal-biodiversity-ecology-wood-debris-sampling]], twigs, or
decaying plant matter in clusters. Their small size and cryptic
growth habit make them easy to overlook, though they are common in
gardens, woodlands, and mulched areas.

## Stage One: Splash Dispersal

The dispersal mechanism exploits the kinetic energy of falling
raindrops:

1. A raindrop strikes the fluid-filled interior of the cup at the
   correct angle
2. The impact generates a splash that ejects one or more peridioles
   from the cup
3. Peridioles are launched upward and outward, landing on nearby
   blades of grass or low vegetation
4. The maximum splash distance is typically 0.5–1 meter from the
   parent fruiting body

The timing is critical: peridioles that fail to land on vegetation
— falling to [[savory-bare-ground-and-soil-exposure]] instead — "wait, and wait longer, and
dehydrate, and die." The system has no backup mechanism for peridioles
that miss their target. As [[nicholas-money]] observed, unsuccessful
spores are destined for "a swift passage to stinkhorn heaven or hell."

## Stage Two: Herbivore Consumption

Peridioles that successfully land on grass blades face their second
challenge: they must be eaten. The strategy depends on herbivores
— cattle, deer, horses, sheep — grazing in the vicinity:

- Herbivores consume the peridioles along with the grass
- The tough peridiole wall protects the spores during mastication
  and passage through the acidic stomach
- Spores survive the digestive tract, sometimes with enhanced
  germination due to scarification by stomach acids
- Deposited in warm manure, spores germinate in an environment
  rich in cellulose and nitrogen

"Cow feces offer perfect residence for a young bird's nest fungus."
The [[psilocybe-cubensis-cattle-dung-substrate-introduction-americas-guzman-1998]] provides precisely the conditions the fungus
requires: moisture, warmth, abundant [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], and a
substrate that few competing fungi can exploit as effectively.

## The Funiculus Attachment

Some bird's nest fungi possess an additional mechanism to improve
dispersal accuracy. The funiculus — a sticky, elastic cord — remains
attached to the peridiole after it is splashed from the cup. As the
peridiole flies through the air, the funiculus unwinds like a
grappling line. If the peridiole wraps around a blade of grass, the
sticky end adheres to the plant surface, anchoring the peridiole in
an elevated position where it is more likely to be encountered by
a passing herbivore.
