# Fungal Metal Transformation and Methylation Processes

Fungi do not merely accumulate heavy metals passively; they actively
transform the chemical state of metals through enzymatic and
non-enzymatic processes. These transformations include methylation,
dealkylation, reduction, and complexation reactions that can
significantly alter metal toxicity and mobility in ecosystems. The
ability of fungi to convert metals between chemical forms has profound
implications for metal movement through food chains and for

## Mercury Methylation

Fischer et al. (1995) showed that methyl mercury in fungal fruit bodies
ranged from 0.2 to 8 mg Hg per gram dry weight, depending on the fungal
species. Bioaccumulation factors for methyl mercury were on the order of
3 to 199, whereas those for total mercury were usually below 1. The
process of methylation thus dramatically increases the concentration
factor in fungal tissue. Since methyl mercury is the most toxic form of
mercury to vertebrates, the presence of methylation processes in fungi
in nature could have important implications for the movement and toxicity
of mercury in the environment and its transfer within food chains.

## Reduction Reactions

Morley et al. (1996) identified other metal transformation mechanisms
in fungi, including the reduction of silver and cobalt using NADPH and
NADH as electron donors. These enzymatic reduction reactions can convert
metals from more soluble oxidized forms to less soluble reduced forms,
potentially immobilizing metals within or around fungal biomass.
Methylation and dealkylation of organometallic compounds can occur
through enzymatic facilitation or abiotic degradation mediated by
fungal metabolites.

## Complexation with Lichen Acids

In lichen associations, metal-lichen acid complexes occur at the
nanometer scale that do not form in the surrounding environment. These
include complexes of ferric oxide in Acarospora sinoptica, aluminum
in Tremolecia atrata, copper oxalate in Acarospora rugulosa and
Lecidia theiodes, and copper-psoromic acid in Lecidella bullata. These
specific complexation reactions effectively immobilize metals in the
lichen thallus, reducing their bioavailability and toxicity to the
lichen symbionts.
