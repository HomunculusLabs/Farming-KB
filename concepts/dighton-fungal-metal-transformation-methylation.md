# Fungal Metal Transformation and Methylation Processes

Fungi do not merely accumulate heavy metals passively; they actively
transform the chemical state of metals through enzymatic and
non-enzymatic processes. These transformations include methylation,
dealkylation, reduction, and complexation reactions that can
significantly alter metal toxicity and mobility in ecosystems. The
ability of fungi to convert metals between chemical forms has profound
implications for metal movement through food chains and for
bioremediation strategies.

## Arsenic Methylation

Byrne et al. (1997) reviewed earlier work showing that numerous
basidiomycete fungi accumulated arsenic in their fruit bodies. Their
discovery of dimethylarsenic acid (DMA) in the ectomycorrhizal
basidiomycete Laccaria amethystina led to investigation of arsenic
transformations in fungal tissues. Methylarsonic acid (MA) was found in
Sarcosphaera coronaria, inorganic arsenic in Entoloma lividum, and a
mixture of inorganic arsenic, MA, DMA, and arsenobetaine (AB) in
Sarcodon imbricatus, Agaricus placomyces, and A. haemorrhoides.
Laboratory experimentation demonstrated that Agaricus placomyces
effected methylation of arsenic when grown on malt extract agar in
the presence of DMA. Arsenochlorine was accumulated by some species,
such as Sparassis crispa.

## Mercury Methylation

Fischer et al. (1995) showed that methyl mercury in fungal fruit bodies
ranged from 0.2 to 8 mg Hg per gram dry weight, depending on the fungal
species. Bioaccumulation factors for methyl mercury were on the order of
3 to 199, whereas those for total mercury were usually below 1. The
process of methylation thus dramatically increases the concentration
factor in fungal tissue. Since methyl mercury is the most toxic form of
mercury to vertebrates, the presence of methylation processes in fungi
in nature could have important implications for the movement and toxicity
of mercury in the environment and its transfer within food chains.

## Reduction Reactions

Morley et al. (1996) identified other metal transformation mechanisms
in fungi, including the reduction of silver and cobalt using NADPH and
NADH as electron donors. These enzymatic reduction reactions can convert
metals from more soluble oxidized forms to less soluble reduced forms,
potentially immobilizing metals within or around fungal biomass.
Methylation and dealkylation of organometallic compounds can occur
through enzymatic facilitation or abiotic degradation mediated by
fungal metabolites.

## Complexation with Lichen Acids

In lichen associations, metal-lichen acid complexes occur at the
nanometer scale that do not form in the surrounding environment. These
include complexes of ferric oxide in Acarospora sinoptica, aluminum
in Tremolecia atrata, copper oxalate in Acarospora rugulosa and
Lecidia theiodes, and copper-psoromic acid in Lecidella bullata. These
specific complexation reactions effectively immobilize metals in the
lichen thallus, reducing their bioavailability and toxicity to the
lichen symbionts.

## Metal Speciation and Toxicity

Changes in the chemical state of heavy metals can significantly affect
the toxicity of the metal to other organisms in the ecosystem. The
presence of a methylation process in fungi could increase the toxicity
of arsenic and mercury by converting them to organometallic forms that
are more readily bioaccumulated. Conversely, reduction reactions may
decrease toxicity by converting metals to less bioavailable forms.
Understanding these transformations is essential for predicting the
ecological consequences of metal contamination and for designing
effective bioremediation strategies.

## Organic Degradation Capabilities

The wood-decaying fungus Phanerochaete chrysosporium has demonstrated
the ability in field trials to degrade toxic organic compounds by
converting chlorine bound in organic form to harmless inorganic forms
and to degrade aromatic hydrocarbons to carbon dioxide and water
(Coghlan, 1994). Combined with metal ion accumulation and enzymatic
competence, these capabilities suggest fungi could be used to
detoxify contaminated land through multiple complementary mechanisms.

## Zinc Uptake Kinetics

Starling and Ross (1991) discussed the uptake of zinc by Penicillium
notatum, showing that zinc was an essential element with different
uptake kinetics at low and high solution concentrations. Zinc uptake
is competitively inhibited by cadmium and noncompetitively by copper,
indicating different interactions between metals and fungal physiology.
These competitive interactions mean that the presence of one metal
can influence the uptake and toxicity of another.
