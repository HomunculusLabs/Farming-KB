# Vilca, Sebil, Genista, and Minor New World Narcotic Plants

Beyond the major New World hallucinogens — peyote, mushrooms, ayahuasca, and San Pedro — a number of lesser-known plants have been used for their psychoactive properties by indigenous peoples. Many of these plants remain poorly characterized chemically, and in some cases their hallucinogenic status is inferred from ethnographic accounts rather than confirmed by modern pharmacology. This page covers vilca, sebil, genista, and several other minor New World narcotic plants.

## Vilca and Sebil (Anadenanthera colubrina)

- **Distribution:** Central and southern South America (Argentina, Peru, Bolivia, southern Brazil)
- **Family:** Fabaceae (bean family), subfamily Mimosoideae
- Snuffs believed to have been prepared from the beans of Anadenanthera colubrina and its variety cébil
- Used in regions where A. peregrina (yopo) does not naturally occur
- Seeds contain the same hallucinogenic principles as A. peregrina: N,N-dimethyltryptamine, N-monomethyltryptamine, 5-methoxydimethyltryptamine, and related bases
- An early Peruvian report dated approximately 1571 states that Inca medicine men foretold the future by communicating with the devil through the use of vilca (also called huilca)
- In Argentina, early Spaniards found the Comechingón Indians taking sebil "through the nose" to become intoxicated
- In another tribe, the same plant was chewed for endurance during long journeys or physical exertion
- Since these Indian cultures have disappeared, modern knowledge of vilca snuffs and their ceremonial use is limited
- The name "huilca" or "vilca" in Quechua languages is associated with sacredness and the supernatural
- The pre-Columbian snuffing tradition of the Andes is evidenced by archaeological snuff tablets and tubes found at sites throughout Peru, Bolivia, and northern Chile

## Masha-hari (Justicia pectoralis var. stenophylla)

- **Distribution:** Brazilian-Venezuelan frontier region (cultivated by Waiká Indians)
- **Family:** Acanthaceae (acanthus family); approximately 300 species of Justicia in the tropics and subtropics of both hemispheres
- A small herb with aromatic leaves that are occasionally dried, powdered, and mixed with Virola snuff
- Other species of Justicia have been reported as the sole source of a narcotic snuff in the region
- Hallucinogenic constituents have not yet been found in Justicia species
- If any species is utilized as the only ingredient of an intoxicating snuff, active constituents must be present
- Represents a case where ethnobotanical evidence of psychoactivity precedes chemical confirmation
- The role of Justicia as a Virola snuff additive may be primarily aromatic or flavor-modifying

## Genista (Cytisus canariensis)

- **Distribution:** Native to the Canary Islands; introduced to Mexico
- **Family:** Fabaceae (bean family); approximately 80 species of Cytisus in Atlantic islands, Europe, and Mediterranean
- Employed as a hallucinogen in the magic practices of Yaqui medicine men in northern Mexico
- Known also by the scientific name Genista canariensis; this species is the "genista" of florists
- Rarely does any nonindigenous plant find its way into the religious and magic customs of a people
- Plants of the genus Cytisus are rich in **cytisine**, an alkaloid of the lupine group
- Cytisine has never been pharmacologically demonstrated to have hallucinogenic activity
- Cytisine is known to be toxic, causing nausea, convulsions, and death through failure of respiration
- Some species are highly ornamental; some are poisonous
- The Yaqui use may represent a case where toxic effects are interpreted as visionary experience

## Sweet Flag (Acorus calamus)

- **Distribution:** Damp places in north and south temperate regions
- **Family:** Araceae (arum family); one of two species of Acorus
- Some indirect evidence that Indians of northern Canada may chew the rootstock as a hallucinogen
- Also used as a medicine and stimulant by northern Canadian indigenous peoples
- In excessive doses, known to induce strong visual hallucinations
- Intoxicating properties may be due to α-asarone and β-asarone, but chemistry and pharmacology remain poorly understood
- Sweet flag has a long history of use in Ayurvedic medicine and as a calamus root in traditional Western herbalism
- The β-asarone content varies significantly between geographic populations

## Shanshi (Coriaria thymifolia)

- **Distribution:** Andean mountains from Mexico to Chile; also found in Mediterranean area eastward to Japan and in New Zealand
- **Family:** Coriariaceae; Coriaria is the only known genus in this family (15 species, mostly shrubs)
- A widespread Andean shrub long recognized as very poisonous to cattle
- Recently reported as a hallucinogen used by peasants in Ecuador (local name: shanshi)
- The fruits are eaten for their intoxicating effects, which include the sensation of flight
- The psychoactive effects are possibly due to an unidentified glycoside
- Chemistry of this species is still poorly understood
- The sensation of flight reported by users parallels effects described for other New World hallucinogens

## Mescal Bean (Sophora secundiflora)

- **Distribution:** Northern Mexico, New Mexico, and Texas
- **Family:** Fabaceae (bean family); Sophora comprises about 50 species native to tropical and warm regions
- Also called red bean or coralillo; a shrub or small tree with silvery pods containing up to 6–7 red seeds
- Before the peyote religion spread north of the Rio Grande, at least 12 tribes practiced the vision-seeking Red Bean Dance
- Also known as the Wichita, Deer, or Whistle Dance; used the beans as an oracular, divinatory, and hallucinogenic medium
- The red bean drink was highly toxic, often resulting in death from overdose
- Arrival of the safer peyote cactus led natives to abandon the Red Bean Dance
- Today the seeds are used as an adornment on the uniform of the leader of the peyote ceremony
- Mescal beans found at archaeological sites dating before AD 1000; one site dates to 1500 BC
- The alkaloid **cytisine** (same as in Genista) causes nausea, convulsions, and death from asphyxiation through depressive action on the diaphragm
- Archaeological evidence points to a prehistoric cult or ceremony centered on these red beans

## Colorines (Erythrina spp.)

- **Distribution:** Mexico and tropical/subtropical regions of both hemispheres
- **Family:** Fabaceae (bean family); approximately 50 species of Erythrina
- Bright red beans resemble mescal beans and may be used as hallucinogens in some parts of Mexico
- Both beans are sometimes sold mixed together in herb markets
- Some species contain alkaloids of the isoquinoline type, which elicit activity resembling that of curare
- No alkaloids known to possess hallucinogenic properties have yet been found in Erythrina seeds
- The resemblance to mescal beans may lead to misidentification in ethnobotanical accounts

## Piule (Rhynchosia spp.)

- **Distribution:** Mexico and tropical/subtropical regions
- **Family:** Fabaceae (bean family); approximately 300 species of Rhynchosia
- Beautiful red and black seeds that may have been valued as a narcotic by ancient Mexicans
- Seeds appear pictured with mushrooms in the Tepantitla fresco of AD 300–400, suggesting hallucinogenic use
- Modern Indians in southern Mexico refer to them as piule, a name also applied to hallucinogenic morning glory seeds
- Seeds of some species have given positive alkaloid tests, but toxic principles have not been characterized
- Seeds of some species are important in folk medicine in several countries

## Patterns and Observations

- Many of these minor hallucinogens belong to the bean family (Fabaceae), which produces diverse alkaloid classes
- Cytisine appears in both Genista and Sophora (mescal bean), linking two geographically separated hallucinogen traditions
- Several of these plants (colorines, piule) may be mistakenly identified in ethnographic accounts due to visual similarity of seeds
- The gap between ethnobotanical evidence of psychoactivity and chemical confirmation remains wide for many species
- Some plants (genista, mescal bean) may produce toxic effects interpreted as visionary rather than true hallucinogenic experiences
- The use of introduced species (genista from Canary Islands) in indigenous ceremonies is exceptionally rare
