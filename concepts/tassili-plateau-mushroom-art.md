# Tassili Plateau Mushroom Art

The Tassili-n-Ajjer Plateau in the Sahara Desert of southern Algeria contains what may be the earliest archaeological evidence for the use of hallucinogenic mushrooms in human culture. Terence McKenna devotes significant attention to these rock paintings in Food of the Gods, arguing that they provide visual confirmation of his theories about the mushroom-using pastoral cultures of ancient Africa.

## The Plateau

Archaeological evidence for the speculative ideas about mushroom-using cultures can be found in the Sahara Desert of southern Algeria in an area called the Tassili-n-Ajjer Plateau. A curious geological formation, the plateau is like a labyrinth, a vast badlands of stone escarpments that have been cut by the wind into many perpendicular narrow corridors.

Aerial photographs give the eerie impression of an abandoned city. The plateau today is a barren wasteland, but during the African Humid Period (roughly 11,000 to 5,000 years ago), it was a lush savanna teeming with cattle, antelope, and human settlements.

## The Mushroom Shamans

In the Tassili-n-Ajjer, rock paintings date from the late Neolithic to as recently as two thousand years ago. Here are the earliest known depictions of shamans with large numbers of grazing cattle. The shamans are dancing with fists full of mushrooms and also have mushrooms sprouting out of their bodies.

In one instance they are shown running joyfully, surrounded by the geometric structures of their hallucinations. The pictorial evidence seems incontrovertible. At Matalen-Amazar and Ti-n-Tazarift on the Tassili, the dancing shamans clearly have mushrooms in their hands and sprouting from their bodies.

The combination of mushrooms, cattle, dancing figures, and geometric hallucinatory imagery creates a complete iconographic program that matches McKenna's description of the original mushroom-using pastoral culture of the African grasslands.

## The Bee-Faced Shaman

One of the most famous images from the Tassili is the so-called "bee-faced mushroom shaman." This figure, depicted in profile, shows a humanoid form with a mushroom-shaped head or headdress, in a dancing posture, holding what appear to be mushrooms. The figure has been interpreted by McKenna and others as a shamanic practitioner in the midst of an ecstatic mushroom experience.

The bee-faced quality of the shaman connects the mushroom imagery to the ancient association between honey and psychoactive plants. Honey has long been used as a preservative for mushrooms in various cultures. In Mexico honey has long been used to preserve psilocybin-containing mushrooms. The fact that Glaukos, the blue-gray one in the Minoan myth, fell into a honey pot and was preserved there until the time of his resurrection is highly suggestive.

Wasson mentions the frequent allusions to honey in connection with Soma in the Rig Veda. He rejects the notion that mead, the fermented form of honey, could have been the basis of Soma: "Honey, madhu, is mentioned frequently in the Rig Veda but mead never. Honey is cited for its sweetness and also is often applied as a metaphor of enhancement to Soma."

## Running Figures

The mushroom runners from Tassili are depicted in full sprint, surrounded by what appear to be geometric hallucinatory patterns -- dots, lines, and zigzag forms that closely resemble the visual phenomena reported by modern subjects under the influence of psilocybin. These running figures suggest the ecstatic, kinetic quality of the mushroom experience.

The sense of motion captured in these paintings is remarkable. The runners appear to be in a state of trance or ecstasy, their bodies elongated and transformed by the experience. The geometric patterns that surround them are not decorative but appear to represent the visual content of the hallucinatory experience itself -- what McKenna would later call the "hyperspace" encountered under the influence of tryptamine compounds.

## Comparison with Other Rock Art

Images similar to those of the Tassili occur in pre-Columbian Peruvian textiles. In these textiles the shamans hold objects that may be mushrooms but may also be chopping tools. With the Tassili frescoes, however, the case is clear. The mushrooms are unmistakable -- they are held in the hand, sprout from the body, and are the central focus of the ritual activity depicted.

The rock art of the Tassili thus represents the oldest known visual documentation of what McKenna calls the "mushroom mystery" -- the ancient partnership between humans, cattle, and psychoactive fungi that he argues gave birth to human consciousness, language, and religion.

## The Migration of Mushroom Culture

The pastoral peoples who produced the Tassili paintings gradually moved out of Africa over a long period of time, from twenty thousand to seven thousand years ago. Wherever they went, their pastoral lifestyle went with them.

The Red Sea was landlocked during much of this time. Lowered sea levels meant that the boot of Arabia was backed up against the African continent. Land bridges at both ends of the Red Sea were utilized by some of these African pastoralists to enter the Fertile Crescent and Asia Minor, where they intermingled with hunter-gatherer populations already present.

The pastoral mode had been well established across the ancient Near East by the time the Indo-European migrations began, carrying with them the memory and practice of the mushroom mystery that would eventually be encoded in the Soma cult of the Vedic peoples.

## The Round Head Civilization

Before the arrival of the cattle pastoralists, the Tassili was home to the so-called "Round Head" civilization, named for the characteristic style of their human figures. These earlier paintings suggest a society of hunter-gatherers in which mushrooms may also have played a role.

The transition from Round Head to pastoralist art styles may mark the shift from a hunter-gatherer mushroom culture to the cattle-dependent pastoral form that McKenna sees as the cradle of civilization. The continuity of mushroom imagery across these different cultural phases suggests an unbroken tradition of hallucinogenic mushroom use spanning thousands of years.

The Round Head Period is believed to have begun very early and probably ended before the seventh millennium B.P. Henri Lhote estimates that the Round Head Period lasted several thousand years, placing its beginning somewhere near the start of the ninth millennium. A painting from Inaouanrhat in the Tassili includes a wonderful image of a dancing woman who, with outstretched arms and horns extended horizontally on either side of her head, is the embodiment of the Great Horned Goddess.

## Paradise Found?

McKenna suggests the Tassili-n-Ajjer of 12,000 B.C. may well have been the partnership paradise whose loss has created one of the most persistent and poignant mythological motifs -- the nostalgia for paradise, the idea of a lost golden age of plenty, partnership, and social balance. The rise of language, partnership society, and complex religious ideas may have occurred not far from the area where humans emerged -- the game-filled, mushroom-dotted grasslands and savannahs of tropical and subtropical Africa.

## Significance for the Stoned Ape Hypothesis

The Tassili plateau paintings are significant because they provide the earliest direct visual evidence linking mushroom use to pastoral culture and shamanic practice. They demonstrate that the human-mushroom-cattle symbiosis was not merely hypothetical but was a lived reality documented by the people who practiced it. For McKenna, these paintings are nothing less than the oldest surviving records of the origin of human religion and consciousness.
