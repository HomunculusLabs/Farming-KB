## Ectomycorrhizal Fungal Ecology

## Overview

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi form symbiotic associations primarily with woody plants in temperate, boreal, and some tropical forests. Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizae, ECM fungi do not penetrate root cells but form a sheath (mantle) around root tips and a Hartig net of intercellular hyphae between root cortical cells. This distinct structural arrangement supports different ecological functions and community dynamics.

## Structural Features

The ECM association involves:
- **Fungal mantle**: Dense layer of hyphae enveloping the root tip, acting as a physical barrier and modified absorptive surface
- **Hartig net**: Labyrinthine network of hyphae between root cortical cells, the primary site of [[fungal-enzymatic-diversity-decomposition]] is critical in ecosystems where [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] shifts as stands mature. This succession reflects both changes in soil chemistry and competitive interactions among fungal species.

## Interplant Connections

ECM networks connect trees of the same or different species:
- Read et al. (1985) showed preferential 14C transfer between pine seedlings sharing the same ECM association compared to plants with arbuscular mycorrhizae
- Simard et al. (1997) demonstrated field [[mycelial-network-resource-sharing-plants]]
- Providing established nutrient pools to establishing individuals
- Enhancing seedling survival during competitive establishment
- Onguene and Kuyper (2002) showed ECM networks enhance seedling survival and mycorrhiza formation in Cameroon rain forests

### Nutrient Cycling

ECM fungi influence nutrient cycling through:
- Competition with saprotrophic fungi for organic matter (Gadgil and Gadgil effect)
- Enzymatic mobilization of nutrients from organic matter
- Hyphal translocation connecting spatially separated nutrient sources and sinks
- Fruiting body production returning nutrients to soil surface

### Carbon Sequestration

ECM associations represent a significant carbon sink:
- 10-20%+ of tree photosynthate allocated to fungal partners
- Wu et al. (2002) showed 24% of underground 14C in pine allocated to extraradical hyphae
- Coniferous trees allocate approximately twice as much carbon to roots as deciduous species
- ECM hyphal biomass contributes to stable soil carbon pools
