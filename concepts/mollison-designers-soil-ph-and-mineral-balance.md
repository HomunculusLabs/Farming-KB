# Soil pH and Mineral Balance

Soil chemistry is the invisible foundation upon which all terrestrial
permaculture systems rest. Bill Mollison treated soil pH and mineral balance
not as abstract academic subjects but as practical design considerations that
directly determine which plants will thrive, which nutrients will be available,
and how effectively biological processes can cycle energy through the system.

## Understanding Soil pH

Soil pH is a measure of the concentration of hydrogen ions in the soil
solution, expressed on a logarithmic scale from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most
alkaline). A pH of 7.0 is neutral. Most agricultural soils fall between 5.0
and 8.5. The pH scale is logarithmic, meaning a change of one unit represents
a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. This makes even small pH
shifts biologically significant.

pH affects [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] and decomposition, function best in
a pH range of 6.0 to 7.5. Below 5.5, aluminium and manganese become soluble at
toxic levels, while phosphorus, calcium, and molybdenum become increasingly
unavailable. Above 7.5, iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and boron become
less available.

The optimum pH for most crops lies between 6.0 and 6.8, a range that balances
availability of all major and micronutrients. However, specific crops have
adapted to more extreme conditions. Blueberries, cranberries, and potatoes
prefer acidic soils (pH 4.5 to 5.5), while asparagus, alfalfa, and many
mediterranean herbs prefer slightly alkaline conditions (pH 7.0 to 8.0).

## The Calcium-Magnesium Ratio

One of the most important concepts in soil [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]] sites. This ratio
profoundly affects soil structure, drainage, [[chelation-and-nutrient-availability]].

Calcium is the dominant cation on most productive agricultural soils. It
promotes flocculation (the aggregation of clay particles into crumbs), which
creates well-structured soil with good drainage and aeration. Magnesium, when
present in excess, has the opposite effect: it causes clay particles to
disperse, creating tight, impermeable soils that resist root penetration and
water infiltration.

The ideal calcium-to-magnesium ratio on the [[cation-exchange-capacity]] is
approximately 7:1 to 10:1 by weight. When magnesium dominates (ratios below
3:1), soils become sticky when wet and hard when dry. Mollison notes that many
degraded agricultural soils suffer from magnesium excess caused by excessive
application of [[dolomitic-limestone]], which contains both [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]]
carbonate.

Correcting imbalance requires selective liming with high-calcium limestone
(calcitic lime, CaCO3) rather than dolomite (CaMg(CO3)2). Gypsum (calcium
sulfate, CaSO4) can also supply calcium without raising pH, making it useful
where soils are already alkaline but structurally poor.

## Cation Exchange Capacity

Cation exchange capacity (CEC) is a measure of the soil's ability to hold
positively charged nutrient ions (cations) against leaching. Soils with high
CEC can store large quantities of calcium, magnesium, potassium, ammonium,
and [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]], releasing them gradually to plant roots.

CEC is determined primarily by clay content [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]. Fine clay
minerals (especially montmorillonite and vermiculite) have very high CEC,
while sandy soils have low CEC. Organic matter contributes significantly to
CEC because [[nitrogen-fixing-trees]] can access minerals from subsoil layers and bring them
to the surface through leaf fall. [[permaculture-design]]
