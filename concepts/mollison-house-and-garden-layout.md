# Mollison: House and Garden Layout

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 12)

## Principles of House-Garden Integration

Mollison emphasises that the house and garden should be designed as an integrated system providing full nutrition, excellent house climate, and low maintenance.

The placement of kitchen, mudroom, compost area, and cool air source with trellis crop all contribute to a design that minimises energy inputs while maximising comfort and productivity.

The house-garden layout is the practical expression of permaculture zone planning, with zone 0 (the house) intimately connected to zone 1 (the garden).

## Zone 1 Garden Design

The kitchen garden occupies zone 1, the area most frequently visited and easiest to maintain.

Mollison places the herb garden, kitchen garden, and small intensive beds closest to the kitchen door for daily access.

The herb spiral and keyhole beds are positioned for easy harvest while preparing meals.

Compost areas are close to the kitchen for convenient disposal of scraps but downwind from living spaces.

## Trellis Systems for Climate Control

Trellis crop in mesothermal climates is an important element of house-garden design.

Peas, beans, and cucurbits will trellis, as will varieties of tomatoes.

Trellis over the garden is inappropriate (unlike in deserts); instead, vertical zigzag, inverted V, or house wall and garden fence trellis is best.

Alignment should be north-south where possible for even sun effects.

Trellis on the north side of buildings (in the southern hemisphere) creates a cool air space, while trellis on multi-storey buildings extends growing space vertically.

## Cool Climate Garden Elements

Small fruit such as currants, gooseberry, raspberry, or trellis blackberry is a feature of cool-area gardens.

Six to fifteen plants of 7-8 species yields a great deal of fresh or frozen food of high vitamin C value.

Many such rows can act as windbreak within the garden.

These berry plantings form the transition between intensive garden beds and the broader food forest system.

## Kitchen Placement

The kitchen should be placed on the sunny side of the house with direct access to the garden through a mudroom.

The mudroom serves as a transition space where garden produce can be cleaned and prepared.

Direct access from kitchen to garden eliminates the energy waste of transporting produce through the house.

Mollison's ideal layout shows the kitchen and mudroom positioned between the house interior and the garden, with the compost area accessible from the kitchen but screened from living areas.

## Thermal Mass and Passive Climate Control

The house-garden layout works with thermal mass principles.

Earth banks behind the house store heat and release it slowly.

Ponds in front of the house moderate temperature extremes.

Deciduous trees shade the house in summer and allow light through in winter.

Trellis on walls provides seasonal shade control.

## Compost Integration

The compost area is positioned for convenience from the kitchen while being downwind of living spaces.

Chicken tractors can be rotated through the garden to scratch and manure beds.

The compost system receives kitchen scraps, garden waste, and animal manures in a closed loop.

Mollison emphasises that the compost area is as essential to the house as the kitchen itself.

## Garden Access and Circulation

Paths in the intensive garden should be designed for efficient movement.

Keyhole beds eliminate unnecessary path space while providing access to all growing areas.

Mollison advocates recessed paths with raised beds, where paths are dug down for drainage and beds are raised.

This creates better drainage, easier access, and more growing surface area.

## Integration with Animal Systems

Chickens, ducks, and rabbits are integrated into the house-garden system through movable housing and rotational grazing.

The chicken tractor system allows birds to prepare beds, control pests, and add manure before planting.

Duck ponds can be integrated into the water system of the garden.

Rabbit housing can be positioned to provide manure for nearby beds.

## Water Systems

Greywater from the house is routed to garden irrigation.

Roof water collection feeds into the garden water system.

Swales and contour beds manage water flow through the garden.

The water system is designed for gravity flow wherever possible, eliminating the need for pumps.
