# Christus, Eros, and Osiris in McKenna's Mythological Framework

[[terence-mckenna]] wove a complex mythological narrative connecting the suppression of psychedelic plant knowledge in Western history to a grand arc of spiritual displacement. In his framework, the figures of Christus (Christ), Eros (the god of love and desire), and Osiris (the Egyptian god of the underworld and rebirth) represent successive cultural configurations of the same divine principle, a condensed body of spiritual knowledge that has been displaced, hidden, and periodically rediscovered throughout human history. This framework is not presented as academic history but as a mythopoetic interpretation that reveals deeper patterns underlying the documented historical record.

## The Hellenistic-Christian-Judaic Tradition

McKenna identifies what he calls the "Hellenistic-Christian-Judaic tradition" as the primary force that disrupted humanity's direct [[i-thou-relationship-with-psychedelic-entities]] plants and the gnosis they provide. This tradition, building a "triumph of mechanism," succeeded in transforming the world through practical achievements, including the alchemical dreams of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, gene transplants, and the transformation of elements. However, it simultaneously alienated humanity from direct experiential knowledge of the divine dimensions accessible through entheogenic plants.

The destruction of the libraries of the Hellenistic world dispersed ancient knowledge and shattered the astronomical machinery that had been the work of centuries. This loss was not merely intellectual but represented a severing of humanity's connection to cosmological understanding that had been cultivated through direct experiential practices, including the use of psychoactive plants. By "ignorance," McKenna means this entire tradition and its consequences: the replacement of direct gnosis with mediated belief systems. The triumph of mechanism was real and productive, but it came at the cost of a different kind of knowing, one based on direct experience rather than abstract reasoning.

The Hellenistic-Christian-Judaic tradition succeeded because its mechanistic approach produced tangible results: architecture, engineering, navigation, medicine, and eventually industrial technology. These achievements created a civilization whose power was undeniable, and whose epistemological assumptions, privileging empirical observation and rational deduction over direct ecstatic experience, became the default framework for understanding reality. The cost of this success was the marginalization and eventual suppression of the older, plant-based approaches to divine knowledge.

The key insight in McKenna's analysis is that this was not a neutral evolution of human understanding but a deliberate displacement. The institutional structures of the Hellenistic-Christian-Judaic tradition, particularly the Church, actively suppressed the competing epistemology of direct gnosis because it represented an existential threat to institutional authority. If divine knowledge could be accessed directly through plants, without the mediation of priesthood, scripture, or ritual, then the entire apparatus of institutional religion became unnecessary.

## The Conquest and the Body of Osiris

McKenna's narrative reaches a pivotal moment with the Spanish conquest of the Americas. Having conquered the New World and driven its people into cultural fragmentation and diaspora, the European inheritors of the Hellenistic-Christian-Judaic tradition came unexpectedly upon what McKenna calls "the body of Osiris, the condensed body of Eros, in the mountains of Mexico where Eros has retreated at the coming of the Christus."

This is a densely layered metaphor. Osiris, in Egyptian mythology, was dismembered by his brother Set and reassembled by his wife Isis. The "body" of Osiris, scattered across the landscape, parallels the scattered fragments of psychedelic knowledge that survived the destruction of [[entheogens-in-ancient-cultures]]. Eros, as the principle of creative desire and union, represents the experiential dimension of this knowledge, the direct encounter with the divine that the mushroom facilitates. Its "retreat" at the coming of Christus represents the withdrawal of this experiential access as mediated religion established itself as the sole legitimate channel to the divine.

The metaphor of dismemberment and reassembly carries additional significance in the context of mycology. Mushrooms reproduce through the dispersal of spores, each carrying the complete genetic information of the organism. The scattered spores of psilocybin-containing mushrooms, surviving across diverse ecosystems from Mesoamerica to Southeast Asia to Europe, represent a literal dismemberment that nevertheless preserves the potential for reassembly when conditions permit. The survival of [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]] in remote Oaxacan villages is thus not merely a cultural persistence but a mycological reality: the organism itself endures through dispersal.

## Theophagy and the Franciscan Encounter

The Franciscans who accompanied the conquistadors assumed they held an absolute monopoly on theophagy, the eating of God through the Eucharist. Yet in the New World they encountered people who called a [[mckenna-mushroom-teonanacatl-franciscan-theophagy-and-oaxaca-survival]], "the flesh of the gods." This discovery threatened the theological foundations of the conquest, as it suggested that direct divine communion through plant substances was not merely possible but had been practiced for millennia by cultures the Europeans regarded as primitive.

The confrontation between Franciscan theophagy and Mesoamerican theophagy was not merely a theological dispute but a clash between two fundamentally different approaches to divine knowledge. The Franciscan position held that divine communion was mediated through institutional ritual, requiring priestly authority and sanctioned substances. The indigenous position held that divine communion was available directly through the mushroom, requiring no institutional intermediary. The power differential between the two positions determined the historical outcome, but not, in McKenna's view, the ultimate truth of either claim.

The Inquisition was mobilized to suppress the old religion, pushing it into the mountains of Oaxaca, where it survived in only a few villages until Valentina and Gordon Wasson found it there in the 1950s. The suppression was nearly complete; McKenna notes that the "classic mushroom cults of Mexico were destroyed by the coming of the Spanish conquest." What survived was a remnant, a hidden thread maintained by a few communities in remote areas, preserved not through written records but through oral tradition and direct experiential practice.

## The Finding of the Mushroom and the Unleashing

McKenna frames the [[wasson-rediscovery-of-psilocybin]] the Psilocybe mushroom cult in Mexico as the unleashing of something that had been deliberately suppressed. The mushroom, as a "thing of pure information" analogous to the buried texts of the Nag Hammadi library, contained consciousness that had been lying dormant. Once the information was activated in human minds, the symbiote came alive. The parallel to Philip K. Dick's Valis is explicit: a creature of pure information, buried as static text, becomes active when read and internalized by conscious minds.

The Wasson expedition of 1955-1956, and the subsequent identification of psilocybin by Albert Hofmann in 1958, represent for McKenna not merely a scientific discovery but a historical turning point. The knowledge that had been suppressed for four hundred years was once again available, and its availability in the form of a specific chemical compound, rather than a culturally guarded secret, meant that it could no longer be contained by geographical isolation or institutional suppression. The synthesis of psilocybin and LSD in the laboratory removed the dependence on natural sources and opened the [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]] to anyone with access to the chemicals.

## Eros, Osiris, and the Psychedelic Continuum

In McKenna's mythological framework, Eros and Osiris are not merely ancient gods but represent a continuous thread of direct experiential spirituality that predates and transcends the institutional religions that replaced it. Eros, as the principle of love, desire, and creative union, connects to the open-hearted quality of the [[dmt-black-hole-effect-and-ineffability-of-tryptamine-experience]]. The entities encountered in the DMT state "bathe one in love," a love that is "not erotic but it is open-hearted" and "certainly feels good."

Osiris, as the god who is dismembered and reassembled, represents the pattern of knowledge that is fragmented and scattered through history, waiting to be reconstituted. The body of Osiris, the condensed body of Eros, is the accumulated wisdom of psychedelic plant use across millennia, preserved in the molecular structures of the plants themselves and in the cultural practices surrounding their use. Its "retreat" at the coming of Christus represents the withdrawal of direct experiential gnosis as institutional religion established mediated access to the divine through priesthood, ritual, and scripture.

The progression from Eros to Christus to Osiris, in McKenna's reading, is not a linear historical sequence but a cyclical pattern of revelation, suppression, and rediscovery. Each configuration represents the same fundamental knowledge dressed in different cultural forms. The knowledge itself, the direct experiential access to the divine through plant substances, is invariant; what changes is the cultural framework within which it is expressed and the institutional response it provokes.

## The Mushroom Consciousness as Pure Information

McKenna's concept of the mushroom consciousness as "a thing of pure information" is one of his most distinctive contributions to entheogenic philosophy. Like the buried Gnostic texts at Nag Hammadi, the mushroom's knowledge exists as a latent potential that requires activation through human consciousness. The parallel to Philip K. Dick's Valis is instructive: Dick described the Logos as "a creature of pure information" that was "buried in the ground at Nag Hammadi" and existed as static information until the texts were translated and read in 1947, at which point "the symbiote came alive."

McKenna extends this logic to the mushroom: "like the mushroom consciousness, Dick imagined it to be a thing of pure information. The mushroom consciousness is the consciousness of the Other in hyperspace, which means in dream and in the psilocybin trance, at the quantum foundation of being, in the human future, and after death." The crucial claim is that all of these locations, previously thought to be "discrete and separate," are revealed in the tryptamine experience to be "part of a single continuum."

## Borges and the Sect of the Phoenix

McKenna draws on Jorge Luis Borges' story "The Sect of the Phoenix" to illustrate the nature of the psychedelic knowledge tradition. Borges describes a secret rite that is universal across all human cultures, transmitted across generations, trivial in execution yet sacred, always somewhat ridiculous, and performed furtively. The Secret is known by many names, and all words allude to it. The adepts do not speak of it. Borges never explicitly names the Secret.

McKenna connects this to another Borges story, "The Aleph," which describes a point in space that contains all other points, allowing the observer to see everything in the universe simultaneously. The Aleph, in McKenna's reading, represents the [[lsd-psychedelic-experience-guide]] itself: a single point of access to totality. The Sect of the Phoenix, then, is the worldwide community of those who have undergone this experience and who maintain its knowledge through secret practice.

When McKenna and his companions asked the mushroom in the Amazon why they had been chosen as ambassadors, the mushroom reportedly answered: "Because you did not believe in anything. Because you have never given over your belief to anyone." This response positions the psychedelic experience outside all institutional belief systems, accessible only to those who approach it without prior ideological commitment. The mushroom's answer suggests that the psychedelic gnosis is not compatible with any fixed belief system because it reveals reality directly, making mediated beliefs unnecessary and potentially obstructive.

## The Wasson-Hofmann Sequence and Historical Impact

The sequence of events following the Wasson expedition deserves emphasis in McKenna's framework. Wasson's 1957 Life magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" introduced [[cap-versus-stem-alkaloid-distribution-in-psilocybin-mushrooms]] to Western popular culture for the first time. Albert Hofmann, the Swiss chemist who had first synthesized LSD in 1943, travelled to Mexico in 1958 and successfully isolated and identified [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] as the active compounds. By 1959, Hofmann had synthesized both compounds in the laboratory, making them available for [[psilocybin-isolation-and-early-scientific-research]] without dependence on natural sources.
