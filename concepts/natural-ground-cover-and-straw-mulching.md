# Natural Ground Cover and Straw Mulching

Ground cover cultivation and straw mulching form the practical foundation of
soil management in [[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s natural farming system. Rather than
managing soil through [[cultivator-compost-preparation-phase-methods]], and clean
cultivation, Fukuoka relied on living ground covers of clover and other green
manure plants combined with the continuous application of uncut straw to build
and maintain soil fertility without any external inputs.

## Why Ground Cover Replaces Fertilizer

Conventional soil management turns soil into mere mineral matter through
repeated plowing, weeding, and chemical application. While these practices may
increase yields in the short term, they progressively destroy soil structure
and biological activity. The result is dead soil that requires ever-increasing
fertilizer inputs to maintain productivity.

Fukuoka's approach reverses this degradation by establishing living ground
covers that perform the functions that chemical fertilizers and mechanical
tillage attempt to replace. The ground cover system operates simultaneously
across fields, gardens, and hillside orchards, enriching all areas at once
rather than managing each in isolation.

When Fukuoka set out to revive his father's citrus orchard after World War II,
the topsoil had washed away entirely, leaving only red clay. Passive efforts to
restore the trees through [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

The dense clover mat suppresses weed growth through light competition rather
than eradication. Weeds that do emerge within the clover tend to be beneficial
species that contribute to soil biology rather than problematic competitors.
The clover cover protects the soil surface from erosion by rain and wind,
prevents moisture loss through evaporation, and moderates soil temperature
fluctuations.

In the rice-barley succession system, clover serves a dual role. During the
grain-growing seasons, it provides fertility and weed suppression. When the
field is briefly flooded to weaken the clover and allow rice shoots to
establish, the dying clover contributes organic matter and releases its stored
nitrogen, creating a natural nutrient pulse precisely when the rice crop needs
it most.

## Straw Mulching as the Primary Input

The only regular input in Fukuoka's system is uncut straw. After each harvest,
whether rice or winter grain, the entire straw is scattered directly over the
field without chopping. This uncut straw performs multiple essential functions.

First, the straw layer suppresses weed germination by blocking light from
reaching weed seeds in the soil. Weeds that do manage to germinate through the
straw tend to be weak and easily outcompeted by the crop.

Second, the straw moderates soil moisture and temperature. During hot, dry
periods, the mulch layer reduces evaporation and keeps soil temperatures lower.
During cold periods, it provides insulation against frost.

Third, as the straw decomposes, it feeds soil microorganisms, earthworms, and
other soil life. This biological activity creates soil aggregates, improves
drainage and water retention, and gradually releases nutrients in forms that
plants can absorb. The decomposition process also generates humic acids that
improve soil structure and [[cation-exchange-capacity]].

Fourth, the straw provides [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects. Spiders, ground
beetles, and other predators that control pest populations thrive in the straw
mulch layer, establishing the biological pest control that eliminates the need
for pesticides.
