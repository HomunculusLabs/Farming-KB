# Catch and Store Energy: Landscape Capital and the Four Storages

## Overview

The second [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] — Catch and Store Energy — addresses the most fundamental challenge facing human civilisation: we have been living by consuming global capital in a reckless manner that would send any business bankrupt. This principle deals with the capture and long-term storage of energy as savings and investment to build natural and human capital, in contrast to Principle 3 ([[holmgren-obtain-a-yield-detailed]]), which addresses income generation for immediate needs.

The icon of sunshine captured in a bottle suggests the preserving of seasonal surplus and a myriad of other traditional and novel ways to catch and store energy. The proverb "make hay while the sun shines" reminds us that we have limited time to catch and store energy before seasonal or episodic abundance dissipates.

## The Modern Vulnerability

In modern affluent societies, the flow of energy in forms useful to people — food, materials, and services — has become so reliably available that [[fukuoka-three-dimensional-solar-energy-capture-rice-paddy]] and [[holmgren-built-environment-household-energy-storage]] have ceased to be major concerns. So long as people have a flow of money to buy, the provision of basic needs has been left to farmers, mining engineers, and others.

In the extreme development of modern urban living, no food or fuel is stored in the house and purchasing power is dependent on credit, which itself is dependent on permanent employment. At the same time, economic rationalism in business and government has led to the decline of large-scale stores of food, fuels, materials, and essential spare parts in the interests of economic efficiency, which increases the likelihood of disruption and even disaster.

The vulnerability of modern systems to slight disturbance was highlighted by the Y2K concern and the enormous cost of reactive prevention strategies. In an emerging low-energy world, we will rediscover opportunities to harvest and store immediately available renewable energies and wasted resources across our rural and urban landscapes, our households, and our local economies. This will be essential to avoid disasters from inevitable disruptions to energy and resource supply lines.

Important sources of energy currently poorly used but commonly available for building greater personal and local self-reliance include:

- **Solar energy**: simple drying of wood and crops, [[passive-solar-design]], water heaters and photovoltaic panels
- **Wind energy**: pumping water and power generation
- **Biomass**: sustainable management of [[pdc-trees-and-forests]] for fuel and construction materials
- **Run-off water**: irrigation, aquaculture, and power generation

In addition, we are surrounded by wastes from agriculture, forestry, and industry — particularly [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] — that can be used for soil improvement, fuel, and animal feed. These manufactured resources are not necessarily renewable, but they can be tapped in the transition to low-energy systems.

## Energy Laws and Entropy

Understanding energy laws is foundational to the permaculture concept. Throughout the universe, energy spreads from centres of concentration to vacant regions, where it tends to remain dispersed and diluted. Energy of high quality degrades into lower-quality forms, reducing its power to affect change. This tendency to disorder is called entropy and affects every living and non-living system.

However, self-organising systems — primarily living ones — can capture and transform a limited proportion of the energy they absorb. This energy is held in storages of varying form and durability for use in self-maintenance, growth, and capture of more energy. This stored energy is generally higher in quality than the source from which it was derived and is capable of driving a wider range of processes than the original source energy.

For living systems, available energy flows are mostly erratic, limited in quantity, and low in quality. Living systems designed to optimise the efficiency of energy transformation and storage tend to prevail through evolution.

## How Nature Catches and Stores Energy

For billions of years life was confined to shallow seas fringing barren landmasses. About half a billion years ago, marine life began colonising the land and creating soil life capable of taming this wasteland. The evolution of terrestrial ecosystems has maximised the power of nature to use both climatic and earth energies.

Solar energy drives photosynthesis, transforming water and carbon dioxide into carbohydrates — the start of the chemical energy supply chain for all living things. Solar energy also drives weather and climate systems delivering energy as rain, wind, lightning, and fire. The pure water in rainfall is embodied solar energy because solar heat evaporates water to create atmospheric moisture for precipitation.

Equally important is the slower nuclear power inside the earth, driving tectonic plate movement, mountain building, vulcanism, and the delivery of rock minerals critical to soil fertility. Subduction of oceanic sediments creates fossil fuels and concentrates rare minerals into ore bodies.

Changes in either climatic or geophysical energies result in radical reorganisation of terrestrial landscapes and ecosystems. These large-scale system changes can cause massive erosion, biodiversity loss, and habitat destruction, as well as building and fertilising new soil and allowing rapid evolution of new life forms. Continental ice sheets expand and contract in pulsing rhythms called ice ages — destroying whole living landscapes but grinding vast amounts of rock into glacial dust, a mixed mineral fertiliser of great potency.

Geologically young regions with recent mountain building and vulcanism tend to be much more biologically productive and have supported large human populations despite their vulnerability to natural disasters. Geologically old regions like most of Australia tend to have low biological productivity. The biological productivity and human support capacity of geologically young landscapes derive from their ability to capture and hold water, essential minerals, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].

## The Four Landscape Storages

[[mollison-climate-zones-permaculture-strategies]] for catching and storing energy in landscape can be grouped under four broad headings:

### 1. Water

Water is perhaps most easily understood as a limiting factor. The erratic nature of rainfall and the constant needs of microbes, plants, and animals for moisture have caused landscapes to evolve as efficient rainwater storages. Vegetation holds substantial quantities of rainwater in its tissues and in the humid air trapped by forest canopies. Forest litter and mulch act as an open sponge. The balance of soil chemistry and humus development provide more stable moisture supply, while deep subsoils provide very stable moisture storage.

The pattern of these storages runs from more ephemeral to more permanent as water moves down under the influence of gravity. Small catchment dams have more benefits and fewer adverse environmental effects than large reservoirs. High water has potential energy to drive mechanical processes. Shallow aquaculture systems on fertile lowlands always produce more protein than grazing or dairying.

### 2. Living Soil

Living soil with good structure and high humus content has a huge capacity to store water, mineral nutrients, and carbon. The differences between soils in their storage capacity is the greatest single factor in the productivity of terrestrial ecosystems and agriculture.

As we have burned nearly half the world's fossil carbon, we have also "burned" over half of the soil carbon in the world's farmlands. This invisible burning of "young coal" has been caused by ploughing that inverts the soil and the use of artificial soluble fertilisers. These accelerate the smaller losses created from export without recycling of organic material from farmland. Soil carbon is the fuel of the largely invisible soil ecosystem that in turn regulates plant nutrition.

Humus is perhaps nature's greatest "invention" because it increases the capacity of soils to store mineral nutrients as well as water and carbon. There are good ecological reasons for the veneration of humus in the organic, biodynamic, and permaculture movements. Under favourable conditions, humus storages can be stable for hundreds and even thousands of years. Wes Jackson of the Land Institute has referred to the loss of this ancient humus from American prairie cropping soils as "the mining of young coal."

The rebuilding of humus in the world's cropping soils should be seen as one of the two great tasks for humanity, alongside reforestation of catchments and degraded rangelands.

### 3. Trees

Trees are especially significant as carbon stores because of their ability to keep accumulating carbon as wood for hundreds or even thousands of years. This long-lived storage of carbon in [[coppice-and-pollard-systems]] is one of the best measures of the ability of terrestrial ecosystems to continue catching and storing energy and to resist seasonal variability and other disturbances.

Timber and fuel forests can be grown on marginal soils which lack the depth, structure, or fertility to support human food crops. This is the single most important reason why carbon storage by perennial plants, especially trees, is central to permaculture strategies for catching and storing energy.

Beyond carbon storage, trees provide a renewable fuel for cooking, heating, smelting, and other tasks — perhaps most important to the post-fossil fuel age. Particular forms of cellulose and lignin provide material for fabrics, paper, ropes, and timber for every imaginable use. Even in temperate ecosystems, trees represent an important store of nutrients which can be recycled to the soil by annual leaf drop, browsing by insects and herbivores, or fire.

### 4. Seed

Saved seed is a particularly potent storage of energy, essential for next season's crop. By catching and storing the energy in seed, growers maintained a genetic and cultural lineage from ancestors to descendants. Seed saving represents the most direct form of energy inheritance, carrying both biological information and the accumulated wisdom of selection across generations.
