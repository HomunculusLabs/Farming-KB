# Edible Wild Mushrooms of North America

A foraging reference for the most sought-after edible wild mushrooms in North America, organized by season and habitat. Always confirm identification with multiple features and a local expert before consuming any wild mushroom. See [[mushroom-identification]] for ID methodology and [[mushroom-species-guide]] for cultivated alternatives.

## Spring (March - May)

### Morel — *Morchella esculenta*, *M. elata*, *M. americana*
- **Habitat**: Burn sites (1-3 years post-fire), dying elm/ash/tulip poplar, old apple orchards, riverbanks
- **Region**: Widespread; black morels in mountains, yellow/gray in lowlands
- **Season**: March (south) through May (north); triggered by soil temperature ~50F
- **ID**: Honeycomb cap with pits and ridges, hollow interior when sliced, cap attached to stem at base
- **Key lookalike**: False morel (*Gyromitra esculenta*) — wrinkled brain-like cap, solid when sliced, contains gyromitrin (toxic). Slice every morel vertically to verify hollowness
- **Preparation**: Must be cooked thoroughly; never eat raw

### King Bolete — *Boletus edulis* (Porcini)
- **Habitat**: [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] with spruce, pine, birch; coniferous and mixed forests
- **Region**: Western mountains, Northeast, Great Lakes
- **Season**: Late summer to fall; some regions spring
- **ID**: Brown cap, thick white stem with fine reticulation (net pattern), pore surface (not gills), white [[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]]
- **Key lookalike**: Bitter bolete (*Tylopilus felleus*) — darker pore surface that turns pink, very bitter taste

### [[oyster-mushroom-pleurotus-ostreatus|Oyster Mushroom]] — *Pleurotus ostreatus*
- **Habitat**: Dead or dying hardwood (oak, beech, aspen); shelf-like clusters on logs/stumps
- **Region**: Widespread across North America
- **Season**: Spring and fall; some winter in mild climates
- **ID**: Fan-shaped cap, white to gray/brown, decurrent gills, white spore print, grows in overlapping clusters on wood
- **Note**: Also easily cultivated at home

## Summer (June - August)

### Chanterelle — *Cantharellus cibarius*, *C. lateritius*
- **Habitat**: Mixed hardwood forests (oak, beech, birch); mossy areas, old growth
- **Region**: Pacific Northwest, Appalachians, Great Lakes, Northeast
- **Season**: June through September; peak July-August
- **ID**: Golden yellow to orange, trumpet-shaped with wavy cap margin, forked ridges (false gills) running down stem, apricot-like smell, white to pale yellow spore print
- **Key lookalike**: Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus olearius*) — true gills (not ridges), grows in clusters on wood, glows in the dark, causes GI distress. False chanterelle (*Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca*) — true gills, orange, thinner flesh
- **Note**: Very forgiving to identify once learned; excellent beginner edible

### Chicken of the Woods — *Laetiporus sulphureus*
- **Habitat**: On living or dead hardwood (oak, cherry, beech); shelf-like brackets
- **Region**: Widespread
- **Season**: May through September; some species fruit in fall
- **ID**: Bright orange to yellow shelf fungus with concentric zones, no stem, soft moist texture when young (like chicken), white pore surface, grows on wood
- **Key lookalike**: Hen of the woods (*Grifola frondosa*) grows at base of oaks (also edible). Berkley's polypore has different pore color
- **Preparation**: Harvest only young, soft margins; older specimens are tough and bitter

### Lobster Mushroom — *Hypomyces lactifluorum* (parasite on *Russula* or *Lactarius*)
- **Habitat**: Under conifers and hardwoods; found wherever host mushrooms grow
- **Region**: Widespread
- **Season**: July through October
- **ID**: Bright orange-red, deformed mushroom shape, hard crusty surface, white interior when cut, seafood/lobster odor and taste
- **Note**: This is a parasitic fungus that transforms its host mushroom; the host is often a *Russula* species

## Fall (September - November)

### Hen of the Woods — *Grifola frondosa* (Maitake)
- **Habitat**: Base of living oak trees (almost exclusively oak); sometimes maple or elm
- **Region**: Northeast, Appalachians, Midwest
- **Season**: September through November; triggered by cool nights and rain
- **ID**: Large clustered mass of overlapping gray-brown caps growing from a single base, pores underneath (not gills), white spore print, found at base of oak trees
- **Note**: One of the most prized edibles; also medicinal (immune support). Can weigh 50+ pounds

### Lion's Mane — *Hericium erinaceus*
- **Habitat**: Dead or dying hardwood (oak, walnut, beech, sycamore); wounds on living trees
- **Region**: Widespread but uncommon
- **Season**: August through November
- **ID**: White cascading spines (teeth) hanging from a single branching base, no cap or gills, fruiting on hardwood logs or wounds; soft and white when fresh, yellows with age
- **Key lookalikes**: Other *Hericium* species (also edible); no poisonous lookalikes in North America
- **Note**: Prized both culinary (tastes like lobster/crab) and medicinal (nerve growth factor stimulation)

### Matsutake — *Tricholoma matsutake*
- **Habitat**: Under conifers (pine, fir, hemlock) in sandy/loamy soils; mycorrhizal
- **Region**: Pacific Northwest (most valued in North America), also Sierra Nevada
- **Season**: September through December
- **ID**: White to brown cap with scales, thick solid stem, partial veil, spicy cinnamon-earthy aroma, white gills, white spore print
- **Note**: Among the most expensive mushrooms in the world (Japanese market); most foragers sell rather than eat

### Honey Mushroom — *Armillaria mellea* complex
- **Habitat**: Base of hardwoods and conifers; causes root rot; often in massive clusters
- **Region**: Widespread
- **Season**: September through November
- **ID**: Honey-colored cap, white partial veil leaving a ring on stem, grows in large clusters at base of trees or on roots, white spore print
- **Warning**: Must be thoroughly cooked (some people get GI upset); can be confused with deadly *Galerina marginata* — always verify white spore print and ring presence
- **Note**: Some of the largest living organisms on Earth are Armillaria clones spanning hundreds of acres

## Winter (December - February)

### Wood Ear — *Auricularia auricula-judae*
- **Habitat**: Dead elderberry, elm, beech; ear-shaped rubbery fruiting body
- **Region**: Widespread; more common in Southeast and mild winter areas
- **Season**: Year-round after rain; most visible in cooler months when deciduous trees are bare
- **ID**: Dark brown to black ear-shaped gelatinous fruiting body, rubbery texture, grows on dead wood
- **Note**: Popular in Asian cuisine; rehydrates well from dried

## Foraging Safety Rules

1. **Identify to species** — never eat a mushroom you cannot positively identify
2. **Use multiple features** — cap, gills/pores, stem, ring, spore print, habitat, bruising
3. **Slice puffballs vertically** — to rule out embryonic Amanita buttons
4. **Make a spore print** — essential for distinguishing many lookalikes
5. **Know the deadly species** — Amanita phalloides, Galerina marginata, Cortinarius spp.
6. **Start with easy species** — morels, chanterelles, chicken of the woods, oysters
7. **Learn from local experts** — join a mycological society; regional knowledge is critical
8. **When in doubt, throw it out** — no mushroom is worth dying for

For detailed identification methodology, see [[mushroom-identification]]. For cultivating these species at home, see [[mushroom-species-guide]] and [[mushroom-log-cultivation]].
