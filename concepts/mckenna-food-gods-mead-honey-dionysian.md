# Mead, Honey, and the Dionysian Connection in Food of the Gods

In *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna explores the complex relationship
between honey, mead, and the religious traditions of the ancient
Mediterranean, arguing that the intoxicating properties of fermented
honey played a crucial transitional role in the shift from mushroom-
based partnership religion to the alcohol-based dominator culture. The
figure of Dionysus, with his deep associations with both honey and
ecstatic ritual, occupies a pivotal position in this transitional
history.

## Honey as Sacred Substance

McKenna emphasizes that honey occupies a unique position in the
ethnobotanical record: it is simultaneously a food, a medicine, and a
precursor to psychoactive preparations. In all traditional cultures,
honey is recognized as a magical and medicinal substance. It has been
used to preserve both human bodies and mushrooms, and its role as a
preservative connects it directly to the ancient mushroom cults that
McKenna traces throughout *Food of the Gods*.

The chemical properties of honey, particularly its antibiotic and
preservative qualities, made it an essential component in the
preparation and storage of psychoactive mushrooms. Dried mushrooms
preserved in honey would retain their potency far longer than those
stored by other means, and the honey itself would become infused with
the psychoactive compounds of the mushrooms, creating a kind of
medicinal confection that could be consumed long after the original
mushrooms had been gathered.

## Mead as the First Fermented Intoxicant

Mead, or fermented honey, appears to have been one of the earliest
fermented intoxicants known to humanity. McKenna notes that very early
in the development of human culture, the intoxicating effects of
fermented honey and fruit juices must have been noticed. Mead was the
recreational drug of the Indo-European tribes, a cultural trait they
shared with the mushroom-using pastoralists of the ancient Near East.

One of the most astonishing murals unearthed at Catal Huyuk apparently
depicts the life cycle and metamorphosis of honeybees, suggesting that
beekeeping and honey processing were important enough to the religious
and cultural life of that Neolithic city to merit prominent artistic
representation. This find connects honey to the partnership cultures
that McKenna sees as the original context for humanity's relationship
with psychoactive plants.

## The Cattle-Bee Connection

McKenna draws attention to a curious belief widely held in the
classical world: that bees were generated from the carcasses of cattle.
This belief, he argues, makes more sense when understood as an effort
to connect bees as a source of honey and mead, the supplanting
intoxicant, with cattle and the older mushroom cult. In the ancient
Near East, cattle dung provided the substrate on which psychoactive
mushrooms, particularly *Stropharia cubensis*, grew in abundance. The
association of bees with cattle may thus encode a cultural memory of
the transition from mushroom-based religion to mead-based intoxication.

The mead cults and mushroom cults that used honey as a preservative may
have developed in close association with each other, with honey serving
as the material link between the old religion of the mushroom and the
new religion of fermented honey. This transitional period, in which
both substances were used simultaneously, may have produced the rich
syncretic mythology that surrounds Dionysus.

## Dionysus and the Nectar of Bees

Dionysus, the Greek god of ecstasy, wine, and ritual madness, is
deeply associated with honey in the classical tradition. McKenna notes
that Dionysus was said by the Roman poet Ovid to have invented honey,
and that the sacred ground on which the maenads, his handmaidens,
performed their ritual dance was said to have flowed with milk, wine,
and the "nectar of bees." The thyrsos staffs carried by the maenads
were likewise said to drip with honey.

The scholar Carl Kerenyi, whom McKenna cites, observes that the honey
offerings given to the "mistress of the labyrinth" in Minoan religion
carry the style of a much earlier period, that stage in which Minoan
culture was still in contact with what he calls an "age of honey." This
age of honey, for McKenna, represents the transitional period between
the original mushroom religion and the later dominance of alcohol.

## The Devolution of Sacramental Substances

McKenna develops a model of progressive "devolution" in which each
intoxicant, each effort to recapture the symbiotic balance of the
human-mushroom relationship in the lost African Eden, is a paler, more
distorted image of the original Mystery than the last. In this scheme,
mushrooms represent the original sacrament; honey-based preparations
represent a first step away from direct mushroom use; mead represents
a further step; and the later dominance of wine and grain-based alcohol
represents the final stage of devolution, in which the original
visionary quality of the sacramental experience is almost entirely
lost, replaced by the ego-reinforcing intoxication of alcohol.

This devolutionary model is central to McKenna's broader argument in
*Food of the Gods* about the relationship between specific psychoactive
substances and specific cultural forms. Each substance, he argues,
promotes a characteristic style of consciousness and a corresponding
style of social organization. The shift from mushrooms to alcohol was
thus not merely a change in drug preference but a fundamental
transformation in the nature of human consciousness and social life.

## Implications

The honey-mead-mushroom complex, as McKenna presents it, illustrates
his larger argument that the history of human intoxication is not a
simple story of progress from ignorance to knowledge but rather a more
complex and often tragic story of gain and loss, in which the pursuit
of altered states of consciousness led humanity progressively further
from the original partnership with the plant kingdom that McKenna
believes was the foundation of human culture.
