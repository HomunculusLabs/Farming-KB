# Insect and Disease Management Philosophy

Sir [[albert-howard]], founder of the [[coleman-deep-organic-farming]] movement, believed that before a plant is attacked by insects or disease, it has already become unhealthy. The predator's purpose in nature's scheme is to restore balance — like a wolf pack bringing down a sick animal that has lived too long. A truly healthy plant will either be nearly unassailable or will outgrow insect damage and successfully resist disease.

This philosophy shapes a fundamentally different approach to pest management than the conventional "identify and spray" paradigm.

## The Soil Fertility Connection

### The Evidence

The key to pest resistance is making perfectly fertile soil — thus growing healthy plants. Evidence supports this:

- In variety trials, all plants of one variety of Brussels sprouts were consistently damaged by aphids while all plants of another, grown side by side in identical conditions, remained almost entirely untouched
- A single diseased tomato plant among healthy siblings of the same variety suggests the problem was a flawed seed or uneven soil amendment distribution
- Cabbages in properly prepared beds grew big and healthy with no problems, while extra seedlings planted in an unmanured fringe with only cheap [[solomon-chemical-fertilizer-incomplete-potassium-yield-tradeoff]] were attacked by flea beetles, then cabbageworms, then root maggots

### The Experiment

Cabbages grown with full organic preparation: 6 lb (2.7 kg) each, tasted fine. Cabbages on the fringe with chemical fertilizer only: 1-2 lb (0.5-1 kg), tough and bitter, attacked by three different pests.

**The lesson**: Plants in poor soil are weakened and become targets. Fix the soil, and most pest problems diminish or disappear.

## When Soil Fertility Isn't Enough

Even with ideal soil conditions, disease or predation may prevail when:

- You encounter long-lasting poor weather
- You grow a species not adapted to your climate or soil
- You live in an area with large, unchecked pest populations

### The Regional Pest Problem

Urban gardeners and those in prosperous agricultural districts inevitably have more pest problems. Why?

- Where there are garden carrots but few wild carrots, carrot rust flies can breed into a serious plague unchecked by predators
- Where garden cauliflower exists but no wild radish or wild cabbage, cabbage pests multiply without natural controls
- In wild, uncultivated areas with diverse plant populations, large stable populations of pest hosts also mean large stable populations of their predators

**Implication**: If you're thinking of buying a new homestead, consider the pest pressure of the area. Gardens in diverse, less-managed landscapes have fewer problems.

## Fertilizer vs. Pesticide

### The First Question

Before rushing to spray poisons (even natural ones), before investing in predators, before fighting a pest: **Is the struggling plant simply not growing fast enough to overcome the problem?**

### Common Causes of Slow Growth

- Unfavorable spring weather (cold, wet)
- Sowing too early for the species — your choice, but one that retarded growth and lowered plant health
- Insufficient nutrients in the soil

### The Best "Cure"

Often the best cure is not a "killer" but liquid [[complete-organic-fertilizer]]:
- **Foliar spray**: Combined fish emulsion and liquid kelp (a triple whammy — two fertilizers, one of which temporarily disguises the plant's odor from predators)
- **Spot fertigation**: A bucket of compost tea or diluted liquid fertilizer at the base of struggling plants

Maybe in a week or two, growing conditions will improve, the soil will warm up, [[solomon-cool-climate-compost-soil-temperature-nutrient-release]] will accelerate, and everything will come right.

## The Sowing Insurance Strategy

### Plant Twice as Much

Instead of planting just enough for what you want (assuming everything goes well), plant twice as much. Then if insects or diseases thin out the weaker seedlings, you still have enough.

### Resow Immediately

If conditions are unfavorable and seedlings are struggling, immediately sow again. Start many more plants than ultimately wanted. The later sowing may end up better than the earlier one, and may grow faster from the start and yield sooner.

### Sow Later, Not Earlier

Planting too early is the biggest single cause of trouble. Pay attention to the fact that growth rates accelerate hugely as the soil warms. Sowing on the first possible day versus sowing two weeks later will result in two crops with a surprising difference in maturity — but the later crop will have far fewer problems.

## Spun-Fabric Row Covers

One of the most effective pest management tools available to home gardeners.

### Benefits

- Provides most of the benefits of a cloche or mini-greenhouse without needing to erect any structure
- Protection against flying insect pests without needing to spray
- Some frost protection (depending on brand)

### Specifications

- About 5-6 feet (150-180 cm) wide
- Available in cut lengths from 20 feet (6 m) up to thousands of feet
- Not completely transparent — some light reduction occurs
- Spread over a growing row or bed and loosely anchored with soil or pins
- Plants lift the almost weightless fabric as they grow — no supporting structure needed
- To keep insects out, the entire perimeter must be carefully anchored with soil

### Brand Differences

| Brand | Frost Protection | Light Reduction | Wind Handling | Durability |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|------------|
| Reemay | Yes (few degrees) | ~25% (undesirable) | Good | Good |
| Other brands | Varies | Less reduction | May blow off plants | May not last as long |

### Downsides

- Reemay's 25% light reduction is undesirable, especially at summer's end when light levels already decrease
- All brands can reduce light levels enough to affect heat-loving crops at summer's end
- In strong wind, lighter fabrics may blow off plants

## Soil Type and Pest Susceptibility

Certain vegetables are fussy about soil type and express their difficulty through susceptibility to insects or disease. This is especially common with weakly rooting species intolerant of clay soils.

### Plants That Dislike Clay

- Globe artichoke
- Celery
- Celeriac
- Melons
- Cauliflower

### Plants That Dislike Light, Loose Soil

- Brussels sprouts (sprouts tend to blow up and be loose; tall plants fall over)

## Gardening Aikido

The martial art philosophy applied to gardening: when a pest or disease annoys you, see it as a guide to becoming a more skillful grower rather than merely an enemy to be destroyed.

### Abandon Poverty Consciousness

Instead of planting a garden from which you'll harvest exactly what you want if everything goes well, plant twice as much. Some years everything goes wrong: tomatoes get blossom end rot, cucumbers succumb to powdery mildew, peppers get viruses, corn is late and not sweet, snap beans are covered with aphids. But if you planted twice what you needed, there will still be enough.

### Accept Loss Gracefully

It's easy to be relaxed about abandoning some seedlings when seeds only cost a few cents each. It's much harder to be dispassionate when $5 seedlings are being knocked over. This is one reason to direct-seed whenever possible and grow your own transplants.

## Beneficial Insect Habitat

One way backyard gardeners in thickly settled territory can fight back is by creating proper [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects. Someone with a bit of acreage can do a great deal to provide permanent cover that assists beneficials. This is a complex subject requiring specific guidance for your climate zone.
