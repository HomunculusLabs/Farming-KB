# Permaculture Household Economics

Permaculture household economics is the practice of applying permaculture design principles to household financial management, resource flows, and livelihood strategies. This approach, articulated by david holmgren in Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability, redefines economic activity from consumption-dependent employment to production-oriented self-reliance.

The permaculture perspective on economics challenges the assumption that money-mediated exchange is the only valid form of economic activity. A household that produces much of its own food, fuel, and materials is economically wealthier than its income suggests, because it has reduced its dependence on the monetary economy for essential needs.

## Rebuilding Household Storages

One permaculture strategy is encouraging household and local economic self-reliance. This rebuilds traditional forms of energy storage in food, fuel, and other resources that complement landscape storages.

At Holmgren's Melliodora, the seedbox, the cellar full of produce, and the stack of firewood are traditional storages of modest wealth. A pantry and cellar full of preserves capture the essence of a bountiful season and provide insurance against a bad one.

A large wood stack with green wood added at one end and two-year seasoned wood used from the other represents both the abundance of nature and honest work maturing through weather and sunshine.

These types of household energy storages are by nature diverse, small, dispersed, easily used, and not rich or portable enough to attract much attention from thieves.

A nation full of such wealth is much more secure and stable than one dependent on the high-throughput, centralized systems of the supermarket and electricity grid.

## The Yield Concept

The [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]] framework redefines yield as the sum of energy conserved, stored, and usefully stored, plus the energy generated as surplus to the system.

For a household, yield includes food produced, energy saved through passive design, resources recycled, skills developed, and community connections maintained. Monetary income is only one measure among many.

The yield of a system is theoretically unlimited. The only limit on the number of uses of a resource possible within a system is in the limit of information and imagination of the designer.

[[permaculture-design]] applies this expanded yield concept to household economics, finding value in resources and relationships that conventional economics ignores.

## Self-Reliance as Economic Strategy

Taking personal responsibility naturally moves people to be more self-reliant and less dependent on centralized sources of needs. In this process, people discover that governments and corporations, while preaching self-reliance, actually need our dependence.

Self-reliance tends to work as a generalized and invisible consumer boycott, undermining the market share of centralized and large-scale economies that support addictive and dysfunctional behavior.

At the same time, self-reliance fosters and stimulates new local forms of economic activity. Home food production tends to foster rather than compete with small commercial organic growers serving local markets.

The amorphous and informal nature of self-reliance makes it hard to identify its effectiveness. This invisibility slows recognition of the power in people's hands but also confounds efforts of media, corporations, and government to track and subvert these processes.

## Livelihood Strategies in Permaculture

Permaculture practitioners often develop diverse livelihood strategies that combine elements of monetary income with non-monetary production.

These strategies typically include: producing food for household consumption, trading surplus production locally, offering permaculture design and consultation services, teaching [[permaculture-design-course-notes]], and developing small-scale enterprises based on site yields.

The transition from permaculture self-reliance and small business to large organizational involvement represents a spectrum of engagement. Many practitioners find their niche at the household and community scale.

The [[permaculture-currency-and-economics]] framework provides models for local exchange systems that complement household self-reliance with community-level economic circulation.

## The Built Environment as Economic Storage

Energy stores in seeds, food, fuels, and other resources are fundamental but only part of the picture. The transformation of energy into tools, buildings, and infrastructure extends well beyond physical resources.

In modern society, the huge harvest of fossil energies has moved down the industrial food chain, resulting in explosive development of cities, technology, and infrastructure. Most of these stores are useless without the rest of the industrial complex.

Permaculturalists pursue the catching and storing of natural site energies and extend this to energy-efficient buildings, appropriate technology, and manufactured goods.

Design criteria include modest scale, long life design, simple maintenance, multi-purpose function, and adaptability to other uses.

The [[permaculture-building-design]] approach creates buildings that serve as productive assets rather than energy liabilities.

## Financial Capital in Descent

Financial capital is the most obvious form of non-material wealth in the modern world. The rapid movement of capital and its focus in destructive short-term investment has been recognized as the most dysfunctional element of modern capitalism.

Ethical investment is one of the most rapidly growing areas of financial services. Returns on investment have generally been as good as or better than market averages.

Much of the value of financial capital may be illusory because it has become disconnected from sources of real wealth. Financial crashes have been increasing since the 1980s and more may come before financial wealth realistically represents potential material wealth.

The [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] approach helps households prepare for financial instability by building non-monetary forms of wealth and reducing dependence on volatile financial systems.

## Energy Storage in Culture

Despite the massive scale of technology and built assets, the largest stores of high-quality embodied energy are in forms people do not recognize as containing energy. Information and organization in government, economy, community, and culture are thought to be ethereal goods unrelated to energy.

The diversity and complexity of these assets reflect past harvesting and storage of energy in useful forms. The weightless economy of information and services is novel but does not free us from the laws of energy, as most economists seem to think.

The [[permaculture-community-design]] approach recognizes that cultural wealth, social networks, and local knowledge are forms of energy storage that become increasingly valuable during energy descent.
