## Fungal Calcium Oxalate Crystals

Calcium oxalate crystal formation by fungi is a widespread biomineralization
process with profound implications for soil chemistry, [[staycare-phytochelatins-and-intracellular-metal-detoxification-in-fungi]]. Fungi are among the most prolific
biological producers of calcium oxalate in terrestrial ecosystems, secreting
oxalic acid as a metabolic byproduct that reacts with environmental calcium to
form crystalline deposits both within and around hyphae.

## Crystal Forms and Chemistry

Fungi produce two main crystalline forms of calcium oxalate in nature: the
dihydrate form, weddellite (CaC2O4.2H2O), and the more thermodynamically
stable monohydrate, whewellite (CaC2O4.H2O). Both forms are commonly
associated with various ecophysiological groups of fungi, from saprotrophic
decomposers to mycorrhizal symbionts and rock-inhabiting species.

Depending on the physicochemical conditions of the surrounding environment,
biogenic fungal calcium oxalate can exhibit a remarkable variety of crystalline
morphologies. Observed forms include tetragonal, bipyramidal, plate-like,
rhombohedral, and needle-shaped crystals. This morphological diversity reflects
the influence of pH, ionic strength, presence of other cations, and organic
molecules on crystal nucleation and growth.

Beyond calcium, fungi can produce metal oxalates with a variety of other metals
including cadmium, cobalt, copper, manganese, strontium, zinc, nickel, and
lead. The oxalate anion has high affinity for divalent cations, making it an
effective chelating agent in [[crystal-structure-and-crystal-defects]] at the
  nanometer scale, revealing nucleation sites on hyphal walls.

### Factors Controlling Crystal Morphology

The morphology of fungal calcium oxalate crystals is controlled by a complex
interplay of environmental and biological factors:

- pH strongly influences which crystal form predominates. Acidic conditions
  (pH 3-5) favor weddellite, while near-neutral pH promotes whewellite
  formation.
- The calcium-to-oxalate ratio affects crystal size and habit. Excess calcium
  tends to produce smaller, more numerous crystals.
- The presence of magnesium ions inhibits whewellite formation and favors
  weddellite or amorphous calcium oxalate.
- Organic molecules excreted by fungi (proteins, polysaccharides, citrate)
  can act as crystal modifiers, altering growth rates along different
  crystallographic axes.
- Temperature affects both the rate of crystal formation and the relative
  stability of the two hydrate forms.
