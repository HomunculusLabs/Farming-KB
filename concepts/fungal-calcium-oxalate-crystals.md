# Fungal Calcium Oxalate Crystals

Calcium oxalate crystal formation by fungi is a widespread biomineralization process with profound implications for soil chemistry, nutrient cycling, weathering, and metal detoxification. Fungi are among the most prolific biological producers of calcium oxalate in terrestrial ecosystems, secreting oxalic acid as a metabolic byproduct that reacts with environmental calcium to form crystalline deposits both within and around hyphae.

## Crystal Forms and Chemistry

Fungi produce two main crystalline forms of calcium oxalate in nature: the dihydrate form, weddellite (CaC2O4.2H2O), and the more thermodynamically stable monohydrate, whewellite (CaC2O4.H2O). Both forms are commonly associated with various ecophysiological groups of fungi, from saprotrophic decomposers to mycorrhizal symbionts and rock-inhabiting species.

Depending on the physicochemical conditions of the surrounding environment, biogenic fungal calcium oxalate can exhibit a remarkable variety of crystalline morphologies. Observed forms include tetragonal, bipyramidal, plate-like, rhombohedral, and needle-shaped crystals. This morphological diversity reflects the influence of pH, ionic strength, presence of other cations, and organic molecules on crystal nucleation and growth.

Beyond calcium, fungi can produce metal oxalates with a variety of other metals including cadmium, cobalt, copper, manganese, strontium, zinc, nickel, and lead. The oxalate anion has high affinity for divalent cations, making it an effective chelating agent in fungal biogeochemical processes.

## Metabolic Origin of Oxalic Acid

Oxalic acid (C2H2O4) is a simple dicarboxylic acid produced by fungi through several metabolic pathways. It can be generated from the oxidation of glycine, from the hydrolysis of oxaloacetate, or from cleavage of isocitrate by isocitrate lyase. The rate and extent of oxalate excretion by fungi is influenced by nitrogen source, with nitrate (NO3-) as a nitrogen source enhancing oxalate production compared to ammonium (NH4+).

The presence of bicarbonate (HCO3-), calcium ions (Ca2+), and certain toxic metals such as copper and aluminum, as well as exposure to minerals like pyromorphite and zinc phosphate, can also stimulate fungal oxalate excretion. This metabolic flexibility allows fungi to modulate their oxalate production in response to environmental conditions, particularly in the context of mineral weathering and metal stress.

## Role in Mineral Weathering

Calcium oxalate formation plays a significant role in fungal-mediated rock and mineral weathering, a process termed bioweathering. When oxalic acid is excreted at the hyphal-soil or hyphal-rock interface, it can dissolve calcium-bearing minerals such as calcite, feldspars, and micas. The dissolved calcium then reprecipitates as calcium oxalate crystals, often directly on or adjacent to the fungal hyphae.

In limestone environments, calcium oxalate formation is particularly significant. Many near-surface limestones, calcretes, calcic, and petrocalcic soil horizons are secondarily cemented with calcite and whewellite. The presence of fungal filaments mineralized with calcite, together with whewellite, has been documented in limestone and calcareous soils from a wide range of localities worldwide.

The weathering activity extends to silicate minerals as well. Jongmans and colleagues demonstrated that fungi produce micromolar concentrations of organic acids including oxalic, citric, succinic, formate, and malate, which can dissolve minerals such as feldspars and hornblende. Thin sections of these minerals examined under microscopy have revealed fungal hyphae bearing cross walls within hyphal-generated tunnels in the rock.

## Ecological Significance

Calcium oxalate acts as a reservoir for calcium in the ecosystem and also influences phosphate availability. When calcium oxalate forms, it can sequester calcium that might otherwise bind with phosphate, thereby potentially increasing the bioavailability of phosphorus in the soil solution. This interaction between oxalate production and phosphorus cycling is particularly important in phosphorus-limited ecosystems.

The formation of calcium oxalate crystals around hyphae can alter the local microenvironment. Crystalline sheaths of calcium oxalate surrounding hyphae, as observed in species like Beauveria caledonica grown on calcium carbonate media, create a distinct microchemical zone that can influence the solubility and transport of other ions in the immediate vicinity of the fungal hypha.

## Soil Carbonate Dynamics

Calcium oxalate can be degraded to calcium carbonate, particularly in semi-arid environments. This transformation process may act to cement pre-existing limestones and contribute to the formation of calcic soil horizons. During decomposition of fungal hyphae, calcite crystals can act as sites of further secondary calcite precipitation, potentially contributing to long-term carbon sequestration in soil.

The coupling of microbial carbonate precipitation with silicate weathering could provide an important sink for atmospheric carbon dioxide in terrestrial environments. As fungi weather silicate minerals through oxalate production and subsequently transform calcium oxalate into calcium carbonate, they participate in a geochemical cycle that moves carbon from the atmosphere into stable mineral forms.

## Metal Detoxification

The formation of toxic metal oxalates provides an important mechanism for fungal tolerance of environments containing high concentrations of toxic metals. Oxalate over-excreting strains of fungi have been shown to transform cadmium, copper, lead, and zinc from a variety of toxic metal minerals into relatively insoluble oxalate forms.

The species Beauveria caledonica, for example, was able to transform these metals from their mineral forms into oxalate crystals and was tolerant to all tested metal-bearing minerals. The precipitation of metal oxalates effectively reduces the bioavailability of toxic metals in the soil environment, immobilizing them and limiting their uptake by plants and other organisms.

This detoxification mechanism has implications for understanding fungal communities in contaminated soils and for potential bioremediation strategies. Fungi that can sequester toxic metals as insoluble oxalate crystals may play important roles in the natural attenuation of metal-contaminated sites.

## Mycorrhizal Oxalate Production

Ectomycorrhizal fungi are known producers of calcium oxalate in forest soils. Extracellular calcium oxalate accumulation by ectomycorrhizal fungi in pure culture has been documented, and these crystals are thought to play roles in both mineral weathering and nutrient acquisition by the mycorrhizal symbiosis.

The white rot wood decay fungus Resinicium bicolor demonstrated the ability to solubilize strontianite sand to release strontium, then translocate it through mycelial cords and secrete it along with calcium oxalate crystals in newly advancing zones of the mycelium in decaying wood. This shows that strontium behaves similarly to calcium in fungal metabolism and that translocation and crystal formation are active, directed processes rather than passive precipitation.

## Nucleation on Fungal Cell Walls

The major component of fungal cell walls, chitin, serves as a substrate on which calcite will readily nucleate. This relationship between cell wall chemistry and mineral nucleation means that fungal hyphae serve as preferred sites for secondary mineral formation in soils. During decomposition of fungal biomass, the remaining chitin-rich hyphal walls can continue to act as nucleation surfaces for calcium carbonate and other mineral phases.

## Distribution and Abundance

Calcium oxalate production has been documented across a wide range of fungal taxa and ecological strategies. Rock-inhabiting microcolonial fungi, saprotrophic wood decomposers, ectomycorrhizal associates, and plant pathogenic species all contribute to calcium oxalate budgets in soil ecosystems. The ubiquitous nature of this process underscores its importance as a fundamental fungal contribution to biogeochemical cycling.
