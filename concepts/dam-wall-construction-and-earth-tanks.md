# Dam Wall Construction and Earth Tanks

## Overview

Dam wall construction and earth tanks are fundamental water storage techniques in permaculture design. Bill Mollison covers a wide range of approaches, from large valley dams with clay cores to simple excavated earth tanks on flat land, along with their associated irrigation systems and ecological applications. The key principle running through all of these strategies is working with landscape contours and materials rather than against them.

## Large Dam Wall Construction

### Clay Core Technique

Large dams — those with walls on the order of 200 feet long and 20 feet wide — follow the same fundamental procedure as small dams, but the stakes are higher and the work more serious. The critical step is **digging a trench down to clay** along the full length of the dam wall foundation. Once the clay substrate is reached, the trench is packed with **selected clay** to form an impermeable core. This clay core is what prevents water from seeping through or under the dam wall. The procedure mirrors small dam construction but demands greater care in material selection, compaction, and attention to detail.

### Keeping Rocks Out

One of the most important rules in dam wall construction is to **keep rocks out of the dam structure itself**. Rocks create pathways for water to flow through, undermining the integrity of the wall and eventually leading to seepage or failure. The wall should be built from well-compacted earth and clay, not rubble. However, rocks do have a specific and intentional use: **dry rock placement on wave-splash zones**. The area of the dam wall exposed to wave action can be armored with dry-laid rock to prevent erosion from wave splash, protecting the earth structure beneath.

### Granitic Country Considerations

In granitic country, where the underlying geology is porous and fractured rock rather than clay, **clay cores become absolutely essential**. Without an impermeable clay barrier, water would simply drain away through the fractured granite. Importing and carefully placing selected clay for the core is not optional in these landscapes — it is the difference between a functioning dam and a dry hole in the ground.

## Contour Dams

On flatter land, permaculture designers shift from valley dams to **contour dams**, also known as rolled earth dams. These are constructed on knolls and slopes by following the contour line and building up an earth wall along it. Unlike valley dams that catch water flowing down a natural watercourse, contour dams intercept sheet flow and direct rainfall runoff across the landscape. They are particularly effective on gently rolling terrain where a series of contour dams can create a stepped water storage system across a property.

## Earth Tanks

Earth tanks are an entirely different approach to water storage, ideally suited to **flat land** where valley dam sites don't exist. An earth tank is simply an **excavated pond** — a hole dug in the ground with the excavated soil piled up around the edges to form banks. Because the water is held below ground level rather than behind a raised wall, earth tanks **can never bust out** in the way a dam wall can fail. This makes them inherently safer and lower-maintenance than conventional dams.

### Advantages of Earth Tanks

- No risk of catastrophic wall failure
- Lower construction complexity
- Well-suited to flat terrain with no valley sites
- The excavated soil becomes the surrounding bank

### Earth Tanks vs Valley Dams

| Feature | Valley Dams | Earth Tanks |
|---------|------------|-------------|
| Terrain | Valleys, gullies | Flat land |
| Wall failure risk | Possible | Essentially none |
| Construction complexity | Higher (clay cores, compaction) | Lower (excavation) |
| Water storage | Behind raised wall | Below ground level |
| Best for | Sloping terrain | Flat, open terrain |

## Sun Traps

The soil piled up during earth tank or dam construction can be strategically used to **create sun traps**. By shaping the excavated earth into curved banks facing the sun, designers create warm, sheltered microclimates that absorb and radiate solar heat. These sun traps extend the growing season and provide frost-free niches for tender plants. The south-facing (in the Southern Hemisphere) or south-facing (in the Northern Hemisphere) banks act as thermal mass, moderating temperature extremes.

## Fish Ponds

Fish ponds require special design considerations beyond simple water storage. The most important is **depth**: productive fish ponds need **kettle-holed deeper areas** — holes dug to approximately 15 feet deep. A pond with a 15-foot deep section can support roughly 50 fish, providing adequate water volume, temperature stratification, and oxygen levels. Without these deeper zones, fish are vulnerable to temperature fluctuations, predation, and oxygen depletion. The deeper kettle holes also serve as refuge areas during dry periods.

## Water Sources

### Diversion Drains vs Springs

Mollison recommends using **diversion drains** rather than springs as the primary water source for dams and ponds. Diversion drains are channels cut across slopes to intercept surface runoff and direct it into storage. They are controllable, predictable, and don't risk depleting a natural spring. Springs, while valuable, can be unpredictable in flow and may be ecologically sensitive.

### Spring Houses

Where springs are used, **spring houses** provide a clean, protected alternative. A spring house encloses the spring outlet, protecting the water from contamination and animal access while providing a cool storage environment. Spring houses represent a traditional, low-technology approach to water management.

## The Four Components of a Dam System

Every properly designed dam system contains **four essential components**:

1. **Diversion channel in** — brings water into the dam from the catchment, whether from a diversion drain, stream, or other source
2. **Irrigation channel out** — distributes stored water to where it is needed for crops, livestock, or other uses
3. **Release device** — controls the outflow of water from the dam, typically through a lock pipe or similar mechanism
4. **Spillway** — provides a safe overflow path for excess water during flood events, preventing the dam wall from being overtopped

### Acceptance of Leakage

A key permaculture principle regarding dams is that **everything leaks a little, and that's normal**. Perfect impermeability is neither achievable nor necessary. Small amounts of seepage are expected and acceptable. The goal is to minimize leakage through proper construction (clay cores, keeping rocks out, good compaction) while accepting that some water loss is natural and part of a healthy hydrological cycle.

## Lock Pipes and Floodgates

### Lock Pipes

**Lock pipes** are the standard release device for dams. They pass through the dam wall at the base, allowing water to be drawn from the full depth of the storage. Lock pipes can be fitted with valves to control flow rate. Combined with **flag irrigation systems** — where water is released in pulses managed by flag markers — they provide precise, gravity-fed water distribution across a property.

### Floodgates

**Floodgates** are sliding doors set in concrete sills that control water flow in channels and spillways. They provide a robust, mechanical means of regulating water levels and directing flow between different parts of a water management system. Unlike simple pipe outlets, floodgates can handle large volumes of water and are essential for managing flood events.

## Mini-Ponds and Wildlife Habitat

### Mini-Ponds for Steep Slopes

On steep slopes where full-size dams aren't practical, **mini-ponds** serve as essential water points for tree planting. Small excavations or lined depressions hold enough water to establish and maintain trees during their critical early growth period. These mini-ponds are placed at intervals along contour lines, creating a distributed water network across steep terrain.

### Habitat Ponds

Small ponds serve critical ecological functions beyond irrigation:

- **Quail and pheasant habitat ponds** — provide drinking water and dusting areas for upland game birds, supporting biodiversity and creating hunting opportunities
- **Pear slug frog ponds** — small, shallow ponds specifically designed to attract frogs, which are natural predators of pear slugs and other orchard pests, integrating biological pest control into the water management system

## Irrigation Systems

### Drip Irrigation

For **high-value tree crops**, drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone with minimal waste. Drip systems are water-efficient, reduce weed growth between plants, and can be automated. They represent the most precise irrigation method and are justified economically for valuable crops where water efficiency directly impacts yield and quality.

### Orchard Irrigation with Swales

Orchards on sloping land can be irrigated using **swales** — level trenches dug along the contour that capture and hold runoff, allowing it to infiltrate into the soil. Swales passively irrigate trees planted on the downhill berm, reducing or eliminating the need for active irrigation once established.

### Reverse Siphons

**Reverse siphons** allow water to flow downhill, under an obstacle such as a road or gully, and back uphill on the other side using gravity and pipe pressure. They are an elegant, gravity-fed solution for moving water across uneven terrain without pumps.

### Plug-Flow Flood Irrigation

**Plug-flow flood irrigation** uses 2-inch pipes and barriers to pulse water through a system in controlled surges. Water is released in discrete plugs that flow through the irrigation channel, filling each section before moving to the next. This approach ensures even distribution, reduces erosion in channels, and makes efficient use of available water pressure. The barriers between sections prevent water from rushing past the upper portions of the field, ensuring that all areas receive adequate irrigation before excess water moves downstream.

## Key Design Principles

- **Clay cores are non-negotiable** for dam impermeability, especially in granitic country
- **Rocks belong in splash zones, not in dam walls**
- **Earth tanks are the safest option** on flat land — they cannot fail catastrophically
- **Four components are essential**: diversion in, irrigation out, release device, spillway
- **Accept minor leakage** as normal and natural
- **Match the water storage type to the terrain**: valley dams for slopes, earth tanks for flats, mini-ponds for steep ground
- **Integrate multiple functions**: every pond or dam should serve irrigation, habitat, microclimate, and ecological purposes simultaneously
