## Mycoremediation Experiments for Education

Mycoremediation — the use of fungi to degrade or sequester environmental contaminants — offers rich, hands-on learning opportunities across age groups and disciplines. The experiments below are adapted from Tradd Cotter's *Organic Mushroom Farming and Mycoremediation* and are designed to be scalable from middle-school science fairs through college-level research.

---

## 1. Soil Erosion Control with Fungi

Soil erosion is a major environmental challenge, and fungal mycelium can dramatically improve soil structure and water retention. This experiment teaches students how to design controlled trials comparing erosion-prevention materials.

### Building Biological Erosion Units

Construct shallow tray-based erosion units (roughly 30 × 60 cm) filled with a standardized soil mix. Each tray receives a different ground-cover treatment and is planted with a common fast-growing species such as ryegrass or clover. Fungal inoculum — fully colonized [[fao-spent-mushroom-substrate-composting-recycling]] — is mixed into the soil of the fungal treatment trays at a known ratio (e.g., 10% by volume).

### Simulating Runoff with "Water Runs"

Set up angled troughs or PVC channels beneath the trays to capture runoff. The troughs direct water into collection containers positioned at the lower end. Each run delivers a measured volume of water (simulating rainfall) at a consistent flow rate. Students record the time it takes water to exit the tray, giving them **retention time** (how long water is held in the soil matrix) and **contact time** (how long water interacts with [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]]).

### Treatment Comparison

A robust experiment includes at least four treatments in triplicate:

| Treatment | Description |
|---|---|
| **Control** | Bare soil, no ground cover or fungi |
| **Erosion fabric** | Commercial geotextile fabric laid over soil |
| **Wheat straw** | A standard agricultural mulch |
| **Mushroom substrate** | Spent or active fungal substrate mixed into soil |

### Measuring Erosion

After each water run, students collect the sediment that washes into the containers, dry it completely, and weigh it. Dry weight of eroded silt per run is the primary metric. Additional observations include turbidity of runoff water, vegetative growth differences among trays, and visible mycelial density at the soil surface.

### Data Analysis

Students run multiple trials, average the replicates, graph results (bar charts comparing mean erosion per treatment), and perform basic statistical tests (e.g., t-tests or ANOVA at higher levels). A **cost analysis** component asks students to compare the price per square meter of each material — often revealing that fungal substrate is competitive with or cheaper than commercial erosion fabric while providing additional ecological benefits such as improved soil biology.

## 2. Testing Contaminated Water Filtration

This experiment introduces students to biological water filtration by passing contaminated water through fungal substrate columns.

### Contaminant Preparation

Prepare dilute solutions of common environmental pollutants: a household herbicide (e.g., glyphosate diluted to field-realistic concentrations), a liquid fertilizer high in nitrogen or phosphorus, or a simulated stormwater mix. Test kits (available from scientific suppliers) measure baseline contaminant levels before and after filtration.

### Filtration Setup

Pack identical columns or buckets with the fungal substrate, ensuring even compaction. Pour a known volume of contaminated water through each column, collect the effluent, and re-test contaminant levels. Multiple passes through the same substrate can be tested to evaluate filtration capacity over time.

### Low-Tech Bioassays

When chemical test kits are unavailable, students can use living bioassays:

- **Bean test**: Soak bean seeds in the filtered water and in unfiltered control water. Germination rate and seedling vigor over 5–7 days indicate residual toxicity.
- **Worm assay**: Place red wiggler worms in containers with the filtered water moistening a neutral substrate. Survival and activity over several days provide a sensitive toxicity readout.

### Analysis

Graph contaminant concentration or bioassay health scores across treatments and filtration passes. Students learn to identify which fungal substrates and passage counts yield the best remediation, connecting biological activity to measurable environmental improvement.

## 3. Remediating Oil Spills with Fungi

Oil spill remediation is one of the most visually compelling mycoremediation demonstrations and illustrates the biochemical power of fungal enzymes.

### Biochemical Basis

Fungi produce **[[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]]** that break down complex organic molecules outside their cells. Many [[mycoremediation-petroleum-hydrocarbons]] are chemically similar to **lignin** — the tough, woody component of plant cell walls that white-rot fungi evolved to decompose. Because of this similarity, lignin-degrading fungi such as *[[pleurotus-ostreatus]]* ([[oyster-mushroom]]) can also break down hydrocarbon chains, converting them into less harmful compounds and ultimately into fungal biomass.

### Classroom Mock Oil Spill

Set up small aquariums or plastic containers with a layer of soil or sand. Add a measured volume of vegetable oil (a safe, non-toxic surrogate for petroleum) to simulate a spill. Cover the oil with shredded dried grass or hay to absorb it, then layer this oiled hay between sheets of **cardboard colonized with oyster [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]]**.

### Observing Bioremediation

Over the following weeks, students observe:

- Mycelial growth spreading through the oiled material
- **Yellow metabolite droplets** forming on the mycelium — these are extracellular enzymes secreted to break down the oil
- Gradual disappearance of the oil layer as it is metabolized
- [[advanced-mushroom-fruiting-techniques]] bodies appearing once significant degradation has occurred

### Worm Bioassay for Progress

At intervals (weeks 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12), students add **red wiggler worms** to samples taken from the remediation containers. Worm survival, growth, and activity serve as a living indicator of how much toxicity has been reduced. A parallel control container (oil with no fungal treatment) should show consistently poor worm survival.

### Timeline

The full experiment can take up to **three months**, making it ideal for a semester-long project. Weekly observations, photographs, and written entries build a rich dataset for analysis and presentation.

## 4. Screening Fungi for Mycoremediation (College Level)

At the college level, students can move beyond pre-selected species and attempt to **discover** or **train** fungi adapted to specific contaminants — a process central to real-world mycoremediation development.

### Preparing Contaminant Traps

Prepare small samples of enriched substrate (sterilized grain or sawdust) amended with a known concentration of the target contaminant (heavy metals, petroleum residues, pesticides). These "traps" establish a baseline contaminant level and a nutritious medium for fungal colonization.

### Field Exposure

Expose the traps to open air at known contaminated sites — roadsides, former industrial areas, agricultural fields with pesticide history, or near gas stations. Fungal spores naturally present at these sites colonize the traps over days to weeks. Different trap placements (soil surface, buried shallowly, hung in vegetation) capture different fungal communities.

### Subculturing and Training

In the lab, subculturing isolates the individual fungal species that colonized each trap. Students then **gradually increase contaminant concentrations** across successive subcultures, "training" the fungi to tolerate and metabolize higher pollutant loads. Growth rate, [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]], and contaminant reduction are tracked at each concentration step.

### Native vs. Lab-Trained Strains

A key comparison involves fungi **already acclimated to contaminated field sites** (collected directly and cloned) versus strains **trained in the lab from less-exposed parents**. Students evaluate which approach yields more robust degraders, testing both under identical conditions. Often, native strains already possess superior adaptation, but lab training can improve strains collected from marginally contaminated sites.

### Selection and Reintroduction

The best-performing isolates can be cloned, mass-produced, and — in advanced courses — reintroduced to contaminated sites to assess in-situ remediation potential. This cycle of collection, selection, improvement, and reintroduction mirrors professional bioremediation practice.

## 5. Engineering Mycoremediation Prototypes

The culmination of educational mycoremediation work is translating lab results into **functional prototypes** — physical filtration systems, erosion barriers, or bioremediation modules designed for real-world deployment.

### Interdisciplinary Collaboration

Effective prototype development requires collaboration between **engineering students** (who design flow systems, structural supports, and scalability) and **microbiology students** (who optimize fungal species, substrate composition, and loading rates). Joint projects teach both groups to communicate across disciplinary boundaries.

### From Data to Prototype

The workflow progresses through three stages:

1. **Data collection** — controlled experiments establish which fungi degrade which contaminants and under what conditions
2. **Prototype testing** — small-scale physical systems (e.g., mycofiltration buckets, erosion mats) are built and tested with real water or soil
3. **Implementation** — successful prototypes are scaled up or deployed in field conditions, with ongoing monitoring to evaluate performance

### Learning Outcomes

Students gain experience in experimental design, data analysis, biological cultivation, engineering prototyping, and environmental science — all within a single project arc. These experiments demonstrate that ecological problems can be addressed with biological solutions that are low-cost, sustainable, and accessible to communities worldwide.
