# Herb Garden Design

## Overview

Herbs should always be included in the organic garden for both practical and aesthetic reasons. They attract useful [[gaias-garden-host-plants-beneficial-insects]] and pollinator-habitat|pollinators, serve as [[weed-suppression-strategies]] for weed control, and can be planted in mixed borders, around shrubs, in containers, or in a dedicated traditional herb garden. Most herbs originate from the Mediterranean and thrive in warm sunshine.

## Site Selection

Locate the herb garden in the sunniest part of the garden. Most herbs prefer full sun, though a few tolerate partial shade (mint, chervil, bay, parsley). Ensure good access with pathways or stepping stones -- herbs are harvested more regularly than most plants.

## Soil Preparation

Herbs ideally need well-drained, light soil with a pH between 7.0 and 7.5. Double dig the area, breaking up subsoil and working in plenty of well-rotted manure or [[compost-building-guide]]. Spent mushroom compost is ideal for achieving the preferred pH.

If soil is wet and heavy and cannot be improved by deep digging and organic matter addition, build a [[raised-bed-gardening]] using brick, stone, or old railroad ties -- 12in (30cm) height is sufficient.

Before planting container-grown herbs in spring or summer, rake in two handfuls of blood, fish, and bone meal per square yard/meter about two weeks before planting. Use bone meal alone for fall and winter plantings.

## Design Approaches

### Traditional Formal Herb Garden

Plants arranged in formal patterns, each herb enclosed by low hedges of box (Buxus sempervirens) or lavender (Lavandula sp.). The intricate patterns are necessary because most herbs die down completely in winter -- the framework provides year-round interest.

### Cottage Garden Integration

Herbs were main constituents of old cottage gardens. Interplant with flowers and vegetables in mixed borders. Variegated sages and thymes make superb ground cover plants, while giant chives, golden balms, and colored hops add dramatic flowers.

### Container Growing

Low-growing species like thyme, rosemary, and parsley are suitable for tubs, window boxes, and [[comparison-cold-frame-gardening-vs-container-gardening-guide]]. Bay (Laurus nobilis) must be grown in containers in frost-prone areas.

## Maintenance

### Feeding

Apply a 2-3in mulch of well-rotted manure or compost over the entire garden in mid to late winter. Guard against [[slugs-and-snails-organic-control]].

### Watering

Some watering may be necessary in dry weather. Use a sprinkler for at least two hours to ensure water penetrates to lower levels.

### Weeding

Remove weeds by hand during early cultivation. Once established, herbs spread and suppress weeds themselves. Ensure the soil is completely free of perennial weed roots before planting -- weeds like ground elder will thrive unnoticed among sprawling herbs.

### Pruning

Trim lavender and thyme after flowering to keep them compact. Regular harvesting keeps most herbs in check. Self-seeding herbs (borage, mint, feverfew) need seedling removal to prevent takeover.

### Propagation

Divide perennial herbs in fall and early winter (or early spring). Propagate shrubby herbs (rosemary, bay, lavender) from softwood cuttings in early summer. See [[plant-propagation-techniques]] for detailed methods.

## Harvesting and Storing

Pick leaves for drying before the plant flowers for best flavor. Tie stems in small bunches and hang in a dry, airy place. Handle carefully to avoid losing essential oils. Crumble dried leaves into airtight jars. For seed saving, hang stems upside down with a cloth beneath to catch falling seeds. Many herbs (basil, parsley, chives, mint, balm) can be frozen in ice cubes or plastic bags.
