# Herb Garden Design

## Overview

Herbs should always be included in the organic garden for both practical and aesthetic reasons. They attract useful [[weed-suppression-strategies]] for weed control, and can be planted in mixed borders, around shrubs, in containers, or in a dedicated traditional herb garden. Most herbs originate from the Mediterranean and thrive in warm sunshine.

## Site Selection

Locate the herb garden in the sunniest part of the garden. Most herbs prefer full sun, though a few tolerate partial shade (mint, chervil, bay, parsley). Ensure good access with pathways or stepping stones -- herbs are harvested more regularly than most plants.

## Soil Preparation

Herbs ideally need well-drained, light soil with a pH between 7.0 and 7.5. Double dig the area, breaking up subsoil and working in plenty of well-rotted manure or [[raised-bed-gardening]] using brick, stone, or old railroad ties -- 12in (30cm) height is sufficient.

Before planting container-grown herbs in spring or summer, rake in two handfuls of blood, fish, and bone meal per square yard/meter about two weeks before planting. Use bone meal alone for fall and winter plantings.

## Design Approaches

### Traditional Formal Herb Garden

Plants arranged in formal patterns, each herb enclosed by low hedges of box (Buxus sempervirens) or lavender (Lavandula sp.). The intricate patterns are necessary because most herbs die down completely in winter -- the framework provides year-round interest.

### Cottage Garden Integration

Herbs were main constituents of old cottage gardens. Interplant with flowers and vegetables in mixed borders. Variegated sages and thymes make superb ground cover plants, while giant chives, golden balms, and colored hops add dramatic flowers.

## Maintenance

### Feeding

Apply a 2-3in mulch of well-rotted manure or compost over the entire garden in mid to late winter. Guard against [[plant-propagation-techniques]] for detailed methods.

## Harvesting and Storing

Pick leaves for drying before the plant flowers for best flavor. Tie stems in small bunches and hang in a dry, airy place. Handle carefully to avoid losing essential oils. Crumble dried leaves into airtight jars. For seed saving, hang stems upside down with a cloth beneath to catch falling seeds. Many herbs (basil, parsley, chives, mint, balm) can be frozen in ice cubes or plastic bags.
