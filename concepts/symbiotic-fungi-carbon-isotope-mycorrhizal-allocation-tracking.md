# Stable Carbon Isotope Labelling for Mycorrhizal Carbon Tracking

Stable carbon isotope (13C) labelling is a powerful technique for tracing
carbon flow from plants through [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AM) fungal
networks into soil [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]]. By tracking the movement of
13C-labelled assimilates, researchers can quantify [[mycorrhizal-carbon-allocation]] to
different fungal compartments, measure [[singh-mycorrhizal-network-carbon-transfer-plants]] between
mycorrhizal partners, and assess the contribution of mycorrhizal
networks to soil carbon dynamics.

## Principles of Carbon Isotope Labelling

Carbon-13 is a stable, non-radioactive isotope of carbon that occurs
naturally at approximately 1.1 percent abundance. By enriching the CO2
available to plants with 13C, researchers create a detectable tracer
that follows the path of recently assimilated carbon through the plant
and into its fungal symbionts. Unlike radioactive tracers, 13C is safe
to use and allows long-term tracking experiments.

A key advantage of 13C labelling is that it can be detected after
extreme dilution. A pure 13C tracer is detectable after dilution of
100,000-fold through natural 12C, enabling detection even when only
small amounts of labelled carbon reach fungal tissues.

## Carbon Pools in Mycorrhizal Systems

Carbon in mycorrhizal systems is distributed among several distinct
pools: plant shoots, plant roots, intraradical [[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]] (within
roots), extraradical fungal mycelium (in soil), fungal spores, and
rhizosphere microorganisms. Each pool can be estimated using 13C
labelling combined with signature fatty acid analysis.

During microbial growth, an added carbon substrate is typically used
approximately 50 percent for energy production through respiration,
with the remainder incorporated into biomass components including
storage compounds. Carbon is continually respired, making the timing
of measurements critical for accurate allocation estimates.

## AM Fungal Carbon Storage in Lipids

A significant proportion of AM fungal carbon is stored as neutral
lipids in vesicles inside plant roots and in spores on the external
mycelium. These storage lipids may persist for long periods, providing
a record of carbon allocation. Neutral lipid fatty acid (NLFA) 16:1o5
is a particularly sensitive signature of AM fungi in both roots and
soil. Since total 13C incorporation in AM [[fungal-hyphae]] correlates
with enrichment in NLFA 16:1o5, analysis of this compound provides a
reliable estimate of carbon uptake by AM fungi.

Phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) 16:1o5, a constituent of AM fungal
membranes, has lower specificity due to bacterial background in soil.
NLFA 16:0 is a general neutral lipid that increases in plant roots
following AM colonization because of the high storage lipid content of
AM fungi.

## Labelling Approaches

### Monoxenic AM Cultures
Established monoxenic cultures in compartmentalized Petri dishes can
be labelled with sterile filtered 13C D-glucose solution placed in a
ring around the initial inoculum plug. Two-compartment dishes with
roots in solid medium and [[bloomfields-orchard-hyphal-growth-and-invasive-biology]] in liquid medium are preferred
because the mycelium can be subjected to treatments that do not
directly affect roots, and carbon translocation by the mycelium can be
estimated more precisely.

### Pot Experiments
Controlled labelling of potted plants uses a greenhouse propagation
box adapted with a fan, thermometer, gas-sampling tubing connected to
an infrared gas analyser (IRGA), and a septum for gas injection. Label
is introduced as 13CO2 (99 percent enrichment) by injection through
the septum, typically 100 ml increasing CO2 concentration from 240 to
500 to 1,000 ppm. A pulse period of 2 to 4 hours is usually
appropriate, as assimilation may be very low during the first hour.
