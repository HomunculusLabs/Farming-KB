# USDA Seed Germination Standards and Law

## Overview

In the United States, Canada, and [[legal-responses-to-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-in-the-european-union]], seed sold commercially is
subject to germination standards enforced by law. These regulations exist to
protect buyers from purchasing seed that is effectively dead. However, the
minimum legal thresholds are far lower than what a serious gardener should
accept, and the [[mycoremediation-regulatory-framework]] contains a significant loophole that
home-garden seed packers routinely exploit.

## How Seed Law Works

Seed law in all three jurisdictions operates on the same basic principle: any
seed package sold above a certain weight threshold must display the results of a
certified laboratory germination test conducted within a specified time window.

- In the US, Canada, and EU, any package over **1 pound (454 g)** must carry a
  label showing a **certified lab germination test** performed **within the last
  6 months**.
- The test result shown must meet or exceed the USDA Minimum Standard
  Germination rate for that species.
- Packages at or below the 1-pound threshold are often **exempt from labeling
  requirements**, which is the critical loophole exploited by many seed sellers
  targeting home gardeners.

This means a small packet of seeds from a rack at a hardware store may never
have been tested at all, or may have been tested years before you buy it. The
seed inside could have germination rates far below the legal minimum, and you
would have no way to know.

## The USDA Minimum Standard Germination Table

The USDA publishes minimum germination percentages for each vegetable species.
These are the legal floor — the lowest acceptable rate at the time of testing:

| Vegetable              | USDA Min | Vegetable       | USDA Min |
|-----------------------|----------|-----------------|----------|
| Beans (snap, dry)     | 70%      | Melons          | 65%      |
| Beets                 | 65%      | Onions          | 70%      |
| Broccoli              | 75%      | Parsnips        | 60%      |
| Cabbage               | 75%      | Peas            | 80%      |
| Carrots               | 55%      | Peppers         | 55%      |
| Cauliflower           | 75%      | Pumpkins        | 70%      |
| Corn (sweet)          | 75%      | Radishes        | 75%      |
| Cucumbers             | 80%      | Spinach         | 60%      |
| Eggplant              | 60%      | Squash (summer) | 75%      |
| Lettuce               | 80%      | Tomatoes        | 75%      |
| Turnips               | 80%      |                 |          |

These numbers may look reasonable, but consider what they mean: a USDA minimum
of 55% for carrots means nearly half the seeds in a legally compliant packet
could be dead at the time of testing — and germination only declines from there.

## The Loophole and How It Is Exploited

The fundamental problem is the gap between **minimum legal standard** and
**actual performance in the garden**:

1. **The minimum is a low bar.** A seed lot that barely meets the USDA minimum
   may have been produced from marginal parent stock, harvested poorly, or
   stored in suboptimal conditions before testing.

2. **Germination declines over time.** Even properly stored seed loses vigor.
   A lot that tested at 70% six months before you plant it may only achieve
   50% or less in your garden.

3. **Small packets are exempt.** Most home gardeners buy seed in packets well
   under 1 pound. These may carry a germination date, but there is no legal
   requirement for testing, and no enforcement ensures accuracy.

4. **Seed may sit on shelves for years.** A packet printed "Packed for 2023"
   may contain seed harvested years earlier and only repackaged when demand
   warranted. The original test result bears little resemblance to actual
   performance.

## Johnny's Selected Seeds: A Higher Standard

Johnny's Selected Seeds, a respected supplier described by [[steve-solomon]] on]] in
*Gardening When It Counts* (Chapter 4), operated on a fundamentally different
principle. Their policy was to **buy seed lots that tested at 85% germination
or higher** at the time of purchase. This commercial-quality minimum served as
a buffer against inevitable decline during storage and handling.

If Johnny's bought seed at 85%, even after 6-9 months of storage and
distribution, seed reaching the gardener would still likely germinate at 75-80%
or better — well above the USDA minimum. A lot that tested at the USDA minimum
of 55% for carrots at purchase could easily drop to 30-40% by planting time.

## Three Tiers of Germination Quality

**1. USDA Minimum Standard Germination** — The legal floor. Provides no safety
margin for storage decline. Barely compliant seed often performs poorly.

**2. Commercial Quality Minimum (e.g., Johnny's 85%)** — A practical threshold
used by quality-conscious [[spanish-cannabis-seed-companies]]. Provides a meaningful buffer so seed
still performs well after months of storage, shipping, and shelf time.

**3. Storage Potential (Maximum Viable Germination)** — Fresh, optimally
conditioned seed from a reputable grower may test at 90-98%. This peak is
rarely what gardeners receive, but illustrates how far retail seed may have
declined from its original quality.

## How Germination Declines Over Time

Seed is a living organism in suspended animation. Even under ideal cool, dry,
dark storage, metabolic processes slowly degrade the embryo:

- **Temperature:** Every 10°F (5.5°C) increase roughly doubles the rate of
  germination decline.
- **Humidity:** Seeds stored above 50% relative humidity absorb water, begin
  respiring, and consume their stored energy reserves.
- **Species variation:** Delicate seeds like onions, parsnips, and spinach
  lose viability within 1-2 years. Hardy seeds like [[query-do-beans-and-peas-feed-nearby-plants-with-nitrogen]] can endure
  3-5 years or more under good conditions.

## Why Gardeners Should Seek Higher Germination Seed

- **Thinning and spacing:** Unreliable germination forces over-sowing and
  thinning, wasting seed, time, and garden space.
- **[[succession-planting]]:** Failed stands discovered too late to replace
  undermine planned successive plantings.
- **Cost efficiency:** A 200-seed packet at 90% germination yields 180 plants;
  at 50%, only 100 — nearly half the value for the same price.
- **Food security:** For gardeners growing a significant portion of their food,
  crop failure from poor seed directly affects household supply.

## Recommendations

- Buy from companies that publish actual germination [[interpreting-soil-test-results]] and dates,
  not just the USDA minimum.
- Look for rates well above the USDA minimum — ideally 80% or higher.
- Check "packed for" dates and calculate time in the distribution chain.
- Store purchased seed in cool, dry, dark conditions to slow further decline.
- Conduct your own simple germination test (damp paper towel in a plastic bag)
  before committing garden space to older seed.
