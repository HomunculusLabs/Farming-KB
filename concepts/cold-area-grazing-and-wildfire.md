## Cold Area Grazing and Wildfire

Cold climates and fire-prone landscapes share a common theme: the fragility of
vegetative cover under stress. Whether the stress comes from frost, overgrazing, or
flame, the loss of plant cover initiates cascading degradation of soil, water, and
productive capacity. Understanding these systems is critical for designing resilient
land management.

## Cold Area Grazing: The Icelandic Model

Iceland provides an instructive case study of cold-climate land management challenges.
Frost penetration to **1.2 metres** in pastures is common, requiring open ditches at
**50-metre spacing** to handle spring thaw runoff from bogs (tile drains remain frozen
in soil until later in spring).

### Seasonal Grazing Pattern

The Icelandic livestock year divides into three phases:
- **6 months** — sheep, cattle, and horses shedded and hay-fed indoors
- **3+ months** — grazed on upland pastures
- **3 months** — grazed on lowland (sometimes improved) pastures

### Pasture Management Challenges

Frost heave in areas of bare-soil erosion requires levelling, re-sodding with peats, or
rest from grazing. Both **mixed-livestock economies** and **long-term rotational
grazing** help keep [[helminth]] (worm) parasites under control.

Available fodder crops include kale, rape, ryegrass, barley, and rye — used as
fodder, silage, or cut for winter hay. [[fukuoka-textdoc-fertilizer-acidification-trace-element-deficiency]] monitoring is essential:
- **Sheep** — cobalt and selenium deficiencies common
- **Pasture** — calcium, copper, and phosphorus must be monitored
- **Shrub and tree browsing** — little developed despite availability of willow and
  dwarf birch species

### Degradation from Deforestation and Overgrazing

Firewood demand has reduced Icelandic forests to **5% of original cover**, and
overgrazing has denuded **50% of total vegetative cover**, especially on fragile
volcanic upland soils. Historically, better animal growth was achieved in the more
wooded, less "improved" highland pastures, but as these break down from overgrazing,
the less thrifty lowlands are being considered as alternatives.

Less than **1% of total area** is cropped. As in most cold, short-season areas,
livestock, sea, and [[mollison-specialist-tropical-crops-forest-products]] are of greatest economic importance.

### Cold Desert Vegetation

In sandy cold deserts, typical species include:
- **Hummock grassland** — thyme, *Festuca*, lichens, dwarf birch (*[[betula-nana]]*),
  *Rhacomitrium*
- **Hayfields** — *Alopecurus*, *Phleum*, *[[poa-pratensis]]* (whitegrass tussock)
- **Bogs** — sedges (*Carex*) and cottongrasses

As bogs are drained or areas fertilised, grasses begin to predominate over shrub and
tussock species, and worm parasites increase — an unintended consequence of
"improvement."

### Eroded Gravel Plains

Overgrazed areas develop into eroded gravel plains. High-energy browse from oaks or
sugar-storing species like striped maple has received no serious consideration.
Deforestation and pasture pressures develop the same spectrum of limiting factors found
in all areas where livestock numbers and clearing expose animals to insoluble disease
levels, combined with **lack of shelter** that can reduce weight gains by **16–30%**.

### Parasite Problems

Lowland sheep in Norway have approximately **ten times the nematode worm population**
of highland animals. It is primarily on the lowlands that high levels of nitrogen
fertilisers are applied, where the consequent lack of energy food in improved pasture
species creates nutritional imbalance. Iceland faces the compound of deforestation,
severe parasite infestation on lowland and fertilised pasture, and lack of shelter and
deep-rooting nutrient-yielding trees.

The solution lies in **innovative forestry** coupled with serious reduction of grazing
on eroding soils. The shift to forest products represents a much-needed option for
cold-climate farmers.

## Wildfire

### Fire as a Landscape Feature

[[wildfire]] is a feature of many sites and climates, and can be created even in hot
[[berry-fruit-production-cool-humid-climates]] by logging or by block plantings of eucalypts and pines. It is
notoriously violent in **summer-dry climates peripheral to large arid areas**; "wet"
savannah or chaparral scrub will burn fiercely when strong advected heat blows in from
deserts.

### Fire Weather and Wind Patterns

Periods of high fire danger coincide with periods of strong ground winds from
continental desert interiors. These winds affect many climatic types on desert borders,
up to **200 km from desert edges**. They are the normal precursors of widespread and
catastrophic wildfires, which in the presence of enough local fuel may develop into
terrifying firestorms — themselves generating a type of fire tornado with fierce
ground winds.

Fire wind rotation follows hemisphere:
- **[[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]]** — anticlockwise
- **Northern hemisphere** — clockwise

### The Compound Problem

In both cold-climate grazing and wildfire-prone landscapes, the underlying pattern is
the same: **loss of vegetative cover initiates a positive feedback loop of
degradation**. Overgrazing removes the plant canopy that protects soil, reduces
infiltration, and prevents erosion. Similarly, fire removes vegetative cover, exposes
soil to wind and water erosion, and opens the way for weed invasion. In both cases,
the solution involves restoring diverse, deep-rooted plant communities — forests,
shrublands, and mixed pastures — that provide both productive [[time-yield-and-ecological-succession]]
resilience.
