# Salvia Divinorum and Salvinorin A

## Overview

Salvia divinorum is a psychoactive plant from the mint family
(Lamiaceae) that contains the neoclerodane diterpene salvinorin A.
Originally used by Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico, it is now grown
and sold in the United States for recreational use. Salvinorin A is
notable for being the first known naturally occurring non-nitrogenous
kappa-opioid receptor agonist, distinguishing it mechanistically from
virtually all other classical hallucinogens.

[[amanita-muscaria-pharmacology]]

## Taxonomy and Classification

Salvia divinorum belongs to the Lamiaceae (mint) family, which
includes many common ornamental and culinary plants. Despite its
relationship to common sage (Salvia officinalis), Salvia divinorum is
chemically distinct and is the only known Salvia species containing
salvinorin A. Other Lamiaceae ornamentals, including Coleus species,
have been chewed by Mazatec Indians as possible substitutes, but to
date only Salvia divinorum has been confirmed to contain the active
compound.

## Pharmacology of Salvinorin A

### Unique Mechanism of Action

Most classical hallucinogens are psychoactive in part because of their
action at the 5-HT2A serotonin receptor subtype. For comparison with other hallucinogenic plants, see [[anticholinergic-hallucinogenic-plants]]. Salvinorin A has no
affinity for serotonin receptors whatsoever. Instead, it is a highly
potent and selective agonist at kappa-opioid receptors, making it
the first naturally occurring non-nitrogenous kappa-opioid receptor
agonist to be identified.

The kappa-opioid receptor mechanism produces effects qualitatively
different from classical psychedelics. Users commonly report
dissociation, spatial disorientation, altered perception of reality,
and sometimes synesthesia or vivid imagery.

### Potency

Salvinorin A is extremely potent. When smoked, psychoactive effects
occur at doses of 200 to 500 micrograms, making it one of the most
potent naturally occurring psychoactive substances known.

## Traditional Use

### Mazatec Indian Practices

Salvia divinorum was traditionally consumed by Mazatec Indians of
Oaxaca, Mexico. Traditional methods include chewing fresh leaves or
drinking the juice of the leaves for absorption of salvinorin A
through the oral mucosa. These methods produce a less intense
intoxation compared to smoking, with effects lasting up to 1 hour.

The Mazatec use Salvia divinorum in divinatory and healing contexts
as part of their broader shamanic tradition.

## Modern Use and Commercial Availability

### Plant Material and Extracts

In the United States, Salvia divinorum plants are grown and sold for
recreational use. The leaves are sometimes smoked, and consumers can
purchase concentrated extracts through Internet-based companies and
smoke shops. These concentrated extracts significantly increase the
potency compared to traditional leaf consumption.

### Duration of Effects by Route

- **Smoked**: Effects last approximately 15 minutes at doses of 200
  to 500 micrograms of salvinorin A, with peak effects within 1 to
  2 minutes of inhalation.
- **Oral/sublingual absorption**: Less intense intoxication lasting
  up to 1 hour, with slower onset and more gradual decline.

## Effects Profile

Effects of Salvia divinorum can include dissociation and feelings of
detachment from reality, spatial disorientation, visual and auditory
distortions, uncontrollable laughter, synesthesia-like experiences,
temporal distortion, and in some cases frightening or overwhelming
experiences. Intensity is strongly dose-dependent and varies based
on preparation method and individual sensitivity.

## Health and Safety Considerations

### Abuse Potential

Salvia divinorum has no known reinforcing properties or addiction
potential. The intense and often disorienting nature of the
experience, combined with the lack of euphoric effects typically
associated with drugs of abuse, makes compulsive use unlikely.

### Primary Risks

Harm most likely occurs from inadequate preparation, being
unprepared for the intensity of the experience, or dangerous settings
such as driving. Concentrated extracts increase the risk of
overwhelming experiences.

## Legal Status

As of the Halpern (2004) review, the DEA had not scheduled Salvia
divinorum but was monitoring it as a possibly emerging drug of abuse.
Since then, many US states have enacted their own restrictions. A
concern is that if Salvia divinorum were placed into Schedule I,
users might seek substitutes among other Lamiaceae plants, creating
legal complications given the popularity of these common ornamentals.

## Related Lamiaceae Plants

Coleus blumei and Coleus pumilus are sometimes chewed by Mazatec
Indians instead of Salvia divinorum. These popular garden plants are
prized for their intense leaf colors. A more determined search for a
Salvia divinorum substitute might be triggered by scheduling.
