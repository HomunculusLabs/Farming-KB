# Self-Sufficiency Through Vegetable Gardening

Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts" is fundamentally about growing
food that truly sustains you. This page synthesizes his key arguments and
practical guidance for achieving meaningful self-sufficiency through home
[[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]].

## Historical Lessons

### The British Wartime Allotment

During World War II, Britain expanded the allotment system (community
garden plots) into a national institution. Each plot had to be at least
300 square yards (2,700 square feet / 250 sq m). People spent time on
their allotments after work. Sunday afternoons became social events at the
gardens. During hard times, having 2,700 square feet of vegetable garden
made the difference between health and sickness, between having enough to
eat and low-grade hunger.

### The Cuban Urban Garden Revolution

After the fall of the Soviet Union, Cuba lost its main trading partner and
food importer. The response was remarkable: the government broke up huge
cooperative sugar farms and converted them to individual holdings. City
people were freely granted garden blocks at the edges of towns and cities,
each a third of an acre. Today, half the produce consumed in Havana is
grown in urban gardens, and urban gardens produce 60 percent of the
vegetables consumed in all of Cuba. Neighborhood gardens not only feed
their members but donate to schools, clinics, and senior centers, and
still have excess to sell at low prices.

All this produce is organically grown. The Cubans are now on the planet's
leading edge in developing holistic non-petroleum-based horticulture.

## How Much Land Do You Need?

Solomon provides a rough gauge based on the British wartime allotment
experience. Britain's cool, frequently cloudy summers mean vegetables grow
more slowly than in most of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], but the mild winters allow
growing frost-hardy crops year-round. The wartime British got about a
third of their family's total caloric intake from their 2,700 square feet
of vegetable gardens, including potatoes.

For a more complete diet producing half or nearly all calories from the
garden, the requirements are much greater. Solomon's own garden in
Tasmania uses about 2,000 square feet (185 sq m) of actual growing area
in beds. Potatoes and sweet potatoes make up about half the daily
calories for two adults. If your goal is complete caloric
self-sufficiency, you would need approximately 4,500 square feet (420 sq
m) of growing area.

## The Critical Role of Potatoes

The healthful potato is the cornerstone of garden-based self-sufficiency.
Producing cereal grains would require five to ten times as much land as
growing an equivalent caloric value of potatoes. For serious calorie
production, potatoes are the thing. See
[[solomon-garden-planning-calorie-production]].

## The Ley System: Rotating with Grass

Solomon practices a ley system: every four or five years, a large section
of his land goes to rest -- not watered, growing rough grass and clover
like a pasture. It is thoroughly mowed a few times each summer, and all
the clippings lie in place to decompose. A ley rebuilds the soil's
content of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] and restores the biological process to a stable,
healthy balance.

Every four or five years, most of the vegetable beds are put to rest in
grass, and the grass beds are turned over to grow vegetables. For the
first two years after breaking the sod, vegetables on the new ground grow
noticeably better than the ones on the old beds. By about the fourth year,
the appearance of disease and slower overall growth signals that it is
time to rotate again. See
[[solomon-vegetable-nutritional-decline-trace-minerals]].
