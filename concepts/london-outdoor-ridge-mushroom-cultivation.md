William Falconer, in his 1891 work *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (Chapter XX), documented these methods in what he and C.W. Shaw called "the first attempt at long and systematic observation of the best culture as it is in London market gardens." This page summarizes Falconer's detailed account of ridge-based outdoor mushroom growing as it existed around London at the close of the Victorian era.

## Historical Context

Mushrooms were once treated as a general field crop — planted as a matter of course alongside snap beans or cauliflower — but by 1891 they had become a specialty pursuit. See [[mushroom-spawn-making-detailed]] and spawn production.

## John F. Barter of Lancaster Street

John F. Barter was regarded by Falconer as "the king of London mushroom growers." He employed men continuously for bed-making from August through March each year.

Barter produced approximately 10,000 bushels of brick spawn annually — equivalent to roughly 160,000 bricks or 160,000 pounds. Spawn sales provided half of his living income. At retail prices (10¢ per pound) this output was worth $16,000; at wholesale (5¢/lb) it fetched $8,000; and at manufacturer's rates (3.5¢/lb) it still represented $5,600.

His operation exemplified the scale achievable when combining mushroom growing with commercial spawn production.

## Manure Preparation

Manure was obtained from city stables and hauled home on the return trips from market, making efficient use of otherwise empty wagons. See [[compost-preparation-agaricus]].

## Site Selection

The ideal site was warm, well-sheltered ground — either an open field or an orchard. Protection from cold winds was considered critical to success.

Many growers placed their ridges under the partial shade of apple and pear trees, but Falconer himself preferred open ground. The land needed to be dry and either slightly elevated or gently sloping, with no risk of standing water.

The ground was marked off in alternating strips: two feet wide for the mushroom bed and six feet wide for the space between beds. Finished ridges measured approximately six feet wide at the base.

Common ridge dimensions were either two feet wide by two feet high, or two and a half feet wide by two and a half feet high, tapering to six to eight inches at the top.

## Bed Construction

The prepared manure was shaken out evenly along the marked strip. It was tamped with the back of a fork and trodden solidly with the feet two to three times during construction. The sides were trimmed to shed rain water cleanly.

Holes were bored from the top nearly to the bottom using a small iron bar, spaced about nine inches apart along the center line. These vented excess heat and prevented the bed from drying out.

Experienced gardeners were careful to avoid using a crowbar and were cautious not to build ridges before the danger of overheating had passed. If a bed did overheat, the manure was tossed to one side and the other (part right, part left), exposed for one or two days to cool, and then rebuilt on the same site.

For more on related bed-building techniques, see [[mushroom-bed-installation-spawning-duggar-1915]].

## Casing (Molding Over)

The casing material — referred to by Falconer as "mold" — was soil taken from ground that had not had manure applied to it for some time. General market gardeners did not use special loam, relying instead on ordinary good soil.

This practical approach contrasted with the more refined casing strategies discussed in [[bulk-substrate-preparation]].
