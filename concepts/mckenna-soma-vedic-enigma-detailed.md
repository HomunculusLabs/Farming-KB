# The Soma Enigma

In Chapter 7 of *Food of the Gods*, McKenna undertakes a detailed
examination of the Soma question — one of the great unsolved mysteries
of ethnobotany and comparative religion. The [[rig-veda-soma-hymns-amanita-muscaria]], composed
roughly 3,500 years ago, contains over 1,000 hymns to Soma, a divine
plant that was pressed, filtered, and consumed in elaborate Vedic
rituals. Yet the identity of this sacred plant has been lost for
millennia.

## Contacting the Mind Behind Nature

McKenna frames the Soma question as fundamentally about humanity's
relationship with what he calls "the mind behind nature" — an
intelligence accessible through psychoactive plants that was once
well-known to our ancestors but has been systematically forgotten.
The Vedic poets describe Soma in terms that leave no doubt it was a
powerful psychoactive substance: it bestowed visions, divine
ecstasy, immortality, and direct communion with the gods.

## The Wasson Amanita Theory

R. [[gordon-wasson-comparative-method-ethnomycology]], the banker-turned-ethnomycologist who introduced
[[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] to the Western world through his 1957 *Life*
magazine article, proposed that Soma was the [[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]] mushroom,
*[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]*. McKenna presents Wasson's argument and its
considerable problems:

### Evidence for Amanita muscaria
- It is the most visually striking and culturally significant mushroom
  in Eurasia
- It has a long history of ritual use among Siberian peoples
- Its bright red cap with white spots makes it an ideal symbolic
  object for religious veneration
- Wasson found linguistic connections between Vedic terms and Siberian
  mushroom-using traditions

### Objections to Fly Agaric
- Its effects (nausea, confusion, agitation) do not match the Vedic
  descriptions of ecstatic bliss and divine vision
- It is toxic — several *Amanita* species are deadly poisonous,
  making identification risky
- The Vedic texts describe a liquid preparation; *Amanita muscaria*
  is not typically prepared as a beverage
- No archaeological evidence of *Amanita* use in Vedic India

McKenna argues that Wasson, despite his enormous contributions to
ethnomycology, was led astray by his fixation on the fly agaric's
dramatic appearance and its prominence in [[harner-amanita-muscaria-siberian-shamanism]].

## Peganum harmala as Soma

McKenna gives serious consideration to *Peganum harmala* ([[golden-guide-syrian-rue-and-maoi-plants]])
as a candidate for Soma. This plant contains harmine and harmaline
— beta-carboline alkaloids that are:

- Potent [[mao-inhibitors-and-psychedelics]] (the basis of the ayahuasca effect)
- Visionary at sufficient doses
- Easily prepared as a yellow-orange liquid (matching Soma's
  description as a golden or tawny beverage)
- Native to the Iranian plateau and Central Asia — the likely homeland
  of the Indo-European peoples who composed the Rig Veda
- Still used ritually in parts of the Middle East and Central Asia

McKenna notes that if *Peganum harmala* was the primary Soma plant,
it could have been combined with DMT-containing plants to produce a
brew analogous to ayahuasca. The beta-carbolines would inhibit the
MAO enzyme, allowing orally active DMT — a combination that would
produce precisely the kind of intense visionary experience described
in the Vedic hymns.

## Soma as Male Moon God

McKenna explores the Vedic mythology surrounding Soma as a deity —
a male moon god associated with the celestial realm, immortality,
and the cosmic order. This mythological dimension supports the
identification of Soma with a psychoactive plant, as the experience
it produces is consistently described in terms of:

- Celestial or cosmic imagery (stars, moon, galaxies)
- A sense of immortality or timelessness
- Divine or superhuman knowledge
