# Garden Myths About Planting and Pruning

Planting and pruning practices are rife with myths that have been repeated so often they achieve the status of accepted wisdom. Many of these practices originated as reasonable approximations that have since been superseded by horticultural research, or as misapplications of observations from one context to all situations.

## Myth: You Must Plant Trees at the Same Depth They Were Growing in the Nursery

The "plant at the same level" rule seems intuitive but is one of the most common causes of tree establishment failure and long-term decline.

### Why It Fails

- **Nursery practices bury the root flare**: Trees in nurseries are often planted too deeply during production, with the root flare (where the trunk widens at the base) buried beneath soil or mulch. If you plant at "nursery depth," you are perpetuating a problem that already exists
- **Root flare must be visible**: The root flare is trunk tissue, not root tissue. When buried, it remains constantly moist, creating conditions for crown rot, canker diseases, and girdling root formation
- **Girdling roots**: Trees planted too deep often develop roots that circle the trunk, eventually strangling it. This is a leading cause of premature tree death in urban landscapes
- **Soil accumulation over time**: Landscaping activities, mulch volcanoes, and grade changes gradually bury root flares even when initially planted correctly

### What Actually Works

- **Find the root flare** before planting: Gently remove soil from the top of the root ball until the first major roots are visible at the base of the trunk
- **Plant with the root flare at or slightly above grade**: The root flare should be visible after planting and mulching
- **Dig wide, not deep**: The planting hole should be 2-3 times the width of the root ball but no deeper than the root ball from flare to bottom
- **Do not amend the backfill**: Research consistently shows that amending planting hole soil creates a "bathtub effect" where roots resist leaving the amended zone, reducing long-term root expansion
- **Mulch properly**: Apply 2-4 inches of mulch in a wide ring, keeping mulch pulled back 3-6 inches from the trunk (no mulch volcanoes)

## Myth: Painting Pruning Cuts Prevents Disease and Decay

Tree wound paints, sealers, and dressings have been used for generations. They are still sold in garden centers despite decades of research showing they are ineffective or harmful.

### Why It Fails

- **Trees do not "heal" wounds**: They compartmentalize them. The tree forms specialized wood (CODIT -- Compartmentalization Of Decay In Trees) that walls off the damaged area from healthy tissue
- **Wound dressings interfere with compartmentalization**: Sealants can trap moisture and decay organisms beneath the coating, actually accelerating decay
- **No evidence of benefit**: Multiple longitudinal studies have shown that wound dressings do not prevent decay, insect entry, or disease spread
- **Cracking and peeling**: Most wound dressings crack as the tree grows, creating entry points for water and organisms

### What Actually Works

- **Make clean cuts**: Sharp tools minimize damage to the compartmentalization zone
- **Prune at the correct location**: Cut just outside the branch collar (the swollen area where the branch meets the trunk). The branch collar contains the tree's natural defense tissues
- **Leave the wound open**: Allow the tree's natural compartmentalization process to work
- **Target pruning to the right season**: For most species, prune during dormancy to minimize disease transmission risk
- **Keep trees healthy**: Vigorous trees compartmentalize wounds more effectively than stressed trees
