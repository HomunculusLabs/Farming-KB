## Winter Spinach as the Dependable Cold-House Crop

Overwintered spinach has become the cornerstone crop of [[eliot-coleman]]'s winter-harvest system at Four Season Farm.
Of all the cold-hardy vegetables grown in the cold houses,
spinach stands out as the most reliable,  productive,
and economically valuable winter green.
Coleman identifies three reasons for its primacy: it is perfectly adapted to cold-house conditions,
it commands strong market demand throughout winter,
and its timing fits precisely into the mobile greenhouse rotation cycle.
Understanding why spinach excels — and how to manage it through the winter — is essential for any grower attempting cold-house winter production.

## Why Spinach Excels in Cold Houses

### Continuous Winter Growth

The most critical advantage of spinach over other cold-hardy greens is that it continues producing new leaves throughout the winter months.
Many cold-hardy crops — kale,
for example — survive winter temperatures but essentially stop growing during the coldest period (the Persephone months,
when day length drops below 10 hours).
They hold their existing leaves but do not generate new growth until day length increases in late January or February.

Spinach,  by contrast,
continues to slowly but steadily produce new leaves even during the shortest days.
While growth rates are reduced compared to fall or spring,
the plant never truly goes dormant under cold-house protection.
This means that each harvest removes older leaves and the plant replaces them with new growth,
allowing for multiple harvests from the same planting.

This characteristic makes spinach unique among winter greens: it is the only multiple-harvest overwintered green crop in the Coleman system.
Other winter crops like lettuce,  radishes,
and baby pak choi are harvested once and then the space is replanted.
Spinach provides four harvests at approximately monthly intervals from a single September sowing.

### Cold Hardiness

Spinach is exceptionally cold-hardy,
with some varieties surviving temperatures well below freezing.
Under the double-layer protection of the cold house,
spinach tolerates the temperatures that occur in Zone 5 Maine winters without significant damage.
The cell structure of spinach leaves includes sugars and other cryoprotective compounds that act as natural antifreeze,
preventing ice crystal formation within cells even when the leaf tissue itself freezes.

The double-layer protection raises effective temperatures by approximately 15°F to 20°F on sunny days and 5°F to 7°F on cold nights.
When outside temperatures drop to -10°F,
the cold-house interior may still be at approximately -3°F to -5°F,
a range that mature spinach can tolerate without significant tissue damage.

### Market Demand

Spinach is enormously popular as a winter crop,
and demand consistently exceeds supply at Four Season Farm.
Winter spinach has several quality advantages over imported or greenhouse spinach from warmer climates.
The cold-growing conditions produce sweeter,
more flavorful leaves due to the concentration of sugars as a cold-protection mechanism,
and the freshness of locally harvested product cannot be matched by shipped produce.

### Perfect Rotation Timing

Mid-September-sown outdoor spinach is hardy enough not to need greenhouse protection until the mobile greenhouse is available in late October.
This creates an elegant sequence: the greenhouse covers heat-loving summer crops through the growing season,
then moves over the spinach in the second half of October.
The spinach has already established a strong root system and leaf canopy outdoors,
so it transitions smoothly into the protected environment.

## The Planting and Harvest Schedule

### Sowing

Spinach for the winter harvest is sown outdoors in mid-September in coastal Maine (USDA Zone 5).
This timing is critical: early enough for the plants to establish robust root systems and a good leaf canopy before the onset of cold weather,
but late enough that the plants do not grow too large or begin to bolt before winter arrives.

Coleman recommends direct sowing rather than transplanting for winter spinach.
Direct-sown plants develop more robust and deeper root systems than transplants,
which improves their ability to survive and grow through the coldest months.
Seeds are sown in rows approximately 12 inches apart,
with thinning to 4-6 inches between plants once established.

### Harvest Cycle

Harvesting begins around Thanksgiving (late November).
Spinach is harvested heavily through December for the pre-Christmas market.
This heavy harvesting reduces the leaf canopy significantly,
and plants need approximately one month to recover before producing enough new growth for the next harvest.
This natural pause coincides with the farm's vacation period.

During the five weeks after winter solstice (late December through late January),
the spinach is largely left alone to recover.
Only candy carrots and overwintered leeks are harvested fresh during this period.
The spinach produces approximately four harvests at monthly intervals before it begins going to seed in late March.

### End of Cycle and Transition

After the final spinach harvest in late March,
beds are prepared for early-April transplanting of the earliest tomato crop.
The cold house becomes a warm house: a propane heater maintains 60°F night temperature for tomatoes.
This transition from winter spinach to early tomatoes is one of the most satisfying sequences in the yearly schedule.

## Mobile Greenhouse Rotation Integration

The spinach cycle is integral to Coleman's mobile greenhouse rotation design.
In Year 1,
the house covers September-sown spinach from late October through March,
then transitions to tomatoes from April through October. In Year 2,
the previous greenhouse site is uncovered for a full growing season of [[green-manure]] (typically red clover),
building soil fertility and biological activity while allowing natural cleansing of pest and disease pressure.
