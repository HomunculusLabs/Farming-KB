Source: Paul Stamets, *The Mushroom Cultivator* (1983)

Spawning is the inoculation of compost or bulk substrates with mushroom mycelium. Spawn running is the colonisation of the substrate. Four critical factors determine success: moisture content, temperature, substrate dry weight, and duration of spawn run.

## Moisture Content

Mushroom mycelium does not grow in substrates that are too dry or too wet:
- **Too dry**: produces fine, wispy mycelial growth and poor mushroom formation (water essential for nutrient transport)
- **Too wet**: inhibits mycelial growth, produces overly stringy mycelia

Controlled experiments with Agaricus show yield depressions when moisture deviates more than 2% from optimum. Deviations greater than 5% generally prevent fruitbody production entirely.

Corrections:
- Dry compost: lightly water and mix well (guard against wet spots)
- Over-wet compost: add gypsum until loose water is bound

## Substrate Temperature

Thermometers placed at the center of the substrate (hottest region) and in room air establish a temperature differential. Optimal temperatures vary by species:
- Agaricus brunnescens: fastest growth at 77F (25C)
- Psilocybe cubensis: prefers 86F (30C)

Temperatures above or below optimum simply slow growth. Growth slows faster as temperature rises above optimum.

### Heat Generation During Spawn Run
- Mycelial metabolism generates tremendous heat
- Peak temperatures normally reached on 7th-9th day after spawning
- Can easily reach 90F; thermophilic microorganisms become active, potentially driving temperatures above 100F
- Temperatures 95-110F can kill mycelium of many species
- Even non-lethal elevated temperatures do irreversible harm to mycelial vitality and fruiting potential
- Elevated temperatures also stimulate competitor molds

Agaricus growers rarely fill more than 12 inches of compost into beds due to enhanced heat generation in deeper fills.

## Dry Weight of Substrate

Dry weight per square foot of cropping surface largely determines total yield:
- Commercial Agaricus growers aim for at least 5 lbs dry weight per sq. ft.
- Some compress up to 8 lbs per sq. ft.
- Cropping efficiency = total yield per sq. ft. / dry weight per sq. ft.
- Efficiencies of 80-100% are considered near maximum yield potential for Agaricus

Tracer element experiments in 3-foot deep beds showed nutrients from the farthest point are transported to growing mushrooms. Yields per sq. ft. increased although at lower substrate efficiency.

## Spawning Methods

### Mixed Spawning
The standard method: complete and thorough mixing of grain kernels throughout the substrate. All parts are equally inoculated, resulting in the most rapid and complete colonisation.

Standard spawning rate: 7 liters per ton of compost or 1 quart per 8 sq. ft.

Best practices:
- Break up grain spawn into individual kernels the day before spawning
- If spawn is fresh, grain should break apart easily
- If not used fresh, refrigerate at 38F
- Work under carefully controlled hygienic conditions
- Disinfect working area and tools with 10% bleach solution one day prior
- Exclude fungus gnats (tight, well-sealed working area essential)

### Application Methods
- Shelves: broadcast spawn over surface, mix in with pitchfork or by hand
- Trays: similar method, or dump substrate on clean surface, mix with spawn, replace
- Bulk room: remove substrate, mix with spawn, place into chosen container
- Level and compress substrate to avoid dehydration from excessive air penetration
- Commercial growers compress with hydraulic press to achieve table-top smoothness for even casing application

## Environmental Conditions for Spawn Run

- **Humidity**: 90-100% critical (below this, water evaporates from substrate surface, harming mycelium)
- **CO2**: 10,000-15,000 ppm considered beneficial and desirable (mushroom mycelium capable of CO2 fixation)
- **Temperature**: controlled by manipulation of surrounding air temperature
- **Air circulation**: steady or periodic recirculation prevents localised over-heating, especially in upper containers

Humidification by steam humidifiers or cold water misters. If steam used, watch for air temperature rise driving substrate too high. Covering substrate with plastic is common for counteracting drying; remove during peak activity if temperatures rise too quickly.

## Duration of Spawn Run

Once colonisation is complete, substrate should be cased (or switched to fruiting mode if no casing). Continuing spawn running beyond this wastes nutrients on further vegetative growth that could produce fruitbodies. If cropping must be delayed, cool the substrate.

## Super Spawning (Active Mycelium Spawning / Hunke-Till Process)

A quantity of substrate is inoculated and colonised normally. The fully run substrate is then used as inoculum for increased amounts of similar substrate. Theoretically, a small quantity of inoculum can be pyramided into considerable colonised substrate.

Requires primary substrate to be contaminant-free, otherwise contamination propagates instead of mycelium. Excellent application: transferring naturally occurring mycelial colonies to non-sterile but mushroom-specific substrates (e.g., Psilocybe cyanescens on wood chips).

## Supplementation at Spawning

Delayed release nutrients encapsulated in denatured protein coat, added at spawning:
- Designed to become available during first three flushes
- Application rate: 5-7% of dry substrate weight
- Yield increases: 0.5 to 1 lb/sq. ft. normal
- Complete thorough mixing essential
- Caution: enriches substrate for contaminants if predisposing factors present

## Supplementation at Casing (S.A.C.)

Materials: soy bean meal, cottonseed meal, ground rye/wheat/kafir corn grains. Mixed with fully colonised substrate at 10% of dry weight. Both substrate and supplements must be clean and contaminant-free. Anticipate high substrate temperatures 2-3 days after supplementation. Yield increases: 0.5-2 lbs/sq. ft.
