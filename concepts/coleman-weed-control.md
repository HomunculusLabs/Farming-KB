# Weed Control

Eliot Coleman approaches weed control with the principle that
prevention is always better than confrontation. Given the intensive
planting density used in greenhouse production, weed management
becomes especially critical. Even with short-season crops like
baby-leaf salad, quick-growing weeds such as chickweed can
dramatically slow harvest. The yield increase possible from close
spacing on fertile greenhouse soil makes any effort toward solving
weed problems a worthwhile investment of time.

## Prevention First

The primary strategy is to ensure that no weed ever goes to seed.
Coleman hand-weeds in fields and greenhouses whenever necessary.
The operation has even hand-weeded green manures to prevent weed
seed production, or tilled green manures under and started again
when weeds became too numerous. Studies show that the serious
weed-seed load in a soil can be greatly reduced over three to five
years if no new seeds are introduced.

## Pre-Emergence Flaming

Given that weeds will appear even under the best conditions, the
second approach is to dispatch weeds after they germinate but
before the crop emerges, using pre-emergence flaming with a propane
flame. The not-yet-emerged crop seeds are insulated from the heat
by the soil.

Timing is the critical factor. Coleman prepares seeded areas up
to two weeks before the sowing date when possible, irrigating as
necessary to ensure weed-seed germination. After sowing without
further cultivation, the ideal number of days is waited and then
the small weeds are flamed off. This is especially important for
crops planted in rows 2.25 inches apart, for which no cultivating
tool has been found that works well.

Two methods exist for choosing the ideal flaming moment. The
European method uses glass panes about a square foot in size held
an inch off the ground in a wooden frame, placed randomly on the
field surface after sowing to speed up crop seed germination.
Flaming occurs immediately upon seeing the first crop seed
germination under the glass. This works well in early spring.

For summer sowings (when glass would get too hot), Coleman pays
attention and keeps notes on days to germination for different
crops at different seasons, then flames off weeds with a one- or
two-day fudge factor.

## When Flaming Works Best

Flaming is most effective for crops that take a while to germinate,
such as carrots, onions, or parsley. For quick-germinating salad
crops like lettuce and arugula planted in close succession, there
is not enough time for weeds to germinate ahead of the crop,
making pre-emergence flaming difficult.

Chickweed is the most troublesome weed in winter greenhouses
because it thrives on fertile soils and can both germinate and set
seed at surprisingly low temperatures. Coleman has observed chickweed
in full bloom in a cold house on January 15.

## How Flaming Works

Flaming involves wilting, not burning weeds. Exposure to the propane
flame causes the water in the plant cells to expand and burst,
destroying the cell structure. The weeds wilt and die within hours.
The technique requires a steady walking pace and the correct torch
height above the weed seedlings. Coleman recommends practicing on a
test area to calibrate speed and torch position.

## Cultivation Tools

For weeds that escape flaming or appear after crop emergence,
Coleman relies on hand cultivation tools, particularly the collineal
hoe and the wire weeder.

The collineal hoe was developed at Four Season Farm through
experimentation with existing hoes. The design features a narrow
blade (7 inches side to side, 1 inch front to back) with the neck
bent to hold the cutting edge in line with the handle at a 70-degree
angle. This collinear relationship between blade edge and handle
makes the hoe cut weeds most effectively when sharpened like a
chisel, with the sharp edge next to the soil. The optimum angle was
determined through repeated field trials with many participants.

The wire weeder (a long-handled tool with a thin wire loop at the
working end) is used for very small weeds in closely spaced rows.
Both forward and return strokes are employed: the forward stroke
cuts weeds and the return stroke (moving the blade back for the next
cutting stroke) can further disturb cut weeds by keeping the blade
in the soil. These are quick but accurate motions, taking about a
second each.

## Technique and Efficiency

For tiny weeds on sunny days, a rapid cultivation technique involves
walking down the path holding the collineal hoe or wire weeder
between rows just slightly under the soil surface, working in a
direction toward uncut weeds. The forward angle of the blade can be
adjusted to compensate for slight discrepancies in row spacing.
Keeping depth consistent (never more than half an inch) ensures all
tiny weeds are cut off or tipped out to wilt on the surface.

Adjusting technique to conditions is key. With experience, workers
automatically make small efficiency adjustments based on previous
experience. Attention to detail is the major secret to success in
any endeavor, and this is especially true for weed control.

Coleman teaches employees these skills carefully, demonstrating
specific techniques such as harvesting spinach leaf by leaf to
show how holding more cut leaves before dropping them in the
container increases efficiency. On average, 40 percent of a
harvester's time is spent moving the hand to the container, so
minimizing hand travel is critical.

## The Long-Term View

Weed management is viewed as a long-term process. By preventing
seed set consistently for three to five years, the weed seed bank
in the soil is substantially reduced. Combined with intensive
cropping (which leaves little bare soil for weed germination),
flaming (which eliminates weeds before crop emergence), and
careful cultivation (which handles any weeds that escape), the
system progressively becomes easier to manage.
