## Pre-Emergence Flaming

Given that weeds will appear even under the best conditions, the
second approach is to dispatch weeds after they germinate but
before the crop emerges, using pre-emergence flaming with a propane
flame. The not-yet-emerged crop seeds are insulated from the heat
by the soil.

Timing is the critical factor. Coleman prepares seeded areas up
to two weeks before the sowing date when possible, irrigating as
necessary to ensure weed-seed germination. After sowing without
further cultivation, the ideal number of days is waited and then
the small weeds are flamed off. This is especially important for
crops planted in rows 2.25 inches apart, for which no cultivating
tool has been found that works well.

Two methods exist for choosing the ideal flaming moment. The
European method uses glass panes about a [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]].

For summer sowings (when glass would get too hot), Coleman pays
attention and keeps notes on days to germination for different
crops at different seasons, then flames off weeds with a one- or
two-day fudge factor.

## When Flaming Works Best

Flaming is most effective for crops that take a while to germinate,
such as carrots, onions, or parsley. For quick-germinating salad
crops like lettuce and arugula planted in close succession, there
is not enough time for weeds to germinate ahead of the crop,
making pre-emergence flaming difficult.

Chickweed is the most troublesome weed in winter greenhouses
because it thrives on fertile soils and can both germinate and set
seed at surprisingly low temperatures. Coleman has observed chickweed
in full bloom in a cold house on January 15.
