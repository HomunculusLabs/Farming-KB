# Panaeolina foenisecii — The Controversial Psychoactive Lawn Mushroom

## Overview

Panaeolina foenisecii (syn. Psilocybe foenisecii), commonly known as the "haymaker's mushroom" or "brown mottlegill," is one of the most widespread and controversial fungi in the neurotropic fungi literature. It is a cosmopolitan species found in grasslands and prairies across much of the world, yet its status as a psychoactive mushroom has been debated for decades. Multiple independent [[guzman-allen-gartz-conflicting-chemical-analyses-neurotropic-fungi]] have yielded contradictory results — some researchers report detecting psilocybin, while others find no psychoactive compounds whatsoever.

## Taxonomy and Naming

Panaeolina foenisecii was originally described in the genus Psilocybe but has since been moved to Panaeolina based on its dark brown spore print and morphological characteristics. The species epithet "foenisecii" relates to hay (foenum) and cutting (secare), reflecting its common association with mowed grasslands and hayfields.

The taxonomic position of P. foenisecii has been unstable over the years, contributing to some of the confusion in the chemical literature. Different researchers have sometimes worked with specimens identified under different names, making it difficult to determine whether the same species was being studied.

## Global Distribution

Panaeolina foenisecii is considered a cosmopolitan fungus, though its true distribution is likely incompletely documented. Notable distributional gaps exist:

- **Japan**: Despite being very common in Japan, it was not recorded by Imazeki and Hongo in their major Japanese mycoflora works (1983, 1987, 1988). Hongo (1986) was one of the few to document it.
- **[[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] and Caribbean**: Surprisingly, there are no reports from this region, despite its prevalence in both North and South America.
- **Africa**: Records are sparse despite the continent's high [[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]].

The species is typically found in grassy fields, lawns, pastures, and prairies — often appearing after rain in mowed areas. It fruits from spring through autumn in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

## The Chemical Controversy

The central debate surrounding P. foenisecii concerns whether it actually contains psilocybin and related indole compounds. The published evidence is deeply divided:

### Studies Finding NO Psychoactive Compounds

Multiple respected researchers have failed to detect psilocybin or psilocin in P. foenisecii:

- **Mantle and Waight (1969)**: No psilocybin detected
- **Ott and Guzmán (1976)**: No psychoactive indoles found
- **Beug and Bigwood (1982)**: Negative results for [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]]
- **Stijve (1987)**: No psychoactive compounds detected
- **Stijve et al. (1984)**: Failed to find psilocybin
- **Allen and Merlin (1992c)**: Discussed doubts on psychoactive properties

### Studies Claiming Psychoactive Compounds

Several other researchers have reported detecting psilocybin:

- **Robbers et al. (1969)**: Reported psilocybin presence
- **Ola'h (1969)**: Found psilocybin in specimens
- **Fiussello and Ceruti-Scurti (1972)**: Reported psychoactive compounds
- **Pollock (1976)**: Detected psilocybin
- **Bresinsky and Besl (1990)**: Listed as containing psilocybin

## Likely Explanation for Conflicting Results

Guzmán (1998) and Allen & Merlin (1992c) suggest that P. foenisecii is more likely a **toxic** fungus than a neurotropic one. The confusion likely arises from several factors:

1. **Misidentification**: Specimens identified as P. foenisecii may in fact have been mixtures of different species. This is a recurring problem in the neurotropic fungi literature, where many chemical studies lacked a proper taxonomic basis.

2. **Contamination in analysis**: Some positive results may reflect contamination of laboratory samples with other psilocybin-containing material.

3. **Chromatographic artifacts**: Early analytical methods (particularly TLC) were prone to false positives when identifying indole compounds.

4. **Species variability**: If trace amounts of indoles are present, they may vary considerably between populations, seasons, and geographic locations.

## Ecological Role

Panaeolina foenisecii is a saprotrophic fungus that decomposes grass and other plant material in meadows and lawns. It is one of the most commonly encountered lawn mushrooms in temperate regions worldwide. Its fruiting is triggered by rainfall and moderate temperatures.

## Relationship to Other Panaeoloid Fungi

The broader Panaeoloideae group is noted for taxonomic and chemical confusion. Many species in Panaeolus, Copelandia, and Panaeolina have been reported with and without neurotropic substances. Guzmán (1998) observed that the "majority of the Panaeolodeous fungi" lack reliable chemical studies, and several species considered toxic may be confused with psychoactive members of the group.

[[copelandia-cyanescens]], for example, is sometimes confused with [[panaeolus-antillarum-misidentification-copelandia-confusion]] — both present white fruit bodies growing in cow dung. However, P. antillarum does not turn blue and is considered edible, whereas C. cyanescens is strongly psychoactive. This kind of field confusion can propagate into the chemical literature when incorrectly identified specimens are analyzed.

## Significance for the Neurotropic Fungi Checklist

Guzmán, Allen and Gartz (1998) included P. foenisecii among the 216 neurotropic fungi in their global checklist, but with the caveat that its status remains uncertain. They classified it among Group 1 (species with psilocybin and related indoles, or those likely to contain these substances), acknowledging the contradictory evidence.

The case of P. foenisecii illustrates a broader methodological problem in mycological chemistry: the need for rigorous taxonomic verification before chemical analysis. As Stijve (1995) and Ott (1993) both emphasized, many published reports of psychoactive compounds in various fungi are doubtful due to identification errors or analytical artifacts.
