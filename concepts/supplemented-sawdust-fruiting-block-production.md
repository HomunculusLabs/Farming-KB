# Supplemented Sawdust Fruiting Block Production

## Overview

Supplemented sawdust is the standard substrate for cultivating gourmet
and [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-extraction-bioavailability]] indoors. The method combines hardwood sawdust
with nitrogen-rich supplements for high yields. It is used for shiitake,
oyster, lion's mane, maitake, reishi, enoki, and many other species.
Production involves formulation, moisture adjustment, sterilization,
inoculation, incubation, and fruiting.

## Substrate Formulation

The base is hardwood sawdust from oak, maple, beech, alder, or birch.
Softwood is generally avoided due to resins and antimicrobial
compounds, though some species tolerate partial softwood content.
Sawdust should pass through a 1/4 inch screen but not be powdery.

**Bran** (wheat or rice): 10-20% of dry weight — provides nitrogen,
B vitamins, and minerals. The most common supplement.

**Gypsum**: 1-3% — buffers pH, improves texture, provides calcium and
sulfur.

A typical formula: 75-80% hardwood sawdust, 15-20% bran, 2-5% gypsum,
at 60-65% moisture content.

## Moisture Content

Target moisture is 60-65% by weight. Too dry: slow colonization and
reduced yields. Too wet: anaerobic conditions, bacterial contamination,
stalled growth.

The squeeze test: a handful squeezed firmly yields a few drops but not
a continuous stream. Digital [[cervantes-moisture-meters-watering-technique]] provide precision for
commercial operations. Water is mixed thoroughly using mechanical
mixers — uneven moisture causes uneven colonization and contamination.

## Bag Filling and Preparation

Substrate is packed into autoclavable polypropylene bags with filter
patches for gas exchange. Bags are typically 2-5 kg capacity. Packing
should be firm enough to hold shape when the bag is removed but porous
enough for air exchange. Bags are sealed with wire ties or impulse
sealers, leaving adequate headspace for the filter patch.

## Sterilization

Supplemented substrates must be sterilized (not pasteurized) because
nutrient-rich supplements support vigorous competitor growth.
Pasteurization leaves heat-resistant spores alive and is insufficient.

**Autoclave**: 121°C at 15-17 PSI for 2-4 hours depending on bag size.
The gold standard for thorough sterilization.

**[[growing-gourmet-atmospheric-steam-sterilization]]**: 8-12 hours at atmospheric pressure — an
alternative for growers without pressure vessels. Less energy-efficient
and requires careful temperature monitoring.

Proper sterilization requires the core temperature to reach target for
the full duration. Cold spots are a common contamination source.

## Cooling and Inoculation

Bags must cool below 30°C before inoculation — hot substrate kills
mycelium. Inoculation is performed in a clean area ([[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]]
or still air box). The bag is opened, grain spawn added at 5-10% of
substrate dry weight, and resealed. Spawn is distributed by shaking or
massaging the bag. The inoculation step is the primary contamination
risk window — strict clean technique is essential.

## Incubation

Inoculated bags are placed in a dark or dim incubation room at 20-25°C
(most species) with moderate humidity (60-70%). Mycelium visibly
whitens the block over 2-4 weeks depending on species, spawn rate,
and temperature. Bags should have spacing for air circulation.

Contaminated bags (green molds, bacterial slime, strange colors) must
be removed promptly to prevent [[adaptive-anemotropism-spore-dispersal-optimization-mushroom-evolution]]. After full colonization,
most species benefit from 1-2 weeks of consolidation before fruiting.
