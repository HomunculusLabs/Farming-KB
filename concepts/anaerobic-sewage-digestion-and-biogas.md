# Anaerobic Sewage Digestion and Biogas Production

Anaerobic sewage digestion is a biological wastewater treatment process where raw sewage decomposes in the absence of oxygen, producing methane-rich biogas as a useful energy byproduct. Bill Mollison documented this process extensively through the Werribee sewage treatment system in Victoria, Australia, demonstrating how polluted influent can be transformed into energy, nutrients, and clean water through staged biological treatment.

## The Three-Stage Pond System

Sewage treatment using natural processes proceeds through three distinct pond types arranged in gravity-fed series:

1. **Anaerobic (methane-producing) ponds**: Raw sewage settles and decomposes without oxygen, generating biogas
2. **Facultative ponds**: Partially anaerobic at the base (sludge), partially aerobic at the surface due to wind and algae
3. **Aerobic (oxygen-producing) ponds**: Fully oxygenated surface water supporting rich ecological communities

At Werribee, Melbourne (population 2,700,000), the sewage lagoons cover 1,500 hectares, providing approximately one hectare of pond per 1,800 inhabitants. Raw sewage flows into about 724 hectares of primary settling ponds, with individual ponds rarely exceeding 7 hectares. Scaled down, this means approximately 1 hectare of settling pond serves 3,800 people.

## Anaerobic Digester Design

One thousand townspeople and their associated industries need as little as 270 square metres of settling pond at 1 metre depth. This can be configured as a long narrow pond 3 metres wide by 90 metres long, or three side-by-side ponds each 30 metres long and 3 metres wide. The length can be halved by doubling depth to 2 metres, or condensed to 3 metres deep by 3 metres wide by 30 metres long.

### Sealed and Agitated Digesters

Long narrow ponds are easily made fully anaerobic by fitting water seals and a weighted cover (of plastic, metal, butyl rubber, or fibreglass). For deeper digester ponds, the sludge must be artificially agitated using pumped biogas to stir it — otherwise it settles and becomes inactive.

Sludge remains "active" only in contact with semi-liquid inputs from the sewer. Stirring the sludge maximises contact and accelerates sewage breakdown to biogas. A critical benefit of sealed and agitated digesters is that no surface scum forms, which would otherwise slow the breakdown process and cause acid conditions.

## Biogas Production and Energy

Over a 20-day retention period at 25-30°C (77-86°F), a very high percentage of total dissolved solids is transferred into methane. As methane forms, the oxygen demand of the effluent falls — approximately one cubic metre of methane generated removes about 2.89 kg of solids, reducing biological oxygen demand (BOD) to that extent.

In the digester, 90-94% of worm eggs are destroyed, along with many harmful bacteria. The useful energy generated can run motors for electricity generation or compress gas for cooking and machinery. The motor simultaneously supplies heat for the digester process and compresses gas for digester agitation and energy supply.

## Heavy Metal Removal in Anaerobic Ponds

The hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) produced as marsh gas combines with soluble forms of heavy metals to produce sulphides, which are insoluble in water above pH 7. A small amount of lime can assist this precipitation. Research by Hussainy (Melbourne Metropolitan Water Board) found the following removal efficiencies in anaerobic ponds:

| Metal | Total Removal | Anaerobic Removal |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| Copper | 97% | 78% |
| Cadmium | 70% | 70% (all) |
| Zinc | 97% | 83% |
| Nickel | 65% | 18% (47% aerobic) |
| Lead | 95% | 90% |
| Chromium | 87% | 47% |
| Iron | 85% | 47% |

Solids, metals, and disease organisms are very greatly reduced by the first anaerobic treatment. The sludge becomes methane — the more sludge present, the more active the pond. A self-regulated equilibrium establishes where input balances gas output. Removing sludge slows or stops the process. This represents a case where active sludge is its own solution; rather than being a problem, it generates a resource.

## Sulphur-Loving Bacteria

In the anaerobic pond, few algae are present, but specialised sulphur-loving bacteria of the genera *Thiosporallum*, *Chromatium*, and *Rhodopseudomonas* thrive. These may appear pink and give the ponds a characteristic colouration. They use hydrogen sulphide as a hydrogen source for carbon assimilation, producing elemental sulphur (S) as a byproduct, which binds to metals present. About 1.8-2.0 mg/L of heavy metals are precipitated as sulphides at 1.0 mg/L of elemental sulphur.



## Facultative Pond Ecology

In facultative ponds, both the life forms and biochemical processes change dramatically. Algae bloom prolifically, with four almost universal sewage lagoon species: *Euglena*, *Chlamydomonas*, *Chlorella*, and *Scenedesmus*. The combined algal and bacterial flora (of many species) are collectively called **phytoplankton**. Bacteria benefit from oxygen produced by the algae, creating a mutually beneficial relationship.

With such rich algal food available, **zooplankton** now thrive: rotifers (*Brachionus*, *Trichocerca*, *Hexarthra*, *Filinia*), cladocerans (*Daphnia*, *Moina*, *Chydorus*, *Pleuroxus*), copepods (*Mesocyclops*), and ostracods (*Candanocypris*, *Cypridopsis*). Protozoan flagellates, ciliates, and some nematodes are also present.

Zooplankton accumulate metals in their tissues (mg/L dry weight): iron 1,200, zinc 152, copper 37, chromium 28, nickel 12.2, lead 10.3, cadmium 1.7. Harvested zooplankton and algae can be added to poultry feed or pumped to forests and fields as manure and trace element sources. In rich algal growth, blooms of *Daphnia* can reach densities of 100 mg/L.



## Aerobic Pond Performance

At the aerobic stage, BOD drops to only 3-57 mg/L (mainly nitrogenous compounds), and suspended solids are 32-50 mg/L (primarily algae and zooplankton). About 80% of the original load has been removed and incorporated into living organisms. Metal levels now meet World Health Organisation standards. The water is suitable for irrigation or can be filtered through rush beds before release to streams.



## pH Progression and Seasonal Variation

pH increases through the pond stages: anaerobic pH 6.2-7.8, facultative pH 7.5-8.2, aerobic pH 7.5-8.5. In clogged algal waters at night, pH may climb even higher.

Seasonal changes are significant:

- **Winter**: More hydrogen sulphide released (8-15 mg/L vs summer's 2-5 mg/L); winds contribute more to oxygenation than algae; more ammonia released to atmosphere; decomposition slows and sludge builds up at 10-15°C
- **Summer**: Residues oxidised to nitrates; oxygen provided more by algae than wind; less hydrogen sulphide; BOD rises from 495 kg/ha/day in winter to 1,034 kg/ha/day in summer — activity nearly doubles with temperature. Almost twice as much methane is produced in summer

In winter, cooling water from methane-powered engines can provide essential heat to digesters via closed-loop piping. This integration of energy production with biological treatment makes the entire system self-sustaining.
