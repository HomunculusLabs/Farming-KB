## Jefferson Airplane and "White Rabbit"

The single most influential mushroom-related song of the psychedelic era is
Jefferson Airplane's "White Rabbit" (1967), written by Grace Slick. The song
uses the framework of Alice in Wonderland as a thinly disguised guide to the
psychedelic experience.

Slick's banshee-like vocal extols the virtues of the psychedelic experience in
an escalating code: "One pill makes you larger, and one pill makes you small."
The famous refrain, "Go ask Alice, I think she'll know," and the line "You've
just had some kind of mushroom, and your mind is moving low," explicitly linked
the Alice story to mushroom intoxication.

The song became an anthem of the psychedelic counterculture and played a major
role in broadcasting to the world the news about mushrooms and psychedelics.
Backed by a classic West Coast psychedelic guitar sound, it captured the
sensibility of the Haight-Ashbury scene with extraordinary precision.

## The Grateful Dead and Mushroom Culture

The Grateful Dead became perhaps the most closely associated band with mushroom
culture. Their extended improvisational style, with its emphasis on musical
exploration and altered states of consciousness, resonated deeply with the
psychedelic experience.

The Dead's community of "Deadheads" developed a distinctive subculture in
which mushroom use was common. The band's association with the Merry
Pranksters, Ken Kesey's LSD-fuelled collective, further cemented the
connection between their music and psychoactive experience.

## The British Underground

In Britain, mushroom references permeated the underground music scene. The
Beatles' association with psychedelia is well documented, though their explicit
mushroom references are fewer than is sometimes assumed. Pink Floyd's early
work, particularly under Syd Barrett's leadership, drew heavily on the
sensibilities of the psychedelic experience.

Nicholas Roeg's 1970 cult film Performance, which blurred the boundaries
between music, violence, and altered consciousness, featured mushroom imagery
and was informed by the same psychedelic sensibility that produced the music of
the period.

## Psychedelic Folk and the Mushroom Connection

Psychedelic folk music, which blended traditional folk forms with psychedelic
sensibilities, often referenced mushrooms explicitly. The genre's emphasis on
nature, mysticism, and pre-industrial culture aligned naturally with the
perception of mushrooms as "natural" and "authentic" psychoactive substances.

Artists in this tradition drew on the same cultural currents that produced the
[[telluride-mushroom-festival-modern-mycological-culture]] that developed
around rave and trance music, mushrooms became the preferred psychedelic for
many dancers. Their effects, perceived as more "organic" and less predictable
than LSD, were considered more compatible with the communal, nature-oriented
ethos of outdoor festivals.

## Mushroom Imagery in Album Art

Psychedelic mushroom imagery became ubiquitous in album art and visual
culture associated with music. The iconic mushroom shape, often rendered in
Day-Glo colours, became a shorthand signifier for the psychedelic experience
across multiple musical genres.
