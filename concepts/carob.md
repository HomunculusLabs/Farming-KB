## Description

These evergreen trees can spread their crowns out to 10 m and prefer
slightly alkaline, sandy soils with good drainage, but will grow in
virtually any soil including rocky ground, with the exception of
compacted clay. Their life span is 500 or more years.

## Climate Requirements

The carob grows in minimum rainfall areas of 250 mm but does better
with higher rainfalls. It is sensitive to cold below -4 degrees C when
young, but mature trees suffer little damage at -8 degrees C. It is
well adapted to Mediterranean climates with hot, dry summers.

## The Pod

They are generally dioecious, requiring male and female trees to set
pods but are occasionally hermaphrodite. The pod is a fleshy bean up
to 300 mm in length and is both flavoursome and nutritious. Nutritional
ranges include:

- Crude protein: 2.25-21.0%
- Crude fat: 0.5-1.5%
- Crude fibre: 4.65-13.3%
- Total sugars: 37.1-56.6%
- Ash: 1.52-2.8%
- Calcium: 0.13-0.28%

The pods are excellent stock feed, commercially sold as a chocolate
substitute for human food, and the seed contains gums with industrial
uses. The bean also has potential to produce alcohol fuel.

## Yields and Economics

Yields of 1 tonne of pods from a single tree have been reported in a
good season, but about 10% of that is more likely and would still yield
approximately 8 t/ha. Australia spends about $60,000 annually on imported
carob powder, with demand increasing at about 20% per year.

Expected year 10 returns per hectare (1985 prices, 90-100 trees/ha):
- Full irrigation: $6,765/ha
- Minimal irrigation: $3,025/ha
- No irrigation: $1,100/ha

## Fire and Salt Tolerance

The carob tree does not burn easily, and when scorched by fire, it
generally recovers, making it valuable in [[tagasaste]], Albizia lophantha, and
[[nurse-plants]]. The deep root system of carob makes it
compatible with many shallow-rooted understorey plants.

## Propagation

Direct seeding of carobs is possible, best done using protective
markers such as old tyres. During germination, moisture must be
maintained, so it is often desirable to start seedlings in deep pots
and move them out after a couple of weeks. One litre milk cartons with
drainage holes make ideal starter pots.

Carob seeds have very hard protective cases and need to be scarified
(cracked). The easiest method is to pour boiling water over the seeds
and allow them to soak overnight. Repeat on any seeds that have not
swollen. Seed can then be sown into pots.

Carobs can be sown at any time of year, although young seedlings are
frost susceptible. Establishing trees over summer eliminates the frost
problem but watering is necessary.

## Planting Out

When transplanting, cut the base from the milk carton and run a cut up
one side, being careful not to penetrate the root system. Gently place
the seedling into a prepared hole so that it sits on firmed-down soil
at ground level. Lightly fill around, then slide the carton vertically
up whilst holding the seedling in place. Water well and press soil
firmly, leaving a slight depression for water collection.

Closer planting allows for natural deaths and final selection of better
fruiting females and well-placed males after the first couple of seasons
of fruiting (7-8 years). Alternatively, known cultivars can be grafted
onto rootstock for earlier production.

## Functions in Permaculture Systems

The carob serves multiple valuable functions in [[permaculture-design]]:

- **Drought-tolerant food production**: Edible pods with high sugar
  content, usable as human food or stock feed
- **Nitrogen fixation**: As a legume, it improves soil fertility
- **Fire resistance**: Does not burn easily and recovers when scorched
- **Deep tap root**: Accesses deep moisture and nutrients, bringing
  them to the surface through leaf fall
- **Long-lived tree**: 500+ year lifespan provides permanent
  landscape structure
- **Salt tolerance**: Suitable for marginal saline lands
- **Shade and shelter**: Large evergreen canopy provides year-round
  protection
- **Economic return**: Pods have commercial value as carob powder
- **Companion planting**: Compatible with grapes, tagasaste, and
  other Mediterranean species
- **Erosion control**: Deep root system stabilises soil on slopes
- **Alcohol production potential**: Pods can be fermented for fuel
  or beverage production

## Cultivars

Western Australia is fortunate to have gained budwood from a carob
variety collection in California that was destroyed by urban sprawl.
This collection was brought to W.A. by Henry Esbenshade and serves as
a source of scion wood for nurseries and landholders. Selecting
high-yielding female trees with well-placed males is important for
commercial production.
