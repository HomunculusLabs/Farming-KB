## Lichen Biogeochemistry and Pioneer Rock Colonization

Lichens are among the most successful pioneer organisms on Earth, among the
first life forms to colonize fresh rock surfaces and initiate the process of
soil formation. An estimated six percent of the Earth's land surface is
covered by lichen-dominated vegetation. These symbiotic organisms —
consisting of a fungal partner (mycobiont) and one or more photosynthetic
partners (photobionts, either algae or cyanobacteria) — perform crucial
biogeochemical functions including nutrient retention and redistribution, soil
formation, and [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] adds another significant biogeochemical function. This

## Rock weathering and soil formation

Lichens are primary agents of initial soil formation. On fresh rock
outcrops — volcanic lava flows, glacial moraines, exposed bedrock —
lichens are often the first organisms to establish. Their weathering
activity gradually converts rock into mineral soil, creating conditions
that allow higher plants to eventually colonize. The process operates over
centuries to millennia, with different lichen species succeeding one
another as the substrate changes from bare rock to thin soil.

Physical weathering by lichens occurs through hyphal penetration into rock
crystal boundaries and the expansion of the lichen thallus during wet-dry
cycles. Chemical weathering is driven by organic acid exudation, which
dissolves primary minerals and releases calcium, potassium, phosphorus,
iron, and other elements. The oxalic acid produced by lichen fungi is a
particularly effective weathering agent, forming [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] within
minutes of rehydration. This ability allows them to persist in environments
where liquid water is episodic — deserts, high mountains, polar regions,
and exposed rock surfaces where water retention is minimal.
