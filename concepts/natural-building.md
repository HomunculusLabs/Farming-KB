# Natural Building

Natural building uses locally sourced, minimally processed materials — earth, straw, wood, stone — to create structures that are healthy, low-cost, and ecologically integrated. On a homestead, these methods excel for outbuildings, animal shelters, and small dwellings at a fraction of conventional cost.

## Wall Systems

### Cob
Monolithic earthen building: subsoil + sand + straw, sculpted by hand in lifts.
- **Soil test:** Drop a 4" ball from waist height — cracks but holds shape = works
- **Mix ratio:** ~1 clay soil : 2 sand : 1 straw (by volume)
- **Wall thickness:** 18–24" load-bearing; thinner for timber frame infill
- **Foundation:** Rubble trench + stone stem wall 12–18" above grade
- **Drying:** Each lift (6–8") dries 1–2 weeks
- **Best for:** Curved walls, ovens, small cabins, garden walls
- **Pros:** Extremely low cost, excellent [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]], sculptural freedom
- **Cons:** Labor-intensive, slow, needs good roof overhang in wet climates

### Straw Bale
Stacked bales as load-bearing ("Nebraska style") or infill within structural frame.
- **Bales:** 2-string (14"×18"×36") standard; must be dry, dense, <14% moisture
- **Compression:** Pre-compress with strapping; use threaded rod through wall
- **Plaster:** 3-coat earthen or [[comparison-lime-plaster-vs-earthen-plaster]], min 1" per side — this IS the weatherproofing
- **Foundation:** Raised stem wall, bales 6–12" above grade, moisture barrier critical
- **Best for:** Rectangular buildings, high insulation (R-30–R-50)
- **Pros:** Superb insulation, fast wall raising, good fire resistance when plastered
- **Cons:** Moisture vulnerability — needs good foundation, overhang, breathable plaster

### Earthbag
Sand/earth-filled bags stacked like masonry with barbed wire between courses.
- **Fill:** Road base, scoria, or raw subsoil — any soil works
- **Method:** Fill bags 80%, lay flat, tamp each course, 2 strands barbed wire between
- **Forms:** Domes (no roof needed) or square buildings with bond beam
- **Plaster:** Cement/lime or earthen over mesh pinned to bags
- **Best for:** Domes, root cellars, retaining walls, disaster-resistant structures
- **Pros:** Works with any soil, flood and earthquake resistant, extremely cheap
- **Cons:** Very labor-intensive, rough finish needs thick plaster

### Timber Frame
Post-and-beam with traditional joinery (mortise and tenon, pegged).
- **Wood:** Oak, [[douglas-fir]], pine, hemlock — local availability matters most
- **Infill:** Straw bale, light clay straw, cob, or conventional framing
- **Tools:** Chainsaw mill, chisels, mallet, drill press
- **Best for:** Barns, homes, greenhouses — where clear-span space is needed
- **Pros:** Beautiful exposed wood, long-lasting, flexible infill, disassemblable
- **Cons:** Needs large timbers, joinery skills, heavy lifting

### Pole Barn
Treated poles set in ground as primary structure — fastest utility building.
- **Poles:** 6"×6" treated posts, 4' in ground, 8–12' spacing
- **Roof:** Metal on purlins; walls open, partial, or enclosed
- **Best for:** Animal shelters, hay storage, workshops, run-in sheds
- **Pros:** Fastest/cheapest enclosed structure, minimal foundation
- **Cons:** Treated wood chemicals, limited aesthetics, 15–30 year lifespan

### Cordwood
Short logs laid in mortar to form walls.
- **Wood:** Softwoods (pine, cedar) best; split logs dry faster; dry 6–12 months minimum
- **Length:** 12–24" (longer = more insulation)
- **Mortar:** 1 portland : 1 lime : 3 sand + sawdust; or lime-putty for breathability
- **Best for:** Rustic cabins, garden walls — where abundant wood is available
- **Pros:** Uses waste wood, good mass + insulation combo, beautiful
- **Cons:** Slow, mortar cracking from wood shrinkage, code challenges

## Foundations

| Type | Best For | Cost | Skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rubble trench + gravel | Cob, straw bale, timber frame | Low | Moderate |
| Pier and beam | Timber frame, pole barn | Low | Low |
| Concrete stem wall | Any (code compliant) | Medium | Moderate |
| Earthbag | Earthbag, cob | Very low | Low |

**Key principle:** Keep organic material 6–12" above grade with a capillary break.

## Passive Solar Design

Natural buildings integrate passive solar via thermal mass:
- **Orientation:** Long axis E-W; south-facing windows for winter gain
- **Overhang:** Shade summer sun, admit winter sun — see [[greenhouse-design]] for angle calculations
- **Thermal mass:** Cob/stone floors absorb daytime heat, release at night
- **Ventilation:** Cross-ventilation (S/N windows) + thermal chimney in summer
- **Earth-sheltering:** Burying N/W walls reduces HVAC load 50%+ — see [[homesteading-infrastructure]]

## Floors and Plaster

**Earthen floors:** 4–6" compacted gravel base + vapor barrier + 1:3 clay:sand troweled in ½" layers. Seal with linseed oil, hemp oil, or beeswax. Warm underfoot, beautiful, repairable.

**Earthen plaster:** 1 clay : 3 sand + chopped straw. Three coats (scratch → brown → finish). Breathable — critical for straw bale and cob. Patch annually.

**Lime plaster:** 1 [[cervantes-hydrated-lime-quicklime-ph-adjustment]] : 3 sand + fiber. Carbon-negative (absorbs CO₂). Better for exterior/wet areas. Caustic — wear protection.

## Building Codes

See [[homesteading-legal-considerations]] for broader context.
- **Permit threshold:** Most jurisdictions require permits for habitable structures >120 sq ft; agricultural structures often exempt
- **Engineer stamp:** Cob, straw bale, earthbag may need structural review ($1,000–3,000)
- **Strategy:** Start with <120 sq ft (no permit), use farm exemptions for ag structures
- **Insurance:** Difficult for non-standard construction — factor in for primary residences
