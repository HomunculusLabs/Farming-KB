## Visitor Policies

Requests for large loans of diverse material often are not granted due
to the resources required to assemble and ship specimens. Instead,
investigators are encouraged to visit the herbarium. Researchers
requiring material for molecular studies are especially encouraged to
visit in person to select their own samples. Herbarium personnel
provide orientation for visitors, and in most institutions technicians
rather than visitors return specimens to cabinets to prevent misfiling.

The U.S. National Science Foundation requires that visitor information
(name, address, institutional affiliation, dates, and purpose of visit)
be included in all proposals for funding from its Biological Research
Collections (BRC) program. Proposals also must list all publications
that cite specimens from the herbarium as evidence of the collection's
scientific importance.

## Record Keeping and Documentation

Comprehensive record keeping documents herbarium use and justifies
funding. Transaction records track specimen movement between
institutions. Staff activity records track time spent on tasks
including pulling loans, mending specimens, processing accessions,
preparing labels, filing, and curation. Annual reports summarizing
activities demonstrate accomplishments and support grant proposals.

## Electronic Specimen Catalogues

Computerized specimen catalogues dramatically increase the power of
herbaria as tools for biological studies. Electronic catalogues enable
querying by specimen number and allow selection based on attributes
other than scientific name, such as locality, habitat, morphological
features, and collector. They help identify taxonomic or geographic
gaps in collections that need to be filled by new acquisitions.

### Database Fields

Most specimen databases include fields for: name data (family, genus,
species, authorities, synonyms, determiner, determination dates); type
information (category of type, bibliographic citation, lectotype
information); collection data (collector, number, date, team members,
project); location data (country, political subunits, locality text,
coordinates, elevation); habitat data (vegetation type, substratum,
site characteristics, host information for parasites); organism features
(size, color, texture, reproductive stage); specimen nature
(preservation type, storage location, duplicates, condition, associated
items); and transaction history.

### Validation and Bar Coding

Authority files (reference tables) for validating entries in critical
fields such as organism names, people, publications, and geographic
units significantly reduce data-entry errors. Bar codes, generated
from the catalogue database, provide rapid and accurate access to
computerized specimen records and facilitate tracking of specimens
through the loan process. Bar code elements include the medium, symbology
(e.g., Code 39), coded value, interpreted value, and a header with the
herbarium name.

## Data Access and Security

When specimen catalogues go online, herbaria must decide which data to
make available and to whom. Making all data freely available is not
generally recommended. Obvious exclusions include exact locality
information for endangered species or species likely to be collected for
nonscientific purposes (such as choice edible or hallucinogenic fungi)
and determinations for specimens that are part of not-yet-published
research projects.

Ultimately, some institution must take responsibility for coordinating
data standards across herbaria to enable queries across multiple
collections. While most herbaria cannot afford to wait for such
coordination, they proceed with awareness that protocols may need
revision for future interoperability.
