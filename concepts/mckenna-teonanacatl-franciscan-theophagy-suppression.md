# Teonanacatl, Franciscan Theophagy, and the Suppression of Indigenous Mushroom Religions

## Overview

In his 1983 Esalen lecture, Terence McKenna traced the history of the suppression of indigenous psilocybin mushroom religions in Mesoamerica, framing it as a catastrophic episode in which institutional ignorance systematically destroyed irreplaceable knowledge.

Central to McKenna's account is the concept of theophagy — the ritual consumption of a divine substance — and the conflict between the indigenous practice of eating [[psilocybin-mushrooms-as-alien-symbiotes]] teonanacatl ("flesh of the gods") and the Catholic Church's claim to a monopoly on sacramental theophagy through the Eucharist.

The Spanish conquest of Mexico, carried forward by Franciscan missionaries and the machinery of the Inquisition, drove the mushroom religion into hiding for nearly four centuries until its rediscovery by R. Gordon Wasson in the 1950s.

## Teonanacatl: Flesh of the Gods

The Nahuatl word teonanacatl (literally "god-flesh" or "flesh of the gods") was the term used by the Aztecs and other Nahua-speaking peoples [[psychoactive-cacti-of-central-mexico]] to refer to psychoactive mushrooms, primarily species of Psilocybe.

The name itself is a theological statement: it identifies the mushroom not as a mere plant or recreational intoxicant but as a literal embodiment of the divine, a substance whose consumption constitutes a form of communion with sacred reality.

This is not metaphorical language but sacramental theology — the mushroom is the body of the god, and to eat it is to ingest the divine.

The Aztec mushroom ceremony, or velada, was a carefully structured ritual conducted under the supervision of a curandera or shaman — a specialist in the mushroom's effects who served as guide and interpreter for the participants' visionary experiences.

The ceremony was typically held at night, in darkness, and involved the consumption of fresh or dried psilocybin mushrooms accompanied by prayer, song, and ritual purification.

The visions produced by the mushrooms were understood as genuine encounters with the sacred — sources of healing, divination, moral instruction, and cosmological knowledge.

The teonanacatl ceremony occupied a central place in the religious and cultural life of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica.

Mushroom stones — carved stone monuments depicting anthropomorphic figures with mushroom-like heads — have been found at archaeological sites throughout the Guatemalan highlands, some dating to the Preclassic period (c. 1000 BCE - 250 CE), indicating that the ritual use of psychoactive mushrooms has a history of at least three millennia in the region.

The Aztecs were merely the most prominent inheritors of a far older tradition.

## The Spanish Conquest and the Franciscan Mission

The arrival of Hernán Cortés in 1519 and the subsequent conquest of the Aztec Empire (completed in 1521) brought the indigenous mushroom religion into direct and fatal conflict with Spanish Catholicism.

Among the most aggressive agents of the spiritual conquest were the Franciscan friars, who arrived in Mexico in large numbers beginning in 1524 and who assumed primary responsibility for the evangelization of the indigenous population.

The Franciscans — motivated by genuine religious fervor, by the institutional imperative to eliminate competing theological systems, and by the broader colonial project of cultural annihilation — moved swiftly to suppress all forms of indigenous religious practice.

The Franciscans understood, perhaps more clearly than any other element of the colonial apparatus, the theological significance of teonanacatl.

The mushroom ceremony was not merely a pagan superstition to be tolerated or gradually displaced; it was a direct competitor to the central sacrament of Catholicism.

The Eucharist — in which bread and wine are transubstantiated into the body and blood of Christ — is the supreme instance of Christian theophagy, the ritual consumption of the divine.

The indigenous practice of eating teonanacatl as the literal "flesh of the gods" constituted an alternative and therefore intolerable form of theophagy.

For the Franciscans, this was not an abstract theological dispute but a matter of spiritual warfare.

If the indigenous people believed that the mushroom was the body of their gods, then the mushroom ceremony was not merely a cultural practice but a rival sacrament — a false Eucharist that claimed to provide the same spiritual benefits as the Christian rite.

The existence of teonanacatl directly challenged the Church's claim to exclusive mediation between humanity and the divine.

Its suppression was therefore not an incidental consequence of the conquest but one of its primary theological objectives.

## The Inquisition and the Mountain Refuges

The machinery of suppression was comprehensive.

The Spanish Inquisition, established in Mexico in 1571, formally prohibited the use of teonanacatl and other indigenous psychoactive substances, classifying them as instruments of devil worship and idolatry.

Indigenous practitioners were persecuted, imprisoned, and sometimes executed.

The old religious practices were driven underground — literally into the remote mountain villages of Oaxaca, Puebla, and other regions where Spanish colonial authority was weakest and where the rugged terrain provided natural fortification against outside intrusion.

The suppression was remarkably effective at the level of public, institutional religious practice.

Within a generation of the conquest, the open, communal mushroom ceremonies of the Aztec cities had been entirely eliminated.

The knowledge associated with the ceremonies — the identification of psychoactive mushroom species, the methods of preparation and dosage, the ritual protocols, the interpretive frameworks for visionary experience — was driven to the margins of Mesoamerican culture, preserved only in a handful of isolated indigenous communities that maintained the practice in secrecy.

What survived was a fragment of the original tradition, preserved by curanderas and their apprentices in villages such as Huautla de Jiménez in the Mazatec region of Oaxaca.

These communities, geographically isolated and culturally marginalized, maintained the velada ceremony in a form that, while modified by four centuries of syncretic interaction with Catholicism, preserved the essential core of the pre-Columbian practice: the consumption of psychoactive mushrooms in a ritual context for purposes of healing, divination, and communion with the sacred.

## Wasson and the Rediscovery

The survival of the mushroom religion remained unknown to the outside world until the 1950s, when R. Gordon Wasson — a vice president of J.P. Morgan and an amateur mycologist — and his wife [[valentina-pavlovna-wasson]], a pediatrician of Russian descent, began investigating the role of mushrooms in human culture.

Their research led them to the Mazatec village of Huautla de Jiménez, where in 1955 Gordon Wasson became the first non-indigenous person in modern times to participate in a velada ceremony, conducted by the renowned curandera María Sabina.

Wasson's account of his experience, published in Life magazine in 1957 under the title "Seeking the Magic Mushroom," introduced psilocybin mushrooms to the Western world and ignited a wave of scientific, popular, and countercultural interest in psychedelic substances.

Albert Hofmann, the Swiss chemist who had first synthesized LSD in 1938, subsequently identified [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] as the active compounds in the mushrooms, confirming their psychoactive properties through chemical analysis and self-experimentation.

The Wasson discovery was a landmark in the history of ethnobotany and psychopharmacology, but McKenna viewed it with a complex mixture of appreciation and melancholy.

The rediscovery of teonanacatl was a triumph of ethnographic persistence and a vindication of the [[psilocybin-chemistry-stability-indigenous-knowledge]] system that had preserved the mushroom ceremony through centuries of persecution.

At the same time, the publicity generated by Wasson's article and its aftermath — the influx of countercultural seekers into Mazatec villages, the commercial exploitation of indigenous ceremonial practices, the eventual arrest and persecution of María Sabina by Mexican authorities — represented a new form of violation and disruption.

## Ignorance Destroying Knowledge

McKenna framed the suppression of the mushroom religion as a paradigmatic instance of institutional ignorance destroying irreplaceable knowledge.

The Franciscan missionaries who persecuted the mushroom ceremonies understood nothing of the pharmacological, psychological, or spiritual dimensions of the practice they were destroying.

They saw only idolatry and devil worship, categories drawn from their own theological framework that bore no relation to the indigenous reality they were annihilating.

The parallel McKenna drew was to the burning of the Hellenistic libraries — the Library of Alexandria, the Library of Pergamum, and others — by forces that valued doctrinal conformity over intellectual diversity.

The knowledge preserved in these libraries, like the knowledge preserved in the mushroom ceremonies, was not merely academic; it represented centuries or millennia of accumulated human experience with the deepest questions of consciousness, meaning, and the nature of reality.

Its destruction was not a neutral event but an irreversible diminishment of the human heritage.

The suppression of teonanacatl was particularly devastating because the knowledge it encoded was experiential rather than propositional.

The mushroom ceremony was not a body of written doctrine that could be reconstructed from surviving texts; it was a living practice, transmitted orally from teacher to student, grounded in direct visionary experience.

When the Inquisition drove the practice into hiding, it destroyed the institutional and cultural infrastructure — the community of practitioners, the chain of transmission, the interpretive traditions — that gave the experience its meaning and coherence.

What survived in the mountain villages was a fragment, a remnant, a shadow of what had once been a sophisticated and widespread religious tradition.

## The Broader Pattern of Suppression

McKenna situated the suppression of teonanacatl within a broader historical pattern of institutional hostility [[weil-cultural-attitudes-toward-altered-states]] of consciousness and the plants and fungi that facilitate them.

The European witch hunts, the prohibition of peyote in the United States, the criminalization of ayahuasca in Brazil, the international scheduling of psychedelic substances under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971) — all of these, in McKenna's analysis, represent variations on the same theme: the systematic suppression of direct, unmediated encounters with the sacred by institutions that claim a monopoly on such encounters.

The common thread is theophagy.

The Catholic Church claims the exclusive right to mediate between humanity and the divine through the Eucharist.

Modern science claims the exclusive right to mediate between humanity and knowledge through the methodology of instrumental empiricism.

Both institutions are threatened by practices — the mushroom ceremony, the psychedelic experience — that offer direct, unmediated access to dimensions of reality that these institutions claim to control.

The suppression of teonanacatl is thus not merely a historical curiosity but a continuing pattern, one that shapes contemporary attitudes toward psychedelic substances and the experiences they produce.
