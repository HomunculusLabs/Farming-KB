# Nutrient Stock Solutions and Concentrates

Proper preparation and management of nutrient stock solutions is a
core skill in hydroponic cannabis cultivation. Jorge Cervantes covers
the practical aspects of mixing, diluting, and maintaining
concentrated nutrient solutions, including two-part and three-part
formulas, EC/ppm monitoring, and common mistakes that lead to
nutrient imbalances.

## Two-Part and Three-Part Fertilizers

Cervantes recommends high-quality two- and three-part hydroponic
fertilizers to supply all necessary secondary and trace elements.
These multi-part systems separate certain mineral compounds that
would react with each other and precipitate (fall out of solution)
if combined in concentrated form. For example, calcium and sulfur
are kept in separate bottles because they form insoluble calcium
sulfate when mixed at high concentration.

In a typical two-part system, Part A contains calcium and
micronutrients, while Part B contains phosphorus, potassium, and
nitrogen compounds. Three-part systems often separate the grow
(vegetative), micro (micronutrients), and bloom (flowering)
components for maximum flexibility across growth stages.

## Mixing Stock Solutions

When preparing nutrient solutions, Cervantes emphasizes several
critical practices:

- Always add nutrient concentrates to water, never the reverse
- Use an electronic EC pen to monitor the level of dissolved solids
  in the solution
- Follow manufacturer dilution rates precisely -- small errors in
  concentrated stock solutions are magnified in the final mix
- Measure the volume of the reservoir accurately using graduated
  markings or an indelible marker line
- Mix each part separately in water before combining to avoid
  precipitation

## Solution Composition Guidelines

Cervantes provides a nutrient composition chart expressed in ppm
(parts per million) as a guideline for satisfactory nutrient limits:

| Element     | Limits (ppm) | Average (ppm) |
|-------------|-------------|---------------|
| Nitrogen    | 150-1000    | 250           |
| Calcium     | 100-500     | 200           |
| Magnesium   | 50-100      | 75            |
| Phosphorus  | 50-100      | 80            |
| Potassium   | 100-400     | 300           |
| Sulfur      | 200-1000    | 400           |
| Copper      | 0.1-0.5     | 0.05          |
| Boron       | 0.5-5.0     | 1.0           |
| Iron        | 2.0-10      | 5.0           |
| Manganese   | 0.5-5.0     | 2.0           |
| Molybdenum  | 0.01-0.05   | 0.02          |
| Zinc        | 0.5-1.0     | 0.5           |

Growers should not deviate too far from these ranges to avoid
deficiencies and excesses.

## Topping Off the Reservoir

Plants use water much faster than nutrients. Cervantes recommends
casually "topping off" the reservoir with pH-balanced water to keep
the solution relatively balanced for a week or two. Some growers top
off with 500-700 ppm-strength nutrient solution every two to three
days. Occasionally, more fertilizer concentrate must be added to
maintain the EC level during topping off.

Key reservoir management rules include:

- Keep the reservoir full at all times
- Smaller reservoirs deplete faster and should be refilled daily
- The greater the volume of nutrient solution, the more forgiving
  the system
- Never let the reservoir go more than four weeks without a complete
  change

## Soluble Salts Range

The relationship between EC and TDS is essential for solution
management. One mS (milliSiemen) is approximately equivalent to
650 ppm total dissolved solids. The desirable EC range is 0.75 to
2.0 mS (500-1300 ppm TDS). Salt damage becomes probable above
3.0 mS (2000+ ppm).

## Hydro-Organic Solutions

For growers using organic nutrients in hydroponic systems, Cervantes
notes that measuring content is difficult because organic nutrients
have complex structures. Taking an accurate EC reading or mixing
exact amounts is very challenging with organic hydroponics. Some
manufacturers have managed to stabilize their organic fertilizers,
but experimentation with feeding amounts and schedules is still
necessary to achieve optimal results.

## Common Mixing Mistakes

- Adding concentrated acids or bases without proper dilution for
  pH adjustment
- Mixing all parts of a multi-part fertilizer together in
  concentrated form
- Not accounting for the nutrient content of source water
- Failing to stir or agitate the reservoir after adding concentrates
- Over-concentrating solutions in hot weather when plants use more
  water than nutrients
