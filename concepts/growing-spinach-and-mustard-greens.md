# Growing Spinach and Mustard Greens

Spinach and mustard are cool-season greens with a twist: their fertilizer demand changes dramatically depending on whether they're grown for spring or winter harvest. For spring crops they are high-demand (because they bolt in spring, needing maximum growth before going to seed). For autumn/winter crops they are medium-demand (because they grow slowly over months before bolting).

## The Demand Difference Explained

| Season | Fertility Demand | Reason |
|--------|-----------------|--------|
| Spring | High | Must push maximum growth before bolting — time is limited |
| Autumn/Winter | Medium | Have months of cool growing weather before bolting pressure |

## Growing Details: Autumn/Winter

### Fertilizer

Work in strong compost or, better, COF (Complete Organic Fertilizer) made with some tankage or even highly potent bloodmeal instead of one part seedmeal. Work it in before sowing, then side-dress closer to the rows of seedlings as soon as they start forming their first true leaf.

### Timing

Start mustard or spinach after the heat of summer has ended, or about a month after the summer solstice in shorter-season climates.

### Thinning

They grow quickly — thin progressively. Competition slows growth and increases bolting tendency.

## Growing Details: Summer

Almost all varieties go to seed shortly after the heat hits. However, there are a few exceptions:

### Bolt-Resistant Spinach Varieties

A few spinach varieties, bred for reluctance to bolt, can be grown in summer. The seed catalog will proudly mention this trait.

### Summer Mustard Greens

There is also one Asian mustard green that will not bolt in summer. It is mild flavored, with broad off-white stalks — not as fancy as some of the tsai-tsoi types, but still pretty good. Johnny's Selected Seeds has a hybrid pac-choi for summer; Territorial still offers the older OP variety.

## Harvest Technique

### Leaf-by-Leaf Method

Do not cut whole plants unless they are starting to put up seedstalks. It's better to cut or break off large outer leaves. Do not take so many that the plant is crippled — **one leaf per week from each plant in the row** is about right.

### Spring Patch Management

In spring, at the first signs of bolting, cut the whole patch and resow the area to another crop. The bed remains but production nearly ceases once bolting starts.

### Autumn/Winter Harvest

In autumn, if growing for autumn harvest, feed them. One leaf per week from each plant works well, but they'll continue producing through the cool season.

## Saving Seed

### Mustard

Mustard is a bee-pollinated brassica. To maintain your stock's vigor, ensure its gene pool contains at least a dozen plants; more would be better.

### Spinach

Spinach is wind-pollinated and also has plants with distinct genders — male and hermaphrodite:

- The males only make pollen
- Because approximately half the plants in a seed-making bed will be females, you need a starting population at least twice as large as the number of seed plants you want
- **Early bolters should be destroyed before they can release any pollen** — a plant that bolts ahead of the others is a highly undesirable trait to perpetuate
- Seed can be saved from either spring-sown or autumn-sown plants (if they survive winter)

## Using as Green Manure

One advantage of growing your own seed is that it costs you nothing. Mustard makes an excellent autumn green-manure crop:

1. Broadcast seed or roughly sow in rows on roughly hoed beds
2. Cover with a thin layer of soil
3. The species makes a dense mass of vegetation that decomposes rapidly and leaves the earth in fine, friable condition
4. As soon as some plants start putting up seedstalks, chop the whole bed with an ordinary sharp garden hoe
5. Wait a week for it to decompose
6. Then, with a bit of raking, you'll have made an excellent seedbed

For spring green-manure use: Sow as early as possible and grow fast before bolting. Mustards can be grown as green-manure crops where winter is severe — start them at least 40 days before killing frosts.
