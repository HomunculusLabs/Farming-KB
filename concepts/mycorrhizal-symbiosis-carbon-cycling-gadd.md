## Carbon Cycling and Sequestration

Mycorrhizas play a central role in the terrestrial carbon cycle. Plants
allocate a substantial portion of their photosynthetically fixed carbon,
often 10 to 20 percent or more of total net photosynthesis, to their
mycorrhizal fungal partners. This carbon flux represents a major pathway
for carbon movement from the atmosphere into soil pools and is a
significant component of the global carbon budget that has historically
been underestimated in ecosystem models and carbon accounting
frameworks.

The carbon allocated to mycorrhizal fungi is used for fungal growth,
respiration, and production of extramatrical mycelium that explores the
soil for nutrients. When fungal hyphae die and decompose, this carbon
enters the soil organic matter pool. Fungal-derived carbon compounds,
including chitin, glomalin, and other recalcitrant molecules, may be
more resistant to decomposition than plant-derived compounds,
potentially contributing to more persistent soil carbon stocks and
longer-term carbon sequestration in forest and [[biodiversity-fungi-grassland-ecosystems]].

## Nutrient Exchange Mechanisms

The mycorrhizal symbiosis is fundamentally a nutrient exchange
partnership. The fungus provides the plant with mineral nutrients,
particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, scavenged from soil volumes far
beyond the depletion zone that develops around uncolonized roots. In
return, the plant provides the fungus with photosynthetically derived
carbohydrates, primarily as hexose sugars taken up by fungal structures
within the root cortex.

This exchange is dynamically regulated by both partners through source-
sink relationships. When plant phosphorus demand is high, carbon
allocation to mycorrhizal fungi increases, stimulating fungal nutrient
foraging. When phosphorus is abundant, the plant may reduce carbon
allocation to conserve resources, demonstrating the sophisticated
regulatory mechanisms that maintain the mutualism and prevent
exploitation by either partner under changing environmental conditions.

## Influence on Plant Community Composition

Mycorrhizal fungi can determine plant community composition through
differential effects on plant species. Some plant species are highly
mycorrhiza-dependent and benefit greatly from the symbiosis, while
others are non-mycorrhizal or benefit less. This differential dependency
can shift competitive balances between species, influencing which plants
dominate in a given community and affecting overall ecosystem diversity
and function.
