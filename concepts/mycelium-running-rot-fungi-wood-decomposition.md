# Rot Fungi and Wood Decomposition

Source: [[growing-gourmet-saprophytic-mushrooms-decomposers]] -- the decomposers -- steer the course for proliferating biological communities, shaping and forming the first menus in the food web from dead plants, insects, and other animals. Most gourmet and medicinal mushrooms are wood decomposers.

## Classification of Decomposers

Saprophytic mushrooms can be separated into three groups based on their position in the decomposition sequence:

**Primary Decomposers:** Typically the first to grow on fresh debris -- twigs, wood chips, logs, stumps, or dead insects. They are fast-growing, sending out rapidly extending strands of mycelium. Examples include oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus species), shiitake (Lentinula edodes), and maitake ([[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]) is the classic example -- the most widely cultivated mushroom in the world. The garden giant (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) is an intermediary between primary and secondary decomposers.

**Tertiary Decomposers:** Found toward the end of the decomposition process, thriving in habitats created by primary and secondary decomposers over years. They include species of Conocybe, Agrocybe, Mycena, Pluteus, and Agaricus. They rely upon highly complex microbial environments.

## Brown Rot Fungi

Brown rot fungi comprise only about 7 percent of mushrooms; of those, about 70 percent are polypores. Their extracellular enzymes break down the white, pulpy cellulose, leaving behind the brownish lignin (hence the name). These fungi cause checkered cubical cracking and shrinking in wood, commonly seen on downed conifer trees.

Brown rot examples include:
- Multicolored gilled polypore (Lenzites betulina)
- Large lentinus (Lentinus ponderosus)
- Sulphur tufts (Laetiporus sulphureus and L. conifericola)
- Velvet polypore (Phaeolus schweinitzii)
- Split-gill polypore (Schizophyllum commune)
- Agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)
- Unzoned rusty gilled polypore (Gloeophyllum trabeum)
- Dry rot house wreckers (Serpula lacrymans and S. himantoides)

Brown rot fungi play a critical role in forest ecology by breaking down cellulose and creating soil. Heart rot from brown rot fungus, probably the velvet polypore, is commonly seen in bear-scratched trees in old-growth forests.

## White Rot Fungi

White rot fungi are more numerous than brown rotters. They produce enzymes that break down the recalcitrant brown fiber (lignin) in wood, leaving the cellulose largely intact, giving the wood a white appearance.

White rot examples include:
- Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)
- Maitake (Grifola frondosa)
- Turkey tail ([[stamets-reishi-ganoderma-lucidum-cultivation]])
- Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)

## Enzymatic Power

Mushroom mycelia exude droplets containing enzymes and antibiotics. The enzymes digest lignin and cellulose, petroleum products, and many molecules held together by hydrogen-carbon bonds. This enzymatic versatility is what makes white rot fungi so effective at mycoremediation -- the same enzymes that break down wood lignin can also break down many environmental toxins with similar chemical bonds.

Baldrian and Gabriel (2003) found that the cellulose- and hemicellulose-degrading abilities of oyster mushrooms were limited by cadmium levels, showing how environmental conditions affect decomposition rates.

## Decomposition and Forest Recovery

In clear-cut forestlands, wood debris -- stumps, brush, and downed trees -- represents locked-away biomass until it decomposes. In order to stimulate decomposition and trigger habitat recovery, we can selectively introduce key saprophytic, parasitic, mycorrhizal, and endophytic fungi. Selectively introducing aggressive saprophytic species such as oysters, turkey tails, or wood lovers confronts parasitic fungi in a form of mycelial combat, lessening their resurgence.

For every 12 inches of wood chips decomposed by oyster mushrooms, approximately 1 to 2 inches of soil are created after 4 years. After several years, a mantle of mycelium forms where the wood chips and soil meet, binding together loose substrate and creating a living membrane.

## The Fungal Cycle of Decomposition

As plants grow, their falling leaves, branches, and flowers enter into the fungal cycle of decomposition. Fungi accelerate the decomposition cycles by processing debris fields. Mycelium decomposes organic matter and releases heat, water, and carbon dioxide. The saprophytes initiate the decomposition process, followed by mycorrhizal fungi through their selection of trees to associate with, streaming nutrients to root zones.

## Parasitic Decomposers

Some fungi initially act as parasites, killing their hosts, and then act like saprophytes on the dead remains. Honey mushrooms (Armillaria species) are good examples, growing both parasitically and saprophytically. One Armillaria mycelial mat in Oregon covers more than 2,400 acres and is possibly more than 2,200 years old. Each time this fungus blight sweeps through, nurse logs are created, soil depth increases, and centimeters of soil accumulate to create ever-richer habitats.
