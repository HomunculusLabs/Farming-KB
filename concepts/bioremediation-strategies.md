## Bioremediation Strategies

Bioremediation encompasses all biological approaches to removing, neutralizing,
or containing environmental contaminants. It leverages the metabolic
capabilities of microorganisms, plants, and fungi to transform pollutants into
less harmful substances or immobilize them in situ. Compared to physical and
chemical remediation methods, bioremediation is typically lower cost, less
destructive to ecosystems, and can be applied to large or difficult-to-access
contaminated sites.

## Classification by Approach

Bioremediation strategies are classified along several axes. In situ
approaches treat contaminants in place without excavation, while ex situ
methods involve removing contaminated material for treatment elsewhere.
Biostimulation adds nutrients, oxygen, or electron donors to stimulate
indigenous microbial communities already present at contaminated sites.
Bioaugmentation introduces specific microbial strains or consortia with known
degradative capabilities to enhance treatment.

Natural attenuation relies entirely on existing biological processes without
human intervention, monitored through long-term sampling programs. Engineered
bioremediation actively manages environmental conditions to optimize
degradation rates and is used when faster cleanup is required due to risk
levels or regulatory timelines.

## Microbial Bioremediation

Bacteria are the most extensively studied agents for bioremediation due to
their rapid growth, metabolic diversity, and the existence of well-characterized
degradative pathways for many contaminants. Aerobic degradation of petroleum
hydrocarbons by bacteria like Pseudomonas, Alcanivorax, and Rhodococcus is
among the most established bioremediation applications, routinely used to
treat oil spills on shorelines and in contaminated groundwater.
