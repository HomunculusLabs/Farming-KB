Masanobu Fukuoka's [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] makes nutrients available in a balanced manner.
Fukuoka was critical of organic farming, calling it "just another type
of scientific farming." The effort to transport, process, and apply
organic amendments produces only local and temporal gains. When
examined broadly, many efforts to protect the natural ecology are
actually destructive. True soil fertility arises from a balanced
ecosystem where plants, microorganisms, and animals interact without
human manipulation. The clover fixed atmospheric nitrogen, occasional
chicken droppings provided phosphorus, and decomposing straw returned
potassium and trace minerals—all without manufactured inputs.
## No Pesticides
Although a thousand diseases attack plants in the fields and forests,
nature strikes a balance; there never was any need for pesticides. Man
grew confused when he identified diseases as insect damage and created
with his own hands the need for labor and toil. Pesticides destroy
not only target pests but also beneficial insects, soil
microorganisms, and the predator-prey relationships that naturally
keep pest populations in check.
Fukuoka illustrated this complexity with pine wilt disease in Japan.
Conventional wisdom blamed the pinewood nematode, but his observations
revealed a cascade: soil acidification killed [[soil-food-web]].
Treating soil with chloropicrin temporarily alleviates bacterial soft
rot but kills the bacteria that moderate disease severity, causing
outbreaks to worsen two years later. Instead of trying to bring nature
around to his own designs, man would be much wiser to step out of the
way and let nature carry on its affairs without his interference.
## No Weeding
Nature does not arbitrarily call one plant a weed and try to
eradicate it. Weeds are pioneer plants that appear for specific
reasons—often to improve soil conditions or protect exposed earth.
Fukuoka's fields were covered with clover, which fixed nitrogen,
suppressed problematic weeds, provided habitat for beneficial insects,
and decomposed into rich humus. One can understand the nature of the
soil more quickly by examining the weeds growing in it than by
examining the soil itself.
Before pulling weeds at the base of rice plants, natural farming asks
why they are there. Are these grasses the by-product of human action
or did they arise spontaneously? If the latter, they are of value and
are left to grow. Those who believe that eradicating weeds will
provide the highest yields are mistaken. Diseased rice grown in
sterile, weed-free fields wastes solar energy. The natural farmer
allows natural plants that protect the soil to carry out their
mission, working with natural succession rather than against it.
## No Pruning
A fruit tree grows best in its natural habitat; the branches do not
tangle, sunlight falls on every leaf, and the tree bears fully each
year. Pruning creates a cascade of problems: once a single bud is
nipped, several adventitious buds emerge from the wound, growing into
branches that crowd and entangle, requiring more pruning the following
year. Even lightly pinching new buds on a pine seedling totally alters
the shape of emerging branches.
If trees are allowed to grow naturally from the start, little pruning
is needed later. Training branches into a shape close to the natural
form of the tree makes the pruning shears unnecessary. Pruning only
creates a need for more pruning. How much wiser to limit oneself to
minimal corrective pruning aimed only at bringing the tree closer to
its natural form rather than practicing a method that requires
extensive pruning each and every year.
## The Philosophy of Mu (Nothingness)
Underlying the five principles is the philosophy of Mu, or nothingness.
The farming village was a society of philosophers without a need for
philosophy. Farmers of old lived each day well, one day being as wide
and infinite as the universe and yet just one small frame in the
unending flow of existence. Theirs was a farming way of life that
violated nothing and was not itself violated.
This is not passive nihilism but active trust in natural processes.
Fukuoka's method was not to add techniques but to subtract them. After
thirty years of eliminating unnecessary practices, he reduced his work
to essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw. Human effort is
unnecessary because nature, not man, grows the rice and wheat. All
begins by relinquishing human knowledge.
## Contrast with Scientific Agriculture
Scientific farming studies crop production through inductive
experimentation, but this proceeds without a clear goal. Scientists
run experiments oblivious to the direction of their research—what
Fukuoka called "aimless wandering." Natural farming uses intuitive
reasoning to draw up an ideal vision and works out a means of
achieving it. True deduction originates beyond the world of
phenomena, arising from philosophical understanding of nature's
essence. Only deductive reasoning can bring fresh ideas with the
potential for positive yield gains.
## Practical Results
Fukuoka consistently harvested close to 22 bushels of rice and 22
bushels of winter grain per quarter-acre on unplowed, unfertilized,
unsprayed land, requiring the labor of only one or two people. The
complete annual cycle involved broadcasting clover and winter grain
over ripening rice, harvesting while treading on young grain shoots,
scattering straw uncut, forming clay pellets with rice seed, and
flooding briefly in early summer. These results demonstrate the five
principles are a practical, productive farming system applicable
everywhere.
