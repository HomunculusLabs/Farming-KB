# Fukuoka Five Principles Natural Farming

Updated: 2026-04-19

Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming is built on what are commonly described as five foundational principles, though Fukuoka himself frames them more as a unified philosophy than a checklist. These emerge from his work in *The Natural Way of Farming* and are rooted in forty years of practical farming experience.

## The Five Principles

### 1. No Cultivation (No Plowing, No Tilling)

The soil works itself. Roots of grasses and green manure crops penetrate twelve to fifteen inches — deeper than any farmer plows. As roots wither and die, microorganisms proliferate, humus increases, and earthworms appear. Moles begin burrowing. Nature has been working at soil improvement with its own methods for thousands of years. "It is in the nature of soil to swell and grow more porous with each passing year." See [[fukuoka-no-cultivation-principle]] and [[fukuoka-critique-soil-cultivation]].

Plowing a field is hard work and usually one of the most important activities in farming. To many people, being a farmer is synonymous with turning the soil with plow or hoe. If working the soil is unnecessary, the image and reality of the farmer change drastically. Plowing ruins the soil by breaking it up into smaller particles, compacting it, and killing the microorganisms that naturally aerate and enrich it.

### 2. No Chemical Fertilizer or Prepared Compost

Crops grow of their own accord, depending on the soil. Fertilizer weakens crops, creates trace element deficiencies, acidifies soil with sulfuric acid, and kills soil microorganisms. Composting is unnecessary labor — fresh straw returned directly to the field is superior. See [[fukuoka-no-fertilizer-principle]] and [[fukuoka-fertilizer-critique-detailed]].

Plowing under chemical fertilizers scattered over the surface of a field will probably increase fertilizer effectiveness. But this is true only for cleanly plowed and weeded fields on which fertilizer is applied. Grassed fields and no-fertilizer cultivation are a different matter altogether.

### 3. No Weeding by Tillage or Herbicides

Weeds should be managed through ground covers (clover), straw mulch, and flooding rather than eliminated. Weeds play ecological roles and their suppression is better achieved through competition from beneficial plants than through killing. See [[fukuoka-weed-ecology]] and [[fukuoka-white-clover-ground-cover]].

What about crops such as rice or barley? The coexistence of surface plants is true to nature, and the no-weeding principle applies also to rice and barley cultivation. The presence of weeds among rice and barley interferes with harvesting, so these weeds have to be replaced with some other herb — in this case, clover and other green manure crops.

### 4. No Dependency on Chemical Pesticides

Pest problems arise from weakened plants grown in imbalanced conditions. Healthy plants in ecologically diverse systems naturally resist pests. Insect pests do not truly exist — what exists are imbalanced conditions that allow certain insects to proliferate beyond their natural bounds. See [[fukuoka-natural-pest-balance]] and [[fukuoka-pest-ecology]].

Fukuoka spent thirty to forty years farming without pesticides and came to believe that while people need doctors because they are sick, rice plants in a natural field do not need pesticides because they are healthy. The moment one begins treating insects as pests, one enters an endless spiral of intervention.

### 5. No Pruning of Fruit Trees

Trees grown from seed in their natural conditions develop orderly, self-regulating forms governed by phyllotaxy. Pruning creates the very problems it claims to solve. See [[fukuoka-natural-tree-form-argument]].

## The Underlying Unity

These five principles are not independent rules but expressions of a single insight: "One thing is all things. To resolve one matter, one must resolve all matters. Changing one thing changes all things." The problems of rice cultivation — plowing, seeding, fertilizing, weeding, pest control — are all organically interrelated. No problem in any one area is truly solved unless a common solution is found for all areas.

Fukuoka states: "My method of natural farming has approached the point of 'doing nothing.' I will admit that I have had my share of failures during the forty years that I have been at it. But because I was headed in basically the right direction, I now have yields that are at least equal to or better than those of crops grown scientifically in every respect."

## The Mu (Nothingness) Philosophy

All five principles point toward the concept of mu — doing nothing, or non-intervention. This is not laziness but the recognition that nature already provides complete systems that human intervention disrupts. The goal is to reduce human action to the minimum that allows natural processes to function. See [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy]] and [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy]].

Natural farming proceeds centripetally inward toward a state of "nothingness" (Mu) by the elimination of human labor. Scientific farming, on the other hand, seeks to appropriate time and space through complex and diverse means, proceeding centrifugally outward. Both exist in a relative relationship, but they move in opposite directions — one headed for zero and the other for infinity.

## The Holistic System

The principles only work as an integrated whole. Applying some while violating others defeats the purpose. Fukuoka's no-tillage rice/barley system demonstrates how eliminating plowing, fertilizers, weeding, pesticides, and pruning simultaneously creates a self-sustaining system that produces yields equal to or exceeding conventional methods.

The method succeeds at only a tiny fraction of the labor and costs of scientific farming, and at no point in the process of cultivation or in the crops is there any element that generates the slightest pollution, while the soil remains eternally fertile.

## The Four Principles vs. Five Principles

Fukuoka himself sometimes states the principles as four (no cultivation, no fertilizer, no weeding, no pesticides), as in the formal statement of his method. The fifth principle — no pruning of fruit trees — appears in discussions of his orchard management. Both formulations point to the same underlying philosophy of non-intervention. See [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]].

## Forty Years of Proof

Fukuoka guarantees that anyone can farm this way. The results are unmistakable: the method has been achieved now for a good many years. Moreover, anyone can do it. This method of "do-nothing" farming is based on four major principles that, together with no pruning, constitute a complete philosophy of agriculture. See [[fukuoka-philosophy-in-practice]].

## Beyond Organic Farming

It is important to distinguish Fukuoka's five principles from the principles of organic farming. Organic farming typically replaces chemical inputs with organic alternatives but still employs tillage, active weed management, and often imported fertility sources. Natural farming eliminates these practices entirely. See [[natural-farming-vs-organic-farming]].

The distinction is one of direction: organic farming moves somewhat toward nature but remains within the framework of scientific agriculture. Natural farming, governed by the philosophy of Mu, moves in a fundamentally different direction — toward the complete elimination of human agricultural intervention.

## The Guarantees

Fukuoka makes two guarantees about his method:

1. It succeeds at only a tiny fraction of the labor and costs of scientific farming

2. At no point in the process is there any element that generates the slightest pollution

These are not theoretical claims but results achieved over decades of practice. The soil remains eternally fertile without any fertilizer inputs, and crop yields equal or exceed conventional methods.

# Fertilizer-Free Agriculture

updated: 2026-04-18

Fukuoka's principle that crops can be grown entirely without chemical fertilizers is one of the five foundations of natural farming. His decades of experimentation demonstrated that nature provides all the fertilization plants need.

## Crops Depend on Soil, Not Fertilizer

When examining how and why crops grow on the earth, they do so independently of human knowledge and action. This means they have no basic need for fertilizers or externally supplied nutrients. Crops depend on the soil for growth — and living, healthy soil provides everything.

## The Logical Trap of Fertilizer Science

Scientists typically analyze rice and barley plants, identify nutrients, apply those nutrients as fertilizer, observe that plants grow, and conclude fertilizer causes growth. But if one takes as the starting point that a tree grows of its own accord, nutrient uptake by roots is no longer a cause but just a small effect.

The buds on a tree are made for budding. The roots, with their powers of elongation, spread throughout the earth. A tree has a shape perfectly adapted to the natural environment. It grows neither too fast nor too slow, but in total harmony with the great cycles of nature.

## The Countless Evils of Fertilizer

Fukuoka documented numerous harmful effects of chemical fertilizer:

1. **Temporary growth acceleration**: Speeds crop growth but only temporarily and locally, with inevitable weakening of crops afterward
2. **Lowered resistance**: Plants weakened by fertilizers have reduced resistance to [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]]
3. **Ineffective in field conditions**: About 30% of ammonium sulfate nitrogen is denitrified by soil microbes and escapes to the atmosphere; phosphate fertilizers only penetrate two inches into the soil surface
4. **Soil acidification**: More than 70% of the "big three" fertilizers is concentrated sulfuric acid; some 1.8 million tons of sulfuric acid are dumped onto Japanese farmlands annually
5. **Trace component deficiency**: Heavy reliance on a few nutrients leads to deficiency in many trace elements essential to crops

## The Absurdity of No-Fertilizer Tests

Scientists hardly ever run experiments on no-fertilizer cultivation. When they do, they plant crops in small earthenware pots or concrete enclosures — dead soil. The growth of trees whose roots are boxed in by concrete is highly unnatural. Concluding that plants cannot be grown without fertilizers based on such conditions is unreasonable.

## Evidence from Mountain Forests

The trees of mountain forests grow under nearly natural conditions. Reforested cedars in a favorable area produce about forty tons per quarter-acre over twenty years — roughly two tons of new growth annually without any fertilizer. Including small branches, leaves, and roots, annual production approaches four tons.

For a fruit orchard, this would translate into two to four tons of fruit each year without fertilizers — about equal to standard production levels from fertilized orchards.

## Fertilizer Was Never Needed

When a farmer clears a forest to plant fruit trees, he destroys the physical structure of the soil. He fells trees, pulls up roots, burns them, turns the soil over repeatedly, drives out air and humus essential to microorganisms, reducing soil to lifeless mineral matter. Then he plants fruit saplings in this dead soil, adds fertilizer, and attempts to grow fruit trees entirely through human forces.

The soil in fields farmed by man grows poorer each year despite large amounts of fertilizer, while uncultivated topsoil becomes darker and richer each year through natural processes.
