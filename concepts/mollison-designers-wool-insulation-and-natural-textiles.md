The energy input for wool insulation processing is estimated at 10 to 15
percent of that required for fibreglass production, and wool produces zero
toxic emissions during manufacture.

## Comparison with Synthetic Insulations

Mollison contrasts wool with common synthetic alternatives:

- Fibreglass: Similar R-value but irritating to skin, eyes, and
  respiratory system during installation. Does not absorb moisture, so
  condensation in wall cavities can cause mould and decay. Not
  biodegradable. Energy-intensive to manufacture.
- Mineral wool (rock wool): Good fire resistance and moisture tolerance,
  but high embodied energy and not biodegradable. Dust is a respiratory
  irritant.
- Expanded polystyrene (EPS/XPS): High R-value per thickness but derived
  from petroleum, non-breathable, flammable (requires flame retardant
  chemicals), and non-biodegradable. Traps moisture in walls.
- Polyurethane foam: Excellent R-value and air sealing but petroleum-based,
  off-gasses during and after installation, and is non-recyclable.

Wool's unique advantage in permaculture building is its combination of
thermal performance with moisture buffering and breathability, which
complements natural wall systems such as cob, rammed earth, [[comparison-cob-construction-vs-straw-bale-construction]],
and [[comparison-bamboo-construction-vs-timber-frame-basics]].

## Natural Textile Production

Mollison extends the discussion to broader textile production for
clothing, bedding, and household use within permaculture settlements:

- [[mollison-designers-wool-processing-natural-dyeing]]: After scouring and carding, wool is spun into yarn
  using drop spindles or spinning wheels. Hand spinning is slow but
  produces high-quality yarn with minimal equipment. Yarn is then woven
  on looms or knitted into garments. Natural dyes from plants (indigo,
  woad, madder, walnut hulls, eucalyptus bark) provide colour without
  synthetic chemicals.
- Cotton: Mollison notes that cotton requires significant water, fertile
  soil, and often pesticides, making it less suited to permaculture
  systems unless grown organically in appropriate climates. Hand ginning,
  carding, and spinning of cotton is labour-intensive.
- Hemp and flax (linen): Mollison favours these bast fibres as more
  sustainable alternatives to cotton. Hemp in particular grows well on
  marginal land, requires minimal inputs, produces strong and durable
  fibre, and has multiple other uses (seed oil, [[fungal-building-material-deterioration]],
  biomass). Processing involves retting (controlled decomposition of the
  pectin binding fibres), breaking, scutching, and hackling before
  spinning.
- Silk: Mulberry silk production can be integrated into permaculture
  systems where mulberry trees serve multiple functions (fodder, fruit,
  shade, soil stabilisation).

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Mollison advocates placing fibre-producing animals and plants within the
permaculture zonation system:

- Sheep in zone 2 to 3 grazing systems, providing wool, meat, manure,
  and grazing management
- Hemp or flax in zone 2 to 3 field systems
- Mulberry trees in zone 1 to 2 for silk production
- Natural dye plants in zone 1 to 2 herb gardens
