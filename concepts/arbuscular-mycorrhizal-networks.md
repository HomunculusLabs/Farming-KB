# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Networks
## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi (phylum Glomeromycota) form the most widespread and ancient symbiosis with land plants, associating with approximately 80% of all vascular plant families. These networks of hyphae connecting plant roots are critical for [[arbuscule-intraradical-hyphae-isolation-mycorrhizal-roots]], arbuscules (nutrient exchange), and vesicles (storage structures) within root cells
- **External phase**: Extraradical hyphae extending into soil, exploring volumes inaccessible to roots, and spores for dispersal

Arbuscules in Early Devonian fossils (Aglaophyton major) are morphologically identical to living AM arbuscules, consisting of a basal trunk and repeatedly branched bush-like tufts within plant cells. The colonization occurs in a specialized meristematic root cortex region that continually provides new cells for infection.

## Nutrient Exchange Mechanisms

### Phosphorus Acquisition

AM fungi dramatically enhance phosphorus uptake through:
- Physical exploration beyond the root depletion zone
- Mycorrhizal hyphae being much smaller in diameter than the smallest root, providing larger surface area for absorption
- Production of phosphatase enzymes cleaving phosphate from [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]]

### Other Nutrients

AM fungi also enhance uptake of zinc, copper, and other micronutrients. In some cases, AM colonization increases total nitrogen assimilation (e.g., Spartina cynosuroides) without affecting shoot biomass or phosphorus content.

## Interplant Carbon and Nutrient Transfer

AM networks connect multiple plants, enabling resource transfer:
- Heap and Newman (1980) demonstrated phosphorus transfer among plants of the same species via shared AM fungi
- Transfer occurs between different species (Lolium and Plantago) connected by the same AM fungus
- Newman and Eason (1989) showed net phosphorus flux from dying roots to living roots through AM connections, but no transfer to non-mycorrhizal Brassica
- Transfer is greater among plants sharing the same mycorrhizal type (AM-AM) than between types (AM-ECM), though magnitude varies by species (Eason et al., 1991)

## Ecological Functions

### Plant Community Structure

Gange et al. (1993) showed AM fungi are determinants of plant community structure in early succession. AM associations:
- Alter competitive balances between species
- Maintain plant diversity by differentially benefiting species
- Influence succession through effects on plant establishment

### Ecosystem Stability

Jasper (1994) identified four ecosystem roles of mycorrhizae:
1. Enhancing plant establishment through improved nutrition
2. Maintaining diversity and altering plant competitive fitness
3. Contributing to resource recycling and increasing ecosystem stability
4. Stabilizing soil

### Revegetation and Restoration

Amaranthus and Perry (1989) showed Douglas-fir survival was 90% in partially cleared sites with remnant AM networks vs. 50% in totally cleared sites without them. The communal AM network provides stability through carbon and nutrient exchange, especially allowing new recruits to access larger nutrient and carbon pools.
