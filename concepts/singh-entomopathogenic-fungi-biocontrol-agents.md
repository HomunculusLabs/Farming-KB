# Entomopathogenic Fungi as Biocontrol Agents

Entomopathogenic fungi are specialized fungal pathogens that infect and
kill insects, playing important roles in natural population regulation
and serving as active ingredients in biological pest control products.
With over 700 species described across Ascomycota and Basidiomycota,
these fungi represent a diverse group of organisms with complex life
cycles involving both saprophytic and pathogenic phases. Singh's coverage
of entomopathogenic fungi highlights their significance in integrated pest
management and their connections to broader themes of
[[singh-fungal-plant-growth-promoting-mechanisms]].

## Major Genera and Species

### Metarhizium
Metarhizium anisopliae (now Metarhizium robertsii in part) is the most
widely studied and commercially developed entomopathogenic fungus. It
infects a broad range of insect hosts including coleopterans, lepidopterans,
orthopterans, and hemipterans. Metarhizium acridum (formerly M. flavoviride
var. minus) is specialized for grasshopper and locust control. Metarhizium
brunneum has emerged as a promising species for controlling soil-dwelling
pests such as white grubs and wireworms. The genus also includes species
with endophytic capability, colonizing plant roots and providing
dual benefits of pest control and plant growth promotion.

### Beauveria
Beauveria bassiana has the widest host range among entomopathogenic fungi,
infecting over 700 insect species. It is the active ingredient in numerous
commercial biopesticides used against agricultural, forestry, and
public health pests. Beauveria brongniartii is more specialized, primarily
targeting coleopteran pests including scarab beetles. Both species produce
characteristic cottony-white colonies and distinctive oval conidia.

### Lecanicillium and Other Genera
Lecanicillium lecanii (formerly Verticillium lecanii) is effective
against soft-bodied insects including aphids, whiteflies, and thrips, as
well as some phytopathogenic fungi, making it a dual-purpose biocontrol
agent. Isaria fumosorosea (formerly Paecilomyces fumosoroseus) targets
whiteflies and thrips. Cordyceps (and its teleomorph connections to
Beauveria and Isaria) includes species with highly specialized host ranges
and complex fruiting body development.

## Infection Process

### Spore Adhesion and Germination
Infection begins when conidia land on the insect cuticle and adhere
through hydrophobic interactions and adhesive proteins on the conidial
surface. Germination is triggered by nutritional cues from the cuticle,
including lipids, amino acids, and chitin degradation products. Germination
time varies from hours to days depending on temperature, humidity, and
the host insect's cuticular chemistry.

### Cuticle Penetration
The germ tube differentiates into an appressorium, a specialized
infection structure that generates mechanical pressure and secretes a
cocktail of cuticle-degrading enzymes. These include proteases
(particularly subtilisin-like serine proteases called Pr1), chitinases,
lipases, and esterases that sequentially degrade the cuticular layers.
The Pr1 protease is considered the most important virulence factor,
as it degrades the protein matrix of the procuticle. Some species also
produce cyclic peptides (destruxins from Metarhizium, beauvericin from
Beauveria) that contribute to pathogenesis.

### Hemocoel Colonization and Death
After breaching the cuticle, the fungus enters the hemocoel (insect body
cavity), where it encounters the host's immune defenses. Fungal cells
switch to yeast-like blastospores or hyphal bodies that evade phagocytosis
and proliferate rapidly in the hemolymph. Toxins including destruxins,
beauvericin, and bassianolide suppress host immunity and cause tissue
damage. The insect typically dies from a combination of toxemia,
nutrient depletion, and organ failure within 3 to 14 days post-infection.

### Saprophytic Phase and Sporulation
After host death, the fungus completes the saprophytic phase by
colonizing the entire cadaver. Under appropriate humidity conditions,
the fungus sporulates on the cadaver surface, producing millions of new
conidia that are disseminated by wind, rain splash, or contact to infect
new hosts. This epizootic potential allows entomopathogenic fungi to
persist and spread in insect populations.

## Commercial Biopesticide Development

Commercial mycopesticides are formulated as wettable powders, oil-based
suspensions, granules, or bait formulations. Key challenges include
maintaining conidial viability during storage and distribution, achieving
adequate field persistence under UV radiation and desiccation, and
ensuring consistent efficacy across varying environmental conditions.
Major commercial products include Metarhizium-based BioBlast, Green
Guard, and Met52, and Beauveria-based BotaniGard, Mycotrol, and Naturalis.

## Mass Production Methods

### Solid-State Fermentation
Most entomopathogenic fungi are produced on solid substrates including
rice, wheat bran, sorghum, or specialized granular media. Solid-state
fermentation produces aerial conidia with high viability and
environmental stability. The process involves inoculating sterilized
substrate with fungal spores, incubating under controlled temperature
and humidity, and harvesting conidia by washing or mechanical
separation. Yields typically range from 10 to the power of 9 to 10 to the power of 10 conidia per gram of substrate.

### Liquid Fermentation
Submerged liquid fermentation produces blastospores or microsclerotia
at higher volumetric productivity than solid-state methods. Blastospores
are more sensitive to environmental stress but can be formulated with
protectants. Microsclerotia of Metarhizium brunneum, produced in liquid
culture, desiccate into durable propagules that germinate rapidly upon
rehydration and can be formulated as granules for soil application.

## Integration with Pest Management

Entomopathogenic fungi are most effective when integrated into IPM programs
rather than used as standalone control measures. They are compatible with
many biological control agents but may be affected by fungicides applied
for plant disease control. Application timing is critical: targeting
susceptible life stages and applying during periods of high humidity
(maximum conidial germination) improve efficacy. Genetic engineering of
entomopathogenic fungi for enhanced virulence, UV tolerance, or
narrow-spectrum activity is an active research frontier.

## Environmental and Non-Target Safety

Entomopathogenic fungi generally have low toxicity to mammals, birds,
fish, and beneficial insects, although some non-target effects on
pollinators and natural enemies have been documented at high application
rates. Their specificity varies: Beauveria bassiana is broadly
pathogenic while Metarhizium acridum is highly specific to acridids.
Risk assessment considers host range, environmental persistence, and
potential effects on non-target organisms.
