# Settlement Vegetation and Fuel Forests

## Overview

Rigorous vegetative design of desert settlements calls for adapted perennials for both food and shade, hardy local species in shrubberies, large areas of overhead vine trellis, and only a strictly regulated amount of lawn. Golf courses, extensive grass lawns, car washes, and large open pools or ponds supplied at public cost are identified as dryland disasters that should be taxed out of existence in arid lands.

## Tree Parks and Broad Windbreaks

A critical settlement strategy is to develop broad **tree parks 300–400 m deep** around or even within the settlement. These eliminate devastating dry desert winds while also providing fuels, mulch, medicines, and supplemental domestic animal forage. This planting is the first line of defence against the desert; within its shelter, gardens and crops can thrive without wind damage or excessive water loss.

Very large buffer zones of ungrazed or lightly grazed desert, pitted or swaled, can be developed around villages. These completely defend against dust, ensuring that both public and environmental health are preserved.

## Water-Dependent Trees in Drylands

Many very hardy and useful trees need only a [[eucalyptus-camaldulensis]]*), *[[casuarina-cunninghamii]]*, and other trees adapted to root water in drylands form fuel forests that are surplus to settlement needs.

### Fuel Forest Design Elements

A productive fuel plantation requires two complementary elements:

#### 1. Perpetual Long-Term Forest Matrix

- Grid or matrix of permanent forest trees, no more than 300 m apart
- At least 8 trees wide to provide effective wind shelter
- Initially established by trickle irrigation
- Selected to be very hardy on rainfall alone once established
- Provides permanent windbreak, habitat, and microclimate

#### 2. Coppice Rotation Block

- In the sheltered spaces between permanent forest blocks
- Close-spaced trees (2–3 m) on trickle irrigation pipes, preferably automatic
- Harvested in a 4–6 year rotation as coppice
- Provides regular fuel supply without depleting the forest matrix

### Species Selection and Management

All fuel tree species should initially be selected as coppice types. Continuing field selection of seed can then proceed for the varieties, sub-species, or provenance (locality) types that respond very well to coppice cultivation.

Keeping in mind the need to cut one-fourth to one-sixth of the crop annually, and having assessed solid fuel need per household (and governed the population level), such coppiced forest areas can provide all essential cooking and public energy needs. This is especially true if the settling pond or tank is anaerobic and harvested as a methane source, and if all organic wastes are shredded and added to this tank.

### Swale-Based Fuel Forests

Where sewage is not available, a series of close-spaced swales 4–8 m wide, with banks between them only 3–5 m wide and supplied by extensive [[interceptor-banks]] harvesting broadscale runoff, will also support a fuel forest.

## Settlement Layout Principles

A carefully designed dryland settlement incorporates these vegetative elements into its spatial layout:

- **Entry roads** sited to enter downslope and crosswind, with swales leading off to forests, preventing dust from blowing into housing
- **Narrow east-west through-roads** that are tree-shaded and swaled, accepting town water
- **Only very narrow and sinuous laneways** connecting main streets
- **Primary windbreak forests** 400 m deep sheltering the settlement and preventing dust and desiccating winds
- **Sewage or swale-fed fuel forests** on 6-year rotations, surrounded by belts of perpetual forests
- **Primary digester and settling ponds** with chipper for organic wastes, alcohol fermenter, and methane engine house
- **Commerce centres** with car parks, trellised lanes, and shaded parks
- **Planted and maintained rangelands** fenced out from the settlement for livestock on long-term rotation
- **Livestock yards, milk sheds, shearing sheds** also serving as recreation grounds

## Garden Integration with Housing

The total integration of house and garden is important. Even in very poor countries, the space between and close to houses—an area rich in nutrients, close to hand, and easily shaded and watered—is often left unplanted while people "go to fields to work."

Vine species suited to desert or dryland conditions include:

- **Grape** (*[[passiflora-edulis]]*)
- **Many varieties of beans and peas** — climbing legumes for food and nitrogen fixation
- **Cucurbits** — squash, melons, gourds
- **Yams** (*Dioscorea* spp.) — productive root crop on vine
- **Forage vines** for domestic rabbits or guinea pigs
- **Honey and pollen vines** — supporting pollinators

Even in sub-arctic climates, the silverberry (*[[actinidia-arguta]]*) provides copious fruit crop and survives hard winters.

## Water Features and Lawn Policy

Settlements in drylands must strictly regulate water-intensive features:

- **Lippia or other carpeting, drought-resistant plants** should replace conventional lawns
- **Golf courses** are incompatible with dryland sustainability
- **Large open pools and ponds** supplied at public cost are dryland disasters
- **Car washes** using potable water should be eliminated

## Rangeland Integration

Within settlement and nearby, gardens can be established for food, and an outer zone reserved for a 6–9 year rotation of domestic rangeland species supplying meat, wool, and other products. This rangeland must be fenced and managed separately from settlement gardens to prevent livestock damage to intensive plantings.
## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
Field trials provide essential data for validating theoretical approaches and refining methodologies.
