## Keypoint and Keyline in Humid Landscapes

In [[permaculture-design]] for humid landscapes (receiving more than 30 inches of rainfall annually), the concept of the **keypoint** and the associated **keyline** system represent the fundamental strategy for water storage and distribution across the landscape. Developed from the work of P.A. Yeomans and adapted by [[bill-mollison]], this approach treats the entire landscape as a unified water harvesting and distribution system — conceptually, "a roof and a tank."

## The Humid Landscape Profile

### Characteristic Shape

Humid landscapes, unless geologically very young (volcanic or newly faulted), develop softly rounded outlines through millennia of water erosion and soil formation. When walking up a valley or along a ridge, the terrain exhibits a characteristic rounded S-shaped profile. This shape is critical to understanding water flow and storage potential.

The main valley carries the primary water flow, fed by many smaller creeks entering from the sides. These creeks originate at the **valley head** — the highest point in the watershed where water begins to collect and flow downhill.

### How Water Moves Across the Landscape

Individual raindrops, wherever they fall, follow paths that cross contours at approximately right angles — because the perpendicular path represents the shortest drop between two contour lines. Water takes the shortest path across the landscape from where it falls to where it reaches the river line. Once in the river valley, it flows rapidly to the sea.

This means that most rainfall runs off the landscape quickly, with minimal opportunity for infiltration, storage, or biological use. The permaculture designer"s task is to intercept this water at the highest practical point and redirect it through as many productive uses as possible before allowing it to leave the system.

## Locating the Keypoint

### Definition

The **keypoint** is the critical point in the valley profile where the landscape changes from convex (bulging outward) to concave (curving inward). It occurs at the valley head where the small creeks begin.

### Identification Methods

**From contour maps:** The keypoint is located where contour lines begin to spread apart. On a topographic map, contours are closely spaced on steep convex slopes and widen as the slope flattens into the concave profile. The transition point is the keypoint.

**In the field:** Walking up the valley from its base, the keypoint is where the slope angle noticeably decreases and the valley begins to widen. Above this point, the ground is too steep for economical earthworks; below it, the land flattens enough for practical water storage and distribution infrastructure.

### Significance

The keypoint is significant because it is the **highest point in the valley profile at which water can be economically stored using mechanical tools.** Above the keypoint, the terrain is too steep for conventional [[dam-construction]]. Below it, dams can be built at progressively lower elevations.

## The Keyline

### Definition and Slope

From the keypoint, the **keyline** starts to fall — gradually, at a rate between one in 1,000 and one in 2,000 below contour. This is an extremely gentle slope: for every 2,000 feet of horizontal distance, the line drops only one foot. Water moving along a keyline moves slowly and evenly, with virtually no erosive energy.

### Construction

The keyline is constructed by cutting a shallow groove or shelf around the hillside, starting at the keypoint and extending outward. This groove functions as a gutter — intercepting water that would otherwise run rapidly downhill and redirecting it along the contour. In effect, the designer has "put a gutter around our roof."

### The Dam at the Keypoint

At the keypoint itself, a small dam is constructed. This is the highest practical dam in the system — not the most economical dam in terms of earth-to-water ratio, but the most strategically valuable because it gathers all the water from the hilltop down to that point. The keypoint dam should be made as large as affordable, because it enables water to be released and distributed to any lower point in the landscape at any time of year.

### Water Distribution System

Water is led out from the keypoint dam through the dam wall, either by siphon or by a lock-pipe (a pipe through the dam wall with an adjustable intake height). The water enters a contour drain that runs along the keyline slope.

Flow in the drain is controlled by a simple device: a sheet of canvas or plastic fastened like a flag to a very light plastic pipe. A chain attached to the other end of the flag serves as a weight. By pegging the flag down within the drain, flow is held back until water accumulates to the desired level. By releasing the peg, water is allowed to flow onward.

This allows the designer to:
- **Hold back water** in the keypoint dam for dry-season release
- **Direct water** to specific areas of the landscape through the contour drain
- **Release water** gradually for irrigation, recharge, or aquaculture

The irrigation channels branching from this system can have slopes ranging from 1:200 to 1:2,000, ensuring gentle, non-erosive water movement across the landscape.

## Strategic Landscape Zoning

### Above the Keypoint: Forestry Zone

The land above the keypoint [[diversion-drain-mollison]] is best suited to forestry. The steep slopes are unsuitable for agriculture or grazing but are ideal for tree planting, which further stabilizes the soil, increases infiltration, and enhances the water-gathering capacity of the upper watershed. Forest cover above the keypoint protects the entire water harvesting system below.

### Below the Keypoint: Irrigated Agriculture

The land below the keypoint, with its gentler slopes and distributed water supply, is suited to irrigated pasture, intensive gardens, and other productive uses. The keyline system provides reliable water delivery to these areas regardless of rainfall patterns.

### Cascading Dam System

In the lower valleys, additional dams can be constructed, each slightly lower than the previous one. These dams will not be at the keypoint level but serve as additional water storage within the irrigated zone. Together with the keypoint dam, they form a cascading system that provides multiple levels of water storage and distribution across the landscape.

## The Roof-and-Tank Model

Mollison"s central metaphor for this system is powerful in its simplicity: having found the keypoint, "we can now treat the whole landscape as if it were a roof and a tank."

- **The roof** is the hillside above the keypoint, collecting rainfall
- **The gutter** is the keyline, intercepting and redirecting the collected water
- **The tank** is the keypoint dam, storing water at the highest practical point
- **The downspout** is the distribution system, delivering stored water where needed

This model makes the entire watershed a single integrated water management system, with the designer controlling the flow from collection to use.

## Design Implications

### Start from the Top

The principle of intervening close to the source is embodied in keypoint design. By storing and distributing water from the highest practical point, the designer gains maximum flexibility and control. Water can be directed to any lower point through gravity alone — no pumps or external energy required.

### Work with Gravity

The entire keyline system operates on gravity. Water flows from the keypoint to the lower landscape through the gentle slope of the keyline and the contour drains. This makes the system energy-neutral in operation — once constructed, it functions indefinitely without external energy input.

### Multiple Functions

The keyline system serves multiple functions simultaneously: water storage, irrigation, flood control, [[dighton-fungal-roles-soil-erosion-prevention]], aquifer recharge, and fire protection (the keypoint dam provides a water source for firefighting). In permaculture terms, each element serves multiple functions, and each function is supported by multiple elements.

## Distinction from Yeomans' Original Keyline

Mollison noted that his treatment of keyline differs significantly from P.A. Yeomans' original formulation. Yeomans developed the keyline system specifically for Australian broadacre grazing properties, with emphasis on contour plowing to improve soil moisture distribution. Mollison adapted the concept more broadly as a water harvesting and landscape design tool, integrating it with dam construction, forestry zoning, and multi-function [[slope-analysis-landscape-planning]]. For a more detailed and technically precise treatment of the original keyline system, Mollison directed readers to Yeomans' *Water for Every Farm — The Keyline Plan*.
