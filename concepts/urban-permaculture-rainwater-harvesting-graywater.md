# Rainwater Harvesting and Graywater Reuse

[Rainwater harvesting and graywater reuse are fundamental [[permaculture]] strategies for reducing dependence on municipal water systems and creating resilient, self-sufficient homesteads. Brad Lancaster's desert homestead in Tucson, Arizona, demonstrates how these systems can be built from salvaged materials on a minimal budget.

## Brad Lancaster's Desert Homestead

Brad Lancaster and his brother Rodd found a decaying 1919 adobe bungalow on a one-eighth acre lot at 9th and University in downtown Tucson. The 746-square-foot house had no water, electricity, sewer, heating, cooling, functional doors or windows, ceilings, a decent roof, floor, or toilet. It was essentially a condemned building -- but it sat on an east-west axis with good solar aspect, and the yard contained a sour orange tree, a pomegranate, a white sapote, and three chaste trees.

The brothers bought the property and began what became both the largest salvage operation they had ever undertaken and an incredible classroom. They did 90% of the work themselves, learning roofing, electrical, plumbing, and carpentry from how-to manuals and knowledgeable friends. Friends like carpenter John Andrews and network organizer Barbara Rose were indispensable.

## 55-Gallon Drum Catchment

One of the first water harvesting installations used salvaged 55-gallon drums. After taking down the chain-link fence around the property, they planted the drums to harvest rainwater from the garage roof. Summer monsoon storms in Tucson flood streets and blow down over-irrigated landscaping -- Lancaster saw this not as chaos but as harvest.

The drums provide immediate, low-cost water storage. Even a small roof can yield significant water: 1 inch of rain on a 100-square-foot roof produces about 62 gallons. Multiple drums linked together create a gravity-fed system that can water nearby garden beds with no pumps or moving parts. See [[swale-design]] for larger-scale water capture.

For urban gardeners, 55-gallon drums are the most accessible entry point into rainwater harvesting. They can be obtained cheaply from food processors, industrial suppliers, or salvage yards. Clean food-grade drums are ideal. Paint them dark to prevent algae growth, and screen the inlets to keep out mosquitoes and debris.

## Ferrocement Cistern

The larger water storage system is a 1200-gallon ferrocement cistern collecting rainwater off the main roof. It is an oval, ferrocement septic tank elevated two feet above the highest point of the property with earth and rock. This elevation allows watering by gravity -- no pumps, no maintenance, no moving parts. This [gravity-fed approach is central to permaculture water design.

The cistern is strategically located along the fence line next to a neighbor's rental cottage, where it serves multiple functions simultaneously:

- **Fire break** -- a solid mass of water and earth between properties
- **Privacy screen** -- blocks views between houses
- **Temperature moderator** -- thermal mass that buffers temperature swings for the adjacent orange tree
- **Water storage** -- gravity-fed irrigation for the garden

The orange tree shades the cistern and drops leaves for mulch, creating a mutualistic relationship. Watering with rainwater combined with [[sheet-mulching]] of organic droppings, prunings, and manure cut all watering needs by two-thirds.

Ferrocement construction is accessible to DIY builders. The process involves applying a cement-sand mortar over a frame of wire mesh and rebar. A salvaged septic tank (as Lancaster used) can serve as a pre-formed cistern shell. For new construction, build over a sand form that is removed through a cleanout port after the cement cures.

## Graywater from the Bathtub

Lancaster refused to watch bathtub water go down the drain. The tub drain was not easily accessible for plumbing modification, so he improvised: a 1/2-inch vinyl tube runs through the window jam, with one end suction-cupped to the bottom of the tub and the other end under the winter-deciduous black mission fig that shades the bedroom in summer. A hand pump starts the siphon, and then gravity does the rest.

For watering the north side of the house, the hose can be hooked to an old half-inch irrigation line that deposits graywater on Asian pears and a loquat. This simple, low-tech system captures every drop of bathing water for productive use.

Graywater guidelines for safe reuse:

- Use only from showers, bathtubs, bathroom sinks, and laundry (no kitchen water with food particles)
- Apply graywater directly to soil (never store for more than 24 hours to prevent bacterial growth)
- Do not use on root crops or plants where edible parts touch the soil
- Use biodegradable, low-sodium soaps and detergents
- Distribute via mulch basins around plants, not through sprinklers

## Passive [[solar-water-heating]]

The gas water heater was removed and replaced with a passive solar "bread box" water heater designed by Tobin Schneider and Bill Cunningham. The design:

- One-inch duct board insulation with heat tape to a double thickness of 2 inches forms the insulated box
- Tempered double-glazed patio door glass set at 45 degrees (maximum solar gain in winter) seals the box
- Inside: a stripped-down gas water heater tank painted black to absorb and store solar heat
- The box rests on a cradle of sheet metal stripped from the old tank
- City water pressure moves water through the system
- No moving parts

The passive solar heater provides hot water without gas or electricity, working year-round in Tucson's sunny climate. Even in less sunny climates, a solar pre-heater can significantly reduce conventional water heating costs by raising incoming water temperature before it reaches the backup heater.

## Passive Solar Building Design

The house was retrofitted for passive solar heating and cooling:

- Rotten, termite-ridden windows replaced with double-glazed units (old glass saved for solar ovens and cold frames)
- All west-facing glass removed except the kitchen window (shaded by a neighbor's house)
- Extended roof overhang blocks summer sun but allows winter sun to penetrate south-facing glass
- The south-facing Arizona room has an exposed concrete slab stained with ferrous sulfate for better thermal mass
- A low granite rock retaining wall (built without cement) provides additional thermal mass, stops erosion, and creates microclimates for diverse plantings
- Rebar trellis with 6-inch concrete mesh supports edible squash vines that shade the house in summer, die at first frost (providing mulch), and clear the trellis for winter solar gain

The concrete floor was treated with a mix of two parts water to one part ferrous sulfate, brushed on and left for a week, then rinsed and oiled with boiled linseed oil and turpentine. The darker concrete absorbs and stores passive solar heat more effectively.

## Living Fence and Buffer Zones

A living fence of ocotillo canes and agave flower stalks borders the property. On either side, 8-10 foot wide buffer zones of native plants serve as noise, pollution, light, and privacy barriers while doubling as wildlife habitat. The native plants used have medicinal, food, habitat, bee forage, and nitrogen-fixing value. Saguaro, barrel, and cholla cacti were salvaged from areas slated for clearing.

The chain-link fencing removed from the property was traded at the salvage yard for used 2x4s to extend the roof overhang, which further protects walls from rain and summer sun. The toxic asphalt roofing was replaced with corrugated steel for rainwater harvesting.

## Composting Toilet Research

A low-flush toilet was installed to meet code, but Lancaster was also researching composting toilet designs to return humanure nutrients to the landscape. The principle is straightforward: what leaves the body as waste should cycle back to the soil that grows the food, closing the nutrient loop. [[composting-toilets]] convert human waste into safe, nutrient-rich compost through aerobic decomposition.

## Key Principles for Urban Application

1. **Start with what falls from the sky** -- roof catchment is the easiest entry point
2. **Use gravity wherever possible** -- elevate storage, eliminate pumps
3. **Every element serves multiple functions** -- cisterns as fire breaks and privacy screens
4. **Salvage everything** -- chain-link fence traded for lumber, election signs for ramada roofs
5. **Work with neighbors** -- mutual aid, shared resources, community building
6. **Start small, observe, expand** -- begin with 55-gallon drums before building ferrocement cisterns
7. **Graywater is a resource, not waste** -- even simple siphon systems capture significant water
8. **Observe before designing** -- watch where water flows on your site before installing systems
9. **Slow, spread, sink** -- the three principles of Permaculture Water Systems in any landscape
