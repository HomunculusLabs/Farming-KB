# [[indigenous-microorganisms]] (IMOs) form the foundation of Dr. [[natural-farming]] Institute in South Korea
beginning in the 1960s. Rather than importing commercial microbial inoculants, KNF
practitioners collect microorganisms adapted to the local environment over many generations.
Dr. Cho argues that organisms found under the sun differ from those in shaded areas like
bamboo groves, and advocates culturing microbes from diverse environments to maximize
microbial diversity. He was imprisoned multiple times by the South Korean government under
pressure from chemical industry lobbies, but KNF has since spread to over 15 countries.

## Why IMOs Matter

Microorganisms serve two critical functions. First, they decompose complex [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]]
— dead plant and animal matter, crop residues, and wastes — converting them into nutrients
plants can absorb. Second, they produce antibiotic substances, enzymes, and lactic acids
that suppress plant diseases. In KNF, farmers do not feed plants directly; they nurture the
soil, and the soil nurtures plants through IMOs. Locally indigenous microbes have survived
extreme climatic conditions far better than artificially cultured organisms and cost nothing.

## IMO-1: Initial Collection

The best material for culturing IMOs is steamed rice — slightly undercooked, not too soft
or sticky, since aerobic microorganisms prefer drier substrates. Plastic containers are
always avoided; wood or bamboo containers are used because they breathe. White plain paper
(not newspaper) covers the rice, secured with rubber bands, allowing air passage.

### Method 1 — Native Soil Collection

A wooden box (12 × 8 × 4 inches, half-inch thickness) is filled with steamed rice no
higher than 3 inches to ensure adequate air supply. The box is covered with porous paper
and placed in a pit excavated 2 inches deep at a forest or field site rich in decomposed
leaf mold. Leaves cover the box, with a bamboo basket protecting it from animals. At 20°C,
colonization takes 5–6 days; at 30–35°C, only 2–3 days. Colorful colonies (white, yellow,
pink) indicate success. Black mold means the batch has been exceeded and must be discarded.

### Method 2 — Bamboo Stump Collection

A healthy bamboo shoot in a grove center is cut 10 cm from the ground. The stump edges are
trimmed to slant inward, preventing bamboo juice leakage. The cavity is filled with boiled
rice above the edge. A wooden box covers the stump, then dry leaves and plastic sheeting.
In 3–5 days, diverse colonies appear along with absorbed bamboo juice. The stump is cut
and colonized rice transferred to a jar as IMO-1.

### Method 3 — Paddy Field Collection

After rice harvest, rice-filled wooden boxes are placed face-down over cut paddy stumps
so rice contacts the stumps directly. Steel wire netting deters rodents. After about a
week, mostly anaerobic microorganisms are collected, including Bacillus licheniformis
(breaks down proteins, fats, carbohydrates) and [[decomposition]].

## IMO-2: Sugar Fermentation

IMO-1 is mixed with jaggery or brown sugar in a 1:1 ratio by weight. The less processed
the sugar, the more effective — white refined sugar is not recommended because it lacks
minerals. Crude unrefined jaggery is ideal. The mixture ferments in a clay pot or glass
jar covered with porous paper: approximately 3 days in summer, 5 days in winter.

## IMO-3: Mass Cultivation on Rice Bran

IMO-2 is diluted with water at 1:1000 and mixed with rice bran or flour to achieve 65–70%
moisture. The squeeze test confirms correct consistency: the mixture forms a lump when
pressed but breaks apart when shaken. Diluted NF inputs (FPJ, FAA, OHN at standard rates)
can enhance the mixture.

The bran is stacked on soil floor (never concrete) in heaps no more than 30–40 cm high,
covered with paddy straw to prevent temperature exceeding 50°C. Straw is flipped every
2 days. Temperature management is critical: below 40°C indicates anaerobic conditions
from excess moisture; above 70°C causes thermophilic protein breakdown and nitrogen loss.
The pile is turned when it reaches 40–50°C. After 5–7 days, white spores cover the
surface, fermentation odor becomes fragrant, and moisture drops to ~40%. This is IMO-3.

## IMO-4: Soil Integration

IMO-3 is combined 1:1 with soil — 50% from the crop field and 50% from fresh sources
(mountain soil, anthill/termite mound red fine soil). This harmonizes wild IMOs with
field-adapted populations. Mixing occurs on soil floor in heaps no more than 20 cm high,
covered for two days. Moisture is maintained at 65–75% using diluted NF inputs. Seawater
is added before field application to provide trace minerals optimizing microbial activity.

## Storage and Preservation

IMO-3 is stored in shaded, cool places with good [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]. Ventilated containers
such as jute or gunny bags are ideal — airtight plastic must never be used because it
creates anaerobic conditions that kill the aerobic microbes. Rice straw or leaf litter is
placed at the bottom of each container before adding IMO-3, creating a buffer layer that
helps regulate moisture and temperature.

During storage, moisture gradually evaporates from the initial 65–70% down to 20–30%.
This drying causes the IMOs to enter a dormant state (state of dormancy), which is entirely
normal and expected. The microbes remain viable and can be reactivated when moisture is
restored. Containers are stacked up to 3 layers maximum and shielded from direct sunlight
and rain. Convection currents created through the gaps between containers provide sufficient
air exchange without the need for active ventilation or turning.

Before using IMO-4 in the field, the moisture content should be readjusted to 65–70%
by adding nutrient liquids from [[natural-farming]] inputs. This reactivation step "wakes up"
the dormant microbes and prepares them for rapid colonization when applied to the soil.

## Utilization Principles

Three key principles guide effective IMO use in the field. First, use IMOs continuously —
they should be collected and prepared every year to maintain active soil microbial
populations. Soil biology cannot be built once and forgotten; it requires ongoing input
and attention, much like any living system.

Second, maintain IMO diversity by collecting from different environments and mixing different
types of microorganisms. Dr. Cho recommends sampling from all four compass directions around
the field, and from varying elevations including mountain summits, valleys, and trenches.
Each micro-environment harbors distinct microbial communities, and combining them creates a
more robust and resilient inoculant.

Third, include "tough guys" — microbes collected from high mountains or uncontaminated
regions that have survived extreme conditions. These hardy organisms add resilience to the
mixture. Since the sunny side of a field hosts different microbes than the shaded side, and
altitude affects microbial composition at every level, sampling broadly ensures the IMO
preparation can handle whatever conditions the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] brings.

## Practical Notes

Dr. Cho emphasizes that farmers are free to experiment on their own and substitute
alternative local materials as ingredients for various preparations. This flexibility
lends scope for innovation and makes the system accessible across different ecosystems.
The technology has been recognized for its ability to produce more, at better quality,
with lower cost than chemical-intensive agriculture, while simultaneously accomplishing
the dream of "[[cho-zero-emission-livestock-natural-farming]]" in livestock management — no wastewater emitted, and all
wastes recycled and converted to resources.
