## Fate of Petroleum in the Environment

When petroleum enters the environment, it undergoes several simultaneous processes:

- **Evaporation:** Low-molecular-weight alkanes (C1-C12) and monoaromatics (BTEX) evaporate rapidly, typically within hours to days, reducing acute toxicity but leaving behind more recalcitrant fractions.
- **Dissolution:** Water-soluble components (BTEX, naphthalene) dissolve into groundwater, creating plumes that can extend far from the source.
- **Photooxidation:** UV exposure oxidizes surface petroleum, generating polar oxygenated compounds (ketones, aldehydes, carboxylic acids) that are more water-soluble and biodegradable.
- **Biodegradation:** The primary long-term removal mechanism, mediated by diverse microbial communities including bacteria, yeasts, and filamentous fungi.

Petroleum composition varies by source but generally includes: aliphatic hydrocarbons (50-95%, including n-alkanes, iso-alkanes, and cycloalkanes), aromatic hydrocarbons (10-40%, including BTEX and PAHs), and resins and asphaltenes (high-molecular-weight polar compounds that are the most recalcitrant fraction).

## Methods of Analysis

Petroleum contamination is assessed through multiple analytical approaches:

- **GC-MS (gas chromatography-mass spectrometry):** The standard method for identifying and quantifying individual petroleum hydrocarbons, including TPH (total petroleum hydrocarbons), BTEX, n-alkane distributions, and PAH profiles.
- **Respirometric tests:** Measure CO2 production and O2 consumption as indicators of microbial petroleum degradation activity in soil or water samples.
- **Soil microcosm tests:** Laboratory-scale simulations of field conditions that evaluate biodegradation potential, kinetics, and treatment optimization before field deployment.

## Hydrocarbon-Utilizing Yeasts and Fungi

A diverse taxonomic range of fungi colonize petroleum-contaminated environments:

- **Yeasts:** Candida maltosa, Candida tropicalis, Candida lipolytica, Candida guilliermondii, Rhodotorula glutinis, Rhodotorula rubra, Aureobasidium pullulans — these are frequently isolated from oil-contaminated soils and waters, particularly during early colonization phases.
- **Filamentous fungi:** Cladosporium resinae (the "kerosene fungus," commonly found in aviation fuel systems), Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus fumigatus, Penicillium spp., Fusarium spp., Trichoderma spp. — filamentous fungi contribute to degradation of higher-molecular-weight fractions.
- **White-rot basidiomycetes:** Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus — particularly effective for PAH and asphaltene degradation through extracellular ligninolytic enzymes. See [[fungal-cytochrome-p450-biodegradation]] for P450 details.

**Aromatic Hydrocarbons (BTEX):** Benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene are degraded through oxidation by cytochrome P450 enzymes, forming catechol intermediates that undergo ring cleavage (ortho- or meta-cleavage pathways). While bacteria are often more efficient for BTEX, fungi contribute significantly in mixed communities and under conditions unfavorable to bacteria (low pH, low nutrient availability).

**Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs):** White-rot fungi excel at degrading high-molecular-weight PAHs (4-6 rings) that bacteria cannot attack due to insolubility and inability to cross cell walls. The extracellular ligninolytic enzyme system -- particularly lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), and laccase -- oxidizes PAHs through radical-mediated mechanisms. See [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] for detailed enzyme biochemistry and [[white-rot-fungi-bioremediation]] for organism-specific enzyme profiles.

## Economic Importance

Beyond environmental remediation, petroleum-degrading fungi have industrial significance:

- **Single-cell protein (SCP):** Hydrocarbon-grown yeasts (Candida, Rhodotorula) produce protein-rich biomass suitable for animal feed supplements, converting waste hydrocarbons into valuable products.
- **Surfactant production:** Petroleum-degrading fungi synthesize biosurfactants that enhance hydrocarbon emulsification and have commercial applications in enhanced oil recovery, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals.
- **Metabolite overproduction:** Fungal metabolism of hydrocarbons yields valuable intermediates including organic acids, alcohols, and specialized biochemicals.

## Fungal-Bacterial Co-cultures

Combined fungal-bacterial systems show synergistic effects in petroleum degradation. Fungi initially attack high-molecular-weight and insoluble fractions (asphaltenes, resins, high-ring PAHs) through extracellular enzymes, producing partially oxidized intermediates that bacteria can then mineralize. This complementary metabolism achieves more complete degradation than either organism alone. Fungi also improve soil structure through hyphal penetration, increasing aeration and hydrocarbon bioavailability for bacterial communities.

## PAHs in Municipal Solid Waste

Distillery and brewery wastes (MSW) contain significant PAH concentrations. Flavodon flavus removed 68% of total PAHs and 98% of toxicity from MSW within 5 days, with MnP and laccase detected during treatment (Raghukumar et al., 2004). Benzo[a]pyrene was identified as a major toxicity contributor.

## Mycorrhizal Contributions

Mycorrhizal fungi contribute to petroleum hydrocarbon degradation in the rhizosphere, enhancing degradation through enzyme production and improving plant tolerance in contaminated soils. They also improve soil structure, increasing aeration and bioavailability of hydrocarbons.
