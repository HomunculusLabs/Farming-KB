# Fungal Decomposition of Lignin and Cellulose

## Overview

Fungi are the primary decomposers of lignocellulose—the
structural polymer complex that constitutes plant cell
walls and accounts for the bulk of terrestrial organic
carbon. Lignocellulose consists of cellulose (40-50%),
hemicellulose (25-35%), and lignin (15-30%). While
cellulose and hemicellulose are carbohydrate polymers
relatively accessible to enzymatic breakdown, lignin is
an irregular, cross-linked phenolic polymer that is among
the most recalcitrant organic molecules in nature. Fungi,
particularly basidiomycetes, are virtually alone in their
ability to efficiently degrade lignin, making them
indispensable agents in global carbon cycling.

## Soft Rot Fungi

Soft rot fungi, mainly ascomycetes and some deuteromycetes,
degrade wood under conditions too wet or too dry for
basidiomycete activity. They create characteristic
cavity-shaped decay within the secondary cell wall,
visible as diamond-shaped or cylindrical pits under
microscopy. Soft rot is slower than white or brown rot
but is ecologically critical in aquatic and extreme
terrestrial environments. Chaetomium and Xylaria species

## Enzymatic Mechanisms

Cellulose degradation involves endoglucanases (EGs) that
randomly cleave internal bonds, cellobiohydrolases (CBHs)
that processively release cellobiose from chain ends, and
beta-glucosidases that hydrolyze cellobiose to glucose.
These enzymes act synergistically, often secreted as free
enzyme cocktails or organized in multi-enzyme cellulosome
complexes.

Hemicellulose degradation requires broader enzyme suites due to hemicellulose structural heterogeneity. Xylanases,
mannanases, galactanases, arabinofuranosidases, and
acetylxylan esterases work together to depolymerize
hemicellulose backbones and remove substituent groups.

Ligninolytic peroxidases require hydrogen peroxide as a
co-substrate, generated by fungal oxidases including
glyoxal oxidase, aryl alcohol oxidase, and methanol
oxidase. Laccases use molecular oxygen directly, making
them potentially more efficient in oxygen-limited
environments. The regulation of these enzyme systems
responds to nutrient limitation—particularly nitrogen
starvation triggers ligninolytic gene expression.

The balance between white rot and brown rot activity in
a forest affects soil carbon storage: brown rot leaves
more recalcitrant lignin residues that accumulate as
soil organic matter, while white rot more completely
mineralizes woody carbon to CO2.

Fungal decomposition rates depend on wood chemistry
(hardwood vs. softwood, heartwood extractives), moisture
content, temperature, oxygen availability, and fungal
community composition. Climate change may alter these
dynamics by shifting temperature and precipitation
patterns, potentially accelerating decomposition and
reducing forest carbon storage.

## Biotechnological Applications

White rot fungi are used in biopulping (reducing energy
and chemical inputs in paper production), bioremediation

biomass). Brown rot Fenton chemistry inspires biomimetic
