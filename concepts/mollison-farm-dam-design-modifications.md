Standard farm dams are typically designed for water storage and reticulation, often following Keyline principles outlined by P.A. Yeoman. However, Bill Mollison argues that several additional features can be incorporated into farm dams to transform them from simple water storages into productive polyculture systems that support fish, waterfowl, forage plants, and integrated [[permaculture-farm-design-principles]].

## Keyline-Permaculture Integration

The combined application of Keyline and permaculture achieves what Mollison describes as a rare conscious integration of water and soil treatment plus integrated biological planning. Keyline dams provide the water storage and reticulation framework, while permaculture modifications add biological productivity. Such combinations give excellent results on small-holdings of 1-15 acres and would ensure stability for any country that applied them on a national scale.

## Polyculture Modifications to Farm Dams

Mollison specifies several modifications that can be added to conventional farm dams to support polyculture:

- Island refuges for breeding waterfowl
- Shallow shelves for waterfowl forage plants at the rear and edges of large dams
- Deep sump refuges for fish in areas where the dam is less than 10 feet deep and summer temperatures are high
- Peninsula structures, with or without a moat, for house fire-protection in fire-critical areas
- South banks to site plants, houses, and glasshouses to gain reflected heat benefits in winter and at low sun angles

Each of these modifications addresses a specific biological or design need. Island refuges provide safe nesting sites for waterfowl, free from land-based predators. Shallow shelves support emergent and marginal aquatic plants that serve as waterfowl forage and provide habitat for invertebrates. Deep sump refuges give fish cool water retreats during hot summer months when surface temperatures become lethal.

Peninsula structures extending into dams create defensible space for buildings during wildfires, as the water barrier prevents fire from approaching from at least one direction. South-facing banks capture and reflect winter sunlight, creating warm microclimates for frost-sensitive plants and structures.

## Pond Nomenclature and Types

Understanding the variety of man-made waterworks is essential for proper design. Following Chakroff and Yeoman, Mollison classifies larger impoundments as follows:

Barrage ponds are constructed across stream courses, filled directly by the stream or by valley runoff.

Diversion ponds are filled by a diversion channel that leads water from a stream or runoff area such as a bare, rocky slope, diverting water from its normal course.

Ring dams, or turkey nests in Australia, are flatland storages built above grade. Water must be pumped into these, forming a series of storage facilities.

Storage ponds are steady-level ponds formed as barrages that take water from upstream barrages and lead it into contour channels or irrigation banks.

Contour dams are built along a contour, with wing banks running upslope to impound water. The contour may be concave or convex on the downhill side.

All of these structures, with the exception of penstocks, are normally subject to fluctuating levels as water is used downstream.

## Small Ponds vs Large Storages

Water storages designed for growing fish and plants are very different structures from those designed solely for stock watering or irrigation. Mollison emphasizes that many small ponds of 100 to 500 square meters (less than 1/8 acre) are far better suited to fish culture than very large storages of 400 square meters (1 acre) or more.

Graded bottoms ranging from 75 cm to 2 m depth suit many fish species, while storage ponds designed purely for water supply need to be 3-6 m deep to be worthwhile on large acreages. The shallow graded bottoms of polyculture ponds create diverse habitat zones that support a wider range of aquatic life. Multiple depth zones allow fish to thermoregulate by moving between warmer surface waters and cooler depths, while shallow margins provide spawning areas and support emergent vegetation that harbors invertebrate food sources.

This preference for small, numerous water bodies aligns with faires-pond-design-and-aquaculture. The biological productivity per unit area of well-designed small ponds far exceeds that of large, deep storages. Ecological research supports this: pond edge-to-area ratio determines the amount of littoral zone habitat available, and smaller ponds have proportionally more edge, supporting more diverse and productive biological communities per unit of water surface area.

## Biological Importance of Small Structures

Mollison draws attention to the ecological significance of small water bodies that designers and engineers frequently overlook. In deserts, even tiny rockholes may be critical for the survival of quail chicks and desert animals. At the other extreme, relatively vast grazing shallows are needed by swan flocks in estuaries. The key insight is that the ecological value of a water body cannot be measured by volume alone — a small, shallow, vegetated pond may support far more species and greater total biomass than a deep, barren reservoir of much larger volume.

Small structures serve critical functions including filter channels, quail ponds, and solar ponds. Engineers seem little concerned with subsurface dams and shoreline or perched impoundments that are critical to the survival of fish populations. The consequence is vast and expensive lakes built for power generation that have very poor biological productivity. The ecological cost of large dams — fragmentation of river ecosystems, disruption of fish migration routes, loss of riparian habitat, and alteration of downstream sediment transport — further argues for the permaculture preference for small, numerous, well-integrated water bodies over single large impoundments. ## Earthworks and Construction Considerations

The physical construction of polyculture dams requires careful attention to soil type, compaction, and [[fukuoka-creating-natural-farm-site-selection-establishment]]. Clay-rich soils (minimum 15-20% clay content) are essential for water retention; sandy or gravelly soils require imported clay or artificial liners. Mollison emphasizes that the dam wall itself should be compacted in 15-20 cm layers (lifts) at optimal moisture content, with each lift thoroughly rolled before the next is added. The core of the dam wall should contain the most impervious material available, with coarser material on the downstream face for drainage.

Keyline principles inform the placement of dams in the landscape: dams should be sited at the highest practical point in the catchment to maximize gravitational water distribution, with the dam wall positioned on the contour. The spillway must be sized to handle peak flood flows without eroding the dam wall — Mollison recommends designing spillways for 1-in-50-year flood events minimum. ## Fish Species Selection for Polyculture Ponds

Mollison advocates for stocking polyculture ponds with complementary fish species that occupy different ecological niches. In temperate climates, this might include bottom feeders (carp, tench) that stir the pond floor and release nutrients, mid-water feeders (silver perch, golden perch) that control insect populations, and surface feeders (mosquito fish) that prevent mosquito breeding. In tropical systems, tilapia species serve similar roles. The key principle is that no single species should dominate; rather, the pond should support a balanced community where each species' waste products become another's food source.

Duck integration adds another productive layer: waterfowl manure fertilizes the pond, stimulating plankton growth that feeds fish, while ducks also consume aquatic weeds and insect pests. Mollison's [[faires-pigeon-quail-and-waterfowl-systems]] approach extends this integration to other small poultry species suitable for smallholding polyculture.

## Seasonal Management and Maintenance

Productive polyculture dams require ongoing seasonal management. In autumn, fallen leaves from surrounding trees should be allowed to accumulate in the pond, providing overwintering habitat for aquatic invertebrates and slowly releasing nutrients as they decompose. In spring, marginal plants should be cut back and the cut material used as mulch in garden beds, recycling the nutrients absorbed from the pond water. During summer, monitoring oxygen levels becomes critical — shallow ponds with high organic inputs can become stratified, with oxygen-depleted bottom water killing fish during hot weather. Mollison recommends installing wind-driven aerators or designing pond depth profiles that allow wind mixing to prevent stratification.
