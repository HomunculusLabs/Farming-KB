# Stropharia cubensis (Psilocybe cubensis) — The Ur Plant

## Overview

Stropharia cubensis, more commonly known as Psilocybe cubensis, is a species
of [[archaeology-of-psychedelic-mushroom-use]] whose principal active compounds are psilocybin and
psilocin. It is the most widely cultivated and commercially available
psilocybin-containing mushroom in the world.

The species was first collected by the American botanist Franklin Sumner Earle
in Cuba in 1906, though current botanical thinking places its point of origin
in Southeast Asia, where it occurs wherever cattle of the zebu (Bos indicus)
type graze.

Terence McKenna famously designated Stropharia cubensis as the "Ur plant" —
the original catalyst for the emergence of human consciousness, self-reflection,
and language in his 1992 book "Food of the Gods."

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

Originally described as Stropharia cubensis by Earle, the species was later
transferred to the genus Psilocybe by Rolf Singer and others. Many mycological
authorities still recognize it under the name Psilocybe cubensis, while some
recent molecular phylogenetic studies have proposed further reclassifications.

The species epithet "cubensis" refers to its initial discovery in Cuba. Common
names include "golden teacher," "[[gartz-wasson-mexican-mushroom-cult-rediscovery]]," and simply "cubensis." Over
100 cultivated varieties exist, marketed under names such as "Thai," "Colombian,"
"Amazonian," "B+," "Golden Teacher," "Ecuador," and "Cambodian."

The species belongs to the family Strophariaceae. It is a coprophilic
(dung-inhabiting) mushroom that fruits on the dung of herbivores, particularly
ruminants. Its natural range spans tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

## Morphology and Identification

Psilocybe cubensis mushrooms are medium to large compared to other
psilocybin-containing species:

- **Cap (pileus):** 1.5 to 8 cm in diameter, conical to convex when young,
expanding to broadly convex with age. Color ranges from golden brown to pale
yellow. The surface is smooth, often viscid when moist.

- **Gills (lamellae):** Adnate to adnexed, close to crowded, grayish when
young, darkening to deep purplish brown or nearly black with maturity.

- **Stem (stipe):** 4 to 15 cm long, 0.5 to 1.5 cm thick, hollow, whitish
to yellowish. Bruises blue where damaged — a key diagnostic feature caused
by the enzymatic oxidation of psilocin.

- **Annulus:** A membranous ring on the stem formed from the partial veil.
Often persistent but can disappear with age.

- **Spore print:** Dark purplish brown to nearly black.

- **Odor and taste:** Mildly farinaceous (mealy) to somewhat raphanoid
(radish-like). Not particularly distinctive, underscoring the importance of
careful identification by experienced foragers.

The blue bruising reaction is one of the most important diagnostic features
shared by most psilocybin-containing mushrooms. However, some toxic species
also bruise blue, so this feature alone is insufficient for identification.

## Distribution and Ecological Habitat

Stropharia cubensis is pantropical and subtropical in distribution. It occurs
naturally throughout [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], South America, Southeast Asia, Australia,
the Pacific Islands, and sub-Saharan Africa.

The species is strongly associated with the dung of herbivorous mammals,
particularly cattle (Bos indicus and Bos taurus). Its [[four-groups-neurotropic-fungi-global-distribution-guzman-1998]] has
been suggested to follow the spread of [[comparison-cattle-husbandry-vs-goat-husbandry]] by humans over
millennia.

At the archaeological site of Non Nak Tha in Thailand, dated to approximately
15,000 years before present, bones of zebu cattle have been found coincident
with human graves. P. cubensis remains common in the area today. McKenna
argued this demonstrates that mushroom use was a human trait that emerged
wherever human populations and cattle co-evolved.

## Chemical Constituents

The primary psychoactive compounds in Stropharia cubensis are:

- **Psilocybin** (4-PO-DMT): A phosphorylated tryptamine prodrug. More stable
than psilocin, it is the predominant compound in dried material. Converted to
psilocin in the body by alkaline phosphatase enzymes.

- **Psilocin** (4-HO-DMT): The pharmacologically active compound. Acts
primarily as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist, mimicking serotonin and producing
alterations in perception, mood, and cognition.

- **Baeocystin and norbaeocystin:** Additional tryptamine alkaloids present
in smaller quantities with less significant pharmacological activity.

Typical psilocybin content in dried P. cubensis ranges from 0.5% to 1.2% by
weight, depending on variety, growing conditions, and harvest timing. Immature
specimens contain significantly higher concentrations of active alkaloids
relative to their weight compared to mature specimens.

## The McKenna "Ur Plant" Hypothesis

McKenna proposed that Stropharia cubensis catalyzed the emergence of human
consciousness. His evidence included:

1. **[[psilocybe-cubensis-cattle-association-african-origin-guzman-1998]]:** The hallucinogenic plant responsible must have been
available on the African grasslands where early hominids lived.

2. **Coprophilic ecology:** Growing on cattle dung, the mushroom was directly
in the path of hominids following herds of ungulates.

3. **No preparation required:** Active in its raw state, unlike ayahuasca
which requires extraction and combination of multiple plants.

4. **Dosage-dependent effects:** Low doses enhance visual acuity; moderate
doses increase sexual arousal; high doses produce visionary experiences.

5. **Pandemic distribution:** Occurs throughout tropical regions wherever
zebu-type cattle graze, suggesting ancient co-evolution.

While controversial, the hypothesis has been influential in psychedelic culture
and continues to generate interdisciplinary discussion about psychoactive
substances in human cognitive evolution.

## Cultivation Significance

P. cubensis is the most commonly cultivated psychedelic mushroom due to fast
colonization, moderate potency, and tolerance of diverse growing conditions. It
was central to the PF TEK (1992), which used brown rice flour, vermiculite, and
half-pint canning jars to make cultivation accessible to non-specialists. The
PF TEK has been adapted worldwide and forms the basis for most amateur methods.

## Legal Status

Psilocybin and psilocin are controlled substances in many countries (Schedule I
in the US). Spores of P. cubensis do not contain these compounds and are legal
to possess in most jurisdictions, though cultivation is illegal where the
active compounds are controlled. Legal status varies significantly by country
and has changed rapidly in some jurisdictions in recent years.
