Coconut palm polyculture is a foundational design strategy for the humid tropics, detailed in Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (Chapter 10, Humid Tropics). Rather than growing coconuts in monoculture, this approach integrates them into diverse multi-layered systems that mimic natural tropical forest structure. The result is higher total yields, greater resilience, reduced pest pressure, and continuous production across multiple species and functions.

This concept connects closely with broader [[polyculture-design-hemenway]] and the specific demands of [[tropical-permaculture-guide]]. A thorough [[permaculture-site-analysis]] should precede any palm polyculture installation to assess soils, water flow, existing vegetation, and community needs.

## Structure and Zoning

Any humid tropical polyculture that imitates tropical forest structure succeeds. Mollison outlines species richness targets by zone and function within the [[permaculture-humid-tropical-garden-design]] framework:

- Village surrounds: 100-150 species

- Palms-with-crops systems: 30-35 species

- Animal forage systems: 8-20 species

- Fuelwood systems: 3-4 species

These targets reflect the carrying capacity and functional complexity appropriate to each context. Village surrounds require the greatest diversity to supply food, medicine, fibre, and materials for daily life.

## Species Selection Criteria

Mollison provides clear criteria for selecting species in palm-dominant polycultures:

- Suited to local soils and climate conditions

- Locally acceptable as food, material, or medicine

- Wide processing potential (food, fibre, fuel, feed)

- Not rampant or invasive in character

- Compatible with the palm overstorey and other intercrops

- Serve essential uses within the household or community economy

Species selection should also consider [[permaculture-pioneer-species]] for early site establishment, and [[legume-nitrogen-dynamics]] for maintaining soil fertility beneath the palm canopy.

## Varietal Selection

Coconut varieties fall into two broad categories: dwarf and tall. Each serves different design contexts.

Dwarf varieties are ideal for windy village areas where falling nuts from tall palms pose a lethal risk to people and livestock. Their compact stature also simplifies harvesting and management.

Because coconuts are typically seed-grown, natural variation occurs in every planting. Approximately 1 in 20 trees will show different characteristics from the parent. This genetic variation is an asset in polyculture design, spreading risk across phenotypes and potentially yielding productive or disease-resistant individuals.

## Clump vs Linear Planting

Mollison strongly advocates clump planting over linear rows for coconut palms. A clump consists of 10-12 coconuts arranged in a circle.

The divergent growth habit of clump-planted palms has significant advantages:

- Frees up to 60% of ground area for intercrops (vs 30% for linear planting)

- Enables easier mulching and irrigation of the central zone

- Nuts drop cleanly into the open centre for efficient collection

- Better mimics natural forest gap dynamics

Linear planting, by contrast, creates continuous shade strips that limit intercrop options and make ground management more difficult.

## Spacing Rules

The fundamental spacing rule for coconut palms is: twice the frond length plus two feet. This ensures adequate light penetration and air circulation between crowns.

Intercrop spacing beneath and between palms follows specific guidelines:

- Cacao: 2 x 3 m

- Banana: 3 x 3 m

- Cassava: 1 x 1 m

These spacings allow understorey crops to mature without competing directly with palm roots for water and nutrients.

## Patterning and Water Management

Earth-sculpturing for runoff absorption is essential in tropical palm polycultures, where intense rainfall can otherwise cause severe erosion and nutrient loss. Key strategies include:

- Cross-slope hollows that intercept and slow water flow

- Absorption swales that direct water into the root zone of palms and intercrops

These techniques align with the broader alexander shulgin and [[keyline-design]] approaches used across permaculture water management.

## Earth-Shaping for Crops

Different crops require different earth-shaping treatments within the palm polyculture:

- Ridges: suited to cassava and sweet potato

- Mounds: ideal for cucurbits (squash, melon, gourds)

- Furrows: appropriate for ginger and pineapple in dry areas

- Basins: designed for dryland taro cultivation

- Palm trunk boxes: constructed from harvested palm trunks for growing yams and vanilla

Each earth-shaping technique matches the moisture and drainage preferences of the target crop while integrating with the overall water management pattern.

## Access and Mulch Provision

Coconut palms are generous mulch producers within the polyculture system. Palm fronds and husks line-mulch approximately 1 in every 8-12 rows, providing continuous ground cover that suppresses weeds and feeds soil biology. This connects to the broader principles in [[mulch-producing-plants]].

Banana plants, commonly intercropped with palms, contribute substantially at harvest: each banana stand provides organic matter equivalent to 25 tonnes per hectare. This mass of material feeds the soil food web and supports the [[compost-building-guide]] cycle on site.

## Economics of Intercropping

Nair (1975) demonstrated significant yield improvements from intercropping within coconut systems:

- Irrigation alone increases coconut yield by 50%

- Complex intercrops of two or more species can triple total system yield

These figures underscore the economic logic of polyculture over monoculture. The additional crops not only boost total caloric and economic output but also spread risk across multiple harvest streams throughout the year.

## Species Suited to Co-Processing

Mollison identifies species combinations suited to shared processing infrastructure, which is critical for village-scale economics:

- Ethanol-oriented systems: interplant cane sugar, Agave, and beet alongside coconuts

- Oil production systems: oil palm combined with mustard and sunflower

Co-processing reduces the need for separate processing equipment and allows communities to diversify their energy and food production from a single integrated system.

## Re-Working Old Plantations

Stands of 60+ years require systematic renewal. Mollison outlines a strategy for phased replacement:

- Tap old palms for sugar production before removal

- Harvest palm hearts as a food source from felled trees

- Maintain continuous replacement at 4% per year

- Use removed palm trunks for earth-shaping, fencing, or mushroom substrate
