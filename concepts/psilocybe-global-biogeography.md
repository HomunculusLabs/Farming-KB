# Psilocybe Global Biogeography

The worldwide distribution patterns of psilocybin-containing mushrooms, analyzed from the comprehensive Guzman, Allen, and Gartz study of 216 neurotropic fungal species across all continents.

## Overview

The worldwide geographical distribution of neurotropic fungi reveals striking patterns of species concentration in the subtropical and temperate regions, with the highest diversity in the Austral hemisphere despite less mycological exploration.

Mexico has the highest number of neurotropic species with 76 species, of which 44 belong to Psilocybe (39% of the world total).

The majority of Psilocybe species are found in or near the Austral hemisphere, mainly in subtropical humid forests.

## Northern vs. Southern Hemisphere Distribution

Of 85 neurotropic species of Psilocybe analyzed by Guzman (1983), the majority occurred in the Austral hemisphere: 59 species in South America and Mexico, versus 18 in the USA and Canada, and only 9 in Europe.

This disparity is remarkable because North America and Europe are more mycologically explored than southern regions.

In the distribution map, there are more localities in the northern hemisphere than the southern, in contrast with the higher number of species in the southern hemisphere.

South America, New Guinea, eastern Australia, and New Zealand present high diversity in Psilocybe while being poorly explored compared to Europe.

## Nine Distribution Types

Following Hongo's (1978) classification of Japanese fungi, nine types of distribution can be observed among neurotropic fungi worldwide.

Cosmopolitan species: [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]], [[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]], Panaeolina foenisecii.

Northern hemisphere species: [[amanita-muscaria-ethnobotany]] (mycorrhizal), [[psilocybin-liquid-culture]], Psilocybe silvatica, Cordyceps species.

Eurasian species: Amanita muscaria.

North American and Eastern Asiatic species: ties exist between Psilocybe graveolens, P. muliercula, P. pintonii, and P. zapotecorum from the Americas, which are closely related to P. argentipes and P. subcaerulipes from Japan.

Southeastern Asiatic species: Psilocybe subaeruginascens var. subaeruginascens from Japan and Java, var. septentrionalis only from Japan.

Tropical and subtropical species: [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]], Psilocybe subcubensis, [[copelandia-genus-species-guide]] species, possibly Gerronema fibula.

Arctic and alpine species: [[psilocybe-azurescens-profile]] var. aztecorum from Mexican high mountains, closely related to [[psilocybe-subaeruginosa-australia]] from the Pacific Northwest and P. quebecensis from Quebec.

Endemic species: Conocybe siliginoides, Hypholoma naematoliformis, Psilocybe muliercula, P. chiapanensis, P. hispanica (Spain), P. serbica (Central Europe), P. portoricensis (Puerto Rico), and many others.

## Trans-American Connections

Northern species such as Psilocybe caerulipes, common in deciduous forests of eastern North America, reach northeastern Mexico (Zacualtipan, Hidalgo) through the same type of vegetation (mesophytic forest with Fagus).

Southern species such as Psilocybe yungensis and P. subyungensis, common in South America, reach Mexico through mesophytic forests with Alnus.

Mexico represents a conjugation of both northern and southern mycobiotas.

Horak observed that South American and Mesoamerican species are closely related to those of Southeastern Asia (New Guinea, Indonesia, Australia).

Psilocybe samuiensis from Thailand and [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] from New Zealand are both closely related to [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] from Mexico and Guatemala (all Section Mexicanae).

[[psilocybe-genus-classification]] from New Zealand is closely related to P. zapotecorum from Mexico and South America (both Section Zapotecorum).

## Range Extremes

Neurotropic fungi have been identified as far north as Alaska and Siberia and as far south as Chile, Australia, and New Zealand.

The altitudinal range extends from sea level to high mountain regions up to 4,000 meters elevation (Psilocybe aztecorum in Mexican high mountains).

Copelandia cyanescens grows at 3,000 meters altitude in the Hawaiian Archipelago.

Mexico City stands at 2,220 meters altitude where Copelandia cyanescens grows in disturbed zones.

## Under-Explored Regions

Gartz (1996) observed: "The mushrooms occur in abundance wherever mycologists abound."

There are no records of neurotropic fungi from southeastern Russia, Mongolia, Arabia, Turkey, or many regions of Africa.

No records of Psilocybe exist from Korea, Malaysia (except Java and Sumatra), and Hawaii.

Even fewer records exist from Alaska and Costa Rica.

In the USA, many states lack records: Arizona, Colorado, Illinois, Maryland, Vermont, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and Pennsylvania.

The interesting paper by Mueller and Halling (1995) on Agaricales biodiversity in Neotropical forests did not consider any Psilocybe species, despite their being very common in the area.

Whatever region is studied, new species are typically found.

## African Gap

Only 6-8 known neurotropic species of Psilocybe exist for the entire African continent, in contrast with its high biodiversity.

Psilocybe cubensis grows on cow dung in Kenya, but cattle in America were introduced by Spanish colonists; P. cubensis does not grow in Europe.

It is probable that this fungus was introduced to America through the slave commerce during Spanish colonial times.

The only known neurotropic Psilocybe from South Africa is [[global-psychedelic-mushroom-species-diversity]].

Psilocybe mairei is known from Northern Africa (Morocco and Algeria) and from Europe (Czechoslovakia).

## Ecological Distribution Patterns

The majority of neurotropic Psilocybe species grow in subtropical, mesophytic, cloud, or deciduous humid forests.

In Mexico, of 42 neurotropic Psilocybe species, 34 are from mesophytic forests, 4 from tropical forests, and 4 from coniferous forests.

Coprophilous species on cow dung include [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]], Psilocybe subcubensis, and [[copelandia-genus-species-guide]] species.

Temperate species include [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]], known from Europe, India, Russia, Canada, USA, Chile, Peru, New Zealand, Australia, and Tasmania, but unknown in Mexico.
