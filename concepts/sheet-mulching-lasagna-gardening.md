# Sheet Mulching and Lasagna Gardening

Sheet mulching (also called sheet composting, composting in place, or lasagna gardening) is one of the basics in the ecological gardener"s toolkit. It is a method of eradicating weeds and building soil that eliminates herbicides and avoids tilling, both of which rupture soil ecology. Sheet mulching is a variation on nature's way of building soil by accumulating and breaking down organic debris from the top down.

## How It Works

In its simplest form, sheet mulching is a two-step process:

1. Apply a layer of weed-suppressing newspaper or cardboard (or even cloth or wool carpet, provided they contain no synthetic fabric)
2. Top with about a foot of organic mulch

Ideally this is done in fall so the mulch rots to become humusy earth over winter. The weed-stopping layer breaks down enough to let spring-planted seeds and transplants thrust their roots deep into the earth.

## The Ultimate Sheet Mulch (Materials List)

From Gaia's Garden, the materials for a thorough sheet mulch on about 200 square feet:

1. **Newspaper or cardboard**: A 2-3 foot stack of newspaper (no glossy sections — standard newsprint inks are soy-based and nontoxic) or about 300 square feet of corrugated cardboard (no staples or plastic tape). Alternatives: cloth, old clothing, or wool carpet (no synthetics — but these take far longer to decay)
2. **Soil amendments**: Depending on your soil's needs — lime (for acid soil), gypsum or sulfur (for alkaline soil), rock phosphate or bonemeal (phosphorus), greensand, kelp meal, or rock dust (trace minerals)
3. **Bulk organic matter**: Straw, spoiled hay, yard waste, leaves, seaweed, finely ground bark, stable sweepings, wood shavings — roughly 4-8 cubic yards, aiming for an overall C:N ratio of 100:1 to 30:1
4. **Compost**: About 1/4 to 1/2 cubic yard (6-12 cubic feet)
5. **Manure**: 1/4 to 1 cubic yard (about 6 cubic feet of composted cattle manure)
6. **Top layer**: 2 inches of weed- and seed-free material such as straw, fine bark, or wood shavings

## Construction Steps

### Preparation (Day Before)

1. **Water the site** well unless ground is moist from rain. Organisms need water to work, and once mulch is in place it takes a lot of water to moisten bottom layers
2. **Slash vegetation**: Clip, mow, scythe, or weed-whack everything down in place. Don't pull up weeds — leave roots as worm food. Remove stumps or big woody pieces

### Amendments

3. **Add soil amendments**: Lime, rock phosphate, bonemeal, rock dust, kelp meal as needed
4. **Open compacted soil**: Push a spading fork into the ground, rock it slightly, and pull it out across the entire site. Don't turn the earth — just poke holes and crack it open for moisture and root penetration

### Nitrogen Layer

5. **Add nitrogen**: A thin layer of manure, blood or cottonseed meal, fresh grass clippings, or cast-off produce. For concentrated matter (rabbit manure, blood meal), sprinkle to just cover the soil; for bedding-rich manure, about an inch thick

### The Sheet

6. **Lay newspaper or cardboard**: Create a continuous light-blocking layer. Overlap sheets by 6 inches to prevent weeds sneaking between. Newspaper should be 1/4 to 1/2 inch thick. **Wet thoroughly** as you go — watch for wind carrying your work away. Don't walk on wet paper

### Second Nitrogen Layer

7. **Another thin nitrogen layer**: Manure, meal, or fresh greens to entice worms up through the rotting sheets

### Bulk Mulch

8. **Add 8-12 inches** of bulk mulch (straw, hay, leaves, etc.). Break bales into thin "books" 1-2 inches thick and lay about three thicknesses. Spray water every few inches — aim for damp but not wet ("wrung-out sponge"). If using high-carbon materials (straw, wood shavings), sprinkle nitrogen (manure, blood meal) or dilute with high-nitrogen mulch

### Compost Layer

9. **Add 1-2 inches of compost**: This acts as the seedbed for planting. If compost is short, substitute manure or several inches of easily compostable material (if you have a few months before planting)

### Final Layer

10. **Top with 2 inches of weed-free material**: Straw, fine bark, or wood shavings. This gives the finished look and suppresses any remaining weeds. Push this aside to reach the compost layer when planting

## Benefits Over Traditional Composting

Toby Hemenway explains why he prefers sheet mulching over traditional compost piles:

- **No soil disturbance**: Composting in place doesn't disrupt soil organisms essential for ferrying nutrients to plant roots
- **Nutrient capture**: Liquors from decomposition stay in the mulch instead of leaching out beneath a pile
- **Worm habitat**: Worms thrive directly in the garden bed, shuttling nutrients to plants rather than freeloading beneath a plantless pile
- **Complete soil ecology**: An intact subterranean ecology develops — mycelium networks, microfauna channels, worm castings — all created simply by piling on mulch

## Known Issues

- **Bindweed**: Can travel for yards beneath the paper layer — avoid introducing via contaminated hay
- **Bermuda grass**: Rhizomes can tunnel through corrugated cardboard tubes
- **Fresh hay**: Contains seeds that can introduce weeds — use straw instead, or keep seed-containing mulches covered by weed-free material
