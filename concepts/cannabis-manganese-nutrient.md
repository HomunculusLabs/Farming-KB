# Cannabis Manganese (Mn)

## Overview

Manganese is a [[cannabis-indoor-soil-mix-and-preparation]] classified as **immobile** in cannabis plants. Manganese deficiency is relatively common indoors. Along with [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]] and [[query-how-to-fix-zinc-deficiency-in-cannabis]], manganese is one of the three most common micronutrient deficiencies in cannabis, particularly in arid climates -- Spain, the Southwestern United States, Australia, etc. -- with alkaline soil and water.

## Role in Plant Biology

Manganese is engaged in the oxidation-reduction process associated with the photosynthetic electron transport. It is a critical cofactor for the water-splitting enzyme in photosystem II, meaning it is directly involved in the photosynthetic process that generates oxygen. Key functions include:

- Activates many enzymes
- Plays a fundamental part in the chloroplast membrane system
- Assists cannabis-nitrogen|nitrogen utilization
- Works with iron in chlorophyll production
- Involved in oxidation-reduction processes during photosynthesis
- Essential for the water-splitting reaction in photosynthesis
- Participates in the synthesis of fatty acids and regulation of carbohydrate metabolism

## Mobility in Soil

Manganese is **immobile** within the cannabis plant -- once deposited in tissue, it cannot be relocated to newer growth. In the soil, manganese availability is highly pH-dependent. It is most available in acidic conditions (pH 5.0-5.5) and becomes increasingly unavailable as pH rises above 6.0. This is the opposite trend of molybdenum, which becomes more available at higher pH. In well-aerated, well-drained soils, manganese is readily available, but in poorly drained, waterlogged soils, manganese can reach toxic levels because reduced conditions convert manganese to more soluble forms. A low pH in hydroponics can cause toxic manganese intake.

## Interactions with Other Nutrients

Manganese, [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]], and [[query-how-to-fix-zinc-deficiency-in-cannabis]] are the three most common micronutrients found deficient, and deficiencies of all three often occur concurrently, especially when soil or water pH is above 6.5. All three share the same initial symptom of deficiency: interveinal chlorosis of young leaves. Excess manganese causes a deficiency of iron and zinc. Excess potassium can impair manganese uptake. High levels of phosphorus also compete with manganese for absorption. Because these interactions are complex, treating the problem should always include adding a chelated dose of all three nutrients.

## Organic Sources

[[organic-fertilizer-sources-guide]] of manganese include:

- Compost (variable content depending on feedstock)
- Well-rotted manures
- Seaweed and kelp products
- Manganese sulfate (Epsom salts contain trace amounts)
- Wood ash (small amounts, but watch the pH)

For hydroponic and soilless growers, the most reliable approach is to use a complete, chelated micronutrient formula that includes manganese alongside iron and zinc.

## Deficiency

Young leaves show symptoms first because manganese is immobile. Severe deficiency can resemble a severe lack of [[cannabis-magnesium-nutrient]], making diagnosis challenging. Often, deficiencies of all three micronutrients (iron, zinc, and manganese) occur concurrently, especially when soil or water pH is above 6.5.

All three share the same initial symptom of deficiency: interveinal chlorosis of young leaves. It is often difficult to distinguish which element -- zinc, iron, or manganese -- is deficient, and all three could be deficient. This is why treating the problem should include adding a chelated dose of all three nutrients.

### Symptoms

- Young leaves become yellow between veins (interveinal chlorosis)
- Veins remain green
- Symptoms spread from younger to older leaves as deficiency progresses
- Necrotic (dead) spots develop on severely affected leaves
- Affected leaves become pale and fall off
- Overall plant growth is stunted
- Maturation may be prolonged

### Deficiency Progression Timeline

1. Interveinal chlorosis of young leaves forms
2. Interveinal chlorosis of progressively older leaves forms
3. Dead spots develop on acutely affected leaves

### Treatment

- Lower the pH to make manganese more available
- Leach the soil to remove competing elements
- Add a complete, chelated micronutrient formula containing iron, zinc, and manganese
- Because all three often share the same initial symptom, treating should include adding a chelated dose of all three nutrients

## Toxicity

Excess manganese causes chlorotic, dark orange to dark rusty-brown mottling on young and newer growth leaves. Tissue damage shows on young leaves before progressing to older leaves.

### Symptoms

- Chlorotic, dark orange to dark rusty-brown mottling on leaves
- Tissue damage on young leaves before progressing to older leaves
- Slower growth; overall vigor is lost
- Toxicity is compounded by low humidity (additional transpiration causes more manganese to be drawn into foliage)
- A low pH can cause toxic intake of manganese
- An excess of manganese causes a deficiency of iron and zinc

## Chelated Manganese

Like other micronutrients, manganese is best applied in chelated form. Chelates keep metal ions soluble in water and suppress reactions with other materials. Different chelate types are effective at different pH ranges:

- DTPA is most effective below pH 6.5
- EDDHA is effective up to pH 8.0
- EDTA is slow to cause leaf burn

Chelates decompose rapidly in low levels of ultraviolet (UV) light, including light produced by HID bulbs and sunlight. Keep chelates out of the light to protect them from rapid decomposition.

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Optimal range | 0.5-5.0 ppm |
| Average target | 2.0 ppm |

## Common Mistakes

- Treating only manganese when iron and zinc are also likely deficient (always treat all three)
- Relying on pH adjustment alone without also adding manganese to the solution
- Storing chelated micronutrient solutions in clear containers exposed to light
- Confusing manganese deficiency with magnesium deficiency (both cause interveinal chlorosis but on different leaf ages)
- Ignoring the narrow range between deficiency and toxicity -- only a few ppm separate them
- Using well water in arid climates without testing for alkalinity, which locks out manganese
