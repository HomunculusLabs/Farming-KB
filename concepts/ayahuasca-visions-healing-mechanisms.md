## Ayahuasca Visions and Healing Mechanisms

## Overview

In the Peruvian Amazon city of Iquitos, ayahuasca is used not as a curative agent in itself, but as a **diagnostic and revelatory tool** that enables healers (ayahuasqueros) to identify the magical causes of illness and neutralize the evil magic deemed responsible. The specific visual hallucinations produced by the brew are deliberately harnessed by healers within a cultural framework that transforms potentially terrifying experiences into therapeutic ones.

## Patterns of Ayahuasca Visions

### Common Vision Content

Informants consistently reported several categories of visions during ayahuasca sessions:

1. **Forest creatures**, particularly boa constrictors and viperous snakes — the most frequently reported visions
2. **The person responsible for bewitching** the patient — appearing in a panorama of activity
3. **Scenes revealing innermost thoughts and feelings** — such as sexual desire, vengeance, or hate directed toward the patient
4. **Manufacture of harmful potions** — sometimes showing a potion being prepared and later thrown across a doorstep

### The Snake as Mother Spirit

The fearsome creatures that appear — boa constrictors and viperous snakes — which in most parts of the world might be considered part of a "bad trip" phenomenon, are **cleverly utilized by healers** to assist in curing. These snakes are believed to be the **reincarnated spirit of the vine itself**. Such mental productions are believed to bring messages of healing and assurance to the patient.

The therapeutic logic operates as follows: if the patient is strong enough to withstand the fright and loss of ego control when such apparitions appear, the **mother spirit of the vine** will teach the patient her songs. During successful sessions, patients can be observed spontaneously breaking out into song, accompanying the healer or his apprentices.

### Controlling "Bad Trips"

Although the physiological effects of hallucinogenic use — ranging from euphoria to great anguish — likely have universal distribution, it is remarkable how such "bad trips" can be minimized or controlled by healing techniques when a belief system frames the experience within an ongoing magical framework. Ayahuasca use is harnessed so that culturally acknowledged frightening mental productions become **controllable by the healer and actually used by him to effect a cure**. However, bad trips do still occur — Dobkin de Rios observed cases where individuals shrieked in fright as viperous snakes and assorted demons appeared before them.

## Mechanisms of Healing

### Ayahuasca as Diagnostic Tool, Not Cure

The use of ayahuasca to heal does not include a conceptualization of the hallucinogen as a curative agent per se. Rather, the vine is seen to operate as a powerful **means to a desired end** — it gives the healer entry into the culturally important area of disease causality, enabling him to:

1. **Identify the nature of the illness** from which a person is suffering
2. **Deflect or neutralize the evil magic** deemed responsible

The drug serves mainly **diagnostic and revelatory purposes**. It is not used to gain verbal insight or to work through psychodynamic materials for long-range cures. The generalized, immobilizing anxiety present in the sociocultural milieu is changed into solid fear placed squarely on the shoulders of some evil-doer by a healer whose presentation of self includes an **omnipotent stance**.

### Patient Selection Process

A selection process takes place so that curers accept patients only if they believe they will have a good chance of success:

- **Simple illnesses** are rarely treated with the drug; herbs, plants, and store-bought medicines are prescribed instead
- **Psychotic patients** are not given ayahuasca
- Only patients suffering from certain types of illness — usually those classified as **psychosomatic** — take ayahuasca

### Complementary Healing Techniques

In addition to the use of the vine, healers practice time-honored Amazonian curing traditions:

- **Whistling and singing** incantations
- **Recitation of orations**
- **Sucking** at afflicted regions of the body
- **Blowing cigarette smoke** over the patient"s body
- **Afternoon consultations** for counseling, advising, and reassurance

## Magical Conditioning

The healer's preparatory work is crucial to therapeutic success. Before allowing some patients to take the purge, a good healer will spend up to two weeks **exorcising the "evil"** afflicting them. This is necessary because patients are drawn from mixed social realities — civilized or transitional Indian groups as well as middle-income segments who seek help only after "rational" techniques have failed. Magical beliefs among this latter segment function close to and at times in competition with rational ones.

### Preparatory Rituals

The preparatory exorcistic rituals — which may include the use of a narcotic-like tobacco (*Nicotiana tabacum*) — provide patients with a **learning experience** that permits them to come to terms with culturally held expectations prior to a session. Patients" expectations, learned either in childhood or reinforced during preliminary sessions, that they will be visited by a boa or other snake, as well as their belief in the curative prediction of success anticipated by that apparition's appearance, provide reassurance that healing is indeed occurring.

### The Healer's Omnipotence

The omnipotence of the healer — which some writers consider crucial in explaining the efficacy of magical psychotherapies — is increased by his **symbolic presentations**:

- Insistence upon the magical world of spirits that he controls
- Conjuring spirits through particular songs and whistling incantations
- Advising patients during visions that the next song will cause specific outcomes or that a difficult moment will pass

The healer serves as a **creative source** who interprets the symbols that appear to patients, seeing in these productions his own symbols which he attributes to magical causality of misfortune or disease.

## Doctrinal Compliance

Ehrenwald (1966), tracing continuity between present-day scientific therapy and primitive healing, coined the phrase **"doctrinal compliance"** to describe the phenomenon whereby a patient ends up doing what their doctor wants regardless of the particular school of allegiance. If a therapist is Freudian, the patient's dreams tend to re-create early memories of childhood or family conflict. Unlike suggestion, which operates on a conscious level, doctrinal compliance is an **unconscious process** occurring in both magical and modern therapy procedures.

This concept is highly pertinent to the ayahuasca healing situation, especially regarding the exorcising of the patient that marks a portion of contemporary healing in Iquitos.

## Placebo Question

Can ayahuasca operate as a placebo? Is faith in the curative powers of the drug enough to heal? Dobkin de Rios argues that this possibility must be dismissed. Ayahuasca is not used to gain verbal insight or work through psychodynamic materials. Rather, the drug is used to **identify the cause of magical illness** — it serves a specific diagnostic function within the cultural framework of Amazonian folk medicine.
