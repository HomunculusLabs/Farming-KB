# Unheated Greenhouse Design and Construction

## The Cold House Concept

Coleman distinguishes between "cold houses" (completely unheated) and "hothouses" (heated). Traditional heated greenhouses maintained 65°F (18°C) for tomatoes or 55°F (13°C) for lettuces. Unheated greenhouses were considered unusable in cold winter climates except for storing hardy potted plants. Coleman's work proves otherwise.

The cold houses are completely passive — no heating system, no water or stone ballast with pumps or fans as a heat-storage medium, no buried insulation around the perimeter. The soil itself serves as the heat-storage medium, as it does in the natural world.

## Structure Specifications

### Primary Houses: 30 × 96 feet
- Standard plastic-covered, gothic-style hoop houses
- Aligned on east-west axis
- Single-layer covering of UV-resistant plastic
- Heated greenhouses use two layers (air-inflated) to minimize heat loss, but cold houses generally need only one layer to maximize light input

### Newer Houses: 22 × 48 feet
- Shorter length dictated by end-wall ventilation needs
- Summer ventilation through end walls is adequate when tunnel length is no more than 3x its width
- Lighter construction with diagonal braces at each corner
- Crossbars on each hoop prevent splaying during moves and provide overhead structure for trellising
- Designed for easier mobility

## Greenhouse Covering

### Single vs. Double Layer Plastic
- Single layer maximizes light input (two layers with air inflation cut an additional 10% of ambient light)
- Air-inflated double layer averages 4°F warmer at night but reduces light
- Coleman double-covers houses only for sowings between December 15 and February 15, when new seedlings need extra warmth
- Houses protecting already-mature crops for midwinter harvest continue with single-layer covering

### Plastic Selection
- Anti-drip coating causes condensed moisture to form a thin film rather than droplets
- Coated covers transmit more light; the moisture film reflects back heat radiating from soil at night
- Cold-climate growers favor covers maximizing daytime heat gain and light levels
- Southern growers may prefer plastics that block infrared to prevent overheating

## Mobility Systems

Multiple approaches have been tried:

1. **Greased wooden skids on wooden rails** — earliest design
2. **Ball casters** — rails on bottom of structure rolling on support posts
3. **Ground-level sled runners** — sliding directly along the ground like a sled
4. **Flanged wheels** — on bottom of each hoop rolling along ground pipes
5. **Pipe skids** — 2-by-3-inch galvanized pipe (current preferred method)

### Pipe Skid System (Preferred)
- Off-the-shelf galvanized pipe support columns adapted as skids
- Hoop-holding base plates bolted to skids
- Three 16-foot pipe lengths connected with milled plastic decking inserts
- Temporary steel ski tips bolted to front of skids for sliding
- 48-foot houses can be moved with a small farm tractor
- 30-foot houses on cedar skids may require a wrecker truck due to higher friction

## Site Preparation

- Greenhouses slide between two alternate sites
- Each site gets uncovered for one year out of every two
- Sites must be level and clear enough for smooth sliding
- Ground anchors secure houses once positioned

## End-Wall Design and Ventilation

The 22 x 48 foot houses use shorter lengths dictated by end-wall ventilation needs. Summer ventilation through end walls is adequate when tunnel length is no more than 3 times its width. This proportion ensures sufficient airflow during warm months when the houses are protecting summer crops before the seasonal move.

## Structural Details: Gothic-Style Hoops

Gothic-style (pointed-arch) hoop design is preferred over Quonset (half-circle) profiles because the gothic shape sheds snow and rain more effectively. In climates with significant snowfall, this is critical — snow loads can collapse flatter structures. The pointed arch also provides greater headroom at the sides of the tunnel, making it easier to work near the edges of the growing beds.

Corner diagonal bracing on the shorter houses provides lateral stability during moves and under wind loads. Crossbars on each hoop serve a dual purpose: preventing splaying during moves and providing overhead structure for trellising tomatoes, cucumbers, and other vining crops during the summer growing season.

## Plastic Replacement and Maintenance

Greenhouse plastic has a finite lifespan, typically 3 to 4 years depending on UV exposure and local conditions. Anti-drip coatings can degrade over time, losing their effectiveness. Coleman recommends regular inspection for tears, UV degradation, and loss of the anti-drip properties. The timing of plastic replacement should be coordinated with the greenhouse movement cycle — replacing the plastic when the house is in one position so the newly covered house can be moved to the alternate site.

## Greenhouse Orientation and Sunlight

Houses are aligned on an east-west axis. This orientation maximizes light capture during the low-angle winter sun, when the sun tracks low across the southern sky (in the Northern Hemisphere). An east-west orientation allows sunlight to penetrate along the full length of the house rather than being blocked by the hoops of an adjacent structure. During summer, the orientation is less critical since the sun is high overhead.

The importance of maximum light input cannot be overstated for winter production. Every percentage point of light transmission matters when days are short. This is why Coleman prefers single-layer covering for most of the winter — two layers of plastic, even with air inflation, cost an additional 10% of ambient light.

## Temperature Performance

In Zone 5 Maine winters, outdoor temperatures can drop to -20°F (-29°C). Despite this, the cold-house system works because of the two-layer protection and the thermal mass of the soil itself. The soil acts as a heat-storage medium, absorbing warmth during sunny days and slowly releasing it at night. This passive system requires no fans, pumps, or supplementary heat storage.

The key insight is that cold-hardy vegetables do not need warm temperatures — they need protection from wind and desiccation. The double-layer system provides a stable environment that dramatically reduces the rate of heat loss from the soil while maintaining humidity around the plants.

## Integration with the Winter Harvest System

The greenhouse design is inseparable from the winter-harvest philosophy. The structures are sized, oriented, and covered specifically to support the three-component system: cold-hardy vegetables, succession planting, and protected cultivation. The mobility option further enhances the system by allowing soil recovery, green manure cropping, and pest/disease prevention on a two-year cycle.

The minimal technology approach extends to all aspects of the design: no automated ventilation, no heating systems, no complicated heat-storage apparatus. The goal is the lowest-tech, most economical path to year-round vegetable production.
