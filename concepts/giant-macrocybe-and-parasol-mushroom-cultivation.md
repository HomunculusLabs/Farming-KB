## Giant Macrocybe and Parasol Mushroom Cultivation

## Overview

This concept covers two remarkable genera of large-gilled mushrooms with unique cultivation requirements: the giant macrocybe (*[[macrocybe-titans]]* and *Calocybe indica*) and the parasol mushroom (*[[macrolepiota-procera]]* and *[[lepiota-americana]]*). Both groups are among the largest gilled mushrooms in the world, share a preference for complex agricultural substrates, and require microbial casing soils for fruiting — a distinguishing feature from many other cultivated species.

## Giant Macrocybe (*Macrocybe titans* and *Calocybe indica*)

### Description and Ecology

One of the largest gilled mushrooms in the world, *Macrocybe titans* is found in subtropical to tropical regions of the Caribbean, Central America, Mexico, and occasionally the Gulf States. Its architecture is impressive: a thick, leathery trunk of a stem supporting a massive cap that can reach **4 feet across**. The fruitbody can persist aboveground for up to **two months** — an extraordinarily long lifespan for a gilled mushroom.

*Calocybe indica* (giant milky) is a related species cultivated primarily in India and Indonesia. While more established in cultivation, the giant macrocybe remains something of a mystery, with uncertain edibility and cultivation parameters still being explored.

An intriguing observation: wild specimens have been found with a powdery fungus (similar in appearance to blue cheese) colonizing the stem base, suggesting an interspecies symbiotic relationship may be required for fruiting. The fruitbodies are also notably insect-free, with no evidence of the larval infestations typical of most gilled mushrooms. This insect resistance, combined with incredible durability, suggests unique defensive compounds worthy of further investigation.

### Mycelium and Spawn

The mycelium is white, linear, and clean, not exuding metabolites unless contamination is present. Its smell is distinctly **like cherries**. Commercial spawn is available as grain spawn or [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] spawn, with grain spawn producing faster fruiting and larger yields.

### Fruiting Body Development

A stemmed mushroom with cap centered on the stem and attached gills. The spore print is white. The stem is extremely tough on the outside with a thick rind (presumably to support the massive cap weight) but soft in the center. The stem surface is rough, covered with granules or a powdery layer of speckled molds. The cap is wavy along the margin when mature.

### Cultivation Conditions

- **Temperature preference:** Above 80°F (27°C) — these are heat-loving tropical species
- **Difficulty:** Outdoor cultivation level 2; Indoor cultivation level 3

### Laboratory Techniques

The mushroom is easily cloned from the soft, cottony interior tissue of the stem. Score the thick outer rind and snap the stem in half to reveal sterile interior tissue. The extreme upper and lower portions of the stem clone best, as the middle can be fibrous and hollow.

A promising **liquid culture storage method** has been developed: blend agar cultures with sterile water, transfer to storage tubes, and keep at room temperature as oxygen-deprived slurries. The liquid mycelium enters a "hibernation state" and shows surprisingly excellent recovery compared to dehydrated agar or refrigerated grain cultures. This technique may have broader applications for other tropical mushroom species.

### Substrates and Casing

Giant macrocybes prefer **composted mixtures** of grasses, cereal straws, wood mulch, spent coffee grounds, and regional agricultural by-products (bean hulls, cornstalks, cotton waste, beet pulp, sugarcane bagasse). They enjoy complex growing media, so providing a variety of materials is important.

A **microbial casing soil is required** — when the substrate surface is a continuous white mat, dress beds with 2–3 inches of hydrated microbial casing soil to stimulate primordia formation.

### Cultivation Methods

**Outdoor and indoor methods are similar:**
1. Shred agricultural by-products into a fluffy consistency
2. Pasteurize in a hot water bath for 1–2 hours
3. Drain, cool, and mix in spawn
4. Fill beds or containers 8–10 inches deep
5. Once colonized, apply 2–3 inches of microbial casing soil
6. Water generously and maintain high humidity until primordia form

### Yields and Storage

- **Inoculation to fruiting:** 3–5 weeks (indoor), depending on temperature and spawn type
- **Expected yields:** Average — two rapid flushes several weeks apart
- **Storage:** Remarkably resistant to decay, lasting **4–6 weeks fresh** at 38–42°F (3–6°C)
- **Interior texture:** Soft as marshmallow, while the cap cuticle and stem rind are extremely tough

## Parasol Mushroom (*Macrolepiota procera*)

### Description and Ecology

The parasol is one of the world's most recognizable mushrooms and a prized edible in Europe and Russia. The tallest and most regal of the gilled mushrooms, it is widespread [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]] at lower elevations. An **interface species**, it prefers to grow where different environments meet — such as at the edges of grassy fields, near tree drip lines, or at meadow-woodland borders.

Parasol mycelium has superior speed, allowing it to colonize early-phase composting biomass when it is still green and just beginning to lower its antifungal defenses. The mycelium races through this premature substrate well ahead of competitors using specialized "infantry cells" to maximize territory.

An interesting ecological note: leaf-cutter and other ant species have been cultivating related mushrooms within the *Macrolepiota* genus for millions of years, suggesting a deep evolutionary relationship between these mushrooms and insect cultivators.

### Mycelium and Spawn

The mycelium is white and linear and almost never exudes metabolites in culture. A notable feature of southeastern US strains is the consistent formation of **sclerotia-like mass formations** at about four weeks of incubation. When transferred to supplemented sawdust, the number of sclerotia decreases but their size magnifies, usually forming a single massive node that puts pressure on the growing container — even under cold storage. This phenomenon does not appear in European stock.

### Fruiting Body Development

Parasols have an extremely large stem-to-cap ratio, making them very tall. The cap is light brown and darker in the middle with a slightly raised umbo (nipple). Young fruitbodies are smooth with streaks of brown fibrils and minute scales; mature specimens become more scaly. A distinctive **double-edged annulus (ring)** near the upper third of the stem can be easily slid up and down. The spore print is creamy white.

**Critical identification warning:** The poisonous green-spored parasol (*[[chlorophyllum-molybdites]]*) can look similar but has a mostly white cap, larger scales, and a distinctive **green spore print** that makes confusion unlikely with careful identification.

### Fruiting Conditions

Parasols are truly "wild" mushrooms that prefer tall, grassy interface environments. Once primordia form, the mushrooms are remarkably resilient in drier conditions and can mature into large fruitbodies even after several days without rain or watering.

### Cultivation Methods

**Outdoor cultivation:**
1. Shred agricultural by-products and soak overnight, then drain
2. Prepare beds by digging holes or trenches in gardens or near tree lines
3. Fill beds 8–10 inches deep
4. Poke narrow holes in a diamond pattern every 6–8 inches across the surface and fill with spawn
5. Cover spawned holes with wet compost to retain moisture and deter insects
6. Cover with perforated plastic or drilled plywood for moisture retention with gas exchange
7. When colonization is complete, dress beds with 2–3 inches of microbial casing soil
8. Cover casing with annual grass seed to provide a grassy microclimate

**Ant-assisted method:** Scatter sawdust or grass seed spawn in fall or spring. Ants grab particles and transfer them underground, threading through the subterranean environment. Expect fruitings in late summer or fall.

### Yields and Storage

- **Inoculation to fruiting:** 4–8 months (outdoor); 6–8 weeks (indoor)
- **Expected yields:** Average to poor outdoors; average indoors — not known to produce commercial quantities
- **Storage:** 2–3 weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C); caps dry well, stems should be powdered

## Medicinal Properties

### Giant Macrocybe
The mycelium is **highly antibiotic**, exhibiting activity against many strains of *E. coli* and *Staphylococcus*. The natural insecticidal nature suggests potential for mycelial extracts as natural foliar sprays to reduce pest pressure in fruiting rooms (untested).

### Parasol
Protein content ranges from **12–22% (dry weight)**. Studies show antiviral, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory compounds, plus suppression of many cancer cell lines.

## Mycoremediation Applications

- **Giant macrocybe:** Recommended for mycofiltration modules given its antibiotic action against waterborne pathogens
- **Parasol:** With affinity for mulch and compost, useful for filtering contaminated compost sediment such as livestock waste leachate, reducing coliform counts through antibacterial properties
