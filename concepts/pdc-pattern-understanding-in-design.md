## Pattern Understanding in Design

As Permaculture Principle #4 says, [[holmgren-observe-and-interact-detailed]] -- both parts are necessary to properly read patterns. Observation allows you to see the pattern, and interaction allows you to understand how the pattern changes in response to external stimulus. If you do X to the existing patterns, what new patterns emerge on the site? What happens to the energy of the old patterns?

Observation involves much more than just the eyes. You observe with all of your senses and all of your mind. How does it smell, how does it sound, what does it remind you of. Close your eyes and just listen to the site, take long slow inhales and learn what the site smells like.

But be careful -- observation is not the same as interpretation. This grass is brownish yellow -- that is an observation. It is dying -- that is an interpretation. Just observe and interact and observe the interactions. Observe under all different circumstances: at night, at dawn, when it is sunny, when it is rainy, when it is windy.

## Creating a Pattern Language

A designer not only learns to read patterns, a designer learns to create a pattern language to aid in the design process. By creating a pattern language a designer can make simple, small patterns that when combined can create a larger more complex design. The pattern language of a Permaculture Design is created in response to the Permaculture analysis.

A pattern language becomes like building legos -- taking simple repeating patterns and connecting them together to create larger intricate structures. [[savory-community-organization-and-shared-grazing]]. These invisible structures often have more influence on whether a design succeeds than the physical elements themselves.

Placing a physical structure in a design will create an invisible structure around it. The designer must be able to identify the micro-climates generated by physical structures and create a design that integrates those micro-climates into an ecologically harmonious whole system. Designing invisible structures entails understanding not only the patterns that govern nature such as seasons, behavior of animals, and micro-climates but also the types of patterns that govern human behavior.

The Permaculture Designer who can read both visible and invisible patterns -- and design interventions that harmonize with both -- will produce the most resilient and productive systems.

## The Permaculture Analysis

A pattern language is created in response to the Permaculture analysis. The analysis involves understanding the historical patterns of the site over time, researching the site's story to gain an understanding of its patterns. Design interventions will alter the patterns of a site -- the designer must understand how to create new patterns which maintain ecological harmony.

It is inevitable that design interventions -- that is, a new element added to an existing site -- will alter the patterns of a site. By creating a pattern language for your design it becomes much easier for the designer to create complex systems. Of course the elements of this new pattern language must themselves mesh into the existing patterns of the site.

When a designer does not understand the patterns on their site, waste will almost always be the result. Conversely, when patterns are well understood and the design language is carefully crafted, the resulting systems tend toward self-regulation, efficiency, and ecological harmony -- hallmarks of good Permaculture Design.

Patterns also scale across different dimensions of design. The same branching pattern seen in a tree can be observed in river deltas, blood vessels, and even the organization of human transportation networks. Recognizing these cross-scale pattern relationships allows a designer to apply insights from one domain to another.

## Common Natural Patterns in Design

Permaculture designers draw on a vocabulary of patterns found repeatedly in nature, each
serving specific functional purposes. Understanding these patterns and when to apply them
is core to the design process:
- **Branching patterns**: Trees, rivers, blood vessels, and lightning all exhibit branching
(dendritic) patterns that optimize distribution — of water, nutrients, energy, or information
— across a surface or volume. In design, branching patterns are applied to road networks,
irrigation systems, and the layout of paths through a property. They minimize the distance
from any point to the main channel while using the least total material.
- **Spiral patterns**: Spirals appear in shells, galaxies, weather systems, and plant growth
(phyllotaxis). The herb spiral is the classic permaculture application, condensing multiple
microclimates into a small vertical space. Spiral patterns in water management can slow flow
velocity and encourage sediment deposition.
