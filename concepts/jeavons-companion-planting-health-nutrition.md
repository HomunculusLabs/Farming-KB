# Companion Planting: Health and Nutrition Relationships

Companion planting is the constructive use of plant relationships by
gardeners, horticulturists, and farmers. Beyond the basic concept of
placing plants together, Jeavons explores the specific categories of
health, nutrition, physical complementarity, and weed, insect, and
animal relationships. This page covers the health and nutrition
aspects in detail.

## The Nature of Companion Planting

A scientific definition: "The placing together of plants having
complementary physical demands." A more complete living description:
"the growing together of all those elements and beings that encourage
life and growth: the creation of a microcosm that includes vegetables,
fruits, trees, bushes, wheat, flowers, weeds, birds, soil,
microorganisms, water, nutrients, insects, toads, spiders, and
chickens."

This field remains largely experimental. The age of the plants, the
percentage of each type grown, and their relative proximity can all
be critical. Causes of beneficial relationships may include root
excretions, plant aroma, or the pollen of composite flowers that
attracts certain [[jeavons-beneficial-herbs-nutrition]]. Use companion planting with caution and careful
observation.

## Health: Better Growth Companions

### Synergistic Pairs

- **Green beans and strawberries** — thrive better together than
  when grown separately
- **Bibb lettuce and spinach** — plant one spinach for every four
  Bibb lettuce plants for improved results

### Antagonistic Pairs

- **Onions, garlic, chives, and shallots** seriously inhibit the
  growth of peas and beans
- **Pole beans and beets** do not get along well, though bush beans
  and beets may be grown together with no particular advantage or
  disadvantage

The difference between bush and pole bean behavior is striking — no
one knows the scientific reason yet for why pole beans antagonize
beets while bush beans are neutral. The nuances of these
relationships demonstrate how much remains to be discovered.

## Crystallization Testing Method

Ehrenfreid Pfeiffer developed a crystallization method for predicting
plant compatibility. Part of a plant is ground and mixed with a
chemical solution; after drying, a crystalline pattern remains. When
two plant solutions are mixed, the patterns may:

- **Both improve** — reciprocal beneficial influence
- **Both deteriorate** — reciprocal negative reaction
- **One improves, one deteriorates** — one-sided advantage
- **Both remain the same** — no particular companion effect

Remarkably, two plants that show a negative reaction in a 1:1 ratio
may show a positive increase in strength at a 1:10 ratio. This
demonstrates that proportion is critical in companion planting.

## All-Around Beneficial Plants

Certain herbs and one tree have a beneficial influence on the entire
plant community. These are traditionally planted along one end of the
bed as perennials so they need not be replanted:

### Lemon Balm
Creates a beneficial atmosphere and attracts bees. Part of the mint
family, it contributes to a thriving ecosystem around it.

### Marjoram and Oregano
Both have a documented "beneficial effect on surrounding plants."

### Stinging Nettle (Urtica dioica)
One of the most valuable companion plants. It helps neighboring
plants grow more resistant to spoiling, increases essential oil
content in many herbs, stimulates humus formation, helps stimulate
[[jeavons-humus-and-soil-microbiology]],
and as a tea promotes plant growth and strength. It concentrates
sulfur, potassium, calcium, and iron in its body.

### Valerian (Valeriana officinalis)
"Helps most vegetables." Stimulates phosphorus activity in its
vicinity and encourages health and disease resistance in plants.

### Chamomile (Chamaemelum nobile)
A lime specialist containing a growth hormone that stimulates yeast.
In a 1:100 ratio it helps wheat growth. As a tea it combats damping
off in young plants. Concentrates calcium, sulfur, and potash.

### Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)
Increases the aromatic quality of all herbs. In small amounts it
helps most vegetables and concentrates potash in its body.

### Oak Tree
Concentrates calcium in its bark (bark ash is 77% calcium). Oak bark
tea helps plants resist harmful diseases. The area under oak branches
creates excellent soil, making it ideal for [[jeavons-composting-methods]] placement (at least 6 feet from the trunk to avoid disease).

## Nutrition: Companion Planting Over Time

Companion planting "over time" is the traditional practice known as
[[jeavons-crop-rotation-detail]]. The sequence follows:
heavy feeders first, then heavy givers (nitrogen fixers), then light
feeders. This agricultural recycling returns as much to the soil as
was taken out. See the dedicated crop rotation page for details on
heavy feeders, heavy givers, and light feeders.

## Soil Life and Improvement

### Sow Thistle (Sonchus oleraceus)
Brings nutrients from the subsoil to enrich depleted topsoil. After
years of decomposition, heavier-feeding grasses return — part of
nature's recycling program that returns leached-out nutrients.

### Root Systems as Underground Composting
A single rye plant in good soil produces an average of 3 miles of
roots per day — 387 miles of roots and 6,603 miles of root hairs
per season. Plants put 800 to 1,500 pounds of roots per acre into
the soil annually, and red clover contributes 1,200 to 3,850 pounds.
This continuous underground composting is essential to sustainable
[[jeavons-soil-fertility]].

## Spacing for Better Companions

Using GROW BIOINTENSIVE [[jeavons-biointensive-spacing]]
with plant leaves barely touching allows good companions to be even
better friends. The living mulch effect created by this spacing
enhances the beneficial interactions between companion plants.
