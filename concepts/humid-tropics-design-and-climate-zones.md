# Humid Tropics Design and Climate Zones

The humid tropics encompass a diverse range of climatic conditions that demand specific design approaches for sustainable land use. Understanding the distinctions between wet tropics, wet-dry tropics, and monsoon tropics is essential for selecting appropriate strategies, species, and earthworks. This page covers the climatic classification of tropical zones and the fundamental design principles that apply to each.

## Introduction to Tropical Permaculture Design

The tropical climatic zones discussed here specifically exclude arid tropics (covered separately). Plants and techniques do not split neatly into climatic areas—any given site may require strategies from multiple chapters. A subtropical site, for instance, can experience severe frosts, cold winds, torrential summer rains, and 7–9 months of drought, demanding strategies suited to temperate, arid, and tropical humid regimes alike.

Special topics of the humid tropics include soils, mulch sources, planning for polycultures, and appropriate house construction. In the wet tropics, heat and high rainfall leach most mobile nutrients from soils, so the biomass of diverse plants—which contains 80–90% of available nutrients—becomes the primary nutrient reservoir. Humus creation must therefore be given considerable emphasis as a prerequisite to sustainability.

## Inappropriate vs. Appropriate Strategies

Inappropriate strategies for the humid tropics include bare-soil cultivation and intensive clearing and burning in short cycles (less than 8 years) for cropping. These approaches have proved disastrous for decades and represent the transfer of temperate-zone techniques to fragile tropical ecosystems.

Appropriate strategies involve complex, multi-storied plant systems designed to yield basic staples, create mulch, and preserve soil nutrients. Complex perennial fodder and food systems are known to be stable, though they have not yet been incorporated into officially funded agricultural programs from institutions such as the World Bank.

James Fox, in *Harvest of the Palm* (1977), analyzed the social changes and loss of self-reliance following the abandonment of ancient, balanced palm polycultures in Indonesia. Ancient tropical civilizations were noted for their stability, indicating that sustainable land use patterns are an essential prerequisite for social harmony.

## Health Realities of the Tropics

Romantic literature about the "easy" tropical life omits critical realities: skin cancers, rodent ulcers, dengue fever, filaria, malaria, chronic bowel and skin disease, and the constant battle with rampant growth experienced daily at latitudes 0–25°. Pythons, ticks, termites, relentless rain, mould, and heat exhaustion-induced lethargy are everyday challenges. With increasing atmospheric ozone loss, fair-skinned people face elevated skin cancer risk from tropical sun exposure.

Humid heat induces lethargy compounded by chronic illness in many populations. Water-borne and mosquito-transmitted diseases are nearly impossible to control completely given the aerial water reservoirs created by palms and bromeliads. Induced cross-ventilation and careful construction for mosquito control are housing essentials, as are plant systems based on tree-species polyculture, which combine effectively to reduce climatic extremes.

## Wet Tropics

The wet tropics are the river basins and wet coasts from latitudes 0–25°. Major localities include the Amazon and Congo basins, Central America, Sri Lanka, Malaya, Borneo coasts, and New Guinea. This climate covers about 10% of Earth's surface (5% of human population). It is heavily occupied only where terraced alkaline volcanic soils enable sustained cultivation (Java). Remnant hunter-gatherer tribes (often pygmoid) persist in remaining forests, but the region is rapidly being degraded by over-exploitation of forests, mining, and extensive cattle rearing by large corporations. Catastrophic wildfires now develop in logged areas such as Borneo, and soils are leached to low fertility or eroded to ferricrete or silcrete subsoils.

### Climate Characteristics

- Temperature fluctuates little at about 21–32°C (70–90°F)
- Humidity is constantly high; frost is unknown
- Rainfall ranges from 152–328 cm (60–129 inches)
- Rain occurs most days with frequent thunderstorms (75–150/year), usually towards evening or late afternoon

### Landscape Features

Perennial streams, deeply weathered and rotten rock over bedrock (regolith), and rounded hills characterize the landscape. Rapid water runoff and evaporation predominate, with swamps confined to coasts and lowlands. Rivers usually have floodplains, extensive alluvial plains, and deltaic deposits. Inland access is often via rivers and tributaries.

### Vegetation and Ecology

Vegetation is luxurious, best developed as broadleaf rainforest with lianas and epiphytes, very much mixed in species composition—up to 800 tree species per square kilometre. The shaded forest floor contains little growth and receives subdued light. Mangroves are extensive on coastal and estuarine sites. Growth is rapid, uninterrupted, and continuous. Insects and birds are plentiful and varied; most fauna is nocturnal and arboreal, with abundant fish and aquatic species. Ground grazers are rare and large herd species do not occur.

About 85% of nutrients are held in plants or animals, so the soils themselves are infertile, especially when clear-cultivated, tending to erode and leach to insoluble oxides of iron and aluminum.

### Staple Foods and Crops

Staple cultivated foods include plantain, banana, cassava, yams, coconut, corn, taro, paddy rice, ducks, pigs, poultry, and fish. Trade and plantation crops include spices, copra, palm oil, cacao, rubber, banana, manilla hemp, rare hardwoods, balsa, tropical nuts, chicle, and drug plants.

### Design Essentials

- Hygienic faeces disposal
- Clean water sources
- Integrated and benign insect control techniques
- Gradual replacement of ground crops by trees
- Preservation of natural stands of trees
- Development of river versus road traffic
- Evolution of natural products
- No-dig (mulch) techniques on root crops
- Domestic foragers for snail and insect pests
- Appropriate medicinal plants

## Wet-Dry Tropics

The wet-dry tropics adjoin the wet tropics but are poleward of them, taking up about 15% of Earth's surface from latitudes 0–25°, unbalanced in favor of the southern hemisphere. The Campos, Llanos, and Gran Chaco areas of South America, parts of Central America, areas encircling the Congo basin, and many central Pacific islands (including Hawaii) are all wet-dry tropical areas, most now developed to grazing.

### Climate Characteristics

Winter (the low-sun period) is the dry time, with clear skies and intense sunlight driving day temperatures to 38°C (100°F) or more. Humidity is low, and strong desiccating winds may blow. Summer (the high-sun period) resembles the wet tropics, but episodic flooding is more common. No frosts occur. Temperatures range 21–27°C (70–80°F) in the wet season and 32–38°C (90–100°F) in the dry season. Rainfall is 25–152 cm (10–60 inches), decreasing towards desert margins. Windward inland slopes and coastal mountains may receive excessive rain (up to 1016 cm or 400 inches), but rain is erratic and least predictable towards desert margins.

### Landscape and Ecology

The landscape features intermittent streams, wadis, flood plains, karst (limestone) areas with sinkholes and cenotes, and absence of surface water in some areas (Yucatan, Mexico). Hills are rounded, but gully erosion develops rapidly on slopes. These regions contain vast savannah grasslands with thorn-bush and flat-topped Acacia trees (Africa), evolving to steppe grassland on plateaus with baobabs and dry-deciduous trees. African areas contain enormous numbers of herd species (zebra, gnu, antelope) and large carnivores.

### Design Essentials

- Small domestic water storage and reticulation
- Hedgerows against winds
- In-crop tree legumes such as Acacia albida
- Improved stock varieties and stock management
- Natural herding systems for local herd species
- Mulch use of grasses
- Increased tree crops of high forage value
- Decreased fire frequency
- Tree stands for fuel and structural timber
- No-tillage (cut and mulch) grain techniques
- Low bunds for water retention
- Chisel plow and sod-seeding techniques
- Greater reliance on in-village tree crops near wells and ponds
- Reclamation of eroded lands using pioneer species
- Keyline techniques of flood control
- Soakage pits and impoundment of runoff by low bunds or swales across slopes

## Monsoon Tropics

The monsoon tropics are a sub-type of wet-dry tropics influenced by nearby continental land masses and oceanic onshore winds. They are confined to the Indo-Thailand region, northern Australia, East Java, Timor, southern New Guinea, and extend from latitude 0° to 35° north in India. Despite covering only about 8–10% of the world's land surface, monsoon areas contain large human populations.

Late summer heating of continents causes onshore sea winds and heavy rains. The dry (winter) season reverses winds from cool interiors to coasts, giving a cool period not experienced in the wet-dry tropics (temperatures 13–21°C / 55–70°F). Temperatures rise with dry hot winds developing to over 38°C (100°F) in spring, increasing until monsoon onset. About 60% of rain falls in summer, but it is erratic and varies from 102–1016 cm (40–400 inches) depending on topography and distance from coast. Floods and droughts are equally unpredictable but common. Most activities are determined by monsoon rain—transport, fishing, and farming.

Tropical forests once clothed the hill slopes and river plains, with grasslands extending towards deserts as savannah. Population pressure, deforestation, and marginal agriculture have devastated this ecology in India. Dry-deciduous broadleaves are common; teak and bamboo were once extensive. Tree canopy is less dense than in wet tropics, allowing dense understory development. Mangroves occupy river mouths and low coasts.

Soils are lateritic, often very hard in the dry season, and of low nutrient status. Some are cracking clays. Housing is often mud-pole structures with thatch, steep roofs, wide eaves, and good drainage.

## The Forest as Model

We can largely emulate tropical forests in garden systems by establishing a dominant series of legumes, palms, and useful trees with a complex understory and ground layer of useful herbaceous and leguminous food and fodder plants. Vines and epiphytes can further complex the system as it evolves. In the wet-dry tropics, more open palm polycultures are appropriate, where the excesses of heat, light, and rain are best modified by an open canopy of palm fronds and the fern-like leaves of tree legumes.

The key insight is that the forest itself is the model for sustainable tropical agriculture—a self-maintaining, multi-storied system that recycles nutrients, moderates climate, suppresses weeds, and produces diverse yields without external inputs.
