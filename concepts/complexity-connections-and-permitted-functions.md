# Complexity, Connections, and Permitted Functions

Understanding how elements in a system connect to one another — and the qualitative difference between beneficial and destructive complexity — is central to [[permaculture-design]]. This concept addresses how many elements are needed, how they should be connected, and the critical distinction between functions that are **permitted** by an element's nature and those that are **forced** upon it.

## Complexity Arises from Connections

The number of elements in a system certainly affects its potential complexity, if complexity is taken to be the number of functional connections between elements. As Waddington (1977) points out, in the case of a single interaction between elements, complexity goes up roughly as the square of the number of elements: "Two's company, three's a crowd"... and five or six is getting to be a shambles.

### The Chicken Example

Consider the potential connections to and from a single element such as a chicken. These potential connections depend on the information we have about the chicken, so that the complexity of a system depends on the information we have about its components — always providing that such information is used in design.

Since we cannot know everything, or even know more than the approximate categories and quantity of things eaten by chickens, in [[permaculture]] we always suppose that the chicken is busy making connections itself, about which we could not know and for which we could not design. **We must simply trust the chicken.**

Thus, in commonsense, we can design for what we believe to be essentials, and let the chicken attend to all the details, checking at later stages to see that yields are satisfactory, the chickens healthy and happy, and the system holding up fairly well.

## Simple Plant Associations, Complex Animal Communities

Very simple plant associations may support very productive and complex animal associations. Mangrove swamps illustrate this principle: a single stand of one mangrove species is not itself so diverse, but the mobile species working at different stages of [[decomposition]] of the mangrove leaf create extraordinary complexity. Each species in turn feeds others.

Mobile species are capable of occupying a great variety of niches in one mangrove tree or swamp stand, from underground to canopy, and of schedules from low to high tide. Time and space are needed for tree species to evolve a complex stand in such situations, and as mangrove communities are often obliterated and re-established by worldwide sea level fluctuations, relatively little time can be allowed for mangrove species to develop and colonize new, potentially short-term, shorelines.

## Desert Diversity: Old vs. New

Old deserts, like that of Central Australia, may exhibit some 3,000 species of woody plants, while recently desertified areas, like those of southwest Asia, may have as few as 150 plants surviving the recent changes from forest. We can act as the agents of constructive change, bringing species to assist local recolonization from the world's arid lands. Such species will assist in pioneering natural reafforestation.

This has not generally been our aim, and we annually destroy invaluable species complexes to grow a single crop such as wheat, thus laying waste to the future.

## Connections Are Not Wires

It is important to concentrate on the nature or value of connections between elements. In nature, we can rarely connect components as easily as a wire or piece of pipe can be fixed into place. We do not "connect" the legume to the orange tree, the chicken to the seed, or the hedgerow to the wind; we have to understand how they function, and then place them where we trust they will work. They then proceed to do additional tasks and to provide other connections themselves. **They do not confine their functions to our design concepts.**

## Benign vs. Destructive Complexity

It is not enough to merely specify the number of connections without noting their value in the system as a whole. Complex social situations and cultivated or chance complexity may occur in natural systems by introductions or migrations. These new events, although increasing complexity, may reduce stability with respect to a desirable local yield.

Thus, where the **benign complexity** of cooperative organisms is useful, **competitive or inharmonious complexity** is potentially destructive. It is a question of matching needs with products, and of the values given to connections.

## Island Evolution and Unique Functions

Evolving complex species assemblies in isolated sites, like the Galapagos Islands, may depend more on a species-swarm arising from pioneer or survivor species than on invaders adapting from borderlands. Only when many niches are empty is a species able to differentiate and survive without competition — thus the [[dodo]] and Darwin's finches arose.

Having arisen, they may then prove very useful to other systems. Unique island species often have functions not easily found in continental and crowded ecologies; frequently, hardy travelers like reptiles and crustaceans take up niches that, on continents, are occupied by species of mammals and birds.

## Order vs. Chaos

Order and disorder arise not from some remote and abstract energy theory but from actual ground conditions or contexts, both in natural and designed systems. **Entropy is the result of the framework, not the complexity.**

- A jumble of diverse elements is disordered.
- An element running wild or in an active destructive mode (bull in a china shop) is disordered.
- Too few or too many forced connections lead to disorder.

**Order is found in things working beneficially together.** It is not the forced condition of neatness, tidiness, and straightness — all of which are, in design or energy terms, disordered. True order may lie in apparent confusion. It is the acid test of entropic order to test the system for yield:

- If it **consumes** energy beyond product, it is in **disorder**.
- If it **produces** energy to or beyond consumption, it is **ordered**.

Thus the seemingly-wild and naturally-functioning garden of a New Guinea villager is beautifully ordered and in harmony, while the clipped lawns and pruned roses of the pseudo-aristocrat are nature in wild disarray.

> **Principle of Order and Chaos**: Order and harmony produce energy for other uses. Disorder consumes energy to no useful end. Neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness signify an energy-maintained disorder in natural systems.

## Permitted vs. Forced Functions

All key living elements may supply many functions in a system, but if we try to force too many work functions on an element, it collapses. One cannot reasonably expect a cow to give milk, raise a calf, forage its own food, plough, haul water, and tread a corn mill.

### The Critical Distinction

Forcing an element to function is a very different proposition from putting it in a position where its natural or everyday behaviours permit benefits to other parts of the system.

**Placed correctly**, a tree or chicken experiences no stress not common to all trees and chickens about their daily business. Further, if we place any of the other elements needed close by, the tree or chicken has less stress than normal. **It is the design approach itself that permits components to provide many functions without forcing functions that are not inherent upon that element.** The chicken may be busy, but not overworked.

### Application to People

People, too, like to be where their very different and complementary capabilities are used rather than being forced to either a single function (like a 300-egg-a-year chicken or a typist confined to a computer operation in an office), or so many functions that they suffer deprivation or overload (like the overworked cow above).

> **Principle of Stress and Harmony**: The yield of a system is theoretically unlimited when all elements are placed so that the needs of one element are supplied by the products of other elements. The role of the designer is to place elements in positions that permit — not force — their natural functions to benefit the whole system.

## Key Design Principles

- **Design for essentials, trust the rest**: We cannot know or design all connections; we must trust elements to make their own beneficial connections.
- **Place, don't force**: Position elements where their natural behaviours create system benefits, rather than demanding unnatural outputs.
- **Value connections over count**: A few high-quality, cooperative connections are worth more than many competitive or destructive ones.
- **Test for yield, not appearance**: Apparent messiness may be true order; apparent neatness may be maintained disorder.
- **Elements exceed their design roles**: Every placed element will do more than we planned for — this is a feature, not a bug.
