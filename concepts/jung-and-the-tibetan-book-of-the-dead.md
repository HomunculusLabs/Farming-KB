## Overview

[[carl-jung]]'s engagement with the *[[bardo-thodol]]* (Tibetan Book of the Dead) represents one of the most significant cross-cultural encounters in the history of psychology. Jung regarded the text as his "constant companion" and credited it with "many stimulating ideas and discoveries, and also many fundamental insights." Yet despite this admiration, Jung fundamentally misunderstood the book's central purpose — a failure that [[lama-anagarika-govinda]] would later correct.

## Eastern vs. Western Psychology

Jung and Leary both recognized that Eastern psychology possesses structural advantages over its Western counterpart when it comes to incorporating modern scientific findings. Eastern philosophy — poetic, indeterministic, experiential, inward-looking, vaguely evolutionary, and open-ended — adapts more readily to discoveries in nuclear physics, biochemistry, genetics, and astronomy than the syllogistic, certain, experimental, externalizing logic of Western psychology.

Jung stands out as the greatest psychologist of the twentieth century by the criterion of "use of available fact." Unlike Freud, whose reference materials were limited to Darwin, classical thermodynamics, the Old Testament, and Renaissance cultural history, Jung drew upon a far broader scope of sources. Both Jung and William James avoided the narrow paths of behaviorism and experimentalism, fought to preserve consciousness as an area of scientific research, and kept open to Eastern scholarship.

## Jung's Praise for the Bardo Thodol

Jung described the *Bardo Thodol* as possessing "an unexampled superiority" in its psychological criticism. He recognized that the text is "in the highest degree psychological in its outlook," contrasting it with Western philosophy and theology, which he characterized as remaining in "the mediaeval, pre-psychological stage."

A key insight Jung drew from the text was its rejection of the Western "either-or" framework in favor of a "magnificently affirmative 'both-and.'" The Bardo Thodol can simultaneously posit deities as both psychological projections and real entities — a conceptual flexibility that the Western mind, committed to unambiguous clarity, struggles to achieve. As Jung noted, one Western philosopher clings to "God is" while another clings equally fervently to "God is not," whereas the Tibetan framework transcends this binary.

Jung's elaboration of the concept of projection was deeply informed by the Bardo Thodol. He recognized that both the "wrathful" and "peaceful" deities are conceived as *sangsaric* projections of the human psyche, while noting that while the European can explain them away as projections, "he would be quite incapable of positing them at the same time as real."

## Jung's Central Misunderstanding

Despite his penetrating analysis, Jung missed the core concept of the Tibetan book. As [[lama-govinda]] would clarify, the Bardo Thodol is **not** a book of the dead — it is a book of the dying, which is to say a book of the living. The concept of actual physical death was an exoteric facade adopted to fit the prejudices of the Bonist tradition in Tibet. Far from being an embalmers' guide, the manual is a detailed account of:

- How to lose the ego
- How to break out of personality into new realms of consciousness
- How to avoid the involuntary limiting processes of the ego
- How to make the consciousness-expansion experience endure in daily life

Jung struggled with this point. He came close but never quite grasped it, primarily because he had nothing in his conceptual framework — that of a psychoanalyst and father of a school — that could make practical sense out of the ego-loss experience. In his autobiography (1960), Jung committed himself wholly to the inner vision, but in 1938, when his Tibetan commentary was written, he was moving in this direction only cautiously.

## The Chonyid State and Deliberate Psychosis

Jung's most profound insight about the Bardo Thodol concerned the [[chonyid-bardo]]. He recognized that "the Chonyid state is equivalent to a deliberately induced psychosis" and described the transition from the Sidpa state as "a dangerous reversal of the aims and intentions of the conscious mind" — a "sacrifice of the ego's stability and a surrender to the extreme uncertainty of what must seem like a chaotic riot of phantasmal forms."

Jung connected this to his concept of individuation: "No one who strives for selfhood (individuation) is spared this dangerous passage, for that which is feared also belongs to the wholeness of the self — the sub-human, or supra-human, world of psychic 'dominants' from which the ego originally emancipated itself with enormous effort."

## The "Closed Book" and Special Training

Jung closed his commentary with a prescient political observation: the Bardo Thodol is a "closed" book that "will only open itself to spiritual understanding," a capacity acquired "through special training and special experience." He noted it is good that such "useless" books exist — they are meant for those "queer folk who no longer set much store by the uses, aims, and meaning of present-day 'civilization.'"

This observation directly anticipates the psychedelic movement's mission: to provide the "special training" for the "special experience" that psychedelic materials make available.

## Lama Govinda's Corrective Interpretation

Lama Anagarika Govinda, freed from European conceptual constraints, moved directly to the esoteric and practical meaning of the Tibetan book. His opening statement — that nobody who has not died can talk about death with authority — is answered with a poetic but biologically grounded observation: "There is not one person, indeed, not one living being, that has not returned from death." Birth and death are two sides of a coin, like a door called "entrance" from outside and "exit" from inside a room.

Govinda articulated the key insight that active memory is only a small part of normal consciousness, while subconscious memory "registers and preserves every past impression and experience." He described the Bardo Thodol as "a key to the innermost recesses of the human mind, and a guide for initiates, and for those who are seeking the spiritual path of liberation."

## The Gates of the Subconscious

Govinda addressed the danger of unprepared access to expanded consciousness: if "the gates of an individual's subconsciousness were suddenly to spring open, the unprepared mind would be overwhelmed and crushed." This is why the gates are guarded by initiates and hidden behind "the veil of mysteries and symbols."

He also described how yogic practices can bring the subconscious into discriminative consciousness, granting access to "the unrestricted treasury of subconscious memory, wherein are stored the records not only of our past lives but the records of the past of our race, the past of humanity, and of all pre-human forms of life."

## Significance for Psychedelic Research

The Jung-Govinda dialogue frames the central challenge of psychedelic therapy: providing adequate preparation ("special training") for the consciousness-expansion experience ("special experience"). Modern psychedelic chemicals provide a key to this forgotten realm, but without the guidance and teachings encoded in traditions like the Bardo Thodol, the potent chemical key is of little value. The psychedelic version of the Tibetan Book of the Dead represents an attempt to bridge this gap — translating ancient wisdom into a modern idiom for those prepared to receive it.
