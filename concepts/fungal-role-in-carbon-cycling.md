# Fungal Role in Carbon Cycling

## Overview

Fungi are central drivers of the global carbon cycle. As primary decomposers of plant-derived organic matter, they break down cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin — the three main components of plant biomass.

They convert fixed carbon back to CO2 through respiration while immobilizing a portion into fungal biomass and recalcitrant soil organic matter.

## Decomposition and Mineralization

In forest ecosystems, the decomposition of leaf litter and woody debris is largely mediated by saprotrophic fungi.

At the soil surface, litter decomposition by the saprotrophic fungal community results in mineralization of nutrients into the soil inorganic nutrient pool.

Materials incorporated into fungal biomass are either retranslocated within the mycelium or released through decomposition and mineralization processes.

The duration of incorporation into biomass represents an immobilization phase, temporarily rendering carbon unavailable to other organisms.

### Enzymatic Machinery

Fungi produce an extraordinary array of extracellular enzymes to decompose plant polymers.

Cellulases (including endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and beta-glucosidases) break down cellulose into glucose monomers.

Hemicellulases (xylanases, mannanases, and related enzymes) hydrolyze the diverse hemicellulose polymers.

Lignin-degrading enzymes — including lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase — attack the complex, recalcitrant lignin polymer through oxidative mechanisms.

Because lignin has no regular repeating structure, its degradation requires a non-specific radical-based attack rather than the lock-and-key approach of hydrolytic enzymes.

See [[mycoremediation-enzymology]] for applied aspects of these enzyme systems.

## Wood Decomposition

Fungi are the dominant decomposers of wood. White-rot basidiomycetes degrade all major wood components including lignin, while brown-rot fungi primarily attack cellulose and hemicellulose, leaving lignin modified but largely intact.

Soft-rot fungi (mostly Ascomycota) degrade wood under conditions less favorable for basidiomycetes.

See [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]] for the temporal sequence of fungal colonizers on wood.

### Succession of Wood Decomposers

Wood decomposition follows a predictable fungal succession.

Early colonizers (typically Ascomycota and Fungi Imperfecti) are opportunistic species that exploit easily accessible sugars and starches in sapwood.

As these resources are depleted, basidiomycete wood-rot fungi become dominant.

White-rot species like Trametes versicolor and Phanerochaete chrysosporium colonize hardwoods, while brown-rot species like Serpula lacrymans and Postia placenta are more common on conifers.

The decomposition process can take decades to centuries for large logs, with carbon release rates declining as the more recalcitrant lignin fraction becomes proportionally larger.

### Brown Rot Mechanism

Brown-rot fungi employ a unique non-enzymatic mechanism called the Fenton reaction to depolymerize cellulose.

They generate hydroxyl radicals (OH•) through the reduction of hydrogen peroxide by ferrous iron (Fe2+), producing highly reactive oxidants that cleave cellulose chains without requiring direct enzymatic contact.

This allows brown-rot fungi to diffuse decay agents ahead of the growing hyphal front, rapidly colonizing and weakening wood from a distance.

The modified lignin left behind gives brown-rotted wood its characteristic brown color and cubical fracture pattern.

## Carbon Sequestration

Fungi contribute to long-term carbon storage through several mechanisms:

- **Glomalin production** — Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi produce glomalin, a recalcitrant glycoprotein that persists in soil for decades. See [[glomalin]] for details
- **Necromass accumulation** — Dead fungal hyphae and melanized cell walls contribute to stable soil organic matter
- **Aggregation** — Fungal hyphae bind soil particles into aggregates that physically protect organic carbon
- **Ectomycorrhizal mats** — ECM fungi form dense mycelial mats that sequester carbon in forest soils

### Glomalin and Soil Carbon

Glomalin, discovered by Sara Wright in 1996, is an iron-containing glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) on their hyphal walls and in the surrounding soil.

It is remarkably resistant to decomposition, with turnover times estimated at 7–42 years.

Glomalin contributes 27% of total soil carbon in some soils, making it one of the most significant pools of stabilized organic matter.

It acts as a biological glue, binding microaggregates into macroaggregates that physically protect occluded organic carbon from microbial access.

Agricultural practices that promote AMF — reduced tillage, diverse rotations, mycorrhizal cover crops — increase glomalin production and thus enhance long-term carbon sequestration.

### Melanin and Recalcitrant Carbon

Many soil fungi, particularly dark-colored dematiaceous fungi and ectomycorrhizal species, produce melanin in their cell walls.

Melanin is a complex, cross-linked polymer that is extremely resistant to decomposition.

Fungal necromass (dead hyphae and melanized structures) contributes to the formation of soil organic matter with mean residence times of decades to centuries.

The ratio of fungal to bacterial necromass in soil is a key predictor of long-term carbon storage capacity — soils dominated by fungal decomposition pathways tend to store more carbon than bacterial-dominated soils.

## Mycorrhizal Contributions

Mycorrhizal fungi play a dual role in carbon cycling: they receive 10-20% of host plant photosynthate as carbon supply while simultaneously enhancing plant nutrient uptake (increasing plant carbon fixation).

Ectomycorrhizal fungi alone may receive up to 15-30% of net primary production in some boreal forests. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for details.

### The Mycorrhizal Carbon Sink

Recent research has revealed that mycorrhizal fungi represent a significant and previously underappreciated carbon sink.

A 2023 study estimated that mycorrhizal fungi sequester approximately 13.12 gigatons of CO2 equivalent per year globally — roughly 36% of annual fossil fuel emissions.

This carbon is stored as living fungal biomass (turnover of months to years), necromass (turnover of years to decades), and mycorrhizal-derived dissolved organic matter stabilized in soil mineral associations (turnover of decades to centuries).

This finding has significant implications for climate change modeling and forest management.

## Fungal Respiration

Fungal respiration returns significant CO2 to the atmosphere. The balance between decomposition (CO2 release) and immobilization (carbon retention in biomass and soil organic matter) determines whether a given fungal community acts as a net carbon source or sink.

### Environmental Controls on Fungal Respiration

Fungal respiration rates are controlled by temperature, moisture, substrate quality, and nutrient availability.

The temperature sensitivity of fungal respiration (Q10) is typically 2.0–3.0, meaning that respiration rates double to triple with each 10°C increase in temperature.

This has important implications for climate change: as global temperatures rise, fungal decomposition rates are expected to accelerate, potentially releasing large amounts of stored soil carbon and creating a positive feedback loop.

However, moisture limitation may counteract this effect in many ecosystems — fungi require adequate water films for metabolic activity, and drought can suppress decomposition even at warm temperatures.

### The Priming Effect

Fungi can accelerate or decelerate the decomposition of existing soil organic matter when fresh carbon inputs are added — a phenomenon known as the priming effect.

When labile carbon (such as root exudates or fresh litter) enters the soil, it stimulates fungal growth and enzyme production, which can increase the decomposition of older, more recalcitrant organic matter (positive priming).

Alternatively, fungi may preferentially metabolize the fresh carbon, reducing their attack on older material (negative priming). The direction and magnitude depends on fungal community composition and environmental conditions.
