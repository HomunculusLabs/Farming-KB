## The 1970s Mushroom Craze

The first signs of a broader phenomenon appeared in the autumn of 1977 and 1978,
which produced a bumper crop of Liberty Caps. In large towns and cities where
access to upland pastures was easy — Manchester, Bristol, Glasgow, Cardiff and
Dundee — hospital doctors noticed a strange new phenomenon: the admission of
people having bad mushroom trips. Most admitted were young males, usually
teenagers. Most had eaten twenty to thirty mushrooms, but all were having a
terrible time.

The National Poisons Service at London's Guy's Hospital saw mushroom-related
enquiries increase steadily from 17 in 1977 to 47 in 1979, 96 in 1980, and 142
in 1981. A survey in Scotland in 1981 at the peak of the era found that 11 per
cent of school children in Tayside had at least heard of the mushrooms, if not
actually tried them.

## Cultural Context

The mushroom craze coincided with the early Thatcher years. With the country in
recession, industry being dismantled, and unemployment skyrocketing, magic
mushrooms offered a convenient, illicit and exciting escape. For another, more
mystical section of society, the advent of the magic mushroom proved even more
promising: it became a symbol of a supposed ancient pagan heritage being
restored at the free festivals that defined the era.
