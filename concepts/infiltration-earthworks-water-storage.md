## Overview

Earthworks are the physical infrastructure of [[water-conservation]] in permaculture design. Bill Mollison categorizes the primary earthwork strategies for infiltrating and storing water into several complementary systems: pitting, swales, water spreading, diversion drains, farm dams, tank storages, and biological reserves. Each has specific appropriate and inappropriate applications depending on climate, slope, soil type, and landscape position.

## Infiltration Strategies

### Pitting, Swales, and Water Spreading

Cheap broadscale earthwork systems can aid the infiltration of overland water flow. The main aids to getting fresh water into deeper storages are:

- **Pitting** — small excavations that catch and hold overland flow, allowing slow percolation
- **Swales** — level channels on contour that intercept runoff and spread it across slopes for absorption
- **Water spreading** — diversion of surface flow to sand basins, dune fields, swamps, and soakage beds

Diversion drains with associated valves, slides, cross-walls, intakes, and irrigation systems enable effective water harvesting and dependable storage. They can recharge sand basins and swales from otherwise wasted overland flow, and also damp down wildfires by maintaining soil moisture.

### Soil Conditioning

Areas of up to 85% runoff can be converted to **zero overland flow** by combining soil conditioning, swales, and water spreading to forests. Soil conditioning ("ripping") followed by tree planting, trace element additions, and conservation farming increases the ability of soils to hold and infiltrate water by factors of **70–85%**.

Since soils can contain many times the water of open storages or streams, both throughflow, baseflow, and plant-available water increase. The cycling of water via evapotranspiration and rainfall also increases as a result.

## Pond and Farm Dam Storages

Wherever precipitation exceeds transpiration and evaporation demands, small dams, wetlands, and swamps can proliferate. These serve as long-term water and wildlife reserves. Many Australian farms have become "drought-proof" through investment in Keyline or similar water conservation systems.

Dams and ponds are also potential **aquaculture sites**. The production of diverse plant and fish or waterfowl protein should be considered during construction. In humid areas, water storages can occupy up to **20% of the landscape** with great benefit, providing fish and aquatic products while moderating drought and flood effects.

## Open Water Storages: Climate Limitations

Open-water (free surface) storages are most appropriate in **humid climates** where potential evaporation is exceeded by average annual rainfall. In arid to subhumid areas, open water storages carry serious risks:

1. **Salinization** — evaporation concentrates dissolved salts, creating water unfit for livestock
2. **Seepage salting** — inevitable seepage from earth dams creates areas of salted or collapsed soils downhill
3. **Deprivation of flush-water** — large barrier dams may prevent sufficient water bypass, depriving downstream agricultural soils, productive lakes, and estuaries of essential silt deposits and flush-water

## Dam Types and Landscape Positions

Mollison identifies several common dam sites in every extensive landscape:

### Saddle Dams

The highest available storages, located on saddles or hollows in the skyline profile of hills. Can be fully excavated below ground or walled on one or both sides. Shapes include circular, oblong, or "shark egg" with horn extensions.

**Uses:** Wildlife, stock water, high storage, fire control.

### Ridgepoint (Horseshoe) Dams

Built on sub-plateaus of flattened ridges, usually on a descending ridgeline below saddle dams. Typically horse-hoof shaped, made below grade or walled by earth banks.

**Uses:** As for saddle dams. Limited irrigation use but valuable for runoff and pumped storages. Can store pumped water for energy generation.

### Keypoint Dams

Located in valleys of secondary streams in humid landscapes at the highest practical construction point — where the stream profile changes from convex to concave. A descending contour then picks up all other keypoints on the main valley. Multiple series can be installed, with spillways eventually returning surplus to streams.

**Uses:** Primarily irrigation water storage for lower slopes.

### Contour Dams

Dam walls built on contour wherever slope is 8% or less. Contours can be concave or convex to the fall line across the slope.

**Uses:** Irrigation, aquaculture, or flood-flow basins in semi-arid areas.

### Barrier Dams

Constructed across flowing or intermittent stream beds. Require ample spillways, careful construction, and fish ladders on biologically important streams. Made most frequently as energy systems, but also used for irrigation when constructed well above main valley floors.

### Turkey's Nest Dams

Above-grade tanks requiring pumped inflow (often by windmill or solar pump). Common in flatlands as stock water tanks or for low-head irrigation.

### Check Dams

Barrier dams not intended to create storages, but to regulate or direct stream flow. Even a 1–3 metre wall provides enough head to drive a hydraulic ram, fit a waterwheel, or divert a stream to a contoured irrigation canal.

## Keyline System

P.A. Yeomans' *Water for Every Farm / The Keyline Plan* (1954) is identified as the pioneering modern text on landscape design for water conservation and gravity-fed irrigation. It also involves patterning, tree planting, soil treatment, and fencing. The **Keyline** itself is a line linking keypoints in primary valleys.

Yeomans estimated that if 10–15% of a normal humid lowland or foothill landscape were fitted with small earth storages, **floods, drought, and fire threat could be eliminated**.

## Tank Storages

Water can be captured off roof areas, roads, and other paved surfaces. Roof water is least polluted and most easily treated for drinking and cooking. Absorption beds are sufficient for muddy or polluted runoff from roads and parking areas.

## Biological Water Storages

The great forests and biological water storages in fruits and nuts (such as the coconut) are the basis for life where no free water otherwise exists. On atolls and arid islands with free-draining sands, biological reserves are the main water reserves. Plants such as cactus, palms, and agaves have specific tissues or organs to store water.

## Design Principles

1. **Site dams at 5% slope or less** for cost-effectiveness; higher slopes require concrete, rock walls, or excavation
2. **Prioritize off-stream dams** — they add to stream flow over the long term, unlike barrier dams
3. **Keep barrier dams low** (1–4m) to allow fish passage and stream oxygenation
4. **Design for gravity flow** wherever possible — place storages above usage areas
5. **Limit open water in arid zones** — prefer soil storage, swales, and subsurface methods
6. **Include biological productivity** in all dam design — aquaculture, waterfowl, irrigation integration
