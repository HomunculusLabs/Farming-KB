## The Concept of Pulsing

Holmgren extends ecological succession theory with the concept
of "pulsing" — the observation that all healthy ecosystems
alternate between phases of accumulation and release:
- **Accumulation phase** — energy, biomass, and nutrients are
  stored in living and dead organic matter. Biomass increases,
  soil deepens, and complexity grows. This phase corresponds
  to the growth stages of forest succession from pioneer
  species through to mature climax communities.
- **Release phase** — disturbance events such as fire, storm
  damage, or pest outbreaks release stored energy and
  nutrients back into the system. This phase creates the
  conditions necessary for renewal and prevents stagnation
  in overly mature or static ecosystem configurations.
- **Pulse timing varies** by ecosystem type. Grasslands pulse
  rapidly with frequent grazing and fire cycles. Forests
  pulse slowly with century-scale disturbance intervals.
  Permaculture design aims to create human-managed pulses
  at timescales beneficial to both production and ecology.

## Permaculture Design Implications

Understanding succession and pulsing directly informs
permaculture design decisions at multiple scales:
- **Accelerating succession** — designers can compress
  timescales by introducing late-successional species
  earlier than they would arrive naturally, using nurse
  plants, and managing competition to speed the transition
  from pioneer to climax community structure.
- **Managing pulses intentionally** — controlled burns,
  rotational grazing, coppicing, and selective harvest
  create productive pulses that yield resources while
  maintaining ecosystem health, avoiding the destructive
  consequences of unmanaged catastrophic disturbance events.
- **Designing for resilience** — incorporating elements
  from multiple successional stages creates systems
  resistant to total disruption. A diverse polyculture
  that includes pioneer, mid-successional, and climax
  elements can absorb shocks at any stage of the pulse
  cycle without complete system failure.

## Agriculture Through a Successional Lens

Holmgren argues that conventional agriculture is inherently
unsustainable because it fights against natural succession:
- **Annual cropping** locks land permanently in the earliest
  successional stage, maintaining bare soil conditions that
  nature quickly moves to fill with perennial vegetation.
  The energy cost of maintaining this arrested state is
  enormous in terms of tillage, fertilizer, and pest
  control inputs required each growing season.
- **Perennial polycultures** represent a managed middle
  successional stage that balances productivity with
  stability, requiring fewer external inputs while
  producing food, fuel, and fiber across multiple layers
  and seasons throughout the calendar year.
- **Restorative grazing** mimics the pulse dynamics of
  natural grassland ecosystems where large herbivores
  periodically remove biomass, stimulating root exudation
  and nutrient cycling that builds soil carbon and organic
  matter over time through repeated defoliation and recovery.

## Social and Economic Succession

Holmgren applies succession thinking beyond ecology to
social and economic systems as well:
- **Civilizational pulses** follow the same accumulation and
  release pattern as ecosystems. Periods of growth and
  complexity building alternate with contraction and
  simplification, often triggered by resource constraints
  or external shocks to the system.
- **Community development** benefits from succession
  awareness. New projects move through pioneer phases
  requiring high energy input, then mature toward stable
  low-maintenance configurations if designed with
  succession principles in mind from the beginning.
- **Energy descent planning** applies pulsing concepts to
  prepare for the inevitable release phase of fossil fuel
  dependency, designing systems that function productively
  at lower energy throughput while maintaining essential
  services and quality of life for human communities.
