Source: David Holmgren, *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002)

Holmgren provides a detailed analysis of ecological succession models and their application to permaculture design, including the concept of "pulsing" ecosystems that alternate between phases of accumulation and release.

## Models of Ecological Succession

### Classic Succession Model
Based on observation of temperate North American and European landscapes. Following disturbance:
1. Herbaceous weeds colonise bare ground
2. Grassland develops
3. Pioneer shrubs and brambles establish
4. Fast-growing forest trees appear
5. Slow-growing, long-lived trees form stable climax ecology

Changes along this gradient:
- Low biomass to high biomass
- Low soil humus to high soil humus
- Low resilience to disturbance to high resilience
- Low diversity to high diversity
- Predominance of competitive relationships to predominance of cooperative and symbiotic relationships

### Agricultural Analogues
- **Crop agriculture**: analogous to herbaceous weed phase (rapid annual growth on bare cultivated ground)
- **Grazed pastures**: managed grassland phase (perennial but herbaceous)
- **Marginal/abandoned land**: pioneer shrubs and fast-growing forest trees
- **Traditional managed forests**: close to climax state

### Succession Forces in Different Land Uses
- **Bare ground**: unstable ecological void; any plant life flows in to bind surface against erosion
- **Pastures**: tend to be invaded by spiny and unpalatable plants, especially shrubs and brambles
- **Pioneer forests**: provide environment for succession to shade-tolerant trees but do not regenerate their own kind

### Initial Floristic Composition Model
Disturbance leads to simultaneous regeneration of all species; short-lived die out, long-lived persist. Diversity greatest following regeneration, lowest at maturity. Describes most Australian ecosystems (heathlands to wet sclerophyll forests).

## Pulsing Ecosystems

The two-phase model of pulsing ecosystem change alternates between:
- **Accumulation phase**: long period of biological rebuilding, energy capture, and storage
- **Release phase**: short, intense pulse of disturbance releasing accumulated energy and nutrients

### Aboriginal Fire Management as Pulsing
- Fire-prone land systems burnt as often as each dry season
- Only driest sites burned; gullies, southerly slopes, fertile sites acted as firebreaks
- Effects: open accessible ground, shaped trees, stimulated green grass, flowering, germination
- Created pattern of localised degradation and enrichment (nutrients lost from burnt areas absorbed by unburnt areas down-slope)
- Unburnt areas underwent ecological succession to less fire-prone vegetation (often rainforest)
- Self-reinforcing pattern: brilliant example of edge use between burnt and unburnt areas

### Traditional European Agriculture as Pulsing
- Tree-based agriculture (food forests, agroforestry, coppice, woodland grazing) in many regions
- Woodlands provided fuel, structural materials, fodder, human food, medicines
- Seasonal movement between permanent arable fields and woodland pastures
- The "shadow of tradition" vs true tradition where people understood functional basis of practices

### Slash and Burn Agriculture
- Most energy-efficient form of agriculture documented
- New Guinea highland swidden: structure mimics rainforest with multiple canopy levels
