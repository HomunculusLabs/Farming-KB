# Magic Mushrooms in the British Underground 1965-1972 The discovery that native
British mushrooms were psychoactive emerged from London's countercultural
underground of the late 1960s and early 1970s — a period when a vibrant
alternative society briefly flourished beneath the surface of conventional life.
## The London Underground Between 1965 and 1972, London was home to an
underground movement with its own cartography and mycelium of connections.
Though never formally mapped, it had clubs and venues, galleries and theatres,
squats and crash-pads, clothing shops, cafes, advice centres, alternative
schools and even a university. Within these spaces, artists, dancers and
musicians pushed boundaries while mystics watched planetary movements through
"Acid-widened eyes." The movement was united by a utopian belief that the
establishment was in its death throes. A new psychedelic Albion was about to
rise from the ashes of political, cultural, sexual and spiritual revolution. Key
venues included the UFO Club, the Roundhouse, and the Arts Lab, all of which
served as nodes in a network that connected musicians, artists, political
activists, and spiritual seekers. The underground press — notably the
International Times (IT), founded in 1966, and Oz magazine — provided the
movement with its intellectual and cultural voice. ## The Delayed Discovery The
first intentional [[african-psilocybin-mushroom-guide]] trip in Britain took place in or around
1970 — remarkably late, given that all the pieces of the puzzle were in place a
decade earlier. LSD had been used illicitly from the early 1960s, and news of
mushrooms, Siberian shamans, and the Mexican pilgrimage had been circulating.
Arthur Koestler published his essay "The Trip to Nirvana" about psilocybin in
the Sunday Telegraph in March 1961, followed by a BBC television documentary.

## The Underground Press and Psychedelic Knowledge

The underground press played a crucial role in disseminating information about
magic mushrooms. Oz magazine, IT, and later publications like Freendz provided
not only cultural commentary but practical guidance — species identification
tips, dosage advice, and trip reports. This information flow was unmediated by
the scientific establishment or the mainstream press, creating a folk
pharmacology that existed in parallel to (and sometimes in tension with)
academic research.
