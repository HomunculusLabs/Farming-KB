# Magic Mushrooms in the British Underground 1965-1972

The discovery that native British mushrooms were psychoactive emerged from London's countercultural underground of the late 1960s and early 1970s — a period when a vibrant alternative society briefly flourished beneath the surface of conventional life.

## The London Underground

Between 1965 and 1972, London was home to an underground movement with its own cartography and mycelium of connections.

Though never formally mapped, it had clubs and venues, galleries and theatres, squats and crash-pads, clothing shops, cafes, advice centres, alternative schools and even a university.

Within these spaces, artists, dancers and musicians pushed boundaries while mystics watched planetary movements through "Acid-widened eyes."

The movement was united by a utopian belief that the establishment was in its death throes. A new psychedelic Albion was about to rise from the ashes of political, cultural, sexual and spiritual revolution.

Key venues included the UFO Club, the Roundhouse, and the Arts Lab, all of which served as nodes in a network that connected musicians, artists, political activists, and spiritual seekers.

The underground press — notably the International Times (IT), founded in 1966, and Oz magazine — provided the movement with its intellectual and cultural voice.

## The Delayed Discovery

The first intentional psilocybin mushroom trip in Britain took place in or around 1970 — remarkably late, given that all the pieces of the puzzle were in place a decade earlier.

LSD had been used illicitly from the early 1960s, and news of mushrooms, Siberian shamans, and the Mexican pilgrimage had been circulating. Arthur Koestler published his essay "The Trip to Nirvana" about psilocybin in the Sunday Telegraph in March 1961, followed by a BBC television documentary.

### Why the Delay?

Several factors may explain why [[liberty-cap-psilocybe-semilanceata-history-biology]] were not discovered sooner:

- British mycophobia — a cultural hostility toward mushrooms inherited from classical tradition
- The focus on LSD as the primary psychedelic available
- Lack of specific information connecting Mexican Psilocybe species to potential British equivalents
- The spurious rules about poisonous vs. edible mushrooms that made experimentation seem dangerous

The [[classical-medieval-mushroom-knowledge]] meant that even well-educated countercultural figures assumed that any wild mushroom consumption was inherently dangerous.

The Dioscorides framework — edible or poisonous, with no third category for psychoactive — effectively blocked the conceptual leap needed to recognise Liberty Caps as intentional entheogens.

## The Discovery

The discovery was recorded in Oz magazine, which along with the International Times (IT), was the most vocal champion of radical political values and psychedelic aesthetics.

The momentous discovery connected the ancient [[classical-medieval-mushroom-knowledge]] with the global psychedelic movement.

Once the connection was made, knowledge spread rapidly through the informal networks of the underground. By the early 1970s, foraging for Liberty Caps had become an established autumn ritual among certain segments of the counterculture.

## The Spread Around the Pacific

Meanwhile, psilocybin mushroom use was spreading around the Pacific rim from its Mexican origins. By the 1970s, mushrooms were being used in Bali, Indonesia, Philippines, and Thailand.

In Thailand, rudimentary cultivation involved watering dung until mushrooms sprouted. They were cooked in omelettes or blended with Coca-Cola.

Though illegal, mushrooms were advertised for young European travellers in seaside full-moon parties.

## Global Diffusion

The remarkable spread took place in a mere thirty years. Propelled by top-down academic inquiry (Wasson, Heim, Hofmann) on one hand, and by gutsy bottom-up folk experimentation on the other, mushroom-consciousness expanded from Mexico to North and South America, then right round the Pacific rim, and finally reached Europe.

This dual-channel diffusion — scientific and popular, formal and informal — is characteristic of psychedelic knowledge transmission throughout the twentieth century.

The [[wasson-hofmann-ruck-collaboration]] research programme provided taxonomic and chemical legitimacy, while underground networks provided practical knowledge and cultural context.

## The Underground Press and Psychedelic Knowledge

The underground press played a crucial role in disseminating information about magic mushrooms.

Oz magazine, IT, and later publications like Frendz provided not only cultural commentary but practical guidance — species identification tips, dosage advice, and trip reports.

This information flow was unmediated by the scientific establishment or the mainstream press, creating a folk pharmacology that existed in parallel to (and sometimes in tension with) academic research.

## The Connection to the [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]]

The British mushroom discovery was part of a broader pattern of psychedelic knowledge exchange across the Atlantic.

The [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] of the 1960s had been primarily centred on LSD, but the discovery that native British mushrooms were psychoactive added a new dimension to the movement.

Foraging for mushrooms required engagement with the natural landscape, connecting urban countercultural ideals to rural environments in a way that synthetic drugs could not.

## Legacy of the British Underground

The underground mushroom culture of 1965-1972 established patterns that would persist for decades.

The autumn foraging ritual, the informal knowledge networks, and the connection between mushrooms and alternative spirituality all have their origins in this brief but fertile period.

The [[magic-mushroom-cultural-history]] of British mushroom use cannot be understood without reference to these foundational years.
