## Dam Types and Landscape Locations

### Saddle Dams

Saddle dams are usually the **highest available storages**, placed on
saddles or hollows in the skyline profile of hills. They can be fully
excavated [[jeavons-root-systems-below-ground-composting]] level, or walled on one or both sides of the
saddle. Their shape can be circular, oblong, or "shark egg" shaped
with horn-like extensions at either end that follow the contour to
capture maximum runoff.

[[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]] cut at 1:250–500 gradient from adjacent hill slopes
feed runoff into saddle dams. Their elevated position makes [[query-what-are-predatory-mites-and-how-can-i-use-them-for-pest-control|them]]
excellent for gravity-fed distribution to lower parts of the property.

**Uses:** Wildlife, stock watering, high-elevation storage, fire
control.

### Ridgepoint Dams

Ridgepoint or "horseshoe" dams are built on the sub-plateaus of
flattened ridges, usually on a descending ridgeline and below saddle
dams. The shape is typically that of a horse's hoof. They can be
made below grade or walled by earth banks, and a person drain
connects them to the next dam in a series.

**Uses:** Similar to saddle dams — wildlife, stock, limited
irrigation. Particularly useful for run-off collection and pumped
storages. Both saddle and ridge dams can act as storages for pumped
water used for energy generation (micro-hydro).

### Keypoint Dams

Keypoint dams are located in the valleys of secondary streams in
humid landscapes, at the **highest practical construction point** in
the hill profile. This is the point where the stream profile changes
from convex to concave — a place that can be judged by eye. A
descending contour from the first keypoint will pick up all other
keypoints on the main valley.

When used in series, no spillway is built between dams; overflow
from one dam flows directly to the next, and eventually to a stream.
This cascading design maximises water storage while ensuring safe
overflow management.

**Uses:** Primarily irrigation water storage for lower slopes. The
keyline (a line linking keypoints in primary valleys) is the
organising principle for the entire water distribution system.

### Contour Dams

Contour dam walls are built on contour wherever the slope is 8% or
less — sufficiently flat to permit practical construction. Contours
and dam walls can be concave or convex to the fall line across the
slope, depending on the shape of the land and the desired water
spreading effect.

**Uses:** Irrigation, aquaculture, flood-flow basins in semi-arid
areas. Practical as part of a general series of storages across a
farm landscape.

### Barrier Dams

Barrier dams are constructed **across a flowing or intermittent
stream bed**. They require ample spillways, careful construction, and
fish ladders on biologically important streams. Barrier dams are made
most frequently as energy systems (micro-hydro) but are also used for
irrigation if constructed well above the main valley floors where
crops are grown.

Barrier dams are the "engineer's dam" — they require the most
careful design because they interact directly with stream dynamics,
fish migration, and downstream water supply.

**Uses:** Energy generation, irrigation, stream flow regulation.

### Turkey's Nest Dams

Turkey's nest dams are **above-grade tanks** — water must be pumped
in, often by windmill or solar pump. They are common in flatlands as
stock water tanks or for low-head irrigation. Their advantage is
placement flexibility: they can be located anywhere on a property
regardless of topography, at the point of greatest use.

**Uses:** Stock watering, low-head irrigation, distributed water
access on flat terrain.

### Check Dams

Check dams are barrier dams not intended to create water storages but
to **regulate or direct stream flow**. Even a 1–3 metre wall across a
small stream provides enough head to drive a [[holzer-water-power-hydraulic-ram]], power a
waterwheel, divert the stream to a contoured canal for irrigation, or
buffer sudden floods. Flood-regulating check dams may have a base
pipe or fixed opening in the streambed that allows manageable flow
downstream while banking up the flood crest behind the dam, spreading
the rush of water over time.
