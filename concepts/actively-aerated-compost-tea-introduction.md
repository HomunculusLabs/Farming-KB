## Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) Introduction

Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) is a liquid extract produced by aerating high-quality compost in water, typically for 24 hours, to multiply and extract beneficial microorganisms. Developed and refined by Dr. Elaine R. Ingham over decades of soil microbiology research, AACT represents a practical method for restoring and maintaining the soil foodweb — the complex community of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods that drives soil health, plant nutrition, and disease suppression.

## Background and Origins

Dr. Elaine Ingham is one of the world"s foremost soil microbiologists. As President of the Sustainable Studies Institute, President of the Soil Foodweb Institute, and Director of Research at Soil Foodweb Inc., she has spent her career elucidating the critical role that soil microorganisms play in plant health, nutrient cycling, and disease suppression. Her work has been instrumental in shifting the paradigm of soil management from chemical inputs to biological approaches.

The Field Guide for Actively Aerated Compost Tea, compiled and edited by Dr. Carole Ann Rollins from Ingham's extensive internet correspondence between April 2001 and June 2003, represents a foundational reference for the AACT community. Published by the Sustainable Studies Institute and Nature Technologies International LLC, it consolidates practical knowledge about tea production, application, and testing that was previously scattered across email threads and online forums.

## What is AACT?

### Definition

Actively Aerated Compost Tea is a water extract of compost that is actively aerated during the brewing process to maintain dissolved oxygen levels above 6 mg/L. This aeration is critical because it selectively promotes aerobic (oxygen-loving) microorganisms — the beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that support plant health — while suppressing anaerobic (oxygen-hating) organisms that can produce phytotoxic compounds and plant pathogens.

### Types of Compost Tea

Ingham distinguishes several types of compost tea, each with different characteristics:

- **Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)**: Compost extracted into water and actively aerated (typically using air pumps and diffusers) for 18–24 hours. This is the recommended method for consistently producing aerobic, biologically rich teas.

- **Fermentative Compost Tea (FCT)**: Compost steeped in water without active aeration, allowing fermentation by both aerobic and anaerobic organisms. FCTs can be beneficial in specific contexts but are less predictable than AACTs.

- **Long-Brewing Compost Tea (LBCT)**: Compost extracted into water and allowed to brew for extended periods (days to weeks). These tend toward anaerobic conditions and may contain metabolites that are suppressive to certain organisms.

- **Not-Aerobic Compost Tea (NACT)**: Compost extracts that have gone anaerobic, typically indicated by foul odours. These are generally not recommended because anaerobic metabolites can harm plants and soil biology.

### Distinguishing AACT from Related Products

It is important to distinguish AACT from several related but distinct products:

- **Plant Tea**: A water extract of plant material (e.g., comfrey, nettle). These contain plant nutrients and some bioactive compounds but lack the diverse microbial community of compost tea.
- **Manure Tea**: A water extract of animal manure. Manure teas contain nutrients and some microorganisms but carry the risk of pathogen contamination and are not considered true compost teas.
- **Compost Extract**: A water extract of compost made without a brewing period — essentially the liquid that drains through compost. Compost extracts contain microorganisms but at much lower populations than actively brewed teas.
- **Compost Leachate**: Liquid that has drained through compost without intentional extraction. Leachate may contain dissolved nutrients and some microorganisms but is not aerated and is qualitatively different from brewed compost tea.
- **Sun Tea**: Compost placed in water and left in the sun without active aeration. These tend to go anaerobic and are not recommended.

## The Soil Foodweb Foundation

### The Living Soil

Ingham's work is grounded in the understanding that **soil is a living ecosystem**, not an inert substrate for holding plants and fertiliser. The soil foodweb — the interconnected community of organisms that live in soil — performs essential functions that no amount of chemical input can replicate:

- **Nutrient retention**: Bacteria and fungi immobilise nutrients in their biomass, preventing leaching and making nutrients available to plants through predation by protozoa and nematodes.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Decomposer organisms break down organic matter, releasing nutrients in plant-available forms.
- **Disease suppression**: A diverse and active soil foodweb outcompetes pathogens for food and space, produces antibiotics and competitive compounds, and stimulates plant immune responses.
- **Soil structure**: Fungal hyphae, bacterial slime, and the glomalin produced by mycorrhizal fungi bind soil particles into aggregates, creating pore spaces for air and water movement.
- **Decomposition of toxins**: Certain soil organisms can degrade pesticides, herbicides, and other chemical contaminants.

### True Compost

The quality of AACT depends entirely on the quality of the starting compost. Ingham defines "true compost" as material that has been through a thermophilic (hot) composting process that:

1. Reaches temperatures of 55–65°C for at least 3 consecutive days, killing most pathogens and weed seeds.
2. Maintains aerobic conditions throughout the process, promoting beneficial aerobic organisms.
3. Supports a diverse microbial community including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes.
4. Is properly cured (allowed to cool and stabilise) before use, ensuring that thermophilic organisms have been replaced by mesophilic (moderate-temperature) organisms suitable for plant interactions.

Compost that has not gone through a proper thermophilic process — including most commercially available composts and all composted manures — may contain pathogens, weed seeds, or anaerobic organisms that can be multiplied by the brewing process.

## The AACT Brewing Process

### Basic Requirements

Brewing AACT requires:

- **High-quality compost**: The single most important ingredient. The compost must contain a diverse and active community of beneficial microorganisms.
- **Dechlorinated water**: Chlorine and chloramine in municipal water supplies kill microorganisms. Water must be dechlorinated (by aeration, off-gassing, or filtration) before use.
- **Aeration system**: Air pump(s) and diffuser(s) capable of maintaining dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brew.
- **Brewing vessel**: Food-grade container (typically 5–200 gallons) cleaned between batches.
- **Food sources (optional)**: Additional nutrients (molasses, kelp, fish hydrolysate, humic acids) to feed specific groups of microorganisms during the brew.

### The Brewing Cycle

1. Fill the brewing vessel with dechlorinated water.
2. Add food sources if used.
3. Begin aeration — ensure dissolved oxygen is above 6 mg/L before adding compost.
4. Add compost (typically in a mesh bag or basket for easy removal).
5. Maintain active aeration for 18–24 hours.
6. Monitor dissolved oxygen throughout — if it drops below 6 mg/L, the brew may be going anaerobic.
7. Apply the tea immediately after brewing. AACT is a living product and begins to degrade as soon as aeration stops.

### Why Aeration Matters

Active aeration is the defining feature that distinguishes AACT from other compost teas. Aerobic microorganisms are generally beneficial to plants and soil, while anaerobic organisms can produce alcohols, organic acids, and other metabolites that are toxic to plants and beneficial soil organisms. By maintaining dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brew, AACT selectively promotes aerobic biology while suppressing anaerobic organisms.

## Making the Shift to Biological Soil Management

Ingham identifies five key functions that a healthy soil foodweb provides — functions that AACT is designed to restore or enhance:

1. **Outcompete disease organisms**: Beneficial organisms occupy the same ecological niches as pathogens, consuming the same food resources and occupying the same physical spaces (leaf surfaces, root zones, soil aggregates).

2. **Retain nutrients**: Bacteria and fungi in the soil immobilise nutrients in their bodies, preventing them from leaching away with rain or irrigation. This is especially important for nitrogen, which is highly mobile in its mineral forms.

3. **Make nutrients available at rates plants require**: When protozoa (which eat bacteria) and beneficial nematodes (which eat fungi) excrete waste, they release nutrients in plant-available forms. This natural nutrient cycling provides a steady, balanced supply of nutrients to plants — unlike synthetic fertilisers, which provide a large pulse of soluble nutrients that plants cannot fully utilise.

4. **Decompose toxins**: Certain bacteria and fungi can break down pesticide residues, heavy metals, and other chemical contaminants in soil, reducing their toxicity to plants and soil organisms.

5. **Build and rebuild soil structure**: Fungal hyphae physically bind soil particles together, while the glomalin produced by mycorrhizal fungi acts as a biological glue. This creates the crumb structure essential for water infiltration, root penetration, and gas exchange.

## Applications and Industries

AACT has been applied across a wide range of industries and contexts, including:

- **Agriculture**: Row crops, orchards, vineyards, and pastures.
- **Horticulture**: Greenhouse production, nursery stock, ornamental landscapes.
- **Turf management**: Golf courses, sports fields, lawns.
- **Forestry**: Reforestation, timber production, forest health.
- **Landscaping**: Residential and commercial landscapes, erosion control, revegetation.
- **Composting**: Accelerating compost processes and improving compost quality.
- **Waste management**: Remediation of contaminated soils and water.

The Field Guide includes one-sheet application rate guidelines for each of these industries, providing practical guidance for field practitioners working with clients unfamiliar with AACT benefits.

## Testing and Quality Assurance

### Monitoring the Foodweb

Ingham emphasises the importance of monitoring soil and tea biology over time. The primary methods include:

- **Direct microscopy**: Examining soil or tea samples under a microscope to identify and quantify different groups of organisms. This is Ingham"s preferred method because it reveals the living, active community.
- **Plate counts**: Growing microorganisms on agar plates and counting colonies. This method is widely used but has significant limitations — most soil organisms cannot be cultured on plates, so plate counts dramatically underestimate total microbial diversity and activity.

### Establishing Baselines

Before applying AACT, Ingham recommends establishing a baseline assessment of existing soil biology. This allows practitioners to:

1. Identify which groups of organisms are present and in what numbers.
2. Determine whether the existing foodweb is balanced for the desired plant type.
3. Track changes over time as AACT applications and other management practices are implemented.
4. Adjust applications based on observed biological responses.

## The AACT Community

The AACT movement represents a pioneering effort to bring soil microbiology out of the laboratory and into practical land management. As Dr. Carole Ann Rollins describes in her forward to the Field Guide, the AACT community is characterised by practitioners who are "working at the breaking speed-of-light to affect these changes" — driven by a sense of urgency and a commitment to restoring soil health through biological methods.

The field guide was created as a practical tool for this community — consolidating scattered knowledge, providing reference materials for client interactions, and establishing data collection protocols to build the evidence base for AACT efficacy. It represents both a snapshot of the state of knowledge in the early 2000s and a foundation for the ongoing development of biological soil management practices.
