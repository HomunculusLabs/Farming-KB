## Fungal Biodiversity Inventory

Fungal biodiversity inventory encompasses the systematic effort to detect, identify, document, and monitor fungal species across habitats and landscapes. It represents a fundamental scientific undertaking with direct applications in conservation, land management, ecosystem health assessment, and pure biodiversity research.

## Why Inventory Fungi?

Fungi are essential to ecosystem functions including decomposition, nutrient cycling, and nutrient transport, and are indispensable for achieving sustainable development. Despite their importance, fungi have frequently been ignored in biodiversity projects because of their perceived intractability. Including fungi in biodiversity considerations is necessary because:

- They play **pivotal roles in all ecosystems** — species composition and abundance serve as indicators of ecosystem health
- They respond to **environmental perturbations** including pollution, climate change, and habitat fragmentation
- They are used to evaluate the effects of **alternative management and land-use schemes**
- Their diversity and abundance have direct **economic implications** (food, medicine, industry, pathology)
- As habitats are degraded and destroyed, hundreds or thousands of fungal species are being lost each year **before they are known to science**

## Scope of Fungal Diversity

Fungi (Kingdom Fungi) and fungus-like groups span an astounding range of taxa:

- **11 phyla** across 3 kingdoms are treated in major fungal biodiversity protocols
- Kingdom Fungi: Chytridiomycota, Zygomycota, Ascomycota, Basidiomycota
- Kingdom Straminipila: Oomycota, Hyphochytriomycota, Labyrinthulomycota
- Kingdom Protista: Plasmodiophoromycota, Dictyosteliomycota, Acrasiomycota, Myxomycota
- Current estimates of **1.5 million species** (Hawksworth 1991) suggest fewer than 5% have been described
- Morphologies range from amoebalike protists and single-celled aquatic chytridiomycetes to large basidiomycete mushrooms

An important estimate from a 1995 workshop suggested that as many as **80% of species in species-rich groups** of fungi (e.g., endophytes, soil fungi) are widely distributed and commonly encountered, leaving only 20% with more limited distributions. If correct, this means sites share enough species for broad-scale inventory work while still harboring sufficient unique species to make each survey valuable.

## Scientific Contributions of Fungal Surveys

### Baseline Documentation
Survey data constitute the **baseline against which to measure changes** in species presence and abundance. During the past 20–30 years in Europe, macrofungal species compositions and abundances at many sites have changed markedly — several previously common species are no longer encountered, and others have been designated rare or endangered. Without baseline data, these changes could not have been detected.

### Ecosystem Health Indicators
Fungi are used as bioindicators for monitoring programs. Examples include fungal community monitoring in old-growth forests of the Pacific Northwest (FEMAT 1993) and the resurgence of Chytridiomycota surveys following the discovery that chytrids contribute to global amphibian population decline (Daszak et al. 1999; Longcore et al. 1999).

### Fundamental Scientific Questions
Rigorous surveys help answer questions about:
- Effects of historical events (glaciations, orogenies, plate tectonics) on fungal distributions
- Levels of host, substratum, habitat, and environmental specificity
- How fungi influence the success or failure of land management practices
- Speciation and evolutionary processes in fungal communities

### Accumulation of Research Resources
Inventories produce:
- **Herbarium specimens** documenting distributions and serving as comparative material
- **Live fungal cultures** for physiological, genetic, and biochemical studies
- Discovery of **many new species** requiring description

## Organizing Fungal Inventories

### By Ecological Group, Not Taxonomy
Fungal inventories are organized around **operational groups based on habitat, substratum use, or ecological habits** rather than strict taxonomic criteria. This is because organisms in the same ecological group require similar sampling techniques, regardless of their taxonomic affiliation. Distinct protocols are necessary for:

- Single-celled aquatic species
- Other aquatic fungi
- Soil-associated species
- Obligate root mutualists (mycorrhizae)
- Endophytes
- Plant pathogens
- Lichens
- Wood-inhabiting macrofungi and microfungi
- Terrestrial macrofungi
- Insect-associated fungi

### Scale Considerations
**Spatial and temporal scale** is a primary consideration when developing sampling protocols (Colwell and Coddington 1994). Different fungal groups operate at vastly different scales — from microhabitat-specialist microfungi to landscape-level ectomycorrhizal networks.

## Dissemination of Results

Inventory results must reach multiple audiences:

- **Scientific community**: Through traditional publications and reports
- **Land managers and policymakers**: Data to inform zoning, park designation, farming, and logging decisions
- **Educators and students**: Classroom materials from primary through graduate level
- **General public**: Field guides, natural history articles, presentations at parks and recreation centers
- **Citizen scientists**: Volunteer involvement in field and laboratory projects
- **Electronic formats**: CD-ROMs, websites, and interactive identification keys are increasingly important

## Resources for Fungal Identification

- **Herbaria**: House voucher specimens, historical distribution data, and comparative material dating back over 100 years
- **Culture collections**: Provide living reference strains for identification and research
- **Internet resources**: Fungus-related websites are increasing rapidly; the Mycological Resources website is particularly comprehensive
- **Published literature**: Widely scattered and voluminous; many important works are out of print
- **International library collaboration**: Facilitates access to rare and out-of-print mycological publications

## Standardization: An Unmet Need

No broad consensus has been reached on standardized protocols for qualitative or quantitative sampling of fungi. The development of standard methods for each operational group is necessary to facilitate quantitative sampling and comparisons across sites, times, and studies. Workers must fully document and justify their techniques so that methods can be evaluated and standard protocols can be designated.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
