# Fungal Endemism and Biogeography

Fungal biogeography studies the distribution of fungal species across space and
time, while endemism refers to species that are restricted to particular
geographic areas and found nowhere else on Earth. For many decades, fungi were
assumed to have cosmopolitan distributions due to the ease with which their
microscopic spores could disperse through the atmosphere. However, molecular
evidence has increasingly challenged this view, revealing substantial geographic
structure in fungal diversity and high levels of endemism, particularly in
tropical and island ecosystems. Understanding where fungi occur and why they
are distributed as they are is essential for both basic science and
conservation planning.

## The Cosmopolitan Hypothesis and Its Reassessment

The traditional view that most fungi are globally distributed was based on the
observation that many morphologically defined fungal species appeared to occur
on multiple continents. This was attributed to the vast numbers of spores
produced by fruiting bodies and their ability to travel long distances in air
currents. The statement "everything is everywhere, but the environment selects,"
originally proposed by Lourens Baas Becking in the 1930s, was widely accepted
for microorganisms including fungi throughout much of the 20th century. This
paradigm suggested that fungal distribution was primarily limited by
environmental conditions rather than dispersal ability or geographic barriers.

Molecular phylogenetic studies have fundamentally revised this picture.
Genetically distinct populations of what were considered single species have
been found on different continents, suggesting limited gene flow despite the
potential for long-distance dispersal. Many fungal species once thought to be
cosmopolitan are now recognized as species complexes containing geographically
restricted taxa. The apparent cosmopolitanism of many fungi was largely an
artifact of insufficient taxonomic resolution, where morphologically similar
but genetically distinct species were conflated under a single name.

## Patterns of Fungal Endemism

Endemism in fungi appears to follow patterns similar to those observed in
plants and animals, with the highest levels in regions that have been
geographically isolated for long periods. Tropical rainforests are hotspots
of fungal endemism, as are oceanic islands and isolated mountain ranges. The
rate of endemism among tropical fungi has been estimated at 70% or higher for
some groups, meaning that deforestation of tropical forests likely drives
extinctions of fungal species that exist nowhere else on Earth. Island
ecosystems, shaped by isolation and unique evolutionary histories, often
harbor fungal communities with high proportions of endemic species that have
evolved in situ over millions of years.

Latitudinal diversity gradients, where species richness increases toward the
equator, are well documented in fungi just as in plants and animals. However,
fungal diversity gradients may be even steeper than those of plants because of
the additional dimension of diversity at microscopic scales that is not
captured by plant surveys. The tropical regions harbor not only more fungal
species overall but also more unique lineages that are absent from temperate
zones, including many early-diverging fungal groups with no close relatives in
higher latitudes. These unique tropical lineages represent millions of years
of independent evolutionary history that cannot be replaced once lost.

## Dispersal Limitation

Evidence for dispersal limitation in fungi comes from multiple lines of
investigation. Phylogeographic studies show genetic structure consistent with
limited gene flow among geographically separated populations. Island biogeography
studies find that fungal communities on isolated islands differ markedly from
those on nearby mainlands and from each other, following patterns predicted by
classical island biogeography theory. Altitudinal gradients on mountains show
clear species turnover with elevation, suggesting that fungi cannot simply
disperse to any suitable habitat regardless of distance or geographic barriers
to gene flow.

The degree of dispersal limitation varies among fungal groups. Macrofungi that
produce large, fleshy fruiting bodies often show stronger geographic structure
than microfungi with small, easily airborne spores. Mycorrhizal fungi, which
depend on host plants for establishment and dispersal over long distances, may
be particularly limited in their geographic ranges. Ectomycorrhizal fungi in
particular have been shown to have strong biogeographic patterns linked to
the distributions of their host trees, with distinct communities associated
with different continents and vegetation types.

## Climate and Fungal Distributions

Climate change is expected to shift fungal distributions in ways that may
increase extinction risk for endemic species with narrow climatic niches. As
temperatures rise and precipitation patterns change, fungal species adapted to
specific microclimatic conditions may be unable to track their preferred
habitats fast enough to survive. Mountain-top endemics are particularly
vulnerable, as they have nowhere to migrate when conditions warm.
Endemic fungi in Mediterranean climate regions face similar threats from
increasing drought frequency and severity. Changes in
fungal distributions will also affect the plants and animals that depend on
mycorrhizal partnerships and other fungal-mediated ecological processes.

## Conservation Implications

The recognition of widespread fungal endemism has profound implications for
conservation biology and policy. Fungi cannot be conserved independently of
their habitats, and the loss of a forest or wetland means the likely extinction
of its endemic fungal species along with the plants and animals that depend on
them. Currently, no fungal species are listed on major international conservation
agreements such as CITES, and very few countries include fungi in their national
biodiversity legislation. The IUCN has begun assessing fungal species for the
Red List, but progress is slow relative to the scale of the problem and the
urgency of habitat loss worldwide. Effective fungal conservation will require
integrating fungi into existing conservation frameworks and protected area
designations as a matter of priority.
