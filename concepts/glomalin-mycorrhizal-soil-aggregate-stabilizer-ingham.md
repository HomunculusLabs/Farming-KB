# Glomalin: Mycorrhizal Soil Aggregate Stabilizer

## Overview

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced by [[fungal-hyphae]] grow, die, and decompose.

Importantly, glomalin is not produced exclusively by VAM fungi. As Ingham
points out, many other organisms in soil make similar materials. Glomalin has
become a general term for many types of compounds that stick things together
in soil. Glues are produced by many bacteria as well as fungi. Bacteria use
their own glues to hold themselves to soil particles and to leaf surfaces.
However, the glomalin produced by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]] hyphae grow through the
soil, glomalin is deposited along the hyphal surfaces and on surrounding soil
particles. This sticky coating binds clay particles, silt, sand grains, and
organic matter particles together. When hyphae eventually die and decompose, the
glomalin persists, leaving behind a stable aggregate structure. The larger
critters — protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods that often come in with
worm compost — then form the larger soil pores and passageways.

This biological aggregation process creates what Ingham describes as the
"hallways and passageways" in soil that allow water and air to move into the
soil profile. The larger pores hold water against the flow of gravity, working
like "swimming pools in the soil" that hold water for later use during dry
periods.

## Role in Soil Carbon Storage

One of glomalin's most significant properties is its extraordinary resistance
to decomposition. Glomalin persists in soil for years — estimates range from 7
to 42 years depending on soil conditions — because its complex protein structure
is difficult for soil organisms to break down. This recalcitrance makes glomalin
a major contributor to stable soil organic carbon pools.

Glomalin typically contains 30 to 40 percent carbon by weight and also contains
significant amounts of iron. Research has shown that glomalin-related soil
protein can account for a substantial portion of total soil carbon — in some
soils, up to 27 percent of the total organic carbon. This means that
mycorrhizal fungi, through glomalin production, are sequestering significant
amounts of atmospheric carbon into stable soil forms.
