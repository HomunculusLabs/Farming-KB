# Compost Biology and the Soil Food Web

Compost is a concentrated universe of [[soil-food-web]] organisms. Per teaspoon of finished compost: up to a billion bacteria, 400 to 900 feet of fungal hyphae, 10,000 to 50,000 protozoa, and 30 to 300 nematodes. Compost, mulch, and compost tea are the three primary tools for introducing and managing soil biology.

## Not All Composts Are the Same

By manipulating ingredients, you can produce compost that is either fungally dominated or bacterially dominated. This matters because different plants prefer different forms of nitrogen -- see [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] and [[root-exudates]].

## Composting Requirements

Five ingredients are essential:
1. **Organic materials** with the right carbon-to-nitrogen ratio
2. **Heat** -- generated by microbial metabolic activity, not the sun
3. **Water** -- for microbial transport and life functions
4. **Air** -- beneficial organisms are aerobic; anaerobic conditions produce alcohols (as little as 1 ppm kills plant cells)
5. **Soil microorganisms** -- the actual workers

### Safe Materials
Grass clippings, autumn leaves, wood chips, straw, sawdust, branches, kitchen scraps (except meats and fats).

### Materials to Avoid
Human and pet feces (disease risk), and the book discourages manures from medicated livestock due to antibiotic and E. coli concerns.

## Three Stages of Composting

### Stage 1: Mesophilic (68-104F / 20-40C)

Even in winter, psychrophilic bacteria operate at temperatures as low as 32F (0C). Their metabolic activity warms the pile enough to activate mesophilic organisms, which begin breaking down cellulose chains into glucose (depolymerization). Brown rot fungi (basidiomycetes) and bacteria (Bacillus spp., Heliospirillum spp.) attack difficult-to-digest materials and produce heat-resistant endospores.

### Stage 2: Thermophilic (104-150F+ / 40-65C+)

Complex carbohydrates are fully broken down. Hemicelluloses decay. More bacteria join in (Arthrobacter spp., Pseudomonas spp., Streptomyces and other [[teaming-with-microbes-overview]]) along with additional fungi.

**Temperature management:**
- A properly made pile reaches 135F (57C) in 24-72 hours and 150F (65C) in three days
- Maintain 140-150F for at least a few days to kill pathogens
- At 150F, weed seeds are destroyed
- Never exceed 155F (68C) -- carbon burns off
- If not heating: turn to add oxygen, or add fresh green material (sugars for bacteria)
- If too hot: turn, add water, or add brown materials

### Stage 3: Maturation (Cooling)

Mesophilic organisms reassert themselves. [[teaming-with-microbes-overview]] continue attacking lignin -- the most resistant plant component -- producing the earthy smell of finished compost. Basidiomycetes remain active. Physical decomposers (nematodes, springtails, worms, ants, beetles) graze on microbes, stimulating bacterial and fungal populations. Worms coat particles with mucus that binds them into aggregates.

Keep the pile between 104-131F (40-55C) after the initial thermophilic peak. Compost is finished when you cannot recognize the original materials.

## C:N Ratio and Bacterial vs. Fungal Compost

The ideal C:N ratio is 25:1 to 30:1.

### Green Materials (Bacterial Foods)
Grass clippings (19:1), fresh weeds, kitchen scraps. Provide simple sugars and lots of nitrogen. The fresher the green, the more nitrogen.

### Brown Materials (Fungal Foods)
Autumn leaves (40:1 to 80:1), bark, wood chips, twigs, sawdust (500:1). Contain lignin, cellulose, and tannin.

### Recipes

**Fungal compost** (for trees, shrubs, perennials):
- 5-10% alfalfa meal
- 45-50% fresh grass clippings
- 40-50% brown leaves or small wood chips

**Bacterial compost** (for vegetables, annuals, lawns):
- 25% alfalfa meal
- 50% green grass clippings
- 25% brown leaves or bark

## pH in Compost

Bacteria buffer compost pH around 7 to 7.5. Fungi buffer around 5.5 to 7. Including some fungi in all composts prevents them from becoming too alkaline.
