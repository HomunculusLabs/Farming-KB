Fungi play a central role in the formation and stabilization of
soil microaggregates, the fundamental building blocks of soil
structure. Through their hyphal networks, [[fungal-exopolysaccharides-and-extracellular-polymers]],
and interactions with bacteria and mineral particles, fungi create
and maintain the physical architecture of soils that underpins
water retention, gas exchange, and root growth.
## Soil Aggregate Hierarchy
Soil aggregates are organized in a hierarchical structure, from
microaggregates (approximately 20 micrometers) through larger units
to macroaggregates visible to the naked eye:
- **0.2 um**: amorphous aluminosilicates, oxides, and organic
  polymers sorbed onto clay plates (permanent inorganic aggregates)
- **2 um**: microbial and fungal debris with humic material
  encrusted with inorganics (persistent organic aggregates)
- **20 um**: plant, fungal, and bacterial debris encrusted with
  inorganics (larger organic-aggregate units)
- **200 um**: roots and fungal hyphae with mineral particles
  (medium-term organic associations)
- **2000 um**: major binding of aggregate units forming solid soil
  structure perforated with pores
## Bacterial Capsules and Initial Microaggregate Formation
The initial step involves bacterial polysaccharide capsules. The
capsule surrounding many bacteria, especially Gram-negative bacteria,
is composed of polysaccharides that physically cause clay particles
to adhere. Together with polyphenols attracted by ionic charges,
the capsule protects the polysaccharide from microbial attack. This
collection of clay particles and bacteria forms a microaggregate of
about 20 micrometers in diameter, driven by sticky polysaccharide
exudates acting as binding agents between mineral particles.
## Fungal Hyphae and Aggregate Stabilization
Saprotrophic fungal hyphae grow between bacterial-clay
microaggregates and continue accumulation to produce larger
aggregates. This bridging function is critical for building from
microaggregates to macroaggregates. Hyphae physically enmesh soil
particles, and fungal exudates chemically bind them together.
Divalent cations (calcium, magnesium) form electrostatic links
between negatively charged sites on both fungal cell wall
polysaccharides and clay [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]], creating stable bridges.
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] contribute even more significantly to
soil aggregation than saprotrophs, due to their greater abundance
in the rhizosphere.
## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi and Glomalin
Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are particularly effective at promoting
soil aggregation. The presence of ryegrass in an alfisol increased
stable macroaggregates from 36% to 78% in 6 months. Conversely,
conversion of pasture with 90% stable macroaggregates into tilled
tomato crop reduced aggregates to 58% due to physical disruption.
The discovery of copious glomalin production from arbuscular
mycorrhizal fungi provided a key mechanistic explanation. Glomalin
is an operationally defined glycoprotein fraction extracted from AM
fungal hyphae. Wright and Upadhyaya (1998) demonstrated a strong
positive correlation between glomalin content and aggregate
stability (r = 0.86 across 17 sites). Glomalin acts as biological
glue, coating hyphae and soil particles with a recalcitrant,
hydrophobic compound that resists microbial degradation.
## Synergistic Effects in Aggregate Formation
Stable [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]] involves synergistic interactions
among multiple components. Arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphal growth is
stimulated by organic matter, particularly sloughed root material.
This increased fungal growth promotes bacterial populations. The
positive feedback loop: organic matter stimulates fungal growth,
fungi bind particles into aggregates, aggregates protect organic
matter from decomposition, and stable aggregates create favorable
microhabitats for further microbial activity.
## Agricultural Implications
Well-aggregated soils resist erosion, maintain porosity for water
infiltration and root penetration, and support diverse microbial
communities. Tillage disrupts fungal hyphae and breaks down
aggregates, while conservation practices enhance soil structure.
Management supporting fungal biodiversity — [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], cover
cropping, and continuous plant cover — improves soil physical
properties. The long-term stability of glomalin means benefits
persist for years after producing hyphae have decomposed.
## Hyphal Necromass and Long-Term Carbon Contribution
Dead fungal hyphae (necromass) contribute to soil organic matter
pools within aggregates. The recalcitrant nature of melanized
hyphal cell walls provides long-term carbon storage within
microaggregates. This necromass serves as a substrate for
successional [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] while maintaining aggregate
integrity through its physical contribution to the soil matrix.
## Seasonal Dynamics
Aggregate stability fluctuates seasonally with root growth cycles
