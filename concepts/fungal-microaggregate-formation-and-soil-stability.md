Fungi play a central role in the formation and stabilization of soil microaggregates, the fundamental building blocks of soil structure.

Through their hyphal networks, extracellular polymers, and interactions with bacteria and mineral particles, fungi create and maintain the physical architecture of soils that underpins water retention, gas exchange, and root growth.

## Soil Aggregate Hierarchy

Soil aggregates are organized in a hierarchical structure, progressing from microaggregates (approximately 20 micrometers in diameter) through larger units to macroaggregates visible to the naked eye.

The hierarchy reflects different binding mechanisms at different scales.

At the smallest scale (0.2 micrometers), amorphous aluminosilicates, oxides, and organic polymers sorbed onto clay plates form permanent inorganic aggregates through electrostatic bonding and flocculation.

At the 2 micrometer scale, microbial and fungal debris consisting of humic material encrusted with inorganics creates persistent organic aggregates.

At 20 micrometers, plant, fungal, and bacterial debris encrusted with inorganics forms larger organic-aggregate units.

At 200 micrometers, roots and fungal hyphae aggregated with mineral particles create medium-term organic associations.

At the largest scale (2000 micrometers), major binding of aggregate units forms the solid soil structure perforated with pores.

## Bacterial Capsules and Initial Microaggregate Formation

The initial step in microaggregate formation involves bacterial polysaccharide capsules.

The capsule surrounding many bacteria, especially Gram-negative bacteria, is composed of polysaccharides.

This polysaccharide layer physically causes clay particles to adhere to the bacteria.

Together with polyphenols attracted by ionic charges, the capsule protects the polysaccharide from microbial attack.

This collection of clay particles and bacteria forms a microaggregate of about 20 micrometers in diameter.

The process is driven by the sticky polysaccharide exudates that bacteria produce, which act as binding agents between mineral particles.

The electrostatic charges on both the bacterial capsule and the clay surfaces facilitate the initial adhesion, while polyphenolic compounds provide cross-linking and protection against enzymatic degradation.

## Fungal Hyphae and Aggregate Stabilization

Saprotrophic fungal hyphae can grow between the bacterial-clay microaggregates and continue the accumulation of material to produce larger aggregates.

This bridging function of fungal hyphae is critical for building up from microaggregates to macroaggregates.

The hyphae physically enmesh soil particles, and fungal exudates chemically bind them together.

The binding mechanisms include cation bridges between hyphal polysaccharides and clay particles.

Divalent cations such as calcium and magnesium form electrostatic links between negatively charged sites on both the fungal cell wall polysaccharides and clay mineral surfaces, creating stable bridges that resist dispersion by water.

Mycorrhizal fungi contribute even more significantly to soil aggregation than saprotrophs.

In both ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, the external (extramatrical or extraradical) hyphae perform the same aggregate-binding function as saprotrophic hyphae, but their abundance in the rhizosphere is typically much greater.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi and Glomalin

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are particularly effective at promoting soil aggregation.

The presence of ryegrass in an alfisol increased the content of stable macroaggregates from 36% to 78% in 6 months.

Conversely, conversion of pasture with 90% stable macroaggregates into tilled tomato crop reduced aggregates to 58% due to physical disruption of the soil by agricultural practices.

Among the hypotheses proposed for the enhanced aggregate-forming abilities of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are that these fungi produce more mucilage than other fungi, produce stickier mucilage, and form more cation bonds with clay particles and other organic matter.

The discovery of copious production of a glycoprotein called glomalin from arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi provided a key mechanistic explanation.

Glomalin is an operationally defined fraction of soil organic matter extracted from AM fungal hyphae.

Using 16 soils from the Middle Atlantic states and one from Scotland, Wright and Upadhyaya (1998) demonstrated a strong positive correlation between glomalin content and aggregate stability, with a correlation coefficient of 0.86 across all sites.

Glomalin acts as a biological glue, coating hyphae and soil particles with a recalcitrant, hydrophobic compound that resists microbial degradation and maintains aggregate integrity over time.

## Synergistic Effects in Aggregate Formation

The formation of stable soil aggregates involves synergistic interactions among multiple soil components.

There is evidence that arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphal growth is stimulated by the presence of organic matter, particularly sloughed material from root surfaces.

This increased fungal growth, combined with the presence of organic matter, promotes increased bacterial populations.

The synergistic effect among organic matter, mycorrhizal hyphae, bacteria, and soil mineral particles creates a positive feedback loop: organic matter stimulates fungal growth, fungi bind particles into aggregates, aggregates protect organic matter from decomposition, and stable aggregates create favorable microhabitats for further microbial activity.

The close juxtaposition of mycorrhizal hyphae with decomposing organic matter optimizes the ability of mycorrhizae to capture mineralized nutrients for plant uptake.

## Agricultural Implications

Soil aggregate stability has important implications for agricultural productivity.

Well-aggregated soils resist erosion, maintain porosity for water infiltration and root penetration, and support diverse microbial communities.

Tillage disrupts fungal hyphae and breaks down aggregates, while conservation practices that promote mycorrhizal colonization enhance soil structure.

The role of fungi in aggregate formation suggests that management practices supporting fungal communities, such as reduced tillage, cover cropping, and maintenance of continuous plant cover, can improve soil physical properties.

The long-term stability of glomalin in soil means that the benefits of mycorrhizal-mediated aggregation can persist for years after the fungal hyphae that produced it have decomposed.
