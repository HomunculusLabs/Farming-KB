# Mollison: Wild Rice and Taro Polycultures

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 13, Section 13.10)

## Wild Rice Culture (Zizania lacustris)

### Origin and Distribution

Wild rice is a tall annual grass (to 2.5 m) with several perennial species in the same genus. It has been gathered as a grain for centuries by Amerindian tribes of Canada and the United States, from Minnesota to Florida. Species or seed for culture trials should be gathered from selected plants at the climatic and water provenance suited to the culture site.

Of the approximately 3 million kg produced, about 50 percent is still wild-gathered in traditional fashion by Amerindians, who beat out the grains into a canoe. The remainder comes from extensive fields of up to 100 hectares created by modern growers.

### Growing Conditions

Wild rice has been brought into culture within the last 30 years, making it one of the most recent grains to be developed. It is peculiarly suited to cold swamps and shallows with intense summer heat. Minnesota has very cold winters and hot summers, the ideal climate.

Seed is saved from selected plants and held over winter in bags under water in icy lakes, or in water in refrigerators at 40 degrees Fahrenheit. It is sown in spring in fields flooded to about 16 to 20 cm deep, and harvested from mid-autumn. The fields are dried out for 2 to 3 weeks before machine harvesting, and must be rested or sown to another crop if a new cultivar is to be introduced, as self-seeding otherwise perpetuates the first-sown variety.

### Yields and Economics

Ideal spacing is 20 x 35 cm. For small plots with selected heads, yield can be 200 to 500 g per head. Commercial yields are low (only 50 to 100 kg/ha) because mechanical harvest gathers only that seed which is ripe at harvest time. However, prices are high ($10 per kg in 1984), so that fields of 100 hectares give a cash return of up to $100,000 annually in rice alone.

Home plots gathered over 3 to 4 weeks are in fact very productive, and only 200 to 800 plants should supply a family if well cared for.

### The Self-Generated Polyculture

In the large fields and deep margin ditches of Minnesota, a self-generated polyculture has evolved:
- Crayfish (Homarus) live in ditches and browse 5 to 10 m into the crop.
- Mink, beaver, and thousands of waterfowl are present.
- Coyote range the bunds.
- Packrats store much of the seed.

Duck potato (Sagittaria) can become a weed but is also a waterfowl food, preferring somewhat deeper water than the rice plant. There is an obvious potential for coldwater fish-rearing in the canals and ditches (trout and bullheads), not yet developed. Azolla manuring is not practiced, and the fields are rested dry in winter.

### Harvest Strategies

A strategy used by Indians to save grain from predation and shattering is to bundle and tie 10 to 20 heads together, and to untie them periodically for threshing into canoes.

Mollison proposes an intriguing potential: packrats carry and store large quantities of sound, cleaned grain into artificial shelters (like engine manifolds), insulated and plugged by Typha (cumbungi, cattail) seed silk. Narrow fields and artificial storage sites may be the most efficient harvesters, and breeding packrats could be provided with alternative food for the winter, or allowed to keep 15 percent of their stores. Bill Mackently uses grey squirrels to collect hickory nuts and acorns in this way, providing buried pipes for storage and leaving them about 15 percent of the harvest.

### Accessory Products

Duck fattening, crayfish harvest, mussel meats, fish production, and vine crop of hops or silverberries (Actinidia arguta) are obvious supplementary products, together with restricted fur production, special forestry, and recreational fishing or tour potential.

Every home gardener who can obtain seed and enjoys a nutritious grain should aim for a small terrace or pond of wild rice. For wildlife (especially waterfowl) refuges, there is no better autumn fodder.

## Taro Culture (Colocasia esculenta)

### Overview

Taro is an herbaceous perennial to 1 m high with large arrow-like leaves and a swollen stem base or tuber which can reach 10 kg but is normally marketed at 1 kg or so, after an 8 to 15 month growing period. There is no "off season" in the subtropics. Taro must be cooked due to stinging crystals of oxalic acid in the leaves.

There were some 1,100 Hawaiian varieties, and perhaps 100 or so are still preserved. It is a staple food in Hawaii, Polynesia, and southeast Asia.

### Propagation

Taro is propagated by small side tubers or more commonly by a cutting based on a crown disc of about 1 cm thick, and the lower 18 cm of stalk from the old tuber at harvest. Cuttings have the leaves removed by a slant cut just above the first leaf node.

### Traditional Fish Culture Integration

There has always been a fish culture tradition with taro, of local prawns, shellfish, and freshwater fish. Tilapia and introduced prawns have extended the potential, as have crayfish (Homarus spp). What is missing is a good variety of edible freshwater mussels to fix phosphates in the terrace mud.

In older times, nutrient supply was of forest leaves (chiefly Aleurites or Hibiscus). The stems of kukui trees (Aleurites) were stood to rot in the upper terraces where they produced a local edible ear fungus. About 80 percent of the bulk of a log was converted to fungus, and the remainder broken up as water mulch.

### Accessory Crops

Accessory margin crops include ti (Cordyline), the Polynesian "wax paper" and wrapper for baked foods, papaya, banana, coconut, and sugar cane. No vine crop was traditionally grown, but as taro appreciates partial shade, there is potential for marginal or wide-spaced overhead kiwifruit, passionfruit, pole bean, or cucurbit crop.

Bunds planted to Dolichos hosei or Phyla nodosa would be useful nitrogenous mulch sources, remaining short and trimmed at the water level by Tilapia or grass carp.

### Dryland Taro

Taro is also grown on dryland areas under irrigation, responding very well to thick mulch and green crop. Intercrop of Brassica, melons, ginger, or lettuce is viable. Beds are normally 1.3 m wide at 30 beds per hectare, with taro spaced at 40 x 60 cm in diamond pattern, giving 55,500 bulbs per hectare averaging 1 kg each, or a yield of 55 tonnes per hectare.

### The Rich Polyculture

Mollison describes the ideal tropical polyculture as: a rich taro, comfrey, papaya, guava, and banana polyculture, with chilis, ginger, peppers, ducks, pigs, and fish, a visit to the sea for limu (seaweed) and crab, and a good earth oven (imu) to cook in.

Every tropical garden deserves a patch or two of taro. It is often grown as a mulched patch or groundcover below fruit trees in higher rainfall areas, or as a subsidiary crop in banana, guava, avocado, and macadamia nut orchards.
