## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Mineral Weathering and Biogeochemical Cycling

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] form mutualistic symbioses with the vast majority of land plants, and their involvement in mineral weathering represents one of the most important fungal contributions to ecosystem-level biogeochemistry. Through proton and organic acid excretion, biosorption, intracellular accumulation, and extracellular precipitation, mycorrhizal fungi transform soil minerals, mobilize nutrients, and influence metal dynamics in the root zone. These activities enhance plant nutrition while simultaneously affecting soil formation and environmental quality.

## Types of Mycorrhiza

### Endomycorrhizas

The fungus colonizes the **interior** of host plant root cells:

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM)**: The most widespread type, forming arbuscules within root cortical cells.
- **Ericoid mycorrhizas**: Associated with plants in the Ericaceae family, important in acidic, nutrient-poor soils.

### Ectomycorrhizas

The fungus is located **outside** the root cells:

- Forms a **hyphal mantle** surrounding the root.
- Develops a **Hartig net** of hyphae between cortical cells.
- Produces an extensive **extramatrical mycelium** that explores soil beyond the root zone.
- Forms **rhizomorphs** (aggregated linear organs) for long-distance translocation.

## Mineral Dissolution Mechanisms

### Organic Acid Excretion

Mycorrhizal fungi excrete low-molecular-mass carboxylic acids that drive "heterotrophic leaching" of soil minerals:

- **Common acids**: Oxalic, citric, malic, succinic, gluconic, and formic acid.
- **Concentrations**: Hyphal tips can produce micro- to millimolar concentrations of organic acids.
- **Target minerals**: Hornblendes, feldspars, granitic bedrock, apatite, potassium feldspar, biotite, microcline, and chlorite.
- **Scale**: In podzol E horizons under European coniferous forests, ectomycorrhizal acid excretion has been attributed to the weathering of primary silicate minerals.

### Proton-Promoted Dissolution

Mycorrhizal fungi acidify the rhizosphere through proton excretion, contributing to proton-promoted (acidolysis) dissolution of calcium-bearing and other soil minerals.

### Carbon Allocation and Growth Regulation

Mycorrhizal mycelia respond dynamically to mineral availability:

- **Greater carbon allocation** occurs when colonizing nutrient-rich mineral patches (e.g., potassium feldspar) compared to inert substrates (e.g., quartz).
- *[[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]]/Pinus sylvestris* ectomycorrhizas allocated significantly more carbon to mycelium colonizing potassium feldspar patches than quartz patches.
- Colonization patterns, carbon allocation, and substrate acidification are all regulated in response to different soil minerals (apatite, quartz, potassium feldspar).

## Phosphorus Mobilization

Mobilization of phosphorus is considered **one of the most important functions** of mycorrhizal fungi:

- Dissolution of soil weatherable calcium-bearing minerals releases phosphate.
- Organic acid excretion solubilizes metal phosphates, making P bioavailable.
- The mycorrhizal contribution to P nutrition is especially critical in P-deficient soils.

## Toxic Metal Mineral Dissolution

Ericoid and ectomycorrhizal fungi can dissolve a variety of toxic metal-bearing minerals:

- **Target minerals**: Cadmium, copper, zinc, and lead phosphates.
- **Mobilization pathways**: Proton-promoted and ligand-promoted dissolution.
- **Environmental implications**: Release of toxic metals into bioavailable forms, with consequences for plant metal uptake and food chain contamination.

## Metal Accumulation and Plant Protection

### Zinc Phosphate Case Study (*Paxillus involutus/Pinus sylvestris*)

Experimental studies of zinc phosphate dissolution revealed complex interactions between mycorrhizal status, metal tolerance, and phosphorus nutrition:

**Under phosphorus-replete conditions:**
- Ectomycorrhizal roots accumulated significantly more phosphorus than non-mycorrhizal roots.
- Total zinc mobilization and accumulation was **least** for associations with zinc-tolerant *P. involutus*.
- The tolerant strain appeared to employ a **toxic metal avoidance strategy**, restricting metal entry to protect plant tissues.

**Under phosphorus-deficient conditions:**
- Zinc-tolerant ectomycorrhiza mobilized and accumulated the **highest** amount of zinc.
- The avoidance strategy was overridden by the imperative to acquire phosphorus.

**Key conclusion**: Zinc phosphate dissolution and host plant protection against zinc was **strongly dependent on the phosphorus status** of the environment.

### Protective Mechanisms

Ectomycorrhizal fungi protect host plants from toxic metals through:

1. **Filtration** in the hyphal sheath or Hartig net by biosorption.
2. **Restricted metal mobility** due to hydrophobicity of the fungal sheath.
3. **Complexation** by released organic acids and other substances.
4. **Extracellular precipitation** of mycogenic toxic metal oxalates.

### Context Dependency

Mycorrhizal protection is most efficient when:
- The mycobiont is **metal resistant**.
- The association has been selected or adapted to contaminated conditions.
- Phosphorus availability is adequate (to avoid the phosphate-deficiency-driven metal mobilization scenario).

However, outcomes are **variable and context-dependent**, responding to biotic and abiotic factors. In some cases, mycorrhizas can **enhance** toxic metal accumulation in host plants, which may be relevant for phytoextraction techniques.

## Rhizomorph Accumulation

Ectomycorrhizal fungi (*Suillus granulatus*, *Paxillus involutus*) accumulate elements released from apatite and wood ash (K, Ca, Ti, Mn, Pb) preferentially in **rhizomorphs** — the differentiated linear organs of aggregated hyphae. This suggests rhizomorphs serve as important sites for metal storage and translocation within the mycelium.

## Applications

### Phytoremediation and Rhizoremediation

Mycorrhizal-metal interactions have potential for:
- **Re-vegetation** of contaminated soils with metal-resistant mycorrhizal associations.
- **Re-forestation** programmes on degraded or contaminated land.
- **Phytoextraction**: Enhanced metal accumulation in host plants for harvest and removal.
- **Rhizostabilization**: Immobilization of metals in the root zone to prevent leaching.

### Limitations

- Interactions are variable and context-dependent.
- Enhanced metal accumulation by mycorrhizas can be either beneficial (phytoextraction) or detrimental (increased food chain contamination), depending on the remediation goal.
- Long-term effectiveness depends on maintaining the mycorrhizal association under field conditions.
