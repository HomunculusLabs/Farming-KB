# Wood Chips in Compost for Fungal Domination

## Summary

Wood chips and other woody materials are the primary tool for creating
fungal-dominated compost, which is essential for perennial crops,
trees, and disease suppression in the soil foodweb. Their high carbon-
to-nitrogen ratio, recalcitrant lignin content, and physical structure
create ideal habitat for saprophytic fungi. Ingham provides specific
guidance on chip size, composting methods, and how wood chip compost
differs fundamentally from bacterial-dominated thermal compost. (see [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]]).

## Why Wood Chips Promote Fungal Growth

All woody material contains hard-to-break-down compounds including
lignin, cellulose, and in some species, protective terpenes and phenolic
compounds. As Ingham explains, "It is the plant's effort to protect
itself against pathogens." These recalcitrant carbon compounds are
difficult for bacteria to metabolize but are precisely the substrates
that saprophytic fungi are adapted to decompose through enzymatic
action. (see [[fungal-decomposition-resource-quality-determinants]]).

Materials with wide C:N ratios selectively favor fungi over bacteria. (see [[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]]).
Ingham lists fungal foods as including "wood chips (not smaller than
1/8 inch, not a lot bigger than 1/2 inch, except for maybe 5% greater
than 1 inch), shredded paper, shredded cardboard, dried leaves. Anything
wide C:N ratio." The wide C:N ratio means there is abundant carbon
relative to nitrogen, which bacteria cannot efficiently exploit but
fungi can, giving fungi a competitive advantage. (see [[ingham-carbon-nitrogen-ratios-compost-tea]]).

The physical structure of wood chips also matters. Their size and shape
create air pockets in the compost pile, maintaining aerobic conditions
that fungi require. Fungal hyphae grow across and between chip surfaces,
using the wood as both substrate and structural support for their
network. This habitat function is as important as the nutritional
function. (see [[compost-tea-ingredients-and-recipes]]).

## Chip Size and Preparation

Ingham provides specific guidance on wood chip sizing for compost piles:
chips should be "not smaller than 1/8 inch, not a lot bigger than 1/2
inch, except for maybe 5% greater than 1 inch." She adds, "don't get
crazy about precision here!" The point is to have chips large enough
to maintain structure and air circulation but small enough to provide
adequate surface area for fungal colonization.

Chips that are too fine (sawdust) compact too tightly, reducing air
circulation and potentially creating anaerobic zones. Chips that are
too large have insufficient surface area relative to volume, slowing
decomposition and fungal colonization. The target range balances these
competing needs.

## Tannins, Terpenes and Phenolic Compounds

Woody materials contain protective compounds that can initially inhibit
biological activity. Ingham addresses this directly: "All woody material
has some level of hard-to-break down material." She recommends chipping
the material and letting it sit in a pile "for about 4 weeks or maybe
longer" to allow terpenes and phenols to volatilize. Turning the pile
helps speed this process.

To accelerate the breakdown of these protective compounds, Ingham
recommends applying "a tea with good fungi in it" and using "material
from an old wood chip pile to enhance the fungi (and maybe even some
bacteria) to chew up the plant compounds." Some terpenes, such as those
from black walnut, may also provide weed suppressant properties that
could be strategically useful.

## Wood Chip Compost vs. Thermal Compost

Thermal compost, the standard approach for most municipal and commercial
operations, relies on rapid bacterial decomposition that generates high
temperatures (135-160 degrees F). This process tends to select for
bacterial dominance and can reduce fungal biomass significantly,
especially if temperatures rise rapidly above 155 degrees F where many
beneficial fungal species cannot survive.

Ingham notes that "worm, thermal, static -- they can all make great
fungal compost" if done correctly. The key is the starting materials:
to make fungal compost, you must include high proportions of woody,
recalcitrant carbon materials. The desired organism levels in fungal
compost include "500 or more (fungal compost) ug total fungal biomass/g
dry weight compost," compared to only "150 (bacterial compost)" ug for
bacterial-dominated compost.

Mushroom compost is an example of a naturally fungal-dominated material,
being "very wide C:N ratio, and thus good food for a limited number of
fungi." However, Ingham cautions that mushroom compost "is high in
Agaricus hyphae...which is all to say that it is very wide C:N ratio,
and thus good food for a limited number of fungi, but not necessarily
the fungi you want."

## Combining Wood Chips with Organism Inocula

Wood chips alone lack the microbial diversity needed for rapid
composting. Ingham describes a practical approach: combining worm
castings or compost tea with wood chips that have been amended with
urea. "The organisms in the castings would use the woody material and
tie-up the nitrate, ammonia and ammonium from the urea quickly." This
is a "nice combination" because wood alone "lacks the organisms to
immobilize the N rapidly."

By adding active organisms through castings or tea, and applying tea
every 7 days, "keeping the active organisms doing their job should make
those wood chips nice compost pretty quickly." The organisms immobilize
nitrogen that would otherwise leach, and the wood chips provide the
carbon habitat that fungi need to thrive.

## Fungal Foods in the Compost Pile

Ingham recommends adding fungal-strand containing material at the
beginning of the composting process, along with fungal foods. Beyond
wood chips, these include humic acids, fish hydrolysates, and protein
meals. The goal is to establish diverse fungal communities from the
start, so that by the time the compost is finished, it contains the
full range of beneficial fungal species needed for tea extraction.

The Compost Tea Brewing Manual lists specific foods that grow bacteria
versus fungi. For fungal compost production, the emphasis should be on
lignin, cellulose, humic and fulvic acid foods rather than starchy,
carbohydrate, or sugary foods, which Ingham notes are "best for the
pathogens."

## Application Rates and Use

Fungal-dominated compost made with wood chips should be used as the
starting material for AACT brewing when the target crop requires fungal
dominance. Perennial crops, trees, shrubs, cane fruit, and many
orchard crops require "at least adequate fungal biomass" in the soil.
The compost can also be applied directly as a mulch or soil amendment
to build long-term fungal habitat in the soil.
