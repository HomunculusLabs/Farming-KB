# Ayahuasca Tourism in Peru — Stuart R. Cautionary Ethnography

R. Stuart's 2002 article "Ayahuasca Tourism: A Cautionary Tale," published in MAPS (volume XII, number 2), is a firsthand ethnographic account of ayahuasca tourism in the Ucayali region of Peru. Written from the perspective of a researcher visiting during a cactus identification project supported by a grant from Bob Wallace to MAPS, the article provides a grounded, skeptical view of the commercialization of indigenous spiritual practice and offers practical advice for prospective ayahuasca tourists.

## Historical Context of Western Spiritual Tourism

Stuart frames ayahuasca tourism within the broader history of [[ayahuasca-tourism-western-seekers-and-integration]] adopting alternative spiritual traditions, tracing a pattern of cultural appropriation that extends back decades:

- **1950s** — The Beats explored Zen Buddhism
- **1960s** — Hippies flocked to Hindu swamis and gurus
- **1970s** — Cult leaders like the Reverend Moon attracted large followings
- **1980s** — New Agers embraced Tibetan lamas and Buddhism
- **1990s** — Shamanism came into vogue as the spiritual practice of choice

Stuart suggests this pattern reflects a recurring Western discontent with Judeo-Christian heritage and a persistent search for authentic spiritual experience through engagement with the "Other." Ayahuasca tourism represents the latest iteration of this pattern, with North Americans and Europeans traveling to South America to find indigenous wisdom and plant medicines.

## The Peruvian Context

Stuart's observations took place in November 2001 in Ucayali, Peru, a major center of [[ayahuasca-tourism-peru]]. The article provides important context about the relationship between ayahuasca and Peruvian society:

- **Legal status** — Unlike the United States, Peru regards [[ayahuasca]] as an herbal tonic rather than an illegal drug. The brew and its raw materials are openly sold in markets
- **Government support** — Government tourist agencies sponsor ayahuasca festivals, and then-president Alejandro Toledo participated in an ayahuasca ceremony
- **Drug war contrast** — Despite Peru's aggressive drug war targeting marijuana and cocaine, ayahuasca is culturally accepted and integrated into daily life
- **Religious coexistence** — While Pentecostal, Adventist, and Catholic missionaries have converted many indigenous people, some converts privately continue to consume ayahuasca and practice native religious traditions

This legal and cultural acceptance makes Peru a particularly attractive destination for ayahuasca tourism, as visitors can participate openly without legal risk.

## Ayahuasca as Religion and Industry

Stuart identifies a fundamental tension in Peruvian ayahuasca practice: it is simultaneously a genuine religious tradition and a commercial industry. The economic dimensions are significant:

- Tourism has created a substantial income stream for communities that were previously among the poorest in Peru
- Shamans catering to tourists can earn hundreds of dollars per month while their neighbors cannot afford a thirty-cent taxi ride
- The price differential between tourist and local ceremonies is enormous — approximately $30 for a tourist versus $2 for a Peruvian
- Towns like San Francisco (near Yarina) have branded themselves as ceremonial centers, with signs proclaiming "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca"

This commercialization raises questions about authenticity, exploitation, and cultural preservation that remain relevant decades later.

## Key Locations in Ucayali

Stuart's ethnography focuses on two primary locations in the Peruvian Amazon that have become centers of [[ayahuasca-tourism-infrastructure-peruvian-amazon]]:

**Iquitos** — The largest city in the Peruvian Amazon and the most popular destination for ayahuasca tourists. Iquitos has the most developed [[ayahuasca-tourism-infrastructure-peruvian-amazon]], with numerous retreats, lodges, and independent shamans offering ceremonies.

**San Francisco / Yarina** — A [[shipibo-san-francisco-ceremonial-center-ucayali]] Shipibo town near Pucallpa that has branded itself as a ceremonial center. Stuart describes it as having at least a couple of ayahuasqueros in almost every Shipibo family, many willing to host ceremonies for both Peruvians and foreign visitors. The enormous sign at the town entrance proclaims it the "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca."

Other destinations mentioned include Brazilian [[ayahuasca-churches-syncretic-ceremonies]] such as the Santo Daime, and American-led retreats ranging from four-star resorts to adventurous expeditions led by experienced guides like Peter Gorman.

## Shaman Profiles and Ethics

Stuart provides candid character assessments of several [[ayahuasqueros-amazonian-shamans]] he encountered, offering a rare unfiltered look at the range of practitioner quality:

**"B" (Yarina)** — An elderly shaman whose son directed the family's religious practice and gave workshops in the United States. Stuart lived in B's ceremonial hut for a week and discovered that B was "a frequent liar, a shoplifter" who "soon asks for money or gifts at every opportunity." Despite this, B's nightly ceremonies attracted up to a couple dozen visitors.

**B's nephew** — An enterprising young shaman earning a couple hundred dollars per month from tourists while his neighbors lived in extreme poverty. He charged $30 per tourist ceremony versus the local rate of $2.

**"E" (San Francisco)** — Widely regarded as an honorable man who charged about $10 per tourist ceremony. At night, icaros could be heard from his home during private family ceremonies, suggesting genuine ongoing spiritual practice beyond commercial work.

**"A"** — A practitioner whose invitations Stuart declined after being swindled on the price of a tobacco pipe.

These profiles illustrate the wide range of integrity and motivation among practicing [[ayahuasqueros]], from genuinely honorable practitioners to opportunistic entrepreneurs.

## The Do-It-Yourself Alternative

Stuart controversially suggests that for those interested specifically in the [[ayahuasca-diy-home-brewing-as-tourism-alternative]] experience rather than cultural immersion, home brewing may be preferable to tourism:

- Ingredients can be ordered from ethnobotanical suppliers
- With an experienced friend as a sitter, one can have an intense entheogenic experience at home
- This approach "could potentially be far more enlightening than what one might experience after traveling all the way to South America"
- The home setting provides safety, comfort, and control over [[lsd-set-and-setting-framework]]
- Cost is significantly lower than international travel

Stuart frames this as a practical consideration rather than a recommendation, acknowledging that cultural immersion has value beyond the entheogenic experience itself. He advises prospective tourists to weigh their motivations carefully: if the goal is cultural learning and adventure, Peru may be worthwhile. If the sole goal is experiencing ayahuasca, home brewing may be more effective and economical.

## Medical Safety Concerns

The article highlights a critical safety issue: [[ayahuasca-drug-interactions]] with prescription medications. Most shamans are unfamiliar with Western pharmaceuticals, so tourists must be responsible for their own safety. Specific risks mentioned include:

- Adverse interactions with medications used to treat AIDS, depression, and psychiatric disorders
- One AIDS patient died during a ceremony in Bolivia despite being sufficiently healthy to dance at the beginning of the service
- The pharmacological basis for these interactions involves MAO inhibition by [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]], which can cause dangerous serotonin syndrome when combined with SSRIs and other serotonergic drugs

Stuart places responsibility for safety squarely on the tourist, since shamans typically cannot assess or manage these pharmaceutical risks.

## Methodology and Positionality

Stuart's ethnographic approach is notably informal and personal rather than academic. He was in Peru as part of a cactus identification research project funded by a Bob Wallace grant to MAPS, and his observations of ayahuasca tourism were incidental to this primary research purpose. This positionality has several implications:

- His access to ceremonies came through natural social connections rather than formal research arrangements
- His assessment of practitioners was based on extended personal interaction (e.g., living in B's ceremonial hut for a week) rather than structured interviews
- His financial transparency about pricing comes from direct observation and negotiation rather than survey data
- His perspective is that of a fellow Westerner rather than an anthropologist studying from outside the culture

This informal approach gives the article an authenticity and accessibility that academic ethnographies sometimes lack, but it also means the observations are necessarily limited to Stuart's personal experience and the specific practitioners and locations he encountered. He acknowledges this limitation explicitly, noting that "my personal observations and encounters will be different than those of any other person travelling to Peru."

## The Problem of Finding a Good Practitioner

One of Stuart's most memorable observations is the bewilderment many tourists experience when they discover that "almost every shaman claims to be the only person in all of Amazonia who knows how to properly brew the magic potion." This creates a paradox for seekers:

- No objective certification or quality standard exists for ayahuasqueros
- Each practitioner has strong incentives to present themselves as uniquely qualified
- Tourists lack the cultural knowledge and language skills to independently evaluate claims
- The commercial incentive structure rewards showmanship and marketing more than genuine spiritual ability
- Short-term visitors cannot distinguish between authentic practice and performance designed for tourists

Stuart does not offer a simple solution to this problem, but his profiles of specific practitioners provide a framework for evaluation: consistency of character, ongoing private practice (not just commercial ceremonies), community reputation, and pricing that is not exploitative relative to local norms.

## Broader Pattern of Spiritual Commodification

Stuart's article can be read as a case study in the broader phenomenon of spiritual commodification, which has been extensively analyzed in the anthropology of tourism. Key features include:

- The transformation of sacred practice into marketable experience
- The creation of a two-tiered pricing system (local vs. tourist) that embeds economic inequality into spiritual practice
- The emergence of "spiritual entrepreneurs" who blend genuine tradition with commercial savvy
- The loss of context and meaning that occurs when ritual practice is extracted from its cultural setting
- The paradox of seekers traveling great distances to find authenticity in commercialized settings

Stuart's contribution is to ground these abstract dynamics in specific, named individuals and locations, making the dynamics of commodification tangible and concrete rather than theoretical.

## Lasting Relevance

Despite being published in 2002, Stuart's observations remain highly relevant to contemporary discussions of [[ayahuasca-tourism-ethics-and-commodification]]. The tensions he identified — between spiritual authenticity and commercial exploitation, between cultural exchange and appropriation, between genuine healing and opportunistic profiteering — have only intensified as ayahuasca tourism has grown into a multi-million dollar industry. His advice to approach ayahuasca tourism with clear expectations, realistic assessment of practitioners, and awareness of [[ayahuasca-tourism-medical-risks-prescription-interactions]] remains sound guidance for prospective participants.
