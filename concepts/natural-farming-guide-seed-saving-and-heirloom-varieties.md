# Seed Saving and Heirloom Varieties

Seed saving is a critical skill for self-reliant farming and a core practice in permaculture. It preserves genetic diversity, reduces dependence on commercial seed suppliers, and allows farmers to select for traits adapted to their specific conditions. Seed sharing is identified as one of the foundational activities of permaculture culture, alongside organic agriculture, forest gardens, and other practices that support self-reliance.

## The Importance of Open-Pollinated and Heirloom Varieties

Heritage varieties are central to natural farming because they breed true from seed, unlike modern hybrids. Heritage fruit trees are more likely to be disease-resistant. Seed saving from hybrids is explicitly cautioned against: "You can save seed from your second crop of grain (unless you're using a hybrid)." Hybrid seeds do not produce offspring with the same characteristics as the parent plant, making them unsuitable for seed saving.

## Seed Saving for Grain Crops

The grain harvest process includes careful seed selection and storage:

1. Save seed from your second crop of grain (not the first, and not from hybrids)
2. Select the best seed heads: unbroken and healthy specimens only
3. Let seed dry in the shock (stook) for at least one month until totally dry
4. Thresh and remove all leaves and twigs to ensure clean seed
5. Store in rodent-proof containers in the house (not the shed), with cats to deter mice
6. Add bay leaves to stored grain to help deter pests
7. Keep containers in a cool, dry place
8. Check periodically for mold, bugs, or signs of rodents
9. Once grain is ground, it must be used immediately (it does not store after milling)

### Protecting Stored Seed from Insect Pests

Two methods for dealing with bugs in stored grain:

Dry ice method: Use 1 tablespoon of dry ice per 5 gallons of grain. Place in an airtight container, put the ice on the bottom, pour the grain on top, wait one hour, then seal. The carbon dioxide produced kills the bugs without affecting the grain.

Heat method: For edible grain only (not seed grain, as heat kills viability). Spread a quarter-inch layer on a pan and heat in the oven at 140 degrees F (60 degrees C) for 30 minutes.

### [[ergot-fungus]] Warning

Grain can develop ergot fungus if it gets damp, turning the grain hard, black, and purple on the inside. Never eat moldy grain or feed it to animals, as it can be fatal to humans and make animals sick. If seed grain gets moldy, throw it out entirely. In absolute desperation, soak the grain in very salty water; the sclerotia (ergot masses) will float to the top where they can be skimmed off, but this is not recommended.

## Self-Seeding and Perennial Techniques

Many plants can be coaxed into replanting themselves, reducing the need for deliberate seed saving and creating a self-sustaining garden. These techniques work best in mild climates but can be adapted with cold frames or greenhouses:

- Lettuce: Allow to go to seed; it scatters and replants itself naturally
- Tomatoes, pumpkins, and melons: Leave in the garden covered with mulch at harvest time; they rot and spill seeds that grow the following season
- Broad beans: Large pods near the base of the plant can be left on the ground to dry, mulched with straw in late summer, and they will sprout in fall
- Potatoes: Leave seed potatoes in the ground in fall, mulch well, and they sprout in spring
- Garlic: Leave in the ground for a couple of years for an everlasting crop
- Leeks: Allow to go to seed; dig up small secondary bulbs growing off the base of the stem for replanting
- Carrots: Keep the tops after eating, store in a cool dark place until they sprout, then set out to grow
- Cabbage: Cut the stalk high on the plant leaving a few leaves; small cabbage heads will spring up for eating or replanting

Volunteer tomatoes and cucumbers from the compost heap can be replanted in empty garden spots. At the end of each season, leaving some plants to go to seed (dill, fennel, carrots, lettuce) maintains the seed bank while simultaneously supporting beneficial insect populations.

## Seed Starting Principles

Seeds germinate when soil is within 50-70 degrees F (15-20 degrees C). Some seeds need light to germinate (carrots, lettuce, spinach, parsley, parsnips, beets); soak these overnight and expose to light before planting. Larger seeds are able to germinate in darkness, and some (like parsley) need darkness. Some seeds (apples, wild rice, some berries) require cold stratification: store in the refrigerator over winter, with wild rice also kept in water.

Planting depth is generally four times the largest diameter of the seed. Deeper planting means darker and wetter conditions; shallow planting means more light but faster drying. Seed packets provide recommended planting depths. Sprouting seeds indoors is more successful than sowing directly in the ground.

## Seed Ball Technique (Fukuoka Method)

For no-till grain planting, seeds can be formed into protective balls that deter birds and improve germination:

- Mix seeds with mud and press through a wire mesh, or
- Wet the seeds down and roll them in fine clay dust until they form a ball shape

The clay mixture can be made from a combination of potter's clay, compost, and sometimes paper mush. Seed balls can be broadcast directly onto fields without any soil preparation.

## Seed as a Cash Crop

Rare or unusual heirloom seeds are identified as a viable small-farm business opportunity. Seed saving preserves genetic diversity and can generate income. The approach fits well with permaculture's emphasis on self-reliance and reducing dependence on external inputs. Seeds, along with rare plants for beneficial insect forage, represent one of the lower-bulk, higher-value cash crop options suitable for small holdings.

## Diversity as Seed Security

The permaculture approach emphasizes planting many species together rather than relying on a single variety. This provides inherent seed security: if one crop fails due to weather, pests, or disease, others succeed. The forest garden model, with plants at different heights and growing cycles, inherently preserves genetic diversity over time. By saving seed from the best-performing plants each year, farmers gradually select for varieties perfectly adapted to their local microclimate and growing conditions. This process of ongoing selection is one of the oldest forms of agricultural improvement and requires no technology beyond careful observation and record-keeping.

## Cold Stratification and Seed Dormancy

Some seeds have evolved to require specific conditions before germinating, preventing them from sprouting at the wrong time of year. Cold stratification mimics the natural winter period that many seeds need. Apples, wild rice, and some berries should be stored in the refrigerator over winter. Wild rice additionally needs to be kept in water during stratification. This dormancy mechanism ensures seeds wait until spring conditions arrive before germinating.

## Seed Viability and Longevity

Seed viability varies dramatically by species. Most vegetable seeds remain viable for 2-5 years when stored properly, while some tree seeds can last decades. Proper storage conditions are critical: cool temperatures, low humidity, and protection from pests and rodents. Rodent-proof containers stored in the house (not the shed) with cats for additional deterrence provide the best protection. Adding bay leaves to stored grain and seeds helps deter insect pests.

## Grain Harvest and Seed Selection Process

The full grain harvest process involves several steps beyond seed selection:

1. Cut grain when stalks are yellow and seed heads droop
2. Bind into sheaves and set in shocks (stooks) to dry for at least one month
3. Thresh by beating or using a flail to separate grain from stalks
4. Winnow by tossing grain in the air on a windy day; chaff blows away
5. Select the best seed heads: unbroken and healthy specimens only
6. Store seed separately from eating grain
7. Save seed from the second crop (first crop may not be fully adapted)
8. Never save seed from hybrid varieties (offspring will not breed true)

## The Coaction Study for Seed and Plant Selection

Understanding which species work well together and which compete helps in selecting what to grow and save seed from. When two species are placed together, observe whether they interact positively, negatively, or neutrally. Track performance over multiple seasons. Plants that perform well in your specific conditions become the basis for your personal seed bank, increasingly adapted to your microclimate with each generation of selection.
