# Guzmán Critique of Stamets World Distribution Map

## Overview
Gastón Guzmán, the foremost authority on the taxonomy and [[distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] fungi,
criticised Paul Stamets' world distribution map of [[psilocybin-mushrooms-as-alien-symbiotes]] exaggerated and
inaccurate. This critique appears in the Guzmán, Allen and Gartz paper on the worldwide
distribution of magic mushrooms and highlights the importance of specimen-based distribution
data over speculative cartography.

## Source
Guzmán, Allen & Gartz: "The World-Wide Distribution of Neurotropic Fungi" in the
comprehensive mycological survey of global neurotropic species. The paper discusses Stamets
(1996) and Horak (1983) in the context of biogeographical analysis.

## The Stamets Map
Paul Stamets' 1996 publication "Psilocybin Mushrooms of the World" included a world
distribution map that visually represented the global occurrence of psilocybin-containing
mushrooms. According to Guzmán's assessment, Stamets filled the map with dots across:
- All of the United States
- All of Mexico
- All of South America
- The central part of Africa
- The central part of Asia
- The south of Australia

## Guzmán's Critique
Guzmán observed that the Stamets distribution map was "inexact or somewhat exaggerated."
The criticism rests on several grounds:

### Over-Representation of Explored Regions
North America and European lands are more mycologically explored than those of the southern
hemisphere. The dense coverage of dots on the Stamets map in these regions reflects collection
effort rather than actual species richness or confirmed occurrence. The map gives the
misleading impression of uniform distribution across vast areas where neurotropic species are
in fact localised or absent.

### Africa Over-Representation
Guzmán specifically noted the Africa problem. Despite the high biodiversity of the African
continent, there are very few records of Psilocybe due to scarce mycological explorations.
Only 6-8 neurotropic species of Psilocybe are known from Africa. Yet Stamets' map showed the
central part of Africa covered with distribution dots, suggesting widespread occurrence that
is not supported by specimen records.

### Asia Over-Representation
Similarly, the central part of Asia was marked with dots despite limited mycological
exploration. The relationship between certain species in Southeast Asia (e.g., P. samuiensis
from Thailand) and South American/Mesoamerican species (e.g., P. mexicana) was documented by
Horak (1983), but this does not justify filling the entire central Asian landmass with
distribution points.

### Southern Hemisphere Under-Exploration Paradox
The most problematic aspect of the Stamets map is that it under-represents the regions of
actual highest diversity. Guzmán documented that the majority of neurotropic Psilocybe
species occur in the [[biogeography-austral-hemisphere-fungi]] (59 species in South America and Mexico vs. 18 in the
USA and Canada, and only 9 in Europe). South America, New Guinea, eastern Australia, and New
Zealand present high diversity but have been poorly explored compared to Europe. The Stamets
map paradoxically shows more dots in the northern hemisphere despite the higher species count
in the south.

## Horak's Biogeographical Observations
Horak (1983) observed interesting relationships among certain agarics and boletes in the South
Pacific hemisphere. South American and Mesoamerican species of these fungi are closely related
to those of Southeastern Asia (New Guinea, Indonesia, and Australia), including species of
Cystoagaricus, Galerina, Mycena, and Paxillus.

This distribution pattern is reflected in Psilocybe:
- P. samuiensis (Thailand) and P. makarorae (New Zealand) are both closely related to
  P. mexicana (Mexico and Guatemala), all belonging to Section Mexicanae
- P. aucklandii (New Zealand) is closely related to P. zapotecorum (Mexico and South America),
  both belonging to [[psilocybe-zapotecorum-section-zapotecorum-americas-pacific-distribution-guzman-1998]]

These trans-Pacific species pairs support the hypothesis of a southern hemisphere origin for
neurotropic fungi, a finding that is obscured rather than illuminated by the Stamets map.

## The Actual Distribution Pattern
Guzmán's own analysis, based on specimen records and taxonomic study, shows:
- Mexico, Caribbean region, Mesoamerica, and Colombia have high concentration of species
- The northern hemisphere has more recorded localities but fewer species
- South America, New Guinea, eastern Australia, and New Zealand have high diversity but poor
  exploration coverage
- Africa remains largely unexplored for neurotropic fungi
- The origin of neurotropic Psilocybe species likely lies in the southern hemisphere

## Implications for Mycological Cartography
The Stamets-Guzmán disagreement illustrates a broader principle in mycological biogeography:
distribution maps based on collection records are strongly biased by exploration effort. Areas
with active mycological communities (Europe, North America) appear species-rich, while
under-explored tropical and southern regions appear depauperate. Accurate distribution mapping
requires standardised survey effort across regions, specimen-based verification, and explicit
acknowledgement of sampling bias.

## Mexico as a Case Study in Exploration Bias
Mexico exemplifies how intensive study reveals hidden diversity. Today Mexico has the highest
number of neurotropic species and varieties of Psilocybe, with 44 taxa, compared to 21 in the
USA and Canada and only 14 in Europe. This is not because Mexico is inherently more mycologically
diverse in all fungal groups, but because Guzmán and colleagues conducted decades of focused
fieldwork there. Of the 42 neurotropic Psilocybe species reported in Guzmán's 1983 monograph,
34 are from mesophytic forests, 4 from tropical forests, and 4 from coniferous forests, despite
coniferous forests being more mycologically worked. This suggests that even within well-studied
Mexico, habitat-specific diversity remains under-documented.

## Hongo's Distribution Types as an Alternative Framework
Rather than dot-density maps, Hongo (1978) proposed nine types of distribution for Japanese
fungi that Guzmán found applicable to neurotropic fungi worldwide:
1. Cosmopolitan species (e.g., Panaeolus spp., [[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]])
2. Northern hemisphere species (e.g., Amanita spp., Psilocybe pelliculosa, P. silvatica)
3. Eurasian species (e.g., Amanita muscaria)
4. North American and Eastern Asiatic species
5. Far Eastern species
6. Southeastern Asiatic species
7. Tropical and subtropical species (e.g., P. cubensis, [[copelandia-cyanescens]])
8. Arctic and alpine species (e.g., P. aztecorum var. aztecorum)
9. Endemic species (e.g., P. muliercula, P. chiapanensis, P. columbiana)

This categorical framework captures biogeographic patterns more accurately than dot-density maps
and avoids the exploration-bias problem inherent in specimen-based cartography.
