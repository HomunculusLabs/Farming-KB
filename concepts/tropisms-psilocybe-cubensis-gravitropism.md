The negative geotropic response of Psilocybe cubensis basidiocarps, its relationship to spore production, and its interaction with anemotropic (wind-directed) growth. Negative geotropism emerges as a second-phase tropism that overrides wind-directed growth, orienting the pileus horizontally for optimal spore dispersal. See also [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-hyphal-orientation]], [[fungi-in-the-environment-fungal-spore-dispersal]].

## Two-Phase Model of Directed Growth

Badham proposed that P. cubensis exhibits two distinct phases of tropic response during basidiocarp development. These phases are not simply additive; the second phase actively masks and overrides the first.

### Phase 1: Anemotropic Growth (Stages 1-2)

- Stipe elongation is directed toward the direction of greatest evaporative demand
- In wind tunnel experiments, stipes grew into the airflow at 9 +/- 3 cm/sec
- This response occurs regardless of fertility (present in sterile/sporeless mutants)
- Operates in both light and darkness
- Individual stipe cells may respond independently to the evaporative stimulus
- If cultures are rotated perpendicular to wind, growth is simply upward (gravity becomes the default direction when wind effect is equalized)
- When basidiocarps were placed with stipes initially directed away from the wind, they bent back toward it
- This demonstrates the strength of the anemotropic response in early development
- The anemotropic mechanism remains operational throughout development but is suppressed by the geotropic signal once sporulation begins

### Phase 2: Negative Geotropism (Stage 2 onward)

- At Stage 2 (pileus nearly spherical), a negative geotropic response emerges
- The pileus turns upward from the direction of the wind, opening with lamellae facing downward
- This recurvature occurs when light is present from above or below
- The pileus orients horizontally regardless of initial growth direction
- This phase masks and overrides the Phase 1 anemotropic response
- The transition coincides with the onset of spore production
- The recurved stipe reorients the cap to face downward for spore release

## Link Between Geotropism and Spore Production

A critical finding is the connection between geotropism and sporulation:

- Negative geotropic recurvature does NOT occur in sporeless (sterile) mutants
- Sporeless mutants continue to grow in the direction of the wind throughout development, never correcting to vertical
- The geotropic response requires some light, but this is because light is necessary for spore production itself
- Few spores formed in darkness, and correspondingly no geotropic response was observed
- When sporeless mutants were grown under identical conditions, they showed purely anemotropic growth through all stages
- The sterile mutant used was derived from the same strain as the fertile cultures, ruling out strain-specific differences

This evidence strongly suggests that a substance produced in the gills during spore formation acts as a hormone or signaling molecule that modifies the first tropism, switching the mushroom from anemotropic to geotropic response.

## Rotation Experiments

Badham tested geotropism by rotating basidiocarps to manipulate gravitational stimulus:

- When basidiocarps were rotated parallel to the airflow (3 rpm), stipe elongation and pileus expansion both occurred horizontally
- This horizontal growth confirmed that the Stage 2 response is genuinely geotropic
- When rotated perpendicular to airflow, growth was upright (gravity equalized on all sides)
- The 3 rpm rotation rate was sufficient to prevent the mushroom from detecting a consistent gravitational vector
- These rotation experiments effectively eliminated gravity as a stimulus while maintaining wind
- The result (horizontal growth into wind) demonstrated that without gravity, the anemotropic response remains operational even at Stage 2
- This confirms that the geotropic response actively suppresses, rather than replaces, the anemotropic mechanism
- The two tropic systems exist simultaneously, with the geotropic system taking priority when active

## Mushroom Hormone Literature

The idea of a mushroom hormone involved in tropisms has been proposed by multiple researchers:

- Gruen (1963): described endogenous growth regulation in carpophores of [[agar]]icus bisporus
- Hagimoto (1963): proposed a mushroom growth hormone controlling geotropic response
- Decapitation experiments linked these hormones to the lamellae (gills)
- Agar diffusates from gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus (Hagimoto and Konishi, 1960)
- Some mushrooms contain indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a higher plant auxin
- Gibberellin-like substances have been found in basidiomycete sporophores (Pegg, 1973)
- However, no evidence suggests these plant hormones regulate mushroom growth (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961)
- Jefferys and Greulach (1956) studied the nature of tropisms in Coprinus sterquilinus
- The gill-produced substance in P. cubensis remains unidentified and may be unique to basidiomycetes

## Adaptive Significance of Negative Geotropism

Plunkett (1961) suggested the negative geotropic response is particularly important for coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) species:

- The dung habitat is unstable and subject to displacement, trampling, and burial
- Horizontal pileus orientation maximizes the distance spores fall before hitting the substrate
- Effective spore release requires lamellae to face downward with the pileus roughly horizontal
- This orientation allows spores to be carried away by air currents rather than falling back onto the substrate
- The veil (partial veil) may protect the upper stipe from wind and light prior to final readjustment before spore release
- For coprophilous species, effective spore dispersal is critical as spores must reach fresh dung for completion of the life cycle
- P. cubensis, while not strictly coprophilous, is often found on bovine dung and shares this adaptive need
- Horizontal pileus orientation also presents the maximum surface area of gills to air currents

## Developmental Stages

Badham defined four developmental stages relevant to tropic behavior:

1. Stage 1: Slight swelling of the pileus (anemotropic growth dominant)
2. Stage 2: Pileus nearly spherical (transition phase, geotropism begins, spore production starts)
3. Stage 3: Open hemispherical pileus, maturity/sporulation (geotropism fully established)
4. Stage 4: Pileus becomes more or less plane, late maturity

The transition between Phase 1 and Phase 2 tropisms occurs at Stage 2, coinciding with the onset of spore production. If upright cultures are placed in the wind tunnel at Stage 2 or later, bending is less pronounced than if placed earlier, suggesting the geotropic response becomes increasingly dominant as development proceeds.

## Relationship to Other Tropic Stimuli

The interaction between gravity and other stimuli reveals a hierarchy of tropic responses:

- Wind (anemotropism) is the primary orienting stimulus in pre-sporulation development
- Gravity (geotropism) overrides wind once spore production begins
- Light influences the system indirectly through its role in spore production
- The physical force of wind may also play a role (Dennison, 1961 demonstrated Phycomyces responds to centrifugal force as a gravitational analog)
- Evaporative demand integrates wind, humidity, light, and temperature into a single gradient
- The geotropic substance from the gills appears to reweight the relative importance of these stimuli, making gravity dominant
- The hierarchy is: gravity > evaporative demand gradient (wind/humidity/light/temperature) in sporulating basidiocarps
- Without the geotropic signal (sporeless mutants, darkness), the evaporative demand gradient becomes the default orienting system
- Understanding this hierarchy is essential for interpreting mushroom growth patterns in both natural and cultivated settings
- The dual-tropism model explains why young mushrooms may grow horizontally (following wind) while mature ones correct to vertical (following gravity)
- This model also provides a framework for understanding abnormal growth patterns seen in cultivation, where environmental variables may disrupt the normal tropic hierarchy
- Manachere (1980) reviewed conditions essential for fruiting of macromycetes, providing additional context for understanding how tropic responses interact with overall fruiting requirements
