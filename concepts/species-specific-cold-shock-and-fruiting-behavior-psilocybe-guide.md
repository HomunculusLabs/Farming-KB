
# Species-Specific Cold Shock and Fruiting Behavior

This concept page summarizes species-specific responses to cold shock treatment and
general fruiting behavior as described in a [[mushroom-species-cultivation-methods]].
However, this technique is not universally beneficial and its effectiveness depends
on the species being cultivated.

## Species That Benefit from Cold Shock

Cold shock treatment is primarily recommended for **harder-to-fruit strains**. These
species tend to require a stronger environmental signal to initiate pinning and may
produce poor or delayed flushes without the temperature drop. Cultivators working
with challenging species should consider cold shocking as a standard part of their
fruiting protocol, as it can improve both the timing and the yield of the first
flush.

## Species That Do Not Require Cold Shock

Several commonly cultivated species do **not** benefit from cold shock treatment.
Applying cold shock to these species is unnecessary and does not meaningfully
improve fruiting outcomes. The guide explicitly identifies the following species as
not requiring cold shock:

- **[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]] (Ecuador strain)** — Described as an easy fruiting strain
  that will pin and fruit reliably without temperature manipulation. Ecuador
  cubensis is considered one of the most straightforward species for indoor
  cultivation.
- **[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]]** — Does not respond positively to cold shock. This species
  has its own distinct fruiting pattern and environmental preferences.
- **[[sclerotia-maturation-timeline-psilocybe-tampanensis-mexicana]]** — Also does not benefit from cold shock. Known primarily
  for producing sclerotia (truffles) in addition to fruiting bodies.
- **[[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]** — A coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) species that does
  not require cold shock to initiate fruiting.

Cultivators working with any of these species can skip the refrigeration step
entirely and move directly from colonization to the [[gottlieb-psilocybin-cultivation-fruiting-chamber-design]], saving time
and simplifying the workflow.

## Fruiting Timeline

The general fruiting timeline for casings placed into fruiting bins is as follows:

1. **Pinning phase**: First pinheads appear **6–15 days** after casings are
   introduced to the fruiting environment. The wide range reflects differences in
   species, strain vitality, and environmental conditions such as humidity, fresh
   air exchange, and light exposure.
2. **Maturation phase**: Once pinheads have formed, mature mushrooms are ready for
   harvest in approximately **5–7 days**. This rapid growth period requires
   consistent environmental conditions to ensure healthy development.

## Flush Characteristics

Most cultivated species produce **three flushes** under standard cultivation
conditions. However, properly maintained casings that receive adequate watering
throughout the fruiting cycle may yield **5–6 flushes** before the substrate is
fully exhausted. The total number of flushes depends heavily on moisture management
and the overall health of the [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]].
