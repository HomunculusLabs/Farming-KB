# Village Development in Permaculture

## Overview

Village development represents the practical culmination of [[permaculture-design]] at the community scale — joining individual design skills in house, energy systems, and property layout with the larger goals of earth repair and ethical real estate development. As individual designers gain field experience and as [[ethical-investment-and-earth-banking]] matures, the idea of "client work" merges with ecological restoration and community creation. Well-designed villages are needed more than any other enterprise today: villages to re-locate those soon-to-be-refugees from sea-level rise, villages to house people from urban slums, and villages where people of like mind can find someone else to talk to and to work with.

## Aims of a Sensible Village

An intentional village should have a group ethic acceptable to all who come there. Ethics, if shared, discussed, and acknowledged, give unity to groups, villages, and nations, indicate a way to go, and control the use of earth resources. They can be reflected in legal, financial, domestic, and public life. The core aims of a sensible village group are:

1. **Reduce the need to earn** by developing food, energy, and shelter self-reliance
2. **Earn within the village if possible**, reducing transport and travel needs, by recruiting people who can fill most essential village occupations or who are self-employed
3. **Produce a surplus** from services to others, maintaining a strong economy and outreach potential
4. **Provide many non-material needs** of people, particularly children, through meaningful work, relevant education, and a rich natural environment
5. **Cooperate** in various enterprises and small associations

## What a Village Can Provide

No isolated or scattered group of people can self-provide all their needs, but it is probable that 30 to 200 houses can support essential services and basic facilities, especially with planning for cooperative funding. What is easy for a group may be impossibly stressful for a nuclear family. A village can provide:

- **Privacy**: In homes and gardens, ensuring personal space and autonomy
- **Access to tools**: Leased, rented, or easily accessed equipment from computers to tractors, shared among residents
- **Entertainment**: From local folk groups to video libraries, creating cultural richness without dependence on external media
- **Conservation**: A village wildlife reserve, water protection areas, and forest reserves managed collectively
- **Recreation**: In the near natural environment, reducing the need for travel to distant leisure facilities
- **Basic life essentials**: Shelter, food, and energy produced largely within the village boundaries

## Financing Village Development

Villages can pool their surplus or current financial resources in a developmental credit union and create land titles to sell in order to develop public service facilities. Nobody need pretend all problems are solved — conspicuous consumption can still ruin the idea of energy self-reliance — but with good management, the village development model comes very close to a sane and sustainable community.

### Methods of Land Acquisition

It is quite practical to create new villages without much initial capital in the development phase, achieved through several approaches:

- **Gift of land** to a land trust intended for village development
- **Option to buy** suitable land from an owner, dependent on pre-sale of the idea to buyers (options can be as cheap as $50 per year for several years if the price offered is about 20 to 30 percent of the developed site value)
- **Tribal or trust lands** already communally owned, worked cooperatively
- **Investor or finance group partnership** to purchase land and stage the development
- **Group capital** from a convened group of people who want such a village, using their pooled resources to stage development

The first few options presume a developer and are faster and probably easier than direct group purchase. All, however, need careful forward planning. The development may give considerable profit (though not guaranteed), and fair or normal profits can be used to benefit both people and land, give young or poorer people titles, and rehabilitate landscapes otherwise neglected.

### Title Distribution Model

As a guide, 30 percent of available titles should cover (in value) all land and development costs, so that surplus titles remain available for community access, profits, gifts, labor equity, and new project development. For a village of 100 titles:

**Development Stage:**
- 60 percent sold by the development group to the village group at market prices (30 percent of that covers all costs and actually sets the land price)
- 30 percent given to the village group for later use
- 10 percent allotted by the developers as labor equity to surveyors, earthmovers, landscapers, and other contributors

**Village Trust Stage** (titles given to the village group by the developer):
- 20 percent sold by the village trust to fund village projects, at lower value (50 percent or less) to low-income families
- 10 percent reserved for sale to crucial new recruits such as medical professionals, computer specialists, or energy producers
- 10 percent issued on easy terms to low-income families
- Remaining titles allocated for community purposes, new project development, or held as strategic reserves

## Site Selection Criteria

There are several types of locations suitable for village development, each requiring a different real estate and planning approach:

- **Type 1**: In a city block or suburbs — gradual takeover or buy-in over some years
- **Type 2**: Adjoining an existing village — probably the easiest to plan and administer, allowing a whole graduation of involvement and commitment while attaching to pre-existing essential services
- **Type 3**: Within a part-vacant village — gradual integration with existing community
- **Type 4**: Isolated from any existing [[village-infrastructure-and-integrated-settlement-design]] — the pioneering or kibbutz approach, needing the most intensive planning, especially for water, market access, and enterprises
- **Type 5**: On the site of a pre-existing but now vacant or destroyed village — reclamation of abandoned settlements
- **Type 6**: As a new suburban development — combining new construction with village principles
- **Type 7**: Specialized settlements on coasts or near wilderness — requiring careful environmental management

Type 2 is recommended as the most practical starting point because it allows immediate access to existing services while enabling progressive development. Purchase of existing homes and lots results in little delay in the pioneering stage.

### Essential Site Requirements

Regardless of type, certain criteria are essential for any village development site:

1. **Water self-sufficiency**: The site must be able to catch, store, reticulate, and clean up its own water supply
2. **Clean energy potential**: Wood, wind, solar, or high-pressure water should be available for energy production, with clear ideas for how clean energy can be developed and maintained
3. **Transport access**: Road, rail, boat, and not-too-distant air access are advantageous for trade and travel, though computer and telecommunications enable most villages to participate in data networks
4. **Mixed ecologies**: Access to or the ability to develop forest, aquatic, marine, agricultural, and market areas gives many more options than a village marooned in a simple ecology

## Procedural Stages for Village Development

### Stage 1: Formation and Site Selection
Form a core group or locate a suitable site. This stage establishes the founding vision, identifies potential members, and scouts locations.

### Stage 2: Site Option or Purchase
Arrange site option or purchase terms. Options are cheaper than outright purchase — often as little as $50 per year for several years if the price offered is about 20 to 30 percent of the developed site value. The seller may retain a house title on the land if required, with the price then discounted.

### Stage 3: Planning Authority Agreement
Obtain an "agreement in principle" from the local shire or planning authority, establishing road type needed, number of allotments allowed, what services the developer must provide, environmental requirements, and timeline for staged development.

### Stage 4: Design and Planning
Commission comprehensive [[permaculture]] designs covering water supply and management systems, energy generation and distribution, road layout and infrastructure, house designs and building materials, food production zones, [[permaculture-designers-manual-wildlife-corridors]] and conservation areas, community facilities and public spaces, and enterprise zones for village businesses.

### Stage 5: Capital Formation and Pre-Sale
Raise capital through investor contributions, pre-sale of titles, and development credit union funds. Pre-sales provide working capital and demonstrate market demand.

### Stage 6: Development Execution
Stage the physical development in phases, beginning with essential infrastructure (roads, water, power) and progressing to house construction, landscaping, and community facilities.

### Stage 7: Community Establishment
As residents move in, establish [[permaculture-village-governance-and-public-financing]], cooperative enterprises, shared resource systems, and community processes for decision-making.

## The Importance of Cooperative Funding

No village can achieve self-reliance without cooperative financial structures. The developmental credit union, common enterprise fund, and village trust work together to ensure that capital flows to where it is most needed — whether that is infrastructure development, community facilities, support for low-income members, or investment in [[mollison-village-enterprises-occupations]]. Good management ensures that the village economy is strong, diverse, and resilient, capable of weathering external economic shocks while maintaining its commitment to ecological sustainability.

## Integration with Bioregional and Extended Family Networks

Village development does not occur in isolation. The most successful villages integrate with bioregional networks for ecological monitoring, resource sharing, and regional planning; extended family networks for social support, capital pooling, and cultural exchange; trust structures for legal protection, asset management, [[property-trusts-and-ethical-investment]]; and other villages through trade, knowledge exchange, and mutual assistance. This web of connections ensures that each village is both self-reliant and deeply interconnected, achieving the permaculture ideal of local autonomy within a global cooperative network.
