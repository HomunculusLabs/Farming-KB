# Deep Organic vs. Shallow Organic Farming Philosophy

Eliot Coleman distinguishes between two fundamentally different approaches to organic farming: "deep organic" and "shallow organic." This distinction is not about the depth of soil cultivation but about the depth of philosophical commitment to ecological principles.
Understanding this distinction is essential for grasping Coleman's approach to agriculture and his critique of the mainstream organic food industry as it has evolved since the USDA National Organic Program (NOP) standards were established.

## The Shallow Organic Approach

Shallow organic farming treats the organic certification standards as a checklist of prohibited inputs and minimum practices.
A shallow organic grower asks: "What can I use that is allowed under the organic rules?" The focus is on compliance rather than ecological understanding,
on substitution rather than transformation.
Synthetic pesticides are replaced with approved organic pesticides,
synthetic fertilizers with approved organic fertilizers,
and conventional practices are maintained with minimal modification.

The shallow organic approach can produce food that meets the legal definition of organic but fails to realize the ecological potential of organic agriculture.
A shallow organic farm may look very much like a conventional farm with different inputs — the same monoculture patterns,
the same dependence on purchased off-farm inputs,
the same separation of crop and livestock systems.
The soil may be merely a substrate for plant growth rather than a living ecosystem.

Coleman is particularly critical of large-scale organic operations that meet the letter of the NOP standards while violating their spirit.
A 5,000-acre organic monoculture of lettuce in California's Central Valley,
dependent on imported organic fertilizers and regular applications of approved organic pesticides,
represents shallow organic at its most extreme.
The food is legally organic but the system is ecologically impoverished.

## The Deep Organic Approach

Deep organic farming treats the organic principles as a starting point for ecological thinking,
not as a ceiling.
A deep organic grower asks: "How can I work with natural systems to create a farm that is more productive,
more resilient,
and more ecologically integrated?" The focus is on understanding and enhancing natural processes rather than substituting approved inputs for prohibited ones.

Key characteristics of the deep organic approach as practiced by Coleman include:

### Biological Soil Management

Deep organic farming prioritizes building and maintaining living soil.
The soil is managed as a complex biological system — a food web of bacteria,
fungi,  protozoa,  nematodes,  earthworms,
and other organisms — rather than as an inert medium for delivering soluble nutrients to plant roots.
Compost,  cover crops,  green manures,
and minimal tillage are the primary tools for soil management.
The goal is to create soil conditions where biological processes make nutrients available to plants in the right forms and at the right times.

### Closed-Loop Systems

Deep organic farming seeks to close nutrient and energy loops within the farm system.
Rather than importing fertility from off-farm sources,
the deep organic grower generates fertility on-farm through compost production,
cover cropping,  crop rotation,
and integration of crop and livestock systems.
The ideal is a farm that is increasingly self-sufficient in fertility,
requiring fewer and fewer purchased inputs over time.

### Ecological Integration

Deep organic farming integrates the farm into the surrounding ecosystem rather than isolating it.
Hedgerows,  wildlife habitat,  beneficial insect corridors,
and water management features are incorporated into the farm design.
The goal is a farm that functions as part of the local ecology rather than standing apart from it.

### Minimal Intervention

Deep organic farming prefers prevention over intervention.
Rather than treating pest or disease problems after they arise,
the deep organic grower designs the system to prevent problems through crop diversity,
rotation timing,  habitat creation for beneficial organisms,
and plant selection for pest and disease resistance.
When intervention is necessary,
the preferred approach is the least disruptive option available.

### Continuous Learning

Deep organic farming is fundamentally an ongoing process of observation,
experimentation,  and adaptation.
Coleman emphasizes that a successful organic grower must be a keen observer of natural systems and a willing experimenter.
The best organic farms are those where the grower has spent years developing intimate knowledge of the specific conditions,
patterns,  and potential of their particular site.

## Coleman's Critique of USDA Organic Standards

Coleman has been openly critical of the USDA National Organic Program standards since their inception.
His central criticism is that the standards codify a shallow organic approach by focusing on input lists and prohibited substances rather than on ecological outcomes.
The standards tell growers what they cannot use but provide little guidance on how to create ecologically integrated farm systems.

The political process that produced the NOP standards — involving intense lobbying from both conventional agriculture interests seeking to weaken the standards and organic industry interests seeking to create a workable federal certification system — resulted in a compromise that Coleman considers inadequate.
The standards permit practices that he considers incompatible with genuine organic agriculture,
including confinement livestock operations,
hydroponic production labeled as organic,
and large-scale monocultures with minimal ecological integration.

Coleman argues that the standards have had the unintended effect of defining organic farming downward — establishing a floor that many operations are content to meet rather than aspiring to exceed.
The organic label now guarantees that certain prohibited substances were not used,
but it does not guarantee that the food was produced in an ecologically sound manner.

## Practical Implications for Small-Scale Growers

For small-scale market growers and gardeners,
the deep organic philosophy has several practical implications.
It suggests that the goal should not be mere compliance with organic standards but the creation of a farm system that is increasingly self-sufficient,
biologically active,  and ecologically integrated. It rewards observation,
experimentation,
and site-specific adaptation over generic recipes and input substitution.

The deep organic approach also has economic implications.
While shallow organic farming may require significant purchased inputs (approved fertilizers,
pest control products,  soil amendments),
deep organic farming progressively reduces dependence on purchased inputs as on-farm biological systems mature.
Over time,
a deep organic farm may have significantly lower input costs than a shallow organic farm producing similar crops.

## The Winter Harvest System as Deep Organic Practice

Coleman's winter-harvest system exemplifies the deep organic approach.
The system works with natural processes — the cold-hardiness of selected crops,
the thermal mass of the soil,
the insulating effects of layered protection — rather than against them.
No synthetic or even approved organic inputs are required for winter production.
The system generates its own fertility through the summer cover-crop rotation that follows the winter harvest phase.
And the continuous cycle of observation and adaptation has refined the system over decades of practice at Four Season Farm.
