# Fish Yield Factors and Polyculture

The productivity of aquaculture systems depends on several key factors that determine how much fish protein can be harvested per unit area. Understanding these factors allows designers to maximize yields through species selection, polyculture design, and proper stocking strategies. This page covers the fundamental principles of fish yield optimization in pond-based systems.

## Trophic Level and Food Conversion Efficiency

Fish occupy different positions on the food chain (trophic scale), and their position directly affects how efficiently they convert available food into harvestable biomass. Fish low on the trophic scale—those eating plankton, algae, or vegetation—consistently produce higher yields per hectare than predatory species.

Typical maximum production rates in fertilized ponds (per hectare per year) illustrate this hierarchy clearly:

- **Bass** (predator): lowest yields
- **Catfish** (omnivore): moderate yields
- **[[bluegill]]** (insectivore): good yields
- **Java Tilapia** (plankton feeder): highest yields

However, these neat hierarchies can shift depending on food management. When pelleted food is supplied, catfish may outperform bluegill—reversing the order found in fertilized-only ponds. Every trial must be evaluated under the specific food management schedule proposed, because different fish harvest different foods with varying efficiency.

## Selecting for Efficient Strains

Within any species, there is enormous variation in food conversion efficiency. By stocking high densities of fingerlings of uniform size (5,000–10,000 per hectare), feeding them, and then netting out only the larger fish as brood stock after a few weeks or months, dramatic improvements can be achieved.

After only one year of such selection, bass fingerlings from fast-growing strains converted food at an efficiency of **3.4:1**, whereas unselected fish converted at **7.3:1** on average, and the worst cases at **24.2:1**. This range of metabolic efficiency within a single species is greater than the differences between species entirely.

This finding has profound implications: careful breeding selection within a chosen species can matter more than species choice itself.

## Functions of Species in Polyculture

Polyculture—the practice of raising multiple species together—can increase yields by 40–200% compared to monoculture. The key is to select species that serve complementary functions. The main functions include:

### Rapid Nutrient Uptake

Fast-breeding species that grow quickly and convert decomposers to food serve to fix fertilizers added to the system and convert wastes produced by dense stocking of other fish. Ideal species breed in ponds, grow fast, and remain as fish food for young pan fish growing up.

**Ideal selections include:**

- **Shrimp**, especially when provided with vegetation or brush bundles for cover; also small crabs, scuds (amphipods), and molluscs
- **Minnows or small free-breeding fish**, provided with cover, screens, or shallow water refuges as large fish grow

**Documented yield increases:**

- Bluegill alone (3,900/ha): 186.4 kg/ha/year
- Bluegill with Gambusia fish: 3,449.4 kg/ha/year (**18× increase**)
- Bluegill, bass, and fathead minnows (polyculture at 350 and 134 fish/ha): 470.6 kg/ha/year (**66% increase**)

Swingle hypothesizes that dedicating 50% of a pond to cover or special habitat for small forage species would not reduce total pan fish yield—much as 30% tree shelter in fields does not reduce livestock weight yield. Planning pond shallows is therefore essential.

### Water Quality Maintenance

Species that consume food waste and improve water chemistry serve dual purposes. Fresh or brackish-water **mussels** actively circulate water, while aquatic **plants** add daytime oxygenation.

- Fish alone: 316.6 kg/ha
- Fish plus mussels: 518+ kg/ha (plus 864 kg of mussel meat; water chestnut could add another 600 kg)

Both mussels and plants increase fish yields while enabling higher stocking rates, and their own yields are entirely additive.

### Predation and Culling

Small numbers of predators prevent over-stocking by consuming excess small fish, ensuring that the remaining population grows to marketable size. Species used include bass, soft-shelled tortoises, snakeheads, and pike.

- Bluegill alone: 316.6 kg/ha
- Bluegill plus bass: 484.4 kg/ha (**~53% increase**)

Yield increases from predation are modest (30–50%), but predators often fetch higher market prices than prey species, making the economic benefit greater than the weight figures suggest.

### Utilization of Different Foods

This is where the greatest potential for yield increases lies. It is common to find carp ponds with 3–7 varieties of carp, all chosen for distinct food preferences, plus the forage and predator species described above.

**Documented polyculture examples:**

- Common carp alone (2,500/ha): 314 kg/ha
- Buffalo fish alone (2,500/ha): 1,086 kg/ha
- Buffalo fish (2,500) plus 250 carp/ha: 925 kg/ha (**~300% increase** over carp alone)
- [[channel-catfish]] alone (4,400): 1,400 kg/ha
- Channel catfish (4,400) plus 1,250 Tilapia: 1,834 kg/ha

Critically, the food conversion efficiency of catfish (1.7:1) was unaffected by the addition of Tilapia—there was no stunting of either species. Similarly, adding pangas (*Pangasius*) to ponds rich in molluscs does not affect carp or Tilapia yields or feeding efficiency but adds to total production.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Select species at different trophic levels** to utilize all available food resources without competition
2. **Include fast-breeding forage species** to convert waste and fertilizers into fish food
3. **Add predators** at controlled levels to prevent over-breeding and stunting
4. **Incorporate filter feeders and plants** for water quality maintenance
5. **Select for efficient strains within species** through simple culling of slow-growing individuals
6. **Provide cover and refuges** for forage species—plan shallows carefully
7. **Test under actual conditions** because food management can reverse expected yield hierarchies
