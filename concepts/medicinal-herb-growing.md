# Medicinal Herb Growing

Growing your own medicinal herbs ensures quality, potency, and a ready supply of remedies. Many medicinal herbs are easy to grow, perennial, and thrive in a variety of conditions. This guide covers 15 essential medicinal herbs for the homestead garden.

## Garden Planning

Most medicinal herbs prefer well-drained soil and full sun (6+ hours). Group plants by water needs and sun requirements. Designate a dedicated medicinal herb bed or integrate them into existing garden beds and [[food-forest-design]] plantings. Many medicinal herbs are also excellent companion plants that repel pests and attract pollinators.

## The Top 15 Medicinal Herbs

### 1. Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla)
Uses: calmative, digestive aid, anti-inflammatory, sleep support, skin soother. German chamomile is annual; Roman chamomile is perennial. Direct sow in spring. Harvest flowers at peak bloom. Dry on screens in a well-ventilated area out of direct sun. Makes tea, tinctures, and infused oils for salves.

### 2. Peppermint (Mentha x piperita)
Uses: digestive aid, headache relief, decongestant, cooling. Very aggressive spreader -- contain in pots or dedicated beds with root barriers. Propagate from cuttings or division (sterile hybrid, seeds are unreliable). Harvest leaves before flowering for highest oil content. Dry or use fresh for tea.

### 3. Lavender (Lavandula angustifolia)
Uses: calmative, sleep aid, antiseptic, insect repellent. Requires excellent drainage and full sun. Prune after flowering to maintain shape. Harvest flower spikes when about half the buds are open. Dry hanging in small bunches. Use in sachets, tinctures, infused oils, and baking.

### 4. Echinacea (Echinacea purpurea, E. angustifolia)
Uses: immune stimulant, anti-inflammatory, wound healing. Native prairie perennial. Requires stratification (cold treatment) for germination. Sow in fall or cold-stratify in refrigerator for 30 days before spring sowing. Roots are the most potent part -- harvest in fall of the second or third year. Tincture fresh or dried roots.

### 5. Calendula (Calendula officinalis)
Uses: wound healing, skin inflammation, anti-fungal, lymphatic support. Easy annual. Direct sow in spring; self-seeds readily. Harvest flower heads every few days throughout the season. Dry for infused oils, salves, and teas. Only the fully open flowers are medicinally active.

### 6. Lemon Balm (Melissa officinalis)
Uses: calmative, antiviral (especially herpes simplex), digestive aid, mood elevation. Member of the mint family -- can spread aggressively. Perennial in zones 4-9. Harvest leaves before flowering. Dries well for tea. Fresh leaves make excellent tinctures.

### 7. Holy Basil / Tulsi (Ocimum sanctum)
Uses: adaptogen, stress relief, immune support, anti-inflammatory. Annual in most climates (tender perennial in zones 10+). Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Pinch flower buds to encourage bushy growth. Harvest leaves throughout the season. Dry for tea or make fresh tinctures.

### 8. Yarrow (Achillea millefolium)
Uses: wound healing (stops bleeding), fever reducer, digestive bitter, anti-inflammatory. Hardy perennial native to many regions. Sow seeds in fall or spring; divide established clumps in spring or fall. Harvest aerial parts (leaves and flowers) when in full bloom. Dries well for tea and tinctures.

### 9. Sage (Salvia officinalis)
Uses: sore throat, oral health, digestive aid, cognitive support, antimicrobial. Perennial shrub. Prefers well-drained, sandy soil. Harvest leaves throughout the growing season; heavier harvest before flowering. Dry for tea, smudge sticks, or use fresh. One of the oldest medicinal plants in recorded history.

### 10. Thyme (Thymus vulgaris)
Uses: respiratory support, antimicrobial, cough remedy, digestive aid. Low-growing perennial. Excellent for rock gardens and pathways. Propagate from cuttings, division, or seed. Harvest stems before or during flowering. Strip leaves from woody stems. Dries well.

### 11. Valerian (Valeriana officinalis)
Uses: sleep aid, anti-anxiety, muscle relaxant, mild pain relief. Tall perennial (3-5 feet). Prefers moist, rich soil and partial shade. Seeds require light to germinate -- press into soil surface, do not bury. Roots are the medicinal part -- harvest in fall of the second year. Strong odor when dried. Tincture fresh roots for best potency.

### 12. Marshmallow (Althaea officinalis)
Uses: sore throat, digestive lining support, cough, skin healing. Tall perennial (4-6 feet). Thrives in moist soil; tolerates clay. Seeds need stratification. Roots are the primary medicine -- harvest in fall of second or third year. Peel, slice, and dry. Also use leaves and flowers for tea.

### 13. Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis)
Uses: cognitive support, circulation, antimicrobial, headache relief. Tender perennial (zone 8+). Overwinter indoors in colder climates. Propagate from cuttings (6-8 weeks to root). Harvest stems anytime. Dry or use fresh for tea, cooking, infused oils, and tinctures.

### 14. Garlic (Allium sativum)
Uses: antimicrobial, immune support, cardiovascular health, antifungal. Plant individual cloves in fall (6 weeks before ground freezes) for summer harvest. Hardneck varieties are hardier and produce scapes (edible). Cure in a dry, well-ventilated area for 2-3 weeks after harvest. Use raw for maximum medicinal effect.

### 15. Elderberry (Sambucus nigra/canadensis)
Uses: immune support, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, fever reduction. Deciduous shrub. Plant at least two varieties for cross-pollination. Harvest berries in late summer (cook before eating -- raw berries cause nausea). Dry for syrup, tinctures, and tea. Flowers are also medicinal -- harvest in early summer for tea and cordials.

## Propagation Methods

- Seed: many herbs grow readily from seed. Stratify per species requirements. Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost.
- Cuttings: softwood cuttings in spring for mint, rosemary, lavender, thyme. Root in water or damp potting mix with humidity dome.
- Division: divide perennials in early spring or fall. Chamomile, echinacea, yarrow, valerian, marshmallow all divide well.
- Layering: bend a low stem to the ground, pin it, and cover with soil. Roots form at the node in 4-8 weeks.

## Harvesting and Drying

### Harvest Timing
- Leaves: harvest before flowering for highest essential oil content
- Flowers: harvest at peak bloom, in the morning after dew dries
- Roots: harvest in fall after the plant has stored energy (usually second year or later)
- Seeds: harvest when fully dry on the plant

### Drying Methods
- Air drying: hang in small bunches in a warm, dry, dark, well-ventilated area. Takes 1-2 weeks.
- Screen drying: spread in a single layer on screens. Good for flowers and leaves.
- Dehydrator: use lowest setting (95-115F) to preserve volatile oils.
- Store dried herbs in airtight glass jars away from light and heat. Label with herb name and harvest date. Most dried herbs retain potency for 1 year.

### Preservation Beyond Drying
- Tinctures: alcohol extracts. 1:2 to 1:5 herb-to-alcohol ratio. 100-proof vodka is standard. Steep 4-6 weeks, shake daily, strain.
- Infused oils: herb steeped in carrier oil (olive, coconut, jojoba). Use for salves and massage oils.
- Salves: combine infused oil with beeswax (1:4 to 1:5 beeswax to oil ratio).
- Honey: herbs infused in raw honey. Excellent for cough syrups and soothing preparations.
