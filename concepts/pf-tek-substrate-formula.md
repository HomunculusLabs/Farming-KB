# PF Tek Substrate Formula

The PF ([[pf-tek-psilocybe-fanaticus]]) substrate formula is a revolutionary [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] medium
developed by Robert McPherson (Psilocybe Fanaticus) in the early 1990s. It represents a
simplified approach to fungal cultivation that uses commonly available kitchen materials
rather than specialized laboratory equipment. The formula combines brown rice powder with
horticultural vermiculite and water to create an ideal growth medium for **[[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]]**
and many other saprophytic mushroom species.

## Core Ingredients

The PF substrate consists of just three ingredients, each serving a critical role:

- **Brown rice powder (BRP):** The primary nutritional source, providing complex carbohydrates,
  proteins, and trace minerals. Freshly ground from whole brown rice using a coffee grinder,
  as pre-packaged rice flour often lacks viability due to oxidation of oils. The standard
  formula calls for 1/8 cup per half-pint jar.

- **Horticultural vermiculite (medium grade):** A hydrated magnesium-aluminum-iron silicate
  mineral that has been expanded through heat. Vermiculite serves as the structural matrix
  of the substrate, providing both water retention and aeration. The finer grade (particles
  averaging ~1mm) is preferred over coarse grades (4-8mm) because it holds significantly
  more water. Standard formula uses 1/2 to 2/3 cup per jar.

- **Water:** Distilled or dechlorinated water, 40-45 mL (approximately 1/4 cup) per jar.
  Water content is the most critical variable — too little and colonization stalls, too much
  and the substrate becomes anaerobic, promoting bacterial contamination.

## Standard Formula (Per Half-Pint / 250 mL Jar)

```
1/8 cup brown rice powder
1/2 - 2/3 cup vermiculite (medium-fine grade)
40-45 mL water (~3 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon)
```

## Maximum Fruiting Formula

An enriched variant that supports heavier flushes at the cost of increased contamination
risk:

```
1/4 cup brown rice powder
1/2 cup vermiculite
60 mL water
```

This formula requires looser jar lids during incubation to allow gas exchange, as the
higher nutrient density generates more metabolic CO2 during colonization.

## Why Vermiculite Is the "Magic" Ingredient

The key innovation of the PF TEK is the use of vermiculite as the substrate base rather
than whole grains. When mycelium colonizes grain substrates, it grows as a dense, compact
mass with minimal air space. In vermiculite-based substrates, the mycelial threads stretch
across and between the mineral particles, creating a much more open, airy colonized matrix.
This has several advantages:

1. **Improved gas exchange:** The porous structure allows CO2 to escape and fresh O2 to
   penetrate throughout the substrate, accelerating colonization.
2. **Better moisture distribution:** Vermiculite absorbs and slowly releases water, creating
   a more uniform moisture gradient throughout the jar.
3. **Easier cake removal:** The non-adhesive nature of vermiculite allows fully colonized
   cakes to slide out of tapered jars intact, which is critical for the fruiting stage.
4. **[[pf-tek-dry-vermiculite-contaminant-barrier]]:** A layer of dry vermiculite placed on top of the wet substrate
   acts as a filter barrier against airborne contaminants during inoculation and incubation.

## Jar Selection

Jars must meet specific criteria for the PF TEK to work reliably:

- **Capacity:** Exactly 1/2 pint (8 oz / 250 mL). Larger jars are unreliable and prone
  to failure from stalled colonization or contamination.
- **Shape:** Tapered sides with no shoulders. The taper allows the colonized cake to
  slide out without damage. Suitable jars include Kerr wide-mouth half-pint canning jars,
  Ball regular-mouth half-pint canning jars, and tapered drinking glasses.
- **Important note:** Kerr regular-mouth half-pint jars are NOT tapered despite visual
  similarity to Ball equivalents.

## Preparation Procedure

1. Mix each jar's substrate individually to ensure formula accuracy
2. Place vermiculite in a mixing bowl first, then layer brown rice powder on top
3. Add water directly onto the rice powder to wet it before mixing
4. Mix thoroughly — the final mixture should feel damp and cohesive, not wet or sloppy
5. Fill jars loosely, leaving 1/2 to 3/4 inch headspace at the top
6. Wipe jar interiors clean down to the substrate level with a tissue
7. Fill the remaining headspace with dry vermiculite as a contaminant barrier
8. Level off the dry vermiculite layer flush with the jar rim

## Steam Sterilization

The PF substrate can be steam-sterilized using a standard kitchen pot with a vegetable
steamer insert, eliminating the need for a pressure canner. This works because the
substrate contains no whole grains that require high-pressure sterilization to penetrate.

- Jars are placed in the pot with lid bands loose for steam penetration
- Jars should not sit in boiling water (risk of cracking); elevated on a steamer basket
- Steam at the lowest possible gentle boil for 60 minutes in a tightly covered pot
- A tight-fitting pot lid is essential — gas stoves offer the best [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]]
- After cooling, tighten lids and store in a cool, draft-free location until inoculation

## Common Problems

- **Dried substrate:** Caused by overheating during sterilization. Spores germinate but
  growth halts at a certain point. Remedy: increase water content in subsequent batches.
- **Sloppy/wet substrate:** Caused by using too much water or coarse vermiculite that
  holds less water than expected. Reduce water volume and verify vermiculite grade.
- **Uneven colonization:** Usually caused by packing the substrate too tightly. Fill jars
  very loosely — the substrate should not be compressed.

## Historical Significance

The PF TEK substrate formula democratized home mushroom cultivation. Before its publication
in 1992, successful cultivation required laboratory equipment, agar work, and cloned tissue
cultures. The PF substrate's simplicity — using powdered rice and vermiculite sterilized in
a kitchen pot — made cultivation accessible to anyone. The technique was rapidly copied
worldwide and formed the basis for the "Magic Mushroom Growers Guide" and countless
derivatives that remain in use today.
