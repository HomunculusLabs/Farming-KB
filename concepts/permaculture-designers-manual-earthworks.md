# Earthworks

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Bill Mollison, 1988), Chapter 9

Updated: 2026-04-18

Earthworks are the physical foundation upon which many permaculture systems are built. As with all techniques, it profits us to make as many uses of earth shaping as we can; it is shameful to see quarries, mines, and roads serving a single purpose, and usually left as a sterile system, when they could be shaped or planted to assist landscape diversity.

## Purposes of Earthworks

Earth can be moved for many productive reasons, classifiable as landscape restitution:

- To create shelter; to assist with foundations and to make areas level for floors
- To terrace hill slopes for stable padi crop, wet terraces, or gardens
- To raise banks or dig ditches as defences against flood, fire, attack, or wandering vegetation-eaters
- To drain or fill areas (to direct water flow or run-off)
- To create access roads to places commonly visited
- To get at earth materials (ochres, clays, minerals, fuels)
- To make holes of greatly varying sizes from fence-posts to dams, wells to bores
- To create special storages and enlarge living space (cellars and caves)
- To stop erosive forces carrying off soils (soil conditioning and erosion control)
- To prevent noise pollution (embankments)
- To permit recharge of groundwaters (swales and ripping)
- To create [[permaculture-designers-manual-ponds-and-dams]] for aquaculture

## Planning Earthworks

It is best to plan all aspects of the earth-moving process before the machines or labourers arrive on site:

1. Make an initial decision where to place the road, [[permaculture-designers-manual-dam-construction]], house site, drains, etc, using contour map and plan if necessary
2. Test the soil by auger holes, soil samples, and soil pits to determine if the soil is good enough (a good clay soil for dams is essential). Seek professional advice before deciding conclusively on placement
3. Peg out the site, using a level (simple or complex), a measuring tape, and stakes with red or white cloth attached (so the earth-moving machine can follow them)
4. Plan a place to store all the TOPSOIL removed during excavations. Never allow topsoil to be mixed with subsoil, but carefully remove it, to be later returned to the site as a growing medium and to stabilise subsoil erosion

## Soil Volume Changes

When soil is moved, it becomes loose. Soil air space and total volume may increase to 145% of the original. Even when compacted by machine, the fill may occupy 10% more space than the cut it came from:

- Conscientiously compacted clay in dam walls may settle as little as 1% over time
- Loose fill will eventually settle to 75% or less of its uncompacted volume
- This has great relevance to house foundations and wall stability

When topsoil is replaced over fill, the area should not be over-compacted, or waterlogging results. When topsoil is replaced over deep or solid subsoil in a cut, rip or loosen the base subsoil to allow root penetration -- just as old roads, quarries, parking areas, or heavily-trafficked fill need ripping before planting.

## House Site Preparation

To prepare a house site with drainage:

1. Careful survey. Place pegs outside the site. Call in the bulldozer
2. Strip off topsoil carefully and mound above and at either end of the site
3. Cut house/garden level; use subsoil fill for access roads only
4. Call in ditcher or backhoe to cut foundation and drainage trenches. Pour foundations and slab, paths; place drains and pipes
5. Call in small blade and bucket machine to replace topsoil and neaten the site. Some soil can be mounded to the windward side for hedges
6. Plant or seed all topsoil to prevent erosion
7. Fine-tune with barrow, rakes; check drainage

## Planting After Earthworks

Every time soil is moved, follow straight on with [[permaculture-designers-manual-plant-toxins-dryland]]. Have planned the planting and stabilisation of the area, and have plant materials on standby.

Two reasons demand immediate planting:

1. To prevent erosion, which can be severe on bare slopes at only 2% slope, especially in rains
2. To prevent invasion by unwanted volunteer plants that may become difficult to displace later

## Swales and Water Control

[[permaculture-designers-manual-swale-design-detailed]] are long, level excavations which can vary from broad, shallow depressions to deeper trenches. They are the primary earthwork for water control on slopes:

- Swales can be widened over time to create terraces
- They provide nutrients via water transport for swale crops
- Plants on swale floors include orchids, fungi, and ginseng in humid climates
- Cross-slope hollows to hold mulch and runoff should precede palm and tree planting
- Broad swales also provide access at harvest

## Interceptor Banks

Interceptor banks run surface water and throughflow off fields to natural valleys, isolating soil blocks for rain leaching and preventing a "cascade" effect downhill of salty overland flow or throughflow. Specifications include:

- Depth of 1.5 m, width of 4 m
- Spacing no more than 100 m apart, or 1 m vertical separation on slopes
- Bulldozer rams subsoil on the downhill wall
- Soil pit below the bank shows no throughflow and no rising salted groundwater

## Terracing

Earthworks for terracing include:
- Bench or road cuts on contour every 25-100 m as a great aid to regenerative forest processes
- Contour banks for permanent rehabilitation of damage
- Sub-terracing of land to help water infiltration on clays and clay-loam soils
- See [[permaculture-designers-manual-terracing-systems]] for detailed methods

## Erosion Control Structures

- "Net and pan" planting patterns are effective on overgrazed, eroded, mined, or bulldozed sites
- Small logs and branches pegged across erosion channels build up a layer-cake of silt and leaves
- Mulch behind logs and barriers quickly stabilises the seed bed for planting
- Small wire netting fences with stone-weighted hay uphill will trap silt and spread water
- Cross-swales of lemongrass or Vetiver grass serve the same function
- On very steep slopes, plant pampas, bamboo, lemongrass, and root-mat pioneers

## Tools and Equipment

Earth-moving tools range from hand-held implements (diggers, augers, rakes, shovels) to mechanical equipment (bulldozers, backhoes, ditchers, excavators, draglines). Explosives, hydraulic jets, and even erosive processes themselves can move earth. The key is matching the scale of earthworks to the site and purpose.
