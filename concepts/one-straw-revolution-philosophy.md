# One-Straw Revolution Philosophy

The One-Straw Revolution (1978) is Masanobu Fukuoka's manifesto of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]], born from a single moment of spiritual insight on a May morning in 1938 when he was 25 years old. Collapsing in exhaustion on a hillside overlooking Yokohama harbor, he experienced a flash of realization: "Humanity knows nothing at all. There is no intrinsic value in anything, and every action is a futile, meaningless effort." He described it as all concepts dissolving, revealing "true nature" standing behind them.

## The Spiritual Crisis

The experience came after a period of intense inner turmoil. Fukuoka had been working as a plant pathology researcher at the Yokohama Customs Bureau, splitting his time between microscopic investigations of citrus diseases and an active social life in the port city. Overwork led to fainting spells in the lab, then acute pneumonia. During recovery, facing the fear of death in a cold hospital room, he fell into deep depression:

"I could not sleep, could not apply myself to my work. In nightly wanderings above the bluff and beside the harbour, I could find no relief."

One night he collapsed against a large tree on a hill overlooking the harbor, neither asleep nor awake, until dawn on May 15th. As the morning mist disappeared and a night heron gave a sharp cry and flew away, everything changed: "In an instant, all my doubts and the gloomy mist of my confusion vanished. Everything I had held in firm conviction, everything upon which I had ordinarily relied was swept away with the wind."

His spirit became light and clear. He danced wildly for joy, hearing small birds chirping, seeing distant waves glistening in the rising sun, leaves dancing green and sparkling. "Something one might call 'true nature' stood revealed." The next day he resigned from his position on the spot.

## The Core Insight: Two Modes of Knowledge

Fukuoka's philosophy rests on a fundamental distinction between two modes of understanding:

### Discriminating Knowledge
The analytic, willful intellect that dissects the world into parts. This is the domain of science, yin-yang philosophy, and all systematic thought. It is necessary for practical affairs but ultimately a limited framework that separates humans from nature. Scientists "pore over books night and day, becoming nearsighted, and then invent eyeglasses to correct the nearsightedness they caused."

### Non-Discriminating Knowledge
Direct, intuitive apprehension of reality as it is, without interpretation by the intellect. This is the way infants perceive the world: they see light and dark, strong and weak, but make no judgments. It precedes and transcends analytical thought.

The tragedy of modern civilization, in Fukuoka's view, is that people believe discriminating knowledge is sufficient to understand reality. Doctors become necessary only because people create sickly environments. Formal schooling has no intrinsic value but becomes necessary when humanity creates conditions where education is required just to get along. Every technological solution creates the need for further technological solutions, in an endless spiral.

## Do-Nothing Farming as Practice

Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-and-spiritual-life]] is not laziness or abandonment. He distinguishes sharply between:

1. **Abandonment** -- leaving things to take their natural course all at once, which leads to failure (as he discovered when his first orchard withered from neglect). "If you apply this way of thinking all at once, before long things do not go so well."

2. **Natural farming** -- a careful, decades-long process of learning what the natural pattern is and then working within it, eliminating unnecessary human interventions one by one. His approach was the opposite of normal agricultural development. Where others asked "How about trying this?", he asked "How about not doing this? How about not doing that?"

Over thirty years, he eliminated plowing, fertilizer, compost, insecticides, and weeding, arriving at a method that requires little more than broadcasting seed and spreading straw. "Since advanced technology had nothing to do with growing this grain, it stands as a contradiction to the assumptions of modern science."

## The No-Stroke School

Fukuoka framed natural farming in martial arts terms, describing three schools:

### The Two-Stroke School (Modern Industrial Farming)
Believes victory is won by delivering the greatest barrage of techniques: plow, fertilize, spray, irrigate, harvest with machines. It "desires heaven's wisdom without grasping its meaning, and at the same time wants to make use of nature. Restlessly searching, it is unable to find anyone to propose to." This school creates the problems it then tries to solve.

### The One-Stroke School (Narrow Natural Farming / Organic Farming)
Seeks victory through skillful, self-conscious application of "natural" methods. The practitioner earnestly loves nature and proposes to her, but the relationship remains tentative. See [[natural-farming-vs-organic-farming]].

### The No-Stroke School (Pure Natural Farming)
Goes nowhere and seeks no victory. Arises spontaneously when unity exists between humans and nature. "The ultimate goal is not the growing of crops, but the cultivation and perfection of human beings." This is the school of [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]]: no tillage, no fertilizer, no pesticides, no weeding by cultivation.

## The Straw as Revolutionary Symbol

The book's title comes from Fukuoka's observation that spreading straw on rice fields -- returning all organic matter to the soil surface -- is simultaneously the simplest and most profound agricultural act. Straw connects with everything: fertility, germination, weed suppression, water management, and [[compost-biology-soil-food-web-inoculation]].

"I believe that a revolution can begin from this one strand of straw. Seen at a glance, this rice straw may appear light and insignificant. Hardly anyone would believe that it could start a revolution. Nevertheless, I have come to realize the weight and power of this straw."

He saw in one strand of straw the power to overturn the entire destructive edifice of modern agriculture. The straw must be scattered uncut and tossed randomly, "just as though the stalks had fallen naturally" -- not laid down neatly like garden mulch. "It has taken me over thirty years to reach this simplicity."

## Implications Beyond Farming

Fukuoka extended his critique to all of modern civilization:

### Pollution
Cannot be solved by treating symptoms. Each technological fix sows seeds for the next, more intractable problem. "The pollution of the earth, the sea, and the air is increasing. Scientists say that if we continue to pollute the environment at the present rate, humanity will perish. But why is it that when people try to clean up the environment, the environment just becomes dirtier?"

### Commercial Agriculture
Is inherently unstable. The farmer becomes a factor in an equation of increasing speed and efficiency, at the mercy of imported inputs and fixed selling prices. Self-sufficient natural farming, by contrast, provides true independence.

### Economic Growth
Does not equal happiness. A 0% growth rate with people living simply, close to the land, would be superior. "When the pursuit of economic growth becomes the goal of life, the result is confusion and suffering."

### True Culture
Is born within nature and is simple, humble, and pure. "Something born from human pride and the quest for pleasure cannot be considered true culture." The highest culture is one that works in harmony with nature rather than trying to dominate it.

### Health and Food
People have come to think of chemically grown vegetables as "clean" while food grown in living soil is considered dirty. The opposite is true. Natural food, grown without chemicals in living soil, is the foundation of true health. "Unless people become natural people, there can be neither natural farming nor natural food."

## The Illusion of Progress

Fukuoka challenged the narrative of continuous human progress. He traced the degradation of farming to a single cause: the human belief that nature can be improved through intervention. This belief led to plowing (destroying soil structure), fertilizing (creating dependency), spraying (killing beneficial organisms), and monoculture (eliminating biodiversity). Each step seemed like progress at the time; together, they constitute systematic destruction of the natural world.

## Testing the Realization

Despite his dramatic experience, Fukuoka remained humble: "I remain a simple man, just an old crow, so to speak." He spent thirty to forty years testing whether his realization was correct. "Not once have I found evidence to oppose my conviction." He told visitors to his orchard "again and again not to try to imitate me, and it really angers me if there is someone who does not take this advice to heart. I ask, instead, that they simply live in nature and apply themselves to their daily work."
