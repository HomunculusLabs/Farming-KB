## No-Tillage Grain Cropping and Simultaneous Rotation

No-tillage grain cropping eliminates ploughing by using continuous mulch cover and combining the traditional rotation sequence (legume/grain/root crop/pasture/fallow/legume) into a single grain/legume mixed crop. Mollison credits [[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s "The One-Straw Revolution" as the system that solved the problem of including grain and legume crops in permaculture without tractor or machinery.

## The Simultaneous Rotation Concept

Traditional rotation separates legume/grain/root crop/pasture/fallow into distinct time periods. Fukuoka "stacked" this rotation by sowing into the preceding crop, using white clover as a permanent nitrogen-fixing base. This collapses the time for successions—Mollison calls it "order in time by apparent disorder in space."

The method combines the usual rotation into a **single grain/legume mixed crop**, making it possible for any smallholder to produce heavy grain and legume crops without machinery. The same approach applies to tree-crop systems, including leguminous trees (wattle, black locust, tree lucerne) in orchards, nut crops, or timber situations.

## The Five-Plot Annual Cycle

### Setup (Summer Before)
After levelling, spread lime or dolomite, water in. Apply complete seed-free mulch cover of straw, seagrass, shredded paper, or sawdust at approximately 900 kg per 1,000 m².

### April
- Broadcast a thin layer of chicken manure
- Sow white clover at 1 kg/ha
- Sow rye and other grains at 7–16 kg/ha
- Sow rice at 6–11 kg/ha
- Use inoculated clover if this is the first crop
- Scatter seed first, then cover with straw to protect from birds
- In the second year, sow rye and clover into the ripe rice crop

### May
- First week: harvest last year's rice
- Dry crop on racks for 2–3 weeks, thresh
- Return all rice straw and husks to the field
- Resow unhusked rice within a month of harvesting

### June–September
- Light grazing of winter crops by sheep or geese assists stooling and adds manure
- When crop reaches ~150 mm, introduce ~100 ducks/ha to reduce pests and add manure
- Fields kept well-drained

### October
- Check rice growth, resow thin patches

### November
- Harvest rye, barley, and other grains mid-month
- Stack to dry 7–10 days
- Rice is trodden but recovers
- Return all straw and husks, rotating each straw type to a different plot

### December
- Only rice remains
- Summer weeds weakened by flooding for 7–10 days (clover yellowed but not dead)

### January–March
- Field kept at 50–80% saturation under rice
- Prepare seeds for April sowing
- The cycle continues using crop straw for mulch

## Key Grain Species

### Rice (Oryza sativa)
- Short-day cereal suited to latitudes up to 40° N and S
- Self-pollinating, responds to nitrogen (chicken manure)
- [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]]: soak seeds in 40% formalin diluted 50 times with water
- Good yields: 3,000–4,000 kg/ha (5,200–5,700 lb/acre)
- Plus 10,000 kg of straw per hectare for mulch

### Rye (Secale cereale)
- Long-day plant suited to cool areas
- Usually winter-grown; ripens in 37–71 days
- Wind-pollinated; autumn-planted at 55–60 kg/ha in irrigated ground
- Control ergot: 20% common salt solution, then rinse
- Good yields: 2,800–5,700 kg/ha

### Wheat (Triticum aestivum)
- Long-day plant for cool areas; some varieties grown in Alaska
- Needs 6–8 weeks sunny period for ripening
- Self-pollinating; won't cross-pollinate over hedge barriers
- Sown at 40–80 kg/ha; responds to nitrogen
- Good yield: ~1,100 kg/ha

### Barley (Hordeum vulgare)
- Long-day plant; sub-tropical to arctic range
- Spring types mature in 60–70 days, winter types in 160 days
- Self-pollinated; sown at 70–120 kg/ha under irrigation
- Has fewer pests than wheat
- Good yields: 3,000–4,700 kg/ha

### Buckwheat (Fagopyrum spp.)
- Wide climate range; F. esculentum best for cool moist climates
- Wide soil tolerance including infertile and acid soils
- Insect-pollinated; needs bees at 2 hives/ha
- Harvest at 10 weeks; threshes easily
- Good yields: 4,200–4,400 kg/ha
- Excellent green manure for poor soils

### Maize (Zea mays)
- Short-day plant suited to 40° latitude and sub-tropics
- Cross-pollinated by wind; needs tall windbreaks for variety purity
- Sow when soil temp is 60°F; thin to 1,200–1,400 plants/ha
- Good interplants: climbing beans on corn stalks; melons or pumpkins at ground level
- Yields: 1,200–1,500 kg/ha

## Legume Species for Mixed Cropping

### Broad Beans (Vicia faba)
- Long-day, cool climate; frost hardy
- Sow Apr–Jun at 200 kg/ha
- Stems resprout following autumn if hard-cut after harvest
- Yield: ~1,500 kg/ha

### Vetch (Vicia spp.)
- Long-day cool climate; grows on dunes and wet soils
- Often mixed with barley, oats, rye, or wheat
- Best sown with oats (seeds mature together)
- Yield: up to 1,000 kg/ha

### Lentils (Lens culinaris)
- Long-day plant for Mediterranean climates
- Very frost hardy; few pests
- Often sown with barley in alternating 2 m rows
- Yield: 600–1,300 kg/ha

### Lupins (Lupinus spp.)
- Long-day or green crop; [[glasshouse-heat-management-cool-climates-mollison]]
- Summer or winter annuals, maturing in 100–150 days
- Useful to pioneer land (if inoculated with phosphates)
- Yield: 800–1,000 kg/ha
- Perennial Russell Lupin being developed as human and stock food

## Three Approaches Compared

Mollison characterizes three [[comparison-nut-trees-vs-grain-farming-for-caloric-staple-production]] approaches as an evolutionary sequence:
- **FAO bulletin and Phillips & Young**: "consciously inorganic"—[[jadam-soil-compaction-heavy-machinery]] and sprays
- **Logsdon**: "consciously organic"—rotation well worked out but separated in time
- **Fukuoka**: "non-consciously organic"—stacked system with clover as permanent base

"The yields increase as the understanding increases, and that about sums it up."

## No-Tillage Adaptation Notes

- Where rice cannot be grown, dry-land rice or other grain species can substitute
- Spray irrigation replaces [[fukuoka-puddling-summer-flooding-soil-death-rice-cultivation]] in non-paddy systems
- In monsoon areas, summer rain suffices
- Where sparrows are a problem: mix grains with mud, press through wire-mesh, roll into clay pellets
- Seed at higher rates until broadcasting skill develops
- [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]] uses 4–5 times less seed than clean-tilled ground
- Fukuoka maintained this no-dig cycle for 25 years with improving soil, no fertilizer other than chicken and duck manure, no sprays, no herbicides
