# Growing Legumes — Beans and Peas

Everybody thinks legumes enrich soil by making nitrates. Actually, all nitrates formed in the roots are immediately incorporated into above-ground parts — leaves and then seeds. Thus legumes do not supply fertility to companion crops. However, if their tops are turned under while still green and lush, their decomposition does add significant quantities of nitrates for the following crop.

## The Nitrogen Myth Clarified

Nitrates are made by specialized soil-dwelling microorganisms that beneficially colonize legume roots, forming **nodules**. These organisms won't be present in soil that is highly depleted of organic matter.

### Inoculation

When [[starting-a-new-garden]] in humus-deficient soil, assume that nitrate-forming bacteria are not present in sufficient numbers:

- Inoculating legume seeds with these organisms makes sense
- People using depleted soil do this to avoid buying nitrate fertilizers
- Inoculants purchased in bulk are far cheaper per garden patch than buying nitrate fertilizers
- Small packets can cost as much as the fertilizer for a garden patch
- After the garden has been fertilized for a year or two, the needed organisms become established on their own
- You can verify this has happened by digging up root systems and finding nodules

## Soil Requirements

Legumes need substantial levels of minerals, especially:
- **Calcium** (lime)
- **Phosphorus**

The old farmer's adage: **"Feed your phosphate to your clover, feed your clover to your corn (plow it in), and you can't go wrong."**

## Peas

### Frost Hardiness

Peas are frost-hardy; some varieties are so winter-hardy they are used as overwintering [[cover-crops]]. They sprout in chilly soils, but it's best to chit the seeds if aiming for the earliest possible crop.

### Bush vs. Climbing Peas

| Type | Maturity | Trellis | Flavor | Yield Pattern |
|------|----------|---------|--------|---------------|
| Bush | Concentrated | No | Good | All at once |
| Climbing | Extended | Yes (6+ ft/180 cm) | Superior | Extended harvest |

### Planting Strategy

For continuous harvest, start three successive shelling pea patches (each with 5-6 short rows), sowing a new patch every 10 days. This provides a continuous harvest lasting more than a month.

### Trellis Construction

Make your own by weaving baling twine into a square-mesh fishnet of about 9 inches (23 cm) on a side. Attach horizontal strings between a top and bottom railing, then tie vertical strings to the horizontals.

### Bush Peas as Green Manure

Because bush peas leave the soil in magnificent condition, sow large areas as a potentially edible green manure on any beds worked in early spring but not needed for a few months. If needed before maturity, yank and compost. If they mature, eat, give away, or freeze the surplus.

### Fava Beans (Broad Beans)

- Even more frost-hardy than garden peas
- In mild climates, sow in autumn for spring harvest
- In cold climates, sow early spring but they take longer to mature
- Usually become diseased in hot weather
- Large-seeded types: mainly for shelling
- Small-seeded types: excellent cool-season green manure

## Snap Beans (French Beans)

### Climate

- Not frost-tolerant
- Sow in spring once soil reaches 60°F (16°C)
- In hot, humid climates: sow again after worst summer heat for autumn harvest

### Bush vs. Climbing

| Type | Root System | Drought Tolerance | Bearing Duration | Flavor |
|------|------------|-------------------|------------------|--------|
| Bush | Much smaller | Far less | ~1 month | Good |
| Climbing | Large | Good | As long as roots can grow | Superior |

**Strategy**: Put in a small planting of a quick-maturing bush variety to fill the gap before climbing beans start bearing.

### Runner Beans (*Phaseolus multifloris*)

- Grow more aggressively than climbing snap beans
- One plant may produce quite a few runners
- British-bred varieties produce elegant snap beans with richest flavor
- *Prizewinner* is recommended
- Difficulty with hot weather — best in maritime climates with cool summer nights
- Regularly spraying water on profusely blooming vines shortly before dusk increases pod set

### Dry and Shelling Beans

Same species as snap beans. Anyone who cooks a few pounds of beans a year might grow their own:
- Fresh beans (less than 1 year old) cook faster and taste better
- You can cook the seeds of any snap bean, but varieties bred for the purpose are usually best
- Almost all commercial dry bean production uses bush varieties
- The variety *Vermont Cranberry* (indeterminate climbing type) is given as an example

### Vegetable Soybeans (Edamame)

- Seeds eaten green, like edamame
- Specific to narrow latitude ranges — a variety that works at 45°N may not work at 35°N
- The plant completely goes into bloom and makes one set of seed that matures together
- For a long edamame harvest, grow several varieties sown at the same time
- To prepare: steam pods 5 minutes until seeds pop when gently squeezed. Don't overcook!

### Southern Peas (Cowpeas)

- Different species (*Vigna unguiculata*) from common beans
- Louisiana State University recommends for Deep South
- Produce only a single harvest per planting
- For continuous harvest, sow a new patch every few weeks
- Make an excellent quick green manure

## Pests: The Bean Weevil

An insect called the bean weevil (pea weevil) tunnels into ripening seeds. Each seed usually germinates successfully but has lost some food supply. These insects may be stopped by **freezing the seed for a few days shortly after harvest**.

## Saving Seed

- Many legumes are bee-pollinated in an organic garden
- Peas are often pollinated by other insects
- If saving seed, grow only one variety of each species
- To keep a variety pure, save seed from the most perfect plants
- For just growing (not seed saving), isolation is less critical
