## Bed Thickness

The thickness of beds depended on circumstances including manure quality,
whether plain or mixed with loam, heated or unheated buildings, and floor
vs. shelf placement. Floor beds were generally nine to fifteen inches deep:
about nine inches for plain horse manure in warm quarters, ten to fourteen
inches when manure and loam were mixed. In cool houses, beds were made a
few inches deeper to maintain a steady, mild warmth for longer periods.
Shelf beds were generally nine inches deep (one board width).

## The Bed-Making Process

The manure was brought in, shaken up loosely, and spread evenly over the
bed area. It was beaten down firmly with the back of the fork as the
builder progressed, continuing until the desired depth was attained. On
floor beds without overhead shelves, the manure was trodden down firmly
and evenly by foot. Well-prepared manure in good condition remained fairly
firm yet springy, while overmoist or poorly prepared material packed
together "like wet rotten dung."

## Temperature Monitoring

A thermometer was inserted into the bed immediately after construction.
Gardeners used "ground" or "bottom-heat" thermometers, but any common
thermometer would suffice. After two or three days the temperature was
checked daily. In tight cellars, warm moisture rising from the bed
condensed on the manure surface, making it wet. To counteract this,
Falconer spread straw or hay loosely over the bed, taking care to avoid
overcovering which could induce overheating inside the bed.

## The Proper Temperature for Beds

Falconer's best beds consistently showed maximum heat between 120 and 125
degrees Fahrenheit. If manure had been used a few days too soon, heat
might rise to 135 degrees, which was too warm. In this case he forked
over the surface a few inches deep to release heat, then compacted the bed
again after a couple of days. Boring holes with a crowbar was the common
method of reducing excessive temperature, filling the holes with finely
pulverized dry loam once heat subsided.

A too-high temperature was sedulously guarded against because it wasted
the substance of the manure, dried the bed interior, and the mushroom
crop was necessarily starved and short. However, beds indicating only 100
to 110 degrees after construction still produced excellent crops.

## Characteristics of Heat

Thicker beds generated higher heat. Firmly built beds warmed more slowly
but retained heat longer than loosely built ones. Cool materials built
solidly into beds were not apt to become very warm afterward. Falconer
always preferred making beds with moderately warm manure.

## Reviving Cold Beds

When circumstances prevented making beds at the optimal time and heat did
not rise above 75 to 80 degrees, the results depended on manure quality.
If otherwise good and fresh, adequate warmth was still possible. But if
the manure was stale, rotten, and inert, Falconer recommended breaking up
the bed, adding fresh horse droppings, mixing thoroughly, and remaking.
