# Avenue Cropping and Barrier Plants

Avenue cropping and barrier plants are two complementary strategies in permaculture design that integrate productive tree systems with protective plantings to optimize yields, conserve soil, and manage microclimates. Both approaches emphasize multi-functional plantings that serve multiple purposes simultaneously—fuel, mulch, soil improvement, wind protection, erosion control, and animal exclusion.

## Avenue Cropping

Avenue cropping is a tropical agricultural system adapted from the work of **Ray Wijewardene** that integrates fuelwood production, mulch generation, and soil improvement with intercropping. Trees are planted in avenues (parallel rows) with sufficient spacing to allow ground-level crops to grow between them, creating a layered polyculture system.

### Key Principles

- **Correct spacing**: Trees must be spaced widely enough to permit intercropping between the avenues while still providing adequate ground shade. Shade from the canopy helps suppress weeds and reduce evaporation, but excessive shade suppresses companion crops.
- **Lopping height**: Legume trees used in avenue systems are lopped (pruned) back to **0.5–1.0 metres** above ground level. This stimulates regrowth, provides a harvest of leafy material for mulch or fodder, and keeps the canopy manageable for intercrop light requirements.
- **Sowing timing**: Tree seeds should be sown **before the rains** arrive so that seedlings are established and ready to take advantage of the wet season growth period.
- **Two-crop sequences**: Avenue cropping typically employs two-crop sequences, where legume tree avenues alternate with rows or alleys of food crops, creating a rhythmic rotation across seasons.

### Species Selection

Primary legume tree species used in avenue cropping systems include:

| Species | Notes |
|---------|-------|
| **Tagasaste** (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) | Deep-rooted, nitrogen-fixing, excellent fodder and mulch |
| **Leucaena** (*Leucaena leucocephala*) | Fast-growing, high leaf protein, vigorous coppicer |
| **Gliricidia** (*Gliricidia sepium*) | Widely used in tropical agroforestry, reliable coppicer |

## Coppicing Limitations

While coppicing (repeated cutting back to ground level) is productive in the short term, it introduces significant long-term problems:

- **Tree weakening**: Repeated coppicing progressively weakens trees over **5–8 years**, reducing vigor, leaf yield, and ultimately killing the trees.
- **Seasonal confinement**: Coppicing should be confined to **warm, wet periods** when trees have the resources to recover rapidly. Lopping during dry or cool seasons stresses trees and slows regrowth.
- **Diminishing returns**: Each successive coppice cycle typically produces less biomass than the previous one as root reserves are depleted.

## Non-Coppiced Alternatives

To overcome the limitations of coppicing, Mollison recommends integrating **non-coppiced tall legumes** that provide seasonal leaf drop without requiring cutting, combined with **non-legume ground mulch species**.

### Tall Legumes for Seasonal Leaf Drop

| Species | Function |
|---------|----------|
| **Erythrina** (*Erythrina* spp.) | Deciduous legume; drops nitrogen-rich leaves seasonally |
| **Tipuana tipu** (*Tipuana tipu*) | Large canopy, excellent shade, self-mulching leaf drop |

These species are allowed to grow to full size, providing overhead shade and depositing leaf litter naturally without the stress of repeated cutting. Their root systems improve soil structure and fix nitrogen over a longer lifespan than coppiced trees.

### Non-Legume Ground Mulch Species

Ground-level mulch plants supplement the leaf fall from tall legumes:

| Species | Role |
|---------|------|
| **Vetiver** (*Vetiveria zizanioides*) | Dense clumping grass; erosion control, mulch, essential oil |
| **Lemongrass** (*Cymbopogon citratus*) | Aromatic grass barrier, mulch source, pest deterrent |
| **Comfrey** (*Symphytum* spp.) | Deep taproot, dynamic accumulator, prolific leaf production |
| **Pennisetum** (*Pennisetum* spp.) | Fast-growing grass for bulk mulch |
| **Nicotiana** (*Nicotiana* spp.) | Leaf mulch, insect deterrent (nicotine) |

These non-legumes provide consistent ground cover, suppress weeds, and contribute biomass for mulch without the management overhead of coppicing cycles.

## Barrier Plants

Barrier plants serve protective functions in permaculture systems—defining garden edges, excluding animals, moderating wind, and controlling erosion. Like all permaculture elements, barrier plants are chosen for **multiple functions**.

### Garden Barriers

Garden barriers are dense plantings that define garden boundaries, suppress grass invasion, and provide additional yields (herbs, mulch, pest deterrence).

Recommended species include:

- **Comfrey** — deep-rooted barrier, mulch factory, medicinal herb
- **Lemongrass** — aromatic edge, pest deterrent, tea herb
- **Vetiver** — dense hedge, erosion control on slopes
- **Sweet potato** (*Ipomoea batatas*) — living mulch, edible tubers, ground cover
- **Impatiens** — shade-tolerant edge plant in moist areas
- **Oncoba** (*Oncoba spinosa*) — spiny barrier for security
- **Echium** (*Echium* spp.) — bee forage, ornamental edge
- **Canna** (*Canna* spp.) — ornamental barrier, edible rhizomes, mulch
- **Agapanthus** (*Agapanthus* spp.) — fire-resistant edge, ornamental

### Animal Barriers (Bomas)

Animal barriers (or bomas) are living fences designed to exclude livestock and wildlife from sensitive areas. They rely on spiny, thorny, or toxic plant species.

| Species | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Lycium ferocissimum** (African boxthorn) | Extremely thorny, impenetrable hedge |
| **Acacia tortilis** (Umbrella thorn) | Thorny branches, durable living fence |
| **Oncoba spinosa** (Greeting thorn) | Dense spiny shrub, physical barrier |
| **Euphorbia tirucalli** (Pencil cactus) | Toxic latex deters browsing animals |

These species form dense, defensive hedges that are more permanent and multi-functional than conventional fencing.

### Windbreaks

Windbreaks reduce wind speed across cultivated areas, protecting crops from physical damage and reducing moisture loss through evapotranspiration. Effective windbreaks are multi-row plantings with a mix of species at different heights to create a permeable filter rather than a solid wall. Tall legumes and non-legume trees can serve dual roles as both avenue cropping trees and windbreak components when positioned at the windward edge of a site.

### Erosion Control

Barrier plants on slopes and contour lines slow water flow, trap sediment, and stabilize soil. **Vetiver** is particularly valued for erosion control due to its dense, deep root system that holds soil on steep banks. Contour-planted grasses (Pennisetum), comfrey, and sweet potato all contribute to slope stabilization through their root networks and canopy cover.

## Design Integration

In a mature permaculture design, avenue cropping and barrier systems work together as a cohesive whole:

1. **Avenues of tall legumes** (Erythrina, Tipuana) provide overhead canopy, nitrogen fixation, and seasonal mulch.
2. **Non-coppiced systems** replace short-lived coppiced trees, reducing long-term management burden and extending system lifespan.
3. **Ground mulch species** (Vetiver, comfrey, lemongrass) fill the understory, providing continuous biomass for soil building.
4. **Barrier plantings** on edges protect the system from wind, animals, and erosion while yielding herbs, mulch, and forage.

This integrated approach creates self-maintaining, productive landscapes that reduce external inputs and increase overall system resilience.

## Key Takeaways

- Avenue cropping integrates trees and crops in a synergistic layered system suited to the tropics.
- Coppicing is effective short-term but unsustainable beyond 5–8 years without replacement strategies.
- Non-coppiced tall legumes (Erythrina, Tipuana) offer a lower-maintenance alternative through natural leaf drop.
- Barrier plants serve multiple functions simultaneously—protection, production, and soil conservation.
- Living fences (bomas) provide durable animal exclusion without the cost and maintenance of conventional fencing.
- Windbreaks and erosion control barriers are essential elements of site design, especially on sloping land.
