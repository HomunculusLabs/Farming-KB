# Rust Fungus Life Cycles and the Complexity of Plant Pathology

## Overview

Rust fungi (order Pucciniales) cause more plant diseases than any other
group of pathogens. Their life cycles rank among the most complex in
biology, involving up to four distinct spore types, infection of two
unrelated plant species, and insect-mediated sexual reproduction. Black
stem rust of wheat (Puccinia graminis) is the most celebrated example,
but the principles extend to hundreds of rust species worldwide.

## Black Stem Rust: Puccinia graminis

Black stem rust attacks wheat through a life cycle requiring two hosts:
a cereal grass and the barberry bush (Berberis vulgaris). The cycle
involves four spore types and three acts of plant penetration.

### Uredial Stage (The "Photocopier")

Only one stage is actually rust-colored. Uredial pustules burst through
leaf and stem surfaces, exposing reddish, spiny uredospores. In heavily
diseased crops, a cloud of uredospores blankets the field after a wind
gust, infecting every plant. With permissive winds, spores travel
hundreds of miles, so one infected crop can spawn a continental epidemic.

Rust species encompass specialized races called formae speciales (f. sp.)
targeting particular crops. Puccinia graminis includes f. sp. tritici
(wheat), f. sp. avenae (oats), and f. sp. secalis (rye), with further
specialization among plant varieties within each crop.

### Germ Tube Navigation and Stomatal Entry

A uredospore germinates on the waxy leaf surface and sends out a germ
tube programmed to find stomata. The tube grows across the leaf, rising
over microscopic ridges formed by epidermal cell junctions and falling
into intervening valleys. Because only one in five or fewer lines of
epidermal cells contain stomata, growing across the leaf dramatically
increases the odds of finding an entry point.

Cornell plant pathologist Harvey Hoch demonstrated that germ tubes
recognize stomatal lips using physical cues alone. He fabricated plastic
replicas of leaf surfaces and showed that germ tubes recognized and
swelled over model stomata. Further experiments with films bearing
microscopic ridges of defined height showed that the fungus recognized
ridges matching the height of stomatal lips (0.5 micrometers) but
crawled over lower or higher ones. Hoch even manufactured microscopic
mazes for rusts, predicting growth patterns before seeding surfaces
with spores.

The cellular mechanism likely involves stretch-activated calcium
channels in the fungal membrane. As the hypha forces itself over a
ridge, channels open, allowing calcium to flood in and trigger cascades
that "inform" the fungus of the obstacle size. This tactile sensitivity
is related to the mechanism of human touch, though tuned to vastly
different spatial scales.

### Telial Stage and Winter Survival

Telial pustules develop within uredial lesions toward the end of the
growing season. Each teliospore is a pair of swollen cells on a stalk,
with thick pigmented walls designed for survival. Teliospores remain in
sores and survive winter in frozen stubble. In spring, warmth triggers
germination: both cells produce short hyphae, and meiosis packages four
daughter nuclei into bean-shaped spores at the tips of spikes.

A critical discovery from A.H.R. Buller's work: a droplet of fluid
appears at the base of every spore before launch. This "Buller's drop"
reveals these third-type spores are basidiospores, proving rusts are
basidiomycete fungi, distant cousins of mushrooms.

### Basidiospore Dispersal and Barberry Infection

Basidiospores are dispersed in air currents above stubble. Survival
depends on delivery to a barberry leaf, where the spore germinates and
penetrates directly through the cuticle without searching for stomata.
Barberry leaves lack the silica-hardened walls of cereal leaves, making
direct penetration feasible.

## Haustoria: The Placenta of Parasitism

Inside either host, the rust mycelium grows between cells and feeds
using haustoria. These breach plant cell walls but do not break plant
membranes; instead, the membrane dimples around each haustorium like a
glove, creating a placenta-like connection. This intimate connection
allows nutrient absorption while keeping host cells alive, prolonging
the parasite's banquet. Other fungi lack this finesse and obliterate
cells with waves of enzymes.

## Rust Sex: Insect-Mediated Fertilization

Rusts cannot reproduce sexually without insects. After growing within
the barberry leaf, the fungus forms flask-shaped spermagonia that
rupture the upper epidermis. Thin hairs protrude, and each spermagonium
exudes sugary nectar with masses of spherical spermatia that function
as sperm cells. Flies and other insects carry spermatia between
spermagonia. When a compatible spermatial nucleus is injected into a
receptive hair, it migrates down and divides repeatedly, transforming
the mycelium from monokaryon to dikaryon.

Buller hypothesized that insects pollinate rusts. By advising plant
pathologist John Craigie to study insects visiting infected leaves, he
played a leading role in solving the puzzle of rust sexuality.

## Flower Mimicry by Puccinia monoica

Some rust infections alter host appearance so that infected stalks
resemble flowers. Puccinia monoica produces clusters of leaves covered
with yellow spermagonia that are such perfect mimics of actual flowers
that fewer surrounding plants with yellow flowers get pollinated and
set seed. The fungus exploits insects' appetite for yellow
advertisements and changes the ecology of its entire habitat.

## Aeciospore Production and Return to Cereal

On the barberry leaf underside, the dikaryotic mycelium forms cup-shaped
aecia that sprout columns of aeciospores. When an aeciospore lands on
wheat, it germinates, penetrates through a stoma, feeds with haustoria,
and after a few days, uredial pustules burst through the leaf. The cycle
is complete: two hosts, four spore types, insect-mediated sex, and three
acts of plant penetration.
