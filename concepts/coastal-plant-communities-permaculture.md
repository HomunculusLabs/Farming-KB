# Coastal Plant Communities in Permaculture

Coastal environments present unique challenges for plant establishment: salt spray, sandy soils with low nutrient and water retention, strong winds, and occasional inundation by seawater. Permaculture design in [[mollison-designers-sea-level-change-coastal-zones]] relies on selecting species adapted to these harsh conditions while building soil, providing food, and creating sheltered microclimates for more sensitive plants. Coastal plant communities can be organised into functional layers — [[coastal-ground-covers-hedges-permaculture]] covers, hedges, shrubs, and trees — each contributing to a resilient coastal system.

## Understanding Coastal Conditions

Coastal sites vary widely, from exposed headlands and sand dunes to sheltered estuaries and mangrove margins. Key stress factors include:

- **Salt spray**: Carried by wind, salt coats foliage and soil, causing dehydration and ion toxicity in non-adapted plants.
- **Sandy soils**: Free-draining, low in organic matter, with poor water and nutrient retention.
- **Wind**: Constant exposure desiccates plants and causes physical damage.
- **Tidal inundation**: Estuarine and mangrove zones experience periodic flooding with saline water.
- **Frost**: Some coastal climates experience frost, requiring cold-hardy selections.

## Mangrove and Estuarine Trees

### Avicennia marina (Grey Mangrove)

The common mangrove tree of Australian estuaries, river banks, and sheltered coasts. Grey mangroves obtain oxygen through specialised **pneumatophore roots** that thrust upward from the mud. The soil is typically soft, moist, saline, and lacks profile development. After three separate boilings, the seed looks and tastes similar to avocado. The golden-yellow flowers yield nectar that produces a light, amber-coloured, pleasant honey from mid-autumn to early winter. Leaves are palatable to stock. The wood is extremely tough and hard, with conspicuous brown growth rings and a density of about 880 kg/m³, used for knees and elbows in boat building.

Companion species include **Casuarina glauca** (swamp oak) on less inundated margins in southern regions and **Ceriops tagal** (small stilted mangrove) in northern areas. The species tolerates some frost.

### Betula populifolia (Grey Birch)

A tree to 9 meters that thrives on sandy or swampy coastal soil. Extremely frost hardy, it is best planted in groves where individual trees provide mutual protection.

### Chrysobalanus icaco (Cocoa Plum, Icaco Palm)

An extremely hardy shrub or tree to 12 meters with branches that creep in all directions. Native to south Florida and the Caribbean, it grows in dense clumps on the seashore and in both fresh and saline swamps, extending well inland into mountain regions. The fruit was an important food of the Seminole Indians — eaten raw, stewed with sugar, dried like prunes, or made into jellies. Seed kernels are roasted and eaten. The tree may prove frost tolerant given its inland mountain range.

## Large Coastal Trees

### Elaeagnus angustifolia (Russian Olive, Bohemian Olive)

A deciduous small tree to 12 meters, native from the Mediterranean through Southwest Asia. Extremely tough as a seaside plant, hardy to -34°C, and capable of growing right to the sea in France. It forms a symbiotic relationship with *Frankia* spp nitrogen-fixing soil microbes, improving fertility on nutrient-poor coastal sands. Prefers dry, well-drained, alkaline soils in full sun. Yellow, sweet berries are eaten fresh or made into pies, sauces, jams, jellies, sherbets, and wines. An excellent windbreak and hedge species providing bird habitat [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control]]. Has thorns, making it a useful barrier plant.

### Metrosideros tomentosa (New Zealand Christmas Tree)

A large tree to 10 meters, somewhat frost hardy, commonly found growing with roots dangling into the sea. Very hardy to salt spray, it provides spectacular red flower displays and is culturally significant in New Zealand.

### Myoporum laetum

A fast-growing evergreen tree to 6 meters native to New Zealand, very hardy in exposed coastal positions and recommended for planting by the sea.

### Nyssa sylvatica (Black Tupelo, Pepperidge)

A frost-hardy tree to 30 meters with edible raw fruit useful in preserves. Related species *[[nyssa-aquatica]]* and *Nyssa ogeche* are also worthy of coastal trial.

### Persea borbonica (Red Bay, Bull Bay)

An evergreen, frost-hardy tree occurring in low areas near the sea along the eastern coast of the USA. A relative of avocado, its leaves are used as flavouring similar to bay leaves and can also be made into tea.

### Populus tremula (European Aspen)

A small, suckering, open-headed tree native to Europe that withstands full exposure to the sea. Hardy to frost and extremely resistant to salt.

### Swietenia mahagoni (West Indian Mahogany)

An evergreen tree to 24 meters native to southern USA and the Caribbean. Common in low-lying limestone swamps close to the sea, it produces wood of exceptional quality and survives just above saline water. Considered frost tender.

### Taxodium distichum (Bald Cypress)

A swamp tree of the Florida Everglades that has been found to be quite frost hardy, suggesting potential for wider coastal planting.

## Coastal Hedge Plants

Hedge plants serve as windbreaks, privacy screens, and [[permaculture-designers-manual-wildlife-corridors]] in coastal gardens:

- **Acanthocerus floridanus**: Thorny, providing effective barrier function.
- **Alyxia buxifolia**: An Australian native coastal hedge.
- **Carissa grandiflora** (natal plum): Thorny with edible fruit — a dual-purpose barrier and food plant.
- **Coccoloba uvifera** (sea grape): A large bush or small tree to 6 meters with scarlet-coloured foliage. Dioecious, producing reddish-purple to off-white fruit eaten out of hand and in jellies, jams, drinks, syrups, and wines. Extremely hardy to wind and salt spray but fairly tender to frost.
- **Coprosma repens** (New Zealand mirror plant): A reliable coastal hedging species.
- **Leptospermum laevigatum** (coast tea tree): Dried leaves used as tea; a good honey plant.
- **Correa alba** (Cape Barren tea): Native to the Bass Strait in Australia; leaves were once used as a tea substitute.
- **Quercus ilex** (holm oak): A classic coastal hedge and specimen tree.

## Coastal Shrub Layer

### Echyalaena tomentosa (Ruby Saltbush, Sturt's Saltbush)

A small shrub to 1 meter found throughout arid Australia in nearly all habitats. Well-adapted to saline and marshy conditions. The fruit is salty-sweet. Leaves can be boiled and eaten as a vegetable. In the Macdonnell Ranges, Aboriginal people soaked the fruit in water and drank the liquid as a sweet tea.

### Baccaris halimifolia (Silvering, Groundsel Bush, Salt Bush)

A frost-hardy deciduous shrub from 1–3 meters, producing pollen in autumn that supports bee populations through lean seasons.

### Ulex europaeus (Gorse)

A native of Europe and north Africa with multiple uses: flower buds are pickled in vinegar and eaten in salads, leaf buds are used as a tea substitute, and flowers can be made into wine. The stems exude a manna used in confectionery and beverages.

### Tamarix spp

Various tamarisk species serve as coastal hedges but tend to concentrate salt into leaves and deposit it on surrounding soil. Stems exude a sweet manna used in confectionery.

## Edible Coastal Ground Covers

### Tetragonia tetragonioides (Warrigal Cabbage, New Zealand Spinach)

A shrubby, creeping herb common to sheltered beaches, saltmarshes, arid woodlands, and plains, extending into central Australia. Also native to the coast of New Zealand. This is the **only plant native to Australia to be cultivated internationally as a vegetable**. Leaves are cooked like spinach and the succulent young leaves can be eaten raw in salads. A valuable addition to any coastal food garden.

### Sesuvium portulacastrum (Sea Purslane)

A fleshy-leaved, shrubby herb native to beaches and mudflats of northern Australia and New South Wales. Leaves are reported to make an excellent vegetable and pickle ingredient.

### Suadea australis (Sea Blight)

A fleshy-leaved plant native to most of the Australian coast. A shrubby herb (15–30 cm) of saltflats, inland saltpans, and sandbanks behind mangroves. Early settlers used the plant as a vegetable and in pickles.

### Sarcocornia quinqueflora (Chicken Claws, Beaded Glasswort)

Forms extensive colonies on tidal mudflats with succulent, jointed, leafless stems rarely exceeding 50 cm. The fleshy stem absorbs the flavour of pickles, though the woody centre within each stem is a nuisance. The English native *Sarcocornia stricta* is preferred for culinary use.

## Salt-Tolerant Forage and Grain Crops for Coastal Areas

### Distichlis palmeri (Palmer Saltgrass)

A perennial to about 0.5 meters growing in tidal flats and salty marshlands off the Gulf of California. It thrives with tidal inundation of seawater. Seed falls to the water and is traditionally washed ashore and harvested by the Seri Indians. Once established, it should not need replanting, as it spreads rapidly by rhizomes. Field tests with hybrid cultivars are producing 1 tonne of grain per hectare — a promising salt-tolerant grain crop.

### Distichlis spicata (Salt Grass)

Distinct inland and seashore ecotypes exist. Approximately 20,000 hectares of salt flats near Mexico City are under cultivation for cattle fodder.

### Leptochloa fusca (Kallar Grass)

A highly salt-tolerant perennial forage species growing best on permanently wet soils in tropical and southern Africa.

### Salsola iberica (Russian Thistle)

A salt and drought-tolerant annual with crude protein of 15–20% and a similar amino acid breakdown to alfalfa, making it a valuable forage species in saline environments.

## Design Principles for Coastal Permaculture

1. **Windbreak first**: Establish a multi-row windbreak of hardy trees and shrubs before planting sensitive species. Use a graduated height profile, tallest on the windward side.
2. **Build soil**: Use ground covers like pigface and African daisy to stabilise sand and build organic matter.
3. **[[query-how-do-i-create-microclimates-in-my-garden]]**: Sheltered pockets behind windbreaks support a wider range of species.
4. **Use pioneers**: Salt-tolerant nitrogen fixers (Russian olive, Casuarina) [[query-how-does-rock-dust-improve-soil-fertility]] for subsequent plantings.
5. **Plan for frost**: In frost-prone coastal areas, select species with demonstrated cold tolerance.
6. **Emphasise food production**: Prioritise edible coastal species (warrigal cabbage, sea purslane, natal plum, sea grape) for immediate yields while establishing longer-term tree crops.
7. **Respect natural zonation**: Mimic natural coastal plant zonation from beach to dune to back-dune in garden layouts.

Coastal plant communities, when designed with appropriate [[mollison-plant-nursery-species-selection]] and layered planting, can transform harsh seaside environments into productive, beautiful, and ecologically functional permaculture landscapes.
