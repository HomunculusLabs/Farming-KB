# Forage Systems and Animals in Permaculture

Mollison declares that "never in the history of the world has anybody designed and implemented animal forage systems." The design of integrated animal-forage-plant systems is one of the most productive and underdeveloped areas of [[permaculture-design]]. Animals are the "mobile part of the forest" -- they cycle nutrients, control pests, prepare ground for planting, and convert low-grade forage into high-quality protein.

## The Principle of Beneficial Interaction

Mollison's fundamental insight is that systems designed with three harmonious elements tend to generate additional benefits beyond those intended. "When we put three elements in conjunction so that they are pretty harmonious, other beneficial results come out that we didn't design." This is the essence of forage system design -- not just feeding animals, but creating cascading beneficial relationships.

He also emphasises that animals on landscape trigger a response: "We let cattle go on landscapes, and the landscapes respond. The cattle disfavor plants that they like and thereby produce a system of plants that they don't like." Some of these unpalatable plants are poisonous to cattle. The landscape actively responds to grazing pressure, and the designer must understand these feedback dynamics.

## Zonation and Animal Placement

Mollison's zonation system places animals strategically according to their management needs and their contribution to the system. Zone two should include the range of high manurial animals like chickens, housed at the edge of zone one. The system deliberately exploits a larger zone to enrich a smaller one: "We are deliberately creating a nutrient vortex."

For goats, a corridor edged with mulberry or other browse plants allows goats to self-prune as they pass through. Chickens and ducks can be contained within zone two because they require daily attention. The pig is stocked to the back of zone two, as they can be messy in confined quarters. Milk sheep come closer in; wool sheep go further out. A milk cow in zone two might extend the zone to two acres -- still a very limited area that can feed many people.

The [[permaculture-zones-system]] principle means that acorns gathered from outer zones and fed to chickens produce high-value manure and mulch. Nut husk mulch is alkaline and high in calcium. Material from outer zones is recycled by animals for use in the intensive zone.

## The Chicken-Orchard System

The chicken-orchard is Mollison's most detailed forage design. The system integrates chickens with fruit trees, shrubs, and ground forages in a mutually beneficial cycle:

- **White mulberry** provides forage equal to "a double crop of grain" at 17% protein. Chickens get seed long after mulberries are gone. Four-foot cuttings root in the first autumn and produce fully by the next year.
- **Hawthorn and mountain ash** provide winter forage.
- **Comfrey, cleavers, and chard** provide summer greens.
- **Chinese chestnuts and hazelnuts** grow within the chicken run, producing double to quadruple the yield of trees outside the pen because chickens cycle nutrients.
- **Black locust** (one acre) produces 10,000 pounds of chicken feed with a [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]] grain plot adding another 2,000 pounds -- enough for 40 chickens year-round.

The system works by eliminating grain purchase, reducing pest pressure (chickens eat encysted parasite eggs, insect larvae, and fallen fruit that hosts pests), and cycling manure directly to tree roots. "These trees around here don't look as if they are ever going to be big. They will get sick before they get big." But in an active chicken system, an English walnut can reach 60 feet in 120 years, yielding heavily.

## Flock Management

Mollison's specific recommendations:
- **Density**: Maximum 400 chickens per acre (break-even point). Above this, pasture weakens from nitrogen overload and chickens eat it out. Optimal flock size is 50-60 per group, with five roosters to prevent hens from wandering.
- **Separation**: Chicken houses 150-200 feet apart prevent flock mixing.
- **Breeds**: Run four different breeds of 60 chickens each. Heavy breeds lay better in winter; light breeds in spring/summer.
- **Health**: Chickens in these systems "seem to maintain good health and they lay until they die" -- often six years or more.

## Chickens on Slopes

Mollison describes using chickens on sloped terraces as a nutrient cycling strategy. Chickens are placed at the top of the terrace system with their house above the garden. The chickens scratch and discharge manure on the paths, and their waste moves downhill through the system. Only three or four terraces are permitted, staggered to create multiple microenvironments. The chicken house can alternatively be placed at the bottom, with chickens above kicking mulch and manure downhill -- a passive fertility system.

## Pigs as System Regenerators

Pigs serve multiple functions in Mollison's designs. When released into zone one and two gardens in the fall, confined by temporary fencing with a strand of electric wire inside, they clean up crop residue, eat grubs, slugs, snails, and even poison snakes. In cool fall weather, the odor is less so pigs can be moved closer to the house. The result is "weed and pest free soil fertilized and lightly cultivated by rooting, ready for spring planting."

Pigs are also useful in pastures where they selectively root out thistle and some other pasture weeds, and in orchards after harvest where they glean drops that could otherwise harbor pests over winter. Mollison describes a Gloucester Old Spot breed specifically developed as an orchard forager. The key insight from the Indian government piggery case study is that pigs can be run on [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] species like breadfruit, eliminating grain feed bills entirely and creating a net export of fruit and pigs.

## Ducks, Sheep, and Integrated Pond Systems

Ducks with sheep create a synergistic system. Ducks eat two things fatal to sheep: shallow water snails that carry fluke, and fluke eggs. "Ducks don't compete with the sheep." Ducks are also excellent fly catchers when baited to insect-attracting plants.

In integrated pond systems, ducks can be kept "on a slowing flow into ponds." The ideal situation keeps ducks flowing through the system, with their manure entering the ponds, supporting algae, mussels, and fish, and producing duck manure, fish, and wild rice as outputs. Mollison calculates eight ducks per quarter acre of pond, noting that the more ducks you add, the more manure you get, and wild rice grows well above the duck level.

## The Apple-Blackberry-Cattle Succession

Mollison describes a remarkable emergent system where an apple seed sprouted in a blackberry clump in a pig pen 17 years ago. The blackberry prevented grass competition at the roots (grasses produce chemicals hostile to trees), acted as living mulch, and pre-pruned the tree to a standard form. Cattle eventually pushed through the blackberries to eat fallen apples, smashing the brambles and releasing the tree. At 17 years old, the tree is 60 feet high and 70 feet across, yielding 70 bushels -- "a phenomenal tree."

This illustrates the broader principle: "Fire helps the grasses; brambles help the trees." The conflict between pioneer species in grasslands can be designed rather than left to chance.

## Forage Forests

Mollison classifies forests by function: essential forests (burial, spiritual), food forests, fuel forests, forage forests (for species other than man), and structural forests. These elements can mix inextricably in a "patchwork situation." The design task is to decide which elements to build into the animal forage systems, which determines which forest elements to favor and which to weaken.

Inner zones need to be far more productive in human and animal forages. The manager looks for high forage-drop species such as oak, cherry, and apple, removing selectively and replanting or encouraging selectively. The forester should be a very busy person, managing for close-in windbreak, then for human and domestic species forages.

## Animals as Phosphate Mobilizers

Mollison argues that removing animals from forests removed critical nutrient cycling. "The forest produced them and sent them out, saying 'Feed me.' In this way, the forest attracted the phosphate to itself." Free-flying pigeons, marmots, gophers, and moles were the phosphate mobilizers of pre-agricultural landscapes. Their removal impoverishes the forest.

## Understanding Yields from Animals

Mollison distinguishes between direct yields (eggs, meat, milk) and derived or secondary yields (manure for methane, pest control, ground preparation). "Yield is the sum of useful energy stores" -- not just pounds of product but the total energy conserved and generated. The designer must understand "the intrinsic functions, what is natural for [an element] to do, the things it can't help doing by virtue of just being itself, being alive."

## Broader Implications

Sixty percent of the world's grain is fed to livestock. Mollison argues that designed forage systems could dramatically reduce this dependence. He challenges the native species purist: "I use only native plants; they are native to the planet Earth." The permaculture approach to forage is pragmatic -- selecting species for function regardless of origin, while being mindful of invasiveness.
