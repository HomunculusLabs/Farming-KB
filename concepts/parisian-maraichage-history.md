## Parisian Maraichage History

La culture maraichère — the intensive market gardening tradition of Paris — represents
one of the most remarkable achievements in the history [[urban-agriculture-organizations-and-networks]]. Between
1850 and 1900, the Parisian maraichers (market gardeners) developed a system of
year-round [[coleman-rye-straw-mat-cold-frame-insulation]]).

## Origins and Development

The system reached its peak between 1850 and 1900, when the maraichers developed a
comprehensive approach combining hotbeds heated with decomposing horse manure, glass-
covered cold frames, glass cloches (bell jars), and intensive interplanting techniques.
The results were extraordinary: Paris was self-sufficient in fresh vegetables year-
round, and the growers exported significant quantities to England.

## Scale and Productivity

The cultivated land of the Parisian growers covered approximately six percent of all
the land within the city limits of Paris — roughly 600 hectares out of a total land
area of 10,000 hectares. Many of the nineteenth-century "gardens" occupied addresses
that are now office buildings and apartment complexes. The system was truly urban
agriculture on a massive scale.

The average Parisian market garden was between one and two acres in size. Despite
this small footprint, productivity was extraordinary. Growers achieved at least four
and usually up to eight harvests per year from the same piece of ground. The
combination of [[cold-climate-gardening]] meant that
multiple crops occupied the same bed simultaneously. A single hotbed might be sown
with broadcast radish and carrot seed, then interplanted with lettuces. The radishes
harvested first, making room for carrots growing among the lettuces. When the lettuces
were harvested, the carrots completed their growth. After the carrots, cauliflower
transplants were set out. This level of [[mollison-designers-market-garden-intensive-production-design]] from small acreage has
rarely been matched since.

## The Horse Manure System

The energy foundation of the Parisian system was horse manure. In the nineteenth
century, Paris was a city of horses — an estimated 100,000 or more horses provided
transportation, haulage, and power. The resulting manure, mixed with straw bedding
from the city stables, was available in enormous quantities. The maraichers used
between 100 and 400 tons per acre annually.

Fresh horse manure was used as the heat source for hotbeds — the decomposing manure
generated sufficient heat to keep cold frames warm enough for producing salads in
January and early cucumbers and melons in May and June. Manure was also packed into
the walkways between glass-covered frames for additional heat. Once the manure had
lost its heat and broken down into crumbly compost, it was shoveled out and used as
a soil amendment. The composted horse manure and straw mixture was widely regarded as
the finest possible fertilizer for vegetable production.

This recycling system was remarkably sustainable. The heat for winter production and
the fertility for the soil were both by-products of composting the "transportation
wastes" of the day. Many growers noted that soil fertility actually increased from
year to year despite intensive production, a testament to the quality and quantity of
organic matter returned to the soil.

## Techniques and Tools

The maraichers developed highly specialized techniques. Hotbeds were covered with
glass frames, and additional insulation was provided by one-inch-thick mats made of
rye straw that could be rolled out over the glass on cold nights. Glass bell jars
(cloches) 18 inches in diameter were used for protecting transplants and mature heads
of lettuce. Some growers owned up to three thousand cloches, each individually
vented on sunny days using a small notched stick to prop up one edge.

Access paths through the gardens were only 10 inches wide — too narrow for
wheelbarrows. Manure was delivered using woven willow basket backpacks called hottes,
which had an extension that arched over the worker's head. The worker bent forward
to deposit the manure where needed. This extreme space efficiency maximized the
proportion of land devoted to growing.
