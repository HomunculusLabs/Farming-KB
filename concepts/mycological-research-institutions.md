# Mycological Research Institutions

The study of fungal biodiversity is conducted by a globally distributed network of research institutions, universities, government agencies, and herbaria. The breadth of institutions involved in mycological research reflects the ecological and economic importance of fungi across virtually every discipline of biology.

## Major Institutional Hubs

### United States

The United States hosts the densest concentration of mycological research centers:

- **USDA Agricultural Research Service** — Multiple laboratories conduct fungal systematics and applied mycology, including the Systematic Botany and Mycology Laboratory (Beltsville, Maryland), the Western Regional Plant Introduction Station (Washington State), the National Center for Agricultural Utilization Research (Peoria, Illinois), and the Plant Protection Research Unit (Ithaca, New York).
- **USDA Forest Service** — The Forest Products Laboratory (Madison, Wisconsin) houses the Center for Forest Mycology Research, a major center for wood-inhabiting fungi. Additional forestry sciences laboratories operate in Corvallis, Oregon and Pineville, Louisiana.
- **Field Museum of Natural History** (Chicago, Illinois) — A major center for fungal systematics, biodiversity inventory, and herbarium collections.
- **Smithsonian Institution** (Washington, D.C.) — Houses extensive fungal collections and supports biodiversity research through the National Museum of Natural History.
- **Harvard University** — The Farlow Herbarium is one of the world's premier collections of fungi and lichens.
- **University of Wisconsin–Madison** — Strong programs in fungal ecology and forest mycology.
- **Oregon State University** — Multiple departments host mycologists working on forest mycology, lichenology, and truffle ecology, including researchers such as [[joseph-spatafora]].
- **Louisiana State University** — Active in fungal biodiversity, systematics, and tropical mycology.
- **University of Michigan** — Notable for work on fungal ecology and conservation.

### Europe

- **Centraalbureau voor Schimmelcultures (CBS)** (Baarn, Netherlands) — One of the world's largest fungal culture collections and a critical resource for fungal identification and taxonomy.
- **CABI Bioscience** (Egham, United Kingdom) — An international organization focused on agricultural and environmental mycology.
- **Freie Universität Berlin** (Germany) — Houses significant lichen and fungal collections.
- **Royal Botanic Gardens** and other European institutions maintain important herbaria with fungal holdings.
- **Universidad Complutense de Madrid** (Spain) — David Hawksworth, a leading figure in fungal biodiversity estimation, is based here; the institution also hosted [[gerald-bills]] during his tenure at Merck.

### Canada

- **Canadian Forest Service** — Pacific Forestry Centre (Victoria, British Columbia) conducts research on forest pathology and fungal biodiversity.
- **University of Toronto** — Departments of Botany and Environmental Biology host mycological research.
- **University of Saskatchewan** — Home to researchers in applied microbiology and fungal genetics.
- **University of Guelph** — Environmental biology programs include significant mycological components.

### Latin America

- **Instituto Nacional de Biodiversidad (INBio)** (Costa Rica) — A pioneering institution in tropical biodiversity inventory that has included fungi in its survey programs. Costa Rica's National Biodiversity Inventory has been a model for comprehensive ATBI (All Taxa Biodiversity Inventory) approaches.
- **Universidade Regional de Blumenau** (Santa Catarina, Brazil) — Active in fungal ecology and biodiversity studies.

## Government and Conservation Agencies

- **USGS Patuxent Wildlife Research Center** (Laurel, Maryland) — Supports fungal biodiversity monitoring and inventory research.
- **Bureau of Land Management** (Boise, Idaho) — Conducts lichen and fungal surveys relevant to land management.
- **Norwegian Institute of Land Inventory** (Ås, Norway) — Engaged in fungal inventory for forest management.

## International Collaboration

The collaborative nature of mycological research is exemplified by the production of comprehensive reference works such as *Biodiversity of Fungi*, which involved more than 88 mycologists from institutions across at least 10 countries. This international cooperation is essential because:

1. Fungal diversity is global, but expertise in particular groups is concentrated in specific institutions
2. Standardized methods require international consensus
3. Many tropical regions with the highest fungal diversity have limited local mycological infrastructure
4. Herbarium and culture collections serve as international resources accessible to researchers worldwide
5. Conservation of fungal biodiversity requires coordinated cross-border efforts

The collaborative model documented in [[biodiversity-of-fungi-database-specimen-management]] shows how institutions share specimen data and coordinate sampling protocols across borders.

## The Role of Culture Collections

Living fungal culture collections such as the **American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)** (Manassas, Virginia) and the **CBS** (Netherlands) serve as indispensable resources for:

- Maintaining reference strains for identification
- Preserving type specimens and authentic cultures
- Providing material for biochemical and molecular analyses
- Supporting biotechnological and pharmaceutical screening programs
- Safeguarding genetic diversity of cultured fungi

These collections are foundational to [[biodiversity-fungal-culture-preservation]] and support both pure and applied mycological research.

## Herbaria as Research Infrastructure

Herbaria such as those at the Field Museum, Smithsonian Institution, New York Botanical Garden, and Harvard's Farlow Herbarium provide the physical infrastructure for fungal biodiversity research. They house voucher specimens that document species distributions, fruiting phenologies, host associations, and historical changes in fungal communities over time spans exceeding 100 years. The practices of specimen curation are detailed in [[biodiversity-of-fungi-herbarium-specimen-curation-best-practices]] and [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]], which establish standards for preserving fungal specimens for future study.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Mycological research institutions play a vital role in supporting mushroom cultivation through culture collections that maintain cultivatable strains, taxonomic expertise that ensures correct species identification, and research programs that develop improved cultivation methods. The connection between institutional research and practical cultivation is documented in [[research-institutions-environmental-mycology]] and related concept pages.
