## Heavy Metals and Fungi in Aquatic, Marine, and Lichen Communities

## Overview

Fungi play critical roles in mediating the impacts of heavy metal contamination across terrestrial, aquatic, marine, and lichen-dominated ecosystems. Their interactions with metals—including uptake, sequestration, detoxification, and trophic transfer—have profound implications for ecosystem health, bioremediation, and environmental monitoring. This concept page synthesizes findings from key studies on fungal responses to heavy metals across these diverse habitats.

---

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizae in Contaminated Soils

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi serve as a critical interface between plants and soil contaminants, particularly heavy metals. These symbiotic fungi can significantly alter metal bioavailability and plant uptake patterns.

### Metal Uptake and Retention

Joner and Leyval (1997) demonstrated that AM fungi enhance cadmium (Cd) uptake from contaminated soils but simultaneously reduce the transfer of Cd to the host plant. The fungal component acts as an effective sink, retaining Cd within its own biomass and thereby shielding the plant from toxicity. This protective mechanism was further confirmed by Tonín et al. (2001), who observed similar results for both Cd and zinc (Zn) in clover systems, where mycorrhizal colonization reduced metal translocation to above-ground plant tissues.

### Colonization Dynamics Under Stress

Heavy metal contamination generally delays AM fungal invasion of root systems, likely due to both direct toxicity to [[fungal-hyphae]] and metal-induced changes in root exudate chemistry. However, the resilience of AM associations is remarkable. Call and Davies (1988) showed that AM inoculation significantly increased grass survival and growth on lignite mine overburden—substrate that would otherwise be hostile to plant establishment. This finding has important practical implications for the use of AM fungi in phytostabilization and revegetation of disturbed, contaminated lands.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Ecosystem Restoration

Beyond passive tolerance, [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] can actively contribute to the degradation of persistent organic pollutants, broadening their utility in ecological restoration.

### PCB Decomposition

Donnelley and Fletcher (1994) conducted a landmark screening of 21 mycorrhizal fungal species for their capacity to metabolize polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs). Of these, 14 species degraded PCBs by at least 20%. Two species stood out for their exceptional performance:

- **[[radiigera-atrogleba]]** and **[[hysterangium-gardneri]]** each degraded approximately 80% of 2,2'-dichlorobiphenyl, demonstrating that mycorrhizal fungi possess significant [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]] for dechlorination of persistent organic pollutants.

### Survival in Extreme Conditions

Certain mycorrhizal species exhibit tolerance to the extreme conditions found in contaminated sites. [[laccaria-proxima]] and [[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]] were shown to survive under the low redox (reducing) conditions characteristic of landfill environments, suggesting their suitability for restoration of anaerobic, metal-contaminated substrates.

### Restoration Caveats

N.C. Johnson (1998) cautioned against the application of high fertilizer rates in taconite (iron ore) mine restoration efforts. Excessive fertilization can suppress mycorrhizal colonization by reducing plant dependency on the symbiosis, thereby undermining the long-term benefits that mycorrhizal networks provide for plant establishment and community stability on degraded sites.

## Heavy Metals in Aquatic Ecosystems

Aquatic hyphomycetes—fungi that decompose leaf litter in streams and rivers—are sensitive indicators of metal pollution and play key roles in detrital food webs.

### Community-Level Impacts

Maltby and Booth (1991) documented the effects of coal mine effluent on aquatic fungal communities in a receiving stream. Upstream of the effluent discharge, 8 hyphomycete species were present, but this number dropped to just 4 species downstream. The reduction in fungal diversity was accompanied by a measurable decrease in leaf litter decomposition rates, demonstrating that metal contamination impairs the ecosystem-level function that these fungi provide [[comparison-soil-protozoa-vs-nematodes-in-nutrient-cycling]].

### Metal Uptake and Trophic Transfer

Duddridge and Wainwright (1980) investigated metal uptake patterns in aquatic fungi and reported the following order of uptake: Zn > Pb > Cd. Interestingly, they found that Cd and Zn were stimulatory to fungal growth at low concentrations—a phenomenon consistent with the concept of hormesis, where low doses of a toxicant produce beneficial effects. However, Cd at higher concentrations is lethal. The study also documented Cd transfer from the fungus Pythium to the freshwater amphipod Gammarus pulex through trophic transfer. At Cd body burdens of 150–170 mg/g, no shrimp survived, highlighting the potential for metal biomagnification through fungal-mediated food chains.

### Water Chemistry Modulates Toxicity

Bärlocher demonstrated that water hardness significantly influences metal toxicity to aquatic fungi. In hard water, calcium ions competitively complex with metal ions, reducing their bioavailability and toxicity. This geochemical buffering effect means that the same metal concentration can have vastly different ecological impacts depending on ambient water chemistry.

## Heavy Metals in Marine Ecosystems

Fungi are ubiquitous in marine environments, including salt marshes, estuaries, and coastal sediments, where they encounter complex mixtures of contaminants.

### Fungal Response to Mercury Contamination

Newell and Wall (1998) investigated fungal activity in a mercury-contaminated salt marsh. Contrary to expectations of suppression, they observed increased fungal activity at the contaminated site, which contained 71 mg/g total Hg and 190 ng/g methylmercury (MeHg). Indicators of elevated fungal activity included higher ergosterol concentrations (a biomarker for fungal biomass) and increased ascospore production. This finding suggests that certain fungal communities in marine environments may develop tolerance or even thrive under metal contamination, potentially playing roles in metal cycling and speciation in coastal ecosystems. The implications for mercury methylation and demethylation processes—and consequently for mercury entry into marine food webs—warrant further investigation.

## Heavy Metals and Lichens

Lichens—symbiotic associations between fungi (mycobionts) and photosynthetic partners (photobionts)—are among the most sensitive and widely used [[biological-indicators-of-soil]] atmospheric metal deposition.

### Bioaccumulation Without Detriment

Richardson (1988) established that lichens accumulate heavy metals from the atmosphere without apparent detriment to their physiology or growth. Because lichens lack roots and absorb nutrients (and contaminants) directly from the air and precipitation, they serve as passive, integrative samplers of atmospheric pollution. Their metal concentrations have been used extensively to map pollutant plumes around point sources such as smelters, incinerators, and industrial facilities.

### Differential Species Sensitivity

Fritze et al. (1989) documented dramatic lichen community changes near a Cu/Ni smelter. Within 500 meters of the smelter, epiphytic lichens were entirely absent, reflecting the extreme sensitivity of many lichen species to copper and nickel deposition. However, differential tolerance among species means that pollution-resistant lichen taxa may persist closer to emission sources, while sensitive species are restricted to more distant, less contaminated sites. This gradient in community composition provides a powerful biological indication of contamination severity.

### Lichens vs. Mosses as Biomonitors

Kosta-Rick et al. (2001) compared metal accumulation in lichens and mosses and found that lichens consistently accumulated higher concentrations of metals including Cd, Cu, Hg, Pb, Sb, Sn, and Zn. This finding reinforces the utility of lichens as superior biomonitors for atmospheric heavy metal deposition, as their higher metal concentrations provide greater analytical sensitivity and more robust spatial resolution in pollution mapping studies.

## Transition to Radionuclides

The principles governing fungal interactions with heavy metals extend directly to radionuclides—radioactive isotopes of metals and other elements. Key parallels include:

- **Bioaccumulation**: Fungi concentrate radionuclides from soil and water, similar to their behavior with stable heavy metals.
- **Trophic transfer**: Radiocaesium (¹³⁷Cs) and radiostrontium (⁹⁰Sr) can enter food chains through fungal mediation, particularly through mycorrhizal associations with plants and through consumption of fruiting bodies by animals and humans.
- **Historical context**: Nuclear weapons testing, the Chernobyl disaster (1986), and the Fukushima Daiichi accident (2011) released substantial quantities of radionuclides into the environment, where fungi play ongoing roles in their ecological cycling and persistence.
- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi** have been shown to accumulate radiocaesium to concentrations orders of magnitude above ambient soil levels, raising concerns about the safety of wild mushroom harvesting in affected regions.

Understanding fungal-metal interactions is therefore essential not only for managing heavy metal contamination but also for predicting and mitigating the long-term ecological and human health consequences of radioactive contamination.

## Synthesis and Implications

Across all ecosystem types examined, several common themes emerge regarding fungal interactions with heavy metals:

1. **Tolerance and Adaptation**: Fungi exhibit remarkable tolerance to metal contamination, often developing resistant populations in polluted environments. This is evident in aquatic hyphomycetes surviving in mine effluent, marine fungi thriving in mercury-contaminated marshes, and lichen communities persisting near smelters.

2. **Protective Symbiosis**: Mycorrhizal associations can shield host plants from metal toxicity by sequestering metals in fungal tissues, offering a natural mechanism for phytostabilization of contaminated sites.

3. **Functional Consequences**: While fungi can tolerate metals, their ecosystem functions—particularly organic matter decomposition [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]]—may still be impaired, as demonstrated by reduced leaf breakdown rates in metal-polluted streams.

4. **Trophic Transfer Risks**: Fungi serve as conduits for metal entry into food webs, with documented transfer from aquatic fungi to invertebrates and from mycorrhizal fungi to plants and their herbivores. This trophic transfer pathway is especially concerning for highly toxic metals like Cd and Hg.

5. **Bioremediation Potential**: Certain fungi actively degrade persistent organic pollutants (e.g., PCBs) and may be deployed in bioremediation strategies, though site-specific conditions must be carefully managed.

6. **Biomonitoring Utility**: Lichens, in particular, serve as cost-effective, integrative biomonitors for atmospheric metal deposition, with metal accumulation patterns that reflect both pollution sources and species-specific sensitivities.
