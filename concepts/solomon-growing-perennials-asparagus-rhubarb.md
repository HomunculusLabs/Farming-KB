# Solomon on Asparagus, Rhubarb, and Perennial Herbs

From [[solomon-gardening-when-it-counts-index]] (2005) by Steve Solomon.

## The Value of Perennials

Solomon places a few perennial crops among his recommended garden plants. Perennials require initial effort and patience at planting, but then return year after year with minimal care. They are especially valuable for the self-sufficient homestead because they provide early-season harvests before annual crops are ready.

## Asparagus

### Why Grow It

Asparagus is one of the first vegetables harvested in spring, providing fresh greens when almost nothing else is available. A well-maintained asparagus bed can produce for 15-20 years or longer.

### Soil Preparation

Asparagus needs well-drained soil and full sun. Solomon emphasizes thorough soil preparation before planting because the bed will be permanent. Dig deeply, work in generous amounts of compost or well-rotted manure, and ensure good drainage. Asparagus is a high-demand crop -- it needs fertile soil.

### Planting

Plant one-year-old crowns (not seeds) in spring. Dig trenches 6-8 inches deep, spread roots over a small mound of soil, and cover with 2-3 inches of soil. As the shoots grow, gradually fill in the trench over the first season.

### Spacing

Space crowns 12-18 inches apart in rows 4-5 feet apart. This wide spacing is important because the plants develop extensive root systems that should not compete.

### Harvesting

Do not harvest at all in the first year after planting. In the second year, harvest lightly for 2-3 weeks. From the third year on, harvest for 6-8 weeks in spring. Cut or snap spears when they are 6-8 inches tall. Stop harvesting when spears become thinner than a pencil.

### Maintenance

After the harvest season ends, let the ferns grow. They build the carbohydrate reserves for next year's crop. Cut ferns to the ground in late autumn after they turn yellow. Mulch with compost or manure annually.

## Rhubarb

### Why Grow It

Rhubarb is another early-spring perennial that provides tart stalks for pies, sauces, and preserves. It is extremely hardy and long-lived.

### Growing

Rhubarb needs deep, fertile soil and full sun or partial shade. Plant crown divisions in spring or autumn. Space plants 3-4 feet apart in all directions. Like asparagus, thorough soil preparation at planting pays dividends for years.

Rhubarb is a heavy feeder. Apply compost or manure annually in spring. It benefits from consistent moisture.

### Harvesting

Do not harvest in the first year after planting. From the second year, pull (don't cut) the largest stalks, leaving at least half the stalks on each plant. The harvest season runs from spring through early summer. Stop harvesting in mid-summer to allow the plant to build reserves.

### Important Warning

Only the stalks are edible. Rhubarb leaves contain oxalic acid and are toxic. Remove leaves promptly and do not feed them to livestock.

## Perennial Herbs

Solomon includes perennial herbs around the fringes of the garden as valuable low-maintenance crops. In his own garden, he has several perennial herbs along the edges.

### Common Perennial Herbs for the Kitchen Garden

- **Sage**: Hardy, drought-tolerant once established. Needs well-drained soil and full sun.
- **Thyme**: Low-growing, very hardy. Many varieties with different flavors.
- **Rosemary**: Tender perennial in cold climates. May need winter protection or indoor overwintering.
- **Oregano/Marjoram**: Vigorous spreaders. Provide excellent ground cover.
- **Chives**: Hardy, easy to grow from seed or division. One of the first herbs up in spring.
- **Mint**: Extremely vigorous; contain in pots or barriers to prevent it taking over.
- **Tarragon**: French tarragon (the culinary kind) must be propagated by division; it doesn't come true from seed.

### Herb Sowing

Tiny herb seeds (basil, sorrel, and most herbs) should fall into tiny cracks of newly raked soil, barely covered with a sprinkling of fine compost. Press the earth down gently to restore capillarity. Fine seeds should only be direct-seeded outdoors in mild temperatures, or the rows must be shaded until germination.

## Integration into the Garden Plan

Solomon places perennials at the edges of the garden or in dedicated permanent beds. They should not be placed where annual beds will be tilled. A few currant bushes, small fruit trees, asparagus, and perennial herbs around the fringes can significantly increase food production without requiring additional annual work.
