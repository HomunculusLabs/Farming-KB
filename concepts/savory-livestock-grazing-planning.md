# Planned Grazing and Livestock Management

Planned grazing is the centerpiece of [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]] Management's practical application. It represents a fundamental departure from both continuous grazing and rigid rotational systems, instead using flexible planning that adapts to ever-changing conditions in nature.

## The Time Dimension: The Fourth Missing Key

The most revolutionary insight in grazing management is that overgrazing is not a function of animal numbers but of time. André Voisin proved that overgrazing occurs when animals remain in any one place too long or return before plants have recovered. The time of exposure is determined by the growth rate of the plants.

This insight, drawn from Voisin's work on European dairy farms, was combined with John Acocks's observation that South Africa was "overgrazed and understocked." The synthesis—concentrating animals but timing their exposure and reexposure to match plant growth rates—became the foundation of planned grazing.

Overgrazing occurs in two situations:
1. Animals stay long enough in one area to regraze fast-growing plants before they recover from a first grazing.
2. Animals return to graze slow-growing plants before they have had time to recover from a previous grazing.

## Overgrazing vs. Overtrampling

Savory draws a crucial distinction between overgrazing and overtrampling, and between both of these and the simple presence of animals:

- **Overgrazing** damages individual plants by defoliating them before they can recover. It is a function of time, not numbers.
- **Overtrampling** damages soil and plants by excessive hoof action. It too is a function of time and concentration, not total numbers.
- **Animal impact** (beneficial trampling) breaks capped soil surfaces, incorporates litter into the ground, and creates conditions for seedling establishment.

The key insight: both the damage and the benefit from animals depend on timing and behavior, not merely on how many animals are present.

## Stock Density

Stock density—the number of animals per unit area at any given moment—is distinct from stocking rate (total animals over total area over time). High stock density for short periods is essential in brittle environments to achieve:

- **Soil surface disturbance**: Breaking capped surfaces and chipping the soil
- **Litter incorporation**: Trampling old plant material onto bare soil as mulch
- **Seedbed preparation**: Creating conditions for new seedlings to establish
- **Even grazing**: Preventing [[dighton-selective-grazing-fungal-community-shifts]] by concentrating animals so they consume all available forage

As paddocks are subdivided and stock density increases, the effect on the land changes dramatically. On the Barlite Ranch in Texas, land near watering points subjected to high animal impact showed dense, complex grassland growing right up to the water, while undisturbed land nearby showed widening bare spaces between plants.

## Grazing Periods and Recovery Periods

The relationship between grazing periods and recovery periods is central to planned grazing:

- **Grazing periods** must be short enough that plants are not regrazed before recovering. The required length depends on the daily growth rate of plants.
- **Recovery periods** must be long enough for severely grazed plants to fully recover. This varies with season, rainfall, and plant type.

A large number of paddocks (fifty to one hundred or more) allows short grazing periods and long recovery periods simultaneously, making growth rate less critical in controlling overgrazing. With fewer paddocks, daily monitoring of plant growth rates becomes essential.

Greatest damage follows short grazing AND short recovery periods during slow daily rates of plant growth. During rapid growth, plants can recover quickly, allowing faster moves.

## The Biological Planning Procedure

Savory developed a thorough but simple planning procedure based on military planning concepts. It proved successful across more than one hundred farms and ranches in Zimbabwe. The procedure handles:

- Complex, multi-enterprise operations with crops, livestock, and wildlife
- Seasonal planning across the full annual cycle
- Varying growth rates and recovery needs throughout the year
- Integration of wildlife with livestock
- Hay cutting, crop planting, and harvesting schedules
- Habitat management for wildlife in critical seasons

The planning procedure allows control of the time dimension more subtly than any fixed system. It enables practitioners to minimize overgrazing and overtrampling while still allowing animals to select the plants and nutrients they require, and to provide adequate soil surface disturbance for new plant establishment.

## Herding Behavior and Predator Influence

Wild herding animals behave differently when feeding versus when moving or threatened by predators:

- **While feeding**: Animals spread out, walk gently, place hooves beside coarse plants rather than on them, and place full weight on hooves for subsurface compaction with minimal surface disturbance.
- **When bunched and moving**: Animals kick up dust, step recklessly, trample coarse old material down as mulch, and chip the soil surface as if a gardener had hoed it.

Domesticated livestock, protected from predators, lose this natural behavioral pattern. Even herded livestock do not behave as they would under threat of predation. Planned grazing uses high stock density and frequent moves to simulate the beneficial aspects of natural herd behavior.

## Monitoring Daily Growth Rate

Where few paddocks exist, monitoring plant growth rates every few days during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] is essential. On bunch grass ranges, the most straightforward procedure involves finding and marking severely grazed plants alongside ungrazed plants of the same species as livestock leave each paddock. Periodically returning to these marked plants and comparing them to the ungrazed controls reveals recovery rates and indicates whether the grazing schedule needs adjustment.

## Results and Evidence

After eight years of planned grazing on the Barlite Ranch in Texas, dramatic improvements were documented: dense grassland replacing bare ground, active erosion healing, and disappearance of locoweed. Similar results have been achieved across thousands of ranches worldwide. Production per acre increases even as costs per acre decrease, because the health of the [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]] blocks—water cycle, [[savory-mineral-cycle-management]], succession, and energy flow—is simultaneously improving.
