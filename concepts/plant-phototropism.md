# Plant Phototropism

## Overview

Phototropism is the directional growth response of a plant toward or away from a light source. The term comes from the Greek "photo" (light) and "tropos" (turn). Most plant shoots exhibit **positive phototropism** (growing toward light), while roots typically show **negative phototropism** (growing away from light). This fundamental behavior ensures that photosynthetic organs receive maximum light exposure for energy production.

## Darwin's Classic Experiments

The most influential early experiments on phototropism were conducted by Charles Darwin and his son Francis, published in their 1880 book *The [[darwin-power-of-movement-in-plants]] in Plants*. Darwin had spent twenty years studying plant movement after the publication of *On the Origin of Species*:

- They grew [[canary-grass-phototropism-model-organism]] (*Phalaris canariensis*) seedlings in total darkness for several days.
- A very small gas lamp was placed twelve feet (3.5 metres) from the pot, so dim that "they could not see the seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper."
- After only three hours, the plants had curved toward the dim light.
- The bending always occurred at the same part of the young plant — about an inch (two centimetres) below the tip.

This led to the critical question: which part of the plant "sees" the light?

## The Five-Seedling Experiment

The Darwins designed a classic experiment using five seedlings under identical dim-light conditions:

1. **Untreated seedling**: Bent toward the light, confirming the [[darwin-five-seedling-phototropism-experimental-conditions]] worked.
2. **Tip pruned off**: Did not bend — the plant went "blind."
3. **Tip covered with a [[lightproof-cap-experimental-design-phototropism]]**: Did not bend — confirming the tip needed to receive light.
4. **Tip covered with a clear glass cap**: Bent normally — confirming that light transmission through the cap was sufficient.
5. **Middle section covered with a lightproof tube**: Bent normally — confirming the bending region did not need to see the light.

This elegant experiment proved that the "eyes" of the plant are located at the **shoot tip** (coleoptile tip in grasses), which detects light direction and sends a signal to the lower stem to bend. The Darwins had demonstrated rudimentary sight in plants.

## The Role of Auxin

The mechanism by which the signal travels from tip to bending region was discovered in the 1920s by Dutch botanist Frits Went:

- A chemical substance produced in the tip migrates down the dark side of the stem.
- This substance, later identified as **auxin** (indole-3-acetic acid, IAA), promotes [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]].
- When light hits one side of the tip, auxin is redistributed to the **shaded side** of the stem.
- Cells on the shaded side elongate more than cells on the illuminated side, causing the plant to bend toward the light.

The auxin redistribution mechanism involves:

- **Auxin efflux carriers (PIN proteins)**: These membrane proteins control the direction of auxin transport. Light causes PIN proteins to redistribute laterally, directing auxin flow to the shaded side.
- **Phototropins**: Blue-light photoreceptors in the shoot tip that detect light direction and initiate the auxin redistribution cascade.

## Blue Light as the Trigger

In 1864, [[chamovitz-julius-von-sachs-blue-light-phototropism]] discovered that **blue light** is the primary colour that induces phototropism. Plants are generally insensitive to other colours for directional bending:

- Blue wavelengths (~450 nm) are detected by phototropin photoreceptors (phot1 and phot2).
- Red and green light have minimal effect on phototropic bending.
- The sensitivity of phototropins allows plants to respond to very low light levels — even a single candle across a room.
- Phototropins contain flavin mononucleotide (FMN) as their chromophore, which undergoes a conformational change upon blue-light absorption.

## Types of Phototropic Response

Plants exhibit several distinct phototropic behaviors:

- **First positive curvature**: A rapid bending response to very low fluence rates of blue light, occurring within minutes of exposure. This is mediated primarily by phot1.
- **Second positive curvature**: A slower but stronger response to higher light intensities, mediated by phot2. This response allows sustained bending under brighter conditions.
- **Gravitropism interaction**: Phototropism competes with gravitropism (the plant's response to gravity). When light comes from the side, phototropism typically dominates; when light comes from directly above, gravitropism ensures the shoot grows upward.
- **Time-lapse photography** reveals that plants continuously adjust their orientation as light moves across the sky throughout the day, tracking the sun's position.

## Phototropism in Roots

Roots exhibit **negative phototropism** — they grow away from light:

- This response is also mediated by blue light and involves auxin redistribution, but in the opposite direction.
- Root phototropism may help roots grow deeper into soil, away from the soil surface where light could cause damage.
- Some roots show a complex response: they are negatively phototropic to blue light but may be positively gravitropic simultaneously.

## Ecological and Agricultural Significance

Phototropism has important practical implications:

- **Seedling establishment**: Emerging seedlings use phototropism to orient their shoots toward light gaps in the canopy, maximizing their chances of survival.
- **Canopy competition**: Plants in dense stands use phototropism to position their leaves to capture light that filters through the canopy above.
- **[[cannabis-indoor-growing-basics]]**: Understanding phototropism helps growers position lights to achieve even canopy development and prevent excessive stretching.
- **Trellis and pruning**: [[permaculture-vine-crops]] (tomatoes, cucumbers) exhibit strong phototropic responses that must be managed through trellising.
