# Cap vs Stem Distribution of Tryptamine Alkaloids in Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

Bigwood and Beug (1982) investigated how psilocybin and psilocin are distributed
between the cap (pileus) and stem (stipe) of *Psilocybe cubensis*. Their
analysis of three distinct strains revealed that the two alkaloids follow
strikingly different tissue-distribution patterns, and that these patterns vary
between strains. Understanding cap-versus-stem distribution is essential for
accurate dosing and for understanding where biosynthetic activity is
concentrated within the fruiting body.

## Analytical Findings by Strain

Three strains were analyzed separately for cap and stem alkaloid content in
their first flush, all cultivated on rye-grain substrate:

### M.R. Strain (First Flush)

| Tissue  | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Caps    | 6.7               | 0               |
| Stems   | 4.2               | 0.35            |

Caps contained 1.6× the psilocybin concentration of stems. All detectable
psilocin was found exclusively in the stems — none was measured in the caps.

### Ecuadorian Strain (First Flush)

| Tissue  | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Caps    | 7.6               | 0               |
| Stems   | 4.7               | 0.4             |

Caps contained 1.6× the psilocybin concentration of stems. As with the M.R.
strain, psilocin was found exclusively in the stem tissue.

### Amazonian Strain (First Flush)

| Tissue  | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Caps    | 5.7               | 0.1             |
| Stems   | 5.7               | 0               |

This strain showed a fundamentally different pattern: caps and stems contained
equal amounts of psilocybin. A trace of psilocin was found in the cap but not
in the stem — the reverse of the other two strains.

## General Patterns

Across the three strains, several consistent patterns emerge:

1. **Psilocybin is generally higher in caps than stems.** In two of three
   strains, caps contained approximately 1.6× the stem concentration. This
   suggests that the cap is the primary site of [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]] or
   accumulation.

2. **[[psilocybin-vs-psilocin-distribution-cap-stem]] is strain-dependent and opposite to psilocybin.**
   In the M.R. and Ecuadorian strains, psilocin was found exclusively in stems.
   In the Amazonian strain, a trace appeared in the cap instead. This suggests
   that the dephosphorylation of psilocybin to psilocin may occur in different
   tissues depending on the strain's enzymatic profile.

3. **Total alkaloid content is higher in caps.** When psilocybin and psilocin
   are summed, caps consistently contained more total tryptamine alkaloids
   than stems, even in the Amazonian strain where psilocybin was equal between
   tissues.

## Biosynthetic Interpretation

The concentration of psilocybin in the cap is consistent with the hypothesis
that psilocybin biosynthesis occurs primarily in the hymenium and adjacent
tissues of the pileus, where the spore-producing structures are located. The
basidia — the spore-bearing cells — are concentrated in the gills on the
underside of the cap, and it is here that the enzymatic machinery for
tryptamine alkaloid production may be most active.

The presence of psilocin in the stem (in two of three strains) is harder to
explain if psilocin is produced by dephosphorylation of psilocybin. If
dephosphorylation were occurring uniformly throughout the fruiting body, we
would expect psilocin to appear wherever psilocybin is present. Instead, the
data suggest that phosphatase enzymes capable of converting psilocybin to
psilocin may be differentially expressed between cap and stem tissues, and that
this expression pattern varies by strain.

One possibility is that psilocin in the stem arises from a separate
biosynthetic route or from translocation and subsequent modification. Another
is that the stem provides a storage environment where the relatively less
stable psilocin molecule is protected from oxidative degradation better than in
the cap tissue.

## Dosage Implications

These findings have direct practical implications for anyone consuming
*P. cubensis*:

- **Whole-dried mushrooms** contain a predictable ratio of cap-to-stem
  material, so tissue distribution is partially averaged out in typical use.

- **Cap-only preparations** would be higher in psilocybin and lower in
  psilocin compared to whole mushrooms, potentially producing a longer-duration
  experience with a more gradual onset.

- **Stem-only preparations** would contain relatively more psilocin (in
  strains where stem psilocin is present), possibly producing a faster-onset,
  shorter-duration experience, though at lower total alkaloid content.

- **The strain matters.** The Amazonian strain showed equal psilocybin in caps
  and stems, meaning tissue-based dosing adjustments would need to account for
  the specific strain being consumed.

## Analytical Method Notes

Caps and stems were separated manually before freeze-drying. HPLC detection
limits of ~0.01 µg were sufficient to detect stem psilocin. Zero readings
indicate concentrations below the detection limit, not complete absence.

## Limitations

Only first-flush mushrooms were analyzed for cap-stem distribution. Since
psilocin levels change dramatically across flushes (generally absent in early
flushes, peaking at flush 4), the cap-stem distribution pattern may also shift
[[psilocin-emergence-in-later-flushes]]. The study analyzed only three strains, and intraspecific
variation may be wider than captured here. All mushrooms were grown on rye
grain; substrate effects on tissue distribution remain unexplored.
