# Seed Saving, Germination, and Sowing Techniques

## Overview

The proper handling of seeds—from saving and storage through germination and sowing—is one of the most critical yet often neglected aspects of successful vegetable gardening. Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* devotes extensive attention to seed quality, germination testing, and sowing techniques, arguing that many garden failures can be traced to poor seed handling rather than problems with soil, water, or pests.

## Seed Quality and Selection

### Understanding Commercial Seed Production

Most gardeners purchase seed without understanding how it was produced. Solomon explains the commercial seed production process:

- **Seed crops** are often grown under conditions very different from home gardens—in different climates, with different soil types, and with agricultural chemical inputs
- **Germination standards**: Federal regulations set minimum germination rates for different vegetable species (e.g., 75% for many vegetables). A seed packet meeting this standard could still have 25% dead seeds.
- **Unpredictability**: The contents of seed packets can vary significantly from what the label promises. Germination rates, genetic purity, and variety trueness all vary between seed lots.
- **Seed company trials**: Some companies run their own germination and variety trials, but these are not universal. Rob Johnston (Johnny's Selected Seeds) is cited for his company's commitment to germination testing.

### Evaluating Seed Quality

Gardeners can evaluate seed quality through:

- **Germination testing**: Sprouting a sample of seeds on moist paper towels to determine actual germination percentage before committing to a planting
- **Seed appearance**: Viable seeds are typically plump, firm, and uniform in color. Shriveled, discolored, or lightweight seeds may be dead or weak.
- **Purchase date**: Seeds lose viability over time. Most vegetable seeds maintain good germination for 2-4 years when stored properly.
- **Reputable sources**: Seed from companies with rigorous quality control is more reliable than bargain-bin seed

### Defective Seeds

Some seeds may appear normal but carry hidden defects—genetic abnormalities, embryo damage, or dormancy issues that prevent germination. Identifying defective seeds before planting saves time and garden space.

## Seed Storage

### Conditions for Longevity

Proper seed storage is essential for maintaining viability. The key factors are:

- **Temperature**: Cool temperatures slow metabolic activity and extend seed life. Refrigeration (not freezing for most vegetables) is ideal.
- **Moisture**: Dry conditions prevent premature germination and fungal growth. Seeds should be stored below 8-10% moisture content.
- **Light**: Darkness prevents photo-degradation of seed tissues.
- **Pests**: Airtight containers protect against insects and rodents.

### Practical Storage Methods

- **Airtight containers** (glass jars with tight lids) in a cool, dark location
- **Refrigeration** for long-term storage of valuable or difficult-to-replace seed
- **Silica gel packets** added to containers absorb residual moisture
- **Proper labeling** with variety name, harvest year, and germination rate

Solomon notes that different species have different longevity. Onion seed, for example, loses viability rapidly and should be purchased fresh each year, while some legume seeds can remain viable for 5-10 years.

## Germination

### Factors Affecting Germination

Successful germination requires the right combination of:

- **Moisture**: Seeds must absorb water to activate metabolic processes. The seedbed should be consistently moist but not waterlogged.
- **Temperature**: Each species has an optimal germination temperature range. Soil thermometers help ensure planting at the right time.
- **Oxygen**: Seeds need oxygen for respiration during germination. Compacted or waterlogged soils exclude oxygen and prevent germination.
- **Soil contact**: Seeds must be in firm contact with soil particles to absorb moisture consistently.

### Soil Temperature and Timing

Soil temperature is more important than air temperature for germination. Solomon recommends using a soil thermometer to determine when soil has warmed sufficiently for each crop:

- **Cool-season crops** (peas, lettuce, brassicas, spinach): Germinate at 40-65°F (4-18°C)
- **Warm-season crops** (tomatoes, peppers, cucurbits, beans): Germinate at 60-85°F (16-29°C)
- **Heat-loving crops** (eggplant, okra, melons): Germinate at 70-90°F (21-32°C)

Planting too early, when soil is still cold, leads to slow germination, increased disease pressure (especially damping off), and poor stand establishment.

### Laboratory Germination Testing

For gardeners who want precise germination data, Solomon describes laboratory germination methods:

- **Germ boxes**: Small containers with moist blotting paper or paper towels where seeds are spaced and monitored for sprouting
- **Temperature control**: Germination cabinets or warm locations maintain consistent temperatures
- **Recording results**: Daily counts of germinated seeds provide both total germination percentage and germination speed (an indicator of seed vigor)

### Chitting (Presprouting)

Chitting, or presprouting, involves germinating seeds under controlled conditions before planting them in the garden. This technique offers several advantages:

- **Elimination of dead seed**: Only seeds that have actually sprouted are planted, eliminating gaps in rows
- **Faster garden emergence**: Presprouted seeds emerge from the garden soil days earlier than dry-sown seeds
- **Moisture economy**: Presprouted seeds need less water in the garden since germination has already begun
- **Reduced pest exposure**: Shortening the time seeds spend in the soil reduces vulnerability to soil-dwelling pests and diseases

The chitting process:
1. Moisten paper towels or cloth without soaking
2. Spread seeds on the moist material, spaced to prevent root tangling
3. Place in a warm location (optimal temperature for the species)
4. Check daily; plant seeds as soon as the radical (first root) emerges
5. Handle presprouted seeds carefully—the young root is fragile

**Gelatin method**: Solomon describes using unflavored gelatin dissolved in water as a moisture-retaining medium for presprouting. The gelatin holds water around the seeds, maintaining consistent moisture without the seeds drying out between checks.

### Direct Seeding vs. Transplanting

Solomon discusses when to sow seeds directly in the garden versus starting them as transplants:

- **Direct seeding** is preferred for crops that resent root disturbance (carrots, radishes, beans, peas, corn) and for large-scale plantings
- **Transplanting** is advantageous for crops with long growing seasons (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant) in short-season climates, and for crops that benefit from a head start on weeds

The decision depends on climate, growing season length, and the gardener's resources for starting transplants.

## Sowing Techniques

### Seed Depth

Proper sowing depth is critical and varies by seed size:

- **Large seeds** (beans, corn, peas): 1-2 inches deep
- **Medium seeds** (cucurbits, brassicas): 1/2-1 inch deep
- **Small seeds** (lettuce, carrots, onions): 1/4-1/2 inch deep
- **Tiny seeds** (celery, basil, certain herbs): Barely covered or pressed into soil surface

A general rule is to plant seeds at a depth of 2-3 times their diameter.

### Seedbed Preparation

Good seedbeds are essential for uniform germination:

- **Fine tilth**: Soil should be worked to a fine, crumbly texture with no large clods
- **Firm surface**: The seedbed should be firmed (but not compacted) to ensure good seed-soil contact
- **Level surface**: Level beds ensure uniform planting depth and even watering
- **Incorporated COF**: Complete organic fertilizer mixed into the seedbed provides nutrients for emerging seedlings
- **Compost layer**: A thin layer of finished compost helps retain moisture and provides biological activity

### Sowing Methods

- **Furrows**: Using a hoe to create shallow trenches at the proper depth, then dropping seeds at the desired spacing and covering
- **Broadcast**: Scattering seeds evenly over a bed (primarily for small-seeded crops like carrots and lettuce), then raking lightly to cover
- **Spot sowing**: Planting 2-3 seeds at each desired plant location, then thinning to the strongest seedling
- **Overseeding**: Planting more seed than needed to ensure adequate stand, then thinning
