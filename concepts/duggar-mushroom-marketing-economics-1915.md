# Duggar Mushroom Marketing and Economics 1915

Benjamin Minge Duggar's 1915 bulletin on [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] provides
a fascinating window into the early [[history-of-commercial-mushroom-cultivation]] industry in
the United States. His analysis of markets, pricing, distribution,
and [[solomon-self-sufficiency-calorie-production-economics]] reveals both the challenges and opportunities
facing mushroom cultivators of the era, many of which remain relevant
today.

## Market Development

In the early 20th century, more than 5,000,000 pounds of mushrooms
were marketed during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] of 1913-14. The majority of
hotels used the greater part of the supply. However, except in a few
larger cities, the retail market had not been adequately developed.

Duggar noted abundant opportunity for development of the retail trade
and direct distribution of mushrooms, identifying this as the great
future direction for the industry. The gap between wholesale and
[[hallucinogenic-mushroom-online-retail-markets]] represented a significant opportunity for smaller
growers willing to develop direct customer relationships.

## Shipping and Packaging

Mushrooms could be shipped considerable distances during winter
months. Duggar provided specific guidance for growers:

- Mushrooms should be shipped immediately after being gathered
- They should never be packed in very large quantities, as heat
  developed in a basket of more than 10 pounds is considerable
- In large quantities, mushrooms undergo a "sweating" process that
  encourages bacteria and molds, leading to loss of flavor and rapid
  deterioration
- Neatness of the package is most important, following the example
  of California fruit growers
- Pack in small baskets as one would pack grapes, ensuring aeration
- Study the demands of the market regarding sorting and selection

Properly packed mushrooms shipped within 36 to 48 hours faced little
danger from shipment deterioration. These packaging principles
remain sound and are followed by modern [[specialty-mushroom-species]]
distributors.

## Price History and Trends

Duggar documented significant changes in mushroom pricing:

- Formerly, prices ranged from 25 cents to considerably more than
  $1 per pound
- By 1915, fancy hotel trade nets typically no more than 50 cents
  per pound, frequently only 40 cents
- Commission men and wholesalers averaged less than the hotel rate
- During heaviest production, large producers were forced to accept
  25 cents or less per pound, sometimes below the cost of production
- Private consumers paid ordinarily 60 to 75 cents per pound

This price compression reflected the classic agricultural pattern of
expanding production driving down wholesale prices while retail
margins remained relatively stable.

## The Seasonal Flooding Problem

A critical challenge Duggar identified was the small grower's
difficulty in handling product due to market flooding at peak
production times. When mushrooms are grown in sheds, cold cellars,
or makeshift houses, beds often fail to bear until spring.

The first continued [[stamets-cultivator-agaricus-bitorquis-warm-weather-cultivation]] then floods most large city markets
with product, depressing prices precisely when yields are highest.
The seasonal pattern indicated that growing conditions had not been
satisfactory for controlled year-round production.

The well-established high-class trade was not materially injured by
seasonal flooding, but smaller growers bore the brunt of price
depression. Duggar advised large growers to anticipate this
inevitable condition through better [[eclss-environmental-control-life-support]] and
market timing.
