## The Vedic Soma and Indo-European Mysteries

## Contacting the Mind Behind Nature

The last best hope for dissolving the steep walls of cultural inflexibility that appear to be channeling us toward true ruin is a renewed shamanism. By reestablishing channels of direct communication with the Other, the mind behind nature, through the use of hallucinogenic plants, we will obtain a new set of lenses to see our way in the world.

No history of plants and peoples could claim completeness without a thorough treatment of the mysterious Soma cult of the ancient Indo-Europeans — a nomadic people associated with patriarchy, wheeled chariots, and the domestication of the horse. Also associated with them is a religion based on the magnificently intoxicating Soma.

## The Nature of Soma

Soma was a juice or sap pressed out of the swollen fibers of a plant that was also called Soma. The texts imply that the juice was purified by being poured through a woolen filter and then in some cases was mixed with milk. Again and again, Soma is intimately connected with the symbolism and rituals related to cattle and pastoralism — a connection McKenna considers central to identifying Soma.

The earliest scriptural writings are the Vedas. The Rig Veda is best described as a collection of nearly 120 hymns to Soma, the plant and the god. The Ninth Mandala of the Rig Veda is entirely composed of paeans of praise for the magical plant:

> Thy juices, purified Soma, all-pervading, swift as thought, go of themselves like the offspring of swift mares; the celestial well-winged sweet-flavored juices, great exciters of exhilaration, alight upon the receptacle.

> It is drunk by the sick man as medicine at sunrise; partaking of it strengthens the limbs, preserves the legs from breaking, wards off all disease and lengthens life. The poor man, in the intoxication of the Soma, feels himself rich; the draught impels the singer to lift his voice and inspires him for song; it gives the poet supernatural power, so that he feels himself immortal.

## Soma and Haoma

Soma was prominent in the pre-Zoroastrian religion of Iran as "Haoma." "Soma" and "haoma" are different forms of the same word, derived from a root meaning "to squeeze out liquid." Soma was thought to have been brought by an eagle from the highest heaven, or from the mountains where it had been placed by Varuna.

## The Indo-European Migration and Plant Knowledge
The Indo-European migrations, which began approximately 4000-5000 BCE from the Pontic-Caspian steppe, carried with them a body of plant knowledge that had accumulated over millennia of pastoralist life on the Eurasian grasslands. The original pastoralist culture that McKenna identifies as the custodian of the [[mckenna-abandonment-of-the-mystery]]. Zoroaster's religious reform, which established the dualistic cosmology of Zoroastrianism with its opposition between Ahura Mazda (the Lord of Wisdom) and Angra Mainyu (the Destructive Spirit), reclassified the ecstatic Haoma experience. In the older Indo-Iranian tradition, Haoma was the plant-god itself — the divine substance that, when consumed, temporarily dissolved the boundary between the worshipper and the divine. Under Zoroaster's reform, Haoma was demoted from the status of a god to the status of a tool — a medicinal plant that could be used in the service of the true God but was not itself divine.
This theological demotion of the sacred plant parallels the broader pattern that McKenna identifies across multiple cultures: the gradual replacement of direct, unmediated experience of the divine through plant entheogens with hierarchical, priest-mediated religious systems in which the sacred is accessed only through authorised channels and the plant itself is reduced to a symbol or a minor sacrament. The Zoroastrian transformation of Haoma anticipates the Christian transformation of wine — which may have originally contained psychoactive additives — into a purely symbolic representation of divine blood.

The Soma hymns also contain what appear to be technical instructions embedded within the poetry. References to the pressing stones, the woolen filter, the mixing with milk and barley, and the timing of the ritual suggest that the hymns served not only as devotional literature but also as a practical guide for the preparation of the sacrament. This dual function — simultaneously religious and technical — is characteristic of shamanic plant knowledge in oral cultures, where the distinction between prayer and recipe, between invocation and instruction, does not exist.

## The Bhang Connection and Cannabis as Soma Candidate
Among the alternative Soma candidates, cannabis (bhang, charas, ganja) deserves particular attention in McKenna's framework. Cannabis was widely used in the Indian subcontinent from the earliest historical periods and remains deeply embedded in Hindu religious practice today. The Atharva Veda (one of the four Vedas, composed somewhat later than the Rig Veda) explicitly references cannabis as a sacred plant that relieves anxiety and was one of five sacred plants. Shiva, one of the principal deities of Hinduism, is associated with cannabis use, and the festival of Holi traditionally involves the consumption of bhang (cannabis preparations).
Cannabis has the advantage over Amanita muscaria of producing effects — including altered time perception, enhanced sensory awareness, creative ideation, and feelings of spiritual significance — that are more consistent with the visionary descriptions in the Rig Veda. However, cannabis lacks the intensely visionary quality that characterises classical psychedelics, and its effects are generally milder than those described in the most ecstatic Soma hymns. McKenna suggests that cannabis may have been one of the substitute plants adopted after the original Soma was lost, rather than the original Soma itself. In this interpretation, cannabis served as a more accessible but less powerful replacement for the original mushroom-based sacrament, preserving some elements of the original experience while losing the most transformative aspects.
