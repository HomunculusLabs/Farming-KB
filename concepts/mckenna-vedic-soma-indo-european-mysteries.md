# The Vedic Soma and Indo-European Mysteries

## Contacting the Mind Behind Nature

The last best hope for dissolving the steep walls of cultural inflexibility that appear to be channeling us toward true ruin is a renewed shamanism. By reestablishing channels of direct communication with the Other, the mind behind nature, through the use of hallucinogenic plants, we will obtain a new set of lenses to see our way in the world.

No history of plants and peoples could claim completeness without a thorough treatment of the mysterious Soma cult of the ancient Indo-Europeans — a nomadic people associated with patriarchy, wheeled chariots, and the domestication of the horse. Also associated with them is a religion based on the magnificently intoxicating Soma.

## The Nature of Soma

Soma was a juice or sap pressed out of the swollen fibers of a plant that was also called Soma. The texts imply that the juice was purified by being poured through a woolen filter and then in some cases was mixed with milk. Again and again, Soma is intimately connected with the symbolism and rituals related to cattle and pastoralism — a connection McKenna considers central to identifying Soma.

The earliest scriptural writings are the Vedas. The Rig Veda is best described as a collection of nearly 120 hymns to Soma, the plant and the god. The Ninth Mandala of the Rig Veda is entirely composed of paeans of praise for the magical plant:

> Thy juices, purified Soma, all-pervading, swift as thought, go of themselves like the offspring of swift mares; the celestial well-winged sweet-flavored juices, great exciters of exhilaration, alight upon the receptacle.

> It is drunk by the sick man as medicine at sunrise; partaking of it strengthens the limbs, preserves the legs from breaking, wards off all disease and lengthens life. The poor man, in the intoxication of the Soma, feels himself rich; the draught impels the singer to lift his voice and inspires him for song; it gives the poet supernatural power, so that he feels himself immortal.

## Soma and Haoma

Soma was prominent in the pre-Zoroastrian religion of Iran as "Haoma." "Soma" and "haoma" are different forms of the same word, derived from a root meaning "to squeeze out liquid." Soma was thought to have been brought by an eagle from the highest heaven, or from the mountains where it had been placed by Varuna.

## The Amanita Theory and Its Problems

Gordon Wasson proposed that Soma was the fly-agaric mushroom, Amanita muscaria. McKenna finds several serious objections to this theory:

1. The Amanita muscaria is not found in the warm lowland areas of India where the Indo-Europeans composed their hymns
2. The effects of Amanita muscaria — primarily nausea, intoxication, and delirium — do not match the poetic descriptions of Soma's sublime effects
3. The connection to cattle is central to Soma, and Amanita muscaria does not grow in cattle dung, whereas psilocybin mushrooms do

## Peganum Harmala as Soma

McKenna argues that Peganum harmala (Syrian rue), rich in beta-carbolines including harmine and harmaline, is a more plausible candidate for Soma. The harmala alkaloids are MAO inhibitors that can potentiate other psychoactive compounds. This pharmacological property could explain Soma's reputation as the supreme intoxicant.

The connection to cattle and pastoralism also supports this identification: the Indo-European pastoralists would have encountered Peganum harmala in the same grassland environments where they herded their cattle. The beta-carbolines would have been a natural companion to DMT-containing plants that may have been present in their environment.

## Soma as the Bridge Between Worlds

The Soma cult represents a critical transition point in human religious history — the point at which the original mushroom-centered shamanism of the African pastoralists was transformed through contact with new plants and new environments into the more formalized religions of the Indo-European peoples. The loss of the original psilocybin sacrament and its replacement with less effective substitutes marks the beginning of the dominator culture's estrangement from the direct experience of the sacred.

- McKenna's critique of dominator culture draws on both anthropological evidence and contemporary political analysis

## Significance in Psychedelic Thought

McKenna's work represents one of the most ambitious attempts to construct a comprehensive theory of the role of psychoactive plants in human history and culture. While academic anthropologists have been skeptical of many specific claims, his ideas have been enormously influential in psychedelic culture and continue to inspire research into the relationship between psychoactive substances and human consciousness. The concept of an Archaic Revival — the recovery of ancient plant-based wisdom — has resonated with the growing interest in ethnobotany, ecological consciousness, and the therapeutic potential of psychedelic substances documented in [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]].

## The Rig Veda as Botanical Literature
The Rig Veda's descriptions of Soma provide remarkably detailed botanical information, though they are embedded in poetic and religious language. Soma is described as a plant without leaves, without blossoms, and without roots — a description that has puzzled scholars but which McKenna interprets as referring to the underground or less visible parts of a fungus rather than a leafy plant. The plant is said to grow on mountains and is associated with the colour tawny (babhru) and golden (hiranyavarna). It is described as being brought from high places by eagles or hawks — a motif that McKenna connects to the high-altitude grassland environments where both psilocybin mushrooms and Peganum harmala would have grown.
The pressing ritual is described in considerable detail. The Soma stalks were gathered by priests, pounded between stones on a hide spread on the ground, mixed with water, and pressed through a woolen filter (pavitra) made from the fleece of a ram. The resulting liquid was collected in wooden or earthen vessels and mixed with milk, barley, or ghee before being consumed. The Rig Veda describes the effects of Soma in terms that closely parallel the phenomenology of psychedelic experience: visions of divine beings, feelings of immortality and omnipotence, enhanced poetic inspiration, healing of the sick, and the experience of flying or transcending physical boundaries.
McKenna notes that the descriptions of Soma's effects in the Rig Veda are far more consistent with a classical psychedelic experience than with the effects of Amanita muscaria. The Rig Veda Soma produces visions, divine inspiration, and a sense of cosmic consciousness — effects that are characteristic of psilocybin-containing mushrooms and DMT-containing preparations, but not of Amanita muscaria, whose primary effects are confusion, dizziness, nausea, and a state of intoxication more akin to alcohol than to a visionary psychedelic.

## The Indo-European Migration and Plant Knowledge
The Indo-European migrations, which began approximately 4000-5000 BCE from the Pontic-Caspian steppe, carried with them a body of plant knowledge that had accumulated over millennia of pastoralist life on the Eurasian grasslands. The original pastoralist culture that McKenna identifies as the custodian of the [[mckenna-ur-plant-hypothesis]] relationship would have encountered a succession of new psychoactive plants as it migrated westward and eastward from its African homeland. In the grasslands of Central Asia, Peganum harmala (Syrian rue) would have been one of the most prominent psychoactive plants available, growing wild in the same disturbed, sandy soils where cattle grazed.
The Indo-European word for the sacred drink — reconstructed by linguists as *sauma- (Sanskrit: soma; Avestan: haoma) — is itself evidence for the antiquity and cultural importance of the substance. The existence of cognate forms in both the Indic and Iranian branches of the Indo-European family suggests that the Soma/Haoma cult predates the split between these branches, placing its origin at least 4000 years ago and possibly much earlier. The persistence of the cult across thousands of years and thousands of miles of migration testifies to the profound importance of the Soma experience in Indo-European religious life.
As the Indo-European peoples migrated into the Indian subcontinent, they encountered new psychoactive plants that were not available in their Central Asian homeland. The subcontinent's tropical and subtropical forests contained a rich pharmacopoeia including DMT-containing plants, cannabis, and various psilocybin mushroom species. McKenna suggests that the original Soma plant may have been progressively substituted with local alternatives as the Indo-Europeans moved into environments where the original plant was unavailable, and that the substitution process may have involved combining multiple plants to approximate the effects of the original sacrament. This multi-plant model anticipates the [[harmala-alkaloids-and-ayahuasca-preparation]] combination of DMT-containing plants with [[beta-carboline-chemistry]] MAO inhibitors, which allows orally inactive DMT to become psychoactive when consumed together with Peganum harmala.

## Zoroaster and the End of Haoma
The transformation of the Haoma cult under Zoroaster (Zarathustra) represents, in McKenna's framework, an early example of the [[mckenna-abandonment-of-the-mystery]]. Zoroaster's religious reform, which established the dualistic cosmology of Zoroastrianism with its opposition between Ahura Mazda (the Lord of Wisdom) and Angra Mainyu (the Destructive Spirit), reclassified the ecstatic Haoma experience. In the older Indo-Iranian tradition, Haoma was the plant-god itself — the divine substance that, when consumed, temporarily dissolved the boundary between the worshipper and the divine. Under Zoroaster's reform, Haoma was demoted from the status of a god to the status of a tool — a medicinal plant that could be used in the service of the true God but was not itself divine.
This theological demotion of the sacred plant parallels the broader pattern that McKenna identifies across multiple cultures: the gradual replacement of direct, unmediated experience of the divine through plant entheogens with hierarchical, priest-mediated religious systems in which the sacred is accessed only through authorised channels and the plant itself is reduced to a symbol or a minor sacrament. The Zoroastrian transformation of Haoma anticipates the Christian transformation of wine — which may have originally contained psychoactive additives — into a purely symbolic representation of divine blood.

## Modern Scholarship and the Soma Question
The identity of Soma remains one of the most debated questions in the study of ancient religions. Wasson's [[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]], first published in 1968, was the first major scholarly attempt to identify a specific psychoactive plant with Soma and was enormously influential despite the objections raised by McKenna and others. Subsequent candidates have included cannabis (proposed by John Brough), various species of Ephedra (proposed by David Flattery and Martin Schwartz), and Psilocybe cubensis (proposed by McKenna). More recently, some scholars have argued that Soma may have been a composite substance — a mixture of several plants combined to produce a specific range of effects — rather than a single species.
The ongoing Soma debate illustrates the broader methodological challenge of identifying psychoactive plants in ancient texts. Ancient writers described their experiences in the language and conceptual framework of their own culture, using botanical terminology that may not correspond to modern taxonomic categories and religious metaphors that make it difficult to distinguish between literal description and poetic embellishment. Despite these challenges, the scholarly consensus increasingly acknowledges that some form of psychoactive substance was central to the Vedic and pre-Zoroastrian religions, a conclusion that lends support to McKenna's broader argument about the importance of [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]] in the development of human religious consciousness.

## The Ecstatic Poetry of the Rig Veda
The hymns of the Rig Veda provide what may be the oldest surviving literary descriptions of the psychedelic experience. The language of the Soma hymns — with its recurring motifs of light, flight, expansion, immortality, and divine communion — closely parallels the phenomenological reports of modern psychedelic researchers. The experience described is not one of stupor or confusion (as would be expected from Amanita muscaria intoxication) but of heightened awareness, visionary clarity, and ecstatic joy. The poet feels themselves to be immortal, omniscient, and one with the cosmic order — experiences that are characteristic of the classical psychedelic state as described in the modern clinical literature on [[psychedelic-consciousness-models]].
The Rig Veda describes Soma as simultaneously a plant, a drink, and a god — a theological formulation that makes sense only if the substance in question produces an experience of divine presence so overwhelming that the boundary between the substance and the deity dissolves. This conflation of the sacrament with the sacred is characteristic of entheogenic religions worldwide and contrasts sharply with the later Indo-European religious traditions in which the divine is conceived as transcendent and separate from the material world. For McKenna, the transition from the immanent, experiential religion of the Soma cult to the transcendent, belief-based religion of the Brahmanical and later Hindu traditions represents the same pattern of [[mckenna-abandonment-of-the-mystery]] that he traces across multiple cultures.
The Soma hymns also contain what appear to be technical instructions embedded within the poetry. References to the pressing stones, the woolen filter, the mixing with milk and barley, and the timing of the ritual suggest that the hymns served not only as devotional literature but also as a practical guide for the preparation of the sacrament. This dual function — simultaneously religious and technical — is characteristic of shamanic plant knowledge in oral cultures, where the distinction between prayer and recipe, between invocation and instruction, does not exist.

## The Bhang Connection and Cannabis as Soma Candidate
Among the alternative Soma candidates, cannabis (bhang, charas, ganja) deserves particular attention in McKenna's framework. Cannabis was widely used in the Indian subcontinent from the earliest historical periods and remains deeply embedded in Hindu religious practice today. The Atharva Veda (one of the four Vedas, composed somewhat later than the Rig Veda) explicitly references cannabis as a sacred plant that relieves anxiety and was one of five sacred plants. Shiva, one of the principal deities of Hinduism, is associated with cannabis use, and the festival of Holi traditionally involves the consumption of bhang (cannabis preparations).
Cannabis has the advantage over Amanita muscaria of producing effects — including altered time perception, enhanced sensory awareness, creative ideation, and feelings of spiritual significance — that are more consistent with the visionary descriptions in the Rig Veda. However, cannabis lacks the intensely visionary quality that characterises classical psychedelics, and its effects are generally milder than those described in the most ecstatic Soma hymns. McKenna suggests that cannabis may have been one of the substitute plants adopted after the original Soma was lost, rather than the original Soma itself. In this interpretation, cannabis served as a more accessible but less powerful replacement for the original mushroom-based sacrament, preserving some elements of the original experience while losing the most transformative aspects.
The possibility that the original Soma was a combination of plants — perhaps [[beta-carboline-chemistry]] combined with a DMT-containing plant in a proto-[[harmala-alkaloids-and-ayahuasca-preparation]] preparation, or Psilocybe mushrooms combined with cannabis — remains one of the most intriguing but least testable hypotheses. The multi-plant model would explain why no single candidate plant satisfies all of the botanical, pharmacological, and textual criteria derived from the Vedic descriptions, and it aligns with the common shamanic practice of combining multiple psychoactive plants to achieve specific ranges of experience.

## Soma's Legacy in Modern Hinduism
Although the original Soma plant was lost, its cultural legacy persists in modern Hinduism in several forms. The soma plant is still mentioned in Hindu rituals and prayers, and the word "soma" has entered English and other languages as a generic term for an intoxicating or visionary substance. Aldous Huxley's novel "Brave New World" (1932) famously used the name "soma" for a government-distributed happiness drug that suppresses dissent — a usage that, for McKenna, ironically inverts the original meaning of Soma as a substance that opened the mind rather than closed it.
In modern Hindu practice, several substitutes for Soma continue to be used in religious contexts. Bhang (cannabis preparations) is consumed during the festival of Maha Shivaratri and during certain Tantric rituals. Soma ras, a non-intoxicating herbal preparation, is offered in some temples as a symbolic substitute for the original sacrament. The ritual of pressing and filtering — central to the original Soma ceremony — survives in modified form in various Hindu puja (worship) ceremonies, though the psychoactive content has been entirely lost.
The persistence of Soma as a cultural symbol, even after the physical substance was lost, testifies to the profound impact that the original Soma experience had on Indo-European consciousness. The Rig Veda describes Soma as the "pillar of the sky" that holds up the heavens, the "master of speech" that inspires the poet, and the "healer" who cures disease — attributes that elevate it from a mere intoxicant to the status of a cosmic principle. For McKenna, the cultural memory of this experience, preserved in the Vedic texts, is evidence that the Soma experience was not a mild intoxication but a genuine [[psychedelic-consciousness-models]] of a magnitude sufficient to shape an entire civilisation's religious and philosophical framework for thousands of years. The recovery of this lost knowledge — through the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] and the modern [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]] movement — represents, in McKenna's vision, the possibility of a renewed partnership between humanity and the plant teachers that once catalysed the emergence of the human mind.
