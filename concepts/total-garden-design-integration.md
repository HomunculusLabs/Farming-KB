## Total Garden Design Integration

A total garden design integrates the house, garden, livestock, water systems, and energy systems into a single functioning ecosystem where every element serves multiple purposes and every output becomes an input for another element. Based on collaborative work between [[comfrey]], [[banna-grass]], lemongrass, and legumes. These are regularly cut and removed either to animal pens as green feed or to garden beds as mulch. The area is sunken and surrounded by a bank supporting pigeon pea, papaya, banana, or all of these. This zone safely processes all toilet and sink water through biological filtration before the nutrients reach food-producing areas.

### Zones D and D*: Avenue Crops

Avenue crops of maize, corn, or other main crops of value are laid out in spiral or zig-zag patterns. They consist of self-mulched and stable intercrop systems such as maize/beans, yam/beans, cassava/beans, or melon/beans, with Leucaena, Gliricidia, Sesbania, or other productive legumes as a living hedge. Sticks from these go to the fire; ash returns to the garden. Hedge clippings and exotic mulch (paper, carpet wastes) top-dress these beds.

### Zone E: Forage-Mulch Hedge

A low, raggedly-trimmed hedge of forage-mulch species provides privacy, windbreak, and occasional food. Just outside it is a very dense weed barrier. In two places, this hedge connects to chicken pens. Selected clumps of sympodial, edible-shoot solid bamboos form part of the hedge for food, mulch, and trellis repairs.

### Zone F: Front Yard

The front yard contains traditional or showy flowers, basic medicinals, pleasant scents, fiber plants (for trellis), and ornamental food plants (cat, agave). It is rarely watered, so all plants are hardy and drought-tolerant.

### Zone G: Keyhole Garden Beds

Keyhole beds (circular with a path indentation for access) hold all preferred vegetables, especially greens. The nearest bed to the kitchen contains bulk culinary herbs. A small spiral herb bed sits beside the outdoor stove, and wet-area herbs grow in pots under the trellis in the pond zone (mint). Rare dry-area herbs may be near the front door.

The rest of the G beds are carefully-planned [[seasonal-vegetable-planting]] crops. They are mulched from the hedge and the [[mollison-designers-greywater-treatment-reedbeds]] zone. Food scraps, ashes, mulch, shredded paper, and plant waste are incorporated in compost pits along with crop wastes and windbreak plant cuttings. The beds are always in production, full of plants.

### Zone H: Fukuoka-Style Grain Plot

A grain plot (mostly for people, partly for poultry) has a low surround of dry-tolerant mulch grasses and is also mulched from the edge hedge and palm clump.

### Zone K: Palm Clump

A circle or clump of 6–12 palms of 1–4 species (ideally 2 coconuts, 2 oil palms, 2 dates, and a sugar palm) forms the structural backbone of the garden. The inside circle is mulched with chopped fronds, husks, wastes, ashes, and green hedge material. In this deep mulch, yam or vine crops grow during the wet season.

### House Trees

A few carefully-selected trees overshade the house roof. Their crown spread shades the roof area, and they may even be placed inside the house with holes in the roof allowing the trunk through. These trees have light crown and/or fruits that fall or can be harvested by a child from the roof. Species include spreading legumes like Prosopis (yielding livestock food), palms, or thin-foliaged trees.

### Additional Fruit Trees

Avocado (on mounds 1 meter high and 5 meters across in wet areas), citrus, nut trees, and oil palms are interspersed with soft ground-cover below (nasturtium, soft legumes, comfrey). Ground-cover is sometimes slashed as mulch for the trees, sometimes fed to livestock.

## Livestock Integration

### Animal Housing

Rabbit hutches, chicken roosts, pigeon lofts, quail houses, or guinea-pig rest areas are distributed through the garden. Each roof catches water and leads it to a small tank or the garden. A lot of the hedge, the greywater zone, the tree herb layer, and spare corners are devoted to plants chosen specifically for the animals.

### Chicken Pens (Y1, Y2)

Two fenced areas are changed over to suit the animals. While one rests, it can be cropped if space allows. Both are well-shaded animal runs. Fencing can be woven bamboo, palm rib, banna grass stalks, or wire netting.

### Trellis Vine Systems

House trellises are carefully fitted with vine crops spaced to let 30% light through. Basic vines include chayote, kiwifruit, beans, cucurbits, and grapes. One or two non-bearing vines can grow over the roof, significantly cooling the house. On the sides of the trellis, ephemeral bean and cucurbit crops grow on leaning bamboo trellis or separate bamboo trellis dividers between garden beds.

## Accessories and Energy

- **Hot water** from a collector or black pipe coil on the roof
- **Cooking** with oil lamps, photovoltaic array, solar oven on castors, efficient cooker, and solar food drier
- **Toilet paper plants** built into the hedges (Nicotiana and Leucaena work well)
- **Dry toilet** if no septic tank; manure placed under trees in pits

## Design Philosophy

The total garden design accepts all water and wastes of use—only plastic, glass, and metal are not directly cycled (though some cans may be buried for slow zinc and iron release). It provides most mulch and forage, which when bulked out by house scraps feeds rabbits, guinea pigs, poultry, and a small pig. The garden is very accessible, designed on a need-to-visit-and-tend basis, and natural in both appearance and function. Every element connects to every other element through flows of water, nutrients, biomass, and energy, creating a resilient and productive living system.
