# Phytochrome Molecular Switch Mechanism

## Overview

Phytochrome is a photoreversible pigment protein that functions as a molecular
switch in plants, enabling them to detect and respond to red and far-red light.
Discovered through the elegant red/far-red reversibility experiments of the
1950s, phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms that toggle between
active and inactive states depending on the quality of light received. This
switching mechanism underpins seed germination, shade detection, de-etiolation,
and photoperiodic flowering.

## Discovery Through Reversibility Experiments

The discovery of phytochrome emerged from experiments beginning in the late
1940s at the US Department of Agriculture laboratory where the Maryland Mammoth
tobacco strain was first studied. Harry Borthwick and colleagues made the
critical observation that far-red light could completely cancel the effect of
red light on flowering.

The experiments demonstrated a remarkable toggle effect using irises. When
given a pulse of red light in the middle of the night, the irises flowered.
But if far-red light was applied immediately after the red pulse, the flowering
response was completely abolished, as if the red light had never been seen.

A subsequent red pulse restored flowering, and a following far-red pulse
cancelled it again. This cycle could be repeated multiple times with the plant
responding only to the last colour of light it received. Only a few seconds
of either colour was sufficient to drive the switch.

This reversibility implied the existence of a single photoreceptor pigment
capable of existing in two states: one induced by red light and the other by
far-red light. In the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler and colleagues isolated
this photoreceptor and named it phytochrome, from the Greek phyton (plant) and
chroma (colour).

## The Pr and Pfr Photochemical Cycle

Phytochrome is synthesised in its inactive form, Pr (absorbing maximally at
660 nm, red light). When Pr absorbs a photon of red light, it undergoes a
rapid photochemical isomerisation to become Pfr (absorbing maximally at 730 nm,
far-red light). Pfr is the biologically active form that triggers downstream
developmental responses. When Pfr absorbs far-red light, it reverts to the
inactive Pr form.

The conversion between forms is extremely rapid, occurring within seconds of
light exposure. Only trace amounts of light are needed. The photochemical
change involves a Z-to-E isomerisation of the double bond between rings C and D
of the linear tetrapyrrole chromophore, inducing a cascade of protein
conformational changes that propagate through the entire protein structure.

In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through thermal relaxation (dark
reversion), which takes hours depending on temperature and phytochrome type.
This dark reversion provides the plant with a built-in timer for measuring
continuous darkness duration, the key parameter in photoperiodic regulation.

Under natural daylight, the equilibrium strongly favours Pfr because sunlight
contains approximately five times more red than far-red light. At sunset, the
light shifts toward far-red due to atmospheric scattering, and the equilibrium
shifts toward Pr. During the night, thermal reversion gradually converts
remaining Pfr to Pr.

## Protein Structure and Domain Organisation

Phytochrome proteins are large (approximately 120 to 130 kDa) dimers:

**N-terminal photosensory module**: Contains the chromophore-binding domain with
phytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole derived from heme biosynthesis, covalently
attached to a conserved cysteine in the GAF domain. Also contains PAS and GAF
domains for chromophore binding and light sensing.

**C-terminal output module**: Contains PAS-A, PAS-B, and a histidine
kinase-related domain (HKRD) for protein-protein interactions. Most plant
phytochromes have lost catalytic kinase activity and function through
protein-protein interaction instead.

The Pr-to-Pfr transition involves a dramatic structural rearrangement. In Pr,
the N-terminal module masks interaction surfaces. Upon Pfr conversion, a
tongue-to-knot transition exposes PAS domains, enabling phytochrome to bind
downstream partners and translocate to the nucleus.

## The Phytochrome Gene Family

In higher plants, phytochromes are encoded by small gene families that have
diversified through gene duplication and neofunctionalisation. Arabidopsis
thaliana possesses five phytochrome genes (PHYA through PHYE), each with
distinct but partially overlapping functions:

- **PhyA**: Dominant in etiolated seedlings. Light-labile; rapidly degraded
  upon light exposure. Mediates very-low-fluence responses (VLFR) and
  high-irradiance responses (HIR) to continuous far-red light. Important for
  seed germination and de-etiolation under canopy conditions.

- **PhyB**: Most abundant in light-grown plants. Primary mediator of
  red/far-red reversible responses. Light-stable; accumulates to high levels.
  Controls shade avoidance, photoperiodic flowering, and light-regulated
  development.

- **PhyC**: Functions in both red and far-red sensing. Contributes to flowering
  regulation. May function as a heterodimer with PhyA and PhyB.

- **PhyD and PhyE**: Primarily involved in shade avoidance responses. PhyE is
  particularly important for detecting far-red light reflected from
  neighbouring vegetation.

## Nuclear Translocation and PIF Signalling

A critical step is light-dependent translocation from cytoplasm to nucleus,
occurring within minutes of red light activation. Inside the nucleus,
phytochrome interacts with phytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs), bHLH
transcription factors that promote growth in darkness.

Active Pfr binds the APB motif in PIF proteins and triggers their
phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and proteasomal degradation. In darkness, PIFs
accumulate and promote etiolation; in light, PIF removal allows photomorphogenic
development. Different PIF family members regulate distinct gene subsets,
enabling coordinated regulation of multiple developmental aspects.

## Ecological Significance

The phytochrome switch is one of the most ecologically important sensory
mechanisms in plants. It enables seed germination only when light conditions
indicate a favourable position near the soil surface. It regulates flowering
timing in response to seasonal day-length changes. And it provides an early
warning system for vegetation competition through far-red detection.

The evolutionary conservation of phytochrome from mosses and ferns to flowering
plants, and its presence even in cyanobacteria, testifies to its fundamental
importance across over a billion years of evolution.
