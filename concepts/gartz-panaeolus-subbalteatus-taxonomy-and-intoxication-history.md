## Historical Intoxication Cases

In 1816, an impoverished man collected mushrooms in London"s Hyde Park
thinking they were edible champignons. Soon after ingestion, his field of
vision darkened and he saw fog while feeling lightheaded and dizzy. He sank
into his chair, felt his strength draining, and grew oblivious to his
surroundings. Symptoms were attributed to "Agaricus campanulatus," but were
likely caused by a Panaeolus species.

A more detailed case occurred in Maine, USA, in 1914. After eating Panaeolus
papilionaceus, a man experienced peculiar bright colors, horizontal stripes of
bright red and green, an irresistible impulse to laugh immoderately, and
hallucinations of hideous human faces in intense colors resembling flames of
fire. The experience lasted about six hours with no ill aftereffects.

In 1915, Panaeolus species spontaneously appeared in a New York mushroom
greenhouse and were accidentally eaten with cultivated champignons, causing
remarkable intoxications. Murrill described the mushroom as "Panaeolus
venenosus." In 1970, another case occurred in Leipzig where Panaeolus
subbalteatus spontaneously emerged among cultivated Stropharia rugoso-annulata.

## Taxonomic Confusion

Panaeolus subbalteatus was first described as from England in 1861. The current
combination term Panaeolus subbalteatus (B. and Br.) Sacc. was first published
in 1887. In the American literature it has been labeled "Panaeolus variabilis"
because some stems imitate the appearances of other species.

The large number of existing synonyms indicates substantial taxonomic confusion.
The species is also known to grow in the immediate vicinity of Panaeolus
foenisecii, creating more opportunities for mistakes. The caps become flat as
mushrooms age, a feature that is taxonomically significant.

## Physical Description

The cap is broadly convex, obtuse, often with a slight umbo in the middle,
usually 3 to 4 cm broad, sometimes up to 5 cm. Hygrophanous, reddish brown
when wet, pale and brownish to flesh-colored when dry. Margins banded with a
dark brown stripe around the edges. Surface smooth or lightly wrinkled, bald,
rather fleshy, no veil.

Gills are reddish brown when young, later sooty or black, rather crowded and
bulgy, 7 to 8 mm wide and somewhat thick. Spores are almost lemon-shaped,
13 to 14 microns long and 8 to 9 microns wide, smooth, black and opaque. Stems
are slender, 4 to 8 cm long, often curvy and reddish brown.

## Habitat and Season

The mushroom grows cespitosely to gregariously from June through October in
grassy areas. It particularly thrives on fertilized fields and pastures, but
occurs there comparatively less often. It also grows on trash heaps, compost,
or straw substrates where mushrooms are commercially cultivated. Unlike
Psilocybe species, Panaeolus can be found anytime from spring through fall.

## Bluing Reaction

European Panaeolus species have a special attribute: they very seldom develop
blue stains when handled or injured. Reports from the Pacific Northwest state
that only one in a hundred mushrooms actually turns blue. A Scottish case in
1977 mentioned blue colorations along caps of stems, but formation of blue
stains is very rare overall.
