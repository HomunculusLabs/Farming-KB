# [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] Taxonomy and Intoxication History

The genus Panaeolus contains dark-spored, dung-inhabiting mushrooms that have
been causing involuntary intoxications since at least the early 19th century.
[[gartz-global-psilocybin-mushroom-distribution]] is the most important psychoactive European
species among the genus, with a long and controversial history of
identification and chemical analysis.

## Historical Intoxication Cases

In 1816, an impoverished man collected mushrooms in London's Hyde Park
thinking they were edible champignons. Soon after ingestion, his field of
vision darkened and he saw fog while feeling lightheaded and dizzy. He sank
into his chair, felt his strength draining, and grew oblivious to his
surroundings. Symptoms were attributed to "Agaricus campanulatus," but were
likely caused by a Panaeolus species.

A more detailed case occurred in Maine, USA, in 1914. After eating Panaeolus
papilionaceus, a man experienced peculiar bright colors, horizontal stripes of
bright red and green, an irresistible impulse to laugh immoderately, and
hallucinations of hideous human faces in intense colors resembling flames of
fire. The experience lasted about six hours with no ill aftereffects.

In 1915, Panaeolus species spontaneously appeared in a New York mushroom
greenhouse and were accidentally eaten with cultivated champignons, causing
remarkable intoxications. Murrill described the mushroom as "Panaeolus
venenosus." In 1970, another case occurred in Leipzig where Panaeolus
subbalteatus spontaneously emerged among cultivated Stropharia rugoso-annulata.

## Taxonomic Confusion

Panaeolus subbalteatus was first described as from England in 1861. The current
combination term Panaeolus subbalteatus (B. and Br.) Sacc. was first published
in 1887. In the American literature it has been labeled "Panaeolus variabilis"
because some stems imitate the appearances of other species.

The large number of existing synonyms indicates substantial taxonomic confusion.
The species is also known to grow in the immediate vicinity of Panaeolus
foenisecii, creating more opportunities for mistakes. The caps become flat as
mushrooms age, a feature that is taxonomically significant.

## Physical Description

The cap is broadly convex, obtuse, often with a slight umbo in the middle,
usually 3 to 4 cm broad, sometimes up to 5 cm. Hygrophanous, reddish brown
when wet, pale and brownish to flesh-colored when dry. Margins banded with a
dark brown stripe around the edges. Surface smooth or lightly wrinkled, bald,
rather fleshy, no veil.

Gills are reddish brown when young, later sooty or black, rather crowded and
bulgy, 7 to 8 mm wide and somewhat thick. Spores are almost lemon-shaped,
13 to 14 microns long and 8 to 9 microns wide, smooth, black and opaque. Stems
are slender, 4 to 8 cm long, often curvy and reddish brown.

## Habitat and Season

The mushroom grows cespitosely to gregariously from June through October in
grassy areas. It particularly thrives on fertilized fields and pastures, but
occurs there comparatively less often. It also grows on trash heaps, compost,
or straw substrates where mushrooms are commercially cultivated. Unlike
Psilocybe species, Panaeolus can be found anytime from spring through fall.

## Bluing Reaction

European Panaeolus species have a special attribute: they very seldom develop
blue stains when handled or injured. Reports from the Pacific Northwest state
that only one in a hundred mushrooms actually turns blue. A Scottish case in
1977 mentioned blue colorations along caps of stems, but formation of blue
stains is very rare overall.

## Alkaloid Content

Stijve concluded that dried fruiting bodies contain about 0.1% [[psilocybin]]
regardless of location, plus a tiny amount of [[baeocystin]]. However, dried
mushrooms from the Pacific Northwest have been reported to contain 0.16% to
0.65% psilocybin. The first North American analyses (1959) led to isolation of
a water-soluble indole compound now generally thought to be baeocystin.

More recent studies of carefully identified European material did not document
substantial amounts of [[psilocin]]. "Chemical races" associated with specific
species could not be established. Almost all accidental intoxications can be
traced to Panaeolus subbalteatus, with the possible exception of one case
caused by an imported tropical species.

## The Serotonin Complication

All Panaeolus species produce 5-substituted indole compounds, including
serotonin and 5-hydroxytryptophan. Serotonin is completely inactive when taken
orally. When performing chromatographies, serotonin can easily be mistaken for
psilocin. This has caused confusion in earlier analyses, with some researchers
reporting psilocin in Panaeolus species where later, more careful studies
found none.

## Ecological Role and Significance

Panaeolus species are primarily dung-inhabiting mushrooms that play an
important ecological role in nutrient cycling. They colonize herbivore dung and
rapidly decompose it, returning nutrients to the soil. Their preference for
nutrient-rich substrates explains their frequent appearance on compost heaps,
fertilized fields, and cultivated mushroom beds. The genus belongs to the
Coprinaceae family of dark-spored mushrooms, related to the ink caps.

## Detection Challenges

Several factors make Panaeolus subbalteatus difficult to detect and study:

1. **Rare bluing reaction:** Unlike Psilocybe species, blue staining occurs
   in perhaps one in a hundred specimens from the Pacific Northwest, and is
   even rarer in European collections. This eliminates the most common field
   identification marker for psychoactive mushrooms.

2. **Variable appearance:** Stems can imitate other Panaeolus species, and
   the large number of synonyms indicates substantial morphological variation.

3. **Association with Panaeolus foenisecii:** The two species grow in close
   proximity, increasing the risk of misidentification during foraging.

4. **Seasonal unpredictability:** Unlike many Psilocybe species with defined
   fruiting windows, Panaeolus can appear from spring through fall, making
   seasonal targeting unreliable.

## Comparison With Other Panaeolus Species

While Panaeolus subbalteatus is the most significant psychoactive European
species, other members of the genus have been studied. Panaeolus
papilionaceus was responsible for the vivid 1914 Maine intoxication. All
Panaeolus species share the characteristic of producing serotonin compounds,
which complicates chemical analysis. The genus as a whole remains
understudied compared to Psilocybe, partly because its members lack the
reliable bluing reaction that draws attention to psychoactive potential.
