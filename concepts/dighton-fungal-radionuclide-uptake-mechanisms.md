# Fungal Radionuclide Uptake Mechanisms

The mechanisms by which fungi accumulate radionuclides involve both
passive physicochemical processes at the cell wall and active metabolic
uptake. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for predicting
radionuclide behavior in contaminated ecosystems and for developing
fungal-based remediation strategies. Research following the Chernobyl
disaster greatly advanced understanding of fungal radionuclide uptake
pathways, specificity, and species variation.

## Cell Wall Ion Exchange

Radionuclide uptake mechanisms in fungi have been related to cell-wall
ion exchange sites and by potassium replacement. The fungal cell wall,
composed of chitin, glucans, and glycoproteins, provides numerous
negatively charged binding sites that can exchange cations with the
surrounding solution. Adsorption of radionuclides onto ion-exchange
sites of fungal hyphal walls has been demonstrated for multiple
isotopes. The efficiency of ion exchange varies with cell wall
composition, which differs among fungal taxa and growth conditions.

## Potassium Replacement

Potassium replacement is species-specific in its mechanism. In the
filamentous fungus [[fusarium-solani]], both rubidium and cesium can
replace potassium in metabolic processes (Das, 1991). In contrast,
in the yeast Candida utilis, only rubidium but not lithium, sodium,
or cesium could replace potassium (Aiking and Tempest, 1977). Olsen
et al. (1990) suggest that the affinity of fungi for alkali metals
decreases in the order K greater than Rb greater than Cs greater than
Na greater than Li, with relative ratios of 100:42:7:4:0.5. However,
Muramatsu et al. (1991) suggested cesium was taken up by Suillus
granulatus and Lactarius hatsudake in preference to potassium.

## Strontium Pathways

Connolly et al. (1998) demonstrated that the wood decay fungus
Resinicium bicolor could utilize strontium from strontianite sand,
translocate it through mycelial cord systems, and deposit it in
calcium oxalate crystals. This suggests that strontium behaves
similarly to calcium in fungal metabolism, and although this work
did not involve radiotracers, it suggests a pathway for translocation
of the radionuclide strontium-90 within decomposer fungi. The
mycelial cord systems that translocate nutrients could thus serve as
conduits for radionuclide movement through soil.

## Mycorrhizal Mediation of Plant Uptake

The role of mycorrhizae in plant radionuclide uptake is complex and
sometimes contradictory. Arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization of sweet
clover and Sudan grass showed slight, insignificant increases in
uptake of cesium-137 and cobalt-60. In contrast, arbuscular
mycorrhizal colonization of soybeans by [[glomus-mosseae]] significantly
increased strontium-90 uptake. However, arbuscular mycorrhizal
symbiosis in Festuca ovina reduced radiocesium uptake into shoots.
Clint and Dighton (1992) showed that radiocesium influx into
mycorrhizal heather (Calluna vulgaris) was lower than into
nonmycorrhizal plants, but internal redistribution allowed greater
proportion of Cs to be translocated to shoots.

## Preferential Translocation to Fruit Bodies

Gray et al. (1995, 1996) used quantitative autoradiography to show
that translocation of radiocesium through fungal thalli of Armillaria
spp. and [[schizophyllum-commune]] was significantly reduced compared
with a diffusion model. They demonstrated preferential movement of
radiocesium to developing fruit bodies, which were acting as nutrient
sinks. This preferential allocation has implications for both food
chain contamination and potential harvesting-based remediation.

## Long-Term Accumulation

Dighton and Horrill (1988) used the Chernobyl isotope ratio of 137Cs
to 134Cs (2:1) to determine that 25 to 92% of 137Cs in ectomycorrhizal
fruit bodies originated from sources prior to the Chernobyl accident.
Similar figures of 13 to 69% pre-Chernobyl accumulation were calculated
from other data. This indicates that fungi act as long-term accumulators
and retainers of radionuclides in the environment. The distribution of
fungal mycelia in upper soil horizons correlates with radionuclide
accumulation and reduced downward movement by immobilization in fungal
tissue.

## Saprotrophic Immobilization Capacity

Dighton et al. (1991) estimated that grassland soil [[saprotrophic-fungi]],
assuming average influx rates of 134 nmol Cs per gram dry weight
mycelium per hour and hyphal biomass of 6 grams dry weight per cubic
centimeter, could take up between 350 and 804 nmol Cs per square
meter per hour. Given micromolar radiocesium concentrations in soil
pore water, fungi have potential to accumulate a large percentage of
total fallout, serving as an effective biological buffer against
radionuclide leaching.
