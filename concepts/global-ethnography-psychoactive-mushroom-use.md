# Global Ethnography of Psychoactive Mushroom Use

Psychoactive mushroom use extends far beyond the well-known Mexican mushroom cults. From ancient Saharan rock art dating to 7,000 B.C. to modern tourist mushroom omelettes in Bali, psilocybin-containing mushrooms have been discovered, used, and incorporated into cultural practices on every inhabited continent. This page documents the global spread of psychoactive mushroom knowledge and usage patterns as compiled by [[jochen-gartz]] from ethnographic, mycological, and medical literature.

## Africa — The Oldest Known Mushroom Cult

### Saharan Rock Art (7,000–5,000 B.C.)

Between 9,000 and 7,000 years ago, the Sahara was a blooming garden. Rock drawings from the Tassili plateau (Southern Algeria), Acacus (Libya), and Ennedi (Chad) at altitudes of ~6,500 ft depict:

- Anthropomorphic beings with **mushroom-shaped heads** — some clearly holding mushrooms
- Tiny horned dancers alongside mushrooms, reminiscent of early Mexican images
- Deities with masks and horns holding mushrooms or with mushrooms attached to body parts
- **Dashed lines** connecting mushrooms to the center of heads — possibly representing energy flow or the mushroom's influence on the soul
- Two distinct mushroom shapes: one resembling *[[psilocybe-semilanceata]]* (acute umbo), another resembling *Amanita* or *Stropharia* species
- Mushrooms drawn in white, ochre, and occasionally **blue** — possibly representing the bluing phenomenon

These images, discovered by G. Samorini and [[terence-mckenna]] in the late 1980s, constitute the oldest known evidence of psychoactive mushroom use, predating Mexican mushroom cults by millennia.

### African Species and Intoxications

- **1912:** R. Maire collected bluing, dark-spored mushrooms on raw compost under cedar trees at Chrea Pass near Blida, Algeria — later classified as *[[psilocybe-mairei]]* / *P. cyanescens*
- **1945:** 22 cases of intoxication in Nairobi from a yellow *Stropharia* species mistaken for a culinary mushroom — symptoms lasted 24–48 hours
- **1949:** European couple in Nakuru ate generous mushroom portions; experienced euphoria, delusions, and visions of pink colors; recovered after stomach pumping
- **1994:** New discovery of *Psilocybe natalensis* Gartz, Reid, Ecker & Smith in Natal Province, South Africa — whitish, no annular ring, does not grow directly on dung
- Two *Panaeolus* species described by Ola'h as strongly psychoactive: *P. africanus* and *P. tropicales*

## Japan — Dancing Mushrooms and Medieval Tales

### The "Laughing Mushroom" (11th Century)

An account from the 11th century describes lumberjacks from Kyoto who encountered Buddhist nuns dancing and laughing uncontrollably after eating wild mushrooms. The lumberjacks ate the same mushrooms and also succumbed to overwhelming laughter and the urge to dance. Since that time, the mushrooms were called:

- **Maitake** (Dancing Mushrooms)
- **Waraitake** (Laughing Mushrooms)

For centuries, the species thought responsible were misidentified as *Panaeolus papilionaceus* and *Gymnopilus spectabilis* ("giant laughing mushroom"). Neither actually contains psilocybin.

### Modern Japanese Species

- *Stropharia caerulescens* (Imai, 1932) — later *S. venenata*, now classified as a close relative of *Psilocybe cubensis*; grows on wood and dung. Caused family intoxication in 1929 when a woman mistook it for honey mushrooms (*Armillaria mellea*)
- *Psilocybe subcaerulipes* Hongo — bluing species
- *Psilocybe argentipes* Yokoyama — bluing species; crystalline psilocybin isolated from dried fruiting bodies
- Japan has a 2,000-year tradition of mushroom cultivation (*Lentinus edodes* / shiitake)

The "urge to dance" effect is unusual from a modern pharmacological perspective — psilocybin typically causes initial laughter followed by relaxation and decreased physical activity. Cultural expectations likely modified the expression of altered consciousness.

## Australia — From "Gold Tops" to Black Markets

### Species

- *Psilocybe subaeruginosa* — colloquial name **"Gold Top"**; grows in NSW, Victoria, and South Australia; bluish-green with age or pressure; contains ~0.45% psilocybin
- *Psilocybe cubensis* — common in subtropical/tropical Queensland pastures on cattle dung; likely introduced by early settlers
- *Panaeolus cyanescens* — colloquial name **"Blue Meanies"** (after the Beatles' *Yellow Submarine*); discovered in the early 1970s
- The historical "Panaeolus ovatus" intoxication accounts were likely *P. cyanescens*

### The Queensland Mushroom Boom (1969)

A 4,000-hectare coastal Queensland region became famous for abundant *P. cubensis* crops after the rainy season. Special transportation companies were founded to deliver mushrooms to major cities at ~$1 per fruiting body. Dried, ground mushrooms in gelatine capsules sold for $6 each in Tasmania. By 1972, LSD had almost disappeared from the black market, replaced by mushrooms.

### Legal History

- May 8, 1971: Queensland governor prohibited possession of *P. cubensis* under the same guidelines as Cannabis and opium
- 74 individuals sentenced in 1972, 27 in 1973
- Usage continued despite harsh penalties
- Common slang: "mushies"

## Europe — The Celtic Revival

### Great Britain

- **1976:** High Court ruling — "Psilocybin is a chemical and mushrooms are mushrooms" — acquittal for possession of dried *P. semilanceata*. (Common law precedent not binding in lower courts.)
- **1976:** First scientific report of *P. semilanceata* use in Britain (M. Carter, *New Scientist*)
- **1978:** 33 medical cases; **1981:** 142 cases — average user age 15–19
- Typical dose: 30–40 fresh mushrooms; some users consumed up to 2–3 pounds (~1–1.5 g pure psilocybin)
- Colloquial names: Liberty Cap, Magic Mushroom, Blue Legs, Pixie Caps
- In 318 poisoning cases: **zero fatalities**, no severe somatic symptoms, no misidentification incidents
- Wales: "Mega Mushroom Festival" with 100 participants, 19 requiring medical treatment
- No longer prosecuted in Britain; usage has declined since its 1980s peak

### Norway

- First described use: fall 1977, Sandnes (Rogaland area)
- December 1981: classified as "absolutely forbidden substance" alongside heroin and LSD
- Knowledge spread quickly through fjord areas, newspapers, and underground magazines

### Other European Countries

*P. semilanceata* use reported (sporadic or rising) in: The Netherlands, Austria, Denmark, Sweden, Belgium, Germany, Switzerland, Russia (near St. Petersburg), Siberia, Italy (from ~1980/81), former Czechoslovakia (*P. bohemica* more common there), Finland (from 1981), Spain (*Panaeolus* species near Barcelona, mid-1980s)

### France — The Naming Prohibition

France has an "absurd prohibition" against exhibiting *Psilocybe* and *Stropharia* species at scientific conventions. French mycologists circumvent this by exhibiting Psilocybes under the old genus name **Geophila** (Quélet).

## Southeast Asia and the South Pacific

### Thailand

- Currently the country with the **largest consumption** of psychoactive mushrooms (J.W. Allen & M.D. Merlin)
- Primary islands: **Koh Samui** and **Koh Pha-ngan** — primarily German tourists
- Mushrooms served as omelettes, soups, teas, pizzas, and juices in restaurants
- Species: *Psilocybe cubensis*, *P. subcubensis*, *Panaeolus cyanescens*
- "Hed keequai" — local name for psychoactive mushrooms
- January 1989: Law prohibiting usage passed after an Australian tourist's panic reaction (caused by synthetic hallucinogen adulteration, not mushrooms)
- Commercial cultivation: rice debris added to fresh buffalo manure
- *Psilocybe samuiensis* discovered August 1991 — similar to *P. semilanceata* but no baeocystin; grows on fertilized soil, not directly on dung
- Mushroom tourism merchandise: T-shirts, postcards, posters, lighters, key rings

### Bali

- Mushroom restaurants served *Panaeolus cyanescens* omelettes as house specialties (1970s–1980s)
- Commercial cultivation: transferring dung with naturally-grown mycelia onto fresh buffalo manure
- Patrons primarily visiting tourists

### Nepal

- Mushrooms found at ~3,000 ft altitude on partially decomposed cow/buffalo manure
- Most abundant fruiting: May–June (pre-monsoon rainy months)
- *Psilocybe cubensis* and *P. subcubensis* identified
- No indigenous usage; Western mycophilic visitors
- Relatively large doses required: 40+ fleshy mushrooms per person

### Other Locations

- **Samoa:** Teenagers discovered psychoactive effects of *P. cyanescens* in the late 1960s; parents considered it "a foolish, but totally harmless episode"
- **New Zealand:** Usage evolved around Australian species with penalties modeled after Australian law; *P. cubensis* not native
- **Vietnam:** *Naematoloma caerulescens* (= *P. cubensis*) collected by Patouillard in 1907
- **Cambodia:** *Panaeolus cambodginiensis* — bluing species with psilocybin (Ola'h & Heim)

## The Soma Hypothesis

Schroeder and Guzmán proposed that **soma** — the divine substance revered by the ancient Aryan civilization — may not have been fly agaric (*Amanita muscaria*) as Wasson suggested, but rather a **psychedelic Psilocybe species**, based on:

- The spectacular psychotropic effects described in Vedic literature
- The mushroom's geographic distribution pattern matching Aryan migration routes
- The effects being more consistent with psilocybin than with ibotenic acid/muscimol

## Patterns Across Cultures

1. **Independent discovery is common** — psychoactive mushroom use arose independently on every continent
2. **Tourism as vector** — since the 1960s, knowledge of psychoactive species spread globally through travelers, often displacing or disrupting indigenous traditions
3. **Cultural modification of effects** — the same alkaloids produce different subjective experiences depending on cultural expectations (Japanese "dancing" vs. European "visions" vs. Mexican "divine communion")
4. **Universally low toxicity** — despite hundreds of thousands of users annually, fatalities are vanishingly rare (the only known case involved a child, possibly with latent epilepsy)
5. **Consistent species** — *P. cubensis* and *P. cyanescens* (dung-inhabiting tropical/subtropical species) appear across most warm-climate continents, while *P. semilanceata* dominates temperate zones
