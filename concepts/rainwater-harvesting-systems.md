# Rainwater Harvesting Systems

Rainwater harvesting captures and stores precipitation for domestic,
agricultural, and landscape use. On a homestead, it reduces dependence on
well or municipal water, provides backup supply, and reduces stormwater
runoff. This guide covers system components, design, sizing, filtration,
and potability considerations.

## System Components

### Catchment Surface
The roof is the most common catchment surface. Metal roofing is ideal
(smooth, non-toxic, long-lasting). Asphalt shingles are acceptable but
release particles and chemicals. Avoid unsealed wood, tar, and treated
materials.

**Catchment area calculation:**
- Roof area (sq ft) x rainfall (inches) x 0.623 = gallons captured
- Example: 1,000 sq ft roof x 1 inch rain x 0.623 = 623 gallons

### Conveyance (Gutters and Downspouts)
- Gutters must be sized for the roof area and rainfall intensity
- Seamless aluminum or galvanized steel gutters are most durable
- Leaf guards or screens reduce debris entering the system
- Downspouts direct flow to the first flush diverter and storage

### First Flush Diverter
The first 0.02 inches of rain per square foot of catchment washes dust,
bird droppings, pollen, and debris from the roof. A first flush diverter
captures and discards this contaminated water.

**Sizing:**
- Rule of thumb: divert 1 gallon per 100 sq ft of roof area
- For a 1,000 sq ft roof: 10-gallon first flush diverter
- Ball-and-seat or tipping-bucket designs are common

**How it works:**
1. Initial rainfall fills the diverter chamber
2. A floating ball seals the chamber when full
3. Clean water bypasses the full diverter and flows to storage
4. Drain the diverter after each rain event

### Storage Tanks
Storage capacity determines how long harvested water lasts between rain
events. See sizing section below.

**Tank types:**
- **Polyethylene (poly) tanks** — most common for residential; lightweight,
  UV-stabilized, 200-10,000+ gallons; food-grade available
- **Ferro-cement** — DIY-friendly; cement and wire mesh over a frame; very
  durable and low-cost at large scale
- **Steel tanks** — corrugated galvanized or stainless; durable but heavy
  and expensive
- **Cisterns** — underground concrete or poly; saves space and keeps water
  cool; more expensive to install
- **Wooden tanks** — traditional; redwood or cypress; aesthetic but
  expensive and less common

**Tank placement considerations:**
- Elevate tanks (at least 2 feet) for gravity-fed distribution
- Place on a level, compacted surface (concrete pad, gravel bed)
- Site in shade to reduce algae growth and water heating
- Protect from freezing in cold climates (insulation, heat tape, or bury)

### Filtration and Treatment

**Multi-stage filtration for potable use:**

1. **Coarse screen** (first flush diverter) — removes large debris
2. **Settling tank** — allows suspended solids to settle (optional)
3. **Cartridge filter** — 20-50 micron sediment filter
4. **Carbon filter** — removes taste, odor, and organic compounds
5. **UV disinfection** — ultraviolet light inactivates bacteria, viruses,
   and protozoa (requires power)
6. **Boiling or chemical** — backup disinfection method

**For non-potable use** (irrigation, livestock, toilet flushing):
- First flush diverter plus a 100-micron screen is typically sufficient

### Distribution
- **Gravity-fed** — simplest; requires elevated tanks and adequate head
  pressure (1 psi per 2.31 feet of elevation)
- **Pump system** — pressure pump and pressure tank for consistent delivery
  regardless of tank level

## Sizing Your System

### Calculate Annual Yield
- Annual rainfall (inches) x roof area (sq ft) x 0.623 = annual gallons
- Multiply by 0.85 for system efficiency losses (spillage, overflow, etc.)

### Calculate Annual Demand
- Indoor use: 30-50 gallons per person per day
- Irrigation: varies widely; 0.5-1 gallon per sq ft per week during growing
  season
- Livestock: 5-30 gallons per animal per day depending on species

### Storage Sizing
- Storage should hold at least 1/4 of annual yield for seasonal buffering
- Minimum practical storage: 1,500-2,500 gallons for a household
- In dry climates with seasonal rainfall: aim for 3-6 months of supply

### Example Calculation (Southeast US)
- Annual rainfall: 50 inches
- Roof area: 1,200 sq ft
- Household: 3 people
- Annual yield: 50 x 1200 x 0.623 x 0.85 = 31,770 gallons
- Annual demand: 3 people x 40 gpd x 365 = 43,800 gallons
- Recommended storage: 5,000-10,000 gallons

## Potability and Legal Considerations

### Is Harvested Rainwater Safe to Drink?
With proper treatment (filtration + UV or boiling), harvested rainwater
meets EPA drinking water standards in most cases. Key risks:
- **Bacterial contamination** — from bird droppings, rodents on roof
- **Chemical contamination** — from roofing materials, atmospheric
  pollution
- **Mosquito breeding** — in improperly sealed tanks

### Legal Status
- **Legal everywhere in the US** for outdoor, non-potable use
- **Colorado** — restrictions were lifted in 2016; two rain barrels (110
  gallons total) allowed without permit
- **Some western states** — water rights laws may restrict large-scale
  collection
- **Check local regulations** before installing a potable system

### Testing
Test water annually for: coliform bacteria, E. coli, pH, turbidity, lead,
and nitrate. Use a state-certified laboratory.

## Maintenance Schedule
- **Monthly** — check gutters, screens, and first flush diverter
- **Quarterly** — inspect tank for sediment, algae, and mosquito larvae
- **Annually** — clean tank interior, replace sediment filters, test water
  quality, inspect all fittings and seals
- **After major storms** — clean gutters and screens of debris
