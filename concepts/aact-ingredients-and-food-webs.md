# AACT Ingredients and Food Webs

## Extract vs. AACT: The Brewing Distinction

A fundamental distinction in Elaine Ingham's methodology separates two liquid products derived from compost:

- **Compost extract** is produced by soaking compost in water and straining through mesh. Organisms are extracted but do not multiply — no food is added, no aeration is provided. Organism numbers reflect only what was in the source compost.
- **Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)** provides both food resources and continuous aeration during a 24–48 hour brewing period. Organisms actively reproduce, increasing by 10–100×. This multiplication step is the critical difference.

An extract cannot compensate for low organism counts in the source material, whereas a well-brewed AACT amplifies whatever beneficial biology is present. However, no amount of food or aeration can create organisms absent from the compost inoculum.

## Worm Leachate: Applications and Limitations

Worm leachate — liquid draining through a worm bin — is frequently confused with compost tea but is fundamentally different:

- May contain anaerobic organisms and phytotoxic compounds from oxygen-depleted bin pockets.
- Lacks the controlled aeration and selective food resources ensuring beneficial organism dominance.
- Can harbor *E. coli* and other pathogens if bin conditions were not consistently aerobic.
- No standardization of quality; results vary dramatically between bins.

AACT made using worm castings as the compost inoculum is safe and effective. Worm leachate should not substitute for properly brewed tea on food crops.

## Testing for Active Organisms: Plate Count Limitations

Ingham strongly advocates direct microscopy over plate count methods. Plate counts — growing colonies on agar — are inadequate because:

- Less than 1% of soil microorganisms grow on any given culture medium; most are non-culturable.
- Cannot distinguish active vs. dormant organisms, or beneficial vs. pathogenic species.
- Provide no information on protozoa, nematodes, or microarthropods driving nutrient cycling.
- Fungal assessment is especially unreliable since filamentous fungi do not form discrete colonies on agar.

The Soil Foodweb approach uses direct microscopic examination to measure total and active bacterial biomass (µg/ml), fungal hyphal length and diameter, protozoa counts by group, and nematode identification by feeding type.

## Direct Soil Amendments: Sugar, Kelp, and Lignite

### Sugar Additions to Soil

Adding simple sugars directly to soil triggers rapid bacterial growth with several consequences:

- **Bacterial bloom**: Simple sugars are immediately available carbon, causing fast bacterial reproduction. Bacteria incorporate nitrogen and other nutrients from soil solution into cellular biomass.
- **Nutrient immobilization**: Available nitrogen is temporarily locked inside bacterial cells and unavailable to plants. This can last days to weeks depending on conditions.
- **Anaerobic risk**: If the bloom consumes dissolved oxygen faster than atmospheric replenishment, soil microsites become anaerobic — especially likely in compacted or waterlogged soils.
- **Pest deterrence hypothesis**: Anaerobic conditions from rapid bacterial respiration have been proposed as a mechanism for deterring root-feeding pests through oxygen depletion. However, this is uncontrolled and harms beneficial organisms too.
- **Soil structure dependency**: Well-aggregated soils with good porosity maintain adequate oxygen during blooms; compacted soils are far more likely to go anaerobic.

### Root-Feeding Nematodes and Sugar: The Alcohol Hypothesis

A hypothesis exists that sugar additions deter root-feeding nematodes through alcohol production. The proposed mechanism: rapid bacterial fermentation of sugars in poorly aerated soil produces ethanol and fermentation byproducts toxic to nematodes. Ingham notes this is plausible but lacks controlled experimental data. Observed pest reduction in field situations could stem from microbial competition, soil chemistry changes, or recruitment of predatory nematodes and microarthropods following bacterial blooms.

### Kelp: Multifunctional Amendment

Cold-water extracted kelp serves multiple roles in brewing and soil application:

- **Micronutrient supply**: Over 60 trace minerals for microbial metabolism and plant nutrition.
- **Plant growth factors**: Retains cytokinins, auxins, gibberellins stimulating growth as foliar spray or soil drench.
- **Fungal surface area**: Physical structure provides attachment sites for fungal hyphae — both food and habitat.
- **Natural chelation**: Chelates micronutrients, improving bioavailability to microorganisms and plants.
- **Dual benefit**: Enhances both bacterial and fungal communities.

Cold-water extraction is critical — heat destroys the sensitive hormones and vitamins.

### Lignite as Complex Fungal Food

Lignite (leonardite) is a soft, low-rank coal serving as exceptionally complex carbon:

- Very long-chain carbon molecules only fungi can initially break down.
- Bacteria cannot access this carbon until fungi decompose the lignite structure — one of the most selective fungal foods.
- Slow-release fungal food supporting long-term fungal biomass maintenance.
- Chemically related to humic acids — essentially ancient, partially decomposed plant material.

## Ingredient Classification by Organism Target

### Simple Sugars — Bacterial Foods

- **White sugar, molasses, honey, fruit juices**: Immediately available simple carbohydrates.
- **Molasses** preferred over plain sugar: diverse sugars (sucrose, glucose, fructose) plus minerals (Ca, Mg, K, Fe) and trace humic material.
- Risk of over-application: excessive sugars drive bacterial growth so rapid that dissolved oxygen is consumed faster than aeration replaces it.

### Complex Sugars — Both Bacteria and Fungi

- **Brown sugar, malt, soy sauce**: Increasing complexity shifts balance toward fungal utilization, though bacteria still respond.
- **Fish waste**: Both groups fed, but high oxygen demand during decomposition.

### Fish Emulsion vs. Fish Hydrolysate

- **Fish hydrolysate**: Cold-processed (enzymatically digested), retains proteins, oils, vitamins, amino acids, fatty acids. Complex proteins serve as excellent fungal food. Preferred form for AACT.
- **Fish emulsion**: Heat-processed, breaks proteins into simpler bacterial foods. Some contain sulfuric acid preservative harming beneficial biology. Oils largely removed.

### Humic Acids — Selective Fungal Food

- Most selective fungal foods available in liquid form.
- Liquid products work better than dry (dry takes too long to solubilize).
- Beyond feeding fungi: chelates minerals, improves soil structure, increases cation exchange capacity.
- Recommended: Hydrahume AN (Helena), Eco-Nutrient blends, Nutri-Tech dry soluble.

### Cold Water Kelp

Uniquely versatile — enhances both bacterial and fungal communities while providing micronutrients and plant growth hormones surviving extraction.

## Various Organic Amendments

### Crab Waste
Granulated crab/shellfish waste provides chitin, calcium, and nitrogen. Chitin stimulates chitinase production by beneficial fungi and actinobacteria — enzymes degrading insect pest exoskeletons and pathogenic fungi.

### Alfalfa Meal
Supplies nitrogen plus triacontanol (natural growth regulator). Feeds both bacteria and fungi with moderate oxygen demand.

### Blood Meal
Concentrated nitrogen (12–14% N) strongly stimulating bacterial growth. Use cautiously in tea due to rapid decomposition and high oxygen demand.

### Fish Waste
Broad nutrient spectrum but highly variable quality. Swollen containers indicate the product was already anaerobic before purchase.

### Cornmeal
Complex carbohydrates and proteins favoring fungal growth, especially combined with humic acid.

### Minerals
Rock dusts, greensand, rock phosphate, azomite, glacial dust supply minerals that microorganisms solubilize biologically. Not directly plant-available — long-term nutrient investments.

## Compost-to-Water Ratio Guidelines

The compost-to-water relationship is non-linear — larger brews need proportionally less compost per gallon:

| Water Volume | Compost Needed |
|---|---|
| 5 gallons | ~1 pound |
| 25 gallons | ~7 pounds |
| 50 gallons | ~10 pounds |
| 100 gallons | ~12 pounds |
| 500 gallons | 15–20 pounds |

A machine maker claiming a 100-gallon machine needs 100 pounds of compost has a poorly designed machine. Total food addition for a 50-gallon brew should generally not exceed about one pound, split among all components.
