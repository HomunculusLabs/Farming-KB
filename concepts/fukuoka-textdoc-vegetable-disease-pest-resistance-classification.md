## Overview

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] provides a detailed classification of vegetable crops by
their natural resistance to disease and insect pests, drawn from his
experience at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural [[fukuoka-straw-scattering-testing-station-resistance]] during and
after World War II. He divides vegetables into three categories: high,
moderate, and low resistance. This classification underpins his argument
that most vegetables can be grown without pesticides when cultivated using
[[cho-poultry-natural-farming-methods]] in healthy soil with proper [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]].

## Historical Context of Pest Control in Japan

Fukuoka recalls that pesticide use on the scale seen in modern Japan is a
very recent phenomenon. He discovered an old, dust-covered booklet he had
written during the war entitled "Proposal for the Control of Disease and
Pest Damage in Vegetables." This booklet contained reference tables on
diseases and insect pests for different vegetables, along with detailed
descriptions of individual diseases, pathogenic microbes, infection
processes, and insect developmental stages. The control methods he
described were all primitive, consisting almost exclusively of skillful
trapping or repulsion. The agents most widely used at the time were herbs
such as pyrethrum, tobacco, and derris root, with minor use of lead
arsenate. Bordeaux mixture served as a universal remedy for bacterial and
fungal diseases, and sulfur preparations saw occasional use.

## The High-Resistance Category

Vegetables with high resistance require no pesticides at all under natural
farming conditions. This is the largest and most diverse group. It includes
the yam family (Chinese yam, Japanese yam), the arum family (tare), the
goosefoot family (spinach, chard, Chinese cabbage), and the carrot family
(carrot, honewort, celery, parsley). The composite family contributes
burdock, butterbur, lettuce, and garland chrysanthemum. The mint family
provides perilla and Japanese mint. The ginseng family includes udo,
ginseng, and Japanese angelica tree. The ginger family offers ginger and
Japanese ginger. The morning-glory family contributes sweet potato. The
lily family is perhaps the most extensive, encompassing Chinese leek,
garlic, scallion, Nanking shallot, Welsh onion, onion, dogtooth violet,
asparagus, lily, and tulip.

## The Moderate-Resistance Category

Vegetables with moderate resistance require little pesticide intervention.
The pea family dominates this group: garden pea, broad bean, adzuki bean,
soybean, peanut, kidney bean, asparagus bean, Egyptian kidney bean, and
sword bean. The mustard family is also well represented, including Chinese
cabbage, cabbage, daikon, turnip, Indian mustard, rapeseed, leaf mustard,
potherb mustard, and sea-kale. Black mustard also falls in this category.
These crops can generally be managed through [[companion-planting-and-polycultures]] proper
timing, with only occasional intervention needed.

## The Low-Resistance Category

Only two families fall into the low-resistance category, which requires
pesticides for reliable production under [[microwave-sterilization-versus-conventional-methods-comparison]]: the gourd
family (watermelon, cucumber, Oriental melon, pickling melon, squash,
white gourd, chayote, bottle gourd) and the potato family (tomato,
eggplant, potato, red pepper, tobacco). Fukuoka notes that these are the
crops for which natural farming methods must be applied most carefully.
Semi-wild cultivation in orchards, where [[companion-planting-with-fungi]] many
different types of vegetables replaces monoculture, can substantially
reduce pest pressure even for these vulnerable crops.

## Traditional Pest Management Without Chemicals

pesticides managed insect damage through simple, effective methods. They
