## Casing Surface Roughness Technique

## Overview

The casing surface roughness technique is a cultivation method in which the casing soil applied to colonized substrate is intentionally left with an uneven, textured surface featuring small valleys and hills rather than being smoothed flat. This technique is described in the *Guid* cultivation guide for *Psilocybe* species on [[negative-geotropism-in-mushroom-fruiting]].

## The Technique in Detail

After colonized grass seed from a 720 ml jar is shaken loose and poured into a 1-litre disposable plastic tray, any lumps within the seed are broken up using the clean rim of the jar. The surface of the grain is then levelled evenly to create a uniform substrate base. At this point, a thin layer (1.5-2.0 cm) of moist casing soil is applied using a large spoon and fork.

The guide provides a specific instruction that captures the essence of the technique: cultivators should "try to keep the casing surface even while at the same time keeping it rough (with small valleys and hills)." This seemingly contradictory instruction — maintain evenness while being rough — is the key to the technique. The overall depth of casing should be approximately uniform across the tray, but the surface topography should be deliberately irregular with small-scale undulations.

### Practical Implementation Steps

1. Level the grain surface in the tray to create a flat substrate base
2. Apply casing soil to a consistent depth of 1.5-2.0 cm across the entire tray
3. Using the fork, create a textured, irregular surface with gentle peaks and valleys
4. Do not tamp, press, or smooth the casing surface after texturing
5. Avoid creating deep trenches (more than 1 cm below the average surface level) as these can become waterlogged and anaerobic
6. Avoid creating sharp peaks that would dry out completely and fail to support mycelial growth
7. The ideal texture has been likened to a freshly raked garden bed or a ploughed field — irregular but not extreme

### Microclimate Variation

Valleys in the casing surface trap moisture and maintain higher relative humidity at the substrate-air interface, while hills provide slightly drier zones with more direct air exchange. This gradient allows the mycelium to encounter optimal pinning conditions across the surface rather than being constrained to a single humidity level. Different parts of the same tray can support different stages of the [[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]* on grass seed substrate. Different species respond differently to surface texture:

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — Highly adaptable species that responds well to both smooth and rough casing. The Ecuador strain mentioned in the guide is noted as an "easy fruiting strain" that may not require the rough casing technique to produce reliably.
- **Psilocybe mexicana** — Benefits significantly from the moisture retention provided by valleys in rough casing. This species typically produces one large flush followed by a smaller second flush; rough casing may help sustain the conditions needed for the second flush.
