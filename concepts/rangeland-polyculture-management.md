# Rangeland Polyculture and Multi-Species Management

Rangeland polyculture is the practice of managing multiple animal and plant species together on range land to achieve far greater total productivity than single-species (monoculture) grazing systems. [[bill-mollison]] argues that judicious selection of complementary animal species can multiply yields while simultaneously improving soil health, water retention, and ecological resilience.

## The Case Against Monoculture Grazing

Conventional range management typically centers on a single livestock species—most often cattle. This approach fails to utilize the full spectrum of available forage and ignores the productive potential of wildlife and other small animals that can coexist with or replace domestic stock. Polyculture systems leverage dietary specialization across species, ensuring that browse, grasses, forbs, and even insects are all converted into usable food and fiber.

## Browse Equivalents: Comparing Animal Productivity

One of the most striking data points from the manual is the concept of **browse equivalents**—the number of individual animals of a given species that consume roughly the same forage as one cow over a year:

| Species | Individuals per Cow Year |
|---|---|
| Blacktailed Jackrabbits | 266 |
| Antelope Jackrabbits | 164 |
| Ground Squirrels | 385 |
| Sheep | 6 |
| Kangaroo | 18 |

This data reveals that small species, managed in aggregate, can convert range forage on a scale comparable to large livestock—often with superior feed conversion ratios. The key insight is that these species occupy different **dietary niches**, so they do not compete directly with cattle but rather utilize resources that cattle cannot.

## Protein Efficiency: Wild vs. Domestic Species

Protein content varies dramatically between wild and domestic range animals:

- **Kangaroo**: 58% assimilable protein
- **Cattle**: 35% assimilable protein

Wild species that have evolved on native rangelands typically produce leaner, higher-protein meat relative to their forage intake. This efficiency advantage is one of the strongest arguments for integrating managed wildlife into range production systems.

## Range Diet Specialization (Table 12.4)

Different herbivores partition range resources by favoring different plant types. The following comparison of elk and antelope diets illustrates this principle:

| Plant Type | Elk Diet (%) | Antelope Diet (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Browse | Higher proportion | Lower proportion |
| Grasses | Significant component | Moderate component |
| Forbs | Moderate component | Higher proportion |

This dietary partitioning means that mixed-species grazing can achieve much higher total utilization of available vegetation without overgrazing any single plant category. The principle scales up: a system managing 40–80 complementary species in an integrated framework can approach total resource utilization.

## Insect Plagues and Range Health

[[savory-grasshopper-and-insect-outbreaks]] are a direct symptom of range depletion. When plant cover diminishes, ecosystems lose their natural checks on insect populations:

- **Grasshopper outbreaks** have been documented to cause up to **67% browse loss** on degraded range
- Plagues are tightly correlated with loss of vegetation diversity and ground cover
- Healthy, diverse plant communities support predatory insects and birds that keep herbivorous insect populations in check

### Integrated Insect Control

Rather than chemical controls, Mollison advocates biological approaches:

- **Fish** in farm ponds and wetlands consume mosquito and insect larvae
- **Guinea fowl** are voracious insect foragers, especially effective against ticks and grasshoppers
- **Turkeys** also provide significant insect predation on range
- **Preserving wetlands** is essential—these serve as breeding habitat for insectivorous birds, amphibians, and fish

### Locust Harvest as Food Strategy

In Botswana, harvested locusts provide **4× the food value of stored grains**. This reframes insect plagues from purely destructive events to potential harvest opportunities—a quintessential [[permaculture]] principle of turning problems into resources.

## Vegetation Cover and Water Infiltration

Plant cover is the single most critical factor governing how much rainfall actually enters the soil versus running off:

| Ground Cover (%) | Rainfall Infiltration (%) |
|---|---|
| 5% | 1.2% |
| 35% | 2.4% |

Even at moderate cover levels, infiltration rates remain alarmingly low. Bare or sparsely vegetated range effectively sheds water, contributing to downstream flooding and depriving the local soil profile of moisture.

## Soil Compaction from Stocking

Continuous trampling by livestock causes severe soil degradation:

- **44% reduction** in soil pore space in the top 2 inches after **9 years of continuous stocking**
- Compacted soils have reduced water-holding capacity, reduced root penetration, and impaired microbial activity
- Recovery of compacted soils is extremely slow without active intervention

## Vegetation Cover, Runoff, and Erosion (Table 12.6)

The relationship between plant community type and erosion is dramatic:

| Plant Community | Runoff (%) | Erosion Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat grass (perennial) | 0.4% | Very low |
| Mixed perennial range | Low | Low |
| Annual weed species | 60.8% | Very high |
| Bare soil | ~100% | Catastrophic |

A threshold of **65–70% plant cover** is needed to effectively [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]]. Below this level, runoff and soil loss increase exponentially. Perennial grasses are vastly superior to annual weeds for holding soil and capturing water.

## Fencing Strategy: Valley to Ridge

[[living-fences-vs-conventional-fencing]] often follows [[holzer-high-beds-property-boundaries]] without regard to landform. Mollison recommends **valley-to-ridge fencing**:

- Aligns fence lines perpendicular to water flow across slopes
- Creates grazing paddocks that follow natural drainage patterns
- Minimizes concentrated stock traffic along waterways
- Reduces erosion by distributing animal impact more evenly
- Allows [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] plans that rest riparian zones during critical periods

## Game Species Management

Managed [[wildlife-farming-and-game-management]] represents the most productive end of the rangeland polyculture spectrum, potentially incorporating **40–80 species** in integrated systems.

### Capture Methods

Several established techniques exist for managing wild game populations:

- **Drift traps** — funnel animals into enclosed areas using directional fencing
- **Yard traps at waterholes** — exploit animals' dependence on water sources
- **Baited box traps** — effective for smaller species like rabbits and foxes
- **Cage traps** — reusable and allow selective harvest
- **Nets** — effective for bird species and fleet animals
- **Chemical pellets** — sedative delivery for larger, dangerous game

### Population Estimation

Effective management requires accurate population data. Methods include aerial surveys, track counts, dung transects, and mark-recapture techniques. Population monitoring determines sustainable harvest rates and detects imbalances before they become crises.

## Summary of Design Principles

1. **Maximize species diversity** to utilize all available forage niches
2. **Maintain 65–70% ground cover** to prevent erosion and maximize infiltration
3. **Use valley-to-ridge fencing** to align management with natural landform
4. **Monitor soil pore space** as an indicator of stocking sustainability
5. **Integrate biological insect control** via wetlands, fowl, and fish
6. **Reframe plagues as harvest opportunities** (e.g., locust collection)
7. **Plan for 40–80 managed species** in fully integrated range systems
8. **Rotate stock frequently** to prevent soil compaction and allow recovery
