# Biological Sewage Treatment Using Higher [[biological-sewage-treatment-systems|systems]]. These systems leverage the natural metabolic capacities of aquatic and semi-aquatic plants to remove nutrients, heavy metals, pathogens, and organic pollutants from sewage and contaminated water. The principle is simple: contaminated water is passed through constructed wetlands, ponds, or planted channels where living plants actively uptake, transform, and sequester pollutants.

Unlike conventional treatment plants that consume significant energy for aeration, chemical dosing, and sludge processing, plant-based systems are largely self-maintaining, produce valuable biomass as a byproduct, and can be integrated into broader permaculture designs for food, fodder, and energy production.

## Proven Large-Scale Systems

### Israel: Canalized Pond System with Algal Mats

The Israeli system uses long, narrow, canalized ponds equipped with paddle-wheel aerators that keep the water slowly moving. Under these conditions, dense algal mats form naturally across the water surface. These algal mats serve a dual purpose:

- **Water purification:** Algae absorb dissolved nutrients and oxygenate the water through photosynthesis during daylight hours.
- **Protein harvest:** The dense algal biomass is harvested and used directly as feed for carp and poultry, where it can replace up to **50% of soybean protein** in rations. This transforms a waste stream into a high-value agricultural input.

To manage the algal mats for efficient harvesting, **microwave radiation** can be applied to break up the dense mats, making them easier to collect and process.

### Holland: Reed and Plant Harvest Systems

Dutch researchers developed systems based on planted reed beds where the plants themselves are the primary treatment agents. In these systems, sewage flows through dense stands of reeds and other macrophytes. The plants absorb nutrients through their root systems while the rhizosphere (the zone of soil immediately surrounding the roots) hosts dense microbial communities that further degrade organic matter.

The above-ground plant biomass is periodically harvested, physically removing the accumulated nutrients from the system entirely. This harvested material can be composted, used as mulch, or in some cases fed to livestock.

## Alternating Aerobic and Anaerobic (A.A.A.) Digestion

One of the most effective treatment strategies described is the **Alternating Aerobic and Anaerobic (A.A.A.)** cycle. Rather than maintaining continuous aeration (which is energy-intensive) or continuous anaerobic conditions (which are slower), the system alternates between the two states:

- **2 hours aerobic** (aerators on)
- **6 hours anaerobic** (aerators off)

This means the system is only actively aerated **30% of the time**, dramatically reducing energy consumption while achieving superior treatment results compared to either steady-state condition alone.

### A.A.A. Performance Metrics

| Parameter | Influent | Effluent | Reduction |
|-----------|----------|----------|-----------|
| Nitrogen  | 20 mg/L  | < 5 mg/L | > 75%     |
| Phosphorus | 8.5 mg/L | < 1 mg/L | > 88%     |

When combined with **ferric chloride** dosing, phosphorus removal is especially effective as the ferric ions precipitate phosphate compounds that settle out of the water column.

The aerobic phase enables nitrification (ammonia to nitrate), while the anaerobic phase enables denitrification (nitrate to nitrogen gas). **Solar ponds** can passively heat anaerobic digesters, reducing external energy inputs.

### Schoenoplectus spp. (Bulrush)

Bulrush species are exceptional at **heavy metal uptake**, particularly:

- Copper (Cu)
- Cobalt (Co)
- Nickel (Ni)
- Manganese (Mn)

Additionally, Schoenoplectus species **exude mould-inhibiting antibiotics** into the surrounding water, providing a natural antimicrobial effect that supplements pathogen removal.

### Scirpus spp. (Club Rush)

Scirpus species are notable for their ability to **break down phenolic compounds**, including the particularly recalcitrant **pentachlorophenol** — a wood preservative and industrial chemical that is highly toxic and persistent in the environment. This makes Scirpus invaluable for treating industrial wastewater contaminated with phenolic compounds.

### Juncus effusus (Soft Rush) and Rush Species

Rushes possess well-developed **aerenchyma tissue** — specialized spongy tissue with large air spaces that allows efficient oxygen transport from aerial shoots down to the submerged root zone. This makes them particularly effective at breaking down **chlorinated hydrocarbons** and other persistent organic pollutants that require aerobic degradation.

Juncus has demonstrated the ability to treat:

- **Cyanide** compounds
- **Thiocyanates**
- **Phenols**

Treatment times of **7+ hours** have been shown to achieve significant reductions in these contaminants.

## Comfrey Beds for Animal Waste

**Comfrey (Symphytum spp.)** beds effectively absorb animal faecal waste. Comfrey has deep roots (2-3 meters) and is a vigorous nutrient accumulator. Waste directed onto comfrey beds is rapidly absorbed, producing harvestable leaf biomass for compost, livestock feed, or mulch.

## Integrated Productive Systems

The **Werribee sewage farm** in Australia demonstrated that beef cattle can be raised on sewage-irrigated pasture for over 35 years without health issues. Treated sewage provides irrigation and nutrients while soil/plant systems polish the water — complete integration of waste treatment and food production.

### Coppiced Poplar Energy Plantations

Fast-growing **poplar (Populus spp.)** plots established on treated sewage land can be managed through **coppicing** (cutting back to ground level to stimulate regrowth). In one documented case, coppiced poplar plots produced enough wood fuel to supply **60% of a town's energy needs**. The poplars absorb large quantities of nutrients and water from the treated sewage while producing a renewable energy crop.

## Water Quality Considerations

### Salinity

A notable concern in sewage reuse systems is **salinity increase** as water passes through towns, typically **300-400 ppm** in total dissolved solids. This must be accounted for in downstream irrigation design, and treatment wetland plant selection should include salt-tolerant species.

## Urine Separation and Reuse

Human urine, when separated from faecal waste at source, is a remarkably valuable resource. It contains the majority of the nitrogen and phosphorus excreted by humans in a form that is immediately available to plants.

### Documented Applications

- **2% urea solution on rice** increases grain protein content by **40%**, significantly improving nutritional quality
- **5% urine solution** applied as a foliar spray controls mould infections on cucurbit crops (squash, cucumber, melon), eliminating the need for chemical fungicides

Urine separation at source allows this nutrient stream to be collected, stored, and applied directly to crops as a high-value liquid fertilizer.

## Design Principles

1. **Use living systems** rather than mechanical-chemical processes wherever possible
2. **Harvest the biomass** produced by treatment systems to close nutrient loops
3. **Combine aerobic and anaerobic phases** for superior nitrogen and phosphorus removal
4. **Select plant species** for specific pollutant targets (metals, phenols, pathogens)
5. **Integrate treatment with production** — food, fodder, energy, and fiber
6. **Minimize energy inputs** through solar heating, passive aeration cycling, and gravity flow
7. **Design for resilience** with diverse plant communities over monocultures
8. **Account for salinity** and cumulative water quality changes
