## Types of Mycorrhizae

### Endomycorrhizae (Arbuscular Mycorrhizae)

Endomycorrhizae, also called arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM), are the most common type, associating with approximately 80-90 percent of all plant species. Key characteristics:

- The fungal hyphae actually penetrate the plant root cell walls
- They form distinctive structures called arbuscules (tree-like branching structures) inside root cells where nutrient exchange occurs
- They also form vesicles — storage organs for lipids
- They associate with most annual and perennial plants, including most crops, vegetables, herbs, and fruits
- They do not produce visible fruiting bodies (mushrooms)
- Spores are relatively large (50-400 micrometers) and must be distributed by water, animals, or inoculation

Major genera include: [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]], [[ectomycorrhizae-forest-life]], [[mycorrhizal-fertilizer-reduction]]), and nitrogen — a plant can get up to 85 percent of its nitrogen through mycorrhizae

This exchange occurs in specialized structures: arbuscules in endomycorrhizae, and the Hartig net in ectomycorrhizae.

### Root Signaling

The symbiosis begins with chemical signaling between plant and fungus:
- Plants release strigolactone hormones that stimulate fungal hyphal growth and branching
- Without these chemical signals from the plant, mycorrhizal spores may germinate but will not colonize the root
- From 25 to 30 chemicals are produced by both participants in the ectomycorrhizal formation process
- This specificity has evolved to ensure appropriate partnerships

### Extended Root Network

Mycorrhizal hyphae extend far beyond the plant's root zone, dramatically increasing the effective absorptive surface area:
- A single mycorrhizal plant can access soil volumes hundreds of times greater than a non-mycorrhizal plant
- Hyphae can access tiny soil pores that roots cannot penetrate
- Ectomycorrhizal hyphae can grow into rock and dissolve minerals, capturing nutrients the plant could never access alone

## Key Benefits for Plants

1. **Enhanced nutrient uptake** — especially phosphorus, nitrogen, and micronutrients
2. **Improved water uptake** — mycorrhizal plants show significantly better drought tolerance
3. **Disease resistance** — the fungal mantle and biochemical defenses protect roots from pathogens
4. **Heavy metal tolerance** — fungi can take up and sequester heavy metals, protecting the host plant
5. **Temperature tolerance** — mycorrhizal associations help plants withstand temperature extremes
6. **Soil structure improvement** — hyphae and [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]], a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi:
- Glomalin acts as a biological glue, binding soil particles into stable aggregates
- It is highly resistant to decomposition and can persist in soil for decades
- It contributes significantly to soil carbon storage
- Soils with healthy mycorrhizal populations have higher levels of soil organic matter and better structure

## Plants That Do NOT Form Mycorrhizae

Not all plants form mycorrhizal associations. Non-mycorrhizal plants are primarily members of these families:
- Amaranthaceae (amaranths)
- Brassicaceae (mustards, cabbages, broccoli)
- Caryophyllaceae (carnations)
- Chenopodiaceae (goosefoots, spinach, beets)
- Polygonaceae (buckwheat, sorrel)
- Portulacaceae (purslanes)
- Proteaceae (proteas)

This is significant for agriculture and crop rotation planning.
