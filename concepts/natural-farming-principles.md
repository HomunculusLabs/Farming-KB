# Natural Farming Principles

Natural farming is an agricultural philosophy developed by Japanese farmer
and philosopher Masanobu Fukuoka (1913-2008) over more than fifty years of
practice and observation on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan. The system
rejects modern agricultural science's premise that nature must be controlled
and improved through human intervention. Instead, it proposes that the most
productive and sustainable approach to growing food is to work within
natural systems rather than against them.

Fukuoka described his method as "do-nothing farming," though this is a
deliberate provocation rather than literal description. The approach
requires deep observation and timing, but eliminates plowing, chemical
fertilizers, pesticides, weeding, and pruning — the five pillars of his
methodology. Natural farming stands in contrast to both conventional
chemical agriculture and organic farming, which Fukuoka viewed as merely
another form of scientific intervention that ultimately depletes the land.

## The Five Principles

### No Tillage

Fukuoka argued that plowing disrupts the soil ecosystem, destroying
fungal networks, earthworm habitat, and the natural structure that
microorganisms and plant roots create over time. In his system, soil is
worked entirely by the action of plant roots, soil animals, and natural
weathering processes.

His fields remained unplowed for over thirty years, yet consistently
produced yields comparable to or exceeding conventional methods. The key
insight is that seeds in nature germinate without plowing; the practice
of tillage was adopted to control weeds and incorporate fertilizers, both
of which become unnecessary under the natural farming framework.

### No Fertilizer

[[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] and prepared compost are both rejected. Applying
organic matter harvested from one area to another merely transfers
fertility, ultimately depleting the source land. Instead, his system
relies on returning all crop residues to the field and maintaining a
permanent clover cover crop.

Fukuoka measured approximately 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria per
gram of soil on his farm, making external fertility inputs unnecessary.

### No Pesticides

Rather than attempting to eliminate insect [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]], natural
farming accepts their presence as part of a balanced ecosystem. Fukuoka
argued that nature maintains its own equilibrium, and that disease and
pest outbreaks are symptoms of an imbalanced system created by human
intervention.

The diverse plant community in his fields — rice, barley, clover, and
various weeds — supported a correspondingly diverse insect population that
kept individual species from reaching damaging levels. Predatory insects
thrived alongside herbivorous ones, [[fukuoka-creating-natural-farm-site-selection-establishment]] [[fukuoka-textdoc-biological-control-fallacy-pheromone-critique]].

### No Weeding

Nature does not arbitrarily designate one plant as a weed. In Fukuoka's
system, so-called weeds play essential roles in soil protection, moisture
retention, and habitat provision for beneficial organisms. Rather than
eradicating weeds, he managed them through competition and timing.

The white clover ground cover serves as a living mulch that suppresses
undesirable weeds while fixing nitrogen. Temporary flooding of rice
paddies weakens the clover enough to give rice seedlings a competitive
advantage without killing the clover outright.

### No Pruning

Fruit trees in their natural habitat grow without pruning, producing
branches that do not tangle and receiving adequate sunlight. Fukuoka
observed that pruning stimulates excessive vegetative growth at the
expense of fruit, and that unpruned trees bore fully every year.

## The Rice-Barley-Clover Rotation

Fukuoka's signature technique is the "direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter
grain/rice succession in a clover cover." The cycle begins in autumn when
clover and winter grain (barley or wheat) are broadcast over the ripening
rice heads before the fall harvest.

After harvesting rice, the straw is spread uncut over the field. Rice
seed encapsulated in clay pellets is scattered before winter. In late
spring, the winter grain is harvested and its straw likewise spread. The
field is flooded briefly to suppress clover and give rice seedlings a
chance to establish, then managed with minimal irrigation through the
growing season.

The entire cycle requires essentially only sowing seed and spreading
straw — the labor of one or two people is sufficient for a quarter-acre.
Yields consistently reached approximately 22 bushels each of rice and
winter grain per quarter-acre, exceeding the local average.

## Philosophical Foundation

Natural farming is rooted in what Fukuoka called "green philosophy" —
the recognition that human knowledge and intervention have caused more
ecological destruction than benefit. He viewed [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] as
reductionist, studying isolated variables while missing the holistic
relationships that sustain natural systems.

## Relationship to Permaculture

Fukuoka's work was a major influence on Bill Mollison and David Holmgren
when they developed permaculture in the 1970s. His principles of working
with nature and minimizing external inputs are foundational to
permaculture, though Fukuoka himself felt permaculture retained too much
human design compared to his ideal of complete non-interference.
