# Mushroom Spawning Techniques and Casing

## Overview

The processes of spawning (introducing mushroom [[mycelium]] into the prepared compost bed) and casing (covering the spawned bed with a layer of soil) represent the critical transition from compost preparation to active mushroom production. These operations require careful timing, precise technique, and ongoing vigilance regarding moisture and temperature. Errors at this stage can destroy weeks of compost preparation work and significantly reduce or eliminate the crop.

## Bed Installation

### Compost Placement and Compression

Whatever type of bed is employed, the compost is pitched into the designated area and immediately arranged and somewhat compressed. Compression may be accomplished with blows from a shovel or by "firming" with a compressing board. The purpose is to prevent the compost from becoming loose and dry.

The correct degree of compression is important: the bed should not be hammered with a mallet, but should be firm enough even before spawning to support a person without sinking more than 1 or 2 inches. Over-compression excludes air and impedes mycelial growth; under-compression leads to excessive drying and poor thermal properties.

## Between Making and Spawning Beds

### Vigilance Required

With the installation of the beds, vigilance must be maintained regarding both moisture content and temperature. The compost must remain moist. If there is rapid drying at the surface, it must be sprinkled, though in a suitable cellar or mushroom house little attention to watering should be necessary during this interval.

### The Hand Test for Moisture

The one practical test for sufficient moisture is simple: compress some compost in the hand. Under such pressure, it should not be easy to squeeze out any drops of water, yet the hand will be distinctly moistened. This condition usually corresponds to a water content of approximately 65 percent, though the qualitative hand test is more meaningful for practical purposes than the quantitative figure.

### Temperature Monitoring and Spawning Timing

Beds must be observed day to day regarding temperature, since it is often advantageous to spawn as soon as the temperature permits. The temperature dynamics after bed preparation follow a characteristic pattern:

- **Initial rise**: For a few days after bed preparation, the temperature may rise as fermentation continues within the bed
- **Subsequent decline**: The temperature promptly declines as fermentation subsides

### Spawning Temperature Guidelines

| Condition | Temperature | Notes |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| **Too hot to spawn** | Above 75°F | Must wait for decline |
| **Maximum permissible** | 75°F | Only when temperature is unquestionably declining |
| **Generally preferable** | 65–70°F | When maintaining 55°F growing temperature |
| **Acceptable range** | 50–65°F | Does no harm; growth will be adequate |
| **Too cold** | Below 50°F | Growth will be very slow |
| **Fatal if maintained** | 85–90°F | Earlier guides erroneously advised this |

### Mould Development Before Spawning

Often, while waiting to spawn the beds, a profuse development of mould occurs, and also the smaller species of ink caps (*[[coprinus-fimetari]]*). This is more frequent where shavings or sawdust constitute a considerable part of the compost. This growth will do no harm, but:

- If profuse before spawning: Wait a few days before proceeding
- If abundant after spawning: May be eliminated by further compression of the compost or by early "casing"

## Spawning Procedure

### Brick Spawn

When [[brick-spawn]] is used, the bricks are broken into about 9 to 12 or more pieces, each piece approximately the size of a hen's egg. One piece is required for each square foot of bed space.

### Flake Spawn

Of [[flake-spawn]], an amount half as large as one's fist is not excessive per planting point.

### Planting Technique

The spawning procedure requires a specific technique:

1. **Lift the compost** at the planting point
2. **Insert the spawn piece deep enough** with the right hand so that it may be fully covered with about 1 inch of manure
3. **Press the compost firmly** over the spawn with the left hand
4. **Create an aeration passage**: When the right hand is removed, an aerating passage is left to one side of the inserted spawn piece—this is intentional and beneficial for gas exchange

With the compost firm about it, the spawn will begin growth in approximately **one week to 10 days**.

## Casing

### Purpose and Timing

Casing consists of covering the bed throughout with a layer of earth from 1 to 1½ inches deep. It is generally not advisable to case until it may be seen that the spawn is running well, unless conditions in the house make casing necessary to maintain satisfactory bed moisture.

The purposes of casing include:

- **Moisture regulation**: Providing a reservoir of moisture at the bed surface
- **Microclimate creation**: Creating a humid micro-environment at the interface between compost and casing that stimulates pinning
- **Physical support**: Providing a medium through which mushrooms can emerge
- **Contamination barrier**: Helping to suppress competitive mould growth on the bed surface

### Suitable Casing Materials

Any fairly rich loam is good for casing. Specific recommendations include:

- **Good garden loam**: Excellent choice
- **Potting soil**: Well-suited for the purpose
- **Soil beneath grass sod**: Excellent, as it typically has good texture and microbial content
- **Calcareous soil mixed with loam**: Used by French growers; desirable from a sanitary standpoint

### Unsuitable Casing Materials

- **Pure sand**: Loses moisture too easily, leading to rapid drying of the bed surface
- **Heavy clay**: Subject to baking, especially where artificial heat is employed; creates hard crusts that impede mushroom emergence
- **Woods mould**: Objectionable due to the abundance of other fungi in this material, and especially because of its tendency to increase the number of mushroom fruits at the expense of individual size

### Casing Soil Preparation

The casing soil should be secured some time in advance of use. It requires screening only if it contains numerous sticks and stones. The soil should be well moistened by repeated sprinkling before application.

## Care After Casing

### The Central Challenge

One of the chief difficulties in mushroom growing is encountered in caring for the beds after spawning, although the only factors to be considered besides temperature are the moisture of the bed and the moisture of the air. This deceptively simple-sounding task is where many growers fail.

### Common Mistakes

Two extremes characterize amateur failures:

1. **Overwatering**: Many amateurs drench the bed with water and destroy the spawn through waterlogging and oxygen exclusion
2. **Underwatering**: Others are afraid to water even when there is rapid drying out, causing the spawn to retreat from the surface and fail to bear mushrooms

### Correct Approach

When the casing material is applied, the compost should be exactly correct as regards moisture. The casing soil should be well moistened by repeated sprinkling before application. After casing, the grower must walk a careful line between these extremes, maintaining adequate surface moisture without waterlogging the bed. This requires regular monitoring, familiarity with the feel of properly moistened casing, and the judgment to apply water in measured quantities when needed rather than in drenching doses.
