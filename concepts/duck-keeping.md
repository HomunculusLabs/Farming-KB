# Duck Keeping

Ducks are among the most practical and enjoyable poultry for the homestead.
They lay large, rich eggs, provide excellent meat, [[query-how-to-control-pests-without-chemicals]], and
fertilize gardens with their nitrogen-rich manure. Unlike chickens, ducks
thrive on wet ground, forage aggressively for insects and slugs, and are
generally more cold-hardy and disease-resistant.

## Breed Selection

### Egg Breeds
- **Khaki Campbell** — Champion layers, 250-340 eggs per year. Active,
  good foragers. One of the best homestead egg breeds.
- **Indian Runner** — Tall, upright posture. 200-300 eggs/year. Excellent
  foragers, entertaining to watch. Come in many colors.
- **Welsh Harlequin** — Beautiful, calm, 240-330 eggs/year. Good dual-purpose.
  Excellent foragers, good mothers. Critically endangered — worth preserving.
- **Ancona** — 210-280 eggs/year. Good foragers, hardy, colorful.

### Meat Breeds
- **Pekin** — Standard commercial duck. 8-12 lbs, fast-growing (7-8 weeks
  to harvest). Good temperament. Most common duck in the US.
- **Rouen** — Large, colorful (Mallard coloring), 6-9 lbs. Slow-growing
  but excellent flavor. Good foragers.
- **Muscovy** — Not a true duck (Cairina moschata). 8-14 lbs. Excellent
  foragers, quiet (no quacking), lean red meat. Roost in trees. Broody
  and good mothers. Climb and fly well.
- **Aylesbury** — Large, white, 8-10 lbs. Good meat breed but rare.

### Dual-Purpose
- **Cayuga** — Iridescent black-green, 6-8 lbs. 100-200 eggs/year. Hardy,
  calm. Attractive black eggs early in the season (fade to white).
- **Buff Orpington** — 7-8 lbs. 150-220 eggs/year. Calm, good mothers.
- **Saxony** — 6-8 lbs. 190-260 eggs/year. Good foragers, beautiful.
- **Swedish Blue** — 5-6.5 lbs. 130-180 eggs/year. Hardy, good foragers.

### Bantam and Ornamental
- **Call Ducks** — Tiny, vocal, primarily ornamental. Good for [[cervantes-mealybugs-scale-insect-control]]
  in gardens.
- **Silver Appleyard** — 6-8 lbs. Good layers, good meat, beautiful.

## Housing

### Shelter Requirements
- Minimum 3-4 sq ft per duck indoors. More is always better.
- Ducks do not roost — they sleep on the ground. Bed with straw or wood
  shavings.
- Floor must stay dry despite ducks' love of water. Deep bedding with
  frequent topping helps.
- Ventilation is essential. Ducks produce a lot of moisture from their
  respiration and water play.
- Nests: floor-level boxes or simple corners with straw. Ducks are not
  particular about nests — they lay where they want.
- Muscovies prefer elevated roosting and nest boxes (they are tree ducks).

### Water Access
- Ducks need water deep enough to submerge their entire head to clean their
  eyes and nares. A bucket or trough works.
- Kiddie pools are ideal for bathing. Change water frequently.
- Water should be near but not inside the sleeping area to keep bedding dry.
- Ducks are incredibly messy with water. Plan for splash zones and drainage.

### Predator Protection
- Secure housing at night. Raccoons, foxes, owls, and mink all prey on ducks.
- 1/2 inch hardware cloth on all openings. Raccoons can reach through
  standard chicken wire.
- See [[livestock-fencing-guide]] for perimeter fencing.

## Feeding

### Layer Feed
- Commercial waterfowl or poultry layer feed (16% protein) is the base.
- Feed free-choice or twice daily. 4-6 oz per duck per day.
- Oyster shell free-choice for calcium (especially for heavy layers).
- Avoid medicated feeds — ducks eat more than chickens and may overdose.

### Foraging
- Ducks are excellent foragers. On good pasture with a pond, they can meet
  30-50% of their nutritional needs.
- They consume enormous quantities of slugs, snails, insects, and tadpoles.
- Excellent [[insectary-plants-for-pest-control]] in gardens and orchards.
- Duck foraging in rice paddies is a traditional Asian polyculture.
- See [[pasture-management]] for integration with grazing systems.

### Supplementary Feeds
- Peas, oats, barley, and wheat as scratch/treats.
- Kitchen scraps: vegetables, fruit, bread (in moderation).
- Fish meal or dried mealworms as protein supplements during molt or breeding.
- Avoid: citrus, onions, spinach (interferes with calcium absorption in excess).

### Water
- Ducks require water with all meals — they need to wet their food to
  swallow.
- Provide water near feeding areas.

## Egg Production

- Duck eggs are larger than chicken eggs (3-3.5 oz vs 2-2.5 oz).
- Higher fat and protein content. Excellent for baking — richer texture,
  better rise.
- Thicker shells, longer shelf life (up to 6 weeks refrigerated).
- Most breeds lay 200-300+ eggs per year.
- Ducks lay primarily in the morning. Collect eggs daily.
- Broodiness varies by breed. Muscovies are excellent setters. Most
  domestic ducks have limited broodiness.
- Incubation: 28 days for most breeds (35 days for Muscovy). Temperature:
  99.5°F, humidity: 55-65% (raise to 80% last 3 days).

## Foraging and Pest Control

- One duck can consume hundreds of [[jadam-pesticide-for-slugs-and-snails]] per day.
- They effectively control mosquito larvae in ponds and water features.
- Ducks in orchards eat fallen fruit (reducing pest habitat) and fertilize
  trees.
- Integrate with [[integrated-homestead-design]] for maximum benefit.
- Ducks can be used in rice paddies, cabbage fields, and vineyards for
  [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]].
- Avoid letting ducks near young vegetable seedlings — they will trample
  and eat them.

## Predator Protection

### Daytime Predators
- Hawks, eagles, dogs, foxes, coyotes.
- Provide overhead cover (netting, shrubs) if raptors are a problem.
- Livestock guardian dogs are very effective.

### Nighttime Predators
- Raccoons, owls, mink, weasels, opossums, foxes.
- Secure coop with 1/2 inch hardware cloth. Lock every night.
- Electric fencing around the coop perimeter is highly effective.
- See [[livestock-fencing-guide]] and [[homestead-poultry-health]] for
  biosecurity measures.

## Health Care

Ducks are generally hardy and disease-resistant compared to chickens.

### Common Issues
- Bumblefoot — bacterial infection of foot pad. More common on rough or
  wire surfaces. Treat with Epsom salt soaks and antibiotic ointment.
