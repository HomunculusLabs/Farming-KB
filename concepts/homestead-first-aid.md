# Homestead First Aid

Rural homesteading means greater distance from emergency services, more exposure to machinery, animals, weather, and wilderness hazards. A robust first aid kit, emergency plans, and self-reliant medical knowledge are essential. This page covers the homestead first aid kit, common injuries, poison control, natural remedies (see [[herbal-medicine]]), and emergency preparedness for power outages, severe weather, and other disasters. Integrates with [[homesteading-infrastructure]], [[homestead-water-systems]], [[food-preservation]], and [[seasonal-planning]].

## Homestead First Aid Kit

### Core Supplies (Keep Two Kits)

**Primary kit** — Home base (bathroom, mudroom, or kitchen):
- Adhesive bandages (assorted sizes), butterfly closures
- Sterile gauze pads (2×2, 4×4), rolled gauze, elastic wrap (Ace)
- Medical tape (paper and cloth)
- Antiseptic: betadine, chlorhexidine, or hydrogen peroxide
- Antibiotic ointment (bacitracin or triple antibiotic)
- Burn gel and non-stick burn dressings
- Eye wash (saline solution + irrigation cup)
- Instant cold packs (×6) and instant heat packs (×4)
- Splint material (SAM splint or aluminum finger splints)
- Triangle bandages (×4), safety pins
- Tourniquet (CAT tourniquet — learn proper use)
- Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot or similar)
- EMT shears, tweezers, tick removal tool
- Disposable gloves (nitrile, ×2 boxes)
- Thermometer, disposable tongue depressors
- Oral rehydration salts (ORS) packets
- Over-the-counter medications: ibuprofen, acetaminophen, diphenhydramine (Benadryl), loperamide (Imodium), pseudoephedrine, antacids
- Hydrocortisone cream (1%), calamine lotion
- Petroleum jelly, antibiotic eye drops
- Emergency blankets (mylar, ×4)
- First aid manual (Wilderness First Aid or similar)

**Portable kit** — Backpack for field use:
- Smaller version of above; add whistle, signal mirror, headlamp
- Keep in truck, tractor, or ATV

### Homestead-Specific Additions
- **Animal handling gloves** (welding gauntlets for goats, poultry handling gloves)
- **Livestock wound care**: VetWrap, chlorhexidine spray, fly repellent wound ointment
- **Tick key/forceps** — essential in rural areas; ticks carry Lyme, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, ehrlichiosis
- **Reptile hook or snake tongs** if in venomous snake territory
- **EpiPen** if anyone has known allergies (bee stings are a real risk with [[beekeeping]])
- **Eye protection** and face shields for chainsaw/chipper work (see [[homesteading-infrastructure]])

### Natural Remedy Additions (See [[herbal-medicine]])
- **Calendula salve** — wound healing, anti-inflammatory
- **Plantain poultice** — drawing salve for splinters, bites, stings
- **Yarrow** — styptic for bleeding, fever reducer
- **Comfrey root poultice** — bone healing, sprains (external use only)
- **Echinacea tincture** — immune support for early infections
- **Elderberry syrup** — antiviral for cold/flu onset
- **Chamomile tincture** — calming, digestive upset, anti-inflammatory
- **Tea tree oil** — antifungal, antiseptic
- **Honey (raw)** — wound dressing, antibacterial, burn treatment
- **Witch hazel** — astringent, insect bites, minor bleeding
- **Apple cider vinegar** — sunburn, insect stings, fungal skin issues

## Common Homestead Injuries

### Cuts and Lacerations
Most common on a homestead — tools, wire fencing, glass, sheet metal.
1. **Assess severity**: Deep cuts (>1/4 inch), heavy bleeding, or cuts on joints/tendons need professional attention. Apply direct pressure with clean gauze.
2. **Clean thoroughly**: Irrigate with clean water or saline under pressure (syringe or irrigation bottle). This is the single most important step for preventing infection.
3. **Close if appropriate**: Steri-Strips or butterfly closures for clean, straight-edged cuts <6 hours old. Deeper or older wounds: pack with moist gauze, dress, and monitor for signs of infection.
4. **Dress and monitor**: Change dressings daily. Watch for redness expanding from wound, heat, swelling, red streaks, fever, or pus — signs of infection requiring antibiotics.

### Burns
Common from cooking, canning ([[food-preservation]]), wood stoves, welding, solar water heaters.
1. **Cool immediately**: Run cool (not cold) water over burn for 10-20 minutes. Never use ice.
2. **Assess**: First degree (red, painful), second degree (blisters), third degree (white/charred, painless). Third degree always needs emergency care.
3. **Treat**: First/second degree — apply burn gel or raw honey (antibacterial), cover with non-stick dressing. Second degree larger than 3 inches: seek medical care.
4. **Don't**: Never pop blisters. Never apply butter or oil.

### Sprains and Strains
Common from livestock handling, fence building, lifting feed/animals, manual labor.
1. **RICE**: Rest, Ice (20 min on / 20 min off), Compression (elastic wrap), Elevation
2. **Comfrey poultice** (external) for sprains — see [[herbal-medicine]]
3. **When to seek care**: Cannot bear weight, joint feels unstable, numbness/tingling, significant swelling

### Animal Injuries
- **Goat handling**: Goats headbutt, crush fingers against fencing, and transmit orf (contagious ecthyma) — wear gloves. See [[goat-keeping]].
- **Duck injuries**: Ducks can inflict painful bites; watch for bumblefoot (footpad infection). See [[duck-keeping]].
- **Bee stings**: Remove stinger by scraping (not squeezing). Ice, antihistamine, watch for anaphylaxis (throat swelling, difficulty breathing) — use EpiPen if prescribed, call 911.
- **Tick bites**: Remove within 24 hours to reduce disease transmission. Use tick key, grasp at mouthparts, pull straight out. Clean with antiseptic. Monitor for rash (especially bullseye for Lyme), fever, flu-like symptoms for 30 days.

### Chainsaw and Tool Injuries
- **Chainsaw**: Always wear chaps, eye protection, hearing protection, steel-toe boots. Kickback is the #1 cause of injury — never cut with the upper tip of the bar. Keep first aid kit within reach when cutting.
- **Axe/maul**: Sharpen regularly — dull tools cause more injuries. Clear swing area. Never chop above shoulder height.
- **Power tools**: Unplug before adjusting. Use push sticks for table saws. Keep guards in place.

## Poison Control

### Toxic Plants on the Homestead
Know these before they cause harm:
| Plant | Toxic Part | Symptoms | Where Found |
|-------|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Poison ivy/oak/sumac | Urushiol oil | Blistering rash, itching | Edges, fencerows, woodlands |
| Rhubarb leaves | Oxalic acid | Nausea, vomiting, kidney damage | Garden beds |
| Tomato/potato leaves | Solanine | GI distress, neurological | Nightshade garden |
| Foxglove | Cardiac glycosides | Heart arrhythmia, fatal | Ornamental gardens |
| Lily of the Valley | Cardiac glycosides | Heart arrhythmia | Shade gardens |
| Jimsonweed (Datura) | Tropane alkaloids | Hallucinations, tachycardia, fatal | Disturbed soil, barnyards |
| Pokeweed | Phytolaccatoxin | Severe GI distress | Fencerows, pastures |
| Mountain laurel | Grayanotoxins | Nausea, cardiac | Woodlands, pastures |
| Yew (ornamental) | Taxine alkaloids | Sudden cardiac death | Landscaping |

**Treatment for plant poisoning**: Call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222). Do not induce vomiting unless instructed. Save a sample of the plant for identification. See [[permaculture-plants-reference]] for plant identification.

### Venomous Snakes (North America)
- **Know your local species**: Rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths (water moccasins), coral snakes
- **Prevention**: Wear boots and long pants in tall grass/brush. Use a walking stick to check ahead. Watch where you place hands (especially around hay bales, rock piles, wood piles)
- **If bitten**: Stay calm, immobilize the bitten limb at or below heart level, remove constricting items (rings, watches), get to emergency care immediately. Do NOT cut, suck, or tourniquet the bite. Antivenom is the only effective treatment.
- **Non-venomous snakes** (garter, rat, king, black rat snake): Beneficial for rodent control. Learn to identify — most snake bites are from non-venomous species.

### Mushroom Poisoning
- **Never eat a wild mushroom unless 100% certain of ID** — see [[edible-wild-mushrooms-of-north-america]] and [[mushroom-identification]]
- **Deadly lookalikes exist** for every edible species — see [[mushroom-identification]]
- **If poisoning suspected**: Call Poison Control. Save uneaten portion of the mushroom (or a photo) for identification. Watch for delayed onset symptoms — some toxins (amatoxins in Amanita) take 6-24 hours to appear but cause liver failure
- **Key deadly genera**: Amanita (death cap, destroying angel), Galerina, Cortinarius, Lepiota

### Pesticide and Chemical Safety
- Store all chemicals (even organic ones) in locked cabinets, original containers
- [[jadam-methods]] inputs are safer than synthetic pesticides but still require handling care
- Keep Poison Control number posted in barn, shop, and house
- Have activated charcoal on hand (binds many ingested toxins — use only if directed by Poison Control)
