# Mescalero Apache Peyote Prohibition and Aggression

## Overview

The Mescalero Apache of New Mexico present a unique case in the ethnographic study of psychoactive plant use: a society that **explicitly banned** the ritual use of peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*) after initially incorporating it into shamanistic practice. This prohibition — driven by the drug's capacity to release repressed aggression within the communal ritual setting — offers important insights into how social dynamics, personality structure, and pharmacological effects interact to determine the cultural fate of a hallucinogenic substance.

## Historical Background

Peyote was introduced to the Apache peoples at some point in their history and was initially incorporated into shamanistic ritual practice, likely through contact with neighboring peoples such as the Carrizo and Lipan Apache, among whom peyote use was documented (Opler, 1936, 1938). However, the Mescalero Apache ultimately proscribed its use entirely, classifying peyote power as **inherently evil** — a categorization that represented a fundamental departure from the broader Apache conceptualization of supernatural power as possessing no intrinsic moral properties.

## The Psychoanalytic Explanation

### Hostility in Apache Socialization

Apache child-rearing practices were found to engender much hostility. Aggression was and is addressed institutionally toward outsiders, witches, ghosts, and cultural bogies in an attempt to produce individual **repression of hostile impulses** originally directed toward familial and societal members. This effort was more effective aboriginally but was never strikingly successful. The unstable repression of hateful impulses toward parent and sibling surrogates was the critical vulnerability that peyote would exploit.

### Peyote Camps and Power Competition

The use of peyote in camps introduced a **foreign element** into Apache shamanistic procedures: the simultaneous assumption of authority by more than one practitioner. In traditional Apache practice, shamanic authority was individual and hierarchically structured. Peyote meetings, however, brought multiple shamans together in a single ritual context, and each vied for supremacy of power and status.

The physiopsychological effects of the hallucinogen reduced the efficacy of their repression of the hostilities resulting from their socialization experiences. Drug-induced regression resulted in releasing aggression in its earlier, childish form — directly toward parent and sibling surrogates. **Bloodshed and feuds occurred**, and the Apache wisely banned the peyote camps.

### Projection of Evil Onto Peyote

The ascription of evil to peyote power involved a **basic deviation** from the Apache concept of power without intrinsic properties of good or evil. This reclassification was intended to **deny the presence of intragroup hostility**. The psychological mechanism was projection: murderous wishes were projected onto the power in question, transforming a social problem (uncontrollable aggression) into a cosmological one (an evil substance).

It is no coincidence that only peyote and owl power have been considered evil in themselves. In each instance, murderous wishes are projected onto the power.

## Dual Fears

The Mescalero, Chiricahua, and Lipan Apache fear the use of peyote for two stated reasons:

### 1. Evil Power (Intrapersonal Aggression)

Peyote is believed to have an evil power which will drive users to do evil. Sexual transgressions arouse little overt anxiety among these Apaches except when intergenerational incest has occurred, but they fear their **poorly controlled aggressive impulses**. The drug is feared precisely because it threatens to release the hostility that Apache socialization has so carefully — but incompletely — repressed.

### 2. Hallucinations (Loss of Perceptual Control)

Peyote causes hallucinations, reducing the user's capacity to perceive and judge external reality accurately. There is fear of visual aberrations and strange qualities of movement. The Apaches may **displace their fear of loss of control over destructive urges** onto fear of loss of control of perceptual accuracy — a safer, less threatening displacement that avoids confronting the true source of anxiety.

## The Alcohol Anomaly

A critical question arises: given that alcohol also produces hallucinosis and exceedingly violent actions among the Apache, why was alcohol use socially permissible while peyote was proscribed?

The answer lies in the **ritual context** of use:

- **Peyote** was incorporated into the shamanistic ritual complex from the time of its introduction. Group situations at peyote meetings fostered **conflict centering on the varying powers** controlled by and controlling particular individuals — aggression was directed at specific persons within the supernatural framework
- **Alcohol** consumption was never culturally acceptable in ceremonial contexts. Aggression released during drinking parties was channeled **outside the personally mediated world of the supernatural** — it was generalized, diffused, and therefore less threatening to the social fabric

## Defiers of the Prohibition

### Powerful Witches

Two shamans continued using peyote in illicit practices despite the ban:

- **Black Eyes**: Intoxicated, he frequently bragged he was a witch and flaunted peyote buttons before the psychoanalytic author
- **Ancient One**: Feared by the entire community, said to have killed many individuals through supernatural means

These men deemed themselves so powerful that they were above social sanctions. They continued using peyote both to demonstrate contempt for fellow Mescaleros and for material purposes — demanding greater recompense and commanding greater respect by performing rituals conceptualized as illegitimate in Apache practice.

### Pseudoshamans

Two pseudoshamans also used peyote despite the prohibition. They had impoverished personalities and were generally scorned by the community. Both confessed while intoxicated that they **doubted their own claims of supernatural power** and consciously sought to deceive others. They used peyote in an attempt to raise their esteem, hoping they would truly become powerful if they could exploit the effects of the hallucinogen.

## Contemporary Implications

Boyer raised prescient questions about future Apache involvement with hallucinogens, given that their use had become commonplace among adolescents and young adults throughout the United States:

- Would the ban against peyote extend to other hallucinatory agents?
- Might acquaintance with some hallucinogens pave the way for re-definition of peyote, especially given the diminished commitment of present-day Apaches to the system of supernatural beliefs associated with shamanism?

These questions remain relevant for understanding how indigenous communities navigate the tension between traditional prohibitions and the availability of psychoactive substances in the modern world.

## Theoretical Significance

The Mescalero case demonstrates that the cultural acceptance or rejection of psychoactive substances cannot be explained by pharmacological properties alone. The same drug can be sacred in one society and evil in another. The determining factors include:

- **Social structure**: How the drug is incorporated into existing power arrangements
- **Personality dynamics**: Whether the drug's effects threaten socially necessary repression mechanisms
- **Ritual context**: Whether drug use creates competitive or cooperative social dynamics
- **Projection mechanisms**: How societies externalize the social problems caused by psychoactive substances
