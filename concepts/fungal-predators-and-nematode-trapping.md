# Fungal Predators and Nematode Trapping

Many fungi are active predators that trap and kill microscopic soil animals, particularly nematode worms, to supplement their nitrogen-poor vegetarian diets. These predatory mechanisms represent remarkable examples of fungal evolution and have significant implications for soil ecology and biological pest control.

## Why Fungi Hunt

Fungi face a serious nitrogen shortage when consuming plant tissues — these food sources yield plenty of sugar but negligible protein. Without proteins or alternative nitrogen-containing molecules, fungi cannot build their own proteins or nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). Two major predatory strategies address this deficit:

1. **Bacterial predation**: Some fungi secrete antibiotics to kill soil bacteria, then absorb nutrients from the leaking cells
2. **Nematode trapping**: Physical traps capture and digest nematode worms

A single gram of soil can house as many as 1,900 microscopic nematodes and more than 1,000 meters of hyphae — at this density, encounters between hyphae and worms are frequent (Money, 2002).

## Bacterial Predation

Mushroom-forming basidiomycetes in the Agaricales (ink-caps, puffballs, and bird's nest fungi) are adept predators of soil bacteria. By secreting antibiotics, they kill competing bacteria and obtain an extra nitrogen source from the dead cells. This is the same mechanism by which penicillin and cephalosporin operate — their natural role is likely antibacterial competition, not human medicine.

## Nematode Trapping Mechanisms

Fungi have evolved diverse structural traps for capturing nematodes:

### Adhesive Hyphal Branches (the simplest system)
- Short hyphal branches exude a powerful adhesive
- Passing nematodes become cemented to the branch tips
- In their struggle to escape, worms become stuck on adjacent branches
- Sometimes branches detach from the mycelium, but the nematode's fate is unchanged
- Within hours, the fungus penetrates the worm's cuticle and dissolves internal tissues

### Adhesive Networks and Cages
- Three-dimensional networks of adhesive hyphae form sticky cages
- Nematodes crawling through the mesh become entangled and glued at multiple points

### Constricting Rings (the most dramatic)
- The most sophisticated trap: an inflatable ring formed by three hyphal cells
- Nematodes are lured to the traps by chemical attractants ("siren songs")
- When the worm glides through the ring opening, the cells detect the touch
- The interior wall of the ring inflates within 0.1 seconds, gripping the animal with pressure up to 18 atmospheres
- The trapped nematode is then penetrated and digested

## Ecological and Agricultural Significance

Predatory fungi are natural regulators of nematode populations in soil. Species like *Arthrobotrys*, *Dactylella*, and *Monacrosporium* have been studied as biological control agents against plant-parasitic nematodes in agriculture. The trapping fungi can reduce nematode damage to crops without chemical nematicides.

In [[soil-food-web]] dynamics, predatory fungi occupy a unique position — they are decomposers that also function as predators, creating a direct link between the decomposition pathway and the microfaunal food web.

## Other Fungal Feeding Strategies

Beyond nematodes, some fungi have evolved additional predatory behaviors:
- **Obligate predators**: Species like *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) produce toxic droplets that paralyze nematodes on contact
- **Endoparasitic fungi**: Spores ingested by nematodes germinate inside the worm's body, consuming it from within
- **Arthropod pathogens**: *Cordyceps* and related fungi infect and kill insects, producing fruiting bodies from the host's body
