# Fungal Sex — Pheromones and Mating Communication

Fungi have evolved an astonishing diversity of mating strategies, many
mediated by [[mycelium]] secretes a steroid hormone
called antheridiol, which diffuses through the water and triggers the male
to produce specialized branches called antheridial hyphae. These grow
chemotropically toward the source of antheridiol, eventually making contact
with the female's oogonia (egg-containing structures). Once the male
responds, the female then produces a second hormone, oogoniol, which
signals the antheridial hyphae to stop growing and begin the process of
fertilization. This two-way hormonal conversation between sessile organisms
is remarkably reminiscent of animal endocrine signaling.

Antheridiol and oogoniol are steroid molecules structurally similar to the
vertebrate sex hormones estrogen and testosterone, respectively. Their
discovery in a fungus raised fascinating questions about the evolutionary
convergence of hormonal communication systems across widely separated
branches of the tree of life. The structural parallels suggest that steroid
chemistry is a particularly versatile solution to the problem of
intercellular signaling in aqueous environments.

## Wall Loosening and Endoglucanase

For antheridial hyphae to penetrate the female's cell wall and deliver their
nuclei, the wall must be locally softened. Thomas and Mullins demonstrated
in 1967 that antheridiol induces the secretion of an endoglucanase enzyme
that degrades the structural glucans in the female cell wall at the point of
contact. Hill and Money later measured the mechanical strength of fungal
cell walls using micropipette oil injection, providing quantitative evidence
for the wall-weakening effects of pheromone-induced enzyme secretion. This
combination of [[spore]] can complete the entire life cycle without finding a partner.
Heterothallic strains, which require cross-fertilization between compatible
mating types, are more common in permanent ponds where stable conditions
allow populations to persist long enough for sexual partners to encounter
one another. This ecological distribution illustrates how mating system
evolution is shaped by the reliability of environmental conditions.
