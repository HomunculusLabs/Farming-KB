## Tree Energy Transactions

Chapter 6 of the Designers' Manual is devoted to the complex interactions between trees and the incoming energies of radiation, precipitation, and wind. Mollison states: "I hope to show the immense value of trees to the biosphere." This chapter represents one of the most thorough treatments of tree-environment interactions in the permaculture literature.

## The Tree as a System

Mollison describes the tree not as an isolated organism but as the center of a complex system that includes soils, fungi, insects, birds, mammals, understory plants, and microorganisms. "It is a clever person indeed who can separate the total body of the tree into mineral, plant, animal, detritus, and life! This separation is for simple minds; the tree can be understood only as its total entity which, like ours, reaches out into all things. Animals are the messengers of the tree, and trees the gardens of animals."

A large tree has 10,000 to 100,000 growing points (meristems), each capable of individual mutation. Unlike mammals, trees produce seed from multitudinous flowers, and any one main branch can be genetically distinct. Trees are best understood as cooperative amalgams of many individuals, some of the tree body and most free-living agents. As Mollison writes, "Life depends upon life. All forces, all elements, all life forms are the biomass of the tree."

## The Biomass Zones of a Tree

A tree is broadly composed of three biomass zones:

1. **The stem and crown** (the visible tree above ground)
2. **The detritus and humus** (the tree at the soil surface boundary)
3. **The roots and root associates** (the underground tree)

The living tree stands in a zone of decomposition. Much of what it sheds is transferred, reborn, transported, or reincarnated into grasses, bacteria, fungus, [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] intercede with water, soil, and atmosphere to manufacture cell nutrients for the tree, they function as its digestive system. ## Temperature Interactions

Trees modify temperature through evapotranspiration and condensation:

- An ordinary elm transpires 15,000 pounds of water on a hot dry day, cooling surrounding air. Evapotranspiration is generally not less than 15 inches per year in a 40-inch rainfall zone.
- By day, evaporation from leaves cools air; by night, condensation of atmospheric moisture on leaves warms air. Leaves are cooler than soil by day and warmer at night because they are 86% water (twice the specific heat of soil).
- Plants may be 15 degrees C warmer than surrounding air temperature.
- Small open water storages or tree clumps upwind of a house have a pleasant moderating effect. Air passing over open water is cooled in summer and warmed in winter.
- Trees in tropical areas can reduce humidity through direct absorption, providing a dehumidifying effect.
