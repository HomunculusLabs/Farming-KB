# Rangeland Ecology and Multispecies Grazing

Rangelands are extensive natural or semi-natural landscapes supporting native vegetation and herbivores. Their productivity, when properly managed as polycultures, rivals or exceeds that of improved cultivated pastures — without external energy inputs, soil degradation, or animal stress.

## Natural Productivity Baselines

Brown (1972) estimated the standing crop of large herbivores on the African savannah at approximately 500 kg/ha — comparable to improved pastures in humid temperate zones. This figure excludes smaller mammals, birds, insects, burrowers, reptiles, and plant products, making it a minimal yield estimate for a natural system.

The savannah supports guilds of **five browser species**, **eleven grazer species**, and **four species that alternate between grazing and browsing**, each with specific food preferences. No cultivated livestock system can match this productivity without external inputs, and similar yields are not achieved without stressing soils, plants, or animals.

## The Stock Unit System

Range managers express [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] in "stock units" — equivalents based on animal liveweight. The standard unit is a European cow and calf (~450 kg liveweight):

|---|---|
| European cow and calf | 1 |
| African cow | 2 |
| European sheep | 5 |
| African sheep | 20 |
| Thompson's gazelle | 25 |
| Dik-dik antelope | 125 |
| Elephant | 10 (per stock unit) |
| Black rhino | 4 |
| Zebra | 15 |
| [[hartebeest]] | 27 |

Browse-day equivalents further illustrate productive efficiency:

- **1 European cow browse year** = 266 black-tailed jackrabbits, or 164 antelope jackrabbits, or 18 kangaroo, or 6 sheep, or 385 ground squirrels

Kangaroo offer 58% assimilable protein versus only 35% for cattle, making them far more productive from a protein perspective when properly managed.

## Range Plant Communities

Range vegetation falls into five broad groups, each utilised by different animal classes:

1. **Forbs**: Annual and perennial herbs, bulbous species — eaten by grazers and mixed feeders
2. **Grasses and monocotyledons**: Annual and perennial — primary food for grazers
3. **[[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]]**: The browse layer — food for browsers (deer, goats, antelope)
4. **Mosses and [[lichens]]**: Cold-area and small herbivore forage
5. **Fungi and saprophytes**: Recyclers and small herbivore food

Diet composition varies dramatically between species. Elk consume approximately 65% browse (up to 85% maximum), 5% grasses, and 3% mosses. Pronghorn antelope take roughly 74% browse, 4% grasses, and 22% forbs. These are not fixed proportions but dynamic seasonal shifts — browse dominates winter diets while grasses and forbs dominate summer.

## Range Utilisation Patterns

Different species exploit range differently:

- **Deer** commonly utilise 92% of available range area
- **Cattle** exploit only 52% of the same area
- **Water dependency**: Cattle range only 3–7 km from water; deer, kangaroo, goats, and small antelope can survive without free surface water except in extreme drought

Feed [[energy-conversion-efficiency]] also varies: deer may convert food to flesh at efficiencies 2–3 times greater than cattle, depending on species.

Range behaviour is determined by species-specific factors plus the availability of critical resources: shelter from extreme heat or cold, water placement, and the presence of predators (wolves, cheetahs). Managing these critical resources (water points, shade, shelter) enables species to utilise otherwise inaccessible range areas.

## Polyculture Advantage

A judicious selection of animal species enables far greater total production than any single species alone. Different species feed at different browse levels (ground-level grazers, mid-level browsers, high browsers), and their complementary dietary preferences prevent any one plant species from being over-utilised while others go ungrazed.

## Consequences of Monoculture and Overstocking

When a single species is overstocked or predators are removed:

- **Preferred plant species disappear** from the range
- **Insect plagues erupt**: Canadian researchers found direct correlation between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks. Infested rangelands can lose 67% of browse to insects
- **Soil degradation accelerates**: Compaction reduces pore space by 44% in the top 5 cm and 60% in the lower 60 cm after only 9 years of overstocking
- **Water infiltration collapses**: Slightly grazed land absorbs at 44.4%/hr; depleted land at only 0.82%/hr — a 54-fold reduction

Total plant cover of 65–70% is needed to [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]] entirely. Runoff from degraded rangelands carries 6–10% silt by volume; in one measured flow of 17,500 t water/hour, 500 t of organic matter were lost — a staggering waste directly attributable to poor [[mollison-pdc-range-management-wildlife]].

## Integrated Range Management

Insects themselves are usable as excellent protein. Fish, guinea fowl, or turkeys on range efficiently convert plague insects into usable protein. In Botswana, the drought harvest of locusts accounts for four times the food value of stored grains by weight. Wetlands and ponds are essential for supporting these insectivorous species and should be created or preserved as integral components of range management.

Fencing strategy is critical: fences falling from valleys to ridges create a water flow pattern following cattle tracks, while straight fences accelerate erosion.

## The Role of Predators

Predators (lions, wild dogs, wolves, tigers, leopards, eagles, foxes) are essential range managers. Their removal requires human culling to replace their function, and the culling is rarely as effective. Where predators have been eliminated locally, population control of herbivores must be managed through capture, culling, or harvest programmes tied to population estimates derived from scat counts, browse impact surveys, herd size, and breeding success monitoring.

The overarching principle: the main function of rangelands is to preserve upland soils and water for more intensive downstream use. Careful harvest at low polyculture [[aquaculture-pond-yields-and-stocking-rates]] is permissible, but only heavily vegetated lowland ranges are truly safe for domestic hooved animal production.
