# Rust Fungi Mycoparasites and Biocontrol

Rust fungi are not only plant pathogens; they are also hosts for specialized
fungi that parasitize rust spores, sori, aecial galls, and germ tubes. In the
biodiversity source, these organisms belong to the broader world of
[[fungicolous-fungi]] and [[fungal-parasites-and-predators]], where fungi act as
predators, antagonists, or biological controls against other fungi.

## Core Idea

Mycoparasites of rust fungi reduce pathogen reproduction by attacking the
structures rusts use to spread. Some penetrate urediniospores directly. Some
occupy entire sori. Others destroy rust galls, inhibit germination, or force
premature developmental transitions.

The practical attraction is clear: if a rust parasite is specific, persistent, and
environmentally compatible, it can function as a living biocontrol agent.

The limitation is equally clear. Many rust mycoparasites are highly dependent on
moisture, host stage, and timing. A parasite may be destructive under humid
conditions yet fail when the target rust has a short or irregular cycle.

## Eudarluca caricis / Sphaerellopsis filum

Eudarluca caricis, with the pycnidial anamorph Sphaerellopsis filum, is described
as the most common mycoparasite of rust fungi. It grows in rust sori, especially
uredinia, and its fruiting structures may eventually occupy the whole sorus.

Key traits:

- attacks a broad range of rust hosts;
- penetrates urediniospores enzymatically;
- inhibits spore germination;
- suppresses branching of germ tubes;
- is cosmopolitan but favored by moisture.

Its breadth makes it ecologically important, but the source notes that it is not
always practical as a field biocontrol agent. A rust with a short or irregular life
cycle may escape before enough parasitic damage accumulates.

## Tuberculina and Gall Displacement

Tuberculina species, with Helicobasidium teleomorphs, are mainly rust parasites.
Many are uredinicolous and often attack aecial stages. Tuberculina persicina is
reported from many rust hosts, while T. maxima may naturally suppress some
Cronartium rusts.

Their strategy differs from simple spore attack. Tuberculina can exploit rust
galls and destroy the nutrient base on which the rust depends. In this case,
biocontrol is displacement of the rust from a gall habitat, not just infection of
individual spores.

## Scytalidium uredinicola

Scytalidium uredinicola was found on aecia of Cronartium fusiforme on pine and
later on galls of Endocronartium harknessii. It penetrates woody gall tissue and
destroys rust colonies inside the gall.

The source also reports inhibition of spore germination by maltol, a metabolite
known from some plants. This makes the interaction a three-way system: plant
gall tissue, rust exploitation of that tissue, and a mycoparasite that disrupts the
rust within the gall.

## Lecanicillium Species

Several Lecanicillium species parasitize rusts. L. muscarium is notable because
the same isolates can parasitize both rusts and aphids. It penetrates
urediniospore walls directly and destroys rust germ tubes.

L. psalliotae also shows enzymatic capacity to penetrate rust spores and has been
studied for control of soybean rust and coffee rust. This dual ecological role
connects rust mycoparasitism with [[entomopathogenic-fungi-biocontrol-mycopesticides]],
though practical use still depends on host specificity, crop safety, and field
persistence.

## Mechanisms of Attack

Rust mycoparasites use overlapping mechanisms:

- direct penetration of urediniospore walls;
- enzymatic lysis of germ pore plugs and spore walls;
- inhibition of germination and germ-tube branching;
- destruction of rust galls or sori;
- production of antifungal metabolites;
- induction of premature teliospore formation;
- hyphal interference on leaf surfaces.

These mechanisms place rust parasites within
[[fungal-mycoparasitism-and-biocontrol-interactions]], where similar modes of
attack recur across fungal host-parasite systems.

## Habitat and Moisture Pattern

The source emphasizes moisture. Rust parasites are especially common under moist
tropical conditions and in temperate habitats with high relative humidity, such
as bogs or areas near water bodies.

Rusts on grasses in raised, drier places may lack parasites, while rusts in moist
sites can host several parasite species. Absence from a sample may therefore
reflect microclimate rather than true geographic absence.

## Methods of Study

Attacks by rust mycoparasites are often inconspicuous. Researchers usually find
them through directed searches, screening many rust collections under a
dissecting microscope. Incubation in a moist chamber can make infections easier
to detect.

Some rust mycoparasites then grow readily on ordinary agar media, allowing
candidate biocontrol organisms to be cultured, identified, and tested.

## Biocontrol Promise and Limits

Rust mycoparasites are promising because they attack pathogen reproduction
rather than the crop. They may reduce spore viability, shorten epidemics, or
suppress rust structures before new infections are launched.

Their limits are ecological. Performance depends on humidity, leaf-surface
conditions, parasite-host timing, host-stage specificity, and reliable taxonomic
identification. This is why [[biodiversity-of-fungi-plant-health-pathogens-biocontrol]]
depends on ecological fit rather than simply naming an antagonist.
