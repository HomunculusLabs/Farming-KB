## Edge Cropping

4. **Achieve near-total edge** \u2014 The entire field becomes edge, approximately doubling yield per unit area.

In the example of wheat and lucerne, sowing a field in 2 m wide alternate strips gives approximately the same yield as sowing twice the area to single-crop stands. Extending this principle to many varied crops on a broader scale approaches a new concept of growing: **zone or edge cropping** \u2014 a matrix of hedgerows or edge-rows, each suited in width to a particular crop.

### Natural Precedents

Zonal strips occur naturally on coasts and around saltpans or waterholes. Such setups are analogous to:

- **French-intensive agriculture** \u2014 Traditional patterns of narrow, intensively managed beds
- **Farmed strips of Quebec** \u2014 Long, narrow field strips producing productive crop mosaics
- **Polycultural systems** \u2014 Composed of mosaics or zonal strips of different species

### Social Applications

Edge cropping has immense potential for small shareholders in a single land trust, each tending one or more crop strips. This collaborative model combines ecological efficiency with social equity.

## Compatible and Incompatible Borders

### The Six Possible Interactions

When two systems are brought into boundary contact, only limited interactions are possible:

| Interaction | Effect |
|---|---|
| (0, 0) | No difference in yields, stability, or growth |
| (+, -) or (-, +) | One benefits, at the expense of the other |
| (+, +) | Both benefit (mutualism) |
| (-, -) | Both decreased in yield or vitality |
| (+, 0) or (0, +) | One benefits, the other unaffected (commensalism) |
| (-, 0) or (0, -) | One decreased, the other unaffected (amensalism) |

### The General Rule

Almost all organisms or systems get along fine. A great many derive mutual benefit, and very few decrease the yield of others or wipe each other out. It simply doesn\u2019t pay to attack others \u2014 in the long run one destroys oneself by accumulated injury or, more certainly, by pathogens in an animal or conflicts within a society that await a monocultural crop or repressive society.

### Design Strategy for Incompatibility

For cases of (-,-) interactions where both crops suffer:

**Place an intervening, mutually-compatible component between two incompatible systems.**

Compatible components may differ in sex, colour, chemistry, belief, or political conviction from the warring parties. In time, a beneficial mosaic will impose itself on all expansionist systems, arising from the potential for differences carried within all life systems. Natural interveners often arise as hybrids between apparently antagonistic systems.

### The Interdependence Principle

The design intention in landscape systems is to:

> **Select and place components so that incompatibility is nullified, interdependence maximized.**

The interdependence of mosaics of belief are called for as much as mosaics of plants. When designers know enough to select mutually-beneficial assemblies of plant and animal species (guilds), they have two powerful interactive strategies:

1. **Edge harmonics** \u2014 Shaping boundaries for maximum productive interface
2. **Species compatibility** \u2014 Selecting components that benefit each other

## The Stupidity Principle

> **Stupidity is an attempt to iron out all differences, and not to use or value them creatively.**

This principle encapsulates the core error of monoculture, uniform landscapes, and repressive social systems. Mosaic design \u2014 the opposite of monoculture \u2014 means creating many small areas of difference. A few mistakes will occur, but good average benefit will result. This was the tribal strategy.

## A Golden Rule of Design

> **Keep it small, and keep it varied.**

This rule connects directly to the concept of **guilds** \u2014 mutually-beneficial assemblies of species. Combined with edge harmonics and species compatibility, guild design provides the practical toolkit for building productive, resilient, interdependent systems.

## Key Principles

1. **Shape edges for specific purposes** \u2014 Different geometries serve different functions; choose deliberately.
2. **Edge cropping doubles effective yield** \u2014 By making entire fields into edge, production approaches double-crop yields.
3. **Almost everything is compatible** \u2014 Destructive interactions are rare; focus on finding beneficial pairings.
4. **Insert mediators for incompatibility** \u2014 When systems conflict, place a mutually-compatible component between them.
5. **Mosaic over monoculture** \u2014 Many small areas of difference produce better average results than uniform systems.
6. **Differences are resources** \u2014 Designing differences into a system builds resources; smoothing differences depletes them.
