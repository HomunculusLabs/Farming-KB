# Cannabis Safety Profile and Overdose Risk

Cannabis occupies a unique position among therapeutic substances in terms
of its safety profile. Drawing on both clinical research and extensive
emergency medical experience, Blesching's Cannabis Health Index provides
a comprehensive assessment of cannabis safety that challenges many
common misconceptions while acknowledging real considerations.

## Lethal Dose and Overdose Risk

### Fatal Overdose Is Virtually Impossible

A 1973 laboratory study reported the median lethal dose of oral THC in
rats as 800-1,900 mg/kg, depending on the sex and genetic strain. Using
body weight as the sole criteria, this suggests 200 grams of herb per
kilogram of body weight is required to approach a lethal dose in humans.
A person weighing 70 kg (154 lbs) would need to consume 14 kg of herb
to approach a fatal dose.

A 2004 study was even more conservative, stating "628 kg of cannabis
would have to be smoked in 15 minutes to induce a lethal effect."

As former U.S. Surgeon General Joycelyn Elders, MD, stated: "Unlike
many of the drugs we prescribe every day, marijuana has never been proven
to cause a fatal overdose."

### Government Death Statistics

Between January 1997 and June 2005, there were zero deaths attributed to
the exclusive use of cannabis according to U.S. government sources.
Deaths in which cannabis was one of several drugs used are not counted
in this figure.

For comparison, CDC estimates suggest tobacco claimed approximately
110,750 lives per year from 2000-2004, and alcohol-related fatalities
averaged 75,766 in 2001.

An FDA report compared marijuana to 17 common FDA-approved
pharmaceutical drugs used to treat similar symptoms. Between 1997 and
2005, no deaths were attributed to exclusive cannabis use, while the
FDA recorded 10,008 deaths due to the 17 pharmaceutical drugs in the
study.

## Emergency Room Experience

From twenty years of emergency medical service experience, Blesching
notes that cannabis-related 911 calls are extremely rare. The
number-one drug responsible for generating 911 emergency calls is
nicotine in tobacco, followed by alcohol, heroin, stimulants (cocaine,
crack, methamphetamine), and PCP. While government statistics show
large numbers of cannabis-related ER visits, closer examination reveals
the majority involved cannabis used in combination with other drugs or
incidences of anxiety.

## Common Adverse Effects

Common adverse effects include increased appetite, reduced attention
span, red sclera, dry mouth, and decreased cognitive and motor skills.
Effects more common when ingested or used at higher than the
[[cannabis-dosing-and-titration]]
include ataxia (unsteady gait), aphasia (inability to speak clearly),
unusual sensory perceptions, anxiety, slight increase in heart rate,
and panic upon first-ever use.

The vast majority of adverse effects are directly related to exceeding
the subjective therapeutic window. Too much can actually increase the
very symptoms being treated.

## Addiction and Dependence

If dependency occurs with cannabis, it is generally an addiction in
psychological terms rather than the physical realm, as is the case with
tobacco, alcohol, and heroin. The large numbers of people in drug
treatment centers for cannabis is often cited to substantiate addiction
claims, but this overlooks the reality that many court judges mandate
drug treatment instead of jail time for cannabis offenses.

Compared to pharmaceuticals with significant addiction potential,
cannabis carries a considerably reduced risk of adverse side effects
including death.

Patients concerned about dependence may reduce risk by:
- Infusing [[cannabis-for-mental-health]] into
  the healing process
- Using raw preparations of cannabis, which have little or no
  psychoactive effect

## Impact on Special Populations

A Duke University study (approximately 38 years of observational data)
concluded that while adult cannabis use has no effect on intelligence,
"cannabis dependency" in adolescents may contribute to reduced IQ test
scores. The study has limitations: subjective data, small sample size
(153 people), and only some IQ-altering factors were considered. Until
more is known, a possible correlation should be assumed.

## Lung and Heart Health

The largest population-based case-controlled study of cannabis-only use
(2,252 people in Los Angeles) found smoking only cannabis was mildly
lung-protective and not associated with increased lung cancer risk.
Cannabis oil has shown therapeutic effects in patients with
[[cannabis-for-rheumatoid-arthritis]] and asthma. Vaporizers can eliminate
inhalation of burned carbon compounds.

Endocannabinoid receptors are present in the heart. THC can increase
heart rate but not dangerously. THC, CBD, and CBN have demonstrated
potent cardio-protective properties in numerous studies.

## Cancer and Schizophrenia Considerations

Cannabis constituents produce apoptosis (cancer cell death) across many
cancer types. Cannabis is not considered a carcinogen in typical medical
use. Observational studies suggest adolescent cannabis use may increase
schizophrenia risk as a co-factor, though cannabis is not itself causal.
Adolescents with family history of psychosis should exercise caution.

## Contaminants and Gateway Theory

Pesticides on cannabis may increase toxic load; this risk is eliminated
by purchasing organic cannabis. Aspergillus mold poses risk to
immunocompromised patients. Proper drying (water activity less than 0.81
at 25 +/- 2 degrees C) prevents fungal growth.

A study of more than 4,000 cannabis smokers concluded that cannabis use
leads to a decrease in the use of alcohol, tobacco, and hard drugs,
contradicting the gateway theory.

## Bottom Line

Extensive evidence indicates that cannabis is neither dangerous nor
harmless. Consideration of its medicinal use should include a risk
versus benefit analysis focused on the specific therapeutic needs and
health challenges of the individual patient.
