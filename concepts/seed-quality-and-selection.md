# Seed Quality and Selection

The quality of seed you plant is one of the most important — and most overlooked — factors in gardening success. Most home gardeners have no idea that the seed sold in [[solomon-garden-centers-seedling-traps]] and on seed racks is often a fundamentally different product from the seed used by commercial farmers and market gardeners. Understanding the [[seed-industry-and-primary-growers]] what separates quality seed from cheap seed is essential for getting reliable results.

## The Seed Industry Structure

### Primary Growers

The actual seed producers are termed "primary growers" — large agricultural companies that grow seed in bulk. These companies supply:

- Mail-order catalog companies
- Picture-packet seedrack companies
- Other seed distributors throughout the United States and Canada

### The Three-Legged Stool

Every producer must balance three factors:

1. **Price**: How cheap can the seed be produced?
2. **Quality**: How genetically pure, vigorous, and disease-free is the seed?
3. **Service**: Reliability, germination rates, variety integrity

**In the garden seed trade, the primary growers have chosen price.** Quality and service are what get sacrificed to meet ever-lower price demands from retailers.

### The "WalMartization" of Seed Racks

The economics of seedrack sales have degraded seed quality progressively:

- Transnational retail chains use enormous buying clout to squeeze seedrack jobbers' profits
- Independent garden centers, unable to compete on price, are vanishing
- Fewer seedrack companies survive, and they are squeezed ever harder
- Desperate rack companies demand lower prices from primary growers
- Primary growers cut quality to maintain margins

## What "Cheap Seed" Actually Means

### The "Sweepings" Reality

A primary grower's sales manager candidly explained the garden seed business:

- For the home garden trade, **germination percentage doesn't really matter** — the important thing is that "the seeds came up"
- The grower can sell the home gardener "the sweepings off the factory floor"
- When seed is harvested, it is mixed with chaff, soil, weed seeds, and other plant material
- It passes through equipment that shakes, blows, and sifts to separate good seed from debris
- Dense, good seed goes into the bag for commercial farmers
- The chaff, light seed, and immature seed tends to fall to the floor — this is what the home gardener gets

### Why Gardeners Don't Notice

- Inexperienced gardeners blame themselves — their soil, the depth they sowed, watering, etc.
- They are uncertain about their own skills and never blame the seed
- They are out only a few dollars for a small packet
- A farmer who loses a field of 80 acres to poor seed has invested hundreds of dollars in seed, thousands in tractor work, and potentially tens of thousands in lost opportunity — they absolutely notice and switch suppliers

### The Degradation Cycle

When seed is produced the cheap way:

1. Off-types are not eradicated from [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] fields
2. No skilled plant breeders maintain pure genetic lines
3. Fields surrounding production areas are not patrolled for cross-pollination risks
4. Plants are not carefully hand-selected for seed production
5. The seed production field grows unsupervised, seed is harvested, and a portion is saved to plant next year's field
6. Each successive generation becomes ever more variable and ever less productive

### The "Heirloom" Labeling Problem

When a cheap grower's variety becomes too degraded:

1. They buy a few pounds of expensive commercial-quality seed of a different but similar-looking variety from a quality company
2. They use this to start their seed production field
3. The harvest is (mis)labeled with the heirloom name
4. The resulting seed has nothing in common with the original variety

This is why many "heirlooms" produced by cheap growers are shadows of the real varieties.

## What Makes Quality Seed

Four factors define commercial-quality seed:

### 1. Genetic Uniformity

When you sow seeds for a crop like cabbage, you want heads that will hold for a week or more without splitting, mature at the same time, and be ready to cut all at once. A large percentage of plants must perform identically. For farmers, variability means unmarketable produce, wasted pesticide and cultivation costs, and lost income.

### 2. High Germination Rate

Savvy market gardeners and farmers require a minimum germination percentage for each vegetable species. If germination falls below that figure, the seed is almost certainly too weak to grow well.

Farmers and market gardeners find out the germination percentage of each lot before agreeing to accept it. Home gardeners have no such information.

### 3. Vigor

Even seeds that germinate may lack vigor — the ability to grow rapidly and strongly under field conditions. Cheap seed may sprout but produce weak, slow-growing plants that never reach full potential.

### 4. Variety Integrity

Maintaining a quality variety requires:

- A highly paid plant breeder doing ongoing variety purification
- Regular patrol of seed production fields to remove off-types
- Patrol of surrounding areas to prevent unintended crosses with other varieties (especially for insect- or wind-pollinated species)
- Careful hand-selection of the plants allowed to make seed
- This skilled work means quality seed must be sold at a rather high price

## The Historical Context

What has been described was standard practice in the home-garden seed trade long before the 1970s. But with retail chains demanding ever lower prices, the primary garden-seed grower must cut more corners to survive.

By the 1980s, a few quality-focused seed companies like Johnny's [[johnnys-selected-seeds]] began to challenge this model, offering genuinely commercial-quality seed to home gardeners. But the overall trend continues toward lower quality at lower prices.

## Practical Recommendations

### Where to Buy Seed

- Seek out specialty seed companies that serve both home gardeners and small market growers
- Companies that also sell to farmers are more likely to offer genuine commercial quality
- Look for companies that publish germination rates and [[solomon-variety-trial-methodology]] results
- Mail-order from reputable specialists is generally superior to seedrack purchases

### What to Avoid

- Seed racks in discount stores and big-box retailers
- [[cannabis-landrace-and-heirloom-varieties]] from unknown sources — many are not what they claim
- Seeds sold primarily by attractive picture packets rather than performance data
- Companies that cannot provide germination information

### Testing Your Seed

Before planting a large area, test a sample:

1. Place 10-20 seeds on a damp paper towel
2. Fold and place in a plastic bag
3. Keep warm (70-80°F / 21-27°C)
4. Check germination after the expected number of days for that species
5. If fewer than 70% germinate, the seed is likely too weak for reliable results

## The Bottom Line

The few dollars saved on cheap seed are almost always lost many times over in poor germination, weak growth, and disappointing harvests. Quality seed is one of the best investments a gardener can make — it costs only slightly more and delivers dramatically better results.
