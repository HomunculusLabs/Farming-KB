## Summit Disease: Altruism or Neurological Hijacking?

The climbing behavior exhibited by infected insects is known as summit disease.

By scaling vegetation, colonial insects remove themselves from their nests, limiting the opportunity for an epidemic to sweep through the colony.

This self-burial strategy may also have evolved as a protective measure to shield the sickened insect's relatives from infection.

Such gallant and seemingly altruistic actions make particular sense for sterile animals like worker ants, which may have hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of identical sisters.

In biological terms, selfless actions serve the individual through the colony because the genetic legacy of each worker depends entirely upon the health of the queen and her suitors.

For a sterile worker ant, there is no individual reproductive future to protect — only the colony's collective fitness matters.

Alternatively, the fungus might be exerting direct control over the insect's behavior by manipulating its brain chemistry.

Although purely hypothetical, the neurological symptoms — convulsions, erratic gait, uncontrollable twitching — make this a very provocative idea.

By inducing the summit response in its host, the fungus would place itself in an ideal position for wind dispersal of its spores, provided that the exposed insects are not picked off by birds.

The two explanations are not mutually exclusive: the altruistic removal behavior may have evolved first in social insects, and the fungus may have subsequently learned to exploit and amplify this pre-existing behavioral program through neurochemical manipulation.

Interactions between fungi and insects often defy simple explanation.

He has found Cordyceps on cicadas, tarantula spiders, ants, and termites, demonstrating the extraordinary adaptability of this fungal genus across multiple arthropod lineages.
