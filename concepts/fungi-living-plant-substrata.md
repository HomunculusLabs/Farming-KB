# Fungi on Living Plant Substrata

## Overview

This topic covers fungi that produce thalli and reproductive structures
on living plants and fruit, exclusive of roots, bark, and dead
structures still attached to the plant. Generally, most fungi living
on plant substrata are microfungi, with representatives in all major
fungal classes and most orders. Many are pathogenic (cause diseases),
many have been well studied by both mycologists and plant pathologists,
and the majority have been collected primarily from leaves.

## Types of Fungal Associations

### Obligate Biotrophs

Obligate biotrophs produce either intercellular or extracellular
mycelia, often with specialized feeding structures (haustoria) that
penetrate and extract nutrients from the host plant. They require a
living host, are unable to live saprobically in nature, and rarely are
cultured in vitro. Many fungi are host-specific to certain plant
families, often making it necessary to identify the host plant to
identify the fungus. Rusts, smuts, and powdery mildews are classic
examples.

### Facultative Biotrophs

Facultatively biotrophic fungi may also cause diseases in living plants
but generally do not fruit on living tissues. Sporulation occurs either
after the plant dies or in islands of dead tissue surrounded by living
cells. They frequently cause tissue death via toxin production or
systemic vascular infections. Because they are able to grow and
sporulate after the plant is dead, they often are cultured easily in
vitro.

### Saprotrophs on Living Plants

Saprotrophs on living plants are primarily epiphytic fungi that fruit
on, but do not penetrate, living plant surfaces such as leaves. Many
obtain nutrients from extracellular plant exudates or from the
excretions of plant-feeding insects, such as aphids. Under field
conditions, symptoms produced by facultative biotrophs can be confused
with symptoms of obligate biotrophic fungi.

## Ecological Importance

Fungi on living plants affect the genetic diversity of their hosts by
selecting for disease resistance. They act as natural thinning and
diversifying agents in dense clusters of seedlings and in stands of
young plants. Seed and seedling pathogens can select for seed dispersal
patterns. The survival of seeds of the tropical tree Platypodium
elegans increased with distance from an adult tree, as distantly
dispersed seeds encountered light gaps with lower disease risk.

## Powdery Mildews

Powdery mildews are obligate biotrophic ascomycetes that produce
characteristic white to grayish powdery colonies on the surfaces of
leaves, stems, and fruits. They are among the most recognizable and
common fungal diseases of plants worldwide. The mycelium is
superficial, producing haustoria that penetrate the epidermal cells.
About 100 species of powdery mildews are known, but many more are
expected to be found, particularly in the tropics.

## Foliage Fungi

Fungi associated with leaves represent one of the largest groups of
plant-associated microfungi. They include pathogens, saprotrophs, and
endophytes. Many leaf spots are caused by facultative biotrophs that
sporulate on dead tissue. Others are caused by obligate biotrophs that
require living tissue. The diversity of leaf-inhabiting fungi is poorly
known in tropical regions.

## Cankers and Galls

Canker-causing fungi produce localized necrotic lesions on stems and
branches. They may be caused by both obligate and facultative
pathogens. Gall-forming fungi induce abnormal growth of host tissue,
creating distinctive structures that may be easy to recognize in the
field. Some rust and smut species are gall-formers. The morphology
of the gall can provide clues to the identity of the causal agent.

## Fungi on Flowers and Fruits

Fungi associated with flowers and fruits include both pathogens and
saprotrophs. Flower-infecting fungi can reduce seed set and fruit
production. Fruit-infecting fungi may cause pre- or post-harvest rots.
Some fungi specialize on particular flower or fruit structures. The
diversity of fungi on flowers and fruits is poorly known in tropical
regions and represents a significant gap in our understanding of
fungal biodiversity.

## Standard Ground-Based Techniques

Standard techniques for collecting fungi on living plant substrata
include visual inspection of plant surfaces, hand lens examination,
and collection of symptomatic tissue. For microfungi, leaves and other
plant parts may be incubated in moist chambers to encourage
sporulation before examination. Surface sterilization techniques may
be needed to distinguish epiphytic from endophytic fungi. See
[[urediniomycetes-rust-fungi-yeastlike-relatives]] and [[ustilaginomycetes-smut-fungi-classification-diversity]] for specific groups.

## Studying Fungi on Living Plants

Field recognition of fungi on living plants relies on recognizing
both host symptoms and fungal signs. Symptoms are the host plant's
response to infection (e.g., chlorosis, necrosis, galls). Signs are
the actual fungal structures (e.g., fruiting bodies, spore masses).
Both types of information should be recorded for each specimen.
Photography is essential for documentation of symptoms and signs.

## Identification Resources

Identification of fungi on living plants often requires host plant
identification as a first step. Many fungi are host-specific or show
strong host preferences. Regional floras and monographic treatments
provide the most reliable identification resources. For many groups,
molecular methods are increasingly used to confirm identifications
and to detect fungi that do not produce distinctive morphological
structures.

## Estimating Species Richness

The total number of fungi on living plants is poorly known but is
certainly very large. Tropical regions are particularly undersampled.
Some estimates suggest that we know fewer than 10% of the fungi
associated with tropical plants. Comprehensive surveys using both
morphological and molecular methods are needed to better document
this diversity. See [[fungal-species-estimates-biodiversity-overview]]
for broader estimates.
