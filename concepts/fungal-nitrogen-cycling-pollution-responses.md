# Fungal Nitrogen Cycling and Pollution Responses

## Overview

Fungi are central mediators of nitrogen (N) cycling in terrestrial ecosystems, governing nitrogen
transformations from organic matter decomposition through to plant-available forms. Their roles
span mutualistic mycorrhizal associations, free-living saprotrophy, and parasitism, each pathway
exerting distinct influences on ecosystem nitrogen budgets. Understanding fungal nitrogen dynamics
is critical in an era of elevated anthropogenic nitrogen deposition, where NOx emissions from
fossil fuel combustion and agricultural ammonia volatilisation are reshaping fungal community
structure and function across the globe.

## Nitrogen Cycling and Deposition

Nitrogen enters forest ecosystems through [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]], atmospheric deposition,
and internal recycling. [[saprotrophic-fungi]] decompose organic substrates—leaf litter, woody debris,
and peat—releasing ammonium that is subsequently nitrified or taken up directly by plants and
microbes. Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi such as *Pisolithus*, *[[paxillus-involutus]]*, *Rhizopogon*,
and *Piloderma* are particularly important in boreal and temperate forests where they mobilise
organic nitrogen from the soil and transfer it to host trees including *Pinus species*,
*[[pseudotsuga-menziesii]]*, *Picea sitchensis*, and *Quercus species*. These fungi produce
extracellular enzymes that access nitrogen bound in protein–phenol complexes within protected
organic matter (POM), effectively mining nitrogen that would otherwise remain locked away.

Anthropogenic nitrogen deposition dramatically alters these cycles. Chronic inputs of NOx and
ammonium reduce plant and fungal dependence on energetically costly mycorrhizal nitrogen
acquisition, leading to shifts in community composition. The landmark NITREX experiment
demonstrated that long-term nitrogen additions to coniferous forests reduced ectomycorrhizal
diversity and shifted the fungal community toward faster-growing, less mutualistic taxa. Under
moderate deposition, nitrogen use efficiency initially increases as plants and fungi absorb
surplus N; however, sustained loading drives nitrogen saturation, where the ecosystem can no
longer retain added nitrogen, resulting in elevated nitrate leaching and run-off that degrades
aquatic ecosystems.

## Mycorrhizal Linkages and Phenology

The phenology of mycorrhizal fungi is tightly coupled to host plant phenology and climatic
drivers. The PacER (phenological and climatic ectomycorrhizal response) framework describes how
temperature and precipitation patterns regulate seasonal fruiting and root colonisation by ECM
fungi. In *Populus* and paper birch stands, ECM colonisation peaks in synchrony with root growth
flushes, ensuring that carbon supplied through root exudates is efficiently allocated to fungal
partners that in return supply nitrogen and phosphorus. The rhizosphere—the zone of intense
microbial activity surrounding plant roots—is the primary interface where these exchanges occur.

[[dark-septate-endophytes]] such as *[[phialocephala-fortinii]]* colonise roots of *Quercus species*,
*Phillyrea*, and *Nothofagus*, contributing to nutrient uptake without forming classic mantle
structures. Their functional niche overlaps with both ECM and ericoid mycorrhizas, providing
nutrient access under stressful conditions including low pH and low phosphorus availability.
Ectomycorrhizal fungi produce phosphatase enzymes that cleave phosphodiesters and phytic acid,
mobilising phosphorus from organic sources. This phosphatase activity is suppressed under high
nitrogen availability, illustrating the tight coupling between nitrogen and phosphorus cycling
mediated by fungi.

## Phyllosphere and Pathogen Interactions

The phyllosphere—the above-ground surface of leaves—hosts diverse fungal assemblages including
yeasts, endophytes, and foliar pathogens. *Phyllos plane* communities influence decomposition
rates of intercepted nutrients and serve as reservoirs for both beneficial and pathogenic taxa.
Fungal pathogens such as *Phytophthora*, *Pythium*, *Rhizoctonia*, and *Phoma* cause root and
crown diseases that alter the [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]] and thereby reshape nutrient cycling.
*Ruderal vegetation* colonising disturbed or nitrogen-enriched sites often supports higher
pathogen loads, accelerating turnover and facilitating succession.

Parasitism by rust fungi such as *Puccinia* on grasses including *Panicum* and crops like peanut
and papaya diverts host photosynthate and nitrogen, reducing primary production. Nematophagous
fungi prey on soil nematodes, including those tended by ants of the genus *Pogonomyrmex*, linking
[[dighton-fungal-trophic-interactions-soil-food-webs]] to broader soil food webs. The net primary production of ecosystems
is thus regulated by the balance between mutualistic nutrient acquisition and pathogenic nutrient
drain, both mediated by fungi occupying distinct niche spaces.

## Phosphorus Coupling

Phosphorus availability constrains fungal nitrogen cycling. Ectomycorrhizal fungi associated with
*Pinus species* and *Picea* produce both phosphatase and protease enzymes, reflecting the
stoichiometric coupling of N and P demand. Litter decomposers including *Phanerochaete* and
*Phallus impudicus* release phosphorus from organic matter alongside nitrogen, while
saprotrophic *Penicillium* species solubilise mineral phosphates through acidification. Fungi
that efficiently access both N and P gain a competitive advantage in oligotrophic soils, a
niche strategy that is disrupted when anthropogenic nitrogen inputs decouple these cycles.

## Radionuclides and Pollution Responses

Fungi accumulate and translocate radionuclides, particularly radiocesium (¹³⁷Cs), from
contaminated soils. Following nuclear fallout events such as Chernobyl, ectomycorrhizal fungi
including *Piloderma* and *Rhizopogon* species concentrated radiocesium in their fruiting bodies
to levels far exceeding those of surrounding soils and host plants. Litter decomposers and
saprobes similarly accumulated radiocesium from organic horizons, creating persistent
biogeochemical reservoirs. The lichen *Platismatia glauca* and other epiphytic fungi serve as
effective biomonitors of atmospheric radionuclide deposition, integrating deposition over months
to years.

Beyond radionuclides, fungi respond to heavy metals such as nickel, polychlorinated biphenyls
(PCB), and other organic pollutants. Certain *Penicillium* and *Paecilomyces* species exhibit
tolerance to elevated nickel concentrations, while white-rot fungi including *Phanerochaete*
degrade PCB through extracellular ligninolytic enzyme systems. Phytoremediation strategies
increasingly harness fungal capabilities: inoculation with pollutant-tolerant mycorrhizal or
saprobes can stabilise contaminated soils, degrade organic xenobiotics, and support revegetation
of degraded sites. *Pleurotus* species, for example, have been deployed for PCB and heavy metal
bioremediation in contaminated industrial soils.

## Restoration and Revegetation

Restoration of degraded ecosystems depends critically on re-establishing functional fungal
communities. In primary successional settings such as volcanic deposits and mine tailings, the
initial colonisation by ruderal vegetation is facilitated by fast-colonising, r-strategist fungi
including *Panaeolus* species and early-stage ECM associates. Inoculation with *Pisolithus*
and *Rhizopogon* improves seedling establishment of *Pinus species* and *Quercus species* on
revegetation sites by enhancing nutrient and water uptake. The interaction between fungal
inoculants and resident [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]]—including *Pseudomonas* bacteria that
promote mycorrhizal function—determines restoration success.

The restoration of peatlands, where nitrogen saturation has altered decomposition pathways,
requires re-establishing the slow-growing fungal assemblages characteristic of waterlogged,
low-nitrogen conditions. In these systems, nitrogen pollution suppresses the Sphagnum-associated
fungal community and shifts decomposition toward bacterial dominance, accelerating peat loss.
Restoration interventions that reduce nitrogen inputs and reintroduce characteristic fungal
partners can restore the carbon and nitrogen balance of these globally important stores.

## Synthesis

Fungal nitrogen cycling and pollution responses represent a nexus where fundamental mycological
processes intersect with pressing environmental challenges. From the rhizosphere to the
phyllosphere, fungi modulate nitrogen availability, mediate pollutant fate, and underpin
ecosystem resilience. Anthropogenic nitrogen deposition and radionuclide contamination perturb
these functions, but fungal diversity and functional plasticity also offer pathways for
remediation and restoration. Effective ecosystem management must recognise fungi not merely as
decomposers but as integral regulators of nutrient cycles, plant health, and environmental
quality.
