# Substrate Pasteurization

Substrate pasteurization is a heat treatment process used in mushroom
cultivation to eliminate competing microorganisms while preserving
beneficial ones. Unlike sterilization, which kills all life,
pasteurization targets pathogens and competitors at temperatures between
60 and 80 degrees Celsius over a sustained period, typically 1 to 2
hours.

## Why Pasteurize

Raw substrates like straw, sawdust, and compost teem with wild bacteria,
mold spores, and insect eggs. Without treatment, these organisms
outcompete mushroom mycelium for nutrients and space. Pasteurization
creates a selective window: the thermophilic (heat-loving) bacteria
survive and temporarily colonize the substrate, producing antibiotics
and consuming simple sugars that would otherwise feed competitors. This
biological shield gives inoculated mushroom mycelium a critical head
start.

## Common Methods

### Hot Water Bath

Involves submerging loose substrate in water heated to 65-75 degrees C
for 60-90 minutes, then draining and cooling before inoculation. A
common setup uses a 55-gallon drum with a propane burner, a mesh basket
to hold the straw, and a thermometer. The straw is fully submerged,
ensuring the innermost material reaches pasteurization temperature. The
hot water bath method simultaneously hydrates the substrate, eliminating
a separate soaking step. However, it consumes large volumes of water
and fuel.

### Steam Pasteurization

Passes steam through a sealed chamber containing the substrate. Bulk
operations use insulated pasteurization rooms or tunnels where compost
or [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] blocks reach target temperatures throughout
their mass. Commercial rooms are typically 50-500 square feet with steam
pipes and perforated distribution ducts. Substrate is loaded on racks or
pallets. Temperatures are raised to 60-65 degrees C and held for 8-12
hours. Temperature monitoring uses multiple probes, since the coldest
spot determines whether pasteurization is complete.

### Hydrated Lime Treatment

A cold pasteurization alternative for straw. Soaking straw in hydrated
lime (calcium hydroxide) at pH 12-13 for 12-18 hours destroys most
competitors through alkalinity rather than heat. The typical ratio is
200-400 grams of hydrated lime per 100 liters of water. After draining,
the straw should be inoculated within a few hours as the pH drops
naturally. Lime pasteurization works best with wheat and [[fukuoka-rice-straw-mulch-central-technique]];
hardwood sawdust is less reliably treated due to its denser structure.

### Cold Water Fermentation

Submerging substrate in cold water for 3-14 days. During anaerobic
fermentation, lactic [[lactic-acid-bacteria-knf]] acidify the substrate to pH 4-5,
creating conditions hostile to most competitor molds and bacteria. This
method requires no heat or chemical input but takes significantly
longer. It has been used traditionally in [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]] for oyster
[[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] on straw.

## Temperature Targets

Maintaining the correct temperature is critical. Below 55 degrees C,
many competitors survive. Above 85 degrees C, beneficial thermophilic
bacteria die off. The ideal range of 60-70 degrees C achieves the right
balance. Internal temperature should be monitored with a probe
thermometer, as surface temperatures can differ significantly from the
core. Digital probe thermometers with at least 30 cm probes are
recommended. The concept of thermal death time is important: the
pasteurization process must ensure the target temperature is sustained
long enough at the coldest point. For most applications, maintaining
60-65 degrees C for 60-90 minutes provides an adequate safety margin.
