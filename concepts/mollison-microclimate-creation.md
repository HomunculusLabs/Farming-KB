# Mollison Microclimate Creation

Bill Mollison treats microclimate creation as one of the designer's most powerful
tools. Rather than accepting the macroclimate as fixed constraint, Mollison
demonstrates how deliberate placement of walls, trees, water bodies, earthworks,
and buildings can create localized conditions differing significantly from the
surrounding regional climate, expanding the range of productive strategies.

## The Science of Microclimate

Every solid object modifies the climate around it. Walls store and radiate heat,
trees create shade and wind shelter, water bodies moderate temperature extremes
through evaporative cooling and thermal mass, and earthworks redirect air flow.
The key insight is that modification operates at every scale: a single rock
creates a tiny microclimate, while a designed system can modify conditions across
an entire property, supporting species that would not normally survive.

## Thermal Mass and Heat Storage

Stone walls, water bodies, and earth berms are the primary thermal mass elements.
Dense materials absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night,
reducing temperature extremes. A stone wall facing the afternoon sun creates a
warm microclimate several degrees warmer than surrounding air through the night.

Water is particularly effective due to high specific heat capacity. A modest pond
stores enormous solar energy during the day and releases it slowly at night.
Mediterranean traditional agriculture uses water bodies near gardens to prevent
frost damage and extend the growing season by several weeks.

Earth berms and earth-sheltered structures use ground thermal mass. Below 1 meter
depth, soil temperature remains nearly constant at approximately the annual average
air temperature. Earth-sheltered buildings and root cellars exploit this stability
for efficient temperature regulation year-round.

## Wind Modification

Windbreaks are the most direct tool for microclimate creation. A well-designed
windbreak creates a protected zone extending 10-15 times its height downwind,
reducing evaporation and wind chill significantly. Mollison prefers multi-row
windbreaks with tall trees, understory trees, and shrubs creating gradually
increasing density. The ideal has 40-60 percent porosity; too-solid barriers
create severe turbulence worse than no barrier at all.

## Frost Protection Strategies

Cold air flows downhill like water, collecting in low points and valleys. Mollison
advises avoiding frost-sensitive species in cold air drainage paths or intercepting
cold air with fences, earth banks, or dense hedges. The thermal mass of water
bodies and stone walls prevents radiation frost by radiating stored heat, keeping
surrounding air above freezing on clear cold nights.

## Shade and Humidity Creation

In hot dry climates, shade and humidity creation are primary goals. Canopy trees
and shade structures reduce ground surface temperatures by 15-25 degrees Celsius,
transforming hostile areas into productive garden zones. Water features increase
humidity, reducing plant water stress and creating conditions for a wider range
of species. Mollison integrates water features with shade to maximize cooling.

## Integration with Built Structures

Mollison integrates microclimate creation with passive solar design so the house
becomes a microclimate modifier. Attached greenhouses, thermal mass walls, and
proper orientation create warm zones for early spring and winter growing. Courtyards
and walled gardens are particularly effective: shaded courtyards with water create
cool oases in hot climates, while south-facing courtyards with thermal mass trap
solar heat and provide wind shelter in cold climates.
