## White Rot Fungi

White rot fungi are a polyphyletic group of basidiomycetes and some ascomycetes
characterized by their ability to degrade all major components of wood,
including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. They are named for the
bleached white appearance of wood they have colonized, resulting from the
selective removal of brown lignin pigments. White rot fungi are the primary
biological agents of lignin decomposition on Earth and are indispensable to
global carbon cycling in forest ecosystems. Their lignin-degrading capabilities
are detailed in agaricomycetes class
of Basidiomycota, particularly within the orders Polyporales, Hymenochaetales,
Agaricales, and Russulales. Notable genera include Phanerochaete, Trametes,
Pleurotus, Bjerkandera, Ceriporiopsis, and Ganoderma.

Phylogenomic analyses suggest that the ancestral condition of Agaricomycetes
was likely white rot, with brown rot and ectomycorrhizal lifestyles evolving
through loss of lignin-degrading enzyme gene families. This hypothesis
explains why white rot capability is scattered across diverse lineages while
brown rot is concentrated in fewer, specialized clades.

## Decay Mechanisms

White rot fungi employ two principal decay strategies that exist on a
continuum rather than as discrete categories. Selective white rot preferentially
removes lignin and hemicellulose while leaving cellulose largely intact,
producing a fibrous, bleached residue. Simultaneous white rot degrades all
wood components at comparable rates, resulting in uniform erosion and eventual
complete decomposition.

## Ecological Roles

In forest ecosystems, white rot fungi drive the decomposition of coarse woody
debris, fallen logs, and standing dead trees. They create habitat complexity
by differentially removing wood components, producing cavities and softened
wood used by invertebrates, birds, and small mammals. Their activity releases
nutrients locked in wood back into the soil nutrient cycle.

White rot fungi also engage in aggressive competition with other decomposers
and pathogens. Their oxidative enzyme systems create chemical environments
hostile to bacteria and non-ligninolytic fungi, giving them competitive
dominance on wood substrates. Some white rot species are mycoparasitic,
attacking other fungi with chitinases and antimicrobial compounds.
