# Turkey Tail (Trametes versicolor)

## Overview

Trametes versicolor, commonly known as turkey tail, is one of the most widespread and studied medicinal mushrooms in the world. Named for its concentricly banded, multicolored fruiting bodies that resemble a wild turkey's tail feathers, this polypore mushroom grows on dead hardwood logs and stumps worldwide. It has a long history of use in traditional medicine and is the source of some of the best-researched medicinal mushroom preparations.

## Taxonomy and Identification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Division**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Polyporales
- **Family**: Polyporaceae
- **Genus**: Trametes
- **Species**: T. versicolor (syn. Coriolus versicolor)

### Morphology

- **Cap**: Fan-shaped to semicircular, 2-10 cm wide, overlapping shelves
- **Surface**: Concentric zones of brown, tan, white, blue, green, orange, and red
- **Texture**: Velvety to hairy when young, becoming smoother with age
- **Pore surface**: White to cream, tiny pores (3-5 per mm)
- **Flesh**: Thin (1-3 mm), tough, leathery, white
- **Spore print**: White
- **Habitat**: Saprobic on dead hardwood (oak, beech, birch, maple); rarely on conifers
- **Distribution**: Cosmopolitan — found on every continent except Antarctica

### Lookalikes

- **False turkey tail (Stereum ostrea)**: Similar colors and shape but lacks pores — has a smooth, sterile undersurface. More orange tones. Not known to be toxic but lacks medicinal value.
- **Trametes hirsuta**: Similar but uniformly gray-brown, less colorful zoning
- **Fomes fomentarius**: Hoof-shaped, much thicker flesh, different growth form

### How to Distinguish

The key feature is the pore surface: true turkey tail has tiny pores on the undersurface, while false turkey tail (Stereum) is smooth. A hand lens helps. See [[fungal-spore-print-guide]] for spore print identification.

## Medicinal Research

Turkey tail is arguably the most clinically studied medicinal mushroom. Two major polysaccharide preparations have been developed:

### PSK (Polysaccharide-K / Krestin)

- Developed in Japan in the 1970s
- Protein-bound polysaccharide extracted from T. versicolor mycelium
- Approved as a cancer adjuvant therapy in Japan since 1977
- Prescribed alongside chemotherapy and radiation
- Marketed under the brand name Krestin

Clinical evidence for PSK:
- Gastric cancer: Multiple RCTs showing improved survival when PSK added to standard chemotherapy
- Colorectal cancer: Improved disease-free survival in post-surgical patients
- Breast cancer: Enhanced immune function during chemotherapy
- Lung cancer: Improved survival in non-small cell lung cancer patients

### PSP (Polysaccharide Peptide)

- Developed in China in the 1980s
- Similar to PSK but with slightly different extraction and composition
- Marketed as Yun Zhi (cloud mushroom) in traditional Chinese medicine
- Approved as a cancer adjuvant in China

### Mechanisms of Action

1. **Immune modulation**: PSK and PSP activate immune cells including NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and T-lymphocytes. They act as biological response modifiers (BRMs).

2. **Dendritic cell maturation**: Turkey tail polysaccharides promote dendritic cell maturation, enhancing antigen presentation.

3. **Cytokine regulation**: Increases production of IL-2, IFN-gamma, and TNF-alpha while modulating excessive inflammatory responses.

4. **Anti-inflammatory**: Downregulates NF-kB pathway and reduces chronic inflammation.

5. **Anti-tumor activity**: While not directly cytotoxic to tumor cells, PSK enhances the immune system's ability to recognize and destroy cancer cells.

6. **Gut microbiome**: PSP and PSK act as prebiotics, promoting beneficial gut bacteria (Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus).

### Active Compounds

- **Beta-glucans (1,3 / 1,6)**: Primary immunomodulatory polysaccharides
- **PSK**: Protein-bound beta-glucan
- **PSP**: Polypeptide-bound polysaccharide
- **Triterpenoids**: Anti-inflammatory compounds
- **Phenolic compounds**: Antioxidant activity (quercetin, baicalein derivatives)
- **Fomentin**: Antibacterial compound

## Cancer Adjunct Therapy

Turkey tail is not a standalone cancer treatment. It is used as an adjuvant (complementary) therapy alongside conventional treatments:

### Integration with Chemotherapy

- Reduces chemotherapy side effects (fatigue, nausea, immune suppression)
- Maintains white blood cell counts during treatment
- Improves quality of life scores
- May enhance chemotherapy efficacy through immune potentiation

### Integration with Radiation Therapy

- Protects against radiation-induced immune damage
- May sensitize some tumor types to radiation

### Dosage in Clinical Studies

- PSK: 3g/day orally, divided doses
- PSP: 1-3g/day orally
- Hot water extract: 2-4g dried mushroom per day, brewed as tea
- Duration: Typically throughout chemotherapy course (3-6 months)

## Preparation and Dosage

### Hot Water Extraction (Tea)

1. Collect or purchase dried turkey tail (verify identity)
2. Break into small pieces or powder
3. Simmer in water (ratio: 1g mushroom per 100ml water) for 30-60 minutes
4. Strain and drink
5. Can be re-extracted 2-3 times
6. Dosage: 1-2 cups per day

### Dual Extraction

Beta-glucans are water-soluble, while triterpenoids are alcohol-soluble. A dual extraction captures both:

1. Hot water extraction (30-60 min simmer)
2. Separate alcohol extraction (ethanol soak, 2-4 weeks)
3. Combine extracts
4. Evaporate alcohol or use as tincture

### Capsules and Powders

Commercial turkey tail products vary widely in quality:
- Look for products that specify beta-glucan content (>30%)
- Verify species identity (some products contain fillers or wrong species)
