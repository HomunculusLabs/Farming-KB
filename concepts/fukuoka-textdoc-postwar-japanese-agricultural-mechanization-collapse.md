Japan"s farming population fell from fifty percent of the overall population at
the end of the war to less than twenty percent by the time of Fukuoka's writing.
This massive labor shift provided the workforce that built Japan's postwar economic
miracle — its skyscrapers, highways, and subways. Without the dexterous,
hard-working farmer, Fukuoka argues, Japan's urban civilization would never have
materialized. Yet this prosperity came at the direct expense of the farming

## From Food Security to Industrial Feedstock
In the mid-1950s, the drive was adequate food supply during acute shortage. By
the late 1960s and early 1970s, food supply stability was achieved and the
economy overflowed with vigor. Politicians and businessmen began strategizing
how to absorb farmers and their land into the industrial apparatus. What began
as food security became a vehicle for industrial expansion. The rapid
mechanization of agriculture lit the fires for the revival and precipitous
growth of the machine industry, while the adoption of pesticides, chemical
fertilizers, and petroleum-based farming materials laid the foundation for
development of the chemical industry.
