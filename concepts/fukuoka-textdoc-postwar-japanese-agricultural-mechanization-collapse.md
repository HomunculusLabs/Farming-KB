    16|# Postwar Japanese Agricultural Mechanization and Village Collapse
    17|
    18|Masanobu Fukuoka presents a detailed analysis of how post-World War II mechanization
    19|and modernization systematically destroyed Japanese farming village culture,
    20|self-sufficiency, and ecological balance. His account traces the transformation from
    21|labor-intensive to capital-intensive agriculture and its cascading social and
    22|ecological consequences.
    23|
    24|## The Transformation of the Farming Population
    25|
    26|Japan's farming population fell from fifty percent of the overall population at
    27|the end of the war to less than twenty percent by the time of Fukuoka's writing.
    28|This massive labor shift provided the workforce that built Japan's postwar economic
    29|miracle — its skyscrapers, highways, and subways. Without the dexterous,
    30|hard-working farmer, Fukuoka argues, Japan's urban civilization would never have
    31|materialized. Yet this prosperity came at the direct expense of the farming
    32|population, who effectively "tightened the noose about their own neck."
    33|
    34|## The Motorized Transport-Tiller as Turning Point
    35|
    36|The arrival of the motorized transport-tiller marked a fundamental turning point
    37|in Japanese agriculture. This was rapidly followed by three-wheeled vehicles and
    38|trucks, then ropeways, monorails, and paved roads extending to the furthest
    39|village corners. These developments completely altered the farmer's notions of
    40|time and space, creating expectations of speed and efficiency that had never
    41|existed in traditional village life.
    42|
    43|## The Chain of Mechanization
    44|
    45|The progression from labor-intensive to capital-intensive farming followed a
    46|predictable sequence:
    47|
    48|1. **Draft animal replacement**: Horse-drawn plows were replaced by tillers,
    49|   then tractors
    50|2. **Chemical input adoption**: Motorized hand sprayers were abandoned in favor
    51|   of helicopter spraying
    52|3. **Traditional abandonment**: Traditional farming with draft animals was
    53|   replaced by heavy chemical fertilizers and pesticides
    54|4. **Industrial catalysis**: Agricultural mechanization fueled revival of the
    55|   machine industry, while chemical adoption laid the foundation for chemical
    56|   industry development
    57|
    58|## From Food Security to Industrial Feedstock
    59|
    60|In the mid-1950s, the drive was adequate food supply during acute shortage. By
    61|the late 1960s and early 1970s, food supply stability was achieved and the
    62|economy overflowed with vigor. Politicians and businessmen began strategizing
    63|how to absorb farmers and their land into the industrial apparatus. What began
    64|as food security became a vehicle for industrial expansion. The rapid
    65|mechanization of agriculture lit the fires for the revival and precipitous
    66|growth of the machine industry, while the adoption of pesticides, chemical
    67|fertilizers, and petroleum-based farming materials laid the foundation for
    68|development of the chemical industry.
    69|
    70|## The Disappearance of Village Culture
    71|
    72|Fukuoka mourns the loss of the sunken hearth (irori), which he identifies as
    73|the center of ancient farming village culture. With its extinction came the loss
    74|of fireside discussions and the village philosophy it nurtured. Where once
    75|woodcutters sang as they sawed, transplanting songs rolled over paddy fields,
    76|and drums surged through villages after harvest, the mechanized landscape
    77|produced only the angry snarl of chain saws and the roar of engines. Everyone
    78|now sits before the TV set, listening to traditional country songs and
    79|reminiscing over the past.
    80|
    81|## The Contradiction of Labor-Saving Technology
    82|
    83|Fukuoka identifies a bitter irony: mechanization was supposed to make farming
    84|easier, but instead it freed farmers from the land only to force them into
    85|harder work at other jobs, wearing away body and mind. The chain saw was
    86|developed to cut trees faster, yet rather than easing the farmer's burden,
    87|mechanized rice transplantation sent them searching for outside employment.
    88|Every labor-saving device created new dependencies and new forms of labor.
    89|Vegetables today are grown in vinyl houses ranged in neat rows like factories,
    90|and fields are automatically sprayed with fertilizers and pesticides.
    91|
    92|## The Philosophy of Mu in the Village
    93|
    94|Fukuoka argues that traditional farming villages were inherently philosophical
    95|communities operating on the principle of "Mu" (nothingness) — the understanding
    96|that philosophy itself was unnecessary. Farmers preferred to live common lives
    97|without knowledge or learning. This was not ignorance but a profound wisdom:
    98|there was no time for philosophizing because life was never so empty as to
    99|provoke existential questioning. Theirs was a philosophy embodied in the
   100|principle that "philosophy is unnecessary." The farming village was, in
   101|Fukuoka's words, "above all a society of philosophers without a need for
   102|philosophy."
   103|
   104|## The Self-Sufficient Village
   105|
   106|Fukuoka describes pre-mechanization villages as places where farmers were
   107|self-supporting and independent, living proud, noble lives in the great
   108|outdoors. Despite poverty and low social status, they experienced daily joy and
   109|pride in tending what he calls "the gardens of God." They went out to work at
   110|sunrise and returned at sunset, each day "as wide and infinite as the universe
   111|and yet just one small frame in the unending flow of existence." These farmers
   112|were "creatures of nature, and being close to God, experienced the daily joy
   113|and pride of tending the gardens of God."
   114|
   115|## The "Do-Nothing" Movement
   116|
   117|Fukuoka frames natural farming as part of a broader "do-nothing" movement — a
   118|spiritual and cultural shift from aggressive expansion to consolidation and
   119|convergence. He argues that the age of materialistic expansion is at an end, and
   120|humanity must hurry to establish a new way of life and a spiritual culture
   121|founded on communion with nature. To achieve this, man must look back over
   122|everything he has done and rid himself of false visions and concepts. Natural
   123|farming is more than just a revolution in agricultural techniques; it is the
   124|practical foundation of a spiritual movement.
   125|
   126|## Ecological Cascades from Pesticide Application
   127|
   128|Fukuoka provides detailed examples of ecological chain reactions triggered by
   129|pesticide use. Toxic chemicals are applied at least eight times per year on
   130|Japanese rice fields. The first application does not kill rice leafhoppers but
   131|eliminates tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard, along with swarms
   132|of fireflies. The second application kills chalcid wasps (important predators)
   133|while also destroying dragonfly larvae, tadpoles, and loaches.
   134|
   135|A particularly devastating chain reaction involves pine forest ecosystem
   136|collapse: clear-cutting and cedar planting eliminates food for small birds,
   137|allowing long-horned beetles to flourish. Beetles carry nematodes that attack
   138|red pines, which are already weakened by the disappearance of edible matsutake
   139|fungus that lived symbiotically on their roots. The matsutake died due to an
   140|increase in harmful Botrytis fungus, caused by soil acidity from atmospheric
   141|pollution and acid rain.
   142|
