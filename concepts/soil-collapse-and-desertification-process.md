## Soil Collapse and Desertification Process

[[bill-mollison]] identified this as one of the most serious and
underestimated threats to arid and semi-arid lands, dramatically accelerated by human
activity.

## Dust Storms and Topsoil Loss

Dust storms represent both a symptom and a driver of soil collapse. Fine topsoil is
stripped from degraded landscapes and deposited on plateaus, gravel ridges, and towns.
In Alice Springs, loess deposits from historical dust storms have covered pioneer
graves to depths of 1-2 metres — a striking visual record of how much soil has been
mobilised. Where grassland growth has been restored since 1966, dust storms have
effectively stopped, demonstrating that vegetation cover and excluding grazing animals
can halt the cycle of topsoil loss and deposition.

## Silty Dust and Aquifer Damage

Silty dust works into rock crevices and fractures, sealing off the water intake
crevices that feed aquifers and springs. This damage is invisible from the surface.
When intake areas are damaged and subsoils become sealed, a cascade of overland flow
and accelerated soil erosion follows. Water that once infiltrated to recharge
groundwater now runs across the surface, deepening gullies. Springs begin to fail and
streamflows decline, often signalling that soil collapse is well advanced.

## Cemented Subsoils and Perched Water Tables

As collapse progresses, the subsoil becomes cemented, sealing the soil off from its
parent rock below. This creates a perched water table — water trapped above the
impermeable layer rather than draining through the profile. The cemented subsoil
blocks both downward water movement and upward root penetration, killing the soil"s
biological function and its ability to support deep-rooted perennial vegetation.

## Healthy Soil Structure

According to Leeper (1980), untouched soil to 1 metre depth typically contains 8-20%
humus, 10-40% clay, and 40-60% air space. The specific gravity of open soil ranges
from 1.0-1.6, while mineral components have a specific gravity of 2.6-3.2. Average air
pore space in healthy soil exceeds 50%. Without adequate air space, roots cannot
penetrate, water cannot infiltrate, and aerobic microorganisms cannot function.

## The Collapse Sequence

Soil collapse follows a predictable four-stage sequence:

1. **Humus removal** — fire, cropping, or over-grazing depletes the organic fraction
   that provides structure and nutrient cycling.

2. **Clay deflocculation** — without humus to bind clay particles, the structure
   disintegrates. Individual platelets pack together densely, eliminating air spaces.

3. **Compaction** — the soil compacts under its own weight and the pressure of hooves
   or machinery. The pore network collapses and infiltration rates plummet.

4. **Cemented surface formation** — the compacted surface hardens into an impermeable
   crust, a cemented "B" horizon with concrete-like consistency.

The result: 1 metre of healthy soil is compressed to 10-20 centimetres overlying the
cemented horizon. Salt previously dispersed throughout the whole layer becomes
concentrated in a distinct salty zone.

## Desertification Through Soil Collapse

This desertification mechanism is distinct from the groundwater-rising salinity seen
in Victoria. In soil-collapse desertification, the profile fails structurally from
above, and salt concentrates in the remaining thin layer. Anaerobic conditions develop
in compacted, waterlogged soils, producing ethylene, methane, and sulphurous smells —
indicators that soil chemistry has fundamentally shifted to toxic conditions for plant
roots.

Mottled and waterlogged soils develop iron stone layers or iron "buckshot" concretions
at the base of slopes, directly above salted areas. These iron features mark the
boundary between oxidised and reduced zones and are reliable field indicators of
advancing soil collapse.

## Landscape Collapse and Salt Pans

Collapsed areas sink 1-2 metres, producing sunken salt pans as air spaces collapse and
organic matter oxidises away. The process spreads outward through peripheral soil
collapse. Salt washes downstream and into dams, contaminating water supplies. Once
established, these processes become self-generating in a positive feedback loop. While
naturally taking 10,000+ years, deforestation and poor land management can compress
this into decades.

## Water Management and Salinity

Flood irrigation loses approximately 60% of applied water to evaporation, resulting in
2-7 times salt concentration increase. Ridge and furrow systems worsen this because
salt increases on ridges where less flushing occurs. Leeper (1982) showed uncropped
soils absorb 30cm of water over 5 hours versus only 5-16cm in cropped soils — a
direct consequence of structure degradation through tillage and organic matter removal.

## Soil Structure and Crumb Formation

In flooded soils containing salts, deflocculation occurs and crumb structure breaks
down. Wheat lands have only 12% well-structured crumbs compared to 92% in free-
draining uncleared soils. Polysaccharides and gels from humus act as biological glue
holding particles in stable aggregates. Burning stubble reduces humus and damages
crumb structure. [[green-manure-crops]] restore crumb structure by providing
polysaccharides and microbial activity — but only if salt deflocculation is also
addressed.

## Remediation Strategies

Foth (1984) found that deep drainage plus gypsum application can restore acidic soils,
though leaching may affect stream health. Interceptor banks redirect water flow and
prevent salt concentration in low-lying areas. Where pH exceeds 8.5, gypsum is
particularly effective at displacing sodium from clay exchange sites, allowing
flocculation and structure recovery. Chemical remediation must be paired with
biological restoration — adding organic matter and establishing permanent vegetation
cover. Soil collapse is preventable through maintaining vegetation, excluding
inappropriate grazing, and preserving organic matter, but once collapse occurs,
remediation requires sustained effort over many years.
