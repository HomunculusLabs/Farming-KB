## Animal Systems in Arid Areas

Small livestock systems are remarkably productive in desert environments when properly designed. The key is working with the natural behaviour and ecology of animals rather than against it, and recognising that deserts support a far wider diversity of edible species than conventional agriculture acknowledges.

## Small Livestock in Deserts

Chickens, quail, guinea fowl, guinea pigs, and even ducks and geese all do well in deserts (though it is often too hot for rabbits in cages). They require:

- **Dense shade shelters** for thermal protection
- **Access to shallow clean water**
- Protection from predators

### Functions Performed
- **Insect and snail reduction** — critical [[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]] service
- **Egg and small meat production** not requiring cool storage
- **Termite control** — they break open mud tunnels in grain storage bins supported on rocks
- **Foraging** on a great many desert seed and grain crops, plus garden and fallen fruit scraps

## Desert Aquatic Systems

Several aquatic and swamp plants thrive in deserts due to:

- **Exotic rivers** flowing into desert regions
- **Run-off from ranges** creating temporary water bodies
- **Claypans and gilgais** (run-off hollows) supporting plants tolerant of hot, brackish, or salty water

### Deserts as Aquatic Islands
Thousands of sites in deserts have ponds and dams discharging into interior flats or flood-outs, some below sea level ([[allegro-dead-sea-scrolls]], Lake Eyre in South Australia). The desert functions as a series of **small aquatic islands** with no danger of species escaping to infest permanent streams — making them ideal sites for small aquacultural assemblies and experiments.

### Natural Water Filtration
All growing plants use up salts in growth, and dense algae desalinate large volumes of water. Many rushes and weeds remove dissolved salts and unwanted pollutants. It is feasible to set up **part-enclosed, part-open natural water filtration systems** to remove faecal, nitrogenous, and metallic pollutants from town water supplies. Such systems:

- Produce useful forests and biogas materials
- Greatly reduce transmissible disease in third-world desert communities
- Provide food and wildlife shelter
- Generate bedding and craft materials from rushes

## Large Livestock on Range

Highly selected and controlled meat and milk flocks (sheep, goats, cattle, donkeys, camels) thrive if:

- **Herded or penned in 15 or so rotated runs**
- Each run receives **2-8 years** for recovery and re-seeding
- Successful Australian graziers allow **7-9 years rest** per run
- This approach never suffers the animal stress brought on by drought

The principle is infinitely preferable: **run small, high-value herds on copious range** rather than risk the inevitable collapse of range and flocks by stressing the vegetation.

### Meat Preservation
- Meats can be **dried in screened and shaded air cupboards**
- Strips beaten with pepper and garlic to 1 cm or less thick and 4 cm wide dry quickly and "glaze" in hours
- This "jerky" keeps indefinitely in insect-proof fine-mesh bags hung from ceilings
- Complete drying takes 3-5 days

## Wildlife Classification by Movement Pattern

### Irruptive and Nomadic Species
Large, fast-moving animals such as kangaroo, antelope, and ostrich that travel long distances to areas of rain. They:

- "Smell the wind" detecting wet earth and vegetation
- Follow a **vacuum effect** — as some move to rain, neighbours find old areas empty and move toward "[[low-stress-training]]" areas
- Provide culling opportunities from young animals (those that die in dry times anyway)
- **Healthy adults must be preserved**

### Sedentary Species
Smaller, slower, or more specialised animals adapted to drought, residing in an area at all times. Their limiting factors include:

- **Water holes** (quail, ground birds)
- **Shade** (large lizards, surface mammals)
- **Burrow sites** (small mammals, lizards)
- **Specialised forage** adapted to dry, salt, or wet areas

[[livestock-grassland-management-strategies]] include small water "ramp dams," rock piles, shaded caves or cliff sites, bulldozed loose earth piles for burrows, and key plants for hungry gaps.

### Plague Species
Rabbits, grasshoppers, and similar species present harvest potential:

- Rabbits can be harvested and dried
- Insects can be converted via domesticated predators (chickens, guinea fowl)
- Chickens, ducks, fish, and guinea fowl convert most insects to **food and high-value manure**
- Breeding or attraction systems (like termite breeders) can take advantage of this resource

### Reptiles
Large lizards are the **"fish" of the desert**, carefully harvested by Aboriginal Australians as a constant, dependable food source. They need only good management and encouragement of insects, fruits, and snails to thrive.

### Arboreal Grubs
The large grubs of moths (Hepialidae, Cossidae, Cerambycidae) have historically formed a large part of reliable foods:

- They live in the **humid niche of bark and roots** in the arid environment
- Several species live as grubs for **7-8 years** with only brief adult lives
- They are **always to be found** and are both palatable and nutritious
- Desert frogs (Hylidae, Leptodactylidae) persist similarly in resting states for up to 8 years

## Traditional Desert Diet Breadth

A typical Aboriginal Australian diet contains:
- 6-10 insect larvae species
- 10-12 reptile species
- 6-12 birds (some taken in moult)
- 3-4 large migratory mammals
- 9-10 smaller sedentary mammals
- 5-8 frog species (mainly females with large ovaries)
- Adult insects, aquatic crustaceans, and molluscs (3-4 species)
- Fish (4-8 species depending on area)

**Total: 48-66 animal foods**, and at least that many plant foods including aquatics from temporary lagoons. This breadth demonstrates that desert productivity is far higher than conventional monoculture systems suggest.

## Species Complementarity

Different species use the desert in **non-competitive, probably complementary ways** with some opportunistic overlap:

- **Kangaroo and cattle** eat dry or green forage not much appreciated by the other at any given time
- Kangaroo eating of coarse dry grasses enables cattle to browse more easily in wet periods
- Kangaroo eat very few grass species compared with cattle, who are less selective
- Cattle prefer annual grasses
- Similar complementarity exists between euro (kangaroo) and sheep

### Nutritional Comparison
Kangaroo meat is a **superior food** to either beef or sheep:
- Beef/sheep: 38-42% digestible protein, saturated fats
- Kangaroo: **58% digestible protein**, low fats (mainly unsaturated)

This suggests that wildlife management should favour **efficient conversion** rather than pre-selected markets that are failing anyway.

## Nomadic Refuge Management

Nomadic tribes of birds and larger mammals provide a "feast and famine" resource depending on rains. They retire to refuges or more humid areas during long droughts. Such refuges should be managed as **"tabu" areas for long-term species preservation**, requiring skilled and cooperative management similar to any herding system. Fences make less sense in deserts because of the need for mammals to migrate and fall back on reserve areas [[gardening-in-hard-times]].
