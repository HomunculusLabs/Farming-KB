## Overview

Fungal conservation is a critically underdeveloped area of biodiversity protection. With an estimated 1.5 million fungal species on Earth and only 5-10% formally described, conservation efforts face a fundamental knowledge gap. Fungi play essential roles [[comparison-soil-protozoa-vs-nematodes-in-nutrient-cycling]], plant health, decomposition, and ecosystem stability, yet they are frequently excluded from [[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]] planning and land-use management decisions (Hawksworth, 1991).

## The Scale of the Problem

- Approximately 74,000 to 120,000 fungal species have been formally described
- Conservative estimates suggest 1,500,000 species exist globally, though evidence indicates this may be too low
- No single site on Earth has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi
- In temperate areas, approximately six times as many fungi occur as native plant species
- A single 200-hectare site can harbor 2,500-3,000 species if studied by multiple specialists over 25+ years

## Challenges in Assessing Rarity

With the present state of knowledge, it is almost impossible to designate truly rare or threatened species that merit conservation efforts. Many taxa known from only single collections may reflect low intensity of collecting rather than genuine rarity. Rarity may be an artifact of the specialized requirements of certain fungal groups (Richardson, 2001). For example, many coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi have narrow host ranges, and with every loss of habitat or host, the potential for loss of the mycobiota is high.

## Key Conservation Strategies

### Habitat Protection

The primary strategy for fungal conservation is habitat protection. Since most fungi cannot be maintained ex situ with the same reliability as plants or animals, conserving the ecosystems they inhabit is essential. Efforts for preservation of habitat-dependent fungi should be directed toward the conservation of habitats that are threatened, especially those with threatened animal or plant hosts.

### Comprehensive Surveys

Surveys are particularly important for regions where native ecosystems are under pressure for commercial development. Survey data provide the baseline against which to measure changes in species presence and abundance at particular sites. Information from inventories serves as the foundation for systematics, ecological, physiological, genetic, and conservation studies.

### Herbarium and Culture Collections

Collections maintained in herbaria and their associated data are invaluable resources for all individuals and organizations interested in biodiversity conservation and land-use planning. Long-term storage of cultures in sterilized screw-cap bottles or under mineral oil preserves genetic material for future study.

### Bioindicator Approaches

Coprophilous fungi and other specialized groups can serve as useful indicators of both biodiversity and habitat quality, allowing for relatively quick, simple, inexpensive quantitative assessment. Hyde and Hawksworth (1997) suggested that realistic estimates of microfungal diversity may be impractical at full scale, but targeted indicator groups can provide actionable conservation data.

### Integration with Land-Use Planning

Fungi must be included in considerations of biodiversity conservation, land-use planning, and management. They frequently have been ignored in these contexts, despite their fundamental roles [[fungi-in-ecosystem-processes-dighton]].

## Why Fungal Conservation Matters

Without fungi, major problems in nutrient cycling would occur, plants would suffer without soil nutrients secured by fungi, many animals would lose food sources, woody materials would not break down, soil structures would differ, and sources of antibiotics and other pharmaceutical products would be diminished.
