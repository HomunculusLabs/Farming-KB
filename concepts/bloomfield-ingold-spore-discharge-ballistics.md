## Ingold, Buller, and the Physics of Fungal Spore Discharge

The mechanisms by which fungi launch their spores into the air represent some
of the most remarkable feats of biological engineering. In *Mr. Bloomfield's
Orchard*, Nicholas P. Money devotes the opening chapter, "Offensive Phalli
and Frigid Caps," and subsequent passages to the pioneering work of A.H.
Reginald Buller, professor of botany at the University of Manitoba, and
C. [[bloomfield-mushroom-evaporative-cooling-and-thermal-regulation]] promotes
the buildup of water on the spore surface by slowing the movement of water
molecules, encouraging them to occupy a condensed liquid state rather than
remaining as vapor. If the mushroom warmed, even slightly, water would
evaporate from the spore surface, Buller's drops would never expand, and the
spores would remain trapped on the gills. The spore surface is sugary and
hygroscopic—comparable, Money suggests, to a cube of bath-salts in a steamy
bathroom. This counterintuitive finding—that the coldest organisms on the
forest floor are also the most actively reproductive—exposed the forest floor
as a thermal mosaic.

## Aerodynamics of the Mushroom Cap

After spores are shot from the hymenium, they fall through narrow air spaces
between gills and emerge beneath the cap. The mushroom stem elevates the cap
above the boundary layer of still air on the ground. Wind tunnel experiments
revealed that when mushrooms are exposed to wind, the airstream divides at
the leading edge of the cap, accelerating above and below in a pattern
reminiscent of an aircraft wing. Air pressure beneath the cap exceeds that
producing lift. This interruption of airflow also slows air movement
immediately beneath the fertile tissues, so spores fall through calm air for
a few tenths of a second before being swept away—reducing the number blown
back into the cap and promoting successful dispersal. The ubiquitous umbrella
form of the mushroom is also essential for maintaining a humid atmosphere
around the hymenium while shielding spores from rain; any fluid running over
the gills would wash away the droplets and spoil the [[coprinus-comatus]]* (the lawyer's wig), are
particularly effective at this aerodynamic management.

## Ingold's Broader Contributions

C. Terence Ingold, to whom Money dedicated the book, made his most celebrated
discovery in 1938 when, as a young professor in Leicester, he found
star-shaped and sigmoid spores in foam collecting in "a little, alder-lined,
babbling brook" near his home. These [[aquatic-fungi-and-ingoldian-spores]], produced by a
previously unknown group of aquatic fungi, opened an entirely new chapter in
mycology—more than 300 species have since been identified. Ingold later
collaborated with Webster on the solution to the ballistospore discharge
mechanism. During his long career he wrote a series of influential books and
continued publishing research into his nineties. Money recounts discovering
as a teenager that his neighbor in Benson, Oxfordshire, was Ingold himself—a
coincidence that cemented his fascination with fungal mechanisms. The common
thread in both Ingold's and Money's work is captivation by the circus of
bizarre mechanisms unique to fungi, from surface tension catapults to
aquatic spore dispersal, all powered by the same miraculous solvent: water.
