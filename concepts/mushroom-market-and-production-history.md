## Mushroom Market and Production History

## Overview

The cultivation and marketing of mushrooms (*[[mushroom-cultivation]], and it remains the historical center of the industry. The extraordinary cave systems around Paris — vast underground quarries created by centuries of limestone mining — provided near-ideal conditions for mushroom growing and were directly responsible for the enormous scale of the French industry.

### Production Statistics

Data from the Central Market (Halles Centrales) of Paris provide the most reliable historical figures for European mushroom production:

- In 1906, approximately 708,000 kilograms of mushrooms passed through the Paris central markets, with a total estimated production reaching roughly 2,800,000 kilograms
- Wholesale prices during this period ranged from 1.20 to 2.20 francs per kilogram
- In 1911, the average wholesale price was 1.50 francs per kilogram, indicating a total value of about 7,500,000 francs (retail value approximately 12,500,000 francs, or about $2,500,000)
- By 1907, the total yield had decreased somewhat, with average prices rising to approximately two francs per kilogram

### Year-Round Production

The monthly production figures for the Paris region demonstrate that mushroom growing in the caves was conducted year-round, taking full advantage of the stable underground environment. Monthly production varied, with some months showing yields of approximately 300,000 to 400,000 kilograms. The ability to produce mushrooms consistently throughout the year was one of the key advantages that cave cultivation offered over surface-based methods.

### Export Trade

France was a major exporter of canned mushrooms, and a very large proportion of this exported product was destined for American markets. In 1900, approximately 3,000,000 pounds of canned mushrooms were consumed in the United States, the great majority imported from France. By 1906, this figure had risen to an estimated four to five million pounds. However, the subsequent growth of domestic mushroom production in the United States was sufficient to hold canned imports at a stationary level.

### Early Development

The [[mushroom-cultivation-history]] is that for several centuries, no progress was made in maintaining or developing distinct varieties. The seed trade everywhere handled spawn simply as "mushroom spawn," with no indication of varieties. It was only around 1900, with the development of pure culture work, that it became possible to propagate specific varieties and to produce spawn from any desired variety.

## Shipment and Pricing

### Shipping Practices

Mushrooms may be shipped considerable distances during the winter months. If properly packed and shipped immediately after being gathered, there is little danger during 36 to 48 hours. They should never be packed in very large quantities, since the heat developed in a basket of more than 10 pounds will be considerable, encouraging bacterial and mold development.

### Price Trends

The price paid for mushrooms was formerly quite variable, ranging from 25 cents to considerably more than $1 per pound. By the early twentieth century, hotel trade yielded no more than 50 cents per pound and frequently only 40 cents. During the season of heaviest production, many large producers were forced to accept 25 cents or less per pound — sometimes below the cost of production.

### The Seasonal Problem

The small grower frequently experienced difficulty when beds produced most heavily during spring, flooding the market. Where mushrooms were grown in sheds, cold cellars, or makeshift houses, the beds might fail to bear until spring. The large grower could anticipate this condition, and a well-established high-class trade was not materially injured by it.
