## Understanding Keyline Geometry

The keyline is defined as a contour line that passes through a keypoint, which
is the point in a valley where the steepness of the hillside changes from
convex (steeper near the top) to concave (flattening near the valley floor).
This is typically found partway down the shoulder of a valley. The keyline
extends this contour outward across the adjacent ridges. Cultivation parallel
to and below the keyline causes the soil surface to gradually drift moisture
from the wetter valley toward the drier ridges, counteracting the natural
tendency for water to concentrate in valleys and leave ridges parched.

Mollison emphasizes that keyline geometry applies to any landform with
sufficient slope to generate runoff but gentle enough for agricultural use.
The system works on slopes from 1 to 20 percent, with greatest effect in
the 3 to 10 percent range. Understanding the relationship between keypoints,
keylines, and the valley-ridge sequence is essential before any earthworks
are planned or any cultivation pattern is established.

## Keyline Cultivation

### Pattern Cultivation

Keyline cultivation follows a specific geometric pattern. Below the keyline,
cultivation is parallel to the keyline itself, which means plow lines gradually
drift downhill as they extend from the valley across the ridge. Above the
keyline, cultivation follows the contour, which is parallel to the natural
land shape. This combination creates a pattern that prevents erosion, improves
water infiltration, and moves soil moisture laterally from wet zones to dry
zones. The result is progressive soil development and increasing fertility
over time without the need for imported fertilizers.

### Subsoiling and Deep Ripping

Keyline cultivation typically uses a chisel plow or subsoiler rather than a
moldboard plow. The chisel plow fractures compacted subsoil layers to a depth
of 30 to 60 centimeters without inverting the soil profile. This deep ripping
improves water infiltration, root penetration, and soil aeration while
preserving surface soil structure and microbiology. Mollison recommends
cultivating on the contour or keyline pattern only when soil moisture is
adequate but not saturated, to avoid compaction and structural damage.

### Soil Building Effects

Repeated keyline cultivation progressively builds deeper, more fertile topsoil.
The increased water infiltration and even moisture distribution encourage
deeper root growth, greater organic matter incorporation, and more active
soil biology. Over years of keyline management, the soil profile deepens,
water-holding capacity increases, and the landscape becomes more resilient
to both drought and flood. These soil-building effects are foundational to
the success of [[dam-construction]] to create a complete on-farm water
storage and distribution network. See [[mollison-designers-roof-water-catchment]] and broader property water planning.

### Spillways and Overflow

Every earthwork must have a safe overflow path to prevent structural failure
during extreme rainfall events. Keyline dams and swales are designed with
spillways at the natural contour, directing overflow water along predetermined
paths to the next storage or treatment element in the sequence. Mollison
stresses that earthworks must be designed for worst-case storm events, with
spillways sized to handle maximum expected flows without erosion or dam
failure. This conservative approach to overflow design is essential for the
long-term safety and viability of any keyline system.

## Summary

Keyline land shaping uses the natural geometry of valleys and ridges to design
cultivation patterns and earthworks that distribute water evenly across the
landscape, forming a core element of [[permaculture]].
By identifying keypoints and keylines, cultivating on specific
patterns, and building [[dam-construction|dams]], and spillways in relation to these lines,
the designer creates a self-fertilizing, drought-resilient landscape. Keyline
is foundational to broadacre permaculture design and connects water management
to soil building, forestry, and productive agriculture. For related water storage
options, see [[backyard-pond-building]].
