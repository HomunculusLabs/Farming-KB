# Falconer Mushroom Growing House Design

While Falconer's cellar management is covered separately, Chapters III
through V of his treatise detail three additional types of above-ground
growing structures: dedicated mushroom houses, sheds, and greenhouses.

## Mushroom Houses (Chapter III)

A mushroom house was a building erected purposely for mushroom culture,
wholly or partly above ground, of wood, brick, or stone, extending to
any desired dimensions.

### Design Principles

Mushrooms in houses were a winter crop, impatient of sudden temperature
changes and hot or arid atmosphere. Key design requirements:

- **Site**: Warm and well-sheltered in winter, to exploit natural warmth
  and minimize artificial heat.
- **Entrance**: From an adjoining building or through a south-side porch,
  to guard against cold drafts when doors opened.
- **Doors**: All outside doors should be double and tightly packed in
  winter.
- **Windows**: Side window ventilators were useful in early season and
  summer but should be double and tightly packed in winter.
- **Walls**: Hollow brick, or double wood -- "built as if for an ice
  house." Bank with earth, tree leaves, or strawy manure in winter.
- **Floor**: Perfectly dry (well-drained); earthen, wood, or cement were
  all acceptable.
- **Roof**: Double and always sloping, never flat. A flat ceiling under a
  single roof condensed moisture, which dripped onto beds. A sloping
  interior roof directed condensation down to the walls. Mrs. C.J.
  Osborne's house at Mamaroneck, N.Y. illustrated this: a flat ceiling
  caused so much drip that her gardener replaced it with a sloping one.
- **Interior arrangement**: Beds alongside walls and, if room permitted,
  along the middle. Shelves in tiers. In cold buildings, shelf-beds
  should be boxed off about 2 inches from walls.
- **Height**: High houses were a mistake. Artificial heat to maintain 55
  degrees in midwinter would be too parching. A second shelf was
  inconvenient; a third or fourth was impractical.

### Private Garden Integration

In large private gardens, mushroom houses were built against the
north-facing wall of a greenhouse, gaining warmth from the wall and
heat from 1-2 hot-water pipes from the greenhouse system. Entry was
through the glass house, exempting the crop from frosty outside air.

### Henshaw's Mushroom House (Staten Island)

Nine feet wide, sixty feet long. One side brick wall, the other double
boarded. Tin roof with three sashes (2 x 5 feet each) for light. Doors
at each end for convenient access without disturbing bearing beds. In
winter, roof covered with salt hay for equable temperature and to prevent
moisture condensation. Earthen floor, entirely covered with beds (no
shelves or walks). A 4-inch hot-water pipe ran the full length about 2
feet from the floor to maintain 60 degrees. A greenhouse on the other
side of the brick wall provided supplementary warmth. The house was
divided into three equal beds for succession: one made in fall for
December bearing, then at monthly intervals.

## Sheds (Chapter IV)

Any snug, warm shed could serve as a mushroom house, provided the floor
was dry and roof water-tight. Close sheds (tool-house, carriage-house)
were better than open sheds, but even a south-side-open shed could work.

### Temperature Considerations

Bed interior should be about 60 degrees during bearing; surface at least
45-50 degrees. Bemycelium, mycelium tended to rest and crops stagnated.
Open sheds could not maintain this temperature in hard frost without
more trouble than the crop was worth.

### Bed Construction in Sheds

- Beds made on the floor, to one side, flat or rounded against shed walls.
- Bank outside with litter, leaves, or earth to exclude frost.
- Deeper beds (15-20 inches) than in cellars or warm houses.
- Shelf beds not recommended in unheated sheds -- too difficult to keep
  warm in winter.

### Heated Sheds (Florists' Potting Sheds)

Excellent places for mushroom beds under the benches, covered with thick
calico, plant-protecting cloth, or light wooden shutters to exclude cold
currents and sudden atmospheric changes. Treatment differed from regular
mushroom houses because of frequent going in and out causing drafts and
temperature fluctuations.

## Greenhouses (Chapter V)

Any greenhouse could accommodate mushrooms -- fruit house, flower house,
or vegetable house. Cool greenhouses (45-50 degrees minimum) were better
than hothouses (60-70 degrees night, rising 10-20 degrees by day). It
was easy to keep a mushroom bed warm in a cool house; difficult to keep
one cool enough in a hothouse.

### Under Greenhouse Benches

Mushrooms grew to perfection in the nearly worthless spaces under stages
where nothing else grew well. Beds were one board deep (8-10 inches of
short, fresh manure), spawned, cased with soil, and covered with straw
litter to prevent drying. About the time young mushrooms appeared, the
mulching was replaced with shutters or calico curtains.

### On Greenhouse Benches

Beds on the benches were one board deep. Their liability to temperature
fluctuations and drying was Falconer's principal objection. In midwinter
they were acceptable, but as spring advanced and sun intensity increased,
susceptibility to drying also increased.

### In Frames Inside Greenhouses

Mr. J.G. Gardner's method at Rancocas Farm: mushroom beds in hotbed
frames inside orchard (peach and nectarine) greenhouses. By ventilating
or keeping close, covering or uncovering, shading or unshading, he
controlled both temperature and light for the mushroom beds independently

### In Rose Houses

Mushrooms were sometimes grown under rose benches. A concern was ammonia
rising from the mushroom beds affecting the rose crop, and drip from
watering roses falling onto mushroom beds.
