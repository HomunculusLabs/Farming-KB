    11|# Falconer Mushroom Growing House Design
    12|
    13|While Falconer's cellar management is covered separately, Chapters III
    14|through V of his treatise detail three additional types of above-ground
    15|growing structures: dedicated mushroom houses, sheds, and greenhouses.
    16|
    17|## Mushroom Houses (Chapter III)
    18|
    19|A mushroom house was a building erected purposely for mushroom culture,
    20|wholly or partly above ground, of wood, brick, or stone, extending to
    21|any desired dimensions.
    22|
    23|### Design Principles
    24|
    25|Mushrooms in houses were a winter crop, impatient of sudden temperature
    26|changes and hot or arid atmosphere. Key design requirements:
    27|
    28|- **Site**: Warm and well-sheltered in winter, to exploit natural warmth
    29|  and minimize artificial heat.
    30|- **Entrance**: From an adjoining building or through a south-side porch,
    31|  to guard against cold drafts when doors opened.
    32|- **Doors**: All outside doors should be double and tightly packed in
    33|  winter.
    34|- **Windows**: Side window ventilators were useful in early season and
    35|  summer but should be double and tightly packed in winter.
    36|- **Walls**: Hollow brick, or double wood -- "built as if for an ice
    37|  house." Bank with earth, tree leaves, or strawy manure in winter.
    38|- **Floor**: Perfectly dry (well-drained); earthen, wood, or cement were
    39|  all acceptable.
    40|- **Roof**: Double and always sloping, never flat. A flat ceiling under a
    41|  single roof condensed moisture, which dripped onto beds. A sloping
    42|  interior roof directed condensation down to the walls. Mrs. C.J.
    43|  Osborne's house at Mamaroneck, N.Y. illustrated this: a flat ceiling
    44|  caused so much drip that her gardener replaced it with a sloping one.
    45|- **Interior arrangement**: Beds alongside walls and, if room permitted,
    46|  along the middle. Shelves in tiers. In cold buildings, shelf-beds
    47|  should be boxed off about 2 inches from walls.
    48|- **Height**: High houses were a mistake. Artificial heat to maintain 55
    49|  degrees in midwinter would be too parching. A second shelf was
    50|  inconvenient; a third or fourth was impractical.
    51|
    52|### Private Garden Integration
    53|
    54|In large private gardens, mushroom houses were built against the
    55|north-facing wall of a greenhouse, gaining warmth from the wall and
    56|heat from 1-2 hot-water pipes from the greenhouse system. Entry was
    57|through the glass house, exempting the crop from frosty outside air.
    58|
    59|### Henshaw's Mushroom House (Staten Island)
    60|
    61|Nine feet wide, sixty feet long. One side brick wall, the other double
    62|boarded. Tin roof with three sashes (2 x 5 feet each) for light. Doors
    63|at each end for convenient access without disturbing bearing beds. In
    64|winter, roof covered with salt hay for equable temperature and to prevent
    65|moisture condensation. Earthen floor, entirely covered with beds (no
    66|shelves or walks). A 4-inch hot-water pipe ran the full length about 2
    67|feet from the floor to maintain 60 degrees. A greenhouse on the other
    68|side of the brick wall provided supplementary warmth. The house was
    69|divided into three equal beds for succession: one made in fall for
    70|December bearing, then at monthly intervals.
    71|
    72|## Sheds (Chapter IV)
    73|
    74|Any snug, warm shed could serve as a mushroom house, provided the floor
    75|was dry and roof water-tight. Close sheds (tool-house, carriage-house)
    76|were better than open sheds, but even a south-side-open shed could work.
    77|
    78|### Temperature Considerations
    79|
    80|Bed interior should be about 60 degrees during bearing; surface at least
    81|45-50 degrees. Below this, mycelium tended to rest and crops stagnated.
    82|Open sheds could not maintain this temperature in hard frost without
    83|more trouble than the crop was worth.
    84|
    85|### Bed Construction in Sheds
    86|
    87|- Beds made on the floor, to one side, flat or rounded against shed walls.
    88|- Bank outside with litter, leaves, or earth to exclude frost.
    89|- Deeper beds (15-20 inches) than in cellars or warm houses.
    90|- Shelf beds not recommended in unheated sheds -- too difficult to keep
    91|  warm in winter.
    92|
    93|### Heated Sheds (Florists' Potting Sheds)
    94|
    95|Excellent places for mushroom beds under the benches, covered with thick
    96|calico, plant-protecting cloth, or light wooden shutters to exclude cold
    97|currents and sudden atmospheric changes. Treatment differed from regular
    98|mushroom houses because of frequent going in and out causing drafts and
    99|temperature fluctuations.
   100|
   101|## Greenhouses (Chapter V)
   102|
   103|Any greenhouse could accommodate mushrooms -- fruit house, flower house,
   104|or vegetable house. Cool greenhouses (45-50 degrees minimum) were better
   105|than hothouses (60-70 degrees night, rising 10-20 degrees by day). It
   106|was easy to keep a mushroom bed warm in a cool house; difficult to keep
   107|one cool enough in a hothouse.
   108|
   109|### Under Greenhouse Benches
   110|
   111|Mushrooms grew to perfection in the nearly worthless spaces under stages
   112|where nothing else grew well. Beds were one board deep (8-10 inches of
   113|short, fresh manure), spawned, cased with soil, and covered with straw
   114|litter to prevent drying. About the time young mushrooms appeared, the
   115|mulching was replaced with shutters or calico curtains.
   116|
   117|### On Greenhouse Benches
   118|
   119|Beds on the benches were one board deep. Their liability to temperature
   120|fluctuations and drying was Falconer's principal objection. In midwinter
   121|they were acceptable, but as spring advanced and sun intensity increased,
   122|susceptibility to drying also increased.
   123|
   124|### In Frames Inside Greenhouses
   125|
   126|Mr. J.G. Gardner's method at Rancocas Farm: mushroom beds in hotbed
   127|frames inside orchard (peach and nectarine) greenhouses. By ventilating
   128|or keeping close, covering or uncovering, shading or unshading, he
   129|controlled both temperature and light for the mushroom beds independently
   130|of the greenhouse atmosphere. This enabled mushroom cultivation in both
   131|cold and warm greenhouses.
   132|
   133|### In Rose Houses
   134|
   135|Mushrooms were sometimes grown under rose benches. A concern was ammonia
   136|rising from the mushroom beds affecting the rose crop, and drip from
   137|watering roses falling onto mushroom beds.
   138|
   139|## Unheated Structures
   140|
   141|An unheated mushroom house was to be regarded as a shed and treated
   142|similarly. The critical distinction was whether artificial heat was
   143|available to maintain the 55-57 degree optimum during the bearing period.
