# Siderophore Production for Iron Acquisition in [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]

Iron is an essential element for nearly all forms of life, yet its
bioavailability in aerobic environments is extremely limited. At pH 7, the
maximum concentration of free ferric iron in aqueous solution is only 10 to the
power of negative 17 molar due to precipitation as oxide-hydroxide polymers.
Under iron-limiting conditions, most bacteria and fungi excrete siderophores as
chelating agents that form soluble complexes with Fe(III) having very high
formation constants, thereby solubilizing ferric iron for uptake.

## What Are Siderophores?

Siderophores are low-molecular-weight organic compounds produced by
microorganisms under iron-limiting conditions. They function as highly specific
iron chelators, binding ferric iron with extraordinary affinity to make it
available for cellular uptake. The term derives from Greek: sideros (iron) and
phore (carrier). Fungal siderophores represent a diverse array of chemical
structures that vary across taxonomic groups.

## Conditions for Siderophore Production

Siderophore production is stimulated by lowering the external concentration of
ferric iron. Under some circumstances, not only omission of iron compounds but
deferration of the nutrient medium may be required. This can be achieved by
adding excess Chelex 100 resin to the medium, shaking for 15 minutes, then
filtering to remove the resin before sterilization.

To study mycorrhizal siderophore release, fungi must be grown in a nutrient
medium adequate not only for fungal growth but, most importantly, for maximum
siderophore release. Since nutrient requirements vary strongly among
mycorrhizal fungi, medium composition must be modified for each species, with
enhancement possible through addition of specific precursors for biosynthesis.

## Classification of Fungal Siderophores

Fungal siderophores fall into several structural categories. The two main
groups found in mycorrhizal fungi are hydroxamate siderophores and
polycarboxylate-type siderophores.

### Hydroxamate Siderophores

Hydroxamates represent the predominant siderophore type in mycorrhizal fungi.
They are further subdivided into:

**Ferrichromes:** Peptidic siderophores with hexa- or heptapeptide rings. Two
subgroups exist: those with N-acetyl side chains varying in amino acid
residues (ferrichrome, ferricrocin, ferrichrome C, tetraglycylferrichrome),
and those with a common ferrichrysin peptide ring but varying N-acyl side
chains (ferrirubin, ferrirhodin, ferrichrome A).

**Coprogens and Fusarinines:** Ester-containing hydroxamates including
coprogen, neocoprogen I and II, fusigen, and triacetylfusarinine C
(triacetylfusigen). These represent the other major group of hydroxamate
siderophores produced by fungi.

### Rhizoferrins

Rhizoferrins are citrate-containing siderophores that do not belong to the
hydroxamates. Originally isolated from Zygomycetes, it remains an open
question whether some mycorrhizal fungi also produce polycarboxylate-type
siderophores in addition to hydroxamates.

## Siderophores in Major Mycorrhizal Groups

Research has identified the main siderophores for several [[mycorrhizal-types]],
though significant knowledge gaps remain:

**Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi** produce ferricrocin or fusigen as their main
siderophores. These ascomycetes associate with plants in the Ericaceae family
growing in acidic, nutrient-poor soils where iron chemistry is particularly
challenging.

**Ectomycorrhizal fungi:** Ferricrocin is the main siderophore of the
widespread [[cenococcum-geophilum]], and has also been demonstrated in the
extraradical mycelium of [[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]], a basidiomycetous
ectomycorrhizal fungus. Wilcoxina species forming ectendomycorrhizae also
produce ferricrocin.

**[[dark-septate-endophytes]]:** phialocephala fortinii, a common dark septate
root endophyte, produces ferricrocin as its main siderophore.

**Orchidaceous mycorrhizal fungi:** A novel hydroxamate siderophore called
basidiochrome has been identified as the main iron-chelating agent released
by a range of orchid mycorrhizal fungi including Ceratobasidium and
Rhizoctonia species.

**[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (Glomeromycota):** It is still unknown whether
and what kind of siderophores are released by this widespread mycorrhizal
group, representing one of the most significant gaps in current knowledge.

## Detection and Analysis Methods

### Chrome Azurol S (CAS) Assay

The CAS assay is the most widely used method for siderophore detection. It
exploits the high formation constant of siderophores for ferric iron: strong
chelators remove iron from the blue ternary complex of chrome azurol S-iron
(III)-detergent complex, decolorizing the solution. The assay works both in
solution and on agar plates for detecting siderophore excretion.

### HPLC Separation

Hydroxamate siderophores can be separated by HPLC using reversed-phase columns
with acetonitrile/water gradients plus trifluoroacetic acid or formic acid.
Detection uses wavelengths of 220 nm or the hydroxamate-specific 435 nm.
Lipophilic siderophores like ferrichrome A, ferrirubin, and triacetylfusarinine
C are generally easy to separate and identify by retention time.

### Mass Spectrometry and NMR

After HPLC purification, identification proceeds through fast atom
bombardment or ion spray mass spectrometry. Fourier transform ion cyclotron
resonance mass spectrometry is particularly useful for identifying novel
siderophores. Final structural elucidation requires proton and carbon-13 NMR
spectroscopy, with 2D-NMR techniques (HSQC, HMBC, COSY, NOESY) determining
connectivity of siderophore constituents.

## Ecological Significance

Siderophore production gives mycorrhizal fungi a competitive advantage in
iron-limited soils. By solubilizing iron that would otherwise be unavailable,
[[mycorrhizal-siderophores]] may enhance iron nutrition not only for the fungus
but potentially for the host plant. The ecological significance extends to
microbial competition, as siderophores can influence community composition
by sequestering iron from competing microorganisms.
