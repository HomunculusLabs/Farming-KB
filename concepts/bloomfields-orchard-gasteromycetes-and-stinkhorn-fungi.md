# Gasteromycetes and Stinkhorn Fungi

## Overview

The gasteromycetes are a section of basidiomycete fungi
defined by their production of spores inside enclosed
fruiting bodies rather than on exposed gills or pores.

This group encompasses some of the most bizarre organisms
in the fungal kingdom: phallic stinkhorns erupting from
buried eggs, cage fungi unfolding like geometric sculptures,
puffballs emitting clouds of spores, and bird's nest fungi
splashing spore packets onto grass blades.

## What Are Gasteromycetes?

The term derives from Greek meaning stomach fungi,
referring to internal spore formation. Unlike typical
mushrooms whose basidia are exposed on gills, tubes,
or spines, gasteromycetes produce spores internally.

The group is polyphyletic, not a natural taxonomic unit.
Different lineages independently evolved enclosed
fruiting bodies from mushroom-forming ancestors.

Genetic data show false truffles are closely related
to boletes. Bolete fertile tubes develop beneath the
soil and become exposed as the mushroom expands its cap.
Very few genes specify emergence, and when these mutate,
the bolete remains buried.

The gradual loss of unfolding capacity results in spore
production within enclosed tissues, precluding the
water-dependent catapult mechanism. This enables
colonization of much drier habitats including sandy
soils and deserts.

## Stinkhorns: Phallus and Relatives

### Phallus Impudicus

The species name means the shameless penis. The fungus
begins as a white egg somewhat larger than a golf ball,
buried in soil or leaf litter, attached by a branched
umbilical cord.

Inside, the entire fruiting body exists prefabricated:
a hollow central receptacle surrounded by green-black
spore mass called the gleba, encased in clear jelly
veiled with white skin.

The eggs have cold, jelly-filled skins and a smell of
dead deer with a hint of sweetness and even semen.
The erection begins at night and completes by sunrise.

The spongy receptacle absorbs water osmotically,
expanding and rupturing the egg skin. Jelly lubricates
the extending shaft while holding the spore mass in
place at the tip.

The mature fruiting body stands 15 centimeters or
taller, with a glistening head coated in dark slime
circled by a raised ring with a small hole at its tip.

### Chemical Attraction

The slime contains hydrogen sulfide, formaldehyde,
methylmercaptan, and unique phallic acids mimicking
rotting flesh. Flies swarm to the head, and slugs
glide 6 meters or more for the reward.

Within hours the head is cleaned to bare white
receptacle tissue and the shaft wilts. Spores pass
through insect digestive tracts and are deposited
in dung. Like humans, stinkhorns are here because
they are very good at making copies of themselves.

### Mechanics of the Erection

The receptacle is built from corrugated hyphae stretched
into a weft of filaments. Most volume is air, but the
structure is maintained by pressurized water within
hyphae, analogous to the mammalian penis maintained by
blood pressure.

The similarity ends there: penile pressure is muscular
while stinkhorn pressure is osmotic, akin to water
soaking into a dry sponge. Small changes in developmental
details account for variety among stinkhorns and cage
fungi.

Weakening tissue along four or five tracks would cause
the shaft to split into arms, potentially transforming
a Phallus into an Anthurus. Natural selection may have
sculpted existing species in as little as a few million
years.

### Cultural History

Stinkhorns featured in Pliny the Elder's Natural History
in the first century A.D. John Gerard depicted them in his
seventeenth-century herbal as Pricke Mushrum.

Charles Darwin's daughter Etty mounted a personal crusade
with gloves and a pointed stick, burning specimens in
secret to protect her servants' moral purity. For
Victorians obsessed with sex, stinkhorns were too much
to bear.

### Related Phallic Species

About thirty phallic species are described. The dog
stinkhorn Mutinus caninus is smaller with a pink shaft
and lacks the swollen head. Dictyophora species feature
a lacy crinoline beneath the head that may serve as a
ladder for wingless insects.

In China, Dictyophora eggs are sold as delicacies and
marketed as aphrodisiacs. Elio Schaechter reported
enjoying cream-filled rings cut from expanded
receptacles, though the tea brewed from Cordyceps is
no Earl Grey.

## Cage Fungi

### Clathrus

Clathrus forms a spherical cage with spores on the inner
bar surfaces. The cage expands from an egg through osmotic
pressurization into an open polyhedral structure. Flies
are usual dispersal vectors, though ants and stingless
bees have been observed on some species.

### Anthurus

Anthurus archeri separates into four or more bright orange
arms curling back over the egg to create a star shape. Arms
are smeared with spores on their inner surface.

Time-lapse video of hatching is shocking: the livid arms
simulate the agonized contortions of a horribly injured
lobster. There is nothing comparable in nature.

### Ileodictyon

The intestinal net fungus grows in New Zealand and
Australia. The Maori accorded it nine names and barbecued
its eggs. The mature white cage detaches from its papery
skin and is blown about by wind, dispersing spores from
its smeared interior.

The Maori rejected the mature fruiting bodies as the
feces of ghosts or of the stars. The polyhedral structure
shares geometry with Buckminster Fuller's geodesic domes
and buckminsterfullerene carbon molecules, reflecting the
strength of lightweight polyhedral forms.

### Laternea

Laternea triscapa produces long arms fused at their tips,
creating a vaulted chamber with a reeking lantern of
spores dangling inside.

## Puffballs and Earth-Stars

Puffballs and earth-balls expel spores through an apical
pore in response to disturbance. A single giant puffball
produces an estimated 7 trillion spores, the most fecund
output of any organism.

Earth-stars split their outer layer into a star, elevating
the inner spore sac above the ground boundary layer for
improved dispersal. After expansion, many function
perfectly during drought because their dispersal does
not depend on the water-dependent catapult mechanism.

## Bird's Nest Fungi

Bird's nest fungi produce tiny champagne-flute-shaped
fruiting bodies a few millimeters tall, containing spore
packets called peridioles. Raindrops splash peridioles
onto grass blades.

Herbivores consume contaminated vegetation, and spores
pass through the digestive tract to be deposited in warm
manure, an ideal growth medium. Unsuccessful spores
dehydrate and die; fortunate ones complete a two-stage
dispersal mechanism through an animal gut.

## Sphaerobolus: The Artillery Fungus

Sphaerobolus grows on wood mulch and launches a black ball
of spores using a cup-shaped fruiting body operating as a
tiny trampoline. The inner cup suddenly inverts, propelling
the spore ball several meters.

The balls adhere with incredible tenacity to smooth
surfaces, ruining car paintwork. Even vigorous cleaning
leaves permanent spots. Like bird's nest fungi, the
fungus is adapted for passage through an herbivore gut,
but its intended targets are grass blades rather than
parked cars.

## Evolutionary Significance

The diversity of gasteromycete forms likely evolved rapidly
through small modifications in developmental gene
expression. Different dispersal vectors drove different
solutions.

Fly-attracted stinkhorns evolved scents paralleling
carrion flowers like Amorphophallus titanum. Wind-
dispersed puffballs invested in massive spore production.
Bird's nest fungi evolved two-stage dispersal through
animal guts.

The loss of the catapult mechanism enables colonization
of drier habitats. The evolutionary history of truffles
illustrates this trajectory. Beginning 300 million years
ago with disc-shaped apothecia on forest floors, successive
modifications produced partially buried fruiting bodies,
then fully subterranean forms attractive to mammals whose
scent-producing pathways evolved to mimic mammalian sexual
attractants.

## Fossil Record

Fossilized hyphae of basidiomycetes date to the Permian
period, 290 million years ago. Exquisitely preserved
mushrooms found in Cretaceous amber from New Jersey are
more than 90 million years old yet instantly recognizable.

Electron microscopy of spores in the amber reveals the
hilar appendix, the decisive signature of the catapult
mechanism. While dinosaurs trotted past and pterosaurs
wheeled, the dew of heaven was beading on a mushroom.
