# Community Finance and Commonwork

Community finance and [[commonwork]] represent core [[permaculture-principles]] for organizing human economies at the local and bioregional scale. Drawing on [[bill-mollison]]'s analysis in *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual*, these concepts address how small communities can create resilient, equitable financial systems and diverse, fulfilling livelihood structures that align with permaculture's three ethics: earth care, people care, and fair share.

## The Ethics of Money

Mollison is emphatic that money itself is not intrinsically evil. What is destructive is the **accumulation and exploitation** of wealth — the hoarding of resources beyond productive use and the extraction of surplus from others' labor. This distinction is critical: permaculture does not reject exchange or medium-of-value systems, but it does reject financial systems that concentrate capital, externalize costs, and create dependency.

### The Fragility of Fiscal Systems

Conventional fiscal systems provide a false sense of security. Historical analysis shows that monetary systems tend to **collapse approximately every 40 years**, driven by cycles of inflation, speculation, and greed. These periodic crises devastate communities that have organized their livelihoods entirely around cash economies, while those with diversified, land-based, and community-based systems fare far better. The lesson for [[bill-mollison]] is clear: build economic resilience through diversity of systems — [[local-currencies]], barter networks, mutual credit, and direct exchange — rather than dependence on a single fragile currency controlled by distant institutions.

### Money as a Substitute for Fair Dealing

In small, unified, face-to-face groups, finance has traditionally been managed through **exchanges, gifts, obligations, and feasts** — mechanisms that embed economic relationships within social relationships and ecological limits. Money, in this view, arose as societies became more mobile and anonymous, replacing direct accountability with abstract tokens. The permaculture approach seeks to restore some of this direct accountability by favoring local exchange systems, mutual credit, and community-managed funds over distant, abstract financial instruments.

## Principles of Community Finance

### No Free Machines or Free Lunches

A foundational principle in Mollison's framework is that **nothing is free**. Every piece of equipment, every infrastructure improvement, and every service has real costs in energy, materials, and labor. The essentials that a community must secure through its own effort and capital include:

- **Land planning** — thoughtful design of settlement and production systems
- **Shelter** — adequate housing for all members
- **Capital base** — shared resources, tools, and infrastructure
- **Livelihoods** — meaningful, productive work for every person

When communities understand that resources must be earned and maintained, they develop greater care for shared assets and more honest accounting of costs.

### Group Funds and Infrastructure Maintenance

Communities benefit from establishing a **group fund** dedicated to maintaining shared infrastructure — roads, fences, water systems, common buildings. Critically, contributions to this fund need not be exclusively monetary. Members may **donate work in lieu of money**, recognizing that skilled labor is often more valuable than cash. This principle keeps the system accessible to those with limited financial means but abundant willingness to contribute, and it values diverse forms of productivity.

### Shared Liability and Democratic Equipment Decisions

One of the most important structural principles for community finance is that **all who vote for equipment must be equally liable to pay for it**. This prevents the classic failure mode of communal systems, where a few enthusiastic individuals advocate for costly purchases while the broader group bears the financial risk without having consented. When liability is shared proportionally with decision-making power, proposals are evaluated more carefully and fewer imprudent investments are made.

Communal systems frequently fail, Mollison observes, when only a small number of people are legally liable for capital risk while the benefits are distributed widely. This mismatch creates resentment on one side and freeloading on the other. The solution is strict alignment of **benefit, decision-making, and liability**.

### The Law of Return Applied to Finance

The [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] of "everything must pay" extends directly into community finance through two linked rules:

- **Proposers pay capital** — those who advocate for a purchase or project bear the initial capital cost or a significant share of it
- **Users pay costs** — ongoing operational and maintenance costs fall on those who actually use the resource

This is the **law of return** applied to economics: every input of energy or capital must yield a return that sustains the system. When costs are externalized or hidden, the law of return is violated and the system degrades.

### Tool Hire and Capital Recovery

When tools or equipment are made available for hire within a community, Mollison recommends that hire rates should be set so that the tool **repays its full cost over approximately half its working life**. This ensures that the capital is recovered while the tool still has significant useful life remaining, providing a buffer against unexpected breakdowns and generating surplus that can be reinvested in new equipment or maintenance.

## Commonwork: Diverse Livelihoods

### The Concept of Commonwork

"Commonwork" is Mollison's term for a system in which individuals hold **varied and rotating roles** within their community. Rather than specializing in a single occupation — as industrial monoculture demands — a person in a commonwork system might serve as a banker one day, a forester the next, a bee-keeper on another, and a writer, printer, or carpenter on still others. This mirrors ecological diversity: just as polycultures are more resilient than monocultures, diverse skill portfolios create more resilient individuals and communities.

Commonwork stands in direct opposition to the industrial model of work, in which trade unions and monoculture industries keep people bound to narrow, repetitive roles. While Mollison acknowledges the historical necessity of trade unions in protecting workers, he argues that the ideal is **self-defined work** — where people choose their own mix of activities based on their skills, interests, and community needs.

### Self-Employment and Cooperative Membership

Mollison identifies **self-employed people and community work cooperative members** as those who are truly self-defined in their labor. They choose what to do, when to do it, and how to do it, subject only to the genuine needs of their community and the constraints of ecological systems. This autonomy is not selfish individualism — it is exercised within a web of mutual obligation and shared responsibility.

## The Problem of Unemployment

Mollison describes unemployment as an **obscene concept** — not because people should not rest, but because there is enormous, urgent work to be done. The planet faces vast ecological damage that requires hands-on restoration: reforestation, soil rehabilitation, water cycle repair, ecosystem recovery, and the construction of sustainable infrastructure. In a world with so much necessary work, the existence of widespread unemployment represents a profound failure of economic design, not a lack of things to do.

He identifies a **second unhappy state**: being employed but not free to use one's initiative. This describes the condition of most wage workers in industrial economies — technically occupied, but alienated from meaningful decision-making about their work. Neither unemployment nor constrained employment represents a permaculture ideal. The goal is **self-directed, purposeful, productive activity** that benefits both the individual and the broader ecological community.

## Balanced Life and Holistic Function

### Three Dimensions of Human Activity

A balanced human life, Mollison argues, should engage **three fundamental areas**:

- **Physical exertion** — working with the body, building, growing, crafting
- **Intellectual pursuits** — learning, designing, teaching, analyzing
- **Emotional and sensual areas** — art, music, relationships, appreciation of beauty

Industrial work systems typically privilege one dimension (usually physical repetition or narrow intellectual specialization) while neglecting the others. Commonwork, by contrast, naturally distributes activity across all three dimensions, since the variety of roles encompasses manual, mental, and creative work.

### Indigenous Models: Central Australian Tribes

Mollison draws on the example of **Central Australian Aboriginal tribes**, who organize their societies into three complementary function groups:

- **Intellectual/orchestrator** — those who plan, coordinate, and maintain knowledge systems
- **Physical/activity** — those who carry out hunting, building, and physical production
- **Emotional/appreciation** — those who tend to ceremony, art, storytelling, and social cohesion

These are not castes in the rigid sense but functional specializations that work in dynamic balance. Each group depends on the others, and prestige is distributed across all three rather than concentrated in a single domain. This model provides a template for permaculture communities seeking to value diverse forms of contribution equally.

## Developing Wealth Like Landscapes

Mollison proposes that communities should **develop wealth in the same way they develop landscapes**. This means applying [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]] to economic systems:

- **Conservation of energy** — minimize waste, cycle resources, use efficient processes
- **Procreative assets** — invest in resources that generate ongoing yields (fruit trees, water systems, skills, tools)
- **Reducing degenerative assets** — divest from systems that consume more than they produce (polluting industries, extractive practices, debt-dependent structures)

Just as a well-designed [[holzer-permaculture-landscape-design]] becomes more productive and self-sustaining over time, a well-designed community financial system should generate increasing abundance with decreasing external input. The goal is not endless growth in monetary terms, but **deepening resilience, diversity, and self-reliance**.

## Key Principles Summary

- Money is a tool, not an end — accumulation and exploitation are the real dangers
- Fiscal systems are historically fragile; build redundancy through diverse [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]]
- All who benefit from and decide on shared resources must share liability for their costs
- Tools should recover their capital cost within half their working life
- Unemployment is a design failure, not a natural condition — there is essential work to be done everywhere
- Commonwork provides diverse, self-directed livelihoods that mirror ecological polyculture
- A balanced life engages physical, intellectual, and emotional dimensions of human experience
- Wealth should be developed like a landscape: conserving energy, building procreative assets, and reducing degenerative ones
- Local exchange, gift economies, and mutual obligation create more accountable financial systems than abstract currency
- Community finance succeeds when benefits, decisions, and liabilities are aligned for every member
