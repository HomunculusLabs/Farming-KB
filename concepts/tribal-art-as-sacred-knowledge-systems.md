## Tribal Art as Sacred Knowledge Systems

## Overview

Tribal art—in the forms of song, dance, sculptural or painted objects, and designs—served fundamentally as a public and continuously renewed mnemonic system for encoding ecological, astronomical, navigational, and social knowledge. The decorative function was incidental to the educational and therefore sacred information function. Understanding this reframes our relationship to both indigenous art and modern design practice.

## Art as Information Storage

Most (if not all) tribal art was created for quite specific practical ends. The primary purposes included:

- **Encoding ecological calendars** — when to plant, harvest, migrate, or perform ceremonies
- **Recording navigational data** — ocean currents, star positions, headland features
- **Storing astronomical knowledge** — lunar and solar cycles, drought/flood predictions
- **Documenting social structures** — caste systems, genetic streaming, occupational hierarchies
- **Preserving behavioral knowledge** — interspecies communication, pulser patterns, medicinal uses

Apparently simple spiral or linear designs can combine thousands of bits of information in a single, deceptively simple pattern. What appears to modern eyes as "decoration" is often a compressed encyclopedia of survival-critical knowledge.

## The Degradation of Sacred Knowledge

Tribal art has been systematically destroyed and devalued through several mechanisms:

### Replacement by Centralized Belief Systems
Sacred calendars were melted down to bullion or objets d"art. Tribal art hidden in museum storerooms represents a form of cultural genocide—removing knowledge from its living context and trivializing objects to mere decorations or loot.

### Fanatic Destruction
Most tribal art has been burnt, looted, destroyed, and broken by invading belief systems. This destruction was carried out by those seeking secret power rather than open knowledge, or by those who were merely destructive. Book-burning and image-breaking is the reaction of the alienated or intellectually-deprived to the accumulated wisdom of their ancestors.

### Modern Plagiarism
Much modern "motif" art plagiarises ancient origins but no longer carries educational or sacred function. Entertainment and decoration are valid functions, but they are minor compared to the original knowledge-encoding purpose.

### The Loss of Pattern Education
Modern monoculture—based on the jingles of advertisements, purely decorative patterns, and education divorced from relevant long-term observation of the natural world—has largely lost the capacity for pattern-based knowledge transmission.

## Patterns of Society

Mollison and his students studied social patterns in Tasmania from 1969 to 1974, revealing structured hierarchies with specific characteristics:

### The "Boat" Pattern of Social Hierarchy
Based on a sample of approximately 35,000 people across the army, multinational companies, churches, and small businesses, a consistent hierarchical pattern emerged with seldom more than seven major occupational levels:

1. **Low Occupational (Resource) Area** — Manual and unskilled urban workers: general dearth of material resources, low status, part-time occupations, remarkable preponderance of male births (~140 males per 100 females), large families, serial polyandry common

2. **Central "Middle Class"** — Adequate resources, nine-to-five jobs, some tenure, normal birth ratio (104:100), mixed white-collar and skilled technical workers, serial monogamy typical

3. **Upper Levels** — Few people, extensive resources, flexible self-set times, high proportion of female children (~100 females to 70 males), urban professionals and managers, small families, effective polygyny

4. **Very High Levels** — Executive directors and landed nobility, variable family sizes with female preponderance, lateral intermarriage for economic alliances via exclusive schools and resorts

The imbalance of sex ratios ensures genetic turnover or diffusion between classes—a streaming of genetic material between levels over generations.

## Applied Art in Modern Design

### Flowform Systems
A rare example of modern applied art is the "Flowform" system developed at the Virbella Institute, Emerson College, Kent, UK. Modelled on older Roman water cascades and natural stream basins carved in sandstone and mudstone, these sculptural forms:

- Are cast in concrete or fibreglass as artificial replicates of natural turbulence basins
- Create three distinct mixing effects: vertical plunge, lateral figure-8 flow, and an interaction rhythm switching the vertical flow side-to-side
- Induce aeration, oxidize pollutants, and provide aesthetically pleasing hydrological pattern models
- Have practical use in primary sewage treatment, organic pollution remediation, and pond oxygenation for aquaculture
