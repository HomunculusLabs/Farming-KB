# Slope Analysis in Permaculture

Slope, aspect, elevation, and orientation are critical landscape factors that modify and refine the basic zone-and-sector framework in [[permaculture-design]]. No site is perfectly flat, and the irregularities of real terrain create opportunities for sophisticated placement of water systems, structures, forests, and cropland. Understanding how to read and use slope is essential to effective permaculture design.

## Why Slope Matters

Slope determines three fundamental flows on a site:

1. **Water flow** — Unpowered movement of water from source to use point, governed entirely by gravity
2. **Thermal flow** — Thermosiphon effects for hot air and hot water systems, where collectors placed below storage allow circulation without pumps
3. **Access and movement** — High roads for heavy cargo and mulch, low roads for fire control

Even very slight slopes of 1:150 can function to collect water and heat if well used in the design. Where no natural slope exists, towers for water tanks and hollows for heat collectors (or solar ponds) can be constructed to achieve the same effect.

## The Broad Landscape Profile

In humid landscapes (where precipitation exceeds evaporation), hill profiles develop a characteristic flattened "S" curve that presents systematic opportunities for placement analysis. Mollison identifies four key landscape zones:

### Zone A: High Plateau (Upper Erosion Surface)
The high plateau serves as a vast roof where rain and snow gather. Trees and shrubs prevent quick water runoff. At night, saturated air deposits droplets on the myriad leaves of ridge forests through condensation. The headwaters of streams begin here, seeking to organize the sometimes indefinite slope pattern below.

**Strategy:** Preserve and restore forest cover. These areas are rarely suitable for agriculture but are critical for water retention and erosion prevention.

### Zone B: Steep Upper Slopes
These steep transitional zones are catastrophically prone to erosion if cleared of protecting forest. They are rarely of practical use to agriculture but are unfortunately often cleared, leading to rapid degradation.

**Strategy:** Maintain permanent forest cover. Never clear steep slopes.

### Zone C: Lower Slopes (The Keypoint Zone)
The gentle foothill country is the most viable area for agriculture, brilliantly analyzed for water conservation by P.A. Yeomans. Here, high runoff from above can be led to midslope storage dams at the "keypoint"—the point of inflection where convex upper slopes transition to concave lower slopes.

Using the high slopes as a watershed, a series of diversion catchment drains and dams conserves water at keypoints for later frugal use in fields and buildings. Water passes with its nutrients to low dams and is released as clean water from the site.

**Strategy:** This is prime settlement and agricultural land. The rounded ridges and foothill pediments are ideal for homes, gardens, and domestic animals. They allow filtration of wastes through lowland forest and lake, converting wastes into useful timber, trees, fruits, and aquatic life.

### Zone D: Plains and Lowlands
Plains are the most open to wind erosion but the most resistant to water erosion. Great damage occurs here through salting—caused by delinquent use of the plough, heavy machinery, clean tillage, removal of trees and hedgerows, and conversion to monocultures of extensive grazing and grain cropping.

**Strategy:** Water is most cheaply stored here in soil and large surface dams. No-tillage crops, copses, and hedgerows are desperately needed. This is where broadscale revolutions in technique can improve soil health, reduce wind and water losses, and produce healthy foods.

## The Keypoint System

The keypoint concept, developed by P.A. Yeomans, is central to slope-based water management. At the point of inflection on a hillside—where the slope changes from convex (accelerating runoff) to concave (decelerating runoff)—water can most efficiently be captured and stored. Dams placed at keypoints harvest the maximum amount of water from the smallest catchment area.

## Forest Effects on Slope Climate

Forests on the high slopes above settlements create a remarkable "thermal belt" effect. On a frosty night, walking towards an uphill forest reveals a warm downdraft from high forests. If these forests are above Zones 1 and 2, they present little or no fire danger while providing erosion control and water retention.

Downslope, reflection from dams adds warmth. Solar collectors placed on lower slopes transmit heat via thermosiphoning (air or water circulating by gravity alone), assisting houses, glasshouses, and gardens without external energy inputs.

## Sun Angle and Aspect

Two additional factors refine slope analysis:

### Sun Angle
Sun angle describes the arc of the sun during summer and winter months, determining:
- Eave and sill placement of windows
- Areas of shade
- Reflection or absorption angles of surfaces

In every situation, even hot deserts, some part of the system should be left open to the sun for its energy potential.

### Aspect
Aspect describes the orientation of the slope relative to the sun. A slope facing the sun receives considerably more direct solar radiation than a shaded slope (south-facing in the southern hemisphere, north-facing in the northern hemisphere). The shaded side of hills may delay thaw and moderate frost effects in vegetation. In mid-latitudes, designers seek the sunny aspect for maximum sunlight absorption.

## Settlement Location Principles

Successful and permanent settlements have historically been sited at critical junctions of two or more natural ecologies—between foothill forests and plains, at the edge of plain and marsh, or at the boundary of land and estuary. Such locations allow inhabitants to draw from the resources of multiple environments.

Planners who place settlements on plains or plateaus may have the "advantage" of flat planning, but abandon inhabitants to failure if transport fuels become scarce. Monocultural landscapes cannot support the varied needs of self-reliant communities.

The difference, Mollison suggests, is that traditional peoples planned for themselves, while modern designers plan for "other people."

## Summary of Slope Design Principles

- Use gravity for water movement and thermal circulation wherever possible
- Preserve forests on upper slopes and ridges as watersheds
- Place settlements at keypoints or lower slopes with multiple ecological resources
- Use aspect and sun angle to maximize solar gain and minimize frost risk
- Create dams at keypoints for efficient water storage
- Apply no-tillage, hedgerows, and copses on plains to prevent wind erosion and salting
- Even minimal slopes (1:150) can be used for water and heat collection
