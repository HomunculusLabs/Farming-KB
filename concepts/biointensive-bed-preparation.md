## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls
Several errors are commonly made by gardeners new to double-digging. Walking
on prepared beds is perhaps the most damaging, as it re-compacts the soil that
was just aerated with considerable effort. Using a kneeling board distributes
weight and prevents this. Another frequent mistake is inverting soil layers --
the goal is to loosen, not flip. When topsoil is buried and subsoil brought to
the surface, the buried organic matter becomes anaerobic and the exposed
subsoil lacks the biological activity to support plant roots. The correct
technique moves topsoil forward in the trench while only loosening the subsoil
below with a
fork.

Attempting to double-dig when soil is too wet is another common error.
Wet
soil, especially clay, compacts easily and forms hard clods when later dried.
A simple test is to squeeze a handful of soil into a ball and then tap it with
a finger -- if it crumbles easily, it is ready to work. If it stays in a solid
ball or feels sticky, it is too wet. Working soil that is too dry is similarly
problematic, as it requires excessive effort and may damage soil structure. In
arid climates, pre-watering the bed a day or two before digging can bring soil
to the right moisture
level.
## Soil Types and Adaptations
Double-digging produces the most dramatic results in compacted clay soils and
depleted sandy soils, but the approach should be adapted to the native soil
type. In heavy clay soils, working in generous quantities of compost (2-3
inches rather than 1 inch) helps create aggregation -- the crumbly structure
that allows water infiltration and root penetration. Adding gypsum (calcium
sulfate) to clay soils can help flocculate clay particles, improving structure
without raising pH. In sandy soils, the focus shifts to increasing organic
matter content, which improves water and nutrient retention. Coarse sand or
perlite can be added if drainage is already
excessive.

Rocky or shallow soils over bedrock may not allow a full 24-inch
depth. In
these cases, the practical approach is to dig as deep as possible while
avoiding hitting the subsoil layer that would damage tools. [[comparison-raised-beds-vs-hugelkultur]] built
on top of shallow soil can effectively increase the root zone depth without
excavation. In areas with hardpan (a dense, compacted layer below the
topsoil), the broadfork is particularly useful for breaking through without
major
excavation.
