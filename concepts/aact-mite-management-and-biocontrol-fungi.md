# AACT Mite Management and Biocontrol Fungi

Mite management through compost tea relies on the same principles as other [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]]: re-establishing the natural predator-prey and parasite-host relationships that keep pest populations in check. The approach emphasizes using local, indigenous organisms rather than broad-spectrum interventions.

## Mite Biocontrol Principles

Just as bacteria can cause disease in humans, specific bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms serve as natural pathogens of mites. Many biocontrol relationships involve parasitic fungi and diseases caused by mite-specific pathogens. The key principle is to use **local, indigenous parasites and diseases** of the target mite species rather than introducing non-native control agents.

This locality requirement matters because mite-pathogen relationships are often co-evolved and species-specific. A pathogen that controls one mite species in one region may be entirely ineffective against a related species in another region. Getting a local microbiologist involved, or working with a local Soil Foodweb (SFI) advisor or lab, ensures the right organisms are being targeted and the correct local strains are being cultivated.

SFI emphasizes field-level microbiology — real-world agriculture and horticulture — rather than laboratory plate cultures incubated at constant [[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]]. Small research plots are typically not meaningful for understanding field-scale biological interactions, because the microbial communities and their interactions with pests operate at landscape scale.

## Bulb Mites and Fungal-Feeding Insects

Bulb mites raise an important question about insect feeding ecology: are they truly plant consumers, or are they actually fungal feeders? Field observations consistently show that insects assumed to eat plants often actually eat fungi. When their fungal food source is depleted, they then attack seedlings and roots as a secondary food source. Once plants are larger and more established, the damage becomes insignificant, but during germination and early seedling stages, the predation can be devastating.

This fungal-feeding behavior has profound implications for pest management. If the mite's primary food is fungi, then managing soil fungal communities may be more effective than targeting the mite directly. Starving the mites of their preferred fungal food could drive them away from the root zone. This also means that healthy soil fungal communities can actually attract these pests — but only when the fungal community is imbalanced and the organisms the mites prefer are overrepresented.

The practical application: rather than trying to kill mites, focus on building a balanced soil fungal community that does not favor the specific fungi that mites feed on. Compost tea with diverse fungal species can help establish this balance.

## Moss and Algae Control Through Protozoa

Protozoa are the primary biological agents for controlling algae in aquatic systems. University of Wisconsin research demonstrated this dramatically on Lake Mendota, where an algal bloom so thick it was practically walkable was resolved by restoring protozoan populations. However, this required understanding and restoring a cascade of ecological relationships:

The lake's problem stemmed from introduced "sport fish" that ate the native fish. The native fish had eaten smaller fish that ate zooplankton (protozoa) that ate phytoplankton (algae). Removing the top-level native predator cascaded through the food chain, eliminating the protozoa that kept algae in check. Restoring the native fish species re-established the full food chain and stopped the bloom.

This is a textbook example of **trophic cascade** — where changes at the top of a food chain propagate through every level below. In aquatic systems, the links are:
- Sport fish → native fish → small fish → zooplankton (protozoa) → phytoplankton (algae)

In ponds on golf courses, the same principle applies: pesticides and high levels of [[ingham-inorganic-fertilizers-soil-biology-damage]] eliminated both fish and the protozoa that eat algae. The algae should add oxygen to the water, but only the green living layer on top contributes oxygen. When the algal layer grows too thick, the bottom layer dies and becomes food for bacteria. These bacteria consume oxygen faster than the living algae can replenish it, creating anoxic conditions that can kill fish and other aquatic life.

The lesson applies broadly: look beyond the obvious symptom. The mechanism for shutting down pathogens is not through toxic chemical interaction — it is through restoring functional food webs where organisms at every trophic level are present and active. Removing one link can cascade into system-wide failure.

## Beauveria as a Biocontrol Agent

### Biology and Mode of Action

Beauveria is a genus of entomopathogenic (insect-killing) fungi that attacks a wide range of insect larvae. It works by contacting the insect cuticle, germinating, and penetrating through the exoskeleton into the insect body, where it proliferates and kills the host. Different Beauveria species and strains show specificity for different insect hosts.

### Fruit Fly Management Protocol

Beauveria is effective against fruit fly larvae in soil. The application protocol requires careful timing because the fungus cannot penetrate into the maggot inside the fruit:

1. **Fall application**: Apply Beauveria-containing compost tea as a soil drench around trees at 150 liters per hectare. This allows the fungus to overwinter in the soil and consume larval stages that drop to the ground with infested fruit.
2. **Spring application**: Another 150 L/HA soil drench before adult emergence, targeting any larvae that survived winter.
3. **Foliar application**: Spray foliage with compost tea containing [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]] that repel adult flies, reducing egg-laying pressure.

### Compatibility with Humic Acids

Beauveria performs well in the competitive environment of compost tea. Certain humic acids appear to be important for maintaining Beauveria viability and activity in the brew. This suggests that combining humic acid as a fungal food source with Beauveria inoculum in tea recipes would be beneficial for maximizing the biocontrol potential of the tea.

## Leaf-Feeding Insect Suppression

### Bacterial Approaches

Dr. Mike Holmes of Holmes Environmental (Corvallis, OR) has developed bacterial inocula for application to leaf surfaces that repel and suppress leaf-feeding insect attack. Tom Piatkowski has developed effective bacterial inocula for soils that control both foliar diseases and some leaf-feeding insects.

Additional sources of bacterial inocula include New Era Farm Service and Agri-Energy. These inocula can be added to compost tea or applied directly.

### Fungal Specificity

There is significant specificity between insect-attacking fungi and their target insects. Bruce Elliott has worked extensively on matching different fungal species to different insect and fungal diseases and pests. A fungus that controls one pest species may be entirely ineffective against another, even a closely related one. This specificity means that proper identification of the target pest is essential before selecting a biocontrol fungal agent.

### Critical Testing Requirement

Before purchasing any commercial inoculum, obtain data showing that:
- The organisms survive and grow in compost tea during the brewing cycle.
- The organisms survive and grow in compost if added to the compost pile.
- The organisms survive and persist in soil or on foliage after application.
- The organisms actually reduce the target pest to below economic damage levels.

Spending money on organisms that cannot survive the production and application process is a common and costly mistake. Always verify survival data before making purchasing decisions.

## The Banana Case Study

Work with an organic banana grower in Australia demonstrated broad-spectrum AACT benefits beyond the initially targeted problems:

- **Root borer** was controlled through soil biology restoration
- **Root rots** were suppressed by competitive exclusion
- **Weed pressure shifted** from problematic couch grass to beneficial ryegrass in the plantation, indicating a fundamental shift in soil ecology

This case illustrates how restoring soil biology creates cascading benefits beyond the initially targeted problem, shifting the entire plant community toward a healthier equilibrium. The weed shift from couch grass to ryegrass is particularly significant — it suggests that the soil biology now favors beneficial ground cover species over aggressive weeds, likely through changes [[comparison-soil-protozoa-vs-nematodes-in-nutrient-cycling]] patterns and soil surface conditions.

## Eastern Rots

Paul Wagner and James Sottilo at the SFI New York lab have accumulated data on managing difficult eastern rots using AACT. The approach requires working on recipes that grow beneficial fungi in the tea — the fungal component is essential for these particular diseases. Jeff Lowenfels has also reported success with these conditions, demonstrating that consistent recipe development and testing can solve even challenging disease problems.

## Integrated Approach

The consistent theme across all these applications is that biological pest [[marijuana-horticulture-cervantes-pest-and-disease-management]] works best as part of an integrated approach:

1. **Build the foodweb** in soil and on foliage through compost and compost tea.
2. **Use specific biocontrol agents** (Beauveria, bacterial inocula) when particular pests are identified.
3. **Maintain coverage** through regular applications — biology must be sustained, not applied once.
4. **Monitor results** and adjust recipes based on what the organisms and the plants are telling you.
5. **Be patient** — biological systems take time to establish and reach equilibrium.
