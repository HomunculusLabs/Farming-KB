# Permafrost and Cold Area Grazing Systems

[[permafrost]] landscapes and cold-climate grazing systems represent some of the most fragile and complex environments addressed in [[permaculture-design]]. [[bill-mollison]]'s treatment of these topics spans snow mechanics, permafrost ecology, and practical grazing management, drawing heavily from Icelandic case studies and northern hemisphere research. Understanding the physical processes at work — from frost shattering to gelifluction — is essential for designing resilient cold-climate systems that work with rather than against extreme conditions.

## Snow Movement and Avalanche Dynamics

Snow is not static on slopes; it moves through several distinct modes, each with different velocities and implications for site design and hazard assessment.

### Modes of Snow Movement

| Mode | Speed | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **Microcreep** | Centimeters/day | Slow, continuous downslope movement of the entire snowpack |
| **Slip / Snow Boards** | Meters/day | Slab-like displacement of cohesive snow layers |
| **Snow Mound Advances** | Variable | Localized accumulation and forward creep of snow bodies |
| **Snow Bodies en Masse** | Variable | Whole-slope movement of deep snow deposits |
| **Snow Slides** | 4–5 m/sec | Sliding movement; **arrestable by terraces** |
| **Avalanches** | Up to 6.5 m/sec | High-energy episodic or periodic events |

### Avalanche Classification and Frequency

Avalanches are classified by their recurrence patterns:

- **Episodic avalanches**: Occur approximately every 35 years
- **Systematic avalanches**: Annual events on known, established paths
- **Periodic (rare) avalanches**: Infrequent but high-magnitude events

### Avalanche Trigger Mechanisms

Approximately **80% of avalanches** result from old snow being dislodged by moisture condensation when humidity exceeds 70%. The remaining 20% are caused by:

- **Gusty winds**: Wind-loading creates unstable slab conditions on lee slopes
- **Meltwater lubrication**: Occurs between midday and 3 PM as solar heating produces water at the snow base

**Critical warning signs**: Sudden snowfalls of 50–69 cm followed by cloud-free weather create highly unstable conditions that designers and land managers should monitor closely.

### Avalanche Impact Data

- **Impact force**: 4–54 tonnes per square meter
- **Compressed air blast**: 330 m/sec preceding the [[avalanche]] front
- **Snowdust avalanches**: 10–100 m/sec at 35 atmospheres of pressure

### Tree Indicators of Avalanche Paths

Trees growing in avalanche zones show characteristic deformation patterns that serve as reliable diagnostic indicators for site assessment and hazard mapping:

- **Downslope lean**: Trees bent in the direction of snow movement
- **Lack of lower limbs**: Abrasion from sliding snow strips branches from upslope side
- **Bare strips of pioneer species**: Aspen and other early-successional species colonize avalanche tracks
- **Blown-down forests**: Tree mortality concentrated at avalanche snout zones where debris fans out

**Rock avalanches** produce similar landscape signatures, with visible effects persisting for 20–30 years after the event.

## Permafrost Ecology and Soil Dynamics

Permafrost underlies vast regions of the northern hemisphere and is acutely sensitive to surface disturbance. Its stability is governed by thermal dynamics at the ground surface.

### Global Distribution

Permafrost occupies **47–50% of the land area** in Canada, Russia, and Greenland. Much of this permafrost is considered "fossil," extending to depths of up to 400 meters. Earth heat reduces fossil permafrost at approximately **1 cm per year**, making it effectively a permanent feature on human timescales — unless surface conditions change.

### The Critical Role of Peat

Peaty surfaces play a dual protective role in permafrost landscapes:

1. **Prevent melting**: Peat acts as thermal insulation, shielding permafrost from solar gain
2. **Admit cold**: Peat's low thermal conductivity allows surface cold to penetrate downward and maintain frozen ground

When peat is removed through clearing, fire, or overgrazing, the consequences are severe and often irreversible:

- Rapid thaw of underlying permafrost
- Formation of bare, sunken clayey depressions ranging from **2–45 meters deep**
- Solar thaw becomes **5,000× more effective** than earth heat when the insulating peat layer is removed

### Frost-Driven Soil Formation

Permafrost regions generate soil through two primary frost mechanisms:

- **Frost shattering**: Repeated freeze-thaw cycles fracture bedrock, creating parent material for soil development
- **Gelifluction**: Slow downslope flow of thawed surface material over frozen substrate, sorting rocks and soils by size and density

### Loess Deposits

Windblown loess deposits in cold regions have distinctive mineral composition and remarkable structural stability:

- **Composition**: 50–80% silica, 20–25% feldspar
- **Stability**: Capable of maintaining steep walls up to 300 meters high
- **Traditional use**: Excavated for underground housing (yaodong) in northern China

## Cold Area Grazing: The Iceland Case Study

Iceland provides a detailed example of both the challenges and strategies for cold-climate grazing systems, illustrating principles applicable across subarctic and alpine environments worldwide.

### Seasonal Management Framework

| Period | Duration | Activity |
|--------|----------|----------|
| **Shedded housing** | ~6 months | Sheep, cattle, and horses housed indoors |
| **Upland grazing** | 3+ months | Summer grazing on higher-elevation pastures |
| **Lowland grazing** | ~3 months | Late-season grazing on lower pastures |

### Physical Landscape Management

- **Frost penetration** reaches 1.2 meters in Icelandic soils
- **Open ditches** spaced at 50-meter intervals facilitate spring thaw drainage
- **Frost heave** requires regular levelling and re-sodding of damaged pasture surfaces

### Livestock Health and Parasite Management

Mixed-livestock systems combined with **rotational grazing** are critical for controlling **Helminth worm** infestations. Parasite problems are notably worse on lowland fertilized pastures, where concentrated stocking creates transmission hotspots.

### Fodder Crops and Trace Element Deficiencies

Recommended fodder species for cold-area systems include kale, rape, ryegrass, barley, and rye. Nutrient deficiencies are a persistent challenge:

| Deficiency | Affected System | Affected Organism |
|------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Cobalt | Animal health | Sheep |
| Selenium | Animal health | Sheep |
| Calcium | Pasture quality | Pasture plants |
| Copper | Pasture quality | Pasture plants |
| Phosphorus | Pasture quality | Pasture plants |

### Vegetation and Deforestation Impacts

Iceland has suffered catastrophic deforestation, with forest cover reduced to approximately **5% of original extent** and **50% of vegetative cover lost** overall. Despite the availability of willow and dwarf birch, shrub and tree browsing by livestock remains underdeveloped. Sandy cold-desert species on degraded land include thyme, Festuca grasses, lichens, dwarf birch, sedges, and cottongrasses.

## Design Principles for Cold-Climate Systems

1. **Protect peat layers** at all costs — they are the primary thermal buffer maintaining permafrost stability
2. **Use terraces and earthworks** to arrest snow slides and manage snow deposition patterns
3. **Implement rotational grazing** with mixed livestock to break parasite cycles
4. **Address trace element deficiencies** through strategic supplementation and diverse pasture species
5. **Prioritize reforestation** using native pioneer species to restore degraded cold landscapes
6. **Reduce grazing pressure** on eroding soils while developing innovative forestry systems
7. **Read landscape indicators** — tree deformation, bare strips, and frost features inform safe placement of structures and grazing zones

The intersection of snow mechanics, permafrost ecology, and grazing management demands integrated design approaches that respect the extreme sensitivity of cold-climate systems to surface disturbance and management change.
