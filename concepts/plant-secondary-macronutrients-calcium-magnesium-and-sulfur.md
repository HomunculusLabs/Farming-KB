# Plant Secondary Macronutrients: Calcium, Magnesium, and Sulfur

While nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium receive the most attention, three other macronutrients -- calcium, magnesium, and sulfur -- are equally essential. These secondary macronutrients are used in large quantities and deficiency of any one will severely limit plant growth.

## Calcium (Ca)

### Key Roles

- **Cell wall structure**: Calcium is a key structural component of cell walls. Since cell walls form around every plant cell, calcium is immediately and obviously important.
- **Signaling molecule**: Under normal conditions, the cytosol has very low calcium concentration, with supplies sequestered in the vacuole and endoplasmic reticulum. Increasing cytosolic calcium concentrations activate enzymes and speed up reactions -- calcium acts as a cellular alarm system.
- **Membrane transport**: Used to transport substances across the cellular membrane.
- **Cell division**: Important for cell division, which is why it concentrates in young undifferentiated meristematic cells.
- **Enzyme component**: Part of many enzymatic reactions.

### Mobility and Deficiency

Calcium is **immobile** once assimilated into a cell. It cannot be relocated to where it is needed. Deficiency appears first in growing areas:
- Roots: malformed root tips
- Shoots: distorted growing points
- Young leaves: malformed, crinkled, or hooked appearance

### Sources

Calcium is extremely abundant in the environment. It comes from weathered minerals such as limestone and chalk. It is held by soil cation exchange sites and is relatively available.

### Garden Significance

Calcium deficiency causes blossom end rot in tomatoes and peppers, bitter pit in apples, and tip burn in lettuce. These are all disorders of rapidly growing tissues where calcium supply cannot keep up with demand.

## Magnesium (Mg)

### Key Roles

- **Chlorophyll center**: Magnesium is the central atom of the chlorophyll molecule (C55H72MgN4O5). Without magnesium, there is no photosynthesis. This single role makes magnesium indispensable.
- **ATP enzyme activation**: Activates enzymes involved in the production and use of ATP, making it essential for respiration.
- **DNA and RNA**: Needed for the creation of both nucleic acids.
- **Phosphorus transport**: Helps move phosphorus compounds through the plant.

### Mobility and Deficiency

Magnesium is **mobile** in plants. When deficient, it is moved from older leaves to new growth. Symptoms:
- **Interveinal chlorosis**: Yellowing between leaf veins while veins remain green. This is because veins are nearest to remaining sugar supplies, but chlorophyll cannot be synthesized without magnesium.
- Older leaves show symptoms first
- In severe cases, leaves may become reddish-purple before dropping

### Sources

Magnesium is a very abundant mineral on Earth, weathering from many minerals. The most familiar form is dolomite (CaMg[CO3]2), a sedimentary carbonate rock. Much magnesium is dissolved in water, making it available in irrigation.

### Garden Significance

Magnesium deficiency is common in sandy, acidic soils with high rainfall (where Mg leaches out). Epsom salts (magnesium sulfate) is a common organic remedy. Heavy potassium applications can suppress magnesium uptake -- another reason balanced nutrition matters.

## Sulfur (S)

### Key Roles

- **Amino acid component**: Sulfur is a component of two critical amino acids:
- **Cysteine**: Important for metabolic activities, including electron transfer during photosynthesis and respiration
- **Methionine**: Found in structural components of cells
- **Protein shape**: The orientation of sulfur atoms' bonds gives proteins specific shapes that influence their metabolic and structural roles
- **Plant defense**: Cysteine plays a role in flavor molecules (glucosinolates) that protect plants from grazing by insects and animals (think of the strong sulfur taste of broccoli, cabbage, and mustard)
- **Electron transfer**: Necessary for transferring electrons during photosynthesis and respiration

### Mobility and Deficiency

Sulfur is **immobile** in plants. Once synthesized into proteins, it stays where it is. Deficiency symptoms appear in younger leaves:
- Yellowing of new growth (similar to nitrogen deficiency)
- Distinguishing feature: unlike nitrogen deficiency (which starts in older leaves), sulfur deficiency starts in the younger leaves

### Sources and Modern Context

Sulfur used to be supplied abundantly by acid rain (atmospheric sulfur dioxide from industrial pollution converting to sulfuric acid). Stricter air pollution regulations have dramatically reduced atmospheric sulfur deposition, making sulfur deficiencies more common.

Sulfur is released from organic material by microbial activity and is weathered from gypsum rock. Sulfur-containing fertilizers and elemental sulfur (which soil bacteria convert to sulfate) are common supplements.

### Garden Significance

Sulfur deficiency is increasingly common as air quality improves. Brassicas (cabbage family) are particularly sulfur-hungry. The characteristic "hot" taste of mustard and horseradish depends on adequate sulfur.

# Calcium-Magnesium Balance

updated: 2026-04-18

Calcium and magnesium are both essential plant nutrients and are intimately connected in soil chemistry through their shared role as cations on the [[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]] (CEC) sites. Their balance in soil has profound effects on plant health, soil structure, and the availability of other nutrients.

## The Roles of Calcium and Magnesium in Plants

Calcium (Ca) is a key structural component of cell walls. Because cell walls form around every plant cell, calcium is of immediate and obvious importance. Calcium is also a part of many enzymes and is important as a signaling molecule. Under normal cell conditions, the cytosol has a very low calcium concentration, with supplies sequestered in organelles such as the vacuole or endoplasmic reticulum. Increasing concentrations of calcium activate enzymes in the cytosol, speeding up reactions. Calcium is also used to transport other substances across the cellular membrane and is important to cell division, which is why calcium deficiencies appear first in the growing areas of roots, shoots, and young leaves -- the result is that they become malformed. Calcium is not mobile once assimilated into a cell.

Magnesium (Mg) has a unique and critical role as the center of the chlorophyll molecule (C55H72MgN4O5). No magnesium means no photosynthesis. Magnesium is also an activator of enzymes involved in the production and use of ATP, so it plays a key role in respiration. Finally, magnesium is needed for the creation of DNA and RNA. When plants develop magnesium deficiency, chlorophyll cannot be synthesized and leaves start to lose their green color in between the leaf veins (interveinal chlorosis), while the veins remain green. Older leaves show the signs first, meaning magnesium is mobile in plants.

## Ideal Ratios

The relationship between calcium and magnesium on soil CEC sites is one of the most discussed topics in soil management. While there is debate about exact ideal ratios, the following general principles from soil science and Lowenfels' discussion are widely accepted:

- **Base saturation** is the percentage of CEC sites occupied by each cation. In most soils, calcium should occupy roughly 60 to 75 percent of base saturation sites, magnesium 10 to 20 percent, potassium 2 to 5 percent, and hydrogen the remainder.
- A common target ratio of calcium to magnesium is approximately 3:1 to 7:1 by weight on the CEC, though this varies with soil type and conditions.
- Too much calcium relative to magnesium can induce magnesium deficiency in plants, even when soil magnesium levels appear adequate. The excess calcium competes with magnesium for uptake sites on root membranes.
- Too much potassium negatively affects magnesium uptake as well. The three cations -- calcium, magnesium, and potassium -- compete with one another for both CEC sites and root uptake.
- Excessive magnesium is not good for calcium availability. High magnesium levels (above 20 percent base saturation) can cause soil to become tight and poorly drained, and it reduces calcium uptake by plants.
- Ammonium nitrogen also competes with calcium and magnesium ions. Apply too much ammonium, and you may end up with not enough of the other cations.

## Liming Materials and Their Effects

The choice of liming material has a direct effect on the calcium-magnesium balance:

**Calcitic limestone (CaCO3):** Provides calcium only. This is the material of choice when soils have adequate or high magnesium levels. A slab of limestone will last thousands of years, but when powdered and exposed to water and carbon dioxide, it breaks down. You can only raise pH about one point per growing season. The best time to apply is in autumn for the following growing season.

**Dolomitic limestone (CaMg[CO3]2):** Provides both calcium and about 10 percent magnesium. Use dolomitic lime when soils are deficient in magnesium. However, be cautious -- dolomite tends to bind soils, and too much magnesium impacts the uptake of other nutrients. Make sure your soil test shows you actually need more magnesium before applying dolomite. The efficacy and duration of limestone in soil is related to existing pH, water, and carbon dioxide content.

**Gypsum (CaSO4):** Calcium sulfate provides calcium without significantly affecting pH. This makes it useful for supplying calcium in soils that are already at the desired pH or are alkaline. Gypsum also helps improve soil structure in clay soils by promoting flocculation (clay particles clumping together), improving drainage and aeration.

## Symptoms of Imbalance

**Calcium deficiency symptoms:** Since calcium is immobile in plants, deficiencies appear in the youngest tissue first -- growing tips of roots and shoots, and young leaves become malformed. Blossom end rot in tomatoes and peppers is the classic calcium deficiency symptom, though it often reflects calcium transport problems rather than true soil deficiency. Other symptoms include stunted roots, curled leaves, and premature death of growing points.
