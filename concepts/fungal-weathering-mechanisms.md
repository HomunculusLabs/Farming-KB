# Fungal Weathering Mechanisms — Biomechanical and Biochemical

## Overview

Fungal weathering of rocks and minerals proceeds through two synergistic categories of action: **biomechanical weathering** (physical disruption) and **[[gadd-fungal-biochemical-weathering-acidolysis-complexolysis-redoxolysis-protonolysis]]** (chemical dissolution, also called "[[staycare-fungal-heterotrophic-leaching-biosorption]]"). While biomechanical processes directly damage mineral substrates, biochemical processes are generally considered far more important in terms of overall mineral dissolution and element mobilization. Both categories are intimately connected, with biomechanical penetration creating new surfaces for biochemical attack.

## Biomechanical Weathering

### Direct Biomechanical Degradation

Fungi can physically penetrate and disrupt mineral substrates through:

- **Hyphal penetration** into decayed rocks along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries in sandstone, calcitic, and dolomitic rocks.
- **Tunnelling** into otherwise intact mineral matter.
- **Turgor pressure**: Hyphae exert considerable mechanical force generated osmotically, capable of forcing entry into mineral matrices.

### Facilitating Factors

Biomechanical penetration is enhanced by:

- **Thigmotropism (contact guidance)**: Directed growth towards grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material. This enables hyphae to locate and exploit weakened sites in mineral surfaces.
- **Mucilaginous slime**: Provides lubrication during penetration and may contain acidic and metal-chelating metabolites that simultaneously weaken mineral bonds.
- **Hydrated mucilage**: Shrinking and swelling effects of the slime produced by many fungi can cause indirect biomechanical stress on rock surfaces.

## Biochemical Weathering ("Heterotrophic Leaching")

Biochemical weathering is the primary driver of mineral dissolution and operates through three main mechanisms: **acidolysis** (proton-promoted dissolution), **complexolysis** (ligand-promoted dissolution), and **redoxolysis** (redox-driven dissolution).

### Acidolysis (Proton-Promoted Dissolution)

Acidolysis occurs when fungi acidify their microenvironment through:

- **Excretion of protons** (H⁺) directly.
- **Organic acid excretion**: Carboxylic acids (oxalic, citric, malic, succinic, gluconic, formic acid) that dissociate to release protons.
- **Respiratory CO₂**: Carbonic acid formation from CO₂ dissolved in water.

The resulting acidification lowers pH and promotes proton exchange at mineral surfaces, dissolving cations from mineral lattices.

### Complexolysis (Ligand-Promoted Dissolution)

Complexolysis involves the excretion of **metal-complexing metabolites** that chelate metal cations from mineral structures:

- **Carboxylic acids**: Especially oxalic and citric acid — these perform "aggressive attack" on mineral surfaces, providing both protons for solubilization and chelating anions for metal complexation.
- **Amino acids**: Metal-binding capacity via functional groups.
- **Siderophores**: High-affinity iron-chelating compounds.
- **Phenolic compounds**: Metal-binding aromatic molecules.

When a fungal culture excretes large amounts of a strong chelator (e.g., oxalate), the solubilization mechanism shifts from primarily acidolysis to predominantly **ligand-promoted dissolution**.

### Redoxolysis

Redoxolysis involves the reduction or oxidation of metals within mineral structures:

- **Reduction**: Some fungi can reduce metals such as Ag⁺, Se⁴⁺, and Te⁴⁺ to their elemental forms, precipitating them within and around cells.
- **Oxidation**: Fungi can oxidize Mn(II) and Fe(II) in minerals such as siderite (FeCO₃) and rhodochrosite (MnCO₃), precipitating them as oxides.
- **Desert varnish**: Dark oxidized metal patinas on rocks in arid regions are believed to involve microbial (including fungal) oxidation of Mn and Fe.

## Metal Immobilization

Metal immobilization mechanisms reduce external free metal activity, shifting equilibrium to release more metal from mineral sources into solution. Fungal immobilization occurs through:

1. **Biosorption**: Binding to biomass — cell walls, pigments, and extracellular polysaccharides. This can be a passive process in both living and dead biomass.
2. **Intracellular accumulation**: Active transport of metals into cells.
3. **Extracellular precipitation**: Formation of [[gadd-mycogenic-mineral-precipitation-secondary-minerals]] (carbonates, oxalates, phosphates, hydroxides) that immobilize metals.

Fungi are highly efficient bioaccumulators of soluble and particulate metals (Ni, Zn, Ag, Cu, Cd, Pb), especially from dilute external concentrations.

## Factors Influencing Mechanism Selection

The dominant weathering mechanism depends on:

- **Nitrogen source**: NO₃⁻ as nitrogen source enhances oxalate excretion compared to NH₄⁺ (which favours acidolysis).
- **Mineral composition**: Acidolysis was the main mechanism for toxic metal phosphate dissolution with NH₄⁺; ligand-promoted dissolution dominated with oxalate-overproducing strains.
- **Presence of HCO₃⁻, Ca²⁺, and toxic metals**: These enhance oxalate excretion and shift mechanisms toward complexolysis.
- **pH and redox conditions**: Determine the relative importance of acidolysis vs. redoxolysis.
- **Fungal species and strain**: Inter- and intraspecific variation in organic acid excretion profiles.

## Environmental Significance

The biochemical weathering activities of fungi have wide-ranging consequences:

- **Soil formation**: Mineral dissolution releases essential nutrients for plant and microbial growth.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Mobilization of P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]] from mineral sources.
- **Toxic metal mobilization**: Release of Cd, Pb, Hg, Al from geological sources into bioavailable forms.
- **Metal immobilization**: Precipitation of secondary minerals can detoxify contaminated environments.
- **Carbon cycling**: [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-and-carbonate-precipitation]] coupled with silicate weathering provides a terrestrial CO₂ sink.
- **[[fungal-building-material-deterioration]]**: Bioweathering degrades building stone, concrete, ceramics, and cultural heritage materials.
