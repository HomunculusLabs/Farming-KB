# Pest Management

Eliot Coleman notes that in the winter harvest, insect and disease
pests have not been a real consideration. The operation has found
little new to report since Coleman discussed this topic in earlier
books. The major pest that has developed is not a bug or a microbe
but a mammal: the meadow vole. This chapter details the battle of
wits with voles and the broader philosophy of pest prevention on
the small farm.

## The Meadow Vole: Primary Greenhouse Pest

Meadow voles (Microtus pennsylvanicus) look like chubby, extra-
furry mice. Their qualities make them extremely difficult to control
on the small farm:

- They live out of doors and are serious pests for fruit-tree
  growers as well, girdling the bark of young trees
- They are vegetarians that seem to like the crops as much as
  customers do
- They need to eat their weight in plant food every day due to
  extremely high metabolism
- They can breed at 35 to 40 days old, breed year-round, and
  have up to ten litters per year with three to six young each
  time
- They tunnel under the soil and thus directly into the greenhouse
- They live in underground burrows and like to line their nests
  with chewed-up shreds of row-cover fabric

Unsurprisingly, voles consider greenhouses an idyllic place to
spend the winter.

## Prevention Challenges

The mobile-greenhouse concept makes vole prevention more difficult.
Coleman has tried designs with more deeply buried edges to keep
voles from burrowing under, and has worked diligently to block any
and all small holes that might allow vole ingress. However, the
forces created by winter freezing and thawing can open new holes.
Even after taking all precautions, voles are often already
surreptitiously established before the houses are moved to their
winter sites. In short, complete prevention has not been successful.

## Trapping Techniques

Coleman relies on snap traps and uses a lot of them. In one year,
over 50 voles were trapped in the vicinity of the greenhouses
during August and September alone, and a neighbor's cat probably
caught almost that many. Come winter, it did not seem as if a dent
had been made. The only effective method found is to keep trapping
year-round.

Standard rat and mouse baits like cheese do not work for these
fruit and vegetable eaters, especially when so much delicious food
is available in the greenhouses. Various alternative baits were
tried: slices of raw fruit, wild-strawberry flavored bubble gum
(surprisingly effective in midwinter when fruit was scarce in the
vole diet), macadamia-nut butter (better than peanut butter), and
macadamia-nut butter with carob powder.

However, the voles learned to avoid the baits. The third vole to
approach a baited trap seemed to think, "the last time I smelled
that, Uncle Harry bought the farm," and shied away. Coleman then
realized the flaw with all baits: the voles became wise to the
strategy and the baits had to be constantly changed.

## Interception Trapping

The breakthrough came from shifting strategy from baited traps to
interception traps placed along vole travel routes. Traps placed
at burrow entrances, across surface runs between burrows, and
against walls (where voles like to scurry along edges) caught voles
based on accurate placement rather than bait attraction.

## The Unbaited Box Trap

The ultimate solution capitalizes on a common vole behavior: the
tendency to enter small dark holes. Coleman built wooden boxes
about 12 inches long, 8 inches wide, and 6 inches deep with a
bottom attached but a removable top. At one corner of each 8-inch
end, a rough mouse-sized hole is drilled at floor level. An
unbaited snap trap is placed inside each entrance hole.

When voles do what voles do (dart into small dark holes), the
traps catch them. After the first vole enters, the box smells
like vole and subsequent voles show no aversion. Every morning
the top is lifted, traps are emptied and reset. This solution
has been, and continues to be, impressively effective. It is
the "surefire homemade vole trap" that Coleman now relies on.

## The Pest Philosophy

Beyond voles, Coleman's broader pest management philosophy
emphasizes prevention over cure. The first response to any
problem is prevention rather than treatment. For insect pests
and diseases, Coleman advocates addressing the underlying causes
(soil health, crop stress, proper rotation) rather than treating
symptoms with sprays or other palliatives.

The winter harvest itself provides a degree of natural pest
exclusion. The cold temperatures that characterize winter
greenhouse production are inhospitable to most insect pests,
and the absence of outdoor host plants during freezing weather
limits reinfection pressure. This is one of the often-overlooked
advantages of winter growing: pest and disease pressure is
naturally much lower than in warm-season production.

## Other Wildlife Considerations

While voles are the primary pest, Coleman acknowledges the
importance of physical exclusion for other wildlife. The
greenhouse structures themselves provide protection against
deer, rabbits, and birds for the crops growing inside. For
outdoor crops, standard fencing and netting approaches apply.
The overall approach favors working with biological systems
rather than engaging in continuous chemical warfare against
pests.
