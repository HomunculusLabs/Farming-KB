## Unirrigated Gardening

Steve Solomon makes the case that gardening without irrigation is not only possible but often preferable. Drawing on the dry-farming wisdom of John Widstoe's 1911 work and William Albrecht's soil science, he shows how proper soil management and plant spacing can eliminate the need for supplemental watering.

## The Historical Norm

Before the 1930s, few farms had electricity. Before 1880, when 90 percent of all North Americans lived on family farms, over 90 percent of all vegetable gardens were grown without running water. After a few weeks without rain, many would begin talking about drought - but other gardeners in the vicinity wouldn't be complaining much.

## Redefining Drought

John Widstoe's Dry Farming (1911) defined drought as "a condition under which crops fail to mature because of an insufficient supply of water." Widstoe argued that Providence is not the cause; man is. True droughts do occasionally occur, but most of what people call drought is actually the result of:

1. Failure to store moisture in the soil during winter
2. Failure to prevent evaporation from the soil surface
3. Failure to cultivate the soil properly
4. Sowing too much seed for the available soil moisture

The fourth cause is the single most important decision the gardener will make about irrigation.

## Why Intensive Methods Demand Irrigation

[[solomon-wide-spacing-versus-intensive-gardening-debate]] methods (close spacing, deeply dug beds, massed plantings) work by loosening the bed to two feet deep so plant root systems can go down instead of out. The high plant density sucks the soil dry so rapidly the gardener must water almost daily during the growing season. This high plant density forms a complete crop canopy quickly - but at the cost of enormous water consumption.

## The Old-Style Alternative

Shifting to old-style spacing won't actually be a sacrifice. Instead of having to water the garden frequently and finding that a veggie garden becomes a problematic obligation during summer, people will discover the old-style garden waters itself. Yields per square foot are only slightly lower, but the gardener saves enormous labor and water cost.

Instead of growing many small, crowded vegetables that take a longer time to harvest and clean, people will spend less time harvesting larger, more manageable produce. Species that produce an ongoing harvest over many weeks will yield surprisingly well when given room and then stopped (as they do in intensive beds from overcompetition).
