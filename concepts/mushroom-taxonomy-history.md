# Mushroom Taxonomy History

## Overview

Fungal taxonomy has undergone dramatic transformations over the past three centuries, from early morphological classification systems to modern molecular phylogenetics. The DNA revolution of the 1990s and 2000s fundamentally reshaped our understanding of fungal relationships, leading to major reclassifications that continue today. See [[fungal-dna-barcoding]] for current [[biodiversity-fungal-molecular-identification-dna-barcoding]] methods.

## Early Classification (Pre-Linnaeus)

Fungi were mentioned by ancient writers but were poorly understood. Theophrastus (370-285 BCE) described mushrooms but classified them as plants. Dioscorides documented medicinal and toxic mushrooms. For centuries, fungi were lumped together with plants, mosses, and "cryptogams" (plants without obvious reproductive organs). No systematic classification existed.

## Linnaeus (1753)

Carl Linnaeus published Species Plantarum in 1753, establishing the binomial naming system still used today. He included fungi within the plant kingdom and placed them in a single genus, Agaricus, which at the time encompassed nearly all gilled mushrooms. This was obviously inadequate, but it established the framework for named species. Linnaeus described only about 100 fungal species.

Key contributions:
- Binomial nomenclature (Genus species)
- Hierarchical classification (kingdom, class, order, genus, species)
- Standardized descriptions

Limitations:
- Fungi treated as plants
- Classification based on gross morphology only
- No understanding of spore characteristics or microscopic features

## Persoon and the First Real Fungal System (1801)

Christiaan Hendrik Persoon published Synopsis Methodica Fungorum in 1801, describing over 2,000 fungal species and establishing the first workable classification system for fungi. He recognized multiple genera within the gilled mushrooms and began using spore color as a taxonomic character. Persoon is often called the "father of systematic mycology."

## Elias Magnus Fries and the "Linnaeus of Mycology" (1821-1832)

Elias Magnus Fries published Systema Mycologicum (1821-1832), which became the starting point for fungal nomenclature (the "Friesian system"). He classified fungi primarily by spore color and hymenophore (spore-bearing surface) morphology.

Friesian system structure:
- **Hymenomycetes**: Fungi with exposed spore-bearing surfaces
  - Based on spore color: white, pink, brown, black, rusty
  - Subdivided by gill attachment, cap shape, and other macroscopic features
- **Gasteromycetes**: Fungi with enclosed spore-bearing surfaces (puffballs, earthstars)
- **Coniomycetes**: Fungi that produce spores in pycnidia or similar structures

The Friesian system dominated mycology for over 150 years and still influences field identification guides today. Many of Fries's genus concepts survive, though their boundaries have been significantly revised.

## The Microscope Revolution (Late 1800s-Early 1900s)

The adoption of microscopy transformed fungal taxonomy. Key developments:

- **Spore morphology**: Size, shape, color, ornamentation became primary characters
- **Basidium structure**: Presence and morphology of basidia (the spore-producing cells) distinguished major groups
- **Hyphal anatomy**: Septate vs. aseptate hyphae, clamp connections, and other microscopic features
- **Cystidia**: Sterile cells on hymenial surfaces became important taxonomic characters
- **Spore print colors**: Formalized as a major identification tool (see [[fungal-spore-print-guide]])

[[bloomfields-orchard-notable-mycologists-buller-and-lloyd]] of this era:
- **Pier Andrea Saccardo**: Published Sylloge Fungorum (1882-1972), an enormous compendium of fungal names
- **Charles Horton Peck**: Described thousands of [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] species
- **Rolf Singer**: Published major monographs in the mid-20th century, introduced modern agaric classification

## Numerical Taxonomy and Phenetics (1960s-1980s)

Numerical taxonomy attempted to classify fungi using many equally weighted characters analyzed by computer. While the approach had limitations (equal weighting of characters is biologically unrealistic), it stimulated more rigorous analysis of morphological data and prepared mycology for the computational demands of molecular phylogenetics.

## The DNA Revolution (1990s-Present)

The introduction of DNA sequencing transformed fungal taxonomy fundamentally.

### Early Molecular Studies (1990s)

- Ribosomal RNA genes (18S, 28S) were among the first loci used
- PCR and Sanger sequencing became accessible
- First molecular phylogenies of major fungal groups appeared
- Revealed that many morphologically similar fungi were not closely related

### Multi-Locus Phylogenetics (2000s)

- Combination of multiple gene regions (ITS, LSU, SSU, RPB1, RPB2, TEF1, beta-tubulin)
- More robust phylogenetic trees
- Discovery of cryptic species complexes
- Refinement of family- and order-level classifications

### Phylogenomics (2010s-Present)

- Whole-genome sequencing and transcriptome data
- Hundreds to thousands of genes analyzed simultaneously
- Resolution of deep phylogenetic relationships
- Assembling the Fungal Tree of Life (AFTOL project)

### Major Reclassifications

The molecular era has produced several seismic shifts:

1. **Loss of the Zygomycota**: The phylum Zygomycota, as traditionally defined, is polyphyletic. It has been split into several phyla: Mucoromycota, Zoopagomycota, and others.

2. **Glomeromycota elevated**: [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] fungi were moved from Zygomycota to their own phylum, Glomeromycota (see [[glomeromycota-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]]).

3. **Agaricales restructured**: The gilled mushrooms have been extensively reorganized. The traditional Friesian genera have been split and recombined:
   - "Coprinus" split into Coprinopsis, Coprinellus, Coprinus (restricted)
   - "Tricholoma" redefined with molecular boundaries
   - Many new genera described for molecularly distinct clades

4. **Pezizomycotina reorganized**: The largest Ascomycota subphylum has been restructured at multiple levels.

5. **Cryptic species revealed**: What was once considered a single widespread species often turns out to be a complex of multiple species (e.g., the Armillaria mellea complex contains at least 10 species).

6. **Lichens reclassified**: Molecular data showed that "[[biodiversity-lichenized-fungi-symbiosis]]" are not a natural group — lichenization has evolved independently many times within Ascomycota.

## Current State and Challenges

### One Fungus, One Name

Historically, fungi were sometimes given different names for their sexual (teleomorph) and asexual (anamorph) stages. Since 2011, the International Code of Nomenclature requires one name per fungus, regardless of which stage is known. This has led to many name changes as older teleomorph names are preserved or older anamorph names take priority.

### Open Questions

- The exact number of fungal species (estimates range from 2.2 to 3.8 million; only ~150,000 described)
- Resolution of the earliest branches of the fungal tree of life
- Integration of molecular and morphological data in field identification
- Standardizing species delimitation criteria

## Timeline Summary

- 1753: Linnaeus — binomial nomenclature, fungi as plants
- 1801: Persoon — first fungal classification system
- 1821-1832: Fries — Systema Mycologicum, Friesian system
- Late 1800s: Microscope revolution, spore and microscopic characters
- 1960s-1980s: Numerical taxonomy
- 1990s: First DNA-based phylogenies
- 2000s: Multi-locus phylogenetics, major reclassifications
- 2011: One Fungus, One Name rule adopted
- 2010s-present: Phylogenomics, whole-genome analysis
