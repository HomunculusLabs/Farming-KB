## Dynamic Accumulators

Dynamic accumulators are plants that concentrate specific nutrients in their tissues at higher levels than most other plants. They can be grown as [[cover-cropping-guide]] or green manure, then composted or mulched to cycle those nutrients back to crops. Some are "hyperaccumulators" that pull minerals from deep subsoil layers.

## The Science Behind Accumulation

There are two types of accumulator plants:

1. **True hyperaccumulators:** Concentrate a mineral even in soils low or deficient in that mineral. Legumes are the classic example — they fix atmospheric nitrogen regardless of soil N levels.

2. **Opportunistic accumulators:** Grow where soil concentrations of certain minerals are high, or send roots down to deeper layers where nutrients are abundant. Their tissue concentrations reflect the soil more than special ability.

Dr. R.R. Brooks of Massey University (New Zealand) demonstrated that some plants are so reliable as indicators that they can be used for mineral prospecting — discovering nickel, copper, and selenium deposits by analyzing plant tissues.

## Key Accumulator Plants by Nutrient

### Nitrogen (N) — Legumes

Nitrogen fixation by legumes per acre:

| Legume | N Fixed (lbs/acre) | Comparable Vegetable Need |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Field beans | 40 | Lowest demand vegetables |
| Peanuts | 40 | Lowest demand vegetables |
| Hairy vetch | 80 | Broccoli |
| Dutch white clover | 100 | Asparagus, lettuce, squash |
| Soybeans | 100 | Asparagus, lettuce, squash |
| Alsike clover | 140 | Sweet potatoes |
| Red clover | 140 | Sweet potatoes |
| Sweet white clover | 160 | Sweet potatoes and above |
| Alfalfa | 250 | Corn, beans, tomatoes and all above |

**Critical timing**: Before flowering, up to 60% of a legume's nitrogen is in its leaves (only half from root nodules). After seed pods form, 70% has moved to seeds and only 8% remains in leaves. The roots and nodules retain only 3-6%. **Till in or cut before flowering** for maximum nitrogen release.

**Myth: Beans Feed Corn** — The idea that beans share nitrogen with corn in the same season is largely false. Legumes bank nitrogen for their seeds, not for neighboring plants. However, when legumes die and nodules decompose, nitrogen becomes available to subsequent crops. Rotation, not interplanting, is the effective strategy.

### Phosphorus (P)
- **Buckwheat** (Fagopyrum esculentum) — dissolves mineral P and excretes excess through roots; fibrous root system improves moisture retention in sandy soil
- **Mustards** (Brassica sp.) — deep taproot, excretes P from roots; improves drainage and tilth of clay soils
- **Alfalfa** — slow-release P improvement when left in field
- **Lupines** (Lupinus sp.) — access insoluble P with acid exudates
- **Comfrey** (Symphytum officinale) — deep-rooted P accumulator

Buckwheat, mustard, and alfalfa not only absorb phosphorus but exhibit "subsequent excretion" — they release absorbed P back into the soil through their roots, making it available to other plants.

### Potassium (K)
- **Bracken fern** (Pteridium aquifolium) — 25-55% potash in leaves
- **Comfrey** — very high K (makes excellent liquid feed)
- **Borage** (Borago officinalis) — K accumulator, bee forage
- **Nettles** (Urtica dioica) — K, Fe, N; excellent compost activator
- **Docks** (Rumex sp.) — K accumulator

### Calcium (Ca)
- **Oak leaves** (Quercus sp.) — accumulate Ca even in low-Ca soils
- **Dandelion** (Taraxacum vulgare) — deep taproot mines Ca
- **Chamomile** (Chamomilla recutita) — Ca accumulator
- **Clovers** (Trifolium sp.) — moderate Ca accumulation

### Iron (Fe)
- **Nettles** (Urtica dioica) — high iron
- **Docks** (Rumex sp.) — Fe accumulator
- **Horsetail** (Equisetum sp.) — silica and iron
- **Chickweed** (Stellaria media) — Fe and other trace minerals

### Broad-Spectrum Accumulators (Multi-Mineral)
- **Comfrey** — N, P, K, Ca, and trace minerals; the king of accumulators
- **Nettles** — N, Fe, K, Ca, Mg, S
- **Dandelion** — K, Ca, Fe, Cu, traces of many minerals
- **Yarrow** (Achillea millefolium) — Cu, K, P
- **Chicory** (Cichorium intybus) — deep taproot, multi-mineral
- **Burdock** (Arctium minus) — deep-rooted mineral miner
- **Plantain** (Plantago sp.) — Ca, Mg, S, K
- **Valerian** (Valeriana officinalis) — P, K, Ca

## How to Use Accumulators

### 1. Comfrey — The Homestead Workhorse
- Deep taproot (6-10 feet) mines subsoil minerals
- "Cut and come again" — harvest 4-5 times per season
- Use as: mulch (lay leaves around plants), compost ingredient, liquid feed (steep in water), [[fermented-plant-extract-fpe]]
- Contains allium poisoning risk for livestock — don't feed fresh to animals

### 2. Nettle — Compost Activator
- Add to compost pile to heat it up and add N
- Make nettle tea (steep in water 1-2 weeks) as liquid fertilizer
- Use as FPJ ingredient in [[knf-preparations]]
- Handle with gloves (stinging hairs)

### 3. Cover Crop Cycling
- Plant accumulator cover crops in rotation or between cash crop cycles
- Buckwheat (summer) + mustard (cool season) for P release
- Clover/vetch as winter cover for N
- Till in or crimp-kill before seed set for maximum nutrient release
- Zinc deficiency in Florida corn was corrected by letting zinc-accumulating weeds grow during fallow years, then tilling them in

### 4. Chop-and-Drop Mulch
- Grow accumulators as understory plants or border plants
- Periodically cut and drop as surface mulch
- Nutrients release slowly as material decomposes
- Works perfectly in [[no-till-gardening-method]] systems

### 5. Fermented Plant Extract
- Use accumulator plants as FPJ/FPE material in KNF
- Comfrey FPJ = excellent all-around growth stimulant
- Nettle FPJ = nitrogen and iron boost
- See [[fermented-plant-extract-fpe]] for process
