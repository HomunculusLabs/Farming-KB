## Evaporative Demand as a Unified Four-Factor Model for Mushroom Tropism

## Overview

In his 1982 study of *[[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] that collectively determine the evaporative demand
gradient across the stipe surface.

## The Four Factors

Badham explicitly identified evaporative demand as "a function of at least four
factors":

1. **Wind** — Airflow velocity and direction redistribute the boundary layer
   around the stipe. Higher wind speeds thin the boundary layer, increasing
   evaporative potential. In Badham"s wind tunnel, a velocity of 9 ± 3 cm/sec
   was sufficient to induce measurable anemotropic curvature.

2. **Humidity** — Relative humidity directly modulates the water vapor pressure
   deficit at the stipe surface. Badham maintained humidity at 94 ± 3% RH in the
   wind tunnel, demonstrating that even near-saturation conditions produce
   sufficient differential drying to drive curvature. The wet and dry bulb
   depression was measured using copper constantan thermocouples.

3. **Light** — Illumination heats the mushroom surface, increasing the kinetic
   energy of water molecules and promoting evaporation. Badham used "cool white"
   fluorescent light at approximately 0.002 calories/cm²/min — roughly one-tenth
   of natural field levels — and noted that if light were stronger, it could
   become the dominant factor of evaporative demand, potentially overriding wind
   effects.

4. **Temperature** — [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]] creates the water vapor pressure
   deficit that drives evaporation.

## The Boundary Layer Redistribution Mechanism

The core of Badham"s hypothesis is that wind creates an asymmetric boundary
layer around the stipe. The leeward (downwind) side develops a thicker boundary
layer than the windward side. This means:

- The windward side has thinner, more rapidly exchanged air, leading to greater
  evaporative demand
- The leeward side has a thicker, more stagnant air layer with higher local
  humidity
- The resulting differential in cell turgor or wall extensibility causes the
  stipe to curve toward the windward side (i.e., into the wind)

This mechanism could also explain why rotation of the basidiocarp perpendicular
to airflow eliminates anemotropic curvature — continuous rotation equalizes the
boundary layer distribution around the circumference of the stipe.

## Light as an Evaporative Factor: Implications for Phototropism

Badham suggested that the mechanism of phototropism in mushrooms may share the
same evaporative demand pathway. If light's primary effect on tropism is through
surface heating and increased evaporation (rather than through a dedicated
photoreceptor pathway), then:

- Phototropism and anemotropism would be manifestations of the same underlying
  mechanism, with different environmental stimuli creating the same type of
  differential evaporative gradient
- The illuminated side of the stipe would experience greater evaporative demand,
  causing curvature away from the light source
- This would explain why very low light levels (1/10 field intensity) showed
  limited phototropic effect in the experiments — insufficient heating to create
  a meaningful evaporative differential

However, Badham also acknowledged that small quantities of light might trigger
"hormonic responses" (hormonal cascades) independent of the evaporative pathway,
which would represent a separate mechanism entirely.

## Water Vapor as the Active Agent

Drawing on work with *Phycomyces* (Johnson and Gamow, 1971), Badham proposed
that water vapor itself — not merely the physical effect of drying — might be
the active signaling agent. In this view:

- The differential distribution of water vapor concentration around the stipe
  creates a chemical gradient that individual stipe cells can perceive
- Cells on the high-evaporation side respond by altered growth rates
- This is analogous to the [[ethylene-involvement-fungal-tropism-avoidance-response]] tropism avoidance response tropism avoidance response in *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*,
  where sporangiophores grow away from high-humidity barriers

Russo et al. (1975) further suggested that ethylene might be involved in the
avoidance response of *Phycomyces*, raising the possibility that other volatile
gases beyond water vapor participate in the tropic signaling cascade.

- Wind and water vapor are both necessary for the *Phycomyces* avoidance

  hypothesis

## Practical Implications for Cultivation

Airflow management is critical during early fruiting — even 9 cm/sec causes
stipe curvature. Humidity uniformity, light placement, and temperature gradients
within the growing chamber all contribute to differential evaporative demand and
tropic distortion of developing fruitbodies.
