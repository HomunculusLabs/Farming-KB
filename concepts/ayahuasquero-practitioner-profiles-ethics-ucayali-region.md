# Ayahuasquero Practitioner Profiles and Ethics in the Ucayali Region

## Overview

The Ucayali region of Peru, centered around the city of Pucallpa and the nearby town of Yarina, is home to a significant concentration of ayahuasca practitioners known as ayahuasqueros. These practitioners range from deeply respected community healers to opportunistic entrepreneurs catering to the tourist trade. The variation in character, motivation, and ethical conduct among ayahuasqueros presents a complex landscape for anyone seeking authentic ceremonial experiences.

R. Stuart's firsthand observations during a visit to the Ucayali region in November 2001 provide rare detailed portraits of individual practitioners and their practices. These profiles reveal the wide spectrum of integrity and competence that exists among ayahuasca practitioners serving the tourist market.

## Practitioner "B": The Elderly Shaman of Yarina

### Background and Practice

Practitioner "B" was an elderly shaman based in Yarina, a town near Pucallpa. His son served as the director of the family's religious practice and was giving workshops in the United States at the time of observation. During the son's absence, "B" assumed leadership of the nightly [[metzner-amazonian-ayahuasca-ceremonies-icaros-and-healing-methods]] held in the ceremonial hut in his backyard.

These nightly ceremonies attracted up to a couple dozen visitors and served both local community members and foreign tourists. The ceremonial hut provided a dedicated space for regular practice, suggesting an established operation with a stable client base.

### Character Assessment

Despite his position as a ceremonial leader, "B" was described as a frequent liar and a shoplifter who regularly asked for money or gifts at every opportunity. These character traits — dishonesty and opportunism — stand in tension with the spiritual authority he claimed through his [[amazonian-boa-spirits-and-water-beings-in-shamanic-practice]].

The observation that "B" was a habitual liar raises questions about the authenticity of his ceremonial practice and the trustworthiness of any guidance he might offer during ceremonies. It also illustrates a fundamental challenge of [[stuart-r-ayahuasca-tourism-cautionary-ethnography]]: the spiritual authority claimed by practitioners is largely self-assigned, and the tourist has limited means of verification.

### Training Context

During the observation period, two other ayahuasqueros were visiting "B's" group for a two-week training period. One was described as "a sweet and wonderful guy," but his partner was constantly hustling the observer for spare change. This pairing illustrates how the ethical quality of ceremonial practice can vary even within a single training group, and how commercial pressure can manifest alongside genuine spiritual dedication.

## Practitioner "E": The Honorable Man of San Francisco

### Background

In the Shipibo town of San Francisco — which proclaimed itself the "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca" with an enormous sign at its entrance — practitioner "E" was widely regarded as an honorable man. His reputation within the community distinguished him from practitioners whose primary motivation appeared to be financial gain from tourists.

### Practice Characteristics

"E" was observed conducting private services for his family, [[metzner-shamanic-singing-icaros-rhythm-and-entrainment]] in the darkness of his home at night. This practice of holding private family ceremonies alongside any tourist services suggests that his ayahuasca practice was rooted in genuine community and family tradition rather than being exclusively oriented toward commercial exploitation.

His pricing was relatively modest — about ten dollars to give ayahuasca to a tourist — compared to the thirty dollars charged by some other practitioners in the region. This lower price point, combined with his reputation for honor, suggests a practitioner whose primary commitment was to the ceremonial tradition rather than to maximizing revenue.

## B's Nephew: The Enterprising Shaman

### Economic Success

B's nephew represented the entrepreneurial end of the ayahuasca practitioner spectrum. He earned a couple hundred dollars per month catering to tourists — a substantial income in a community where his neighbors could not afford a thirty-cent taxi ride. His pricing structure reflected the economic power dynamic of [[ayahuasca-tourism]]: thirty dollars to serve a [[ayahuasca-ceremony-pricing-disparity-tourist-versus-local]] about two dollars for a Peruvian.

This fifteen-fold price differential between tourist and local rates reveals the degree to which ayahuasca tourism has created a dual economy. Practitioners who successfully cultivate a tourist clientele can achieve income levels far above the local norm, while those who serve only the local community remain in relative poverty.

### Community Impact

The economic disparity between tourism-focused practitioners and their neighbors has the potential to reshape [[dighton-aquatic-hyphomycete-conidia-community-dynamics]]. When one family member earns hundreds of dollars per month while nearby families struggle with basic transportation costs, the social fabric of the community can be strained. The concentration of tourism wealth in the hands of a few practitioners may generate resentment, envy, or pressure on other community members to enter the tourism market regardless of their genuine interest or qualification in shamanic practice.

## Practitioner "A": The Swindler

One practitioner identified as "A" shared his life history with the observer before inviting him to participate in a ritual. However, the observer declined the invitation after "A" swindled him on the price of a tobacco pipe. This incident illustrates how the commercial interactions surrounding ayahuasca tourism can extend beyond the ceremony itself, with practitioners seeking to extract additional income through various means.

The willingness to swindle a potential client suggests either a lack of concern for reputation or an assumption that tourists are sufficiently numerous and transient that losing one customer's business is inconsequential. Either interpretation reflects poorly on the ethical standards of the tourism market in the region.

## The Broader Ethical Landscape

### No Certification or Standards

The ayahuasca tourism industry in the Ucayali region operates without any formal certification process, licensing system, or quality control standards. There is no governing body that verifies a practitioner's training, competence, or ethical conduct. Anyone can claim to be an ayahuasquero, and the tourist has limited means of distinguishing between genuine practitioners and charlatans.

### Motivation Spectrum

The practitioners described above represent points along a spectrum of motivation:

- **Community healers**: Practitioners like "E" who serve their community first and accept tourists as a secondary source of engagement and income.
- **Family tradition bearers**: Practitioners like "B" who inherit a ceremonial role within their [[md-family-structure-activity]], regardless of their personal character.
- **Entrepreneurs**: Practitioners like B's nephew who are primarily motivated by the economic opportunity that tourism presents.
- **Opportunists**: Practitioners like "A" who combine ceremonial practice with petty dishonesty in their commercial dealings.

### The Information Asymmetry Problem

A fundamental challenge in ayahuasca tourism is the severe information asymmetry between practitioners and tourists. Practitioners know the local landscape, understand the [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]], and control the ceremonial experience. Tourists are typically unfamiliar with the language, culture, and pharmacology of ayahuasca, and must rely on the practitioner for virtually every aspect of the experience.

This information asymmetry creates conditions ripe for exploitation. Tourists cannot easily verify a practitioner's claims about their training, experience, or the quality of their brew. They cannot independently assess whether the dose they receive is appropriate or whether the ceremony follows authentic traditional practices. The dependency inherent in the tourist-practitioner relationship means that the ethical character of the practitioner becomes the primary safeguard against exploitation.

## Implications for the Ayahuasca Tourism Industry

The variation in practitioner ethics and competence has several implications for the broader ayahuasca tourism industry:

- **Reputational risk**: Dishonest or exploitative practitioners generate negative experiences that can damage the reputation of the entire regional tourism industry.
- **Self-selection**: Tourists who have negative experiences may share their stories, potentially deterring future visitors and reducing demand for all practitioners.
