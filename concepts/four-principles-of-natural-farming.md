# Four Principles of Natural Farming

The four principles of natural farming were formulated by Masanobu Fukuoka as the
foundational practices of his agricultural philosophy. These principles are not
techniques to be applied mechanically but rather guidelines for removing
unnecessary human intervention from the farming process. Fukuoka spent fifty years
demonstrating their validity on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan, achieving
above-average yields while eliminating the labor and inputs that conventional
agriculture considers essential.

The four principles are: no cultivation (no plowing), no fertilizer (neither
chemical nor prepared compost), no weeding (by hand or herbicide), and no
pesticides. Fukuoka later added a fifth element, no pruning of fruit trees,
extending the same logic of non-intervention to perennial crops.

## Principle One: No Cultivation

The first principle holds that plowing and tilling the soil are unnecessary and
harmful. Fukuoka observed that no plant in nature is so weak that it can only
germinate in plowed soil. Seeds in natural ecosystems germinate and grow without
any human tillage. The soil is naturally worked by the roots of plants, by
earthworms, and by other small animals and microorganisms that create channels
for air and water movement.

Plowing, in Fukuoka's analysis, is self-defeating. When a field is flooded with
water and stirred with a plow, the ground sets hard as plaster. This creates the
very condition that makes plowing appear necessary the following season. Farmers
thus create the conditions that make the plow useful, then credit the plow with
solving the problem it created. Natural farming maintains soil structure by
leaving it undisturbed. The result is a living soil ecosystem teeming with
microbial activity. Fukuoka reported that one gram of soil on his farm contained
approximately 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other soil-enriching
microbes.

## Principle Two: No Fertilizer

The second principle rejects both [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] and prepared compost.
Fukuoka argued that plants in nature grow without fertilizer, drawing nutrients
from the soil as it enriches itself through natural [[fungal-decomposition-processes]]. He
acknowledged that the ancient practice of using manure and compost does help
speed crop growth, but identified several problems.

[[teaming-with-nutrients-fertilizer-application]] depletes the land from which the organic material was
originally taken. Plants grown with fertilizer develop weaker tissues and lowered
resistance to disease and pests. Fertilizer applied to soil is usually less
effective than in laboratory experiments because field conditions involve complex
interactions that laboratory settings cannot replicate. The gains from fertilizer
application are local and temporary. When examined from a broader perspective,
fertilizer use degrades the long-term fertility of the soil.

In his own fields, Fukuoka maintained soil fertility by scattering uncut rice and
barley straw over the field after each harvest. This mulch decomposed naturally,
returning organic matter to the soil without the labor of compost preparation.
Chicken droppings were occasionally scattered over the straw when available, but
this was supplemental rather than essential.

## Principle Three: No Weeding

The third principle holds that weeding is unnecessary. Fukuoka challenged the
fundamental distinction between crops and weeds, arguing that nature does not
arbitrarily designate certain plants as undesirable. In his farming system, a
ground cover of white clover served as a living mulch that suppressed competing
vegetation while fixing nitrogen in the soil.

Rather than battling weeds, Fukuoka's approach used strategic plant succession.
By broadcasting clover and winter grain seeds over ripening rice before harvest,
he established a cover crop that outcompeted undesired plants. The clover cover
also protected the soil from erosion, retained moisture, moderated soil
temperature, and provided habitat for beneficial insects. When it was time to
plant rice, a brief flooding of the field for four to five days weakened the
clover enough for rice seedlings to break through.

## Principle Four: No Pesticides

The fourth principle rejects all chemical pesticides. Fukuoka argued that
although thousands of diseases may attack plants in fields and forests, nature
strikes a balance and there was never any need for pesticides. He observed that
in his own rice paddies, whenever a large outbreak of leafhoppers occurred, spider
populations multiplied proportionally, bringing the pest population back into
check. As long as natural enemies are present, a natural balance asserts itself.

He was particularly critical of the cascading ecological damage caused by
pesticide application. The first application of pesticide in a rice field might
fail to kill the target pest but would eliminate tens of thousands of young
spiders per square yard and cause swarms of fireflies and dragonflies to vanish.
Subsequent applications would kill off chalcid wasps, dragonfly larvae, tadpoles,
and loaches. Each application destroyed more of the natural predator network,
making future pest outbreaks more likely and more severe. Fukuoka noted that
hardly any agricultural scientists had investigated why insect damage in heavily
sprayed fields remained largely the same as in unsprayed fields.

## No Pruning

Though often discussed alongside the four principles, Fukuoka also advocated for
no pruning of fruit trees. He argued that a tree grows best in its natural form,
with branches that do not tangle, sunlight falling on every leaf, and the tree
bearing fully each year rather than in alternate years. Pruning disrupts the
tree's natural shape, creates wounds that invite disease, and stimulates
excessive vegetative growth at the expense of fruit production. The natural form
of a tree, with its erect central trunk, permits the best distribution of
sunlight and nutrients throughout the canopy.

## Interdependence of the Principles

The four principles are not independent rules but components of a unified system.
Eliminating any one of them can undermine the effectiveness of the others. When
pesticides are applied, for example, the natural predator network that keeps pest
populations in check is destroyed, making the no-weeding principle harder to
maintain because the ecosystem is no longer self-regulating. When the soil is
plowed, the [[soil-edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]] that fix nitrogen and suppress disease are
disrupted, making the no-fertilizer principle more difficult to sustain.

Fukuoka's system works because the principles reinforce each other. The clover
cover (no weeding) fixes nitrogen (no fertilizer), provides habitat for predators
(no pesticides), and protects soil structure (no tillage). The straw mulch from
the previous crop returns organic matter to the soil, suppresses weeds, and
provides the conditions for seeds to germinate through the clay pellets. The
entire system is designed as a self-sustaining cycle where each element supports
the others.
