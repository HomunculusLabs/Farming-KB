## Alternative: Mechanical Force Hypothesis

An alternative explanation is that the mushroom stipe responds directly to the mechanical force of the wind rather than to evaporative gradients. Dennison (1961) showed that *Phycomyces* sporangiophore growth responds to gravitational and centrifugal forces, demonstrating that physical forces can directly influence fungal tropisms. The wind could exert a bending moment on the stipe, and the growing cells could respond by elongating more on the compressed side (similar to how trees develop reaction wood).

The evaporative demand hypothesis and the mechanical force hypothesis are not mutually exclusive — both mechanisms could contribute to the observed anemotropic curvature. However, the observation that rotation eliminates curvature even while the mushroom continues to experience some wind contact (albeit equalized) is more consistent with the evaporative demand hypothesis.

## Implications for Mushroom Morphology

The evaporative demand framework has implications for understanding natural variation in mushroom morphology:

- Mushrooms growing in windy, exposed environments may develop more curved or leaning stipes compared to those in sheltered locations
- Species growing in very humid environments ([[fruiting-chambers-and-environmental-control]] promotes normal development, but excessive airflow may cause unwanted curvature
- **Humidity control**: Maintaining high humidity (90%+) reduces evaporative stress and allows more uniform growth
- **Lighting**: Even, diffuse lighting from above minimizes phototropic interference with geotropic growth
- **Watering**: The finding that cultures were watered when they lost more than 10 ml of water (determined by weight) highlights the importance of maintaining adequate moisture levels to prevent excessive evaporative demand from stressing the developing fruiting bodies
