# Evaporative Demand and Fungal Growth

Evaporative demand — the rate at which water evaporates from a surface into the surrounding air — is a fundamental driver of directional growth (tropism) in mushrooms. In *[[anemotropism-in-psilocybe-cubensis]]*, differential evaporative demand across the stipe surface appears to be the primary mechanism underlying anemotropic (wind-directed) curvature during early [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]]. This concept, proposed by Badham (1982), provides a unifying framework for understanding how multiple [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] — wind, humidity, light, and temperature — interact to control mushroom growth direction.

## The Evaporative Demand Hypothesis

The evaporative demand hypothesis states that asymmetric water loss across a growing stipe causes differential cell elongation, resulting in curvature toward the side experiencing greater evaporative demand. The hypothesis was developed to explain the observation that *P. cubensis* stipes grow into the wind in controlled wind tunnel experiments.

The physical basis for the hypothesis rests on boundary layer dynamics:

- **Boundary layer**: A thin layer of relatively still air adjacent to any surface, through which heat and water vapor must diffuse. The boundary layer is the primary resistance to evaporation from biological surfaces.
- **Windward side**: Airflow thins the boundary layer, reducing resistance to water vapor diffusion and increasing evaporative demand
- **Leeward side**: The boundary layer is thicker due to flow separation, increasing resistance and reducing evaporative demand

This asymmetric boundary layer distribution creates a water vapor concentration gradient across the stipe. Cells on the windward side lose water faster than cells on the leeward side, and this differential water loss may drive differential cell expansion through changes in turgor pressure, cell wall extensibility, or both.

## The Four Factors of Evaporative Demand

Badham identified four environmental factors that interact to determine the magnitude and spatial distribution of evaporative demand around the mushroom stipe:

### 1. Wind Velocity

Wind is the primary driver of boundary layer asymmetry. In still air, the boundary layer is symmetric around the stipe and no directional growth stimulus exists. As wind velocity increases:

- The boundary layer becomes progressively thinner on the windward side
- The difference between windward and leeward evaporative demand increases
- The anemotropic curvature becomes more pronounced

In Badham's experiments, a wind velocity of 9 ± 3 cm/sec was sufficient to produce clear anemotropic curvature. This relatively low velocity suggests that even gentle air currents in natural environments may influence mushroom growth direction.

### 2. Humidity

Relative humidity determines the water vapor concentration gradient between the stipe surface and the surrounding air. At very high humidity (approaching 100%), the evaporative demand is minimal regardless of wind speed, and the tropic response should be attenuated. At lower humidity, the gradient is steeper and the differential evaporative demand between windward and leeward sides is amplified.

Badham's experiments used 94 ± 3% RH — a very high humidity typical of the tropical environments where *P. cubensis* naturally occurs. Even at this near-saturation humidity, anemotropic curvature was observed, suggesting that the mushroom is sensitive to very small differences in evaporative demand.

### 3. Light

Light influences evaporative demand through two distinct mechanisms:

1. **Radiative heating**: Light absorbed by the stipe surface increases surface temperature, which increases the saturation water vapor pressure at the surface and thus increases the driving force for evaporation. This effect is independent of wind direction and can create its own gradient if light comes from one side.

2. **Hormonal effects**: Small quantities of light may trigger hormonal responses that influence cell growth independently of evaporative effects. Badham acknowledged this possibility but noted that the experimental light flux (approximately one-tenth of expected field conditions) was probably too low for significant radiative heating effects.

If light's only effect on tropism is through evaporative demand (mechanism 1), then phototropism and anemotropism share a common mechanistic basis. If hormonal effects are also involved (mechanism 2), the relationship is more complex.

### 4. Temperature

Temperature affects evaporative demand through its influence on the saturation water vapor pressure. Higher temperatures increase the air's capacity to hold water vapor, increasing the potential evaporative demand. Temperature also affects the metabolic rate of stipe cells, which may modulate their sensitivity to evaporative signals.

In Badham's experiments, temperature was maintained at 24.5 ± 1.5°C, which is within the optimal range for *P. cubensis* growth and near the natural temperature of its tropical habitat.

## Evidence Supporting the Evaporative Demand Hypothesis

Several observations from Badham's experiments support the evaporative demand mechanism:

1. **Windward growth**: Stipes consistently bent toward the wind, consistent with greater evaporative demand on the windward side driving greater cell elongation (or alternatively, reduced elongation on the leeward side)

2. **Rotation eliminates curvature**: When basidiocarps were rotated perpendicular to the wind at 3 rpm, the boundary layer asymmetry was equalized around the stipe, and growth proceeded straight upward ([[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]] dominated)

3. **Dark response**: Anemotropic curvature occurred in both light and darkness, ruling out phototropism as the sole mechanism and supporting a physical (evaporative) basis

4. **Sterile mutant response**: Sterile basidiocarps showed the same anemotropic curvature as fertile ones, indicating the response does not require gill-produced substances

5. **Phycomyces analogy**: Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated that wind and water vapor are both necessary for tropic responses in *Phycomyces*, supporting the general importance of evaporative demand as a fungal growth-regulating stimulus

## Alternative: Mechanical Force Hypothesis

An alternative explanation is that the mushroom stipe responds directly to the mechanical force of the wind rather than to evaporative gradients. Dennison (1961) showed that *Phycomyces* sporangiophore growth responds to gravitational and centrifugal forces, demonstrating that physical forces can directly influence fungal tropisms. The wind could exert a bending moment on the stipe, and the growing cells could respond by elongating more on the compressed side (similar to how trees develop reaction wood).

The evaporative demand hypothesis and the mechanical force hypothesis are not mutually exclusive — both mechanisms could contribute to the observed anemotropic curvature. However, the observation that rotation eliminates curvature even while the mushroom continues to experience some wind contact (albeit equalized) is more consistent with the evaporative demand hypothesis.

## Implications for Mushroom Morphology

The evaporative demand framework has implications for understanding natural variation in mushroom morphology:

- Mushrooms growing in windy, exposed environments may develop more curved or leaning stipes compared to those in sheltered locations
- Species growing in very humid environments ([[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]], near waterfalls) may show reduced tropic responses overall
- The relationship between evaporative demand and cell elongation may contribute to the variation in stipe thickness, length, and curvature observed both within and between species

## Cell-Level Mechanism: How Differential Water Loss Produces Curvature

The precise cellular mechanism by which differential evaporative demand translates into differential cell elongation remains to be fully characterized, but several plausible mechanisms exist:

1. **Turgor pressure differential**: Cells on the side with greater evaporative demand may develop lower turgor pressure as they lose water more rapidly. In plant cells, reduced turgor slows wall expansion. However, in fungal cells the relationship may be inverse — reduced turgor could trigger compensatory water uptake and osmotic adjustment that paradoxically drives greater elongation.

2. **Cell wall extensibility changes**: Differential water loss could alter the pH or ionic composition of the cell wall matrix on the windward side, activating or inhibiting expansin-like proteins that control wall extensibility. More extensible walls elongate faster.

3. **Stress-induced growth**: Mechanical stress from differential drying could stimulate growth on the stressed side through a thigmomorphogenic response. Fungi are known to alter their growth patterns in response to mechanical stimuli.

4. **Hormonal redistribution**: Evaporative gradients could drive the redistribution of endogenous growth regulators within the stipe, concentrating growth-promoting substances on the windward side. This is consistent with the general model of hormone-mediated tropisms in both plants and fungi.

Understanding which of these mechanisms operates in *P. cubensis* would require detailed cytological and physiological experiments that have not yet been performed.

## Connection to Cultivation Practice

For cultivators of *P. cubensis* and similar species, understanding evaporative demand has practical implications:

- **Air circulation**: Gentle air circulation in [[fruiting-chambers-and-environmental-control]] promotes normal development, but excessive airflow may cause unwanted curvature
- **Humidity control**: Maintaining high humidity (90%+) reduces evaporative stress and allows more uniform growth
- **Lighting**: Even, diffuse lighting from above minimizes phototropic interference with geotropic growth
- **Watering**: The finding that cultures were watered when they lost more than 10 ml of water (determined by weight) highlights the importance of maintaining adequate moisture levels to prevent excessive evaporative demand from stressing the developing fruiting bodies
