## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka provides a detailed firsthand account of a botrytis
fungus outbreak that devastated loquat orchards in his village, tracing
its origins to the disruption of the orchard microclimate and the
introduction of new pesticides after World War II. His investigation of
this incident led him to a broader and more disturbing conclusion: that
the widespread spraying of mutagenic pesticides on easily mutated
microbes poses a grave and largely unrecognized threat to agricultural
ecosystems. This page explores the loquat incident, the biology of the
botrytis fungus, and Fukuoka's experiments with cross-breeding pathogenic
fungi that demonstrate the real risk of creating new pesticide-resistant
pathogens.

## The Loquat Bloom Rot Incident

Fukuoka's village was noted for its Karakawa loquats. One year, while
touring the village with officials from the local agricultural
cooperative, someone remarked that the loquats had been hit again by cold
weather and were not blooming well. Finding this hard to believe, Fukuoka
stopped the car and went into the orchard to investigate. He found most of
the flower corollas rotted and noticed on these the spores of a botrytis
fungus. This was not cold-weather damage at all but a botrytis disease.

Fukuoka described how the problem could be addressed by spraying and
suggested two or three ways of doing so. The astonished head of the
horticulture cooperative immediately contacted the local agricultural
testing station and, with the whole village cooperating in a pesticide
spraying program, the organism was soon brought under control. The
loquats gradually came back and everything appeared to look rosy again.

## The Root Causes of the Outbreak

One critical question remained unanswered: why had this outbreak occurred
in the first place? Fukuoka's theory was that it was triggered by the
sudden spraying of a whole series of new pesticides following the war in
an attempt to control citrus diseases. The organism responsible was likely
a botrytis fungus, either Botrytis cinerea, which causes gray mold in
citrus fruit, or a variant of the same species. Several factors
contributed to the conditions that allowed the outbreak:

First, the interplanting of citrus trees in loquat groves due to the boom
in citrus production brought new fungal inoculum into the loquat
orchards. Second, the rapid transition in local orchards from clear
cultivation to mulching and sod cultivation created a soil surface
environment of increased moisture ideal for the propagation of microbes.
Third, the practice of thinning fruit, where young fruit were dropped to
the ground, provided colonizable substrate for the fungi. Fourth, the use
of Bordeaux mixture, which was effective against fungi, was discontinued
and replaced with new pesticides.

The fungus is partially saprophytic and inflicts serious damage when
present in large numbers. Causes for emergence are usually poor orchard
sanitation, excess humidity, low tree vigor, and entanglement of branches
and foliage. Since the single largest factor is the microclimate in the
orchard, the chief cause was probably excess humidity. Fukuoka notes that
he may have been partly to blame, as he had encouraged farmers after the
war to sow clover in citrus orchards and idle village land and to raise
goats, practices that resulted in sod orchards with higher humidity.

## The Sclerotium and Genetic Spore Danger

The fungus remains dormant throughout the winter in the corollas of fallen
flowers. Following this dormancy, the hyphae fuse to form a sclerotium
about the size of a poppy seed. A small mushroom forms within this
sclerotium, and in the mushroom is formed an ascospore, or spore-
containing sac. This sac measures less than one millimeter across and
contains eight tiny, genetically distinct spores. If the ascospores of
this fungus are octopolar, then it may be capable of producing more
variants than even the tetrapolar shiitake fungus.

This genetic complexity is what makes the botrytis fungus so dangerous
when subjected to mutagenic pesticide exposure. Each of the eight spores
in the ascospore is genetically distinct, meaning that a single fruiting
body can produce eight different genetic lineages in a single reproductive
cycle. When these spores are exposed to sublethal doses of mutagenic
pesticides, the survivors may carry genetic mutations that confer
pesticide resistance or increased pathogenicity.

## The Danger of Pesticide-Resistant Mutants

Although new strains of advanced animals and plants do not arise easily,
the chances of this happening in lower bacteria and fungi are very great
and can lead to frightening consequences. Spraying pesticides with high
residual toxicity and mutagenic chemicals onto easily mutated microbes is
asking for trouble, for who knows what strange mutants may arise. The
result may very well be new pesticide-resistant pathogens and highly
parasitic microbes.

Fukuoka supports this warning with a personal experiment. Because the
resin disease fungus that attacks lemon and grapefruit trees grown in the
United States and the fungus that attacks Satsuma oranges and summer
oranges in Japan bear different scientific names, he initially thought
they were different species. But when he tried crossing them, mycelial
conjugation took place and ascospores were formed. By crossing these eight
spores in various ways, he was able to produce different strains of the
fungus, demonstrating that pathogenic fungi classified as separate species
can in fact interbreed and produce novel genetic combinations.

## The False Victory of Chemical Control

The villagers celebrated after the spraying program brought the botrytis
disease under control, but Fukuoka questions whether they truly had cause
for celebration. Having identified the problem as a botrytis disease and
sprayed with strong pesticides such as zineb, organoarsenic, or organo-
chlorine agents and applied herbicides, farmers were now rejoicing that
the disease had been brought under control. But the chemical control
addressed only the symptom, not the cause.

humidity, disrupted microbial communities, and the presence of
mutagenic chemicals in the environment, remained unchanged or were
actually worsened by the spraying program. The pesticides killed not only
