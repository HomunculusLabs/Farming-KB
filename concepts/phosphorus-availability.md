# Phosphorus Availability

## Overview

Phosphorus (P) is critical for plant energy (ATP), DNA, RNA, and cell membrane integrity. Despite its importance, phosphorus is notoriously unavailable in most soils. An acre of soil might contain only 1 pound of phosphorus actually dissolved in water and available to plants. Understanding why -- and how to work around it -- is essential for gardeners.

## Why Phosphorus Gets Tied Up

### Chemical Fixation

Inorganic phosphorus from weathered rock (primarily apatite) is adsorbed to the surfaces of clay particles and organic matter through ionic attraction, binding it tightly in place. Phosphorus does not move much in soil as a result. Some positively charged areas on clay sheet surfaces attract phosphate anions despite their negative charge. In tropical soils with highly weathered clays, even sulfates and nitrates can be held.

### pH Lockup

At high pH (alkaline conditions), phosphorus reacts with calcium to form insoluble calcium phosphates. At low pH (acidic conditions), phosphorus reacts with iron and aluminum to form insoluble compounds. The "sweet spot" for phosphorus availability is generally between pH 6.0 and 7.0.

### Peak Phosphorus Concern

More than 22 million tons of phosphorus are applied to agricultural fields annually. The Global Phosphorus Research Initiative estimates that mined supplies may be insufficient in only 30 to 40 years, with 95 percent of remaining mineable phosphorus concentrated in Morocco, China, South Africa, Jordan, and the United States.

## How Plants Access Phosphorus

### Root Growth

Because phosphorus is so immobile in soil, roots must continuously grow to maintain contact with new phosphorus sources. Root interception is the primary way plants access fixed phosphorus.

### Mycorrhizal Fungi

Around 95 percent of all plants form [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide|mycorrhizal]] associations that are critical for phosphorus uptake. Mycorrhizal fungi:

- Extend the effective surface area of root systems by 700 to 1000 times
- Produce acids that unlock, retrieve, and transport chemically locked-up phosphorus back to the host plant
- Form intricate webs that can carry phosphorus to roots of different plants, not just their host
- Access phosphorus in soil pores too small for roots to reach

The plant provides carbohydrates (from root exudates) to the fungi in exchange. Without mycorrhizal fungi, plants cannot obtain the quantities and kinds of phosphorus needed to perform at their best.

### Phosphate-Solubilizing Bacteria

Some bacteria produce organic acids that dissolve phosphate minerals, making phosphorus more available. These are among the biofertilizers that can be applied to improve phosphorus availability.

## Organic Sources of Phosphorus

| Source | N-P-K | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| Bone meal | 3-15-0 | Slow-release, good for banding |
| Bat guano (high P) | 0-10-0 | Readily available phosphorus |
| Colloidal rock phosphate | 0-2-0 | Very slow release, good for banding in autumn |
| Crab shell meal | variable | Contains chitin that feeds fungi |
| Fish meal | 10-6-2 | Moderate P, also provides N |

## Application Strategy

Because phosphorus is so immobile, broadcasting it on the surface is highly inefficient -- it stays where placed and rarely reaches root zones. Banding (placing phosphorus 2 inches away from and below seeds or transplants) increases uptake by approximately 50 percent compared to broadcasting.

## Deficiency Symptoms

- Stunted growth (no energy for cell production via ATP)
- Bluish-green leaf color (accumulation of unused sugars)
- Poor root development and blooming
- Symptoms appear first in older leaves (phosphorus is mobile in plants)

## Key Points

- Phosphorus is one of the most immobile nutrients in soil
- Mycorrhizal fungi are the primary biological solution for phosphorus access
- pH management is critical for phosphorus availability
- Banding phosphorus near root zones is far more efficient than broadcasting
- Peak phosphorus concerns make conservation and recycling increasingly important
