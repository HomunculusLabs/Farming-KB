### Rhizomorphic vs. Cottony Growth

- **Rhizomorphic mycelium** (ropey, braided strands): Indicates a vigorous, fruiting-capable strain. Selecting rhizomorphic zones for transfer encourages continued strong growth. This type of mycelium directly supports primordia formation
- **Cottony mycelium** (tomentose, aerial growth): Often indicates lower vigor or an aging strain. When a once-rhizomorphic strain becomes cottony, it is actively degenerating

### Color Changes as Health Indicators

Most mushroom species produce mycelia that undergo characteristic color transformations as they age. Cultivators must learn the natural progression of colorations for each species:

- **Green**: Universally bad in mushroom culture. Indicates Penicillium, Aspergillus, or Trichoderma contamination
- **White**: Most common color for healthy saprophytic mushroom mycelium
- **Yellow**: Normal aging in many species (Lion's Mane, some Oysters)
- **Pink**: Can indicate specific species characteristics or bacterial contamination
- **Blue/Purple**: Seen in some Psilocybe species and their relatives

### Other Mycelial Indicators

- **Hyphal aggregates**: Favorable formations in Shiitake that often evolve into primordia
- **Sclerotia**: Dense spherical structures (particularly in Morels) that indicate strong strain vitality
- **Sectoring**: Development of diverging zones with different growth patterns signals genetic instability and imminent degeneration
- **Volunteer fruiting**: Formation of miniature mushrooms on agar indicates a strain ready and willing to fruit

## Evaluating Strain Characteristics

### Rate of Growth

Faster-growing strains generally:
- Colonize substrate more quickly, reducing the window for contamination
- Reach fruiting sooner
- Often (but not always) produce higher yields
- Must be balanced against other quality characteristics

### Morphology on Agar

- Dense, thick growth is generally preferable to thin, wispy growth
- Rhizomorphic strands indicate strong fruiting potential
- Zonations (concentric rings) can indicate circadian growth cycles but may also signal uneven nutrition
- Uniform growth without sectoring indicates genetic stability

### Primordia Formation

The ability to form primordia (the earliest visible stage of mushroom formation) on agar media is a valuable trait:
- Indicates the strain is in a fruiting state of mind
- Suggests strong enzymatic activity
- Allows early screening of fruiting potential without committing to full grow cycles

### Volunteer Fruiting on Supplemented Sawdust

Many strains produce mushrooms within incubation bags without environmental triggers:
- [[medicinal-mushroom-guide-lions-mane]]: Dense, star-like zones form
- [[medicinal-mushroom-guide-lions-mane]] (Hericium erinaceus)

- ATCC #62771 is an excellent, high-yielding strain
- Wild collections vary significantly in fruitbody size at maturity
- Clone from mid-section of stem of very young specimens for best results
- Mycelium forms distinctive triangular zones of collected rhizomorphs
- Requires liquid culture techniques for efficient spawn generation due to slow growth on agar

### [[ganoderma-lucidum-reishi-comprehensive-profile]] (Ganoderma lucidum)

- Yellow, red, purple, and black strains available
- Forintek's 34-D: Popular North American strain producing reddish-brown fruitbodies
- ATCC #52412: Used as isotype in taxonomic discussions
- Clone from young fruitbodies using a sturdy, razor-sharp surgical scalpel (woody texture requires sharp tools)
- Recovery ("leap-off") from tissue culture may take two weeks

### maitake-cultivation|Maitake (Grifola frondosa)

- One of the more challenging gourmet species to cultivate indoors
- Strains vary in their preference for substrate composition and environmental parameters
- The species produces cottony mycelium typical of polypores

## Strain Preservation and Longevity

To maintain strain quality over time:
- Keep master cultures at low P-values (P1-P3 ideally)
- Store multiple backup slants in refrigeration
- Use cryogenic (liquid nitrogen) storage for irreplaceable strains
- Periodically test stored strains for viability by subculturing
- Limit commercial production cultures to P10 maximum
- Add end-substrate extracts to agar media to maintain enzymatic readiness

## Sourcing Strains

Strains can be obtained from:
- Commercial culture libraries and spawn suppliers
- Wild collection and tissue cloning
- Spore germination (produces new genetic combinations)
- Exchange with other cultivators

Wild cloning captures proven, locally-adapted genetics. Spore germination allows strain development but introduces genetic variability requiring screening.
