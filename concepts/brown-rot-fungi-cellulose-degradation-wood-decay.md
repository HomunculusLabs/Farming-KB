Brown rot fungi represent a specialized guild of wood-decaying basidiomycetes that selectively decompose cellulose while largely leaving lignin intact. Understanding their biology is essential for forestry, wood preservation, and selecting appropriate fungal species for bioremediation applications. Brown rot and white rot fungi constitute the two primary strategies of wood decomposition, each targeting different structural components of woody tissue.

## The Brown Rot Strategy

Only about 7 percent of mushroom species are brown rot fungi, and of those, approximately 70 percent are polypores. Brown rot fungi employ extracellular enzymes that break down the white, pulpy cellulose in wood, leaving behind the brownish lignin (hence the name). This selective removal of cellulose produces the characteristic checkered, cubical cracking and shrinking seen in affected wood, a pattern commonly observed on downed conifer trees in forest ecosystems.

## Common Brown Rot Species

Several brown rot species are frequently encountered in forest and built environments:

- Multicolored gilled polypore (Lenzites betulina)
- Large lentinus (Lentinus ponderosus)
- Sulphur tufts (Laetiporus sulphureus and Laetiporus conifericola)
- Velvet polypore (Phaeolus schweinitzii)
- Split-gill polypore (Schizophyllum commune)
- Agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)
- Dry rot house wreckers (Serpula lacrymans and Serpula himantioides)

Sulphur tufts (chicken of the woods) are among the most visible and recognizable brown rot fungi, producing brilliant orange shelf-like fruiting bodies on living and dead hardwood trees.

## Comparison with White Rot Fungi

White rot fungi are more numerous than brown rotters and employ a fundamentally different decay strategy. White rot fungi produce enzymes that break down the recalcitrant brown lignin fiber, leaving the cellulose largely intact and giving the wood a bleached, white appearance. Key white rot species include:

- Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)
- Maitake (Grifola frondosa)
- Turkey tail (Trametes versicolor)
- Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)
- Artist conk (Ganoderma applanatum)
- Crust fungus (Phanerochaete chrysosporium)

## Species with Mixed Decay Strategies

Some mushroom species produce both types of rot, leaving a mottled white and brown discoloration in the wood. This makes identifying rot types tricky in the field. Bear's head (Hericium abietis) causes a white rot in the core of a stump while the outer regions become brown. The clustered woodlover (Hypholoma capnoides), though belonging to the Strophariaceae family of white rotters, is frequently found emerging from dark-colored wood that appears brown-rotted.

## Enzymatic Machinery

The enzymatic systems of brown rot and white rot fungi differ significantly. White rot mushrooms produce lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases as their primary decay enzymes. Notably, only white rot mushrooms seem to produce manganese-dependent peroxidase, an enzyme that mineralizes wood and is particularly efficient in breaking down complex aromatic pollutants.

Brown rot fungi rely primarily on cellulases and hemicellulases, often employing a non-enzymatic Fenton chemistry system involving hydroxyl radicals generated from hydrogen peroxide and iron to depolymerize cellulose. This radical-based mechanism allows brown rot fungi to attack cellulose without needing to fully penetrate the lignin barrier.

## Relevance to Mycoremediation

White rot mushrooms are the primary mycoremediators of toxins held together by hydrogen-carbon bonds, owing to their lignin-degrading enzyme systems. Brown rot fungi, while less commonly employed in mycoremediation, play crucial roles in carbon cycling and soil formation. Understanding the distinction between decay types is essential for selecting the appropriate fungal species for specific remediation targets.
