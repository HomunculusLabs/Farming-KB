# Shamanic Healing and Divination Paradigms

Ralph Metzner's comparative analysis of hallucinogenic drug use across cultures
identified shamanic rituals of healing and divination as one of four major
paradigms for working with psychoactive plants and fungi. While Western
psychotherapy and indigenous shamanism may use similar substances, Metzner found
"profound differences between them in underlying worldview and assumptions about
the nature of reality."

## The Shamanic Ceremonial Form

Traditional shamanic [[darkness-low-light-facilitation-entheogenic-ceremonies]] are carefully structured experiences
in which a small group (typically six to twelve people) come together with
respectful, spiritual attitudes to share a profound inner journey of healing and
transformation. A "journey" is the preferred metaphor in shamanistic societies for
what Western psychology calls an "altered state of consciousness."

Three features distinguish shamanic ceremonies from Western psychedelic therapy:
minimal or no talking among participants (except in preparatory or integrative
phases); singing or chanting by the shaman, considered essential to success; and
darkness or low light, which facilitates the emergence of visions. The exception
is the peyote ceremony, conducted around a fire, where participants may see
visions as they stare into the flames.

The rhythmic chanting, like shamanic drumming widespread in Northern Hemisphere
shamanic societies, "seems to give support for moving through the flow of visions,
and minimizes the likelihood of getting stuck in frightening or seductive
experiences."

## Variations Across Indigenous Traditions

Metzner documented several major variations of [[metzner-five-experience-features-entheogenic-shamanic-practice]] ceremony:

**Peyote ceremonies** of the Native American Church: participants sit in a circle
around a blazing fire in a tipi, with a roadman presiding, assisted by a drummer,
firekeeper, and cedar-man. Almost constant singing of traditional peyote songs
continues throughout the night.

**[[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]] (velada):** participants sit or lie in a very dark
room with only a small candle. The healer sings almost uninterruptedly throughout
the night, weaving the names of Christian saints, spirit allies, and the spirits
of the Earth, elements, animals, and plants into the chants.

**Amazonian ayahuasca ceremonies:** a small group sits in a circle in
semidarkness while initiated healers sing *icaros*—songs through which healing
and diagnosis takes place. Shamanic "sucking" methods for extracting toxic psychic
residues or sorcerous implants are sometimes employed.

**Bwiti iboga ceremonies** in Gabon and Zaire: an altar with ancestral and deity
images, people sitting on the floor with chanting and dancing. A mirror in the
assembly room allows initiates to "see" their ancestral spirits.

**San Pedro cactus ceremonies** in the Andes: sometimes around a fire with
singing, sometimes with the curandero setting up an altar with symbolic figurines
representing light and dark spirits.

## Five Common Experiential Features

Metzner identified five features common to shamanic entheogenic experiences:

1. **The central role of the guide:** must be a person with extensive personal
   experience, who provides initiatory experiences. The guide does much or all
   of the singing, which profoundly shapes the quality and content of the
   experience.

2. **Multi-level healing:** physical, psychic, and spiritual healing occur,
   with three main variations—extraction of a toxic implant placed by sorcery,
   retrieval of a split-off psychic fragment or "soul," and dismemberment
   followed by reconstitution with a healthier body.

3. **Access to hidden knowledge:** divination, "seeing," prophecy, or
   visioning—equivalent to diagnosis in [[fukuoka-eastern-western-medicine-healing-world-crisis]]. Indigenous healers
   refer to the plants as "plant teachers" that communicate interiorly.

4. **Access to metaphysical realms:** the "inner world," "spirit world," "upper
   or lower world," "dreamtime," or "otherworld." Boundaries between worlds
   become more permeable during the experience.

5. **Perception of spirit beings:** entities associated with particular animals,
   plants, trees, places, ancestors, or other nonordinary beings. Healing is
   experienced as done by or with the assistance of these "allies," "power
   animals," or "guardians."

## The Worldview Challenge

Metzner emphasized that two elements in shamanic traditions pose "the most direct
and radical challenge to the accepted Western worldview": the belief in multiple
realities or worlds accessible in expanded [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]], and the belief
that spirits encountered in dreams and visions are just as real as the physical
organism. Such conceptions are "considered completely beyond the pale of both
reason and science" in the Western paradigm, yet they become "quite common" in
the hybrid shamanic-therapeutic rituals that have been developing in recent
decades.

## Initiation and Training

The role of the guide or shaman is always described as central and essential in
shamanic societies. This must be a person with extensive personal experience in
the use of these medicines, who agrees to provide an initiatory experience to a
seeker or training to an apprentice. Typically, a shamanic healer working with
entheogenic plants undergoes a lengthy initiation and training—sometimes lasting
years—under the guidance of an experienced elder before working with others.

This requirement mirrors the consensus in Western psychedelic therapy that the
therapist must have prior personal experience with the substances. In virtually
all entheogenic rituals, the guide does much or all of the singing, and this
singing profoundly shapes the quality and content of the experience.

## Comparison with Western Psychotherapy

In comparing Western psychoactive-assisted psychotherapy with shamanic entheogenic
[[shamanic-entheogenic-healing-rituals]], Metzner found that the role of an experienced guide is equally
central in both, and the importance of set (intention) and setting is implicitly
and explicitly recognized in both. Healing and insight may take place with both
approaches, though the underlying paradigms of illness and treatment are
completely different.

The role of divination is implicitly acknowledged in the Western models through
the amplifier analogy and the microscope metaphor, but the shamanic recognition
of multiple dimensions and spirit beings represents a fundamentally different
ontology. Metzner noted there is "a great deal of as-yet-poorly or not-at-all
understood about the processes of illness and healing" in both paradigms.
