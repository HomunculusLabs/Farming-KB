# Mycorrhizal Fungi in AACT

Elaine Ingham addresses the role of mycorrhizal fungi in relation to compost tea (AACT) with both enthusiasm and important caveats. While compost and compost tea strongly support mycorrhizal function, AACT itself does not typically contain viable mycorrhizal spores — the benefit comes from creating conditions where existing mycorrhizal fungi can thrive.

## Do Compost and Compost Tea Help Mycorrhizal Fungi?

"A resounding yes." The mechanisms include:
- **Food resources**: Compost and tea provide nutrients VAM, ectomycorrhizal, and ericoid fungi can draw from
- **Moisture maintenance**: They help keep soil moist, providing an evaporation barrier
- **Building blocks**: They supply materials that help mycorrhizal fungi build macroaggregates (critical for soil structure)
- **Inter-plant support**: They feed mycorrhizal fungi during periods when the host plant isn't providing carbon

## Colonization Thresholds

SFI data shows clear relationships between mycorrhizal colonization and root health:

| Colonization Level | Status | Action Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Below 10–15% | Low | Inoculum of mycorrhizal spores placed next to roots |
| 15–40% | Moderate | Add humic acids (fungal foods) to feed existing fungi |
| Above 30–40% | Good | Root disease problems, root-feeding nematodes begin to disappear |

When VAM colonization is below 40%, root disease problems, root-feeding nematodes, and other root pests are prevalent. Once colonization exceeds 30–40%, these problems begin to resolve.

## Mycorrhizal Spores Cannot Be Delivered via AACT

Mycorrhizal fungal spores are too large and too few to be effectively delivered through compost tea. The spores:
- Must be placed directly next to roots or on/around seed
- May not survive the brewing process
- Hang up on organic matter when applied as a soil drench (they don't reach roots)
- Require specific conditions to germinate and establish colonization

This is a common reason for "failed" mycorrhizal inoculation — the spores were applied incorrectly.

## Terminology

Ingham emphasizes correct terminology:
- **Mycorrhizal fungus**: The fungus alone, not associated with a plant
- **Mycorrhiza**: A single association of root and mycorrhizal fungus
- **Mycorrhizae**: Plural — all associations of roots and fungi

## Application Methods

Mycorrhizal fungi can be applied in many ways depending on plant, system, and requirements:
- Direct placement next to roots at planting
- Seed coating with spore mixes
- Root dip for transplants
- Banding in the root zone
- Injection for established trees

There are conditions where surface application works perfectly and conditions where spores would never reach roots. The key is getting spores next to the root without harming roots or killing spores, along with fungal foods.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Fungi

- **VAM (Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhizae)**: Associated with most crop plants, vegetables, and grasses
- **Ectomycorrhizae**: Associated with trees, especially conifers and many hardwoods
- **Ericoid mycorrhizae**: Associated with plants in the Ericaceae family (blueberries, rhododendrons)

Applying the wrong type is ineffective — ectomycorrhizal fungi on strawberries will do no good, for example.

## Cover Crops and Mycorrhizal Support

Non-host cover crops for specific nematodes must also be compatible with mycorrhizal colonization. The root system must be colonized with mycorrhizal fungi. Beneficial fungi from aerobic compost include nematode-trapping and parasitic fungi that help control root-feeding nematodes.

## Why Mycorrhizal Inoculation "Fails"

Common reasons cited in literature for failure, analyzed by Ingham:
- Spores applied to soil surface without reaching root zone
- Wrong type of mycorrhizal fungi for the plant species
- Overloading seedlings with too many spores (Goldilocks problem)
- No fungal foods provided alongside spores
- Soil conditions (compaction, chemistry) incompatible with fungal growth
- The researchers didn't actually know how to properly apply or grow the fungi
