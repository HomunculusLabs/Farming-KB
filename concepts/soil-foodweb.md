# Soil Food Web

The soil food web is the complex community of organisms living in soil — bacteria, fungi, protozoa,
nematodes, microarthropods, and larger organisms — that interact with each other and with plants in a
mutually beneficial ecosystem. The concept was popularized and extensively researched by Dr. Elaine R.
Ingham, founder of Soil Foodweb Inc. and one of the world's foremost soil microbiologists.

## Overview

Healthy soil is not merely a growing medium; it is a living ecosystem. The soil food web describes the
interconnected feeding relationships among soil organisms and how those relationships affect plant
health, nutrient cycling, and [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]]. In natural ecosystems, undisturbed soils maintain
robust food webs. Conventional [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] — tillage, [[teaming-with-nutrients-natural-vs-synthetic-fertilizers]], pesticides —
decimate these communities, creating dependence on chemical inputs.

## Key Organism Groups

### Bacteria

Bacteria are the most numerous organisms in soil, with populations ranging from 100 million to 1 billion
per gram of healthy soil. They are primary decomposers that break down simple [[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]] — sugars,
proteins, and carbohydrates. Bacterial-dominated soils are characteristic of early successional
ecosystems and are preferred by annual crops, brassicas, and leafy vegetables. Beneficial bacteria
compete with pathogens for food resources and root exudate space, produce antibiotics that suppress
disease organisms, and retain nutrients in their biomass that would otherwise leach.

### Fungi

Fungi are the dominant decomposers in mature ecosystems — forests, prairies, and perennial plant
communities. They break down complex organic materials including lignin, cellulose, and woody debris.
Fungal hyphae create extensive networks that transport nutrients over distances, effectively increasing
the root zone of plants. Fungal-dominated soils are preferred by perennials, trees, and woody plants.
The ratio of fungi to bacteria is a key indicator of soil health and suitability for different plant
types.

### Protozoa

Protozoa — flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates — are single-celled predators that consume bacteria.
This predation is critical for nutrient cycling: when protozoa eat bacteria, they release excess
nitrogen and other nutrients in plant-available forms (ammonium). A single protozoan can consume
thousands of bacteria per day. Their grazing activity stimulates bacterial growth and turnover,
maintaining an active and dynamic [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]].

### Nematodes

Nematodes are microscopic roundworms that occupy multiple trophic levels in the soil food web.
Bacterial-feeding nematodes function similarly to protozoa, cycling nutrients by consuming bacteria
and releasing plant-available nitrogen. Fungal-feeding nematodes regulate fungal populations and
contribute to decomposition. Predatory nematodes feed on other nematodes and help control
plant-parasitic species. Root-feeding nematodes can be problematic, but healthy food webs keep
their populations in check through predation and competition.

### Microarthropods

Mites, springtails, and other microarthropods are the larger visible members of the soil food web.
They feed on fungi, decaying organic matter, and each other. Their activity fragments organic
material, increasing surface area for [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] colonization. They also help distribute
fungal spores and [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] throughout the soil profile.

## Nutrient Cycling

In a healthy soil food web, nutrients are retained in organism biomass rather than leaching away.
Bacteria and fungi immobilize nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and other elements in their bodies.
When [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] consume these microbes, they excrete excess nutrients in
plant-available forms. This natural nutrient cycling means that plants in biologically active soil
have continuous access to nutrients without [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] inputs.

The process works as follows: plant roots exude simple sugars and amino acids that feed bacteria and
fungi. These microorganisms multiply around the root zone (rhizosphere). Protozoa and nematodes are
attracted to the concentrated bacterial [[staycare-chitosan-and-fungal-biomass-derivatives-in-biosorption]]. As they feed, they mineralize nutrients
that plants immediately absorb. This is the "microbial loop" — a closed-cycle system where nothing
is wasted.
