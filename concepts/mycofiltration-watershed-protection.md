## Mycofiltration — Watershed Protection with Fungal Mats

Using mycelial mats to filter water, remove pathogens, capture sediments, and protect watersheds. From [[paul-stamets]]' pioneering work in *Mycelium Running* (2005). See also [[mycofiltration-overview]].

## The WSDOT Experiment: Proving the Concept

### Background

Washington State Department of Transportation (WSDOT) needed to address runoff from road construction sites contaminating watersheds. [[paul-stamets]] partnered with WSDOT for a landmark field trial.

### The Skookum Inlet Farm Tests

Stamets conducted tests at his farm on Skookum Inlet, Washington:

- **Pathogen target**: Coliform bacteria (including E. coli) from upstream farms
- **Primary species**: King stropharia (Stropharia rugoso-annulata)
- **Setup**: Mycelial mats installed in drainage pathways between upstreampollutionsources and downstream waterways
- **Results**: Mycelial mats significantly reduced coliform bacteria counts in water passing through them
- **Mechanism**: Mycelium's extracellular enzymes and antibacterial secretions killed or trapped bacteria; the physical mat structure filtered sediments

### E. coli Die-Off

One of the most significant findings:
- Water passing through Stropharia rugoso-annulata mycelial mats showed dramatic E. coli die-off
- The mycelium produces antibacterial compounds that target gram-negative bacteria
- The dense mycelial network physically traps bacteria and fine sediments
- This has profound implications for agricultural runoff, septic systems, and watershed protection

## Road Construction Runoff

### Tahuya River Road Reclamation

A demonstration project for filtering runoff from road construction:

- **Problem**: Road construction on steep slopes sends sediment-laden water into streams, suffocating salmon eggs and degrading water quality
- **Solution**: Installed mycofiltration barriers using bunker spawn of Stropharia rugoso-annulata and other species
- **Method**: Layered wood chips inoculated with mycelium across drainage pathways
- **Results**: Significant reduction in sediment and turbidity in downstream water

### Mycelial Mat Construction

Layered mycofilter design (from bottom to top):
1. **Gravel layer**: For drainage and structural support
2. **Myceliated wood chips**: Primary filtration layer — 4-12 inches deep
3. **Straw layer**: Additional filtration and erosion control
4. **Myceliated cardboard**: Sheet mulch barrier
5. **Wood chip/mulch top layer**: Protection and moisture retention

## Agricultural Applications

### Farm Runoff Filtration

Mushroom mycelial mats can be placed in drainage ditches, swales, and waterways to:
- Remove excess nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) from agricultural runoff
- Filter animal waste pathogens (E. coli, Salmonella, Cryptosporidium)
- Trap sediments and prevent erosion
- Reduce the need for constructed wetlands (which require more space)

### Manure Pile Filtration

Placing myceliated straw or wood chip barriers around manure piles:
- Captures nutrients before they leach into groundwater
- Reduces pathogen loading in runoff
- The mycelium benefits from the nutrient flow
- After the mycelium is spent, the enriched material becomes valuable compost

## Mercury Biosorption in Water

Arica et al. (2003) demonstrated using fungi for water filtration:

- **Species**: Trametes versicolor (turkey tail) and Pleurotus pulmonarius var. 'sajor-caju'
- **Method**: Combined mycelial beads with alginic acid to remove 97% of mercuric ions from water
- **Key finding**: Dead mycelium removed 73-81% of mercury — physical cellular architecture bonds with mercuric ions
- **Application**: Mycofiltration cartridges for industrial wastewater treatment

## Species Selection for Mycofiltration

| Species | Target | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| Stropharia rugoso-annulata (wine cap) | Bacteria, sediments | Best for E. coli; easy to grow on wood chips |
| Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster) | Hydrocarbons, bacteria | Most versatile; tolerates diverse conditions |
| Trametes versicolor (turkey tail) | Heavy metals, bacteria | Dead biomass works for mercury |
| Pleurotus pulmonarius (phoenix oyster) | Heavy metals | Mercury biosorption partner |
| Phanerochaete chrysosporium | Chemical contaminants | Lab-proven for diverse toxins |

## Bunker Spawn for Mycofiltration

The primary deployment method for mycofiltration installations:

1. Fill burlap sacks with wood chips (preferably from hardwood species)
2. Inoculate with commercial or naturalized spawn at 10-30% rate
3. Incubate outdoors in shade for several months until fully colonized
4. Install colonized bunker spawn in drainage pathways, ditches, or waterways
5. The mycelium leaps off onto surrounding substrates, expanding the filtration zone

See [[bunker-spawn-method]] for detailed construction.

## Limitations and Considerations

- Mycofiltration installations require maintenance — monitoring colonization, replenishing as needed
- Flow rates must be managed — too fast and water bypasses the mycelial mat
- Seasonal variations affect performance — cold temperatures slow mycelial activity
- The skill of a mushroom cultivator is critical to success (Sasek 2003)
- In situ trials have met with less success than lab studies, primarily because mycelia failed to colonize targeted substrates
- Native strains have advantages over imported ones — already acclimated to local conditions
