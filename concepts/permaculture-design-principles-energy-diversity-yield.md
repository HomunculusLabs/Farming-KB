# Permaculture Design Principles: Energy, Diversity, and Yield

The theoretical foundation of [[permaculture]] rests on a set of principles, directives, and observations derived from the study of natural systems. In his 1981 Design Course, [[bill-mollison]] laid out these principles systematically, distinguishing between axioms (established, self-evident truths), principles (basic truths or rules of conduct), laws (statements backed by verified hypotheses), and directives (practical guidance for proceeding). Together, these form the intellectual framework that distinguishes [[permaculture-design]] from both conventional agriculture and ad hoc gardening.

## The Energy Model: Source, Storage, and Sink

Mollison's energy model begins with the Earth as a system receiving a fairly constant energy input from the sun. Between the energy source and the energy sink lies the zone of human intervention — the area where design operates.

The core design task is to create useful storages between source and sink. The more effectively energy can be captured, held, and directed through multiple uses before it dissipates, the more productive the system becomes. Water illustrates the principle perfectly: if engineers store water in a valley (where it is least useful for distribution), they create a single-purpose reservoir. If instead they store water at the keypoint — high in the landscape, close to where it falls — they can run it through irrigation channels, animal watering systems, aquaculture ponds, domestic water supply, and microhydro generation before it reaches the valley.

Mollison emphasized that energies unusable in a mechanical sense are often highly usable in a biological sense. A designer needs both biological and mechanical storages. Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed — so the choice is always whether to store it or let it leave the system. Once released, recovery requires enormous additional energy input.

The closer to the source that intervention occurs, the more useful the network of storages and flows that can be established. This principle has direct implications for [[water-management]], [[fukuoka-three-dimensional-solar-energy-capture-rice-paddy]], nutrient cycling, and even social organization.

## Resource Classification

Mollison developed a hierarchy of resource types that governs how they should be managed:

### Resources That Increase with Use
Some resources actually grow when used. Information is the primary example — it is preserved and amplified by sharing, not depleted. Certain browse plants increase when grazed (if managed correctly). Some categories of plants and animals increase each other through mutual interaction. Annual grass pastures, if not used, decrease — they accumulate as wildfire fuel, then are consumed catastrophically.

### Resources Unaffected by Use
A scenic view can be observed all day without degradation. These resources are essentially infinite for practical purposes.

### Resources That Decrease with Use (Finite Resources)
Most conventional resources — soil, fossil fuels, minerals — fall into this category. They require careful management to maintain. Overuse leads first to diminishing returns, then to system collapse.

### Resources That Degrade Everything Else
The most dangerous category includes uranium, plutonium, and persistent organic pollutants like dioxins. Using these resources doesn't merely deplete them — it actively destroys other resources and biological systems. Mollison argued that any rational design system would exclude these from consideration entirely.

### The Overuse Principle
A critical insight governs all resource management: any integrated system can only accept that amount of energy (or resource input) that it can productively use. Beyond the point of productive use, additional input first produces diminishing returns, then actively harmful effects. You can over-manure a field, over-heat a greenhouse, over-plow a soil. Whether the input is money, manure, or water, the pattern is identical: initial increase in yield, then plateau, then decline, then collapse. The Hong Kong agriculture case demonstrated this starkly — modernization increased energy input by 800% for a 15% yield gain, and continued energy increases actually reduced yields to 4–6% of input recovery.

## Diversity: Connections, Not Count

Mollison offered one of his most important clarifications about diversity: "Diversity isn't involved so much with the number of elements in a system as it is with the number of [[component-analysis-and-functional-connections]] between these elements." This distinction is fundamental to [[mollison-rabbits-in-permaculture-design-and-soil-preparation]] is frequently misunderstood.

A system with 3,000 species but no functional connections between them is less stable and less productive than a system with 30 species and 3,000 beneficial interactions. Mollison illustrated this by analyzing a greenhouse connected to a chicken coop, identifying 129 beneficial connections between just these two elements — heat exchange, CO₂ cycling, manure production, insect control, food production, weed seed consumption, and many more.

Diversity of itself, without functional organization, provides no stability or advantage. Social groups with diverse but conflicting interests demonstrate this clearly. What matters is the degree of functional organization — the number of beneficial connections, not the number of components.

## Guilds and Functional Design

The practical expression of [[fungal-functional-diversity-decomposition]] is the guild — a group of elements (plants, animals, structures) that work harmoniously together. In a permaculture garden, elements serve multiple roles: some are manurial or energy-exchange systems for other elements; others provide defensive protection (pest deterrence, wind shelter, shade); some act as trellis systems or structural supports. The rules governing placement include orientation to sun and wind, zonation by frequency of use, and interaction effects between neighboring elements.

The goal is not to maximize species count but to maximize beneficial function. As Mollison put it: "It is the number of niches in a system that will allow a number of species and varieties to co-survive. It is the woodpecker's hole within the forest."

## Yield: Redefining Productivity

Mollison's definition of yield fundamentally departs from conventional agriculture: "Yield is the sum of useful energy stores. It is the sum of energy conserved and generated in systems. It is never just product yield — not the number of pounds of tomatoes, or pounds of fish, or of acorns — but the sum of the energy in useful storages."

This redefinition has profound implications. A conventional farmer measures yield in bushels per acre. A permaculture designer measures yield in total system energy — including soil organic matter accumulated, water stored, biodiversity supported, microclimate moderated, and social cohesion generated. Under this definition, yield is a function of design, and is theoretically unlimited. Mollison stated he had never seen a system where better design could not increase the yield.

Yield depends on the designer's understanding of the system. Understanding itself has multiple dimensions — intellectual, empathetic, observational. Different people perceive different aspects of reality (some handle symbols, others numbers, others spatial relationships), which is why Mollison advocated small group collaboration to bring different perspectives to bear on design problems.

## Order, Tidiness, and Creative Disorder

One of Mollison's most memorable observations distinguishes order from tidiness: "Tidiness is something that happens when you have frontal brain damage." Tidiness — the rigid, monofunctional, visually uniform landscape of conventional agriculture — reflects limited perception and functional disorder. Creativity, which arises from a "fairly whole brain," is inherently disorderly. The tolerance for disorder is, in Mollison's view, one of the few reliable signs of health.

The straight American cornfield and the tidy British garden both represent what Mollison called "extraordinary tidiness and functional disorder" — they look neat but yield poorly relative to their potential. What permaculture seeks is "creative disorder" — functional complexity that may appear messy to the tidiness-trained eye but actually produces more yield, more stability, and more resilience.

## The Necessitous Act Principle

Drawing on tribal lore, Mollison proposed that one should only carry out necessitous acts — that non-necessitous behavior tends to be very destructive. He illustrated this with a personal experiment: living in the bush and deciding not to cut down a tree unless absolutely necessary. He never had to. The principle extends to resource use generally: petrol, in Mollison's assessment, has almost never been productively used — it creates disorder in society and ecology alike. The few exceptions ("restitutional uses") are vastly outnumbered by wasteful and destructive applications.

## The Landscape Responds

Mollison observed that landscapes actively respond to disturbance in self-regulating ways. When fish are placed with palatable algae in a pond, they consume the preferred species until it is eliminated, then the unpalatable algae proliferate and suppress the fish population — algae destroys fish. When cattle graze a landscape, they selectively favor plants they dislike, which proliferate and eventually make the landscape inhospitable to cattle. This feedback dynamic operates at every scale, from ponds to continents.

This observation leads to a design principle: every intervention in a living system triggers a response, and the response is often disproportionately strong. "If you kick a living system, it kicks back harder." Design must therefore anticipate not just the immediate effect of an action but the systemic response it provokes.

## Design for Remedial Action

Against the backdrop of environmental crisis, Mollison argued that design for permanence means moving toward forests, permanent pastures, lakes and ponds, and non-tillage agriculture. The practical strategies include converting investment capital to low-energy systems, adding greenhouses to residences, creating self-reliant bioregions, and training large numbers [[preface-and-author-context-of-permaculture-designers-manual]]. The ethic is cooperative, not competitive; the knowledge is open, not proprietary; the timescale is urgent, not leisurely.
## Design Considerations

Permaculture design emphasizes working with natural patterns rather
than against them. Key principles include observation of energy
flows, stacking functions, and maximizing beneficial relationships.
Zone planning places frequently accessed elements closer to the
dwelling while less intensive activities occupy outer zones. Sector
analysis maps external energies to optimize element placement.
Effective designs integrate multiple outputs from each element,
creating resilient and productive biological systems.

## Practical Applications

Implementation requires careful site analysis followed by phased
development building soil fertility and ecological complexity.
Swales and earthworks capture and infiltrate water. Guild planting
combines species with complementary functions. Animals are integrated
as workers providing fertilizer, pest control, and food production.
Succession planning guides the evolution from pioneer species to
mature food forest systems over time.

## Challenges and Considerations

Implementing permaculture systems requires patience, as ecological
processes develop over years rather than single growing seasons.
Initial establishment costs can be significant, though long-term
operational costs decrease as systems mature and self-regulate.
Local climate, soil conditions, and available resources constrain
design options. Knowledge of local ecology and indigenous practices
informs appropriate species selection and management strategies.
Community support and knowledge sharing networks facilitate
successful implementation and ongoing adaptation.
