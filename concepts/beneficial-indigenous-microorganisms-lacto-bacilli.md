## Beneficial Indigenous Microorganisms (BIM) and Lacto Bacilli

## Overview

Beneficial Indigenous Microorganisms (BIM) are locally occurring microorganisms that are cultured and applied [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]] systems to improve soil health, plant vitality, animal welfare, and waste decomposition. Rather than purchasing commercially produced microbial inoculants, the natural farming approach empowers farmers to collect, culture, and multiply microorganisms already present in their local environment. This philosophy of self-reliance is central to the work of **Gil A. Carandang** of **Herbana Farms** in Burol, Calamba City, Laguna, Philippines, and is rooted in the teachings of his mentor, **Dr. [[korean-natural-farming]] Association.

## The Philosophy: Farming Self-Reliance

Dr. Han Kyu Cho articulated a vision of farming that restores independent authority to farmers. He observed that modern farmers had become dependent on purchased inputs, following programs dictated by sellers rather than exercising their own judgment. His call was to recover the spirit of farming by making and applying necessary farming materials from local resources. The core idea is that farmers should not need to buy microbial cultures — they can grow their own from materials already present in their environment.

This philosophy extends beyond agriculture into a broader agroecological worldview. While Western organic and biodynamic farmers focus on composts, cover crops, rotations, and cosmic influences, natural farmers of the East emphasize microbial farming — microorganisms, enzymes, energy (chi), and the balance of opposites (yin and yang). Health, in both plants and animals, is understood as a function of balance and harmony. Disease arises from imbalance; restoring microbial equilibrium restores health.

## What Are Beneficial Indigenous Microorganisms?

Beneficial Indigenous Microorganisms (BIM) are naturally occurring microorganisms — including lactic acid bacteria, rhizobia, yeasts, and other probiotic species — that are harvested from the local environment and cultured for agricultural use. They serve as the "ultimate digester, user, and re-users" in the nutrient cycle, breaking down organic matter, making minerals available to plants, and creating the conditions for sustained soil fertility.

The term "indigenous" is critical: these are not imported or exotic strains but microorganisms already adapted to the local climate, soil, and ecological conditions. By culturing locally sourced microbes, farmers ensure that the organisms they apply are already suited to thrive in their specific environment.

BIMs are used alongside **bionutrients** — fermented extracts from local plants, herbs, and weeds that serve as food for the [[bionutrients-fermented-plant-extracts]] for use as fertilizers, pest controls, and soil amendments. The system is built on principles of:

- **Self-reliance**: Farmers produce all necessary inputs from local materials
- **Microbial farming**: Microorganisms and enzymes are the primary agents of soil fertility
- **Balance and harmony**: Yin-yang philosophy guides the selection and application of inputs
- **Nutrient cycling**: Complete cycles with no waste, where decomposition feeds new growth

Carandang credited Dr. Cho as his mentor and also drew from the natural farmers of Japan, with whom he studied and apprenticed.

## Lactic Acid Bacteria: The Major Workhorse

Among the many [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] in the BIM spectrum, **lactic acid bacteria** (lacto bacilli) stand out as one of the most important and versatile. They are described as a "major workhorse" in natural farming due to their wide range of applications:

### Composting and Odor Control

Lactic acid bacteria thrive on the ammonia released during anaerobic decomposition — the compound most responsible for foul odors. By consuming ammonia, they effectively arrest unpleasant smells associated with decomposing organic matter. This makes them invaluable for composting operations of any scale, allowing waste to decompose without creating odor problems.

### Natural Piggery

In natural piggery systems, pig pens are constructed with floors made of compost, soil, and organic matter such as sawdust. The substrate is sprayed with lactic acid bacteria serum. There is no need to clean the pens of excretions and urine — the lactic acid bacteria process the waste in place. The result is an odor-free environment that simultaneously produces organic fertilizer as the bedding material breaks down over time.

### Poultry

As demonstrated at Herbana Farms, lactic acid bacteria are applied to poultry litter systems. The deep litter method, combined with regular BIM spraying, eliminates odors, reduces toxins, and converts waste into usable organic fertilizer. Chickens raised this way show improved health without the need for vaccines or antibiotics.

### Aquaculture

Lactic acid bacteria are also applied in aquaculture settings, where they help maintain water quality by breaking down organic waste and reducing harmful ammonia levels, creating healthier conditions for fish and other aquatic organisms.

### Plant Spraying

When sprayed directly onto plants, lactic acid bacteria colonize leaf surfaces and increase the activity of phyllosphere bacteria. These bacteria process organic matter (such as dust) on leaf surfaces, making nutrients directly available to the plant. Spraying BIMs has been observed to improve plant vigor, reduce pest pressure, and suppress [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]]

## Connection to Living Soil and Soil Fertility

The concept of **living soil** is central to the BIM philosophy. Carandang described living soil as "the alpha and omega, the beginning and end" of soil fertility. What makes soil alive is the existence of life forms — microorganisms. Without them, soil fertility and health cannot be created or sustained.

The role of BIMs in creating and maintaining living soil includes:

- **Nutrient cycling**: BIMs are the "start of the cycle, the ultimate digester, user and re-users." They break down organic matter, release bound minerals, and make nutrients available for plant uptake. Plants absorb these nutrients, and when plant material decomposes, the cycle begins again.
- **Mineral extraction**: Microorganisms open "mechanisms and bridges to extract natural minerals" from the soil matrix, making them available to plants. Plants store these minerals, which are later broken down and mineralized for future use.
- **Soil structure**: Microbial activity improves soil aggregation, water retention, and aeration — all critical for plant root health.
- **Disease suppression**: A healthy population of beneficial microorganisms outcompetes pathogens, creating an environment where diseases cannot establish.
- **Completing the nutrient cycle**: In well-managed systems (such as the Herbana Farms poultry operation), no waste is created. Organic matter is decomposed by microorganisms, nutrients are recycled, and fertility is maintained without external inputs.

The investment in "natural capital" — the soil itself — through microbial proliferation is the foundation of [[phosphate-accumulators-sustainable-agriculture]]. By culturing and applying BIMs, farmers actively build the living soil ecosystem that sustains long-term productivity.
