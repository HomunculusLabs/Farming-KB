# Fungal Responses to Grazing and the Sit-and-Wait Strategy

## Overview

Foraging cord-forming basidiomycetes face continuous pressure from soil invertebrate grazers, particularly Collembola (springtails). Grazing triggers dramatic changes in mycelial morphology, foraging patterns, and physiology — analogous to herbivory effects on plants. At the other extreme of the foraging spectrum, large, persistent [[mycelial-networks]] employ a "sit and wait" strategy, capitalizing on resources that arrive within their already-established territory. These responses highlight the remarkable plasticity and resilience of fungal mycelial systems.

## Invertebrate Grazing Effects

### Grazing Intensity and Species Dependence

The impact of Collembola grazing on mycelia depends on:

- **Grazing intensity** (density of grazers)
- **Collembolan species** (*Folsomia candida* has the largest effects)
- **Fungal species** (different cord-formers respond differently)
- **Resource status** of the wood inoculum (size, age, state of decay, nutrient status)

### Morphological Responses to Grazing

*Hypholoma fasciculare* (short-range forager) responds to grazing by shifting from **slow, dense exploitative growth** to **less dense explorative growth**:

- Mycelia from more luxuriant systems (larger inocula, older colonized wood, nutrient-rich colonization) are more resistant to grazing damage.
- Less luxuriant systems (many fine hyphae, fewer cords) suffer dramatically reduced coverage and extension.
- Grazing often results in **points of more rapid outgrowth** as cords with fanned margins.

### Species-Specific Grazing Patterns

Different fungi are preferentially grazed in different regions:

| Fungal Species | Preferred Grazing Region |
|---|---|
| *H. fasciculare* | Hyphal tips at growing margins |
| *R. bicolor* | Indiscriminate on cords and fine mycelium, but generally close to wood inoculum |
| *Phan. velutina* | Fine mycelium within the colony and hyphal tips at the growing margin |

Intense grazing (80 Collembola per 24 × 24 cm tray) by *F. candida* completely removed hyphal tips around large parts of the circumference of *Phan. velutina*, yielding much smaller systems than ungrazed controls.

### Parallels with Plant Herbivory

The effects of invertebrate grazing on mycelia closely mirror herbivory on plants:

- **Browse lines**: Grazing around the entire margin of *Phan. velutina* is analogous to browse lines on trees in wood pastures grazed by cattle.
- **Shape distortion**: Sawfly feeding on young *Eucalyptus* produces multiple leading shoots and bushy form; tent caterpillars on aspen produce shorter internodes and smaller leaves. Similarly, grazing changes mycelial branching patterns.
- **Apical dominance**: The terminal bud on a plant branch produces hormones suppressing branching at lower nodes, analogous to fungal hyphal apical dominance. When herbivores remove the terminal bud, lower buds develop — when grazers remove hyphal tips, new branching points activate.

### Physiological Responses to Grazing

Beyond morphology, grazing induces physiological changes:

- **Ligninolytic enzymes** were switched on by *Phan. velutina* in the presence of nematodes.
- **Protease expression** changed in *Mortierella isabellina* during Collembola grazing.
- Critically, these enzyme changes occurred **distant to the site of grazing**, suggesting system-wide signalling.
- **Nitrogen inputs** from invertebrate faeces provide direct nutritional benefits.

### Fungal Predation on Invertebrates

Some basidiomycetes reverse the trophic relationship:

- ***Pleurotus*** species kill nematodes by immobilization with toxins, then penetrate and feed on cadavers.
- ***Laccaria bicolor*** (ectomycorrhizal) captures and kills *Folsomia candida* Collembola.
- Some fungi produce **nematode-trapping structures** (e.g., *Coprinus comatus* with novel spiny structures).

**Macroorganism parallels**: Insectivorous plants (sundew, Venus flytrap) and insect parasitoids that oviposit on or near arthropod hosts.

## The Sit-and-Wait Strategy

### Scale of Mycelial Networks

In natural forests, genets of saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes can cover areas from several square metres to many hectares:

- ***Armillaria ostoyae***: One genet spans **965 hectares** with a maximum separation of 3810 m, estimated at 1,900–8,650 years old — potentially the largest organism on Earth.
- Phosphorus moves via saprotrophic cord systems over **>1 m** between litter resources.
- Carbon transfers between different tree species via ectomycorrhizal connections.

### Capitalizing on Arriving Resources

Large mycelial networks are ideally positioned to capture resources that arrive within their territory:

- **Saprotrophs**: Dead organic material arrives via litter fall or root death.
- **Ectomycorrhizas**: New seedlings germinate in their vicinity; litter resources become available.
- **Canopy networks**: In tropical forests, rhizomorphic fungi form suspended networks that **trap and colonize falling litter** — likened to animal "filter feeders."

### Temporal Coordination of Nutrient Partitioning

In a landmark experiment, new wood resources were added to established *Phan. velutina* mycelia (up to 50 cm diameter) at 10-day intervals:

- Total ³²P allocated to new resources increased linearly with time.
- The main sink was **not the most recently added resource** but the **better-colonized** earlier resources.
- As newer resources became better colonized, they became the main sinks, supported by efflux from earlier resources.
- This demonstrates sophisticated **spatial and temporal coordination** across a large network.

### Uptake Patches on Nutrient-Poor Soil

When resources are insufficient for colonization of newly arriving resources, mycelia develop **ephemeral uptake patches**:

- Much-branched fine hyphae develop from cords, creating large surface area for nutrient absorption.
- These patches are **ephemeral**, regressing more rapidly than cords.
- They are **sites of mineral nutrient uptake** (not sinks), absorbing ³²P-orthophosphate from soil.
- Development ceases when soil nutrient status is restored to normal.

## Network Connectivity and Resilience

### Architecture

Mycelial networks maintain connectivity through:

- **Radial connections**: Continuity with immediate ancestors (parent hyphae).
- **Tangential connections**: De novo cross-links (anastomoses) between neighbouring regions.
- **Loop formation**: Combined radial and tangential connections create many connected loops.

### Resilience

Network architecture determines resilience to disturbance:

- **Highly connected networks** (many loops) are more resilient to invertebrate grazing because alternative pathways exist when connections are severed.
- **Reconnection capacity**: Resilience depends not only on architecture at the time of disturbance but on the ease and efficiency of **re-growth and reconnection** following damage.
- Routes between different regions, shortest path lengths, and overall network efficiency can be analysed using **graph/network theory**.

### Parallels with Clonal Organisms

The sit-and-wait strategy with persistent networks mirrors many clonal organisms:

- **Clonal plants**: Genets can be long-lived and occupy very large areas; resource movement between ramets occurs in both directions depending on need.
- **Determinate organisms**: Mantis (stationary body, mobile head), ant lions (pit traps), spiders (webs) all employ sit-and-wait strategies with dynamic components.
