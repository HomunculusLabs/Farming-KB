## Permaculture Slope Analysis and Reading

Understanding slopes, contours, and landform is essential to permaculture design. Bill Mollison devotes significant attention in the Permaculture Design Course to reading landforms and working with topography to manage water, access, and planting. Slope analysis determines where water flows, where frost settles, where soils are deep or shallow, and where different land uses are appropriate.

## Why Slope Matters

Every design decision on a sloped site is affected by gravity and water movement:

- **Water flows downhill**: The primary principle governing placement of all earthworks
- **Cold air drains downhill**: Frost collects in valleys and low points
- **Soil depth varies**: Ridge tops are often shallow; valleys accumulate deeper soil
- **Solar aspect changes**: South-facing slopes (Northern Hemisphere) receive more sun
- **Access design**: Roads and paths must follow the contour or account for gradient
- **Erosion risk**: Steeper slopes are more vulnerable to erosion without management

## Reading Contour Lines

Contour lines connect points of equal elevation. On a topographic map:

- Closely spaced contour lines indicate steep slopes
- Widely spaced lines indicate gentle slopes
- Contour lines crossing a river form a V pointing upstream
- Concentric closed contours indicate a hilltop or summit
- Hachured (hatched) closed contours indicate a depression

On the ground, you can find contour by using:
- An A-frame level
- A bunyip water level
- A laser level
- A simple spirit level on a long board

## Keyline Concept

Mollison describes the keyline system (originally developed by P.A. Yeomans) as a fundamental tool for slope management:

### The Keypoint

The keypoint is a point in the valley profile where the slope changes from convex (steepening) to concave (flattening). This is where:

- The water first begins to slow and spread
- It is the most economical place to begin water storage
- Above the keypoint: consider forestry and conservation
- Below the keypoint: suitable for irrigation, pasture, crops, and orchards

### Keyline

The keyline is a contour line that runs through the keypoint. It extends across the landscape, connecting keypoints in adjacent valleys. Cultivation and water distribution follow the keyline pattern, spreading water from the valleys out toward the ridges.

### Keyline Principles

- Water collected above the keypoint is the most valuable for irrigation
- Ditches and channels follow the keyline contour to distribute water across the hillside
- Slopes above 19 degrees generally should not be cultivated
- The keyline system doubles as the road system -- roads follow the contour channels
- Storage dams on the ridges (saddle dams) are the highest and most valuable water storages

## Slope Categories and Land Use

### 0-3 degrees (Nearly Flat)
- Easy to work with machinery
- Water drainage can be a problem
- Suitable for intensive gardens, orchards, buildings
- May need drainage channels or raised beds

### 3-10 degrees (Gentle Slope)
- Good drainage with manageable water flow
- Excellent for most agricultural uses
- Contour planting is beneficial
- Ideal for keyline cultivation and swale systems

### 10-19 degrees (Moderate Slope)
- Good water harvesting potential
- Swales and terraces effective
- Trees and permanent crops preferred over annuals
- Access roads need careful design
- Sandier soils can be cultivated at steeper angles than clay

### 19+ degrees (Steep Slope)
- Generally should not be cultivated
- Best suited for forestry, tree crops, and conservation
- Terracing possible but expensive
- Prone to erosion if cleared
- Mollison advises not cultivating beyond 19 degrees regardless of soil type, with rare exceptions on clay soils

## Valley and Ridge Profiles

Mollison distinguishes between two landform profiles:

### Valley Profile
- Water collects and flows downhill
- Keypoint dams are the primary water storage
- Soils are generally deeper and more fertile
- The valley floor is the most productive agricultural land

### Ridge Profile
- Water drains away from ridges
- Saddle dams (in saddles between ridge tops) are the highest possible water storages
- Soils are often thinner
- Ridge tops provide water pressure if storage is placed there
- Ridge dams fill first during rains, providing early buffer against flooding

## Practical Techniques

### Finding Contour in the Field
- Use an A-frame level: two legs connected by a crossbar, with a plumb bob or spirit level
- Walk one leg forward until level, mark the spot, repeat
- Flag or stake the contour line as you go

### Water Management on Slopes
- Always slow, spread, and sink water on slopes
- Use swales on contour to catch and infiltrate runoff
- Place ponds above gardens for gravity-fed irrigation
- Design overflow paths for extreme rainfall events

### Access on Slopes
- Roads should follow the contour wherever possible (keyline principle)
- Avoid roads running directly downhill (they become water channels in storms)
- Access paths can cross contours at right angles with steps or switchbacks
