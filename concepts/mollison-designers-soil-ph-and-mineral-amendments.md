# Soil pH and Mineral Amendments
Soil pH is one of the most critical factors governing nutrient availability,
microbial activity, and plant health in any permaculture system. Bill Mollison
treats pH not as an end in itself but as an indicator of the broader mineral
and biological balance of soils. The goal is never to force soil to a textbook
pH but to understand what the pH reveals about underlying conditions and to
address causes rather than symptoms through biological and mineral strategies.
## Understanding the pH Scale and Its Design Implications
The pH scale runs from 0 (strongly acid) to 14 (strongly alkaline), with 7 as
neutral. Most agricultural soils fall between 4.5 and 8.5. Mollison emphasises
that pH is a logarithmic measure: a change of one unit represents a tenfold
change in hydrogen ion concentration. This means that small numerical shifts
in pH represent large changes in soil chemistry.
In the [[mollison-designers-climate-classification-and-design-zones]] framework,
acidic soils (pH below 5.5) are common in high-rainfall humid zones where
leaching removes basic cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium), while
alkaline soils (pH above 7.5) dominate arid and semi-arid zones where
evaporation exceeds precipitation and salts accumulate at the surface.
The critical insight from the Designers' Manual is that pH controls the
solubility of essential plant nutrients. At low pH, aluminium and manganese
become toxic while phosphorus, calcium, and molybdenum become locked. At high
pH, iron, manganese, boron, copper, and zinc become unavailable. The ideal
range for most crops (6.0 to 7.0) represents the zone of maximum nutrient
availability, though many permaculture species tolerate much wider ranges.
## Mineral Deficiency Symptoms and Diagnosis
Mollison stresses that visual diagnosis of mineral deficiencies is an
essential field skill for the permaculture designer. Key deficiency indicators
include:
- **Nitrogen**: General yellowing (chlorosis) of older leaves first; stunted
  growth. Common in leached tropical soils and after heavy rains.
- **Phosphorus**: Dark green or purplish leaves, especially on older foliage;
  poor root development and delayed maturity. Widespread in acidic tropical
  soils where phosphorus fixes to iron and aluminium oxides.
- **Calcium**: Terminal bud dieback; distorted new leaves; blossom end rot in
  tomatoes and peppers. Common in acidic sandy soils.
- **Magnesium**: Interveinal chlorosis on older leaves (yellow between green
  veins). Common in acidic, leached soils and where potassium is over-applied.
- **Iron**: Interveinal chlorosis on young leaves (opposite pattern to
  magnesium). Typical of alkaline calcareous soils, not true deficiency.
- **Zinc**: Small, distorted leaves; shortened internodes (little leaf).
  Common in alkaline soils and cold wet conditions.
- **Boron**: Death of terminal growing points; thickened, brittle leaves;
  hollow stems in brassicas. Leached from sandy acidic soils.
## Liming Materials and Their Properties
For acidic soils, liming is the primary amendment strategy. Mollison details
several liming materials, each with distinct properties:
- **Agricultural limestone (calcium carbonate)**: The standard liming material,
  with a neutralising value (NV) of 100. Fineness determines speed of reaction;
  fine lime acts within weeks, coarse lime may take years. Apply based on soil
  test and target pH shift.
- **Dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate)**: Provides both calcium and
  magnesium. Preferred when magnesium is also deficient, which is common in
  acidic soils derived from granitic parent material (see
  [[mollison-granitic-landscapes]]). NV approximately 108.
- **Quicklime (calcium oxide)**: Very reactive, NV 150-179. Burns organic
  matter and soil life. Rarely recommended in permaculture; used only for
  urgent pH correction where biological methods are insufficient.
- **Hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide)**: Moderately reactive, NV 120-134.
  Useful for clay soil structural improvement as well as pH correction.
- **Oyster shell flour**: A slow-release source of calcium with NV around 50.
  Preferred in organic systems due to its gradual action and trace mineral
  content. Good for [[mollison-zone-one-garden-design]].
## Organic and Biological Approaches to pH Management
Mollison's approach to soil pH consistently favours biological solutions over
chemical amendments. The [[mollison-humus-and-organic-matter-in-soils]] buffer is central: well-humified organic matter acts as a
pH buffer, resisting both acidification and alkalinisation. Soils with 5% or
more organic matter are remarkably stable in pH.
Key biological strategies include:
- **Composting**: Well-made compost has a near-neutral pH regardless of input
  materials, and its humic acids buffer soil against pH swings. Regular
  compost application is the single most effective long-term pH management
  strategy. See [[mollison-composting-methods]].
- **Green manuring with deep-rooted species**: Deep taprooted plants such as
  lucerne (alfalfa), chicory, and daikon radish bring calcium and other
  minerals from subsoil layers to the surface. See
  [[mollison-green-manure-soil-fertility]].
- **Legume integration**: Nitrogen-fixing legumes generally prefer slightly
  acidic conditions and their [[root-exudates]] help solubilise phosphorus in
  acid soils. The [[mollison-avenue-cropping-with-legumes]] system exploits this relationship.
- **Wood ash application**: A readily available source of potassium and
  calcium carbonate, wood ash raises pH rapidly. Use sparingly (1-2 kg per
  square metre) as it can create localised alkalinity and potassium excess.
  Best from hardwoods burned at moderate temperature.
## Acidifying Amendments for Alkaline Soils
In arid and semi-arid climates where soil pH exceeds 7.5, Mollison recommends
several strategies:
- **Elemental sulphur**: The most effective acidifying agent for alkaline
  soils. Applied at 100-500 kg/ha depending on current pH, soil texture, and
  target pH. Soil bacteria oxidise sulphur to sulphuric acid over weeks to
  months. More effective in warm, moist soils.
- **Organic matter addition**: Decomposing organic matter produces carbonic
  and organic acids that gradually lower pH. Particularly effective when
  combined with [[mollison-mulching-and-soil-health]]
  practices.
- **Pine needle and peat moss mulches**: These naturally acidic materials can
  lower surface soil pH over time. Useful for creating acid-loving plant
  niches (blueberries, rhododendrons, azaleas) within otherwise alkaline sites.
- **Acid-forming fertilisers**: Ammonium sulphate and urea are acid-forming
  nitrogen sources, though Mollison cautions against reliance on purchased
  fertilisers when biological nitrogen fixation is available.
## Rock Minerals and Trace Element Supplementation
Beyond pH correction, Mollison advocates for comprehensive mineral
replenishment using rock dusts and natural mineral deposits:
- **Basalt rock dust**: A broad-spectrum mineral amendment rich in silica,
  calcium, iron, magnesium, phosphorus, and trace elements. Applied at 2-5
  tonnes per hectare. Provides slow-release minerals over years as it weathers.
- **Granite dust**: Similar to basalt but with higher potassium and silica
  content. Readily available from quarry operations.
- **Phosphate rock (soft rock phosphate)**: A slow-release phosphorus source
  (16-20% P2O5) that also provides calcium and trace minerals. More effective
  in acidic soils where phosphorus solubility is higher. See
  [[mollison-phosphorus-cycling-and-conservation-in-permaculture-systems]].
