## Tropical Soils and Fertility Management

> From *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, Chapter 10 — Bill Mollison

Tropical soils present unique challenges for agriculture and land management. Unlike younger [[cool-temperate-soils-and-water-conservation-mollison]], tropical soils are ancient and deeply weathered, resulting in nutrient profiles that demand careful, strategies differ markedly from those used in temperate zones.

## Nature of Tropical Soils

Tropical soils are old and deeply leached by high rainfall over geological timescales. Two critical mineral components — silica and calcium — are commonly in low supply. This depletion fundamentally alters soil chemistry and structure.

### Clay Chemistry and Mineral Substitution

In tropical clays, aluminium ions frequently substitute for silica in the crystal lattice. This substitution gives the clay particles a net negative charge, which has important implications for nutrient holding:

- **[[green-manure-crops]]** and **perennial plants** restore the humus layer.
- Key species include **Leucaena** and other **tree legumes**, which fix nitrogen while producing leaf litter.
- Perennials are preferred over annuals because their root systems provide continuous organic inputs and prevent leaching.

### Mineral Amendments

Tropical soils need targeted mineral inputs:

- Add **small quantities of superphosphate frequently** rather than large single applications, which would leach away.
- Apply **fine crushed basalt**, **cement powder**, or **bamboo mulches** to supply silica and calcium — the two minerals most depleted in tropical soils.
- After trees are established and providing shade, **increase pH with lime** to improve CEC [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]].

## Special Soil Types

### Coral Cays

Coral cays present a distinct set of problems. Their sands are calcium-rich, which creates a phosphate-binding issue:

- Phosphate applied to these soils binds to form **insoluble calcium triphosphate** (known as *platin* or *calcrete*), making it unavailable to plants.
- **Fine rock phosphate** is more effective than superphosphate on coral cays, as it releases more slowly and is less prone to immediate fixation.
- **Crop waste mulch** is critical — it can reduce soil pH from 8–9 down to 6.5–7, dramatically improving phosphate availability.

### Volcanic Soils

Volcanic soils are among the most fertile in the tropics:

- They are sufficiently rich for intensive agriculture.
- However, **constant cropping without rest exhausts them** just as it does any tropical soil.
- They benefit from the same perennial-based strategies but can support higher initial production.

## Deep Granitic Sands

Deep granitic sands are among the most challenging tropical soil types:

- They are open and coarse-textured.
- They **will not retain mulch beyond one season** — [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] oxidises and disappears rapidly.
- Water and nutrients drain straight through the profile.

### Broadscale Approach

For large areas of granitic sand, Mollison recommends establishing a tree-based system:

1. Plant **palms, Albizzia, Inga, Acacia**, and other **leguminous trees** to establish a canopy.
2. The **mycelial web of pioneer legume roots** is critical — it enables nutrient retention in an otherwise leaky soil profile by holding nutrients in biological cycles.
3. **Palms are planted in mulch-filled pits** — holes dug and filled with organic matter to create concentrated nutrient zones around each tree.

This approach mimics the natural savannah/forest transition, where tree roots and their fungal associates create islands of fertility in poor soils.

### Garden-Scale Approach

For intensive food production on granitic sands, two construction methods are recommended:

1. **Excavated trench gardens**: Dig long trenches 1–2 metres wide and 1–1.5 metres deep. Lay a **sheet-plastic base** to prevent leaching, then backfill with imported sandy loam. The plastic acts as a nutrient barrier, while the imported soil provides a workable growing medium.

2. **Log-box gardens**: Build raised beds using log frames above ground. These are filled with better soil or compost and avoid contact with the draining sand entirely. They are easier to construct than deep trenches and can be positioned anywhere.

Both methods effectively create an artificial soil profile that overcomes the inherent drainage and fertility problems of deep sands.

## Soil Life in the Tropics

Tropical soil ecosystems are driven by a different set of organisms than temperate soils. The key mesofauna — **termites, ants, and worms** — play outsized roles in [[soil-protozoa-nutrient-cycling-and-mineralization]] soil structure.

### Functions of Tropical Soil Mesofauna

These organisms are continuously active in three critical processes:

- **Transporting rotted rock to the surface** — termites and ants bring mineral-rich material from deep subsoil layers up into the root zone, effectively mining nutrients from below.
- **Opening galleries and tunnels** — their burrowing creates channels for water infiltration and root penetration, improving soil structure and aeration.
- **Breaking down plant material** — they accelerate decomposition, converting coarse organic matter into forms available to plants and soil microbes.

### The Decisive Role of Termites

Termites are arguably the most important soil organisms in the tropics:

- They play a **decisive role in the balance between erosion and soil replacement** — their mound-building and gallery construction continuously move soil from depth to the surface, counteracting erosion losses.
- They influence **plant succession** — termite activity modifies soil chemistry and structure in ways that favour certain plant species over others, driving [[ecological-succession]].
- They affect **species distribution in savannah** landscapes — the pattern of termite mounds across the landscape creates a mosaic of soil conditions that determines which plants can grow where.

### Termite and Ant Mounds as Soil Refuges

The mounds built by termites and ants serve as protected micro-environments:

- They **protect soils from fire** — mound material is compacted and often moist, resisting burning that would destroy surrounding topsoil.
- They prevent **waterlogging** by elevating soil above the water table.
- They improve **aeration** — the porous mound structure allows gas exchange even when surrounding soils are compacted or saturated.

Mollison emphasises that working with these soil organisms — rather than against them — is essential for sustainable tropical land management. Any strategy that eliminates termites or ants ultimately undermines the soil's capacity for self-renewal.

## Summary of Key Principles

| Principle | Detail |
||---|
| Tropical soils are old and leached | Silica and calcium are depleted; CEC is pH-dependent |
| Clearance causes rapid fertility loss | [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs|Tree]] legumes rebuild humus and retain nutrients |
| Mineral amendments must be frequent and targeted | Small doses of superphosphate; basalt/cement for silica and calcium |
| Granitic sands require structural solutions | Plastic-lined trenches or log-box gardens |
| Termites are allies, not pests | They drive [[soil-ph|soil]] building |
| [[soil-ph-management]] is critical | Lime after trees; [[mulching-guide|mulch]] on coral cays to reduce alkalinity |
