## Key to Fungi Edibility and 19th Century Mycological Bibliography

## Overview

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]]'s *About Mushrooms* includes a systematic key for
determining the edibility of large fleshy fungi, alongside an extensive
bibliography of 19th-century mycological literature. Together these sections
reveal both the state of amateur mycological knowledge in late-Victorian
America and the scholarly resources available to interested readers at the
time.

## The Classification Key System

Palmer's key is designed as a practical table-based tool for identifying
edible fungi. Rather than requiring the collector to name a species first and
then consult an authority on its safety, the key works in the opposite
direction: it guides the user from observable morphological features toward
an edibility judgment.

The system is structured around the most conspicuous macroscopic character
of any fungus — the nature of its **[[spore]]-bearing surface**. By sorting
specimens into one of four broad categories, the key narrows the field
rapidly and reduces the risk of misidentification.

## The Four Spore-Bearing Surface Types

Palmer's key divides large fleshy fungi into four groups based on how their
spores are produced and displayed:

1. **Gills (Lamellae):** The agarics and their allies — fungi whose
   spore-bearing surface consists of radiating blade-like plates beneath the
   cap. This is the largest and most complex group, requiring further
   sub-division (see Series Classification below).

2. **Pores (Tubes):** The polypores and boletes — fungi whose spores are
   released from the inner surfaces of tiny tubes on the underside of the
   cap. Many boletes are edible and relatively easy to identify.

3. **Teeth (Spines):** Fungi such as *Hydnum* whose spore-bearing surface
   consists of hanging spine-like projections. These are comparatively
   few in number and many are edible.

4. **Enclosed Spores:** Fungi whose spores develop internally — puffballs,
   earthstars, truffles, and related forms. When collected young and before
   the spore mass darkens, many puffballs are among the safest edible fungi
   for beginners.

This four-fold division follows a long tradition in mycological keys and
mirrors the approach used by Cooke, Stevenson, and other British and
American authors of the period.

## Series Classification for Agarics

Within the gilled fungi (agarics), Palmer adopts a **series-based** approach
to further identification. Rather than using a strict botanical hierarchy,
the series groups agarics by shared visible characteristics that are
accessible to the amateur collector:

- **White-spored series:** Includes many common edible species (e.g.,
  *[[agaricus-campestris]]*, the meadow mushroom) but also deadly members of
  the genus *Amanita*.
- **Pink- and salmon-spored series:** Contains numerous desirable edibles.
- **Brown-spored series:** A large group encompassing many *Collybia*,
  *Cortinarius*, and *Inocybe* species, some poisonous.
- **Black-spored series:** Includes *Coprinus* (inkcaps) and allies.
- **Ochre- and rusty-spored series:** Notable for the genus *Lactarius*,
  many of which are acrid or peppery in taste.

The series approach allows the collector to narrow candidates significantly
using a simple [[spore-print]] test before consulting species-level
descriptions.

## Color and Bruising Reactions as Edibility Indicators

Palmer emphasizes that color changes upon cutting or bruising can serve as
important secondary indicators. For example:

- A blue bruising reaction in boletes is often associated with bitter or
  toxic species, though some edible *Boletus* species also bruise blue.
- The milk ("latex") exuded by *Lactarius* species ranges from white to
  colored and may change on exposure to air; acrid-tasting species are
  generally to be avoided regardless of color.
- Rapid discoloration of the flesh — turning black, red, or vivid blue —
  is noted as a warning sign in several groups.

Palmer cautions that color reactions alone are never definitive but should
be read alongside spore color, habitat, and other features.

## Criticism of Government Publications

Palmer is sharply critical of certain USDA pamphlets on mushrooms,
accusing them of a dangerous vagueness. He singles out their failure to
clearly distinguish the **deadly *Amanita*** from merely **acrid
*Lactarius*** species — both of which can cause gastrointestinal distress,
but only one of which is lethal. For Palmer, a government publication that
does not make this distinction does the public a disservice and may
actually endanger lives by encouraging careless consumption.

## Truffle Cultivation and American Truffles

Palmer references the European tradition of truffle cultivation and raises
the question of whether true truffles (*Tuber* species) exist in North
America. He notes that while various hypogeous (underground) fungi are
found across the continent, the prized culinary species of France and Italy
have no confirmed American equivalents — a question that would occupy
American mycologists well into the 20th century.

## 19th-Century Mycological Bibliography

Palmer provides a [[coleman-historical-reading-list-winter-harvest-literature]] that surveys the major mycological works
available to English-speaking amateurs between 1823 and 1889. Key authors
and works include:

| Author | Date | Work |
|--------|------|------|
| Badham, C. D. | 1847, 1863 | *A Treatise on the Esculent Funguses of England* |
| Berkeley, M. J. | 1860 | *Introduction to Cryptogamic Botany* |
| Cooke, M. C. | 1871–1874 | *Handbook of British Fungi* |
| Cooke, M. C. | 1875 | *Fungi: Their Nature, Influence, and Uses* |
| Hay, Rev. | 1887 | *An Amateur's Illustrated Book of Fungi* |
| Peck, C. H. | Various | Annual reports of the New York State Botanist |
| Stevenson, J. | 1886 | *Mycologia Scotiae* |
| Stevenson, J. | 1879–1882 | *[[british-mycological-society]] Flora* (sections) |

The list also includes earlier European works, periodical articles from
the *Journal of the Linnean Society*, the *Grevillea*, and the *Botanical
Gazette*, as well as several USDA bulletins.

## Significance

Palmer's key and bibliography together document a pivotal moment in American
[[toadstool-eating-and-amateur-mycology]]. The classification system reflects a pragmatic, safety-
first philosophy — prioritizing the identification of edible species over
precise taxonomic placement. The bibliography reveals a field in rapid
growth, with British and American authors building on European foundations
to create English-language resources for a widening audience of foragers
and naturalists.
