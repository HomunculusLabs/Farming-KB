# Windward-Leeward Boundary Layer Redistribution and Stipe Curvature

## Overview

Badham (1982) proposed that stipe curvature in *Psilocybe cubensis* is
driven by redistribution of the boundary layer around the [[mushroom-stipe-development-stages]]
when exposed to wind. The windward side experiences a thinner boundary layer
with greater evaporative demand, while the leeward side develops a thicker,
more humid boundary layer. This differential creates asymmetric cell
expansion rates that produce the observed curvature into the wind
(anemotropism). This page examines the boundary layer mechanism and its
physical basis.

## The Boundary Layer Concept

A boundary layer is the layer of relatively still air or fluid adjacent to
a surface. For a cylindrical object like a mushroom stipe in an airflow,
the boundary layer thickness varies around the circumference:

- **Windward side**: The oncoming airflow compresses the boundary layer,
  making it thinner. Air velocity near the surface is higher, enhancing
  convective [[fungal-growth-kinetics-mass-transfer]] (evaporation of water vapor and other gases).
- **Leeward side**: Flow separation occurs downstream, creating a wider
  wake region with a thicker boundary layer. Air near the surface is more
  stagnant, reducing evaporative losses.

## Evaporative Demand as the Tropic Signal

Badham proposed that the differential boundary layer creates a gradient of
evaporative demand across the stipe circumference. This evaporative demand
is a function of four [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]]:

1. **Wind velocity**: Higher wind speed reduces boundary layer thickness
   and increases evaporative demand
2. **Humidity**: Lower ambient humidity increases the [[cervantes-vapor-pressure-deficit-transpiration]]
   gradient driving evaporation
3. **Light**: Illumination heats the stipe surface, increasing water
   temperature and evaporation rate
4. **Temperature**: Higher temperature increases the saturation vapor
   pressure, enhancing evaporation

On the windward side, all four factors conspire to create maximum
evaporative demand. The windward cells lose water faster, which Badham
suggests causes differential [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] or differential cell wall
extensibility, leading to faster elongation on the leeward side. The
result is curvature toward the wind (anemotropism).

## The Physical Model

The mechanism can be modeled as follows:

1. Wind creates asymmetric boundary layer around the cylindrical stipe
2. Windward boundary layer is thin → high evaporation rate → lower cell
   turgor or reduced cell expansion rate
3. Leeward boundary layer is thick → low evaporation rate → higher cell
   turgor or faster cell expansion rate
4. Differential expansion across the stipe circumference produces bending
   toward the wind (upwind)

This is analogous to the mechanism proposed for *Phycomyces* sporangiophore
avoidance responses (Cohen et al., 1975), where Johnson and Gamow (1971)
demonstrated that wind and water vapor are both necessary components of
the tropic response.

## Supporting Evidence from Badham's Experiments

### Rotation Experiments

When basidiocarps were rotated with the long axis perpendicular to the wind
(at 3 rpm), growth was upward ([[negative-geotropism-in-mushroom-fruiting]]) rather than into the
wind. This is consistent with the boundary layer model: rotation equalizes
the boundary layer around the stipe circumference, eliminating the
evaporative demand gradient. With the gradient removed, the default
gravitropic response dominates.

### Parallel Rotation

When basidiocarps were rotated parallel to the airflow, stipe elongation
and pileus opening occurred into the wind. This maintains the asymmetric
boundary layer along the stipe's length axis while equalizing gravitational
effects.

### Light Interaction

When light was the dominant evaporative demand factor (strong light,
low wind), phototropism became the primary response. Badham noted that the
experimental light intensity was approximately one-tenth of natural
conditions, so wind was the dominant factor in most experimental
configurations.

### Sporeless Mutant

Sterile (sporeless) mutants showed the same anemotropic response during
Stages 1-2, confirming that the boundary layer mechanism is independent of
spore production and the later geotropic response.

## Alternative Explanations Considered

Badham evaluated two competing hypotheses:

1. **Evaporative demand (boundary layer) model**: Supported by rotation
   experiments and the multi-factor nature of the response
2. **Mechanical force model**: The mushroom is responding to the physical
   force of wind, similar to *Phycomyces* responses to gravity and
   centrifugal force (Dennison, 1961)

Badham favored the evaporative demand model because it better explains
the multi-factor response (wind, humidity, light, temperature all
contributing) and the rotation experiment results. The mechanical force
model would predict curvature proportional to wind speed regardless of
humidity, which was not observed.

## Gaseous Factors Beyond Water Vapor

Badham noted that wind redistribution could affect gases other than water
vapor. Russo et al. (1977) suggested that ethylene may be involved in the
avoidance response of *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*. An analogous ethylene
gradient could contribute to the differential growth response in *P.
cubensis*, with ethylene accumulating in the leeward boundary layer and
affecting cell expansion rates.

## Implications for Cultivation

The boundary layer model explains several cultivation observations:

- **Drafty rooms cause misshapen mushrooms**: Unidirectional drafts create
  asymmetric boundary layers, producing curved stipes
- **Still air promotes straight growth**: Without wind, the boundary layer
  is symmetric and geotropism dominates
- **Fanning increases evaporation stress**: Oscillating airflow may
  periodically equalize and then re-establish boundary layer asymmetry,
  creating irregular growth patterns
- **Humidification reduces anemotropic curvature**: Higher ambient humidity
  reduces the evaporative demand gradient, weakening the anemotropic signal
