## Weil — Cultural Attitudes Toward Altered States

## The Central Observation

A major thread in *The Natural Mind* (1972) is the striking contrast between Western and non-Western cultural attitudes toward altered states of consciousness. [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]] aria mushrooms in shamanic practice. Shamans consume the mushrooms to enter trance states for divination, healing, and journeying to the spirit world.
- **East African cultures** use chat (kh catha edulis ulis) for social and religious purposes.
- **South American cultures** use coca leaves for energy, social bonding, and ritual purposes — a practice that predates the Inca empire.

In all of these societies, consciousness-altering practices are embedded in cultural frameworks that give them meaning, regulate their use, and integrate their effects into daily life. The experiences produced are typically interpreted as valuable — as sources of healing, knowledge, spiritual insight, or social cohesion. Problematic use exists but is generally managed through cultural mechanisms rather than through prohibition.

## The Western Exception

Western industrial society, by contrast, treats altered states with suspicion, fear, or outright prohibition. Weil identifies several cultural factors that contribute to this attitude:

- **The Protestant work ethic:** The privileging of rational, productive, waking consciousness as the only legitimate state. Altered states are associated with idleness, irrationality, and moral danger. This ethic, rooted in Calvinist theology, equates moral worth with self-discipline and productive labor — states of consciousness that reduce productivity or disrupt rational self-control are therefore morally suspect.
- **Scientific materialism:** The assumption that ordinary waking consciousness is the only "real" state, and that all other states are forms of delusion, illness, or chemical disturbance. This view treats subjective experience as epistemologically unreliable unless it conforms to the standards of empirical observation.
- **Medicalization:** The pathologization of altered states as symptoms of mental illness or addiction, to be treated with pharmaceutical intervention. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM) classifies various altered states as disorders, reinforcing the idea that non-ordinary consciousness is inherently pathological.
- **Legal prohibition:** The criminalization of consciousness-altering substances, which both reflects and reinforces the cultural stigma against non-ordinary states. Drug prohibition creates a self-reinforcing cycle: illegal drugs are used in dangerous underground contexts, producing problems that are then cited as evidence that the drugs themselves are dangerous.
- **Capitalist economic imperatives:** An economic system that requires a particular kind of consciousness — alert, competitive, future-oriented, capable of sustained focused attention on external tasks — naturally disfavors states that disrupt these capacities.

Weil argues that these attitudes are not only irrational but actively harmful. By denying the innate drive to alter consciousness and providing no legitimate outlets for it, Western society forces the drive into underground, unregulated, and often dangerous expressions.

## The Role of Technology and Approved Intoxication

Weil also observes that Western culture has developed its own forms of consciousness alteration — but ones that are culturally sanctioned because they serve the values of productivity and consumption:

- **Alcohol** is the most widely used psychoactive substance in Western society. It is legal, heavily marketed, and socially encouraged, despite causing vastly more social harm than all illegal drugs combined. Alcohol facilitates social bonding (valuable for commerce) and does not typically produce the kind of consciousness-expanding experiences that threaten the status quo. The distinction between alcohol and "drugs" is, in Weil"s framework, a cultural distinction without a pharmacological basis.
- **Caffeine** is the second most widely used psychoactive substance. It increases alertness, attention, and productivity — making it the ideal drug for an industrial work ethic. It is consumed daily by the vast majority of adults and is rarely recognized as a consciousness-altering substance. Caffeine dependence is widespread but is almost never framed as a drug problem.
- **Nicotine** provides stimulation, stress relief, and a socially acceptable ritual of altered consciousness (the cigarette break). The ritual dimension of nicotine use — the shared activity, the break from work, the social bonding — illustrates how even a relatively simple psychoactive substance can be embedded in a cultural framework that gives it meaning beyond its pharmacological effects.
- **Prescription stimulants** (amphetamines, methylphenidate) are prescribed to children and adults to enhance focus and productivity, reflecting the cultural value placed on sustained attention and task completion. The increasing prescription of these drugs to children for "ADHD" represents, in Weil's framework, a culturally sanctioned form of consciousness alteration that serves the demands of the educational system.
- **Prescription sedatives and antidepressants** are used to manage the psychological distress produced by the very social conditions that pathologize non-ordinary consciousness. The epidemic of antidepressant use in Western societies can be understood, in Weil's framework, as a culturally sanctioned form of consciousness alteration — one that smooths over the distress caused by the suppression of the intoxication drive rather than addressing its root cause.

The difference, Weil suggests, is not pharmacological but cultural: these substances are approved because they facilitate (or at least do not disrupt) the dominant mode of [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-kykeon-and-eleusinian-mysteries]] sinian Mysteries, which likely involved the use of ergot-derived psychoactive substances (kykeon) and were the most important religious initiation in the Hellenic world for nearly 2,000 years.
- [[solanaceous-hallucinogens-of-medieval-european-witchcraft]] society tolerated and sometimes valued various forms of consciousness alteration, including the use of psychoactive plants in folk medicine and witchcraft practices.
- The deliberate narrowing of Western consciousness began with the Reformation and the Scientific Revolution, which established rational, empirical awareness as the only legitimate mode of knowing.
- The full criminalization of consciousness-altering substances is a 20th-century phenomenon, driven largely by American foreign policy and international drug control treaties.

This historical perspective undermines the claim that Western attitudes toward altered consciousness are based on timeless scientific truths or universal moral principles. They are, rather, the product of specific historical developments that served specific social and economic interests.

## Implications

The cross-cultural analysis in *The Natural Mind* serves several functions in Weil's overall argument:

1. It provides empirical support for the thesis that the desire to alter consciousness is innate and universal — if it were merely cultural, we would expect to find societies without it.
2. It demonstrates that non-Western societies have developed sophisticated, functional frameworks for managing drug use — frameworks that produce fewer problems than the Western approach of prohibition.
