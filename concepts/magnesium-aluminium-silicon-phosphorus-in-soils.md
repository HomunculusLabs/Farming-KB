# Magnesium, Aluminium, Silicon, and Phosphorus in Soils

## Overview

Magnesium, aluminium, [[silicon]], and phosphorus are four critical elements in soil science, each playing distinct roles in plant nutrition, soil structure, and ecosystem function. Their interactions with pH, other nutrients, and biological processes make them central to understanding soil health and fertility management.

## Magnesium (Mg)

### Chemical Properties

With calcium, magnesium is found in [[dolomite]]. It is needed by plants and is common in most subsoils. It forms the central atom of the chlorophyll molecule, activates enzymes, and concentrates in seeds. Like nitrogen, it is very mobile in the plant.

### Soil Availability

Magnesium is likely to be deficient only in sands and sandy soils. It is present in the clay fraction of soils and is released by soil acids and [[humus]]. It is supplied by dolomite application on farmlands, as tablets of dolomite for animals, and mixed in animal foodstuffs.

### Buffering Action

Magnesium carbonate from dolomite buffers plant and soil acids, as does lime, but it can cause plant poisoning if not balanced by high lime (calcium) levels. This is an important caution for soil amendment programs.

### Human Health

Magnesium is essential for chlorophyll and in the mitochondria of human cells, hence energy metabolism. It is present in all green plants. Deficiencies do not normally occur where people are healthy and are not subject to diarrhoea. When required by people it is given as an intravenous fluid at 10 minimoles per kg bodyweight.

## Aluminium (Al)

### The "Jekyll and Hyde" Element

Aluminium forms a large part of soils and red clays (with iron), or as poorer bauxitic dryland deposits. It is poisonous as aluminium sulphate, formed in acid rain or sulphur-polluted soils (e.g., in industrial areas), and is also released when cooking onions in aluminium cookware.

### Health Concerns

Heavy aluminium silica uptake in humans may assist the development of senile dementia. Calcium in the soil and as tablets is a partial defence. Some areas are naturally high in acid-aluminium soils, making adequate calcium levels necessary in all gardens and even in rainwater tanks (as marble or limestone chips, whole sea shells, gravels of lime).

### Cooking Precautions

Avoid aluminium utensils when cooking salty, acid, or sulphurous substances (vinegar, onions). Use iron, enamel, stone, or steel cookware instead.

### Toxicity Thresholds

- Soluble aluminium at 0.1 to 1.0 ppm is tolerable
- Above 1.0 ppm, susceptible plants start to die
- At 15 ppm or higher, many plants die

## Silicon (Si)

### Role in Plant Structure

Silicon is an important part of cell walls in many grasses and bamboo. It is essential in soil [[cation-exchange-capacity]] of deep, red, heavily-leached tropical soils. An application of cement dust can assist this exchange in such conditions.

### Conifer Concerns

Pine trees and conifers generally are poor nutrient recyclers and can produce nutrient-deficient silica soils under their litter, thus losing calcium and other elements to leaching. Therefore, the recommendation is to use grasses and broadleaf, leguminous, or soil-building trees with conifers to maintain soil health.

### Nutrient Cycling

Many trees deposit salt, phosphorus, manganese, zinc, potash, and other elements at high topsoil levels due to good nutrient recycling via leaf fall. Silica is normally at 20–40 ppm in soil waters, and in highly alkaline, wet, warm areas can be leached away altogether.

### Practical Applications

- In silica-deficient tropical areas, tree crops are the only sustainable solution
- Cement (calcium silicate) can be used to restore silica to plants
- Bamboos are good sources of calcium and silica as garden mulch in the tropics
- In ponds, diatoms need silica to proliferate; these are excellent fish food

### Rock and Soil Formation

Silicates make up much of the bulk of normal soils, but very high silica in rock may produce acid soils in high rainfall areas (silicic acid).

## Phosphorus (P)

### Essential Plant Element

Phosphorus (as phosphate) is an essential, common plant element recycled by many trees and fixed by root associates of several trees (Casuarina, Pultenea, Banksia), by algae, in the mud of ponds, in bones, and in freshwater mussels. It is essential to energy metabolism, photosynthesis, respiration, cell division, root development, and protein formation. It is highly mobile in plant tissue.

### Global Deficiency Crisis

There is a general world soil phosphate deficiency, especially in poorer countries, while most western soils are now over-supplied with a very large unused soil bank of phosphorus. Of all the phosphatic fertilisers used, Europe and North America consume 75% and get least return from this input because of overuse, over-irrigation, and poor soil economy.

### Contamination Risks

In some oceanic guano deposits, phosphates can be contaminated by cadmium, mercury, uranium (40 ppm), and fluorides. Cadmium levels in inorganic market garden crops may commonly exceed health limits as a result.

### pH and Availability

- Calcium, iron, and aluminium immobilise phosphate
- A pH of 6–7.5 releases phosphate for plant uptake
- Sulphur can adjust availability in drylands
- About 45 ppm phosphate in soil is needed for grains (optimum pH 6.0–6.5)

### Natural Sources

Phosphorus is found concentrated in seeds, bones of vertebrates (especially fish), freshwater mussels and their mud, bird manures (derived from fish bones and seeds), and bat guano. Traditional sources include bone meal and pigeon flocks kept as phosphate factories on farms.

### Sustainable Phosphorus Management

- Conservation farming loses about one-half to one-third the phosphorus of contemporary agriculture
- Non-tilage farming would lose even less
- Bioregional farming and home gardening with wastes returned to soils would lose even less
- Devoting 30% of land surface to trees might create a sustainable phosphorus system

### The Critical Limit

Of all elements of critical importance to plants, phosphorus is the least commonly found, and sources are rarely available locally. Next to clean water, phosphorus will be one of the inexorable limits to human occupancy on this planet.

### Loss Rates

- Uncut forests may lose 0.1 kg/ha/year of phosphorus
- Clear-cropping can lose 100 kg/ha/year or more — a 1000 times increase
- About 15–20% of inessential phosphorus use is in detergents
