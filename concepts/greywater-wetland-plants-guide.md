# Greywater Wetland Plants for Backyard Treatment Systems

## Overview

Backyard greywater wetlands are shallow, gravel-filled basins planted with bog and water plants that purify household wastewater from showers, laundry, and bathroom sinks. The plants are the active treatment agents: their roots supply oxygen to the surrounding soil, creating [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-aerobic-and-anaerobic-composting]] microsites that support diverse pollution-eating microbes. A well-designed greywater wetland merges the beauty of water gardens with ecological responsibility, transforming waste into a productive garden feature. The selection of appropriate plant species is critical to treatment effectiveness.

## How Wetland Plants Purify Water

Wetland plants purify greywater through several interconnected mechanisms:

- **Root-zone oxygenation**: Special wetland plants (cattails, bulrushes, reed canary grass, canna lilies) can supply oxygen to their roots and the surrounding soil. This creates an aerobic zone around each root, resulting in countless aerobic and anaerobic microsites in the wetland and plenty of edge between them.
- **Microbial habitat**: The diverse micro-niches created by root-zone oxygenation support many different kinds of pollution-eating microbes. Without these special plants, a wetland might not clean water effectively.
- **Nutrient uptake**: Plants absorb nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients from the water, incorporating them into biomass.
- **Pathogen reduction**: Microbial communities in the root zone break down pathogens and organic compounds.
- **Filtration**: The gravel substrate physically filters particles from the water as it percolates through.
- **Evapotranspiration**: Plants remove water through transpiration, reducing the volume that must be discharged.

## Essential Plants for Greywater Wetlands

One or more of the following essential species should make up the majority of any greywater wetland. They are specially adapted water-cleaning plants whose root-zone oxygenation capacity is the key to effective treatment.

### Cattail (Typha spp)
- **Primary function**: Wildlife habitat, robust nutrient uptake.
- **Notes**: One of the most effective water-purifying plants. Can spread aggressively; may need containment in small wetlands. The dense root system creates extensive treatment zones.
- **Wildlife value**: Excellent habitat for birds, amphibians, [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]].

### Bulrush (Scirpus validus)
- **Primary function**: Core water purification, root-zone oxygenation.
- **Notes**: A fundamental constructed wetland species used worldwide in municipal and residential treatment systems. Less aggressive than cattails in many conditions.

### Reed Canary Grass (Phragmites communis)
- **Primary function**: Nutrient accumulation, soil stabilization.
- **Notes**: Extremely effective at nutrient uptake but can be invasive. In small backyard systems, monitor spread carefully. Known as a nutrient accumulator, concentrating minerals from greywater into its biomass.
- **Caution**: Classified as invasive in many regions. Consider containment strategies.

### Canna Lily (Canna spp)
- **Primary function**: Ornamental water treatment, root-zone oxygenation.
- **Notes**: Provides excellent treatment capacity while adding significant ornamental value to the wetland. Available in many flower colors. Less aggressive spreaders than cattails or reeds.
- **Additional benefit**: Edible rhizomes in some species.

## Additional Wetland Plants for Diversity

Once a preponderance of essential plants are established, the following species can be added to increase biodiversity, wildlife habitat, and aesthetic value:

### Ornamental and Habitat Species
- **Arrowhead** (Sagittaria spp): Ornamental, attracts pollinators.
- **Chokeberry** (Aronia spp): Wildlife value, edible berries.
- **Cranberry** (Vaccinium macrocarpon): Wildlife value, attractive foliage, edible fruit.
- **Elderberry** (Sambucus spp): Ornamental flowers, wildlife habitat, edible berries.
- **High-bush blueberry** (Vaccinium corymbosum): Wildlife value, edible fruit, attractive fall foliage.
- **High-bush cranberry** (Viburnum trilobum): Wildlife value, ornamental, edible fruit.
- **Lotus** (Nelumbo lutea, N. nucifera): Ornamental, cultural significance.
- **Ostrich fern** (Matteuccia pennsylvanica): Ornamental, edible fiddleheads.
- **Pickerel weed** ([[pontederia-cordata]]): Ornamental blue flower spikes. Note: Can be invasive.
- **Water canna** (Thalia spp): Ornamental tropical accent.
- **Yellow iris** (Iris pseudoacorus): Ornamental, vigorous grower.

### Functional and Accumulator Species
- **Comfrey** ([[comfrey-symphytum-officinale]]): Mulch production, insectary plant, nutrient accumulator. Can be cut periodically and used as mulch elsewhere in the garden.
- **Horsetail** (Equisetum spp): Nutrient accumulator. Note: Can be invasive in wet conditions.
- **Soft rush** (Juncus effusus): Fiber plant, adds textural interest.
- **Spike rush** (Eleocharis spp): Fine-textured accent.
- **Sweet flag** (Acorus calamus): Aromatic, ornamental. Note: Can be invasive.
- **Hedge hyssop** (Gratiola virginiana): Aromatic, ornamental, less common.

## Design Considerations for Plant Selection

### Climate
Greywater wetlands only function effectively when plants are growing. In very cold climates:
- Install a diverter valve to direct water to sewer or septic when plants die back in winter.
- This also prevents outdoor greywater plumbing from freezing.
- Select cold-hardy species such as cattails, bulrushes, and native sedges.

### Proportions
- Essential plants (cattail, bulrush, reed canary grass, canna lily) should comprise the majority of the planting.
- Additional species should be interspersed to increase diversity without displacing essential treatment plants.
- Aim for at least 60-70% essential species by area.

### Sizing
The general rule for sizing a backyard greywater wetland is approximately 1.25 square feet of wetland per gallon of greywater per day. A family producing 100 gallons of greywater daily would need a wetland roughly 10 by 12 feet.

### Water Level
The water level should be maintained about 2 inches below the top of the gravel to prevent mosquito breeding. This level is controlled by an adjustable water-level outlet pipe.
