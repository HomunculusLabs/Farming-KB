## Preventing Re-Entry and Choosing the Post-Session Personality

This concept covers the critical transitional phases described in the [[bardo-thodol]] as adapted by Leary, Metzner, and Alpert in *The [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]]*. It addresses two key concerns: (1) how to prevent premature re-entry into ego-consciousness during a [[psychedelic-session-instructions]], and (2) how to consciously choose one's post-session personality upon returning. Together these teachings form the culmination of [[the-second-bardo-and-hallucinatory-visions]] instructions and the general conclusion of the manual.

## Four Meditative Methods for Preventing Re-Entry

When the voyager has achieved ego-loss and wishes to prolong that liberated state, four meditative methods are prescribed. These techniques are designed to prevent the mind from re-entering the habitual patterns of the ego and the game-structures of ordinary consciousness. Each method offers a different approach to maintaining the state of non-dual awareness.

### 1. Meditation on the Buddha or Guide

The first method involves fixing the mind upon a revered figure — the Buddha, a spiritual teacher, or the session guide. By concentrating all attention on this figure, the voyager stabilizes awareness and avoids being drawn back into worldly thought-patterns. The guide serves as an anchor of clarity and compassion, a focal point that holds consciousness in the liberated state. This method draws on the devotional traditions of Tibetan Buddhism, where visualizing and meditating upon a deity or guru is considered a powerful means of maintaining meditative absorption.

### 2. Concentration on Good Games

The second method involves deliberately contemplating positive, constructive "games" — the worthwhile activities and roles one engages in during ordinary life. Rather than allowing the mind to drift into anxious or compulsive patterns, the voyager focuses on the noblest and most fulfilling aspects of their life. This technique acknowledges that re-entry is inevitable but seeks to ensure that when it occurs, it does so in the most positive possible direction. By pre-selecting the highest games, the voyager creates a mental attractor that shapes the quality of return.

### 3. Meditation on Illusion

The third method is the contemplation of all phenomena as illusory — a core Mahayana Buddhist insight. The voyager recognizes that every vision, every thought, every emotional state arising during the session is ultimately empty of inherent existence. By maintaining this meta-awareness, the voyager avoids becoming attached to or repelled by any particular experience. This method cultivates the middle way between grasping and aversion, preventing the mind from latching onto any single experience as "real" and thus preventing re-entry into dualistic consciousness.

### 4. Meditation on the Void

The fourth and most advanced method is meditation on the Void itself — the ultimate emptiness that underlies all phenomena. Here the voyager rests in pure awareness without object, without direction, without content. This is the state of *shunyata*, the recognition that consciousness itself is empty and luminous. By abiding in the Void, the voyager goes beyond even the concept of liberation, remaining in the primordial state from which all experience arises and to which it returns.

## Why These Methods Work: The Suggestibility of the Psychedelic State

A crucial point emphasized in the text is that these meditative techniques are effective not only for experienced meditators but for ordinary persons as well. The reason lies in the dramatically increased suggestibility of the psychedelic state. Under the influence of a psychedelic substance, the mind becomes extraordinarily receptive to direction and intention. Thoughts that would normally be mere background noise become powerful formative forces. This heightened plasticity means that focused intention — such as meditating on a guide, concentrating on good games, contemplating illusion, or abiding in the void — carries far greater weight than it would in ordinary consciousness.

This is why the manual stresses the importance of preparation and setting: whatever the voyager holds in mind during the session tends to manifest with amplified intensity. The increased suggestibility is a double-edged sword — it can lead to liberation or to confusion, depending on how it is directed.

## Methods of Choosing the Post-Session Personality

As the session moves toward its conclusion and re-entry becomes imminent, the voyager faces what the Bardo Thodol describes as the art of choosing one's post-session personality — the specific life-form or level of existence into which consciousness will re crystallize. In the original Tibetan context, this refers to literal rebirth; in the psychedelic adaptation, it refers to the psychological pattern or ego-structure that will dominate consciousness as the session ends.

### Choosing as a Profound Art

The text emphasizes that this choice is not to be made hastily or carelessly. It is a profound art requiring clarity, calm, and deliberate intention. The voyager should not rush to re-enter any available game or identity simply out of habit or anxiety. Instead, one should survey the options with the impartial eye of a liberated consciousness and select the path that offers the greatest potential for growth and happiness.

### Banishing Fear First

Before any choice can be made wisely, fear must be banished. The psychedelic state can produce terrifying visions, especially as the ego attempts to reassert itself. The voyager must recognize that all fears encountered at this stage are projections of the ego's desperation to re-establish its dominance. By refusing to be moved by fear, the voyager gains the freedom to choose from a position of clarity rather than from a position of retreat.

### Using Limited Foreknowledge Wisely

The voyager possesses limited foreknowledge about what each re-entry path entails. This foreknowledge, derived from preparatory reading and previous experience, should be used as a guide but not as a rigid framework. The actual experience of each level may differ from its description, and the voyager must remain open and adaptive.

## The Six Levels of Re-Entry

The Bardo Thodol describes six possible levels of re-entry, each associated with a characteristic color of light and specific geographical or environmental symbols. These correspond to the six realms of existence in Buddhist cosmology:

| Level | Color of Light | Geographical Symbols |
|-------|---------------|---------------------|
| Devas (gods) | White light | Temples, jewelled mansions |
| Asuras (jealous gods) | Green light | Magical forests, fire |
| Humans | Yellow light | — |
| Animals | Blue light | Caves, deep holes |
| Neurotics (pretas) | Red light | Desolate plains, forest wastes |
| Hell | Smoke-colored light | Gloomy lands, black roads |

The deva realm, associated with white light and magnificent architecture, represents the highest form of ego-attachment — blissful but ultimately stagnant. The human realm, with its yellow light, is considered the most favorable for spiritual progress because it balances pleasure and suffering in a way that motivates growth. The lower realms — animal, neurotic, and hell — represent increasingly painful and constricted [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]]. The voyager is instructed to move toward the higher lights while recognizing that even the highest realm is still a form of bondage compared to liberation.

## The Importance of Impartiality and Natural Return

The text stresses that the ideal attitude during this phase is one of complete impartiality — freedom from both desire and fear. The voyager should not grasp at the higher realms or flee from the lower ones, but should remain in a state of equanimity, allowing consciousness to return slowly and naturally to its ordinary mode of functioning. The instruction is to enjoy every second of the experience, neither rushing toward re-entry nor clinging to the liberated state. This middle way ensures the smoothest possible transition.

## General Conclusion of the Bardo Thodol

The manual's adaptation of the Bardo Thodol's general conclusion presents three categories of liberation, arranged in order of efficacy:

1. **[[transference-principle]] at the Moment of Ego-Death**: Liberation achieved at the precise instant of ego-loss by applying the transference technique — consciously merging with the [[clear-light]] at the moment of ego-death. This is the highest and most direct method.

2. **Recognizing the Clear Light in the First Bardo**: Liberation achieved during the First Bardo by recognizing the primordial Clear Light as one's own true nature. If the transference principle is missed, this recognition provides a second opportunity.

3. **Liberation During Second Bardo Visions**: Liberation achieved during the Second Bardo by recognizing the visionary experiences as projections of one's own mind and maintaining non-attachment throughout their unfolding.

### Graded Teachings for Weaker Persons

For those unable to achieve liberation through the three primary methods, the Bardo Thodol offers graded teachings — progressively more concrete instructions designed for persons with less spiritual preparation. These teachings are compared to several vivid analogies:

- **The trough in a broken water drain**: Like a sheep that has fallen into a trough in a broken water channel, the weaker person needs to be guided step by step, shown each possible path and its consequences, and directed toward the correct one through explicit instruction.

- **The catapult**: The teachings act as a catapult, launching the consciousness of the unprepared person toward liberation even though they lack the power to reach it on their own. The force of the teaching itself provides the necessary momentum.

- **The wooden beam floated on water**: Like a person floating on a wooden beam in a flood, the voyager is carried to safety by the teaching despite having no control over the currents. The teaching itself becomes the means of deliverance.

- **The horse and bridle**: Like a skilled rider who guides a horse with a bridle, the teaching directs the wandering consciousness of the confused voyager toward liberation, providing structure and direction where none existed before.

### The Liberative Power Without Ritual

A crucial point in the Bardo Thodol's conclusion is that these teachings possess inherent liberative power. They do not depend on ritual, ceremony, ordination, or any external validation. The text itself, when understood and applied, is sufficient to guide consciousness toward liberation. This principle — that direct understanding is more powerful than institutional mediation — is central to the Tibetan Dzogchen and Mahamudra traditions and is carried forward into Leary's psychedelic adaptation.

## Technical Comments: Use of This Manual

The manual's technical commentary section provides practical guidance for applying the Bardo Thodol teachings to [[bardo-instruction-texts-psychedelic-sessions]].

### Preparatory Reading as the Most Important Use

The single most important use of the manual is preparatory reading before the session. By familiarizing themselves with the descriptions of the various [[bardo-states]], voyagers prime their minds to recognize these states when they arise. The key word of the entire manual is **recognition** — the ability to identify each experience as it occurs and to respond appropriately.

### Avoiding Paranoid Traps and the Medical Model

The text warns against several common pitfalls: falling into paranoid interpretation of visionary experiences, using the medical model of "drug-induced psychosis" to frame the session, and allowing guides to impose their own anxieties on the voyager. The psychedelic experience should be approached on its own terms, not through the distorting lens of pathological models.

### Constructing Programs for Sessions

The manual advocates constructing deliberate programs for psychedelic sessions — planned sequences of intention, setting, and activity that maximize the potential for insight and minimize the risk of confusion.

## Planning a Session: The Four Hindu Goals

[[psychedelic-session-planning]] is organized around the four traditional Hindu goals of life, which provide a framework for intentional psychedelic exploration:

1. **Personal Power (Dharma)**: Sessions aimed at enhancing competence, confidence, and effectiveness in one's chosen activities.
2. **Duty and Help of Others (Seva)**: Sessions directed toward understanding one's responsibilities and developing compassion for others.
3. **Fun and Sensuous Enjoyment (Kama)**: Sessions devoted to the pure appreciation of sensory experience, beauty, and pleasure.
4. **Transcendence and Liberation (Moksha)**: Sessions oriented toward ego-loss, mystical union, and liberation from conditioned existence.

### Extroverted vs. Introverted Experiences

The manual distinguishes between extroverted psychedelic experiences (where consciousness flows outward into sensory appreciation and interpersonal connection) and introverted experiences (where consciousness turns inward toward visionary landscapes and abstract thought). Both are valid and valuable; the balance between them depends on the voyager's intentions, the setting, and the substance used.

### Eight Types of Experience

The text identifies eight fundamental types of psychedelic experience, divided into four positive and four negative:

**Four Positive Types:**
- Sensual (aesthetic appreciation)
- Cognitive (intellectual insight)
- Mystical (ego-transcendence and unity)
- Aesthetic (creative and artistic inspiration)

**Four Negative Types:**
- Paranoid (fear and suspicion)
- Depressive (sadness and hopelessness)
- Somatic (physical discomfort and anxiety)
- Confused (disorientation and cognitive overload)

Understanding these categories helps voyagers and guides navigate the session with greater awareness, recognizing that negative experiences are temporary states that can be transformed through recognition and acceptance, while positive experiences should be deepened and integrated.
