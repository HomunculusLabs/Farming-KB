# Ololiuqui

Ololiuqui is the Aztec name for the seeds of Turbina corymbosa (syn.
Rivea corymbosa), a morning glory vine native to Mexico. Among the
most sacred psychoactive plants of Mesoamerica, ololiuqui seeds were
revered by the Aztecs and continue to be used today by indigenous
communities in Oaxaca for divination and healing rituals. The seeds
contain ergoline alkaloids chemically related to those found in the
[[ergot-fungus]], making ololiuqui one of the most pharmacologically
significant entheogens in the Americas.

## Historical Background

The name ololiuqui comes from the Nahuatl language and was applied by
the Aztecs to the small, round, brown seeds of a climbing morning
glory vine. The seeds were among the most important hallucinogenic
substances in pre-Columbian Mexico, alongside teonanacatl (sacred
mushrooms, see [[teonanacatl-history]]) and peyote (see
[[peyote-ceremony]]).

Spanish chroniclers, including the physician Francisco Hernandez, who
was sent to Mexico by Philip II in 1570, documented the use of
ololiuqui extensively. Hernandez reported that the Aztec priests
consumed the seeds to induce a visionary state and to communicate
with the gods. He described how the priests would grind the seeds,
soak them in water, and drink the resulting liquid to enter a state
of divine intoxication.

The seeds were also employed in medicinal contexts, particularly for
the treatment of gout, flatulence, and various other ailments.
Hernandez noted that the indigenous healers were skilled in the use
of ololiuqui and understood its dosage and effects with precision.

The use of ololiuqui persisted despite Spanish colonial suppression
of indigenous religious practices. The Inquisition in Mexico targeted
the use of ololiuqui along with other "diabolical" plants, but the
practice survived in remote communities. Today, the seeds continue to
be employed in magico-religious and curing ceremonies by the Zapotec,
Mixtec, Mazatec, and Chinantec peoples of Oaxaca, southern Mexico.

## Botanical Description

Turbina corymbosa is a perennial climbing vine belonging to the
Convolvulaceae (morning glory family). It features heart-shaped
leaves and produces white, funnel-shaped flowers that open in the
morning. The seeds are small, oval, brown or black, and contained in
round, woody capsules that split open when ripe to release the seeds.

The plant grows in tropical and subtropical regions of Mexico and
Central America. It is frequently found in disturbed areas, along
fences, and in forest margins. The seeds are collected from the dried
seed pods for ceremonial use. Related species used in similar
contexts include Ipomoea violacea, known as badoh negro, which is
discussed in [[morning-glory-entheogens]].

## Traditional Use

In traditional practice, the seeds are ground into a powder on a
metate (grinding stone) and soaked in water or cold alcohol to
extract the active compounds. The resulting liquid is then consumed
by the participants in the ceremony. Dosages typically range from 50
to 100 or more seeds, depending on the desired intensity of effects
and the experience of the practitioner.

The ritual use of ololiuqui is typically conducted by a curandero or
curandera (healer) in a controlled ceremonial setting. The seeds are
used for divination, diagnosis of illness, and communication with the
spirit world. The ceremony is usually conducted at night, in a
darkened room, and involves prayers and invocations alongside the
consumption of the seed preparation.

The experience is described as producing a state of lassitude and
drowsiness, followed by dream-like visions that are often interpreted
by the curandero for the benefit of the patient or community. The
visions may include encounters with spirit beings, glimpses of future
events, or symbolic imagery that requires expert interpretation.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The psychoactive constituents of ololiuqui are indole alkaloids
closely related to those found in [[ergot-fungus]]. The principal
active compounds are:

- **Lysergic acid amide (LSA / ergine)**: The main psychoactive alkalo
  id, producing effects similar to but milder than LSD
- **Lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide (LSH)**: Present in trace amounts,
   contributing to the overall experience
- **Isolysergic acid amide**: An isomer with reduced psychoactive acti
  vity

These alkaloids are water-soluble and can be extracted into aqueous
solutions, which explains why the traditional method of soaking the
ground seeds in water is effective. The chemical similarity between
the ergoline alkaloids of ololiuqui and those of ergot (Claviceps) is
a remarkable example of convergent biochemistry between a fungus and
a higher plant.

The active principles were first identified by [[albert-hofmann-eleusinian-potion-analysis]] in
1960, when he isolated lysergic acid amide and lysergic acid
hydroxyethylamide from the seeds. This discovery was significant
because it demonstrated that ergoline alkaloids were not exclusive to
the ergot fungus but could also be produced by higher plants, raising
fascinating questions about the evolutionary and ecological
relationships involved.

## Effects

The effects of ololiuqui are characterized by a distinctive profile
that differs from both classical hallucinogens and deliriants:

- A period of initial drowsiness, lassitude, and physical heaviness
- Nausea is commonly reported, especially at higher doses, and is some
  times considered a purifying aspect
- Dream-like, closed-eye visions that unfold gradually
- Altered time perception, with time seeming to slow down
- A sense of tranquility, introspection, and emotional openness
- Some users report synesthetic experiences, particularly blending of 
  visual and auditory perception
- The experience is generally contemplative rather than ecstatic

The intoxication is generally milder than that produced by LSD, to
which the alkaloids are chemically related. The experience typically
lasts 6 to 8 hours, with the visionary phase beginning after the
initial period of drowsiness and lasting 3 to 4 hours.
