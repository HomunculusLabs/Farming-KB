# Tidal and Estuarine Aquaculture

updated: 2026-04-18

Permaculture Two (1979) Sections 7.5-7.6 document strategies for tidal flat management, estuarine fishery enhancement, and sea-land nutrient cycling. This extends [[aquaculture-in-permaculture]] into brackish and marine environments.

## Salicornia Flats Management

The intergrade estuary or inlet to land is often over Salicornia flats, weakly inundated by tides twice daily. These flats support:

- Swan grazing at high tide
- Domestic geese appreciating succulent forage and sea grasses
- Mosquito breeding grounds (few if any fish occupy salt flats)

It is more productive to vary the system by pond and bank designs than to try to manage the unmodified system.

### Pond Design for Salicornia Flats

Colin Sumner (Tasmania Fisheries) recommends:

- Ponds about 1.8 m deep
- Very gradual bank slopes (1:3 or so)
- One foot of tide as flushing
- Browsers: periwinkles, Salinator shellfish, shrimp, mullet to keep algae to tolerable levels
- Oyster culture: broodstock at mid-bank, spat caught on broken shell in trays (40% air time exposure, 60% submerged)
- Geese on range, ti-tree or coastal shrubs on embankments

Mullet species are viable pond species with some food available or cultured. Crab, octopus, seaweeds, and shrimp are other probable cultures.

## The Case for Shore-Based Fisheries

"Water-based ecosystems are as complex as those on land. The ecosystem approach must be applied there just as in terrestrial food production." -- Sciences Council of Canada, 1979

Offshore and intensive process fisheries are doomed in energy terms. Sedentary, shore-based, estuarine, inland, and intertidal or tidewater fisheries:

- Yield higher than deep-sea fisheries
- Reduce or eliminate costs of transport, packaging, and storage
- Employ more people locally
- Allow local industry development

Very few shellfish and inshore species are not susceptible to rearing or management in pond, barrage, raft culture, or fenced impounded tidal areas.

## Essential Structures for Mudflat Reclamation

Structures to reform millions of hectares of invariable mud-flats and intertidal sands:

- Reef walls of tyres, pipes, stone
- Drift fences to catch seagrass and direct fish
- Rafts for rope suspension of mollusc spat and algae
- Ring-rafts to rear fish in tideways
- Flow-governed tide pools for correct exposure for oysters
- Fry traps providing stock for inshore and Salicornia pools
- Paddle-powered electric or mechanical systems at restricted tide-flow points
- Evaporative pans for salt, chemical, and brine-shrimp production (latter as fry food)
- Islands for marine wildfowl refuges and phosphate collection
- Deeps for fish refuges with cover nets
- Manured sea-grass fields, wave-protected by low bunds
- Sub-surface (permeable) walls to retard tide flow in scoured estuaries
- Trials of substrate materials to catch new fry or algal forms

## Tidal Stone Traps

Where tides fall 1.2 m or more on rocky coasts, stone traps enclose rocky clefts or tidal flats with a 90 cm wall. Small school fish (garfish, squid, mullet, perch) enter over the wall at high tide, feed on algae, and are trapped as the tide falls. A refuge pool or roofed, slotted tank holds the catch at low water. Late night tides give the best catch. These traps provide small fish for stocking manured ponds and are themselves substrate for oyster and mussel.

## Stillwater Funnel Traps

When tide-fall is too slight, or in lakes and lagoons, a lead fence plus series of funnels guide fish (eels, trout, perch) to enclosures. On dams, the end enclosure can be under the house verandah, with fish always available. Traps can be made of brush, stone, wire, or fabric netting.

## Seagrass Resources

Zostera, Heterozostera, and Posidonia grow in Australian estuaries. Uses include:

- Waterfowl grazing in spring
- Heavy summer seed production (forage seeds or flour)
- Building insulation (resistant to burning, almost immune to rot)
- Under-slab insulation when fumed with creosote or encased in thin plastic

In Adelaide, Zostera is a "nuisance" on beaches -- careful usage converts a nuisance to a conservative resource.

## Sea-Land Nutrient Cycling

### Guano Collection

Guano from seafowl, easily caught as liquid run-off from solid rafts or stony islands, provides essential local phosphate and nitrogenous fertilizer for adjacent land crops. Large artificial platforms have proved commercially viable off southwest Africa where pelican and cormorant deposit tons of guano.

### Seagrass Mulch

Seagrass mulch and guano close the sea-land cycle of nutrients, making growing of crops near seashores and waterways very profitable. Seafowl rookeries (especially seagull rookeries near towns) are seldom structured to be used as guano reserves.

### Muttonbird Industry

The muttonbird (Puffinus) industry of Tasmania yields millions of birds annually. Under good management, rookeries are fast increasing. By-products of down make the best insulation. Muttonbirds can be cultivated and rookeries established in new areas; adults survive well in captivity and progeny return to new rookeries.

### Seal Rookeries

Seal rookeries can be developed (as on the Pribiloff Islands) to yield skins, guano, and protein foods. Shallows off such rookeries are very productive of seagrasses and fish. The guano of seals and seabirds is the basis of high manurial turnover in inshore sea pastures.

## Mixed Species Management

Swan, geese, and some duck species prefer saltwater locations. Mullet species, eels, and bream are all easily managed fish species with different food requirements, giving management potential as mixed-species stocks. Mussels and oysters attach to stone or still-pond Zostera, hold sand banks with byssal "roots," and provide both bream and human food.
