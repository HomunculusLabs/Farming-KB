## What Is Vinegar

Vinegar is a sour liquid produced by the oxidation of [[ethanol]] (alcohol) into acetic acid by acetic acid bacteria ([[acetobacter]] species). The word comes from the French "vin aigre," meaning sour wine. Vinegar has been produced for thousands of years and is used in cooking, food preservation, cleaning, and medicine.

The vinegar-making process involves two distinct stages of fermentation:

1. **Alcoholic fermentation:** Yeast converts sugars into ethanol (this step produces wine, beer, cider, or mead)
2. **Acetic fermentation:** Acetobacter bacteria convert ethanol into acetic acid (this step produces vinegar)

For home vinegar making, you can start from an alcoholic beverage (wine, beer, cider) or from raw fruit juice and let both fermentations happen sequentially.

## Mother of Vinegar

The "mother" is a gelatinous, [[cellulose]]-based biofilm that forms on the surface of fermenting vinegar. It consists of Acetobacter bacteria and their cellulose matrix. The mother is not required for vinegar production (acetobacter are naturally present in the environment) but significantly speeds up the process and protects the vinegar from contamination.

### Obtaining a Mother

- Purchase raw, unfiltered vinegar (Bragg Apple Cider Vinegar is widely available) — it contains a natural mother
- Obtain a SCOBY or mother from another vinegar maker
- Grow your own: combine raw vinegar with wine or cider and wait for a mother to form (1-2 weeks)
- Some wine sediment naturally develops into a vinegar mother over time

### Caring for the Mother

- Store in a non-metallic container (glass, ceramic, or food-grade plastic)
- Keep submerged in vinegar or alcoholic liquid — never let it dry out
- Room temperature storage is fine; refrigeration slows activity
- The mother grows and multiplies — share extras or [[compost]] them
- A healthy mother is cloudy, slightly gelatinous, and has a mild vinegar smell
- Dark spots or mold indicate contamination — discard

## Starting Materials

### Wine Vinegar

- Red wine vinegar: use dry red wine (merlot, cabernet, pinot noir)
- White wine vinegar: use dry white wine (sauvignon blanc, pinot grigio)
- Use wine that is still drinkable — vinegar bacteria need ethanol to work
- Opened wine that has been sitting for a few days is ideal
- Avoid heavily oaked wines — oak flavors can become harsh during vinegar fermentation

### Beer Vinegar

- Use any style of beer — ales, IPAs, stouts, and porters all make interesting vinegars
- Beer vinegar (sometimes called "alegar") has a malty, complex flavor
- Hoppier beers produce more bitter vinegars
- Flat beer works perfectly

### Cider Vinegar

- Use hard cider (fermented apple juice with alcohol)
- Apple cider vinegar is the most popular homemade vinegar
- Use unfiltered, unpasteurized cider for the most complex flavor
- The finished product is similar to commercial raw apple cider vinegar

### Fruit Vinegar (From Juice)

For vinegars made directly from fruit juice, both fermentations happen in sequence:

1. Start with fresh fruit juice (apple, grape, pear, or other fruit)
2. Add wine or champagne yeast to begin alcoholic fermentation (1-2 weeks)
3. Once fermentation slows and alcohol is present (specific gravity below 1.000), add a vinegar mother or raw vinegar
4. Acetic fermentation begins (2-4 weeks)

### Other Bases

- Rice vinegar: from sake or rice wine
- Mead vinegar: from honey wine (mead)
- Palm vinegar: traditional in Southeast Asia
- Coconut water vinegar: from fermented coconut water

## The Vinegar Making Process

### Equipment

- Glass jar or crock (wide-mouth is best for oxygen exposure)
- Breathable cover (cheesecloth, coffee filter, or paper towel secured with a rubber band)
- Non-metallic spoon for stirring
- pH strips or meter (optional but helpful)

### Steps

1. Fill a clean glass jar 3/4 full with your starting liquid (wine, beer, cider, or alcoholic ferment)
2. Add the mother of vinegar (a piece from raw vinegar or a previous batch)
3. If no mother: add 1/4 cup of raw, unpasteurized vinegar as a starter
4. Cover with a breathable cloth — acetobacter need oxygen to work (this is an aerobic process)
5. Store at room temperature (65-85F / 18-29C), ideally 70-80F (21-27C)
6. Keep in a dark or dimly lit area — direct sunlight can harm the bacteria
7. Stir gently every few days to introduce oxygen
8. A mother will form on the surface within 1-2 weeks
9. Taste periodically — vinegar is ready when it reaches the desired acidity (typically 4-6 weeks)
10. Target acidity: 4-6% acetic acid (standard table vinegar is 5%)

### Timeline

- Week 1-2: mother forms on surface, subtle vinegar aroma develops
- Week 2-4: noticeable sourness, active bubbling on the surface
- Week 4-6: vinegar flavor develops, acidity increases
- Week 6-8: fully developed vinegar; test and taste

### Testing Acidity

- Taste: should be sharply acidic but not harsh or burning
- Smell: clean, sharp vinegar aroma; no alcohol smell remaining
- pH: finished vinegar typically has a pH of 2.5-3.5
- If alcohol smell persists, continue fermenting

## Harvesting and Bottling

1. When vinegar reaches desired flavor and acidity, carefully remove the mother from the surface
2. Reserve the mother in a jar with some of the finished vinegar for the next batch
3. Strain the vinegar through a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth to remove sediment
4. Bottle in clean glass bottles
5. Seal bottles (unlike the fermentation stage, sealed storage is fine for finished vinegar)
6. Store in a cool, dark place

## Pasteurization (Optional)

Pasteurization stops all fermentation and prevents a new mother from forming in the bottle:

1. Heat vinegar to 140-160F (60-71C) in a non-reactive pot
2. Hold at temperature for 10 minutes
3. Cool and bottle
4. Pasteurized vinegar has a longer shelf life but loses some probiotic benefit
5. Raw, unpasteurized vinegar retains live bacteria and enzymes

## Flavoring Vinegar

Infusing finished vinegar with herbs, fruits, and spices creates versatile flavored vinegars for cooking:

### Popular Infusions

- Tarragon vinegar: 3-4 sprigs fresh tarragon per cup of vinegar
- Raspberry vinegar: 1/2 cup fresh raspberries per cup of vinegar
- Garlic and herb vinegar: garlic cloves, rosemary, thyme, bay leaf
- Hot pepper vinegar: whole chili peppers (jalapeno, habanero, Thai bird's eye)
- Lemon or citrus vinegar: lemon peel, zest, or juice
- Rosemary vinegar: 2-3 sprigs fresh rosemary
- Mint vinegar: fresh mint leaves
- Vanilla vinegar: 1 split vanilla bean
