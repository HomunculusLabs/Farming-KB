# Seven Stages of Mushroom Cultivation

Mushroom cultivation follows seven essential stages that mirror the fungal life cycle:
media preparation, inoculation and container filling, the spawn run, complete
colonization, initiation and pinning, maturation and harvest, and rest. Mastery of
these stages allows growers to cultivate diverse mushroom species on many different
substrates, from easy species like oysters and shiitakes to more challenging fungi.

## Stage One: Media Preparation

All mushrooms require an energy source called a growing medium or substrate. Some
species like the phoenix oyster (Pleurotus pulmonarius) are omnivores that will
feed on wheat straw, coffee grounds, hardwood, and conifers. Others are finicky:
maitake, for instance, prefers mostly oak. Choosing and preparing the right
substrate is the critical first step. The most common outdoor substrates are
hardwood chips, manure-based composts, and hardwood logs. Indoor cultivators
typically use shredded pasteurized cereal straw or sterilized sawdust for
artificial logs.

Growing medium particle size matters enormously. Mushrooms prefer fine, dense
substrate rather than fluffy, airy material. Think of mycelium expansion like
building a network of highways with bridges spanning gaps between substrate
particles. The smaller the particles, the less energy mycelium spends building
bridges. However, particles that are too small become compacted, creating
anaerobic conditions where carbon dioxide cannot escape. The grower must find
the sweet spot between these extremes.

## Stage Two: Inoculation and Container Filling

Inoculation introduces mushroom mycelium (spawn) into the prepared growing medium.
Each piece of spawn radiates hyphae outward until they meet hyphae from another
piece. If genetically identical, the mycelia fuse together and increase the
spreading biomass. This phenomenon is strain-specific: two non-identical strains
will remain independent, leaving an uncolonized zone between them. Spawn is
available as dowels, grain, sawdust, and cardboard, each suited to different
cultivation methods.

Proper hygiene during inoculation is essential since the cultivator is the most
frequent vector of contamination. Work quickly and cleanly when mixing spawn with
substrate. After filling containers, label them with the date, substrate type, and
spawn strain. Record-keeping enables you to track performance and improve yields
over time.

## Stage Three: The Spawn Run

During the spawn run, mycelium colonizes the substrate. This stage generates
considerable heat (thermogenesis) and carbon dioxide. The mycelium must have
adequate gas exchange; without oxygen, the substrate becomes anaerobic, creating
dead marbled areas that compromise yields. Temperature management during this
stage is critical. Warmer temperatures accelerate colonization but also
encourage contamination, while cooler temperatures slow everything down.

For bulk substrates, the spawn run may take weeks. Oyster mushrooms on pasteurized
straw typically colonize in two to three weeks, while shiitake on sterilized
sawdust may take four to six weeks. Monitor containers for even white growth and
watch for any signs of contamination such as green, yellow, or black patches.

## Stage Four: Complete Colonization

Complete colonization means the mycelium has fully permeated the substrate,
creating a unified fungal biomass. At this point, the fungus has captured the
substrate and is ready to shift its energy toward reproduction. Visual inspection
should reveal an even white mat throughout the substrate. Some species benefit
from continued incubation after apparent full colonization, as the mycelium
continues to strengthen its hold and accumulate energy reserves.

For species requiring a casing soil, such as Agaricus and king stropharia, this
is the stage at which the non-nutritive soil layer is applied. The casing
introduces microbial communities that can stimulate fruiting and provides a
high-humidity microenvironment for primordia formation. Casing should only be
applied after colonization is complete to reduce the chance that competitor
organisms take over the substrate.

## Stage Five: Initiation and Pinning

Initiation (also called pinning) is triggered by environmental cues including a
drop in temperature, increase in fresh air exchange, introduction of light, and
often a brief cold shock. Primordia, or baby mushrooms, form as tiny knots of
hyphal tissue. These are extremely fragile and must be protected from drying out.
Maintaining high humidity (85 to 95 percent) during pinning is critical. A
humidity tent or automatic misting system can prevent primordia from aborting.

The exposed surface area of the substrate relative to its total volume directly
affects pinning outcomes. Too much exposed area overstimulates the mycelium,
causing it to produce more mushrooms than it can support, leading to widespread
abortion. Too little surface area limits gas exchange and prevents proper
development. Understanding this balance is key to optimizing yields.

## Stage Six: Maturation and Harvest

Once pins form, mushrooms can double in size daily under optimal conditions.
Maturity is reached when the mushroom stops growing and the cap begins to open.
Harvest timing affects both quality and shelf life. For most species, harvesting
just before or as the veil tears yields the best quality. Use a twisting motion
to remove the entire mushroom from the substrate, then trim the base.

The second flush typically produces half the weight of the first, and the third
produces half of the second (for example, 5 pounds, 2.5 pounds, and 1.25 pounds
from the same container). Biological efficiency, the ratio of fresh mushroom
weight to dry substrate weight, is the standard metric for comparing
cultivation performance across species and methods.

## Stage Seven: Rest

After harvest, the mycelium needs a rest period before producing another flush.
During rest, the fungus recovers energy and prepares for the next reproductive
cycle. For indoor cultivation, this may involve reducing misting and allowing
the substrate to dry slightly for one to two weeks. After the rest period,
resuming humidity and watering triggers the next flush.

Outdoor patches may cycle through rest and fruiting seasons naturally, while
indoor systems can be managed to stagger flushes for continuous production.
Understanding the rest stage helps growers plan their cultivation schedule and
maintain consistent harvests throughout the year.

## Key Points

- The seven stages are: media preparation, inoculation, spawn run, complete
  colonization, initiation/pinning, maturation/harvest, and rest
- Adherence to these stages is constant regardless of species, substrate, or
  method chosen
- Always prototype on the smallest scale before scaling up production
- Particle size in substrate affects colonization efficiency and yield
- Gas exchange is critical during both the spawn run and fruiting stages
- Yields decrease with each successive flush (roughly halving each time)
- Contamination prevention during inoculation is the grower's primary
  responsibility
- Record-keeping across all stages enables continuous improvement
