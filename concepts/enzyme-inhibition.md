## Overview

Enzyme inhibition is the reduction of catalytic activity when a molecule binds to an enzyme, changes the enzyme's accessible
conformations, or covalently modifies a functional group required for catalysis. The inhibitor may resemble a substrate, mimic a
transition state, occupy an allosteric pocket, chelate a metal cofactor, or react with a catalytic residue.

The concept is central to biochemistry, pharmacology, toxicology, pesticide chemistry, and metabolic regulation. Many
successful medicines are enzyme inhibitors, including angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, HIV protease inhibitors, statins,
kinase inhibitors, and cyclooxygenase inhibitors.

Inhibition is not synonymous with enzyme destruction. A reversible inhibitor can leave the enzyme and permit catalysis to resume,
whereas an irreversible inhibitor forms or triggers a persistent chemical change. The distinction matters for dosing, antidote
design, experimental kinetics, and safety assessment.

## Reversible Inhibition

Reversible inhibitors bind through noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds, ionic contacts, hydrophobic packing, metal
coordination, and pi stacking. They are described by binding constants and by how they alter the apparent kinetic parameters of an
enzyme-catalyzed reaction.

Competitive inhibitors bind the free enzyme in a way that prevents substrate binding. They increase the apparent Michaelis
constant because more substrate is required to reach half-maximal velocity, but the maximal velocity can be restored at
sufficiently high substrate concentration.

Uncompetitive inhibitors bind only the enzyme-substrate complex. They lower both apparent Km and Vmax because the inhibited
complex removes active enzyme from the productive pathway after substrate has already bound.

Noncompetitive inhibition is the special case in which inhibitor binds enzyme and enzyme-substrate complex with equal affinity. In
the ideal model, Vmax decreases while Km remains unchanged, because substrate binding affinity is not altered even though
catalytic turnover is reduced.

Mixed inhibition is more common than perfect noncompetitive inhibition. The inhibitor can bind both free enzyme and enzyme-
substrate complex, but the affinities differ, so both apparent Km and Vmax shift.

## Irreversible and Time-Dependent Inhibition

Irreversible inhibitors produce long-lasting loss of activity by covalently modifying the enzyme, generating a reactive
intermediate at the active site, or causing very slow dissociation. Classic examples include organophosphate inhibition of
acetylcholinesterase and aspirin acetylation of cyclooxygenase.

Mechanism-based inhibitors, also called suicide substrates, are converted by the enzyme into a reactive species that inactivates
the same enzyme. This makes them selective because bioactivation occurs in the catalytic environment of the target rather than
throughout the cell.

Time-dependent inhibition must be interpreted carefully. Slow tight-binding reversible inhibitors can appear irreversible during a
short assay, while true irreversible inhibitors show progressive loss of active enzyme as incubation time increases.

For irreversible inhibitors, potency is often summarized by kinact/KI rather than by a simple IC50. KI describes formation of the
reversible enzyme-inhibitor complex, and kinact describes the rate at which that complex becomes inactive.

## Kinetic Interpretation

The measured strength of an inhibitor depends on substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, assay time, preincubation, pH,
ionic strength, cofactors, and product accumulation. An IC50 value is therefore assay-dependent and should not be treated as an
intrinsic thermodynamic constant.

Ki is closer to an intrinsic affinity, but even Ki values depend on the kinetic model being used. Converting IC50 to Ki requires
knowing whether inhibition is competitive, uncompetitive, noncompetitive, or mixed and what substrate concentration was present.

Lineweaver-Burk plots historically helped classify inhibition, but modern analysis usually fits the rate equation directly by
nonlinear regression. Direct fitting avoids distortion of error that occurs when reciprocal transformations overemphasize low
substrate concentrations.

Progress curves provide additional information when product formation is followed continuously. They can reveal slow onset,
hysteresis, substrate depletion, product inhibition, or enzyme instability that would be hidden in a single endpoint measurement.

## Biological Regulation

Cells use enzyme inhibition to regulate flux through metabolic pathways. End-product inhibition prevents wasteful synthesis when
downstream products accumulate, as when a biosynthetic pathway product inhibits an early committed step.

Allosteric inhibitors bind outside the active site and alter conformational ensembles. This allows regulation by metabolites that
do not resemble the substrate and permits cooperative behavior in multimeric enzymes.

Protein inhibitors also control enzymes. Serpins regulate proteases, cyclins and CDK inhibitors regulate cell-cycle kinases, and
many [[plant-signaling-pathways]] depend on protein- protein interactions that occlude active sites or change catalytic conformations.

Reversible inhibition is especially useful for physiology because it can be rapidly tuned. Irreversible inhibition is more common
in defense, toxicity, and pharmacology where durable suppression of a target is desired.
