# Polyculture Design Guidelines

Temperate-climate polyculture design is still in its infancy. Plenty
of research still needs to be done to develop successful combinations
and to broaden the number of plant varieties that work well. Ianto
Evans has developed a set of guidelines based on his long experience.
These ten time-tested tips form the foundation for designing your own
polycultures.

## Guideline 1: Seed Several Varieties of Each Species

This lengthens the harvest season, provides information on the best
types to plant, and more fully occupies ecological niches. Different
varieties of the same species may ripen at different times, tolerate
different conditions, or fill slightly different roles in the
polyculture.

## Guideline 2: Don't Sow Seed Too Thickly

The recommended sowing rates on seed packets are based on heavy
thinning. In a polyculture, you will be eating nearly every plant,
so you will be "thinning" after your plants are at least adolescent
size, which is later than in conventional gardens.

If you sow ten species, reduce your sowing density accordingly, to
about 80 percent of the suggested rate. One seed per couple of
square inches is plenty; any more than that and you will be
overwhelmed with hundreds of tiny salad plants about one month after
sowing. This not only makes harvesting tedious, but the speedier-
growing greens can overwhelm the less sturdy herbs.

## Guideline 3: Begin Your Harvest Early

Harvest your plants, especially greens, when they begin to crowd,
not when they are mature. Overcrowding will inhibit rapid growth.
Young plants are especially tasty, as the continuing infatuation
with baby vegetables demonstrates. Early harvesting opens space for
slower companions to develop.

## Guideline 4: Mix Plant Families, Not Just Species

Closely related plants compete for the same nutrients, so a
polyculture that is heavy on the brassicas (broccoli, cauliflower,
cabbage, kale, Brussels sprouts) or any other single genus will not
grow well. In addition to preventing fertility shortages, diversity
will confuse pests in search of big blocks of their favorite food.

## Guideline 5: Include Many Seeds of Fast-Growing Species

Radishes, mustard greens, fenugreek, and buckwheat will cover the
soil quickly to thwart weeds, and will get your harvest off to a
fast start. You will eat a lot of these small plants, so plant them
the thickest. These quick-covering species create the living mulch
that protects slower-germinating companions.

## Guideline 6: Overlap the Harvests

To extend the harvest season, plant several varieties of each
species, each with a different ripening time. For example, leaf
lettuce is ready for the salad bowl much sooner than head lettuce.
In addition, blend fast-growing vegetables with slow ones; early-
season crops with late. Examples include radishes followed by
cabbage, peas followed by bush beans followed by fall fava beans,
or spring herbs such as dill succeeded by summer basil.

## Guideline 7: Avoid Root and Light Competition

Sprawling plants such as tomatoes and potatoes may not be appropriate
for polycultures; they will shade out many other plants. A
preponderance of root crops will compete for soil space. Think about
the mature size and shape of each variety before you plant, and avoid
competition. The goal is complementary, not competitive, placement.

## Guideline 8: Harvest Whole Plants

With the exception of some long-lived herbs that can be prevented
from bolting by regular trimming, polyculture plants should be
pulled up whole. This allows room for the other many contenders for
the same space. Be gentle; do not disturb the roots of adjoining
plants. Harvest from the densest zones, and select plants whose
harvest will release their slower companions from competition.

## Guideline 9: Save a Few Plants for Seed

Let a few of the healthiest individuals of each species go to seed,
for natural reseeding or for seed saving. Plants saved for seed
should, if possible, be left on the north side of the bed, so they
will not shade the other flora. This ensures a self-renewing
polyculture that improves itself over time.

## Guideline 10: Examine Your Polyculture Every Day

Things happen fast in polycultures. After about three weeks, your
polyculture will be at maximum density, and will need daily harvest
to continue rapid growth. The payoff is a nice salad or stir-fry
every day. Your polyculture will appreciate this daily attention: as
the Chinese say, the best fertilizer is the gardener's shadow.

## Regional Adaptation

A polyculture that succeeds in one climate, such as the Northwest
with its long, cool spring and fall, might bolt or bake in the
hotter shoulder seasons of the South or Midwest. Gardeners are
encouraged to experiment with their own polyculture versions and
adapt these guidelines to local conditions.
