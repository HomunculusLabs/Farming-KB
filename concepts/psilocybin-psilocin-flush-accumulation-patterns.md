# Psilocybin and Psilocin Variation Across Flushes in Psilocybe Cubensis

The alkaloid content of cultivated Psilocybe cubensis varies significantly between
successive flushes, with psilocybin and psilocin following distinctly different
temporal patterns. This finding, documented by Bigwood and Beug in 1982 at
The Evergreen State College, has important implications for both cultivators
and consumers who may assume consistent potency across harvests.

## Experimental Design

Bigwood and Beug grew an Amazonian strain of Psilocybe cubensis originating
from a spore print collected near Pucallpa, Peru (documented by Repke et al.,
1977). Mycelium was maintained as a stock culture on various agar media.
Since only one flush could be obtained from agar plates, a rye-grain
miniculture method adapted from San Antonio (1971) and Oss and Oeric (1976)
was employed.

Wide-mouth half-pint jars of approximately 250 ml were charged with 10 grams
of rye grain and 15 ml of water, then autoclaved. Each jar was inoculated
under sterile conditions with agar-grown mycelium culture. Every four days
for a period of 28 days, the jars were shaken to distribute the growing
mycelium evenly on the grain. After full colonisation, the grain was cased
with a mixture of 2 parts peat, 1 part calcium carbonate, and 2 parts
perlite and/or vermiculite to a depth of approximately 2 cm.

Mushrooms were watered once every two days with 1 ml of sterile water via
syringe. The first flush occurred four to five weeks after inoculation,
approximately two weeks after casing. Harvested mushrooms were immediately
freeze-dried, sealed in plastic, and stored at minus 5 degrees Celsius
until analysis. Voucher specimens were deposited in the University of
Washington Herbarium (WTU).

Analytical methodology used reversed-phase high performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC) quantified with a Hewlett-Packard 8380A reporting
integrator-plotter, calibrated against standards from the National
Institute on Drug Abuse. A linear relationship with 10 percent repeatability
was found between concentration and peak area from 0.2 to 3 micrograms
total psilocybin or psilocin. The detection limit was approximately
0.01 micrograms. Results were qualitatively confirmed by thin-layer
chromatography using butanol-acetic acid-water (12:3:5).

## Psilocybin Variation Across Flushes

Psilocybin levels varied unpredictably from one flush to the next across
three replicate minicultures:

| Flush | Culture 1 (mg/g) | Culture 2 (mg/g) | Culture 3 (mg/g) |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 1     | 8.3               | 5.1               | 7.6               |
| 2     | 6.5               | 7.0               | 6.2               |
| 3     | 13.3              | 4.7               | 5.3               |
| 4     | 4.8               | 3.7               | 3.2               |
| 5     | (consumed)        | 5.2               | 6.7               |
| 6     | 6.8               | (ended)           | (ended)           |

No clear upward or downward trend in psilocybin was observed as fruiting
progressed. Levels fluctuated by a factor of approximately four within a
single culture over multiple flushes. The last flush generally contained
comparable psilocybin levels to the first flush, which is notable because
some cultivators assume potency depletes with successive flushes.

## Psilocin Accumulation Pattern

Psilocin followed a strikingly different and much more predictable pattern
than psilocybin. The near-complete absence of psilocin in early flushes
is a robust finding observed across all three replicate cultures:

- **First flush**: Psilocin was generally absent or near-zero
  (0 mg/g in two of three cultures; 0.5 mg/g in the third)
- **Second flush**: Still low or absent (0 to 1.5 mg/g),
  with two cultures still showing zero
- **Third flush**: Beginning to accumulate (0.9 to 1.7 mg/g),
  present in all cultures for the first time
- **Fourth flush**: Peak psilocin levels (1.8 to 2.9 mg/g),
  the highest recorded values
- **Fifth flush**: Declining but still elevated (1.7 to 2.2 mg/g)
- **Sixth flush**: Returning toward baseline (0.5 mg/g)

This pattern suggests that psilocin accumulates as the mycelial culture
ages, peaking around the fourth flush before beginning to decline. The
authors note they could rarely follow the decline beyond five flushes
because the mycelium typically stopped fruiting by that point.

## Psilocybin to Psilocin Ratio Shift

Psilocybin was nearly always at least twice the level of psilocin, but
the ratio shifted substantially across flushes. Early flushes show a
ratio of approximately 10:1 or higher when psilocin is near zero.
Mid flushes narrow to approximately 3:1 to 5:1 as psilocin accumulates.
Peak psilocin flushes reach a ratio of approximately 2:1.

This ratio change means that early flushes and later flushes may produce
qualitatively different experiences even at equivalent total dry weight,
because the relative proportion of psilocin (which is more rapidly
psychoactive due to lacking the phosphoryl group) changes significantly.

## Cap Versus Stem Distribution

In additional strains tested, caps generally contained approximately
twice as much psilocybin as stems. However, the distribution of psilocin
differed by strain. In the M.R. and Equadorian strains, the small amount
of psilocin present was entirely in the stems. In contrast, the Amazon
strain had a trace of psilocin in the cap but not in the stem, with equal
psilocybin distribution between cap and stem.

## Street Sample Variability

Five street samples of dried Psilocybe cubensis of unknown flush number
and precise growing conditions showed highly variable psilocybin levels
(0.7 to 6.2 mg/g) and consistently low psilocin levels (0 to 0.3 mg/g).
The tenfold variation in psilocybin between street samples underscores
the unpredictability of dosage in informal settings.
