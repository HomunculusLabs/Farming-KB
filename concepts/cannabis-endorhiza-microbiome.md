# Cannabis Endorhiza Microbiome

The endorhiza — the internal root tissue colonized by bacteria — represents one of the most intimate and cultivar-specific microbial compartments in the Cannabis plant. Unlike the rhizosphere and bulk soil, which are shaped predominantly by edaphic (soil) factors, the endorhiza microbiome is primarily determined by host genotype, making it a key area of study for understanding plant-microbe partnerships in Cannabis.

## Definition and Scope

The endorhiza refers specifically to bacteria that have colonized root tissue internally, as distinct from several other root-associated compartments:

- **Rhizosphere**: Soil loosely attached to roots, heavily influenced by root exudates and rhizodeposition
- **Rhizoplane**: The root surface where bacteria adhere and form biofilms
- **Bulk soil**: Soil distant from any root influence, representing the background microbial community
- **Endorhiza**: Bacteria living inside root tissue itself, in the apoplastic space or within cells

Endorhiza bacteria occupy the apoplastic space within root tissues and can also enter plant cells. They are a subset of endophytes — microorganisms that live within plant tissues without causing disease. In Cannabis, the endorhiza has been shown to harbor distinct bacterial communities that differ significantly from both rhizosphere and bulk soil assemblages.

## Core Endorhiza Community in Cannabis

Research by Winston et al. (2014) identified a conserved core bacterial community across multiple Cannabis cultivars in the endorhiza. This core community includes several well-characterized bacterial groups:

- **Pseudomonas**: A diverse genus of Gammaproteobacteria, many species of which are known plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR). Pseudomonas species produce siderophores, antibiotics, and phytohormones that benefit host plants.
- **Cellvibrio**: An aerobic cellulolytic bacterium that degrades plant cell wall components. Unlike most core endorhiza members, Cellvibrio is not typically classified as an endophyte, suggesting a unique role in Cannabis root ecology, possibly in remodeling root cell wall architecture.
- **Oxalobacteraceae**: A family of Betaproteobacteria commonly found in soil and plant-associated environments. Members of this family are often involved in nitrogen cycling and organic acid metabolism.
- **Xanthomonadaceae**: A family within the Gammaproteobacteria. While some Xanthomonas species are well-known plant pathogens, many relatives in this family are benign or beneficial root colonizers that contribute to nutrient cycling.
- **Actinomycetales**: An order of Gram-positive bacteria that includes Streptomyces and related genera. These are prolific producers of bioactive compounds and are well-documented endophytes in many plant species.
- **Sphingobacteriales**: An order of Bacteroidetes commonly isolated from soil and plant environments, often associated with decomposition of organic matter.

The consistent presence of these taxa across cultivars suggests they play fundamental roles in Cannabis root function and health, though the specific mechanisms remain under investigation. The dominance of Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria in the endorhiza aligns with observations from other plant systems, suggesting these groups possess conserved traits that facilitate internal root colonization.

## Cultivar-Specific Differentiation

Despite the shared core community, significant differences exist between Cannabis cultivars in their endorhiza microbiomes. In controlled experiments comparing multiple cultivars:

- **Endorhiza communities showed statistically significant separation by cultivar** using both weighted and [[weighted-vs-unweighted-unifrac-cannabis-strain-microbiome]] distance metrics (ADONIS R² = 0.59, p = 0.004 for weighted; R² = 0.39, p = 0.003 for unweighted).
- In contrast, rhizosphere and bulk soil communities did **not** show significant cultivar-level differences when soil type was held constant.
- The abundance of certain genera varied dramatically between cultivars. For example, *Methylophilus* comprised 13% of the endorhiza community in Bookoo Kush but only 0.13% in Burmese and was entirely absent in Sour Diesel.
- These differences were consistent across replicates, indicating robust genotype-level selection rather than stochastic variation.

This [[cannabis-microbiome-cultivar-specificity]] suggests that different Cannabis genotypes selectively filter or recruit different bacterial assemblages from the available soil-derived pool, likely through differences in root exudate composition, root architecture, mucilage chemistry, or immune signaling pathways.

## Functions of Endorhiza Bacteria

Endorhiza bacteria contribute to plant health through multiple mechanisms that have been documented across many plant systems and are presumed to operate similarly in Cannabis:

### Plant Growth Promotion
- **Phytohormone production**: Many endophytic bacteria produce auxins (particularly indole-3-acetic acid), cytokinins, and gibberellins that stimulate root growth, branching, and overall plant development.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Some endorhiza bacteria possess nitrogenase enzymes and can fix atmospheric nitrogen into forms available to the plant, supplementing soil nitrogen supplies.
- **Phosphate solubilization**: Endophytes can solubilize mineral phosphates through organic acid production, making phosphorus more bioavailable for root uptake.
- **ACC deaminase activity**: Some bacteria produce 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, which reduces ethylene levels in roots under stress conditions, promoting continued root elongation.

### Disease Suppression
- **Antibiotic production**: Endophytic bacteria produce antimicrobial compounds including phenazines, pyrrolnitrin, and hydrogen cyanide that inhibit pathogenic fungi and bacteria.
- **[[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] resistance (ISR)**: Some endophytes prime the plant immune system through jasmonic acid and ethylene signaling pathways, providing enhanced resistance to subsequent pathogen attack.
- **Niche competition**: By occupying root tissue space and consuming available nutrients, endophytes physically prevent colonization by pathogenic organisms.

### Stress Tolerance
- **Phytotoxicant degradation**: Endorhiza bacteria can help plants tolerate environmental toxicants including heavy metals and organic pollutants by degrading or sequestering harmful compounds.
- **Osmotic regulation**: Under drought or salinity stress, endophytes can produce osmoprotectant compounds like trehalose and proline that help maintain cellular homeostasis.
- **Heavy metal sequestration**: Some endophytes can bind heavy metals through extracellular polymeric substances, reducing their bioavailability and toxicity to the host plant.

## Phylum-Level Shifts from Soil to Root

Transitioning from bulk soil to endorhiza involves predictable and consistent changes in phylum-level bacterial composition that reflect the changing selective pressures:

- **Acidobacteria** typically decrease dramatically in the endorhiza compared to bulk soil. Acidobacteria are oligotrophic organisms adapted to low-nutrient conditions, and the relatively nutrient-rich root interior may be less favorable for them compared to copiotrophic competitors.
- **Proteobacteria** (particularly Alpha- and Gammaproteobacteria) generally increase in relative abundance in the endorhiza, reflecting the copiotrophic (nutrient-loving) nature of many endophytic taxa that thrive in the carbon-rich root environment.
- **Actinobacteria** often maintain or increase their presence in the endorhiza, consistent with their well-documented endophytic lifestyle and ability to produce antibiotics that may help them compete in the root interior.
- **Bacteroidetes** may show variable responses depending on the specific taxa present, but Sphingobacteriales members are consistently represented in Cannabis endorhiza communities.

## The Two-Tier Selection Context

The endorhiza is best understood within the two-tier selection model for plant-microbiome assembly. In the first tier, [[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]] such as soil pH, organic carbon content, salinity, and texture determine the bulk soil microbial community. This soil community then serves as the source pool for rhizosphere recruitment. In the second tier, the plant exerts genotype-dependent selection on the rhizosphere community as bacteria migrate into internal root tissues. This two-stage filtering process explains why endorhiza communities show the strongest cultivar signal, rhizosphere communities show an intermediate signal, and bulk soil communities show no cultivar signal at all.

The practical implication is that while soil management is essential for providing a diverse bacterial source pool, the specific endorhiza community that ultimately colonizes a Cannabis plant is largely determined by the plant's own genetics. This means that breeding programs can indirectly influence the microbiome, and that microbial inoculants must be compatible with the target cultivar to successfully establish in the endorhiza.

## Research Methods and Sampling Considerations

Studying the Cannabis endorhiza requires careful sample preparation to distinguish internal root colonizers from rhizoplane and rhizosphere contaminants:

1. Roots are gently excavated and loosely adhering soil is removed for rhizosphere samples
2. Roots are then surface-sterilized, typically using sequential rinses with ethanol and sterile water
3. Surface-sterilized roots are macerated or homogenized for DNA extraction from internal tissues
4. DNA extraction from endorhiza samples often requires modified protocols with heat pretreatment to lyse tough bacterial cell walls

The choice of DNA extraction method, primers, and sequencing depth all influence the ability to detect cultivar-specific differences. The 16S rRNA V4 region sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform has proven effective for characterizing Cannabis endorhiza communities, with sequencing depths of 3,000-45,000 sequences per sample providing adequate coverage.

## Implications for Cannabis Cultivation

Understanding the endorhiza microbiome has practical implications for both commercial and home Cannabis cultivation:

- **Cultivar-specific inoculants**: Because different cultivars host different endorhiza communities, probiotic or bioinoculant products may need to be tailored to specific cultivars or chemotype classes for optimal effectiveness.
- **Soil health management**: Since the endorhiza is ultimately derived from soil bacteria, maintaining diverse and healthy soil microbiology through composting, cover cropping, and minimal tillage is the foundation for beneficial [[fungal-endophyte-colonization-patterns]].
- **Organic production**: Organic growing systems that promote native soil microbial diversity may support more robust and diverse endorhiza communities than sterile or heavily amended conventional systems.
- **Terroir effects**: Just as endophytes contribute to the terroir of wine grapes, endorhiza bacteria may influence the terpene and cannabinoid profiles of different Cannabis cultivars, contributing to the unique chemical characteristics of cannabis grown in different regions.

## Future Research Directions

Several areas of Cannabis endorhiza research remain largely unexplored and represent opportunities for advancing cultivation science:

- **Functional metagenomics**: Most studies to date have characterized endorhiza communities through 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, which reveals taxonomy but not function. Shotgun metagenomic approaches could identify the specific genes and metabolic pathways active in the Cannabis endorhiza.
- **Temporal dynamics**: How the endorhiza community changes across the Cannabis life cycle — from seedling through vegetative growth to flowering — is poorly understood. Longitudinal sampling studies could reveal when microbial interventions would be most impactful.
- **Secondary metabolite interactions**: Cannabis produces a unique array of [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] including cannabinoids and terpenes. How these compounds influence endorhiza community composition, and conversely how endorhiza bacteria influence cannabinoid and terpene biosynthesis, represents a frontier research area.
- **Cross-compartment interactions**: The relationship between endorhiza bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi, and the phyllosphere microbiome has not been systematically studied in Cannabis. Understanding these cross-compartment connections could reveal holistic management strategies.
- **Breeding for microbial partnerships**: If specific endorhiza communities enhance yield or secondary metabolite production, Cannabis breeding programs could potentially select for genotypes that recruit beneficial microbial partners, analogous to how crop breeding has selected for mycorrhizal responsiveness.
