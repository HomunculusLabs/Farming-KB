## Interplant Carbon Transfer

### Classical Evidence

- **Bjorkman (1960)**: Demonstrated ¹⁴C transfer from pine to the achlorophyllous (non-photosynthetic) plant *Monotropa hypopitys* via ectomycorrhizal connections. This was the first definitive evidence of carbon movement between plants through shared fungal networks.
- **Read et al. (1985)**: Showed preferential ¹⁴C transfer to neighboring pine seedlings, suggesting that established trees can subsidize the growth of seedlings through common mycorrhizal networks.

### Community-Level Carbon Transfer

In *Allium vineale* populations, soil fungal communities dominated by mycorrhizae were beneficial for growth, especially when neighboring plants were genetically identical or from the same population. This indicates **population-level specificity** for hyphal network efficiency -- genetic similarity between connected plants enhances carbon transfer.

### Limits of Transfer

Not all mycorrhizal networks facilitate significant interplant transfer:
- Ikram et al. (1994): No nitrogen or phosphorus transfer detected between AM-associated *Pueraria phaseoloides* and *Hevea brasiliensis*.
- Transfer appears to be strongly influenced by source-sink dynamics, species compatibility, and environmental conditions.

## Mineral Weathering and Nutrient Access

Mycorrhizal fungi and their bacterial consortia enhance nutrient acquisition through rock dissolution. Saprotrophic and ECM fungi secrete organic acids (succinic, citric, oxalic, formate, malate) at micromolar concentrations to dissolve feldspars and hornblende. Mycorrhizae-bacteria consortia (e.g., *Glomus fasciculatus* with *Pseudomonas*/ *Agrobacterium*) enhance P acquisition from rock phosphate (Azcon et al., 1976).
