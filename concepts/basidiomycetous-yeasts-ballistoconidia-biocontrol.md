## Basidiomycetous Yeasts: Ballistoconidia and Biocontrol

Basidiomycetous yeasts are fungi that exist predominantly
in a unicellular (yeast) state but belong phylogenetically
to the Basidiomycota. Unlike the well-known ascomycetous
yeasts such as [[smut-fungi]]. The
  [[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]] at
the drop-spore interface overcomes the attachment force,
and the spore is launched into the air.

The distance of ballistoconidium discharge can reach
several millimeters, which is remarkable for a unicellular
organism. This active dispersal mechanism allows
ballistoconidium-producing yeasts to rapidly colonize new
surfaces and escape from competing microorganisms. The
spores are small (typically 3-10 micrometers) and can be
carried by air currents to distant substrates.

### Ecological Significance

The ability to actively discharge spores provides a
competitive advantage in the phyllosphere, where yeasts
must colonize new leaves, escape from older and
resource-depleted leaves, and maintain populations across
seasonal changes. Ballistoconidia can also serve as
inoculum for aerial dispersal to soil, water surfaces,
and other habitats.

### Major Ballistoconidium-Producing Genera

- **Sporobolomyces**: Red-pigmented yeasts commonly
  isolated from leaf surfaces, tree exudates, and soil.
  Sporobolomyces roseus has been isolated from the
  surfaces of basidiocarps, including Leccinum species.
  These yeasts form part of the normal phyllosphere
  mycobiota on many plant species.
