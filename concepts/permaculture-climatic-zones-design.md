# Permaculture Climatic Zones Design

updated: 2026-18-04

Permaculture design strategies differ significantly across climatic zones. Chapter 5 of Mollison's Designers' Manual establishes the broad climatic classification framework, while later chapters provide design specifics for each zone. A basic strategy is to spread the risk of crop failure by a mixture of crop species, varieties, and strategies.

## Broad Climatic Classification

Mollison uses these broad categories (based on Koppen classification modified by Trewartha):

- TROPICAL: no month under 18 degrees C mean temperature
- SUBTROPICAL: coolest months above 0 degrees C but below 18 degrees C (frost-free)
- TEMPERATE: coldest months below 0 degrees C, warmest above 10 degrees C
- POLAR: warmest month below 10 degrees C
- ARID: mean rainfall 50 cm or less (where evaporation exceeds precipitation)
- DESERT: mean rainfall 25 cm or less

The main qualifying factors on broad climatic classification are special mountain conditions, modifying effects of coasts, local energy transfer by winds and oceanic currents, and long-term cyclic factors.

## Tropical and Humid Tropics

Design strategies (Chapter 10):
- Multi-storied polycultures emulating natural forest structure
- Rapid nutrient cycling through mulch and decomposition
- Integration of tree crops with understory food plants
- Palm polycultures and vine systems on trellis
- House and fence form essential trellis for garden production
- Chicken tractor systems for land preparation before planting
- Forage systems under tree crops for livestock integration
- Padi (rice/taro) terraces combined with fish and duck production
- Slope stabilisation through permanent vegetation cover

The FAO has demonstrated that monoculture in the humid tropics fails compared to polycultural systems. Local polycultures consistently out-perform introduced temperate techniques.

## Arid and Desert

Design strategies (Chapter 11):
- Water harvesting as the primary design priority
- Earth-sheltered or underground housing for thermal comfort
- Narrow east-west streets, dense clustered housing
- White-painted massive walls, small windows, ventilation towers
- Evaporation cooling from water features, mulch, and vegetation
- Wadi and foothill sites preferred for settlement
- Thermal belt 10-20 m above peneplain for frost-free house sites
- Infiltration systems: swales, pits, gabions, check dams
- Floodwater harvesting from braided streams and wadis
- Drought-adapted species with deep root systems
- Phosphate cycling and nutrient concentration strategies

## Cool Temperate

Design strategies (Chapter 12):
- Settlement at mid-slope thermal belt on sun-facing slopes
- 30-100% glazing on sunward walls, minimal glazing on west walls
- Earth floors for thermal mass, insulated perimeters
- Dense windbreak polewards and to east/west (suntrap structure)
- Conjoined housing with barns and storage (reduce outdoor exposure)
- Crop rotation with ley periods, permanent pasture management
- Winter food storage: root cellars, silage, pit storage
- Keyline water management integrating storage with soil conditioning
- Frost avoidance: site above frost lines, use forest clearings
- Humus-rich soils, liming for pH adjustment
- Forest garden systems with hardy fruit and nut species

## Key Climate Design Principles

In house design, the interactions of thermal mass (heat storage) and insulation (buffering for temperature extremes) plus sensible siting permit efficient housing over broad climatic ranges. Strategies such as water storage and windbreak modify extreme effects.

As designers, we are as interested in extremes as in means. "Average rainfall" has little relevance to specific sites. More valuable are data on seasonal fluctuation, dependability, intensity, and the limits of recorded ranges. It is obligatory to study regional long-term human and agricultural adaptations to climate.

Avoid introducing temperate (European) techniques and species to tropical and arid lands on any large scale. Native management practices are often finely tuned to survival and independent of outside aid.

## Altitudinal Effects

The altitudinal factor is crucial to design strategies. For every 100 m of altitude gain, temperature drops approximately 0.6 degrees C. This creates compressed climatic zones on mountain slopes, allowing multiple climate strategies within a single property. The thermal belt on hillsides (above cold air drainage) is a critical site selection factor.

## Merged: Urban Bathtub Greywater Harvesting And Homestead Renovation

# Urban Bathtub Greywater Harvesting and Homestead Renovation

The Headington family homestead demonstrates practical urban [[comparison-greywater-vs-rainwater-harvesting]], rainwater collection, and energy-conscious renovation techniques applicable to any small urban property.

## Water Harvesting System

### Cistern Design
A 1,500-gallon cistern was installed, replacing a septic tank elevated two feet above the highest point of the property with earth and rock. This allows watering by gravity -- no pumps, no maintenance, no moving parts. The cistern is located along the fence line where the neighbour has a rental cottage. In this location, the cistern serves multiple functions simultaneously:

- Fire break
- Privacy screen
- Temperature moderator for an orange tree just to the north
- The tree shades the cistern and drops leaves for mulch

This stacking of functions exemplifies permaculture climatic zones design design principles applied to urban infrastructure.

### Bathtub Greywater Harvesting
The bathtub drain was inaccessible for conventional greywater plumbing, so a simple solution was devised: a 1/2-inch vinyl tube runs through the window jamb, with one end suction-cupped to the bottom of the tub and the other end lying under the winter-deciduous black mission fig which shades the bedroom in summer. A hand pump starts the siphon going.

For distributing water to the north side of the house, the hose is hooked to an old half-inch irrigation line which deposits the water on Asian pears and a loquat. This simple, low-cost approach to [[gaias-garden-greywater-systems]] requires no electricity and minimal infrastructure.

### Water Conservation Results
Installing a low-flush toilet combined with the cistern and greywater system cut all watering needs by two-thirds. Composting toilet designs were also being researched to provide humanure to fruit trees and other plants.

## Building Renovation

### Plaster and Walls
Rather than using dry wall, existing wood lath was cleaned and patched with new plaster by grooving mortar joints a quarter to a half inch -- no chicken wire was needed. Replastering used dual-purpose gypsum (easier to work with, slower setting time), mixed at 3 parts mortar sand to one part gypsum.

### Natural Paint
Paint recipe: equal parts boiled linseed oil and turpentine mixed with concrete pigments, then washed on with rags or a sponge. This simple, non-toxic paint formula produces a beautiful eggshell white finish.

### Floor and Crawl Space
Layers of old carpet and linoleum were peeled up to reveal extensive termite damage. The floor joists were found to be resting on dirt. A 2-3 foot crawl space was excavated under the entire floor, providing cool storage for root vegetables and other crops.
