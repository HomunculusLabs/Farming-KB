# Permaculture Biogas Systems

Permaculture biogas systems use anaerobic digestion of organic waste to produce methane gas for cooking, heating, and electricity generation while simultaneously treating sewage, reducing pathogens, and producing nutrient-rich effluent for agriculture. This technology closes the loop between waste and energy production.

[[permaculture-design]] describes biogas systems extensively in Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, using the Melbourne sewage treatment system at Werribee as a large-scale model. The [[biogas-digester-basics]] page provides introductory coverage, while this page explores the permaculture design integration of biogas systems.

## The Biology of Biogas Production

Biogas is a mixture of methane (CH4), carbon dioxide (CO2), and ammonia gas (NH3), with traces of hydrogen sulphide. It is a useful fuel gas for engines or cooking gas for homes.

The production process involves three stages of ponds or digesters: anaerobic (methane-producing), facultative (part methane, part oxygen-producing), and aerobic (oxygen-producing).

In the anaerobic stage, sludge breaks down through bacterial action in the absence of oxygen. Of the total dissolved solids entering a digester over a 20-day period at 25-30 degrees Celsius, a high percentage of the mass is transferred into methane.

The methane produced removes biological oxygen demand (BOD), reducing pollution. One cubic meter of methane generated removes about 2.89 kg of solids from the waste stream.

## Heavy Metal and Pathogen Removal

Anaerobic digestion is remarkably effective at removing contaminants from sewage. Research from the Werribee system documented the following removal rates achieved anaerobically: copper 78%, cadmium 70%, zinc 83%, nickel 47%, lead 90%, chromium 47%, iron 47%.

Overall removal rates including facultative and aerobic stages reach 85-97% for most heavy metals. The hydrogen sulphide produced combines with heavy metals to form insoluble sulphides, effectively locking them out of the food chain.

In the digester, 90-94% of worm eggs are destroyed, as are many harmful bacteria. This makes anaerobic digestion a powerful sanitation technology.

## Small-Scale Biogas Design

For a community or homestead scale, biogas systems can be remarkably compact. One thousand people need as little as 270 square meters of settling pond one meter deep.

This can be achieved as a long narrow pond 3 meters wide by 90 meters long, or three side-by-side ponds 30 meters long and 3 meters wide. Doubling the depth to 2 meters halves the surface area needed.

A deeper agitated digester pond 3 meters deep, 3 meters wide, and 30 meters long can serve the same population. Such a long narrow pond is easily made totally anaerobic by fitting water seals and a weighted cover of plastic, metal, butyl rubber, or fibreglass.

The sludge must be artificially agitated using pumped biogas to stir it, otherwise it settles and becomes inactive. Scum formation on the surface can also slow the process and cause acidic conditions.

## System Outputs

Biogas systems produce multiple valuable outputs. The methane gas can fuel engines for electricity generation, be compressed for cooking or machinery, or provide heat for the digester process itself.

The nutrient-rich effluent from facultative and aerobic ponds supports rich algal growth. Four almost universal sewage lagoon algae are Euglena, Chlamydomonas, Chlorella, and Scenedesmus.

This algal food supports zooplankton including rotifers, cladocerans, copepods, and ostracods. Both zooplankton and algae can be harvested as poultry feed, providing a complete waste-to-food cycle.

Pumped into forests or fields, the treated effluent provides manures and trace elements for plant growth. In this way, [[permaculture-earthworks]] such as treatment ponds become productive rather than purely functional.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Biogas systems integrate with multiple elements of a permaculture site. The methane produced can fuel cooking stoves, space heaters, water heaters, or electricity generators, connecting to [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] goals of local energy self-reliance.

The heat output from biogas combustion or from the digester itself can warm [[permaculture-building-design]] elements such as greenhouses. Solar ponds can provide accessory heat to maintain optimal digester temperatures.

The treated effluent flows into [[permaculture-aquaculture]] systems or irrigates [[food-forest-design]] plantings. The algal and zooplankton harvest feeds [[permaculture-domestic-animals]] including poultry and fish.

In [[permaculture-zone-planning]], biogas digesters are placed in Zone 1 or Zone 2 due to the need for regular management and the frequent use of gas for cooking.

## Climate Considerations

Biogas production is temperature-dependent, with optimal activity at 25-30 degrees Celsius. In cold climates, the digester may need heating from solar collectors or from the methane produced to maintain activity.

In tropical and subtropical climates, ambient temperatures often support continuous biogas production without supplementary heating, making the technology particularly valuable in these regions.

The [[permaculture-climate-strategies]] framework helps designers assess whether biogas is appropriate for their site and what modifications may be needed for their climate zone.

## Environmental Significance

Biogas is also a gaseous component of the atmosphere creating the greenhouse effect. Capturing and using it rather than releasing it to air is an environmental benefit of anaerobic digestion systems.

The combination of energy production, waste treatment, pathogen destruction, heavy metal removal, and nutrient recycling makes biogas one of the most multi-functional technologies available to permaculture designers.

The [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] approach identifies biogas as a key technology for maintaining energy services during the transition away from fossil fuels, particularly for cooking and small-scale electricity generation.
