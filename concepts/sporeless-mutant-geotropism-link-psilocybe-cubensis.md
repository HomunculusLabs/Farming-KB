# Sporeless Mutant and Geotropism Link in Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

One of the most significant findings from Edmond R. Badham's 1982 wind tunnel study of *Psilocybe cubensis* was the discovery that **sporeless (sterile) mutants fail to exhibit the negative [[light-role-spore-formation-geotropic-sensitivity-psilocybe-cubensis]] in mushrooms is mechanistically linked to spore production, likely through a gill-derived hormonal signal.

## The Geotropic Response in Fertile Basidiocarps

In normal (fertile) *P. cubensis* development, the basidiocarp undergoes a dramatic reorientation at approximately **Stage 2** of development. The stipe, which has been growing anemotropically (toward evaporative demand), abruptly changes direction. The pileus turns **upward** and the lamellae face downward — a classic negative geotropic response. This reorientation coincides with the onset of spore formation and serves to position the gills optimally for basidiospore release.

### Key Features of the Normal Geotropic Response

- Occurs during the transition from Stage 2 to Stage 3.
- The pileus rotates upward from the direction of wind, opening with lamellae facing downward.
- Happens when light is incident from above or below the cultures.
- Does not occur in complete darkness (which prevents spore formation).
- Masks the earlier Phase 1 anemotropic response.

## The Sterile Mutant Phenotype

### Source of the Mutant

Badham studied a sterile mutant derived from the same isolate used in his previous work on [[pf-tek-vermiculite-brown-rice-substrate-dry-barrier]] alongside the fertile strain under identical conditions.

### Absence of Geotropic Recurvation

The critical observation was that **sterile basidiocarps continued growing in the direction of the wind throughout their entire development**. They did not exhibit the negative geotropic curvature (recurvation) that characterizes Stage 2–3 in fertile mushrooms. Specifically:

- In wind tunnel experiments, sterile basidiocarps grew persistently into the airflow from Stage 1 through maturity (Figure 2c in the original paper).
- No pileus reorientation occurred at any developmental stage.
- The stipe continued elongating in the anemotropic direction without the geotropic override.

### Normal Phase 1 Anemotropism in Sterile Mutants

Importantly, the sterile mutants displayed **perfectly normal Phase 1 anemotropic behavior**. Their stipes curved into the wind just like fertile basidiocarps during Stages 1–2. This demonstrates that:

1. The sensory and growth machinery for tropic curvature is intact in sterile mutants.
2. The defect is specific to the Phase 2 geotropic switch, not to tropic responsiveness generally.
3. Phase 1 anemotropism operates through a mechanism independent of spore production.

## Implications for the Mushroom Hormone Hypothesis

### Historical Context

The existence of a "[[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]* carpophores.
- **Hagimoto (1963)** linked a mushroom hormone to geotropic responses in *A. bisporus*.
- **Hagimoto and Konishi (1960)** showed that agar diffusates from gills could partially replace pileus influence.
- **Decapitation experiments** had connected these hormones to the lamellae.

### The Spore-Geotropism Connection

Badham's sterile mutant data provided strong evidence for the hormone hypothesis:

- Since sterile mutants lack functional gill tissue (no spore production), they also lack the gill-derived substance that triggers geotropic reorientation.
- The fact that Phase 1 anemotropism remains intact while Phase 2 geotropism is absent argues for **two distinct regulatory mechanisms**.
- The simplest explanation is that a substance produced in the gills during spore formation modifies the tropic response, switching the mushroom's sensitivity from evaporative demand (wind) to gravity.

### Proposed Mechanism

Badham suggested that a substance produced in the **lamellae** during spore formation:

1. Is either absent or non-functional in sterile mutants.
2. Normally acts to override the Phase 1 evaporative demand response.
3. Causes the stipe to respond primarily to gravity during sporulation.
4. May be the same as or related to the "[[mushroom-growth-hormone-decapitation-gill-diffusate-evidence-basidiomycetes]]" described by earlier researchers.

This is consistent with the decapitation experiments of Hagimoto and Konishi, who showed that agar diffusates from gills could partially replace pileus influence on stipe growth direction.

## Role of Light in the Spore-Geotropism Axis

The relationship between light, spore production, and geotropism creates a three-way dependency:

- **Light is required for spore production** — in complete darkness, few or no spores form.
- **Spore production is required for geotropism** — sterile mutants do not reorient.
- **Some light is required for the geotropic response** — but this requirement may be entirely indirect, mediated through spore formation rather than a direct phototropic signal.

This was confirmed by the observation that when fertile mushrooms were rotated horizontally to equalize gravity under constant illumination, both stipe elongation and pileus expansion proceeded horizontally — the mushroom grew perpendicular to gravity even when evaporative demand was equalized on all sides.
