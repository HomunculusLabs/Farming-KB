# Windbreak and Shelterbelt Design

Windbreaks and shelterbelts are linear plantings of trees and shrubs arranged to reduce wind speed, protect crops and buildings, conserve soil moisture, and provide wildlife habitat. They are foundational elements in [[permaculture-design|permaculture]] site design and [[hedgerow-planting]].

## Windbreak vs Shelterbelt

- **Windbreak**: 1-3 rows of trees and shrubs, typically protecting a specific area (garden, livestock, building)
- **Shelterbelt**: Wider planting (5+ rows) designed for large-scale agricultural protection, often including multiple species in a complex structure

## Design Principles

### Height

Windbreak height determines the protected zone:

- Wind speed is reduced for a distance of **10-15 times the windbreak height** on the downwind side
- A 30-foot-tall windbreak protects an area extending 300-450 feet downwind
- Maximum protection (50-60% wind reduction) occurs within 3-5 times the windbreak height
- Plan for the mature height of selected species, not the planting height

### Density

Optimal density allows 40-60% of wind to pass through the barrier:

- **Too dense (80%+)**: Wind is forced over the top and creates damaging turbulence on the lee side
- **Too open (under 30%)**: Wind passes through with little reduction
- **Ideal**: A mix of deciduous and evergreen species with varied heights and densities
- Dense evergreen rows on the windward side, more open deciduous rows on the leeward side

### Length

- Windbreaks should extend **at least 10 times their height** beyond the area being protected on both ends
- This prevents wind from wrapping around the ends and undermining protection
- Example: A 30-foot windbreak protecting a 200-foot field should be at least 200+ feet long
- If space limits length, consider an L-shaped or U-shaped windbreak

### Orientation

- Orient perpendicular to the prevailing wind direction
- In most of North America, prevailing winter winds are from the northwest; summer winds from the southwest
- In regions with multiple wind directions, a multi-row design with different orientations may be needed
- Use local weather data to determine predominant wind direction

## Row Design

### Multi-Row Structure

A well-designed windbreak uses multiple rows with different functions:

**Row 1 (Windward side — facing the wind)**
- Low-growing, dense shrubs
- Traps snow, reduces wind at ground level
- Species: Caragana, dogwood, lilac, ninebark, juniper (low varieties)

**Row 2**
- Medium-height evergreen trees
- Main wind-deflection layer, year-round protection
- Species: Colorado blue spruce, white spruce, red cedar, Austrian pine, Douglas fir

**Row 3**
- Tall deciduous trees
- Extends windbreak height, provides summer shade, allows winter sun through bare branches
- Species: Hackberry, green ash, honey locust, cottonwood, hybrid poplar, bur oak

**Row 4 (Leeward side)**
- Medium evergreen or dense deciduous trees
- Fills gaps, extends protection
- Species: White pine, Norway spruce, red pine, linden, hackberry

**Row 5 (Leeward edge)**
- Low shrubs
- Filters remaining wind, provides wildlife habitat and food
- Species: Elderberry, serviceberry, chokecherry, hazelnut, American plum

### Row Spacing

- Space rows 12-20 feet apart
- Within-row spacing depends on species:
  - Large trees: 8-16 feet apart
  - Medium trees: 6-12 feet apart
  - Shrubs: 4-8 feet apart
- Stagger planting within rows so plants are not directly behind those in adjacent rows

## Species Selection

### Deciduous Trees
- **Fast-growing**: Hybrid poplar, cottonwood, silver maple (short-lived, 20-30 years)
- **Medium**: Green ash, hackberry, honey locust, bur oak, red maple, linden
- **Slow-growing**: White oak, bur oak, sugar maple, black walnut (long-lived, 100+ years)

### Evergreen Trees
- **Conifers**: White spruce, Colorado blue spruce, red pine, white pine, Douglas fir, balsam fir, Austrian pine
- **Broadleaf evergreens**: Eastern red cedar, American holly

### Shrubs
- Caragana (Siberian pea shrub): Hardy, nitrogen-fixing, edible seeds
- Dogwood (Cornus spp.): Red stems, wildlife food
- Lilac (Syringa vulgaris): Fragrant flowers, dense growth
- Ninebark (Physocarpus opulifolius): Hardy, attractive foliage
- Serviceberry (Amelanchier spp.): Edible berries, multi-season interest
- Chokecherry (Prunus virginiana): Wildlife food, erosion control
- Hazelnut (Corylus spp.): Edible nuts, wildlife food

### Considerations
- **Native species preferred**: Better adapted, support more wildlife, less maintenance
- **Mixed species**: Avoid monoculture — diverse plantings resist pests and diseases
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Include leguminous trees and shrubs to improve soil fertility
- **Wildlife value**: Select species providing food, nesting, and cover

## Establishment

### Site Preparation
- Begin 1-2 years before planting
- Control weeds and sod in the planting strip (cultivation, sheet mulching, or herbicide if organic methods are insufficient)
- Test soil and amend if needed (pH correction, phosphorus addition)
- Mark rows and planting positions
- Install tree protection (tubes, fencing) before planting if deer or livestock pressure is expected

### Planting
- **Timing**: Bareroot trees in early spring (before bud break) or fall; container trees can go in spring through early fall
- **Bareroot planting**: Dig hole wide enough to spread roots without crowding; plant at original soil line (find root collar)
- **Container planting**: Remove from pot, loosen circling roots, plant at same depth as in container
- **Water immediately**: Deep watering after planting is critical
- **Mulch**: 3-4 inches of organic mulch over root zone, keep away from trunk

### Early Care (Years 1-3)
- Water weekly during growing season if rainfall is insufficient (1 inch per week)
- Weed control is the most critical maintenance — competition kills more young trees than any other factor
- Mow or weed between rows; hand-pull weeds within 3 feet of trees
- Replace dead trees in the first two years to maintain row continuity
- Protect from animal damage: tree tubes, fencing, repellents
