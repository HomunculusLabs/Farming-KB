Mycorrhizal fungi are most commonly associated with soil-grown plants,
but research has demonstrated that these symbiotic organisms can also
thrive in [[cannabis-grow-hydroponic-growing-systems]], providing many of the same
benefits they offer in traditional soil environments. The application
of mycorrhizal fungi to hydroponics represents a growing area of
interest for commercial growers and home hobbyists alike, as it offers
a biological approach to enhancing nutrient uptake, disease resistance,
and overall plant vigor without relying solely on chemical nutrient
solutions.

## Why Mycorrhizae Matter in Hydroponics

In a hydroponic system, plants receive their nutrients from a liquid
delivery system rather than from soil. Despite this fundamental
difference, mycorrhizal fungi provide the same core benefits in
hydroponic substrates as they do in soil:

- Inoculated roots show greater resistance to common hydroponic
  pathogens including Rhizoctonia, Fusarium, Pythium, and
  Phytophthora, which are among the most problematic diseases in
  recirculating water systems.
- Mycorrhizal fungi create physical barriers around roots that
  protect them from infection by pathogenic fungi and bacteria.
- Colonized plant roots branch more extensively and develop more
  feeder roots, increasing the surface area available for nutrient
  retrieval from the growing substrate.
- The mycorrhizosphere supports [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] that
  produce protective metabolites, creating a more resilient root
  environment.

The key advantage is that mycorrhizal fungi are exceptionally
efficient at delivering phosphorus and nitrogen to their host plants.
Even in systems where these nutrients are supplied in solution, the
fungal hyphae expand the effective root zone into the surrounding
substrate -- whether that substrate is rockwool, coconut fiber, oasis
cubes, gravel, vermiculite, perlite, clay pebbles, or other inert
media. The same hyphal extension that occurs with colonized roots in
soil also develops in hydroponic systems, provided conditions are
suitable.

## Critical Environmental Requirements

For mycorrhizal fungi to establish and function in hydroponics,
several [[maitake-environmental-parameters]] must be carefully managed. These
requirements differ from those of traditional soil-based systems and
demand additional attention from the grower.

### Oxygenation

A hydroponic system supporting [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] must be fully
aerobic. Dissolved oxygen levels should be maintained at 6 to 8 parts
per million. For context, most tap water provides approximately 5 ppm
of oxygen, which is below the ideal range. Air stones or bubblers can
be used to supplement oxygen levels. Growers should note that hydrogen
peroxide, sometimes used to oxygenate hydroponic water, will kill both
mycorrhizal fungi and host plant tissue and must never be used in
systems where mycorrhizae are present.

### Phosphorus Management

Phosphorus levels are perhaps the most critical factor. When
phosphorus concentrations reach approximately 70 parts per million in
the nutrient solution, fungal spores go dormant and will not germinate.
This is a significant concern because hydroponic nutrient formulations
often contain elevated phosphorus levels. Growers must monitor
phosphorus carefully and avoid over-fertilization to encourage
mycorrhizal formation. Some practitioners recommend maintaining
phosphorus below 30 ppm for optimal colonization.

### Water Quality

Chlorine and chloramines in municipal water supplies can adversely
affect mycorrhizal growth. Chlorine dissipates from water in about
eight hours when left standing, but chloramines take days to evaporate
and must be actively removed using appropriate filtration methods.
Hydroponic suppliers can recommend specific water treatment approaches
compatible with mycorrhizal inoculation.

### pH Range

Most mycorrhizal fungi require a pH between 5.5 and 7.0 for optimal
growth and colonization. This range overlaps with the requirements of
many hydroponic crops, making compatibility generally manageable, but
pH must still be monitored and adjusted as needed.

### Temperature

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] thrive at temperatures of 65 to 75
degrees Fahrenheit (24 to 30 degrees Celsius), which aligns with the
[[query-what-is-the-ideal-temperature-range-for-growing-cannabis]] for most cultivated plants. However, vesicles,
spores, and hyphal tissue will not survive temperatures above 120
degrees Fahrenheit (49 degrees Celsius). This temperature sensitivity
also applies to the storage of inoculant products.

## Interaction with Trichoderma

Many hydroponic growers already use Trichoderma species as biological
control agents against root pathogens. Trichoderma fungi protect plants
from pathogenic organisms and assist with nutrient cycling from organic
matter. Mycorrhizal fungi and Trichoderma are compatible and
complementary: Trichoderma cycles nutrients from organic amendments,
while mycorrhizal fungi actively transport nutrients directly into
plant cells. Using both together can provide broader protection and
improved nutrient access than either alone.

## Inoculation Methods for Hydroponic Systems

Several approaches exist for introducing mycorrhizal propagules into
hydroponic systems. Hydroponic supply sources offer mycorrhizal blends
specifically formulated for soilless growing, available in both liquid
and powder forms.

The most effective method is to ensure inoculants make direct contact
with rooted or unrooted cuttings before placement into the hydroponic
system. Starter cubes can be soaked in liquid formulations or in
granular formulations mixed with water. Once the spores contact the
roots, they germinate in response to root exudate signals, just as they
would in soil.

Many mycorrhizal formulations can be added directly to the nutrient
delivery system. The particles in these mixes are small enough to pass
through irrigation lines and emitters without causing clogs. However,
it is important to follow the manufacturer's recommendations regarding
[[ingham-compost-tea-application-rates-and-timing]] timing.

## Reproduction Considerations

Unlike in soil environments, mycorrhizal fungi do not generally
reproduce prolifically in hydroponic systems. This means that periodic
re-application of inoculants is necessary to maintain colonization
throughout the life of the plant. As roots develop and the root mass
increases, additional inoculant should be added to the nutrient
delivery system. Fast-growing plants with rapid root development require
more frequent supplementation.
