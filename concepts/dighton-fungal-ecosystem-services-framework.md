# Fungal Ecosystem Services Framework

Fungi provide a wide range of ecosystem services that underpin the
functioning of terrestrial, freshwater, and [[fungal-biodiversity-marine-ecosystems-coastal-deep-sea]]. These
services include soil formation, fertility maintenance, primary
production support, plant community regulation, secondary production
as food sources, pollutant modification, and [[gadd-fungal-carbon-sequestration]]. The
first law of thermodynamics indicates that matter can neither be created
nor destroyed; fungi transform elements between organic and inorganic
pools, driving the nutrient cycles that sustain all life.

## Soil Formation Services

[[query-how-do-fungi-contribute-to-carbon-sequestration]] soil formation through rock dissolution and particle
binding. Lichens, saprotrophs, and mycorrhizae all participate in
the chemical and physical breakdown of parent rock material. Lichen
acids dissolve minerals at the rock-lichen interface, releasing
cations and contributing to proto-soil formation. [[comparison-mycorrhizal-vs-saprotrophic-fungi]]
and mycorrhizae produce organic acids and chelating agents that
further weather minerals. Fungal hyphae physically bind soil particles
into aggregates, establishing the soil structure necessary for plant
establishment and water retention.

## Soil Fertility Maintenance

Decomposition of organic residues by saprotrophic fungi (including
ericoid and ectomycorrhizal species) and the consequent nutrient
mineralization are perhaps the most fundamental fungal ecosystem
service. Fungi produce [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] including cellulases,
ligninases, phosphatases, and proteases that break down complex
plant polymers into forms available for plant uptake. The balance
between decomposition and mineralization rates and plant nutrient
uptake determines soil fertility. Fungi also stabilize soil
aggregates through glomalin production by [[glomeromycota-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]]
and hyphal enmeshment of soil particles.

## Primary Production Support

Fungi support primary production through direct production by lichens
[[fungal-biodiversity-in-extreme-environments]], nutrient accessibility through mycorrhizal
associations, plant yield enhancement, defense against pathogens
provided by mycorrhizae, endophytes, and saprotrophs, and defense
against herbivory by endophytic fungi. Lichen soil crusts in deserts
fix carbon and nitrogen, enabling plant colonization. Mycorrhizal
fungi extend the effective nutrient-absorbing surface area of plant
roots by orders of magnitude beyond the root-hair system alone.

## Plant Community Structure Regulation

Fungi regulate plant community structure through plant-plant
interactions mediated by mycorrhizae and pathogens. Mycorrhizal
networks can facilitate or inhibit seedling establishment depending
on the compatibility between existing fungal networks and incoming
plant species. Pathogenic fungi can selectively reduce dominant plant
species, maintaining species diversity. Endophytic fungi alter
competitive relationships among plant species through effects on
growth rate, [[fungal-lichen-stress-tolerance-and-poikilohydry]], and herbivore resistance.

## Secondary Production as Food Source

Fungi serve as a food source for diverse animal groups. As secondary
production, fruit bodies and mycelia are consumed by vertebrates and
invertebrates. The fungal biomass in soil supports populations of
collembola, mites, nematodes, and other microarthropods. In aquatic
ecosystems, fungal-colonized leaf litter supports shredder
invertebrate populations. Population and biomass regulation by
pathogenic fungi affects the dynamics of plant and animal
populations across all ecosystem types.
