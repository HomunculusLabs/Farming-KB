## Ecological Significance

Fungal secondary productivity is a critical link between decomposition and higher trophic levels in all ecosystems. By converting low-quality plant detritus into nitrogen-rich fungal biomass, fungi upgrade the nutritional quality of organic matter for consumers, facilitating energy transfer through food webs. This fungal loop is particularly important in forest ecosystems where fungal biomass can constitute the majority of microbial biomass and where the majority of energy reaching higher trophic levels passes through fungal biomass rather than direct detritus consumption.

The scale of fungal secondary productivity has practical implications for both natural resource management and human food systems. Wild mushroom harvesting provides food and income for communities worldwide, while cultivated mushroom production converts agricultural waste into nutritious food at industrial scales. Understanding the factors that regulate fungal biomass production is therefore relevant to sustainable forestry, wildlife management, and food security.

## Research Methods

Fungal secondary productivity is measured through ergosterol quantification for living fungal biomass, phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis for total fungal biomass, and direct hyphal length measurements by microscopy. Decomposition experiments using litterbags with different mesh sizes separate fungal and faunal contributions to mass loss. Stable isotope techniques using 13C and 15N trace the flow of fungal-derived carbon and nitrogen through food webs, while molecular gut content analysis reveals the dietary composition of fungal grazers.
