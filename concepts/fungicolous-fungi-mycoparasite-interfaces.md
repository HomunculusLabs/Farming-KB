# Fungicolous Fungi and Mycoparasite-Host Interfaces

## Definition and Scope

Fungicolous fungi are fungi that are consistently found in association with other fungi. This broad category encompasses fungi that colonize sporocarps, mycelia, sclerotia, spores, or fruiting structures of other fungal species. A notable subset is **lichenicolous fungi**, which grow on or within lichen thalli. Another recognized subgroup is **sporocarp-inhabiting fungi (SCIF)**, species that specifically colonize the fruiting bodies of macrofungi.

The diversity of fungicolous fungi is considerable. Hawksworth (1979) estimated approximately 1,700 non-lichenicolous and over 1,500 lichenicolous species worldwide, underscoring the ecological significance of fungus-fungus interactions in virtually all terrestrial ecosystems. Chapter 17 of *The Mycota* (Gams, Diederich, Põldmaa) provides a foundational synthesis of this field.

## Types of Fungicolous Associations

Fungicolous relationships span a continuum of interactions, which can be classified into three broad categories:

- **Neutralistic**: The two fungi coexist without measurable positive or negative effects on one another. Many commensal SCIF species likely fall into this category.
- **Mutualistic**: Both partners derive benefit from the association. These relationships are less commonly documented among fungicolous fungi but do occur.
- **Antagonistic / Competitive**: One fungus suppresses, damages, or kills the other. This category includes the mycoparasites, which derive nutrition from living fungal tissue.

The term **mycoparasite** specifically refers to a fungus that uses another living fungus as a nutrient source. Mycoparasitism is a major ecological strategy and has attracted interest for biocontrol applications, particularly against plant pathogenic fungi.

## Trophic Relationships

### Biotrophy vs. Necrotrophy

The nutritional strategy of a mycoparasite can be placed along a biotrophy–necrotrophy spectrum:

- **Biotrophic mycoparasites** maintain a balanced relationship with their host, extracting nutrients without causing rapid cell death. The host remains alive and functional during the interaction.
- **Necrotrophic mycoparasites** actively kill host cells, typically through the secretion of lytic enzymes, toxins, or both, and then colonize the dead tissue.
- Many species exhibit **intermediate strategies**, combining features of both biotrophy and necrotrophy depending on environmental conditions or the specific host involved.

A striking example of host-dependent trophic plasticity is *Hypomyces chrysospermus*, which behaves as a necrotroph when colonizing mushroom sporocarps (e.g., *Boletus* species) but acts as a biotroph when growing on *Botrytis* or *Trichothecium*.

### Rudakov's Classification System

Rudakov proposed six groups to categorize fungicolous fungi by their trophic mode:

1. **Obligate biotrophs**: Fully dependent on a living host; cannot be cultured independently.
2. **Facultative biotrophs**: Primarily biotrophic but capable of limited saprotrophic growth.
3. **Obligate necrotrophs**: Kill host tissue as a prerequisite for nutrient acquisition.
4. **Facultative necrotrophs**: Can switch between necrotrophic and saprotrophic modes.
5. **Semisaprophytic mycophiles**: Occupy a transitional niche, deriving some nutrients from senescent or moribund host tissue.
6. **Saprophytic associates**: True saprotrophs that colonize dead fungal material but are consistently found on fungal substrates.

## Hyphal Interference

**Hyphal interference** is a form of antagonism in which one fungus reduces the growth rate of another at a distance—typically within 50 mm—without direct physical contact. This phenomenon is mediated by diffusible metabolites, including antibiotics and volatile organic compounds.

Microscopic examination of affected hyphae reveals characteristic symptoms: pronounced vacuolation, accumulation of lipid droplets, and invagination of the plasmalemma. These changes indicate metabolic disruption and eventual cell death. Hyphal interference is ecologically important as a mechanism of competitive exclusion and is exploited in biocontrol agents such as *Trichoderma* species.

## Mycoparasite-Host Interfaces

Jeffries and Young (1994) described five distinct types of structural interfaces between mycoparasites and their hosts. These categories reflect the degree of physical intimacy and the mode of nutrient acquisition:

### 1. Contact Necrotrophic

The mycoparasite grows alongside the host without penetrating its cells. Damage is mediated through extracellular enzymes and toxic metabolites that act at the contact surface or via hyphal interference. No specialized penetration structures are formed.

**Examples:** *Arthrobotrys* spp. and *Tilletiopsis* spp. on powdery mildew (*Sphaerotheca*). The interaction relies heavily on antibiosis and cell wall degradation from the exterior.

### 2. Invasive Necrotrophic

The mycoparasite physically penetrates the host hyphae, growing within the host tissue and causing progressive cell death. Penetration is typically achieved through appressorium-like structures and enzymatic dissolution of the host cell wall.

**Examples:** *Trichoderma* spp. (widely used biocontrol agents) and *Talaromyces* spp. These fungi secrete chitinases, glucanases, and proteases that breach the host cell wall and facilitate internal colonization.

### 3. Haustorial

The mycoparasite forms a short, specialized branch—a **haustorium**—that penetrates the host cell wall and establishes contact with the host plasmalemma. Nutrients are withdrawn through the haustorial interface while the host cell remains alive, at least initially.

**Examples:** Members of the Zygomycota, Tremellales, and genera such as *Sporidesmium*. The haustorium is analogous to structures formed by biotrophic plant pathogens.

### 4. Fusion (Plasmatic Connection)

Micropores develop in the cell walls of both partners at points of contact, enabling direct cytoplasmic exchange. This is the most intimate non-intracellular interface and may allow bidirectional flow of organelles and genetic material.

**Examples:** *Gonatobotrys*, *Hansfordia*, and *Tetragoniomyces*. In the *Absidia glauca* / *Parasitella parasitica* system, plasmatic connections have been shown to enable **gene transfer**, producing pararecombinants with genetic contributions from both partners—a remarkable example of horizontal gene transfer among fungi.

### 5. Intracellular

The entire thallus of the mycoparasite resides within the host cell. This strategy is characteristic of certain endoparasitic groups.

**Examples:** Chytrids and Oomycetes that parasitize other fungi. The parasite develops entirely within the host cytoplasm, releasing zoospores upon host lysis.

## Host Defense Responses

Fungi subject to mycoparasitic attack are not passive. Documented defense mechanisms include:

- **Callosities and wall thickenings**: Localized deposition of additional wall material at penetration sites, analogous to papillae formed by plants against fungal attack.
- **Toxin production**: Some hosts produce antibiotics or secondary metabolites that inhibit parasite growth (e.g., certain *Hypomyces* species produce hypodoratoxide, a toxic compound).

Conversely, mycoparasites have evolved specialized organelles for nutrient acquisition, including the **colacosome** (a dense, osmiophilic body) and the **lenticular body**, both implicated in nutrient exchange at the host-parasite interface.

## Methods for Determining Mode of Interaction

Resolving the trophic strategy of a fungicolous fungus requires multiple complementary approaches:

| Method | Application |
|--------|-------------|
| **Light microscopy** (water mounts, lactic acid, cotton blue) | General morphology and structural interface type |
| **Phloxine staining** | Distinguishes live (unstained) from dead (stained) host cells |
| **Fluorescence microscopy** | Visualizes viability and structural details with greater contrast |
| **Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)** | Reveals ultrastructural details of penetration, haustoria, and wall modifications |
| **Radiolabeled compound tracing** | Tracks nutrient flow; e.g., ³²P transfer from *Rhizoctonia* to *Arthrobotrys* confirmed direct nutrient extraction |
| **Cellophane disk method** | Tests production of diffusible antibiotics or enzymes |
| **Paired plate method** | Detects volatile metabolites acting at a distance |

Antibiotic and volatile metabolite production is a major mechanism in genera such as *Trichoderma*, which employs multiple modes of action simultaneously—enzymatic lysis, antibiosis, and competition for space and nutrients.

## Ecological and Applied Significance

Fungicolous fungi and mycoparasites play critical roles in natural ecosystems by regulating fungal populations, contributing to nutrient cycling, and maintaining community structure. Their biocontrol potential has been extensively explored, particularly species of *Trichoderma*, *Gliocladium*, and *Coniothyrium*, which are deployed commercially against soil-borne and foliar plant pathogens.

The discovery of horizontal gene transfer via plasmatic connections (*Absidia*–*Parasitella*) adds an evolutionary dimension, suggesting that fungicolous associations may serve as conduits for genetic exchange in natural populations.
