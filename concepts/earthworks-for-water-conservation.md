# Earthworks for Water Conservation

## Overview

Earthworks are the physical infrastructure of water management in permaculture design. They encompass dams, tanks, swales, diversion channels, and irrigation layouts that capture, store, and distribute water across the landscape. While engineers have long focused on the technical aspects of dam construction, permaculture emphasises the placement, linking, and biological integration of earthworks within the total designed landscape. This concept covers the principles, types, and strategic use of earthworks for [[water-conservation]].

## Foundational References

Two essential texts form the foundation of earthworks for water conservation:

### Nelson's Small Dam Construction Manual (1985)

Kenneth D. Nelson's comprehensive text covers catchment treatments, run-off calculations, soils, construction methods, outlet systems, volume and cost estimates, and detailed drawings for most adjunct structures. However, like most engineers, Nelson concentrates mainly on valley dams (barrier or embankment dams) and less on placement within the total designed landscape, or the biological uses of dams.

### Yeomans' *Water for Every Farm / The Keyline Plan* (1981)

P.A. Yeomans' pioneering work, first written in 1954, is without doubt the foundational modern text on landscape design for water conservation and gravity-fed flow irrigation. It also addresses patterning, tree planting, soil treatment, and fencing — making it the first book on functional landscape design in modern times.

## Two Basic Strategies of Water Conservation

In run-off areas, water conservation follows two complementary strategies:

1. **Diversion of surface water to impoundments** (dams, tanks) for later use
2. **Storage of water in soils** through infiltration

Both strategies result in recharge of groundwater. As with all technologies, earthworks have specifically appropriate and inappropriate uses depending on climate, soil type, and landscape position.

## Main Productive Earthwork Features

- **Dams and tanks** — storages for water
- **Swales** — absorption beds that allow water to infiltrate into soil
- **Diversion systems or channels** — directing water from catchment to storage
- **Irrigation layouts** — particularly those designed for flood or sheet irrigation

## Small Dams and Earth Tanks

### Primary Uses

Small dams and earth tanks serve two primary purposes:

1. **Watering points for rangelands, wildlife, and domestic stock** — these can be modest systems, widely dispersed and static
2. **Storage of surplus run-off water** for use over dry periods for domestic use or irrigation — these require careful design with respect to safety, water harvesting, landscape layout, outlet systems, draw-down, and placement relative to the usage area (preferably providing gravity flow)

### Aquaculture and Polyculture

A separate category of water storages includes ponds or wet terraces created specifically for water crop production — vegetation or mixed polycultural systems of aquatic animal species. Dams and ponds are potential aquaculture sites, and the production of diverse plant and fish or waterfowl protein should be considered during construction.

In humid areas, water storages can occupy up to **20% of the landscape** with great benefit, providing fish and a great variety of aquatic product while moderating the effects of drought and flood.

## Open Water Storages: Humid vs. Arid Climates

### Humid Climates

Open-water (free water surface area) storages are most appropriate where the potential for evaporation is exceeded by average annual rainfall. In these areas, ponds, wetlands, and swamps can proliferate as long-term water and wildlife reserves.

### Arid to Sub-Humid Climates

Open water storages in drier areas carry serious risks:

1. **Salinisation of stored water**: evaporation concentrates dissolved salts, producing water that can affect animal health
2. **Soil salting downhill**: inevitable seepage from earth dams creates areas of salted or collapsed soils downstream
3. **Deprivation of flush-water**: large barrier dams may allow so little water to bypass that downstream agricultural soils, productive lakes, and estuaries lose more productive capacity through deprivation of flush-water and silt deposits than can be made up by irrigation

### The Yeomans Principle

P.A. Yeomans (personal communication, 1978) stated that if 10–15% of a normal, humid, lowland or foothill landscape were fitted with small earth storages, **floods and drought or fire threat could be eliminated**.

## Tank Storages

Water can be captured off roof areas, roads, and other paved areas for drinking water, shower, or garden use, providing it can be stored:

- **Roof water** is least polluted, or most easily treated for drinking and cooking
- **Absorption beds** are sufficient for the muddy or polluted run-off from roads and parking areas

## Safety of Small Dams

Earth dams or weirs with retaining walls of 6 m (19 feet) high or less, and which have a large or oversized stable spillway, are **no threat to life or property if well-made**. They:

- Need not displace populations
- Need not stop flow in streams
- Need not create health problems
- Need not fill with silt
- Need not block fish migrations

Dams or storages made anywhere but as barriers on streams effectively **add to stream flow in the long term**.

## Low Barrier Dams

Low barrier dams of 1–4 m (3–13 feet) high provide multiple benefits:

- Assist stream oxygenation
- Provide permanent pools for wildlife
- Can be stepped to allow fish ladders or bypasses
- Provide local sites for modest power generation

While almost all modern assessments would condemn or ban large-scale dams on the record of past and continuing failures, a sober assessment of small water storages shows **multiple benefits** with minimal risk.

## Limitations on Landscape Water Storage

Not all landscapes can cost-effectively store a large proportion of free surface water. Limitations include:

- **Free-draining soils or deep/coarse sands** — water percolates away too quickly
- **Rocky terrain** — insufficient excavatable material for dam walls
- **Fissured limestone** — water disappears into underground channels
- **Steep or unstable slopes** — construction risks and structural concerns

However, very few landscapes will not store more soil water if humus, soil treatment, or swales are applied. The soil itself is the **largest water storage system** in the landscape if allowed to absorb.

## Dam Construction Criteria

Almost every type of dam is cost-effective if located in an area of **5% slope or less**. However, many essential dams can be built at higher slopes or grades if made of concrete, rock-walled, or excavated rather than embankment-built.

Each and every dam needs careful soil and level surveys and planning for local construction methods.

## Key Principles

- Earthworks must be placed within the total designed landscape, not just as isolated structures
- Two complementary strategies exist: diversion to storages and infiltration into soil
- Open water storages are beneficial in humid climates but dangerous in arid ones due to salinisation
- 10–15% small earth storages in a humid landscape can eliminate flood, drought, and fire threat
- Small dams (≤6 m wall height) with oversized spillways are safe and beneficial
- Every dam requires careful soil and level surveys before construction
- Biological productivity (aquaculture, wildlife habitat) should be designed into all water storages
- Gravity flow should be maximised in dam placement relative to point of use
