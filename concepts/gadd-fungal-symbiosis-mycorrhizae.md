# Fungal Symbiosis -- Mycorrhizal Associations

## Overview

Mycorrhizae are mutualistic symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots. The term, derived from the Greek words for "fungus" and "root," describes one of the most widespread and ecologically important symbioses on Earth. An estimated 80-90% of all land plant species form mycorrhizal associations, which fundamentally alter plant nutrient uptake, stress tolerance, and competitive interactions. In exchange for photosynthetically derived carbohydrates from the host plant, mycorrhizal fungi provide mineral nutrients (especially phosphorus and nitrogen), enhance water uptake, and confer protection against pathogens and environmental stresses. Mycorrhizal fungi are particularly important in forest ecosystems, where they constitute a major pathway of carbon flow from trees to soil.

## Types of Mycorrhizae

### Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)

Ectomycorrhizae are formed primarily by basidiomycetes (with some ascomycetes) on the roots of woody plants, especially members of the Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae, Salicaceae, and Myrtaceae. Key features include:

- **Hartig net**: A labyrinthine network of fungal hyphae that penetrates between root cortical cells without entering the cells themselves. This is the primary site of nutrient exchange.
- **Fungal mantle**: A dense sheath of fungal tissue that completely encloses the root tip, often visible as a thickening or color change. The mantle can constitute a substantial proportion of root biomass.
- **Extraradical mycelium**: An extensive network of hyphae extending from the mantle into the surrounding soil, greatly increasing the effective absorptive surface area of the root system. Individual ectomycorrhizal networks can extend for meters from the root tip.
- **Rhizomorphs and cords**: Specialized hyphal aggregates that facilitate long-distance transport of water and nutrients.

Ectomycorrhizal forests were once thought to occur primarily in temperate to boreal zones, but they are now known to abound in many tropical forests as well. The fungi involved are diverse taxonomically, spanning many genera of basidiomycetes and ascomycetes, with many producing conspicuous above-ground sporocarps (mushrooms, boletes, truffles) that have been central to mycological inventory work.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM)

Arbuscular mycorrhizae, formed by fungi in the Glomeromycota (formerly placed in the Zygomycetes), are the most widespread type of mycorrhizal association, occurring in approximately 80% of all plant species including most agricultural crops, grasses, and tropical trees. Key features include:

- **Arbuscules**: Highly branched, tree-like fungal structures that form inside root cortical cells. Arbuscules are the primary site of nutrient exchange and have an enormous surface-area-to-volume ratio.
- **Vesicles**: Lipid-rich storage organs formed by many (but not all) AM fungal species within root cells. Vesicles serve as carbon storage reserves.
- **Intraradical hyphae**: Fungal hyphae that grow between and within root cortical cells.
- **Extraradical hyphae**: Extending from roots into soil, these hyphae explore soil volumes inaccessible to roots and can transport nutrients over considerable distances.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi produce individual spores (150-1000 micrometers diameter) in soil or roots. Spores are large relative to those of other fungi but are produced individually rather than in aggregated fruiting bodies. AM fungal species diversity has been historically underestimated because identification requires microscopic examination of root colonization patterns and spore morphology. Molecular methods have revealed much greater AM fungal diversity than culture-dependent methods.

### Other Mycorrhizal Types

- **Ectendomycorrhizae**: An intermediate form showing characteristics of both ecto- and endomycorrhizae, found primarily on members of the Pinaceae and Ericaceae.
- **Ericoid mycorrhizae**: Formed by ascomycetes on the roots of Ericaceae (heaths, heathers, blueberries). These associations are critical for plant nutrition in acidic, nutrient-poor soils.
- **Arbutoid mycorrhizae**: Similar to ectomycorrhizae in structure but with intracellular penetration, formed by basidiomycetes on Arbutus and Arctostaphylos.
- **Orchid mycorrhizae**: Formed by basidiomycetes on orchid roots. Orchid seeds require mycorrhizal infection for germination, and many orchids remain dependent on their fungal partners throughout their lives.

## Carbon Economy of Mycorrhizae

The fungus absorbs water and mineral nutrients from the soil and transfers them to the host plant; in return, the fungus receives photosynthates (primarily carbohydrates) from the host. This bidirectional carbon-nutrient exchange is the fundamental basis of the mutualism. The carbon cost to the host plant can be substantial -- estimates suggest that plants may allocate 10-30% or more of their total photosynthate to mycorrhizal fungi. In forests, mycorrhizal fungi represent a major carbon sink, and the flux of carbon from trees to mycorrhizal mycelium and subsequently to the soil food web is a significant component of ecosystem carbon budgets.

## Ecological Significance

Mycorrhizal associations have profound ecological consequences:

- **Nutrient acquisition**: Mycorrhizal hyphae access soil volumes and nutrient pools unavailable to roots alone, particularly immobile nutrients like phosphorus. The fine diameter and extensive branching of extraradical hyphae give them a much larger absorptive surface area per unit biomass than roots.
- **Plant community dynamics**: Mycorrhizal associations influence plant competitive interactions and community composition. Different plant species may form associations with different fungal partners, creating complex networks of interaction.
- **Stress tolerance**: Mycorrhizal plants typically show improved tolerance to drought, heavy metals, salinity, and pathogen attack compared to non-mycorrhizal plants.
- **Soil aggregation**: Extraradical hyphae and associated fungal secretions (glomalin-related soil proteins produced by AM fungi) contribute to soil aggregate stability and soil structure.
- **Common mycorrhizal networks**: Individual mycorrhizal fungal networks can simultaneously connect multiple host plants of the same or different species, creating pathways for resource sharing and signaling between plants.
- **Succession**: Mycorrhizal fungal communities change predictably during forest stand development, with different fungal assemblages characteristic of young, mature, and old-growth forests.

## Sequestrate Mycorrhizal Fungi

Many mycorrhizal fungi have evolved sequestrate (truffle-like) fruiting forms in which spores are not forcibly discharged but are retained within an enclosed fruiting body. Sequestrate fungi have arisen independently in multiple evolutionary lineages and rely on animals for spore dispersal. Aromatic compounds attract mammals, which eat the fruiting bodies and disperse spores through their feces. These fungi represent evolutionary convergence toward protection of sporocarps from climatic stress (heat, drought, freezing) and adaptation to animal-mediated spore dispersal.

In old-growth Pinaceae forests of the Pacific Northwestern United States, sequestrate ectomycorrhizal fungi form a critical link in the food web: the trees require the fungi for mineral nutrition, the fungi depend on the trees for energy, sequestrate fruiting bodies are a primary food of the northern flying squirrel, and the squirrel is in turn the primary food of the northern spotted owl.

## Research Methods

Mycorrhizal fungi are studied through a combination of field surveys (sporocarp collection and identification), root sampling and microscopic examination (clearing and staining of root tips to visualize fungal structures), and molecular methods (DNA extraction from roots or soil, PCR amplification of fungal ribosomal genes, and community profiling). Molecular approaches have revealed that the diversity of mycorrhizal fungi, especially the "hidden" species that do not produce conspicuous sporocarps, is much greater than previously recognized. Competitive RT-PCR and microarray-based transcript analyses are being applied to study gene expression in mycorrhizal associations in situ.
