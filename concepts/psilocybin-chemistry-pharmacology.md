# Psilocybin

Psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, or 4-PO-DMT) is the
primary psychoactive prodrug found in hallucinogenic mushrooms of the genus
[[psilocybe-cubensis]] and related species. It belongs to the
indolealkylamine class of hallucinogens, the same chemical family as LSD.
In the body, psilocybin is dephosphorylated to psilocin
(4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, or 4-HO-DMT), which is the
pharmacologically active compound responsible for the drug's psychedelic
effects.

## Chemical Classification

Psilocybin and psilocin are structurally similar to serotonin
(5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT), a key neurotransmitter involved in mood
regulation, perception, and cognition. This structural similarity underlies
their mechanism of action at serotonin receptors. The hallucinogen class
encompasses three major chemical groups:

- **Indolealkylamines (tryptamines)**: LSD, psilocin, psilocybin,
  DMT, and bufotenin
- **Phenylethylamines**: mescaline, found in peyote cactus
- **Cannabinoids**: compounds found in Cannabis sativa

Psilocybin is the phosphate ester of psilocin, making it more stable in
air and water-soluble. Two additional tryptamines, baeocystin and
norbaeocystin, may also be present in hallucinogenic mushrooms but are
thought to be less active than psilocybin and psilocin.

## Mechanism of Action

Psilocin acts primarily on the serotonin system as a 5-HT2A
post-synaptic agonist or partial agonist. The 5-HT2A receptor is widely
expressed in the cerebral cortex and is considered the principal site
responsible for the hallucinogenic effects of psychedelics. Activation of
these receptors leads to altered sensory processing, changes in
perception, mood elevation, and cognitive effects characteristic of the
psychedelic experience.

The term "hallucinogen" is somewhat misleading because these drugs do not
generally cause true hallucinations (sensory perceptions in the absence
of external stimuli) at typical recreational doses. The effects are more
accurately described as perceptual distortions that extend beyond
perception to include changes in thought patterns, mood, and self-awareness
(personality integration).

## Dosage and Potency

The psychoactive dose of psilocybin is estimated to be between 45 ug/kg
body weight and 315 ug/kg body weight. However, determining exact dosage
from mushroom weight is challenging because potency varies significantly
depending on:

- **Species and variety**: Psilocybe semilanceata may contain up to 1%
  psilocybin by dry weight, while Psilocybe cubensis typically contains
  up to 0.6%
- **Origin and growing conditions**: Environmental factors including
  substrate, temperature, and humidity affect alkaloid production
- **Age of the mushroom**: Potency can change as mushrooms mature or
  deteriorate

The recommended recreational dose is typically 1 to 3.5 grams of dried
mushrooms, or approximately 10 to 50 grams of fresh mushrooms. Short-term
tolerance develops rapidly to both physical and psychological effects,
meaning that subsequent doses taken within a short period require
increasing amounts to achieve the same effect.

## Pharmacokinetics

After ingestion, psilocybin is rapidly dephosphorylated by alkaline
phosphatase enzymes in the body to form psilocin. Onset of effects
typically occurs within 10 to 60 minutes, depending on the mode of
ingestion, the user's physical condition, food intake, and prior
experience. The duration of effects usually lasts 2 to 6 hours, with
aftereffects such as difficulty sleeping persisting an additional 2 to 6
hours.

## Acute Effects

### Psychological Effects

Subjective effects described by users span a wide spectrum:

- Mild relaxation, giddiness, uncontrollable laughter, energy, joy, and
  euphoria at lower doses
- Visual enhancement (brighter colours), visual disturbances (moving
  surfaces, waves), altered perception of events, and imagery at moderate
  doses
- Delusions, depersonalisation, impaired judgement of time and distance,
  and feelings of unreality at higher doses

Positive experiences ("good trips") are associated with relaxation, mental
stimulation, new insights, and rapidly changing emotions. Negative
experiences ("bad trips") involve anxiety, panic reactions, confusion,
and psychosis-like states.

### Physiological Effects

Physiological effects are generally mild and may include dizziness,
nausea, weakness, muscle aching, shivering, abdominal pain, pupil
dilation (mydriasis), mild-to-moderate increases in heart rate and
breathing rate (tachycardia, tachypnea), and elevated blood pressure.
Body temperature typically remains normal. However, pronounced symptoms
such as severe stomach pain, persistent vomiting, and diarrhoea have been
recorded. Over a quarter of recent users surveyed reported experiencing
nausea or vomiting.

## Toxicity and Safety

The acute toxicity of psilocybin is considered low. Fatal intoxications
directly attributed to psilocybin-containing mushrooms are extremely rare.
There is no evidence of psychological or physical dependence, and no
withdrawal symptoms occur with cessation of use. There is no reported
irreversible organ damage caused by psilocybin. Insufficient data exist
regarding mutagenicity and teratogenicity to draw firm conclusions about
long-term risks.

The tendency for temporarily increased blood pressure may pose a risk
for users with cardiovascular conditions, especially untreated
hypertension. Benzodiazepines are reported to be the safest and most
effective medication for managing acute adverse reactions.

## Terminology

The drugs have been known by various names reflecting different
perspectives: "psychedelic" (mind-expanding, preferred by users),
"psychotomimetic" (resembling psychosis), "psychodysleptic"
(mind-disrupting), and "hallucinogenic" (the term adopted by the
scientific community despite its inaccuracy). In practice, the terms
"hallucinogen" and "psychedelic" are used interchangeably.
