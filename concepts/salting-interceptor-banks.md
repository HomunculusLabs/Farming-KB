# Salting and Interceptor Banks

Dryland salinity is one of the most devastating forms of land degradation in agricultural regions, particularly in Western Australia. It results from the clearing of deep-rooted native vegetation and its replacement with shallow-rooted annual crops and pastures. This causes groundwater tables to rise, mobilizing ancient salt stores in the subsoil and bringing them to the surface. The result is soil collapse, formation of saltpans, loss of productive land, and contamination of waterways.

## The Salinity Problem

Deforestation, overgrazing, and wheat cultivation have produced widespread secondary salting across millions of hectares of former forest and marshland. The damage manifests as:

- **Collapsed soil** at seepage lines, where rising saltwater destroys soil structure
- **Saltpan formation** in once-forested marshes converted to wheatlands
- **Indicator species** appearing, such as sea barley grass (Hordeum maritimum), which signals soil collapse
- **Anaerobic subsoils** that are dry, cemented, or subject to rising groundwater

Near Quairading, Western Australia, what was once a forested marsh has been reduced to a bare saltpan—a stark visual record of the consequences of vegetation clearance in susceptible landscapes.

## Interceptor Banks: Design and Construction

Interceptor banks are earthworks designed to intercept both surface runoff and subsurface throughflow, directing salty water away from productive soil blocks and into natural drainage lines or designated discharge areas. They are a primary tool in halting the downhill "cascade" effect of saline overland flow and throughflow.

### Specifications

- **Depth**: 1.5 meters
- **Width**: 4 meters
- **Spacing**: No more than 100 meters apart, or 1 meter of vertical separation on slopes
- **Construction**: A bulldozer rams subsoil on the downhill wall of the bank
- **Termination**: Banks end on the streamline, typically marked by existing tree lines

### Function

The interceptor bank serves two critical functions:

1. **Surface water diversion**: Captures overland flow before it can spread saline water across productive areas
2. **Subsurface throughflow interception**: Cuts through the water table to prevent salty groundwater from flowing downslope into cropped areas

Soil pits dug below properly constructed interceptor banks show no throughflow and no rising salted groundwater, confirming the bank's effectiveness.

## Evidence of Effectiveness

At Beermullah, Western Australia, interceptor banks have demonstrated clear results:

- **Flood prevention**: Banks have prevented soil collapse by flooding, with rushes growing on the upslope side indicating captured water
- **Crop protection**: Areas below interceptor banks are protected from saline water damage
- **Systematic application**: A series of interceptor banks across wheatfields isolates individual soil blocks from both overland flow and salting effects

## Indicator Species for Soil Collapse

Several plant species serve as early warning indicators of impending soil salinization:

- **Sea barley grass** (Hordeum maritimum): Indicates soil collapse from salting
- **Spike rush** (Juncus): Signals imminent soil collapse, surface flooding, and anaerobic conditions
- **Rushes** on upslope sides of banks: Indicate water accumulation and successful interception

## Integration with Broader Landscape Management

Interceptor banks work best as part of an integrated land management strategy:

1. **Reforestation**: Replanting deep-rooted perennials (trees, shrubs, perennial pastures) above the interceptor zone helps lower groundwater tables over time
2. **Drainage planning**: Banks must connect to safe disposal points—natural valleys, evaporation basins, or saline-tolerant vegetation zones
3. **Isolated soil blocks**: Between banks, soil blocks can be rain-leached to flush accumulated salts
4. **Monitoring**: Regular observation of indicator species, soil pits, and vegetation health provides feedback on system performance

## Limitations

Interceptor banks are a reactive measure—they manage the symptoms of rising water tables but do not address the root cause (excessive groundwater recharge from cleared land). The long-term solution requires restoring deep-rooted perennial vegetation across the catchment to re-establish the original hydrological balance. Banks buy time and protect existing productive land while broader landscape restoration proceeds.

## Design Considerations

- Banks should follow contour lines as closely as possible
- They must be properly compacted to prevent preferential flow through the bank itself
- Maintenance is required after major rainfall events to repair any erosion damage
- The discharge end must be protected from scouring
- Integration with fencing and revegetation maximizes the protected area and accelerates recovery
