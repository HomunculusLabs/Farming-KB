# Natural Hallucinogen Sources Beyond Mushrooms

## Overview

The EMCDDA's 2006 thematic paper contextualises [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-emerging-trend-europe]]
within the broader landscape of naturally occurring hallucinogenic
substances. These compounds derive from a wide variety of plant, fungal,
and even animal sources, and have been used for thousands of years
across diverse cultures for religious, spiritual, and healing purposes.

## Plant-Derived Hallucinogens

### Cannabis (Cannabis sativa)

Perhaps the most widely used psychoactive plant globally. Contains
tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and other cannabinoids that act on CB1/CB2
receptors. While sometimes grouped with hallucinogens due to perceptual
effects at higher doses, cannabis is pharmacologically distinct from
classical hallucinogens, producing effects through the endocannabinoid
system rather than [[powell-psilocybin-serotonin-pathways-environmental-chemistry]].

### Peyote Cactus (Lophophora williamsii)

A small, spineless cactus native to the Rio Grande Valley of
southern Texas and northern Mexico. Contains mescaline
(3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine), a phenylethylamine hallucinogen.
Peyote has been used in ceremonial contexts by indigenous peoples for
over 5,000 years, most notably by the [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] Church, which
gained legal protection for peyote use in [[entheogenic-folk-religious-ceremonies]] in the
United States.

### Ayahuasca

A psychoactive brew prepared from the [[ayahuasca-and-banisteriopsis-caapi]] vine
(contains [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]] acting as MAO inhibitors) combined
with [[chacruna-psychotria-viridis]] or Diplopterys cabrerana leaves (containing
DMT). The MAO inhibition allows orally active DMT, which would
normally be broken down by monoamine oxidase enzymes in the gut.
Used traditionally by indigenous Amazonian peoples and now in
syncretic religious movements like Santo Daime and União do
Vegetal throughout [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

### Morning Glory (Ipomoea tricolor, Turbina corymbosa)

The seeds of certain morning glory species contain lysergic acid
amide (LSA), a compound structurally related to LSD. Used
traditionally by Aztec and Mazatec peoples of Mexico in ritual
contexts. LSA is notably less potent than LSD but produces similar
though milder effects. Morning glory seeds were noted in the EMCDDA
report as the active ingredient in "Purple Ohms" capsules marketed
as legal alternatives [[legal-responses-to-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-in-the-european-union]] in the UK.

### Iboga (Tabernanthe iboga)

A shrub native to West Central Africa, particularly Gabon. The root
bark contains ibogaine, a complex indole alkaloid with both
hallucinogenic and anti-addictive properties. Used traditionally in
the Bwiti spiritual practice of Gabon. Ibogaine has attracted
research interest for its potential in treating substance dependence,
particularly opioid addiction.

### Salvia divinorum

A sprawling perennial herb that grows wild only in the Sierra
Mazatec region of Mexico. Its leaves contain salvinorin-A, an
extremely potent kappa-opioid receptor agonist — notably the most
potent naturally occurring hallucinogen known. Salvinorin-A is
structurally unique, belonging to the neoclerodane diterpene class
rather than tryptamine or phenylethylamine families. Unlike
classical hallucinogens, salvia acts on kappa-opioid receptors rather
than [[psilocybin-pharmacology-and-serotonin-receptors]]. The EMCDDA notes that Salvia divinorum
was widely available since the mid-1990s, primarily as a smoked herb,
and was commonly sold under the "herbal highs" product label in
European smartshops.
