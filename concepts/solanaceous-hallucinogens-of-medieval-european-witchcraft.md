## Solanaceous Hallucinogens of Medieval European Witchcraft

The nightshade family (Solanaceae) contributed several of the most
important ingredients to the hallucinogenic brews and ointments of
medieval European witchcraft. Belladonna, henbane, mandrake, and datura
formed the core pharmacopoeia of the so-called witches' brews, all
sharing a common chemistry of [[weil-the-natural-mind-altered-states-normalcy]] of
consciousness.

## Belladonna (Atropa belladonna)

Belladonna is well known as a highly poisonous species capable of
inducing various kinds of hallucinations. It entered into the folklore
and mythology of virtually all European peoples, who feared its deadly
power. The attractive shiny berries still often cause accidental
poisoning. The name "belladonna" ("beautiful lady" in Italian) comes
from a curious custom practiced by women of high society during medieval
times, who would drop the sap of the plant into the eye to dilate the
pupil enormously, inducing a kind of drunken or glassy stare considered
to enhance beauty and sensuality.

The main active principle is hyoscyamine, but the more psychoactive
scopolamine is also present. Atropine has also been found. Belladonna
is a commercial source of atropine, widely used in modern medicine as
an antispasmodic, antisecretory, mydriatic, and cardiac stimulant. Four
species of Atropa are distributed in Europe and from central Asia to the
Himalayas.

## Henbane (Hyoscyamus niger)

Henbane was often included in the witches' brews and other toxic
preparations of medieval Europe to cause visual hallucinations and the
sensation of flight. An annual or biennial native to Europe, it has long
been valued in medicine as a sedative and anodyne to induce sleep. The
principal alkaloid is hyoscyamine, with the more hallucinogenic
scopolamine also present in significant amounts, along with several
other alkaloids in smaller concentrations. Henbane is one of 20 species
of Hyoscyamus, all members of the Solanaceae, native to Europe,
northern Africa, and western and central Asia.

## Mandrake (Mandragora officinarum)

Mandrake is a hallucinogen with a fantastic history, long known and
feared for its toxicity. Its complex history as a magic hypnotic in the
folklore of Europe cannot be equaled by any species anywhere. Mandrake
was a panacea whose folk uses were inextricably bound up with the
"Doctrine of Signatures," an old theory holding that the appearance of
an object indicates its special properties. The root was likened to the
form of a man or woman, hence its magic. According to superstition, if
a mandrake were pulled from the earth, its unearthly shrieks could
drive its collector mad. Many regions claimed strong aphrodisiac
properties. Mandrake with [[mckenna-witch-ointments-tropane-alkaloids-flying-salves]] was one of the most potent
ingredients in the witches' brews of Europe.

credited it with causing the intoxicating smoke associated with the

identified with this species. Avicenna mentioned it under the name
jouzmathel in the eleventh century. The plant was held sacred in China,
where people believed Buddha's preaching caused heaven to sprinkle the
plant with dew. In Asia, seeds of Datura are often mixed with food and
tobacco for illicit use. The principal alkaloid is scopolamine, found
in heaviest concentrations in the leaves and seeds.

## Shared Tropane Chemistry

All of these solanaceous hallucinogens share a common chemical basis in
the tropane alkaloids: hyoscyamine, scopolamine, and atropine. These
compounds are anticholinergic agents that block the neurotransmitter
acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors. The resulting syndrome includes
dry mouth, dilated pupils, blurred vision, tachycardia, amnesia, and
