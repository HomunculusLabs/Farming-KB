# Paddy Levee Construction and Water Management in Natural Farming

Water management, consisting of irrigation and drainage, is the most important part of crop management in rice cultivation. In Fukuoka's natural farming system,
where rice and barley are grown in succession without tillage, the management of water — and critically, the construction and maintenance of earthen levees that
hold that water — becomes the single most technically demanding and practically consequential aspect of farm work. Fukuoka treated levee construction not merely
as infrastructure maintenance but as a form of artistic and religious work that reveals the sophistication of traditional agricultural technology.

## The Centrality of Water Management

The success of rice and barley succession cropping depends first on germination and weed control, with the first ten to twenty days being especially critical.
During the first half of the rice growing season, the crop does well under conditions similar to upland rice cultivation — the field is surface-drained to grow
hardy plants. But in the second half, irrigation should increase with plant growth. After heading, the rice requires abundant water, and without careful
attention could become dehydrated.

Fukuoka's irrigation protocol follows a specific pattern: in early June, when the rich growth of clover threatens to choke out young rice seedlings, the levees
are plastered with mud and water held in the field for four to seven days to weaken the clover. After this, the field is surface-drained to grow hardy plants.
During the first half of the rice growing season, irrigation is not strictly necessary, but water may be passed briefly over the field once every week to ten
days. Irrigation continues intermittently during the heading stage, but water is never held for more than five days at a stretch. A soil moisture level of
approximately eighty percent is adequate for yields of about one ton per quarter-acre.

## The Problem of Communal Irrigation

Farmers practicing Fukuoka's direct-seeding methods face a distinctive challenge: they seed and irrigate at times different from neighboring farmers who
transplant their rice. Because irrigation canals are communally controlled, one cannot simply draw large amounts of water from a long canal whenever one
pleases. If you irrigate when neighboring fields are dry, water leakage into other fields can greatly inconvenience adjacent farmers.

When leakage problems arise, the immediate remedy is to plaster the levees with mud. With intermittent irrigation — the pattern Fukuoka advocates — fissures
tend to develop in the levee, causing persistent leakage. This makes levee maintenance a constant and critical concern for the natural farmer, requiring
vigilance and regular attention throughout the growing season.

## The Mole Problem

Fukuoka devoted considerable attention to the problem of moles, which he regarded as the most serious threat to levee integrity. Most people might dismiss a
mole tunnel as nothing much to worry about, but a mole running along the length of a freshly plastered levee can in one night dig a tunnel 40 to 50 feet long,
ruining a good levee.

The cascading damage works as follows: by burrowing straight through a levee, a mole weakens it so that water starts leaking out of mole cricket and earthworm
holes. Before the farmer notices, these small openings develop into sizable holes. A hole may appear small from the outside but inside widens into larger
pockets that cannot be stopped up with a handful or two of mud. If dirt has flowed out of a hole for an entire night, the farmer may need to carry in 50 to 100
pounds of earth to repair it.

**Preventing mole damage** requires several measures: the grass along the top and sides of the levee must always be neatly cropped — cut at least three times a
year — otherwise there is no way of knowing where the entrance or exit of a mole tunnel is. Grass cuttings and bundles of straw must not be left on a levee
because these draw earthworms, which in turn attract moles.

**Repair technique:** Use stiff earth to plug holes; soft earth may work free overnight. Avoid makeshift repairs, as these only lead to eventual crumbling of
the levee, which spells real trouble.  Moles can be caught by placing a simple bamboo tube capped at both ends with valves at a hard point in the mole tunnel.
Fukuoka noted there is a trick to catching moles, but once the farmer has gotten the knack and can keep the entire field filled with water by plugging all
holes, he has become a "full-fledged rice farmer."

## The Failure of Modern Levee Materials

Fukuoka criticized the modern practice of constructing levees from concrete or covering footpaths with vinyl sheeting. While these appear to be easy ways of
holding water, the earth at the base of the concrete or below the sheeting provides ideal habitat for moles. Within two or three years, repairs on these modern
levees become more difficult than on normal earthen levees. In the long run, such methods do not make things easier for the farmer.

This critique exemplifies Fukuoka's broader argument: modern technological solutions that appear to reduce labor actually create deeper, more intractable
problems by disrupting the natural relationships within the farming ecosystem. Concrete levees represent a static, permanent solution that degrades over time,
while earthen levees require annual renewal that keeps the farmer in direct contact with the soil and the living systems around it.

## Building an Earthen Levee

Fukuoka described the traditional method of building an earthen levee in precise detail, arguing that it represents a sophisticated technology superior to
modern alternatives:

1. **Cut the grass** on the old levee carefully with a sickle 2. **Break down the levee** with an open-ended hoe 3. **Dig up the soil** at the bottom of the
levee 4. **Draw some water alongside** and break up and knead the earth with a three-pronged cultivator 5. **Build up the levee** and let it stand for a while
6. **Plaster the top and sides** with earth

All the traditional farming tools used from ancient times in Japan come into play during this process. Fukuoka observed that these simple yet refined implements
efficiently modify the arrangement of soil particles in the paddy field, representing — even in soil engineering terms — a very sophisticated technology. Such a
technology is clearly superior to poured concrete and vinyl sheeting.

## Drainage Channels

The first step in preparing a normal paddy field for direct-seeded no-tillage cropping of rice and barley is digging drainage channels. Two to three weeks
before rice harvest, a water outlet is cut through the levee and the field surface drained. A row of rice about the perimeter is dug up with a cultivator,
transferred inward, and a drainage channel dug.

For good drainage, the channel must be dug deeply and carefully: a furrow is made with the end of a long-handled sickle, rice plants along the furrow are dug
up, and a channel about 8 inches deep and 8 inches wide is shaped by lifting soil aside with a hoe. After rice harvest, similar drainage channels are dug at
intervals of 12 to 15 feet across the field. These provide sufficient drainage for green manure crops and barley even in a moist field. Once dug, these drainage
channels can be used for many years in both rice and barley cultivation.

## Levee Building as Art and Spiritual Practice

Fukuoka elevated the seemingly mundane task of levee construction to the level of art and spiritual practice. He wrote: "Erecting a well-built levee in a paddy
field is akin to making a work of art." He contrasted this view with the perception of modern man, who "sees the mud-coated farmer plastering his levees and
transplanting his rice as a throwback to a crude, pre-scientific age."

The mission of natural farming, as Fukuoka defined it, is to peel away this narrow vision and show such labor in its true light as artistic and religious work.
This framing connects the physical labor of farming to Fukuoka's broader philosophical framework, in which farming is not merely an economic activity but a way
of life rooted in communion with nature.

The annual rebuilding of levees represents a cyclical practice of renewal that mirrors the cycles of nature itself. Unlike concrete and vinyl sheeting — static,
permanent solutions that degrade over time — earthen levees require annual attention and renewal, keeping the farmer in direct, intimate contact with the soil
and water that sustain the crops. The traditional earthen levee thus becomes a symbol of Fukuoka's larger argument: that the accumulated wisdom of generations of farmers, embodied in simple tools and cyclical practices, represents a form of knowledge far deeper and more reliable than the laboratory-derived prescriptions of modern agricultural science.

All one needs to do, then, is to rebuild the levees each year. After having experienced the tribulations of water management,
the farmer will be better prepared to fully appreciate the hardships and rewards of natural farming. The work demands precision,
patience, and an intimate understanding of the land — the same qualities that Fukuoka saw as the essence of farming itself.
