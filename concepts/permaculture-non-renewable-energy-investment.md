## The Transition Imperative

Permaculture design should aim to make best use of renewable natural resources to manage and maintain yields, even if some use of non-renewable resources is needed in establishing the system. The key distinction is between using non-renewable energy for one-time establishment infrastructure and using it for ongoing system maintenance. Systems that require continuous non-renewable inputs to function are fundamentally unsustainable, regardless of their initial productivity.

In restoring the balance between renewable and non-renewable resource use, it is often forgotten that these ideas were the norm not so long ago. The joke about the environmentally aware person using a solar clothes dryer (a washing line) is funny because it works on the very recent nature of much of this takeover of functions by technology and fossil fuels.

## Renewable Services Versus Consumptive Use

A critical insight from Holmgren's framework is the distinction between renewable resources and renewable services. Renewable services, also called passive functions, are those gained from plants, animals, living soil, and water without them being consumed. When we use a tree for wood, we are using a renewable resource. When we use a tree for shade and shelter, we gain benefits from the living tree that are non-consuming and require no harvesting energy.

This simple understanding is obvious yet powerful in redesigning systems where many simple functions have become dependent on non-renewable and unsustainable resource use. Permaculture design should make best use of non-consuming natural services to minimise consumptive demands on resources and emphasise the harmonious possibilities of interaction between humans and nature.

The horse provides the most important example in history of human prosperity derived from non-consuming use of nature's services. Domestication of the horse for transport, soil cultivation, and general power replaced the need for vast consumptive resource inputs while providing an intimate empathetic context for the extension of human ethical concerns to include nature.

## The Energy Quality Hierarchy

To harvest, store, and use renewable energy sources requires varying inputs of high-quality, generally non-renewable, energy. EMERGY evaluations show that the inputs for good-quality tidal and hydropower sites are very low because landscape-forming processes have already created most of the infrastructure needed. In the case of biomass from forest waste or thinnings, the photosynthesis and natural processes of the forest have already done most of the work.

For wind power, the lower quality and erratic nature of the energy resource requires much greater harvesting infrastructure. For solar electric power, the resource is most abundant but so low in quality that vast infrastructure is required relative to the quantity of harvested energy. This energy quality hierarchy is crucial for understanding the true cost of renewable energy transitions.

## Modern Agriculture as Non-Renewable Consumption

Modern agriculture is the most pervasive and important example of how increases in productivity from renewable resources have been achieved through additional non-renewable energies, materials, and technology. Although these processes have increased total yields, they have transformed agriculture from humanity's prime means of harvesting renewable resources to one of our largest consumers of non-renewable resources.

Holmgren illustrates this with a telling observation: a glass of milk in Australia is perhaps twenty percent oil, while in Europe the figure is perhaps fifty percent, and in Israel, from what he observed of dairy farming there, eighty percent of a glass of milk must be oil. This proportional analysis reveals the hidden energy dependency behind modern food systems.

## Solar Cells: Renewable Salvation or Technological Diversion?

Despite the limitations of energy quality, solar photovoltaic cells have become the great symbol of renewable energy, out of all proportion to their importance in a future with declining energy. They are seen by many as the environmentally clean source of power that will sustain a high-tech but energy-efficient society.

Holmgren takes the heretical view that a high-tech society running on solar cells is the stuff of dreams. While solar cells are a convenient way to generate electricity at locations remote from the power grid and may contribute during a transitional period, EMERGY analysis suggests they may be a net energy loss. The differences between embodied energy methods and EMERGY evaluation are mostly due to differences in the evaluation of human labour and services.

## Resource Replacement Time and Product Half-Life

To decide whether use of a renewable resource is reasonable, Holmgren proposes asking: "Will the function or product which the resource is being used for last at least as long as it took nature to generate the resource?" This is the resource replacement time test.

It is appropriate to make relatively ephemeral daily use of the sun, tides, water, and wind because they are daily or seasonally renewable energies. The use of timber resources from trees is more problematic because of the time trees take to grow. Paper products have a half-life of maybe a few years, but the trees used to make paper may have taken decades or hundreds of years to grow. If the same tree is used to make a fine dining table with a half-life of hundreds of years, this is a much more appropriate use.

## Multiple Products from Every Resource

By-products with varying degrees of value are the inevitable outcome of any sensible use of a natural resource. We should be suspicious of any use that achieves one hundred percent conversion to a single product because this probably represents a devaluing of many potential uses. The same mentality on a much larger scale drives whole-of-tree harvesting for wood fibre or fuel, ignoring the potential of some trees for higher-value uses such as fence posts or furniture timber.

## Total Process Evaluation

It is not possible to fully evaluate the appropriate use of natural resources in isolation from the wider industrial context. If the furniture manufacturing process demands cutting and milling large numbers of trees to generate enough high-quality timber, then are those tables any more appropriate than low-value disposable uses? Alternatively, a craftsperson using wood left behind in the forest after logging to make a unique piece of furniture may represent the best example of appropriate use.
