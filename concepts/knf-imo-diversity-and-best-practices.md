# KNF IMO Diversity and Best Practices

Indigenous Microorganisms are the foundation of Korean Natural
Farming's approach to soil health. Dr. Cho established specific
principles for collecting, maintaining, and applying IMOs that
maximize microbial diversity and ensure sustained soil fertility.
This guide covers the best practices and diversity principles from
Cho's Global Natural Farming.

## Why Microbial Diversity Matters

Microorganisms perform two major functions in farming:

1. Decomposition: Microorganisms decompose complex organic compounds
   such as dead bodies of plants and animals and wastes into
   nutrients, making them easily absorbable by plants. Without this
   decomposition, organic matter would accumulate and nutrients would
   remain locked in unavailable forms.

2. Protection: They create compounds such as antibiotic substances,
   enzymes, and lactic acids that suppress various diseases and
   promote healthy soil conditions. A diverse microbial community
   provides broader protection against a wider range of pathogens.

IMOs are used primarily to create fertile and healthy soil conditions
that are ideal for farming and to prevent plant diseases. In Natural
Farming, we do not feed the plant directly; we nurture the soil and
the soil nurtures the plant through the IMOs.

## Principle of Diverse Collection

Organisms found under the heat of the sun are largely different from
those found in shaded areas such as under bamboo trees. Dr. Cho
advocates culturing microorganisms from different areas in order to
collect different kinds of microorganisms, a practice called microbial
diversity.

It is also good to culture microorganisms at different weather
conditions and to mix different types of microorganisms. This
diversity ensures that the resulting IMO preparation contains
organisms capable of thriving under the varied conditions encountered
in the field throughout the year.

## Seven Best Practices for IMO Management

### 1. Use IMOs Continuously
Since IMOs are used to make soil fertile and healthy, these should
be collected and prepared every year. IMOs are not a one-time
application but require continuous use and maintenance. The
microbial community must be sustained in the soil through regular
applications to maintain soil fertility and plant health.

### 2. Maintain IMO Diversity
Avoid being selective when collecting microorganisms. Instead,
microorganisms from different environments should be collected and
mixed together. It is recommended to collect IMOs from all four
directions of the field (north, south, east, west) to capture the
full range of local microbial life.

### 3. Collect from Varied Terrain
We can also collect microbes from mountains, summits, valleys, and
trenches. Each terrain type harbors distinct microbial communities
adapted to specific conditions. Mountain microbes may be more
resilient to temperature extremes, while valley microbes may be
better adapted to moisture retention.

### 4. Include the Tough Guys
The types of microorganisms present in an area vary between
locations because each area has distinct environmental conditions.
For example, the sunny side of the field has different IMOs from
the shaded side. Altitude also affects microbial variety at every
level. To include resilient organisms into the IMO mixture, samples
from high mountains or uncontaminated regions should be collected.
These "tough guys" survive harsher conditions and bring resilience
to the entire microbial community.

### 5. Use Soil Floor, Never Concrete
All IMO mixing and application must occur on a soil floor, never on
concrete. The soil floor allows microbial exchange between the IMO
preparation and the earth. Concrete floors kill microorganisms by
preventing this natural exchange and trapping moisture.

### 6. Match Collection Site to Application Site
It is important to collect IMOs in a place with characteristics most
similar to the land being worked. Microorganisms adapted to the local
conditions of a specific environment will perform better when applied
to similar conditions. Collecting from a site that matches your
field's altitude, vegetation, and soil type yields the most
effective results.

### 7. Time Collection Appropriately
It takes 3 days in summer and 5 days in winter for IMO collection.
Black molds on the steamed rice indicate the process has gone too
far. Timing collection to match seasonal conditions ensures optimal
microbial quality.

## IMO in the Nutritive Cycle

IMOs form the biological foundation of the entire Nutritive Cycle
Theory. They enable the soil to deliver the right nutrients at the
right time by maintaining a balanced microbial community that
processes organic matter, solubilizes minerals, and protects plants
from disease throughout all growth stages.

## Integration with Other NF Inputs

IMOs work synergistically with all other KNF inputs:
- With FPJ and FAA during IMO-3 production for enhanced nutrition
- With LAB for phosphate solubilization and ammonia neutralization
- With OHN for sterilization and plant robustness
- With BRV in IMO-3 and IMO-4 preparation
- With FMC as the primary microbial inoculant in compost

## Long-Term Soil Building

The ultimate goal of IMO application is building self-sustaining
soil ecosystems. Over time, regular IMO application creates soil
with good structure, abundant earthworms, and balanced microbial
communities that maintain fertility without continuous heavy inputs.
The soil becomes soft, well-drained, and rich in humus, reducing
the farmer's dependence on external inputs while improving crop
quality and yield year after year.
