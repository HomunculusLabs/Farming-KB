# MushWorld: Oyster Mushroom Substrate Materials — Worldwide Survey

## Overview

Jozef Poppe's comprehensive worldwide survey cataloging approximately **200 different agricultural and forestry wastes** proven suitable as substrates for oyster mushroom (*Pleurotus* spp.) cultivation. Published in the Mushroom Growers' Handbook 1 (MushWorld, 2004), Part II Chapter 5: Substrate. The survey demonstrates that oyster mushrooms possess the broadest substrate range of any cultivated mushroom, thanks to their "multilateral enzyme system" capable of biodegrading nearly all types of available organic wastes.

## The Scale of Waste-to-Mushroom Potential

The survey opens with a striking calculation of the untapped potential:

- Using just **25% of yearly burned cereal straws** worldwide could yield **317 million metric tons** of fresh mushrooms per year (Chang & Miles, 1989)
- Total yearly world mushroom production at time of survey: only **6 billion kg** — roughly 1kg per person per year, or 3g per day (Courvoisier, 1999)
- Total available agricultural waste: ~500 billion kg dry; forestry waste: ~100 billion kg dry
- If fully utilized across all 600 billion kg, could produce **60kg of mushrooms per person per year**
- **200g of mushrooms** can efficiently replace **100g of meat** as a protein source (Souci et al., 1975-1989)
- 30% of the world population is protein-deficient — mushroom cultivation on waste substrates offers a partial solution

## C/N Ratio and pH Guidelines

Most surveyed wastes fall within C/N ratios of **32–600** and pH of **5.0–7.5** (Poppe, 2000). Key implications:

- High C/N substrates (like sawdust at C/N=244) require **nitrogen supplementation** via wheat or rice bran
- Legume straws naturally higher in nitrogen serve as useful supplements
- Oyster mushrooms require **less nitrogen** than button mushrooms (*Agaricus bisporus*) but most main substrate materials still need supplementation to reach optimal C/N ratio
- Inorganic materials (gypsum, lime, calcium carbonate) are usually included to adjust pH

## Categorized Substrate List

### Cereal Straws

- **Rice straw** (*Oryza sativa*): 41% cellulose, 13% lignin, 0.8% N, C/N=58–70. Most widely used in Asia for *Volvariella* and *Pleurotus*. Immense quantities burned or left rotting annually. Also used as chief component of synthetic *Agaricus* compost
- **Wheat straw** (*Triticum aestivum*): 40% cellulose, 13% lignin, 39% hemicellulose, C/N=104. Main component of fermented *Agaricus* compost; pasteurized (not fermented) for *Pleurotus*. USD 0.1–0.2/kg. Was burned in voluminous amounts until laws changed
- **Barley straw**: 96% biological efficiency for *Pleurotus* (Martinez-Carrera, 1989). 43% cellulose, 36% hemicellulose, 14% lignin. Suitable for *Agaricus*, *Pleurotus*, *Volvariella*, *Stropharia*
- **Oat straw**: 40% cellulose, 32% hemicellulose, 17% lignin. Suitable for *Agaricus*, *Pleurotus*, *Stropharia*
- **Maize/corn straw**: 18% cellulose, 52% biological efficiency for *P. sajor-caju* in India (Pani et al., 1997)
- **Sorghum stover**: Selected substrate for *P. sajor-caju* in Africa, used alone or in combination with cotton waste (Tagwira et al., 1999)
- **Ragi straw** (*Eleucena coracana*): Enriched with cottonseed meal for *P. flabellatus* in India (Bano, 1979)
- **Buckwheat straw** (*Polygonum fagopyrum*): For *Pleurotus*

### Legume Straws and Pods

- **Soybean stems**: Best substrate for *P. sajor-caju* in India at **77% biological efficiency** (Pani et al., 1997). Soybean husks and straw also good for *P. ostreatus* in Yugoslavia
- **Pea straw** (*Pisum* sp.): 43% cellulose, 15% lignin, 0.9% N, C/N=45. Good basic substrate for *Pleurotus*
- **Bean pods and straw**: Useful as substrate component or in bulk for *Pleurotus* (Poppe et al., 1995)
- **French bean haulms**: For *Pleurotus* (Sohi et al., 1989)

### Agricultural Residues

- **Corncobs**: 40% cellulose, 15% lignin, C/N=129. Used for *Pleurotus* and shiitake since 1956 in Hungary. Hammer milled or crushed. Also: corn stalks (C/N=63), corn stover (65% polysaccharides + 30% lignin), corn fiber (excellent yield supplement in Japan)
- **Sugarcane bagasse** (*Saccharum officinarum*): 0.7% N. Pure bagasse only 15% BE — relatively low. Better as compost component or mixed with other materials. In Puerto Rico, spent *Pleurotus* bagasse compost used as commercial fertilizer substitute
- **Cottonseed hulls** (*Gossypium hirsitum*): 1% N. **Best substrate for *Pleurotus* without any thermic treatment** (Sun Pei-Ji, 1989). No pasteurization required
- **Cotton waste** (gin waste, mill droppings, husks): 56–86% BE for *Pleurotus*. Variable N content (0.25–1.45%). Widely regarded as premium substrate. More than 50 author references cited
- **Groundnut shells**: Successful for *P. sajor-caju* in Africa; adding 10% water hyacinth increased production by 22% (Tagwira et al., 1999)
- **Sunflower seed hulls**: Useful **without pasteurization**; millions of kg burned annually before mushroom use. As supplement on straw/maize stalk: 8% higher yields in Yugoslavia (Bugarski et al., 1997)
- **Banana leaves**: 1.45% N, very productive in bulk for *Pleurotus* or in combination for *Volvariella*
- **Banana pseudostems**: Chopped, gave **better results than sawdust or rice straw** for *P. sajor-caju* (Jandaik et al., 1976). Jandaik was first to use this substrate

### Grasses and Aquatic Weeds

- **Wild grasses**: ~3% protein, ~12% lignin, ~23% hemicellulose, ~18% cellulose. Should be dried for hay before use. In India, used dried and cut for *P. sapidus* (Kiran et al., 1989). Not enough research on lawn grass utilization despite endless quantities available. Roadside grasses of various genera and species also suitable for *Pleurotus*, *Agaricus* and *Stropharia*
- **Elephant grass** (*Pennisetum purpureum*): Tested in Cameroon by Poppe (1987) with satisfactory results. In Zambia, used for *Agaricus* compost. Fast-growing tropical grass with potential as bulk substrate
- **Water hyacinth** (*Eichhornia crassipes*): 50% BE for *P. sajor-caju* in India (Gujral et al., 1989). Abundant invasive aquatic weed in Philippines, Indonesia, Africa, Bangladesh. Must be dried before use. Described as "feared boat propeller disturber" that has "gained prominence as a food source through cultivation of edible mushrooms"
- **Water spinach** (*Ipomoea aquatica*): Used in India for *P. sajor-caju* (Gujral et al., 1989). Another aquatic plant with substrate potential
- **Alang-alang grass** (*Imperata cylindrica*): Abundant herb in Asia, especially Indonesia (Poppe et al., 1997)
- **Reed** (*Phragmites communis*): 20% lignin, C/N=50, as substrate component for *Agaricus* and *Pleurotus*. Chopped before use
- **Fern species** (high, low, kukot): Tried in Asia for *Pleurotus* with semi-satisfactory results (Poppe et al., 1997)
- **Papyrus plants**: Aquatic and abundant; to be dried for *Pleurotus* (Poppe, 1995)

### Wood and Sawdust

- **General sawdust** (beech/oak): 44% cellulose, 26% lignin, 0.2% N, C/N=244. Used as single substrate for *Pleurotus*, *Auricularia*, *Flammulina*, *Tremella*, *Pholiota*, *Hericium* — mostly sterilized. Oak sawdust + 10% millet was best pasteurized/sterilized substrate for shiitake in Canada
- **Rubber tree sawdust**: Main substrate material in Thailand. Imported as control in substrate research experiments
- **Coco lumber sawdust**: Used in tropical regions. Readily available in coconut-producing countries
- **Wood logs**: 75+ hardwood species suitable for *Pleurotus*; 10+ for shiitake. *Populus* and *Salix* (willow) logs tested; willow gave slightly higher *P. ostreatus* production
- **Wood shavings**: 0.3% N, useful for *Pleurotus*, *Pholiota*, *Flammulina*, *Auricularia*, *Hericium*

### Other Notable Materials

- **Coffee pulp and parchment**: Suitable with or without pasteurization. Good production in Mexico when mixed with sugarcane pulp and corncobs
- **Tea leaves**: Partial or integral substrate for different Asian mushrooms
- **Newspapers** (shredded): Combined with rice bran or sawdust for *Pleurotus* (Hashimoto, 1976). Also useful for *Stropharia*
- **Paper waste and pulp by-products**: Used in South Africa as substrate component
- **Tree bark**: Alone or combined with wheat straw, corncobs, feather meal. Origin mainly from cellulose paper manufacturers
- **Textile industry waste**: Card sweeping, chimney waste, weaving waste for *Pleurotus* (Khan et al., 1989)
- **Vegetable biomass**: Bitter gourd, chili, cowpea, French beans, winged bean, pumpkin, tomato, okra — all gave good results with *P. sajor-caju* in India (Ganeshan et al., 1989)
- **Cactus, Agave and Yucca**: Drought-resistant plants useful as mushroom substrate component (Stamets, 1993)
- **Cardamon pulp**: 113% BE for *Pleurotus* (Martinez-Carrera, 1989)
- **Lemon grass leaves**: 113% BE for *Pleurotus*
- **Pepper leaves**: 57% BE for *Pleurotus*

### Spent Substrates — Circular Cultivation

- **Spent *Volvariella* compost**: Dried and reused for *P. sajor-caju* with **80% biological efficiency** (Chang & Miles, 1989). Demonstrates the viability of sequential mushroom cropping
- **Spent *Pleurotus* substrate**: Suggested as substrate for King Stropharia (*Stropharia rugosoannulata*). Also: half spent *Volvariella* + 20% rice bran for efficient *Pleurotus* (Quimio, via Oei 1991)
- **Spent *Agaricus* compost**: Amended with cotton waste for satisfactory *Volvariella* cultivation. Shows how one mushroom's waste becomes another's food
- Spent composts increase crude protein vs raw straw, usable as animal feed (broiler chickens showed greater weight gains) or soil conditioner (comparable to poultry manure for vegetable crops)

## Key Principle

The survey's central conclusion: **"every grower producing oyster mushrooms can make their own best substrate choice from among all those genera or species."** Local availability and cost matter far more than theoretical yield maximization. About 90 categories of waste are cataloged, but subdividing by species (e.g., different cereal straws, different wood species) yields approximately **200 distinct substrate materials**.

The survey's closing reflection captures the broader significance: "We must not be at all surprised that the evaluation of all these kinds of different wastes leads us to a renewed appreciation for what is called a waste. Growing mushrooms gives so much satisfaction and produces so much food and income that further use of this practice can result in a great complete contentment of families and villages!"

## Practical Implications for Growers

The practical recommendation is strongly against rigid substrate recipes — instead, growers should experiment with locally available materials, as the specific composition of wastes varies by region, season, and processing method. Supplementation with nitrogen sources (wheat bran, rice bran) and pH adjusters (lime, gypsum) can optimize almost any locally available waste into a productive substrate.

Key practical takeaways from the survey:

1. **Start with what's free or cheapest locally** — rice straw in Asia, wheat straw in temperate zones, bagasse in tropical sugarcane regions
2. **Cotton waste and cottonseed hulls** are premium substrates that may not require pasteurization at all
3. **Sunflower seed hulls** work without pasteurization — millions of kg currently burned could be diverted
4. **Nitrogen supplementation** is almost always needed for high-C/N materials like sawdust (C/N=244)
5. **Spent substrate recycling** enables 2-3 cycles of mushroom production from the same initial material
6. **Water hyacinth** turns an invasive weed problem into a productive resource
7. **Mixing substrates** (e.g., 50% Brassica + 50% rice straw) often outperforms single-material substrates
