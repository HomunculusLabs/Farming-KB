# Bare Ground and Soil Exposure

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]],
bare ground is identified as one of the most visible and consequential
symptoms of ecosystem deterioration. Soil exposure directly impairs all
four [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]] and
is a primary indicator of advancing [[savory-desertification-causes]]. Savory emphasizes that managing soil surface condition
is central to the management of all ecosystem processes.

## The Cascade of Damage

When soil is exposed, a cascade of negative effects begins. Raindrop
impact on bare ground destroys crumb structure, freeing organic material to
wash away while fine particles settle and seal the surface. This capping
prevents water infiltration, leading to more runoff, less effective
precipitation, and accelerated erosion.

Bare ground also means reduced plant volume per unit area, which directly
lowers [[savory-energy-flow-and-solar-capture]]. Fewer plants
mean less root mass, which impairs both the [[savory-water-cycle-management]] and [[savory-mineral-cycle-management]]. The
microenvironment at the soil surface becomes more extreme, with wider
temperature fluctuations and less moisture retention.

## Causes of Bare Ground

Bare ground arises from multiple causes that often compound one another:

- Prolonged [[savory-rest-as-management-tool]] in [[savory-brittle-environments]], which allows old growth to choke
  plants and soil surfaces to cap
- [[savory-holistic-resource-management-overgrazing]] that reduces plant vigor
  and root volume
- [[savory-fire-as-management-tool]], which removes ground cover and
  exposes vast areas of soil
- Low [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] that fails to
  break soil capping or trample litter
- Continuous grazing that produces both overgrazed and overrested plants

## Bare Ground in Brittle vs Non-Brittle Environments

The significance of bare ground varies dramatically with the degree of
brittleness. In [[savory-non-brittle-environments]]
bare areas tend to recover rapidly because biological decay processes
return litter quickly and the successional process advances fast. Close
plant spacings mean that surrounding plants fall and provide cover in a
short time.

In brittle environments, none of this happens. Old plant material only
falls onto the soil surface after long delay because decay occurs mainly
through oxidation and physical weathering. Wider plant spacings allow wind
and water to carry litter away. Once bare, soil in brittle environments
may remain exposed for years or decades without active intervention.

## Fire and Bare Ground

[[savory-fire-as-management-tool]] is perhaps the most dramatic cause
of bare ground. Bare ground is conspicuous right after a fire and until new
growth appears. More critical is the time required to build up litter
between plants, which depends on brittleness, grazing pressure, rest,
animal impact, and other factors.

Soil exposure has the most impact where soil cover takes longest to form,
which is in lower rainfall brittle environments. Lower rainfall produces
less vegetation that might restore cover, and bare soil makes rain less
effective, compounding the problem. If fire is combined with either rest or
low animal impact, soil cover accumulates even more slowly.

## Effective Precipitation and Bare Ground

What really matters for plant growth is effective precipitation, which is
the water that soaks in and becomes available to plant roots. On bare,
capped ground, much of the rainfall that falls is lost to evaporation and
runoff. In very rough figures, it takes approximately six hundred tons of
water to produce one ton of vegetation, so no waste can be afforded.

Savory frequently observed that of fourteen inches of rain received in arid
areas, only five or six inches were actually effective. The rest was lost
due to bare ground, capping, and poor [[savory-water-cycle-management]] effectiveness.

## Capping on Bare Ground

Capping is a particularly insidious consequence of bare ground. When
raindrop impact breaks down surface crumb structure, organic and lightweight
material washes away while heavier fine particles settle and seal the soil.
Some soils develop a cap so hard that only a sharp object can break it. You
can tap severely capped soil with your fingers and hear a hollow drum-like
sound.

Capping prevents water infiltration, reduces soil aeration, inhibits seed
germination, and blocks the establishment of new plants. In brittle
environments, capped soil can persist for thousands of years without some
form of disturbance to break it.

## Plant Spacings and Bare Ground

Bare ground between plants is a direct measure of ecosystem health. In
healthy grasslands, plants are closely spaced and little bare soil is
visible. As deterioration proceeds, spaces widen between plants. Eventually
only isolated tufts remain, widely separated by bare, capped soil.

Savory documented this progression on the Barlite Ranch in Texas. Land
fenced off and undisturbed for years showed progressively widening plant
spacings as old plants stagnated and new seedlings failed to establish on
bare capped soil. Nearby land subjected to high [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] and planned grazing showed dense, closely
spaced plants.

## Reversing Bare Ground

Reversing bare ground requires addressing the root cause. In brittle
environments, this means providing periodic soil disturbance through
[[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] to break capping,
combined with adequate [[savory-recovery-and-grazing-periods]] to allow plants to establish. The approach depends on the degree
of brittleness:

In non-brittle environments, simply removing the cause of exposure (usually
overgrazing or fire) allows natural recovery. In brittle environments,
active disturbance is needed to break the cycle of capping and plant loss.

## Monitoring Bare Ground

[[savory-land-monitoring]] should track the percentage of
bare ground as a key indicator. Photographs taken at fixed points over time
reveal whether bare ground is increasing or decreasing. Transect
measurements can quantify the proportion of bare soil versus plant cover
and litter.
