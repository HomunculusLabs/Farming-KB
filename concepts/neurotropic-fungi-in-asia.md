The region spans from temperate Japan and Siberia to tropical Southeast Asia and the Indian
subcontinent, encompassing a wide range of habitats suitable for these organisms.

## Japan

Japan has a notable neurotropic mycota, reflecting Hongo's nine types of fungal distribution:
- **Psilocybe argentipes** and **P. subcaerulipes** - Closely related to American species P. graveolens,
  P. muliercula, P. pintonii, and P. zapotecorum, suggesting ancient connections between East Asian
  and American mycobiotas
- **P. subaeruginascens** var. subaeruginascens - Known from Japan and Java (Southeast Asian
  distribution); var. septentrionalis is endemic to Japan
- **Amanita muscaria** - Widespread in boreal and temperate forests
- **Conocybe cyanopus** - Multiple Japanese reports
- **Gymnopilus** species: G. aeruginosus, G. spectabilis, G. purpuratus
- **Panaeolina foenisecii** - Very common in Japan, though omitted from some major Japanese
  mycological references (Imazeki & Hongo 1983, 1987)
- **Panaeolus** species: P. castaneifolius, P. sphinctrinus, P. subbalteatus
- **Gerronema fibula** - Reported from Malaysia and Japan
- **Copelandia cyanescens** - Known from disturbed habitats
- **Claviceps paspali** and **C. purpurea** - Ergot fungi on grasses
- **Boletus nigroviolaceus** - A sacred bolete used traditionally

## India and Sri Lanka

- **Panaeolina** species - Several species described by Hongo from Japan and by Natarajan & Raman
  from India, many endemic
- **Panaeolus** species: P. castaneifolius, P. sphinctrinus
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** - Reported from temperate regions of northern India
- **Conocybe** species reported
- **Claviceps purpurea** - Present on cereal grasses

## Southeast Asia (Mainland and Islands)

Southeast Asia has rich diversity but is poorly surveyed for neurotropic fungi:

### Thailand
- **Psilocybe samuiensis** - From Koh Samui, closely related to P. mexicana (Mexico/Guatemala)
  and P. makarorae (New Zealand), all in Section Mexicanae
- **Copelandia** species: C. cambodginiensis, C. cyanescens, C. tropicalis
- Documented traditional use by indigenous peoples (Allen & Merlin, 1992)
