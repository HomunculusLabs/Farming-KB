# Growing Mushrooms in Ridges Outdoors

## The London Market Garden Tradition

In the preface to Kitchen and Market Gardening (London), the authors
noted that William Falconer and Mr. C.W. Shaw made the first attempt
at long and systematic observation of mushroom culture as practiced in
London market gardens. Although Falconer had been disconnected from
the London market gardens for seventeen years by the time of his
writing, he kept informed through revisits, correspondence, and the
horticultural press.

At that time, mushrooms were one of the general crops, planted as a
matter of course alongside snap beans or cauliflower. By Falconer's
era they had become a specialty. Some gardeners devoted their whole
energy to mushroom-growing alone, making from $2,000 to $5,000 a year
clear profit from one acre of mushrooms grown in ridges in the open
field. Falconer considered this the most profitable field crop known.

## The Spawn Industry

London gardeners who restricted themselves to mushrooms only devoted
the summer months to making spawn for their own use and for sale.
Mr. John F. Barter, the "king of London mushroom growers," had
about 10,000 bushels (160,000 bricks) of spawn made up for sale
annually, earning half his living from spawn alone.

## Manure Preparation

The manure was obtained from city stables and hauled home by the
gardeners on their return trips from market. The manure collected
after midsummer was used for mushrooms, with every effort made to
save the very best horse manure for this purpose.

When enough had accumulated for a bed, the manure was turned and
well shaken, removing only the rougher part of the straw, and thrown
into a large pyramidal pile to heat. This shape was adopted as being
better than the flat form for keeping out rain. In three or four days
the manure was again turned, shaken out, and piled up as before.
After this it was turned every second day, unless it rained, until it
had been turned six or seven times in all. It should then be ready
for making into ridges.

## Site Selection and Layout

The site should be warm, well-sheltered, and dry, either in open
ground or orchard. Much pains should be taken to protect it from cold
winds. The land should be slightly elevated or sloping so that no
pools of water collect. The ground was marked off into strips two feet
wide (for the bed) and six feet wide (for pathways) alternately.

## Ridge Construction

Common ridge sizes were two feet wide by two feet high, or two and
one-half feet wide by two and one-half feet high, tapering to six or
eight inches wide at the top. The manure was carted to the place,
wheeled upon the beds, shaken out well and evenly to cause it to hold
together, and tamped with the back of the fork. Two or three times
before the ridges were completed, the grower walked upon and trod the
manure down solidly with the feet, and trimmed down the sides to turn
the rain water.

## Ventilation and Overheating Prevention

Two days after the bed was made up, holes were bored from the top to
nearly the bottom with a small iron bar, about nine inches apart
along the center of the bed, to let the heat off and prevent the
inside from becoming too dry. The old gardeners did not use the
crowbar. They were very particular not to build their ridges before
the chances of overheating were considered past.

Notwithstanding all their care, some beds would get overwarm. When
this happened, without a moment's hesitation they tossed the manure
over, part to the right and part to the left, left it thus exposed
for a day or two to cool, and then made up the beds again on the
same site.

## Spawning and Casing

Brick spawn was always used. When the heat had fallen to between 80
and 90 degrees Fahrenheit, the ridges were spawned. The pieces were
inserted in three rows along each side, leaving about nine inches
between pieces. A dibber should not be used on any account. The
spawn was put in tightly with the hand and the manure pressed down
level with the face of the bed.

After spawning, the beds were molded over with soil about two inches
thick at the base and one inch thick at the top, well firmed by
beating with the back of the spade. The surface was left smooth and
even to shed rain water. After molding, the beds were covered with
the rankest straw that had been shaken out of the manure, to a depth
of four to ten inches depending on weather.

## Protection from Weather

Drenching or long drizzling rains were considered more injurious to
the beds than cold. Old Russia mats and any other sort of cloth or
carpet covering obtainable was laid over the litter on the beds and
weighted down with poles, boards, stones, or anything convenient.

The covering was not disturbed for about four weeks, then on a dry
day it was stripped off and the litter shaken up loosely to dry. If
there was white mold on the surface of the soil, a handful of straw
was used to rub it off. If the bed was cold, a layer of clean, dry
hay was placed next the bed, and the littery covering replaced.

## Harvesting Schedule

The first beds were made in August, with one or more every month
after until March. In fall and spring, beds yielded mushrooms in about
six weeks after spawning; in winter they took eight to nine weeks.
In cold weather, mushrooms were gathered at midday. Three men worked
together: one rolled the straw down, another gathered mushrooms by
pulling them out by the roots, and a third covered up the bed. The
first cutting was light; subsequent pickings occurred twice weekly for
three weeks. Altogether ten or eleven good pickings were gathered from
each bed.

Mushrooms were graded as "broilers" (large, wide-spread), "cups"
(full-sized with slightly broken frill), and "buttons" (small, white,
unbroken frill). All were kept separate and marketed in chip baskets,
boxes, or punnetts holding one pound each.

## Companion Cropping and Spent Manure

Some London gardeners ran their beds east and west to grow early
tomatoes against the south side in summer. The spent manure from
mushroom beds, being thoroughly rotted, was excellent for cauliflower,
lettuces, snap beans, and other crops, and always in demand. Mushroom
growers could dispose of spent manure at one-half the cost price.
