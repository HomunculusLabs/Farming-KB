# Claviceps purpurea: Ergot Alkaloids and Phytochemistry

## The Fungus and Its Biology

*Claviceps purpurea* (Fr.) Tulasne is a parasitic fungus that infects
the inflorescence of rye (*Secale cereale*) and other grasses. While
Ergot infects a number of different grasses, it is best known as a
parasite on rye. The hard, brown or purplish black sclerotium
originating in the caryopsis of rye is exceedingly common in Europe.
When grain is infected by Ergot, long black growths appear on the
heads, called sclerotium. The specific name *purpurea* means "purple"
-- a color that in antiquity was linked with powers of the underworld.

The native nomenclature of *Claviceps purpurea* is remarkably complex.
Ergot, the French word for "spur" of a cock, is now generally
employed in numerous languages. There are two dozen other words for
the sclerotium in French; sixty-two vernacular names in German
(*Mutterkorn* being the most common); twenty-one in Dutch; fifteen
in the Scandinavian languages; fourteen in Italian; and seven in
English. This proliferation of vernacular terminology indicates the
great importance of the fungus in European countries.

## Chemistry of Ergot

The active ingredients in Ergot are **indole alkaloids**, all derived
from the same basic compound, **lysergic acid**. The most important
alkaloids in Ergot of rye are **ergotamine** and **ergotoxine**, in
which lysergic acid is connected with a peptide radical consisting of
three amino acids. These alkaloids and their derivatives have various
medicinal uses.

In toxic doses they cause gangrene because of their vasoconstricting
properties. Ergot from wild grasses, however, contains essentially
simple **lysergic acid amides**: ergine (lysergic acid amide), and
lysergic acid-hydroxyethylamide (found only in traces in Ergot of
rye). These psychotropic alkaloids may have played a role in the
convulsive form of ergotism. They occur as the main active principles
in the Mexican Morning Glory [[plants-of-the-gods-turbina-corymbosa-ololiuqui]]
(*Turbina corymbosa*) and other bindweeds (*Ipomoea violacea*,
*Argyreia nervosa*).

### Timeline of Chemical Discovery

- **1582**: Lonicer of Frankfurt reported the obstetric value of Ergot
- **1676**: Dodart added much scientific knowledge about Ergot
- **1764**: von Munchhausen declared Ergot was a fungal infection
- **1815**: A.P. de Candolle proved the fungal nature of Ergot
- **1808**: Dr. John Stearns published a widely acclaimed report on
  Ergot efficacy
- **1836**: Ergot accepted in the Pharmacopoeia
- **1921**: Ergotamine isolated from *C. purpurea*
- **1935**: Ergonovine discovered
- Subsequently, many related alkaloids have been discovered

## St. Anthony's Fire: Ergotism

Although its medicinal use was unknown in classical times, Ergot was
early recognized as a poison. As far back as 600 B.C., the Assyrians
called it a "noxious pustule in the ear of the grain." The sacred
books of the Parsees (about 350 B.C.) warned of "evil grasses that
cause pregnant women to drop the womb and die in childbed." The
earliest undoubted reports of Ergot poisoning appeared during the
Middle Ages, when bizarre epidemics broke out in various parts of
Europe, taking thousands of lives.

These epidemics manifested in two forms: nervous convulsions and
epileptic symptoms; and gangrene, mummifications, atrophy, and loss
of extremities. Delirium and hallucinations were common symptoms.
The "Holy Fire" was always characterized by a feeling of burning in
the feet and hands. It was not until 1676 -- some five hundred years
after the height of St. Anthony's fire -- that the real cause of
ergotism was discovered. Even today, occasional outbreaks occur; the
most notorious recent attacks occurred in France and Belgium in 1953
and in the Ukraine and Ireland in 1929. Suggestions have been made
that the alleged witchcraft outbreaks in Salem, Massachusetts, may
have been due to Ergot poisoning.

## The Eleusinian Mysteries

It has been convincingly argued that Ergot played a role in the
Eleusinian mysteries of ancient Greece. In an interdisciplinary study
based on ethnomycology, classical studies, and chemistry, the secret
rites -- which had remained a puzzle for four thousand years -- are
associated with intoxication caused by *Claviceps paspali*, and
possibly other species, growing on various *Loliums* and other cereal
grasses native to Greece. The ancient testimony about Eleusis
describes "trembling, vertigo, cold sweat, and then a sight that
made all previous seeing seem like blindness" -- symptoms
unmistakably the experience induced by a hallucinogen.

## Medicinal Applications

European midwives had long known that Ergot could aid in difficult
childbirth. Chemicals isolated from Ergot are still official drugs to
induce contraction of involuntary muscles in stubborn childbirth.
Ergot alkaloids or derivatives are the basis of numerous modern
medicines. The discovery that LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), the
most potent hallucinogen known, is a semi-synthetic derivative of
ergot alkaloids transformed the understanding of this fungus from a
feared poison to one of the most pharmacologically significant
organisms known to science.

## Relationship to Other Lysergic Acid Plants

The lysergic acid alkaloids found in Ergot establish a chemical
bridge to several other important hallucinogenic plants. The ergoline
alkaloids connect Ergot to [[plants-of-the-gods-turbina-corymbosa-ololiuqui]],
[[schultes-morning-glory-ipomoea-seeds]] seeds, and
the broader family of indole alkaloids found in
[[plants-of-the-gods-tabernanthe-iboga]] and
[[schultes-psilocybe-little-flowers-gods]] mushrooms. See
[[plants-of-the-gods-chemical-structures-hallucinogens]] for details on molecular relationships.
