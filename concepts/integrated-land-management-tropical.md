# Integrated Land Management in the Humid Tropics

Integrated land management in the humid tropics is modeled on traditional geomorphic and sociological systems such as the Maori marae and the Hawaiian ohana, where land and people were organized as unified sustenance units. These systems likely evolved from early errors of over-clearing, excessive burning, and the extinction of useful animals before reaching ecological equilibrium. The stable tropical landscape requires a holistic hill-to-shore design that protects water, soil, and biodiversity while providing for human needs at every elevation zone.

## The Hill-to-Shore Profile

Assuming a volcanic cone profile (common in the humid tropics), the stable tropical landscape requires the following features from summit to sea:

### Protected Skyline and Hill Forests

The upper slopes—often steep—must remain under permanent forest cover. These forests:

- Protect soils from erosion and watersheds from degradation
- Mine and release plant nutrients from upper slopes
- Serve as limited forage resource and mulch provision
- Require iron-clad protection from clearing

Clearing skyline forests brings compound catastrophes: landslide, loss of nutrients in water and crop, desertification, and severe social disruption. At the base of these forests, as the slope eases to 15° or less, water can be diverted or harvested to replenish groundwater and irrigate terraces and crops.

### Midslope and Keypoint

The keypoint is a critical topographic position where stream water can be diverted out to ridges for terrace crop and village use. Cropping commences below this point. Human occupation, complex cultivated forests, and gardens are established downslope. The stable plateau, hill rising above the valley, and bench sites above flood reach and sheltered from hurricane and tsunami are prime cluster settlement (village) sites, with scattered housing on higher ridges and the forest edge.

### Low Slopes (2°–15°)

These areas are well-suited to earth-shaping as terraces and padi (rice paddies), with limited grazing and innovative forestry. They represent the sustainable agricultural areas where attention to sub-contoured agriculture, windbreak, and access helps direct runoff and water to crops.

### Coastal Flats and Valley Floors

Rich and often deep humus soils accumulate on valley floors. Greywater and processed manures from settlement and livestock add to fertility. These areas support extensive aquacultures or rich forest/orchard crops.

### Shorelines

Shorelines should be preserved for approximately 100 meters inland for essential windbreak, shore stability, and cloud generation over land. Selected quiet harbors and refuges accept fishing settlements based on sea resources. Shores with 15–20 year tsunami history need sacrificial palm/Casuarina barriers to reduce storm damage ashore. Lightly-built temporary shore housing is used only during coastal work, with more substantial homes and gardens safely inland or behind earth embankments.

### Lagoon and Estuary Sea Impoundments

Ancient and innovative seawalls—usually of semi-permeable coral blocks with entries, flood-tide gates, and a series of inner pens—make excellent use of shallows. Features include:

- Restricted mangrove plantings that stabilize banks and provide sea mulch while catching silt washed from upslope
- Warm-water lagoons rich in algae, good sites for cultivated seaweed
- Impoundment systems that fatten mullet, mangrove fish, oysters, eels, shellfish, and prawns
- Rapid fixation of mobile phosphates in plants and mud within days, making them available for growth

### Marine Constructs

Artificial reef systems of tires (on sand), coral blocks, and boxes of palm trunks greatly enlarge habitat for sedentary fish and lobsters. Within a year, these constructs are consolidated or cemented by corals. Weak electrolysis on metal mesh creates artificial but permanent coralline deposits more rapidly. In extensive sandy shallows, log barriers full of sand and stabilized with mangroves provide shore barriers, lagoon walls, and productive "edge" in the sea.

## Windbreaks for Tropical Settlements

Primary tall windbreaks of Araucaria, Cupressus, Casuarina, Pinus, hardy Phoenix palms, and even mangroves may be needed in front-line locations, followed in the lee by hardy quickset species such as [[coprosma-repens-entity]], and [[echium-fastuosum]]. It is always best to find local plant species well-adapted to the district.

[[acacia-tortilis]] is ideal for cut-and-build fences. When cutting Euphorbia, eye protection with goggles is essential, and skin should be covered if allergic to the milky sap.

## Erosion Control

Contours at 10-meter intervals on medium slopes (2°–7°) and at 5 meters on steep slopes can be planted with root sets of Canna, vetiver grass, lemongrass, or pampas grass at 0.3–0.6 meter spacing. These form unbroken cross-slope hedges or crowns on earth walls and dam banks at spillways. They disperse water and create silt traps. Behind such self-perpetuating walls, soil deepens and trees or crops can be planted. The system is cheap, effective, and provides mulch. In desert areas, yuccas, agaves, and aloes may provide similar structural effects.

## Design Philosophy

The integrated land management approach recognizes that:

- Every elevation zone has optimal uses that differ from adjacent zones
- Water flows from hill to shore and must be managed at every transition
- Settlement patterns must respect natural hazards (tsunami, hurricane, flood)
- The coastal zone is a productive extension of the land system, not a boundary
- Traditional systems achieved equilibrium through long evolutionary trial; modern systems must deliberately design for it
- Policy changes toward environmentally sound land management are essential at government level
