# Nematode Roles in Soil

Updated: 2026-04-18

Nematodes are nonsegmented, blind roundworms that mineralize nutrients
contained in bacteria and fungi. Their name comes from the Greek nema
("thread"). They average 2 millimeters in length and 50 micrometers in
diameter. Over 20,000 species have been identified, and scientists estimate
there may be as many as 1 million species total. Nematodes are the second
most dominant form of animal life after arthropods.

A teaspoon of good garden soil averages about 20 bacteria-eating nematodes,
20 fungal feeders, and a few predatory and plant-eating nematodes -- totaling
40 to 50 nematodes per teaspoon.

## Five Feeding Types

Nematodes are classified by their specialized mouthparts and eating habits:

### 1. Bacterivores (Bacteria Eaters)

Bacterivores have a hollow tube mouthpart and can consume multitudes of tiny
bacteria per hour. They are crucial mineralizers, releasing nutrients locked
in bacterial bodies back into the soil in plant-available forms.

### 2. Fungivores (Fungi Eaters)

Fungivores have stylets for puncturing the chitin cell walls of [[fungal-hyphae]].
Like bacterivores, they mineralize nutrients from their prey. The ratio of
fungal to bacterial nematodes directly reflects the food sources available
in the soil.

### 3. Herbivores (Root Eaters)

Plant-parasitic nematodes have needle-like stylets to puncture plant cell
walls. Some are ectoparasitic (feeding on root surfaces) while others are
endoparasitic (entering the root). They create lesions, cysts, and root knots
that damage crops.

### 4. Predators

Predatory nematodes feed on protozoa, algae, other nematodes, grubs, weevils,
wasps, and even small invertebrates like slugs. They prevent overgrazing of
bacteria and fungi and keep destructive nematode populations (especially
herbivores) in check.
