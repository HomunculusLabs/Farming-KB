# Fungal Roles in Landfill Remediation

Landfills contain complex mixtures of organic and inorganic contaminants including heavy metals, PAHs, pesticides, chlorinated compounds, and leachate rich in dissolved organic matter. Fungi contribute to landfill remediation through biodegradation of organic pollutants, biosorption of metals, and participation in composting-based treatment processes.

## Composting as Fungal-Driven Remediation

Composting is a primary fungal-based strategy for treating contaminated landfill materials. The thermophilic phase of composting (50-70C) supports thermophilic fungi that degrade recalcitrant organics. Key aspects:
- Fungi in composting consortia include Thermomyces lanuginosus and various white-rot species
- Laccase activity has been detected during the thermophilic stage of municipal solid waste composting
- Composting effectively treats PAH-contaminated soils from landfill sites
- Spent mushroom substrate (SMS) from commercial cultivation provides cheap, abundant fungal inoculum for remediation

Spent oyster mushroom substrate and spent sawdust cultures of shiitake mushroom have demonstrated capacity to transform pentachlorophenol, a common wood preservative found in landfill materials.

## Leachate Treatment

Landfill leachate contains high concentrations of dissolved organic carbon, ammonia, heavy metals, and recalcitrant organic compounds. Fungal approaches to leachate treatment include:
- White-rot fungi degrade phenolic compounds and color-causing melanoidins in leachate
- Fungal biomass in packed-bed reactors removes heavy metals from leachate through biosorption
- Ligninolytic enzymes (laccase, MnP, LiP) oxidize recalcitrant organic contaminants
- Combined fungal-bacterial systems achieve broader spectrum contaminant removal

## Fly Ash Bioleaching

Municipal solid waste incineration fly ash contains high concentrations of heavy metals (Cd, Cu, Ni, Pb, Zn) requiring treatment before landfill disposal. Aspergillus niger effectively mobilizes metals from fly ash through organic acid production:
- Produces mainly gluconic acid in the presence of fly ash
- Almost 100% lead mobilization at higher fly ash concentrations
- Effective for cadmium, lead, and zinc recovery
- Metal concentrations in fly ash approach those of low-grade ores, making fungal bioleaching economically attractive

## Electronic Waste Treatment

Electronic waste from landfills contains valuable metals (Al, Cu, Ni, Pb, Sn, Zn, Ag, Au). Fungal bioleaching offers an alternative to conventional pyrometallurgical recovery:
- Organic acid production by fungi solubilizes metals from shredded electronic waste dust
- Aspergillus niger and Penicillium species are primary bioleaching agents
- Process operates at ambient temperature and pressure, reducing energy costs
- Enables metal recovery from fine-grained residues that would otherwise require landfill disposal

## Soil Excavation and On-Site Treatment

Contaminated landfill soils can be excavated and treated using fungal-based methods:
- **Land farming:** Contaminated soil spread in thin layers, inoculated with fungi, and periodically tilled
- **Biopiles:** Engineered piles with controlled aeration and moisture, amended with fungal inoculum on wood chip carriers
- **Composting windows:** Contaminated soil mixed with bulking agents and fungal inoculum in aerated piles

## Mycorrhizal Approaches for Cover Soils

Mycorrhizal fungi can be used to establish vegetation on landfill cover soils:
- VAM and ectomycorrhizal fungi improve plant establishment on nutrient-poor cover soils
- Mycorrhizal plants show enhanced tolerance to residual contaminants
- Fungal hyphal networks stabilize soil and prevent erosion
- Glomalin produced by VAM fungi sequesters potentially toxic elements

## Limitations

- Heterogeneous nature of landfill waste complicates treatment design
- High concentrations of toxic compounds can inhibit fungal growth
- Anaerobic zones within landfills limit fungal activity
- Scale-up from laboratory to field conditions remains challenging
- Economic viability depends on contaminant types and regulatory requirements

## Specific Fungal Species in Landfill Remediation

Several fungal species have demonstrated particular effectiveness in landfill-related remediation applications:

- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium:** The most extensively studied white-rot fungus for pollutant degradation, P. chrysosporium produces lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese peroxidase (MnP) that mineralize a broad range of organic contaminants. It has been applied to PAH-contaminated landfill soils, achieving 60-80% degradation of high-molecular-weight PAHs in bench-scale studies. Its primary limitation is sensitivity to nutrient nitrogen levels, which must be nitrogen-limited for ligninolytic enzyme production.
- **Trametes versicolor:** Produces laccase as its primary ligninolytic enzyme and is more tolerant of environmental conditions than P. chrysosporium. T. versicolor has been used in bioreactor systems for landfill leachate treatment, effectively degrading phenolic compounds and reducing chemical oxygen demand (COD) by 40-60%. Its ability to produce laccase under non-limited nitrogen conditions gives it practical advantages for field applications.
- **Pleurotus ostreatus (Oyster mushroom):** Grows rapidly on a wide range of lignocellulosic substrates and has demonstrated capacity to degrade PAHs, PCP, and dioxins in contaminated landfill soils. Its spent substrate from commercial cultivation is widely used as an inexpensive inoculum source for field-scale remediation. P. ostreatus also accumulates heavy metals in its fruiting bodies, offering biosorption capacity.
- **Aspergillus niger:** The primary fungal agent for bioleaching applications in landfill contexts, A. niger produces organic acids (predominantly gluconic acid and citric acid) that solubilize heavy metals from fly ash and electronic waste. It tolerates high metal concentrations and produces acid under a wide range of conditions, making it industrially robust for metal recovery from landfill-incinerated materials.
- **Bjerkandera adusta:** Produces MnP and laccase at high levels and has shown effective degradation of azo dyes and phenolic compounds in landfill leachate. B. adusta is notable for its tolerance of high contaminant concentrations and ability to grow under semi-solid conditions relevant to composting-based treatment.

## Engineering Approaches for Applying Fungi to Landfills

Translating laboratory-scale fungal remediation to landfill conditions requires specific engineering strategies:

- **Biopile construction:** Contaminated soil or excavated landfill material is mixed with bulking agents (wood chips, straw, compost) and fungal inoculum, then arranged in aerated piles. Forced aeration systems deliver oxygen to support fungal activity, while moisture is maintained at 50-70% of water-holding capacity. Temperature is monitored and controlled; thermophilic fungi active above 45°C can be harnessed during the self-heating phase. Biopile treatment times typically range from 3 to 12 months depending on contaminant type and concentration.
- **In-situ mycoremediation:** For landfills where excavation is impractical, fungi can be introduced through injection wells, perimeter trenches, or surface application. Spore suspensions or mycelial slurries are injected into the contaminant plume, often with nutrient amendments to support fungal growth. Oxygen delivery through bioventing or air sparging is critical, since fungi are obligate aerobes and most of the landfill mass is anaerobic. In-situ approaches are best suited for the aerobic cap zone and contaminant plume margins.
- **Leachate bioreactors:** Fungal leachate treatment employs packed-bed or fluidized-bed bioreactors containing fungal biomass immobilized on supporting matrices (polyurethane foam, wood chips, or plastic carriers). Packed-bed reactors using T. versicolor or P. chrysosporium achieve significant reduction in leachate toxicity and organic load. Reactor design must address clogging from fungal growth, maintain adequate oxygen transfer, and manage pH shifts from organic acid production.
- **Mycelial mats and barrier systems:** Live mycelial mats grown on straw or wood substrate can be installed as permeable reactive barriers at landfill boundaries. As leachate migrates through the fungal mat, contaminants are degraded or immobilized. This approach is most effective for shallow groundwater plumes and surface runoff management.

## Monitoring and Assessing Remediation Success

Effective monitoring is essential to evaluate fungal remediation performance at landfill sites:

- **Chemical contaminant analysis:** Periodic soil, leachate, and groundwater sampling with GC-MS or HPLC analysis tracks concentration changes in target contaminants (PAHs, phenols, heavy metals). Reduction percentages and degradation rate constants are calculated to assess remediation progress against regulatory cleanup targets.
- **Enzyme activity assays:** Laccase, MnP, and LiP activities in soil or bioreactor samples serve as indicators of active fungal remediation. Laccase activity can be measured colorimetrically using syringaldazine or ABTS as substrates. Sustained enzyme activity indicates viable fungal populations are actively degrading contaminants.
- **Toxicity testing:** Bioassays using earthworms (Eisenia fetida), seed germination (Lactuca sativa), and luminescent bacteria (Vibrio fischeri) assess whether contaminant concentrations have been reduced to ecologically acceptable levels. Toxicity reduction often precedes complete chemical degradation and provides a more biologically relevant endpoint.
- **Molecular monitoring of introduced fungi:** qPCR with species-specific primers tracks the survival and spread of inoculated fungi in treated material. Terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism (TRFLP) or next-generation sequencing of ITS regions monitors shifts in the overall fungal community structure during remediation.
- **Respiration measurements:** Soil CO₂ evolution rates indicate overall microbial activity, including fungal metabolism. Increased respiration rates in treated zones compared to controls suggest active biodegradation. However, elevated respiration can also reflect general microbial activity on available carbon, so it should be interpreted alongside contaminant-specific analyses.
- **Vegetation surveys:** For mycorrhizal cover soil applications, plant survival rates, biomass accumulation, and species diversity serve as integrative indicators of overall site improvement and contaminant toxicity reduction.

## Integration of Multiple Remediation Approaches

Effective landfill remediation typically requires combining fungal-based treatments with other remediation strategies:

- **Sequential treatment trains:** A common approach combines physical separation (sorting, screening) with composting (fungal-bacterial degradation of organics), followed by leachate treatment (fungal bioreactors), and final establishment of vegetative cover (mycorrhizal inoculation). Each stage targets different contaminant classes and prepares the material for the next treatment step.
- **Combined fungal-bacterial systems:** Fungi and bacteria have complementary degradation capabilities. White-rot fungi degrade complex aromatic compounds (PAHs, phenols) that bacteria cannot easily metabolize, producing intermediate products that bacteria can then mineralize. Engineering these consortia for landfill applications is an active area of research, with promising results from co-culture systems.
- **Phytoremediation integration:** Establishing metal-accumulating plants (hyperaccumulators) on landfill cover soils works synergistically with mycorrhizal fungi that enhance plant metal tolerance and uptake. The fungal partner improves nutrient acquisition while the plant extracts and concentrates metals, which can then be harvested and processed for metal recovery.
