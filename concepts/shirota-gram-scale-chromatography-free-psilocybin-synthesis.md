# Gram-Scale Chromatography-Free Synthesis of Psilocybin

## Overview

In 2003, Osamu Shirota, Wataru Hakamata, and Yukihiro Goda at
Japan's National Institute of Health Sciences published a concise
large-scale synthesis of psilocin and psilocybin — the principal
hallucinogenic constituents of "magic mushrooms" — achieved
entirely **without chromatographic purification**. This was a
significant practical achievement, as previous syntheses of
psilocybin had required chromatographic steps that complicated
scale-up, and isolation of pure psilocybin from mushroom tissue
was impractical on gram scale due to psilocin's instability and
psilocybin's high polarity.

The synthesis was motivated by regulatory needs: since June 6,
2002, fungi containing psilocin and psilocybin had been regulated
by Japan's Narcotics and Psychotropic Control Law. Chromatographic
identification methods (TLC, GC, HPLC) required standard compounds,
which were difficult to obtain in pure form from natural sources.

## The Psilocin Synthesis Route

The synthesis started from commercially available **4-hydroxyindole**
with simple protection of the hydroxyl group by acetylation. This
yielded 4-acetylindole (compound 4) in quantitative yield as ivory
white crystals. The choice of acetyl protection over benzyl ether
protection (also used in prior work) was strategic: benzyl
protection required a separate deprotection step, while acetyl
protection could be removed in the same reduction step that
produced psilocin.

The next step converted 4-acetylindole to 3-dimethylaminooxalyl-
4-acetylindole (compound 6) through a two-step acylation-amidation
conversion. Treatment with oxalyl chloride in ether produced the
glyoxalyl chloride intermediate (compound 5) as yellow crystals,
which were converted to the glyoxalylamide by reaction with
dimethylamine. This two-step sequence proceeded in over 80% yield
without isolation of the intermediate.

The final step — reduction of the glyoxalylamide with lithium
aluminum hydride (LiAlH4) — afforded psilocin (compound 1) in
over 85% yield as white crystals. The acetyl group was
simultaneously removed during reduction, eliminating the need for
a separate deprotection step. This three-step sequence from
commercial starting material to psilocin represented a concise
and efficient route suitable for scale-up.

## The Phosphorylation Challenge

Converting psilocin to psilocybin required introducing a phosphate
group at the 4-hydroxy position — a challenging transformation.
The researchers evaluated several phosphorylation methodologies
including the phosphoryl iodide method and the pyrophosphate
method. Most methods failed to consume psilocin at all, but the
pyrophosphate method using tetrabenzylpyrophosphate and n-BuLi
showed promise and was selected for its easy handling and reagent
stability.

During this reaction, an unexpected but fortuitous transformation
occurred. After the usual aqueous workup, the 1H NMR spectrum
showed complicated signals. Rechecking TLC revealed an additional
spot at the origin — a whitish material that no longer dissolved
in dichloromethane. This observation had been previously reported
by Nichols and Frescas, who concluded that hydrolytic cleavage of
one O-benzyl group rapidly occurred, producing a zwitterionic
O-monobenzyl phosphate mixture.

## The Zwitterionic Intermediate Discovery

Purification by preparative reversed-phase HPLC afforded a single
compound (compound 8), analyzed by NMR. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra
in CD3OD showed signals for two sets of benzyl groups and a
psilocin core, with the proton signal of one benzyl methylene
shifted to high field (δH 4.56) compared to the other (δH 4.98,
4.96). The 31P NMR spectrum confirmed the phosphate moiety.

HMBC experiments revealed that one benzyl group was directly
linked at the nitrogen of the N,N-dimethyl part, forming a
quaternary ammonium ion. NOESY spectra supported these linkages.
Compound 8 was identified as a **zwitterionic N,O-dibenzyl
phosphate derivative** — a novel intermediate not previously
fully characterized in psilocybin synthesis.

The conversion from the expected O,O-dibenzyl phosphate
intermediate (compound 7) to the zwitterionic N,O-dibenzyl
phosphate (compound 8) occurred simply by suspending the worked-up
reaction mixture in CH2Cl2 overnight. This spontaneous
intramolecular benzyl migration was the key discovery that made
chromatography-free synthesis possible.

## Isolation Without Chromatography

The zwitterionic nature of compound 8 was the crucial feature
enabling chromatography-free isolation. Because compound 8 was
insoluble in CH2Cl2 while excess dibenzyl phosphate remained
soluble, simple filtration separated the product from impurities
in over 85% yield. The excess dibenzyl phosphate was removed by
washing with CH2Cl2.

Catalytic hydrogenolysis of compound 8 then led to psilocybin
(compound 2) as a crystalline product without any chromatographic
purification — unlike the Nichols and Frescas method which
required anion-exchange resin. The isolated yield of psilocybin
from psilocin was greater than 72%, even for gram-scale
production.

## Analytical Characterization

The researchers provided complete spectroscopic data. Psilocin:
mp 169-174°C (dec), UV (MeOH) λmax 222.5, 268.0, 284.5, 294.0
nm. Psilocybin: mp 190-198°C, UV (MeOH) λmax 221.0, 267.5,
278.5, 290.0 nm. HR-ESI-MS confirmed molecular formulas:
psilocin C12H17N2O ([M+H]+ 205.1303) and psilocybin
C12H18N2O4P ([M+H]+ 285.0991). The 31P NMR of psilocybin
showed δ -4.48, confirming the phosphate group.

## Significance

The Shirota synthesis represented a significant practical
advancement over prior methods. Previous psilocybin syntheses
reported yields of only 20% and 47%. The zwitterionic
intermediate approach achieved over 72% yield from psilocin
on gram scale, with no chromatographic purification at any
stage. The total route from 4-hydroxyindole to psilocybin
required only five chemical steps (three to psilocin, two to
psilocybin) with simple crystallization and filtration as the
only purification methods.
