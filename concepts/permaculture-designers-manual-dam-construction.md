# Dam Construction
#permaculture #earthworks #water #dams #storage

Updated: 2026-04-18

> From: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers Manual* (1988), Chapter 7, Section 7.3.

## Overview

For the serious small dam and earth tank builder, there is no substitute for comprehensive texts such as Kenneth D. Nelson's (1985). Mollison concentrates on the placement of dams in the total designed landscape, and the biological uses of dams and the necessary modifications that create biological productivity in water systems.

P.A. Yeomans' *Water for Every Farm/The Keyline Plan* (1981) is the pioneering modern text on landscape design for water conservation and gravity-fed flow irrigation. As it also involves patterning, tree planting, soil treatment, and fencing alignment, it is the first book on functional landscape design in modern times.

## Types of Dams

### Keypoint Dams
Built at the keypoint of a valley -- the point where the slope changes from convex to concave. A second or third series can be run below this primary series of dams, and the spillway of the last dam in a series can be returned "upstream" to meet the main valley, effectively spilling surplus to streams. Primarily used to store irrigation water.

### Contour Dams
Contour dam walls can be built on contour wherever the slope is 8% or less, or sufficiently flat. Contours (and dam walls) can be concave or convex to the fall line across the slope. Used for irrigation, aquaculture, or flood-flow basins in semi-arid areas.

### Barrier Dams
Always constructed across a flowing or intermittent stream bed. These dams need ample spillways, careful construction, fish ladders on biologically important streams, and are made most frequently as energy systems, but are also used for irrigation.

### Turkey's Nest Dams
Above-grade tanks; water has to be pumped in, often by windmill or solar pump. Common in flatlands as stock water tanks or for low-head irrigation.

### Check Dams
Many forms of barrier dams not intended to create water storages, but to create flat areas where silt loads can usefully deposit, and so form absorption beds in flood conditions. For farm construction, walls 0.5-2 m (2-6.5 feet) high are usual.

### Gabion Dams
In drylands, permeable barriers of rock-filled mesh "baskets" (gabions) will create silt fields and water-spreading across eroding valleys. Eroding gullies are best stabilised using strong wire baskets to contain stones or shingle; these resist floods well.

## Building Dams

### Site Selection
Storage dams should be carefully located with respect to:
- Earth type (core out a sample pit for assessing clay fractions)
- Grade behind wall (lower slopes give greatest capacity)
- Downstream safety of structures and houses
- Height above use points (gravity flow is desirable)
- Available catchment or diversion

### Construction Specifications
- Tamped earth with some clay fractions of better than 50% is a waterproof barrier up to heights of 3.6 m (12 feet)
- Practical depths: 4.5-6 m (15-20 feet) for small earth dams
- Slopes to crest should be concave
- Every 25 cm (10 inches) should be tamped down
- Exclude boulders, logs, grass clumps, and topsoil (critical to earth stability)
- Earth so rolled should be neither so dry as to crumble nor so wet as to slump
- Shrinkage of well-compacted dams is less than 1%

### Wall Specifications
- A key should be cut to prevent shear and cut off base seepage (needed on all walls 1.8 m/6 feet or more high)
- Slopes safe at 3:1 (inner) and 2 or 2.5:1 (outer)
- Freeboard at 0.9 m (3 feet)
- Key at 0.6-0.9 m (2-3 feet) deep
- In suspect soils, the whole core can be of carted clay

### Spillway and Outlets
- The spillway base should be carefully surveyed at 1 m below crest and away from the wall
- A siphon or base outlet pipe fitted with baffle plates placed to draw off water
- A broad spillway should make the dam stable and safe forever

### Efficiency
The efficiency in capacity of dams depends on the flatness of the area behind the wall. The key to efficiency is the length of the dam wall, compared with the "length" of water dammed. If the back-up is greater than wall length, this is a measure of increasing efficiency. Slopes of 7-9 degrees are about the steepest practical cheap storages.

## Biological Enhancement

Small dams of this nature are a jewel in the landscape. Fenced and planted to 30-60 m of forest and fruit surround, they will provide biologically clean, if sometimes muddy, water. For water cleanliness and parasite control, cattle, sheep, and other animals should be watered at spigots or troughs, not directly at the dam. Troughs are easily treated with a few crystals of copper sulphate to kill snails and parasitic hosts; dams stocked with fish will do the same job.

Crests can be gravelled and safely used as roads to cross valleys or bogs, and special deep areas, islands, peninsulas, and shelves or benches made inside the dam for birds, plants, and wild-fire-immune houses.

## Sealing Leaky Dams

### Gley
A layer of mashed, wet, green, sappy plant material sealed off from air. Cattle manure is preferred, but shredded sappy vegetation will also work. Carefully laid as a continuous 15-23 cm (6-9 inch) layer over the base and gently sloping sides (ratio of 1:4) of a pond, covered completely with earth, cardboard, thick wet paper, plastic sheets, or rolled clay. Produces a bacterial slime which permanently seals soil, sand, or small gravels.

Modifications:
- Pen and feed a herd of cattle in the dry dam until the bottom is a manurial pug
- Strew bales of green hay and manure on ponds that leak slightly
- Sow down green crop in the dry dam, spray irrigate and feed it off regularly with cattle

### Bentonite
A slippery clay-powder derived from volcanic ash. It swells when watered and will seal ponds.

### Other Methods
- Explosives (to fracture and compact)
- Clay carted to site
- Impermeable membranes

## Open vs. Closed Storage

Open-water storages are most appropriate in humid climates, where the potential for evaporation is exceeded by average annual rainfall. In arid to subhumid areas, open water storages can concentrate dissolved salts, affect animal health, and create areas of salted or collapsed soils downhill from seepage.
