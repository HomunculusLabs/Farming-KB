# Greenhouse Growing

The greenhouse is one of the most valuable assets in the organic garden, providing the space and environment to extend your gardening activities throughout the year. It serves for raising seedlings, growing tender crops, overwintering frost-sensitive plants, and producing early yields of fruit and vegetables.

## Choosing a Greenhouse

### Size

Buy the biggest greenhouse you can afford, or consider one designed to take additional sections at a later date. In practice, a greenhouse of any size will always be full. Even a tiny greenhouse can accommodate a large quantity of plants and produce, especially if used in conjunction with a [[extending-growing-season]]. If you cannot heat it all, a large greenhouse can easily be divided.

### Shape

- **Traditional "barn" shape**: Four vertical sides with a span roof — the most popular and practical design, making the best use of space
- **Circular/lantern**: Six, nine, or twelve sides — attractive but limited working space inside
- **Sunrooms/lean-to**: Attached to the house — convenient for heating and care, but reduced light from three sides only

### Materials

- **Aluminium frames**: Let in more light (thinner glazing bars); need no painting
- **Wooden frames**: Slightly cheaper to heat (wood is warmer), more attractive, but need regular painting (except cedar). Never use creosote — its fumes are toxic to plants
- **Glass**: The most common glazing material
- **Plastic sheeting**: Considerably cheaper but must be replaced every couple of years. Plastic greenhouses are reserved for crops needing no supplementary heating

### Ventilation

Adequate ventilation is vital. A 6 x 8ft (2 x 2.5m) greenhouse should have at least two roof vents. Plastic greenhouses need permanent ventilation — an open-mesh panel in the door for smaller structures, or a mesh "skirt" along the base for larger ones.

## Heating

### Temperature Control

The important statistics are the minimum night temperature and the maximum day temperature. The variation between the two should be no more than about 50F (10C). A maximum/minimum thermometer records daily extremes.

### Methods of Heating

- **Electricity**: One of the cheapest options and certainly the most convenient. Temperature control is very accurate. Professional installation of permanent systems is essential.
- **Gas**: Convenient, controlled by thermostat. Requires a fairly large capital outlay. Can emit fumes.
- **Solid fuel**: Cheap but requires considerable capital outlay and frequent refueling.
- **Oil/paraffin**: Lower cost but thermostat control is difficult; heaters can emit fumes.

### Reducing Heating Costs

- Locate the greenhouse in a bright, sheltered position for maximum free solar heat
- Insulate with bubble-wrap to cut heat loss by 40-50%
- Use a thermal screen drawn at night and pulled back during the day
- Divide the greenhouse with a plastic curtain to heat only a small area
- Use a propagator to restrict heated space to seed trays only
- Install a hot bed using fresh straw horse manure as organic bottom heat

## Growing in the Greenhouse

### Potting Composts

Many organic gardeners prefer soil-based composts, which retain moisture longer and contain trace elements. Peat-free alternatives include green compost mixed with peat (50:50) plus Dolomite lime, or worm-worked compost mixed with leaf mold and vermiculite. Pelleted chicken manure with liquid kelp feed works well as a nutrient source.

### Greenhouse Borders

If the soil is well prepared with organic matter, greenhouse borders can be very productive. To avoid soil-borne disease build-up, avoid growing the same crop year after year. Replace border soil periodically or use growing bags for vulnerable crops like tomatoes.

### Feeding

Greenhouse plants must be fed regularly — compost nutrients are used up within about eight weeks due to reduced soil life and constant watering. Liquid kelp contains the full range of nutrients needed and is ideal for most purposes. Heavy-cropping plants like tomatoes and cucumbers need feeding every time you water at the height of the season.

### Watering

- Install a hose that reaches inside the greenhouse
- Use warm water for seedlings — keep a tank inside and top it off after each watering
- Water in the morning, not later in the day
- Delicate seedlings need a fine mist spray
- Automatic systems: capillary matting or trickle irrigation are efficient and simple

### Shading

In summer, the greenhouse must be shaded to prevent sun-scorching and excessive temperatures. Roller blinds are the best method. A cheaper alternative is commercial shading paint applied to the exterior glass, which wipes off when no longer needed.

### Humidity

Most greenhouse plants require a moist, humid atmosphere. This is achieved by "damping down" — wetting the paths, staging, and plant foliage in the early morning using a fine mist sprayer.

## Maintenance

- Keep the greenhouse spotlessly clean to discourage pests and diseases
- Remove all plant debris promptly
- Wash with warm, soapy water at the end of the season
- Leave open for frost to sterilize during winter
- Wash pots and trays in boiling water before reuse
- Keep glass clean for maximum light transmission

## Greenhouse Pests and Diseases

Common problems include aphids, whitefly, red spider mite, vine weevils, botrytis (gray mold), mildew, damping off, and virus diseases. Control methods include biological controls (Encarsia formosa for whitefly, Phytoseiulus for red spider mite), insecticidal soap, good ventilation, and scrupulous cleanliness.
