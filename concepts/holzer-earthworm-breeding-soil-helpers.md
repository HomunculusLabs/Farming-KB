Sepp Holzer considers earthworms among the most important helpers in any garden or farm. He calls them "nature's ploughs" and emphasizes their role in soil aeration, drainage, nutrient cycling, and [[sepp-holzer-composting-and-humus-building]] production.

## Three Key Earthworm Species

### Brandling Worm (Eisenia foetida)
- Dark red colour with distinctive yellow bands
- Epigeal (lives on the soil surface)
- Processes large amounts of organic material
- Produces the best [[sepp-holzer-composting-and-humus-building]]
- The primary composting worm

### Common Earthworm (Lumbricus terrestris)
- The "typical" earthworm that most people recognize
- No distinctive banding
- Creates permanent burrows up to 3 metres deep
- Searches for food at depth
- Creates an ingenious drainage system with its tunnels

### Red Earthworm (Lumbricus rubellus)
- No distinctive banding
- Spends youth on the soil surface
- Later burrows into deeper soil layers
- Aerates soil at multiple depths

## How Earthworms Help

### Aeration
All three species complement each other. Surface dwellers process organic matter while deep-burrowing species aerate the soil at depth, creating channels for air and water movement.

### Drainage
The tunnel systems of common and red earthworms function as natural drainage. Soil retains more moisture, dries out less quickly, and is protected from surface erosion.

### Nutrient Cycling
Earthworm casts contain much more nitrogen, potassium, phosphorus, and calcium than the best garden soil. Their crumbly consistency provides excellent soil structure, leading to better plant development and greater disease resistance.

### Root Access
Plant roots can extend through earthworm tunnels more easily, reaching deeper soil layers for nutrients and water.

## Breeding Earthworms

### Small-Scale Setup
A wooden box with 1 cubic metre capacity is sufficient for home breeding.

### Substrate
- Mixture of straw, cardboard, soil, and a little dung
- Natural fabrics (cotton, hemp) can also be used
- The soil should be loose and well aerated
- Incorporate layers of branches, leaves, and roots for structure

### Feeding
- Any cooking waste can be used as food
- Exception: do not feed onions or garlic (worms seem to dislike them)
- Worms particularly like used coffee filters complete with coffee grounds
- Provide regular supply of organic matter
- Adjust food amount to worm population size
- Optimal rate: worms break down food as fast as new food accumulates
- This prevents harmful mould build-up

### Environmental Conditions
- Room temperature is ideal
- Steady balance of moisture required
- Good oxygen supply essential
- Drill holes in bottom of worm box to prevent water buildup
- Soil should be neither completely dry nor completely wet
- Too much water makes worms pale

### Monitoring
- Observe worms regularly
- You will quickly recognize whether they are comfortable
- Intuition is an important factor in creating optimal conditions
- If the pile seems too dry when feeding, fresh waste will moisten it again

## Large-Scale In-Ground Breeding

In his greenhouses, Holzer no longer breeds earthworms in boxes but directly in the soil:

1. Use the standard soil substrate (straw, cardboard, soil, dung)
2. Cover with earth
3. Add worms to the pile
4. Create a shallow depression in the middle
5. Put fresh organic waste in the depression daily
6. Cover with a few handfuls of soil
7. If well ventilated, feeding every 2-3 days is sufficient
8. Can be left unattended over weekends

## Deploying Worms

When the weather is wet:
1. Place bred earthworms along with some soil and worm eggs in a bucket
2. Scatter them over new [[holzer-terrace-construction-guide]] and [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] in the evening
3. The evening timing protects worms from UV rays during establishment

## Using Worm Humus

The nutrient-rich, fine crumbly worm humus from breeding boxes is used for:
- Especially valuable and demanding plants
- Balcony flowers
- Seed starting
- Top dressing for established plants

## Creating Earthworm Habitat

To attract and retain earthworms in your garden:

- Maintain permanent soil cover (earthworms are sensitive to UV rays)
- Use [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] with mixed crops
- Avoid harvesting large areas all at once
- Apply [[comparison-sheet-mulching-vs-chop-and-drop]] to attract earthworms
- Keep soil moist but not waterlogged
- Avoid chemical inputs (pesticides, herbicides, synthetic fertilizers)
- Provide diverse organic matter inputs

## The Ecological Lesson

Holzer notes that breeding earthworms teaches observation skills and empathy for other creatures. Your ecological understanding improves through the practice of caring for these soil helpers. This attentiveness transfers to all aspects of farm and garden management.

## Indicators of Soil Health

Very few earthworms in a garden indicates poor soil health. If this is the case, breeding and introducing earthworms should be a priority. Their presence is one of the most reliable indicators of [[soil-compaction-solutions-ingham]] with good structure, organic matter content, and biological activity.
