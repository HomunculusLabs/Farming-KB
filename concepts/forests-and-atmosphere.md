# Forests and the Atmosphere

Forests are among the most powerful regulators of atmospheric conditions on Earth. Their influence extends far beyond their physical boundaries, affecting wind patterns, rainfall distribution, humidity, air quality, temperature, and the global water cycle. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for permaculture design, as removing forests triggers cascading environmental collapse.

## Wind Interactions

### Wind Absorption
Wind completely disappears within an effective forest within approximately 1,000 meters. The forest absorbs the total force of even gale-force winds, except at the crown where some effect persists. The energy of the wind is converted within the forest — likely into cell production, transpiration pumping, or increased stem diameter. This has been observed empirically: anchored trees maintain constant stem diameter while wind-exposed trees rapidly increase theirs.

### Rainfall Enhancement
The forest forces 60% of incoming wind upward, creating:
- **High pressure** on the windward side with decreased evaporation
- **15–20% increased rainfall** on the windward face (measured in Holland and Sweden)
- **Sidewise spiraling** that creates belts of rain transverse to wind direction, producing alternating wet-dry patterns at intervals of about five tree lengths past the forest edge
- **Warmer, drier, turbulent descending winds** on the leeward side, causing drying

When forests are cut, regional rainfall decreases by 10–30%. This is not merely theoretical — it has been measured and documented across multiple continents.

### Windbreak Design
- **Effective width**: About five trees deep, though even a single belt at 40% penetrability has measurable effect
- **Diminishment**: At 40% penetration, wind drops rapidly within 100–200 meters and becomes negligible
- **Hedge rows**: Should reach to the ground or start above a stone wall; trimming may create undesirable wind tunnels below the canopy
- **Critical importance**: Clearing forests without replanting close-in windbreaks cuts productivity on site through wind stress

## Humidity and Fog Interception

### Positive Interception
On foggy nights with humid air moving into a forest, the forest reduces air humidity by approximately 50% within 100 meters through condensation on leaf surfaces. This is called **positive interception** and is a major factor in coastal forests and ridge forests within fifty miles of the coast.

The mechanism: warm humid air contacts cool leaf surfaces (leaves lose heat through radiation at night), and moisture precipitates onto the myriad leaf surfaces. In Tasmania, up to 60% of total precipitation comes from this effect, with only 14% falling as rain — trees catch the remaining 86%.

### Practical Implications
- Individual gardens near forests experience constant nighttime dripping even with no cloud cover
- Screens that imitate trees create high precipitation — demonstrated with artificial tree screens
- Cutting trees removes 86% of moisture capture in affected coastal forests
- A modest line of trees on a desert edge causes local rainfall through transpiration-reprecipitation cycles

## Particle and Nuclei Dynamics

### Inorganic Particle Capture
Within 100 meters of forest edge, windborne particles — dust, soil, industrial pollutants — are reduced to about one-quarter of their previous concentration. Forests entrap enormous tonnages of material blown off seacoasts and across soils. This leads to the conclusion that there is no absolute shortage of any mineral anywhere on Earth — everything is on the move, being netted by forests.

### Organic Particle Release
Forests release massive quantities of organic particles — pollens, bacteria, oil droplets — that serve as far more effective precipitation nuclei than inorganic particles. Early voyagers approaching North America reported pollen coating ship decks 80 miles offshore, turning the sea yellow and being mistaken for volcanic sulfur.

### Net Effect on Rainfall
About 60% of inland rain falls from forest-generated clouds, not sea clouds. While forests absorb inorganic particles, they release organic nuclei that travel downwind and promote condensation and rainfall far inland. Forests are net producers of the conditions that generate precipitation.

## Water Storage and Movement

### The Forest as a Water Mass
Eighty-six percent of a forest's mass is water. Ninety-six percent of leaves and twigs are water. This represents an enormous weight on the Earth's surface — effectively, forests are vast vertical [[water-storage-tanks]].

### Rainfall Interception by Canopy
- **Light rain**: The canopy may absorb 100% of rainfall — roads under trees stay dry in light rain
- **Heavy rain**: The canopy becomes saturated, then water descends via multiple pathways — bark channels (10–20× rainfall concentration around stems), crown drip, branch runoff
- **Snow**: Each tree intercepts snow differently based on crystalline structure and branch architecture; the forest delays snowmelt by at least a month compared to open ground

### Ground-Level Water Dynamics
- **Litter and humus layer**: Absorbs water until fully charged — may hold 6 inches to a foot of water before saturation
- **Mineral soil**: Every foot holds approximately 1 inch of rain; 30 inches of soil absorbs a 3-inch rainfall with zero runoff
- **Root channels**: Old rotted root traces bore through soil, creating conduits for deep percolation
- **No significant evaporation** occurs from below-canopy water — roots draw water downward, not upward to the soil surface
- **No salting**: Since water never migrates upward through evaporation, salts do not accumulate at the soil surface

### Runoff Characteristics
Forest floor runoff is extremely slow due to accumulated twigs, leaves, fallen logs, and debris that repeatedly halt and divert water. Water persists far longer in forested landscapes compared to open ground where it "goes whistling" into rivers.

### Mineral Retention
Forests collect, hold, and cycle minerals — particularly calcium. When forests are cut, these minerals flush into streams as dissolved salts. Tasmania measured more tonnage of essential material (particularly calcium) running off cut-over forest land as dissolved salts than as silts.

## Temperature and Energy Transactions

### Canopy Energy Absorption
The forest canopy absorbs nearly all the mechanical energy of rainfall, even from violent thunderstorms. Rain never reaches the ground as pelting force — it arrives as fine mist. This prevents erosion and creates a profound cooling effect as water evaporates from the crown.

### Heat Storage
Forests function as heat storage systems. On bitter nights in Tasmania, cold air entering the upper canopy creates a slow warm down-draft through thousands of water-filled storage tanks (trees) that absorbed heat all day. Some tree species are deliberate radiators; birches are reflectors. The quality of light within forests changes — UV is totally intercepted while more red light passes through.

### Air Quality
- **Negative ions** increase sharply in air passing through forests, causing particle clumping and precipitation
- **Carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide** are absorbed efficiently by trees — even a small plant in an office handles CO₂ problems
- **Obnoxious gases** are filtered effectively

## Forests and Geological Stability

Forests represent enormous water masses. Loading and unloading this mass through deforestation can trigger geological movements. Modest dams are known to cause local earthquakes; removing forests unloads equally massive water weight from continents. This may be a contributing factor to continental fracture and seismic activity that has never been adequately acknowledged or measured.

## The Critical Conclusion

Bill Mollison states with certainty: **By the removal of ridge forests alone, we can produce deserts in any climate. By the removal of forests alone, we can remove soils.** The removal of forests has been the main cause of the collapse of nations. When forests go, there is insufficient water, soil, or climate quality to sustain human life. The imperative is not to learn to live without forests, but to learn to live with them.
