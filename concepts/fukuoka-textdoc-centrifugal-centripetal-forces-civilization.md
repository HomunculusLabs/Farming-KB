## Mahayana and Hinayana as Metaphors

Fukuoka draws on Buddhist terminology to illustrate these two
approaches. The Hinayana path corresponds to the centrifugal — a
rigorous, analytical discipline that seeks perfection through
accumulation of knowledge and technique. The Mahayana path corresponds
to the centripetal — a broader, more compassionate approach that seeks
enlightenment through letting go of attachments, including the
attachment to knowledge itself.

In farming, the Hinayana approach is the scientist who believes that
by understanding every component of the system in isolation, he can
construct a perfect agriculture. The Mahayana approach is the natural
farmer who recognizes that perfection is the original condition of
nature and that human effort should aim not at construction but at
the removal of interference.

## The One-Straw Revolution as Centripetal Practice

Fukuoka's own farming method is the practical expression of centripetal
philosophy. Rather than adding anything to the land, he removes
interventions one by one: first plowing, then fertilizing, then
weeding, then applying pesticides. Each removal brings the farm closer
to its natural state. The method is sometimes called "do-nothing
farming," but this is misleading. The farmer is intensely active in
observation and response — he simply does not impose his will upon the
land through mechanical or chemical means.
