# A Natural Orchard with Ground Cover

From "The Natural Way of Farming: The Theory" by Masanobu Fukuoka.

## Building a Three-Dimensional Orchard

To create a natural orchard, one must observe the principle of the right crop for the right land. Avoid the monoculture of fruit trees. Plant deciduous fruit trees together with evergreen fruit trees and never forget to interplant green manure trees -- acacias which produce nitrogenous fertilizer, myrtle which produces phosphoric acid and potash, alder, and podocarpus.

This polycultural approach creates multiple canopy layers that intercept light at different heights, maximizing photosynthetic efficiency. The upper canopy of tall nitrogen-fixing trees provides dappled shade that protects understory fruit trees from excessive sun exposure while still allowing sufficient light for fruit production. The [[permaculture-multipurpose-trees]] in permaculture design draws directly from this principle, where each tree species fulfills multiple functions: nitrogen fixation, mulch production, wind protection, pollinator attraction, and fruit or nut yield.

Leguminous green manure plants and other herbs that enrich the orchard soil may be planted as orchard undergrowth. Forage crops and semiwild vegetables can also be grown in abundance, and both poultry and livestock allowed to graze freely in the orchard.

A natural orchard in which full, three-dimensional use of space is made is entirely different from conventional orchards that employ high-production techniques.

## Building Up Orchard Earth Without Fertilizers

Soil management must promote the conversion of weathered material from bedrock and stone into living, organic soil. Unfortunately, soil management as normally practiced consists basically of clean cultivation that turns soil into mere mineral matter. The soil in many orchards has become depleted with constant plowing and weeding.

Soil management divorced from the field, garden, and hillside is meaningless; only a method that enriches all at the same time makes any sense. In order to make full use of the soil, soil management must be based on the use of a ground cover. The decomposition|decomposition process of ground cover residues builds soil organic matter incrementally, creating a self-renewing fertility cycle that eliminates the need for imported amendments.

## Ladino Clover as Primary Cover

Fukuoka planted the seeds of thirty legumes, crucifers, and grasses throughout his orchard and concluded that he should grow a weed cover using ladino clover as the primary crop and herbs such as alfalfa, lupine, and bur clover as secondary crops. Key features of ladino clover:

1. When used as a cover crop, this eliminates weeds. Annual weeds are displaced in one year, biennials in two years. After 2-3 years, almost all garden weeds have vanished.
2. Improves soil down to a depth of 16 to 18 inches.
3. Seed does not have to be sown again for another 6 to 8 years.
4. Does not compete strongly with fruit trees for fertilizer or moisture.
5. Grows back easily after being cut and remains healthy even when trampled.
6. Does not hinder farming operations.

The only disadvantages are susceptibility to summer-killing and sclerotium disease during hot, dry weather, and retarded growth in shade. To mitigate summer-killing, Fukuoka maintained a diverse secondary ground cover including bur clover and alfalfa that persisted through the hottest months. For shade tolerance, he selected planting positions beneath fruit tree canopies where light conditions favored the most shade-tolerant clover varieties.

Fukuoka's approach to ground cover management represents a fundamental departure from conventional orchard floor management in temperate fruit production. Where conventional orchards maintain bare soil or mowed grass strips between tree rows, natural farming orchards develop a complex, self-regulating herbaceous layer that provides multiple ecosystem services simultaneously: soil protection, nutrient cycling, weed suppression, moisture conservation, and habitat for beneficial organisms.

## Key Points

1. Central to Fukuoka Natural Orchard Ground Cover is the idea that nature already possesses perfect wisdom and human intervention often disrupts natural balance.
2. Fukuoka's approach emphasizes observation over action — understanding natural patterns before making any changes to the landscape.
3. The principle of 'doing nothing' (mu) means eliminating unnecessary human labor, not abandoning the land entirely.
4. Natural farming seeks to create conditions where crops, weeds, insects, and microorganisms coexist in a self-regulating ecosystem.
5. Soil health is the foundation — building rich, living soil through natural mulching and decomposition rather than imported fertilizers.
6. Seed balls (tsuchi dango) allow natural dispersal and germination without tilling or transplanting.
7. The interplay between ground cover plants, crops, and trees creates a multi-layered productive system.
8. Success in natural farming comes from long-term patience and trust in ecological processes rather than quick technological fixes.
9. Fukuoka demonstrated that yields comparable to or exceeding conventional methods are achievable through natural farming practices.
10. The philosophy extends beyond agriculture to encompass a broader relationship between humans and the natural world.

## Practical Applications

- Start with a small area and observe natural vegetation patterns before introducing any cultivation.
- Use straw mulch and living ground covers (such as white clover) to suppress weeds and build soil organic matter.
- Introduce seed balls in early spring or fall to establish crops without tilling the soil.
- Allow beneficial weeds to grow alongside crops -- they often serve important ecological functions.
- Rotate between rice in summer and winter grains (barley, wheat) using the same flooded field without tilling.
- Create a diverse orchard with ground cover plants that fix nitrogen, attract pollinators, and suppress unwanted vegetation.
- Apply a thin layer of chicken manure or compost only if soil fertility is visibly deficient.
- Harvest at natural maturity rather than forcing early or late harvests with chemical inputs.

Fukuoka's orchard methods have been successfully adapted by practitioners around the world using locally available ground cover species. In Mediterranean climates, subterranean clover and annual medics replace ladino clover as the primary cover crop. In tropical regions, species such as peanut (Arachis pintoi) and centrosema serve similar functions. The [[fukuoka-natural-alfalfa-black-wattle-ground-cover]] combinations developed by Fukuoka provide a template for designing locally appropriate ground cover polycultures.

## Managing Clover Decline in Summer

During hot, dry periods, ladino clover can experience summer-killing, particularly when grown beneath fruit trees that cast significant shade. Fukuoka addressed this vulnerability by maintaining a secondary layer of herbaceous plants that could fill gaps when clover receded. Alfalfa (lucerne), with its deep taproot system reaching several feet into the subsoil, remained green and productive even during drought conditions, accessing moisture and minerals unavailable to shallow-rooted species. Bur clover (Medicago hispida) similarly persisted through adverse conditions and self-seeded aggressively, ensuring continuous ground coverage.

The strategy of combining multiple ground cover species with different growth habits, root architectures, and drought tolerances creates functional redundancy. Where one species falters due to pest pressure, disease, or weather stress, others expand to occupy the vacant niche. This polycultural approach to ground cover management mirrors the [[comparison-food-forest-vs-orchard]] model used in permaculture-design|permaculture, where multiple layers and species interact to create resilient, self-maintaining systems. The [[apple-centered-guild-design]] approach in temperate permaculture draws directly on Fukuoka's orchard ground cover principles, integrating comfrey, daffodils, and alliums beneath fruit trees in combinations that suppress weeds, attract beneficial insects, and cycle nutrients from deep soil layers.

## Nitrogen Fixation and Soil Building Dynamics

The leguminous ground covers in Fukuoka's orchard system served a critical nitrogen-fixing function. Plants in the Fabaceae family form symbiotic associations with Rhizobium bacteria housed in root nodules, converting atmospheric nitrogen (N2) into plant-available ammonium (NH4+). In a mature orchard ground cover system, this biological nitrogen fixation can contribute 50 to 200 kg of nitrogen per hectare annually, substantially reducing or eliminating the need for external nitrogen inputs.

Beyond nitrogen, the ground cover plants contributed to soil building through their root exudates and eventual decomposition. Root exudates — sugars, amino acids, and organic acids released by living roots — serve as the primary energy source for soil microorganisms, including the [[mycorrhizal-helper-bacteria-mycorrhizosphere-interactions]] that associate with fruit tree roots. Research has shown that root exudates can constitute 10 to 40 percent of total photosynthate in some plant species, representing a massive belowground carbon investment that fuels the soil food web. When ground cover plants die back seasonally, their decomposing residues add organic matter to the soil, improving water-holding capacity, cation exchange capacity, and aggregate stability. Over years of continuous ground cover management, the orchard soil develops a rich humus layer that supports abundant [[nutrient-retention-soil-food-web]] activity.

## Integration with Livestock and Poultry

Fukuoka's orchard design incorporated free-range poultry and livestock as integral components of the ground cover management system. Chickens allowed to range through the orchard scratched through the clover and herb layer, consuming insects and weed seeds while depositing manure that further enriched the soil. Their scratching activity lightly disturbed the soil surface without uprooting the ground cover plants, providing a natural alternative to mechanical cultivation.

Ducks served a similar function in orchard areas with lower ground cover, consuming slugs and snails that might otherwise damage fruit. The integration of livestock with orchard ground covers represents an early example of what [[holzer-livestock-philosophy-pig-breeds]] and other permaculturalists would later formalize as integrated animal-plant systems. The key principle is that each animal performs multiple functions within the system — pest control, nutrient cycling, soil aeration, and ground cover management — eliminating the need for separate mechanical or chemical interventions. The [[query-how-to-design-a-chicken-tractor-system]] concept popularized in permaculture literature extends this principle by making poultry mobility systematic and controllable.

## Comparison with Conventional Orchard Floor Management

Conventional orchard management typically relies on herbicide strips beneath tree rows, combined with mowed grass alleys between rows. This approach aims to reduce competition between ground vegetation and fruit trees for water and nutrients. However, research has shown that well-managed living ground covers can actually improve tree performance by enhancing soil biological activity, increasing mycorrhizal colonization of tree roots, and moderating soil temperature extremes during summer and winter.

Fukuoka's approach contrasts sharply with conventional methods in its rejection of the competition paradigm entirely. Rather than viewing ground cover plants as competitors to be eliminated, natural farming treats them as collaborators in soil building and ecosystem maintenance. The clover and herb layer shades the soil, reducing evaporative water loss and maintaining root-zone moisture. The continuous living root systems of the ground cover prevent soil erosion on sloping orchard sites and create channels for water infiltration during heavy rains. Research from temperate orchard systems has confirmed that living mulches can reduce soil temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius during summer heat events, protecting shallow feeder roots of fruit trees from heat stress. This philosophy of cooperation over competition extends throughout Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-philosophy]] and aligns closely with the [[holmgren-permaculture-ethics-foundations]] developed by David Holmgren.

The [[fukuoka-natural-cover-grass-green-manure]] work demonstrates that his orchard methods were part of a comprehensive approach to soil management applicable across diverse farming contexts, from grain production to vegetable growing to tree crops.
