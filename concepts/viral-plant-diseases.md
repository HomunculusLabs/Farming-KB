# Viral Plant Diseases

Plant viruses are submicroscopic infectious agents that replicate only inside living host cells. They cannot be controlled with chemical sprays -- there are no curative treatments once a plant is infected. Management relies entirely on prevention through sanitation, vector control, and resistant varieties. Viruses are typically spread by insect vectors (especially aphids, thrips, and whiteflies), contaminated tools, seed, or mechanical contact.

## Major Viral Diseases

### Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV)

One of the most studied and widespread plant viruses. TMV is extremely stable -- it can survive in dried plant debris for years and is easily transmitted by touch. It has an exceptionally broad host range including tobacco, tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, cucumbers, and many ornamentals.

Symptoms:
- Mosaic pattern of light and dark green patches on leaves
- Leaf curling, distortion, and mottling
- Stunted growth
- Yellow-green mottling on fruit (tomatoes)
- Reduced yield and fruit quality

TMV is mechanically transmitted -- touching an infected plant then a healthy one can spread it. It survives in tobacco products, so smokers can transmit it to plants via contaminated hands.

Management:
- Wash hands thoroughly with soap before handling plants
- Do not smoke or use tobacco products near susceptible crops
- Disinfect tools with 10% bleach or trisodium phosphate
- Remove and destroy infected plants immediately
- Use TMV-resistant tomato varieties (many carry the Tm-2 gene)
- Do not save seed from infected plants
- Rotate crops -- remove all solanaceous debris

### Cucumber Mosaic Virus (CMV)

Named for its initial identification in cucumbers, CMV actually infects over 1,200 plant species including tomatoes, peppers, spinach, celery, lettuce, beans, and many ornamentals. It is one of the most economically important plant viruses worldwide.

Symptoms:
- Severe leaf mosaic and mottling
- Leaf shoestring appearance (narrow, distorted leaves)
- Stunted growth
- Ring spots or line patterns on leaves
- Fruit distortion and reduced set
- Bunchy top growth

Transmission: Primarily by aphids (over 60 aphid species) in a non-persistent manner -- aphids acquire and transmit the virus in seconds while probing. Also transmitted mechanically and by seed in some hosts.

Management:
- Control aphid populations early in the season
- Remove weed reservoirs ([[chickweed]], pokeweed, milkweed are common hosts)
- Use reflective mulches to deter aphid landing
- Plant CMV-resistant varieties when available
- Remove infected plants immediately to reduce source
- Eliminate perennial weeds near garden edges
- Use floating row covers to protect young plants from aphids

### Potato Virus Y (PVY)

One of the most damaging viruses affecting solanaceous crops, especially potatoes and peppers. PVY exists in multiple strains (ordinary, necrotic, and recombinant strains) with varying symptom severity. Can cause significant yield loss (up to 80%) in potatoes.

Symptoms in potatoes:
- Mild to severe mosaic patterns on leaves
- Leaf necrosis (dead tissue) in severe strains
- Crinkling and distortion of leaf margins
- Stunting
- Necrotic rings on tubers

Symptoms in peppers:
- Mosaic and mottling on leaves
- Leaf distortion and shoestringing
- Stunted growth
- Fruit necrosis and mosaic patterns
- Severe yield reduction

Transmission: Primarily by aphids (especially green peach aphid, Myzus persicae) in a non-persistent manner. Also spread by infected seed tubers and mechanically.

Management:
- Plant certified virus-free seed potatoes
- Use PVY-resistant varieties (many available for both potatoes and peppers)
- Rogue infected plants early
- Manage aphid populations (though complete control is impractical)
- Isolate potato and pepper plantings from each other
- Remove volunteer potatoes from previous season
- Avoid mechanical transmission during horticultural operations

## General Virus Prevention Strategies

Since viruses cannot be cured once plants are infected, all management is preventive:

### Sanitation
- Disinfect tools, stakes, and equipment between plants and between seasons
- Remove and destroy (burn or bag, not [[compost]]) infected plants immediately
- Clean greenhouse benches and surfaces thoroughly
- Wash hands before and between handling plants

### Resistant Varieties
- The single most effective management tool
- Check seed catalogs for virus resistance codes (TMV, CMV, PVY, etc.)
- Resistance is often virus-specific -- a TMV-resistant variety may still be susceptible to CMV

### Vector Control
- Monitor aphid, thrips, and whitefly populations with yellow sticky traps
- Apply insecticidal soap or neem oil at first sign of vector activity
- Use floating row covers on young, susceptible plants
- Encourage natural enemies (ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps)
- Remove weed hosts that harbor both viruses and vectors

### Cultural Practices
- Rotate crops by family
- Remove volunteer plants and weed reservoirs
- Avoid working in wet conditions (reduces mechanical spread)
- Do not save seed from infected plants
- Use reflective mulches to deter aphid vectors
- Time plantings to avoid peak vector populations when possible
