# Permaculture Site Analysis (Mollison)
**Source:** Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison (1988)
**Updated:** 2026-05-09

## Overview
Site analysis is the essential first step in any permaculture design.
Mollison dedicates substantial portions of the Designers' Manual to
methods for reading a landscape before modifying it. Thorough analysis
reveals inherent potentials and limitations of land, guiding designers
toward interventions that work with natural forces rather than against
them. Skipping this step leads to designs requiring constant energy
input to maintain.

## The Site Analysis Process
Mollison outlines a systematic approach: first, map what exists
(topography, water, soils, vegetation, climate); second, identify
forces acting on the site (sun, wind, fire, frost, flood); third,
assess existing resources and infrastructure; fourth, note constraints
and hazards. This feeds directly into zone and sector planning, element
placement, and species selection.

## Climate Analysis
Climate sets the outer boundaries of what is possible. Mollison directs
designers to collect data on temperature extremes, growing season
length, rainfall distribution, humidity, frost dates, and prevailing
wind. Local microclimate data is especially valuable -- a cold air
drainage pocket or heat-reflecting wall can shift effective climate by
a full hardiness zone. [[permaculture-pattern-understanding-design-process]] helps designers identify these microclimatic variations.

## Topography and Slope Analysis
Contour maps reveal water movement, erosion risk, solar access, and
cold air drainage. Mollison emphasises working with slope: placing dams
in valleys, swales on contour, roads on ridges, and buildings on convex
rises where air drainage prevents frost. Keyline design (P.A. Yeomans)
is a central tool for organising water and soil on sloping land. Slope
analysis also determines machinery access, terracing needs, and
gravity-fed water system placement.

## Water Resources
Mapping all water features -- springs, streams, wetlands, seasonal
flows, and groundwater -- is critical. Mollison classifies water
resources by reliability and quality. Permanent springs are the highest-
value source, followed by perennial streams, dams, and roof catchment.
The designer calculates total yield, seasonal variation, and
distribution costs. Water design follows the principle of capturing
water at the highest possible elevation and using gravity to distribute
it, eliminating pumping energy where possible.

## Soil Assessment
Mollison advocates hands-on soil examination: texture by feel, structure
by digging a soil pit, depth to hardpan or bedrock, pH, and organic
matter content. Key factors include infiltration rate (affects [[swale]]
spacing), water-holding capacity (irrigation needs), erosion
susceptibility (guides earthworks), and biological activity (regenerative
potential). Soil maps from agricultural agencies provide regional context
but must be verified on-site.

## Vegetation and Existing Ecosystems
Existing vegetation reveals site history, soil conditions, microclimate,
and water availability. Pioneer species indicate recent disturbance;
climax species indicate stability. Weed composition can signal soil pH,
compaction, or nutrient excess. Mollison advises preserving productive
ecosystems where possible and using native vegetation as a template for
productive [[query-what-is-polyculture-and-how-does-it-differ-from-monoculture]]s. Existing trees, hedgerows, and water
bodies become design anchors.

## Sector Analysis
Sectors are directional energies crossing a site regardless of internal
boundaries. Mollison identifies sun sectors (summer and winter angles),
wind sectors (prevailing and storm), fire risk, noise, pollution
sources, and wildlife corridors. The designer maps each sector and plans
responses: windbreaks for cold winds, fire-resistant plantings for fire
sectors, shelterbelts for dust and noise. Sector analysis ensures the
design buffers harmful energies while capturing beneficial ones.

## Infrastructure and Mapping
Existing roads, fences, buildings, and water systems shape what is
practical to change. Mollison recommends producing a base map (1:500 to
1:5000) with transparent overlays for each analysis layer: topography,
water, soils, vegetation, sectors, and zones. These overlays reveal
interactions between factors and allow testing of alternative placements
before committing to earthworks or plantings.
