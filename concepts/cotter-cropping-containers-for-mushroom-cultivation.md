# Cropping Containers for Mushroom Cultivation

Mushrooms can be grown in virtually any container, provided the grower understands
the "mushroom math" of substrate volume versus exposed surface area. The right
container choice minimizes environmental impact, maximizes yield, and makes the
best use of available fruiting space. This page covers plastic bags, columns,
nursery pots, buckets, wooden trays, and everyday scavenged items.

## Surface Area and Gas Exchange

The total volume of substrate versus the surface area exposed to air is a
critical factor for mushroom formation and yields. If there is not enough
substrate volume relative to exposed surface area, mushroom beds will abort.
Mushrooms may initiate but will quickly dry up and never mature. Conversely,
too little exposed area prevents gas exchange, creating anaerobic dead zones
inside the biomass.

Consider a hypothetical series of bags, each packed with identical substrate
and capable of producing 10 pounds of mushrooms, but with different numbers of
holes punched. One hole produces one massive cluster weighing far less than 10
pounds due to limited gas exchange (approximately 40 percent of substrate
remains uncolonized). Four evenly spaced holes produce four clusters averaging
2.5 pounds each for the full 10-pound yield, which is the recommended target
size for selling oyster mushroom clusters. Fifty holes cause overstimulation,
with clusters competing for nutrients and 10 to 30 percent yield loss from
aborted primordia.

## Photosensitivity and Container Opacity

Light affects mushroom initiation timing and location. Using clear plastic bags
can be disastrous since mushrooms form wherever light conditions are right rather
than where gas conditions are right (the ventilation holes). Small mushrooms
form all over the biomass under clear plastic, wasting resources on trapped pins
that cannot mature. If this happens, slip a dark bag tightly over the clear one,
poke holes through both, and switch to opaque containers for future batches.

Opaque containers are preferred for production. A few smaller transparent "spy
bags" can be used for tracking contamination and spawn run progress in larger
batches. In opaque containers, mushrooms naturally form near ventilation holes
where oxygen is available, placing them in the perfect position to escape and
mature into healthy clusters.

## Plastic Bags

Plastic bags are widely available, easy to use, and come in many sizes. Bag
thickness is critical: thin-mil plastic stretches easily and allows tight packing
of spawned substrate. If tied while compressed, bags become springy with
pressure, which is the goal. As mushrooms form and the biomass shrinks, the
plastic relaxes and maintains a tight fit, limiting air pockets and improving
yields.

For sterilized substrates such as supplemented sawdust for shiitake, lion's
mane, and maitake, use autoclavable bags with micron filter breathing patches.
These allow contaminant-free gas exchange during colonization. After fruiting,
autoclavable bags can be rinsed, dried, and reused for several sterilization
runs, provided they have never contained contaminated media or had holes poked
in them.

## Plastic Columns and Hard Columns

Polyethylene tubing (poly tubing) can be cut to length for vertical cultivation.
The diameter should be at least 8 inches and up to 16 inches; anything larger
creates an anaerobic core. Columns make excellent use of vertical space and
eliminate the need for shelving. Cut the tubing 2 feet longer than desired
length, tie one end, pack tight with substrate, and tie the other end. Hang in
the fruiting room and poke holes after moving (poking before moving risks tearing).

Hard columns made from metal or PVC culverts are reusable alternatives without
plastic waste. Predrill columns with half-inch holes staggered about 6 to 8
inches apart around the surface. Install removable end caps, fill with spawned
fruiting medium packed tightly, and arrange in frames. Drill fruiting holes
only on aisle-facing sides for easy picking. Spent columns can be emptied using
a packing device matching the column diameter.

## Nursery Pots and Buckets

Black nursery pots are excellent for mushroom production. Available for free or
at low cost from landscapers, they can be washed with a 10:1 water to bleach
solution and reused indefinitely. For vertical growers like oyster mushrooms,
stack pots three or four high with offset holes or slits in the sides. Stacked
pots inoculated with the same strain fuse into one organism, sharing water and
fruiting information. Do not separate fused pots during colonization.

For horizontal growers like Stropharia and Agaricus species, pack substrate
tightly into pots, leaving 2 inches of space at the top for casing soil. After
colonization, separate the pots, apply casing, and place individually on the
ground or shelves. Buckets work similarly: drill half-inch holes every 6 to 8
inches for vertical growers, or crack the lid slightly for horizontal growers.

## Wooden Trays and Scavenged Containers

Wooden trays of untreated conifer (rough-cut pine works well and lasts about
three years) are ideal for horizontal fruiters requiring an open top like
Agaricus and Stropharia. For every cubic inch of growing medium, allow
approximately half a square inch of exposed surface area. Beyond containers
specifically designed for mushroom cultivation, growers can use large soda
bottles, terra-cotta pots, garbage bags, large-diameter bamboo, dried gourds,
woven baskets, and livestock feed bags. Avoid clear containers and biodegradable
containers that the mycelium will consume.

## Key Points

- Surface area to volume ratio determines yield success; too much area causes
  abort, too little causes anaerobic conditions
- Four to six evenly spaced holes in a bag is optimal for 1 to 2.5 pound
  oyster clusters
- Use opaque containers to prevent photosensitivity-driven trapped pins
- Thin-mil plastic bags should be tied while compressed for springy pressure
- Poly tubing columns should be 8 to 16 inches in diameter maximum
- Autoclavable bags with filter patches are required for sterilized substrates
- Stacked nursery pots with the same strain will fuse into one organism
- Scavenged containers work well if they respect surface area to volume rules
