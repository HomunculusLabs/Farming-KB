# Foliar Nutrient Diagnosis — Visual Deficiency Identification

Foliar nutrient diagnosis is the practice of identifying plant [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]]
toxicities by observing visual symptoms on leaves, stems, and growing tips. It is a
foundational skill for agronomists, horticulturists, and serious growers, providing a
rapid first-pass assessment before confirming with laboratory tissue testing. While
visual diagnosis alone is not definitive, it guides timely corrective action.

## Mobile vs Immobile Nutrients — Symptom Location

The most important principle in foliar diagnosis is distinguishing mobile from immobile
nutrients. Mobile nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) can be
relocated within the plant from older tissues to supply new growth. Deficiencies of
these elements therefore appear first on the **older, lower leaves**, as the plant
sacrifices them to feed expanding meristems.

Immobile nutrients (calcium, boron, iron, manganese, zinc, copper) cannot be
readily translocated once deposited in tissue. Their deficiencies appear first on
the **newest growth** — young leaves, terminal buds, and root tips — because the
plant cannot cannibalize older tissues to supply them. This distinction is the
single most useful diagnostic clue in the field.

## Macronutrient Deficiencies

### Nitrogen (N)
Nitrogen deficiency causes uniform pale-green to yellow **chlorosis** across the
entire leaf, starting with older foliage. Growth is stunted and spindly, with
reduced tillering or branching. Under severe deficiency, leaves may senesce and
drop prematurely. Nitrogen is highly mobile, so symptoms progress upward as
the plant exhausts reserves in lower canopy leaves.

### Phosphorus (P)
Phosphorus-deficient plants develop dark green or purplish discoloration,
especially on leaf margins and undersides. Growth is slowed, root systems are
restricted, and maturity may be delayed. Purpling results from anthocyanin
accumulation triggered by impaired sugar metabolism. Cold soils exacerbate
[[query-how-to-fix-phosphorus-deficiency-in-cannabis]] even when soil levels are adequate, because root uptake
slows dramatically at low temperatures.

### Potassium (K)
[[knf-potassium-deficiency-and-excess]] produces characteristic **marginal scorch** — yellowing
and necrosis beginning at leaf tips and edges, progressing inward while the
midrib remains green. Stalks become weak, increasing susceptibility to lodging.
Potassium regulates stomatal function and water relations, so deficient plants
may wilt more readily under drought stress. Symptoms appear on older leaves
first due to potassium's mobility within the plant.

### Calcium (Ca)
Calcium deficiency affects new growth: distorted, cupped, or crinkled young
leaves, terminal bud dieback, and necrotic spotting near growing points. In
tomatoes and peppers, it causes **blossom-end rot** — a leathery black lesion at
the fruit apex. In lettuce, it produces **tip burn**. Calcium moves via the
transpiration stream and does not redistribute, so rapid growth or low humidity
can trigger localized deficiency even when soil calcium is sufficient.

### Magnesium (Mg)
[[query-how-to-fix-magnesium-deficiency-in-cannabis]] causes **interveinal chlorosis** — yellowing between veins
while veins remain green — on older leaves. In severe cases, chlorotic areas
may become necrotic. Magnesium is the central atom of chlorophyll, so
deficiency directly impairs photosynthesis. Acidic sandy soils and high
potassium or calcium levels can induce deficiency through competitive uptake.

## Micronutrient Deficiencies

### Iron (Fe)
Iron deficiency produces bright yellow interveinal chlorosis on the **youngest
leaves**, while veins remain green — a sharp, high-contrast pattern distinct
from magnesium deficiency. In extreme cases, new leaves appear nearly white.
Iron deficiency is common on alkaline or calcareous soils where high pH
renders iron insoluble. Excess manganese or zinc can also induce it.

### Manganese (Mn)
[[query-how-to-fix-manganese-deficiency-in-cannabis]] resembles iron deficiency — interveinal chlorosis on young
leaves — but is generally less severe and may include small necrotic spots.
It is strongly associated with high-pH soils and is common in organic soils
after liming. Corrective foliar manganese sprays provide rapid relief.
