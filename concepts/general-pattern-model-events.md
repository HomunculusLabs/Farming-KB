# General Pattern Model of Events

The general pattern model of events is [[bill-mollison]]'s unifying framework for understanding natural forms and processes. Derived from a stylised tree shape, this single model encompasses waves, streamlines, spirals, lobes, branches, scatters, nets, and the full range of natural phenomena generated by growth and flow. It serves as the foundational pattern language for [[permaculture-design]].

## The Core Model

The general core model is built around a **stylised tree form** that spirals out of a plane (P) existing between two media (M1 and M2). This deceptively simple "apple core" shape, when understood in all its projections and sections, contains each of the major natural pattern types within it.

The model is derived from real sections, paths, or projections of a tree:
- Cross-sections reveal target/ring patterns
- Longitudinal sections show branching and streamlines
- Plan views display canopy spread and root zones
- Streamline paths trace the flow of materials through the system

Around this central tree form, various cross-sections, plans, longitudinal sections, and streamline paths are arranged — all derived from real tree anatomy.

## The Concept of an Event

The evolution of the core model from an initial point in space-time is called an **event**. Events can be abstract or palpable. They share common characteristics:

- **Origin (O):** A starting point or rupture at the interface between two media or systems
- **Growth phase (T1–T6):** An expression of energy potential, expanding the initial rupture
- **Maturity:** The full expression of the pattern in space and time
- **Decay:** Reduction of energy and material flow
- **Dissolution:** Breakdown into other events of like or unlike form

A tree event is at least three-dimensional and must be thought of as extending into and out of a plane. However, many similar events — migration patterns, glaciation — can be portrayed as two-dimensional, as they appear in aerial photographs.

## The Tree as Universal Pattern Generator

The tree form serves as a general teaching model for geography, ecology, and evolution. It portrays the movement of energy and particles in time and space. The tree generates virtually every natural pattern type through its various projections:

### Waves
When a tree is viewed in cross-section, the annual growth rings form concentric waves — each ring recording a year of growth, drought, fire, or abundance. These wave patterns propagate outward from the origin, just as ripples propagate from a disturbance on water.

### Streamlines
The curvilinear streamlines (S1–S9) of the model curve or spiral through the origin, just as phloem (storage cells) and xylem (sap-bearing cells) spiral through the stem axis of a real tree. Traffic through these streamlines moves in both directions: sugars and photosynthetic products travel from crown to root margin, while water and minerals travel from roots to crown.

### Spirals
Screw palms (*Pandanus* spp.) develop ascending stem spirals reminiscent of fan turbine blades. Sunflowers create open seed spirals in two directions, common in many whorled plants. The stem itself forces open an ever-expanding flow through the plane between the media, allowing more material to pass through as time accumulates.

### Branches
The branching pattern of the tree — from trunk to major limbs to minor branches to twigs — exemplifies the universal tributary pattern. Rivers converging, blood vessels distributing, neural networks, communication systems, and explosive shatter zones all follow this same branching logic.

### Nets
The root system viewed from above, the canopy viewed from below, and the pattern of veins in leaves all display net-like structures. These arise from the intersection of multiple branching systems operating in the same space.

### Scatters
Seed distribution, clonal colonies, and the spatial arrangement of tree clumps in landscapes follow scatter patterns — clustered distributions governed by the reach of the parent event.

## Two-Media Exchange

A critical feature of the model is the concept of **two-media exchange**. The tree (and every event) exists at the boundary between two media (M1 and M2) — for a tree, earth and atmosphere. Each margin of the pattern is both collecting and distributing materials from different media:

- Roots collect water and minerals from soil (M1), distribute them to the canopy
- Leaves collect sunlight and CO₂ from air (M2), distribute sugars downward
- Active water and gaseous exchange occurs with both media continuously

This two-way trade is the normality of plants, organs, and natural forms. The stem, at the zone of the plane (P), contains cells that interweave or cross over as they spiral out of or into the media — xylem is external to stems but internal to roots, while phloem is the reverse.

## Dendrochronology: Pattern as Record

The crosscut of a tree stem reveals a **target pattern of expanding growth** — the basis of dendrochronology. From these growth rings we discover:
- Past occurrences of drought and seasonal changes
- Atmospheric composition at the time of growth
- Fire history and frequency
- Wind patterns and intensity
- The general health and vigor of the tree over its lifetime

The event expands the initial rupture of the surface between the media, allowing greater flow to take place, and this expansion is permanently recorded in the target pattern at the point of germination of the event.

## Matrices and Tessellation

### The Matrix Concept

A set of intersecting sine waves developed over a regular square or hexagonal matrix will set up a surface composed of the core model shapes. Whether the sine waves are seen as static or flowing, the core model maintains its shape — flow in the system does not necessarily deform the pattern.

Such a **pattern matrix** shows that the models tessellate (from Latin *tesserae*, meaning tiles) to create whole surfaces. If landscapes are a set of such models, they must be able to tessellate.

### Natural Matrices

Convection cells on deserts arise from a roughly hexagonal matrix of air cells 1–5 km across. Matrices underlie the spacing of trees in forests. Glacial landscapes show whole series of such patterns, as do regular river headwaters. Even irregular models tessellate — all are centred on nets or regular grids.

### The Euclidean Foundation

The Euclidean concept of points and lines underlies curvilinear forms. We could equally create a matrix by adding samples of the core pattern as we add tiles to a floor. The hexagonal or square grid provides the invisible scaffolding upon which organic patterns are built.

## Applications of the Event Model

### Landscape Design

Understanding that landscapes are mosaics of tessellating events allows the designer to:
- Identify the scale and type of events present on a site
- Work with existing patterns rather than against them
- Insert new events (plantings, water systems, structures) that tessellate with natural patterns
- Predict how new elements will interact with existing event flows

### Water and Energy Flow

The streamline model reveals that all events involve bidirectional flow between media. Designing for efficient capture, storage, and distribution of water and energy means aligning human systems with these natural streamline paths.

### Temporal Design

Since events have distinct phases (origin, growth, maturity, decay), temporal design — planning what happens when — becomes as important as spatial design. [[succession-planting]], crop rotation, and phased development all reflect the temporal dimension of the event model.

### Scale Recognition

The event model operates at all scales: a single germinating seed, a garden bed, a farm, a watershed, a continent. The same principles of origin, growth, exchange between media, and tessellation apply regardless of magnitude. This scaling property makes the model universally applicable.

## Form vs. Substance

Mollison cautions: "We should not confuse the comprehension of FORM with the knowledge of SUBSTANCE — 'the map is not the territory'." However, understanding form gives us better comprehension of function and suggests appropriate strategies for design. The pattern model is a tool for thinking, not a substitute for direct observation and experience with real materials and conditions on real sites.
