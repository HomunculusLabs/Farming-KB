# Seed Germination Standards and Testing

Germination percentage is the single most important quality indicator for seed, yet most home gardeners have no access to this information. Understanding germination standards, how they are (and are not) enforced, and how to test your own seed can save entire crops from failure.

## Why Germination Matters

A seed lot that germinates at 95% in the lab will produce vastly better field results than one testing at 70%. The relationship is roughly linear: a 95% lab lot might produce 85% field emergence, while a 78% lab lot might produce only 25% field emergence. The difference between a successful garden and a disappointing one often comes down to seed vigor.

### The Three Levels of Germination Quality

1. **USDA Minimum Standard Germination**: The legal floor — the lowest acceptable germination percentage. Seed below this may not be sold unless plainly marked "BELOW STANDARD."
2. **Quality company minimum**: Companies like Johnny's [[johnnys-selected-seeds]] set internal standards 5-15 percentage points above USDA minimums
3. **Commercial quality**: What farmers and market gardeners demand — the highest standard

### Germination Standards by Crop (Selected Examples)

| Crop | USDA Minimum | Johnny's Minimum | Commercial Quality |
|------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Bean | 70% | 80% | 85% |
| Cabbage | 75% | 80% | 85% |
| Carrot | 55% | 75% | 80% |
| Corn | 75% | 85% | 85% |
| Cucumber | 75% | 80% | 85% |
| Lettuce | 80% | 85% | 85% |
| Onion | 65% | 75% | 80% |
| Pea | 80% | 85% | 85% |
| Tomato | 75% | 80% | 85% |

Note: USDA minimums for some crops (like carrot at 55% and onion at 65%) are shockingly low. Seed meeting only the minimum standard is likely to perform poorly in the garden.

## Seed Law and Enforcement

### What the Law Requires

In the US, Canada, and EU, any package of seed weighing more than one pound (454 grams) must show the results of a germination test done at a certified laboratory within the previous six months. This law protects commercial buyers.

### The Small-Packet Exemption

[[garden-seed-management]] packets (typically 1-5 grams) are exempt from this labeling requirement. If they were required to show germination data, it would cost only a few cents more per packet. Instead, a regulation called "USDA Minimum Standard Germination" was created specifically for the small-packet trade — this allows packets to be sold without showing actual germination, as long as the seed presumably meets the minimum.

### Enforcement Reality

When a seed seller wishes to cut costs, one approach is to make no effort to have its packets exceed minimum standard germination. This does not mean all packets will germinate well — it means not all of them will. If a company is both dishonest and unethical, it may make no effort to insure its seeds are above minimum standard at all. The only consequence if caught is a minuscule fine from the state Department of Agriculture.

## Storage and Germination Decline

### How Long Seed Remains Viable

Germination is not static — it declines over time. A seed lot that tests at 90% when fresh will test lower each subsequent year. The rate of decline varies by species:

- **Fast decline** (1-2 years): Onion, parsnip, corn, some lettuces
- **Moderate decline** (3-4 years): Bean, pea, most brassicas
- **Slow decline** (5+ years): Tomato, pepper, some squash

A quality seed seller must buy seed at germination levels significantly higher than their sell-at minimum to ensure seeds are still above minimum when gardeners plant them 6-9 months later. When the author ran [[territorial-seed-company]], he tried to buy seeds at levels at least 5 percentage points above Johnny's minimum levels.

## How to Test Your Own Seed

### The Paper Towel Method

1. Place 10-20 seeds on a damp paper towel
2. Fold and place in a plastic bag
3. Keep warm (70-80°F / 21-27°C)
4. Check after the expected germination days for that species
5. Count sprouted seeds and calculate the percentage

### The Lab Method

A germination lab uses:

- **Sterile media** (blotting paper or fine soil mix)
- **Pre-moistened to a precise level** (squeeze all surplus water out)
- **Controlled temperature** (constant or cycling 16 hours warm / 8 hours cool)
- **Petri dishes** (shallow, airtight, clear plastic containers)
- **Species-specific protocols** prescribing ideal conditions and the maximum number of days allowed for germination

### Interpreting Results

Seeds that emerge quickly in a test are almost always the most vigorous seeds. Any seeds that emerge after the protocol's maximum day limit are considered too weak to survive field conditions. The batch that germinates quickest also germinates at the highest percentage.

## Field vs. Lab Germination

Field germination is always lower than lab germination because:

- Real soil teems with microorganisms, some hostile to seedlings
- Outdoor temperature is never constant and often colder than ideal
- Soil can get too wet or too dry
- Soil moisture is never stable

### The Linear Relationship

Despite the differences, there is a roughly linear relationship between lab and field germination. A cabbage lot testing 95% in the lab might produce 85% field emergence with all seedlings fully developed in 5 days. A lot testing 78% might produce only 25% field emergence over 9 days.

**Conclusion**: To get the best outdoor germination, start with vigorous seeds and create soil conditions that match lab conditions as closely as possible.

## Practical Recommendations

1. **Buy from companies that publish germination data** or that serve commercial growers (who demand this information)
2. **Test a sample** of any seed before committing a large area to it
3. **Don't assume old seed is fine** — even properly stored seed declines over time
4. **Sow extra seed** as insurance when using seed of unknown quality
5. **Keep records** of germination performance so you can identify reliable suppliers over time
