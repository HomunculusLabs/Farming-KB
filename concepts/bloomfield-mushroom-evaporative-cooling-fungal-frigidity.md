# Mushroom Evaporative Cooling and Fungal Frigidity

## Historical Context: Falck's Paradox

In the early 1900s, German mycologist Richard Falck reported that some
mushrooms appeared up to 9°C warmer than the surrounding air. He
hypothesized that self-generated warmth might create convection currents
to assist [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]], functioning as a biological chimney.

If true, mushrooms would join a tiny group of thermogenic organisms
alongside the sacred lotus (*[[nelumbo-nucifera]]*) and some social insect
nests. A.H.R. Buller contested Falck's conclusions but never made his
own temperature measurements. [[nicholas-money]] revisited the phenomenon
decades later at Miami University in Ohio.

## The B-List Experiment

Money classified research into A-list projects (reasonable funding
probability) and B-list projects (unconventional ideas that would
provoke NSF amusement followed by rejection letters). Measuring mushroom
temperatures — an endeavor that would "get anyone other than a
mycologist certified" — qualified as perfect B-list work.

The methodology involved inserting thin wire thermocouples between gills
and spines, or inside bolete tubes. Students measured temperatures from
the hymenial surfaces of eighteen species in beech-maple woodlands.

## The Discovery: Mushrooms Are Cold

Contrary to Falck's century-old claim, the experiments revealed that
mushrooms were consistently *colder* than ambient air. Results were so
counterintuitive that Money spent days searching for methodological
errors, but the thermocouples were accurate and the chilling was real.

Cooling was most extreme on warm days — gills chilled by as much as 5°C
below ambient. Even on cold November mornings the effect continued.
Laboratory-grown mushrooms confirmed the pattern: cooling was stimulated
by air passage around the [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]], strongly suggesting an
evaporative mechanism, the same principle that produces a chill after
swimming.

## Reconciling the Historical Error

Falck's reported warming was likely a methodological artifact. He had
plucked mushrooms and measured them in the laboratory. The warming was
probably decomposition beginning in detached [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]], not active
thermogenesis. Once severed from their mycelial connection, mushroom
tissues undergo rapid autolysis generating heat through microbial and
enzymatic activity.

John Gerard recognized fungal coldness centuries earlier. In *The
Herball or Generall Historie of Plantes* (1636), he described them as
"very cold and moist, and therefore do approch unto a venomous and
murthering facultie."

## The Rooting Shank

*Xerula radicata* (the rooting shank) forms an elegant [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] at
the base of deciduous trees. On a warm summer day its cap can feel
"positively frigid" to the touch. Pluck a fresh specimen and press its
cap against your cheek — the rooting shank is the most dramatic example
due to its large cap surface area and prolonged spore-release period.

## The Paradox: Cold Mushrooms and Condensation

Mushroom cooling seems contradictory to the spore-discharge mechanism,
which depends on water condensation. The [[bloomfield-buller-drops-and-surface-tension-spore-catapult-mechanism]] (Buller's
drop) requires a liquid bead at the base of each spore. If the mushroom
were cold, wouldn't this hinder condensation?

The resolution lies in differential surface properties. Spore surfaces
are sugary (hygroscopic), while basidia and gill tissues are not. Water
vapor streams from most mushroom tissues, but a small quantity condenses
on spore surfaces because sugars hydrate and attract water molecules. The
spore is comparable to a cube of bath salts in a steamy bathroom.
