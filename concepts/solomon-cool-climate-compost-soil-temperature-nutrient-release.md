# Cool-Climate Compost and Soil Temperature Effects on Nutrient Release

## Source
- **Book:** Gardening When It Counts: Growing Food in Hard Times
- **Author:** Steve Solomon (2005)
- **Chapter:** Chapter 7 - Compost; Chapter 2 - Basics

## Overview

Steve Solomon identifies soil temperature as a critical but often overlooked variable that determines how effectively compost and organic amendments release nutrients to growing plants. In cool-climate regions (maritime climates, high altitudes, northern latitudes), soils may never warm sufficiently during the growing season to decompose organic matter at rates needed by high-demand vegetables. This page examines the relationship between soil temperature, microbial activity, and nutrient availability, and provides Solomon's recommendations for adapting composting and fertilization strategies to cool growing conditions.

## The Temperature-Dependent Nature of Organic Nutrient Release

Unlike synthetic fertilizers, which dissolve readily in water regardless of temperature, nutrients bound in organic matter (compost, manure, humus) must be released through microbial decomposition. Soil microorganisms — bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes — are the biochemical engines that break down complex organic compounds into plant-available mineral forms (ammonium, nitrate, phosphate, potassium ions).

### Microbial Activity and Temperature

Microbial activity follows a roughly exponential relationship with soil temperature:

- **Below 5°C (41°F):** Microbial activity nearly ceases. Organic matter decomposition stops. Nutrients locked in compost remain unavailable.
- **5-10°C (41-50°F):** Slow microbial activity begins. Decomposition proceeds but at rates far too slow to supply demanding crops.
- **10-15°C (50-59°F):** Moderate activity. Suitable for low-demand vegetables with modest nutrient requirements.
- **15-25°C (59-77°F):** Optimal microbial range. Rapid decomposition. Organic amendments release nutrients at rates matching most crop demands.
- **Above 30°C (86°F):** Activity begins to decline as heat stresses many soil organisms.

The critical threshold for adequate nutrient release from compost is approximately 15°C (59°F) in the root zone. Below this temperature, even high-quality compost cannot deliver nutrients fast enough for high-demand crops.

## Cool-Climate Growing Challenges

### Maritime and Coastal Climates
Maritime climates (Pacific Northwest coast, British Columbia, New Zealand, parts of the British Isles, Scandinavia) are characterized by cool summers with frequent cloud cover. Despite adequate rainfall and moderate winter temperatures, summer soil temperatures in these regions often remain in the 12-16°C range throughout the growing season. This means:

- Spring gardens start slowly because the soil has not warmed enough to activate microbial nutrient release
- Compost applied in spring may not release significant nutrients until mid-summer
- High-demand vegetables (tomatoes, corn, peppers, eggplant) struggle to obtain sufficient nutrition from organic sources alone
- The effective growing season is shorter than frost-free days would suggest because soil temperature, not air temperature, governs plant growth

### High-Altitude and Northern Latitudes
Similar challenges face gardeners at elevation or in northern regions where even midsummer soil temperatures may rarely exceed 15°C. Short growing seasons compound the problem: by the time soil warms enough for compost to release nutrients, the season is already half over.

## Solomon's Adaptation Strategies

### Increase Amendment Quantity
In cool climates, Solomon recommends applying significantly larger quantities of compost and organic amendments than would be needed in warmer regions. He provides specific scaling guidelines:

- **Low-demand vegetables:** Use medium-quality compost instead of low-grade, applied at 25-50% greater depth
- **Medium-demand vegetables:** Use high-quality compost at standard rates, or medium-quality compost at double rates
- **High-demand vegetables:** Supplement all organic amendments with complete organic fertilizer (COF) applied at full recommended rates

### Use Concentrated Nutrient Sources
Compost alone is insufficient in cool climates for most food crops. Solomon specifically recommends combining compost with concentrated, rapidly available nutrient sources:

- **Complete organic fertilizer (COF):** Provides immediately available minerals (seedmeal, bone meal, kelp meal, lime) that do not depend on microbial decomposition
- **Composted chicken manure:** Higher nitrogen content than steer manure, with nutrients in more available forms
- **Fish emulsion and kelp foliar sprays:** Deliver nutrients directly through leaves, bypassing soil temperature limitations
- **Liquid organic fertilizers:** Faster nutrient availability than solid amendments

### Start Amendment Application Earlier
Apply compost and organic fertilizers in late winter or early spring, as soon as the ground can be worked. This gives microorganisms more time to begin decomposition before planting. In very cool climates, applying amendments the previous autumn can provide a head start.

### Choose Appropriate Varieties
Select vegetable varieties bred for cool climates or short seasons. These varieties typically require less total nutrient uptake and mature faster, reducing the window during which they depend on soil-released nutrients.

### Use Raised Beds for Warming
Raised beds warm faster than flat ground in spring because they have greater surface area exposed to the sun and air. Even a modest 4-6 inch raise can increase soil temperature by 2-4°C, significantly improving microbial activity and nutrient release rates.

## Hot-Climate Contrast

Solomon notes the opposite effect in hot climates: organic matter decomposes so rapidly that maintaining soil organic matter levels is challenging. In consistently warm soils (25°C+), high-quality compost can alone support high-demand vegetables. The problem shifts from insufficient nutrient release to insufficient organic matter retention.

This creates a paradox: the climates that most need compost's soil-building benefits (hot climates where organic matter burns away) are the ones where compost is most effective as a nutrient source. Meanwhile, cool climates that can build soil organic matter easily are the ones where compost is least effective as a nutrient source.

## Quantitative Framework

Solomon provides rough guidelines for adjusting compost application rates by climate:

| Climate Type | Summer Soil Temp | Compost Multiplier | COF Needed? |
|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Hot (Mediterranean) | 25-35°C | 0.75× (standard) | Optional |
| Warm (Temperate) | 18-25°C | 1.0× (standard) | For high-demand only |
| Cool (Maritime) | 12-18°C | 1.5× standard | Recommended for all |
| Cold (Northern) | 8-15°C | 2.0× standard | Essential for all |

These multipliers assume the gardener is using the standard application rates described in Solomon's "Soil Improving in a Nutshell" sidebar from Chapter 2. The COF column indicates whether complete organic fertilizer should be used alongside compost as a supplemental nutrient source.

## Monitoring Soil Temperature

Solomon recommends that gardeners in marginal climates monitor soil temperature directly using a soil thermometer inserted 3-4 inches deep. This is more useful than air temperature readings for predicting plant growth rates and amendment effectiveness. Key temperature milestones:

- **10°C:** Beans and corn can be planted; lettuce and peas grow well
- **13°C:** Most vegetables begin active growth; compost decomposition noticeable
- **16°C:** Tomatoes and peppers can be set out; good microbial activity
- **18°C:** Optimal for most vegetable growth; rapid compost nutrient release

## The Role of Soil Moisture

Soil temperature is not the only factor — adequate moisture is also required for microbial activity. In cool maritime climates, soils are often wet, which can further slow decomposition by limiting oxygen availability in waterlogged conditions. Solomon advises ensuring good drainage and avoiding over-irrigation in cool springs, as saturated soils warm more slowly and support less aerobic microbial activity. A well-drained, moderately moist soil will warm faster and support more vigorous decomposition than a waterlogged one, even at the same ambient temperature.

## Long-Term Soil Building in Cool Climates

Despite the challenges, Solomon is cautiously optimistic about cool-climate soil building. The very slow decomposition that limits nutrient release also means that organic matter added to cool soils persists longer. A cool-climate gardener who consistently adds compost and organic matter each year will build soil humus levels faster than a warm-climate gardener, because less is lost to rapid oxidation. Over a period of 5-10 years, this accumulated humus can significantly improve soil structure, water-holding capacity, and cation exchange capacity — benefits that persist even if nutrient release remains temperature-limited.

## Key Takeaways

- Nutrient release from compost is governed by soil temperature through its effect on microbial activity
- Below ~15°C soil temperature, compost cannot release nutrients fast enough for most food crops
- Maritime, high-altitude, and northern climates face chronic soil temperature limitations
- Cool-climate gardeners must supplement compost with concentrated fertilizers (COF, fish emulsion, kelp)
- Increasing compost quantity helps but cannot fully compensate for temperature limitations
- Raised beds, early application, and cool-adapted varieties are additional mitigation strategies
- The cool-climate/hot-climate paradox means compost's strengths and weaknesses are inversely related to where they are most needed
- Solomon's experience gardening in the Pacific Northwest (Cascadia) and Tasmania directly informs these recommendations
- Gardeners should measure soil temperature, not air temperature, when making planting and fertilization decisions
