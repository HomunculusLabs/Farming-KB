# Soil Mineral Depletion and Plant Nutrition

## The Mineral Balance Problem

[[steve-solomon]] argues that the nutritional quality of homegrown vegetables
depends far more on the mineral balance of the soil than most gardeners realize.
Plants synthesize complex organic chemicals, vitamins, amino acids, and other
substances from the mineral nutrients supplied by the soil ecology. Russian
researcher Krasil'nikov called these "biotic vitamins." When soil minerals are
deficient or imbalanced, the resulting produce is less nutritious and less
flavorful.

Solomon points to a striking geographical correlation: in Missouri, the prairie
soils north and west of the Missouri River are highly fertile, while those south
and east are worn-out and infertile. A map of Missouri's soil quality closely
matches a map of human health outcomes in the same region, with the poorest
health found where the soils are most depleted. This demonstrates the direct
connection between soil mineral content and the health of those who eat food
grown on it.

## Nutrient Transport and Soil Depletion

When you gather vegetation from one place and compost it or spread manure on
your garden, you are transporting minerals in their proper proportions to your
garden and increasing the overall level of soil fertility. However, this only
works well if the source material came from mineral-balanced soils. If the
surrounding soils do not contain a perfect balance of minerals, importing
organic matter alone cannot fix the deficiency.

The fundamental problem is that mineral nutrients gradually wash out of soils
and end up in the ocean. Even oceanic minerals, once abundant, are being
depleted. As soil fertility decreased over generations, all too soon the land
was worn out. Solomon emphasizes that increasing soil fertility is the
gardener's main concern because without it, neither yield nor nutritional
quality can be optimized.

## Potassium Pushing and Nutritional Dilution

Commercial growers, both chemical and organic, routinely push soil potassium to
high levels because it increases bulk yield. However, Solomon warns that this
increased yield comes in the form of starch and fiber, not protein, vitamins,
enzymes, and minerals. The nutritional content of vegetables actually decreases
by about the same percentage that the yield goes up. The higher bulk yield that
potassium triggers is primarily calories, not nutrition.

This has practical implications for the home gardener: if you grow a starchy
potato variety with lots of moisture and excess potassium fertilizer, you get a
larger harvest of lower-protein, lower-mineral spuds. Conversely, growing a
"boiling variety" (often yellow-fleshed with waxy texture) with moderate
moisture and mineral-rich but lower-potassium soil produces smaller but
significantly more nutritious potatoes.

## Trace Elements and Complete Nutrition

Solomon stresses that plants require not just NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus,
potassium) but a full spectrum of trace minerals including calcium, magnesium,
and micronutrients. His Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) formula incorporates
kelp meal or basalt dust specifically to supply these trace elements. He notes
that "most composts don't" contain significant amounts of phosphorus, potassium,
calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals in the right proportions.

The consequence of mineral-depleted soils extends beyond human nutrition. Plants
growing in imbalanced soil are more susceptible to insects and diseases. Much of
what appears to be drought damage is actually nutrient deficiency induced by dry
surface soil when plant nutrients are located only in the plowed surface layer.
Well-mineralized plants develop stronger immune responses and can resist pests
that would devastate weak plants.

## The Nutritional Quality of Modern Varieties

Solomon challenges the popular belief that old heirloom varieties are inherently
more nutritious than modern ones. Drawing on the work of biophysicist Alan
Kapuler, he explains that for thousands of years, families raised their own seed
varieties selected generation by generation for both taste and healthfulness.
Before modern medicine, children fed more nutritious vegetables had higher
survival rates, so better varieties persisted.

However, after about 1870, when vegetable growing shifted from subsistence to
commercial activity, varieties were selected for marketability rather than
nutritional quality. Modern hybrids and even many open-pollinated varieties
developed since then have been bred for appearance, shipping quality, and yield
at the expense of nutrition. Much of what passes for "heirloom" seed today is
actually degraded commercial varieties or mislabeled modern strains.

## Clay Soils and Mineral Holding

Clay soils present a particular challenge for mineral nutrition. Solomon
describes clays as acting "like discharged lead-acid batteries" -- they have the
capacity to soak up huge quantities of plant nutrients. Until they have been
"charged to full," they tend to withhold nutrients from plant roots. This means
gardeners with clay soils may need to apply about 1.5 times the usual amount of
fertility amendments for the first few years. The remedy is not adding more
organic matter (which decomposes rapidly in clay and can cause nutritional
imbalances) but ensuring complete mineral nutrition through balanced fertilizers
like COF.
