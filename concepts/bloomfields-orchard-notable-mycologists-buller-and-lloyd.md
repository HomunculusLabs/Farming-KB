# Notable Mycologists: Buller and Curtis Gates Lloyd

## Overview

Arthur Henry Reginald Buller and Curtis Gates Lloyd represent
two extraordinary and contrasting figures in the history of
mycology. Buller was a meticulous British-born Canadian
academic whose six-volume Researches on Fungi remains
unmatched in its experimental rigor on spore discharge
and fungal biology.

Lloyd was an iconoclastic American amateur who compiled
the greatest private mycological library ever assembled,
corresponding with hundreds of colleagues worldwide.
Their 1920 meeting in Cincinnati was a landmark encounter
between institutional and independent science.

## Arthur Henry Reginald Buller (1874-1944)

### Early Life and Education

Buller was born in Birmingham, England, in 1874 into a
family of modest means. His father died young, leaving his
mother to raise four children. Despite financial hardship,
Buller excelled academically and won a scholarship to Mason
College, later absorbed into the University of Birmingham.

He continued to Birmingham's predecessor institution and
won an entrance scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge,
in 1897. At Cambridge, Buller studied the Natural Sciences
Tripos, specializing in botany.

He was elected a Fellow of Trinity College, placing him
among Cambridge's intellectual elite. His early research on
plant physiology laid groundwork for his later fungal
studies. The Cambridge environment fostered both rigorous
experimentation and broad intellectual curiosity.

### Migration to Canada

In 1904, Buller accepted the founding chair of Botany at
the University of Manitoba in Winnipeg, a position he held
for four decades. The move from Cambridge to the Canadian
prairie was dramatic.

Winnipeg was a frontier city of 40,000, and the university
was a modest institution. Buller built the botany department
from nothing, recruiting staff, establishing laboratories,
and creating a research program of international caliber.

The prairie environment itself offered rich mycological
opportunities. The wheat fields of Manitoba harbored
devastating rust fungi, and the boreal forests to the north
teemed with mushroom diversity. Buller's Canadian years
proved more scientifically productive than he could have
achieved at any British institution.

### Researches on Fungi

Buller's magnum opus, Researches on Fungi, comprised six
volumes published between 1909 and 1934 by Longmans, Green
and Company. The work remains the most comprehensive
experimental study of fungal spore discharge ever produced.

Volume I appeared during his early Winnipeg years and
established his reputation. Subsequent volumes documented
decades of patient experimentation on the mechanics of
spore liberation, hyphal growth, and the physics of
fungal dispersal.

Buller developed ingenious experimental apparatus. He used
microscopes to observe spore discharge directly, designed
chambers to measure spore trajectories, and employed
high-speed photography to capture the invisible process
of ballistospore release.

His measurements of spore velocities, distances, and the
role of surface tension were revolutionary. No one before
or since has matched the breadth and precision of his
experimental work on fungal spore liberation.

### The Drop Mechanism

Buller's most celebrated discovery was the mechanism of
ballistospore discharge in basidiomycetes. He demonstrated
that a droplet of fluid, called Buller's drop, forms at the
base of each spore and grows rapidly through condensation.

When the drop reaches critical size, it merges with a film
on the spore surface. The sudden reduction in surface area
launches the spore. The entire process takes microseconds.
This mechanism is one of the fastest cellular movements
in nature.

### Scientific Method and Personality

Buller was known for extraordinary patience and meticulous
experimental design. He would spend weeks or months conducting
hundreds of repetitions of a single experiment. He was also
an engaging lecturer who performed dramatic demonstrations
for his students.

Beyond mycology, he wrote popular science essays published
in magazines like The Nineteenth Century and later in
collected volumes. His death in 1944 marked the end of an
era in experimental mycology.

## Curtis Gates Lloyd (1859-1926)

### Early Life and Business Career

Lloyd was born in 1859 in Florence, Kentucky, across the
Ohio River from Cincinnati. He received minimal formal
education but developed an insatiable curiosity about
natural history.

He and his two brothers, John Uri and Benjamin, entered
the wholesale drug business. The business prospered,
providing financial independence that Lloyd leveraged
entirely for mycological pursuits.

The Lloyds' drug business was substantial, dealing in
botanical drugs and pharmaceutical supplies. This
commercial work familiarized Curtis with fungi used in
medicine and fermentation, sparking deeper interest.

### The Lloyd Library and Museum

Curtis Gates Lloyd assembled the greatest private
mycological library ever collected. Beginning in the
1890s, he purchased rare works, exchanged publications
with colleagues worldwide, and built a collection spanning
centuries of mycological literature.

The Lloyd Library in Cincinnati housed thousands of
volumes, including original editions of Persoon, Fries,
and other founding taxonomists. After Lloyd's death, the
library became a public institution.

It remains one of the world's foremost collections of
botanical and mycological literature, serving researchers
to this day. The library's holdings include herbals from
the sixteenth century, extensive runs of mycological
journals, and Lloyd's voluminous correspondence.

### Mycological Contributions

Lloyd was a prolific taxonomist who specialized in
gasteromycetes, the puffballs, stinkhorns, and bird's
nest fungi. He described hundreds of species and produced
extensive illustrated monographs.

His Mycological Notes, published in multiple series from
1898 onward, contained taxonomic descriptions,
observations, and trenchant opinions on nomenclatural
disputes. Lloyd championed the importance of amateur
naturalists and criticized professional scientists who
he felt had become disconnected from field observation.

### The Slime Mold Dispute

One of Lloyd's most notable scientific positions was his
advocacy for classifying slime molds as fungi. Myxomycetes
had been placed with fungi by early taxonomists, and Lloyd
maintained this traditional classification against growing
evidence from cytologists that slime molds were
fundamentally different organisms.

His stubborn position on this issue illustrated both his
strength and weakness: loyalty to observational tradition
over emerging cellular evidence. Modern molecular
phylogenetics has confirmed that slime molds are not
fungi but belong to separate eukaryotic lineages.

### Correspondence Network

Lloyd maintained a vast correspondence with mycologists
across every continent. He wrote thousands of letters,
exchanging specimens, opinions, and gossip. His letters
were characterized by blunt humor, strong opinions on
taxonomic matters, and genuine warmth toward colleagues
he respected.

The correspondence network made Lloyd a central node in
the global mycological community despite his amateur status
and geographic isolation from major research centers. He
was particularly generous with specimens, distributing
dried collections to researchers who lacked access to
tropical and subtropical diversity.

Many species described by European and American mycologists
were based entirely on Lloyd material. His generosity with
specimens and his encyclopedic knowledge of gasteromycete
diversity made him indispensable to the taxonomic
enterprise.

## The 1920 Meeting

### Background

Buller and Lloyd corresponded for years before their
meeting. Buller admired Lloyd's taxonomic expertise and
specimen collection, while Lloyd respected Buller's
experimental genius.

Their correspondence reveals mutual regard tinged with
gentle philosophical disagreement about the proper
direction of mycological research. Both recognized the
value in the other's approach even if neither fully
adopted it.

### The Encounter

Buller visited Cincinnati in 1920 and spent several days
with Lloyd. The meeting combined examination of Lloyd's
library and specimen collections with vigorous debate
about fungal biology.

Buller was impressed by the library's scope and by Lloyd's
encyclopedic knowledge of gasteromycete diversity. Lloyd
was fascinated by Buller's experimental apparatus and by
the physical insights his experiments had produced.

### Scientific and Philosophical Differences

The meeting highlighted fundamental differences in
approach. Buller believed mycology should be an
experimental science grounded in physics and chemistry.

Lloyd valued field observation, taxonomic accuracy, and
the accumulated wisdom of centuries of descriptive work.
Both positions had merit, and both men recognized the
value in the other's approach.

### Legacy of the Meeting

The meeting produced no joint publication but symbolized
the breadth of mycological inquiry. The field needed both
experimentalists and taxonomists, both Bullers and Lloyds.
The friendship that developed between them endured through
subsequent correspondence until Lloyd's death in 1926.

## Historical Context

### The Golden Age of Mycology

Buller and Lloyd worked during what might be called the
golden age of descriptive and experimental mycology.
Between 1880 and 1930, the basic frameworks of fungal
classification were established.

The major mechanisms of spore discharge were characterized,
and the ecological roles of fungi began to be understood.
Both men contributed enormously to this period of rapid
progress.

### Institutional vs. Independent Science

Their careers illustrate two paths available to scientists
of the era. Buller represented the institutional path:
university appointment, laboratory resources, graduate
students, and the authority of academic credentials.

Lloyd represented the independent path: private wealth,
self-directed research, freedom from institutional
constraints, and the credibility earned through expertise
alone. Both paths produced outstanding science, and the
tension between them persists in modern research.

### The Role of Amateurs

Lloyd's career demonstrates the historical importance of
amateur naturalists in building scientific knowledge. Before
government funding and university departments became the
dominant model, much biological knowledge was produced by
physicians, clergy, businessmen, and landed gentry who
studied nature as avocation.

The mycological community was particularly welcoming of
amateurs, and many significant species were first described
by non-professionals. Lloyd's legacy reminds us that
scientific contribution is not limited to those with
formal credentials.
