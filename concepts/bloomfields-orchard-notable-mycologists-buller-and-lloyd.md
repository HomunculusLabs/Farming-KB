# Notable Mycologists: Buller and Curtis Gates Lloyd

## Overview

Arthur Henry Reginald Buller and Curtis Gates Lloyd represent
two extraordinary and contrasting figures in the history of
mycology. Buller was a meticulous British-born Canadian
academic whose six-volume Researches on Fungi remains
unmatched in its experimental rigor on spore discharge
and fungal biology.
Lloyd was an iconoclastic American amateur who compiled
the greatest private mycological library ever assembled,
corresponding with hundreds of colleagues worldwide.
Their 1920 meeting in Cincinnati was a landmark encounter
between institutional and independent science.

## Arthur Henry Reginald Buller (1874-1944)

### Early Life and Education

Buller was born in Birmingham, England, in 1874 into a
family of modest means. His father died young, leaving his
mother to raise four children. Despite financial hardship,
Buller excelled academically and won a scholarship to Mason
College, later absorbed into the University of Birmingham.
He continued to Birmingham's predecessor institution and
won an entrance scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge,
in 1897. At Cambridge, Buller studied the Natural Sciences
Tripos, specializing in botany.
He was elected a Fellow of Trinity College, placing him
among Cambridge's intellectual elite. His early research on
plant physiology laid groundwork for his later fungal
studies. The Cambridge environment fostered both rigorous
experimentation and broad intellectual curiosity.

### Migration to Canada

In 1904, Buller accepted the founding chair of Botany at
the University of Manitoba in Winnipeg, a position he held
for four decades. The move from Cambridge to the Canadian
prairie was dramatic.
Winnipeg was a frontier city of 40,000, and the university
was a modest institution. Buller built the botany department
from nothing, recruiting staff, establishing laboratories,
and creating a research program of international caliber.
The prairie environment itself offered rich mycological
opportunities. The wheat fields of Manitoba harbored
devastating rust fungi, and the boreal forests to the north
teemed with mushroom diversity. Buller's Canadian years
proved more scientifically productive than he could have
achieved at any British institution.

## The 1920 Meeting

### Background

Buller and Lloyd corresponded for years before their
meeting. Buller admired Lloyd's taxonomic expertise and
[[fungal-specimen-collection-herbarium-management]], while Lloyd respected Buller's
experimental genius.
Their correspondence reveals mutual regard tinged with
gentle philosophical disagreement about the proper
direction of mycological research. Both recognized the
value in the other's approach even if neither fully
adopted it.

### The Encounter

Buller visited Cincinnati in 1920 and spent several days
with Lloyd. The meeting combined examination of Lloyd's
library and specimen collections with vigorous debate
about fungal biology.
Buller was impressed by the library's scope and by Lloyd's
encyclopedic knowledge of gasteromycete diversity. Lloyd
was fascinated by Buller's experimental apparatus and by
the physical insights his experiments had produced.

### Scientific and Philosophical Differences

The meeting highlighted fundamental differences in
approach. Buller believed mycology should be an
experimental science grounded in physics and chemistry.
Lloyd valued field observation, taxonomic accuracy, and
the accumulated wisdom of centuries of descriptive work.
Both positions had merit, and both men recognized the
value in the other's approach.

### Legacy of the Meeting

The meeting produced no joint publication but symbolized
the breadth of mycological inquiry. The field needed both
experimentalists and taxonomists, both Bullers and Lloyds.
The friendship that developed between them endured through
subsequent correspondence until Lloyd's death in 1926.
