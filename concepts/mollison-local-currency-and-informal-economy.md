# Mollison: Local Currency and Informal Economy

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.11)

## The Informal Economy

Barter is a common economy practiced particularly in rural or neighbourhood areas where people are more likely to know one another. At the household level, people exchange garden products and plants, share labour, and exchange goods and services. Occasionally people form 3 to 5 person work groups to build houses, create gardens, or clean up housework.

On a community level, or with more than 6 to 8 people, labour exchange may need to be coordinated or regulated.

## Labour Exchange Systems

The Bendigo Home Builder's Club (Victoria, Australia) is a group of 35 people building individual homes. They pay $5 a year per family, mainly for printing and distribution costs of the Club's newsletter. The units of exchange are hours of labour, and all labour is considered equal. Using a standard labour exchange form (legally binding), the recipient is debited and the donor credited for every hour's work. There is a Labour Organiser to sort out the balance of payments, and to dispatch labourers to a recipient (who must have at least 60 hours in credit).

A Community Barter Club also works on a system of debits and credits, where residents offer goods, services, and skills. A credit is calculated at one hour, and the donor and recipient agree among themselves what they consider the job is worth. People are not limited to a one-to-one exchange; as the Club organiser keeps records, transactions occur as long as services are desired.

## The LET System (Local Employment Trading System)

The LET System centres in a community: every joining member must be willing to consider trading in local "green" dollars. Key features:
- LET dollars carry no interest; administration costs are charged on a "cost of service" basis.
- Any taxes applicable are the responsibility of members.
- Any member can know the turnover or balance of any other member.
- Every member gets periodic statements of accounts.
- The currency, although equivalent to legal tender, is not issued and cannot be cashed in.
- Green dollars are "earned" by goods or services to others, and "lost" by using services or goods.
- All trade, or credit standing, is a public act, and refers to the community as a whole.
- Unlike simple barter, a member in credit can spend over the whole range of services or goods offered.

Production, as time spent by members in service to others, is thus never limited by the lack of money. Businesses can charge federal currency for spare parts, and green dollars for labour. "Foreign" goods are thus more expensive, and local components increase; local businesses thrive. Charities and local farms benefit greatly. Anyone who wants work can offer services; they need not wait for "jobs."

As only members can trade with each other, the community account is at all times balanced. In effect, any member (by working or selling) issues their own currency, and could return any community to full employment. An ideal member has many transactions, but accumulates modest debits and credits.

## Community Money vs Conventional Money

Conventional money derives from many agencies external to a community, and circulates throughout all communities, tending to be accumulated in cities, multinational coffers, and banks supporting large investors. Community money (or credit), however, is not usable or necessarily wanted outside that community, hence circulates indefinitely in the community, providing a constantly available resource.

## Internal Exchange Infrastructure

Internal economics are greatly aided by:
- Exchange newsletters, computer services, and advertisement.
- Brokerage houses dealing in large surplus barter systems using a Trade Unit (T.U.) valued at about $1 for pricing and exchange value.
- Small charges on successful swaps by barter centres.
- Volunteer labour, exchanges of gifts, and taking responsibility for community projects.

## The Formal Economy: Cooperatives

A cooperative is a group of people acting together for the benefit of members. It is a legal entity with limited liability and perpetual succession. Principles include:
- Open membership.
- Democratic organisation (one member, one vote).
- Strictly limited interest on share capital.
- Surplus belongs to members.
- Education of members, officers, employees, and the public.
- Cooperation between cooperatives.

The Mondragon cooperatives (Basque region of Spain): 96 worker-cooperatives, 17,000 worker-members in less than 30 years. Key features: 10% of profits returned to community for public services; 20% held as capital reserves; 70% distributed to workers; ratio of lowest to highest paid never greater than 1:5; no redundancies -- workers retrained; cooperative bank finances new cooperatives up to 90%; each cooperative averages 200 to 300 worker-owners.

## Credit Unions and Revolving Loan Funds

Anyone belonging to an identifiable group of 30 or more people can start a credit union. The purpose can be to fund local self-reliance. Credit unions can pay routine household accounts; some have cheque account services. Money borrowed to save money (gardens, fuel conservation, energy generation) is soon repaid and safe to lend.

A revolving loan fund: people put in $500 to $5,000 capital at nominated interest (0 to 10%) into an established financial institution, loaned out to new community businesses. The group checks references, offers advice, and acts on recommendations of volunteers. Loans at low local interest (6 to 11%) are fair. The skills group helps select equipment, test markets, train entrepreneurs in bookkeeping, and find resources.

Every place where this has started (since 1980, there are dozens or hundreds of funds, currencies, and local money systems operating) has proven that publicly needed, publicly funded, and publicly open businesses very rarely fail.

## Local Currency Experiments Worldwide

Since Mollison wrote the Designers' Manual, local currency experiments have proliferated worldwide. The Ithaca Hours system in Ithaca, New York, launched in 1991 and became one of the most well-documented local currency systems in the United States. Ithaca Hours were denominated in hours of labor, with each Hour equivalent to approximately $10 of local purchasing power. The system facilitated thousands of transactions among local businesses and residents before declining in the 2000s. In the United Kingdom, the Totnes Pound (launched 2007) and Brixton Pound (launched 2009) introduced local currencies specifically to strengthen local economies against economic globalization. The Chiemgauer in Germany, the WIR in Switzerland (which has operated continuously since 1934 and processes billions of francs annually), and numerous time bank systems worldwide have demonstrated that complementary currencies can function at meaningful scale.

## The Demurrage Concept

Some local currency systems incorporate demurrage, a concept originally proposed by Silvio Gesell, where currency loses value over time unless spent. This design feature encourages rapid circulation of money, preventing hoarding and ensuring that currency fulfills its function as a medium of exchange rather than a store of value. The Wörgl experiment in Austria during the 1930s demonstrated that a demurrage currency could stimulate local economic activity during a depression, with the stamped currency circulating much faster than conventional money and funding public works projects. Demurrage currencies align with Mollison's emphasis on keeping resources circulating within communities rather than accumulating in stagnant pools.

## Digital and Blockchain Applications

Modern technology has created new possibilities for local currency systems. Community currencies can now be managed through mobile applications, reducing the administrative overhead that limited earlier systems. Blockchain technology enables the creation of locally-scoped digital currencies with transparent transaction records, automatic mutual credit accounting, and low transaction costs. Projects like the Bristol Pound in the UK have demonstrated that digital local currencies can achieve significant adoption, with millions of pounds worth of transactions processed through the system. These technological tools address one of the main practical barriers to local currency implementation: the cost and complexity of maintaining transaction records.

## Barter Networks and Mutual Credit

Beyond the formal LET System model, informal barter networks and mutual credit systems operate in many communities. Time banking, where participants exchange hours of service rather than goods, has grown substantially since the 1990s. In a time bank, one hour of any service is valued equally with one hour of any other service, embodying the same principle of labor equality that Mollison describes in the Bendigo Home Builder's Club. Time banks have been used to coordinate elder care, home repairs, tutoring, transportation, and community services. The mutual credit model, where participants extend credit to each other through a shared accounting system without requiring currency, enables trade even among participants with little conventional money.

## Community Currency and Social Capital

Local currency systems generate social capital by creating networks of reciprocal exchange relationships. Participants in local currency systems develop trust, shared identity, and mutual obligation through repeated transactions. This social capital has economic value that is not captured by conventional measures of financial transactions. Studies of local currency systems have found that participants report increased sense of community connection, greater awareness of local businesses and their products, and more willingness to cooperate on community projects. The social capital generated by local currency systems contributes to community resilience by strengthening the social bonds that enable collective action during crises.

## Ecological Economics and Local Circulation

Mollison's emphasis on keeping money circulating locally aligns with principles of ecological economics, which recognizes that the scale and speed of economic circulation affect both ecological and social outcomes. Money that circulates rapidly within a community creates more economic activity per unit of currency than money that flows through quickly. The concept of the "local multiplier effect" quantifies this: each dollar spent at a locally-owned business generates more economic activity within the community than a dollar spent at a chain store, because locally-owned businesses are more likely to source locally, pay local wages, and spend profits locally. Local currencies amplify this multiplier effect by making local circulation visible and incentivized.

## Permaculture Village Economies

The ultimate expression of Mollison's local economy thinking is the permaculture village, a community designed from the ground up to meet most of its needs from local resources. In a permaculture village, food, energy, water, building materials, and cultural needs are provided primarily from within the village and its immediate bioregion. Money and currency serve primarily for transactions that cannot be handled through direct exchange, barter, or mutual credit. Several intentional communities inspired by permaculture principles, including Crystal Waters in Australia and The Farm in Tennessee, have implemented elements of this local economy vision. While complete local self-sufficiency is neither achievable nor desirable, the degree of local self-reliance that permaculture villages demonstrate shows what is possible when communities design their economies to prioritize local circulation and ecological integration.

## Women and the Informal Economy

Mollison's analysis of the informal economy touches on a critical but often unacknowledged dimension: the informal economy is disproportionately populated by women. Women's work in home food production, childcare, elder care, community organizing, and informal trade generates enormous economic value that is invisible in conventional economic statistics. The LETSystem and local currency models that Mollison advocates can help make women's economic contributions visible and valued by creating formal accounting systems that recognize and reward this work. Several community currency experiments have found that women are among the most active and creative participants, using local exchange systems to build businesses, share skills, and create social networks that enhance community well-being.

## Gift Economy and Beyond

Beyond monetary exchange, barter, and mutual credit, Mollison recognizes the role of gift economies in community resilience. Gift economies, where goods and services are given without expectation of direct return, create social bonds and reciprocal obligations that strengthen community fabric. Potlatch traditions in Pacific Northwest indigenous cultures, where wealth was accumulated specifically to be given away, demonstrate that gift economies can operate at significant scale and complexity. In modern communities, practices like seed sharing, tool libraries, skill-sharing workshops, and community meals embody gift economy principles. These non-monetary economic relationships provide a foundation of trust and reciprocity that makes monetary exchange systems, whether conventional or alternative, function more effectively.

## Limitations and Criticisms of Local Currency Systems

Despite their promise, local currency systems face significant limitations that Mollison's discussion does not fully address. Many local currency experiments have failed after initial enthusiasm waned, with participation declining as the novelty wears off. The administrative burden of maintaining transaction records, resolving disputes, and managing member accounts can overwhelm volunteer-run systems. Local currencies may face regulatory challenges from tax authorities and banking regulators who are unfamiliar with or hostile to alternative monetary systems. Additionally, local currencies are most effective in communities with existing strong social bonds and diverse economic activity; they are less effective in communities that are economically homogeneous or socially fragmented. These limitations do not invalidate Mollison's vision but highlight the importance of careful design, adequate resources, and realistic expectations.
