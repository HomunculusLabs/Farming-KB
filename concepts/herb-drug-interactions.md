# Herb-Drug Interactions

Herb-drug interactions occur when herbal medicines affect the pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion) or pharmacodynamics (receptor-level effects) of conventional pharmaceutical drugs. These interactions can reduce drug effectiveness, increase drug toxicity, or produce unexpected physiological effects. As herbal medicine use becomes increasingly common, often alongside conventional pharmaceuticals, understanding potential interactions is essential for safe practice.

The most clinically significant interactions involve changes in drug metabolism through the cytochrome P450 enzyme system, additive or synergistic pharmacological effects, and alterations in drug absorption or elimination.

## Cytochrome P450 Enzyme Interactions

The cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme system, located primarily in the liver, is responsible for metabolizing approximately 70% to 80% of all clinically used drugs. Herbal compounds can inhibit (slow down) or induce (speed up) specific CYP enzymes, altering the blood levels and effects of drugs metabolized by those enzymes.

### CYP3A4 Interactions

CYP3A4 is the most abundant CYP enzyme and metabolizes the largest number of pharmaceutical drugs. St. John's wort (Hypericum perforatum) is the most potent herbal inducer of CYP3A4, significantly reducing blood levels of drugs metabolized by this enzyme.

Drugs with clinically significant interactions with St. John's wort include oral contraceptives (reduced effectiveness, risk of breakthrough bleeding and unintended pregnancy), warfarin (reduced anticoagulant effect, risk of blood clots), cyclosporine (reduced immunosuppression, risk of organ rejection in transplant patients), HIV protease inhibitors and non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (reduced antiviral effectiveness), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and other antidepressants (risk of serotonin syndrome), theophylline (reduced bronchodilator effect), and statin medications (reduced cholesterol-lowering effect).

St. John's wort should generally be avoided by patients taking any of these medications. If used concurrently, drug levels should be closely monitored and doses adjusted by the prescribing physician. The inducing effect of St. John's wort persists for approximately 2 weeks after discontinuation.

### CYP2D6 Interactions

CYP2D6 metabolizes approximately 25% of all drugs, including many antidepressants, antipsychotics, beta-blockers, and opioid analgesics (codeine, tramadol, oxycodone). Goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis) may inhibit CYP2D6, potentially increasing blood levels of drugs metabolized by this enzyme.
