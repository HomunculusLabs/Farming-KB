# Cannabis Honey Oil Production

Honey oil is a highly refined cannabis extract characterized by its translucent amber color and thick, honey-like consistency. It represents one of the most potent forms of cannabis preparation achievable through relatively simple chemical techniques. The term "honey oil" refers specifically to the visual appearance of the finished product rather than any actual honey content, though its viscous, sticky texture is remarkably similar.

## Overview of Honey Oil

Honey oil occupies a middle ground between basic cannabis extracts and pharmaceutical-grade cannabinoid isolates. Where crude extraction yields a dark, greenish-black oil containing significant impurities, and advanced laboratory techniques can produce nearly pure THC as a thin transparent liquid, honey oil offers an accessible path to a premium product with potency far exceeding that of the starting material.

According to Ben Lemon's [[lemon-cannabis-alchemy-advanced-refinement-techniques]], the translucent amber oil produced by charcoal filtering the ether phase of the extraction can contain between thirty and sixty percent THC, depending on the quality of the starting material and whether isomerization has been performed. When the oil is also isomerized to convert CBD to THC, the effective potency can be substantially higher.

## Charcoal Types and Alternatives

Different types of activated charcoal vary in their adsorptive properties based on the source material and activation method. Wood-based activated charcoal tends to have a more open pore structure suitable for adsorbing larger organic molecules like pigments. Coconut shell-based charcoal has a finer pore structure that may adsorb smaller molecules more effectively but could also remove some desirable terpenes.

Animal [[knf-bone-charcoal-preparation]] (bone char) has been used historically for decolorization in sugar refining and may offer different selectivity than plant-derived charcoals. Each type of charcoal will produce slightly different results in terms of cannabinoid yield, terpene retention, and color removal.

Some modern processors use silica gel as an alternative or supplement to activated charcoal. Silica gel can be functionalized with specific [[guzman-allen-gartz-four-chemical-groups-neurotropic-fungi]] to selectively adsorb particular classes of impurities. This allows more targeted purification than the broad-spectrum adsorption of activated charcoal.

## Troubleshooting Charcoal Filtration

If the filtrate remains dark after charcoal treatment, the most likely causes are insufficient charcoal, inadequate contact time, or charcoal that has been depleted of its adsorptive capacity. Adding fresh charcoal and extending the contact time typically resolves this issue. If the oil takes on an off-flavor after charcoal treatment, pre-washing the charcoal with alcohol before use can remove any substances that might leach into the product.

If yield drops below expectations, reducing the charcoal-to-oil ratio or shortening the contact time may help. The processor should also verify that the charcoal has been properly stored in a sealed container, as activated charcoal can adsorb moisture and [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] from the air, reducing its effectiveness for the intended purification.
