# Natural Pest Control [[organic-pest-and-disease-management]]

## Organic Strategies for Protecting Mushroom Crops

Pest pressure is inevitable in [[mushroom-training-straw-mushroom-cultivation]] at any scale. The primary
goal is preventing outbreaks through proper media treatments, cleanliness, and
tool sanitation. The cultivator is the most frequent contamination vector during
media preparation and spawning. Mushrooms absorb gases, liquids, and sometimes
heavy metals from their environment, making it critical to never spray pesticides
or synthetic chemicals on fruiting mushrooms. Mushrooms can break down some
herbicides in substrate via [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]], but directly sprayed
chemicals translocate into fruitbodies. Effective organic alternatives exist.

## Prevention Through Growing Space Design

### Partitioning and Isolation

Large open spaces facilitate pest and disease spread between cultivation
stages. Partitioning into smaller rooms or sections dramatically improves
control. Space allocation should reflect the time each cultivation stage
requires. For example, a shiitake operation with 2 weeks colonization, 4 weeks
browning/curing, and 21 weeks fruiting allocates approximately 7.4% of space to
colonization, 14.8% to curing, and 77.8% to fruiting.

### Tunnel Systems

Many commercial operations use linked shipping-container-like tunnels. Each
tunnel holds a single batch for its full spawn run and fruiting cycle, then is
emptied and sanitized. This batch isolation eliminates cross-contamination
between crops and can reduce or eliminate the perceived need for pesticides. In
the event of an outbreak, individual tunnels can be shut down independently.

### Cleanliness Protocols

Sweep floors free of organic debris (mushroom bits, cut stems) that attract
insects and support mold. Never leave mushroom remnants on growing medium.
Clean drains and plumbing traps weekly with diluted bleach. Eliminate standing
water, which breeds pests, bacteria, and mold. Treat contamination like fire:
lock it down and deal with it immediately.

## Insect Control

### Fungus Gnats and Fruit Flies

The most common indoor pests, including *Sciaridae* family gnats and
*Drosophila* fruit flies. They fit into mushroom gills, lay eggs, and larvae
devour mushrooms from the inside out. Adults spread contaminant spores between
cultures. Key strategies:

- **Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Bti)**: A bacterium lethal to
  larvae but harmless to mycelium. Mix diluted Bti into substrate during
  inoculation. For severe outbreaks, inject Bti solution into oyster mushroom
  column holes. Do not spray on mature mushrooms.
- **Vinegar/soap traps**: Half cup apple cider vinegar or milk with a few
  drops of dish soap in small bowls attracts and drowns adult flies. Hang
  traps by cutting windows in plastic bottles.
- **Yellow sticky traps**: Bright yellow adhesive ribbons attract flying
  insects. Use them to monitor population trends and assess control
  effectiveness.
- **Timely harvesting**: Pick gilled mushrooms before cap edges turn upward
  or ruffle, when gills have not yet spread to accommodate egg-laying insects.
- **Wet/dry vacuum**: A last resort for adults embedded in gills. Fill the
  vacuum canister one-third with soapy water, rake gills with the nozzle while
  tapping caps. Repeat daily for one week with Bti and sticky mats.

### "Pleasing" Fungus Beetles

These rapidly reproducing beetles bore tunnels into caps and stems, eating flesh
while breeding. Control by harvesting before gills fully spread. For infested
mushrooms, hold caps over a bucket containing 1-2 inches of strong bleach
solution with liquid dish soap, tap caps to dislodge beetles. The solution
remains effective for about a month.

### Mites

Mites cause blotchy, incomplete colonization. Inspect fruitbodies with a hand
lens for crab-like bodies. For confirmation, wash a liter of fluid through
growing medium, strain through coffee filter, and examine debris under a
dissecting microscope. Mites are repelled by cinnamon: incorporate cinnamon
extract into daily disinfection solutions for laminar flow surfaces and working
areas, leaving a trace residue that deters mite populations.

### Slugs and Snails

Attracted to gases produced by maturing mushrooms. Place shallow pans of
inexpensive beer around logs, racks, trays, or bed surfaces. Slugs locate the
traps from many feet away, enter, and drown.

### Biological Predators

Introducing predators into fruiting rooms leverages [[fukuoka-bushman-world-ecological-balance]].
Spiders in rafters weave webs between mushroom columns, catching fungus gnats.
Anole lizards patrol racks and tables, snapping up beetles. Leopard tree frogs
raised from tadpoles in aquariums position themselves near column exit holes,
waiting to consume emerging gnats. These predators fill [[mushroom-habitats-and-ecological-niches]] that
naturally slow reproductive pest species.

## Mold Control

Molds are a major threat when growing on pasteurized or sterilized substrate.
Common genera include *Aspergillus*, *Rhizopus*, and *Trichoderma*. Some
*Trichoderma* species are mycoparasites that directly attack mycelium, visible
as dark green mold parasitizing white fungal growth. Molds can sporulate even
under high CO2 concentrations found in [[mushroom-substrates]].

### Spot Treatment Protocol

1. Sprinkle mold-contaminated area with agricultural lime.
2. Fill a spray bottle with 10:1 dilution of bleach.
3. Spray one or two hard blasts directly onto the contaminated area.
4. For holes in columns or bags, wipe the surface with 70% isopropyl alcohol.
5. Allow to dry, then tape the hole with clear packing tape to monitor whether
   mold is contained.
6. In the lab, remove all contaminated agar plates, bags, or containers
   immediately. Rescue cultures using [[endophyte-isolation-techniques]].

## Bacteria Control

Bacterial problems in cultivation stem from two main causes. First, super-
supplemented substrates with high nitrogen levels promote bacterial growth over
mycelial growth, generating heat above 100 degrees F (38 degrees C) that cooks
mycelium. Thermophilic bacteria then outcompete fungi, creating marbled and
uneven colonization. Second, inadequate gas exchange or drainage creates
anaerobic zones populated by anaerobic bacteria. Solutions include lowering
nitrogen supplementation, ensuring adequate ventilation holes early in the spawn
run, providing drainage, and keeping spawn run temperature below 85 degrees F
(29 degrees C).

## Container Considerations

Containers and bags should minimize pest access. After inoculation, poke only
minute ventilation holes too small for flies to enter. After full colonization,
enlarge holes for mushroom emergence. Daily inspection of spawned media
monitors mycelial progress; fully colonized bags have lower contamination risk.
