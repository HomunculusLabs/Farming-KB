# [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] in Hydrocarbon Remediation

Arbuscular [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF) form symbiotic associations with the
roots of the vast majority of terrestrial plant species and play crucial
roles in nutrient uptake, particularly phosphorus acquisition. In
hydrocarbon-contaminated soils, AMF contribute to bioremediation
primarily by enabling plant establishment and survival on polluted sites,
thereby facilitating phytoremediation processes. Their role differs from
that of Mycorrhizal Soil Bioremediation, which
can directly degrade organic pollutants through ligninolytic enzyme
systems.

## AMF Symbiosis Fundamentals

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi belong to the phylum Glomeromycota and
form obligate symbiotic relationships with plant roots. The fungal
hyphae penetrate root cortical cells, forming highly branched arbuscules
that serve as the primary interface for nutrient exchange between the
fungus and host plant. The extensive external mycelial network extends
far beyond the root zone, greatly expanding the soil volume explored for
nutrient acquisition. In return for photosynthetically derived carbon
from the host plant, the fungus provides enhanced access to soil
nutrients, particularly immobile phosphate ions.

## Impact of Hydrocarbon Contamination on AMF

Hydrocarbon contamination of soil has complex effects on AMF populations
and their function. Field studies have shown that native AMF propagules
can survive habitat contamination by petroleum hydrocarbons. However,
several adverse effects have been documented:

- **Reduced colonization capacity**: The incorporation of hydrocarbon-
  containing sludges into soil reduces the capacity of native AMF
  propagules to initiate root colonization. Recovery to levels
  comparable to non-polluted soils is slow and begins from
  neighbouring uncontaminated areas.
- **Structural damage**: Pollution reduces the percentage of
  arbuscular structures within colonized roots. Since arbuscules
  are the primary sites for nutrient exchange between the fungal
  endophyte and host plant, this reduction indicates impairment
  of symbiotic function.
- **Altered community composition**: Hydrocarbon contamination can
  shift the dominant AMF species, with certain taxa showing greater
  tolerance than others.

## AMF in Oil-Contaminated Soils

AMF have been found colonizing plant roots on soils contaminated with
crude oil and petroleum derivatives. Studies have documented AMF
associations on plants growing at oil spill sites, on soils treated
with hydrocarbon-containing sludges, on oil shale wastes, and on coal
waste deposits. The ability of AMF to persist in these environments
suggests some degree of adaptive tolerance, although the specific
mechanisms of tolerance are not fully understood.

The mycorrhizal mycelium is more resistant to abiotic stressors than
the root itself, and this enhanced resistance may compensate for
reduced root growth under contaminant stress. By maintaining nutrient
uptake through the mycelial network, AMF can support plant survival
and growth in soils where root function would otherwise be severely
impaired by hydrocarbon toxicity.

## Mechanisms of Hydrocarbon Amelioration

AMF contribute to hydrocarbon remediation through several indirect
mechanisms rather than direct metabolic degradation of hydrocarbon
compounds:

- **Plant stress mitigation**: AMF enhance plant tolerance to
  hydrocarbon stress by improving nutrient and water uptake,
  allowing plants to survive and grow on contaminated sites where
  non-mycorrhizal plants would fail.
- **Rhizosphere enhancement**: The mycorrhizal mycelium modifies
  the rhizosphere environment, potentially stimulating the activity
  of hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria through improved soil aeration,
  root exudate production, and creation of microhabitats favorable
  for microbial activity.
- **Soil structure improvement**: AMF hyphae help bind soil
  particles, improving soil aggregation and potentially influencing
  the bioavailability and distribution of hydrocarbon contaminants.

## [[glomus-mosseae]] in Contaminated Soils

Glomus mosseae has been specifically studied for its ability to
colonize plants in hydrocarbon-contaminated soils. Inoculation of
alfalfa (Medicago sativa) with G. mosseae in soil plots treated
with hydrocarbon-containing sludges dramatically increased root
colonization compared to non-inoculated controls: 41% versus 5%
after 3 months, and 70% versus 15% after 12 months. These results
demonstrate that inoculation with selected AMF strains can
significantly enhance mycorrhizal establishment on contaminated
sites, supporting plant growth and potentially improving remediation
outcomes.

## Metal Contamination and AMF Tolerance

AMF are also found on plants colonizing soils contaminated with heavy
metals and metalloids. There is evidence that AMF evolve resistances
to metal contaminants, enabling them to colonize mine wastes. AMF
strains isolated from arsenic-contaminated mine spoils have been
shown to further enhance arsenate resistance of already-tolerant
grass species, suggesting that metal-resistant AMF may confer
additional protective benefits to their host plants beyond basic
nutrient acquisition.

## Limitations and Research Needs

AMF cannot be cultured in the absence of their host plant, which
complicates research and large-scale inoculum production. The
extracellular hyphal network of AMF associations is less extensive
than that of ectomycorrhizal fungi, potentially limiting their direct
impact on contaminated soil volumes. The POP-degrading activities of
AMF remain largely unexplored, and their primary contribution to
hydrocarbon remediation appears to be through plant support rather
than direct metabolism of contaminants.
