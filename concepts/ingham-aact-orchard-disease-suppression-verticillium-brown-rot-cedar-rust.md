# AACT Orchard Disease Suppression — Verticillium, Brown Rot, Cedar Apple Rust, and Late Blight

## Overview

Dr. [[elaine-ingham]]'s field guide documents extensive experience us [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] tea (AACT) to suppress major orchard and vine diseases including Verticillium wilt, brown rot (cherries), cedar apple rust, and late blight. These woody perennial and tree crop diseases require a comprehensive spray program that addresses both above-ground and below-ground infection pathways. The common thread across all these diseases is the need for **fungal-dominant teas** applied with precise timing relative to the plant's growth cycle and the pathogen's infection window.

## Verticillium Wilt

Verticillium wilt is a vascular disease that affects a wide range of fruit trees including apples, cherries, potatoes, and many ornamentals. Ingham's approach focuses on preventing the pathogen from accessing food, space, and leaf surfaces.

### The 70% Coverage Protocol

Ingham specifies a clear target for Verticillium suppression:

- Cover at least **70% of leaf surfaces, blossoms, bark, and dead leaves on the orchard floor** with both bacteria and fungi
- **Fungi must comprise at least 5% of that 70% coverage** — this minimum fungal threshold is critical
- Begin spraying **two weeks before bud break** to establish protective biology before the pathogen becomes active
- Continue applications through [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]

### Seasonal Application Schedule

1. **Fall application** — spray the orchard floor and downed leaves to decompose infected debris before overwintering
2. **Pre-budbreak** (2 weeks before bud break) — foliar spray to establish protective biology on emerging tissue
3. **Bloom through fruit set** — regular [[ingham-foliar-applications-compost-tea-disease-suppression]] every 10-14 days
4. **Post-harvest** — soil drench to protect roots during dormancy

### Why Verticillium Requires Fungi

Verticillium produces microsclerotia — hardy survival structures persisting in soil for years. Bacteria alone cannot effectively compete. Fungal competitors physically entrap microsclerotia in hyphal networks, produce degradative enzymes, and outcompete Verticillium for [[root-exudates]].

## Brown Rot (Cherries)

Brown rot, caused by *Monilinia* species, is a devastating disease of stone fruits, particularly cherries. Ingham notes that while there is **no replicated data on brown rot**, anecdotal field results have been consistently positive.

### AACT Approach for Brown Rot

The primary recommendation is to **make the tea as fungal as you can manage**:

1. Use well-aged fungal compost with visible white mycelial growth
2. Include fungal food sources: humic acids, fish hydrolysate, kelp
3. Minimize molasses — excessive sugars favor bacteria over fungi
4. Brew for 24-36 hours to maximize fungal hyphal development
5. Apply as a foliar spray targeting blossoms and developing fruit

### Critical Timing Points

- **Pre-bloom** — establish beneficial biology on branch surfaces before blossoms open
- **Bloom** — protect blossoms, which are the primary infection point
- **Fruit development** — maintain coverage on expanding fruit surfaces
- **Pre-harvest** — final protective spray before fruit ripens

### Why Fungal Dominance Matters

Brown rot colonizes fruit surfaces rapidly during warm, moist conditions. A pre-established fungal community prevents *Monilinia* spores from finding germination sites and competes for sugars exuding from ripening fruit.

## Cedar Apple Rust

Cedar apple rust (*Gymnosporangium juniperi-virginianae*) requires two hosts — cedar (juniper) and apple — to complete its life cycle. Ingham states that **AACT with a great set of fungi will stop cedar apple rust**.

### Application Strategy

- Apply fungal-dominant AACT to both the cedar host and the apple host
- Timing should correspond to the rust's teleospore release period (typically spring rains)
- Coverage must be thorough on both upper and lower leaf surfaces
- Multiple applications may be needed during extended wet spring periods

### Mechanism of Suppression

The fungal community suppresses cedar apple rust by occupying leaf surface sites where teliospores would germinate, competing for nutrients, producing antifungal compounds, and potentially parasitizing rust pustules.

## Late Blight

Ingham reports extensive experience with late blight (*[[bloomfield-potato-blight-phytophthora-infestans]]*), though less with early blight. Late blight is an oomycete (water mold) rather than a true fungus, but the AACT suppression approach follows similar principles.

### Late Blight Experience

The field guide documents AACT work with compost tea makers in preventing diseases including late blight, with positive results. The approach involves:

1. Regular foliar applications of fungal-dominant AACT
2. Combined [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] coverage of leaf surfaces
3. Prevention-focused timing — begin applications before disease pressure arrives
