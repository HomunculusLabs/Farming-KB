## Cellulolytic Enzyme Systems

Fungal cellulose degradation involves three main classes of enzymes working
synergistically. Endo-1,4-beta-glucanases randomly cleave internal beta-1,4-glycosidic
bonds in the cellulose chain, creating new chain ends. Exo-1,4-beta-glucanases (also
called cellobiohydrolases) processively remove cellobiose units from the reducing or
non-reducing ends of the cellulose chain. Finally, 1,4-beta-glucosidases hydrolyze
cellobiose and other short oligosaccharides to glucose, which can then be taken up and
metabolized by the fungus.

These three enzyme classes work in concert: endoglucanases create access points for
exoglucanases, which generate cellobiose that is then cleaved to glucose by glucosidases.
The efficiency of the complete system depends on the relative activities and spatial
coordination of all three components. The [[fungal-decomposition-kinetics]] and explains
why lignin-rich tissues decompose so slowly.

Temperature positively influences extracellular cellulase activity, representing a key
link between environmental conditions and decomposition rates. The rate of cellulose
degradation is thus a product of both the enzymatic competence of the fungal community
and the physical accessibility of cellulose within the plant litter matrix.

## Successional Patterns in Cellulose Decomposition

During fungal succession on decomposing plant material, the initial colonizers typically
use soluble carbohydrate sources such as sugars and starches. As these labile resources
are depleted, the community shifts toward fungal species with greater enzymatic
competence for cellulose and hemicellulose degradation. Frankland (1992) described
changes during decomposition of Pteridium aquilinum (bracken fern) litter, documenting
the progression from lesion-forming fungi on standing dead material through basidiomycete
colonization in relation to the rate of cellulose and lignin loss.

Ponge (1990, 1991) characterized four stages of pine needle colonization: initial
decomposition by fungi present at abscission (Lophodermium, Ceuthospora), followed by
invasion by decomposing microfungi (Verticicladium), then basidiomycete fungi (Marasmius,
Collybia), and finally invasion by soil arthropods and mycorrhizal fungi. The transition
from early-stage to late-stage fungi corresponds with progressive depletion of labile
carbon and increasing reliance on structural polysaccharides.

## White Rot vs. Brown Rot Strategies

The fungal approach to cellulose decomposition differs fundamentally between white rot
and brown rot decay types. White rot basidiomycetes possess the full complement of
cellulases and can simultaneously degrade both cellulose and lignin, producing a
characteristic white, cellulose-rich residue. Brown rot fungi, in contrast, use a
non-enzymatic Fenton chemistry (generating hydroxyl radicals from hydrogen peroxide and
ferrous iron) to depolymerize cellulose while leaving behind a brown, lignin-enriched
residue.

achieve more complete decomposition of lignocellulose, while brown rot fungi rapidly
