# Dissolved Oxygen Critical Thresholds in Compost Tea

## Overview

Dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration is the single most critical variable in actively aerated compost tea (AACT) brewing. Maintaining adequate oxygen levels determines whether beneficial or detrimental organisms dominate the final product. Dr. Elaine Ingham's research has established clear thresholds that govern organism survival, particularly for the beneficial fungi essential to disease suppression.

## The Critical 5.5 ppm Threshold

Beneficial fungi require a minimum dissolved oxygen concentration of **5.5 to 6 ppm** to survive and function. When oxygen levels drop below this threshold during brewing, composting, or in soil, the beneficial fungal community is rapidly lost. This finding has been replicated consistently across multiple tea brewers and compost samples tested at the Soil Foodweb laboratory.

The consequences of oxygen dropping below 5.5 ppm are severe:

- Beneficial fungal hyphae die off rapidly
- Anaerobic bacteria begin to proliferate
- The fungal:bacterial ratio shifts toward bacterial dominance
- Disease suppression capacity is compromised
- Pathogens such as *E. coli* can establish

This threshold applies equally to tea, compost, and soil environments. Any system that allows dissolved oxygen to fall below this level will lose its beneficial fungal component regardless of other conditions.

## Oxygen Dynamics During Brewing

During a typical 24-hour AACT brew cycle, dissolved oxygen follows a predictable pattern driven by microbial respiration:

1. **Initial phase**: Oxygen levels are typically at saturation (8 to 10 ppm depending on temperature and altitude). Organisms extracted from compost begin metabolizing food resources added to the brew.

2. **Rapid growth phase**: Bacteria begin multiplying rapidly, consuming oxygen at increasing rates. If bacterial foods such as molasses at concentrations between 0.5 percent and 3 percent are present, this phase can cause an extremely rapid drop in dissolved oxygen as bacterial biomass explodes.

3. **Equilibrium phase**: In well-designed brewers with adequate aeration, the rate of oxygen input through bubbling or pumping equals or exceeds the rate of microbial consumption, stabilizing DO above the critical threshold.

4. **Oxygen depletion**: In poorly aerated systems or when excessive food resources are added, oxygen consumption outpaces input. DO drops below 5.5 ppm, beneficial fungi die, and anaerobic conditions develop.

## Food Resource Impact on Oxygen

The type and amount of food added to a brew directly affects oxygen dynamics:

- **Low concentration molasses (below 0.5 percent)**: May not provide enough energy for significant microbial growth, resulting in modest oxygen demand.

- **Medium concentration (0.5 to 3 percent)**: This range is the most dangerous for oxygen management. Bacteria bloom rapidly, consuming oxygen faster than most aeration systems can replenish it. This is the zone where brews most frequently go anaerobic.

- **High concentration (above 5 percent)**: Paradoxically, high sugar concentrations can prevent anaerobic conditions through osmotic shock. The elevated osmotic pressure inhibits the rapidly-growing bacteria that would otherwise consume all available oxygen. Fewer bacteria growing means lower oxygen demand, allowing the brew to remain aerobic. Beneficial fungi, which tolerate higher osmotic conditions, gain a competitive advantage.

## Why pH Is Not a Reliable Oxygen Indicator

Ingham has noted that measuring pH changes too slowly to serve as a useful indicator of declining oxygen levels in compost tea. By the time pH shifts enough to signal a problem, dissolved oxygen has likely already dropped below the critical threshold and beneficial organisms have been lost. Direct dissolved oxygen measurement using a calibrated DO probe remains the only reliable method for monitoring aerobic conditions during brewing.

Active bacteria and active fungi assays are the next best indicators if a DO probe is unavailable. These measurements reveal whether organisms remained metabolically active throughout the brew cycle, which is only possible if adequate oxygen was maintained.

## Anaerobic Conditions and Selective Factors

When oxygen drops below the critical threshold, three selective factors create conditions favoring disease organisms over beneficials:

1. **Reduced oxygen**: Anaerobic and facultatively anaerobic bacteria outcompete strict aerobes. Organisms like *Streptococcus*, *E. coli*, and fecal coliforms gain competitive advantages.

2. **Accumulation of undigested organic acids**: Anaerobic metabolism produces organic acids (acetic, butyric, lactic) that are toxic to many beneficial organisms but tolerated by pathogens.

3. **High nitrate concentrations**: In compacted or anaerobic soils, nitrate accumulates because denitrification is incomplete. High nitrate favors certain pathogenic organisms.

These three factors work synergistically. The dairy case study documented by Ingham demonstrated how un-composted dairy waste applied to soil creates precisely these conditions, leading to *Streptococcus* proliferation and suppression of beneficial biology. Once these selective conditions are established, simply applying compost tea may not be sufficient to restore the beneficial community. The underlying conditions (compaction, excess nitrate, undigested organics) must also be addressed.

## Oxygen and Fungal Species Sensitivity

Not all fungi respond identically to low oxygen. The beneficial fungi needed for disease suppression, including those that produce antibiotics, compete for space, and consume pathogens, are consistently the most oxygen-sensitive organisms in the compost tea system. When dissolved oxygen drops below 5.5 ppm, these beneficial species are lost first.

Pathogenic fungi and facultative anaerobes tolerate lower oxygen conditions. This creates a perverse selection effect: when oxygen becomes limiting, the organisms that are lost are the ones performing disease suppression, while organisms that may cause disease survive. The result is a tea that looks cloudy and has high total organism counts but lacks the specific beneficial species needed for plant protection.

This is why direct microscopy assessment is essential. Total bacterial or fungal counts alone cannot distinguish between a tea dominated by beneficial species and one dominated by opportunists or pathogens that survived anaerobic conditions.

## Water Temperature Effects

Water temperature directly affects the amount of dissolved oxygen a liquid can hold. At higher temperatures, oxygen solubility decreases. This interaction between temperature and oxygen capacity means that summer brewing requires more careful monitoring than spring or fall brewing. Ingham notes that at water temperatures near 100 degrees Fahrenheit (38 degrees Celsius), even well-aerated systems may struggle to maintain adequate oxygen.

The implication is that molasses concentrations that are safe in cool spring conditions may drive a brew anaerobic in hot summer conditions. Brewers should adjust their recipes seasonally, using less food during warm periods and monitoring dissolved oxygen more frequently.

## Monitoring and Management Strategies

Successful oxygen management requires:

- **Calibrated DO meter**: Measure dissolved oxygen at the beginning, middle, and end of every brew cycle. Target minimum 6 ppm throughout.

- **Temperature awareness**: Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen. As water temperature approaches 100 degrees Fahrenheit (38 degrees Celsius), even well-aerated systems may struggle to maintain adequate oxygen levels.

- **Food resource management**: Start with less food rather than more. A brew that grows slowly but remains aerobic is far superior to a brew that grows rapidly but goes anaerobic.

- **Compost removal**: Taking compost out of the brewer partway through the brewing cycle can help maintain aerobic conditions. Organisms growing within the compost mass can be difficult to aerate effectively, and their continued respiration may deplete oxygen faster than the aeration system can replenish it.

- **Brewer design matters**: Some commercial brewers consistently maintain adequate DO while others do not, even with identical compost, recipes, and water. The efficiency of air diffusion, water circulation patterns, and compost containment design all affect oxygen availability.

## Soil Oxygen and Root-Feeding Pests

The relationship between soil oxygen and pest populations provides additional evidence for the importance of maintaining aerobic conditions. Root-feeding nematodes, cutworms, and wireworms prefer reduced oxygen conditions, likely in the range of 5 to 6 ppm dissolved oxygen. When soil becomes anaerobic due to excess sugar addition or compaction, these pests may be temporarily driven away. However, this approach is problematic because anaerobic conditions also harm seed germination and root growth.

The more productive strategy is to add just enough sugar to stimulate beneficial bacterial growth without driving the soil anaerobic. Beneficial bacteria produce glues that form microaggregates, improving soil structure and allowing oxygen to diffuse more rapidly into the soil. As soil structure improves and aerobic conditions return, the beneficial organisms that prey on root-feeding pests can recolonize. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: better structure leads to more oxygen, which supports more beneficial predators, which reduce pest populations.

## Compost Oxygen Requirements

For compost production, the same oxygen principles apply but are expressed differently:

- Maintain dissolved gases at or above 5.5 ppm throughout the composting cycle
- Turn compost whenever temperature approaches 68 to 70 degrees Celsius (155 to 158 degrees Fahrenheit) to re-introduce oxygen
- The compost should maintain a pleasant smell throughout the process; offensive odors indicate anaerobic pockets
- Moisture content should remain between 45 and 75 percent to support aerobic microbial activity while allowing gas exchange
- For vermicompost: maintain adequate earthworm populations, room temperature, and oxygen levels throughout processing

## Signs of Oxygen Depletion

Several indicators can signal declining oxygen before a DO meter confirms the problem:

- **Foul or sour smell**: The most reliable field indicator. Aerobic compost and tea smell earthy and pleasant. Anaerobic material smells sour, putrid, or like sewage.

- **Color changes**: Compost that was dark brown may develop grey or black patches where anaerobic conditions prevail.

- **Temperature patterns**: In compost, failure to reach thermophilic temperatures or unexpected cooling may indicate oxygen limitation preventing microbial activity.

- **Tea appearance**: Foam or scum on the tea surface, cloudy or milky appearance, and reduced foam production can all indicate shifts in microbial populations associated with oxygen stress.
