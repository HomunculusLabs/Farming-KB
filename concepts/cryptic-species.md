# Cryptic Species

Cryptic species are two or more distinct species that are morphologically
identical or nearly indistinguishable but reproductively isolated and
genetically distinct. They cannot be reliably told apart by traditional
morphological examination and are recognized only through molecular,
behavioral, ecological, or reproductive data. Cryptic speciation is
particularly prevalent [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]].

## Prevalence [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]] organism's genome.

- **Convergent evolution**: Similar ecological pressures can produce
  morphologically identical structures in unrelated lineages. Mushroom
  morphology, in particular, has evolved independently many times. The classic
  gilled mushroom form has arisen in at least a dozen independent lineages.

- **Asexual reproduction**: Many fungi reproduce primarily or exclusively
  asexually, eliminating the biological species concept as a practical
  framework. Asexual lineages can accumulate substantial genetic divergence
  without any morphological change. This is especially common in
  Ascomycota, where many important genera (*Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*,
  *Fusarium*) contain predominantly asexual species.

- **Phenotypic plasticity**: Fungal morphology can vary dramatically with
  environmental conditions, making it difficult to assess whether morphological
  differences reflect genetic divergence or environmental effects.

Molecular phylogenetic studies consistently reveal that described fungal
"species" often comprise multiple cryptic lineages. For example, the
cosmopolitan moaspergillus-fumigatusatus* was found to contain at least two
cryptic species with different ecological niches and drug susceptibilities.
Similarly, the common plant pafusarium-graminearumminearum* was split into
at least 15 phylogenetically distinct species with different host ranges
and toxin profiles.

## Detection Methods

Several approaches are used to uncover cryptic species:

### Molecular Phylogenetics

[[environmental-dna-sequencing-fungi]] of conserved loci (ITS, LSU, RPB1, RPB2, TEF1) reveals
phylogenetic lineages within morphologically defined species. Concordance
across multiple gene trees strengthens the case for cryptic speciation.
Multi-locus [[fungi-multilocus-sequence-typing-molecular-epidemiology]] (MLST) and whole-genome sequencing provide
increasing resolution. Whole-genome data can reveal speciation events
through patterns of linkage disequilibrium and recombination.

### Reproductive Isolation Tests

For sexually reproducing fungi, mating experiments can demonstrate
reproductive barriers between populations. Inter-fertility (or lack
thereof) provides direct evidence of species boundaries, though
asexual lineages require other approaches. Laboratory mating trials
between isolates from different geographic regions or host species
have revealed many cryptic species complexes.

Cryptic species often occupy different ecological niches, host

Coalescent-based methods and species delimitation algorithms (GMYC,
BPP, STACEY) use patterns of genetic variation within and between
populations to statistically test for cryptic species boundaries.
These approaches can identify independently evolving lineages even
when morphological data are uninformative.

## Implications for Biodiversity Estimates

Cryptic species significantly inflate the gap between described and
estimated [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]]. If even 30% of described fungal species
contain unrecognized cryptic lineages, the true species count could be
substantially higher than current estimates suggest. This has profound
implications for conservation, as cryptic species may have different
conservation statuses, ecological roles, and geographic distributions
than their "parent" species.
