## Fall and Winter Planting Schedules for Cold Houses

## Overview

Successful winter harvesting depends on precise planting schedules that account for decreasing day length, declining temperatures, and the specific requirements of each protected growing environment. [[eliot-coleman]]'s [[four-season-farm]] system uses four distinct planting sites for fall and winter production: outdoor fields (harvested until early November), early greenhouses, later greenhouses, and a dedicated [[cool-greenhouse]]. Each site has its own planting window, determined by when protection becomes available and how cold the environment gets.

## The Four Planting Sites

### 1. Outdoor Fields

Outdoor sowings are made earliest, typically beginning in late July through mid-September. These crops grow in open conditions and must reach harvestable size before hard frosts end the outdoor season in early November. The outdoor season serves as a transitional bridge between summer production and the winter greenhouse harvest.

### 2. Early Greenhouses

Early greenhouses receive their first sowings in late August through early October. These structures provide protection from frost sooner than later houses, allowing for an extended harvest window. Crops sown here benefit from warmer soil temperatures in early fall and the passive solar heating that even unheated greenhouses provide.

### 3. Later Greenhouses

Later greenhouses are planted from mid-September through mid-October. The slightly later planting dates reflect the fact that these structures may not be fully enclosed or moved into position until later in the season. The harvest from later greenhouses typically begins in late fall and extends through the deepest part of winter.

### 4. Cool Greenhouses

The cool greenhouse represents the most protected but coldest growing environment. Sowings here range from early October through early December. The cool greenhouse is used for crops that need minimal heat but reliable frost protection. Multiple succession sowings are common, with some crops like arugula receiving sowings as late as December 10 to ensure continuous winter harvest.

## Key Crop Schedules

### Arugula

Arugula is one of the most cold-hardy salad greens and responds well to succession planting across all four sites. Outdoor sowings begin September 4 and continue through September 16. Early greenhouse sowings run from September 22 through October 2. Late house plantings occur around September 10. The cool greenhouse receives the latest sowings, from October 20 through December 10, with multiple successions to maintain steady production through winter.

### Lettuce

Lettuce is planted across all sites with careful attention to variety selection and timing. Outdoor sowings span August 29 through September 6. Early greenhouse dates run September 13-17. Late greenhouse plantings are the most extensive, with sowings from September 19 through October 12 in multiple successions. Cool greenhouse sowings begin October 24 and continue through November 11. Lettuce growth slows dramatically as day length drops below 10 hours, so sowings must be timed so plants reach near-full size before the Persephone period.

### Mizuna

Mizuna, a Japanese mustard green, is sown in outdoor fields beginning August 26 through August 30, in early greenhouses around September 2-6, in late greenhouses around September 10, and in the cool greenhouse from October 1 onward. Its cold tolerance makes it one of the most reliable winter crops.

### Mâche (Corn Salad)

Mâche is the quintessential winter salad green, naturally adapted to short days and cold temperatures. Sowings in the cool greenhouse run from October 6 through November 5, with multiple successions. Mâche grows very slowly but can survive temperatures well below freezing, making it a cornerstone of the winter harvest.

## Bold Dates and Succession Planting

In the planting schedule system, dates printed in bold indicate sowings planned for multiple harvests. These are typically crops that can be [[cut-and-come-again]], harvested repeatedly over weeks or months. The distinction matters for planning purposes: single-harvest crops (like head lettuce or root vegetables) need new successions for continued production, while cut-and-come-again crops (like arugula, mizuna, and claytonia) can be picked over many times from a single sowing.

## The Importance of Keeping Notes

Coleman emphasizes that the published dates are a starting point, not a fixed prescription. He explicitly states: "We are not entirely satisfied with the precision of these dates yet and will be adding more dates as we fine-tune our production in the years to come." The best advice for any grower adopting this system is to sow often and keep detailed notes. Local microclimate, soil conditions, variety selection, and the specific design of the growing structure all influence optimal planting dates. Each farm must develop its own schedule through careful observation and record-keeping over multiple seasons.

## Relationship to Day Length

The fundamental constraint on all winter planting schedules is day length. When days drop below 10 hours, plant growth effectively stops regardless of temperature. The goal of fall planting is to have crops reach harvestable size before this Persephone period begins. After the winter solstice, as days lengthen again, growth resumes and the stored crops begin producing new leaves. This biological reality is what makes the timing of fall sowings so critical — too early and crops may bolt or become overmature before winter; too late and they won't have enough vegetative mass to survive the period of dormancy.

## Historical Context and Reading

The [[winter-harvest-system]] builds on centuries of intensive vegetable production knowledge. Key historical texts include works by the French maraîchers (market gardeners) of Paris, who developed sophisticated systems for year-round vegetable production using manure-heated hotbeds, cloches, and glass structures in the 19th century. British market gardeners adapted many of these techniques. Modern practitioners like Coleman have updated these methods using plastic greenhouse technology, floating row covers, and improved cold-hardy varieties. Important references include Weathers' *French Market-Gardening* (1909), Smith's *French Gardening* (1909), and Lawrence's *Science and the Glasshouse* (1948), as well as the French technical manual *Légumes sous Baches* by Gerst (1993).

## Practical Considerations

- **No dates for uncertain crops**: The schedule deliberately omits planting dates for crops where recommendations are not yet proven. This honesty prevents growers from wasting seed and space on poorly timed plantings.
- **Multiple successions are essential**: Rather than a single sowing date per crop, the system uses multiple dates spread across weeks. This spreads risk (if one sowing fails, others may succeed) and ensures continuous harvest rather than a glut followed by scarcity.
- **Site-specific timing**: Each of the four planting sites has its own microclimate and protection level. The same crop may be sown 6-8 weeks earlier outdoors than in the cool greenhouse, because the greenhouse extends the growing season into much colder months.
- **Bold = multiple harvests**: The bold formatting convention in the schedule helps growers identify which sowings are expected to produce repeated cuts, informing decisions about how much space to allocate and when to expect diminishing returns from older plantings.
