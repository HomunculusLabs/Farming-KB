# Neurotropic Fungi: Classification and Taxonomy

## Overview

Neurotropic (hallucinogenic, psychotropic, or entheogenic) fungi represent a diverse
assemblage of mushroom species distributed across multiple fungal families and orders.
The seminal work by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000) catalogued 216 species of
neurotropic fungi worldwide, organized into four principal groups based on their
active chemical constituents and ethnobotanical significance. The taxonomy of these
fungi remains complex and subject to ongoing revision, with frequent misidentifications
and contradictory chemical analyses complicating the literature.

## The Four Groups of Neurotropic Fungi

### Group 1: Psilocybin and Related Indole Compounds

The largest group encompasses fungi containing psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin,
and/or norbaeocystin. These are primarily tryptamine derivatives structurally
similar to serotonin. The group spans 13 genera:

- **Psilocybe** — 116 species, the dominant genus
- **Gymnopilus** — 14 species
- **Panaeolus** — 13 species
- **Copelandia** — 12 species
- **Hypholoma** — 6 species
- **Pluteus** — 6 species
- **Inocybe** — 6 species
- **Conocybe** — 4 species
- **Panaeolina** — 4 species
- **Gerronema** — 2 species
- **Agrocybe**, **Galerina**, **Mycena** — 1 species each

The bluing reaction (blue color change when tissue is bruised or damaged) is a
key field characteristic strongly correlated with psilocybin content, though not
universal across all psilocybian species. Within Psilocybe, the bluing sections
include Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae,
Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum.

### Group 2: Ibotenic Acid-Containing Fungi

This group contains species with ibotenic acid and muscimol as primary active
compounds, structurally unrelated to tryptamines:

- **Amanita muscaria** (Fly Agaric) — the most culturally significant species
- **Amanita pantherina** (Panther Cap)
- **Amanita regalis**

These species form mycorrhizal associations with Pinus and Betula in northern
hemisphere forests. Their chemistry and effects differ markedly from psilocybin-
containing fungi. Several closely related Amanita species are lethally toxic,
contributing to their lower recreational popularity.

### Group 3: Ergot Fungi

The Claviceps group produces ergot alkaloids, including lysergic acid derivatives:

- **[[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]** — the classic ergot parasite of cereals
- **Claviceps nigricans**, **C. paspali**, **C. rolfesii**, **C. tripsicii**
- **Cordyceps capitata** and **C. ophioglossoides**

The Cordyceps species are taxonomically related to Claviceps and have been used
sacredly by indigenous peoples in Mexico, particularly in the Nevado de Toluca
region, where they are consumed alongside Psilocybe muliercula in nocturnal
ceremonies. The host genus Elaphomyces (truffle fungi) is placed at the center
of the ceremony as a "director."

### Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Confirmed Chemistry

Several genera are used as sacred fungi by indigenous peoples but lack reliable
chemical analyses confirming neurotropic properties:

- **Boletus** — 6 species
- **Heimiella** — 2 species
- **Russula** — 6 species
- **Gasteromycetes** (puffballs and stinkhorns) — 6 species across 3 genera

Heim (1963, 1966, 1978) reported boletes as hallucinogenic in New Guinea, though
Ott (1993) later stated they do not possess neurotropic properties. The Mixtec
Indians of Oaxaca traditionally used puffballs ([[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]], L.
marginatum) as narcotic fungi, but Ott et al. (1975) demonstrated these were
edible species used in contexts suggesting confusion with psilocybin fungi.

## Taxonomic Challenges

### Misidentification Problems

Many chemical studies lack a solid taxonomic foundation. Guzmán found that material
used by Leslie and Repke to isolate psilocybin was actually a mixture of Panaeolus
spp., Psilocybe mexicana, and P. coprophila, all identified as P. coprophila.

[[panaeolus-antillarum-misidentification-copelandia-confusion]] is frequently confused with Copelandia spp. by recreational
users because both present white fruit bodies growing on cow dung. However,
P. antillarum does not bruise blue and is considered an edible species.

### Conflicting Chemical Reports

[[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]] illustrates the contradictions in the literature. Multiple
studies found no psilocybin (Mantle & Waight, 1969; Ott & Guzmán, 1976; Stijve,
1987), while others reported its presence (Robbers et al., 1969; Pollock, 1976).
The consensus is that it is more likely toxic than neurotropic.

### Specimen Age and Potency Degradation

The age of studied specimens significantly affects chemical analysis results.
Repke et al. (1977) showed variations in baeocystin content according to specimen
age, with no detectable indoles in Psilocybe baeocystis and P. cyanescens specimens
analyzed 20-60 days after collection. Ohenoja et al. (1987) found decreasing
psilocybin levels in [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] of P. semilanceata: 0.014% (1869), 0.67%
(1954), and 0.84% (1976) dry weight. This confirms [[mexican-indigenous-nomenclature-sacred-mushrooms]] wisdom that
specimens stored for more than one year are not suitable for use.

## Five Historical Periods

Guzmán proposed five periods in the study of neurotropic fungi:

1. **Ancient use** by indigenous peoples in Mesoamerica, Siberia, and New Guinea
2. **Erroneous early studies** in the early 20th century
3. **Scientific investigation** beginning with Wasson's studies in the 1950s
4. **Recreational use era** and decline of traditional practices (1960s onward)
5. **Recapitulation** — renewed species descriptions and chemical analyses
