# Mahayana vs Hinayana Natural Farming

In "The Natural Way of Farming," Masanobu Fukuoka draws on Buddhist terminology to
categorize three distinct approaches to agriculture: **Mahayana natural farming**,
**Hinayana natural farming**, and **[[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-food-chain-unity]]. The farmer
devotes himself entirely to the service of nature, living freely as an integral
part of the natural world and subsisting on its bounty without resorting to
purposeful effort.

Fukuoka describes this relationship as like an ideal marriage in which partners
realize a perfect life without asking for, giving, or receiving anything of each
other. Mahayana farming is the embodiment of life in accordance with nature.
Those who live such a life are hermits and wise men. It transcends time and space
and reaches the zenith of understanding and enlightenment.

This is the "no-sword school" — comparison with other approaches is impossible
because Mahayana farming exists on an entirely different plane. Nature encompasses
everything. No matter how desperately man struggles, he will never be more than a
small, imperfect part of its totality. Scientific farming, being inherently
incomplete, can never hope to attain the immutable absoluteness of natural farming.

## The Relative World: Hinayana Natural Farming

Hinayana natural farming exists in the same world of relativity as scientific
farming, and the two may be directly compared. This approach arises when man
earnestly seeks entry to the realm of Mahayana farming — desirous of the true
blessings and bounty of nature, he prepares himself to receive it. The
relationship between man and nature is like that of a lover who yearns after his
loved one and asks for her hand, but has not realized full union.

Hinayana farming belongs to what Fukuoka calls "idealistic farming," encompassing
what is commonly called natural farming and [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]]. Here, man exists in a state of contradiction: basically
