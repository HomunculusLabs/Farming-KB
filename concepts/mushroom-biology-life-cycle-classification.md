# Mushroom Biology: Life Cycle and Classification

## From the Mushroom Growers' Handbook 1: Oyster Mushroom Cultivation

Understanding mushroom biology is fundamental to successful cultivation. Mushrooms
occupy a unique position in the biological kingdom — neither plant nor animal, they
belong to the Kingdom Fungi and play essential roles as decomposers, symbionts, and
recyclers in virtually every terrestrial ecosystem.

## Definition and Kingdom Classification

A mushroom is defined as "a macrofungus with a distinctive fruiting body which can
be either epigeous (above ground) or hypogeous (below ground)." Macrofungi have
fruiting bodies large enough to be seen with the naked eye and picked up by hand.

Mushrooms were once classified in Kingdom Plantae but now belong to Kingdom Fungi
due to unique fungal characteristics. Unlike green plants, mushrooms are heterotrophs
— lacking chlorophyll, they cannot generate nutrients through photosynthesis and must
obtain nutrients from external sources.

## The Five Phyla of Fungi

The Kingdom Fungi encompasses five major phyla, each representing distinct
evolutionary lineages and ecological strategies:

- **Ascomycota**: Sac fungi, ranging from yeast to large cup fungi. Many important
  plant pathogens and the truffle belong here
- **Basidiomycota**: Higher fungi including toadstools, puffballs, and bracket
  fungi. Most [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]] (including oyster mushrooms) are basidiomycetes
- **Zygomycota**: Molds, mycorrhizal fungi, and soil decomposers. Bread mold
  (Rhizopus) is a well-known member
- **Chytridiomycota**: Primitive fungi, chytrids. Mostly aquatic, some are
  important pathogens of amphibians
- **Deuteromycota**: Fungi that reproduce only asexually (the "imperfect fungi")

## The Mushroom Life Cycle

Mushrooms breed by spores, analogous to seeds in plants. Under proper conditions,
spores germinate into hyphae — the filamentous threads that collectively form the
[[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] (mycelium). The life cycle proceeds through distinct stages:

### Vegetative Growth Phase
Germinated hyphae form primary mycelia, which then fuse through plasmogamy
(hyphal fusion) to form secondary mycelia. This mycelial network colonizes the
substrate, secreting digestive enzymes that break down complex organic molecules
into simpler, absorbable nutrients. This is the growth phase where the fungus
accumulates energy and resources.

### Reproductive Growth Phase
When [[fungal-fruiting-body-formation-environmental-triggers]] occur — typically lower temperature, high humidity,
increased oxygen, and sometimes light — the mycelium ceases vegetative growth and
forms primordia (pins). These pins differentiate into caps and stems, developing
into mature fruiting bodies. Under the cap, spores are produced in the gills.
Mature fruiting bodies release spores to begin the next generation.

## Trophic Patterns: How Mushrooms Feed

Mushrooms can be classified by their trophic (feeding) pattern into three categories:

### Saprophytes
Decomposers that grow on dead organic matter — wood, leaves, straw, and other plant
residues. Most commonly cultivated mushrooms are saprophytes. Primary decomposers
(like oyster mushrooms) can break down raw lignocellulosic materials directly.
Secondary decomposers (like button mushrooms) require substrate already partially
degraded by bacteria or other fungi.

### Parasites
Fungi that grow on [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]], feeding on them while contributing nothing to
their host's survival. Some cultivated mushrooms have parasitic phases, though most
commercial cultivation focuses on saprophytic species.

### Mycorrhizae
Fungi that form symbiotic associations with plant roots. The mycelium extends the
root system's reach for water and minerals, while the plant provides carbohydrates
to the fungus. Many forest mushrooms (chanterelles, porcini, matsutake) are
mycorrhizal and extremely difficult to cultivate because they require living host
plants.

## Nutritional Requirements

Mushrooms require carbon, nitrogen, and inorganic compounds. The main nutrient
sources are carbon-containing compounds: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Most
organic matter containing these compounds can serve as substrate.

Different species have different nutritional preferences. Button mushrooms require
relatively high nitrogen (optimal C/N ratio of 17), while oyster mushrooms and
shiitake require less nitrogen and more carbon. Mushrooms secrete digestive enzymes
into the substrate and absorb dissolved nutrients.

Mushrooms are also influenced by substrate acidity, with optimal pH ranging from
6 to 8 depending on species.

## The Unique Role of Fungi in Ecosystems

Fungi occupy a unique ecological niche as the primary decomposers of lignin and
cellulose — the two most abundant organic compounds on Earth. While bacteria can
decompose many organic materials, only fungi produce the lignin-degrading enzymes
(peroxidases and laccases) necessary to break down woody materials.

This makes mushrooms the only organisms by which cellulose is dissolved, absorbed,
and transformed into food for mankind. They are essential agents in the global
carbon cycle, converting dead plant material into forms usable by other organisms.

## Why Mushroom Biology Matters for Cultivation

Successful cultivation requires understanding optimal conditions for both vegetative
and reproductive phases of each species. Mushroom mycelia grow best at 20-30°C,
while pinning occurs at 10-20°C. Over 80% of the fruitbody is water, requiring
careful [[mushroom-casing-moisture-management]]. Being aerobic fungi, mushrooms need fresh air — more
during reproductive than vegetative growth.

The key insight: no matter how well the substrate is colonized, cultivation fails if
environmental conditions don't trigger the transition from vegetative to reproductive
growth. The most important factor in mushroom cultivation is [[chen-maitake-growth-parameters-environmental-control]].
