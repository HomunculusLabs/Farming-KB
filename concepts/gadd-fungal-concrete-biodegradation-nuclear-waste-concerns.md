# Fungal Deterioration of Concrete and Nuclear Waste Storage Concerns

## Overview

Any type of building or ceramic material, including concrete and cement, can be degraded by fungi. This capacity for biodeterioration arises from the same mechanisms fungi employ in [[fungal-bioweathering-and-mineral-transformations]] of natural rocks and minerals — a combination of biomechanical and biochemical processes acting in synergy. Under certain conditions, the deterioration of concrete by fungi may be **more efficient** than that caused by bacteria, making fungi a particularly significant agent of [[mycorestoration]] in built environments.

The recognition that fungal attack on concrete proceeds via the same mechanisms as mineral weathering underscores the geological reach of fungal biogeochemical activity: the same organisms that weather mountainsides and shape soil profiles can also compromise engineered structures designed to last for millennia. This convergence of natural and engineered mineral degradation pathways demands attention from both materials scientists and geomicrobiologists.

## Scope of Fungal Attack on Building Materials

Fungal weathering has been documented across a wide range of building stone types:

- **Limestone, sandstone, marble, and granite** — all subject to fungal biodeterioration
- **Concrete and cement** — susceptible to fungal degradation under appropriate conditions
- **Glass** (both natural and artificial, including antique and medieval) — also vulnerable

Clump-like colonies of epilithic and endolithic darkly pigmented microcolonial fungi are common inhabitants of limestone, sandstone, marble, and granite in hot and cold deserts and semi-arid regions, where they are associated with pitting and etching of rock surfaces. These communities are ubiquitous components of the microflora of all rocks and building stone, spanning a wide range of geographical and climatic zones.

Microcolonial fungi are also common inhabitants of biogenic oxalate crusts on granitic rocks, where their [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] contributes to the ongoing transformation of mineral surfaces. Fungi are also known to contribute to the weathering of silicate-bearing rocks (mica and orthoclase) and iron- and manganese-bearing minerals (biotite, olivine, and pyroxene), further illustrating the breadth of their mineral-degrading capabilities.

The occurrence of lithic (rock-surface), hypolithic (under pebbles), chasmolithic (in crevices), endolithic (within rock), and cryptoendolithic (within pore spaces) fungal communities demonstrates the full spectrum of rock-colonizing strategies available to these organisms, ensuring that few mineral surfaces remain entirely free from fungal influence.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Deterioration

### Biomechanical Weathering

Fungi degrade mineral substrates through biomechanical and biochemical weathering acting in synergy. Two synergistic actions — biomechanical and biochemical — drive the degradation of mineral substrates.

Biomechanical weathering can be direct or indirect:

- **Direct biomechanical degradation** occurs through extensive penetration by fungal hyphae into decayed rocks and tunnelling into otherwise intact mineral matter, progressing along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries in calcitic and dolomitic rocks, sandstone, and concrete.
- **[[hyphal-turgor-pressure-fungal-growth]]** — fungal hyphae can exert considerable mechanical force derived from osmotically generated turgor pressure within the hyphae, enabling penetration into concrete and rock matrices.
- **Thigmotropic growth** — contact guidance (thigmotropism) directs fungal growth towards grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material, enabling hyphae to explore and exploit weakened sites, cracks, and fissures in mineral surfaces.
- **Indirect biomechanical weathering** can occur through shrinking and swelling effects of the hydrated mucilaginous slime produced by many fungi.

Biomechanical penetration into mineral matter is facilitated by thigmotropic reactions and lubrication with mucilaginous slime, which may itself contain acidic and metal-chelating metabolites, blurring the boundary between mechanical and chemical degradation. All biomechanical weathering processes are strongly connected with biochemical processes, which are believed to be much more important than mechanical mineral degradation alone.

### Biochemical Weathering

The main mechanisms of solubilization of minerals and insoluble metal compounds by fungi are [[fungal-bioweathering-and-mineral-transformations|acidolysis]], complexolysis, and redoxolysis, all of which may be enhanced by metal accumulation in and/or around the fungal biomass. The primary fungal impact on biogeochemical cycling appears to result from acidolysis and complexolysis.

#### Acidolysis

Acidolysis (proton-promoted dissolution) occurs when fungi acidify their microenvironment through:

- Excretion of protons
- Excretion of organic acids
- Formation of carbonic acid from respiratory CO₂

Many fungi are capable of excreting large amounts of organic acids, with [[fungal-bioweathering-and-mineral-transformations|acidolysis]] identified as the primary mechanism of fungal dissolution of minerals grown in the presence of ammonium as a nitrogen source. This process is central to what has been termed "[[staycare-fungal-heterotrophic-leaching-biosorption]]" — the fungal-mediated release of metal cations from mineral matrices through acidification. The process of respiration-driven CO₂ dissolution in water to form carbonic acid represents an additional, often overlooked, pathway by which even non-acid-excreting fungi may contribute to mineral dissolution.

#### Complexolysis

Fungi excrete metal-complexing metabolites associated with complexolysis (ligand-promoted dissolution), including:

- Carboxylic acids (especially oxalic and citric acid, which perform aggressive attack on mineral surfaces)
- Amino acids
- Siderophores
- [[ingham-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds]]

Carboxylic acids provide both a source of protons for solubilization and chelating anions that can complex metal cations. When a fungal culture is capable of excreting large amounts of a strong chelator (e.g., oxalate), the solubilization mechanism shifts toward ligand-promoted dissolution. Organic acid excretion by fungi is inter- and intraspecific and can be strongly influenced by the presence of toxic metal minerals.

#### Redoxolysis

Redoxolysis constitutes an additional mechanism by which fungi can transform mineral substrates, operating through redox reactions that alter the oxidation state of constituent metals. This process contributes to the broader suite of biochemical transformations fungi can inflict on mineral matrices, and fungi with specific redox-transforming activities (e.g., Cr(VI)-reducing strains) illustrate the metabolic versatility underlying these transformations.

## Metal Immobilization and Its Circumvention

Metal immobilization mechanisms — including biosorption to biomass (cell walls, pigments, and extracellular polysaccharides), transport and intracellular accumulation, and extracellular precipitation — can reduce external free metal activity and may shift the equilibrium to release more metal into aqueous solution. These immobilization processes can ultimately be circumvented by persistent fungal metabolic activity, meaning that even apparent detoxification mechanisms do not guarantee long-term stability of mineral-bound contaminants.

Fungi can be highly efficient bioaccumulators of soluble and particulate forms of metals (e.g., Ni, Zn, Ag, Cu, Cd, and Pb), especially from dilute external concentrations. Metal binding by fungi can be an important passive process in both living and dead fungal biomass. The formation of secondary mycogenic minerals — including organic and inorganic precipitates such as carbonates, phosphates, and hydroxides — can occur through both metabolism-independent and metabolism-dependent processes, further complicating predictions of long-term barrier stability.

## Experimental Evidence

### Aspergillus niger Concrete Corrosion

[[aspergillus-niger]] has been demonstrated to corrode concrete surfaces over a 2-year experimental period, producing visible cracking and surface deterioration in concrete blocks. Scanning electron microscopy of the weathered specimens revealed widespread hyphal networks colonizing the deteriorated concrete surface. *Aspergillus niger* and related species (including *[[penicillium-expansum]]*, *P. simplicissimum*, and *Scopulariopsis brevicaulis*) have also been shown capable of degrading a range of other silicate and mineral substrates, including olivine, feldspar, and basalt.

### Microfungi on Barrier Concrete

An experimental study of the effect of microfungi on barrier concrete showed that fungi were able to colonize and corrode the surface of concrete, **actively avoiding areas filled with granite** — a hostile substrate. This selective colonization behavior demonstrates the ability of fungi to discriminate between substrate types and preferentially attack vulnerable concrete matrix while steering clear of resistant mineral fills. The implications are clear: fungi do not attack indiscriminately but rather target the weakest components of engineered barrier systems.
