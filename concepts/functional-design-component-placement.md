# Functional Design and Component Placement

Functional design is a core methodology in [[permaculture]] that focuses on placing system components in careful relation to each other so that each element performs multiple functions and each function is supported by multiple elements. The central principle is that intelligent placement of components minimizes maintenance work, reduces the need to import external energies, and can generate modest surpluses for sale, trade, or export.

## The Principle of Relative Placement

The fundamental insight of functional design is that by placing essential components carefully relative to each other, we create self-regulating systems that reduce human labor. Surplus in such systems arises from the conversion of "wastes" into useful products through appropriate placement and connections between elements.

### The Integrated Farm Example

The classic illustration involves placing a chicken-house so that it both heats and is heated by a glasshouse, with both also heated by a chimney. Chickens range in the orchard, providing manure and consuming orchard wastes and pests, while interplants from the woodlot or forest components supplement their diet. A glasshouse also heats the house, and part of the woodlot serves as both forage system and shelter-belt.

This creates a cascade of beneficial relationships:

- **Chicken → Glasshouse**: Body heat warms the growing space; manure fertilizes plants
- **Glasshouse → House**: Passive solar heating extends into living spaces
- **Chicken → Orchard**: Pest control, manure deposition, forage utilization
- **Woodlot → Multiple zones**: Shelter, forage, fuel, and windbreak functions

Market and investment control are centralized in the house alongside an information system (computer), linking the farm to the broader world. This demonstrates how functional design extends beyond biological components to include economic and informational systems.

## Information vs. Resources

A critical distinction in functional design separates **resources** (practical, useful energy storages) from **information** (potential resources not yet put to use). This distinction guards against what Mollison calls "the academic fallacy": the belief that assembling information is equivalent to acting on it ("I think, therefore I have acted").

An analytical design developed at a desk produces good ideas about patterns and self-regulatory systems, but until a chicken is actually heating a greenhouse, manuring an orchard, or producing methane for a house, the system remains only information or potential. No real resources are produced and no real problems are solved until components function in their placed relationships.

This has an important corollary: analytical design can operate without site experience, which is both its strength (accessibility) and its weakness (lack of grounded reality). The transition from analytical design to implemented, functioning systems requires site-specific testing and adaptation.

## Cooperation Over Competition

Functional design naturally leads to the recognition that cooperation within systems conserves energy, while competition absorbs energy and consumes resources. The ideal is to allow the free expression of all beneficial characteristics of each component, avoiding conflict and enabling the system to self-regulate based on real-life experience on site.

## Observation as a Design Tool

Observation-based design complements analytical methods by starting directly on and around the site. It rests on the principle that no complex of map overlays, library data, computer analysis, or remote sensing can supplant direct field observation for dependability and relevance.

### Why Observation Excels

[[maps]] and fixed data are static — they cannot reveal processes or dynamic interactions such as heavy rain and subsequent runoff. Process and events as encountered on a real site are never fully captured by maps. It is from observing these processes that designers can devise strategies of "least change," saving energy and time in ways no static method can achieve.

### Four Approaches to Observation

1. **Child-like and non-selective approach**: Beginning observations with "I wonder why..." before any specific observation, maintaining openness and curiosity
2. **Thematic approach**: Focusing observation on a single theme such as water flow, potential energy sources, or conditions for natural regeneration
3. **Instrumental approach**: Using measurement equipment to quantify factors like temperature gradients, wind speed and direction, or light reflection from vegetation
4. **Experiential approach**: Using all the senses as instruments — being fully conscious of specific details, sensations, and the total ambience of the site

A camera, notebook, and video recorder are valuable aids to observation, allowing re-examination of information. A good memory for events also serves the observer well.

### The Five-Stage Observation Procedure

The observational design methodology follows a structured progression:

**Stage 1 — Value-free documentation**: Record what is seen, measured, or experienced without interpretation. For example, note that "moles have thrown up earth mounds on the field" without guessing why or judging whether this is good or bad. This requires discipline but yields valuable primary data.

**Stage 2 — Speculation**: Select observations of interest and list possible meanings. A single observation (molehills) can generate many speculations: that molehills are only conspicuous on fields, that they actually occur elsewhere, or that fields are particularly attractive to moles. These speculations are hypotheses — guesses about which more information can be gathered.

**Stage 3 — Confirmation or denial**: Test speculations through multiple strategies:
- Library research on the subject and allied species
- Asking other people about local behavior
- Devising additional focused observations on a single theme
- Recalling knowledge of the subject in other areas or circumstances

**Stage 4 — Pattern examination**: Review all accumulated evidence for emergent patterns or modes of operation. What other creatures burrow in fields? Are they predators, prey, or companions? What connections exist between the observed phenomenon and other site characteristics?

**Stage 5 — Application**: Determine how to use the gathered information in design. A mole may be identified as a fine soil aerator and seed-bed provider to be encouraged, or as a pest to be excluded. Strategies may emerge for where moles are beneficial versus where they should be controlled.

## The Value of Observation in Design

As research and observation progress, initially isolated observations become connected to broader ecological relationships. The mole becomes linked to earthworms, upturned soils, fields, lawns, gardens, pastures, water percolation, and soil production. Dozens of useful strategies can evolve from a single initial observation, and the site begins to "design itself."

Cautious trials and further observation confirm the benefits (or drawbacks) of incorporating observed elements into the total system. Practical information gathered this way carries over to other sites and to allied observations. Studies of related organisms (such as earthworms alongside moles) may co-evolve, revealing the interconnectedness of natural systems.

The end result of systematic observation is a set of strategies ready for application in design. A secondary but equally valuable outcome is the personal, involved understanding gained of the totality and interdependence of natural systems. No analytic method involves the designer in the world as deeply as observation, and observers frequently stumble upon unique and effective strategies, becoming innovators in the process.
