# Substrate Preparation for Mushroom Cultivation

Substrate preparation is the process of converting raw organic materials into a
sterile or pasteurized growing medium suitable for mushroom mycelial colonization.
The quality of substrate preparation directly determines contamination rates,
colonization speed, and ultimately the yield of the mushroom crop. Oyster
mushrooms in the genus Pleurotus are exceptionally versatile in their
substrate preferences, capable of fruiting on a wide variety of agricultural and
forestry byproducts.

## Lignocellulosic Materials

Oyster mushrooms are white-rot fungi that degrade lignin, cellulose, and
hemicellulose through the secretion of extracellular enzymes including laccases,
manganese peroxidases, and cellulases. Any material rich in these components can
serve as a substrate. Common materials include:

- **Cereal straw** (wheat, barley, oat, rice): The most widely used substrate
  for oyster mushroom cultivation globally. Straw is inexpensive, widely
  available, and provides good aeration. It should be chopped to lengths of 3 to
  10 centimeters for optimal packing density.
- **Hardwood sawdust and wood chips**: Oak, beech, maple, and similar hardwoods
  are preferred. Softwoods are generally avoided due to resinous compounds that
  inhibit mycelial growth, though some species tolerate partially supplemented
  softwood blends.
- **Agricultural waste**: Corn cobs, sugarcane bagasse, cotton seed hulls, coffee
  grounds, and banana leaves have all been used successfully. Coffee grounds
  require no sterilization if used fresh but are highly susceptible to
  contamination if aged.
- **Paper and cardboard**: Shredded corrugated cardboard without heavy ink can be
  composted and used as a substrate base, though nutritional value is low
  without supplementation.

## Hydration

Most dry substrates must be hydrated to an appropriate moisture content before
treatment. The target moisture content ranges from 60 to 75 percent by weight,
depending on the material. Straw is typically soaked for 12 to 24 hours in water,
then drained. A squeeze test is commonly used: the substrate should release a few
drops of water when firmly squeezed but not drip freely. Overly wet substrates
promote bacterial contamination and anaerobic conditions, while excessively dry
substrates slow colonization.

## Supplementation

Nutritional supplements are added to increase biological efficiency by providing
additional nitrogen, minerals, and carbohydrates. Common supplements include:

- **Bran** (wheat bran, rice bran): Added at 5 to 20 percent dry weight. Bran
  significantly boosts yields but also increases contamination risk because
  competing organisms readily utilize the same nutrients.
- **Legume meals** (soybean meal, alfalfa): Rich in protein and minerals,
  typically added at 2 to 10 percent.
- **Calcium compounds** (gypsum, calcium carbonate): Added at 1 to 5 percent to
  buffer pH and provide calcium. Gypsum improves substrate structure and reduces
  clumping.

When supplements are used, the substrate must be sterilized rather than merely
pasteurized, because the added nutrients create a favorable environment for
thermophilic and mesophilic competitors that survive pasteurization.

## Pasteurization

Pasteurization involves heating the hydrated substrate to 60 to 80 degrees
Celsius for 1 to 3 hours. This eliminates most harmful organisms while
preserving beneficial thermophilic bacteria that can help suppress contaminants
during colonization. Common pasteurization methods include hot water immersion,
steam pasteurization chambers, and solar pasteurization. Pasteurization is
adequate for unsupplemented substrates like plain straw. See [[pasteurization-in-mushroom-cultivation]] for detailed protocols.

## Sterilization

Sterilization, typically achieved with autoclaving at 121 degrees Celsius for 60
to 90 minutes, eliminates all living organisms from the substrate. This is
necessary when using supplemented substrates where the added nutrients would
allow competitors to outgrow the mushroom mycelium if any survived. Pressure
cookers and autoclaves are standard equipment for sterilization. The substrate
is sealed in heat-resistant bags with filter patches before sterilization to
maintain sterility through cooling and inoculation.

## Post-Treatment Handling

After pasteurization or sterilization, the substrate must be cooled to below 30
degrees Celsius before inoculation. Hot substrate will kill or weaken the spawn.
Cooling should occur in a clean environment to prevent recontamination. Once
cooled, inoculation should proceed as quickly as possible, ideally within a
laminar flow hood or still-air box for sterilized substrates.
