# Wasson's Revelation: The Discovery of the Magic Mushroom Cult

## The Mycophile and the Mycophobe

R. Gordon Wasson, a vice president of J.P. Morgan & Co. and an amateur
mycologist, proposed a fundamental cultural dichotomy in humanity's
relationship to fungi. In his sweeping cross-cultural analysis, Wasson
divided the world's societies into **mycophilic** cultures — those that
revere and cultivate mushrooms — and **mycophobic** cultures — those that
regard all fungi with suspicion and disgust. The Anglo-Saxon and
Germanic traditions fell firmly into the latter camp, while Slavic,
French, and many indigenous American societies embraced mushrooms as
food, medicine, and sacrament. This taxonomy was not merely descriptive;
Wasson believed it revealed a deep psychological and spiritual fault line
running through human civilization, one that had profound implications
for understanding the role of psychoactive fungi in human history.

## The Journey to Huautla de Jiménez

In 1955, Wasson and his wife [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]] in Mesoamerica. The
Wassons had been inspired by reports from the sixteenth-century Spanish
chroniclers — most notably Bernardino de Sahagún — who described with
horror the "devilish" mushroom rituals of the native peoples. Despite
centuries of colonial suppression, the mushroom ceremonies survived among
the Mazatec people of the highland cloud forests. The Wassons made their
way to the small village of Huautla de Jiménez, where they sought out
a curandera (healer) named María Sabina, who was known as one of the
most skilled practitioners of the mushroom velada.

## The Velada with María Sabina

On the night of June 29–30, 1955, the Wassons participated in a **velada**
— an all-night [[amanita-muscaria-legal-alternative-psilocybin-mushrooms-europe]] she called *los niños
santos* ("the holy children") and began to sing her *alabados*, sacred
chant-like invocations. The Wassons were then served the mushrooms and
experienced what Wasson later described as an encounter with the divine.
The experience was not recreational; it was embedded in a framework of
healing, divination, and communion with the sacred that had been
maintained, largely in secret, since before the Spanish Conquest. The
Wassons thus became the first known outsiders — the first Westerners —
to participate in a [[terence-mckenna]] culture. It demonstrated
that indigenous societies had preserved sophisticated psychoactive
plant knowledge — knowledge that modern science had entirely
overlooked. It catalyzed the scientific study of psilocybin, leading
to decades of psychiatric research. It also raised enduring questions
about the responsibilities of ethnographers toward the communities
they study, the tension between scientific openness and cultural
preservation, and the costs of bringing sacred knowledge into the
profane marketplace of global media. For McKenna, Wasson's revelation
was a pivotal moment in the history of humanity's relationship with
psychoactive plants — a moment when the ancient partnership between
humans and the mushroom was abruptly thrust into modern consciousness,
with consequences both liberating and devastating.
