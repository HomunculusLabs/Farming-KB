# Fungal Radionuclide Accumulation

## Overview

Fungi play a remarkably significant role in the cycling and bioaccumulation of radionuclides in terrestrial ecosystems, particularly following nuclear accidents such as Chernobyl (1986). Certain fungal species — especially ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes — concentrate radiocaesium (¹³⁷Cs) and radiostrontium (⁹⁰Sr) at levels far exceeding those of surrounding soils, making them both important vectors of radionuclide transfer to food webs and valuable bioindicators of environmental contamination.

## Radionuclides of Concern

### Cesium-137 (¹³⁷Cs)

¹³⁷Cs is the most studied radionuclide in fungal research due to its environmental persistence (half-life ~30 years) and chemical similarity to potassium, which allows it to be readily taken up by biological systems. After the Chernobyl disaster, ¹³⁷Cs was deposited widely across Europe, with forest ecosystems acting as particularly effective long-term sinks. The chemical mimicry of potassium means that ¹³⁷Cs enters fungal cells through the same transport proteins and channels used for potassium acquisition, leading to substantial bioaccumulation in potassium-demanding fungal tissues.

### Strontium-90 (⁹⁰Sr)

⁹⁰Sr (half-life ~29 years) behaves chemically like calcium and is incorporated into fungal cell structures, particularly in those species that accumulate calcium. Its uptake by fungi is generally less studied than ¹³⁷Cs but remains ecologically significant, especially in areas of high fallout. Calcium oxalate crystal formation, documented by Cromack et al. in fungi like *Hysterangium*, may provide a mechanism for strontium co-precipitation and sequestration in fungal tissues.

## Uptake Mechanisms

### Mycorrhizal Uptake Pathways

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]], particularly ectomycorrhizal species, are the primary agents of radionuclide accumulation in forest ecosystems. Research by Dighton and Horrill on *[[lactarius-rufus]]* and *[[inocybe-longicystis]]* demonstrated species-specific differences in ¹³⁷Cs uptake, with some ectomycorrhizal fungi acting as effective biological filters between soil and host plant. Key mechanisms include:

- **Potassium analogue substitution**: ¹³⁷Cs exploits potassium transport channels in fungal hyphae. Ectomycorrhizal fungi with extensive mycelial networks can access cesium from soil horizons beyond the reach of plant roots alone.
- **Hyphal translocation**: Mycelial networks redistribute radionuclides within soil profiles, potentially moving ¹³⁷Cs from deeper mineral soil layers into the organic horizon where fruiting bodies form.
- **Selective bioaccumulation**: Some fungal species actively concentrate cesium in their tissues at ratios of 10:1 to 1000:1 (fungus:soil), a process termed the "aggregation factor" or "concentration ratio."
- **Host-mediated effects**: Clint and Dighton's work on radiocaesium uptake by mycorrhizal heather (*[[calluna-vulgaris]]*) showed that the mycorrhizal association significantly altered cesium dynamics compared to non-mycorrhizal plants.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Involvement

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi also contribute to radionuclide cycling. Research by Berreck and Hasselwandter on AM symbiosis and cesium uptake showed that AM fungi can mediate cesium transfer to host plants, though generally at lower concentration ratios than ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes. AM fungi may nonetheless be important in grassland and agricultural contexts where ectomycorrhizal partners are absent.

### Ecological Factors Affecting Uptake

Multiple environmental variables modulate radionuclide accumulation by fungi:

- **Soil pH and [[cation-exchange-capacity]]**: Acidic soils with low [[query-what-is-cation-exchange-capacity-and-why-does-it-matter]] promote greater cesium bioavailability.
- **Organic matter content**: Forest humus layers retain ¹³⁷Cs and serve as the primary substrate for fungal uptake.
- **Species-specific physiology**: Different fungal species vary enormously in their accumulation capacity; related research by Dighton and Terry examined caesium uptake variation across different UK soil types and fungal taxa.
- **Nitrogen availability**: Work by Arnebrant on nitrogen effects on ectomycorrhizal mycelium suggests that nitrogen deposition (from [[lichen-bioindicators-atmospheric-pollution]]) can alter mycelial growth patterns and potentially modify radionuclide uptake efficiency.
- **Soil mineralogy**: Clay content and specific mineral types (illite, vermiculite) influence cesium fixation and thus the fraction available for fungal uptake.

## Forest Ecosystem Dynamics

### The Forest as a Radionuclide Sink

Forest ecosystems are particularly effective at retaining ¹³⁷Cs. The combination of organic soil horizons, dense mycorrhizal networks, and slow decomposition rates means that radiocaesium deposited on forests can persist for decades. This "closed" nutrient cycling results in ongoing biological recycling of ¹³⁷Cs between soil, mycelium, fruiting bodies, and fauna. The fungal mycelium effectively prevents ¹³⁷Cs from leaching into groundwater or being transported to aquatic systems.

### Transfer Through Food Webs

Wild mushrooms represent a significant pathway for radiocaesium entry into human diets. Barnett et al. documented radiocaesium intake from wild fungi, demonstrating that mushroom consumption can constitute a major fraction of internal radiation dose in affected populations. This is particularly relevant in Eastern and Central Europe where [[emcdda-wild-mushroom-foraging-misidentification-hazards]] is culturally important. Game animals that consume fungi, such as wild boar and deer, can also accumulate significant radiocaesium body burdens, creating secondary pathways to human consumers.

## Fungi as Bioindicators

Basidiomycetes, especially ectomycorrhizal species, serve as effective bioindicators of radioactive contamination. Bohac et al. demonstrated the role of fungi in biogenous migration and radionuclide accumulation, establishing mushrooms as monitoring organisms. The advantages of fungal bioindicators include:

- **Wide distribution**: Ectomycorrhizal fungi occur across diverse forest types.
- **Quantifiable concentration ratios**: Species-specific accumulation factors allow comparison across sites and time.
- **Integrative sampling**: Fruit bodies integrate radionuclide availability over their growth period, providing a time-averaged contamination signal.

Arnolds' work on changing macromycete flora in Europe, including the decline of ectomycorrhizal fungi, raises concerns about the long-term utility of these bioindicator systems under ongoing environmental change.

## Extreme Environments: Chernobyl Reactor Fungi

The Chernobyl reactor itself harbored extremophilic fungal communities, including species of *Cladosporium*, that colonized the highly radioactive environment. These fungi exhibited several remarkable properties:

### Fungal Melanins

Melanized fungi found in and around the reactor (including *Cladosporium* species) possess dense melanin pigments in their cell walls. Melanin has been proposed to:

- **Shield against ionizing radiation**: Melanin absorbs and dissipates radiation energy, potentially protecting cellular machinery from oxidative damage.
- **Mediate radiotrophic growth**: Some evidence suggests melanized fungi can harness ionizing radiation as an energy source through a process analogous to radiosynthesis, though this remains debated and requires further investigation.
- **Bind metal ions**: Melanin's chemical structure provides abundant binding sites for metal cations, which may extend to radionuclides like ¹³⁷Cs.

### Radiotropism

Certain fungi from the Chernobyl environment exhibit radiotropism — directed growth toward sources of ionizing radiation. This behavioral response suggests an adaptive capacity to colonize and exploit radioactive environments, possibly linked to melanin-mediated energy transduction. Radiotropism represents one of the most striking examples of fungal adaptation to extreme anthropogenic environments.

## Broader Context: Fungi and Metal/Metalloid Interactions

The radionuclide accumulation capacity of fungi parallels their broader role in heavy metal and metalloid cycling. Blaudez et al. documented differential responses of ectomycorrhizal fungi to heavy metals, while Bradley et al. explored mycorrhizal infection and heavy metal resistance. Colpaert investigated zinc and cadmium toxicity in ectomycorrhizal *Pinus*, demonstrating that mycorrhizal associations can protect host plants from metal toxicity. Byrne et al. investigated arsenic and silver accumulation in mushrooms alongside radioactivity in Slovenian fungi post-Chernobyl, and identified arsenic compounds subject to mycelial methylation. These parallel pathways — radionuclides, heavy metals, and metalloids — share common features:

- Cation transport channel exploitation
- Intracellular sequestration (often via metallothioneins or vacuolar compartmentalization)
- Extracellular binding to cell wall components
- Species-specific variation in tolerance and accumulation

## Interactions with Environmental Change

Cairney and Meharg reviewed anthropogenic pollution [[faunal-grazing-effects-on-mycorrhizal-function]] communities, noting that multiple stressors (nitrogen deposition, acidification, heavy metals, sulfur dioxide as studied by Boddy et al.) can alter community composition and function. Conn and Dighton showed that litter quality affects ectomycorrhizal communities, which in turn influences radionuclide cycling capacity. The decline of ectomycorrhizal fungal diversity documented by Arnolds in Europe may ultimately reduce the fungal community's capacity for radionuclide sequestration.

## Temporal Dynamics of Radiocaesium in Fungi

The behavior of ¹³⁷Cs in fungal fruiting bodies changes over time following a deposition event. Initially, surface-contaminated fungi show high activity from direct interception of fallout. In subsequent years, as radionuclides migrate into soil profiles, uptake shifts to root-mediated and mycelial pathways. Long-term monitoring has revealed that certain fungal species continue to bioaccumulate ¹³⁷Cs at elevated levels decades after initial contamination, particularly in regions with organic-rich forest soils. This persistence underscores the importance of fungi in the long-term ecological half-life of radiocaesium in terrestrial systems.

## Methodological Considerations

Sampling and analysis of fungal radionuclide content requires attention to several methodological factors:

- **Species identification**: Accurate taxonomic identification is essential, as accumulation varies enormously between species.
- **Tissue-specific analysis**: Caps and stipes of fruiting bodies can differ in radionuclide content, reflecting differential physiological roles.
- **Sampling depth**: Surface wash procedures are needed to distinguish internal bioaccumulation from external contamination.
- **Seasonal and annual variation**: Fruit body radionuclide concentrations can fluctuate between years and within growing seasons.

## Implications and Significance

- **Human health**: Wild mushroom consumption remains a quantifiable source of internal radiation exposure decades after initial fallout events.
- **Ecological monitoring**: Fungi provide accessible, cost-effective bioindicators for long-term radiological surveillance programs.
- **Bioremediation potential**: Understanding fungal radionuclide accumulation mechanisms may inform phytomycorrhizal remediation strategies for contaminated sites.
- **Fundamental biology**: Radiotrophic fungi challenge our understanding of energy metabolism and the limits of biological adaptation to extreme environments.
