# Louis Savier and the Modernization of French Intensive Market Gardening

The story of Louis Savier represents a critical bridge between the 19th-century Parisian maraîcher tradition and modern organic market gardening. When [[eliot-coleman]] visited Savier's farm outside Paris in the 1970s and 1980s, he found a living embodiment of intensive vegetable production techniques that had evolved from the legendary Parisian market gardens of 1850-1900 while incorporating 20th-century innovations. Savier's operation demonstrates both the enduring principles and the practical evolution of French intensive gardening.

## The Parisian Maraîcher Legacy

The Parisian market gardening system (la culture maraîchère) that reached its peak between 1850 and 1900 was one of the most productive and sophisticated vegetable production systems ever devised. Operating on one to two acre plots within the city limits of Paris, these growers (maraîchers) produced four to eight harvests per year from the same ground — an astonishing level of productivity achieved through:

- Intensive interplanting and [[succession-planting]]
- Heavy use of composted horse manure from city stables
- Protected cultivation using cold frames and glass cloches
- Year-round production using manure-heated hotbeds
- Narrow access paths (10 inches) maximizing production area
- Detailed knowledge of crop timing and complementary growth habits

The system fed Paris year-round with the widest variety of fruits and vegetables, and was so productive that growers exported surplus to England. It was powered by 100 to 400 tons of horse manure per acre, recycled from the city's transportation system. Vincent Gressent noted in 1870 that chemical fertilizers "stimulate the plant and produce quantity, but to the detriment of quality" and that pest problems were unknown where "copious compost use and rational crop rotations are practiced."

## Finding Louis Savier

Coleman first learned of Savier through a French seed company contact. Despite initial difficulty locating the farm — the original name had changed after Savier's daughter married — Coleman eventually found the 2.5-acre operation on the outskirts of Paris. Savier had acquired the market garden from his father in the early 1960s but had been displeased with the chemical inputs adopted during his father's later years. He returned to organic methods, then progressed to biodynamic practices, and never looked back.

The results were dramatic: pests and diseases disappeared, and crop quality soared. Savier reported that these improvements confirmed the old organic methods as superior to the chemical approach his father had briefly adopted.

## Savier's Crop Portfolio

The crops listed in Coleman's notes from the visit reveal a carefully curated selection optimized for the French market and the intensive system:

**Salad Crops:** Lettuces (butterhead, crisp, and romaine), endive, escarole, mache, radishes

**Cooking Greens:** Spinach, chard, parsley

**Root Crops:** Leeks, carrots, turnips, onions

**Alliums:** Onions, leeks, scallions

Every bed was part of a planned crop rotation and featured ingenious interplantings that took advantage of complementary maturity times and growth characteristics. Notable pairings included:

- **Leek/carrot interplanting:** The slow-growing leeks and carrots occupied different soil horizons and matured on different timelines
- **Mache/onion interplanting:** Low-growing mache filled space between onion rows
- **Radish/romaine interplanting:** Fast-maturing radishes were harvested before the romaine needed the full bed space

The parsley was planted in marginal corners that might otherwise have gone unused and was harvested successively for three cuttings, demonstrating the intensive gardener's principle of using every square foot.

## The Rail Track Innovation

One of the most distinctive features of Savier's operation was a space-efficient system of 30-inch-wide lightweight rail tracks that provided access to all parts of the 2.5 acres. This was a modernized version of the narrow paths of the 19th-century gardens, but with an important innovation: pushcarts ran along the rail tracks.

Three specialized rail carts served different functions:

1. **Compost delivery cart** — Transported composted manure from the composting area to the beds
2. **Harvest cart** — Moved crates of freshly harvested produce to the packing shed
3. **Irrigation cart** — A self-propelled cart (powered by water pressure) with long arms dispensing water through nozzles on both sides, towing the hose behind it as it moved down the track

This system eliminated the need for wheelbarrow-wide paths between beds, recovering significant production area while reducing labor. The 19th-century maraîchers had used willow basket backpacks (hottes) carried on the back to deliver manure along their 10-inch paths — an ingenious but labor-intensive solution that Savier's rail system improved upon dramatically.

## Modernization of the Old System

Savier's garden retained many elements of the classic Parisian system while making strategic updates:

**Retained from the 19th century:**
- Intensive bed preparation and planting
- Composted horse manure as the primary fertilizer
- Glass-covered cold frames for protected cultivation
- Interplanting and succession planting
- Crop rotation planning

**Updated for the 20th century:**
- Glass cloches replaced by glass and plastic greenhouses covering about one-quarter of the cropped area
- Manure-heated hotbeds replaced by hot-water pipe heating systems
- Rototillers for bed preparation (replacing hand labor)
- Faster transport to market
- Rail-track pushcart system instead of basket backpacks

**Labor patterns:** The average labor force of six workers per 1.5 acres remained the same as in the 19th century, but the 16-hour workdays of the Parisian growers had been reduced to approximately 11 hours per day. Savier noted that his suburban neighbors worked 8 hours plus 3 hours commuting; he preferred to spend those 3 hours in his garden rather than in transit.

## The Garden as a Living Painting

Coleman's description of harvesting day at Savier's farm evokes the aesthetic dimension of intensive market gardening. On a misty fall day in 1989, he watched Savier's daughter and one other employee harvesting 'French Breakfast' radishes. They wore waterproof pants with rubber bands on a belt dispenser, and deposited the bunched radishes in a line on the soil behind them, ready to be crated and washed.

"The ease of organization of the work spoke of efficiency, but the colors and composition of the scene evoked a French impressionist painting." This observation captures something essential about the French intensive tradition: it was not merely productive but beautiful. The density of planting, the diversity of crops, the careful organization of space — these created gardens that were as visually compelling as they were agriculturally productive.

## Lessons for Modern Growers

Savier's operation demonstrates several principles applicable to contemporary small-scale vegetable production:

1. **Intensive spacing pays dividends** — Every square foot should earn its keep through interplanting, succession planting, or multiple harvests
2. **Invest in infrastructure for efficiency** — The rail track system recovered production area and reduced labor, paying for itself quickly
3. **Organic methods work at commercial scale** — Savier's return to organic methods eliminated pest and disease problems while improving quality
4. **Evolve, don't abandon** — The best systems incorporate new technologies (rototillers, plastic greenhouses, irrigation) without losing the core principles (compost, rotation, intensive spacing)
5. **Location matters** — Like the 19th-century Parisian gardens, Savier's suburban location minimized transport time and connected the grower directly to the market
6. **Quality creates loyalty** — The flavor and freshness of intensively grown organic vegetables creates customer relationships that survive competition from cheaper, lower-quality alternatives

## The Four Factors of Economic Success

Coleman identifies four factors that brought economic success to the 19th-century Parisian maraîchers and that remain equally valid for 21st-century market gardeners:

1. **Location** — Proximity to the market, reducing transport costs and connecting grower directly to consumer
2. **Sustainability** — Building soil fertility through recycled inputs rather than depleting resources
3. **Quality** — Producing vegetables of exceptional flavor and nutritional value that command premium prices
4. **Productivity** — Achieving high yields per unit area through intensive techniques

These four factors, rather than any specific technique or technology, are the enduring lessons of the French intensive tradition. Techniques evolve and technologies change, but the economic logic of local, sustainable, high-quality, productive farming remains constant.

## Biodynamic Evolution

Savier's progression from organic to biodynamic practices represents an interesting case study in the evolution of growing philosophies. While Coleman does not extensively detail the specific biodynamic preparations or methods Savier employed, the fact that pests and diseases disappeared after the transition suggests that the biodynamic approach addressed soil health issues that organic methods alone had not fully resolved. This aligns with the deep-organic principle that the goal is to create conditions where natural biological balance prevents pest problems.
