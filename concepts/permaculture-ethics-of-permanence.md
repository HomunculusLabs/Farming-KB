## Permaculture Ethics of Permanence

## The Ethical Foundation

[[permaculture]] ethical framework rests on three pillars:

1. **Care of the Earth** — All life systems must be preserved and allowed to flourish. This includes implacable opposition to further disturbance of remaining natural forests, vigorous rehabilitation of degraded systems, and the establishment of plant and animal refuges for rare or threatened species.

2. **Care of People** — Cooperation and mutual support replace competition. The observation that cooperative species and associations of self-supporting species (like [[soil-life]] biomass, and forest biomass
- **Social**: Employment, food quality, human and environmental health, and quality of life as "right livelihood"

The key insight is that selected forests not only yield more than annual crops, but provide a diverse nutrient and fuel resource for such crops. Without permanent agriculture, there is no possibility of a stable social order.

## Anthropocentric vs. Nature-Centered Design

Permaculture acknowledges a tension between human-centered and nature-centered ethics. In cultivated ecosystems, the great majority of species and biomass are intended for human use. Household design is thus anthropocentric—a valid aim for settlement design. However, a nature-centered ethic is also needed for wilderness conservation.

The practical resolution: if we can achieve self-sufficiency from our existing settlements, we can withdraw from much of the agricultural landscape and allow natural systems to flourish. Even anthropocentric people are well-advised to assist in forest conservation and land rehabilitation, since our own survival demands it.

## The Hypocrisy Test

Mollison is blunt about contradictions: it is hypocrisy to pretend to save forests while buying daily newspapers and packaged food; to preserve native plants while relying on chemical production for food; to adopt a diet requiring broadscale food production. Truly responsible conservationists have gardens that support their food needs and are working to reduce their own energy needs to what can be supplied locally by wind, water, forest, or solar power.

## Two Responsibilities

Mollison distills the ethical imperative to two responsibilities:

1. **Primarily**: Get our house and garden—our place of living—in order so that it supports us.
2. **Secondarily**: Limit our population on earth, or we ourselves become the final plague.

These duties are intimately connected, as stable regions create stable populations. If we do not get our cities, homes, and gardens in order so that they feed and shelter us, we must lay waste to all other natural systems.
