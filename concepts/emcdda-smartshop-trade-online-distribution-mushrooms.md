    18|# European Smartshop Trade and Online Distribution of Hallucinogenic Mushrooms
    19|
    20|The EMCDDA 2006 case study documents the emergence and rapid expansion of a
    21|commercial market for hallucinogenic mushrooms in Europe during the late 1990s
    22|and early 2000s, centered on Dutch smartshops and later expanding through
    23|online retail. This market existed in a legal grey area until successive
    24|national bans closed the loopholes.
    25|
    26|## The Smartshop Model
    27|
    28|The Netherlands served as the primary hub for the legal trade in hallucinogenic
    29|mushrooms. An estimated 120 to 150 smartshops operated in the Netherlands,
    30|concentrated in Amsterdam, selling legal and predominantly natural products.
    31|According to the Dutch Risk Assessment Report, magic mushrooms constituted
    32|approximately 50 percent of smartshop turnover. These shops also operated
    33|alongside coffee shops, headshops selling drug paraphernalia, and grow shops
    34|catering to home marijuana cultivators.
    35|
    36|## The "Paddo" Delivery Culture
    37|
    38|In the Netherlands, hallucinogenic mushrooms were known as "paddos," and a
    39|distinctive delivery culture emerged. "Paddo cabs" made home deliveries of
    40|hallucinogenic mushrooms, paralleling the cannabis delivery services already
    41|operating in Dutch cities. This normalized the substance as a consumer product
    42|available on demand. A 2001 study among representative Dutch young people aged
    43|12 and above found that 64 percent of those aged 18 and older who had used
    44|magic mushrooms in the past year purchased them in smartshops.
    45|
    46|## Product Types and Pricing
    47|
    48|The European smartshop market offered multiple product formats with the
    49|following price ranges (January 2006 data):
    50|
    51|- **Fresh Psilocybe cubensis (Mexican)**: 8.5 to 45.28 EUR per 30 grams
    52|- **Fresh Copelandia cyanescens**: 13 to 17.5 EUR per 10 grams
    53|- **Truffles/sclerotia**: Psilocybe mexicana "Philosopher's stones" at 10 to
    54|  17.5 EUR per 15 grams
    55|- **Spawn bags**: Psilocybe azurescens mycelium on hardwood chips, 24 to 25
    56|  EUR each
    57|- **Grow kits**: Complete cultivation boxes with colonized substrate, 25 to
    58|  100 EUR
    59|- **Spore prints and syringes**: 9 to 28 EUR per print or syringe
    60|
    61|The "Free Spore Ring Europe" (FSRE) nonprofit distributed spore prints of
    62|various strains for approximately 1 EUR per print, making cultivation materials
    63|widely accessible. Instructions for growing mushrooms from spore prints and
    64|substrate could be easily found on the internet.
    65|
    66|## The UK Market Before the Ban
    67|
    68|Prior to July 2005, an estimated 300 shops and market stalls across the UK
    69|sold hallucinogenic mushrooms. The UK Treasury collected sales tax estimated at
    70|up to 175,000 GBP annually on a turnover of approximately 1 million GBP. The
    71|2004 Mixmag survey found that 54.6 percent of users purchased their mushrooms
    72|from clubs, shops, or stalls, 16 percent obtained them from friends, and 4.2
    73|percent bought them online. Camden Market in London was a notable retail
    74|location for fresh mushroom sales.
    75|
    76|## The Online Retail Explosion
    77|
    78|A January 2006 snapshot survey using Google and AltaVista identified 39
    79|distinct online shops selling hallucinogenic mushroom products. Analysis
    80|revealed that 82 percent were based in the Netherlands, with the remainder
    81|including four Austrian shops, two German sites, and one UK site linked to a
    82|Dutch operation. These shops offered international shipping with multilingual
    83|interfaces in English, French, and German to attract foreign customers.
    84|
    85|Website analytics from one Dutch online shop showed access patterns between
    86|May 2004 and February 2006: 73 percent from the Netherlands, 14 percent from
    87|Belgium, 2 percent from Germany, and 2 percent from France. Most sites
    88|refrained from shipping to countries where sales were prohibited, though
    89|enforcement was inconsistent. Online shops sold fresh mushrooms, mushroom
    90|prints, spawn bags, and grow kits.
    91|
    92|## Market Responses to Legal Changes
    93|
    94|When the UK reclassified hallucinogenic mushrooms as Class A drugs in July
    95|2005, retailers pivoted rapidly to legal alternatives. Amanita muscaria (fly
    96|agaric) gained new market attention, as did Amanita pantherina (panther cap).
    97|Purple Ohms capsules containing Hawaiian Baby Woodrose (Argyreia nervosa) with
    98|LSA were promoted as having similar effects to LSD. UK retailers such as
    99|EveryOneDoesIt.com and Allsalvia.co.uk switched their product lines
   100|accordingly. This pattern demonstrates how prohibition tends to displace demand
   101|toward other psychoactive substances rather than eliminating it.
   102|
   103|## Brand Names and User Terminology
   104|
   105|The commercialization of mushrooms through smartshops generated new marketing
   106|language overlapping with user terminology. Hallucinogenic mushrooms were
   107|commonly known as "magic mushrooms" but widely called "paddos" in the
   108|Netherlands, "shrooms" in the UK, and "Zauberpilze" in Germany. Other English
   109|names included mush, mushies, boomers, psilocybes, cubes, liberty caps,
   110|philosopher's stone, sacred mushrooms, teonanacatl (the Aztec name meaning
   111|"divine flesh"), Mexican mushrooms, and moon children. The term "philosopher's
   112|stone" described Psilocybe mexicana sclerotia, while "Herbal highs" became an
   113|umbrella label including mushroom products alongside herbal ecstasy and Salvia
   114|divinorum.
   115|
   116|## Seizure Data and Enforcement
   117|
   118|In 2004, police or customs seizures of hallucinogenic mushrooms were reported
   119|across twelve European countries including Czech Republic, Estonia, Germany,
   120|Greece, Hungary, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal,
   121|Slovakia, Slovenia, and Sweden. The largest single seizure was 11.5 kg in
   122|Poland. Estonia confiscated 14 mail deliveries of mushrooms, spores, or
   123|mycelium. After the UK ban, seizures included a batch worth 6,000 GBP seized
   124|in Glasgow in 2005. Reported seizure trends were mixed, with Sweden and
   125|Germany reporting increases while Norway reported a decrease.
   126|
   127|## Legal Loophole Strategies
   128|
   129|Different countries employed creative strategies to control mushrooms within
   130|existing legal frameworks. Denmark explicitly listed specific mushroom species
   131|and spores. Italy used a catch-all term prohibiting cultivation of plants from
   132|which narcotics can be extracted, despite the taxonomic issue of fungi not
   133|being plants. Germany changed its controlled substance definition from "plant"
   134|to "organism" in March 2005 to close the fungal loophole. The Dutch Supreme
   135|Court ruled that dried, mashed, or processed mushrooms were controlled but
   136|fresh ones were not, creating a decade-long grey market that the smartshop
   137|industry exploited.
   138|
   139|## The Internet Information Ecosystem
   140|
   141|Since the late 1990s, extensive internet repositories of information on
   142|hallucinogenic mushrooms emerged, contributing to accessibility of information
   143|on effects, dosages, chemistry, and user experiences. US-based sites included
   144|Erowid, Lycaeum, Mycotopia, Shroomery, MushroomJohn, and The Entheogen Review.
   145|Dedicated national sites appeared across Europe, including Champis (France),
   146|Daath (Hungary), Norshroom (Norway), and Taikasieniforum (Finland). These sites
   147|typically provided information on effects, dosages, laws, and trip reports,
   148|with many including photo galleries, cultivation advice, and community forums.
