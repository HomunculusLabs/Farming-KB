# Cool House vs Cold House Economics in Winter Vegetable Production

## Overview

In The Winter Harvest Handbook, Eliot Coleman distinguishes between two types of unheated and minimally-heated greenhouse production: the **cold house** (completely unheated) and the **cool house** (maintained just above freezing at ~32°F/0°C using minimal supplementary heat). This distinction is economically significant because the choice between these two approaches affects crop selection, harvest frequency, yield per year, heating costs, and overall profitability. Coleman experimented extensively with both systems at Four Season Farm in coastal Maine (Zone 5) before ultimately favoring the cold house approach for philosophical and economic reasons.

## Definitions

### Cold House
- **Heating**: None. The structure is a standard plastic-covered gothic-style hoop house with no heating system
- **Protection**: Single-layer UV-resistant plastic covering plus an inner layer of floating row cover over crops
- **[[query-what-is-the-ideal-temperature-range-for-growing-cannabis]]**: When outdoor temperature drops to -15°F (-26°C), temperature under the inner row cover averages 15-18°F (-10°C to -8°C)
- **Crop count**: 3-4 harvests per winter season
- **Cost**: Minimal — only the structure itself and row cover material

### Cool House
- **Heating**: Minimal supplementary heat (propane heater with thermostat set just above 32°F/0°C)
- **Protection**: Single or double-layer plastic covering, plus inner row cover
- **Temperature range**: Maintained above freezing at night; daytime temperatures determined by solar gain
- **Crop count**: 5-6 harvests per winter season (2-3 more than cold house)
- **Cost**: Propane fuel costs plus heating infrastructure

### Cool House Advantages
The cool house accelerates plant growth enough to harvest two additional crops per winter compared to unheated houses. Specific advantages include:

- **Baby turnips and radishes**: Available all winter in cool houses; freezing limits their production in cold houses
- **Earlier spring carrots**: Cool house carrots ready by April 1, versus May 5 in cold houses — a six-week difference
- **Broader crop range**: Some crops that tolerate cold but not freezing can be maintained in cool houses
- **Earlier start on summer crops**: Warm-season transplants can be started in the cool house during late winter

### Cold House Advantages
Despite lower productivity, the cold house offers compelling advantages:

- **Zero fuel cost**: No propane, wood, or other heating fuel required
- **Simplicity**: No heater, thermostat, fuel storage, or associated infrastructure
- **Reliability**: No risk of crop loss from heating system failure during extreme cold events
- **Alignment with "winter harvest" philosophy**: The original goal was to explore what could be achieved without heating

## Economic Analysis

### The One-Crop-Pays-for-Heat Rule

Coleman found that when heating fuel prices were low, a single additional crop could pay for the cost of the propane to fuel the heater:

- **Fuel cost**: One extra crop's revenue covered propane costs
- **Net gain**: All additional crops beyond the first were pure profit
- **Additional benefit**: Earlier start on warm-season summer crops provided further economic advantage

### When Economics Shift

The economics of minimal heating depend on several variables:

- **Fuel price**: Rising propane or natural gas prices reduce the economic advantage
- **Crop value**: High-value crops (baby salad greens, specialty herbs) make heating more economical than low-value crops
- **Market demand**: If winter markets pay premium prices, the additional yield from heating is more valuable
- **Climate**: In warmer zones (Zone 6+), the heating advantage diminishes because cold houses perform nearly as well without heat
- **Fuel availability**: Access to cheap local wood can make heating economical; dependence on delivered propane makes it less so

## The Green Manure Advantage of Mobile Greenhouses

Coleman's [[coleman-mobile-greenhouse-system]] adds another economic dimension. A greenhouse that moves between two sites provides:

1. **Year 1**: Greenhouse over summer crops (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers)
2. **Year 1 (fall)**: Greenhouse moves to cover winter crops sown outdoors in August
3. **Year 2**: Previous greenhouse site is uncovered, allowing a deep-rooting leguminous [[holzer-green-manure-crop-compendium]] to grow for 10-13 months
4. **Year 2 (fall)**: Greenhouse moves back to the original site for winter crops again

The uncovered year provides soil-restoration benefits that are economically significant:

- **Soil health improvement**: Sun, rain, wind, and snow cleanse the soil of potential [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] buildup
- **Green manure nitrogen**: Legume cover crops fix substantial nitrogen, reducing fertilizer costs
- **Reduced pest pressure**: Breaking the continuous-cover cycle interrupts pest and disease life cycles
- **Soil aeration**: Root growth and weather exposure [[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]]

This rotation is only possible with mobile greenhouses; permanent structures (both cool and cold) cannot provide this soil-restoration benefit.
