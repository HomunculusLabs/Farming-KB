## Levels and Surveying Tools for Earthworks

## Overview

Accurate leveling is essential in [[permaculture-earthworks]] to ensure that spillways function correctly, drains flow at the right gradient, dam walls are level, and house sites are properly prepared. While professional surveyors use sophisticated instruments, many simple, handmade tools can achieve excellent results for on-farm and residential earthworks. Levels are taken to ensure that water flows where intended, structures are stable, and earthworks perform their designed functions.

## The Fundamental Principle

The most ancient and still reliable leveling reference is the **surface of water**, which always finds its own horizontal level. Even sophisticated instruments like the theodolite use a bubble level to set up the base plate and to verify the telescope alignment before proceeding with tilt or swivel measurements. A designer can always check a horizontal against the sea horizon, or verify a drain or gutter works by flowing water through it.

## The Hose (Bunyip) Level

The hose level, known as the "Bunyip" level in Australia, is one of the most practical and versatile surveying tools available to farmers and homeowners. It can survey many meters or even kilometers of [[diversion-drain-mollison]], level a house site from corner to corner, or set a wall or spillway at a predetermined level.

### Materials Required

- 20 m (65 feet) or more of clear plastic hose, approximately 10 mm (1/2 inch) diameter
- 2 corks or stoppers that fit the hose ends, each with a breather hole
- 2 small cork balls that freely fit inside the clear tube below the corks (optional but helpful)
- 2 stakes (2 m or 6.5 feet), marked with measurements — dressmakers' tapes glued or screwed to the stakes
- 4 clamps or tape to connect the hose to the stakes

### Calibration Procedure

1. Uncork one end and fill the entire hose with water, ensuring all air bubbles are removed (only certain with a completely clear hose)
2. Place both stakes together on a known level surface (level concrete or wood)
3. Keeping stakes vertical, mark the water level in the clear tubes by adjusting the measuring strips until the water level (or both cork floats) reads the same measurement on both stakes
4. Recork the tubes

### Field Operation

Two people work together, walking past each other alternately:

1. Drive a stake every 6 or 15 m (20 or 50 feet) to guide diggers or machine operators
2. Allow for the rise or fall of drains by permitting one cork ball to sit slightly lower than the other at the next peg
3. Move the pole up or downhill to achieve the desired gradient
4. **Keep stakes vertical** when taking measurements

### Gradient Calculations

A practical example: 1 cm fall in 5 m equals a 1:500 gradient (1 cm / 500 cm), which is ample for most [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]] carrying run-off to dams. Dead level lines can also be run across landscape, and dam walls checked for level or gentle rise.

Remarkably, a 12 km drain flowing at 1:2,000 gradient in sand was laid not by a skilled surveyor but by two women trained for just 30 minutes on a hose level, demonstrating the accessibility and reliability of this tool.

## The Plane Table

The plane table is a sturdy tripod holding a U-shaped piece of plastic tube or glass, corked and de-bubbled like the hose level, but able to swivel about to sight in all directions.

### Construction Variants

**Model A**: A bowl of water on which floats a piece of painted wood with two nails of equal length as sights. One nail may need tapping in slightly for adjustment.

**Model B**: A self-leveling design using a U-tube mounted on a tripod, providing more consistent readings.

### Calibration Check

1. Set up two marked stakes with visible white masking tape a little uphill from the survey point
2. Drive the stake tops to level using the plane table
3. From position B, check that the level shows new level marks at exactly the same distance below the stake tops
4. Verify by measuring this distance

### Applications

The plane table is particularly useful as an across-valley level for:
- Setting in siphons
- Determining where a dam outlet will send water by pipe and gravity flow
- Checking whether a distant hill (for a tank placement) is higher than a shower head at home
- Long-distance leveling where a hose level would be impractical

## The A-Frame Level

The A-frame is one of the oldest and simplest leveling devices, used for centuries to lay out contours and drains.

### Construction

1. Two wooden or metal legs nailed, welded, or firmly joined at the apex
2. A cross-piece fixed about two-thirds down the leg length (nailed, welded, or glued)
3. A plumb bob or weight hanging from a cord at the center of the apex
4. A mark made on the cross-piece where the cord touches it
5. The frame is reversed and another mark made
6. The mid-point between the two marks is the true level position — where the cord hangs when the feet are level

### Field Operation

A person swivels the frame about one leg and sets the second leg where the cord hangs vertical. A temporary peg is placed to judge where to position the next leg. Many meters of drain have been set using A-frames, often made on-site from lumber, cord, and a weight for the plumb bob.

### Advantages and Limitations

- Extremely simple and inexpensive to construct
- Requires no water (unlike hose and plane table levels)
- Can be made from materials found on any farm or construction site
- Slower to use than a hose level for long distances
- Best suited for shorter runs and contour marking

## The Long Inclinometer

Ralph Long of Australia devised a tractor-mounted level successfully used to cut drains, using the tractor itself as a check on levels. This is a bubble level that can be fitted to any vehicle on farms. It allows a single operator to both drive and level simultaneously, significantly speeding up drain construction.

## Professional Instruments

When in doubt about accuracy, or for very precise work:

- **Dumpy level** — a simple optical level for reading elevations at distance
- **Theodolite** — a precision instrument for measuring both horizontal and vertical angles
- **Hire a surveyor** — whose real job is mapping, not necessarily [[mollison-course-swales-and-earthworks-design]]

Thousands of miles of drains have been surveyed and levels set by the simple tools described above. Professional instruments are tools of convenience and precision, not necessity, for most permaculture-scale earthworks.

## Key Principles for Accurate Leveling

1. **Level only once if done right** — take the time to calibrate and check your instrument before starting
2. **Check calibration regularly** — verify your tool against a known level surface before each use
3. **Keep stakes vertical** — tilted stakes introduce systematic error into all measurements
4. **Work with a partner** — two people working together with a hose level are faster and more accurate than one person with any tool
5. **Allow for gradient** — most drains need a fall, not dead level; calculate the required drop per distance
6. **Mark pegs clearly** — use visible tape or cloth so machine operators can follow the intended line
7. **Verify with water** — the ultimate test of any drain or spillway is whether water flows as intended
