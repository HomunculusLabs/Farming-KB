# Mycorrhizal Interactions with Plant Pathogens

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play a significant role in defending host plants against root pathogenic fungi and nematodes. These interactions occur at the soil–root interface, where elevated populations of fungi and bacteria are encouraged to grow in the rhizosphere by the presence of readily available carbohydrates in the form of [[root-exudates]] and dead root cells. The mycorrhizal fungal community associated with a plant's roots has special significance in creating a defense mechanism against root pathogenic fungi.

## Mechanisms of Protection

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae

In arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) systems, protection operates primarily through indirect mechanisms rather than physical barriers. The protective effect of AM fungi is attributed to:

- **Improved host plant nutrition**: AM fungi enhance phosphorus and other nutrient uptake, making the host plant more vigorous and better able to resist pathogen attack
- **Altered plant physiology**: AM colonization changes host plant physiology in ways that enhance the plant's ability to defend itself
- **Reduced nematode fecundity**: AM fungi can reduce fertility and egg production of plant-parasitic nematodes
- **Decreased root penetrability**: AM colonization can make roots less accessible to invading pathogens

A meta-analysis by Borowicz (2001) of studies published between 1970 and early 1998 revealed that approximately 50% of studies showed AM fungi afforded some degree of protection to their host plant against pathogenic fungi and nematodes. The effect of pathogenic fungi usually reduced the growth of the AM fungus, but this effect was less frequently observed for nematodes. Mutual suppression of both competing fungi occurred in only 16% of reported cases.

### Ectomycorrhizae

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi provide protection through additional mechanisms:

- **Physical defense**: A fungal sheath (mantle) envelops short roots colonized by the ECM fungus, creating a physical barrier against pathogen entry
- **Chemical (antibiotic) interference**: ECM fungi produce antibiotic compounds that inhibit pathogen growth
- **Biocontrol potential**: ECM fungi have been investigated as biocontrol agents in nursery conditions

## Documented Protective Effects

### Glomus mosseae Against Pod Rot Pathogens

Abdalla and Abdel-Fattah (2000) demonstrated significant protective effects of the AM fungus *[[glomus-mosseae]]* against two pod rot pathogens, *Fusarium solani* and *Rhizoctonia solani*, on peanut plants. Key findings included:

- Mycorrhizal inoculation increased peanut plant growth and yield
- Pathogen inoculation significantly reduced shoot and root dry weights, pod number, and seed weight
- Mycorrhizal plants showed significantly higher growth and biomass than non-mycorrhizal plants, both with and without pathogens
- Although pathogens reduced root colonization by the mycorrhizal fungus, pathogen propagule numbers were significantly lower in mycorrhizal compared to non-mycorrhizal plants
- *G. mosseae* not only protected plants from infection but also reduced the fecundity of the fungal pathogen

### Glomus intraradices Against Burrowing Nematodes

Elsen et al. (2001) showed that the AM fungus *Glomus intraradices* significantly reduced the reproductive capacity of the burrowing nematode *[[radopholus-similis]]* on carrot root tissue. The mycorrhizal fungus suppressed the nematode population by approximately 50%, with statistically significant reductions in juvenile, female, and male nematodes in root tissue and females in the growth medium.

### Ectomycorrhizal Protection of Tree Seedlings

Multiple studies have demonstrated ECM protection of tree seedlings:

- *Laccaria laccata* suppressed *Fusarium oxysporum* on Douglas fir seedlings (Sylvia and Sinclair, 1983)
- The same ECM fungus protected *Pinus banksiana* seedlings from Fusarium (Chakravarty and Hwang, 1991)
- Four ECM fungi (*Laccaria laccata*, *[[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]]*, *H. sinapizans*, and *[[paxillus-involutus]]*) protected chestnut seedlings from *Phytophthora cambivora* and *P. cinnamomi* (Branzanti et al., 1999)

## Interactions with Hemiparasitic Plants

The relationship between mycorrhizae and root hemiparasitic plants is complex. Salonen et al. (2001) investigated the effect of AM fungi on hemiparasitic plants of clover and grass:

- AM colonization of annual grass (*Poa annua*) had little effect on plant growth, while hemiparasite infection (*Odontites vulgaris*) significantly reduced host biomass
- AM status of *P. annua* did not affect the biomass or flower production of the parasitic plant
- AM colonization of clover (*[[trifolium-pratense]]*) greatly increased host plant biomass, but hemiparasite infection (*Rhinanthus serotinus*) had no effect
- AM fungi increased parasitic plant biomass and flower production when attached to mycorrhizal clover hosts

These results suggest that improved host performance due to mycorrhizae can inadvertently benefit parasitic plants, highlighting the complexity of multi-species interactions.

## The Balance Between Mycorrhizae and Pathogens

Newsham et al. (1994) demonstrated a fine balance between beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and detrimental pathogenic fungi using fungicide experiments on the annual grass *Vulpia ciliata*:

- The fungicide perchloraz did not affect AM abundance or plant performance
- Benomyl significantly reduced mycorrhizal colonization but did not significantly lower plant growth or phosphorus inflow
- Benomyl did increase plant fecundity (seed number), possibly by simultaneously reducing root pathogenic fungi alongside mycorrhizal fungi
- The lack of mycorrhizal response (reduced growth with reduced colonization) was offset by improved growth when the plant was relieved of stress from weak root pathogens such as *Fusarium oxysporum* and *Embellisia chlamydospora*

## Knowledge Gaps and Open Questions

Much of the documented evidence for [[mycorrhizal-protection-against-plant-pathogens]] pathogens comes from agricultural ecosystems or forest nursery studies, where host plant diversity is low (usually monospecific crops). Critical unanswered questions include:

- How do these interactions function in natural ecosystems with diverse plant communities?
- Has an evolutionary balance been maintained between mycorrhizal and pathogenic fungal functional groups?
- How much do these interactions contribute to plant fitness and [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]]?
- Could the balance shift with plant phenology or changes in environmental conditions, potentially leading to either enhanced growth or significantly reduced growth?
- Are these interactions a coevolutionary pathway to maintain balance between different functional groups of fungi?

## Climate Change Implications

Simulation models predict significantly enhanced risks of fungal pathogen outbreaks and increased severity of these outbreaks on crop plants with predicted increases in temperature and reduced rainfall (Jahn et al., 1996; Luo et al., 1995). Phytophthora-induced decline of oaks in Europe is also predicted to increase due to climate change (Brasier, 1996). These predictions underscore the importance of understanding mycorrhizal-pathogen interactions in the context of changing environmental conditions.
