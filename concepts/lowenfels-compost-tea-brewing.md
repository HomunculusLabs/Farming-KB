# Compost Tea Brewing

Compost tea is a liquid extract of compost that is actively brewed to multiply
beneficial microorganisms. In "Teaming With Microbes," Lowenfels and Lewis
present compost tea as a practical tool for inoculating soil and plant surfaces
with diverse microbial communities. Unlike passive compost extracts, actively
aerated compost tea (AACT) is brewed with controlled aeration and food sources
to produce a concentrated suspension of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and
nematodes that can be applied as a soil drench or foliar spray.

## Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)

The distinction between actively aerated compost tea and anaerobic compost tea
is critical. AACT uses continuous aeration to maintain dissolved oxygen levels
above 6 parts per million, ensuring that aerobic microorganisms dominate the
brew. Anaerobic teas, in contrast, can produce compounds toxic to plants and
beneficial organisms. AACT produces a microbial community that mirrors the
biology found in healthy compost, but at concentrations many times higher per
unit volume.

The aeration system typically consists of an air pump connected to diffusers
or bubblers placed at the bottom of the brewing vessel. This creates a column
of rising bubbles that circulates the water, extracts microorganisms from the
compost, and provides the oxygen that aerobic organisms need to reproduce. A
standard brew cycle lasts 24 to 36 hours, during which microbial populations
can increase 10,000-fold or more from their starting concentrations in the
compost.

## Brewing Methods and Equipment

A basic AACT setup requires several components:

- **Brewing vessel:** A food-grade container, typically 5 to 50 gallons,
  equipped with a lid to prevent contamination.
- **Aeration system:** An air pump rated for the volume of the vessel,
  connected to air stones or diffusers that produce fine bubbles.
- **Compost:** High-quality, aerobic, finished compost is essential. The
  compost must be biologically active and free of pathogens. Compost from
  diverse feedstocks provides the broadest microbial inoculum.
- **Food sources:** To fuel microbial reproduction during the brew. Common
  food sources include unsulfured molasses (feeds bacteria), fish hydrolysate
  (feeds both bacteria and fungi), kelp meal (provides micronutrients and
  microbial stimulants), and humic acids.

The compost is placed in a mesh bag or basket suspended in the water. Food
sources are dissolved in the water, and aeration begins. Temperature should
be maintained between 65 and 75 degrees Fahrenheit for optimal microbial
growth. The tea is ready when it has a sweet, earthy smell and a foamy surface.

## Controlling Microbial Diversity

The microbial composition of compost tea can be adjusted by modifying the food
sources used during brewing. This allows growers to produce teas tailored to
specific needs:

- **Bacterial-dominant tea:** High in molasses and simple sugars, favoring
  rapid bacterial growth. Suitable for annual crops, vegetables, and early
  successional soils as described in [[lowenfels-soil-food-web-succession]].
- **Fungal-dominant tea:** Includes fish hydrolysate, kelp, and small amounts
  of humic acid, with minimal simple sugars. Suitable for perennials, trees,
  and soils needing more fungal biomass for
  [[lowenfels-soil-fungi-decomposition]].
- **Balanced tea:** A combination of food sources that supports both bacteria
  and fungi, providing the broadest microbial diversity.

The quality of the starting compost ultimately determines the microbial
diversity of the tea. Compost made from diverse materials, properly managed
with adequate aeration and moisture, contains the widest range of beneficial
organisms.

## Application Rates and Methods

Compost tea can be applied as a soil drench or as a foliar spray. As a soil
drench, typical application rates are 10 to 20 gallons per acre, diluted with
enough water for even coverage. The tea should be applied within 4 to 6 hours
of brewing to ensure maximum microbial viability. As a foliar spray, compost
tea coats leaf surfaces with beneficial bacteria that compete with pathogens
for space and resources, and can trigger induced systemic resistance in plants.

Application timing matters. Early season applications establish beneficial
microbial populations before pathogen pressure builds. Regular applications
every 2 to 4 weeks maintain microbial coverage on plant surfaces. Soil
drenches are most effective when applied to moist soil, as this allows
microorganisms to establish and begin cycling nutrients for the
[[lowenfels-rhizosphere-bacteria-plant-interaction]].

## Disease Suppression Mechanisms

Compost tea suppresses plant diseases through several mechanisms:

- **Competition:** Beneficial organisms occupy space and consume nutrients
  on plant surfaces, denying these resources to pathogens.
- **Antibiosis:** Some compost microorganisms produce antibiotics or other
  antimicrobial compounds that inhibit pathogen growth.
- **Predation:** Protozoa and nematodes in compost tea consume pathogenic
  bacteria and fungal spores.
- **Induced systemic resistance:** Exposure to beneficial microbes primes
  the plant's immune system, making it more resistant to subsequent pathogen
  attack.
- **Enzyme activity:** Microbial enzymes in tea can degrade pathogen
  structures directly.

## Quality Control and Testing

Reliable compost tea requires quality inputs and monitoring. The compost
should be tested for biological activity before use. During brewing, dissolved
oxygen levels, temperature, and pH should be monitored. Finished tea should
smell earthy and pleasant; foul or putrid odors indicate anaerobic conditions
and potential pathogen growth. Microscopic examination can verify the presence
and diversity of organisms in the finished tea.

## Integration with Soil Food Web Management

Compost tea is most effective as part of a comprehensive soil biology
management program. It works synergistically with [[lowenfels-cover-crops-soil-biology]]
that maintain living roots, minimal tillage that preserves established
biological networks, and [[lowenfels-glomalin-soil-aggregation]] practices
that build soil structure. Tea applications supplement rather than replace
these foundational practices, providing periodic boosts of microbial diversity
and activity to support the entire [[lowenfels-soil-food-web-succession]].
