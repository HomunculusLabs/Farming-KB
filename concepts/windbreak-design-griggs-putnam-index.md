# Windbreak Design and the Griggs-Putnam Index

## Using Trees as Wind Indicators

Trees are living records of local wind history. They permanently "flag" or deform in prevailing strong winds and can be used to assess the effects of such winds on any site. By reading tree deformation patterns, a [[permaculture]] designer can determine:

- **Prevailing wind direction** — trees flag away from the strongest winds
- **Wind intensity** — the degree of deformation correlates with mean wind speed
- **Optimal windbreak placement** — to protect homes, crops, and livestock
- **Wind energy potential** — for siting wind machines

## The Griggs and Putnam Index

The Griggs and Putnam Index is a field assessment tool that correlates observed tree deformations with annual mean wind speed. It provides approximately **17% accuracy** in predicting mean wind speeds, making it a valuable first-pass assessment for site design.

### Wind Deformation Scale

| Index | Description | Visual Indicator | Mean Wind Speed |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 0 | **No effect** | No deformations of branches | < 3.3 m/sec (< 7.5 mph) |
| 1 | **Brushing** | Branches or needles bent away from prevailing winds; crown slightly deformed | 3.3–4.2 m/sec (7.5–9.5 mph) |
| 2 | **Slight flagging** | Small branches bent; tree markedly asymmetric | 4.3–5.1 m/sec (9.6–11.5 mph) |
| 3 | **Moderate flagging** | Larger branches bent away from the wind | 5.2–6.2 m/sec (11.6–14 mph) |
| 4 | **Strong flagging** | All branches swept to leeward; tree becomes a "banner" | 6.3–7.5 m/sec (14–17 mph) |
| 5 | **Partial throw** | Trunk bent to leeward but vertical near ground | 7.8–8.5 m/sec (17–19 mph) |
| 6 | **Complete throw** | Trunk now quite bent to leeward | 8.6–11 m/sec (19–24 mph) |
| 7 | **Carpeting** | Tree becomes a creeping shrub-like layer of prostrate growth | > 11 m/sec (> 24 mph) |

> **Note:** The above effects are standard for trees not affected by salt, ice, or snow load. Trees exposed to coastal salt spray or heavy ice/snow loads may show additional or confounding deformations.

### Field Application

When visiting any site, examine the condition of **older trees** — they are the best guide to gauge long-term wind effects. The methodology is:

1. **Observe the crown shape** from multiple angles
2. **Note the direction of flagging** — this indicates prevailing wind direction
3. **Assess the degree of deformation** using the Griggs-Putnam scale
4. **Cross-reference multiple trees** for consistency
5. **Note any edge effects** — trees on forest edges show stronger deformation than interior trees

## Windbreak Design Principles

### Permeability and Compression

Research in Holland and Sweden using fine grids of rain gauges has demonstrated that a hedgerow or windbreak of approximately **40% permeability** creates the strongest compression effects on wind streamlines. This compression:

- Extends to **twenty times the tree height** above the windbreak
- Can cause up to **40% of measured rainfall** downwind to result from streamline compression
- Creates **Ekman spirals** — a series of compression fronts repeated 5–8 times downwind
- At wind speeds above 62 km/h, streamlines may be preserved and rain falls perpendicular to the windbreak
- At lower (normal) wind speeds, turbulence and overturn occurs, producing the spiral pattern

### Forest Edge Protection

**Design principle: Forest edges are essential and permanent protection and should never be cut or removed.**

The windward forest edge contains thick-stemmed, specially wind-adapted trees that buffer the frontline attack of wind. If this edge is removed:

- **Windburn** by salt, dust abrasion, or wind force may kill the inner forest
- Weaker-stemmed and less resistant interior species become vulnerable
- This phenomenon, called **"edge break,"** is commonly observed worldwide

Conversely, new forests can be established by:
1. Planting tough, resistant tree species as the initial windbreak
2. Protecting subsequent downwind plantings with this first line of defence
3. Eventually establishing a multi-layered, self-protecting forest edge

### Wind Direction Deflection

Wind crossing tree lines is deflected in a new direction. The Ekman spirals created over hedgerows or forest edges deflect wind by **5–15 degrees** from its previous course due to drag and the Coriolis force. Strategically placed ranks of hedgerows could theoretically bring wind around in a great ground spiral, offering significant control over microclimate.

## Practical Windbreak Design

### Siting for Home Protection

- Place windbreaks to **reduce heat loss** from buildings in winter
- Avoid placing windbreaks where they would **channel catastrophic winds** toward structures
- Use tree deformation indicators on site to determine **prevailing wind direction** before placing windbreaks
- Consider the **Griggs-Putnam Index value** when designing for extreme wind events

### Siting for Wind Energy

- Trees showing Index 3+ flagging indicate sites with **sufficient wind resource** for power generation
- Use the deformation patterns to identify the **best wind corridors** between trees or topographic features
- Place wind machines in positions **steered by tree placement** to capture accelerated airflow

### Siting for Agriculture

- Windbreaks reduce wind speed for approximately **10–15 times their height** downwind
- For maximum crop protection, design windbreaks with **40% permeability**
- Use **tough pioneer species** on the windward edge, with more sensitive species protected behind
- Multi-row windbreaks with varying heights create layered protection

### Hedgerow Systems

- In flat country, systematic hedgerow placement can significantly increase local rainfall through streamline compression
- Hedgerows perpendicular to prevailing moist winds are most effective for precipitation enhancement
- Spacing of hedgerows should consider the **20× height** compression zone above and the **10–15× height** protection zone downwind

## Wind Effects on Tree Biology

### Mechanical Adaptations

Trees have evolved several strategies to withstand wind:

- **Heavy rigid trees** spread wide root mats, relying on weight for stability
- **Rock-clinging trees** insert gnarled roots deep into crevices for anchorage
- **Forest edge trees** grow thick and sturdy stems
- **Flexible species** bend and sway, each with its own characteristic amplitude
- **Special wood cells** are created to bear tension and compression

### Leaf Adaptations

- Some leaves **twist and reverse** to show white undersides to wind, reflecting light energy
- **Light-coloured leaves** are typically found only in forest edge species
- **Strand trees** (palms, pines, Casuarinas) develop tough stems or thick bark to withstand wind particle blast
- Even **tussock grasses** slow wind and cause dust loads to settle

### The Tethering Effect

If a tree is tethered halfway up its trunk, it **stops thickening below the tether** and grows in diameter only above the fixed point. This has implications for tree staking practices — over-staking young trees can weaken their lower trunk development.

## Summary

The Griggs-Putnam Index and the understanding of tree-wind interactions provide permaculture designers with a free, on-site assessment tool for wind conditions. By reading existing trees, designers can place windbreaks to protect buildings, crops, and livestock; site wind energy systems; increase local precipitation through streamline compression; and protect forest edges from catastrophic "edge break" failure.
