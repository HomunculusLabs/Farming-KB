# permaculture ethics and principles

Permaculture is a comprehensive design system for creating sustainable human
settlements and food production systems. Developed by Bill Mollison and David
Holmgren in the 1970s, it draws from patterns found in nature to create
productive, resilient systems that require minimal external inputs. The term
combines "permanent" and "agriculture" (or "culture"), reflecting its goal of
creating enduring, self-sustaining systems modeled on natural ecosystems.

## The Three Ethics

An ethic is like a code of honor that guides behavior and shapes outcomes.
Permaculture rests on three foundational ethics:

### Care for the Earth

All things, living or non-living, have intrinsic worth. This ethic demands
that we actively regenerate soils, protect waterways, preserve biodiversity,
and design systems that give back to the land rather than deplete it. Healthy
ecosystems are the foundation upon which human survival depends.

### Care for People

Humanity is cared for through self-reliance and community responsibility.
This means designing systems that meet human needs for food, shelter, and
community while fostering cooperation and mutual aid. It extends beyond
individual self-sufficiency to include building resilient communities that
can support all members.

### Return of Surplus

The surplus produced by a well-designed system must be shared to fulfill the
other two ethics. Surplus can take the form of excess food, knowledge, time,
or resources. When each resource is valued rather than exploited, and people
care for themselves as well as their community, an abundance of resources is
the natural result. This creates a circular system where surplus feeds back
into caring for earth and people.

## Linear vs. Circular Systems

In conventional systems, everything follows a linear path from source to
landfill. Resources are used once or twice and then discarded into water and
air. Sustainable permaculture systems are circular: used items return to their
source, undergo natural recycling processes, and become available for reuse
with very little energy expenditure. This mirrors the way natural ecosystems
operate, where nothing is wasted and every output becomes an input elsewhere.

## The Seven Principles of Permaculture

Every design system has guiding principles. Permaculture follows seven core
principles that inform all design decisions:

1. **Everything is connected to and supported by everything else.** No
   element exists in isolation; every plant, animal, structure, and water
   feature affects and is affected by everything around it.

2. **Every element should serve many functions.** A single element should
   fulfill multiple roles. A pond, for example, provides irrigation water,
   habitat for wildlife, fire protection, and microclimate moderation.

3. **Functional design is sustainable.** Design must provide a useful product
   or surplus. If it does not, it creates pollution (an overabundance of an
   unused resource) and unnecessary work.

4. **Maximize useful energy in any system.** Permaculture seeks to capture,
   store, and use energy efficiently while minimizing waste of energy at every
   step of the process.

5. **Successful design provides diversity.** Many useful connections between
   elements create resilience. Diversity ensures that if one element fails,
   the system as a whole continues to function.

6. **Pollution leads to chaos.** When resources are wasted or outputs become
   pollutants, the entire system destabilizes. Good design eliminates waste
   by ensuring every output has a use.

7. **Build cooperation into systems.** Opposition and disorder waste energy.
   Only use what can be returned to the soil, and build harmony and
   cooperation into the functional organization of every system.

## Sustainable Land Goals

When designing a sustainable piece of land, these measurable goals guide the
process. The system should become self-sustaining and productive in the long
term, with every creature living on the land getting its food and personal
needs from that land. About a third of the land should grow food for humans,
and the rest for animal living space and fodder. The land should produce more
than needed, with surplus sold or given away. A single person can manage less
than 25 acres effectively; smaller is often better.

## Measuring Success

For land to be considered sustainable, it must produce at least as much as it
consumes. Success is measured not just in pounds of food, but in energy
available in the system and intangible benefits. Water storage should cover
10 to 20 percent of the land. Tree windbreaks and forests should cover 20 to
30 percent. The farmer should perform less physical labor over time due to the
interconnectedness of the system, and the land should have recreational and
generational value.

## [[patterns-in-nature]]

Almost all [[permaculture-design]] strategies are drawn from recurring patterns
found in nature. The Fibonacci sequence creates proportional spirals found in
everything from snail shells to sunflower heads. Intertwining coils appear in
plant stems and DNA. These patterns exist because they work. Understanding
ecological succession, the process by which cleared land repopulates through
stages of pioneer plants, herbs and shrubs, and finally trees, allows us to
accelerate natural recovery and use these stages for our benefit.

## The Edge Effect

The edge of a forest gets more light and nutrients than the center, making it
far more productive. The line between two ecosystems, such as ocean and shore
or mountain and valley, contains organisms from both sides plus unique species.
Permaculture uses circles, spirals, curves, trellises, and zigzags to
maximize edge space and its productive potential.
