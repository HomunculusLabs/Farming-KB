## Establishing a Healthy Soil Foodweb

A healthy soil foodweb is the foundation of sustainable growing. By restoring the full community of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms, growers can shift away from chemical inputs and let biology do the work of nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil structure building. This page covers the benefits of a living soil system, the step-by-step process for assessing and balancing soil biology, and practical methods for inoculating and maintaining a thriving foodweb.

## Benefits of Returning a Healthy Foodweb to Soil

### 1. Out-Compete Disease Organisms

A diverse and active soil foodweb suppresses disease not through a pesticide effect, but through competition. When beneficial bacteria and fungi fully occupy root surfaces and soil particles, there is no space or food available for disease-causing organisms to establish. No toxic chemicals are needed — the biology itself provides the protection.

### 2. Retain Nutrients

In healthy soil, nutrients are held within the bodies of microorganisms and in soil organic matter. Bacteria and fungi immobilize nitrogen, phosphorus, and other minerals, preventing them from leaching into groundwater or running off the surface. This means soluble nutrients stay where plants need them.

### 3. Make Nutrients Available at the Right Rates

Protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi, releasing nutrients in plant-available forms at the rate plants actually demand them. This natural nutrient cycling can eliminate the need for inorganic fertilizers. The plant drives the system through root exudates that feed specific microbial communities around the rhizosphere.

### 4. Decompose Toxins

Bacteria and fungi are capable of breaking down a wide range of toxic compounds, including pesticides, herbicides, and petroleum residues — but only if the right organisms are present and actively fed. Without them, toxins persist and accumulate in soil. This is a critical function in remediation and transition from conventional management.

### 5. Build and Rebuild Soil Structure

Soil aggregation — the formation of micro- and macro-aggregates with pores and channels — is strictly a biological process. Bacterial glues bind micro-aggregates together, fungal hyphae form the scaffolding for macro-aggregates, and the resulting pore network dramatically increases water holding capacity and rooting depth. This structure cannot be achieved with mechanical methods or chemical amendments alone.

## Assessing Your Soil: The Starting Point

Before making any amendments, you must first understand what biology is already present. This is done through a **soil foodweb analysis**, which measures:

- **Total and active bacterial biomass**
- **Total and active fungal biomass**
- **Protozoa populations** (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates)
- **Beneficial nematode communities**
- **Mycorrhizal colonization percentage**
- **Microarthropod presence**

Once you have results, compare them against desired ranges for your crop type and climate. Then check the **ratios** between groups — for example, the fungal-to-bacterial ratio is a key indicator of successional stage and suitability for different plant types.

## The 12 Steps to Balance the Soil Foodweb

### Step 1: Total Bacterial Biomass Too Low → Add Bacterial Inoculum

If total bacterial biomass is below the desired range, introduce a quality bacterial inoculum. Sources include well-made aerobic compost, compost tea, or commercial bacterial products (though commercial products often contain only a few species and may lack the diversity needed for full function).

### Step 2: Active Bacterial Biomass Too Low → Add Bacterial Foods

If total bacteria are adequate but activity is low, the bacteria are dormant and need food. Apply bacterial foods such as simple sugars, molasses, or light protein meals. **Critical warning:** do not apply large amounts all at once, or the bacterial bloom will consume oxygen rapidly and drive the soil anaerobic. Apply in small, repeated doses.

### Step 3: Total Fungal Biomass Too Low → Add Fungal Inoculum

If total fungal biomass is insufficient, introduce fungal inoculum from fungal-dominated compost, compost tea, or specific fungal amendments. Fungi are especially important for perennial crops, pastures, and woodlands.

### Step 4: Active Fungal Biomass Too Low → Add Fungal Foods

If fungi are present but inactive, feed them with complex carbon sources: humic acids, fulvic acids, fish hydrolysate, kelp, or finely ground woody materials. These slower-to-break-down materials favor fungal growth over bacterial dominance.

### Step 5: Mycorrhizal Colonization Needs Improvement

Mycorrhizal fungi form a symbiotic relationship with plant roots, extending the root network and improving nutrient and water uptake.

- **0–15% colonization:** Add VAM (vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal) spores directly adjacent to roots or seeds at planting time. Place them where they will contact emerging roots.
- **15–40% colonization:** The mycorrhizal network is developing but not yet robust. Apply humic acids to feed existing mycorrhizal fungi and encourage further colonization.

### Steps 6–8: Protozoa Too Low → Add Protozoa Inoculum

Protozoa (flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates) are the primary grazers of bacteria and are essential for nutrient cycling.

- If populations are low, add protozoa inoculum from **worm compost** (vermicompost is typically rich in protozoa) or commercial protozoa products.
- **High ciliate numbers** are a red flag — they indicate compacted or anaerobic soil conditions. Address compaction and aeration before attempting further biological amendments.

### Steps 9–11: Beneficial Nematodes Too Low → Add Nematode Inoculum

Nematodes are categorized by their feeding habits and each group plays a distinct role:

- **Bacterial-feeding nematodes:** cycle nutrients by grazing bacteria. Aim for at least **6 species**.
- **Fungal-feeding nematodes:** graze fungi and help regulate fungal biomass. Aim for at least **6 species**.
- **Predatory nematodes:** consume other nematodes and keep root-feeding species in check. Aim for at least **2 species**.

If root-feeding nematodes are present, the solution is not a nematicide — it is to improve the full community of beneficial organisms and mycorrhizal colonization. Predatory nematodes and a robust foodweb will suppress root-feeders naturally.

### Step 12: Check Microarthropods, Earthworms, and Higher Predators

These larger organisms are the top of the soil foodweb. Microarthropods (springtails, mites) shred organic material and fungal hyphae, making nutrients available. Earthworms aerate soil, create channels for roots and water, and produce nutrient-rich casts. Their presence indicates a mature, functioning foodweb. If they are absent, focus on building the lower trophic levels first — they will arrive as conditions improve.

## Inoculation Sources and Materials

### Compost Types

| Compost Type | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Thermal compost** | Hot composting process; fungal-to-bacterial ratio can be controlled by feedstock selection | General soil inoculation; adjust F:B with food choices |
| **Worm compost (vermicompost)** | Rich in protozoa and beneficial bacteria; can be made fungal-dominant by feeding cardboard | Protozoa source, gentle inoculum for seedlings |
| **Static compost** | Anaerobic conditions inside; do not open until fully finished | Use with caution; can contain anaerobic organisms |

### Compost Tea

Compost tea is a liquid extract made by aerating compost in water, which multiplies the beneficial organisms. It can be applied as a **foliar spray** (to protect leaf surfaces) or as a **soil drench** (to inoculate the rhizosphere). The tea can be bacterial-dominant or fungal-dominant depending on the compost source and the foods added during brewing.

### Evaluating Commercial Inocula

Many commercial bacterial inocula contain only one or a few species and may not provide the functional diversity needed for a complete foodweb. Additionally, **fermentative bacteria** (common in some commercial products) are not the organisms that protect roots. Always test biological materials before purchasing in bulk. Prioritize maximum species diversity in any inoculum.

## Placement Methods for Amendments

The way you apply biological amendments matters as much as what you apply:

- **Mulch:** Selects for fungi by providing a steady supply of complex carbon on the soil surface. Best for building fungal-dominated soils.
- **Plugs:** Fill seedling trays with aerobic compost mixed with VAM spores. Transplant the plug for direct root contact with mycorrhizae.
- **Cores:** For established perennials that lack mycorrhizal colonization, remove cores of soil around the root zone and replace with inoculum-rich compost.
- **Incorporated:** Mix amendments directly into soil. Use when wind or water erosion may remove surface mulch.
- **Drip:** Add compost tea directly into drip irrigation systems for efficient root-zone delivery.
- **Spray:** Apply compost tea as a foliar spray or surface application for rapid coverage.

## Key Principles

- **Inoculate maximum diversity.** The more species present, the more resilient and functional the foodweb.
- **Feed the biology, don't just add it.** Organisms without food go dormant or die.
- **Avoid anaerobic conditions.** Apply foods in small doses and ensure adequate soil aeration.
- **Test before and after.** Soil foodweb analysis is the only way to know if your amendments are working.
- **Be patient.** Building a mature foodweb takes multiple growing seasons, especially on degraded land.
