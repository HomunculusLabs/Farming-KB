# Hot Composting Method

> Note: This page is compiled from general knowledge, not sourced from a specific file in the wiki's raw/ directory.

Hot composting (thermophilic composting) is a managed decomposition process that uses aerobic microorganisms to rapidly break down organic materials at elevated temperatures. When done correctly, a hot [[mushroom-cultivator-compost-pile-management]] reaches internal temperatures of 130-160F (55-71C), significantly speeding decomposition compared to cold composting while destroying weed seeds, pathogens, and disease organisms.

## The Three Temperature Stages

### Stage 1: Mesophilic Phase (Room temperature to 105F / 40C)
- **Duration**: First 1-3 days
- **What happens**: Mesophilic (moderate-temperature) bacteria and fungi begin decomposing readily available sugars and simple compounds. They multiply rapidly, generating heat as a byproduct of [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]].
- **Oxygen demand**: High — the pile needs good aeration
- **Moisture**: Should be 50-60% (feels like a wrung-out sponge)

### Stage 2: Thermophilic Phase (105-160F / 40-71C)
- **Duration**: Days 3 to 14 (or longer, depending on management)
- **What happens**: Thermophilic (heat-loving) bacteria take over as temperatures rise. These include thermophilic fungi (Thermomyces, Humicola) and actinomycetes. Decomposition accelerates dramatically. Complex compounds like cellulose and lignin begin breaking down.
- **Critical temperature windows**:
  - 105-130F (40-55C): Optimal for most decomposer activity
  - 131-150F (55-65C): Kills most weed seeds, many pathogens, and fly larvae. Optimal zone for rapid decomposition.
  - 150-160F (65-71C): Maximum thermophilic activity. Beyond this, microbial populations begin to decline. Excessive temperatures destroy nutrients as nitrogen is lost as ammonia gas.
- **Management**: This phase requires monitoring. If temperature exceeds 160F (71C), the pile should be turned to release heat and reintroduce oxygen.

### Stage 3: Cooling and Curing Phase (Back to ambient)
- **Duration**: Weeks to months
- **What happens**: Thermophiles die off as food supply diminishes and temperature drops. Mesophilic organisms return, followed by larger decomposers (springtails, mites, earthworms). Remaining [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] slowly converts to humus. The pile matures and stabilizes.
- **When finished**: Compost is dark brown, crumbly, earthy-smelling, and none of the original materials are recognizable. Internal temperature matches ambient air temperature.

## Carbon-Nitrogen Ratio (C/N)

The ideal starting C/N for hot composting is 25-30:1. This provides enough carbon for energy and enough nitrogen for microbial protein synthesis.

### C/N of Common Materials

| "Green" (Nitrogen-rich, low C/N) | C/N | "Brown" (Carbon-rich, high C/N) | C/N |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh grass clippings | ~20:1 | Dry leaves | ~60:1 |
| [[oyster-mushroom-coffee-grounds-cultivation]] | ~20:1 | Straw | ~80:1 |
| Kitchen scraps | ~15:1 | Sawdust | ~400:1 |
| Fresh manure (cow) | ~20:1 | Shredded paper | ~175:1 |
| Fresh manure (horse) | ~25:1 | Cardboard | ~350:1 |
| Fresh manure (chicken) | ~10:1 | Corn stalks | ~60:1 |
| Green garden waste | ~20:1 | Wood chips | ~400:1 |
| Alfalfa/lucerne | ~12:1 | Tree bark | ~100:1 |
| Seaweed | ~19:1 | Pine needles | ~80:1 |
| Blood meal | ~4:1 | Peat moss | ~58:1 |

### Achieving the Right Ratio

A practical rule of thumb: build the pile with roughly 2-3 parts "brown" material to 1 part "green" material by volume. If the pile smells of ammonia, add more carbon. If it doesn't heat up, add more nitrogen.

## Pile Construction

### Minimum Size
- Minimum volume: 1 cubic metre (3x3x3 feet / 1x1x1 m)
- Below this, heat dissipates faster than it is generated
- Ideal size: 1.5-2 cubic metres (4x4x4 feet)

### Building the Pile (Layer Method)

1. **Base layer** (15-20 cm): [[fungal-diversity-leaf-litter-coarse-woody-debris]] material, branches, or wood chips for drainage and aeration
2. **Alternate layers** of greens and browns, each 5-10 cm thick
3. **Moisten each layer** as you build — aim for 50-60% moisture
4. **Optional activators**: A thin layer of finished compost, manure, or garden soil between layers to inoculate with microorganisms
5. **Cap with finished compost or soil** (5 cm) to insulate and retain heat, reduce odour, and prevent flies

### Moisture Management
- Target: 50-60% moisture content
- Test: Squeeze a handful — it should feel like a wrung-out sponge. A few drops of water come out, but it doesn't drip.
- Too dry: Add water when turning, or add more green material
- Too wet: Add brown material, turn to aerate, or build a roof/cover

## Turning Schedule

Turning is critical for hot composting — it:
- Reintroduces oxygen for aerobic decomposition
- Redistributes moisture
- Moves undecomposed material from the outside to the hot centre
- Breaks up compaction

### Standard Turning Schedule

| Day | Action | Expected Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build pile | — |
| 2-3 | Pile begins heating | 90-110F / 32-43C |
| 4-5 | **Turn #1** (when temp reaches 130F / 55C) | Drops to ~100F, rebounds to 130-150F |
| 7-8 | **Turn #2** | Similar rebound |
| 10-11 | **Turn #3** | May not reach as high |
| 14 | **Turn #4** (optional) | Temperature declining |
| 21-28 | Curing phase | Ambient |

The pile should be turned each time the internal temperature peaks and begins to decline (typically every 3-5 days). After 3-5 turns, let the pile cure for 2-4 weeks before use.

### How to Turn

Use a pitchfork or compost aerator:
1. Move material from the outside of the pile to the inside
2. Move material from the top to the bottom
3. Break up any compacted clumps
4. Add water if the material is dry
5. Reshape the pile

## Hot Composting Benefits

- **Speed**: Finished compost in 4-6 weeks (vs. 6-12 months for cold composting)
- **Weed seed destruction**: Temperatures above 131F (55C) for 3+ days kill most weed seeds
- **Pathogen kill**: Human and plant pathogens are destroyed at thermophilic temperatures
- **Volume reduction**: 50-75% reduction in volume from start to finish
- **Nutrient retention**: Faster processing means less nitrogen loss compared to slow decomposition

## Common Problems

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Pile won't heat | Too small, too dry, C/N too high, insufficient nitrogen | Enlarge pile, add water, add green material or manure |
| Bad odour (ammonia) | Too much nitrogen, C/N too low | Add carbon material (leaves, straw, paper) |
| Bad odour (rotten eggs) | Anaerobic, too wet, too compacted | Turn the pile, add dry browns, improve drainage |
| Pile too hot (>160F) | Too much nitrogen, too large, poor aeration | Turn to release heat, add carbon, reduce pile size |
| Pests (flies, rodents) | Food scraps exposed, insufficient covering | Bury greens under browns, cover pile, avoid meat/dairy |
| Finished but still has big pieces | Not enough turns, pieces too large | Screen finished compost, return large pieces to new pile |

## What NOT to Compost

- Meat, fish, bones (attract pests, though possible with careful management)
- Dairy products (attract pests)
- Diseased plant material (unless pile reliably reaches 131F+)
- Persistent weeds with mature seeds (unless pile reliably reaches 131F+)
- Pet waste (pathogen risk)
- Treated/painted wood (toxic chemicals)
- Coal ash (heavy metals)
- Glossy/coated paper (plastic coating)
