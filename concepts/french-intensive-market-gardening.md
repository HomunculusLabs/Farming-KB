## French Intensive Market Gardening

French intensive market gardening (maraîchage) is a historical system of highly productive, small-scale vegetable production that originated in the market gardens (maraîchers) of Paris and surrounding areas in the 17th through 19th centuries. This system achieved extraordinary yields per acre through careful soil management, close spacing, succession planting, and the use of protective structures. Its principles continue to influence modern organic farming, winter harvesting, and small-scale commercial vegetable operations worldwide.

## Historical Origins

The Parisian market garden system (culture maraîchère) was one of the most productive agricultural systems ever devised. Operating within and around Paris from the 1600s through the early 20th century, these gardens supplied the city with fresh vegetables year-round. The system was so productive that by the mid-19th century, Paris was fed by approximately 1,800 hectares of intensively cultivated market gardens, each typically ranging from 1 to 6 acres.

The system relied on deep, thorough soil preparation; massive applications of composted horse manure (readily available from the city's horse-drawn transport system); careful crop rotation; interplanting of compatible species; and the use of glass cloches, cold frames, and later small greenhouses to extend the growing season. The Parisian growers developed remarkably sophisticated techniques for their era, including heated beds using fresh manure decomposition and successive planting schedules that ensured continuous harvests.

Louis Savier's farm in France, visited by Eliot Coleman in the late 1980s, represented a living continuation of this tradition. Savier had acquired his market garden in the early 1960s from his father but rejected the shift to chemical inputs that had occurred during his father's lifetime. Instead, he returned to organic methods and eventually adopted biodynamic practices, reporting that pests and diseases had disappeared and crop quality had soared.

## Core Techniques

### Crop Rotation and Interplanting

Every bed in the French intensive system was part of a carefully planned crop rotation. Ingenious interplantings took advantage of complementary maturity times and growth characteristics of paired crops to maximize yields per square foot. Classic pairings included:

- **Leek/carrot**: Carrots mature slowly beneath the faster-growing leek tops, with the leeks providing some shade that benefits the carrots.
- **Mâche/onion**: Mâche (corn salad) grows low and slowly beneath or alongside onions, which grow upward and mature earlier.
- **Radish/romaine**: Radishes mature quickly (25–30 days) and are harvested before the romaine lettuce needs the full space.

These interplantings effectively double or triple the productive capacity of each bed compared to monoculture planting.

### Compost-Based Fertility

Composted horse manure was the primary fertilizer. In the original Parisian system, the sheer volume of manure available from the city's horse-drawn transport fleet made this both practical and economical. Fresh manure was also used for its heat-generating properties in hotbeds — a technique where deep layers of fresh manure were placed beneath growing frames, and the heat of decomposition provided bottom warmth to protect crops from freezing.

In the modern continuation observed by Coleman, composted manure remained the fertilizer of choice, though hot-water pipes had replaced manure-based heating for hotbeds.

### Protective Structures

The Parisian growers employed several types of protective structures:

- **Glass cloches** (bell jars): Individual plant covers that trapped heat and accelerated growth. These were labor-intensive but highly effective.
- **Cold frames** (châssis): Bottomless boxes with glass lids, used for starting seedlings and growing hardy crops through winter.
- **Greenhouses**: Later additions covering roughly one-quarter of the cropped area in Savier's operation.

In Savier's updated version, the labor-intensive glass cloches had been abandoned in favor of glass and plastic greenhouses, but cold frames remained central to the operation.

### Space-Efficient Infrastructure

A distinctive feature of the Parisian gardens — and of Savier's farm — was the use of 30-inch-wide lightweight rail tracks instead of roads for internal transport. Pushcarts ran along these tracks:

- One cart delivered compost to the beds
- One cart moved crates of freshly harvested produce to the packing shed
- A third cart carried the irrigation system, with long arms dispensing water through nozzles, self-propelled by water pressure

This rail system dramatically reduced the labor of moving materials across the farm, since the beds themselves were narrow enough that all work could be done from the paths between them.

## Labor and Economics

The original Parisian system employed approximately six workers per 1.5 acres — a remarkably labor-intensive operation. Workers commonly put in 16-hour days. By the time of Coleman's visit to Savier's farm in the 1980s, the average had been reduced to about 11 hours per day, thanks to rototillers and faster transport to market.

Savier noted that when his suburban neighbors boasted of working only 8-hour days, he pointed out their 3-hour average commute time and suggested he preferred spending those hours in his garden rather than in transit.

## Key Factors for Economic Success

Coleman identified four factors that brought economic success to the 19th-century maraîchers and which he argued would bring economic success to 21st-century market gardeners:

1. **Location**: Proximity to markets, reducing transport costs and time
2. **Sustainability**: Use of renewable inputs and practices that maintain soil health over time
3. **Quality**: Superior flavor, freshness, and nutritional value of the produce
4. **Productivity**: Extraordinary yields per unit area through intensive techniques

## Modern Relevance

The French intensive system remains highly relevant to modern small-scale farming for several reasons:

- **Urban agriculture**: Its space-efficient design makes it ideal for small plots near urban markets
- **Organic methods**: It demonstrates that high productivity is achievable without synthetic inputs
- **Season extension**: Its protective structures and planting schedules provide a model for winter harvesting
- **Economic viability**: The combination of high yields, premium quality, and low-input costs can make small farms economically sustainable

The techniques have been widely adapted and modified by subsequent generations of growers who incorporated new technologies and labor-saving improvements without losing their commitment to flavor, nutritional quality, and the beauty of good work.
