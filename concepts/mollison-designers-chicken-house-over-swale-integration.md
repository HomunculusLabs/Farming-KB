# Chicken House Over Swale Integration

A classic permaculture design pattern from bill mollison's
Permaculture: A Designers' Manual that positions poultry housing directly
above or adjacent to swales to create automatic nutrient transport from
chicken manure into the agricultural landscape via gravity and water flow.

## Core Design Concept

The chicken house is sited on the uphill side of a swale berm so that
manure, bedding, and urine wash directly into the swale during rainfall or
can be hosed down intentionally. This eliminates the labour of manually
collecting and spreading manure while delivering nutrients precisely where
water accumulates for tree crops and gardens downhill.

The swale itself serves as a moisture distribution system carrying the
nutrient-laden water laterally along contour to irrigate and fertilise a
broad planting zone. This is a textbook example of relative location
in permaculture design, where each element performs multiple functions and
supports adjacent elements.

## Interswale Spacing

Mollison specifies inter-swale spacing of 3 to 18 metres depending on
slope, rainfall, and intended use. Steeper slopes and higher rainfall
demand closer spacing (3-6m), while gentle slopes in drier regions can
support wider spacing (10-18m). The chicken house sits on one swale while
downhill swales support food forests, orchards, or garden systems fed
by the enriched water.

Key spacing considerations:

- 3-6m spacing: steep slopes above 15 degrees, high rainfall zones
- 6-10m spacing: moderate slopes of 5-15 degrees, temperate climates
- 10-18m spacing: gentle slopes below 5 degrees, arid or semi-arid zones

The area between swales becomes the productive zone where trees and crops
benefit from the subsurface moisture lens created by swale infiltration.

## Mulch Pits

Mulch pits are excavated holes (typically 30-60cm deep) placed between the
chicken house and the downhill planting zone. Filled with woody material,
weeds, and green manure, they act as nutrient sponges that absorb and
slowly release the chicken manure flowing from the house above. As mulch
breaks down, it creates humus-rich pockets that attract earthworms and
improve soil structure.

Mulch pits are typically planted with fast-growing [[symbiotic-fungi-nitrogen-fixing-symbioses-agriculture]]
shrubs or comfrey to mine nutrients and cycle them into leaf biomass
that further feeds the system.

## Tyre Ponds

Used car tyres can be stacked and sealed (or half-buried) downslope of
the chicken house to create small watering points. These tyre ponds serve
ducks or chickens directly while also growing duckweed, azolla, and water
hyacinth for poultry feed. They provide drinking water, dust bathing areas,
and supplementary forage all in one integrated unit.

Tyre ponds should be placed on contour so overflow feeds into the next
swale. In tropical climates, water plants in tyre ponds can double as
nutrient biofilters before water reaches food production zones.

## Windbreaks on Every 6th to 10th Swale

Mollison recommends planting windbreaks on every 6th to 10th swale
depending on wind exposure. These planted windbreaks reduce evaporation
from the swale system, protect poultry from cold winds, and create
microclimates that extend the growing season for understory crops.

Windbreak swales typically carry timber trees, casuarina, or
multi-purpose species that yield fodder, mulch, and wildlife habitat in
addition to wind protection. They are oriented perpendicular to prevailing
winds for maximum effect.

## System Integration Benefits

The chicken-over-swale pattern demonstrates several [[permaculture-principles]] operating simultaneously:

- Each element performs multiple functions (chickens produce eggs, manure,
  pest control, and soil aeration)
- Nutrient cycles are closed on-site rather than requiring external inputs
- Gravity replaces fossil-fuel energy for manure transport
- Water harvesting and nutrient cycling are combined in one earthwork
- The system is self-maintaining once established

## Practical Considerations

- Ensure the chicken house floor slopes slightly toward the swale for
  effective drainage
- Use deep litter bedding to absorb excess nitrogen before it reaches
  the swale
- Size the swale to handle expected runoff plus periodic cleaning flushes
- Plant the berm immediately with hardy species to prevent erosion
- Provide adequate ventilation in the coop to prevent ammonia buildup
