# Early American Mycology and Palmer's Contributions

Julius Auboineau Palmer Jr. (1894) stands as one of the earliest
American popularizers of mycology, the [[roger-heim-scientific-study-psilocybin]] of fungi.
His book "About Mushrooms: A Guide to the Study of Esculent and
[[ramsbottom-poisonous-fungi-identification-guide]]" collected articles published over twenty years
in the Popular Science Monthly, the Boston Medical and Surgical
Journal, the Moniteur Scientifique of Paris, and other periodicals.
Palmer's work illuminates the state of mycological knowledge in
the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] during the late [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]], a period he
characterized as one of profound ignorance and institutional
neglect.

## The State of American Mycology in the 1870s

Palmer began studying [[fungi-around-the-world-species-distribution]] 1874 and found the field in a
state of remarkable backwardness. As he wrote in an 1893 lecture
before the Essex Historical Society and the Newton Natural History
Society, geology, botany, astronomy, and other natural sciences
had been simplified and made accessible even to children. Mycology,
by contrast, remained a labyrinth of tradition, error, and
uncertainty.

Palmer estimated that there were probably not twelve men in all
of America who could tell an ordinary person much more about
mushrooms and toadstools than they already knew. This virgin sea
for exploration was what irresistibly led him to study the subject.
The mystery, the general ignorance, and the absence of reliable
guides made mycology both frustrating and compelling.

### Medical Ignorance

The medical profession shared in this ignorance. A prominent
physician wrote to Palmer in 1877 pleading for any author or
article on the subject, confessing what he called "a lamentable
ignorance" shared with every medical man he had consulted. Dr.
D. G. Brinton of Philadelphia, who copied Palmer's work into
the Medical and Surgical Reporter, introduced it by noting that
information on the subject in medical textbooks was "so vague"
that it could not fail to be welcome.

### Botanical Neglect

Professional botanists were little more helpful. Dr. Curtis of
[[north-carolina-mycodiversity]], despite his courage in eating many edible fungi,
expressed surprise that a mushroom which would liquify could be
eaten. Mr. Charles James Sprague, described as a Boston
mycologist of note, ate only canned mushrooms. Professor Farlow
of Harvard was an authority on microscopic fungi but had published
nothing on those of larger growth. Professor Peck of the
University of New York and Mr. Frost of Vermont affixed names to
varieties without describing their edibility or qualities.

### Foreign Textbooks

The standard European texts were equally unhelpful. They printed
the word "poisonous" indiscriminately against any unpleasant
plant, grouping the deadly Amanita with the merely acrid Boleti,
the Lactarii, and the Russulae under a single warning label. Two
charts sent to Palmer by Professor Palmer of Harvard from a
European trip, presumably new and authoritative, repeated the same
old, worthless traditions and folk signs for distinguishing
edible from poisonous species.

## The Mushroom versus Toadstool Fallacy

Palmer's most enduring contribution to popular mycology was his
insistent demolition of the distinction between mushrooms and
toadstools. He demonstrated that the two words are as
interchangeable as "plant" and "vegetable" or "bush" and "shrub."
Every mushroom is a toadstool, and every toadstool is a mushroom.

The popular belief that the terms denote separate categories of
safe versus dangerous fungi arose, Palmer explained, because only
one variety (the [[duggar-cultivated-agaricus-taxonomy-and-variety]] bisporus) had worldwide
commercial reputation and value. Therefore this one variety was
called a mushroom, while the ninety and nine others were all
called toadstools. The only excuse for this popular ignorance,
Palmer argued, was the commercial success of a single species.
