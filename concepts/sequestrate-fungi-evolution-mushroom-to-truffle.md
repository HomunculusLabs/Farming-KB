# Sequestrate Fungi Evolution
Sequestrate fungi, including true and false truffles, represent one
of the most remarkable examples of convergent evolution in the
[[bullers-drop-spore-discharge]]. Without forcible
discharge, no orderly hymenial palisade is required to expose
ascus or basidium tips to the air.
Molecular evidence indicates that such morphological changes do
not require massive numbers of mutations. The transition from
epigeous to sequestrate forms has occurred independently in
multiple lineages, representing one of the most repeated
evolutionary transitions in the fungal kingdom.
## Spore Dispersal Adaptations
Loss of forcible [[bloomfield-fungal-spore-discharge-and-dispersal]] must be accompanied by mutations
adapting sequestrate fungi to alternative dispersal tactics. In
most cases, dispersal agents are animals, from arthropods and
gastropods to mammals and birds.
Aromatic compounds, including pheromones, attract animals when
spores are mature. Each sequestrate species produces its own array
of aromas, usually a combination of several compounds. Immature
sporocarps have little or no distinctive odor; as spores mature,
the attractant compounds are produced and aroma increases in
intensity and pungency.
## Animal Dispersal Strategies
Mammals eat sequestrate fungi for their nutritional value
(carbohydrates, nonprotein amino acids, proteins, and elevated
mineral concentrations). Sterile tissues are digested, but spores
pass through the digestive tract unharmed. As spore-containing
feces weather, spores are washed into the soil and contact
receptive mycorrhizal host feeder roots.
Some genera (Gummiglobus, Mesophellia, Castoreum, Radiigera,
Scleroderma, Elaphomyces) have a powdery gleba. Mammals peel off
the outer peridium to reach a central columella, releasing powdery
spores to the air, fur, or soil.
