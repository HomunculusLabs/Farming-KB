# Multipurpose Trees in [[permaculture-design]]

## Overview

Trees are the most productive and multifunctional elements in any permaculture
system. They capture solar energy through photosynthesis, draw water and
nutrients from deep soil layers, create microclimates, build soil through leaf
litter and root turnover, and yield food, fiber, fuel, and timber.

Selecting trees that serve multiple functions maximizes the productivity and
resilience of any design.

## The Case for Trees

Trees reach deep into the earth for nutrients and water, and far and wide
into the sky for solar power. They are life's largest, most effective natural
collectors of energy and matter.

An acre of wheat provides a mere 1 to 2 tons of grain requiring annual
replanting. An acre of apple trees yields 7 tons of fruit. An acre of
honey-locust trees produces 15 tons of protein-rich pods without annual
replanting.

Beyond direct yields, trees perform essential ecological services. They
produce soil-enriching leaf litter, fill the earth with humusy roots, quell
temperature swings, hold moisture, arrest erosion, and offer tiers of habitat
for animals.

In a forest garden, these services work on the gardener's behalf, reducing
maintenance needs and increasing total system productivity.

## Selecting for Multiple Functions

The permaculture principle "each element performs multiple functions" is
especially important when selecting trees. A well-chosen tree might provide
food, fodder, timber, mulch, bee forage, nitrogen fixation, wind protection,
and wildlife habitat simultaneously.

When evaluating tree species for a design, the designer should identify at
least three distinct functions for each species selected.

Key functions include food production (fruit, nuts, leaves, pods), fodder for
livestock, timber and wood products, firewood, nitrogen fixation, soil
stabilization, windbreak and shelter, shade and microclimate modification,
bee forage, wildlife habitat, erosion control, and water table management.

## Honey Locust ([[gleditsia-triacanthos]])

Honey locust is a prime example of a multipurpose permaculture tree. This
moderately fast growing, long-lived legume reaches 24-30 meters with a trunk
diameter of 60-90 centimeters.

Its deep root system provides exceptional drought tolerance, and it tolerates
alkaline and acid soils as well as salt. The sweet, nutritious pods are
relished by all classes of stock: pigs, sheep, cattle, and poultry.

Being deciduous, honey locust allows winter sunlight through to pasture or
understory plants. Mature trees resist frost. Thorny branches protect against
ring barking by livestock, though thornless cultivars exist.

The wood is hard, strong, and durable, suitable for fence posts and
construction. Its open canopy allows interplanting with pasture or crops,
making it ideal for [[silvopasture-systems]].

## Black Locust ([[robinia-pseudoacacia]])

Black locust is another exceptionally multipurpose nitrogen-fixing legume.
Reaching 25 meters and living up to 200 years, it is native to the
Appalachian and Ozark mountains.

The foliage is readily eaten by all classes of livestock, and seed is suitable
as poultry feed. Black locust produces honey regarded as one of the finest in
the world, blooming profusely in late spring.

The timber is hard, heavy, durable, strong, and extremely rot resistant. It
is used extensively for round, hewn, or split mine timbers, fence posts,
railway sleepers, stakes, and electrical insulator pegs.

It coppices vigorously and is a very good firewood that burns like coal.
Commonly planted for erosion control, shelter belts, fodder, and wildlife
habitat. Its main challenge is vigorous suckering, controlled by selecting
non-suckering cultivars.

## Leucaena ([[leucaena-leucocephala]])

Leucaena is often called the fastest growing tree in the tropics, with the
giant form reaching over 20 meters. It withstands rainfalls as low as 250mm
but performs best between 600-3000mm.

Young leaves, pods, and flower buds are edible by humans, used raw, steamed,
in soups, or cooked with rice and spices. As livestock fodder, leucaena
produces some of the highest weight gains ever recorded.

Animals recover from browsing in just two weeks. However, leucaena should form
only about 30 percent of the long-term diet due to mimosine toxicity.

The timber has similar density and strength to oak, ash, birch, and sugar
maple. It is fine textured and easily worked. It is one of the best tropical
hardwoods for paper pulp and rayon manufacture.

Stands 10 meters or more wide can suppress fire spread, and plants recover
quickly after fire.

## Tagasaste ([[chamaecytisus-palmensis]])

Tagasaste combines nitrogen fixation with outstanding fodder production in
Mediterranean climates. Growing to 7 meters on as little as 300mm annual
rainfall, it produces foliage with crude protein of 17-31 percent of dry
matter, exceeding lucerne, rye grass, and wheat grain.

Beyond fodder, tagasaste provides winter bee forage when little else blooms,
nitrogen-rich mulch when cut, and effective nurse crop services for fruit and
nut trees. In New Zealand trials, trees within 1 meter of tagasaste
consistently outperformed controls.

The wood makes good firewood and has potential for tool handles and craft work.

## Alders (Alnus spp)

Alders deserve special mention as non-leguminous nitrogen fixers. Through
root nodules formed by Schinzia alni bacteria, they fix up to 300 kilograms of
nitrogen per hectare per year.

Oregon alder ([[alnus-rubra]]) produces 10-11 cubic meters per hectare per year,
making it one of North America's most productive trees. Its vigorous growth
makes it an ideal nurse crop.

Alder wood is valued for smoking fish, turning, and carving. Common alder
wood actually grows harder when permanently submerged in water, making it
ideal for waterside structures, pumps, and sluices.

The bark provides dyes in red, black, yellow, and cinnamon tones.
Medicinally, bark decoctions treat inflammation and fever.

Alders thrive in riparian areas and wet soils where few other nitrogen fixers
grow, making them ideal for streambank restoration.

## Integration Principles

When integrating multipurpose trees into a permaculture design, consider their
placement relative to other elements. Nitrogen fixers should be positioned
upslope or upwind of nutrient-demanding species.

This ensures leaf litter and root-zone enrichment flow toward beneficiaries.
In a [[food-forest]], nitrogen fixers are interspersed at roughly one per two to
four productive trees, adjusted based on soil fertility and climate.

Timber trees can serve as windbreaks protecting more delicate species. Fodder
trees should be accessible to livestock management systems. The goal is every
tree performing at least three functions simultaneously.
