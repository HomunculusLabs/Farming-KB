## Integrated Pest [[azadirachta-indica]]*): One of the most important medicinal and pesticidal plants in the tropics. Neem leaves and oil deter pests in stored foods and have been used for this purpose for centuries in India. Neem also controls aquatic organisms.

These plants should be integrated into home gardens and planted in small clumps throughout crop areas to provide a distributed, on-site source of pest control materials.

### Broad-Scale Mosquito Control

For large-area mosquito control, Mollison recommends a combination approach applied from the air or as ground mists:

- **Fats or oils** (e.g., lecithin): Create a film on water surfaces that suffocates mosquito larvae
- **Poison** (neem oil): Provides insecticidal action against adult and larval mosquitoes
- **Infective agent** (*Bacillus thuringiensis*): A soil bacterium that specifically targets mosquito larvae while being harmless to most other organisms

This three-part strategy addresses multiple life stages simultaneously and reduces the likelihood of resistance development compared to any single agent.

### Open Water Biological Allies

Open water systems can be augmented with:
- **Small fish**: Consume mosquito larvae
- **Notonectids** (backswimmers): Aquatic predatory insects that actively hunt mosquito larvae and other small invertebrates

## Vertebrate Pest Controllers

### Ground Foragers

Ground-foraging animals provide essential pest control by consuming fallen fruit (which would otherwise harbor larval insect infestations) and ground-level pests:

- **Chickens**: The most versatile and widely available ground forager, consuming fruit fly larvae, codling moth larvae, slugs, snails, and a wide variety of ground-dwelling insects
- **Pigs**: Ideal scavengers in tree crops below palms and fruit trees, consuming fallen fruit and the pest larvae it contains
- **Cattle**: Large-bodied foragers that disturb and consume ground-level insect populations
- **Large tortoises**: In suitable climates, consume fallen fruit and low-level pests

### Canopy Foragers

Aerial and arboreal foragers address pest populations in the canopy:

- **Birds**: The primary canopy insect predators, consuming caterpillars, beetles, and other foliage-feeding insects
- **Frogs**: Both terrestrial and arboreal species attend to infestations in foliage and low canopy
- **Small skink lizards**: Forage actively on trunks and branches for insects and their eggs

### Ground-Level Specialists

- **Tiliqua** (blue-tongued lizards): Forage specifically for snails and slugs at ground level
- **Ducks**: Consume slugs, snails, and other soft-bodied ground pests; especially valuable in wet areas
- **Guinea pigs**: On range in small houses, they free trees from grass competition and provide manures. They are specifically noted for easing the problem of kikuyu grass.

### Pasture and Mulch Foragers

- **A variety of birds and small mammalian and marsupial insectivores** eagerly seek out pasture grubs
- **Tropical land crabs**: Seek larval insects in mulch and provide useful food themselves — demonstrating that even "pests" have their place in the system

## Nematode Management

In tropical tree crop monocultures, soil fungi and nematodes can become persistent pests. Mollison recommends two specific plant-based strategies:

### Marigolds (*Tagetes* species)

Marigolds often serve to reduce or eliminate nematode populations when interplanted with susceptible crops. Their roots release compounds that are toxic to nematodes, providing a natural chemical barrier in the root zone.

### Crotolaria as a Trap Crop

*Crotolaria*, a leguminous green manure crop, traps nematodes in its root mycelia. Nematodes are attracted to Crotolaria roots but cannot complete their life cycle there, effectively reducing the population in the surrounding soil. This function is additional to Crotolaria's primary role as a nitrogen-fixing green manure.

### The Role of Soil Humus

Mulch and green manures (soil humus) often buffer the effects of nematodes and fungal pests by hosting fungal predators that keep pathogenic organisms in check. Healthy soils with high organic matter content have naturally balanced soil food webs where no single pest organism can dominate.

## The Coconut/Palm-Dominant Polyculture

Mollison devotes detailed attention to coconut and palm-dominant polycultures as a specific case study in [[integrated-pest-management]] and multi-strata production.

### Animal Integration in Palm Systems

Palm groves provide sheltered and shaded aspects for both intercrop and livestock. The recommended animal suite includes:

- **Chickens**: For controlling pests such as rhinoceros beetle larvae, plus manure production
- **Guinea pigs**: For grass control and manure
- **Geese**: For pond-edge grass control
- **Land tortoises**: For reducing grass competition
- **Pythons**: For rat and mouse control (where culturally acceptable)
- **Owls**: Described as "the best rodent predator" — barn owls and other species can consume hundreds of rodents per year
- **Bees**: For pollination of palm flowers and intercrop species
- **Pigs**: Ideal scavengers in tree crops below palms and fruit trees

### Weed Control Through Livestock

Chickens and ducks are especially valuable for weed control in specific crops:

- **Pineapple**: Chickens and ducks control competing vegetation
- **Ginger**: Same approach reduces weed pressure without herbicides
- **Taro**: Poultry manage weed competition in waterlogged conditions

Where comfrey or Tradescantia become excessive, poultry can control them — these same plants also control grasses when planted as part of a weed barrier, creating a managed dynamic equilibrium.

### The Recycling Function of Animals

Mollison identifies the main function of animal species in the tropics as "recycling" plant wastes and helping control the rampant growth of ground cover. The usual domestic species — pigs, chickens, geese, guinea pigs, rabbits, pigeons, and milking goats, cattle, or buffalo — each fill specific niches in this recycling function, while horses and bullocks perform draught animal functions.

### Fence Design in Tropical Areas

A critical practical consideration: where coarse tropical grasses grow rapidly, electric fences become ineffective because vegetation bridges the wires. For this reason:

- Fence lines must be planted with a dense perennial ground carpet of low herbaceous plants to exclude grasses
- Alternatively, fence lines should be overshaded by tree canopies that suppress grass growth below
- These measures are necessary because electric fences become inoperative against python, pig, bandicoot, and fox when grass touches the wires

### Predator Management

Where foxes or pythons threaten poultry, special housing solutions are needed:
- Elevated roosts for pigeons
- Secure night housing for chickens and ducks
- In areas without significant predators, flocks of guinea fowl, hens, ducks, and bantams perform invaluable pest-control and manurial/scavenging roles with minimal infrastructure

### Endemic Species

Mollison advocates for examining useful endemic animal species, especially island species of restricted natural range, for their particular uses and selecting them for wider trials outside their normal range. This is a call for expanding the toolkit of integrated pest management beyond the familiar domestic species.

## Windbreaks and Erosion Control

### Windbreak Design

Primary tall windbreaks of *Araucaria*, *Cupressus*, *Casuarina*, *Pinus*, hardy *Phoenix* palms, and even mangroves may be needed in front-line (windward) locations. These are followed in the lee by quickset species such as:

- **Euphorbia tirucalli**: Living fence and windbreak, drought-tolerant
- **Coprosma repens**: Hardy coastal shrub
- **Echium fastuosum**: Ornamental and functional windbreak species

The principle is to always find local plant species that perform well in the district rather than importing exotic solutions.
