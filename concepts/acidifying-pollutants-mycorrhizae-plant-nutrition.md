# Acidifying Pollutants, Mycorrhizae, and Plant Nutrient Uptake

Acidifying pollutants from atmospheric deposition exert profound effects on mycorrhizal symbioses and plant nutrient acquisition. The mechanisms involve both direct toxicity to fungal structures and indirect effects through altered soil chemistry and host plant physiology.

## Critical Loads and Assessment Methods

The concept of "critical loads" — the threshold pollutant level beyond which harmful effects occur — can be assessed through multiple approaches:
- Chemical analysis of soil and tissue concentrations
- Changes in organism abundance (particularly macrofungal fruit bodies)
- Changes in the physiology of biological indicator species

In European forest monitoring, the composition and abundance of ectomycorrhizal Basidiomycotina fruit bodies have been adopted as standard biological indicators. Fellner (1988) and Colpaert & Van Tichelen (1996) established protocols for using fungal fruit body surveys to assess terrestrial forest ecosystem health.

## The Two-Directional Impact Model

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed a mechanistic model explaining how acidifying pollutants reduce mycorrhizal associations through two convergent pathways:

### Aboveground Pathway: Carbohydrate Starvation

Acidifying pollutants (SO₂, O₃) damage tree canopies by:
1. Disrupting cuticular waxes that protect foliage
2. Reducing photosynthetic capacity of needles/leaves
3. Decreasing overall carbon fixation

This reduces the carbohydrate supply (primarily sucrose and simple sugars) available for translocation to roots. Since mycorrhizal fungi are obligate heterotrophs dependent on host photosynthate for their carbon supply, reduced carbohydrate flux to roots directly limits fungal growth and colonization. The fungal species most affected are those with high carbohydrate demands, while species adapted to low-carbon conditions persist.

### Belowground Pathway: Soil Acidification and Metal Toxicity

Acidifying pollutants lower soil pH through deposition of sulfuric and nitric acids. At lower pH:
1. Aluminum (Al³⁺), manganese (Mn²⁺), and other metals become more soluble
2. Toxic metal ions enter the rooting zone at higher concentrations
3. Root growth is directly damaged
4. Fungal hyphae are inhibited by metal toxicity

This pathway was demonstrated by:
- **Van Breemen & Van Dijk (1988)** and **Skeffington & Brown (1986)** showing increased metal solubility under acidification
- **Schier (1985)** documenting direct root damage from elevated Al availability
- **Tyler et al. (1987)** and **Ruark et al. (1991)** confirming metal mobilization patterns

## Differential Species Sensitivity to Aluminum

Ectomycorrhizal fungal species vary enormously in their tolerance to aluminum toxicity, creating selective pressure that reshapes community composition:

| Fungal Species | Al Tolerance | Response to Acidification |
|---|---|---|
| *Suillus* spp. | Very high | No growth reduction below 1000 mM Al |
| *Cenococcum geophilum* | Low-Moderate | Growth suppressed at 146 mM Al; but can increase in polluted stands due to stress tolerance of sclerotia |
| *Pisolithus* spp. | Low | Growth suppressed at 146 mM Al |
| *Thelephora* spp. | Low | Growth suppressed at 146 mM Al |

**Thompson & Medve (1984)** demonstrated these differential tolerances in pure culture. The Al:Ca, Al:Mg, and Al:PO₄ ratios were found to be more important than absolute Al concentration in determining fungal growth (**Jongbloed & Borst Pauwels, 1988, 1989**).

A key detoxification mechanism was identified by **Kottke & Oberwinkler (1990)**: at low Al:PO₄ ratios, aluminum is absorbed and immobilized into polyphosphate droplets within fungal hyphae. This explains why some ectomycorrhizal species can tolerate high Al concentrations when phosphorus is available.

## Field Fumigation Experiments

### The SO₂ and O₃ Fumigation Study

In a field fumigation experiment by **McLeod et al. (1992)**, SO₂ and O₃ were released over circular plots containing monospecific stands of Scots pine, Sitka spruce, and Norway spruce. Key findings:

- **Shaw et al. (1992, 1993)** found that only SO₂ fumigation of Scots pine significantly altered mycorrhizal community structure, reducing *Paxillus involutus* mycorrhizae on roots
- Paradoxically, *Paxillus involutus* fruit bodies were more abundant in high-SO₂ Scots pine plots — the opposite of the root colonization pattern
- This discrepancy highlights the disconnect between aboveground fruiting and belowground colonization

### Provenance Differences in Ozone Sensitivity

**Qui et al. (1993)** studied two provenances of Loblolly pine (*Pinus taeda*) under combined acid rain and ozone exposure:
- The ozone-tolerant provenance showed less reduction in root surface area, less change in ectomycorrhizal colonization, and less shift in species composition
- The ozone-intolerant provenance suffered greater mycorrhizal disruption
- This demonstrates that plant genotype mediates the impact of pollutants on mycorrhizal associations

## Nitrogen Deposition and Mycorrhizal Function

### Throughfall and Canopy Interception

Atmospheric deposition to forest floors occurs via:
1. **Bulk deposition** — direct fallout to the soil surface
2. **Throughfall** — precipitation passing through the canopy

Throughfall can concentrate deposition because canopy leaf surface area frequently exceeds the soil surface area below. However, foliar uptake of deposited nitrogen can reduce the amount reaching the forest floor. Spruce trees can absorb 2–8% of their total N demand through leaves (**Boyce et al., 1996**).

### Nitrogen Effects on Root Systems

The response of root systems to nitrogen addition varies with initial N status:
- **N-limited ecosystems:** Additional nitrogen acts as fertilizer, increasing plant growth (**McNulty & Aber, 1993**)
- **N-saturated ecosystems:** Effects become negative — excess nitrate leaches into watercourses, and extreme saturation increases N₂O and CH₄ greenhouse gas production (**Tietema et al., 1993; Aber, 1992a,b**)

### Belowground Mycorrhizal Community Responses

**Kårén & Nylund (1997)** showed that ammonium sulfate addition to Norway spruce forests decreased fine root biomass but did not significantly change the degree of ectomycorrhizal colonization. However, changes in community structure were evident.

**Arnebrant & Söderström (1992)** demonstrated that total mycorrhizal colonization of Scots pine roots was reduced from 70% to 55% after 13 years of fertilization with 1700 and 950 kg N ha⁻¹.

### Mycelial Growth and Physiology Under Elevated N

- **Arnebrant (1994)** showed that ammonium sulfate and ammonium nitrate at 1–4 mg g⁻¹ peat significantly reduced mycelial growth of *Paxillus involutus* and *Suillus bovinus* on lodgepole pine seedlings
- **Kieliszewska-Rokicka (1992)** found that small amounts of NH₄-N (0.17–19 mM) increased both growth and acid phosphatase activity of *Paxillus involutus* mycelia — possibly a synergistic effect where additional N induces temporary P deficiency, stimulating phosphatase production via positive feedback
- **Wallander et al. (1999)** demonstrated that fungal isolates with inherently high NH₄ uptake affinity had N uptake rates more inhibited by elevated N than low-affinity isolates, suggesting that low uptake rates may enable ectomycorrhizal fungi to avoid stress from elevated nitrogen loading

### Phosphorus Availability Under Acidification

**Carreira et al. (2000)** showed that acidifying pollutants alter the inorganic P subcycle by increasing P sorption capacity and decreasing labile P concentration. Acid phosphatase activity in soil decreased under acid-misted conditions (263 mg pNP h⁻¹ g⁻¹ vs. 382 mg pNP h⁻¹ g⁻¹ in non-misted soil), increasing the organic P component. The proposed mechanism: nitrogen saturation reduces P availability through both direct soil chemistry changes and increased plant P demand.

### The Vosges Forest Decline

**Estivalet et al. (1990)** linked forest decline in the Vosges region of France to reduced P availability caused by shifts in the rhizospheric microbial community. Soils from declining forests had higher frequencies of *Penicillium*, *Trichoderma*, *Acremonium*, and *Cylindrocarpon* — fungi that may antagonistically (but not pathogenically) suppress ectomycorrhizal development of Norway spruce.

## Heathland Ecosystems and Ericoid Mycorrhizae

Given the close association between ericaceous plants and ericoid mycorrhizae — and the dependence of this symbiosis on extracting nitrogen from organic sources — elevated nitrogen deposition was expected to significantly affect heathland ecosystems. The heathlands of The Netherlands have indeed suffered considerable decline.

However, **Johansson (2000)** found no significant effects of adding ammonium nitrate (0–75 kg ha⁻¹ y⁻¹ N) on root production or mycorrhizal colonization of heather (*Calluna vulgaris*). She concluded that heather decline under elevated nitrogen is unlikely caused by direct impacts on ericoid mycorrhizae, suggesting other mechanisms (such as competitive displacement by nitrophilic grasses) are responsible.
