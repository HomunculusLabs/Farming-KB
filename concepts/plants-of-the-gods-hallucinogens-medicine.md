# Uses of Hallucinogens in Medicine — Hofmann's Framework

## Philosophical Foundation: The Sender-Receiver Model

[[consciousness-and-psychedelics]] explores how hallucinogenic substances
alter fundamental aspects of conscious experience. Albert Hofmann proposed a fundamental model for understanding
hallucinogenic experience in the context of medicine. In this framework,
our experience of reality depends on two components: a sender (the
external world) and a receiver (the deep self or consciousness). The
**ego** acts as a translator between incoming sensory signals and
conscious awareness. In the absence of either sender or receiver,
reality does not exist.

Under the influence of hallucinogens, the brain undergoes dramatic
biochemical changes that alter the receiver's sensitivity, effectively
"changing the wavelength" on which consciousness operates. From this
perspective, subjective experience of reality is infinite in its
potential, depending on the capacity of the receiver, which can be
greatly modified through biochemical alteration of brain function.

## Normal vs. Altered States of Consciousness

In normal everyday consciousness, the ego and the outside world remain
clearly separated — one stands face to face with the external world as
an object. Under the influence of hallucinogens, the borderline between
the experiencing ego and the outside world dissolves or becomes blurred.
A feedback mechanism develops between receiver and sender: part of the
ego reaches into the external world, and objects begin to come alive,
acquiring deeper and different meaning.

This experience may culminate in a state of cosmic consciousness
related to spontaneous religious ecstasies: the unio mystica of
Christian mysticism, or the samadhi and satori of Eastern religious
traditions. In these states, creation and ego, sender and receiver,
are experienced as One.

## Therapeutic Applications

The pure substances most commonly used in medical applications are
mescaline, psilocybin, and LSD. LSD, the most powerful hallucinogen
known, is a chemically modified derivative of the active principle in
[[schultes-ololiuqui-vines-serpent]] (ololiuqui).

### Hallucinogens vs. Tranquilizers

Hallucinogenic effects are fundamentally opposite to those of
psychotropic drugs known as tranquilizers. Tranquilizers tend to
suppress problems and conflicts, making them appear less serious. In
contrast, hallucinogens bring conflicts to the surface and intensify
them, making them more clearly recognizable and open to psychotherapy.

### Reviviscence vs. Reminiscence

A critical distinction in hallucinogen-assisted therapy is between
ordinary remembering (reminiscence) and actually reliving an experience
(reviviscence). Hallucinogens frequently cause forgotten or repressed
past experiences to be clearly recalled — not as distant memories but
as present experiences being undergone again. This capacity for
reviviscence, described by French psychiatrist Jean Delay, is central
to the therapeutic value of these substances.

## Psycholysis

[[schultes-hallucinogens-medical-psychotherapy]] documents the broader clinical
history of these approaches. Developed in European hospitals by English psychotherapist Ronald A.
Sandison of the Jungian school, psycholysis involves administering
medium doses of hallucinogens on a number of successive occasions at
specific intervals. The term "psycholysis" (from Greek *lysis* meaning
"dissolving") refers to the dissolving of psychological tensions and
conflicts.

After each session, the patient's experiences are discussed in group
therapy and expressed through painting, drawing, and other creative
modalities. This method allows gradual processing of material brought
to conscious awareness.

## Psychedelic Therapy

The second approach, preferred in the United States, was termed
"psychedelic therapy" — a word coined by psychiatrist Humphrey Osmond
meaning "mind manifesting." After intensive psychological preparation
appropriate to each individual, the patient receives a single very high
dose of hallucinogen. This is intended to produce a mystical, religious
state of ecstasy that provides a starting point for restructuring the
patient's personality.

### Patient Preparation and Selection

Both psycholysis and psychedelic therapy require very careful
preparation. Patients must not be frightened by the unusual effects.
Careful patient selection is essential — not every type of psychic
disorder responds equally well. Successful treatment requires special
knowledge and experience from the therapist.

### Therapist Self-Experimentation

One of the most important aspects of clinical training is
self-experimentation with hallucinogenic substances by therapists.
Through direct personal experience, therapists gain understanding of
the worlds their patients enter and achieve much greater comprehension
of unconscious dynamics.

## Experimental Psychiatry

[[plants-of-the-gods-chemical-structures-hallucinogens]] describes the
molecular structures underlying the effects discussed here. Hallucinogens serve as research tools for studying the nature of
mental disorders. Certain abnormal mental states produced by
hallucinogens in normal subjects resemble, in some respects, symptoms
of schizophrenia. While hallucinogenic intoxication was once considered
a "model of psychosis," major differences have been found between
psychotic states and hallucinogenic inebriation. Nevertheless,
hallucinogenic intoxication remains valuable for studying biochemical
and electrophysiological changes associated with abnormal mental states.

## Care of the Dying

One of the most ethically significant medical applications involves
the care of terminal patients. Doctors in American hospitals observed
that the severe pain suffered by cancer patients, which no longer
responded to conventional analgesics, was sometimes dramatically
relieved through hallucinogenic therapy. Beyond pain relief, these
substances helped patients confront their mortality with reduced fear
and greater acceptance.

## Safety Profile

Hallucinogen-assisted psychotherapy, while still subject to dispute
in medical circles, carries far less risk than other psychiatric
techniques such as electroshock, insulin treatment, and
psychosurgery. In expert hands, hallucinogens may be regarded as
virtually without physical risk, though psychological preparation
remains essential.
