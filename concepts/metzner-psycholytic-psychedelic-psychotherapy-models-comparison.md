# Psycholytic and Psychedelic Psychotherapy Models

The two dominant paradigms for using hallucinogenic drugs in psychotherapy that emerged in the mid-20th century were the **psycholytic** model and the **psychedelic** model. Despite apparent theoretical differences, both share fundamental conclusions about the nature of psychedelic-assisted therapy. Ralph Metzner provided a comprehensive comparison of these two approaches in his seminal 1998 review.

## The Psycholytic Model

The psycholytic model ("psycholytic" meaning "psychic loosening or opening") was the dominant approach in Europe. It originated from the first research papers out of Sandoz laboratories, where Albert Hofmann synthesized LSD. The concept described LSD as bringing about "seelische Auflockerung" — a loosening or opening of the psyche.

### Method

In psycholytic therapy, neurotic patients suffering from anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorders, or psychosomatic conditions were given LSD in a **series of sessions at gradually increasing doses**. These sessions were conducted alongside more or less standard analytic interactions using a Freudian perspective. The rationale was that through the psycholysis — the loosening of psychological defenses — the patient would become more vividly aware of previously unconscious emotional dynamics and reaction patterns acquired in early family interactions.

### Grof's Perinatal Discovery

The Czech psychiatrist **Stanislav Grof**, working within the psycholytic model, made a startling discovery. In a series of sessions involving increasing doses, patients could experience an even deeper psychic opening — to birth and prebirth memories. After resolving conflicts stemming from Freudian dynamics of early childhood, patients would find themselves reliving the significant sensory-emotional features of their birth experience. Grof gave the name **perinatal matrices** to these patterns.

## The Psychedelic Model

The psychedelic model became the preferred approach in Anglo-American psychological and psychiatric circles. The English psychiatrist **Humphrey Osmond**, who worked in Canada with Abram Hoffer on treating alcoholism with LSD, introduced the term "psychedelic" (meaning "mind-manifesting") in correspondence with Aldous Huxley.

### Method

Psychedelic therapy typically involved **one or a small number of high-dose sessions**, during which the contents of the unconscious mind would be manifested in the form of vivid hallucinatory imagery, leading to insight and transformation. The model was first used in treating alcoholics, where it was thought to simulate the often life-changing "bottoming out" experience.

### Centers of Development

A second center for psychedelic therapy developed in early 1960s Southern California, where **Sidney Cohen**, **Oscar Janiger**, and others began providing psychedelic experiences to clients in the Hollywood film, arts, and media community — work that brought considerable publicity and notoriety to psychedelics.

## The Harvard Research Project

**Timothy Leary**, Frank Barron, and Richard Alpert conducted research at Harvard that expanded beyond treatment. Their work explored the possibilities and values of the psychedelic experience for "normals" — mostly graduate students, artists, musicians, poets, and writers — when provided in a relatively unstructured but supportive, home-like setting. Leary introduced the concept of **consciousness expansion**, which could be contrasted with the contracted, fixated awareness characteristic of narcotic addictions and obsessions.

## Shared Fundamental Conclusions

Despite their theoretical and practical differences, both models share five significant conclusions:

1. **Profoundly expanded consciousness**: Psychotherapy with hallucinogens invariably involves an experience of profoundly expanded states of consciousness, where individuals can gain therapeutic insight and may transcend fundamental self-concepts.

2. **Set and setting as primary determinants**: The internal set (intention, expectation, motivation) and external setting are the most important determinants of psychedelic experiences. The drug plays the role of a catalyst or trigger, in contrast to psychiatric drugs where pharmacological action is paramount.

3. **Amplifier and microscope analogies**: The drug functions as a nonspecific amplifier of psychic contents, and as a microscope that opens direct, repeatable observation of realms of the human mind previously hidden or inaccessible.

4. **Therapist personal experience as essential**: Without prior personal experience with psychedelics, communication between therapist and client in a psychedelic state is severely limited. This implies a significant role for psychedelic experience in the training of psychotherapists.

5. **Access to transcendent dimensions**: Mystical and spiritual experiences can and often do occur, challenging and promising the psychological disciplines. Albert Hofmann testified that his ability to recognize LSD's psycholytic properties was based on their similarity to his childhood mystical experiences in nature.

## Extension to Empathogens

A significant extension occurred with **Alexander Shulgin**'s discovery of phenethylamines such as MDA, MDMA, and 2-CB, which expand awareness primarily on the emotional or heart level, with minimal perceptual changes. MDMA (Ecstasy) was used with impressive success in psychotherapy, often facilitating significant opening of relationship communication and helping heal disabling trauma. The name **empathogens** ("generating a state of empathy") has been suggested for this class.

## The Set and Setting Hypothesis

Leary was responsible for introducing and popularizing the **set and setting hypothesis**, which states that the primary determinants of a psychedelic experience are:

- **Set**: The internal state of the individual — intention, expectation, motivation, personality, mood, and preparation
- **Setting**: The external environment — the physical space, the presence and behavior of guides or therapists, music, lighting, and social context

This model can be extended beyond psychedelics to understand other modalities of altered states of consciousness, including hypnosis, meditation, rhythmic drumming, sensory isolation, and fasting. In contrast to psychiatric drugs such as tranquilizers or antidepressants, where the pharmacological action seems paramount and set and setting play a minor role, with hallucinogens the drug plays the role of a catalyst or trigger while the psychological context is determinative.

## Military and Espionage Interest

When the potent mind-altering qualities of LSD were first discovered at the height of World War II in a Swiss pharmaceutical lab, they were initially characterized as "psychotomimetic" (mimicking psychosis) and "psycholytic." Military and espionage agencies of both superpowers devoted considerable research effort to determining the most effective surreptitious delivery systems for maximum confusion, disorientation, or embarrassment. Ironically, it was LSD's capacity to tap into mystical potentials of the human mind that ruined its applicability as a weapon of war — rather than making subjects predictably submissive, LSD had the propensity to suspend existing mental programming and release subjects into cosmic consciousness.

## Transpersonal Dimensions

Stanislav Grof found that after resolving biographical childhood issues and perinatal traumata, individuals would often find themselves in realms of consciousness completely transcendent of time, space, and other parameters of the ordinary worldview. He gave the name **"transpersonal"** to these realms and **"holotropic"** ("seeking the whole") to the predominant quality of consciousness in these states. He later developed holotropic breathwork as a non-drug method for accessing these same states.

Timothy Leary, stimulated by his association with Aldous Huxley, Huston Smith, and Alan Watts, devoted considerable effort to exploring the spiritual dimensions of psychedelic experience. This work resulted in adaptations of the Tibetan Buddhist Bardo Thödol and the Chinese Taoist Tao Te Ching as guidebooks for psychedelic sessions. Leary recognized that the fundamental mystical vision emerging in these states was an **evolutionary remembering** — an experience of reconnecting with biological and cosmological evolution.

## Comparison: Psycholytic vs. Psychedelic Models

| Feature | Psycholytic | Psychedelic |
|---------|------------|------------|
| Dose strategy | Series of gradually increasing doses | One or few high-dose sessions |
| Primary region | Europe (Sandoz, Czechoslovakia) | Anglo-American (Canada, USA) |
| Therapeutic orientation | Freudian psychoanalysis | Peak experience and insight |
| Key researchers | Grof, Passie | Osmond, Hoffer, Cohen, Janiger |
| Session format | Multiple sessions over time | Single intensive session |
| Target conditions | Neurotic disorders, anxiety, depression | Alcoholism, existential crisis |
| Depth of exploration | Gradual defense lowering | Immediate breakthrough |

## Key Figures

- **Albert Hofmann** — Sandoz chemist who synthesized LSD; recognized its psycholytic properties through similarity to childhood mystical experiences in nature
- **Humphrey Osmond** — Coined "psychedelic"; worked on alcoholism treatment in Canada; provided Aldous Huxley with his first mescaline experience
- **Abram Hoffer** — Canadian psychiatrist who collaborated with Osmond on LSD alcoholism treatment
- **Stanislav Grof** — Czech psychiatrist; developed perinatal matrix theory and holotropic breathwork from psycholytic therapy
- **Timothy Leary** — Harvard researcher; introduced consciousness expansion, set-and-setting hypothesis, and evolutionary remembering
- **Frank Barron** — Harvard collaborator on creativity and psychedelic research
- **Richard Alpert** — Harvard collaborator (later known as Ram Dass)
- **Sidney Cohen** — Early critic of 1950s psychedelic research; Southern California practitioner
- **Oscar Janiger** — Southern California researcher providing psychedelics to the arts community
- **Alexander Shulgin** — Chemist who discovered empathogenic phenethylamines (MDA, MDMA, 2-CB)
- **Ralph Metzner** — Harvard collaborator; comprehensive reviewer who identified the four paradigms of entheogenic practice

## Terminology of Psychedelic Substances

Metzner discussed the three principal terms used for these substances:

- **"Psychedelic"** — Coined by Humphrey Osmond and Aldous Huxley; means "mind-manifesting"; popularized by Leary and the Harvard group
- **"Hallucinogenic"** — Most common in psychiatric research literature; the original Latin alucinare means "to wander in one's mind"; travel or journeying in inner space are appropriate descriptive metaphors
- **"Entheogenic"** — Proposed by R. Gordon Wasson and Jonathan Ott; has the same root as "enthusiasm"; means "releasing or expressing the divine within"

These terms are used interchangeably in the literature, though each carries different connotations about the nature of the experience and the appropriate context for use.
