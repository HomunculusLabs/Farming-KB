# Design Methods and Site Analysis

There is no one true [[hemenway-garden-design-process-observation-and-visioning]] -- design is a creative endeavor and can vary from individual to individual. However it is good to have a design process outlined to follow for two main reasons: if you have never designed before you may need structure, and if you are in a design team you all need to agree on a group process to integrate design ideas in a timely and efficient manner.

## Design Process Steps

The recommended [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] process follows these major phases:

1. **Get a client and a site** -- the best way to look for Permaculture clients is to be part of a community, such as your local [[query-what-is-a-permaculture-guild-and-how-do-i-design-one]].
2. **Assemble team** -- teams should be composed of people with some level of expertise in various Permaculture elements: gardeners, builders, landscapers, illustrators, community organizers. It is not necessary that everyone have a PDC, though it would help.
3. **Organize design team** -- use an organizational structure such as the Organizational Mandala where each member is a Design Steward responsible for specific tasks. Each task must be assigned to someone.
4. **Analysis** -- before making ANY design decisions, analyze the site thoroughly.
5. **Design** -- make design decisions based on analysis.
6. **Charettes** -- collaborative design sessions to integrate ideas.
7. **Presentation** -- present the design to the client.

## Analytical Tools for Site Analysis

Before one can make any design decisions, one must first analyze the site. Permaculture Design is all about making choices appropriate to a specific site. The common analytical tools:

### Base Map

The map of the entire site within legal property limits. Obtain from county or city office, or create using Google Earth. Keep an original aside as reference and create working copies. Use tracing paper or transparencies for data overlays, with related data on each sheet (e.g., sectors and zones on one sheet, surface water flow and soil types on another).

### Broad Scale Site

When zoomed in on your site, also look at all local factors: nearby roads, towns and cities, trash dumps or other toxin sources, large tracts of forests, neighbor situations. Understand where the site sits in the larger picture.

### Client Interview

A Permaculture Design will always be done for a client -- even if designing for yourself, treat yourself as the client. The goal is to get into the client's head and learn to think like them. Design is a manifestation of philosophy; you are attempting to manifest your client's philosophy, not yours. The interview should cover:

- How well does the client understand Permaculture?
- Willingness to let the design progress through [[holmgren-ecological-succession-and-pulsing]] stages?
- Health needs and dietary preferences
- Physical or mental limitations
- Budget and time frame
- Vision and mission statement for the design
- How hands-on does the client want to be?
- How many people will the design serve? Pets?
- Social habits and intermittent impact patterns

### General Climate Data

Any Permaculture analysis should include solar altitude and azimuth, prevailing seasonal winds, flood plains, average winter and summer temperatures, average annual rainfall, and identification of any microclimates on the base map.

### History of the Site

Permaculture Principle #1 calls for designing from patterns to details, including understanding the historical patterns of the site over time. Research major climactic events and human habitation. Look for historical plants and crops, design precedence from the past. Putting the history together tells the story of the site.

### Indicator Species

Observe the site for plant and animal indicator species. Their presence or absence tells you about the local environment.

### Inventories

Lists of available resources that already exist on the site: plants, animals, potential [[natural-building-materials-guide]], salvageable materials, water sources, people power, community resources, and any other relevant resources.

### Observation and Interaction

Permaculture Principle #1 calls for observation and interaction. The hardcore will tell you to observe for a full year to see how the site acts in each season. Observe as often as possible under all different conditions: day, afternoon, evening, morning, during rain, after rain, when dry. Camp out on the site several times. Do small projects to see what effect they have. Observe with all your senses.

### Sectors

Analytical tool to understand the flow of forces across the site. Presented as a circle centered on Zone 0 with pie slices representing forces.

### Soil Samples

Take soil samples and test infiltration rates from around the site. Create a grid or sample where you observe differences. Clearly number samples and map them onto the working base map.

### Surface Water Flow

Chart the flow of water across the site following topographical information. This determines what earthworks are needed to slow, spread and drop water flow.

### Zones

Analytical graphic tool ranked 0-5 denoting the amount of human impact on an area.

### Zoning Ordinances

Research local building and land-use codes before designing.

## Sector Analysis Details

Common sectors include fire hazard, summer wind (about 80 degrees wide), winter wind, noise sectors, summer sun (matching solar azimuth angles), winter sun, undesirable views, and desirable views. Use concentric rings for each sector to avoid visual overlap. Once complete, the sector analysis tells you where to place elements to block forces, allow access, or catch and harness them.
