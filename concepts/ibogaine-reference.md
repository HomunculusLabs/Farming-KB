# Ibogaine Reference

Ibogaine is a naturally occurring psychoactive alkaloid found in the root bark of the African shrub Tabernanthe iboga. It is renowned for its capacity to interrupt substance dependence, particularly opioid addiction, often producing a single-treatment interruption of withdrawal symptoms and drug craving. Ibogaine is simultaneously one of the most promising and most dangerous substances in the psychedelic medicine toolkit, with well-documented cardiac risks that require medical screening and monitoring.

## Chemistry

- IUPAC name: (12bS)-7-ethyl-6,6a,7,8,9,10,12,12b-octahydro-2-methoxy-6a,9-methanobenzofuro[2,3-h]quinolizin-12-ol
- Chemical formula: C20H26N2O
- Molecular weight: 310.43 g/mol
- CAS number: 83-74-9
- Structure: indole alkaloid with a complex polycyclic structure
- Classification: indole alkaloid, iboga alkaloid family
- Physical form: white to off-white crystalline powder
- Solubility: soluble in ethanol, slightly soluble in water
- Melting point: approximately 152-153°C
- Extracted from: Tabernanthe [[iboga-root-bark-preparation|iboga root bark]] (typically 1-6% ibogaine by dry weight)

Ibogaine belongs to the iboga alkaloid family. Tabernanthe iboga root bark contains multiple alkaloids:
- Ibogaine: primary psychoactive compound (1-6% dry weight)
- Ibogamine, tabernanthine: related alkaloids
- Noribogaine: active metabolite (may be responsible for anti-addictive effects)

Ibogaine is a chiral molecule — the natural form is a racemic mixture of (+)- and (-)-enantiomers.

## Natural Source: Tabernanthe iboga

### Botanical Profile

- Species: Tabernanthe iboga (Apocynaceae family)
- Native to: West-Central Africa (Gabon, Congo, Cameroon)
- Growth form: evergreen shrub, 4-6 feet in cultivation
- Roots: psychoactive root bark (iboga) harvested from plants 5+ years old
- Root bark contains 1-6% ibogaine by dry weight

### Traditional Use

- Central role in Bwiti spiritual practice (Fang, Mitsogo peoples in Gabon)
- Used in initiation ceremonies, healing rituals, and communal worship
- Considered a "plant teacher" providing access to ancestral spirits
- Lower doses used as stimulant and aphrodisiac
- Used for centuries, possibly millennia

### Conservation

- Tabernanthe iboga classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List
- Overharvesting for international demand is a concern
- Cultivation programs working to ensure sustainable supply

## Pharmacology

### Mechanism of Action

- NMDA receptor antagonist: contributes to dissociative effects and anti-addictive mechanism
- Opioid receptor modulation: kappa agonist + mu antagonist/modulator — interrupts opioid dependence by reducing withdrawal (kappa) and resetting receptor sensitivity (mu)
- Serotonin transporter inhibition + 5-HT2A agonism: contributes to visionary effects
- Sigma-2 receptor agonism, nicotinic receptor modulation
- hERG potassium channel blockade: CRITICAL CARDIAC RISK MECHANISM (prolongs QT interval)

### Active Metabolite: Noribogaine

- Ibogaine metabolized by CYP2D6 (significant genetic polymorphism) into noribogaine
- Noribogaine half-life: 28-49 hours (vs. ibogaine's 4-7 hours)
- Retains activity at opioid and serotonin receptors
- Many researchers believe noribogaine is primarily responsible for anti-addictive effects, persisting days to weeks after a single dose

### Pharmacokinetics

- Onset (oral): 30-60 minutes; peak: 2-4 hours
- Acute experience duration: 4-8 hours; residual effects: 12-24+ hours
- Half-life (ibogaine): 4-7 hours; half-life (noribogaine): 28-49 hours
- Metabolism: primarily CYP2D6; excretion: primarily feces

## Dosage

### For Addiction Treatment (Flood Dose)

- Total ibogaine HCl: 15-25 mg/kg (single administration)
- Typical total dose: 1,000-1,500 mg for an average adult
- Produces a profound visionary experience (4-8 hours acute, 12-24+ hours aftereffects)
- Patient lies down in quiet, dark room with medical monitoring

### For Psychotherapy (Medium/Low Dose)

- 5-10 mg/kg (500-800 mg range)
- Shorter, less intense experience; may be repeated in multiple sessions

### Microdosing / Psychospiritual

- 50-200 mg (very low dose, sub-perceptual or mildly stimulating)
- Used for mood enhancement and craving reduction; less studied

## Effects

### Acute Experience (Flood Dose)

Phase 1 — Waking dream state (hours 1-4):
- Intense visual imagery ("waking dream" or panoramic life review)
- Encounters with deceased relatives, childhood memories, symbolic scenarios
- Auditory phenomena, physical heaviness/immobility (ataxia)
- Nausea and vomiting (common, considered part of therapeutic process)

Phase 2 — Cognitive processing (hours 4-12):
- Visionary effects diminish, replaced by heightened introspection
- Deep processing of addiction-related issues and traumatic memories
- Emotional catharsis and resolution

Phase 3 — Residual effects (hours 12-72):
- Prolonged reflection and integration; reduced or eliminated drug craving
- Sleep patterns may take 1-3 days to normalize
- Fatigue, difficulty eating, sensory sensitivity
- Sense of having been "reset"

### Anti-Addictive Effects

The most remarkable property of ibogaine is its ability to interrupt substance dependence:

- Opioid addiction: ibogaine can eliminate withdrawal symptoms and dramatically reduce craving, often for weeks to months. Some patients report long-term remission
- Stimulant addiction (cocaine, methamphetamine): reduction in craving and use
- Alcohol dependence: reduced craving and consumption
- Nicotine: reduced craving (less studied)

Proposed anti-addictive mechanisms:
- Opioid receptor reset: normalizes endorphin function and receptor sensitivity
- Neuroplasticity: promotes structural changes in addiction-related brain regions
- Psychological: visionary experience provides insight into addictive behavior roots
- Neurochemical: noribogaine's long presence maintains therapeutic effects
- Glial modulation: may reduce neuroinflammation associated with addiction

## Cardiac Risks

### The Critical Safety Concern

- hERG potassium channel blockade: prolongs QT interval on ECG
- Prolonged QT can lead to Torsades de Pointes (potentially fatal ventricular arrhythmia)
- Multiple deaths documented, primarily from cardiac arrest
- Risk factors: pre-existing heart conditions, long QT syndrome, electrolyte imbalances, CYP2D6 poor metabolizer status, concurrent QT-prolonging medications, liver impairment, advanced age, female sex

### Pre-Treatment Screening Requirements

No one should receive ibogaine without comprehensive medical screening:
- 12-lead ECG to measure QT interval (QTc <440 ms men, <450 ms women)
- Blood chemistry: electrolytes, liver and kidney function
- Complete medical history, medication review, CYP2D6 genotype if available
- QT-prolonging medications must be discontinued before treatment

During treatment, continuous ECG telemetry, regular vital sign checks, emergency cardiac equipment, ACLS-trained personnel, and IV access are required.

## Other Risks and Contraindications

- Severe nausea and vomiting (universal during flood dose)
- Ataxia and injury risk (patients must be supervised)
- Psychological risks: may destabilize those with severe psychiatric conditions
- Seizure risk (rare), hepatotoxicity at high doses
- Interactions with many medications via CYP2D6 and CYP3A4

Absolute contraindications:
- Known cardiac arrhythmia or long QT syndrome
- Recent heart attack, severe liver/kidney impairment
- Active psychosis, pregnancy
- Use of QT-prolonging medications that cannot be discontinued

## Legal Status

- United States: Schedule I
- UN: not scheduled internationally (not in 1971 Convention)
- Canada: not specifically scheduled (controlled under Food and Drugs Act)
- UK: Class A; Australia: Schedule IV
- Mexico, Brazil, South Africa: not specifically scheduled
- Gabon: iboga is a national cultural treasure; export restricted
- Treatment clinics operate in Mexico, Costa Rica, Brazil, South Africa, New Zealand, Netherlands

## Research Status

- Research hampered by Schedule I status and cardiac safety concerns
- Observational studies show promising results for opioid, cocaine, and alcohol dependence
- No large-scale randomized controlled trials completed
- 18-MC (18-methoxycoronaridine) developed as safer analog but development has been slow
- Clinical guidelines being developed by Global Ibogaine Therapy Alliance (GITA)
