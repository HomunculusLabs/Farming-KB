Soil is the foundation of all terrestrial life and the primary medium
through which permaculture designers work. Understanding soil as a
complex, living system is essential for any design that aims to be
sustainable, productive, and regenerative. Bill Mollison's treatment
of soils in the Designer's Manual begins not with chemistry or
classification, but with a philosophical and practical framework for
thinking about earth.

## The Nature of Soil

Soils resist precise treatment because their structure, permeability,
organic content, gaseous components, mineral composition, pH, and
water status change constantly. They vary from hour to hour with soil
depth and treatment, and in response to micro-elevations in the
landscape. Many soils are complex mixtures derived from a variety of
rock types, and they may have had a very long and varied geological
history.

Despite all our knowledge, despite soil services and soil analysis,
and despite the best attempts of people to care for land, we are
losing topsoil at an ever-increasing rate. Australia has perhaps 30%
of its original soils in fair condition. The rest have been washed
away, blown away, or sadly depleted in structure and yield. This is
true of most countries where extractive agriculture and forestry are
practiced.

## Science vs. Field Knowledge

Soil science concentrates very much on what is there, on
classifications, but not on how to evolve soil. It is often left to
amateurs, gardeners and farmers, to create good soil through water
control, modest aeration, and plant and animal management.

Mollison observes that farmers and gardeners are so often the
practical, innovative, experimental, and successful group, while
often ignored by academics. Science is good at explaining why things
work, and thus making skills teachable, but it is not so good at
initiating field work, or in training people already in the field to
work effectively.

Farmers' field days advance knowledge far more effectively than
scientific papers, and local educational sessions more than either of
these. However, both scientists and farmers have much to give and
take. This sort of coalition is slowly starting to happen as a result
of joint concern on a private level.

## How Soils Form

Soils can be residual, resting in place over their present rock, or
transported by water, ice, gravity, and wind. In their formation the
key factors are rock type, climate, and topography.

Water has a key role in rock breakdown. It combines with common
minerals such as felspars in rock to swell and fracture the rock, then
hydrates the felspars to kaolin, clay, and potassium carbonate. The
carbonates released make of soil water a stronger carbonic acid than
rainwater alone.

Atmospheric oxygen dissolves in rain to oxidise iron minerals such as
pyrites, forming both haematite and sulphuric acid, which further
dissolves metals. Water makes an effective rock decomposer even without
invoking the expansion of ice or the grinding power of glaciers.

Plants also contribute to rock weathering by wedging open rocks and
mineral particles, recreating acids, and transporting minerals in their
sap to other locations. All of this work creates a mantle of topsoil.

## The Soil Formation Rate vs. The Soil Loss Rate

Topsoil builds at about 2 to 4 tonnes per hectare per year as
uncompacted material. However, we remove soil at a rate of 40 to 500
tonnes per year in cropping and soil tillage. Even the most ideal
tillage just keeps pace with the most ideal conditions of soil
formation, and in the worst cases we can remove 2000 years of soil in
a single erosion season.

## Where Soils Are Conserved

The only places where soils are conserved or increased are:

- In uncut forests, where deep root systems and leaf litter maintain
  the soil mantle without disturbance
- Under the quiet water of lakes and ponds, where sediments
  accumulate in still conditions
- In prairies and meadows of permanent plants, where continuous
  root systems hold soil in place
- Where we grow plants with mulched or non-tillage systems, mimicking
  natural soil-building processes

These are the core subjects of sustainable societies of any
conceivable future. They are not, Mollison notes, the subjects most
taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses, nor do they
occupy the minds of politicians, investment bankers, or media figures.

## Values and Perspectives on Soil

Before approaching the complex subject of soils, Mollison urges
designers to consider the question from several different viewpoints:

- Health: Both human and plant health must be carefully and
  conservatively managed, especially regarding biocides and high
  levels of artificial fertilisers
- Yield: While monocultures can out-yield other systems in the short
  term, the long-term public cost, sustainability, and nutritional
  consequences must be weighed
- Life in soil: Soil organisms are a major factor with myriad effects
  on pH, mineral availability, soil structure, and erosion
- Ethics: The purpose is not to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced
  market gardens, but to restore what has been destroyed while
  attending to the modest area needed for survival

## Sustainable Societies Depend on Soil

John Jeavons estimates that, on the basis of gardens, we could return
perhaps 94% of land to its own natural processes. Colin Tudge comes
down with a conservative estimate of perhaps 60% of the British Isles
that could be given back to nature.

A reduction of the ecological deserts that we have called agriculture
is well overdue, as is a concomitant reduction in newspapers and
packaging derived from ancient forests. The largest job is the
restoration of soils and forests for the sake of a healthy earth
itself, not to clear or ruin any more land, but first to put in order
what we have destroyed.
