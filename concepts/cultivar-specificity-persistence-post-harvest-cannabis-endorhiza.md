## Overview

One of the most surprising findings from the Winston et al. (2014) Cannabis microbiome study was that cultivar-specificity in the endorhiza bacterial community remained detectable even eight weeks after harvest, when root tissue was undergoing active senescence and decay. This persistence of genotype-driven microbiome structure despite the absence of active plant metabolism suggests that plant genotype imprints a durable signature on root-associated microbial communities that outlasts the plant's own physiological activity. The finding has significant implications for understanding plant-soil feedbacks, crop rotation practices, and the mechanisms by which host plants shape their microbiomes.

## The Two Experiments: A Natural Comparison

The Cannabis microbiome study was conducted in two separate experiments, each sampling at different points relative to harvest. The first experiment sampled nine plants of three cultivars (Burmese, Bookoo Kush, Sour Diesel) eight weeks after the flowering bud and foliage had been harvested. The second experiment sampled six plants of two cultivars (White Widow, Maui Wowie) two weeks before harvest, while plants were still actively growing. This unplanned temporal difference created a natural experiment for assessing the durability of cultivar-specific microbiome signatures.

In the first experiment, with post-harvest samples, [[weighted-vs-unweighted-unifrac-cannabis-strain-microbiome]] analysis showed that endorhiza communities still clustered significantly by strain (ADONIS: R² = 0.39, p = 0.003), and weighted analysis confirmed this pattern even more strongly (ADONIS: R² = 0.59, p = 0.004). This was remarkable because the plants had been dead for eight weeks, meaning no active root exudation, no photosynthate allocation to roots, and no immune system regulation of the endorhiza environment.

## The Decay Confound: Cellvibrio as Evidence

The clearest evidence that root decay was occurring in the first experiment came from the dramatic enrichment of *Cellvibrio*, a known aerobic cellulolytic bacterium. In the first experiment, *Cellvibrio* comprised 16.9% (s = 13.0%) of taxonomy-assigned reads in the endorhiza, compared to only 0.095% (s = 2.7%) in the actively growing plants of the second experiment. This 178-fold difference strongly indicated that post-harvest root tissue was being actively decomposed by cellulolytic bacteria.

Despite this massive community perturbation driven by decay, the underlying cultivar-specific signal was still detectable. The Cellvibrio bloom was superimposed on, rather than replacing, the genotype-driven community structure. This suggests that cultivar-specificity operates at a level of community organization that is somewhat independent of the dominant successional dynamics occurring during tissue decomposition.

## Mechanisms of Persistence

### Biofilm and Physical Habitat Modification

Once endophytic bacteria colonize root tissue, they can alter the physical and chemical microenvironment in ways that maintain community structure even after the host plant ceases active regulation. Established bacteria may deposit biofilms on root cell walls, modify local pH through [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]], consume specific substrates, or produce antimicrobial compounds that inhibit colonization by competing taxa. These physical modifications create persistent niches that favour the continued dominance of the original community members.

### Resource Partitioning Legacy

During active growth, different cultivars exude distinct profiles of organic acids, sugars, amino acids, and [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] from their roots. This cultivar-specific exudate profile selects for complementary microbial communities. After harvest, the residual organic matter within root tissue reflects the cumulative history of these exudates and may continue to support the adapted microbial community for weeks, even as the root cells themselves undergo senescence.

### Cross-Feeding Networks

Established endorhiza communities often contain cross-feeding networks where metabolic byproducts of one bacterial taxon serve as substrates for another. These interdependent metabolic networks can be self-sustaining for extended periods, as long as the base substrate (root cell wall components, intracellular contents released during senescence) remains available. The cultivar-specific selection that initially shaped these networks would determine their composition and stability, creating a persistent genotype signal.

## Implications for the Two-Tier Selection Model

The two-tier selection model posits that soil type determines the pool of available microbes (tier one), while host genotype selects from this pool to shape the endorhiza community (tier two). The persistence of cultivar-specificity post-harvest strengthens the second tier of this model by demonstrating that genotype-dependent selection is not merely a transient, metabolically-driven process but creates lasting structural changes in the root microbiome.

The finding also suggests that the second tier operates through mechanisms more durable than simple exudate-mediated selection. If cultivar-specificity depended solely on the continuous input of genotype-specific metabolites, it should disappear rapidly after the plant stops exuding. The fact that it persists for weeks indicates that the host plant, during its active growth phase, establishes a root endorhiza community architecture that has inherent stability.

## Alpha Diversity Reduction as Supporting Evidence

The alpha diversity data from the pooled analysis provided additional support for the decay interpretation while also illustrating the persistence effect. When samples from both experiments were rarified to the same sequencing depth, endorhiza samples from the first experiment (post-harvest) showed dramatically reduced alpha diversity (chao1: m = 916.1, s = 161.7) compared to endorhiza from the second experiment grown in the same Mo-Bio soil (chao1: m = 1413, s = 280.1) and Orange County soil (chao1: m = 1374, s = 64.4). This reduction is consistent with the early stages of root decay selectively favouring a narrow group of cellulolytic organisms while suppressing the broader endophytic community.

Despite this diversity collapse, the cultivar-specific signal remained statistically significant, indicating that the surviving community retained the genotype imprint even as overall diversity declined.

## Broader Implications for Crop Rotation

The persistence of cultivar-specific microbiome signatures in decaying root tissue has practical implications for crop rotation and succession planting. If the microbial legacy of a previous crop persists in root debris for weeks or months after harvest, it could influence the microbiome of subsequently planted cultivars through shared soil. This "soil memory" effect could be beneficial (if the legacy community includes plant-growth-promoting or disease-suppressive organisms) or detrimental (if it includes pathogens or competitors that inhibit the new crop).

For Cannabis cultivation specifically, the finding suggests that the microbial consequences of cultivar choice may extend beyond a single growing season. Farmers rotating between different Cannabis cultivars in the same soil may find that the microbiome carries an imprint of previous cultivars, potentially affecting establishment success and yield of subsequent crops.

## Comparison with Active-Growth Cultivar-Specificity

In the second experiment (two weeks pre-harvest, actively growing plants), the cultivar-specificity signal was even more pronounced than in the first experiment, which would be expected given the absence of the decay confound. Using weighted UniFrac, strain explained R² = 0.27 (p = 0.001) across all pooled samples, and individual sample type analyses showed particularly strong effects in the endorhiza. The weighted analysis was consistently more sensitive to strain effects than the unweighted analysis, suggesting that cultivar-specificity manifests primarily through [[otu-differential-abundance-cannabis-microbiome]] of shared taxa rather than through the presence or absence of unique taxa.

This abundance-based signal is consistent with a model where all cultivars draw from the same soil-derived species pool but selectively enrich or suppress specific taxa based on genotype-dependent factors. The persistence of this abundance signal post-harvest indicates that the enrichment patterns established during active growth are maintained by the community even without ongoing host regulation.

## Temporal Dynamics of Microbiome Persistence

The Winston et al. study provides only two time points — eight weeks post-harvest and two weeks pre-harvest — leaving a gap in understanding the temporal dynamics of microbiome persistence. Key questions that remain unanswered include: How quickly does cultivar-specificity decay after harvest? Is the signal linear in its decline, or does it remain stable for a period before collapsing? At what point does the community transition from a genotype-imprinted assemblage to a purely successional decay community?

The fact that the signal was still detectable at eight weeks suggests a relatively slow decay trajectory, but the lack of intermediate time points makes it impossible to determine whether the signal was strong throughout the eight-week period or had already weakened substantially from its peak during active growth. Future studies incorporating serial sampling of root endorhiza communities after harvest would be needed to map the full temporal trajectory of cultivar-specificity persistence.

## Methodological Implications for Microbiome Studies

### Sampling Timing as Critical Variable

The finding that post-harvest sampling introduces both a decay signal (Cellvibrio enrichment) and potentially distorts cultivar-specificity measures has important methodological implications. Studies comparing microbiomes across cultivars should standardize sampling time relative to plant growth stage and harvest to avoid confounding genotype effects with decay dynamics. The Cannabis study inadvertently demonstrated this by showing that post-harvest sampling can simultaneously reveal genuine cultivar effects and introduce decay artifacts.

### Interpretation of "Core" Community Lists

The persistence of cultivar-specificity despite decay also complicates the identification of "core" microbiome members. A bacterium that appears to be a consistent member of the endorhiza across multiple cultivars and studies may actually be a decay-associated opportunist rather than a genuine symbiont. The presence of Cellvibrio in the putative core community of the first experiment (before the second experiment revealed its decay association) illustrates this risk. Core community assignments should be validated against actively growing plant datasets before being accepted as biologically meaningful associations.

### Weighted vs. Unweighted Metrics in Post-Harvest Context

The differential sensitivity of weighted and unweighted UniFrac metrics to strain effects provides a methodological lesson for post-harvest microbiome studies. Weighted UniFrac, which accounts for taxon abundance, was more sensitive to cultivar-specificity in both experiments, suggesting that abundance-based metrics may be more robust for detecting genotype effects in samples where community composition has been perturbed by decay. Researchers working with post-harvest samples may benefit from relying more heavily on weighted metrics and confirming findings with complementary abundance-based analyses.

## Limitations and Caveats

Several limitations qualify the interpretation of cultivar-specificity persistence. First, the two experiments used different cultivars (Burmese/Bookoo Kush/Sour Diesel vs. White Widow/Maui Wowie), different soils, and different sample sizes, making direct quantitative comparison of effect sizes between experiments problematic. The qualitative observation of persistent cultivar-specificity is robust, but the magnitude of persistence cannot be precisely estimated.

Second, the study did not include a true time-series, so the trajectory of decay and its interaction with cultivar-specificity over time can only be inferred. Third, the Cannabinoid concentration data from the second experiment showed significant correlation with soil type rather than directly with microbiome structure, limiting conclusions about the role of secondary metabolites in maintaining cultivar-specific communities. Fourth, the pseudoreplication design of the second experiment (triplicate samples from different roots on the same plant) means that some of the observed community variation reflects within-plant heterogeneity rather than true biological replication.

Finally, the use of 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing provides compositional rather than functional data, leaving the metabolic mechanisms of persistence to be explored through alternative approaches such as metagenomics or metatranscriptomics. Understanding which metabolic pathways remain active in the post-harvest endorhiza community, and whether these pathways are cultivar-specific, would substantially advance our understanding of the persistence phenomenon.

## Directions for Future Research

Several research directions emerge from the finding of cultivar-specificity persistence. Longitudinal studies that sample the same root systems at multiple time points before and after harvest would map the temporal trajectory of both the decay signal and the cultivar-specificity signal, revealing whether the two processes are independent or interacting. Metatranscriptomic profiling of post-harvest root communities could identify which bacterial functions remain active and whether these functions differ between cultivars, providing mechanistic insight into the persistence of community structure.

Manipulative experiments where decaying root tissue from one cultivar is introduced into soil before planting a different cultivar could directly test the "soil memory" hypothesis and its practical significance for crop rotation. Similarly, sterilization experiments comparing the establishment of endorhiza communities in soil with and without root debris from previous crops would isolate the legacy effect from other soil factors. Cultivation of grafted plants combining rootstock and scion from different Cannabis cultivars could separate the effects of root genotype from shoot genotype on endorhiza community establishment and persistence, providing insight into whether the persistence signal is driven primarily by root-level or whole-plant genotype effects.
