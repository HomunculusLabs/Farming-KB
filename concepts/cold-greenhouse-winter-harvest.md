# Cold Greenhouse Winter Harvest

## Overview

The cold greenhouse is an unheated structure designed to [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]
season for cold-hardy vegetables through winter. Refined by Eliot Coleman at
Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine, this approach eliminates artificial
heating by relying on passive solar energy and protective layers. Unlike
heated greenhouses that consume fossil fuels, cold houses work with nature,
making [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] ecologically sound and economically viable.

## Cold Houses vs. Heated Greenhouses

Traditional greenhouse production depends on maintaining warm temperatures
year-round through propane, oil, or electric heating. These heated structures
allow warm-season crops to grow through winter, but energy costs are
substantial. Coleman distinguishes three temperature regimes:

- **Cold house**: Completely unheated. Temperature follows outdoor conditions
  with only passive solar gain and thermal mass buffering. Suitable for
  cold-hardy vegetables that tolerate freezing.
- **Cool house**: Minimally heated to maintain temperatures just above freezing
  (approximately 32°F). Provides [[cervantes-microclimate-frost-protection]] but adds fuel costs.
- **Hot house**: Fully heated for warm-season crops. Highest energy input.

The cold house approach is the most sustainable and cost-effective for winter
vegetable production, capitalizing on the natural cold tolerance of selected
species rather than fighting against winter temperatures.

## The Double-Layer Protection System

The core innovation behind successful cold greenhouse winter harvest is a
two-layer protection strategy. Each layer provides approximately 5°F to 10°F
of temperature gain over ambient outdoor conditions, and the layers compound
to create a significantly warmer microclimate.

### Layer One: The Greenhouse Structure

The outer layer is a gothic-style hoop house oriented along an east-west axis
to maximize solar exposure on the long southern face. Coleman's standard
dimensions are 30 feet wide by 96 feet long, providing approximately 2,880
square feet of growing space. The gothic arch shape sheds snow effectively
and provides superior headroom compared to quonset-style structures. The
covering is UV-resistant greenhouse polyethylene plastic, selected for its
light transmission and durability. This outer layer captures solar radiation
during the day and creates a wind-free environment that reduces convective
heat loss from the soil and plants.

### Layer Two: The Inner Row Cover

Inside the greenhouse, a second layer of protection is provided by lightweight
floating row cover fabric suspended over the crops on wire wickets. The
wickets are made from heavy-gauge wire bent into arches approximately 12
inches tall and spaced every 4 feet along the beds. The row cover rests on
these wire supports, creating an enclosed tunnel directly over the plants.
This inner layer traps heat radiating from the soil surface and prevents
frost from forming on leaf tissue, even when outdoor temperatures plunge well
below zero.

## Soil as Thermal Mass and Heat Storage

The soil inside the greenhouse serves as the primary heat storage medium.
During sunny winter days, solar radiation passes through both layers of
covering and warms the soil surface. The soil absorbs this heat and releases
it slowly during cold nighttime hours, buffering air temperature around the
plants. This thermal mass effect is fundamental — the greenhouse is not a
heater but a heat retainer, slowing the rate at which soil loses warmth.

Healthy, biologically active soil rich in organic matter retains moisture and
heat more effectively than depleted soils. Regular compost additions and
careful soil biology management contribute directly to thermal performance.

## Temperature Differentials Achieved

In Coleman's experience in coastal Maine, the double-layer system provides
remarkable protection even in extreme cold:

- At **-15°F** outside, the temperature under the inner row cover reaches
  **15°F to 18°F** above zero — a gain of approximately 30°F to 33°F over
  ambient conditions.
- Each layer contributes roughly 10°F to 15°F of temperature improvement.
- The sunnier the day preceding a cold night, the more heat the soil absorbs
  and the warmer the interior remains through the following night.

These gains are sufficient to keep cold-hardy vegetables alive and harvestable
through winter, even in USDA Hardiness Zone 4 or colder.

## E.M. Emmert's Pioneering Work

The scientific foundation for the inner-layer concept was established by
E.M. Emmert, a horticulture professor at the University of Kentucky, during
the 1950s. Emmert conducted extensive experiments with polyethylene film as
a [[coleman-greenhouse-covering-materials-comparison]] and was among the first researchers to document the
effectiveness of a second inner layer for frost protection. His work
demonstrated that the inner air layer provided insulation comparable to
traditional glass greenhouses, at a fraction of the cost. Coleman credits
Emmert's research as the intellectual starting point for his own winter
harvest system.

## Economic Advantages

The economic case for cold greenhouse production is compelling:

- **Lower capital investment**: Hoop houses with polyethylene covers cost a
  fraction of glass or rigid-panel greenhouses.
- **Reduced operating costs**: No fuel bills, no heating system maintenance,
  no ventilation fans for winter [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]].
- **Premium winter market**: Fresh, locally grown winter vegetables command
  high prices in direct-market and restaurant settings.
- **Extended land productivity**: Ground that would otherwise sit idle
  through winter generates income for twelve months of the year.
- **Simplified infrastructure**: No plumbing, heating ducts, or backup
  generators — the system is inherently low-tech and resilient.

## Practical Considerations

- **Ventilation**: On sunny winter days, temperatures can rise rapidly.
  Roll-up sidewalls or end-wall vents must be opened to prevent overheating.
- **Snow load management**: The gothic arch helps shed snow, but heavy
  accumulations may require manual removal to prevent structural damage.
- **Row cover management**: The inner cover must be pulled back on sunny days
  for light access and air circulation, repositioned each evening.
- **Crop scheduling**: Planting must be timed so crops reach near-mature size
  by the time daylight shortens significantly in late fall. The "second
  spring" planting window begins around August 1.
- **Bed preparation**: Raised beds improve drainage and allow soil to warm
  more quickly. Well-prepared beds with adequate compost are essential.
