# Well Drilling and Maintenance

A reliable well provides a consistent water supply independent of rainfall
and surface water availability. This guide covers drilling methods, pump
types, well maintenance, water [[cannabis-quality-testing]], and considerations for the
off-grid homestead.

## Site Selection

### Finding Water
- **Geological surveys** — state geological surveys provide well logs and
  groundwater [[maps]] for your area
- **Dowsing/water witching** — anecdotal; not scientifically validated
  but some drillers respect the practice
- **Neighboring wells** — ask nearby property owners about well depth,
  yield, and water quality
- **Topography** — water tends to accumulate in valleys, low-lying areas,
  and near rock fractures
- **Vegetation indicators** — lush vegetation in dry areas may indicate
  shallow groundwater

### Legal Considerations
- **Water rights** — vary by state; riparian rights (eastern US) vs prior
  appropriation (western US)
- **Permits** — most jurisdictions require a drilling permit
- **Setback requirements** — minimum distances from septic systems,
  property lines, and surface water
- **Report completion** — most states require a well completion report filed
  with the regulatory agency

## Drilling Methods

### Mud Rotary Drilling
The most common method for modern wells. A rotating drill bit cuts through
soil and rock while a bentonite mud slurry is pumped down the drill string
to carry cuttings to the surface and stabilize the borehole wall.

**Characteristics:**
- Fast in unconsolidated formations (sand, gravel, clay)
- Can drill through soft to moderately hard rock
- Depth range: up to 1,000+ feet
- Cost: $15-40 per foot depending on geology and depth
- Best for: most residential and agricultural wells

### Cable Tool Drilling (Percussion)
A heavy drill bit is raised and dropped repeatedly, crushing rock at the
bottom of the borehole. Water is added periodically to create a slurry
that is bailed out.

**Characteristics:**
- Slower than rotary but works in any formation
- Produces minimal disturbance to the aquifer
- Good for hard rock formations
- Depth range: up to 2,000 feet
- Cost: $15-50 per foot
- Best for: hard rock, boulder-heavy formations, shallow wells

### Air Rotary Drilling
Compressed air is used instead of mud to lift cuttings. Effective in hard
rock and provides immediate indication of water-bearing zones (water blows
out with the air).

**Characteristics:**
- Fast in hard rock
- No mud contamination of the aquifer
- Not suitable for unconsolidated formations (cave-ins)
- Cost: $20-45 per foot

### DIY Drilling (Hydraulic Ram or Hand Auger)
For very shallow wells (50-100 feet) in sandy or clay soils, a driven
well point or hand-augered borehole can work.

- **Driven well point** — a screened pipe driven into the ground with a
  post driver; suitable for sandy water tables near the surface
- **Hand auger** — manual or powered auger; limited to 30-50 feet in
  soft soils
- Cost: $200-1,000 in materials
- Not suitable for rock or deep aquifers

## Well Components

### Casing
Steel or PVC pipe that prevents the borehole from collapsing and
protects the water from surface contamination. Typically 4-6 inches in
diameter for residential wells.

### Screen
Slotted or perforated section at the bottom of the casing that allows
water to enter while keeping sediment out. Stainless steel or PVC screen
with slot size matched to the aquifer grain size.

### Grout
Cement-bentonite grout seals the annular space between the casing and
the borehole wall, preventing surface contamination from migrating down
along the outside of the casing. Required by code in most jurisdictions.

### Well Seal
A sanitary well cap at the surface that prevents insects, animals, and
surface water from entering the well. Must be vented but insect-proof.

## Pump Types

### Submersible Pump
The most common type for deep wells. The pump sits at the bottom of the
well below the water level.

- **Depth range**: 50-1,000+ feet
- **Flow rate**: 5-25+ GPM typical for residential
- **Advantages**: quiet, efficient, pushes water up (rather than pulling),
  long lifespan (10-15 years)
- **Disadvantages**: must be pulled for repair; requires power at the well

### Jet Pump
Mounted at the surface; uses suction (shallow well) or a dual pipe system
(deep well) to lift water.

- **Depth range**: 25 feet (shallow) to 120 feet (deep well jet)
- **Flow rate**: 5-15 GPM
- **Advantages**: accessible for maintenance; lower cost than submersible
- **Disadvantages**: less efficient than submersible; limited depth;
  can lose prime

### Hand Pump
Manual pump for off-grid or emergency backup use.

- **Depth range**: up to 300 feet (with deep-well mechanisms like Bison
  or Simple Pump)
- **Flow rate**: 3-5 GPM sustained manual effort
- **Advantages**: no power required; emergency backup
- **Disadvantages**: labor-intensive; not suitable as primary supply for
  a household

### Solar-Powered Pump
DC submersible pump powered by a solar panel array with battery backup or
direct drive.
