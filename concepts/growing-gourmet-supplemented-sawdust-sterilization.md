# Supplemented Sawdust [[cotter-pasteurization-sterilization-methods]]
Once substrate bags are filled, the supplemented sawdust must be heat
treated for an extended period before inoculations can proceed. The
sterilization of supplemented substrates is one of the most technically
demanding aspects of [[oyster-mushroom-cultivation-economics]]. Failure to achieve adequate
sterilization results in contamination losses that can devastate an
entire crop cycle.
## Pressure Sterilization: Small Scale
In a small growing gourmet sterilization, 2-3 hours
of sterilization at 15 psi (250 degrees F) usually suffices for
supplemented sawdust substrates. This is the standard approach for
home cultivators working with 10-50 bags per batch.
## Pressure Sterilization: Large Scale Autoclaves
When sterilizing more than 100 bags in a large pressure vessel, the
thermodynamics of the entire mass must be carefully considered. Hundreds
of bags tightly packed in an autoclave achieve different degrees of
"sterilization." When bags are stacked against one another, the entire
mass heats up unevenly.
An autoclave filled tightly 5 bags high, 6 bags wide, and 8 bags deep
(240 bags) requires exposure to steam pressure for 5 hours at 18 psi to
assure full sterilization. The lower, central core is the slowest to
heat up. By placing heat-sensitive sterilization indicator strips
throughout the mass of sawdust-filled bags, a profile of sterilization
can be outlined.
## The Cardinal Rule of Sterilization
As the percentage of nitrogen supplements increases relative to the base
substrate, the greater the likelihood of contamination, and thus the
greater the need for full and thorough sterilization. Fresh hardwood
sawdust needs 2-3 hours at 15 psi. The same mass of sawdust supplemented
with rice bran needs 4-5 hours.
Woods of higher density, such as oak, have greater thermal inertia per
scoop than alder. Each run through the autoclave is uniquely affected by
changes in the substrate formulation.
## Boyle's Law and the Cold Mass Problem
When a cold mass is introduced into an autoclave, Boyle's Law (the
arithmetic relationship between temperature and pressure) does not come
into play until the thermal inertia of the affected mass is overcome. As
hot steam is forcibly injected, there is a lag time as heat is absorbed
by the cold mass. The cold air, if not vented, gives a false
temperature/pressure reading. At 15 psi, the temperature within the
autoclave should be 252 degrees F (121 degrees C).
The duration of the autoclave run should not be timed until the
differential between the pressure/temperature gauge and the remote bulb
sensor has been largely eliminated (a differential of 10 degrees F should
be considered negligible). This typically takes about two hours after
start-up.
## Over-Sterilization Risks
Packing an autoclave deeper than 4 bags (32 inches) runs contradictory
risks. In the attempt to achieve full colonization, sterilization time
is typically extended, potentially causing over-sterilization of the
outer zones. Over-sterilization usually occurs when wood substrates are
subjected to steam pressure for more than five hours. The sawdust takes
on a dark brown color, has a distinctly different odor signature, and
resists decomposition by mushroom mycelium.
Prolonged steam sterilization results in complex chemical
transformations. Turpentines, volatile oils, and toxic by-products are
responsible for this radical shift in the substrate's receptivity. In
the end, the substrate is rendered entirely inhospitable to mushroom
mycelium.
## Unloading the Autoclave
When the steam supply is cut off, the autoclave should ideally achieve a
vacuum as it cools. An autoclave can swing in pressure from 20 psi to
-20 psi within several hours after steam injection has stopped. This
40-psi pressure swing is devastating at the cellular level, disabling
any surviving endospores of bacteria or conidia of contaminating molds.
Once a vacuum has been achieved, pressure must be returned to atmospheric
before the door can be opened. A gate valve installed on the clean room
side, with a microporous filter canister, ensures the rush of air into
the autoclave does not introduce contaminant spores. As the mass cools,
air is being drawn in. If that air is full of dust, contamination is
likely. The laboratory should be thoroughly cleaned while the autoclave
is running.
## Atmospheric Steam Sterilization (Super-Pasteurization)
Many cultivators cannot afford large production-style autoclaves. An
alternative method is atmospheric steam sterilization, also called
super-pasteurization. Once filled to capacity with bags of supplemented
sawdust, steam is forcibly injected, bringing the mass to 190 degrees F
(90 degrees C) for a minimum of 12 hours, preferably 14-16 hours.
Since water at sea level boils at 212 degrees F (100 degrees C), the
mass cannot be elevated beyond this temperature unless pressure within
the vessel is raised above 1 psi. Remote sensing thermometers, placed at
a variety of depths, are used to determine a temperature profile. When
the coolest probe reads 190 degrees F, steam is continuously supplied
for the minimum period.
## Super-Pasteurization vs. Pressure Sterilization
Comparative studies by Badham (1988) showed no appreciable differences
in yields of Shiitake between supplemented sawdust blocks subjected to
high pressure autoclaving vs. atmospheric steam sterilization for the
first flush. In comparing total yields, however, more mushrooms can be
grown per pound of sawdust if pressure sterilization is employed.
The greater yield from sterilized sawdust, according to Royse et al.
(1985), is not due to the survival of contaminants but a function of
the rendering of sawdust into a form more readily digestible to the
Shiitake mycelium. Those using the more rapidly decomposing hardwoods
such as alder have not found yields on super-pasteurized sawdust to be
depressed compared to sterilized sawdust.
## Retrofitted Steam Chambers
Many cultivators retrofit cargo containers, large commercial laundry
washers, cement mixers, cheese-making vats, beer fermentation vessels,
railroad cars, semi-truck trailers, grain hoppers, and even large
diameter galvanized drain pipe into functional steam chambers for the
bulk processing of wood or straw-like substrates.
