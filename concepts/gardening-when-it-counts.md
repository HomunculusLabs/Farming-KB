# Gardening When It Counts

**Gardening When It Counts** is a book by Steve Solomon, founder of Territorial Seed Company, that advocates for practical, low-input vegetable gardening methods suited to hard economic times. Published in 2005, the book represents a significant shift from the intensive raised-bed gardening paradigm that Solomon himself helped popularize in the 1970s and 1980s.

## Background and Author's Journey

Steve Solomon created Territorial Seed Company in 1979 as a homestead-based mail-order vegetable seed business. He initially recommended intensive gardening methods, writing three books on the subject during the 1980s. However, through variety trials conducted as part of his seed business, he discovered that intensively grown vegetables did not necessarily outperform widely spaced plants. His trial plots, where plants were given enough room to develop to their full potential, required less water, produced larger and better-tasting vegetables, and in many cases yielded more per unit of space than crowded intensive beds.

After selling his seed company in 1986, Solomon researched dryland gardening — growing vegetables without irrigation at all — which primarily involves spacing plants extremely far apart. This led to his book *Waterwise Vegetables*, which is now out of print. By the time he wrote *Gardening When It Counts*, Solomon no longer used extreme intensive methods and irrigated much less than most gardeners.

## The Decline of Row Gardening

During the 1990s and early 2000s, row gardening — growing well-separated plants in rows far enough apart to walk between — was universally denounced as wasteful of space, inefficient with water, and low-producing. Densely packed, deeply dug, super-fertile, massively irrigated raised-bed systems became fashionable. John Jeavons was the leading advocate of this intensive approach. Solomon's book was a direct challenge to this orthodoxy, arguing that intensive methods are resource-intensive and poorly suited to conditions of economic constraint or water scarcity.

## The Coming Hard Times

Solomon identified several converging forces that he believed would make traditional intensive gardening impractical:

- **Energy scarcity**: As oil becomes scarcer, everything made with or transported by oil costs more — gasoline, food, clothing, transportation, heating.
- **Declining purchasing power**: Global economic competition means working people in industrialized nations will have less real income while costs rise.
- **Water scarcity**: Rural well flow is dropping, watersheds are degrading, and energy-dependent water purification and pumping costs will increase. Irrigated gardening as commonly practiced will become economically painful.
- **Fertilizer costs**: Chemical fertilizers and many organic ones are made with petroleum or natural gas, so they will become more expensive.

## Key Principles

1. **Wider spacing**: Plants spaced further apart need less water, grow larger, taste better, and often yield more per unit area than crowded plants.
2. **Minimal irrigation**: In most temperate climates, vegetables can be grown with little or no irrigation using techniques our ancestors knew before pressurized water systems.
3. **Efficient soil building**: Rather than building up soil excessively (which wastes money, effort, and lowers vegetable quality), Solomon advocates modest use of manure and compost supplemented with a complete organic fertilizer (COF) blend made from agricultural waste products and crushed rock.
4. **Nutritional focus**: Food grown with properly balanced COF contains far more human nutrition than vegetables grown by any other method, including commercial organic.
5. **Tool simplicity**: Successful vegetable gardening requires only a few hand tools used properly, not the extensive equipment promoted by gardening magazines and garden centers.
6. **Smart seed buying**: Garden-center seedlings and mass-market picture-packet seeds are often of poor quality. Successful gardeners must start with strong seeds from reputable sources.

## Soil Fertility Philosophy

Solomon argues against over-fertilization, noting that pushing soil potassium to high levels increases bulk yield (mostly starch and fiber) while decreasing protein, vitamins, enzymes, and mineral content. His approach prioritizes nutritional density over sheer volume of production.

## Historical Context: Wartime and Crisis Gardening

The book draws on historical examples of productive gardening during hard times:
- British WWII allotment gardens (2,700 square feet minimum per plot)
- Cuban urban gardens established after the fall of the Soviet Union, which produced 60% of vegetables consumed in Cuba

These examples demonstrate that substantial food production is possible with relatively simple methods when economic necessity demands it.

## Philosophy of Crisis Gardening

Gardening When It Counts emphasizes food production during periods
of resource scarcity, supply chain disruption, and economic
uncertainty. The approach prioritizes calorie-dense crops over
novelty varieties.

## Essential Crops for Food Security

The most calorie-efficient garden crops include potatoes, sweet
potatoes, beans, corn, and winter squash. These staples provide
reliable yields across diverse growing conditions.

## Resource-Efficient Methods

Low-input gardening techniques minimize dependency on external
inputs: composting for fertility, seed saving for genetic
adaptation, and water harvesting for irrigation independence.

## Soil Preparation Methods

Deep soil preparation through double-digging or broadfork aeration
creates optimal root zones that maximize nutrient availability
without requiring external fertilizer inputs.

## Water Conservation Strategies

Drip irrigation, mulching, and strategic shade structures reduce
water consumption by 50-70% compared to conventional overhead
watering methods.
