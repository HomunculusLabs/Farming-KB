## Homestead Permaculture Design

Permaculture design applied to the homestead integrates animals, gardens,
orchards, water systems, and human habitation into a self-sustaining,
resilient whole. Rather than treating each element separately, permaculture
designs systems where the output of one element becomes the input of another
— creating closed loops that reduce external inputs and increase overall
productivity. This guide focuses specifically on integrating livestock with
the broader homestead ecosystem.

## Zone Planning with Animals
### Zone 0 — Home and Immediate Surroundings
- No livestock in this zone. Focus on intensive gardens, herbs, and
  kitchen access.
- Compost system may be adjacent — designed for easy kitchen scrap delivery.
### Zone 1 — Intensive Garden and Small Animals
- **Rabbits and quail:** Quiet, space-efficient, produce manure for gardens.
  Place near compost and garden areas.
- **Ducks in garden:** Use temporary fencing to allow ducks into harvested
  garden beds for slug control, pest cleanup, and fertilization. See
  [[duck-keeping]].
- **Compost system:** Central to Zone 1. All animal manures and garden
  waste converge here. See [[composting-systems]].
- **Worm bins (vermicompost):** Fed with rabbit manure, kitchen scraps, and
  garden waste. Castings for garden beds.
### Zone 2 — Orchards, Food Forests, and Medium Livestock
- **Chickens and ducks in orchards:** Poultry forage fallen fruit, control
  codling moth larvae, and fertilize trees. Portable housing allows rotation.
- **Goats:** Browse hedgerows, clear brush, produce milk and meat. Fence
  separately from young trees (goats debark and kill them). See
  [[goat-husbandry]].
- **Sheep:** Graze orchard aisles between tree rows. Manage grazing to
  avoid damaging tree roots. See [[sheep-husbandry]].
- **Pigs:** Used in fall to clean up dropped orchard fruit (prevents pest
  overwintering). Rotational pig paddocks adjacent to orchards. See
  [[pig-husbandry]].
- **Bee hives:** Pollination for orchard and garden crops. Place with
  morning sun, afternoon shade, wind protection.
### Zone 3 — Pastures, Large Livestock, and Main Crops
- **Cattle and sheep:** Main rotational grazing area. See
  [[cattle-husbandry]], [[pasture-management]].
- **Hay production:** Part of Zone 3 dedicated to hay cutting for winter
  feed. See [[hay-and-forage-management]].
- **Farrowing paddocks:** Pig breeding and farrowing on pasture.
- **Multi-species grazing:** Cattle followed by sheep followed by poultry.
  Each species exploits different forage and reduces parasite load for the
  next species.
### Zone 4 — Foraging, Woodlot, and Extensive Grazing
- **Silvopasture:** Cattle or sheep grazing under managed forest canopy.
  Trees provide shade, fodder (leaf hay), and timber.
- **Pigs in woodlot:** Pigs rooting for acorns, roots, and grubs in a
  managed rotation. Renovates forest floor and controls invasive species.
- **Goats on brush:** Clearing brushy margins, fence lines, and invasive
  plant species.
- **Hunting and foraging:** Wild game, mushrooms, wild greens, nuts.
### Zone 5 — Wilderness
- Unmanaged area for wildlife habitat, biodiversity, and ecosystem services.
- Provides predator prey base (reduces pressure on livestock).
- Watershed protection, pollinator habitat, seed sources.
## Integrating Animals with Gardens

### Manure Management Loop
- Animal manure is the primary fertility source for garden beds.
- Rabbit and goat manure: can be applied directly (cold manure).
- Chicken, duck, pig, and cattle manure: hot — compost before garden use.
- Composting: manure + carbon (straw, leaves, wood chips) + moisture + oxygen
  = finished compost in 3-12 months. Deep bedding becomes compost.

### Pest Control Integration
- **Ducks:** Consume slugs, snails, and insect pests in harvested beds.
  Use portable fencing.
- **Chickens:** Scratch for insect larvae and weed seeds between crops or
  during fallow periods (they damage seedlings).
- **Guinea fowl:** Aggressive tick and insect hunters. Range freely.

### Grazing Cover Crops
- Plant cover crops after harvest (winter rye, crimson clover, oats, peas).
- Allow chickens or ducks to graze in spring before tilling — they fertilize
  and eat insect eggs. Sheep/goats graze taller covers (rape, kale, turnips).

## Integrating Animals with Orchards

### Poultry in Orchards
- Chickens and ducks forage for codling moth larvae, plum curculio, and
  other pests that drop to the ground to pupate.
- Use portable electric net fencing to rotate through rows. See
  [[livestock-fencing-guide]].

### Livestock in Orchards
- Sheep and cattle graze between tree rows (manage stocking to avoid soil
  compaction). Goats only in mature orchards with protected trunks.
- Pigs: excellent for cleaning up windfall fruit (prevents disease carryover).
- Protect young trees with guards — cattle rub, goats debark, sheep are
  safest.

## Integrating Animals with Water Systems

### Duck Ponds and Wetlands
- Constructed duck ponds serve multiple functions: water for ducks,
  habitat for beneficial insects and amphibians, nutrient capture from
  uphill runoff, and irrigation water source.
- Duck pond water, rich in manure, can be diverted to gardens as liquid
  fertilizer (dilute 1:10 with fresh water).
- Constructed wetlands filter greywater and animal yard runoff before
  entering waterways.

### Keyline Design and Water
- Design grazing paddocks along contour to slow, spread, and sink water.
- Livestock watering points placed at high points in paddocks encourage
  even grazing distribution.
- Stock tanks can serve as wildlife water sources.
- See [[water-management]] for broader water system design.

### Riparian Grazing Management
- Exclude livestock from stream banks with fencing. Provide alternative water
  sources. Allow managed grazing only during dormant season.
- Riparian buffers (20-50 ft of unfenced vegetation) filter runoff and
  provide wildlife habitat.

## Multi-Species Grazing

### Principles
- Different livestock species prefer different plants and have different
  grazing behaviors.
- Cattle prefer grasses, sheep prefer forbs and grasses, goats prefer
  browse (woody plants).
- Sequential grazing: cattle first (tall grass), then sheep (shorter
  grass and forbs), then poultry (insect larvae in manure pats).
- This system breaks parasite life cycles (parasite species are generally
  host-specific).
- Stocking rate can increase 20-40% compared to single-species grazing.

## Practical Implementation
- Design paddock fencing to accommodate all species. See
  [[livestock-fencing-guide]] for multi-species fencing.
- Provide species-appropriate shelters and mineral supplements.
- Monitor body condition of all species — some may outcompete others.
