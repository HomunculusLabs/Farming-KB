Fungal mycelial networks possess a remarkable capacity for translocating carbon, nutrients, water, and other resources over distances ranging from micrometers to meters.

This internal transport system, operating through cytoplasmic streaming, diffusion, and active transport mechanisms, allows fungi to exploit spatially separated resources, buffer environmental heterogeneity, and connect organisms across ecosystem scales.

## Mechanisms of Translocation

Resource translocation in fungal mycelia occurs through multiple overlapping mechanisms.

Cytoplasmic streaming involves the bulk flow of cytoplasm, including organelles and dissolved metabolites, through hyphal tubes.

This advective transport can move materials rapidly over considerable distances and is directed toward growing hyphal tips where demand for resources is highest.

Diffusion operates as a passive transport mechanism, driven by concentration gradients between source and sink regions of the mycelium.

Diffusive translocation is important for short-range movement of solutes within individual hyphae and between adjacent cells.

Active transport involves membrane-bound carrier proteins and requires metabolic energy (typically ATP).

Active transport allows fungi to move substances against concentration gradients and is particularly important for nutrient uptake from dilute soil solutions.

Olsson and Jennings (1991) demonstrated that translocation of carbon-14 and phosphorus-32 through hyphal systems of Rhizopus, Trichoderma, and Stemphylium occurred by diffusion, while other solutes may require active transport mechanisms.

## Mycelial Cords and Translocation Pathways

Mycelial cords (also called rhizomorphs in some contexts) are differentiated, linear aggregations of hyphae that function as dedicated translocation conduits.

Cords consist of a central core of wide-diameter, vessel-like hyphae for bulk flow, surrounded by narrower hyphae that provide structural support and insulation.

This differentiated anatomy allows cords to translocate resources over distances of centimeters to meters with relatively low resistance.

Wells and Boddy (1990) demonstrated that 75% of the phosphorus added to a decomposed wood resource was translocated to newly colonized wood resources through mycelial cord systems of Phanerochaete velutina, while Phallus impudicus translocated 13%.

Maximum rates of phosphorus translocation were measured at 7,225 nmol P per square centimeter per day through cords.

Cords are only formed in unsterile soil, suggesting that the trigger for cord formation is derived from interactions with other organisms.

This response to biological competition indicates that cord development represents an adaptive strategy for resource capture in the face of competition.

## Factors Affecting Translocation Rates

Translocation rates are influenced by multiple environmental factors.

Temperature positively affects translocation velocity, with greater rates of phosphorus movement at higher temperatures.

The change from wet to dry soil conditions induces a thickening of the cord system of Phanerochaete velutina and a reduction in the translocation of phosphorus to new wood resources, while wetting appears to have no effect on cord structure or phosphorus movement.

Source-sink relationships determine the direction and magnitude of translocation.

Carbon is translocated acropetally (toward the tips) to support hyphal extension at advancing fronts.

When resources are distributed patchily, translocation moves materials from resource-rich to resource-poor regions of the mycelium, maintaining metabolic activity across the entire colony.

In field experimental manipulations, translocation has been demonstrated over distances of up to 75 cm between decomposing resources on the forest floor, with resources moving into living wild strawberry and moss plants.

## Carbon Translocation

The rate of carbon translocation within the fungal thallus reacts in real time to provide directional flow to the building phases of hyphae.

In the face of high demand for nutrients and carbon at advancing hyphal fronts, carbon is translocated through cytoplasmic flow and diffusion in both the cytoplasm and apoplasm.

Studies using radioisotope tracers have shown that carbon translocation responds dynamically to local demand.

When a new resource patch is encountered, carbon flow is redirected toward the exploitation of that patch.

This reallocation capacity allows fungi to optimize their foraging strategy across heterogeneous resource landscapes.

## Phosphorus and Nitrogen Translocation

Phosphorus translocation through cord systems has been particularly well studied.

Using the radioisotope phosphorus-32, researchers demonstrated translocation through cords of the wood-decomposing fungi Phanerochaete velutina and Phallus impudicus from decayed wood blocks to newly colonized wood resources.

The rate of phosphorus translocation is on the order of 7 micromoles per square centimeter per day through cords.

The translocation of nitrogen follows similar patterns to phosphorus, with fungi moving nitrogen from nutrient-rich to nutrient-poor regions of their mycelial network.

This capacity is particularly important in wood decomposition, where nitrogen is typically the most limiting nutrient and must be obtained either from the wood substrate or from the surrounding soil.

## Implications for Ecosystem Processes

Fungal translocation has important consequences for ecosystem nutrient cycling.

By moving nutrients from areas of surplus to areas of deficit, fungi smooth out the spatial heterogeneity of nutrient availability in soil.

This spatial buffering capacity means that nutrient release from decomposing organic matter is distributed more evenly across the landscape than would occur without fungal translocation.

Translocation also affects the temporal dynamics of nutrient availability.

Different nutrient elements may be immobilized for long periods in fungal biomass, particularly in the case of elements that are incorporated into structural components of the cytoplasm or hyphal wall.

This temporal buffering creates a lag between nutrient release from organic matter and its availability to plants, influencing the timing of nutrient limitation in plant communities.

## Modeling Translocation

Mathematical models have been developed to simulate nutrient translocation within mycelial networks.

These models explicitly include anastomosis (hyphal fusion) and translocation, allowing growth to be optimized in spatially explicit habitat configurations.

The models distinguish between active translocation (requiring metabolic energy) and passive diffusive translocation, and demonstrate that active translocation is crucially involved in the initial exploitative phase of resource colonization, while diffusive translocation serves mainly for short-range redistribution.

A remarkable result from modeling studies is that when the active translocation term is turned off, the model no longer predicts effective resource exploitation in nutrient-rich, uniform habitats.

This demonstrates that active translocation provides a significant fitness advantage even in relatively favorable environments.
