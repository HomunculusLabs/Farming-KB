Fungal Respiration and Carbon Flux

Fungal respiration is a major component of the global carbon cycle,
contributing to the release of CO2 from soils to the atmosphere. Understanding
the magnitude and regulation of fungal respiration is essential for
predicting ecosystem carbon storage, soil carbon turnover, and feedbacks
between climate change and terrestrial ecosystems.

Fungal Contribution to Soil Respiration

Soil respiration, the total CO2 efflux from soil, comprises root respiration
and decomposer (heterotrophic) respiration. Fungal respiration represents a
significant fraction of total soil respiration, though estimates vary widely.

In forested ecosystems, root and mycorrhizal activity accounts for 70-80% of
total soil respiration (Bowden et al., 1993). Nearly two-thirds of soil
respiration in temperate mixed hardwood forests comes from root activity,
with decomposition of root litter and associated mycorrhizal fungi making
substantial contributions. The relative contribution of fungal versus
bacterial respiration to decomposition depends on substrate quality, moisture,
and temperature conditions.

Fungal respiration accounts for the majority of heterotrophic respiration
in acidic, organic-matter-rich soils where fungi dominate the decomposer
community. In agricultural soils with higher pH and more rapid nutrient
cycling, bacterial contributions to respiration increase relative to fungi.

Carbon Allocation to Fungal Biomass

Plants allocate substantial carbon to support mycorrhizal fungal partners.
In arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis, Douds et al. (1988) demonstrated 5-6
times greater carbon translocation to mycorrhizal root systems than to
non-mycorrhizal roots. This belowground carbon allocation represents a
significant carbon cost to plants but is offset by improved nutrient uptake.

Mycorrhizal fungi receive 10-20% of host net photosynthesis as carbon.
This carbon supports fungal respiration, biomass production, hyphal
extension, and extraradical mycelial networks. A significant portion of
this carbon is respired rather than stored, contributing to soil CO2 efflux.

In heathland ecosystems, root turnover contributes disproportionately to
nutrient cycling: Molinia root turnover accounts for 67% of total litter
production, 87% of litter nitrogen loss, and 84% of total litter phosphorus
loss (Aerts et al., 1992). Fungal decomposition of this root litter drives
the associated carbon and nutrient fluxes.

Temperature Effects on Fungal Respiration

Fungal respiration is temperature-dependent, typically increasing with
temperature following Q10 relationships. Q10 values for fungal respiration
generally range from 2 to 3, meaning respiration approximately doubles to
triples for each 10-degree Celsius increase in temperature.

Bonan and Van Cleve (1992) simulated the effects of a 5-degree Celsius
temperature increase on boreal forest soil carbon, predicting increased
soil respiration losses of 439 g C/m2 in birch forests and 675 g C/m2 in
black spruce forests. In white spruce ecosystems, decomposition losses
exceeded carbon gains from increased photosynthesis, resulting in net
carbon loss of 129 g C/m2. This positive feedback between warming and
decomposition has important implications for climate-carbon cycle
interactions.

Warming increases the activity of saprotrophic microbial communities
including fungi, accelerating decomposition rates. The magnitude of this
effect depends on substrate quality: recalcitrant organic matter in cold
boreal forests may become more decomposable as temperatures rise.

Carbon Fluxes in Aquatic Decomposition

In aquatic and salt marsh ecosystems, fungal decomposition drives
significant carbon fluxes. Fungal biomass forms up to 15% of total
detrital mass during early stages of plant decomposition in aquatic
systems. The proportion of carbon released as dissolved and fine
particulate organic matter varies with plant species and decomposition
rate: Spartina (cordgrass) in salt marshes releases more carbon than
sycamore in freshwater streams due to faster decomposition.

Effects of Elevated CO2 on Fungal Carbon Flux

Elevated atmospheric CO2 increases belowground carbon allocation by
plants, potentially increasing fungal biomass and respiration. Dhillion
et al. (1996) showed that doubling atmospheric CO2 in a Mediterranean
old-field grassland increased root biomass by 31%, root length by 88%,
soil microbial biomass by 42%, soil fungal hyphal length by 20%, and
total root colonization by arbuscular mycorrhizae by 57%.

However, increased fungal respiration may partially offset carbon
sequestration benefits of elevated CO2. Sanders et al. showed that
increased mycorrhizal colonization can increase soil respiration,
counteracting enhanced carbon fixation. The net effect of elevated CO2
on ecosystem carbon balance depends on the balance between increased
primary production and increased decomposer respiration.

Root Respiration and Mycorrhizal Contributions

Root respiration values vary considerably among ecosystems, from an
estimated 35% of soil respiration in tulip poplar (Liriodendron tulipifera)
to 62% in slash pine (Pinus elliottii) (Edwards and Harris, 1977; Nakane
et al., 1983). Coniferous trees allocate approximately twice as much carbon
to roots as deciduous species (Bonan, 1993), a trade-off related to
nitrogen acquisition: coniferous leaf litter has low quality and low
decomposition rates, reducing nitrogen mineralization and demand.

The distinction between root respiration and mycorrhizal respiration
is rarely quantified, yet mycorrhizal fungi may contribute significantly
to total root-zone respiration. This belowground carbon allocation to
mycorrhizal components represents a potentially significant carbon sink
in a world of increasing atmospheric CO2.

Ecological Implications

Fungal respiration and carbon flux have several key ecological implications:
- Soil carbon storage depends on the balance between primary production
  inputs and respiratory losses, with fungi controlling much of the
  efflux side
- Climate warming may accelerate fungal respiration, creating positive
  feedback to atmospheric CO2
- Changes in plant community composition (forest to grassland, or species
  shifts) alter fungal community composition and associated carbon fluxes
- Management practices that affect fungal communities (tillage, fertilization)
  indirectly affect soil carbon dynamics through changes in fungal
  respiration and biomass turnover
