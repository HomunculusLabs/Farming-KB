## Pollination Requirements

### Self-Incompatibility

Many fruit trees, especially apples and pears, cannot self-pollinate, so they must have pollinating varieties available. This means that a single isolated tree will produce little or no fruit. Pollination requires a different variety of the same species flowering at the same time, within pollinating distance (typically within 50-100 metres, closer is better).

Holzer addresses this challenge through several strategies:

### Multi-Grafting onto Single Trees

The most elegant solution for small gardens is to graft multiple varieties onto a single tree. This guarantees that pollination partners are immediately available. It also extends the harvest period, as different varieties ripen at different times, and provides insurance against crop failure of any single variety.

### Fruit Forest Diversity

In the fruit forest system, natural diversity ensures pollination. When hundreds or thousands of seedling trees are growing together, there is always a range of flowering times and genetic types present. The ungrafted seedlings serve as pollinators for the grafted cultivated varieties. This is one of the key advantages of the sowing method over planting [[holzer-wild-fruit-trees-and-hedges]] planted as [[query-how-to-design-a-food-forest-guild-for-fruit-trees]].

The fruit blossoms provide a great number of insects with a rich source of food. Bees, which play a substantial role in pollinating fruit trees, particularly benefit from the fruit blossoms.

## Apple Harvesting

### Timing by Altitude

The ripening times Holzer provides are averages for an altitude of approximately 1,000m above sea level. They depend a great deal on altitude and climate. At higher altitudes, everything is delayed:

- White Transparent apple ripens mid-August at 1,100m but September at 1,500m
- At 1,100m: ripens quickly, becomes floury when harvested late, barely suitable for pressing after that
- At 1,500m: ripens in September, much firmer and juicier, stores for over a month, still excellent for pressing

This demonstrates that higher altitude does not necessarily mean lower quality -- in fact, the slower ripening can produce better fruit with more intense flavour.

### Harvest Maturity vs Storage Maturity

Different apple varieties have different relationships between harvest maturity and eating maturity:

- Summer apples (White Transparent, Stark Earliest, Alkmene): Must be eaten or processed soon after harvest. They cannot be stored for long.
- Autumn apples (Gravenstein, Jacques Lebel, Danziger Kantapfel): Can be stored for a few months under proper conditions.
- Winter apples (Boiken, Bohnapfel, Ontario, Winter Rambou): Develop best flavour after a period of storage. Some can be kept until June.

### Signs of Ripeness

Holzer recommends careful observation to determine the right harvest time:
- The fruit should separate easily from the tree when lifted and twisted
- Seeds should be brown (not green)
- The ground colour (not the red blush) should have changed from green to yellowish
- Fruit should feel firm but not rock-hard
- Taste testing: fruit should be sweet and aromatic, not starchy

### Commercial Harvesting Problems

Holzer describes problems with the modern commercial apple industry. A farmer was asked by the marketing cooperative to harvest his apples within a set period. The rates paid were low, and the farmer was not happy with them. When Holzer asked why he was still harvesting and delivering green apples, the farmer explained that the apples had to be green, otherwise they would be discarded as cheap pressing apples.
