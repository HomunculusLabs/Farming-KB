## Critique of the Inductive and Deductive Methods
From "The Natural Way of Farming" by Masanobu Fukuoka, Chapter 1 of "The Theory
of [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] believes that by applying specialized research to parts of
the whole, partial improvements can be made which will translate into overall
improvement of the whole. But nature should not forever be picked apart.
Fragmented research only produces results of limited utility.
Respective ten-percent improvements through new varieties of rice, tilling and
seeding techniques, fertilization, and pest and disease control might be expected
to add up to an overall increase of forty percent in yields, but actual
improvements in the field amount to from two to ten percent, at best.
Why do 1 + 1 + 1 not make 3, but 1? For the same reason that the pieces of a
broken mirror can never be reassembled into a mirror more perfect than the
original.
## The Inductive Method's Limitations
Scientific agriculture first conducts research primarily by an inductive, or a
posteriori, process. One normally begins by drawing up a general proposition from
a number of facts or observations. But do a number of disparate improvements add
up to the best overall result? This problem lies behind the notable failure of
most research to achieve higher yields.
Such inductive experimentation is done without a clear goal. Scientists run
experiments oblivious to the direction in which their research takes them. In the
absence of a clear goal by which to set their course, this activity is just
aimless wandering. It is not progress.
## Natural Farming's Deductive Approach
Natural farming arrives at its conclusions by applying deductive, or a priori,
reasoning based on intuition. Natural farming begins by formulating conclusions,
then seeks concrete means of attaining these. This contrasts sharply with the
inductive approach, whereby one studies the situation as it stands and from this
derives a theory.
Natural farming uses [[fukuoka-textdoc-critique-inductive-deductive-reasoning-agriculture]] has always been the
driving force behind rapid leaps in development.
Clearly, the inductive method is useful only in a negative sense, as a means for
preventing a decline in crop yields. Only deductive reasoning can bring forth
fresh ideas having the potential of leading to positive gains in yields. True
deduction originates at a point beyond the world of phenomena. It arises when one
has acquired a philosophical understanding of the true essence of the natural
world.
## The Broken Mirror Analogy in Detail
Fukuoka's broken mirror metaphor is not merely poetic — it describes a genuine
epistemological problem in reductionist science. When researchers isolate
variables in controlled experiments, they strip away the complex interactions
that characterize real ecosystems. A soil scientist who studies nitrogen uptake
in isolation from mycorrhizal associations, soil structure, microbial
communities, and plant competition produces knowledge that is technically
accurate but practically limited. The mirror pieces — individual research
findings — reflect fragments of reality, but reassembling them into a coherent
whole requires an understanding of the relationships between the parts that
reductionist methods systematically exclude. This is why Fukuoka insists that
agricultural truth must be grasped intuitively and holistically before it can be
meaningfully investigated in detail.
## Historical Context of Agricultural Science
The dominance of the inductive method in agricultural science has deep historical
roots. The agricultural experiment station model, established in the 19th century
(Morrill Act land-grant universities in the US, Rothamsted Research Station in
the UK), was designed to produce incremental improvements through controlled
experimentation. This model has yielded genuine achievements: higher-yielding crop
varieties, improved understanding of plant nutrition, and effective disease
