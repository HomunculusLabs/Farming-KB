## Inoculation Methods by Scale

### Home Garden Scale

For home gardeners, the most effective inoculation methods include:

**Direct Root Contact at Transplanting:**
- Roll bare roots in granular or powder inoculant before planting
- For container-grown transplants: dust root ball with powder, add granules to planting hole
- Ensure physical contact between inoculant and roots — this is the most critical factor

**Seed Coating:**
- Roll seeds in granular inoculant (slightly dampen seeds first for better adhesion)
- Spray seeds with liquid inoculant formulations
- This establishes mycorrhizae from the first day after germination

**Soil Mixing:**
- Mix granular inoculant into potting soil or garden beds at planting time
- Apply 1-2 teaspoons per plant, or broadcast at recommended rates
- Mix throughout the root zone, not just on the surface

**Existing Plant Inoculation:**
- For shallow-rooted plants: apply granular inoculant to the soil surface
- For deeper-rooted plants: create channels to the root zone using a dibber or similar tool
- Liquid formulations can be applied as a soil drench near the root zone

### Commercial Agricultural Scale

Large-scale agriculture requires methods that can cover many acres efficiently:

**Seed Treatment:**
- Commercial seed coating equipment can apply mycorrhizal inoculants to seeds at planting
- Inoculant can be mixed with standard seed treatments
- Most effective for large-scale row crops (corn, soybeans, wheat)

**In-Furrow Application:**
- Granular inoculant applied directly into the seed furrow at planting
- Ensures close contact between propagules and developing roots
- Can be integrated into existing planting equipment

**Broadcast Application:**
- Granular or powder inoculant broadcast and incorporated before planting
- Less efficient than direct contact methods but workable for large areas
- Best combined with reduced tillage to preserve applied propagules

**Irrigation System Application:**
- Liquid formulations added to drip irrigation or center pivot systems
- Allows inoculation of established crops
- May require repeated applications for full colonization

## Timing Considerations

### Early Inoculation is Best

Mycorrhizal fungi provide the greatest benefit when established early in a plant's life cycle:
- Seed inoculation or in-furrow application at planting is optimal
- Transplant inoculation is the standard for vegetable starts and nursery stock
- Later inoculation still provides benefits but misses early growth advantages

### Recovery Timeline

- In containers: mycorrhizal establishment can take up to **2 months**
- In fields: establishment typically takes **3-4 months** or longer
- This is why early inoculation is critical — especially in regions with short growing seasons

### Seasonal Considerations

- Spring inoculation (at planting) provides the longest growing season for colonization
- Fall inoculation (for perennials, trees, and cover crops) allows establishment before winter dormancy
- Avoid inoculating into cold, waterlogged, or frozen soils

## Matching Inoculant to Crop

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Crops

For crops that form arbuscular mycorrhizae (most vegetables, grains, legumes):
- Use commercial AMF inoculants containing multiple species (Rhizophagus, Funneliformis, Gigaspora, etc.)
- Multi-species inoculants provide insurance — some species will match better with specific crops
- Ensure viability by checking production dates (propagules typically last 1-2 years)

### Ectomycorrhizal Crops

For trees and shrubs requiring ectomycorrhizae:
- Use species-specific inoculants when available
- For pines: Hebeloma arenosum, [[thelephora-terrestris]], Laccaria laccata, Inocybe species

### Non-Host Crops

Do NOT inoculate non-mycorrhizal crops (brassicas, beets, buckwheat, spinach):
- The inoculum will be wasted
- Non-host plants do not produce the root exudates needed for fungal colonization
- Planning crop rotations to avoid planting non-hosts before dependent crops is essential (see [[mycorrhizal-fertilizer-reduction]])
