# Banisteriopsis caapi: Ayahuasca

## The Magic Drink of the Amazon

Ayahuasca is the most important hallucinogenic plant complex in
the western Amazon basin, consumed ceremonially across vast areas of
[[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. Known by many names — Ayahuasca, Caapi, Yaje,
Natema, Pinde — the drink is prepared primarily from the bark of
giant forest lianas belonging to the genus Banisteriopsis
(Malpighiaceae). The term Ayahuasca derives from the Quechua
words "aya" (soul, spirit, dead) and "huasca" (vine, rope), thus
meaning "vine of the soul" or "vine of the dead."

## Botanical Description

Banisteriopsis caapi and B. inebrians are the two principal
species employed. Both are lianas — woody climbing vines — with
smooth brown bark and dark green, chartaceous, ovate-lanceolate
leaves up to about 7 inches (18 cm) in length and 2-3 inches (5-8
cm) wide. The inflorescence is many-flowered. The small flowers are
pink or rose-colored. The fruit is a samara with wings about 1.3
inches (3.5 cm) long. B. inebrians differs from B. caapi in its
thicker, ovate, more attenuate leaves and in the shape of the
samara wings.

The vines grow in tropical zones of northern South America and
the West Indies. They are the basis of the hallucinogenic drink
ritually consumed in the western half of the Amazon Valley and
by isolated tribes on the Pacific slopes of the Colombian and
Ecuadorean Andes.

## Preparation and Additives

The bark of Banisteriopsis caapi and B. inebrians is prepared in
cold water or after long boiling. It may be taken alone, but various
plant additives are often used to alter the effects of the drink.
Especially important are the leaves of Diplopterys cabrerana, known
as Oco-Yaje, and [[chacruna-psychotria-viridis]] (Chacruna). These additives
contribute N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) to the brew.

The liana contains MAO (monoamine oxidase) inhibitors, primarily
beta-carboline alkaloids. These [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]] — harmine,
harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine — inhibit the enzyme MAO in the
digestive tract, allowing the orally active DMT from companion
plants to reach the bloodstream and brain. Without this
pharmacological synergy, DMT would be inactive when taken orally.

## Cultural Significance

In South America, Ayahuasca is believed to reveal the real world,
while daily living is considered an illusion. The drink allows the
soul to leave the body and wander freely, communicating with
ancestors and forces of the spirit world. The Jivaro believe that
Ayahuasca makes possible communication with ancestors and that,
under its influence, a man's soul may leave the body and wander
free.

The drinking of Caapi is described as a return "to the maternal
womb, to the source and origin of all things." Participants report
seeing "all the tribal divinities, the creation of the universe,
the first human beings and animals and even the establishment of
the social order" (Reichel-Dolmatoff).

## Ritual Context

Ayahuasca is usually drunk in [[entheogenic-folk-religious-ceremonies]]. The famous
Tukanoan Yurupari ceremony in Colombia is an adolescent
[[eleusinian-initiation-ritual-sequence-and-comparative-mysteries]] for boys that involves the consumption of this
drink. The ceremony is one of the most important cultural events
among these peoples, integrating the youth into adult society
through the experience of the sacred plant.
