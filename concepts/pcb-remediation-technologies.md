# PCB Remediation Technologies

Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are synthetic organic chemicals once widely used in electrical equipment, hydraulic fluids, and other industrial applications. Their production was banned in many countries by the late 1970s due to their environmental persistence, bioaccumulation, and toxicity. PCB contamination remains widespread in soils, sediments, and water bodies, and a variety of remediation technologies have been developed to address this persistent environmental problem.

## PCB Properties and Environmental Behavior

PCBs were first identified as environmental contaminants of public and regulatory concern in 1966. They are highly lipophilic, chemically stable, and resistant to biodegradation, which contributed to their widespread environmental distribution. PCBs sorb strongly to soil organic matter and sediment particles, limiting their bioavailability for biological degradation. Their persistence in different environmental matrices has driven the development of numerous treatment approaches.

Bioavailability is a critical factor influencing PCB remediation effectiveness. Based on the persistence of PCBs in nature, seven hypothesized mechanisms influence how bioavailability affects bioremediation: sorption to soil particles, non-aqueous-phase liquid partitioning, micropore and nanopore sequestration, organic complexation, insolubility, and absorption into native organic matter. Methods for measuring PCB bioavailability in soils or sediments remain underdeveloped.

The concentration of PCBs at contaminated sites varies depending on the industrial activity involved. Common sources include creosote production and wood preserving facilities, gas works and manufacturing gas plants, and petrochemical operations. PCB mixtures, sold commercially as Aroclors, vary in their chlorine content, which affects their physical properties, environmental behavior, and susceptibility to degradation.

## Physical and Chemical Treatment Technologies

Several non-biological technologies have been applied to PCB remediation. Incineration destroys PCBs at high temperatures but is expensive and raises public concern about emissions. In situ vitrification melts contaminated soil into a glass-like matrix, permanently immobilizing PCBs but at very high cost. Landfill cap systems contain PCB contamination and prevent migration but do not destroy the contaminants.

Chemical dehalogenation methods include alkali metal polyethylene glycolate and base-catalyzed dechlorination, which remove chlorine atoms from PCB molecules, producing less chlorinated and more biodegradable biphenyls. Fenton's reagent, a mixture of hydrogen peroxide and ferrous iron, generates hydroxyl radicals that can oxidize PCBs in aqueous solution. Solvent extraction removes PCBs from soil using organic solvents, concentrating them for subsequent treatment. Each physical and chemical technology has specific advantages and limitations related to cost, effectiveness, secondary waste generation, and applicability to different contamination scenarios.

## Aerobic Bioremediation

Aerobic bioremediation of PCBs relies on microorganisms that can oxidize biphenyl and lower-chlorinated PCB congeners. Bacterial aerobic degradation of PCBs involves initial oxidation by dioxygenases to form cis-dihydrodiols, which are then converted to diphenols and subsequently cleaved by additional dioxygenases. Further catabolism results in formation of tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates.

Fungal species belonging to several genera can grow on biphenyl and produce hydroxylated biphenyl metabolites. Aspergillus niger degrades lower chlorinated PCBs present in Clophen A30, producing hydroxylated trichlorobiphenyls and chlorobenzoic acids. [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]] and other white-rot fungi have been studied for their ability to transform di-, tetra-, and hexachlorinated PCBs through ligninolytic enzyme systems. Trametes multicolor degrades a wide range of PCB congeners using its laccase enzyme system. These fungal systems are particularly important because they can attack higher-chlorinated PCB congeners that resist bacterial aerobic degradation.

## Anaerobic Bioremediation

Anaerobic bioremediation targets the reductive dechlorination of highly chlorinated PCBs, converting them to less chlorinated congeners that are more susceptible to aerobic degradation. Anaerobic dechlorination is carried out by specialized bacteria that use PCBs as electron acceptors, removing chlorine atoms and replacing them with hydrogen atoms. This process progressively reduces the chlorine content of PCB mixtures, making them more amenable to subsequent aerobic treatment.

Combined anaerobic-aerobic treatment trains represent the current best practice for PCB bioremediation. Anaerobic treatment first dechlorinates highly chlorinated PCBs to lower-chlorinated congeners, which are then degraded by aerobic microorganisms. This sequential approach addresses the full range of PCB congeners that cannot be effectively treated by either process alone.

## Technology Selection

The selection of PCB remediation technology depends on multiple factors including PCB concentration and congener profile, soil or sediment type, site hydrogeology, cleanup timeline, regulatory requirements, and available budget. For highly contaminated industrial sites, ex situ treatment using chemical dechlorination or incineration may be most appropriate. For lower-level contamination over large areas, in situ bioremediation or phytoremediation may offer the best balance of cost and effectiveness.
