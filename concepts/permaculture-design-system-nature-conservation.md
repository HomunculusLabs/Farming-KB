# Permaculture Design System and Nature Conservation

## Overview

[[permaculture]] as a design system addresses three parallel responses to environmental degradation: caring for surviving natural assemblies, rehabilitating degraded land, and creating complex living environments that meet human needs on minimal land. The system distinguishes between anthropocentric (human-centered) settlement design and nature-centered conservation ethics, arguing that both are essential and mutually reinforcing.

## The Design Framework

[[permaculture-design]] is defined as the result of a unique assembly of constructs, species, and social systems into a unique pattern suited to a specific site and set of occupants. The design process integrates:

- **Inorganic elements**: Earth-shaping, water supply
- **Organic-derived elements**: Species, guilds, planting patterns
- **Organic elements**: Community, technology
- **Socio-legal elements**: Trusts, companies, cooperatives, community credit unions

The goal is to achieve the best flow, function, and yield while conserving resources. Design is evolutionary — a total system in evolution to maturity, guided by continuous observation, evaluation, and control.

## Anthropocentric vs. Nature-Centered Design

The critical distinction Mollison draws is between cultivated (designed) ecosystems and natural systems. In a natural ecosystem, humans are only a small part of the total species assembly, and only a small fraction of its yields are directly available to people. In a cultivated ecology like a garden, almost every plant is selected to provide or support some direct yield for people. Household design is thus fundamentally human-centered (anthropocentric).

This is presented as a valid aim for settlement design, but it must be paired with a nature-centered ethic for wilderness conservation. The logic is straightforward: people cannot do much for nature if they do not govern their own greed, and if they do not supply their needs from existing settlements. If human needs can be met from settled areas, much of the agricultural landscape can be withdrawn and natural systems allowed to flourish.

## The Four Ethical Statements on Natural Systems

Mollison articulates a set of ethics for natural systems:

1. **Implacable opposition** to further disturbance of any remaining natural forests where most species are still in balance
2. **Vigorous rehabilitation** of degraded and damaged natural systems to stable states
3. **Establishment of plant systems** for human use on the least amount of land necessary for existence
4. **Establishment of plant and animal refuges** for rare or threatened species

Permaculture as a design system deals primarily with the third statement, but Mollison argues that all responsible people subscribe to the first and second as well.

## Three Parallel Responses to Environmental Change

In a world losing forests, species, and whole ecosystems, Mollison identifies three concurrent strategies:

1. **Care for surviving natural assemblies**: Leave wilderness to heal itself without human intervention
2. **Rehabilitate degraded or eroded land**: Use complex pioneer species and long-term plant assemblies (trees, shrubs, ground covers) to restore stability
3. **Create complex living environments**: Incorporate as many species as can be saved or have use for, from wherever on earth they originate

## The Global Species Perspective

Mollison challenges strict nativism in conservation thinking. While local flora and fauna are valued as "native," all life can logically be seen as "native to earth." Jet travel has accelerated a process already well-established by continental drift, bird migration, wind transport, and water rafting of debris. In any garden, there are likely plants, animals, and soil organisms from every major landmass and many islands.

The rate of extinction and species exchange is accelerating. Rather than entirely new species, adapted hybrids are arising as organisms from many continents meet, mix, and produce new accommodations to their environments. The very chemistry of air, soil, and water is in flux — metals, chemicals, isotopes, gases, and plastics are present that have never before existed or existed in such quantities.

## The Two Responsibilities

Mollison distills the practical response to environmental crisis into two duties:

1. **Primary**: Get one's house and garden in order so that it supports the inhabitants — producing food, catching water, generating energy locally
2. **Secondary**: Limit human population on earth, or humanity itself becomes the final plague

These duties are intimately connected because stable regions create stable populations. If cities, homes, and gardens are not designed to feed and shelter people, all other natural systems must be laid waste to meet those needs.

## The Hypocrisy Test

Mollison poses a sharp test of environmental sincerity: it is hypocrisy to pretend to save forests while buying daily newspapers and packaged food; to preserve native plants while relying on agricultural production for food; to adopt a diet that calls for broadscale food production. Truly responsible conservationists have gardens that support their food needs and are working to reduce energy consumption to modest levels supplyable by local wind, water, forest, or solar power.

## Wilderness and Species Refuges in Practice

The practical approach to conservation within permaculture includes:

- Forming or subscribing to institutes or organizations whose legal charter mandates conservation activities
- Reserving small areas in even the smallest garden for insect, lizard, frog, or butterfly habitat
- Fencing off forest and wetland areas of critical value to local species in larger gardens and farms
- Such conservation areas should be dedicated solely to local species preservation

## The Revolutionary Nature of Design

Permaculture as a design system contains nothing fundamentally new — it arranges what was always there in a different way so that it works to conserve energy or generate more energy than it consumes. What is novel, and often overlooked, is that any system of total commonsense design for human communities is inherently revolutionary.

The end result of adopting permaculture strategies is a dramatic reduction in the agricultural area needed by households and settlements, releasing much of the landscape for wildlife and re-occupation by endemic flora. Respect for all life forms is not merely an ideal but an essential ethic for all people.
