# Mollison on Aquatic Plant Systems

**Source:** Bill Mollison, [[permaculture-design]] Course, Pamphlet XI ([[water-in-permaculture]]), 1981

## Overview

Mollison provides detailed guidance on designing productive aquatic plant systems, from marginal vegetation to deep-water species. This extends the aquaculture content in [[mollison-aquatic-polyculture-systems]] and [[mollison-fish-pond-construction]]. Aquatic plant systems are among the most neglected yet most productive components of permaculture design, often outyielding terrestrial agriculture per unit area.

## Pond Zonation and Plant Species

### Marginal and Edge Plants

- **Bamboo**: Lives up on well-drained soil but sends feeder roots down to water; must not have sodden conditions

- **Poplar and willow**: Grow where water table is 3-4 feet deep; produce both durable and lightest woods in world

- **Arrowheads (Sagittaria)**: Duck potato, important wildfowl food; 80+ species, wide climate range

- **Wild rice (Zizania)**: Very important for ducks; needs mud ball seed placement; likes 18 inches to 3 feet depth with some water movement

- **Cattails (Typha)**: Good for paper, fodder, mats; control seeding to prevent pond closure; roots used as food

- **Papyrus**: Grows in shallow water; good for paper and fodder

- **Chinese water chestnut**: Hardy, forms large tubers; mud rush growth habit

- **Indian water chestnut**: Floating with anchor, harvested by wading

- **Mint (black mint)**: Very productive but invasive; 2-3 acres can bring $70,000 from menthol distillation; control with dense bushes on sides

### Floating and Submerged Plants

- **Water lilies**: Best planted in manure-filled bags inside tires for easy harvesting

- **Duckweed (Lemna)**: Useful surface plant

- **Water hyacinth**: Used in restricted locations to clear pollution; bad in large slow rivers

## Pond Design Principles

- Ideal structure: sloping or stepped floor, fully drainable, drain into another pond for dryland cycle

- pH 6-8 (100-200x lower acid than garden soils); lime base of new pond

- White painted disc test: disappears at 2.5 feet = well-manured pond; visible at 5 feet = needs manure

- Gley ponds: once established, gley perpetuates itself in soil

- Intensively cultivated ponds should go through dry stage; 3-4 years dryland crops after 2-3 years as pond

- 8 ducks per quarter acre; ducks add manure and assist energy turnover

## Plant Aquaculture Potential

Mollison emphasizes that aquatic plant systems are neglected: "There are very few, if any, books on plant aquaculture... we are going to get more yield out of those plants than we get from animals." A small 6-foot diameter production pond with watercress and cattails produces yields comparable to land vegetables.

## The Case for Plant Aquaculture

Mollison makes a compelling argument that aquatic plant systems deserve far more attention than they receive in both conventional agriculture and permaculture practice.

- **Higher productivity per unit area:** Aquatic plants have direct access to water and dissolved nutrients without the limitations of soil structure, moisture availability, or root depth that constrain terrestrial plants. This gives them inherently higher potential productivity per square meter.

- **Continuous harvest:** Unlike seasonal field crops, many aquatic plants produce continuous harvests throughout the growing season. Watercress, duckweed, and water hyacinth can be harvested weekly during the growing season.

- **Multiple products from single species:** Cattails alone produce edible shoots (spring), edible pollen (summer), edible rhizomes (fall/winter), leaf material for weaving and thatch, and seed fluff for insulation. No terrestrial crop matches this multi-product versatility.

- **Low input requirements:** Once established, aquatic plant systems require minimal labor and no external inputs if integrated with animal systems (ducks provide manure, fish provide nutrient cycling). The water medium eliminates irrigation needs entirely.

- **Marginal land utilization:** Pond-based plant production can be established on land unsuitable for conventional agriculture -- steep slopes, rocky areas, seasonally waterlogged areas, and degraded land.

## Species Profiles and Uses

### Watercress (Nasturtium officinale)

- One of the most nutritious leafy greens, rich in vitamins A, C, K, and minerals

- Grows in flowing or gently moving water; prefers slightly alkaline conditions

- Continuous harvest by cutting above water line; regrows rapidly

- Excellent for [[mollison-sewage-biogas-systems]] final polishing stage -- absorbs dissolved nutrients from treated effluent

### Cattails (Typha latifolia, T. angustifolia)

- One of the most versatile multi-use plants in permaculture

- Edible rhizomes (high in starch, can be processed into flour), edible shoots (asparagus-like), edible pollen (protein-rich)

- Leaf material for weaving mats, baskets, and thatch roofing

- Seed fluff used as insulation material (comparable to kapok)

- Root systems filter water and stabilize pond edges

- Must be controlled by harvesting to prevent pond closure from aggressive spread

### Duckweed (Lemna spp.)

- Fastest-growing higher plant on Earth; can double biomass in 2-3 days under optimal conditions

- 25-45% protein by dry weight -- comparable to soybeans

- Excellent animal feed for poultry, fish, and livestock

- Harvests continuously from the water surface with nets or scoops

- Concentrates nutrients from water, making it effective for water purification

### Wild Rice (Zizania aquatica)

- Premium market value as a specialty grain

- Requires specific conditions: 18-36 inches water depth, some water movement, mud bottom

- Seeds must be planted in mud balls to prevent washing away

- 3-week dropping period for harvest; collect seeds as they fall into water

- Superb duck food; integrates with [[mollison-pond-design-freshwater-aquaculture]] duck systems

### Water Hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes)

- Extremely productive but dangerously invasive in warm climates

- Can double biomass in 1-2 weeks under tropical conditions

- Excellent for pollution cleanup -- absorbs heavy metals, nutrients, and organic contaminants

- Restricted use only: never introduce to natural waterways, large rivers, or unconfined water bodies

- In controlled ponds, provides biomass for composting, biogas production, or animal feed

## Integration with Animal Systems

Aquatic plant systems achieve their highest productivity when integrated with animal systems in a polyculture design.

- **Duck integration:** 8 ducks per quarter-acre provide manure that fertilizes the aquatic plant system, while the plants provide food, shade, and habitat for the ducks. Ducks also control insect pests and eat mosquito larvae. This is the primary integration strategy in [[mollison-pond-design-freshwater-aquaculture]].

- **Fish integration:** Fish excrete nutrients that feed aquatic plants. Plants provide shelter, spawning substrate, and food (insects living on plant surfaces) for fish. The classic polyculture sequence of duck marsh --> rice paddy --> shrimp ponds --> trout pond relies on plant systems at each stage.
