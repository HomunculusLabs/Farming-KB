# Actively Aerated Compost Tea Brewing

Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) is a liquid biological inoculant
produced by brewing high-quality compost in water with continuous
aeration. The process extracts beneficial microorganisms from compost
and provides conditions for them to multiply, creating a concentrated
liquid that can be applied to soil and plant surfaces.

## Definition and Core Purpose

AACT is distinguished from other compost teas by its strict reliance on
sustained aeration throughout the entire brewing process. This aerobic
environment selectively supports the growth of beneficial bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes extracted from the compost starting
material.

The primary purpose of AACT is to rapidly increase both the population
and diversity of beneficial soil organisms and deliver them in a liquid
form that can cover large areas efficiently. This allows biological
inoculation of soil, foliage, and growing media at scale.

## Compost Selection and Quality Standards

The quality of compost used for brewing is the single most important
factor determining AACT quality. Compost must be fully mature, aerobic,
and biologically diverse. Immature or anaerobic compost will introduce
harmful organisms and toxic metabolites into the finished tea.

Good brewing compost should possess a diverse bacterial and fungal
community. Thermal compost made with diverse feedstocks typically
provides the broadest range of organisms. Vermicompost can also be
used, though it tends to be more bacterial than fungal in composition.

Compost should be tested before use when possible. The presence of
indicator organisms such as E. coli and Salmonella signals anaerobic
conditions during the composting process. Visual inspection for a
pleasant earthy smell, dark brown color, and crumbly texture provides
a basic quality check when laboratory testing is not available.

Clopyralid and other persistent herbicide contamination is a serious
concern for compost tea brewers. These chemicals survive the composting
process and can damage or kill sensitive plants when contaminated tea
is applied. Source-aware compost selection is essential.

## Brewing Equipment Requirements

AACT brewers range from simple five-gallon home setups to large
commercial units holding hundreds of gallons. Regardless of size,
every system requires a vessel, an aeration source capable of
maintaining aerobic conditions, and a means of suspending compost
in the water column.

The air pump or compressor must deliver sufficient dissolved oxygen
to maintain aerobic conditions throughout the entire brew cycle. Most
systems use air diffusers or bubblers positioned to distribute oxygen
evenly. Dissolved oxygen levels should remain above 6 mg/L at all
times during the brew.

Compost is typically placed in a mesh bag or basket suspended in the
water. This allows organism extraction while preventing large
particulate matter from clogging sprayers and application equipment.
The mesh size should be fine enough to retain compost particles while
allowing organisms to pass through.

## Brewing Time and Temperature Control

Standard AACT brew cycles run between 24 and 36 hours. During this
period, organism populations multiply significantly — bacteria can
increase from millions to billions per milliliter of tea. Brewing
beyond 36 hours risks depleting food resources and shifting the
biology toward anaerobic conditions.

Water temperature directly affects brewing speed and organism
composition. Ideal brewing temperatures range from 65 to 75 degrees
Fahrenheit. Cooler temperatures slow organism multiplication, while
temperatures above 85 degrees Fahrenheit can select for thermophilic
organisms and reduce overall biological diversity.

The brew should start with clean, dechlorinated water. Chlorine and
chloramine in municipal water supplies can kill beneficial organisms
on contact. Water can be dechlorinated by aerating it vigorously for
several hours before adding compost, or by using a carbon filter or
chemical dechlorination treatment.

## Aeration Requirements and Dissolved Oxygen

Continuous aeration is the defining characteristic that separates AACT
from other compost tea methods. The air supply must maintain dissolved
oxygen above 6 mg/L at all times throughout the brew cycle. If
dissolved oxygen drops below 5.5 mg/L, anaerobic organisms begin to
proliferate and produce metabolites that can harm plants.

Airflow rate depends on brewer volume and design configuration. As a
general guideline, the water surface should appear to be actively
boiling from the aeration. Vigorous mixing also helps distribute
organisms and food resources evenly throughout the brew volume.

## Food Additives and Recipes

Food additives such as molasses, kelp meal, fish hydrolysate, and humic
acids can be added to feed specific organism groups during the brew.
Molasses selectively feeds bacteria, while kelp and fish hydrolysate
support both bacteria and fungi in the growing tea.

Excessive food additions can cause oxygen demand to exceed the supply
capacity of the aeration system, leading to anaerobic conditions. This
is one of the most common brewing mistakes. Start with small amounts
of food resources and monitor dissolved oxygen closely.

Different recipes produce teas with different organism profiles.
Fungal-dominant teas for perennial systems may include humic acids and
fish hydrolysate with minimal simple sugars. Bacterial-dominant teas
for annual crops use more molasses and simple carbohydrate sources.

## AACT vs. Non-Aerated Teas and Extracts

AACT differs fundamentally from non-aerated compost teas and compost
extracts. Non-aerated teas are steeped without active aeration, which
allows anaerobic conditions to develop. These teas risk containing
phytotoxic compounds produced by anaerobic organisms.

Compost extracts are made by washing compost through water without a
brewing period. They extract existing organisms but do not allow
significant multiplication. Extracts are useful for rapid application
but contain lower organism densities than a full AACT brew cycle.

AACT provides the highest organism diversity and density when brewed
correctly. The aerobic environment ensures that only beneficial aerobic
organisms thrive, making AACT the preferred method for biological
inoculation in most agricultural and horticultural applications.
