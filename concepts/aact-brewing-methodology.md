# Actively Aerated Compost Tea Brewing

Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) is a liquid biological inoculant
produced by brewing high-quality compost in water with continuous
aeration. The process extracts [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] from compost
and provides conditions for them to multiply, creating a concentrated
liquid that can be applied to soil and plant surfaces.

## Definition and Core Purpose

AACT is distinguished from other [[bacterial-vs-fungal-compost-teas]] by its strict reliance on
sustained aeration throughout the entire brewing process. This aerobic
environment selectively supports the growth of beneficial bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes extracted from the compost starting
material.

The primary purpose of AACT is to rapidly increase both the population
and diversity of beneficial soil organisms and deliver them in a liquid
form that can cover large areas efficiently. This allows biological
inoculation of soil, foliage, [[hamilton-potting-compost-recipes-and-growing-media]] at scale.

## Compost Selection and Quality Standards

The quality of compost used for brewing is the single most important
factor determining AACT quality. Compost must be fully mature, aerobic,
and biologically diverse. Immature or anaerobic compost will introduce
harmful organisms and toxic metabolites into the finished tea.

Good brewing compost should possess a diverse [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]]
community. [[ingham-thermal-compost-versus-worm-compost]] made with diverse feedstocks typically
provides the broadest range of organisms. Vermicompost can also be
used, though it tends to be more bacterial than fungal in composition.

Compost should be tested before use when possible. The presence of
indicator organisms such as E. coli and Salmonella signals anaerobic
conditions during the composting process. Visual inspection for a
pleasant earthy smell, dark brown color, and crumbly texture provides
a basic quality check when laboratory testing is not available.

Clopyralid and other [[aact-clopyralid-persistent-herbicide-compost-contamination-ingham]] contamination is a serious
concern for compost tea brewers. These chemicals survive the composting
process and can damage or kill sensitive plants when contaminated tea
is applied. Source-aware compost selection is essential.

## Brewing Equipment Requirements

AACT brewers range from simple five-gallon home setups to large
commercial units holding hundreds of gallons. Regardless of size,
every system requires a vessel, an aeration source capable of
maintaining aerobic conditions, and a means of suspending compost
in the water column.

The air pump or compressor must deliver sufficient [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]]
to maintain aerobic conditions throughout the entire brew cycle. Most
systems use air diffusers or bubblers positioned to distribute oxygen
evenly. [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-anaerobic-aact-brewing-management]] levels should remain above 6 mg/L at all
times during the brew.

Compost is typically placed in a mesh bag or basket suspended in the
water. This allows organism extraction while preventing large
particulate matter from clogging sprayers and application equipment.
The mesh size should be fine enough to retain compost particles while
allowing organisms to pass through.

## Brewing Time and Temperature Control

Standard AACT brew cycles run between 24 and 36 hours. During this
period, organism populations multiply significantly — bacteria can
increase from millions to billions per milliliter of tea. Brewing
beyond 36 hours risks depleting food resources and shifting the
biology toward anaerobic conditions.

Water temperature directly affects brewing speed and organism
composition. Ideal brewing temperatures range from 65 to 75 degrees
Fahrenheit. Cooler temperatures slow organism multiplication, while
temperatures above 85 degrees Fahrenheit can select for thermophilic
organisms and reduce overall [[fungi-convention-on-biological-diversity]].

Aact Brewing Methodology has significant applications in sustainable agriculture, ecological design, and regenerative practices. Practitioners and researchers continue to explore innovative methods for implementing these concepts in diverse climates and scales of operation.
