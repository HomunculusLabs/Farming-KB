# Windbreak Design and Establishment

## Overview

### Fire and Wind Resistance Overlap

Many fire-resistant plants are also wind resistant. Additionally, some drought-resistant but fire-prone species (notably pines) will withstand wind due to their shared adaptations against desiccation.

### Coastal Growth Constraints

On dry salt-wind coasts, tree canopies rarely gain more than **46 cm height in 1 m width** (18 inches in 3 feet). Considerable width must be given to pioneer windbreaks in these situations unless hardy pioneers like Norfolk Island pines are nursed to grow to windward.

## Establishment Methods

### Direct Plant Protection

Individual plant protection during establishment is essential in hostile areas:

- **Individual open-ended plastic bags** around stakes—a common and effective method
- **Earth mounds** or side-cast earth banks of greater length than the tree line
- **Brush fences**, wire-mesh fences, or staked fences with 40% wind penetrability
- **Tussock or tough unmown grass** to windward (leave if already present)

All of these can be used in combination in very hostile areas.

### Structural Windbreaks

**Substantial trellis** provides more immediate protection:

- **Brick or mud brick:** Make sinuous (curved) to withstand persistent forces
- **Timber:** Make zig-zagged for stability against wind
- **Earth mounds:** Better streamlined, less sensitive to windthrow; the hollow from which earth is taken can hold water or protect young plants
- **Tyre walls:** Create warmth inside the tyres; cheap and removable once effective shelter is established
- **Mesh fences:** If stoutly built with a heavy top rail, serve as the basis for **fedges** (fence-hedges) of thick-leaved vines

### Fedge (Fence-Hedge) Systems

On coasts, fedges may be completely mounded over with tough semi-succulents such as:
- *Rhagodia*
- *Tetragonia*
- *Carpobrotus*
- *Mesembryanthemum*

Rock walls and tyre walls may be similarly mounded with scramblers or cacti, some of which provide bee forage, berries, or edible fruits.

### Essential Establishment Practices

1. **Good pioneer species selection** for rapid establishment
2. **Initial protection** from mechanical or wind damage (bagging, fencing)
3. **Periodic or trickle irrigation** to reduce desiccation during establishment
4. **Anchoring** by stones or mulch to prevent windthrow
5. **Appropriate species spacing** for 40–50% penetrability in the front line

## Coastal Windbreak Garden Systems

### The Compact Garden Model

A special charm exists in the 3–3.5 m (10–12 feet) high dense coastal shrubberies where:
- Small shacks nestle within the windbreak
- Sandy paths wander through
- Small patches of scattered garden are created using wastewater and mulch
- Fruit trees, once shaped, seldom need pruning
- The wind forces compact and careful work, punishes carelessness, and promotes wastewater use

### Minimal Shelter Gardens

Even a **46–62 cm (18–24 inch) high** fence or earth mound will grow sweet potato, strawberry, or cabbage in its lee. Hard-pruned canopies need not be barren—many dwarf fruit, vine, and flower crops grow below these if mulch and water are provided.

In windbreak forests near coasts, **small openings of 6–9 m (20–30 feet)** provide garden shelter while admitting light.

### Traditional Hawaiian Example

Across the flat peninsula of Kalaupapa on Moloka'i, Hawaiians built tiny stone fences of 25–50 cm (10–20 inches) high, only 4.5–5 m (15–18 feet) apart, behind which they grew sweet potato crops with tough fern mulch. Wild date palms still struggle to 4.5 m (15 feet) in the teeth of the trade winds, demonstrating the persistent challenge of coastal wind exposure.
