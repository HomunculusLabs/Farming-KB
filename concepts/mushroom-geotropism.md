## Mushroom Geotropism

Geotropism (also called gravitropism) is the growth response of an organism to gravity. In most mushrooms, the stipe exhibits **negative geotropism** — growth directed away from the center of gravity, causing the mushroom to grow upward. This upward orientation is critical for [[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]] in mushrooms causes:

- The stipe to grow vertically upward regardless of the initial orientation of the primordium
- The pileus to orient horizontally with the gills facing downward, optimizing the vertical drop of spores from basidia
- Curvature correction when a growing mushroom is displaced from vertical — the stipe bends to restore upward growth

This response has been documented in numerous mushroom species including *[[schizophyllum-commune]]*, *Polyporus brumalis*, and *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]*.

### The Geotropic Shift in Psilocybe cubensis

Badham's (1982) wind tunnel experiments with *P. cubensis* revealed a particularly interesting aspect of geotropism: it is developmentally regulated and linked to spore production.

During **Stage 1 and early Stage 2** of [[wind-tunnel-methodology-mushroom-tropism-research]]. Several lines of evidence support this connection:

1. **Sporeless mutants**: Strains of *P. cubensis* that cannot produce spores do not exhibit the Stage 2 geotropic shift, even though they show normal anemotropic growth during Stages 1–2
2. **Decapitation experiments**: In *[[agaricus-bisporus]]*, removal of the pileus (which contains the spore-producing lamellae) alters tropic responses, suggesting that a substance produced in the gills influences growth direction (Gruen, 1963; Hagimoto, 1963)
3. **Gill diffusates**: Agar diffusates collected from gills can partially replace the tropic influence of the pileus (Hagimoto and Konishi, 1960), indicating a diffusible signaling molecule produced in the lamellae
4. **Developmental timing**: The geotropic response appears precisely when spore production begins, suggesting a causal rather than coincidental relationship

### The Hormone Hypothesis

The leading explanation for the geotropic shift is that spore production (or a related developmental event in the lamellae) triggers the release of a hormone or growth regulator that modifies the stipe's response to environmental stimuli. This hypothetical substance would:

- Be produced in or near the lamellae of the pileus
- Be released around the time of spore maturation
- Alter the stipe's sensitivity to gravity relative to other stimuli (wind, light, evaporative demand)
- Be absent in sporeless mutants, explaining their lack of geotropic response

While the identity of this substance remains unknown, the gill-diffusate experiments suggest it is a small, diffusible molecule rather than a structural signal.

## Mechanism of Gravity Sensing

How mushrooms detect gravity is not fully understood, but several mechanisms have been proposed:

### Statolith Model

In higher plants, gravity sensing involves statoliths — dense starch-filled organelles (amyloplasts) that sediment to the bottom of specialized cells (statocytes), triggering a signal transduction cascade. Some fungi may use a similar mechanism:

- Dense cellular inclusions could sediment under gravity within stipe cells
- The sedimentation could trigger differential growth on the upper vs. lower sides of the stipe
- In *Phycomyces*, the sporangiophore contains lipid globules that have been proposed as statolith-like gravity sensors

### Buoyancy/Pressure Model

The large central vacuole of stipe cells could create a hydrostatic pressure gradient under gravity. The differential pressure between the upper and lower cell walls might be detected by mechanosensitive ion channels or stretch receptors, triggering asymmetric growth.

### Hormone Redistribution

If the gravity-sensing gill-derived hormone is produced at the pileus and transported down the stipe, gravity could affect its distribution. A higher concentration on the lower side of the stipe could stimulate differential cell elongation, causing upward curvature. This model is consistent with the observation that decapitation (removing the hormone source) alters tropic responses.

## Interaction with Other Tropisms

### Geotropism vs. Anemotropism

In *P. cubensis*, anemotropism dominates during early development (Stages 1–2) while geotropism dominates after sporulation begins (Stage 2+). The transition appears to be a shift in the relative weighting of stimuli rather than a simple on/off switch:

- When mushrooms are rotated horizontally to equalize the gravitational stimulus under constant illumination, growth and pileus expansion are both horizontal — suggesting that without a clear gravity signal, the anemotropic/phototropic signal prevails
- The fact that rotation perpendicular to wind produces upright growth (equalizing wind from all directions while gravity acts normally) confirms that gravity becomes the dominant orienting stimulus at maturity

### Geotropism vs. Phototropism

Light is necessary for the geotropic response in *P. cubensis*, but this may be because light is required for spore production rather than for geotropism per se. The situation is complicated by:

- Light having its own direct tropic effect (phototropism)
