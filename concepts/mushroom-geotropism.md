# Mushroom Geotropism (Gravity Response)

Geotropism (also called gravitropism) is the growth response of an organism to
gravity. In mushrooms, negative geotropism causes the stipe to grow upward
(opposing gravity) and the pileus (cap) to orient horizontally — a critical
adaptation for effective [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]]. The relationship between geotropism
and spore production in mushrooms was elucidated by Badham (1982) in studies
of [[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]].

## Overview

Mushroom fruitbodies exhibit negative geotropism: the stipe grows upward
against gravity, and the pileus orients horizontally so that the gills face
downward. This positions the spore-producing surfaces for optimal spore
release and dispersal. In P. cubensis, negative geotropism emerges at a
specific developmental stage and is linked to spore production.

## Developmental Stages in P. cubensis

Badham defined four stages of [[two-phase-directed-growth-psilocybe-cubensis-basidiocarp-development]] relevant to tropic
responses:

| Stage | Description | Dominant Tropism |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| Stage 1 | Slight swelling of pileus | Anemotropism (growth toward wind) |
| Stage 2 | Pileus nearly spherical | Transition: geotropism emerges |
| Stage 3 | Open hemispherical pileus (sporulation) | Negative geotropism dominant |
| Stage 4 | Pileus becomes plane (late maturity) | Negative geotropism maintained |

## The Geotropic Response at Stage 2

When P. cubensis basidiocarps reach Stage 2, a negative geotropic curvature
of the stipe occurs. The pileus turns upward from the direction of the wind
and opens with the lamellae (gills) facing downward. This response:

- Occurs under a 12-hour photoperiod with light from above or below
- **Does NOT occur in sterile (sporeless) mutant strains**
- **Does NOT occur in the absence of light** (which prevents spore formation)
- Is associated with the onset of spore production

## Evidence for Hormonal Control

The connection between geotropism and spore production has been supported by
multiple lines of evidence:

### Decapitation Experiments

Studies on [[agaricus-bisporus]] and other species have shown that removing the
pileus (decapitation) eliminates the geotropic response. This links the
tropism to substances produced in the gills or cap tissue.

### Agar Diffusate Studies

Hagimoto and Konishi (1960) demonstrated that agar diffusates collected from
the gills of Agaricus bisporus can partially replace the influence of the
pileus on geotropic behavior, suggesting a diffusible substance from the gills
modulates stipe growth direction.

### Sporeless Mutant Evidence

Badham's key finding was that sporeless mutants of P. cubensis did not
exhibit negative geotropism at Stage 2. The sterile basidiocarps continued to
grow in the direction of the wind (anemotropic response) without the
corrective upward bending seen in fertile specimens. This strongly suggests
that the geotropic response is either:

1. Triggered by a substance produced during spore development, or
2. Directly dependent on the physical presence of developing spores

### Light Requirement

Some light is necessary for the negative geotropic response, but possibly
only because light is necessary for spore production. When mushrooms were
grown in darkness, few spores formed and the geotropic recurvation did not
occur. This supports the hypothesis that geotropism is downstream of
sporulation rather than directly light-dependent.

## Rotation Experiments

When fertile basidiocarps were rotated horizontally (parallel to the air
flow) at 3 rpm under constant illumination, growth and pileus expansion were
both horizontal. This demonstrates that:

- Equalizing the gravitational stimulus prevents the upward curvature
- The geotropic response requires a consistent gravitational vector
- Light alone does not override the gravity signal when it is equalized by
  rotation

## Proposed Mechanism

Badham proposed a two-phase model for directed growth in P. cubensis:

### Phase 1: Evaporative Demand-Directed Growth

During primary stipe elongation (Stage 1), growth is oriented toward the
direction of greatest evaporative demand (wind, low humidity, light). The
cells of the stipe may respond individually to this stimulus through
differential growth rates.

### Phase 2: Hormone-Modulated Geotropism

At Stage 2, a substance produced in the gills (associated with spore
production) modifies the evaporative demand response, causing the mushroom to
respond primarily to gravity. The pileus turns upward and opens horizontally.

## The "Mushroom Hormone" Hypothesis

Several researchers have postulated the existence of one or more mushroom
hormones involved in tropic responses:

- **Gruen (1963)** — described endogenous [[brassinosteroid-cross-kingdom-growth-regulation-plants-fungi]] in A. bisporus
  carpophores
- **Hagimoto (1963)** — proposed a "mushroom growth hormone" linked to
  geotropic response
- **Hagimoto and Konishi (1960)** — demonstrated extractable activity from
  gill tissue

Some mushrooms contain higher plant hormones (auxins, gibberellins), but no
evidence suggests these regulate mushroom growth (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961;
Jeffereys and Greulach, 1956; Pegg, 1973).

## Adaptive Significance

Negative geotropism serves a critical ecological function:

- **Horizontal gill orientation** — positions the spore-producing surfaces
  facing downward so that spores can drop free from the gills and be carried
  by air currents
- **Elevated cap position** — lifts the cap above the substrate boundary
  layer where air is more turbulent, improving spore dispersal distance
- **Stability** — upright growth keeps the cap elevated for maximum spore
  release height

Plunkett (1961) noted that negative geotropism is especially important for
lignicolous (wood-dwelling) and coprophagous (dung-dwelling) species whose
habitats are physically unstable and where effective spore dispersal demands a
horizontal pileus.

## Comparison with Other Fungi

- **Phycomyces** — sporangiophores respond to gravity and centrifugal force
  (Dennison, 1961), but the mechanism involves different structures
- **[[schizophyllum-commune]]** — shows phototropic and geotropic responses
  during fruitbody development (Schwalb and Shanler, 1974)
- **Coprinus** — tropic changes during stipe development have been well
  documented (Plunkett, 1961)
