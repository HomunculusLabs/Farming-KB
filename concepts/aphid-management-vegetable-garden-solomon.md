# Aphid Management in Vegetable Gardens

## Overview

Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* approaches aphid management
with characteristic pragmatism: healthy plants resist most aphid damage,
tolerance of minor damage is rational, and intervention should be
proportionate to actual harm rather than triggered by the mere sight of
insects. His integrated approach combines prevention through plant
vigor, biological controls, and targeted organic sprays when necessary.

## Aphid Biology

Aphids (family Aphididae) are small, soft-bodied insects that cluster on
leaves and stems to suck plant sap. Key characteristics:

- They multiply with astonishing rapidity, exploding from nothing to a
  serious threat in days through asexual (parthenogenetic) reproduction
- They can also persist at a low stable level without causing much
  trouble for extended periods
- Some species transmit plant viral diseases, making early detection
  important in crops susceptible to specific viruses
- They have a close mutualistic relationship with ants, which "farm"
  aphids much as humans graze livestock on pasture — ants place aphids
  on leaves and then harvest the sweet honeydew secretions

## The Damage Threshold

Solomon emphasizes that the presence of aphids does not automatically
warrant action. The critical threshold is approximately **5% of total
leaf area** covered by aphids. Below this level, plant growth and
production are generally unaffected. Key points:

- A few aphids cause no significant damage unless they transmit disease
- In large numbers they cause leaf curl, stunting, and weakening
- There is a big difference between a plant showing the occasional
  insect and one that has been severely damaged
- If a plant is still growing vigorously, a few (even a few hundred)
  aphids do not matter — only when [[cannabis-resin-production-and-quality-traits-clarke]] start to
  lessen does intervention become worthwhile

## Prevention Through Plant Health

The first line of defense is growing healthy, vigorous plants:

- **Proper soil fertility**: Solomon's experiment with extra cabbage
  transplants planted in unmanured fringe soil with only cheap
  chemical fertilizer showed dramatically worse pest susceptibility.
  The properly prepared bed produced large, healthy cabbages with no
  problems; the neglected plants were attacked by flea beetles, then
  cabbageworms, then root maggots.
- **Liquid [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]]**: For struggling plants, a foliar spray
  of fish emulsion combined with liquid kelp provides a triple benefit —
  two fertilizers plus one that temporarily disguises the plant's odor
  from predators.
- **Avoid sowing too early**: The single biggest cause of pest trouble.
  Sowing on the earliest possible day versus two weeks later produces
  dramatically different outcomes — the later crop typically has far
  fewer problems.
- **Grow twice what you need**: Plant redundancy ensures that even if
  some plants are severely affected, there will still be enough harvest.

## Biological Context

Solomon provides important ecological context for pest pressure:

- **Location matters**: Urban gardeners and those in prosperous
  agricultural districts inevitably have more pest problems because
  large monocultures of host crops sustain large pest populations
- **Wild hosts sustain predators**: Where wild carrots (Queen Anne's
  lace), wild cabbage, and wild radish grow near gardens, stable
  populations of pest predators keep aphids and other insects in check
- **Farmscaping**: Creating [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects is a
  long-term strategy. Rex Dufour's ATTRA publication "Farmscaping to
  Enhance Biological Control" provides regional guidance.
- **Disease-carrying aphids**: You cannot spray something that would
  kill every single aphid before it enters your garden. It is better to
  make plants healthy enough to resist most diseases most of the time.

## Control Methods (Escalating Response)

### Level 1: Hose and Nozzle

Blast aphids off leaves with a strong jet of water. The dislodged
aphids probably will not find their way back, although new ones may
arrive. This is the simplest and least disruptive approach.

### Level 2: Insecticidal Soap

Safer's insecticidal soap (made from special fats) is effective and
virtually nontoxic to animals and most other insects. Important caveats:

- Can burn sensitive plant species (especially spinach) if applied in
  full-strength dilutions
- Test on a few leaves a few days before spraying the whole patch
- Often works fine at weaker dilutions, which are gentler on plants

### Level 3: Ant Control

Since ants farm and protect aphids, eliminating ant nests near affected
plants can significantly reduce aphid populations.

### Level 4: Pyrethrin-Rotenone Mix

A combination of pyrethrin (from [[permaculture-pyrethrum-daisy]]) and rotenone (from
tropical legume roots) kills aphids on contact. However, Solomon notes
significant drawbacks:

- Both are natural poisons that unfortunately decompose within hours
  in sunlight, requiring frequent reapplication
- This mix also kills [[query-how-to-attract-native-bees-and-beneficial-insects]] insects
- Should be considered a last resort, not a first response

### Level 5: Neem Spray

Neem spray, derived from the neem tree seed, is a longer-lasting
natural remedy that Solomon recommends as one of the preferred organic
spray options when intervention is truly necessary.

## Attitude Adjustment

Solomon challenges what he calls the "American Sanitary System" — the
belief that food should be perfectly clean and free of any insect
damage. He suggests reconsidering what constitutes acceptable table
fare: remove visible insects when washing, and discreetly slide the rare
one that escapes the cook's scrutiny to the side of the plate. A few
holes in spinach leaves or scars on a cucumber's skin do not affect
nutrition or safety.
