# JADAM Ultra-Low-Cost Philosophy

JADAM (Jayonul Damun Saramdul, meaning "people who are like
nature") is a Korean organic farming system founded by Youngsang Cho
that pursues farming at radical cost reduction while simultaneously
increasing yield, quality, and ecological harmony. Its production cost
target is approximately 100 dollars per acre per year for all
fertilizers and pesticides combined, compared to roughly 6,000 dollars
for typical organic farming.

## Core Motivation: Farming Independence

The central thrust of JADAM philosophy is restoring farming to farmers.
Cho argues that agriculture has been progressively taken over by
fertilizer and pesticide corporations, transforming farmers from
self-sufficient producers into dependent consumers who must purchase
seeds, seedlings, fertilizers, pesticides, and microorganisms from the
marketplace. This transfer of control and knowledge has driven farming
costs upward while impoverishing growers. JADAM seeks to reverse this
by equipping farmers with the knowledge to produce all necessary inputs
themselves from locally available, inexpensive materials.

Cho explicitly chose not to patent any of his inventions including
[[jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] and [[jadam-sulfur-germicide-js]], instead
opening the knowledge publicly through books, workshops, and the
website en.jadam.kr so that all farmers worldwide can benefit.

## The SESE Principle

JADAM promotes four guiding aims, abbreviated as SESE:

- **Simple**: principles are simple and accessible
- **Easy**: easy to make and apply
- **Scientific**: methodology is grounded in observation and reason
- **Effective**: process is highly effective and low-cost

The SESE framework was inspired by the philosophy of Laozi, who taught
Cho to make JADAM organic farming "like water, so that ultra-low-cost
method will seep into farmers." Water does not conflict yet it
conquers. Only when a farming system achieves SESE will farmers embrace
it voluntarily in significant numbers.

## Oriental Philosophy Foundation

JADAM draws on several philosophical traditions. From Confucianism comes
the concept of the [[jadam-good-and-bad-are-one]]: living
without imbalances, excess, or want. In farming this translates to
avoiding both the extreme of chemical dependence and the extreme of
rigid ideological purity. From liberation theology (Gustavo Gutierrez)
and ecological theology (Leonardo Boff), Cho derived the conviction that
farming should empower the excluded and heal the environment.

The principle of "I and others are one" means that a farmer should put
themselves in the position of their crops and soil when making
decisions. If a method would cause suffering to a plant root, it should
be reconsidered. This empathic stance leads naturally toward
[[jadam-coexisting-with-wild-grass]], cover
cropping, and gentle soil management rather than aggressive chemical
interventions.

## Economics of Ultra-Low-Cost

JADAM's [[jadam-ultra-low-cost-agriculture]] (ULC) rests on the observation that modern organic farming
has become too expensive to compete with conventional agriculture. If
organic products cannot compete on both price and quality, the movement
will remain marginal. Cho argues that viability means a farmer should
still make ends meet after selling at market price because they have
reduced costs to an ultra-low level, not because they charge premium
prices.

The system replaces virtually all purchased inputs with self-made
alternatives. [[ultra-low-cost-jadam-microorganism-solution-and-soil-foundation]] is cultured from local leaf mold using potatoes and sea salt.
[[jadam-animal-byproduct-liquid-fertilizer]] are made from wild grass,
crop residues, food waste, and water. Natural pesticides are produced at
roughly 1/50th the cost of chemical equivalents. The Bongha village
case study demonstrated that rice farming with JADAM methods required
only 9 dollars per acre for pesticide across four applications.

## Critique of Commercial Agriculture

Cho identifies a structural conflict of interest: when corporations
control agricultural technology, farming becomes more difficult, more
costly, and less productive because simplicity and self-sufficiency
would eliminate their market. He compares the situation to medicine,
where the system profits from illness rather than health. Government
subsidies, in his view, often flow through to input manufacturers rather
than reaching farmers.

JADAM explicitly critiques the Japanese Korean Natural Farming (KNF)
system promoted by [[cho-han-kyu-life-and-philosophy]] for its reliance on
imported inputs like sugar and molasses, arguing that Korea produces no
sugar and that such dependencies contradict the spirit of organic
farming. See [[jadam-rejection-of-sugar-molasses]] for details.

## Relationship to Nature

Unlike some romantic interpretations of organic farming, JADAM takes a
clear-eyed view of nature as a realm of constant struggle where all
living forms compete for survival. This "check and balance" ecology
means that farming without any pest intervention is impossible: healthy
trees with tasty fruit attract more pests, not fewer. The solution is
not to eliminate pesticides but to produce them naturally and cheaply.
See [[jadam-natural-pesticide-philosophy]].

The method of "asking nature" is JADAM's core investigative approach.
When faced with a farming problem such as [[jadam-salt-accumulation-in-soil]] or [[jadam-monoculture-disorder-crop-residues]], the question is always posed to nature first: how does the
natural ecosystem handle this? The answers consistently point toward
maximizing microbial diversity, retaining crop residues, and
maintaining soil contact with the sky through elimination of
[[jadam-antibiotic-soil-contamination]].

## Practical Transition

JADAM recommends beginning transition by applying the method to one
tenth of total cropland, mastering [[jadam-soil-management-no-till-and-weed-control]] and [[jadam-animal-byproduct-liquid-fertilizer]]
before advancing to pest control. Key questions for self-assessment
include confidence in [[ultra-low-cost-jadam-microorganism-solution-and-soil-foundation]], [[jadam-cover-crop-soil-temperature-management]],
[[jadam-calcium-fertilizer]], and the
ability to control aphids, mites, and powdery mildew with natural
pesticides. See [[jadam-transition-conventional-to-organic]].
