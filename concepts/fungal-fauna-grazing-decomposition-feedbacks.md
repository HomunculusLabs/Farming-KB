# Fungal-Fauna Grazing Effects on Decomposition

## Overview

Grazing of fungal hyphae by soil fauna is a major interaction that influences both the
rate and pathway of decomposition in terrestrial ecosystems. Soil microarthropods,
nematodes, and other fungivorous organisms modulate fungal community structure, alter
competitive dynamics among fungal species, and affect nutrient mineralization rates.
These grazing interactions represent a critical link between
[[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] structure and
[[fungal-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]].

## Effects of Fungal Species Diversity on Decomposition

Decomposition rates are reduced as the number of fungal species is increased, due to the
fact that metabolic activity of competing fungi is greater than the activity of an
equivalent biomass of a single fungal species (Wicklow and Yocum, 1982; Robinson et al.,
1993). A significantly higher level of respiration occurs when fungal species compete for
a resource than could be predicted from the combination of respiration of each fungus
alone.

Because of selective grazing of fungi by soil microarthropods, the diversity of fungal
species effecting decomposition is often reduced (Lussenhop and Wicklow, 1985). This
reduction in competitive intensity can paradoxically increase decomposition efficiency by
redirecting fungal energy from combat to enzymatic activity.

## Positive and Negative Feedback Loops

The effects of faunal grazing on decomposition can be positive or negative depending on
the ecosystem context and faunal density. Coleman et al. (1990) showed that reduction in
microbial predators in ecosystems with high densities of soil fauna (forests) led to
increased decomposition of litter through relief of grazing pressure. In contrast, in
systems with low densities of soil fauna (agricultural soils), the effect of faunal
reduction was to reduce decomposition, suggesting a synergistic interaction.

In a comparison of tilled and untilled agricultural soils, Beare et al. (1992) showed
that exclusion of fungivorous soil arthropods reduced litter dry mass loss by only 5%,
but significantly altered nitrogen dynamics in surface litter of no-till soil.
Saprotrophic fungi were responsible for as much as 86% of net nitrogen immobilization
(1.8 g per square meter) into surface litters with the exclusion of fungivorous
microarthropods.

## Selective Grazing and Fungal Community Structure

Collembola and other soil microarthropods show distinct feeding preferences that can
restructure fungal communities during decomposition. Newell (1984a, 1984b) demonstrated
that grazing by collembola altered the vertical distribution of competing mycelia of
Mycena galopus and Marasmius androsaceus in a spruce forest floor. Preferential grazing
of Marasmius restricted its growth to lower depths, allowing Mycena to dominate in the
upper soil horizon.

Chen and Ferris (1999) showed that decomposition of alfalfa residues and cellulose was
increased by the presence of fungal-feeding nematodes. Where residues were colonized by
the preferred fungal food (Rhizoctonia solani) for nematodes, both nematode populations
and nitrogen mineralization were significantly higher than when the less preferred fungal
food (Trichoderma sp.) was available. This suggests that nematode feeding increased
either the biomass or at least the activity of the preferred fungal food.

## The Lycoriella Experiment

Lussenhop and Wicklow (1985) demonstrated complex interactions between fungal diversity
and faunal grazing. They measured the effect of increasing complexity of the saprotrophic
fungal community on rabbit dung and found that as species complexity increased there was
less decomposition and lower spore production. When they introduced the mycophagous fly
larvae of Lycoriella mali, however, there was a 10% increase in the rate of decomposition
at high fungal species diversity and a 1500% increase in spore production.

Three hypotheses were proposed for this effect: the larvae could directly compete with
fungi for water-soluble compounds, larval grazing on mycelia could slow hyphal growth and
reduce competitive interactions so fungi invest more resources in decomposition, or
larvae could concentrate enzymes as the number of fungal species increases.

## Impacts on Mycorrhizal Function

Faunal grazing on arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal extraradical hyphae reduces the
efficiency of the mycorrhizae in acquiring nutrients, particularly phosphorus, for the
plant. Warnock et al. (1982) showed a strong interaction between collembolan density and
host plant growth. This effect is likely more important in agroecosystems, where the
diversity of soil fauna is reduced and high densities of collembola can occur in the
absence of predators.

Nematode feeding on mycorrhizal fungal hyphae can also reduce the effectiveness of the
mycorrhizal association and has the effect of altering a plant's competitive fitness
(Brussard et al., 2001). These impacts on [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] function cascade through
to affect [[fungal-roles-built-environments]] and plant community
dynamics.

## Nutrient Mineralization Consequences

The increase in fungal biomass resulting from the alleviation of grazing pressure is
correlated to an increase in nitrogen retention (25% higher than plots with faunal
populations intact). This increase in nitrogen content is related to nitrogen
immobilization in fungal tissue. In some studies, 85% of the net immobilized nitrogen was
associated with the saprotrophic fungal community.

The activities of soil fauna thus not only moderate fungal growth but allow greater rates
of nutrient mineralization than when animals are removed, leading to greater soil
fertility. These findings underscore the importance of intact
[[fungal-interactions-soil-fauna]] for maintaining functional
decomposition processes in terrestrial ecosystems.
