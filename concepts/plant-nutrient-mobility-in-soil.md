# Plant Nutrient Mobility in Soil

## Overview

Nutrient mobility -- the ability of mineral nutrients to move through soil and within plants -- is a fundamental concept in [[plant-nutrient-uptake|plant nutrition]] that directly affects fertilizer management, deficiency diagnosis, and plant health. Understanding which nutrients are mobile and which are immobile, both in the soil environment and inside plant tissues, is essential for effective [[soil-fertility-management|soil fertility management]].

## Soil Mobility

### Factors Affecting Nutrient Movement in Soil

Multiple factors influence how nutrients move (or fail to move) through soil:

- **pH:** Soil acidity or alkalinity dramatically affects nutrient solubility and availability
- **Moisture:** Nutrients move with water; dry conditions limit mobility
- **Clay content:** Negatively charged clay particles adsorb and hold cation nutrients
- **Organic matter:** Provides cation exchange sites and chelating compounds that affect mobility
- **Nutrient interactions:** The presence of one nutrient can enhance or inhibit the mobility of another

### Highly Mobile Nutrients in Soil

These nutrients move readily with soil water and are susceptible to leaching:

- **Nitrate (NO3-):** The most mobile form of nitrogen; does not bind to soil particles and moves freely with water. This mobility is why nitrate pollution of groundwater is a major environmental concern. Gardeners use three times the nitrogen per acre that farmers do, leading to significant excess nutrients washing into waterways.
- **Sulfate (SO4 2-):** Anion form of sulfur; moderately mobile
- **Boron (B):** Moves readily in soil solution; easily leached from sandy soils
- **Chlorine (Cl-):** Highly soluble and mobile

### Moderately Mobile Nutrients

These are adsorbed by clay particles and organic matter but remain available for plant uptake:

- **Ammonium (NH4+):** Less mobile than nitrate because it is held by cation exchange sites
- **Potassium (K+):** Held by cation exchange sites but available for root uptake
- **Calcium (Ca2+):** Moderately available, though can be depleted in acidic, leached soils
- **Magnesium (Mg2+):** Similar behavior to calcium
- **Molybdenum (Mo):** As molybdate anion, moderately mobile

### Immobile Nutrients in Soil

These nutrients become chemically bound or fixed in soil and move very little:

- **Phosphorus (P):** The most problematic immobile nutrient. Inorganic phosphorus is tightly adsorbed to clay particles and organic matter. An acre of soil might have only 1 pound of phosphorus dissolved in water and available to plants. This tight binding is why tremendous amounts of phosphorus are applied to fields (22+ million tons globally per year), and why the world is approaching peak phosphorus production.
- **Iron (Fe):** Becomes unavailable at high pH; precipitates as insoluble compounds
- **Manganese (Mn):** Similar to iron; pH-dependent availability
- **Zinc (Zn):** Adsorbed to clay and organic matter; limited mobility
- **Copper (Cu):** Strongly bound to organic matter; very limited soil mobility

Note: These immobile nutrients can become mobile when chelated (bound to organic molecules that keep them soluble).

## Plant Mobility

### Mobile Nutrients Inside Plants

These nutrients can be relocated within the plant from older tissues to areas of new growth:

- **Nitrogen (N):** Highly mobile; plants will rob nitrogen from older leaves to support new growth. This is why nitrogen deficiency symptoms appear first in older (lower) leaves as yellowing.
- **Phosphorus (P):** Mobile; deficiency symptoms (bluish-green coloration) appear first in older leaves.
- **Potassium (K):** Mobile; deficiency symptoms (dead spots on leaf margins) appear on older leaves.
- **Magnesium (Mg):** Mobile; deficiency (interveinal chlorosis) shows in older leaves.
- **Molybdenum (Mo):** Mobile; chlorosis develops in older leaves as Mo is redirected.
- **Nickel (Ni):** Immobile once assimilated.

### Immobile Nutrients Inside Plants

Once incorporated into plant tissues, these nutrients cannot be relocated. Deficiency symptoms therefore appear first in new growth (young leaves, terminal buds, root tips):

- **Calcium (Ca):** Immobile; deficiencies appear as malformed growing tips and young leaves.
- **Boron (B):** Immobile (binds to sugar molecules); deficiency causes damage at growing tips and flower/fruit problems.
- **Sulfur (S):** Immobile; deficiency yellowing appears in younger leaves (distinguishable from N deficiency).
- **Iron (Fe):** Immobile; young leaves become bleached/chlorotic while veins remain green (interveinal chlorosis).
- **Manganese (Mn):** Immobile; interveinal chlorosis in young leaves.
- **Copper (Cu):** Immobile; chlorosis and leaf curling in new growth.

### Special Cases

- **Chlorine (Cl):** Mobile, but deficiency symptoms appear in top (newer) leaves
- **Zinc (Zn):** Mobile, but deficiency symptoms show up in middle-aged leaves
- **Boron (B):** Mobility depends on carrier molecules; this unreliability means visual diagnosis alone can be misleading

## Practical Implications

### Foliar Feeding Limitations

Nutrient mobility inside plants explains why [[foliar-feeding|foliar feeding]] is not always effective. Only mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg) can be translocated from leaf surfaces to where they are needed in the plant. For immobile nutrients (Ca, B, Fe), foliar application only benefits the tissues directly sprayed.

### Fertilizer Placement

- **Mobile nutrients** (nitrogen) can be surface-applied; they will move to root zones with water
- **Immobile nutrients** (phosphorus) should be incorporated into the root zone at planting time, as they will not move significantly through soil

### Deficiency Diagnosis

The pattern of symptom expression on the plant (older leaves vs. newer growth) provides a first approximation of which nutrient may be deficient, based on its mobility classification. However, as Lowenfels emphasizes, all sorts of chemical interdependencies can mask the real deficiency, and only a soil or tissue test will tell what is actually going on.
