# AACT Ingredient Ratios, Compost Volume, and Recipes

Getting the right proportions of compost, food sources, and water is essential for producing effective [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] tea. The relationships between these ingredients are not always intuitive — more is not always better, and ratios are often non-linear.

## Compost-to-Water Ratio

The amount of compost per volume of water is **non-linear** — larger volumes require proportionally less compost per gallon. This relationship is specific to well-designed machines:

| Water Volume | Compost Needed |
|---|---|
| 5 gallons | ~1 pound |
| 25 gallons | ~7 pounds |
| 50 gallons | ~10 pounds |
| 100 gallons | ~12 pounds |
| 500 gallons | 15–20 pounds |

A machine maker claiming that a 100-gallon machine requires 100 pounds of compost is either selling compost or has a very poorly designed machine. Well-designed brewers extract and multiply organisms efficiently, requiring far less compost.

## Ingredient Categories by Organism Fed

Understanding which ingredients feed which organisms allows targeted tea recipes:

### Bacterial Foods
- **Simple sugars**: Molasses, white sugar, fruit juices
- **Fish emulsion**: Oils removed, primarily bacterial nutrition
- **Crab waste**: Rapid bacterial growth

### Fungal Foods
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Contains oils and complex proteins favoring fungi
- **Humic acids**: Most selective fungal food available
- **Lignite/leonardite**: Very complex carbon, fungi only initially
- **Cornmeal, wheat bran, corn bran**: Protein types favor fungal growth
- **Oatmeal**: Good fungal food, especially when combined with humic acid

### Both Bacteria and Fungi
- **Cold water kelp**: Enhances both groups, provides micronutrients
- **Complex sugars**: Feed both, but bacterial response is faster
- **Fish waste**: Both groups, but oxygen demand is high

### Nutrients (for microbes and plants)
- **Kelp**: Micronutrients, plant growth factors, fungal surface area
- **Minerals** (greensand, rock phosphate, rock dusts): Require biological cycling

## Molasses: Machine-Dependent Usage

Molasses amount relates more to machine design than to plant needs. Different machines have different capacities to maintain adequate [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] when organic matter is being rapidly consumed:

- In the Growing Solutions machine, **no molasses at all** should be used — it consistently produces E. coli when any significant food source is added.
- Most reputable tea machine makers have done testing to determine what recipe results in good tea in their specific equipment.

Always ask your machine maker for their tested recipe, or perform your own testing.

## Kelp Amount: Plant-Need Based

Kelp serves two functions in tea: providing surface area for fungi and supplying nutrients. The amount should be determined by:

1. **Fungal surface area needs**: Fungi need only a small amount of surface area — approximately 1 ounce in 5 gallons is sufficient.
2. **Plant nutrient needs**: The amount of micronutrients the plant requires should guide kelp quantity. A plant tissue test is the most reliable way to determine this.

## Compost Sources: 50-50 Mix

A recommended compost base combines:
- **Good aerobic [[aact-vermicompost-vs-thermal-compost-ingham]]** — must be truly aerobic throughout the composting process
- **Good aerobic worm castings (vermicompost)**

A 50-50 or 25-75 mix of thermal compost to worm castings works well. Both must be aerobic and biologically active.

### Cedar Grove Warning

The source material specifically warns: "Do not use Cedar Grove products, they do not make compost." This highlights the importance of verifying that any purchased compost is genuinely biologically active and aerobic.

## Recipe Development Protocol

### Step 1: Make a Test Brew
Brew tea with your chosen ingredients and observe:

- **Smell test**: If the tea smells bad (sour, putrid, sewage-like), too much food was added for the organisms relative to the oxygen supply.
- **Sealed container test**: Put brewed tea in a sealed plastic container, half full, in a warm place for 24 hours. If it still smells good, the tea was properly made with adequate organism diversity.

### Step 2: Send for Analysis
Submit a sample to a microbiology lab (ideally Soil Foodweb or equivalent) to verify:
- Total and active bacteria (target: above 300 µg/ml for good leaf coverage)
- Total and active fungi
- Protozoa
- Beneficial organism presence

### Step 3: Iterate
If [[aact-compost-organism-levels]] are inadequate, adjust the recipe:
- Too few bacteria: Add more bacterial foods or better compost
- Too few fungi: Add more fungal foods (humic acid, fish hydrolysate, oatmeal)
- No protozoa: Ensure compost source contains protozoa; they cannot grow from nothing in a short brew cycle

## The Fungal Priority

For [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]] and general plant health, building fungal biomass in tea is often the limiting factor. While bacterial biomass is usually easy to achieve, maintaining adequate fungal biomass — especially late in [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] — requires deliberate fungal food inclusion in the recipe. Humic acid and oatmeal are consistently recommended as the best fungal food sources for compost tea.

## Practical Recipe Template

For a typical 50-gallon brew in a well-designed machine:

1. **10 pounds** compost (50-50 thermal compost + worm castings)
2. **1–2 ounces** kelp (cold water extracted)
3. **1–4 tablespoons** humic acid (adjust based on desired fungal dominance)
4. **0–4 tablespoons** molasses (machine-dependent; start low)
5. **Optional**: 1–2 tablespoons fish hydrolysate for additional fungal nutrition
6. **Brew time**: 24–36 hours with continuous aeration

The exact amounts must be calibrated to your specific machine, water quality, compost source, and target crop needs. The testing protocol above is the only reliable way to finalize a recipe.
