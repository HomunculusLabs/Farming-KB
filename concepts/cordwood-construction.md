# Cordwood Construction

Cordwood construction (also called cordwood masonry or stackwall) is a building technique where short logs (cordwood) are laid in a bed of mortar, similar to bricks, to create walls. The log ends are visible on both the interior and exterior faces, creating a distinctive and beautiful pattern. This method combines the insulation of wood with the [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] of masonry.

## History

Cordwood construction has been practiced for over a thousand years across Europe and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]. Pioneer settlers in Wisconsin, Michigan, and Ontario built many surviving cordwood homes in the 1800s. The technique saw a revival in the 1970s alternative building movement and remains popular among homesteaders and natural builders today.

## Core Advantages

- Low cost: Uses short pieces of wood that would otherwise be firewood or waste
- DIY friendly: No special skills or heavy equipment required
- Excellent insulation: Wood has an R-value of about 1.0 per inch
- Thermal mass: Mortar absorbs and releases heat slowly
- Aesthetic appeal: Unique, beautiful wall patterns
- Local materials: Can use wood from your own land
- [[cervantes-case-study-energy-efficient-sea-of-green]]: Thick walls (16-24 inches) provide high total R-value

## Wood Selection and Preparation

### Best Species

Softwoods are preferred because they dry faster and shrink less:
- Cedar (all types) — naturally rot-resistant, excellent choice
- Pine — readily available, good insulation
- Spruce — lightweight, good for long walls
- Larch — rot-resistant, strong
- Poplar — easy to work, dries quickly
- Oak — durable but heavier and harder to work

Avoid:
- Highly resinous woods (fresh pine) that inhibit mortar curing
- Rotting or punky wood
- Wood with heavy bark that will fall off

### Log Preparation

1. Cut logs to consistent lengths matching your wall thickness (typically 16-24 inches)
2. Debark completely — bark will detach over time, creating gaps in the wall
3. Split any logs larger than 6 inches diameter — solid logs above this size crack excessively
4. Dry logs for at least 6 months (longer is better). Dry wood shrinks less after wall construction.
5. Store off the ground with [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]
6. Sort by diameter for organized building

### Optimal Moisture Content

Wood should be below 20% moisture content before building. Test with a moisture meter or by the tap test — dry wood sounds hollow; wet wood sounds dull. Building with wet wood leads to excessive shrinkage and mortar separation.

## Mortar Mixes

### Standard Cordwood Mortar (Portland-based)

- 1 part Portland cement
- 2 parts sand
- 1 part soaked sawdust (passed through 1/4 inch screen)
- 1 part lime

The sawdust replaces some of the sand to improve insulation and reduce weight. Soak sawdust in water for at least 24 hours before mixing, then drain excess water (damp, not dripping).

### Lime Putty Mortar (More Breathable)

- 3 parts lime putty
- 1 part sand
- 1 part soaked sawdust

Lime mortar is more breathable than Portland cement, allowing walls to dry. It is also self-healing — small cracks fill with lime as it slowly carbonates. However, it gains strength more slowly.

### Papercrete Mortar (Experimental)

Some builders use papercrete (shredded paper, sand, and cement) as a lightweight, insulative mortar. Less proven but interesting for experimental builders.

### Mortar Tips

- Mix mortar to a stiff but workable consistency (should hold shape when squeezed)
- Add a small amount of dish soap or air-entraining agent to improve workability
- Keep mortar damp during curing — mist walls daily for the first week
- Protect from direct sun and freezing for the first 7 days

## Wall Construction

### Foundation

Cordwood walls are heavy (100-150 lbs per [[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]]). They require a solid foundation:
- Concrete footing and stem wall, or
- Rubble trench foundation with a concrete cap, or
- Frost-protected shallow foundation

The foundation should extend at least 8 inches beyond the wall face on each side to support the mortar joints.

### Building the Wall

1. Lay a mortar bed 1-2 inches thick on the foundation
2. Place cordwood logs in the mortar, leaving 1-2 inch gaps between logs
3. Fill all gaps with mortar — no voids allowed
4. Use a stick or trowel to poke mortar deep between logs
5. Clean the exposed log ends of mortar immediately with a wet brush
6. Build corners first, then fill between them
7. Use a level and string line to keep walls plumb and straight
8. Build no more than 3-4 courses per day to allow settling

### Insulation Options

The center of a 24-inch cordwood wall is largely air space between log ends. This space can be filled for additional insulation:

- Sawdust (least effective, cheapest)
- Cellulose insulation (good insulation, moderate cost)
- Roxul/mineral wool batts (excellent insulation, higher cost)
- Spray foam (excellent insulation, highest cost)
- Mix of sawdust and lime (traditional, moderate insulation)

## Wall Performance

### Insulation Value

A 16-inch cordwood wall has approximately R-16 to R-20 depending on wood species and insulation fill. A 24-inch wall reaches R-24 to R-30. This exceeds standard 2x6 framed walls with fiberglass insulation.

### Thermal Mass

The mortar provides significant thermal mass, absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night. This makes cordwood walls ideal for [[passive-solar-design|passive solar]] homes where thermal mass is desirable.

## Code Considerations

Cordwood construction is not addressed in most building codes. Approaches:

- Work with an engineer to get a custom design approved
- Some rural areas have no building codes or allow alternative methods with owner-builder permits
- Build small structures first (sheds, outbuildings) to demonstrate the technique
- Consider insulating cordwood (split face cordwood) where one face is masonry and the other is framed and insulated — this may be easier to permit

## Common Problems

1. Mortar separation from wood due to shrinkage — use dry wood and add sawdust to mortar
2. Insect damage — debark completely, consider borate treatment of logs
3. Air infiltration — fill all gaps thoroughly, use expansion foam at joints with framing
4. Moisture problems — provide wide roof overhangs, keep walls above grade, use breathable finishes
5. Freeze-thaw damage — protect fresh mortar from freezing during curing
