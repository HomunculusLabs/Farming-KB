# Latua Pubiflora (Arbol de los Brujos)

Latua pubiflora, known in Spanish as the arbol de los brujos ("sorcerers'
tree") or latue, is one of the most potent and feared psychoactive plants
of South America. It is used by the Mapuche Indian medicine men of Valdivia,
Chile, to cause delirium, hallucinations, and occasionally permanent
insanity. The alkaloids hyoscyamine and scopolamine have been isolated from
the fruit and are responsible for its potent effects. Latua pubiflora is
the only species in its genus and is confined to the coastal mountains of
central Chile. It belongs to the nightshade family, Solanaceae.

## The Sorcerers' Tree: A Name Earned Through Fear

The Spanish name "arbol de los brujos" (sorcerers' tree) reflects the deep
fear and respect with which this plant is regarded in Mapuche culture. The
Mapuche name "latue" similarly conveys a sense of dread and supernatural
power. Unlike many psychoactive plants that are integrated into communal
religious ceremonies, Latua pubiflora has no cult or ritual surrounding its
use. Instead, it is the closely guarded tool of individual medicine men
who employ it for specific, often dangerous purposes.

The absence of formalized ritual surrounding Latua use is unusual among
New World hallucinogenic plants. Most psychoactive plants in indigenous
American cultures are embedded within elaborate ceremonial frameworks that
regulate their use and protect participants from harm. The fact that Latua
is used outside of such structures, by individual practitioners operating
independently, suggests that its effects are considered too dangerous and
unpredictable for communal ceremonial use.

## Effects: Delirium, Hallucinations, and Permanent Insanity

The effects of Latua pubiflora are described by Schultes as including
delirium, hallucinations, and occasionally permanent insanity. The
possibility of inducing permanent mental damage through the use of this
plant is perhaps its most alarming characteristic and distinguishes it from
most other ethnobotanically documented hallucinogens, which typically
produce only transient effects.

It is widely believed among the Mapuche that a madness of any desired
duration may be induced by a medicine man who knows how to measure the doses
properly. This belief reflects both the perceived potency of the plant and
the specialized knowledge required to use it safely (or to use it as a
weapon). The idea that dosage can be calibrated to produce specific
durations of insanity suggests a sophisticated, if frightening,
understanding of the plant's pharmacological properties.

## Dosage: A Closely Guarded Secret

Dosages of Latua pubiflora are described as a closely guarded secret among
Mapuche medicine men. This secrecy serves multiple purposes: it protects
the medicine man's professional authority and specialized knowledge, it
prevents accidental poisoning by unauthorized persons, and it ensures that
the plant remains a tool of the initiated rather than a freely available
substance. The secrecy surrounding Latua dosage is analogous to the
restricted knowledge of many potent psychoactive plants in indigenous
cultures worldwide, where specialized training is required before a
practitioner is permitted to work with dangerous substances.

## Chemistry: Hyoscyamine and Scopolamine

The chemical investigation of Latua pubiflora has identified two major
tropane alkaloids: hyoscyamine and scopolamine. These are the same
alkaloids found in other Solanaceous deliriants such as Datura stramonium
(jimsonweed), [[belladonna-atropa-belladonna-ethnobotany]] (deadly nightshade), and [[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]]
(henbane). The presence of these well-known deliriant compounds confirms
the pharmacological mechanism underlying Latua's dramatic effects.

Hyoscyamine and scopolamine are anticholinergic compounds that block the
action of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors in the brain and peripheral
nervous system. This blockade produces a characteristic syndrome of effects
including dry mouth, dilated pupils, tachycardia, urinary retention,
hyperthermia, and most significantly from a psychoactive perspective, a
state of delirium characterized by realistic but bizarre hallucinations,
confusion, disorientation, and inability to distinguish fantasy from
reality. The deliriant state produced by tropane alkaloids is
fundamentally different from the hallucinogenic state produced by classical
hallucinogens like psilocybin or mescaline.

## Method of Administration

The Mapuche employ the fresh fruits of Latua pubiflora for their
psychoactive effects. The use of fresh rather than dried plant material is
noteworthy, as it suggests that the active compounds may degrade during
drying or that fresh fruit provides a more predictable or controllable dose.
The specific method of fruit [[magic-mushroom-preparation-and-consumption-methods]] is not detailed in
the available sources, but it likely involves eating the fruit directly or
preparing a fresh juice or infusion.

## Geographic Distribution

Latua pubiflora is confined to the coastal mountains of central Chile, a
relatively narrow geographic range that parallels that of other Chilean
endemic psychoactive plants such as [[keule-gomortega-keule-chilean-entheogen]] (keule) and
[[taique-desfontainia-hookeri-andean-shrub]] (taique). The concentration of endemic psychoactive
plants in central and southern Chile reflects the region's botanical
uniqueness and the long history of Mapuche ethnopharmacological
investigation of the local flora.

## Monotypic Genus

Latua pubiflora is the only known species in the genus Latua, making it a
monotypic genus. This taxonomic isolation, shared with other Chilean
psychoactive plants like Gomortega keule and Methysticodendron amesianum,
is consistent with the general pattern of endemic plant evolution in the
Chilean flora, where geographic isolation and diverse microclimates have
produced numerous species with restricted distributions and unique chemical
profiles.

## Comparison with Other Solanaceous Deliriants

Latua pubiflora can be compared to other well-known Solanaceous deliriants
including Datura species, Atropa belladonna, Hyoscyamus niger, and
[[mandrake-mandragora-officinarum-in-european-folklore]]. All share the common mechanism of tropane alkaloid
toxicity, though their specific alkaloid profiles, potencies, and cultural
contexts of use differ. Latua is distinguished by its exceptionally
dangerous reputation and the reported possibility of permanent insanity,
which exceeds the documented risks of most other tropane-containing plants.
