# Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons -- Properties and Toxicity

[[fungal-pah-degradation-and-soil-bioremediation]]. Understanding their chemical properties, environmental behavior, and toxicological significance is essential for developing effective remediation strategies.

## Chemical Nature and Sources

PAHs are non-polar, hydrophobic organic compounds with two or more fused benzene rings. Naphthalene (two rings) is the simplest. Sources include:
- **Pyrolysis** of organic materials -- the primary source of PAHs in air, sediments, water, and food
- **Crude oil, creosote, and coal tar** -- PAHs are major constituents
- **Fossil fuel combustion** -- diesel and gasoline engines, coal-fired power plants
- **Industrial processes** -- coal gasification, incineration, wood treatment
- **Petroleum spills** -- accidental spillage of petroleum hydrocarbons
- **Grilled and smoked foods** -- PAHs form during cooking
- **Forest fires and farm debris burning**

## Physical and Chemical Properties

PAHs have several properties that affect their environmental fate and [[staycare-bioavailability-pollutants-fungal-bioremediation]]:

### Low Water Solubility
Water solubility decreases as the number of condensed aromatic rings increases. Benzo[a]pyrene (five rings) has solubility of 0.001-0.006 mg/L, while naphthalene (two rings) has 30 mg/L. This low solubility is a major factor in their environmental persistence.

### Low Vapor Pressure
Except for two- and three-ring PAHs, vapor pressures are low, so PAHs do not readily volatilize. They accumulate in freshwater and marine sediments, particularly near industrial areas.

### High Photoelectric Charging
PAHs are abundant in the smallest fractions of atmospheric particulate matter and in sediments due to their photoelectric properties.

### Ionization Potential
The ionization potentials of PAHs have been correlated with degradation by [[white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization]]. This is a key factor in determining which PAHs are susceptible to lignin peroxidase attack.

### Octanol-Water Partition Coefficient (Kow)
Log Kow ranges from 3.58 (naphthalene) to 5.9 (benz[a]anthracene), indicating strong preference for organic phases over water. This drives sorption to soil organic matter.

## Environmental Distribution

- **Atmosphere** -- from diesel exhaust, power plants, tobacco smoke, fires
- **Water** -- PAHs are widespread in freshwater and seawater, especially near petroleum, coal, or heavy industry
- **Sediments** -- common in estuarine sediments; if trapped in woody debris, may not significantly affect aquatic organisms
- **Soils** -- receive PAHs mainly from atmospheric deposition; background levels of 1-5 mg/kg in urban areas, 10-20x higher in industrial areas
- **Stormwater runoff** -- combustion products are major PAH sources in urban runoff

## Toxicity and Carcinogenesis

PAHs are on the US EPA priority pollutants list due to their toxicity. The history of PAH carcinogenesis research spans over two centuries, from John Hill's 1761 report on tobacco snuff and nasal cancer.

### Four Mechanisms of PAH Carcinogenesis
1. **Dihydrodiol epoxide mechanism** -- metabolic activation by P450 enzymes creates reactive epoxide and diol-epoxide intermediates that form covalent DNA adducts, leading to mutations and tumorigenesis (the most significant mechanism)
2. **Radical-cation mechanism** -- one-electron oxidation generates radical-cation intermediates that attack DNA, causing depurination
3. **Quinone mechanism** -- enzymic dehydrogenation yields quinone intermediates that enter redox cycles generating reactive oxygen species
4. **Benzylic oxidation mechanism** -- formation of benzylic alcohols converted to reactive sulfate esters

### Metabolic Activation Requirement
PAHs must be metabolically activated by mammalian microsomal enzymes to express mutagenic, genotoxic, and carcinogenic properties. Critically, fungi mimic mammalian metabolism and can form reactive intermediates like dihydrodiol epoxides or quinones. This means fungal PAH degradation pathways must be carefully characterized to ensure bioremediation does not produce toxic intermediates.

### Acute Toxicity
Not all PAHs are acutely toxic, but the combination of anthracene with solar UV radiation is acutely toxic and immunosuppressive to fish. Benzo[a]pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, and chrysene are mutagenic after metabolic activation.

## Implications for Fungal Bioremediation

These properties create both challenges and considerations for [[mycoremediation-of-pahs]]:
- Low water solubility limits bioavailability for both microbial and enzymatic attack
- Sorption to soil organic matter sequesters PAHs, especially in aged contaminated soils
- The need to avoid producing toxic intermediates during bioremediation
- PAHs persist in anoxic environments but can be degraded under anaerobic conditions with nitrate, sulfate, or ferric iron as electron acceptors
- The ionization potential relationship means LiP can oxidize PAHs with IP below approximately 9.0 eV

## Specific PAH Compounds and Their Properties

The US EPA has designated 16 PAH compounds as priority pollutants based on their frequency of occurrence and toxicity. These range from the simplest two-ring naphthalene to the complex five-ring benzo[ghi]perylene:

- **Naphthalene (2 rings)**: The simplest PAH and the most water-soluble (30 mg/L). Widely used in industrial applications and historically in moth repellents. Relatively easily degraded by both bacteria and fungi but acutely toxic to aquatic organisms at low concentrations.
- **Anthracene and phenanthrene (3 rings)**: Common products of incomplete combustion. Anthracene is photosensitizing and becomes acutely toxic to fish when combined with UV radiation. Phenanthrene is one of the most abundant PAHs in the environment and serves as a model compound for studying fungal PAH metabolism.
- **Fluoranthene and pyrene (4 rings)**: Fluoranthene is one of the most abundant PAHs in urban soils and sediments. Pyrene is frequently used as a marker for PAH contamination because it is consistently present in combustion-derived PAH mixtures.
- **Benzo[a]pyrene (5 rings)**: The most potent carcinogenic PAH and the reference compound against which other PAH carcinogens are compared. It has extremely low water solubility (0.001-0.006 mg/L) and high environmental persistence, making it a priority target for bioremediation.
- **Benzo[ghi]perylene and indeno[1,2,3-cd]pyrene (6 rings)**: Among the most persistent PAHs due to their high molecular weight and extremely low bioavailability. These compounds accumulate in sediments and are difficult to degrade by any biological system.

## Health Effects Beyond Carcinogenesis

PAH exposure is associated with a broader spectrum of health effects than carcinogenesis alone. Respiratory exposure to PAH-containing particulate matter is linked to cardiovascular disease, including atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction, through inflammatory pathways triggered by PAH binding to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Developmental toxicity is a major concern: prenatal exposure to PAHs has been associated with reduced birth weight, premature delivery, and neurodevelopmental effects including lower IQ scores and behavioral problems in children. Immunotoxicity has been demonstrated for several PAHs, which suppress immune function through mechanisms involving the AhR pathway. Dermal exposure to PAH-contaminated soils or creosote can cause skin irritation, photosensitivity, and in occupational settings, increased rates of skin cancer. The combination of multiple PAHs in environmental mixtures may produce additive, synergistic, or antagonistic toxicological interactions that are not predictable from the study of individual compounds.

## Environmental Persistence and Aging

The environmental persistence of PAHs increases dramatically with molecular weight. Two- and three-ring PAHs have environmental half-lives of days to weeks in aerobic surface soils, while five- and six-ring PAHs can persist for decades to centuries. Over time, PAHs in soil undergo "aging," a process in which the compounds become increasingly sequestered within soil organic matter and soil microaggregates. Aged PAHs are far less bioavailable than freshly deposited PAHs, which reduces both their toxicity and their susceptibility to biodegradation. This aging effect is a major challenge for bioremediation of historically contaminated sites, where PAHs may have been present for decades. Bioavailability-enhancing techniques such as surfactant addition, soil washing, or phytoremediation with exudate-producing plants may be needed to mobilize aged PAHs for [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]], are the most extensively studied fungal PAH degraders. Their ligninolytic enzyme system, which evolved to decompose the structurally similar plant polymer lignin, is nonspecific enough to oxidize a wide range of PAH compounds. Lignin peroxidase (LiP) oxidizes PAHs with ionization potentials below approximately 9.0 eV, generating PAH radical cations that undergo further reactions including ring opening and eventual mineralization to carbon dioxide. Manganese peroxidase (MnP) degrades PAHs indirectly through the generation of Mn(III)-chelate complexes and lipid peroxidation products that can attack PAH molecules. Laccase, a multicopper oxidase, oxidizes PAHs in the presence of mediator compounds such as 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HBT) or natural mediators, extending the range of PAHs that can be degraded beyond those accessible to LiP and MnP alone. Non-ligninolytic fungi including Cunninghamella, Penicillium, and Aspergillus species degrade PAHs through cytochrome P450 mono-oxygenase systems that mirror mammalian metabolism, producing hydroxylated intermediates that may then be conjugated with sugars or organic acids.

## Field Applications and Monitoring

Field applications of fungal PAH bioremediation include land farming (mixing contaminated soil with nutrient amendments and fungal inoculum), biopile treatment (excavated soil piled with aeration and moisture control), and mycofiltration of PAH-contaminated water. Phanerochaete chrysosporium and Pleurotus ostreatus have been the most commonly used species in field trials. Successful field applications require careful management of environmental conditions: moisture content, oxygen availability, temperature, pH, and nutrient balance all influence fungal activity and PAH degradation rates. Monitoring of PAH degradation in the field typically involves periodic soil sampling followed by solvent extraction and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis. Toxicity testing using bioassays such as the Microtox assay (bioluminescent bacteria), seed germination tests, or earthworm survival tests provides complementary information on whether the toxicity of the contaminated material is decreasing alongside the chemical concentration of PAHs. Molecular techniques including quantitative PCR of ligninolytic enzyme genes and metatranscriptomics are increasingly used to monitor fungal community activity during bioremediation treatment.

## Comparison of Fungal and Bacterial PAH Degradation

Fungi and bacteria differ significantly in their approaches to PAH degradation, and these differences have practical implications for bioremediation strategy. Bacteria typically degrade PAHs through well-defined metabolic pathways involving dioxygenase enzymes that incorporate both atoms of molecular oxygen into the aromatic ring, producing cis-dihydrodiol intermediates. Bacterial degradation is most effective for low-molecular-weight PAHs (two to three rings) and requires direct contact between cells and the dissolved PAH. Fungi, by contrast, use extracellular enzymes that can attack PAHs without requiring direct cellular contact, and the nonspecific nature of ligninolytic enzymes allows degradation of higher-molecular-weight PAHs (four to six rings) that are recalcitrant to bacterial attack. The extracellular mode of fungal degradation also means that fungi can access PAHs that are sorbed to soil particles or incorporated into soil organic matter, where bacteria cannot reach them. The combination of fungal and bacterial degradation in sequence or in co-culture often produces more complete mineralization of PAHs than either group alone, as fungi initiate degradation by oxidizing the aromatic rings and bacteria then further metabolize the partially oxidized intermediates.

## Regulatory Framework and Cleanup Standards

PAH-contaminated sites are regulated under various national and international frameworks that establish acceptable concentration limits for soil, water, and sediment. In the United States, the EPA Region 9 preliminary remediation goals (PRGs) for PAHs in soil range from 0.066 mg/kg for benzo[a]pyrene to 3,900 mg/kg for naphthalene, reflecting the wide range of toxicity among PAH compounds. The European Union sets similar limits under the Water Framework Directive and the Soil Framework Directive. Cleanup standards typically require PAH concentrations to be reduced below these regulatory limits, or risk assessments must demonstrate that residual contamination poses no unacceptable risk to human health or the environment. Fungal bioremediation offers a cost-effective alternative to physical remediation methods such as excavation and incineration, which can cost hundreds of dollars per ton of contaminated soil. However, achieving regulatory cleanup standards through biological methods alone can be challenging, particularly for high-molecular-weight PAHs in aged contaminated soils where bioavailability limits the rate of degradation. A combined approach using physical, chemical, and biological treatment steps is often the most practical strategy for achieving compliance.
