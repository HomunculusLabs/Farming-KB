## Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons -- Properties and Toxicity

. Understanding their chemical properties, environmental behavior, and toxicological significance is essential for developing effective remediation strategies.

## Chemical Nature and Sources

PAHs are non-polar, hydrophobic [[staycare-bioavailability-pollutants-fungal-bioremediation]]:

### Low Water Solubility
Water solubility decreases as the number of condensed aromatic rings increases. Benzo[a]pyrene (five rings) has solubility of 0.001-0.006 mg/L, while naphthalene (two rings) has 30 mg/L. This low solubility is a major factor in their environmental persistence.

### Low Vapor Pressure
Except for two- and three-ring PAHs, vapor pressures are low, so PAHs do not readily volatilize. They accumulate in freshwater and marine sediments, particularly near industrial areas.

### High Photoelectric Charging
PAHs are abundant in the smallest fractions of atmospheric particulate matter and in sediments due to their photoelectric properties.

### Ionization Potential
The ionization potentials of PAHs have been correlated with degradation by [[white-rot-fungi]]-effluent-decolorization. This is a key factor in determining which PAHs are susceptible to [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]] attack.

### Octanol-Water Partition Coefficient (Kow)
Log Kow ranges from 3.58 (naphthalene) to 5.9 (benz[a]anthracene), indicating strong preference for organic phases over water. This drives sorption to soil organic matter.

## Environmental Distribution

- **Atmosphere** -- from diesel exhaust, power plants, tobacco smoke, fires
- **Water** -- PAHs are widespread in freshwater and seawater, especially near petroleum, coal, or heavy industry
- **Sediments** -- common in estuarine sediments; if trapped in woody debris, may not significantly affect aquatic organisms
- **Soils** -- receive PAHs mainly from atmospheric deposition; background levels of 1-5 mg/kg in urban areas, 10-20x higher in industrial areas
- **Stormwater runoff** -- combustion products are major PAH sources in urban runoff

## Toxicity and Carcinogenesis

PAHs are on the US EPA priority pollutants list due to their toxicity. The history of PAH carcinogenesis research spans over two centuries, from John Hill's 1761 report on tobacco snuff and nasal cancer.

### Four Mechanisms of PAH Carcinogenesis
1. **Dihydrodiol epoxide mechanism** -- metabolic activation by P450 enzymes creates reactive epoxide and diol-epoxide intermediates that form covalent DNA adducts, leading to mutations and tumorigenesis (the most significant mechanism)
2. **Radical-cation mechanism** -- one-electron oxidation generates radical-cation intermediates that attack DNA, causing depurination
3. **Quinone mechanism** -- enzymic dehydrogenation yields quinone intermediates that enter redox cycles generating reactive oxygen species
4. **Benzylic oxidation mechanism** -- formation of benzylic alcohols converted to reactive sulfate esters

### Metabolic Activation Requirement
PAHs must be metabolically activated by mammalian microsomal enzymes to express mutagenic, genotoxic, and carcinogenic properties. Critically, fungi mimic mammalian metabolism and can form reactive intermediates like dihydrodiol epoxides or quinones. This means fungal PAH [[staycare-chlorophenol-degradation-pathways-white-rot-fungi]] must be carefully characterized to ensure bioremediation does not produce toxic intermediates.

### Acute Toxicity
Not all PAHs are acutely toxic, but the combination of anthracene with solar UV radiation is acutely toxic and immunosuppressive to fish. Benzo[a]pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, and chrysene are mutagenic after metabolic activation.

## Implications for Fungal Bioremediation

These properties create both challenges and considerations for mycoremediation-of-pahs:
- Low water solubility limits bioavailability for both microbial and enzymatic attack
- Sorption to soil organic matter sequesters PAHs, especially in aged contaminated soils
- The need to avoid producing toxic intermediates during bioremediation
- PAHs persist in anoxic environments but can be degraded under anaerobic conditions with nitrate, sulfate, or ferric iron as electron acceptors

## Specific PAH Compounds and Their Properties

The US EPA has designated 16 PAH compounds as priority pollutants based on their frequency of occurrence and toxicity. These range from the simplest two-ring naphthalene to the complex five-ring benzo[ghi]perylene:

- **Naphthalene (2 rings)**: The simplest PAH and the most water-soluble (30 mg/L). Widely used in industrial applications and historically in moth repellents. Relatively easily degraded by both bacteria and fungi but acutely toxic to aquatic organisms at low concentrations.
- **Anthracene and phenanthrene (3 rings)**: Common products of incomplete combustion. Anthracene is photosensitizing and becomes acutely toxic to fish when combined with UV radiation. Phenanthrene is one of the most abundant PAHs in the environment and serves as a model compound for studying fungal PAH metabolism.
- **Fluoranthene and pyrene (4 rings)**: Fluoranthene is one of the most abundant PAHs in urban soils and sediments. Pyrene is frequently used as a marker for PAH contamination because it is consistently present in combustion-derived PAH mixtures.
- **Benzo[a]pyrene (5 rings)**: The most potent carcinogenic PAH and the reference compound against which other PAH carcinogens are compared. It has extremely low water solubility (0.001-0.006 mg/L) and high environmental persistence, making it a priority target for bioremediation.
- **Benzo[ghi]perylene and indeno[1,2,3-cd]pyrene (6 rings)**: Among the most persistent PAHs due to their high molecular weight and extremely low bioavailability. These compounds accumulate in sediments and are difficult to degrade by any biological system.

## Health Effects Beyond Carcinogenesis

PAH exposure is associated with a broader spectrum of health effects than carcinogenesis alone. Respiratory exposure to PAH-containing particulate matter is linked to cardiovascular disease, including atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction, through inflammatory pathways triggered by PAH binding to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Developmental toxicity is a major concern: prenatal exposure to PAHs has been associated with reduced birth weight, premature delivery, and neurodevelopmental effects including lower IQ scores and behavioral problems in children. Immunotoxicity has been demonstrated for several PAHs, which suppress immune function through mechanisms involving the AhR pathway. Dermal exposure to PAH-contaminated soils or creosote can cause skin irritation, photosensitivity, and in occupational settings, increased rates of skin cancer. The combination of multiple PAHs in environmental mixtures may produce additive, synergistic, or antagonistic toxicological interactions that are not predictable from the study of individual compounds.
