## Overview

A backyard orchard brings tree fruit production to the home landscape. Even small yards can support multiple fruit trees through careful planning, appropriate rootstock selection, and smart spacing. This guide covers layout principles, spacing, pollination groups, rootstock selection, and understory planting.

## Layout Principles

### Maximize Sun Exposure
- Fruit trees need minimum 6-8 hours of direct sun daily
- Plant the tallest trees on the north side of the orchard area (northern hemisphere)
- Shorter trees and bushes go on the south side
- Avoid planting where buildings, fences, or mature trees cast significant shade
- Observe your yard's sun patterns across seasons before planting

### Air Circulation
- Good airflow reduces fungal disease (especially critical for stone fruits)
- Space trees adequately — do not crowd
- Orient rows to align with prevailing winds for natural ventilation
- Avoid planting in low frost pockets where cold air settles
- Leave space between the orchard and fences or buildings

### Access and Maintenance
- Leave 3-4 feet between trees for walking, mowing, and equipment access
- Plan paths for wheelbarrow access to all trees
- Consider future tree size when locating paths and structures
- Group trees by maintenance needs (spray schedule, pruning timing)
- Position the orchard where you will see it daily — neglected trees produce poorly

### Water Access
- Ensure irrigation reaches all trees
- Group trees with similar water needs
- Avoid planting near the drip line of large shade trees that compete for water
- Plan for drip irrigation lines during layout — easier to install before planting

## Spacing

### Spacing

| Tree Type | Rootstock | Spacing |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Apple | Standard | 20-25 ft |
| Apple | Semi-dwarf | 12-15 ft |
| Apple | Dwarf | 8-10 ft |
| Pear | Semi-dwarf | 12-15 ft |
| Sweet Cherry | Standard | 25-30 ft |
| Sweet Cherry | Dwarf | 10-12 ft |
| Peach | Standard | 15-20 ft |

### High-Density Planting
- Space trees 3-6 feet apart for maximum variety; use dwarf rootstocks
- Requires annual rigorous pruning to maintain size
- More variety per square foot but more maintenance

## Pollination Groups

Most fruit trees require cross-pollination from a different variety that blooms at the same time.

### Apple Pollination
- Most apples are not self-fertile
- Plant at least two different varieties with overlapping bloom periods
- Bloom groups: Early, Mid, Late — plant within same or adjacent group
- Crabapples are universal pollinators for apples (long bloom period)
- Triploid varieties (Bramley, Jonagold, Mutsu) need two other pollinizers
- See [[apple-tree-care]] for variety recommendations by pollination group

### Pear Pollination
- Most European pears require cross-pollination
- Bartlett and Seckel may not pollinate each other (incompatible)
- Asian pears pollinate European pears in most cases
- Some European pears are partially self-fertile: Moonglow, Kieffer, Starkrimson

### Sweet Cherry Pollination
- Most sweet cherries require cross-pollination
- Self-fertile varieties: Stella, Lapins, Black Gold, White Gold, Columbia
- Plant two different varieties that bloom simultaneously
- Sour cherries are self-fertile

### Stone Fruit Pollination
- Peaches: mostly self-fertile (one tree is sufficient)
- Plums: Japanese plums need cross-pollination; European plums are mostly self-fertile
- Apricots: mostly self-fertile but set better with a partner
- See [[stone-fruit-tree-care]] for detailed pollination requirements

### Pollination Distance
- Trees should be within 50-100 feet of each other for reliable cross-pollination
- Bees travel further but pollen viability decreases with distance
- Closer spacing (20-50 feet) ensures better fruit set
- Wind direction affects pollination — plant pollinizer upwind if possible

## Rootstock Selection

Rootstocks determine tree size, hardiness, soil adaptation, and precocity (how quickly the tree bears fruit).

### Apple Rootstocks
- **M27**: Ultra-dwarf, 4-6 ft; **M9**: Dwarf, 6-8 ft, needs staking
- **M26**: Dwarf, 8-10 ft; good for most backyards
- **MM106**: Semi-dwarf, 12-15 ft; well-anchored, productive
- **MM111**: Semi-standard, 14-18 ft; drought-tolerant

### Pear Rootstocks
- **OHxF 333**: Semi-dwarf, 12-15 feet, disease-resistant, widely recommended
- **OHxF 87**: Semi-dwarf, similar to 333
- **Quince**: Dwarfing for pears, 8-10 feet, some incompatibility with certain varieties

### Cherry Rootstocks
- **Gisela 5**: Dwarf, 8-10 feet, precocious, needs staking
- **Gisela 6**: Semi-dwarf, 10-14 feet, productive
- **Mazzard**: Standard, 20-30 feet, full-size tree

### Peach/Plum Rootstocks
- **Lovell**: Standard, well-anchored, tolerant of wet soils
- **St. Julian A**: Semi-dwarf for plums, compatible with many varieties
- **Citation**: Semi-dwarf, good for peaches and plums

### Choosing Rootstocks
- Small yard: Dwarf (M26 for apples, Gi5 for cherries)
- Medium yard: Semi-dwarf (MM106 for apples, OHxF 333 for pears)
- Poor soil: MM111 (apples) or Lovell (peaches)
- Cold climate: Ensure rootstock is rated for your zone
- Container growing: Dwarf rootstocks only

## Understory Planting

The space beneath and between fruit trees can be productive if designed carefully.

### What to Plant Under Trees
- **Spring bulbs**: Daffodils, crocus, allium — bloom before trees leaf out, suppress weeds, deter rodents
- **Comfrey**: Deep-rooted dynamic accumulator; chop and drop for mulch. Plant at the drip line, not near the trunk
- **Nitrogen-fixing ground covers**: White clover, crimson clover
- **Herbs**: Chives, garlic, chamomile — deter pests, attract pollinators
- **Berry bushes**: Currants, gooseberries tolerate partial shade
- **Nasturtiums**: Pest deterrent, edible flowers, ground cover
