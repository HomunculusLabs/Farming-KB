# Smartshops and Hallucinogenic Mushroom Retail

The commercial retail of hallucinogenic mushrooms emerged as a significant market phenomenon in Europe during the late 1990s and early 2000s, driven by the "return to nature" consumer trend and facilitated by the expansion of internet commerce. The market was dominated by the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, with online retailers extending distribution internationally.

## What Are Smartshops?

A smartshop is a specialised retail outlet that sells psychoactive herbal substances alongside vitamins, mineral supplements, and other health products. The concept originated in the Netherlands, where smartshops operated legally and became the primary distribution channel for hallucinogenic mushrooms. Most Dutch smartshops also sell new synthetic drugs that have not yet been placed under legal control. The term distinguishes these outlets from "headshops" (which sell drug paraphernalia such as pipes) and "grow shops" (which sell equipment for cultivating cannabis).

## The Dutch Smartshop Market

The Netherlands was the epicentre of the hallucinogenic mushroom retail trade:

- An estimated 120 to 150 smartshops operated in the country, concentrated in Amsterdam
- Magic mushrooms were estimated to constitute approximately 50% of smartshop turnover
- Some coffee shops (where adults can purchase cannabis) also sold hallucinogenic mushrooms
- In some areas, "paddo cabs" provided home delivery services
- Headshops and grow shops sometimes also carried mushroom products

The most commonly sold species were [[Psilocybe cubensis]] varieties, particularly the Mexican strain, none of which grow wild in Europe. All commercially sold mushrooms were cultivated rather than wild-harvested, primarily because P. cubensis is easy to grow in terrariums and has a low water content that allows fresh mushrooms to remain viable for 7 to 10 days.

A 2001 study among a representative sample of young persons aged 12 and above in the Netherlands found that 64% of those aged 18 and older who had used magic mushrooms in the past year purchased them in smartshops.

## The UK Market

The UK market expanded rapidly in the early 2000s:

- Approximately 300 shops and market stalls sold hallucinogenic mushrooms across the country
- Market stalls at locations such as Camden Market in London were prominent retail venues
- The UK Treasury collected sales tax estimated at 175,000 GBP per year on a turnover of approximately 1 million GBP

A 2004 Mixmag survey found that 54.6% of respondents purchased their last batch of mushrooms in a club, shop, or stall, 16% obtained them from a friend, and 4.2% purchased them via the internet. The UK market was prohibited in July 2005 when fresh mushrooms were classified as Class A controlled drugs.

## Online Internet Shops

### Market Survey (January 2006)

A systematic search conducted via Google and AltaVista in January 2006 identified 39 online shops selling hallucinogenic mushroom products. The search used keywords including "magic mushrooms shop," "buy magic mushrooms," "psilocybe cubensis shop," and "buy psilocybe cubensis." Analysis of the first 300 results per search engine per keyword revealed:

- 82% of online shops were based in the Netherlands
- 18% included four Austrian shops selling growkits and spore prints, two German sites, and one UK site linked to a Dutch online shop
- One Polish site was found that primarily sold cannabis products

### Geographic Reach

Website statistics for one Dutch online shop showed visitor origins between May 2004 and February 2006:

- Netherlands: 73%
- Belgium: 14%
- Germany: 2%
- France: 2%
- Other countries: 9%

Most online shops offered international shipping, though many did not ship to countries where sales were prohibited. Sites typically had multiple language versions (mainly English, French, and German) to attract foreign customers.

### Products and Pricing

Online shops sold a variety of mushroom products:

| Product | Quantity | Price Range (EUR) |
|---------|----------|-------------------|
| Fresh [[Psilocybe cubensis]] (Mexican) | 30g | 8.50 to 45.28 |
| Fresh Copelandia cyanescens | 10g | 13 to 17.50 |
| [[Psilocybe mexicana]] (philosopher's stones) | 15g | 10 to 17.50 |
| Spawn bags (Psilocybe azurescens) | 1 unit | 24 to 25 |
| Growkits | 1 unit | 25 to 100 |
| Spore prints | 1 print or syringe | 9 to 28 |

The Free Spore Ring Europe, a non-profit organisation, distributed spore prints for approximately 1 EUR each.

### Impact of Legislation on Online Sales

The UK prohibition in July 2005 led to immediate changes in online retail:

- UK-based internet sites stopped selling hallucinogenic mushrooms
- The site allsalvia.co.uk claimed to be "sold out" of growkits in July 2005
- The Irish online headshop THC removed mushroom products after they became illegal on 31 January 2006
- Retailers began switching to legal alternatives such as [[Amanita muscaria]] and Purple Ohms capsules (containing Hawaiian Baby Woodrose with LSA)

## Brand Names and User Terminology

Hallucinogenic mushrooms are known by numerous names reflecting both user culture and marketing strategies:

- **Common English terms**: magic mushrooms, shrooms, mush, mushies, boomers, psilocybes, cubes, liberty caps, caps
- **Cultural names**: teonanacatl (Aztec: "divine flesh"), sacred mushrooms, philosopher's stone, moon children
- **European language terms**: paddos (Netherlands), Zauberpilze (Germany), champignons hallucinogenes or champis (France), funghi magici (Italy), magiske svampe (Denmark/Norway)
- **Retail labels**: Herbal highs, herbal xtc (though these categories include many non-mushroom products)

The term "philosopher's stone" refers specifically to the sclerotia of [[Psilocybe mexicana]], marketed by many smartshops. The "herbal highs" label, following the late 1990s trend toward organic products, groups mushrooms with other products like Salvia divinorum and herbal stimulant blends.

## Risk Information Provided by Retailers

A 2006 snapshot of 21 online shops found that the quality and completeness of risk information varied considerably:

- The majority warned against use with medication, alcohol, or other drugs
- Only two-thirds recommended against use by people with depression or psychosis
- About half provided dosage information and safe use practices (empty stomach, drink liquids, safe environment)
- Most sites described sought-after effects but few provided information on negative side effects
- Only a minority explicitly mentioned nausea and sickness
- Information was biased toward positive effects

Smartshop products in the Netherlands sometimes carried warning labels advising against use by persons under 18, pregnant women, those on medication, those with mental illness, and those driving or operating machinery.

## Contributing Factors to Market Growth

Several factors facilitated the expansion of the hallucinogenic mushroom retail market:

- **Consumer megatrends**: Increasing demand for intense experiences and preference for natural/organic products over synthetic substances
- **Recreational drug settings**: The established dance music scene provided a platform of acceptability for psychoactive substance use
- **Internet expansion**: Enabled both retailers and pro-mushroom lobby groups to reach international audiences with minimal regulatory oversight
- **Legal loopholes**: The biological nature of mushrooms created confusion and gaps in drug control legislation
