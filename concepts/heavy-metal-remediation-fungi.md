# Heavy Metal Remediation by Fungi

Fungi play integral roles in environmental cycling of metals and other
elements including carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus. Their filamentous
growth habit and high surface area to mass ratio make them important bioactive
components of metal biogeochemistry. Fungi interact with toxic metals through
multiple mechanisms that are relevant to environmental bioremediation,
including biosorption, solubilization, precipitation, and intracellular
sequestration. This page focuses on [[heavy-metal-bioremediation]] mediated by
fungal organisms.

## Metal Interactions with Fungi

Metals and their compounds interact with fungi in a variety of ways depending
on the metal species, organism, and environmental conditions. Fungal
metabolism can dramatically influence metal speciation, and therefore
mobility and toxicity. Key mechanisms include:

- **Solubilization:** Release of metals from insoluble compounds through
  protonation and organic acid production
- **Immobilization:** Sorption onto cell components, extracellular
  sequestration, transport, and precipitation
- **Methylation:** Conversion of metals to methylated forms
- **Volatilization:** Release of metals in gaseous forms

Fungal isolates from soils with high metal contents often exhibit higher metal
tolerance than isolates from agricultural soils, suggesting adaptive
mechanisms of resistance.

## Biosorption

[[heavy-metal-biosorption]] refers to the metabolism-independent binding of
metals to fungal biomass, occurring whether the biomass is dead or alive.
Cell walls of fungi contain chitin, glucans, and proteins that provide
abundant binding sites for metal ions through functional groups including
carboxyl, amino, phosphate, and sulfhydryl groups.

Biosorption techniques have been explored for treatment of metal-containing
wastewaters. Studies have shown optimal biosorption occurs at approximately
pH 4 for many metal-fungal systems, while biosorption does not occur
effectively above certain pH thresholds. Despite laboratory promise, attempts
to commercialize biosorption have met with limited success, with no widespread
industrial adoption to date.

## Metal Solubilization Mechanisms

Fungal solubilization of insoluble metal compounds, including certain oxides,
phosphates, sulfides, and mineral ores, occurs through several mechanisms.
Solubilization by protonation of the anion decreases its availability to the
cation. The proton-translocating ATPase of the plasma membrane and production
of organic acids serve as proton sources.

Organic acid anions are frequently capable of forming soluble complexes with
metal cations, thereby increasing metal mobility. The incidence of metal-
solubilizing ability among natural soil fungal communities appears to be high;
approximately one-third of tested isolates could solubilize at least one
insoluble metal compound, and approximately one-tenth could solubilize all
three tested compounds.

Siderophores, low-molecular-weight iron-chelating compounds, are the most
common means of iron acquisition by fungi. The most common fungal siderophore
is ferrichrome. Siderophore production can also mobilize other metals from
mineral sources.

## Metal Immobilization

Metal immobilization by fungi may be metabolism independent or metabolism
dependent. Metabolism-dependent processes sequester, precipitate, internalize,
or transform metal species and produce extracellular metabolites. These
processes are significant for plant productivity and [[phytoremediation-mechanisms]].

Mycorrhizal fungi play an important role in metal immobilization. Some
ecotypes such as Glomus claroideum appear adapted to increased metal
concentrations. Amelioration of metal phytotoxicity by mycorrhizas has been
frequently described, with the fungal partner reducing metal bioavailability
and reducing or preventing translocation to the plant.

## Biogenic Precipitation

Fungi can precipitate metals as insoluble compounds. Notable examples include
the biogenic production of lead oxalate dihydrate observed during fungal
transformation of pyromorphite, a stable lead mineral. Such fungal
precipitation mechanisms may have consequences for metal mobility between
environmental compartments.

## Intracellular Detoxification

For copper and cadmium, intracellular detoxification in fungi predominates
over extracellular mechanisms. Phytochelatin molecules play important roles
in metal tolerance. Heterologous expression of phytochelatin synthase genes
has been shown to dramatically enhance metal tolerance, suggesting potential
for genetic approaches to improving bioremediation capacity.

## Significance for Bioremediation

Several fungal processes are directly relevant to environmental bioremediation
of metal-contaminated sites:

- Removal of metals from industrial wastes and by-products
- Metal recovery and recycling from low-grade ores
- Treatment of metal-containing wastewaters using live or dead biomass
- Combined biosorption and biodegradation processes for metal-cyanide
  complexes
- Amelioration of metal phytotoxicity through mycorrhizal partnerships
- Soil remediation through immobilization and precipitation

Despite the potential, much research remains laboratory-based, with fewer
developments to pilot scale and little commercial operation. There are some
examples of large-scale bioremediation where microorganisms, including fungi,
have been used to treat metalloid-contaminated soil.

## Environmental Significance

Solubilization of insoluble toxic metal compounds may have adverse effects if
potentially toxic metal ions are released into soil and water systems.
Metal-citrate complexes are highly mobile and not readily degraded. Some rock
phosphate fertilizers contain cadmium, and their solubilization could release
both phosphate and cadmium ions simultaneously.
