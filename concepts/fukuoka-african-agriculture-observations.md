    11|# Fukuoka African Agriculture Observations
    12|
    13|[[masanobu-fukuoka]] spent forty days in Africa, primarily in Somalia near
    14|Ethiopian refugee camps, where he tested natural farming methods for
    15|desert revegetation. His observations form a sharp critique of both
    16|the causes of African [[desertification]] and the failure of conventional
    17|aid approaches.
    18|
    19|## The Landscape
    20|
    21|Flying over the Somali savanna, Fukuoka saw a splendid panorama of
    22|vast grassland dotted here and there with thorny bushes and the
    23|isolated round huts of nomads. Despite the arid appearance, he was
    24|surprised to find stream and river beds, with at least one live spring
    25|within any field of view. Two great rivers at least a thousand
    26|kilometers long flowed through the desert. The desert sand contained
    27|clay, which meant there was hope for revegetation.
    28|
    29|Fukuoka told the young people of Somalia: "This earth is young, that
    30|it is sleeping. The land must be awakened from its slumber with
    31|natural farming."
    32|
    33|## The Aid System Failure
    34|
    35|Fukuoka found that while food, clothing, and other articles were being
    36|offered to Africa from various quarters, no institution existed for
    37|providing direct agricultural guidance to farmers and peasants. His
    38|plan to bring seeds and seedlings was hindered by the Somali
    39|government, which was promoting cash crop plantations. His intention
    40|to teach self-sufficiency through natural farming was viewed as
    41|rebellion.
    42|
    43|Refugee food consisted entirely of wheat from the U.S., old rice from
    44|Japan, and macaroni from Italy -- no vegetables. The refugees
    45|themselves joked: "America's generosity is aimed at turning us into
    46|bread-eating people. As for Italy, it figures that by giving us free
    47|macaroni for three years, we'll turn into macaroni eaters."
    48|
    49|A typical hut had just one pan, one knife, and one cup as tableware --
    50|often the sum total of a family's possessions.
    51|
    52|## Failed Agricultural Projects
    53|
    54|Several foreign-funded farms had already been built in the region, all
    55|with poor results:
    56|
    57|- A Russian-aided farm with long irrigation canals on great levees
    58|  was abandoned, canals left to crumble
    59|- A U.S.-funded 500-hectare farm had all low bushes and shrubs
    60|  cleared by bulldozers before planting -- and the attempt to plant
    61|  crops was unsuccessful. When visited, it looked like an empty
    62|  abandoned airfield
    63|- A French farm with tomatoes and peppers had green fields, but only
    64|  because it was located on land with large trees, and the actual
    65|  farmers were Somalian. Plants were swarming with mites
    66|- Large farms built by leveling land with bulldozers, building high
    67|  levees, and pumping river water caused salt build-up, making the
    68|  method unsustainable
    69|
    70|Fukuoka's alternative: "plant irrigation" -- creating a green belt of
    71|various tree types whose roots draw in river water underground and
    72|slowly penetrate dry ground away from the riverside. He also proposed
    73|using special plants to draw up and store groundwater for use.
    74|
    75|## Natural Farming Success
    76|
    77|Despite the obstacles, Fukuoka achieved significant results:
    78|
    79|- He distributed seed to children, who planted in sand dunes, watered
    80|  for three days, and returned days later to show sprouting daikon,
    81|  onions, and cucumbers
    82|- Women and old people gathered to receive seed; bags disappeared
    83|  rapidly
    84|- He scattered seed in clay pellets over rock-strewn savanna with
    85|  40-50 people, mixing rice seed with barnyard millet and proso
    86|  millet
    87|- Practically all vegetables germinated
    88|- After his return to Japan, large vegetables had grown, and little
    89|  gardens appeared around huts
    90|- Fruit trees (lemopersimmonmmons, pomegranates) grew especially
    91|  fast, reaching five meters in half a year
    92|- The Somali government eventually recognized the benefits of home
    93|  gardens
    94|- A proposal by the young Japanese volunteer was accepted at the
    95|  United Nations, starting a garden project in Somalia and Ethiopia
    96|  with over a million dollars in funds
    97|
    98|## Causes of Desertification
    99|
   100|Fukuoka identified both internal and external causes of African
   101|desertification:
   102|
   103|**Internal causes:** Nomadic peoples, no longer content with hunting
   104|and gathering, began raising large numbers of goats and cattle,
   105|reducing vegetation variety and triggering rapid disappearance of
   106|plant life. The goats and cattle consumed vegetation faster than it
   107|could regenerate, creating a negative cycle of bare ground, rising
   108|soil temperatures, and further vegetation loss.
   109|
   110|**External causes:** Agricultural modernization abandoned
   111|time-honored methods of self-supporting agriculture that produced
   112|many different grains and vegetables. These were replaced with large
   113|plantations growing cash crops (coffee, cocoa, sugar cane, cotton),
   114|causing rapid desertification of flat plains. Conservation movements
   115|destroyed rules observed since antiquity (some found in the Koran)
   116|and ended free movement of nomadic people, eliminating the rest
   117|periods essential for natural recovery of the land.
   118|
   119|## The Clay in Desert Sand
   120|
   121|The discovery that Somali desert sand contained clay was significant
   122|for Fukuoka's methodology. Clay content meant that seed balls would
   123|work -- the clay pellets could protect seeds and hold moisture long
   124|enough for germination. This finding extended the applicability of
   125|his desert greening techniques beyond California to genuinely arid
   126|African environments.
   127|
   128|## Fukuoka's Verdict
   129|
   130|Fukuoka concluded that people in developed countries should get out
   131|of Africa (other than providing technical aid). True happiness, he
   132|argued, does not lie in the direction advanced nations are heading.
   133|Africa's people possess an excellent spirit of independence and
   134|self-reliance, and know what the ideal society of the future is.
   135|
   136|The first step in building an ideal society, Fukuoka argued, is the
   137|establishment of domestic life through natural farming. As a life of
   138|autonomy and self-sufficiency is built up through natural farming,
   139|revegetation of the deserts will surely follow. However, the only
   140|way to revegetate an entire vast region at once is to scatter seeds
   141|of grasses, trees, and vegetables for greening the deserts from
   142|airplanes.
