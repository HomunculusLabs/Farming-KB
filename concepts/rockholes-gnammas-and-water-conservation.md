## Rockholes, Gnammas, and Water Conservation

In arid and semi-arid landscapes, natural rock basins and artificial water storage systems provide critical water resources for wildlife, livestock, and human settlements. Understanding evaporation dynamics, transmission losses, and conservation techniques is essential for maximising the utility of every litre of water captured.

## Rockholes and Gnammas

Rockholes (also called gnammas in Australian contexts) are natural rock basins that frequently hold water. They arise from several geological processes:

### Origins of Rock Basins

- **Rock scouring (corrosion)**: Stream flow grinding and eroding rock surfaces in stream beds over long periods
- **Freeze-thaw cycles**: Alternating wet-dry swelling and shrinking combined with rock mineral hydration, causing gradual disintegration and basin formation
- **Salt erosion**: Particularly common near coastal areas where salt crystals expand in rock fractures

### Enhancing Rockhole Water Conservation

Several techniques can improve the water-holding capacity and reduce losses from rockholes:

- **Pebble filling**: Fill the hole with pebbles to lower evaporation (some water is lost to the pebble volume)
- **Thatching or covering**: Cover the hole with thatch or other material to reduce direct evaporation
- **Gutter systems**: Build gutters to direct additional runoff into the hole
- **Dew collection fences**: Erect metal mesh fences to direct dew condensation into the hole
- **Deepening and sealing**: Deepen the hole and plaster with cement if it leaks

## Evaporation and Evapotranspiration

### The Evaporation Deficit

In the sub-humid to arid areas relevant to [[acacia-albida]]** and other Acacia species
- **Prosopis spp.** (mesquite)

### Quantifying Evaporation Reduction

- Evaporation from open pans outside crop/tree shelter: up to **16 mm/day**
- Evaporation 300 m into crop: only **5 mm/day**

The crop itself acts as windbreak, with a consequent gain in yield due to reduced evaporation.

### Shading and Screening Strategies

For restricted areas of crop, narrow canals, or home gardens, additional protection includes:

- **Shade cloth**: 50% or more shade rating
- **Vine crops**: Living shade canopy
- **Palm and umbrella Acacia canopies**: Natural overhead shade
- **Ground mulch**: Drastically reduces water loss from open storages and crop root zones

## Sandy River Beds

Where deep sands fill rivers, palms and large trees establish in small embayments off the main channel, where flow damage to trunks is minimised. A practical guide is to observe where native trees survive and where the largest trees grow (assuming cattle have not killed out the range).

Notably, desert river banks in hills often support quite separate species on each bank:
- **Slip-off (silt) slope**: One set of species thriving on deposited silts
- **Erosion bank**: Different species thriving on harder country rock
- Almost all burrowing animals use the silted or slip-off slope

## Barrier Dams in Arid Areas

### Lifespan Differences

- **Wooded, stable hills**: Barrier dams can last 100–300+ years before silt and humus fill the catchment
- **Arid areas**: Dams must be planned from the outset to fill to the brim with silt and rubble in the first few years; water is stored in sands and rubble rather than open water

### Siting Clearwater Dams

Clearwater dams in deserts should be:
- Built well away from turbulent valley sites
- Fed via bled-off channels from wadis or foothill streams
- Supplied through almost-level channels fitted with sand traps
- Protected from fast water running in over loose surface material
- Fenced with margins planted to rushes and shrubs

## Conservation of Water in Transit

### Transmission Losses

More water can be lost in canals (especially in loose soils or shattered rock) than is lost in actual use. Even open concrete channels lose significant quantities to air. There is no real substitute for long runs of pipe.

### Pipe Systems

For small systems, extruded PVC pipe (flexible or rigid) is most effective. Runs of 100 m or more can be made in one length. Long rolls of collapsible pipe are available for surface use, unrolled as needed.

### Flexible Water Storage Bags

An innovative approach for storing opportunistic runoff: tough plastic or neoprene bags placed in swales, operating from fixed-diameter pipe inlets. As water fills the bag, it unrolls along the swale forming a "sausage" shape.

The same technique can capture fresh water flowing into salt lakes — the freshwater sausage floats and remains available as unmixed water on demand. These can be made bulkier with greater depth-to-width ratios since there is no evaporation concern.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Minimise open water surface** — deep and narrow reservoirs over shallow and wide
2. **Protect from wind** — tree windbreaks, shade cloth, vine canopies
3. **Prevent transmission losses** — pipe over canal, sealed systems
4. **Use every storage option** — rockholes, sand dams, flexible bags, soil moisture
5. **Plan for the worst** — arid dams fill with silt; design for that eventuality
6. **Buffer crops from desert** — 400 m tree borders where desert meets cropland
