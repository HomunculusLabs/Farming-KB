# Beekeeping and Honey Production in Permaculture

Bill Mollison's [[permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo]] treats bees as
essential partners in any permaculture system. Honeybees provide
pollination services that dramatically increase yields in [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]], vegetable gardens, and seed crops,
while simultaneously producing honey, wax, propolis, and pollen as
valuable yields. Their presence is an indicator of ecosystem health
and a keystone of [[permaculture-principles]] regarding biological
resources.

## Hive Types and Selection

Mollison discusses several hive designs, evaluating each against
permaculture criteria of accessibility, minimal intervention, and
suitability for local conditions:

- **Langstroth hives:** The standard movable-frame hive, offering
  good honey production and inspection access. Mollison acknowledges
  its efficiency but notes that commercial Langstroth management
  often involves chemical treatments and practices that stress
  colonies.

- **Top-bar hives:** A simpler design using horizontal bars instead
  of frames. Top-bar hives allow bees to build natural comb, reducing
  stress and eliminating the need for foundation wax. They produce
  less honey but require less equipment and are more accessible for
  low-resource beekeepers.

- **Warre hives:** Vertical top-bar hives that mimic a hollow tree.
  Bees build downward through added boxes, and the beekeeper harvests
  from the top. Warre hives prioritize colony health and minimal
  disturbance over maximum honey extraction.

- **Log hives and skeps:** Traditional fixed-comb hives that represent
  the simplest beekeeping approach. While honey harvest requires
  comb destruction, these hives provide maximum colony autonomy and
  are suitable for conservation-oriented apiaries.

Mollison favors hive types that allow natural bee behavior, including
natural cell size, swarm impulses, and minimal chemical intervention.
He views the health of the colony as more important than honey yield
per hive.

## Apiary Placement

Proper apiary siting follows [[permaculture-zone-planning]] principles:

- **Zone 1-2 placement:** Hives near the home ensure regular
  observation and ease of management. The beekeeper passes hives
  daily during routine site activities, enabling early detection of
  problems.

- **Aspect and shelter:** Hives face southeast or east to catch
  morning sun, which warms the colony and triggers early foraging.
  Windbreaks on the north and west sides protect against cold winter
  winds. Dappled shade from trees prevents overheating in summer.

- **Water access:** Bees need a reliable water source within 200
  meters. Ponds, streams, or shallow water dishes with stones for
  landing reduce the energy cost of water collection.

- **Forage proximity:** The closer diverse forage is to the hive, the
  more efficient the colony. Mollison recommends planting bee forage
  species throughout [[permaculture-zone-planning]] Zones 1-4, with the densest
  concentration near the apiary.

## Forage Plants and Floral Resources

Mollison catalogs bee forage plants for temperate and tropical
systems. The goal is continuous bloom across the active season:

- **Early spring:** Crocus, snowdrop, willow, maple, dandelion,
  fruit tree blossoms (cherry, apple, pear, plum).
- **Late spring:** Clover, alfalfa, blackberry, raspberry, thyme,
  rosemary, sage, borage, phacelia.
- **Summer:** Lavender, buckwheat, sunflower, clover, basswood
  (linden), sweet clover, echinacea.
- **Autumn:** Aster, goldenrod, ivy, heather, Japanese knotweed
  (controversial but valuable), autumn-blooming sedum.

Mollison emphasizes that [[mollison-designers-herb-spiral-design-construction]] and
[[mollison-designers-community-garden-design-urban-permaculture]] spaces can contribute
significantly to urban bee forage by including flowering herbs and
perennials.

## Swarm Management

Swarming is the natural reproductive process of honeybee colonies.
Mollison views swarming positively as a sign of colony vitality and
genetic diversity, but recommends management to prevent loss and
capture swarms for colony increase.

Techniques include providing adequate brood space through supering,
making nucs (nucleus colonies) from crowded hives in spring, and
setting bait hives in likely swarm destinations. Captured swarms are
a free source of new colonies and are typically vigorous and
disease-free.

## Honey Harvest and Processing

Mollison describes harvesting methods appropriate to different hive
types. In frame hives, supers are removed when frames are at least
two-thirds capped. Extraction uses a tangential or radial extractor
to spin honey from combs without destroying them. In top-bar and
Warre hives, comb honey is cut from bars and crushed, with honey
strained through cloth.

Post-harvest processing includes settling honey in settling tanks
to allow wax and debris to rise, then bottling. Mollison advocates
minimal filtration to preserve pollen, propolis, and enzymes that
contribute to honey's medicinal qualities.

## Wax Processing

Beeswax is a secondary product of significant value. Mollison
describes rendering wax from cappings and old comb by melting in
water and straining through cloth. Clean wax is cast into blocks for
use in candles, salves, furniture polish, and hive products (foundation,
drip boards). Beeswax production is energetically expensive for bees,
so Mollison recommends returning wax to the hive where possible.
