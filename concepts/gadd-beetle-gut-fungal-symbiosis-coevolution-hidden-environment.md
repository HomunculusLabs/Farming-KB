## Introduction to Insect–Fungal Symbiosis

Over the past century, recognition of endosymbionts in arthropods has become well
established (Buchner, 1965). While bacterial endosymbionts — particularly the
rickettsial Wolbachia — have received intense study for their roles in inducing
sterility and promoting speciation, fungal endosymbionts of insects have received
far less attention. Many early reports of fungal endosymbionts were not substantiated
after initial observations (Buchner, 1965). However, more recent work indicates that
insect–yeast interactions abound in nature, although the exact nature of many
interactions remains poorly understood (Suh & Blackwell, 2005; Vega & Dowd, 2005).

## The Pichia stipitis Clade and Wood-Digesting Beetles

The wood-ingesting beetle *Odontotaenius disjunctus* (Passalidae) consistently
yielded clones of a yeast similar to *Pichia stipitis*, a xylose-fermenting and
-assimilating yeast — rare attributes among Saccharomycetes (Jeffries & Kurtzman,
1994). This yeast is one of the few known to possess a holdfast structure by which
its branched filaments attach to the gut cuticle. Other members of the *P. stipitis*
clade have also been isolated from associations with wood-boring beetles in several
distantly related families. The high correlation between xylose-processing yeasts
and wood-ingesting beetles is significant because xylose subunits form the backbone
of the hemicellulose component of plant [[anaerobic-gut-fungi]] represent novel, potentially coevolved
lineages (Zhang et al., 2003).

The question of whether gut yeasts drive rapid radiation of beetle lineages
(because they perform essential services) or whether beetle lineages diverge when
freed of gut yeasts remains open. It is also possible that only the yeast benefits
from such associations, being bathed in undigested nutrients within the beetle gut.

## Compartmentalization and Parental Care in Passalid Beetles

Comparison of adult and larval hindguts of *O. disjunctus* reveals that the larval
hindgut is much smaller and less differentiated morphologically. The larval hindgut
is sparsely populated by yeasts and bacteria compared with adults. This difference
may be a factor in the evolution of parental care, where adults feed larvae a
mixture of frass and chewed wood (Nardi et al., 2006). Yeasts and bacteria isolated
from gallery walls on which this material is plastered may serve as the initial
microbial source for larvae. Because the hindgut cuticle is shed during moults,
microbes must be replenished between larval moults and after metamorphosis of the
non-feeding pupa to the adult.

## Functional Roles of Gut Fungi

Several functions have been proposed for beetle gut fungi and other gut
microbes: cellulose, hemicellulose, and [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]]; and vitamin synthesis (Martin, 1987;
Suh & Blackwell, 2005). The presence of xylose-fermenting yeasts in wood-boring
beetles supports a direct role in hemicellulose digestion. The symbiosis of
*Symbiotaphrina* with anobiid beetles involves detoxification of food resources
(Dowd, 1989, 1991), and yeast-like endosymbionts (YLSs) contribute to planthopper
nutrition (Noda & Koizumi, 2003). However, care is needed because some studies
have shown that arthropods, including termites and wood-boring beetles, produce
endogenous cellulases (Watanabe et al., 1998; Lee et al., 2004).

## Implications for Forest Ecology and Evolution

The vast, largely untapped diversity of beetle gut fungi has profound
implications for understanding forest ecology and insect evolution. Because almost
all dissected beetles in Cucujoidea and Tenebrionoidea bore yeasts, the enormous
diversity of beetles supports high estimates of undiscovered yeasts. The fungal mutualism between beetles and their gut microbes parallels that seen in termites
and wood roaches, where gut microbes are also restricted to the hindgut and perform
essential digestive functions. A community-level approach will be essential to
understand the full role of gut inhabitants in beetle biology and ecosystem
functioning.
