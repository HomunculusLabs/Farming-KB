# Growing Season and Non-Growing Season Management

The distinction between growing and non-growing seasons is central to
holistic grazing planning. In brittle environments, the growing season may
last only 140 to 180 days, while the non-growing (dormant) season can extend
equally long or longer. How livestock are managed through each period has
profoundly different consequences for plants, soils, animals, and wildlife.

## Growing Season Dynamics

During the growing season, the primary objective is to prevent [[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]]. The
highly mineralized soils in drier areas produce grass that remains an asset
as good forage long into the dormant season or even beyond. Savory has
observed animals living on forage two years old or older in such areas.

The same animals in higher rainfall areas could starve on coarse grass of the
year. In nonbrittle or brittle high rainfall areas where soil is highly
leached, old grass does not tend to cure well and rapidly loses nutritional
value. In such areas, old unused grass is often a liability to animal
performance.

## Managing Multiple Growth Rates

A particularly challenging situation arises when warm and cool season grasses
grow at different rates within the same period. Cool season grasses may be
going into dormancy while warm season grasses are growing fast, or vice
versa. This creates a dilemma: moving faster to avoid overgrazing the fast-
growing plants risks overgrazing the slow-growing ones, and moving slower to
protect slow-growing plants risks overgrazing the fast ones.

With only a few paddocks, the manager must make difficult compromises. With
thirty-five or more paddocks per herd, recovery periods of thirty to ninety
days mean grazing periods of only one to three days, which largely resolves
the dilemma. At ultra-high paddock numbers approaching fifty to one hundred,
[[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] is usually high, and exposure of plants
and animals to one another is very short.

## The Advantage of Many Paddocks

With one hundred paddocks and a large herd, average grazing periods of about
two days through the growing season ensure that overgrazing cannot occur
even at the most severely bitten plant. Animals move so frequently that they
enjoy good nutrition throughout the growing season and suffer little
parasitism or disease. During the non-growing season, one selection per
paddock at such short intervals maintains a reasonable plane of nutrition
without supplementation.
