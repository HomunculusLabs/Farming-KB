These fungi are active under snow cover, where the insulating layer

## Polar and Alpine Fungi

### Arctic and Antarctic Soils

Species diversity of fungi isolated from dry Arctic soils was similar
to that from dry regions of Antarctica. As research activity has
increased at the poles, so has the detection of previously unknown
fungi. Cameron and colleagues (1976) listed from Antarctic soils:

- Two species of phycomycetes
- Two species of Ascomycetes
- 27 species of mitosporic Ascomycetes, of which Chrysosporium,
  Penicillium, and Phialophora represented the majority
- 10 species of yeasts

### Alpine Environments

In alpine regions, psychrophilic and psychrotolerant fungi are
associated with snowfields, glaciers, and cold soils. Their
distribution is coupled to water availability, as it is in warm
desert ecosystems. The occurrence of fungi in these environments is
often seasonal, with peaks of activity during snowmelt and the brief

- Production of antifreeze proteins that inhibit ice crystal growth

## Stress Tolerance Framework

Psychrophilic and psychrotolerant fungi represent one extreme of the
stress-tolerance spectrum. Within the broader framework of fungal
ecological strategies, these fungi are typically S-selected
(stress-tolerant) organisms that exhibit slow increase in biomass,
specialized physiological adaptations, and temporal persistence.
They show low competitive ability in the absence of environmental
stress but may compete among themselves under cold conditions.

In polar and alpine regions, species richness is low, with relatively
few species adapted to the abiotic conditions of these environments.
