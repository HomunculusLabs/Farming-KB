# Psychrotolerant and Psychrophilic Fungi - Ecology and Distribution

Psychrotropic microorganisms are those capable of growth below 10°C.
They are widespread in natural environments as well as in foods, which
is not surprising given that 80% of the biosphere exists at -3 to -7°C
and that 90% of the volume of marine waters is below 5°C. Gounot (1986)
noted that 0°C is not in itself an extreme condition and that cold
environments may be considered extreme only if another factor creates
adverse conditions, such as low water activity in arid Antarctic soil
or low nutrient availability and high pressure in the deep sea.

## Definitions and Categories

### Psychrophilic Fungi

True psychrophilic microorganisms are restricted to permanently cold
habitats, such as oceans, polar areas, alpine soils and lakes, snow and
ice fields, and caves. They have optimum growth temperatures that range
up to 16°C and a maximum growth temperature of 20°C, but they also can
grow at 0°C. These organisms are obligately adapted to cold
environments and cannot grow at moderate temperatures.

### Psychrotolerant Fungi

Psychrotolerant microorganisms have maximum growth temperatures above
20°C, although they can grow at 10°C or lower. These fungi are more
widespread than psychrophiles and are capable of growth across a broad
temperature range. They are commonly encountered in temperate soils
and are the predominant fungi recovered from cold environments that
also experience seasonal warming.

## Genera and Species

Well-known psychrophilic and psychrotolerant fungi are found in genera
such as Alternaria, Cladosporium, Keratinomyces, Leptomitus, Mucor,
Penicillium, Rhizopus, and Typhula (Gounot 1986). In polar regions,
yeasts tend to predominate in undisturbed areas of the dry interior
valleys of Antarctica. Cryptococcus albidus is one of the most
prevalent psychrophilic species in Antarctic soils.

## Community Composition at Low Temperatures

The composition of the fungal community isolated from forest soils
differs dramatically depending on incubation temperature. In a study
of Rhode Island forest soils, the fungal community obtained at 0°C
differed completely from that obtained at 25°C:

- **At 0°C**: The psychrophiles were predominately Mortierella
  species and Mucor species, representing Zygomycetes
- **At 25°C**: The community was dominated by mitosporic Ascomycetes

This complete turnover in community composition with temperature
demonstrates the importance of incubation temperature in surveys of
[[soil-fungal-diversity]] and explains why cold-adapted fungi are
under-represented in standard surveys conducted at room temperature.

Eighteen isolates representing five species of mitosporic Ascomycetes
(Heteroconium chaetospira, Leptodontidium obscurum, Microsphaeropsis
species, Myrioconium species 1 and species 2) and five sterile strains
were isolated from soil and plant litter samples collected from glacier
cones, snow fields, and a pond at a glacier front in Switzerland.
Those taxa were considered psychrophilic.

## Snow Molds

Snow molds are low-temperature-tolerant soil-borne fungi that can
damage and kill grasses, cereals, and other plants. The name is based
on the fact that these fungi can grow at the bases of snow-covered
plants, and often their extensive mycelial mats are located in
association with damaged plants soon after snow melts. Snow molds also
may damage fruits and crops stored at low temperatures.

These fungi are active under snow cover, where the insulating layer
maintains temperatures at or near 0°C while the soil beneath remains
unfrozen. The snow mold syndrome represents one of the most economically
significant impacts of psychrotolerant fungi, causing substantial crop
losses in northern temperate regions.

## Polar and Alpine Fungi

### Arctic and Antarctic Soils

Species diversity of fungi isolated from dry Arctic soils was similar
to that from dry regions of Antarctica. As research activity has
increased at the poles, so has the detection of previously unknown
fungi. Cameron and colleagues (1976) listed from Antarctic soils:

- Two species of phycomycetes
- Two species of Ascomycetes
- 27 species of mitosporic Ascomycetes, of which Chrysosporium,
  Penicillium, and Phialophora represented the majority
- 10 species of yeasts

### Alpine Environments

In alpine regions, psychrophilic and psychrotolerant fungi are
associated with snowfields, glaciers, and cold soils. Their
distribution is coupled to water availability, as it is in warm
desert ecosystems. The occurrence of fungi in these environments is
often seasonal, with peaks of activity during snowmelt and the brief
growing season.

### Ecological Adaptations

Fungi inhabiting cold environments have evolved several physiological
adaptations:
- Production of cold-adapted enzymes with high catalytic efficiency
  at low temperatures
- Modification of membrane lipid composition to maintain fluidity
  at low temperatures
- Accumulation of compatible solutes and cryoprotectant compounds
- Altered cell wall composition to prevent ice crystal damage
- Production of antifreeze proteins that inhibit ice crystal growth

## Stress Tolerance Framework

Psychrophilic and psychrotolerant fungi represent one extreme of the
stress-tolerance spectrum. Within the broader framework of fungal
ecological strategies, these fungi are typically S-selected
(stress-tolerant) organisms that exhibit slow increase in biomass,
specialized physiological adaptations, and temporal persistence.
They show low competitive ability in the absence of environmental
stress but may compete among themselves under cold conditions.

In polar and alpine regions, species richness is low, with relatively
few species adapted to the abiotic conditions of these environments.
Other stressful environments, however, may contain diverse communities
of fungi that are active for short periods, such as soil or dung in
desert ecosystems.
