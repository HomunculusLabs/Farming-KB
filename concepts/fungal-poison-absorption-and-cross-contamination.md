## Fungal Poison Absorption and Cross-Contamination

## Overview

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]]"s *About Mushrooms* (1894) presents a series of observations and personal experiments documenting the alarming capacity of poisonous mushroom alkaloids — particularly those of *[[amanita]]* species — to transfer to otherwise harmless organisms, objects, and substances through multiple routes of exposure. His work underscores that the poisonous principle of noxious fungi is not confined to the mushroom body itself but can contaminate its surroundings through atmospheric exhalation, direct contact, and fluid absorption.

Palmer's work is notable for its breadth: he documented gastrointestinal, dermal, and respiratory routes of exposure, each producing characteristic symptom profiles. His findings challenge the common assumption that mushroom poisoning results solely from the deliberate ingestion of a toxic specimen, revealing instead a more insidious landscape of risk involving proximity, shared storage, and solvent-mediated poisoning.

## Routes of Absorption

### Gastrointestinal Absorption

Palmer theorized that mushroom alkaloids enter the human system primarily by **absorption through the intestinal villi**. This mechanism is relevant not only when a poisonous mushroom is directly ingested but also when an edible mushroom has been compromised by prior contact with a toxic specimen. The absorptive surface area of the intestinal lining makes it highly efficient at taking up dissolved alkaloids, contributing to the rapid onset of symptoms observed in poisoning cases.

The intestinal villi present an enormous surface area — estimated in modern physiology at roughly 250 square meters in the adult human — through which dissolved substances can pass into the bloodstream. Palmer's emphasis on this anatomical feature was prescient, as modern toxicology confirms that α-amanitin is absorbed primarily in the small intestine, with enterohepatic recirculation prolonging its toxic effects on the liver.

### Dermal (Cutaneous) Absorption

One of Palmer's most striking claims is that the poisonous principle of *Amanita* can be absorbed directly through the **skin pores** of the hands. He reported that simply holding an *Amanita* specimen in a closed, warm hand — particularly when the skin is perspiring — is sufficient to produce the full spectrum of poisoning symptoms, including convulsions. This suggests that the active alkaloid ([[amanitine]] or [[lsd-structure-activity-relationships-related-compounds]]) is capable of transdermal penetration under conditions of warmth and moisture, which dilate the pores and facilitate absorption.

Palmer provided a vivid personal anecdote in support of this claim. After a long walk during which he was perspiring, he carried a bunch of *Amanita* specimens in his warm, closed hand while riding in a close carriage. He soon developed **fearful nausea**, which he attributed to dermal absorption of the poisonous principle through his dilated, perspiring skin.

While modern toxicology does not widely recognize transdermal amatoxin absorption as a significant poisoning vector, Palmer's observation raises questions about the conditions under which cutaneous exposure might become clinically relevant — particularly when the skin barrier is compromised by heat, moisture, or abrasion.

### Respiratory / Olfactory Absorption

Palmer noted that **inhaling the odor** of *Amanita* specimens confined in a closed box produced measurable toxic effects: vomiting, a sense of oppression in the chest, and a visible change in the sclera (the whites of the eyes became livid) approximately three hours after exposure. Mushrooms, he argued, "make use of the atmosphere" and their exhalations carry their characteristic properties into the surrounding air, rendering it hazardous to breathe in enclosed spaces.

The three-hour latency between exposure and symptom onset is notable and may reflect the time required for [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]] to accumulate to a threshold concentration, or for metabolites to reach toxic levels after inhalation and systemic distribution. The livid discoloration of the sclera suggests a vascular or hemodynamic effect, possibly related to [[blesching-endocannabinoid-autonomic-nervous-system-adrenal-glands]] disturbance triggered by inhaled toxins.

## Cross-Contamination Between Mushrooms

### Atmospheric Inoculation

Palmer observed that mushrooms can **inoculate each other with or without direct contact**. When edible and noxious species were gathered together in the same closed box for approximately twelve hours, the edible specimens were found to have absorbed the noxious properties of their poisonous companions. This cross-contamination occurred even in the absence of physical touching, implicating shared atmospheric gases or volatile compounds as the vector.

This observation has practical implications for mushroom foraging and storage. Palmer's findings suggest that separating edible and poisonous specimens is insufficient if they share the same enclosed airspace. The recommended practice — keeping toxic and edible collections in entirely separate containers and ideally separate rooms — follows directly from this principle.

### Personal Experience of Cross-Contamination Poisoning

Palmer recounted a vivid personal experience reinforcing this danger: he ate edible mushrooms that had been **stored in proximity to poisonous ones** and within thirty minutes suffered violent perspiration, vertigo, trembling, chills, and nausea. The speed and severity of the reaction underscored that the transferred poisonous principle retained full potency after absorption by the edible fungus.

The thirty-minute onset is particularly alarming, as it is faster than many reported timelines for direct *Amanita* ingestion, where symptoms may take 6–24 hours to appear in classic [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]]. This accelerated timeline supports Palmer's broader conclusion about the [[staycare-surfactant-enhanced-bioavailability-in-fungal-bioremediation]] of transferred poisons.

### Key Conclusion on Absorbed Poisons

A central finding from Palmer's observations is his striking conclusion: **the poisonous principle, when absorbed by a harmless element, acts more quickly** than when the poisonous mushroom is ingested whole. The theory is that the alkaloid, once dissolved into or adsorbed by another substance, is in a more readily bioavailable form, bypassing the slower processes of digestion and extraction that occur when intact fungal tissue must first be broken down by gastric action.

This principle has implications beyond mushroom-to-mushroom transfer. It suggests that any substance which absorbs or dissolves amanitine — including liquids, fabrics, or other foods — may become a more efficient delivery vehicle for the poison than the original fungal tissue.

### Experimental Observations on Transfer Speed

Palmer's experimental framework was relatively simple but compelling in its directness. By comparing symptom onset times across different exposure scenarios — direct ingestion, cross-contaminated ingestion, dermal contact, and olfactory exposure — he assembled a comparative timeline that consistently showed accelerated toxicity when the poisonous principle had been transferred to a secondary medium. The cross-contaminated edible mushrooms produced symptoms within thirty minutes, while chewing a small piece of the poisonous toadstool itself produced throat constriction and vertigo almost immediately but vomiting only after seventeen hours. This differential suggests that the matrix in which the alkaloid is delivered plays a critical role in determining both the speed and severity of toxic effects.

## Solvent Interactions and Remedies

### Vinegar as a Solvent and Poison Amplifier

Palmer described a dangerous phenomenon involving **vinegar (acetic acid)** as a solvent for amanitine:

- When sections of *Amanita* were **soaked in vinegar** and then eaten, the vinegar solution became **more dangerous than the whole toadstool itself**. The acidic medium apparently extracted and concentrated the alkaloid into a freely dissolved form, accelerating its absorption upon ingestion.
- A dog was given vinegar as an antidote after *Amanita* poisoning and suffered **aggravated symptoms**, supporting the idea that vinegar worsens absorption rather than neutralizing the poison.

The chemistry behind this observation is plausible: amatoxins are peptides that are more soluble in aqueous acidic solutions. Vinegar (typically 5% acetic acid) would create an extraction medium that liberates the alkaloid from the fungal matrix, producing a solution in which the toxin is freely dissolved and immediately available for absorption — far more so than when bound within the cellular structure of intact mushroom tissue.

### Vinegar as a Surface Neutralizer (Contradictory Observation)

In a seemingly contradictory report, Palmer noted that one poisoning survivor attributed his recovery to having **put vinegar on the toadstool before eating it**. The proposed mechanism is that vinegar applied to the *surface* of the mushroom may have partially neutralized the alkaloid at the point of contact before ingestion, in contrast to the scenario where vinegar is used to soak extracted sections — which dissolves and mobilizes the poison.

The distinction between surface application (limited penetration, potential partial neutralization) and soaking (deep extraction, full mobilization of toxin into solution) may resolve the apparent contradiction. Surface vinegar treatment might denature or bind a fraction of the alkaloid at the interface, while the majority remains locked within the undigested fungal tissue.

### Sweet Oil as an Effective Remedy

Palmer found that administering **sweet oil** (a mild vegetable oil) to a poisoned dog induced vomiting of the fungal material and led to recovery. The oil likely served as an emetic and may have coated the stomach lining, slowing alkaloid absorption and facilitating expulsion of the ingested material.

This observation aligns with the general principle that lipid-based substances can delay gastric emptying and reduce the rate of alkaloid absorption by the intestinal villi, buying time for natural elimination processes. The emetic effect of the oil would have been the primary therapeutic mechanism, physically removing the poison from the digestive tract.

### Failure of Conventional Antidotes

Palmer warned that remedies intended to **precipitate alkaloids or amalgamate with gastric juices** after ingestion may paradoxically *assist* absorption rather than counteract it. By altering the solubility or distribution of the poison within the digestive tract, such interventions can increase the rate at which the alkaloid reaches the intestinal villi.

This cautionary note is consistent with modern understanding of chemical antidotes: interventions that change the physical state of a toxin without reliably binding or inactivating it may redistribute the poison to more readily absorbed forms, worsening the clinical outcome.

Palmer's remedy observations are especially valuable because they represent some of the earliest documented attempts at systematic evaluation of mushroom poisoning treatments. His use of animal subjects (the dog experiments with vinegar and sweet oil) demonstrates an effort to control variables and observe outcomes objectively, even if the sample sizes were small and the methodology informal by modern standards. The contrast between the worsened outcome with vinegar and the successful recovery with sweet oil provides a clear comparative result that, while preliminary, points toward the importance of physical removal (emesis) over chemical modification of the toxin.

|---|---|---|
| Ingestion of cross-contaminated edible mushrooms | Violent perspiration, vertigo, trembling, chills, nausea | ~30 minutes |
| Dermal contact (perspiring hand, closed fist) | Full poisoning symptoms including convulsions | Not specified (acute) |

## Historical Context and Significance

Palmer's observations, while anecdotal and predating modern toxicological methods, anticipate several principles later confirmed by mycological science:

- **Volatile amatoxin transfer** is now recognized as a risk in [[mushroom-identification]] and handling.
- **Amanitine (α-amanitin)** is understood to be a potent inhibitor of RNA polymerase II, with absorption kinetics influenced by solubility and delivery medium.
- **Cross-contamination risks** in mushroom foraging and storage are acknowledged in modern mycological safety guidelines.

His work serves as an early and dramatic warning about the **multifaceted nature of fungal toxicity** — extending far beyond the simple act of eating a poisonous mushroom to encompass the invisible perils of shared air, shared containers, and shared surfaces.

## Practical Implications for Foragers and Mycologists

Palmer's findings, if taken at face value, carry several actionable implications for anyone handling wild mushrooms:

- **Never store edible and unidentified or known-poisonous mushrooms in the same container.** Even brief co-storage in an enclosed space may allow atmospheric transfer of toxic principles.
- **Avoid prolonged skin contact with any mushroom of unknown toxicity**, particularly when the skin is warm or moist. The use of gloves during handling is advisable.
- **Do not rely on vinegar or acidic solutions as antidotes or neutralizing agents.** Palmer's evidence strongly suggests that vinegar amplifies rather than mitigates amanitine toxicity by extracting the alkaloid into a freely absorbable form.
- **Exercise caution when opening containers of collected specimens.** The volatile compounds exhaled by poisonous mushrooms may produce toxic effects in poorly ventilated spaces.

## Related Concepts

- **Amatoxin poisoning** — The clinical syndrome produced by ingestion of amatoxin-containing mushrooms, characterized by a latent period followed by hepatic and renal failure.
- **Mycotoxicology** — The study of fungal toxins, their mechanisms of action, and their effects on biological systems.
