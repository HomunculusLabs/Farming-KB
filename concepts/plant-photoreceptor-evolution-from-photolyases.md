# Plant Photoreceptor Evolution from Photolyases

## Overview

The photoreceptors that enable plants to sense their light environment did not
arise de novo but evolved from pre-existing enzymes with unrelated functions.
The most striking example is the cryptochrome family of [[phytochrome-red-far-red-light-switch]]/far-red photoreceptors
share deep evolutionary ancestry with bacterial sensor histidine kinases.
These origins reveal how metabolic enzymes were co-opted for sensory roles,
a process that has profoundly shaped photosynthetic organism biology.

## DNA Photolyase Ancestry of Cryptochromes

Cryptochromes are flavoproteins that evolved from class I cyclobutane
pyrimidine dimer (CPD) photolyases. CPD photolyases are ancient enzymes that
use [[fungal-environmental-sensing-signal-transduction-read]]
cascades leading to changes in gene expression throughout the plant.

## Molecular Fossils of Common Ancestry

The evolutionary pathway is reconstructable because cryptochromes retain
significant sequence and [[psilocybin-serotonin-structural-homology-endogenous-familiarity-argument]] with photolyases. Crystal
structures reveal a nearly identical core alpha-beta fold housing the flavin
chromophore. Conserved residues involved in flavin binding are recognisable in
both families, providing molecular fossils of common ancestry. The transition
likely occurred through gene duplication and neofunctionalisation.

## The CRY-DASH Intermediate Forms

In plants, cryptochrome evolution produced a diverse family through successive
gene duplication events. The earliest-diverging plant cryptochromes are the
CRY-DASH (Drosophila, Arabidopsis, Synechocystis, Human) family, named for
their wide taxonomic distribution.

CRY-DASH proteins retain residual DNA repair activity specific to single-
stranded DNA. They may represent an intermediate evolutionary stage between
full photolyase function and dedicated photoreceptor function. Higher plant
cryptochromes (CRY1 and CRY2 in Arabidopsis) have lost all DNA repair activity
and function exclusively as photoreceptors.

## Animal Cryptochromes: Divergent Evolutionary Paths

Animal cryptochromes also evolved from photolyase ancestors through different
trajectories. In Drosophila, cryptochrome (dCRY) functions as a circadian
photoreceptor absorbing blue light and triggering TIMELESS degradation to
reset the circadian oscillator.

In mammals, CRY1 and CRY2 have lost light-absorbing ability and serve as
light-independent transcriptional repressors in the circadian clock feedback
loop. This represents complete neofunctionalisation where the protein retained
photolyase fold but adopted entirely different cellular function.
