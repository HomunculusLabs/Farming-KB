## [[soil-tilth-and-seedbed-preparation|Soil]] Preparation and Plant Spacing

## Overview

Soil preparation and plant spacing are the two foundational physical practices that determine garden success. In *[[gardening-when-it-counts]]*, Steve Solomon devotes extensive attention to these topics because they establish the conditions under which everything else — fertilization, watering, pest management, and harvest — either succeeds or fails. His approach emphasizes working with natural soil processes rather than fighting against them, and providing plants enough space to develop deep, drought-resistant root systems.

## Soil Preparation

### Understanding Your Soil

Before any physical preparation begins, Solomon emphasizes understanding what type of soil you have:

- **Clay soils** (pages 34-37, 48-49, 59, 166): Heavy, sticky when wet, hard when dry. Excellent nutrient-holding capacity but poor drainage and aeration. Prone to crusting (pages 63-64, 122-123, 152, 167) which prevents seed emergence and water infiltration.
- **Sandy soils** (pages 154, 155, 158-159, 166): Free-draining and easy to work but poor at retaining moisture and nutrients. Compost (page 64) helps improve water retention. Asparagus can thrive in sandy soil (page 317).
- **Soil tests** (pages 31, 35, 156-158): [[solomon-soil-profile-assessment-fractional-analysis-jar-test]] tests for clay soils (pages 35, 156-158) help quantify clay content and guide amendment decisions. pH testing determines acidity (page 23) and lime requirements.
- **Gravel in subsoil** (pages 156-157): Can create drainage problems or, conversely, perched water tables that restrict root depth.

### Goals of Soil Preparation

1. **Relieve compaction**: Loosen soil to allow root penetration and water infiltration
2. **Incorporate amendments**: Mix in compost, lime, and fertilizer to the root zone
3. **Create fine seedbeds**: Provide a smooth, friable surface for seed germination and seedling establishment
4. **Improve structure**: Build crumb structure that resists both erosion and compaction

### Methods of Soil Preparation

#### Hand Digging (Pages 34-37, 36, 53-56)

Hand digging is Solomon"s preferred method for most garden situations:

- **Double digging**: Removing soil from one trench and using it to fill the next, while loosening the subsoil below. This creates a deep, well-aerated root zone without mechanical compaction.
- **Single digging**: Inverting the top 8-12 inches with a spade, suitable for already-improved soils.
- **Grass removal** (pages 54-56): Hand digging is the most effective way to remove sod and its associated root systems when converting lawn to garden.
- **Root care** (pages 52-53, 58): Careful digging preserves existing root channels and soil structure.

#### Rototilling (Pages 47, 49-53, 56)

Solomon expresses reservations about rototilling but acknowledges its utility:

- **Advantages**: Fast, efficient for large areas, effectively kills grass and weeds
- **Disadvantages**: Creates a compacted "hardpan" layer at the depth of the tines; destroys soil structure; destroys earthworm channels; pulverizes soil into dust that easily crusts
- **Killing grass** (pages 48, 49-50, 61): Rototilling can effectively kill grass, but regrowth from rhizomes often requires multiple passes
- **Cross-tilling** (pages 51-52): Making multiple passes at different angles can help break up plow pan but doesn't fully eliminate the compaction problem

#### Grass Killing Without Digging

For gardeners who want to avoid both hand digging and rototilling:

- **Sheet composting** (pages 186-189, 190): Layering [[organic-garden-soil-preparation|organic]] materials over grass to smother it while building soil
- **Solarization**: Covering soil with clear plastic during hot weather to kill vegetation and soil-borne pathogens
- **Mulch gardening** (pages 42, 151, 182-183, 265): Heavy mulching over grass to kill it over time

### Compost in Soil Preparation

Compost plays multiple roles in soil preparation:

- **In seedbeds** (pages 65, 68, 89, 122-123): A thin layer of finished compost worked into the top few inches provides nutrients and improves soil structure for seed germination
- **In pre-planting** (pages 186-189): Sheet composting and pre-planting compost applications build long-term [[humus-and-soil-organic-matter]]
- **For soil improvement** (pages 32, 39): Compost improves both clay and sandy soils, though the mechanisms differ (improving drainage in clay, improving water retention in sand)
- **To prevent puddling or crusting** (pages 64, 152): Compost creates stable soil aggregates that resist both surface crusting and erosion
- **Measuring compost** (page 66): Solomon provides guidance on how much compost to apply, emphasizing that more is not always better

### Acidity and Liming

- **Acidity of soils** (page 23): Most vegetables prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 6.0-7.0)
- **Agricultural lime** (page 31): Applied to raise pH in acidic soils, particularly important for clay soils
- **Dolomite** (page 22): A source of both [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]], used when both pH adjustment and magnesium supplementation are needed
- **Sulfur** (pages 17, 18, 21): Applied to lower pH in alkaline soils

### Raised Beds (Pages 57-59)

Raised beds offer advantages for soil preparation:

- **Perimeter boards** (pages 57-58): Contain raised soil and define bed edges
- **Retaining walls** (pages 57-58): For deeper raised beds on slopes
- **Benefits**: Improved drainage, earlier spring warming, reduced soil compaction (since beds are not walked on), easier weed management
- **For large plants** (pages 58-59): Raised beds work well for crops like potatoes (pages 253-258) that benefit from deep, loose soil

### Survival Gardening Soil Preparation (Pages 60-62)

In a survival gardening context, where labor and inputs are minimal:

- **Hills** (pages 61-62): [[making-hills-for-planting]] large-seeded crops like corn and squash in untilled ground, with fertilizer concentrated in the hill
- **Fish as fertilizer** (page 61): Traditional method of burying fish scraps under planting hills
- **Priorities**: Focus effort on the root zone rather than attempting to improve the entire garden area

## Plant Spacing

### The Spacing Philosophy

Solomon's approach to plant spacing is one of the most distinctive aspects of his [[solomon-hard-times-gardening-philosophy-self-sufficiency]]. He argues strongly against the [[intensive-planting-spacing]], close-spacing approach popularized by authors like John Jeavons, advocating instead for wider spacing that allows each plant to develop a deep, extensive root system.

### Intensive vs. Extensive Spacing (Pages 1-2, 56-57, 146, 147, 152)

**Intensive gardening** (the dominant approach in most gardening books):
- Plants spaced very closely together
- Goal: Maximum yield per unit area
- Requires: High soil fertility, frequent watering, careful management
- Result: Shallow root systems, inter-plant competition, high water demand

**Extensive gardening** (Solomon's recommended approach):
- Plants spaced wider apart
- Goal: Maximum yield per unit of labor and water
- Requires: [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]], adequate initial soil preparation
- Result: Deep root systems, less competition, greater [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], lower water needs

### The Spacing Chart (Pages 147-152)

Solomon provides a detailed spacing chart (pages 148-149) and thorough explanation of how to use it:

- **Climate considerations** (pages 147-149): In drier climates, wider spacing is essential. In wetter climates, somewhat closer spacing is possible but still wider than intensive recommendations.
- **Water availability** (pages 150-152): Spacing must be adjusted based on how much water is available for irrigation. Less water requires wider spacing.
- **Amount of produce** (page 128): Closer spacing may produce more total weight per area, but individual plants are smaller and may be of lower quality.
- **Uniform rows** (pages 126-127): Proper spacing at sowing time reduces the need for thinning later.

### How to Get Uniform Spacing

Several techniques help achieve consistent plant spacing:

- **Germ boxes** (pages 91-92): Starting seeds in containers for precise placement
- **Presprouting** (pages 124-127): Handling presprouted seeds allows precise positioning
- **Marking tools**: Using string lines, measuring sticks, or marking grids
- **Thinning** (pages 127-131): Even with careful sowing, thinning is often necessary to achieve proper final spacing

### Spacing for Specific Crops

Different crops have different spacing needs based on their mature size and root systems:

- **Large plants** (pages 74, 130-131, 166): Crops like corn, tomatoes, brassicas, and cucurbits need substantial spacing to develop properly
- **Hills** (pages 59-60): For large plants like corn (page 274), planting in hills with several seeds and thinning to the strongest seedlings
- **Root vegetables**: Spacing affects root size — closer spacing produces smaller roots (pages 292-293 for carrots, page 262-263 for beets)
- **Leafy greens**: Can be spaced somewhat closer as the entire plant is harvested

### Spacing and Weeding

Plant spacing directly affects weeding requirements (pages 74-75, 151):

- **Too close**: Canopy closes early, reducing light reaching weeds, but hand weeding becomes very difficult
- **Optimal**: Plants fill the space by maturity but leave room for hoe cultivation until canopy closes
- **Too wide**: More space for weeds to establish, requiring more cultivation
