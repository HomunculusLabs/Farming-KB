# A.H. Reginald Buller and Experimental Mycology
Arthur Henry Reginald Buller (1874 to 1944) is widely regarded as the
greatest experimental mycologist in history. Over a forty-year career
at the University of Manitoba in Winnipeg, Canada, Buller conducted
pioneering investigations into spore discharge, fungal genetics, and
the life cycles of pathogenic fungi that laid the foundations for modern
mycological science. His seven-volume masterwork, *Researches on Fungi*
(1909 to 1950, the final volume published posthumously), remains one of
the most comprehensive and meticulous bodies of experimental work ever
produced in any field of biology. Buller combined extraordinary
experimental ingenuity with a flair for elegant prose and a personality
that ranged from charming to insufferable.
## Arrival in Winnipeg and Early Experiments
Buller arrived in Winnipeg in 1904, a thirty-year-old PhD from the
University of Birmingham (with additional doctoral work at Leipzig)
who had studied under some of the leading biologists of his era. He
took up a professorship in botany at the University of Manitoba and
would live in a two-room suite at the McLaren Hotel for the next forty
years. His early experiments focused on mushroom spore discharge—a
phenomenon that had been observed but never systematically
investigated.
His approach was characteristically inventive. To measure the
velocity of falling spores, Buller constructed a glass-sided chamber
holding a thin slice of mushroom, positioned a horizontally mounted
microscope to observe the space beneath the gills, calibrated the
field of view with silk threads in the eyepiece, and connected an
electric tapper to an ink pen on a rotating drum. Each falling spore
was registered as a pair of marks on the drum, allowing Buller to
calculate its terminal velocity by dividing the distance between the
threads by the time interval. Using this apparatus, he confirmed that
mushroom basidiospores obey Stokes' law for the behavior of particles
in viscous media.
## The Discovery of Buller's Drop
Perhaps Buller's most celebrated discovery was the fluid drop that
bears his name. In 1910, he observed that a few seconds before a
basidiospore is discharged from its basidium, a small droplet of fluid
appears at the base of the spore—the hilar appendix. This drop
rapidly expands until it contacts the surface of the spore itself,
whereupon the sudden redistribution of fluid causes the spore to be
catapulted from the basidium. The entire discharge event takes less
than a hundredth of a second.
Buller recognized that this surface-tension catapult mechanism was the
fundamental engine of spore release in all gilled mushrooms, boletes,
and their relatives. The discovery resolved the long-standing puzzle
of how mushrooms launch their spores and provided a rare example of
surface-tension forces being harnessed for biological ballistics. The
mechanism is exquisitely sensitive to humidity; in dry conditions, the
drop evaporates before it can achieve contact with the spore, and
discharge fails. This explains why mushrooms are most active spore
releasers during damp mornings and after rain.
## Spore Clouds and Wind Dispersal
Buller went on to study the behavior of spore clouds—the invisible
aerosols of millions of spores that mushrooms release continuously
during their fertile period. Using strong beams of light to visualize
the spore rain, he estimated that a single large mushroom could
release billions of spores over its lifetime. He investigated the
electrostatic charge carried by spores, their trajectories after
leaving the gill surface, and the aerodynamic properties of mushroom
caps in wind. His observation that the mushroom stem elevates the cap
above the boundary layer of still air at ground level, and that the
umbrella shape of the cap creates an aerodynamic profile similar to an
aircraft wing, anticipated modern computational fluid dynamics studies
of spore dispersal by several decades.
## Work on Rust Fungi and Crop Pathogens
Beyond spore mechanics, Buller made fundamental contributions to the
study of rust fungi (Pucciniaceae), which remain among the most
destructive pathogens of cereal crops worldwide. Rusts have
extraordinarily complex life cycles involving up to four distinct spore
types and requiring infection of two different plant species to
complete sexual reproduction. The sexual process, in particular, had
resisted explanation until the 1920s.
Buller hypothesized that insects might transfer sex cells between
compatible rust strains on infected leaves, analogous to insect
pollination of flowers. He advised the plant pathologist John Craigie
to study insects visiting infected leaves, and this insight proved
correct. Craigie's subsequent work confirmed that the rust life cycle
depends on insect-mediated transfer of pycniospores between
compatible infections, and that this sexual process generates the
genetic variation that allows rusts to overcome resistant crop
varieties. This discovery had enormous practical significance for
agriculture, as it explained the relentless emergence of new rust
races capable of defeating previously resistant wheat varieties.
## Contributions to Fungal Genetics
Buller's experiments on ink-cap mushrooms (genus *Coprinus*) were
among the earliest investigations of fungal sexuality and genetics. He
studied the processes of hyphal fusion (somatogamy) and nuclear
migration that precede mushroom formation in compatible mycelia. His
observations of nuclear behavior in living hyphae, made before the
invention of fluorescent DNA staining, demonstrated that nuclei from
two compatible parents could coexist and divide synchronously within
the same cytoplasm—the dikaryotic condition that is the hallmark of
basidiomycete genetics. Contemporary molecular research on mating-type
genes and compatibility systems has built directly upon the foundations
that Buller laid.
## Personality, Eccentricities, and Legacy
Buller was a man of formidable intellect and equally formidable ego.
His list of academic titles and honors grew to absurd length:
"A.H. Reginald Buller, F.R.S.; B.Sc. (Lond.); D.Sc. (Birm.);
Ph.D. (Leip.); LL.D. (Man., Sask., and Calcutta); D.Sc. (Penn.);
F.R.S.C., etc.; Professor of Botany at the University of Manitoba."
He was elected to both the Royal Society of Canada and the Royal
Society of London. His Saturday-morning lectures on cereal diseases
at the Winnipeg farmers' market became a local institution.
But Buller also alienated colleagues with his arrogance and
intellectual vanity. Disputes over credit and university politics
poisoned his relationships at Manitoba, and his retirement in 1936
was marked by petty cruelties—his office was taken, his furniture
dumped in a rainstorm, and his treasured billiard table refused by
the faculty club. Stranded in New York by the outbreak of World War
II while attending a scientific congress, he returned to Winnipeg
and continued writing the *Researches* until his death from a brain
tumor in January 1944.
Despite the personal controversies, Buller's scientific legacy is
indisputable. His experimental methods established mycology as a
rigorous experimental discipline, and his discoveries about spore
discharge, fungal aerodynamics, and rust genetics remain foundational
to the field.
