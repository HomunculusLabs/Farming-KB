### Facultative and Aerobic Ponds

The second stage consists of shallow facultative ponds where algae and
aquatic plants absorb dissolved nutrients. Algal growth oxygenates the water
and provides food for zooplankton and small fish. Water hyacinth (Eichhornia
crassipes) and duckweed (Lemna spp.) are particularly effective nutrient
absorbers and can be harvested as livestock feed or compost material. These
ponds should be designed with a retention time of at least 20 to 30 days to
ensure pathogen die-off through UV exposure and biological competition.

### Fish Production Ponds

After sufficient biological treatment, the water enters fish production ponds
where species such as tilapia, carp, catfish, or perch convert algae,
zooplankton, and detritus into animal protein. Stocking rates depend on
nutrient loading, pond depth, and climate. Mollison recommends polyculture
of complementary species that feed at different trophic levels: surface
feeders, mid-water feeders, and bottom feeders. This maximizes nutrient
utilization and total fish yield. Fish from the final polishing pond,
where pathogen levels are lowest, are safe for human consumption when
properly cooked.

### Irrigation and Reuse

The final effluent from a well-designed sewage aquaculture system is nutrient-
rich water suitable for irrigation of tree crops, pastures, and fuelwood
plantations. Mollison cautions that this water should not be used on root
crops or vegetables eaten raw, but is excellent for [[ingham-chelation-microbial-nutrient-processing]] before any water reaches groundwater or waterways.

## Species Selection for Polyculture

Choosing the right combination of aquatic species is essential for system
performance. Algae and duckweed form the primary productivity base. Herbivorous
fish such as tilapia consume algae and duckweed directly. Omnivorous species
like carp feed on detritus and invertebrates. Predator fish such as perch
control population explosions of smaller species. Freshwater prawns and
snails add diversity and additional yields. Water plants like water chestnut,
lotus, and taro can be grown in the shallower stages, providing human food
while contributing to water treatment.

## Health and Safety Considerations

Pathogen reduction is the critical design constraint. Mollison insists on
multi-stage systems with minimum retention times, adequate depth for UV
penetration, and separation of treatment stages from food production stages.
No food products should be harvested from the primary treatment ponds.
Regular testing of effluent quality is recommended where feasible. Community
education about proper handling and cooking of aquaculture products is
essential. These systems are inappropriate for industrial toxic waste streams
and must be limited to organic domestic and agricultural wastewater.

## Integration with Broader Systems

Sewage aquaculture integrates with many other permaculture elements. The
biogas from anaerobic digestion supplements household energy needs. Aquatic
plants harvested from treatment ponds feed [[permaculture-design]] turning a linear
waste stream into a closed-loop productive system.
## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Sewage Aquaculture Systems requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
