# Gabion Dams and Check Dams

## Overview

Gabion dams and check dams are permeable or semi-permeable barriers used across slopes, valleys, and watercourses to manage water flow, control erosion, and create conditions for soil and water restoration. They are fundamental earthworks in permaculture and dryland restoration, serving purposes that range from silt trapping and flood retardation to water diversion and energy generation. Unlike large storage dams that impound free surface water, check dams and gabion structures are often designed to slow, spread, and infiltrate water rather than store it at the surface.

## Gabion Dams

### Definition and Construction

Gabions are wire mesh cages or "baskets" filled with rocks, stones, or shingle. In dryland and eroding landscapes, these permeable barriers are placed across valleys, gullies, and watercourses to create silt fields and spread water across the landscape. For farm-scale construction, gabion check dams typically range from 0.6 to 1.5 meters (2–5 feet) in height.

The primary purpose of gabion dams is **not** to store free surface water, but rather to create flat areas where silt loads can deposit usefully, forming absorption beds during flood conditions. Over time, these silt deposits build productive soil in areas that were previously eroding.

### Advantages in Drylands

Gabions are particularly effective in arid and semi-arid environments because they:

- **Resist flood forces** far better than solid concrete or earth walls, as water flows through the rock fill rather than pounding against a solid surface
- **Create silt traps** that gradually build fertile soil behind the structure
- **Spread water** across broad areas rather than concentrating destructive flow in narrow channels
- **Are inexpensive** compared to concrete alternatives, using locally available stone and wire mesh
- **Are flexible**, accommodating ground movement without cracking

### Silt Check Dams

Silt check dams can be constructed from earth, concrete, or gabions. Their function is to hold back silt fields, spread water, and reduce the silt load carried by streams. By catching suspended sediments before they reach downstream waterways, silt check dams protect water quality and gradually build productive alluvial soils upstream.

## Types of Check Dams

### Diversion Check Dams

Diversion check dams are positioned on intermittent watercourses to divert flow to ridges, storage systems, or canals aligned on contour. They are critical components of water harvesting systems, capturing ephemeral flows that would otherwise be lost to evaporation or rapid downstream discharge.

### Concrete Flood Check Dams

Concrete flood check dams are designed to allow normal low flows to pass through while retarding flood peaks and preventing rapid flood discharge downstream. They typically feature a base opening that allows silt scour during normal flows, keeping the dam free of siltation. During flood events, the structure slows the water, reducing destructive peak flows and allowing more infiltration upstream.

### Energy Check Dams

Check dams can also be designed to provide hydraulic head for mechanical power generation. When positioned to create a sufficient drop (head), they enable the operation of ram pumps, water wheels, or other water-powered devices. This transforms the energy of flowing water into useful mechanical work for pumping, grinding, or electricity generation.

## The Landscape as Layered Catchment Zones

Mollison conceptualizes the landscape as a series of layered zones when viewed in cross-section through contours:

1. **Ridges and Skyline** — the primary catchment zone where most precipitation falls
2. **Upper Slopes** — water collection and concentration zones
3. **Mid-Slopes** — areas of greatest water use and production
4. **Lower Slopes and Valleys** — storage and revitalization zones

Check dams and gabions are strategically placed within this gradient to intercept, slow, and redistribute water from upper zones to lower zones, maximizing infiltration and biological use at every elevation.

## Building Small Earth Dams

While gabion and check dams are relatively simple structures, the broader topic of dam construction is essential context. Key considerations for building small earth dams include:

### Site Selection

Storage dams should be carefully located with respect to:

- **Soil type** — core out a sample pit to assess clay fractions; at least 50% clay is needed for a waterproof barrier in earth dams up to 3.6 meters (12 feet) high
- **Grade behind the wall** — lower slopes give the greatest storage capacity for the least earth moved
- **Downstream safety** — a key factor, especially for larger dams, to protect structures and houses
- **Height above use points** — gravity flow is always desirable to avoid pumping costs
- **Available catchment or diversion** — the dam must have sufficient water supply

### Construction Standards

- **Slopes to crest** should be concave, with compaction every 20–25 cm using a roller or bulldozer tracks
- **Earth should be neither too dry** (crumbles) **nor too wet** (slumps under the roller)
- **Boulders, logs, grass clumps, and topsoil** must be excluded from the wall body — these create weak points and seepage paths
- **Shrinkage** of well-compacted dams is less than 1%
- **A key (trench)** should be cut into the foundation to prevent shear failure and cut off base seepage, required on all walls 1.8 meters (6 feet) or higher
- **Safe slope ratios** are 3:1 (inner/upstream) and 2:1 to 2.5:1 (outer/downstream)
- **Freeboard** should be 0.9 meters (3 feet) above expected water level
- **Key depth** should be 0.6–0.9 meters (2–3 feet)

### The Keyline Connection

P.A. Yeomans' Keyline system, developed in Australia, provides the foundational framework for comprehensive water management on foothill farms. The system integrates:

- **Water storage** at multiple scales (tanks, dams, soil moisture)
- **Contour-aligned access roads** that double as diversion channels
- **Tree belts** along water lines for soil stabilization and production
- **Soil creation** through rapid humus accumulation in Keyline-plowed soils
- **Low tillage** methods that preserve soil structure and moisture

Yeomans' property "Yobarnie" demonstrated that after 17 years of Keyline development, covering approximately 307 hectares (758 acres), the system provided effective drought-proofing with very low maintenance and operating costs. The Keyline approach was the first comprehensive English-language treatment of total water design for farms, linking together access, tree systems, soil creation, and creative water storage into an integrated whole.

## Dam Efficiency

The efficiency of any dam depends on the **flatness of the area behind the wall**. The key metric is the ratio of dam wall length to the length of water backed up:

- A "V" valley, "U" valley, plateau, or flat field is ideal for maximum efficiency
- If the back-up length exceeds the wall length, the dam is increasingly efficient in terms of energy or earth moved per unit of water obtained
- **Short dams at constricted sites** with flattish valleys behind them are extremely cost-effective
- Careful survey of grade plus dam length provides this data before construction begins

## Ecological Integration

Small dams, when properly integrated into the landscape, become biological assets:

- **Fenced and planted** with 30–60 meters of forest and fruit tree surround, they provide biologically clean water
- **Topsoil returned to edges** and planted with appropriate species reduces mud and eventually clears the water
- **Crests can be gravelled** and used as roads to cross valleys or bogs
- **Special features** — deep areas, islands, peninsulas, and shelves — provide habitat for birds, aquatic plants, and can support fire-immune housing
- **Fish stocked** in dams control snails and parasites
- **Cattle and sheep** should be watered at troughs, not directly at the dam, for parasite control and water cleanliness

## Practical Applications

In eroding gullies, strong wire gabion baskets containing stones or shingle are the preferred stabilization method. They resist flood forces while allowing water and fine sediments to pass through. Over time, the captured silt builds new soil, and the stabilized gully can be planted with trees and other vegetation, transforming an erosion problem into a productive landscape feature.

The combination of gabion check dams, silt traps, diversion structures, and carefully planned earth storage represents a comprehensive approach to watershed management that works with natural processes rather than against them.
