# Mycorrhizal Nutrient Transport

The physical movement of water, minerals, and carbon between mycorrhizal fungi and their host plants is the engine of the symbiosis. Understanding how nutrients cross the fungal-plant interface explains why mycorrhizal plants outperform non-mycorrhizal ones by orders of magnitude in nutrient acquisition. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the broader relationship and [[mycorrhizal-types]] for how different fungal types associate with roots.

## The Interfacial Apoplast

When arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi colonize a root cell, the plant's plasmalemma (cell membrane) envelops the invading hyphae, creating a narrow space called the interfacial apoplast. This interface is more acidic than the cytoplasm of either organism because it accumulates hydrogen ions (H+). The pH gradient and ion concentration differences drive nutrient exchange. The plant membrane controls what enters — the fungus cannot force nutrients in. Transport occurs via high-affinity transporter proteins embedded in both the fungal and plant membranes.

## Transporter Proteins

Both organisms invest significant energy in producing embedded membrane proteins:

- **Phosphate transporters** — attract and move phosphate (PO4³⁻) from the fungus into the plant cell
- **Ammonium transporters** — move NH4+ across membranes
- **Nitrate transporters** — carry NO3− into the fungal and plant cells
- **H+-ATPases** — synthesize ATP to power active transport against concentration gradients
- **Aquaporins** — facilitate water movement across membranes
- **Chitinases and hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins** — support the structural interface

These carriers have been identified on both sides of the symbiosis, proving that nutrient transfer is a controlled, active process rather than passive diffusion. See [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] for how these transport processes fit into the broader soil ecosystem.

## Extracellular Digestion and Uptake

Fungal digestion is always extracellular — enzymes are produced inside hyphal cells and exported outside through exocytosis at the hyphal tip. The Spitzenkörper (a vesicle-rich organelle at the growing tip) assembles and releases isozymes that break down lignin, cellulose, and organic matter into simple sugars and amino acids small enough for absorption (under ~6000 Daltons).

Two biological processes move nutrients across cell membranes:
- **Passive transport** — small molecules (water, O2, CO2, simple sugars) diffuse from high to low concentration
- **Active transport** — larger molecules are pumped against the concentration gradient using ATP energy via specialized transport proteins

Molecules too large for passive diffusion require transport proteins embedded in the fungal plasma membrane, which control entry and exit much like transporters in animal and plant cells. See [[fungal-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]] for how decomposition feeds this uptake chain.

## Phosphorus: The Critical Nutrient

Phosphorus is the nutrient most limited by mycorrhizal absence. Key facts from Lowenfels:

- 75-95% of applied phosphorus fertilizer is never taken up by plants
- Phosphate anions bind to soil cations (iron, aluminum, calcium) and become immobilized
- Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi provide up to **4× more phosphorus** than plants can access alone
- Fungal hyphae extend beyond the root's depletion zone into fresh soil
- Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria in the mycorrhizosphere assist by breaking chemical bonds

Phosphorus reserves are nonrenewable — expected depletion in 50-100 years. Runoff from excess phosphorus application kills aquatic life. Mycorrhizal fungi offer the best solution: they access bound phosphorus that roots cannot reach. See [[phosphorus-availability]] for soil chemistry details and [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] for application in farming.

## Nitrogen Uptake

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi access nitrogen in multiple forms:

- **Ammonium (NH4+)** — the primary form; relatively immobile in soil, depleted near roots
- **Nitrates (NO3−)** — more mobile but still enhanced by fungal networks
- **Amino acids** — directly absorbed and transported within fungal cytosol

Inside the fungal membrane, nitrogen is assimilated into amino acids, circulated in the cytosol, and some is converted back to ammonium for transport into the apoplastic interface. Mycorrhizal establishment also increases nitrogen-fixing nodule activity in legumes because the extra phosphorus supplied by fungi fuels the nitrogen fixation process. See [[mycorrhizal-crop-studies]] for crop-specific data.

## Enhanced Micronutrient Access

Beyond N-P-K, mycorrhizal fungi enhance uptake of sulfur, copper, zinc, boron, iron, magnesium, and manganese. These nutrient metals are relatively immobile in soil and must bind to clay and organic matter before root-only absorption. Fungal hyphae extend far beyond the root zone, sometimes increasing ion absorption by up to **60×**. Given the cost of fertilizers, fungal nutrient transport is a clear economic alternative.

## Carbon Flow to Fungi

Plants manufacture carbon through photosynthesis and convert it to hexose sugars for their fungal partners. The fungus uses this carbon to produce transport proteins, extend hyphae, and fuel extracellular digestion. Plants can supply up to **80% of their photosynthate** to mycorrhizal networks. See [[mycorrhizal-carbon-nitrogen-economics]] for the full carbon budget and [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]] for how fungal carbon compounds build soil structure.

## Practical Implications

- **High-phosphorus fertilizers suppress mycorrhizal colonization** — the plant stops investing in the symbiosis when phosphorus is abundant
- **Tillage destroys hyphal networks**, severing nutrient transport highways
- **Fungicides kill mycorrhizal fungi** along with pathogens, eliminating nutrient benefits
- **Cover crops and no-till practices** maintain intact mycelial networks between growing seasons
