# Harbored Anger, Guilt, and Martyrdom

## Overview

Within the mind-body medicine framework, constricting emotions are not
themselves harmful to health provided they are expressed and released
appropriately. However, three emotions — guilt, martyrdom, and harbored
(righteous, fanatical) anger — tend to have only negative health consequences.
While these patterns may appear stubborn to eradicate, they can be seen for
what they are, their ill-effects can be owned, they can be forgiven, and they
can be healed.

## Guilt

### The Viral Nature of Guilt

Guilt is often thought of as a feeling, but actually it functions more like a
virus that infects our capacity to think and feel. When guilt infects anger,
it turns a normal human emotion into something we think we should not have. As
such, it forces us to live a lie, hidden in the deep recesses of the mind
where repressed anger boils, injecting toxic stress into body and mind.

Without guilt, anger can flow freely, powerfully, and without hurting anyone.

### Physiological Impact of Guilt

A study conducted at the University of California suggests that when we engage
in negative self-talk or self-blame, we increase not just shame and guilt but
also:
- The number of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the body
- Tumor necrosis factor–alpha (TNF-α) receptor levels
- Systemic inflammation markers

This creates a direct pathway from emotional self-punishment to physical
inflammation and disease.

### Guilt in the Grieving Process

When guilt shows up in the grieving process, it can keep grief frozen and
stuck in place. Once guilt is directed to give way to an underlying anger
(for example), the experience of grief often shifts and puts us in touch with
the love we feel for the missing person.

### Guilt as Unhealthy Motivation

Guilt is an unhealthy way to motivate behavior. Many people use guilt to
blame, seek revenge, or punish. More specifically, guilt operates as a
construct that seeks purification through punishment (of self or other).

### Preventing Guilt

A person guided by ideals (healthy motivations) and principles (specific
behaviors that steer toward ideals), with the character to consistently apply
those principles to inform all behavior, has no need for guilt.

## Martyrdom

### The Nature of Martyrdom

At its extreme, martyrdom can involve torture and horrific death. It is a form
of fanatical anger that is simultaneously arrogant, self-important, and
destructive to anyone nearby.

Martyrdom is a construct that emerges from the toxic emotional brew of:
- Feeling unappreciated
- Feeling as if one is treated wrongly
- Feeling misunderstood by the whole world and everyone in it

Martyrdom describes people who use self-sacrifice and self-inflicted suffering
as a front to punish and destroy others. This ranges from extreme cases (suicide
bombers) to everyday family dynamics where a member consistently suffers "for"
another and considers it self-sacrifice.

### Preventing and Ending Martyrdom

It is impossible to feel martyrdom when we are in love, feel grateful, and/or
provide a gentle, caring intimacy to ourselves or another. No martyr has ever
been accused of having had too much fun, love, or enchantment in their life.

Additional strategies:
- Communicate when giving too much or being given too much to do
- Set positive boundaries to stay within limits and capabilities
- Practice gratitude for what is received rather than tracking sacrifice
- Recognize that self-care is not selfish

## Harbored (Righteous) Anger

### The Recycling Loop

Harbored anger is anger that is recycled as soon as it has been expressed, and
as such is never released. It continues to impact the body and mind negatively
through a never-ending, self-feeding loop of the same emotional reaction to a
specific trigger. This pattern is often referred to as "ruminating."

### Underlying Belief Structures

Fanatical anger persists because of underlying beliefs that need to be released
and changed. Common examples:
- "Without this anger I am weak"
- "Without this anger I lose my friends, family, or congregation"
- "My anger is justified and therefore necessary"

## Releasing the Three Patterns

These three destructive patterns are all mental constructs that create very
unhealthy anger. Guilt creates repression, martyrdom creates destruction, and
righteous anger makes anger a constant presence. The key insight is that all
three are in the domain of conscious intervention. Each is based on a more or
less conscious choice and as such can be changed or undone by another choice.

### Forgiveness as a Release Tool

When we do not let ourselves take the easy way (of choice) because we hold a
judgment, we can employ forgiveness. As we forgive guilt, martyrdom, and
harbored anger, we significantly strengthen our capacity to heal.

**Technique — Forgiving judgments on feeling angry, guilty, or martyred:**
1. Focus on where in the body the trouble concentrates or collects
2. Ask yourself: "What am I judging here?"
3. See what comes up and release it by forgiving yourself
4. Reframe: "I am bad because I am angry" becomes "It is okay to be angry"
5. "I forgive myself for judging my anger"

### Written Anger Release

On a piece of paper, write out your anger. Get descriptive and detailed. Use
as many senses as possible. The idea is not to show anyone but to get the
anger out of your system. When you feel done, safely destroy the paper. This
gives your subconscious the message that this is how you want to deal with
anger: honestly, in detail, and definitively.
