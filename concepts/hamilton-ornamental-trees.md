# Hamilton: Ornamental Trees — Selection, Planting, and Care

**Source:** Geoff Hamilton, *Organic Gardening* (Nick Hamilton edition)

**Updated:** 2026-04-21

Ornamental trees serve multiple purposes in the organic garden. They provide shade, extending the range of plants that can be grown beneath them. Along with [[hamilton-hedges-and-windbreaks]] and [[hamilton-organic-lawn-care]], they form the framework of the garden and can be planted as windbreaks. Most importantly for the organic gardener, they attract wildlife—birds use them for perching, nesting, and food (especially berrying and fruiting trees), and trees with deeply furrowed or flaking bark (such as birches, Betula sp.) provide homes for insects that in turn feed birds. Flowering trees attract pollinating insects, essential for fruit production.

[[geoff-hamilton-organic-techniques]] emphasised that every tree in the organic garden should earn its place by providing multiple benefits: beauty, wildlife habitat, shade, wind protection, or food.

## Choosing Trees

The first consideration should be ultimate height and spread. Large trees compete with nearby plants for water and nutrients and throw the garden into deep shade. Many gardeners regret planting weeping willow (Salix alba "Tritis"), which reaches 40ft (12m). Small alternatives exist: Kilmarnock willow (Salix caprea "Kilmarnock") makes an attractive weeping tree reaching only 10ft (3m).

### Wildlife Value

- **Berrying/fruiting trees**: Cotoneaster (Cotoneaster waterii), mountain ash (Sorbus sp.), flowering crab apple (Malus sp.)

- **Insect habitat** (furrowed/flaking bark): Birches (Betula sp.)

- **Pollinator attractors**: Flowering crab apple Malus "Golden Hornet" — bred for maximum flower with ample pollen, pollinates most apple varieties.

### Spacing

Do not plant vigorous trees less than 40ft (12m) from the house. Roots of willows (Salix sp.) and poplars (Populus sp.) will seek out cracked drains and can cause soil shrinkage on clay soils, leading to foundation cracking.

Consider how the tree's canopy will affect the [[hamilton-garden-layout-beds-borders-paths]] as it matures. A tree planted too close to a border may eventually cast such dense shade that only [[hamilton-shade-garden-plants]] can survive beneath it.

## Buying Trees

- **Container-grown**: Smaller and more expensive, but can be planted at any time of year.

- **Bare root**: Larger and cheaper, but can only be planted during the dormant season.

## Planting Container-Grown Trees

1. Dig a hole large enough to accommodate the root ball. Water the tree while still in its container to help the root ball slide out.

2. Place the tree in the hole, leaving roots undisturbed unless they have begun circling. Check depth by laying a spade across the hole.

3. If soil is short of organic matter, add compost. Since compost contains few nutrients, supplement with two handfuls of [[mollison-aquaculture-harvest-and-fish-nutrition]].

4. Mix soil and compost around the hole edge. Cover the root ball, firming gently with the ball of your foot. Do not use spent mushroom compost (contains lime, harmful to lime-haters like Amelanchier lamarckii).

5. Build a small soil retaining wall around the tree for thorough watering.

6. Stake with two 2x2in stakes driven 18in (45cm) into the soil walls, one-third of the way up the trunk, with a crossbar nailed between them. This allows trunk movement in wind, which thickens the base and improves the root system.

7. Tie trunk to crossbar with a special tree tie (with collar to prevent chafing). Never use wire or nylon twine.

8. Water thoroughly and check regularly for drying out. After 4-6 weeks, level the soil wall and mulch thickly with well-rotted manure or compost.

## Planting Bare Root Trees

Plant only in the dormant season, never in freezing or waterlogged conditions. If trees arrive when ground is frozen, keep unpacked in a frost-free building or "heel in" in a V-shaped slit trench.

1. Keep roots covered with burlap until planting area is prepared.

2. Plant at the level of the old soil mark on the stem. The stake should be at least twice as thick as the stem and come one-third of the way up.

3. Drive stake 18in (45cm) into soil.

4. Cover dug-out soil with a bucketful of organic matter and two handfuls of bone meal.

5. Place tree in hole, put fine soil over roots, jerk stem up and down to settle soil around roots.

6. Refill half the hole, pack with the ball of your foot (firm but not over-compressed). Fill completely and pack again.

7. Tie stem to stake with tree tie or thick plastic in a figure eight, forming a collar.

8. Mulch immediately with compost or manure.

## Maintenance

### Feeding

Trees in borders are fed with the rest of the plants: two handfuls of [[mollison-aquaculture-harvest-and-fish-nutrition]] every spring, plus a layer of manure, compost, or alternative organic matter every fall (avoiding spent mushroom compost). Trees in grass should be fed the same way but slightly earlier—midwinter—so fertilizer washes down before grass starts growing and competing for nutrients.

For trees showing signs of [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]], such as yellowing leaves or poor growth, a spring application of [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]] around the root zone can restore health.

### Pruning

Little pruning needed in early years: remove dead, diseased, crossing, or overcrowded branches during dormancy. Remove branches growing toward the center or across other branches promptly to avoid removing large branches later, which spoils tree shape.

### Tree Ties

Check at least every fall. Ties may become too loose for winter storms or too tight, restricting growth. Remove and replace as necessary.

## Notable Deciduous Trees

- **Willow-leaved pear** (Pyrus salicifolia "Pendula"): Gray-green willow-like leaves, max 15ft (4.5m), good for small gardens.

- **Wild cherry** (Prunus avium "Plena"): Double white flowers, plum-colored fall fruits, red autumn color. Up to 40ft.

- **Mountain ash** (Sorbus "Joseph Rock"): Cream flowers, amber-yellow fall fruits, fiery red autumn color. Up to 18ft.

- **Autumn cherry** (Prunus subhirtella "Autumnalis"): White flowers intermittently from fall to spring. Up to 30ft.

- **Laburnum** (Laburnum vossii): Profuse bright-yellow flowers, all parts poisonous. Up to 18ft.

- **Gum tree** (Eucalyptus gunnii): Round leaves when young, evergreen (constantly shedding). Sheltered position. Up to 50ft.

- **Birch** (Betula platyphylla): White bark, insect habitat. Good for small gardens.

## Trees for Specific Situations

### Small Gardens

Choose trees with a mature height under 20ft. Good options include Japanese maples (Acer palmatum), crab apples (Malus sp.), and Amelanchier lamarckii (serviceberry), which offers spring flowers, summer fruit, and autumn colour.

### Shade Trees

A well-placed shade tree transforms a sunny garden into one with multiple microclimates, enabling the cultivation of [[hamilton-shade-garden-plants]] beneath its canopy. Deciduous trees are preferable to evergreens for shade, as they allow winter light to reach the garden when it is most needed.

### Wildlife Trees

Native trees support the greatest diversity of insect species, which in turn support birds and other wildlife. Oak, willow, birch, and hawthorn are among the most valuable native species for wildlife in the British garden context.
