# Hamilton: Ornamental Trees — Selection, Planting, and Care

**Source:** Geoff Hamilton, *[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]]* (Nick Hamilton edition)

**Updated:** 2026-04-21

Ornamental trees serve multiple purposes in the organic garden. They provide shade, extending the range of plants that can be grown beneath them. Along with [[hamilton-organic-lawn-care]], they form the framework of the garden and can be planted as windbreaks. Most importantly for the organic gardener, they attract wildlife—birds use them for perching, nesting, and food (especially berrying and fruiting trees), and trees with deeply furrowed or flaking bark (such as birches, Betula sp.) provide homes for insects that in turn feed birds. Flowering trees attract pollinating insects, essential for fruit production.

## Planting Bare Root Trees

Plant only in the dormant season, never in freezing or waterlogged conditions. If trees arrive when ground is frozen, keep unpacked in a frost-free building or "heel in" in a V-shaped slit trench.

1. Keep roots covered with burlap until planting area is prepared.

2. Plant at the level of the old soil mark on the stem. The stake should be at least twice as thick as the stem and come one-third of the way up.

3. Drive stake 18in (45cm) into soil.

4. Cover dug-out soil with a bucketful of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] and two handfuls of bone meal.

5. Place tree in hole, put fine soil over roots, jerk stem up and down to settle soil around roots.

6. Refill half the hole, pack with the ball of your foot (firm but not over-compressed). Fill completely and pack again.

7. Tie stem to stake with tree tie or thick plastic in a figure eight, forming a collar.

8. Mulch immediately with compost or manure.

## Maintenance

### Feeding

Trees in borders are fed with the rest of the plants: two handfuls of [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]], such as yellowing leaves or poor growth, a spring application of [[hamilton-shade-garden-plants]] beneath its canopy. Deciduous trees are preferable to evergreens for shade, as they allow winter light to reach the garden when it is most needed.

### Wildlife Trees

Native trees support the greatest diversity of insect species, which in turn support birds and other wildlife. Oak, willow, birch, and hawthorn are among the most valuable native species for wildlife in the British garden context.
