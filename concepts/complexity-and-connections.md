# Complexity and Connections

In [[permaculture-design-course-methodology]], complexity refers not merely to the number of elements in a system but to the number and quality of functional connections between those elements. Understanding complexity and connections is fundamental to designing productive, self-maintaining systems.

## The Nature of Complexity

The number of elements in an aggregate or system certainly affects its potential complexity, if complexity is taken to be the number of functional connections between elements. As Waddington (1977) points out, in the case of a single interaction (a conversation) between elements, complexity goes up roughly as the square of the number of elements: "Two's company, three's a crowd... and five or six is getting to be a shambles!"

This is bad enough, but if we consider the number of possible connections to and from an element such as a chicken, we can see that these potential connections depend on the information we have about the chicken, so that the complexity of a system depends on the information we have about its components, always providing that such information is used in design.

As we cannot know everything, or even know more than the approximate categories and quantity of things which are eaten by chickens, in permaculture we always suppose that the chicken is busy making connections itself, about which we could not know and for which we could not design. We must simply trust the chicken.

## Designing for Essential Connections

Thus, in commonsense, we can design for what we believe to be essentials, and let the chicken attend to all the details, checking at later stages to see that yields (our ultimate products) are satisfactory, the chickens healthy and happy, and the system holding up fairly well.

It is important to concentrate on the nature or value of connections between elements. In nature, we can rarely connect components as easily as a wire or piece of pipe can be fixed into place. We do not "connect" the legume to the orange tree, the chicken to the seed, or the hedgerow to the wind; we have to understand how they function, and then place them where we trust they will work. They then proceed to do additional tasks and to provide other connections themselves. They do not confine their functions to our design concepts!

## Benign vs Destructive Complexity

It is not enough to merely specify the number of connections, and not note their value in the system as a whole. It may be possible that complex social situations and cultivated or chance complexity may occur in natural systems by introductions or migrations. These new events, although increasing complexity, may reduce stability with respect to a desirable local yield.

Where the benign complexity of cooperative organisms is useful, competitive or inharmonious complexity is potentially destructive. Again, it is a question of matching needs with products, and of the values given to connections.

## Order vs Chaos

Order and disorder arise not from some remote and abstract energy theory but from actual ground conditions or contexts, both in natural and designed systems. Entropy is the result of the framework, not the complexity. A jumble of diverse elements is disordered. An element running wild or in an active destructive mode is disordered, and too few or too many forced connections lead to disorder.

Order is found in things working beneficially together. It is not the forced condition of neatness, tidiness, and straightness -- all of which are, in design or energy terms, disordered. True order may lie in apparent confusion; it is the acid test of entropic order to test the system for yield. If it consumes energy beyond product, it is in disorder. If it produces energy to or beyond consumption, it is ordered.

Thus the seemingly-wild and naturally-functioning garden of a New Guinea villager is beautifully ordered and in harmony, while the clipped lawns and pruned roses of the pseudo-aristocrat are nature in wild disarray.

## Key Principle

Order and harmony produce energy for other uses. Disorder consumes energy to no useful end. Neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness signify an energy-maintained disorder in natural systems.

## Diversity vs Complexity

Diversity is the number of different components or constructs in the system -- an enumeration of elements and of parts. It has no relationship to connections between components, and little to the function or the self-regulating capacity of any real system. Diversity either of components or assemblies does not of itself guarantee either stability or yield.

The diversity of beneficial functional connections certainly is a goal. Complex interconnections between species is what we are really seeking in food systems. Such complexity has its own stability, especially if derived from natural systems.

## The Analytic Method: Component Analysis

In permaculture design, we analyze each component for its needs (inputs), products (outputs), and intrinsic behaviours. By listing the needs of one element and the products of another, we can place them so that one supplies the other. This matching of needs and products creates functional connections.

For example, a chicken needs:
- Food (insects, seeds, kitchen scraps)
- Water
- Shelter
- Dust for bathing
- Grit for digestion

And produces:
- Eggs
- Manure
- Feather mulch
- Carbon dioxide
- Heat
- Pest control (by eating insects)

Each product can be matched to the need of another element, creating a web of beneficial connections that reduces external inputs and waste.

## Guilds as Connection Networks

A guild is a harmonious assembly of species clustered around a central element. This assembly acts in relation to the element to assist its health, aid our work in management, or buffer adverse environmental effects. Guilds are the practical expression of complexity and connections in permaculture design.

In designing guilds, we try to maximize the benefits of each species to the others. Species are placed in association to:
- Reduce root competition from invasive grasses
- Assist pest control through anti-feedants and predator hosting
- Create open soil surface conditions or provide mulch
- Provide free nutrients through nitrogen fixation
- Provide physical shelter from frost, sunburn, or wind
