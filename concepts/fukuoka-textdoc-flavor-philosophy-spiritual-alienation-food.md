## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka presents a radical philosophy of flavor that challenges
the entire foundation of modern food culture. He argues that man has
become spiritually alienated from food, that the human sense of taste
has gone haywire, and that the pursuit of deliciousness has paradoxically
made truly delicious food increasingly rare. This page explores his
arguments about flavor, instinct, and the relationship between spirit
and sustenance.

## The Illusion of Tasty Rice

Fukuoka begins his flavor critique with the Japanese campaign for "tasty
rice" that emerged during the postwar economic boom. Consumers demanded
better-tasting varieties, politicians and traders added their voices, and
agricultural technicians were ordered to stop growing standard rice and
switch to palatable varieties. Fukuoka argues this entire movement is
built on a fantasy. The difference in taste between varieties is very
small, and depending on growing conditions, weather, and processing,
poor-tasting varieties may approach tasty ones in flavor while tasty rice
can become less palatable than poor-tasting rice.

## The Thousand-to-One Odds of Delicious Rice

Fukuoka calculates that, considering just a few production conditions,
the chances that a tasty variety of rice will be grown, properly
processed, and cooked skillfully to give rice of outstanding taste is
not more than one in a thousand. Even with the best of luck, someone may
encounter truly tasty rice perhaps once in every two or three years. If
that person is not very hungry at the time, all will have been for
naught. The taste can be destroyed or retained depending on how the rice
dealer processes the grain, and even cooking methods at home can
override the inherent quality of the variety.

## Flavor Cannot Be Analyzed Scientifically

Scientists attempt to understand flavor by analyzing food ingredients
and investigating correlations between minerals and the five tastes:
sweet, sour, bitter, salty, and hot. But Fukuoka argues that flavor
cannot be understood by relying on chemical analysis or sensations at
the tip of the tongue. Even were the five tastes perceived by five
different organs, a person would be unable to sense true flavor if his
instincts themselves were confused. A computer programmed to think that
sweet is delicious is not likely to conclude that sour is delicious.
Instinct does not investigate instinct; wisdom does not turn back and
scrutinize itself.

## The Root Cause: Spiritual Alienation

The fundamental problem, as Fukuoka sees it, is that man has become
spiritually alienated from food. True flavor can be perceived only with
the five senses, the mind, and the spirit. Flavor must be in consonance
with the spirit. People who think flavor originates in the food itself
eat only with the tip of the tongue and are easily deceived by the
flavor of instant cooking. Evidence that the human sense of flavor has
gone haywire is that people chase after flavor because they have lost
it. If this sense were intact, they would judge accurately for
themselves.

## The Polished Rice Metaphor

Fukuoka uses polished rice as a metaphor for modern alienation. An
adult who has lost his instinctive sense of taste no longer appreciates
the taste of rice. He eats white rice prepared by polishing brown rice
to remove the bran, then adds meat sauce or sashimi to make up for the
lost flavor. Tasty rice thus becomes rice that is easy to flavor and
season. People delude themselves into thinking of white rice, stripped
of the aroma and taste peculiar to rice, as high-grade rice. Refinements
in flavoring with petroleum-derived protein have created people unaware
that their entire diet has been converted into an artificial diet.

## Culture, Nature, and True Cooking

Fukuoka argues that true culture arises from within nature and is pure,
modest, and simple. A culture born of human recreation and vanity that
is divorced from nature cannot become a true culture. He cites the
Japanese aristocratic game of bunko, in which players guessed the
fragrances of burned incense. When the nose could no longer distinguish
aromas, the player bit into a daikon root to restore the sense of smell.
This shows that taste and aroma are exuded by nature, not manufactured
by human artifice. Zen and food are one. The kitchen knife, like a
sword, can lead to the way of Zen or do evil, depending on who wields it.

## Eating with the Head Versus Eating with the Body

Fukuoka draws a sharp distinction between natural man, who eats with
his body, and modern man, who eats with his head. Man originally ate
because he was alive, because something was delicious, but modern man
eats to live and thinks that if he does not prepare and dine on choice
cuisine, he will not be able to eat delicious food. When people eat
bread, it is not the flavor of the bread they enjoy but the flavor of
the seasonings added to the bread. People nowadays seem to live by
feeding on a mist of notions. In our efforts to make bread tastier,
bread has ceased to taste good.

## The Solution: Stop Trying to Create Delicious Food

Fukuoka's prescription is counterintuitive: all we have to do is stop
trying to create delicious food and we will be surrounded by it. True
cooking and the pursuit of true flavor are to be found in a
comprehension of the subtle and exquisite flavors of nature. People who
cannot eat wild herbs without removing their natural astringency are
unable to enjoy the flavors of nature. The practical wisdom of early
man who sun-dried root vegetables and pickled them in salt, rice bran,
or miso, enjoying their special taste and aroma, understood the essence
of natural flavor far better than modern gourmands.
