# Vegetable Seed Saving and Germination

The seed industry is a complex business with significant economic forces
shaping what gardeners can buy and grow. [[steve-solomon]]'s *Gardening When
It Counts* provides an insider's perspective — he founded Territorial Seed
Company — and explains both the commercial realities and the practical
techniques gardeners need to get seeds to grow reliably. Understanding seed
economics, germination science, and seed-saving methods can save
substantial money and improve garden results.

## The Seed Business: Economics and Realities

Running a seed company is a narrow-margin business with considerable risk.
Seed must be grown in locations with the right climate, harvested at the
correct time, cleaned, tested for germination, packaged, and shipped — all
before it loses viability. Seed companies must also maintain inventory of
hundreds or thousands of varieties, many of which sell in small quantities.

A telling example: Johnny's sells a mini-packet of kohlrabi (~120 seeds) for
$2.85, while 1,000 seeds of the same variety cost $7.60. Eight mini-packets
would cost $23.75 — more than triple. The key to savings: certainty that
you'll grow the variety for multiple years and that the seed remains viable.

Even using half a bulk purchase still works out cheaper than buying multiple
retail packets. If Solomon seeds 100 kohlrabi plants using 300 seeds per
year, he uses 1,000 seeds within three years. The trick is proper storage.
## Seed Viability and Storage Science

Seed viability — the ability to germinate and produce a healthy seedling —
depends on three factors: the seed's initial vigor, its moisture content
during storage, and the storage temperature. The relationship is remarkably
precise.

A dormant embryo slowly consumes its internal food reserves while waiting
to sprout. If stored too long, reserves run out before the embryo can fully
develop. A seedling with insufficient reserves may emerge, produce two small
seed leaves, then sit stunned, unable to make its first true leaf, and die —
a death by starvation, not disease.

The rule of thumb from seed science: for every 10°F (5°C) increase in
storage temperature combined with a 1 percent increase in the seed's
moisture content, the storage life of the seed is cut in half. Conversely,
for every 10°F decrease in temperature combined with a 1 percent decrease
in moisture, the storage life doubles.

Standard conditions are 70°F (21°C) at about 13 percent moisture — typical for
a temperate-climate closet shelf. Dropping to 50°F with 50 percent relative
humidity (seed moisture ~10 percent) extends life to four times standard.
Keeping seeds at 80°F in a humid greenhouse cuts life in half.

Solomon's practical method: store seeds in airtight containers with silica gel
desiccant (one cup per half-gallon of seed volume). Crystals change color —
blue when dry, pink when saturated — and can be reactivated by baking at
230°F for a few hours. Sealed containers in a refrigerator extend life even
further, provided seeds are thoroughly dry before cold storage.

## Germination Techniques: Laboratory Lessons

The best way to understand seed germination is to study how germ
laboratories do it — sterile media, precise temperature, ideal moisture.
Two labs testing the same seed lot should produce nearly identical results.

Field conditions are never this ideal. Real soil teems with microorganisms,
temperatures fluctuate, and moisture is unstable. Solomon's field data shows
cabbage seed testing at 95 percent germination in the lab produces about
83 percent field emergence, while 78 percent lab germination drops to only
20 percent in the field. Conclusion: start with vigorous seeds and create
soil conditions that approximate laboratory ideals.

## Watering and Temperature for Germination

Overwatering seedbeds is a common error. Every watering drops soil
temperature, slowing seed development. Wet, cool soil promotes damping-off
(a [[bloomfield-amphibian-decline-fungal-disease-climate-interactions]] that kills seedlings at the soil line) and [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]]
on cucurbits. Water seedbeds less frequently than intuition suggests.

The best watering time is late morning, when the sun can reheat soil
quickly. Evening watering is acceptable but leaves soil damp through cooler
nighttime hours.

A useful technique: after tilling, press the soil down to restore capillary
connections before sowing. In loose soil, moisture cannot rise to reach
seeds. Cover the drill with fine compost, which holds far more moisture
than mineral soil and rarely forms a crust.

## Chitting: Presprouting Seeds

Chitting means initiating sprouting under nearly ideal conditions, then
placing the sprouted seed in soil once a root has emerged but before it
grows long enough to break. The seedling is then immune to drying out.
Chitting also reveals dead seed immediately — if only 10 out of 50 seeds
sprout, the seed lot is poor quality before garden space is wasted.

For beans and peas: soak in tepid water for six to eight hours, drain,
place in a jar covered with fly screen. Rinse twice daily. Roots appear
in three to four days. Plant two per station, root pointed down.

For cucurbits: these require at least 70°F (21°C). Place seeds between
damp paper towels in a sealed container. Plant as soon as roots appear,
before they exceed half an inch.

For small seeds: blend sprouted seeds into a starch gelatin (cornstarch
boiled in water and cooled). Squeeze the seed-gel mixture into furrows
from a zip-lock bag with a small hole. This requires far less seed per
foot of row and eliminates thinning of gaps.

## Growing Your Own Seeds

The feasibility of saving your own seed depends on the species' method of
pollination. Self-pollinating vegetables — beans, peas, lettuce, tomatoes,
peppers — can be saved from a single plant year after year with minimal
isolation. A few have a slight tendency to outcross and need about 20 feet
of separation from other varieties.

Outcrossing species — alliums, beets, brassicas, cucurbits, corn, carrots
— require a minimum population to avoid [[cervantes-hybrid-vigor-inbreeding-depression]]. For
unrefined species like kale, as few as a dozen plants may suffice. For
corn, about 50. For other species, 200 may be necessary. The math is
sobering: 200 cabbage plants at four square feet each requires 800 square
feet — two thirds of a typical garden.

Dry seed should mature fully on the plant and dry steadily. Wet seed
(tomatoes, squash, cucumbers, melons) should come from dead-ripe fruit
approaching rotting. Tomato seeds benefit from a few extra days of
softening. Squash seeds improve with a month of curing after harvest.

## Hybrids: Myths and Realities

Solomon, a former seed company owner, offers a nuanced view. Hybrid
solanums (tomatoes, peppers, eggplants) must be hand-pollinated — each
flower individually worked every morning. This drives hybrid tomato seed
to nearly $1,000 per pound, produced only where labor is cheapest.

OP varieties *can* be developed from hybrid parents — Solomon bred OP
carrots from a hybrid, and his friend Tim Peters developed OP broccoli
from Green Valiant hybrid. The process takes several generations. The
real concern is that hybrid economic superiority has caused many OP
varieties to disappear from commercial trade, leaving home gardeners with
degenerated, low-quality open-pollinated stock.
