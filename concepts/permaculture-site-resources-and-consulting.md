## Permaculture Site Resources and Consulting

[[permaculture]] site design begins with a thorough inventory of available resources—both
intrinsic to the land and extrinsic to the surrounding district. As a consultant, your
first responsibility is to identify what the site already provides before proposing any
changes. Resources fall into several broad categories: earth (soils, minerals, clays),
plant (existing vegetation, timber, forage), energy (sun, wind, water power), and water
(springs, streams, runoff patterns). Mapping these elements creates the foundation for
all subsequent design decisions.

## Identifying Intrinsic Site Resources

Walk the site systematically, cataloging every resource present. Earth resources include
topsoil depth, subsoil mineral content, clay deposits, and rock formations suitable for
construction. Plant resources cover existing tree crops, native vegetation corridors,
weedy species indicating soil conditions, and potential timber. Energy resources
encompass solar aspect, prevailing wind directions, microclimate pockets, and any
existing water power potential such as falls or steady flow. Water resources are mapped
by source type—spring, surface flow, seasonal runoff—and by quality and reliability.

## Resource Rights and Legal Considerations

Understanding what you legally can and cannot use is essential. Mining rights may exist
on a property that limit your ability to extract clay, gravel, or stone. Water rights
vary dramatically by jurisdiction—riparian rights, prior appropriation, and licensed
extraction all determine how water can be captured and used. Coastal sites may have
seaweed harvesting rights that provide exceptional mulch and trace mineral inputs.
Always verify rights before incorporating a resource into your design; assuming access
can derail an entire strategy.

## Extrinsic District Resources

No site exists in isolation. The surrounding district offers markets for surplus
production, waste products from industry or agriculture that can serve as inputs, and
unfulfilled demands your design might address. A nearby sawmill produces sawdust for
[[composting]]; a brewery outputs spent grain for livestock. The community may lack fresh
greens in winter—a gap a well-designed greenhouse can fill. Mapping these extrinsic
resources transforms your design from self-sufficient island to integrated community
participant.

## Consulting Ethics and Working with Clients

Permaculture consultants serve the land first and the client second. Rich clients may
demand landscapes that waste resources on ornamental excess; your job is to redirect
that energy toward productive, regenerative systems without alienating the client.
Frame proposals in terms the client values—lower maintenance costs, increased property
value, food security. Never install systems you know will fail simply because the
client insists. [[holzer-high-beds-property-boundaries]] must be respected and clearly understood; many
conflicts arise from designs that encroach on neighbors or assume access to shared
resources.

## Site Observation Tools

Effective observation requires more than walking the land. Use topographic maps,
aerial photographs, soil surveys, and historical records. A compass, clinometer, and
long tape measure help quantify slopes and distances. Keep a detailed field journal
documenting seasonal changes—frost pockets, water flow patterns, wind tunnels, and
shade movements. Photographs taken at regular intervals across seasons reveal patterns
invisible during a single visit.

## Retrofit Design vs. New Design

Most consulting work involves retrofitting existing properties rather than designing
from scratch. Retrofit design starts with the house: improve insulation, orient
windows for passive solar gain, reduce energy consumption before adding external
systems. Only after the dwelling is energy-efficient should you expand outward into
the landscape. New designs offer greater freedom but must still follow the same
principle: settle the home site first, then layer infrastructure around it.

## Site Planning Sequence

Mollison prescribes a strict order for site development: **access first, water second,
house sites third**. Roads and pathways define the skeleton of the design—once
compacted, they are nearly impossible to relocate. Water systems follow because
gravity-fed distribution depends on elevation relationships established by access
routes. House placement comes last, positioned relative to the access and water
infrastructure already determined. Reversing this sequence creates costly rework and
compromised function.

## Type-One Errors: What to Avoid

Type-one errors are irreversible mistakes that permanently compromise a site. Siting
a house on a ridge top exposes it to extreme wind and fire risk. Placing structures
in a fire funnel—a valley or slope that channels wildfire—invites catastrophe.
Building in dense bush guarantees conflict with wildlife and creates a psychological
burden of guilt when animals are displaced or destroyed. These errors cannot be fixed
retroactively; they must be prevented through careful initial placement.

## The Problem of Bush and Scrub Locations

Clients often want to live surrounded by natural bush, attracted by the aesthetic.
This desire conflicts with ecological reality: houses in scrub require clearing, which
destroys habitat, attracts displaced animals to the dwelling, and generates ongoing
predator-prey conflicts. The result is a guilt complex where residents cycle between
defending their garden from wildlife and feeling remorse for the destruction. The
permaculture solution is to site the house at the bush edge, not within it, and use
the transition zone for productive integration rather than hostile exclusion.

## Conservation Areas

Every design should include dedicated conservation zones—areas set aside for
regeneration, [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], and genetic preservation. These are not wasted space;
they provide pest control services, pollination, water filtration, and microclimate
moderation for the productive zones. Size conservation areas proportionally to the
property, but never omit them entirely. Even small urban lots benefit from a
designated wild corner.

## Intensive Gardening in Tight Spaces

Where land is limited, intensification replaces expansion. Use [[coleman-greenhouse-tomato-vertical-growing]],
[[succession-planting]], small-scale aquaculture, and multi-strata polycultures to
maximize output per square meter. A well-managed intensive garden in a small courtyard
can out-produce a poorly managed broadacre plot. The key is [[biological-efficiency]]:
every element serves multiple functions, and every space is occupied productively.

## The Zone Concept

The zone system organizes the site by frequency of use and intensity of management.
Zone zero is the house interior. Zone one contains daily-visit elements: culinary
herbs, salad greens, seedling nurseries. Zone two holds less-frequent crops—fruit
trees, poultry, [[composting-systems]]. Zone three is occasional-visit broadacre:
orchards, pastures, main crops. Zone four is managed forest and forage. Zone five
is unmanaged wilderness—the conservation area. This graduated structure ensures
energy is invested where it yields the greatest return and that no element is placed
farther from the house than its maintenance requirements demand.
