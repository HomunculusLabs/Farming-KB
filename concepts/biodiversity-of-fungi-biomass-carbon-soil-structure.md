# Fungal Biomass, Carbon Storage, and Soil Structure in Farming Systems
Updated: 2026-04-18
Source: Biodiversity of Fungi (Hyde et al., eds.), Chapters 11, 13, and related sections
## The Scale of Fungal Biomass in Soils
Fungi are the dominant living organisms in many soils by biomass. In grassland soils, fungi comprise an estimated 78-90% of total decomposer biomass. In a British deciduous forest, fungal mycelia contributed approximately 89% of total living microbial biomass. In the total litter and humus of a British deciduous forest, including woody debris and dead roots, basidiomycetes alone accounted for approximately 60% of total living microbial biomass.
This immense fungal biomass represents a substantial carbon and nutrient pool in agricultural soils. Changes in fungal biomass -- driven by management practices -- can significantly alter soil carbon storage, nutrient cycling, and soil physical properties.

## Fungal Carbon Pathways
### Carbon Flow Through Fungi
In the [[soil-food-web]], carbon flows from plant material through decomposer fungi before reaching higher trophic levels. The fungal pathway has several distinctive features:
1. **High carbon use efficiency**: Fungi convert a larger proportion of consumed carbon into biomass (higher CUE) compared to bacteria. Typical fungal CUE ranges from 30-60%, compared to 10-30% for bacteria.
2. **Carbon sequestration**: Fungal biomass and necromass contribute to stable soil organic matter. Fungal cell walls contain chitin and melanin that are resistant to decomposition.
3. **Recalcitrant metabolites**: Fungi produce glomalin (from AMF), melanin, and other recalcitrant compounds that persist in soil for years to decades.
4. **Hyphal turnover**: As fungal hyphae grow and die, they continuously deposit organic material throughout the soil matrix, not just at the surface.
See also: [[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]], [[fungal-carbon-use-efficiency]], [[fungal-respiration-carbon-flux]]
### Fungal Necromass and Humus Formation
When fungal hyphae die, the resulting necromass becomes a component of soil organic matter. Fungal necromass is particularly important for humus formation because:
- Chitin in fungal cell walls is relatively resistant to decomposition
- Melanized hyphae of dematiaceous fungi decompose very slowly
- Fungal necromass has a higher C:N ratio (15-20:1) than bacterial necromass (5-8:1)
- The filamentous nature of fungal necromass creates a scaffold for aggregate formation

## Glomalin and Carbon Sequestration
### What Is Glomalin?
[[fungal-glomalin-and-soil-carbon]]: Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]. It was first identified in 1996 by Sara Wright at the USDA. Key properties:
- Operationally defined by extraction in citrate buffer at high temperature
- Highly resistant to decomposition -- persists in soil for decades
- Contributes 27% of total soil carbon in some soils
- Acts as a biological glue, binding soil particles into aggregates
- Concentration in soil correlates with aggregate stability and soil carbon content
### Agricultural Significance
Glomalin is significant for farming because:
- It is a major component of stable soil organic matter
- It contributes directly to soil aggregation and structure
- Its concentration responds to management practices (tillage, cover crops, rotations)
- It represents a long-term carbon sink in agricultural soils
- Glomalin-related soil protein (GRSP) is used as an indicator of mycorrhizal activity
Management that promotes mycorrhizal activity (reduced tillage, diverse rotations, mycorrhizal-dependent crops) increases glomalin accumulation and associated soil carbon sequestration.

## Fungal Contributions to Soil Structure
### Aggregate Formation
Fungi contribute to soil aggregation through multiple mechanisms:
1. **Physical entanglement**: Hyphae physically wrap around soil particles, binding them together. A single fungal mycelium can connect multiple soil particles and microaggregates into larger, more stable aggregates.
2. **Biological glue**: Fungal exudates including polysaccharides, glycoproteins (glomalin), and other sticky compounds act as cementing agents between soil particles.
3. **Organic matter incorporation**: Fungal decomposition of organic matter produces compounds that serve as nucleation sites for aggregate formation around organic particles.
4. **Root-fungus partnership**: Mycorrhizal associations enhance root growth and root exudation, both of which promote aggregate formation.
See also: [[fungal-soil-aggregation]], [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure]], [[fungal-microaggregate-formation-and-soil-stability]]
### Aggregate Stability
Fungal-dominated soils typically have more stable aggregates than bacterial-dominated soils because:
- Fungal hyphae are more resistant to degradation than bacterial capsules
- Fungal decomposition products (melanin, chitin) are more recalcitrant
- Fungal hyphae create more extensive physical connections between particles
- Fungal exudates form stronger bonds between mineral particles
Soil aggregate stability is critical for:
- Water infiltration and retention (reducing runoff and erosion)
- Aeration and gas exchange
- Root penetration and growth
- Habitat creation for soil organisms
- Protection of organic matter within aggregates from decomposition
## Fungal Biomass in Different Soil Types
### Grassland Soils
Grassland soils have high fungal biomass concentrated in the root zone. Grass roots are annual, finely divided, and contribute a biomass that far exceeds above-ground biomass. This creates a distributed organic matter profile throughout the soil, supporting fungal growth at depth.
### Forest Soils
Forest soils concentrate fungal biomass in the litter and humus layers. The massive above-ground biomass plus substantial input from mycorrhizae creates a rich organic layer at the soil surface. Ectomycorrhizal [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are particularly important, contributing the majority of living microbial biomass in the litter and humus.
### Agricultural Soils
Agricultural soils typically have lower fungal biomass than natural ecosystems due to:
- Tillage disruption of hyphal networks
- Reduced organic matter inputs (especially in monoculture systems)
- Fertilizer effects on mycorrhizal dependency
- Pesticide impacts on non-target fungi
### Comparison of Ecosystem Types
| Ecosystem | Fungal Biomass (dominance) | Key Fungal Groups |
|---|---|---|
| Native grassland | Very high (78-90% of decomposer biomass) | AMF, saprobic ascomycetes |
| Temperate forest | High (89% of microbial biomass) | Ectomycorrhizae, litter decomposers |
| Cropland (conventional) | Low to moderate | Reduced diversity, fewer basidiomycetes |
| Cropland (no-till) | Moderate to high | Recovering AMF, saprobic communities |
| Pasture | Moderate to high | AMF, endophytes, saprobes |
## Management to Enhance Fungal Biomass
### Reducing Tillage
No-till or reduced tillage practices preserve hyphal networks and allow fungal biomass to accumulate. The transition from conventional to conservation tillage is typically accompanied by measurable increases in fungal biomass, soil aggregation, and soil organic carbon.
See also: [[soil-disturbance-mycorrhizal-impact]]
### Organic Amendments
Adding compost, manure, or other organic materials provides substrate for saprobic fungi and stimulates fungal biomass growth. The quality of amendments matters: materials with higher C:N ratios and more recalcitrant compounds favor fungal-dominated decomposition pathways.
