# Fukuoka No Cultivation Principle and Soil Self Tillage

## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka identified no cultivation as one of the four foundational principles of natural farming, alongside no fertilizer, no weeding, and no pesticides. His argument against tilling challenges one of the most deeply held assumptions in all of agriculture: that plowing is essential for crop production. Fukuoka demonstrated over thirty years of continuous no-till rice and barley cultivation that soil works itself, and that human intervention through plowing actively damages the soil structure it claims to improve.

## The Conventional Rationale for Plowing

Farmers and agricultural scientists justify cultivation on several grounds:

- **Aeration**: Plowing introduces air into the soil, which is believed to benefit root respiration and microbial activity.
- **Nutrient availability**: Turning the soil is thought to increase nitrogen availability through nitrification, and to incorporate surface applied fertilizers into the root zone.
- **Weed control**: Tilling buries weed seeds and uproots existing weeds, reducing competition with crop plants.
- **Soil softening**: Plowing breaks up compacted soil layers, creating a looser tilth that roots can more easily penetrate.

## Fukuoka's Counterargument: Plowing Ruins the Soil

### The Kneading Effect

Fukuoka compared plowing to kneading bread dough. When a farmer turns the soil with a plow or hoe, the action breaks soil aggregates into smaller and smaller particles. These finer particles settle into an increasingly regular arrangement with smaller interstitial spaces between them. The apparent loosening immediately after plowing is temporary; as the soil settles, it becomes harder and denser than before. This creates a positive feedback loop: the harder soil seems to require more plowing, which makes it even harder.

### Evidence from Herbicide Adoption

After World War II, when herbicides became available to Japanese rice farmers, growers discovered something unexpected. When they sprayed fields with herbicides and reduced the frequency of plowing and intertillage, their yields improved. This demonstrated that intertillage had been effective as a weeding operation but had been worthless, or actively harmful, as a means of loosening the soil. The weeds themselves had been providing soil structure benefits that were lost when they were removed.

### The Soil Plows Itself

Fukuoka argued that soil is a living system capable of maintaining its own structure and fertility. The mechanisms of natural soil cultivation include:

- **Root penetration**: The roots of grasses and green manure crops work the soil to depths of twelve to fifteen inches or more, exceeding the typical farmer's plowing depth of four to eight inches. Air and water penetrate alongside the roots.
- **Microbial activity**: When roots wither and die, microorganisms proliferate in the decaying organic matter. These organisms are replaced by successive populations, building humus and softening the soil.
- **Earthworm activity**: Where humus accumulates, earthworms appear. Their burrowing further aerates and mixes the soil.
- **Mole activity**: As earthworm populations increase, moles begin burrowing, working the soil at even greater depth.
- **Plant succession**: Different plant species work different soil layers. Grasses handle the topsoil while tree roots work deeper strata.

### Forests as Proof of Self Working Soil

Fukuoka pointed to mountain forests as evidence that soil requires no human intervention. Forest trees grow to magnificent heights without benefit of hoe or fertilizer. Cedar grows where conditions suit cedar; pine germinates where pine thrives; ferns colonize infertile ground while deep soil species occupy valleys. Plants locate themselves precisely where they can grow, without human direction. The grasses, trees, earthworms, and moles have collectively acted as the plow horses and oxen, completely rearranging and renewing the soil over thousands of years.

## The Cycle of Soil Degradation

Fukuoka traced a causal chain by which human cultivation practices create the very problems they then claim to solve:

1. Man clears land and removes natural vegetation, robbing deeper soil layers of humus and halting microbial proliferation.
2. Transplanting saplings with entangled or clipped roots prevents normal root development and the ability to penetrate hard ground.
3. Chemical fertilizer application encourages shallow root structures along the topsoil.
4. Weeding halts the natural aggregation and enrichment of topsoil by removing the plants whose roots would maintain soil structure.
5. The resulting compacted, lifeless soil appears to require plowing to loosen it.
6. Plowing further degrades soil structure, creating dependence on continued cultivation.

## Fukuoka's Thirty Year Demonstration

Fukuoka's own paddy field had not been plowed or turned in over thirty years at the time of his writing. He practiced direct seeded no tillage winter grain and rice succession in a white clover cover. The field consistently produced close to 22 bushels of winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter acre, with a goal of eventually reaching 33 bushels. This was achieved without any plowing, chemical fertilizers, prepared compost, pesticides, or herbicides.

The labor required was limited to sowing seed and spreading straw. The clover and winter grain roots maintained soil structure. Rice straw spread on the surface decomposed into humus. Chicken droppings were occasionally scattered as a supplement. The soil improved each year rather than degrading.

## Philosophical Implications

Fukuoka connected the no cultivation principle to his broader philosophy of Mu, or nothingness. The belief that plowing is necessary stems from the same discriminating knowledge that leads man to see himself as separate from nature. When man perceives the soil as imperfect and in need of improvement, he reaches for the hoe. When he trusts the soil and leaves its working to nature, the soil reveals its inherent perfection.

The deeper implication is that human intervention in natural systems creates dependency cycles. Each act of cultivation creates conditions that seem to require further cultivation. Natural farming breaks this cycle by refusing to begin it. The farmer who does not plow never needs to plow. The soil that is left to itself becomes and remains self working.
