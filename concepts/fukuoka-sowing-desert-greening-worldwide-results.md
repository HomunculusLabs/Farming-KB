# Desert Greening Projects — Worldwide Results

*Documented results of Masanobu Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-aerial-seeding-clay-pellets-desert-revegetation-technique]] efforts across
Africa, India, the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], Europe, and Asia, as described in "Sowing
Seeds in the Desert."*

## California, United States

Fukuoka first saw the desert in 1979 when he flew to the United States for the
first time. He expected the American continent to be a vast, fertile green plain
but found it a brown, desolate semi-desert. He gave a talk at California's
Department of Conservation warning that careless agricultural practices, poor
water management, overgrazing, and overlogging were creating a "Great California
Desert."

He conducted an experiment on a hot, dry plateau in the Coast Range about one
hundred miles from Sacramento. About twenty young people from several countries
were living there on national forest land with not a spot of green in sight.
Fukuoka noticed that water soaking a mouse's nest had caused weed seeds to
sprout, and realized the native perennial grasses had been chased out by
introduced annuals.

He broadcast seeds of Japanese vegetables amid the dried grasses, mowed them
down, and brought water by plastic pipe from a spring. The foxtail weeds
sprouted, withered, and became mulch, while Japanese pumpkin, cucumbers,
tomatoes, okra, daikon, and corn flourished in their place. He recommended the
state government broadcast seeds of perennial grasses from the air in clay
pellets, using the [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] for revegetating the
desert. He was surprised to find the large Juba River flowing through the
semi-desert year-round, its source in the mountains of Ethiopia. From accounts
by an Ethiopian elder and Somali farmers, the main cause of deforestation was
colonial agricultural policies that introduced and exclusively grew commercial
crops (coffee, tea, sugarcane, cotton, tobacco, peanuts, and corn). Production
of personal food crops was forbidden. Seeds of self-sufficiency crops had all
but disappeared.

When he went to apply for a visa, the Somalian government told him that any
instruction encouraging farmers to become self-sufficient would not be welcome —
it would be considered treason.

At an Ethiopian refugee camp, Fukuoka handed out seeds to children. After four
or five days, about twenty to thirty children led him to circular patches in the
sand four to five feet in diameter where cucumber, squash, beans, eggplant, and
daikon plants had sprouted. Three youths started beating on a broken bucket and
singing. Even women and the elderly joined in sowing seeds.

The speed of plant growth was remarkable. Oranges, grapes, and pomegranates grew
two to three times faster than in Japan. Papaya bore about ten fruits in four to
six months, bananas set fruit in one year. All modern farms using pumped river
water for irrigation had failed and turned to salt fields.

Fukuoka advised planting acacia trees with vegetables and grains, including
poisonous plants that goats would not eat, and trees effective at bringing water
up from underground. He told a tribal elder: "The soil of Africa is just
resting. The red clay is taking a nap. If the people will work to awaken the
sleeping soil, then you will be able to grow anything."

## India — The Major Revegetation Campaign

India became Fukuoka's largest-scale desert revegetation project. A Japanese
woman named Kimiko Kubo donated money "as thanks for the elephant Hanako that
Prime Minister Nehru sent to the children of Japan after the war," enabling
Fukuoka to pursue his dream of regreening India's deserts.

**Bengal mangrove success:** At the mouth of the Ganges River, seedlings of
twenty to thirty varieties of mangroves had sprouted on a sandbar from [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]], did
practical research for almost ten years, then set up her [[fukuoka-creating-natural-farm-site-selection-establishment]] in four
years mainly by scattering seeds and planting trees. Despite the Philippines'
poor soil and reckless desertification|deforestation, she created a true paradise with banana,
papaya, guava, durian, coffee trees, and a thick [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-ground-cover-cultivation]] of perennials and
[[comparison-green-manure-guide-vs-mulch-types-and-effects]]. Orchids bloomed everywhere, birds flew about, and fish swam in the
ponds. Fukuoka identified the secret element as "the harmony that existed
between Ms. Aveliw and nature."
