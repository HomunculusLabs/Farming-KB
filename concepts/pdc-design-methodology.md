# PDC Permaculture [[mollison-designers-permaculture-principles-and-ethics]] Methodology

In Pamphlet VIII of the 1981 Permaculture Design Course, Bill permaculture-design|Mollison provides a detailed step-by-step methodology for professional permaculture design. This page synthesizes that methodology, which applies whether designing for a suburban backyard or a thousand-acre ranch.

## The Design Sequence

### Step 1: Define Property Boundaries
"On a big property, you might have to fly the bounds. Photography isn't much good to you. Maps aren't really, either, except to find out where you are going." It is "really just a matter of playing around on your own."

### Step 2: Experience the Site
"The most important step is to experience the site. You are your own best compound tool. You have senses for judging wind and temperature and evaporation and slope. You have eyes. Observation is your best tool. Experience is your second best tool."

Mollison emphasizes making it "absolutely plain to people that you must have a few hours on your own. You might use a spade. Look at the quality of the peat."

### Step 3: Identify Resources
Categories of resources to assess:
- **Client resources**: Skills, experience, material resources, capital, desired lifestyle
- **Site resources**: Earth (clay, peat, minerals), plants, animals, insects, water, wind, wood
- **Energy resources**: Water falls, wind sites, solar potential, salable energy
- **Social resources**: Teaching potential, recreation suitability, seminar capacity
- **Extrinsic district resources**: Markets, waste products, unfulfilled demands

Critical: "If you are good at seeing the site's resources, then you have earned your fee."

### Step 4: Place Access
"Placing access is your first move." Roads should:
- Run gently across slope, doing little turns on plateaus
- Run up the center of ridges for drainage
- Always finish access upgrade to the dwelling, even if dropping a little
- Be designed to minimize upkeep
- In winter climate, be positioned in the sun to reduce shoveling

"A misplaced road into the site will cost more over time than almost anything else, including the house itself."

### Step 5: Locate the House Site
Criteria for house placement:
- Keep off ridge tops (fire, cold, wind exposure)
- Keep out of bush (creates wildlife conflict, turns gentle people into killers)
- Keep out of converging valleys (fire funnels, wind tunnels)
- Position just below ridgeline on sun-facing side
- Ideal: "sitting on a little ridge between two ridges" backing into mountains looking toward the sun

The rule: "A place like that changes hands every four years, because nobody can stand the misery. If a place is a happy place, it very rarely changes hands, maybe three or four times in 200 years."

### Step 6: Lay Out Water Systems
"Try to lay out the water, access, and house sites as a single entity." Water placement interacts with everything: tanks above house level for gravity flow, dams for fire protection, swales for soil building.

### Step 7: Define Zones
Zone 1 (20-30 feet from house): High-frequency visit areas. Annuals, small animals requiring daily care. Little 4-foot ponds for watercress and tadpoles. "If you have any sense, you get all your food except a couple of items from there."

Zone 2: Larger gardens, orchards, animals requiring less frequent attention. Mulched gardens (tested against clean-till methods at Orange Bathurse Agricultural College -- "Ours was so good that the vegetables tasted better than theirs").

Zone 3+: Less frequently visited. Pastures, woodlots, wildlife areas. "The least-visited place around this house may be just below this window."

### Step 8: Address Themes Affecting the Whole Site
Fire protection, trust structures, community formation, commercial crop strategies -- cross-cutting concerns that affect all zones.

### Step 9: Detail Each Area
Work through each numbered area developing design in depth, starting with the house site and intensive garden.

## The Six-Step Report Methodology

1. **Collect all data**: Client requirements, maps, local regulations
2. **Analyze the client's problems**
3. **Recognize permaculture principles** that fit this particular situation
4. **Apply principles specifically** to the problems involved
5. **Design solutions** in the form of edges, [[permaculture-pattern-literacy]]ing, increased functional relationships
6. **Check everything over** to make sure the solution adequately meets all client needs

## Report Structure

1. **General Description of Site**: Brief description, main map, site broken into identifiable areas
2. **Themes**: Fire protection, trusts, community, commercial strategies
3. **Details of the Areas**: Numbered areas with individual design development
4. **Principles of House Design**: Energy requirements, architecture recommendations, retrofit suggestions
5. **Priority Staging/Cost Analysis**: Where to start, what it costs, management policy
6. **Bibliography**: Referenced books plus relevant others
7. **Plant List**: Documented, avoiding illegal or potentially rampant species
8. **Resource List**: Local people, hardware, tools, technologies
9. **Conclusion**: No guarantees (natural phenomena and changing conditions), invitation for follow-up questions at no extra charge

## The Ten Type-One Errors

Errors to avoid (check-list before writing reports):
1. Undertaking design for environmentally destructive clients
2. Not explaining reasons for recommendations
3. Recommending technology beyond client's ability
4. Not supplying adequate management data
5. Depersonalized reporting style
6. Failing to be specific (vague expressions like "fairly large")
7. Poor patterning of edges and link-ups
8. Failing to recommend essential pre-treatments (soil conditioning)
9. Recommending illegal plant species
10. Failing to fully define and explain on-site resources

## The Functional Stacking Approach

The overarching design philosophy: "We are laying on functions." Each element should serve multiple purposes. A pond is not just water storage -- it grows food, breeds pest-eating frogs, reflects light to extend seasons, provides fire protection, creates microclimate, and may produce salable water or fish.

The designer's job is not to tell people how to garden, build, or farm -- "your function as a designer is to place things in the environment, and place them in such a way that you use their multiple functions, that you create low energy inputs for high yield and stability."
