# Invertebrate Fungivory and Feeding Preferences

Invertebrate fungivory — the consumption of fungi by invertebrate animals — is a fundamental trophic interaction in soil and litter ecosystems. Soil microarthropods, nematodes, and insect larvae depend on fungi as primary or supplementary food sources, and their selective grazing profoundly shapes fungal community composition, competitive dynamics, and ecosystem-level processes such as decomposition and [[nutrient-mineralization]].

## Diversity of Fungivorous Invertebrates

The fungal-feeding invertebrate community is taxonomically diverse, encompassing:

- **Collembola** (springtails): Among the most abundant and well-studied fungal grazers in soil and leaf litter
- **Nematodes**: Ubiquitous soil organisms with dedicated fungivorous taxa (e.g., Aphelenchoides, Aphelenchus)
- **Diptera larvae**: Fly larvae that colonize and consume fleshy mushroom fruiting bodies
- **Mites** (Acari): Both prostigmatid and mesostigmatid mites include fungal-feeding species
- **Isopods**: Contribute to litter comminution and fungal biomass stimulation
- **Earthworms**: Selective fungal feeders that alter community composition through gut passage

## Feeding Preferences and Selectivity

### Collembolan Preferences

Collembola exhibit marked and consistent feeding preferences among fungal species. Shaw (1988) established a clear hierarchy of preferences for the collembolan [[onichiurus-amatus]] when offered a range of ectomycorrhizal and [[saprotrophic-fungi]]:

| Fungal Species | Colony Consumed (%) | Fecal Count |
|---|---|---|
| [[marasmius-androsaceus]] | 72.2 | 74.8 |
| Mycena galopus | 68.2 | 19.2 |
| Lactarius rufus | 55.7 | 64.5 |
| Suillus luteus | 50.7 | 48.9 |
| Laccaria proxima | 41.4 | 70.8 |
| Rhizopogon roseolus | 20.7 | 13.8 |
| Paxillus involutus | 21.3 | 13.1 |
| Hebeloma crustuliniforme | 1.7 | 1.0 |

Preferences are species-specific — Thimm and Larink (1995) found that each of four collembolan species (Folsomia candida, Onychiurus fimatus, Sinella coeca, Proisotoma minuta) preferred a different arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus, while Xenylla grisea showed no fungal preference and fed mainly on non-mycorrhizal root tissue. Some fungi are actively avoided: Parkinson et al. (1979) demonstrated that O. amatus actively avoided a basidiomycete that caused its death, even without direct ingestion, suggesting detection of fungal secondary metabolites.

Reddy and Das (1983) found that mites showed little food selection among Trichoderma, Cladosporium, and Pythium, while collembola preferred a mixed microfungal diet. Different fungi produced markedly different outcomes for animal populations — after nine weeks, collembola numbers varied from 4 to 50 depending on the fungal food source, compared to a control value of 50.

### Nematode Preferences

Nematode feeding preferences have important implications for mycorrhizal function and [[soil-food-web]] dynamics. Ruess and Dighton (1996) offered seven ectomycorrhizal and one ericoid fungal species to Aphelenchoides saprophilus and found dramatic differences:

- **Laccaria laccata** supported the largest population (2.4 × 10⁵) with the highest proportion of females (11.4%), indicating superior food quality for both growth and fecundity
- **Cenococcum geophilum** and **Lactarius rufus** also supported good population growth
- **Paxillus involutus** and **Amanita rubescens** supported the poorest nematode populations (0.8 and 0.6 × 10⁵, respectively)
- Sutherland and Fortin (1968) found that Rhizopogon roseolus actually had a negative impact on Aphelenchus avenae numbers

The intensity of nematode grazing can be sufficient to prevent mycorrhizal formation — Sutherland and Fortin showed that A. avenae could prevent mycorrhizal symbiosis between Suillus granulatus and Pinus resinosa in tripartite culture, though such intense grazing pressure is unlikely in natural ecosystems.

Nematode dietary preferences are not static. Ruess et al. (2000) demonstrated that Aphelenchoides sp. shifted its preferred fungal species over time, potentially as a defense against accumulation of toxic compounds from any single fungal food source.

### Dipteran Larvae and Polyphagy

Dipteran larvae breeding in mushroom fruiting bodies tend to be polyphagous. Hanski (1989) documented the degree of polyphagy across nine genera of Agaricales, with Russula supporting 17 Diptera species and Lactarius supporting 14. This polyphagous strategy is likely an adaptation to the ephemeral and spatially unpredictable availability of fungal fruiting bodies — consuming multiple species increases the probability of finding food at any given time.

Toxicity to humans does not predict invertebrate palatability. Jaenike et al. (1983) found many Drosophila species tolerant of α-amanitin from Amanita species.

## Ecological Consequences of Selective Grazing

### Alteration of Fungal Competition

Selective grazing by invertebrates can fundamentally alter competitive relationships among fungi. Newell (1984a,b) provided a classic example: the collembolan Onichiurus latus preferentially feeds on Marasmius androsaceus over Mycena galopus. At high collembolan densities, grazing significantly reduced Marasmius growth to the extent that Mycena dominated the leaf litter. In the absence of grazing, Marasmius occupied leaf litter (its optimal habitat) while Mycena preferred soil — grazing effectively forced both species into suboptimal niches, likely reducing their functional efficiency.

### Growth Form Changes

Faunal grazing can induce morphological changes in fungi that alter their ecological function. Dowson et al. (1988) showed that arthropod grazing on the cord-forming fungus Steccherinum fimbriatum induced a shift from slow, dense growth to fast-growing diffuse mycelium. Hedlund et al. (1991) demonstrated that collembolan grazing caused Mortierella isabelina to shift from appressed to aerial hyphal growth. These morphological changes can significantly alter resource colonization rates and utilization efficiency.

### Decomposition Rate Effects

The interplay between invertebrate grazing and decomposition is complex and context-dependent. In some cases, grazing reduces fungal diversity and increases decomposition efficiency (Lussenhop and Wicklow, 1985). In others, removal of arthropods increases fungal biomass and nitrogen immobilization (Beare et al., 1992). The outcome depends on the specific fungal community, grazer identity and density, and environmental conditions.

## Complex Fungal-Animal Mutualisms

Some fungal-animal interactions have evolved beyond simple predation into near-mutualisms. Bultman et al. (2000) described a remarkable interaction between yucca moth larvae (Botanophila files) and the ascomycete Epichloë elymi: flies transfer fungal spermatia among hosts during egg-laying, and developing larvae consume ascospores. Counterintuitively, fungal reproductive output (number of perithecia) increased with egg load, suggesting a balanced antagonism rather than simple parasitism.

## Climate Change Implications

Couteaux and Bolger (2000) reviewed climate change effects on soil fauna and concluded that changes in fungal community composition or abundance due to disturbance will have major impacts on fungivore populations and communities. Arid and semiarid regions, where fungal-dominated ecosystems support fungivore-dominated faunal communities (Whitford, 1989), may be particularly vulnerable. Some fungivorous mites can enter cryptobiosis, and nematodes can undergo anhydrobiosis to survive desiccation, but the interactive effects of climate change on fungal communities, fungivores, and ecosystem processes remain poorly understood.
