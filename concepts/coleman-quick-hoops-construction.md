# Quick Hoops Construction

Quick hoops are a low-cost, versatile season-extension structure
developed at the [[coleman-four-season-farm]] Farm. Made from simple, readily available
materials, they provide effective winter protection for field crops at
a fraction of the cost of a greenhouse. Three rows of quick hoops
cover the same area as a 22-by-48-foot greenhouse at one-twentieth the
cost.

## Materials and Dimensions

The supports are 10-foot lengths of half-inch electrical conduit,
either plastic or metal. Bowed into a half circle, they cover two
30-inch beds side by side with a path between them. Each end of the
conduit is inserted about 10 inches into the soil on either side of
the two beds, forming a hoop approximately 30 inches high at the
midpoint.

Plastic conduit can be bent by hand as each support is put in place.
Metal conduit must be pre-bent to the ideal shape using a tubing
bender. Hoops are placed every 5 feet along the length of the beds,
which are usually 50 or 100 feet long. In heavy snow areas using
plastic conduit, hoops should be spaced 2.5 feet apart for additional
strength.

## Covering System

The hoops are initially covered with 10-foot-wide spun-bonded row
cover held down by sandbags placed every 5 feet along the edge. The
sandbags are the same type used to hold down the plastic on the
rolling greenhouses. Once real winter weather threatens (late
November or early December), a sheet of 10-foot-wide clear plastic is
added over the row cover to make the tunnels more snow-proof and
wind-resistant.

### Tightening the Plastic

To stiffen the structure against wind and snow load, it is important
to tighten the plastic. This is done by:

1. Driving a stake into the ground 4 feet from the last hoop at each
   end of the low tunnel.
2. Tying a rope to each end of the plastic.
3. Pulling tightly on both ends and securing the ropes to the stakes.
   This makes the plastic cover taut lengthwise.
4. Shifting the sandbags on top of the edges of the plastic to make
   it taut from side to side.

### Sandbag Advantages

Using sandbags to secure the edges is much faster and much less work
than burying the edges in trenches, although not as permanent. In
really strong wind, covers can blow off, but once winter arrives and
everything freezes to the ground, nothing moves. Sandbags also allow
covering every pair of beds side by side across a field because there
is no need to dig trenches or throw soil aside.

## [[oss-oeric-harvesting-and-crop-management]]

Onions, scallions, spinach, and lettuce are the primary overwintering
crops grown under quick hoops. Overwintering onions are planted during
the last week of August, with hoops and row cover placed in
mid-October. Onions are planted five rows to a bed so the two
intermediate rows can be harvested as scallions starting in spring,
leaving space for the remaining three rows to develop bulbs.
Overwintered onion varieties mature at the end of June, providing
onions to sell five weeks or more before spring-planted crops are
ready.

For spinach and lettuce, crops are either direct-sown or started in
soil blocks. Transplants are often better because outdoor germination
at this time of fall can be spotty. Soil blocks are seeded on October
1 and transplanted two weeks later. Direct-sown seeds go in at a
slightly earlier date. Overwintered lettuce and spinach provide
outdoor crops up to a month earlier than the best spring transplants.

## Spring Ventilation

When spring arrives, ventilation begins on sunny days. A few sandbags
are removed along the southern edge and a notched prop is inserted to
hold up the edges of the plastic and fabric. Once outdoor temperatures
moderate to the point where fabric alone provides enough protection
(late March), the plastic layers are removed and stored for the next
winter.

## Economics and Flexibility

Quick hoops can be erected to cover 1,000 square feet (the size of a
22-by-48-foot greenhouse) for only one-twentieth (5 percent) of the
cost of a greenhouse. Furthermore, quick hoops used for overwintering
can be disassembled and moved to cover other crops later in spring.
Because of their simplicity and long season of use, quick hoops have
completely replaced curved wire wickets that were previously used to
support fabric covers over field crops.

## Potential for Northern Growers

Quick hoops give northern growers the opportunity to explore late-fall
field planting of spring crops -- a strategy that growers in warmer
climates use routinely. Key research questions include:

- How early can fall-seeded crops be planted to mature sooner in
  spring without triggering them to go to seed rapidly?
- At what size or stage of growth are seedlings hardiest for
  surviving cold winter weather?
- What crops can be sown just before the ground freezes (late
  November/early December) to winter-over as seeds and germinate
  extra early in spring?

The farm has recorded temperatures as high as 55 degrees Fahrenheit
inside double-covered quick hoops on sunny days in mid-February,
suggesting significant potential for early spring crop production.
