# Quick Hoops Construction

Quick hoops are a low-cost, versatile season-extension structure
developed at the [[oss-oeric-harvesting-and-crop-management]]

Onions, scallions, spinach, and lettuce are the primary overwintering
crops grown under quick hoops. Overwintering onions are planted during
the last week of August, with hoops and [[coleman-row-cover-fabric-management]] placed in
mid-October. Onions are planted five rows to a bed so the two
intermediate rows can be harvested as scallions starting in spring,
leaving space for the remaining three rows to develop bulbs.
Overwintered onion varieties mature at the end of June, providing
onions to sell five weeks or more before spring-planted crops are
ready.

For spinach and lettuce, crops are either direct-sown or started in
soil blocks. Transplants are often better because outdoor germination
at this time of fall can be spotty. Soil blocks are seeded on October
1 and transplanted two weeks later. Direct-sown seeds go in at a
slightly earlier date. Overwintered lettuce and spinach provide
outdoor crops up to a month earlier than the best spring transplants.

## Spring Ventilation

When spring arrives, ventilation begins on sunny days. A few sandbags
are removed along the southern edge and a notched prop is inserted to
hold up the edges of the plastic and fabric. Once outdoor temperatures
moderate to the point where fabric alone provides enough protection
(late March), the plastic layers are removed and stored for the next
winter.

## Economics and Flexibility

Quick hoops can be erected to cover 1,000 square feet (the size of a
22-by-48-foot greenhouse) for only one-twentieth (5 percent) of the
cost of a greenhouse. Furthermore, quick hoops used for overwintering
can be disassembled and moved to cover other crops later in spring.
Because of their simplicity and long season of use, quick hoops have
completely replaced curved wire wickets that were previously used to
support fabric covers over field crops.

## Potential for Northern Growers

Quick hoops give northern growers the opportunity to explore late-fall
field planting of spring crops -- a strategy that growers in warmer
climates use routinely. Key research questions include:

- How early can fall-seeded crops be planted to mature sooner in
  spring without triggering them to go to seed rapidly?
- At what size or stage of growth are seedlings hardiest for
  surviving cold winter weather?
- What crops can be sown just before the ground freezes (late
  November/early December) to winter-over as seeds and germinate
  extra early in spring?

The farm has recorded temperatures as high as 55 degrees Fahrenheit
inside double-covered quick hoops on sunny days in mid-February,
suggesting significant potential for [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] crop production.
