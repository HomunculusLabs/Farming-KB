## Establishing the Orchard: Planting Methods

Citrus trees may generally be grown from nursery plants grafted with rootstock, which, although shallow rooted, are cold-hardy. Apple trees can be trained into dwarf trees by using dwarfing stock, but it may also be interesting in some cases to plant seed directly and grow the young saplings into majestic trees having a natural form.

### The Choice Between Nursery Stock and Seed

There is an important trade-off between using nursery stock and planting seed directly. Nursery stock from grafted rootstock provides known fruit quality and earlier bearing, but the grafting process creates an unnatural union that affects the tree's long-term health and root development. Seed-grown trees bear fruit of vastly differing sizes and shape that is unfit for the market. Yet, on the other hand, there always exists the possibility that an unusual fruit will arise from the seed. Indeed, why not multiply the joys of life by creating a natural orchard full of variety and surprises?

### The Initial Planting Approach

To establish a natural orchard, one should dig large holes here and there among the stumps of felled trees and plant unpruned saplings and fruit seed over the site, leaving these unattended just as one would leave alone a reforested stand of trees. Of course, suckers grow from the cut tree stumps and weeds and low brush flourishes. Orchard management at this stage consists primarily of coming in twice a year to cut the weeds and underbrush with a large sickle.

This approach deliberately allows a period of wild growth. The newly planted fruit trees must compete with the existing vegetation, which strengthens them naturally. Trees that cannot survive this competition are culled by nature itself, ensuring that only the strongest specimens remain. The weeds and brush also provide shade that prevents the soil from drying out and protects young trees from wind damage.

## Correcting Tree Form: The Critical Early Pruning

Some pinching back is generally necessary on a young transplanted sapling to correct the arrangement of the branches. This is because, if dieback occurs at the tip or if too much of the root system has been cut, an unnaturally large number of suckers may emerge, causing the branches to become entangled. When the young tree lies in the shadow of a large tree, it tends to become leggy, in which case the lower branches will often die back. Left to itself, such a tree will acquire an unnatural form that will result in years of unending labor for the grower; to hasten the tree's approach to a more natural form, shoots and buds emerging from unnatural places must be nipped off as soon as possible.

### The First Cuts: Determining the Tree's Future

Trees that show normal, steady growth right from the start assume a nearly natural form and can thereafter be left alone. Cutting the first one or two shoots is therefore very important. How well this is done can determine the shape of the tree over its entire lifetime and is a major factor in the success or failure of an orchard.

These initial cuts are deceptively simple. The grower must evaluate the young sapling's growth pattern and identify which shoots will become the primary scaffold branches. A shoot removed too early cannot be recovered; a shoot left too long may establish an unnatural branch angle that affects the tree for decades. The decision requires intimate knowledge of the natural form of the species and careful observation of the individual tree's growth tendencies.

### The Difficulty of Early Decisions

It is often hard to tell which shoots to leave and which to pinch off. The grower may decide, often prematurely, which branches are to be the primary scaffold branches and which the secondary scaffolds when the tree is still very young only to find later that these branches have tangled under other, unanticipated growth conditions. Early pruning can turn out to be unnecessary and even harmful when done unwisely.

This uncertainty is inherent to the process. The tree's growth is influenced by factors that cannot be fully predicted: weather patterns, competition from neighboring trees, soil conditions, and pest pressure. A branch that appears promising in its first year may develop problems in its third year. The grower must balance the need for early guidance against the risk of premature intervention.

### Natural Form Through Attention, Not Abandonment

It is all too easy to assume that a tree grown in a natural state will more easily acquire a natural form anyway. Yet it is not through abandonment that a cultivated tree takes on a natural form, but only through the most careful attention and protection. A tree that has become fully shaped while young will not need heavy pruning when mature. However, if left to grow untended when young, the tree may require considerable thinning and pruning each year and may even need major surgical reconstruction when fully grown.

Considering the many years of toil and the losses that may otherwise ensue, it is certainly preferable to choose to do some formative pruning early on. ### Phase One: Mixed Woody Vegetation (Years 0 to 4)

Four to five years after planting fruit trees, eulalia and other weeds grow thickly among the brush and assorted trees. Weeding is not easy and sometimes it is even hard to locate the fruit trees. Although the growth of fruit trees among this other vegetation was irregular and yielded poor harvests in some cases, there was very little damage from disease and insects. I found it hard to believe that, with the odd assortment of trees in my orchard and some of the fruit trees even growing in the shadow of other trees, these were spared attack by diseases and pests.

This phase is often discouraging to conventional orchardists, who see the weed competition as a problem to be solved. Fukuoka, however, recognizes it as a necessary stage in the establishment of a balanced ecosystem. The diverse plant community supports a diverse insect community, which in turn provides natural pest control. The fruit trees that survive this competitive phase are inherently stronger and better adapted to the site.

### Phase Two: Transition to Herbaceous Weeds (Years 4 to 6)

Later, with continued cutting back of the underbrush, the non-fruit trees recede and weeds such as bracken, mugwort, and kudzu grow up in their place. This represents a natural succession from woody vegetation to herbaceous plants. The woody plants are gradually shaded out and outcompeted as the soil surface becomes covered with herbaceous growth.

At this point, Fukuoka was able to control or suppress weed growth by broadcasting clover seed over the entire orchard. The timing of the clover introduction is critical: too early, and the clover cannot compete with the woody vegetation; too late, and the herbaceous weeds have already established dominance.

### Phase Three: Clover Dominance (Years 6 Onward)

After broadcasting clover, the ground cover gradually shifts to a clover-dominated system. The clover suppresses remaining weeds while fixing nitrogen  soil. This phase marks the transition from active weed management to clover maintenance. With proper care, the clover cover will persist for six to eight years before needing to be reseeded.

## Terracing: Building the Infrastructure

Five to six years after planting, when the trees begin to bear fruit, it is a good idea to dig up the earth on the uphill side of the fruit trees with a hoe and construct terrace-like steps and a road on the orchard slope. Once these terraces have been built and the original weeds replaced, first with soft weeds such as chickweed, knotweed, and crabgrass, then with clover, the orchard begins to look like an orchard.
