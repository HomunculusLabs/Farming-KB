# Mushrooms in Roman Times and Food Value

## Overview

The Roman relationship with mushrooms provides a fascinating window into the ancient history of mycophagy. References in Horace, Pliny, Juvenal, and Martial reveal both sophisticated culinary appreciation and social stratification around fungal foods. Complementing this historical perspective, an understanding of the nutritional composition of mushrooms helps place their modern culinary value in proper context — they are properly valued as a relish and flavoring rather than as a primary protein source.

## Mushrooms in Roman Times

### Sources of Knowledge

Sufficient information exists to indicate the esteem in which mushrooms were held by the Romans. The principal references are found in:

- **Horace** — through Catius, who repeated the substance of a discourse on things to eat
- **Pliny** — referred to mushrooms as dainties recently come into fashion
- **Juvenal** — numerous references to mushroom consumption and luxury
- **Martial** — references to boleti as delicacies

The Romans apparently obtained their knowledge of mushrooms through the Greeks, as there is no evidence they were used at Rome in very early times. The Roman writers who referred to these plants were active from less than half a century B.C. to about one century A.D.

### The "Boletus" — Roman Delicacy

The "boletus" (supposedly referring to *Amanita Casarea*, the Royal Agaric) was always reckoned among the delicacies or luxuries:

- Martial says "it is hard to give up the boleti"
- In reproving Cacilianus for gluttony: "In the presence of an invited assemblage you alone eat boleti"
- The boletus was clearly a luxury item, distinguishing the wealthy diner from common guests

### The "Fungi Suilli" — Common Fare

If *Boletus edulis* is the same as the fungus known to the Romans as "suilli," then this species has through many centuries formed a considerable part of the food of the common people of many sections of Europe. The Romans constantly referred to fungi under this name, but they were not considered the best mushrooms:

- Martial complains that at a dinner his host feasted upon oysters and "boleti," while he must be content with the "fungi suilli"
- The fungus designated as suilli was certainly cheap and common
- Frequently the intimation is given that fungi suilli were doubtfully edible
- Such untried mushrooms were placed before poorer persons at feasts
- The name may have referred to various species of the present genus Boletus

### Meadow Mushrooms

Catius states that "the fungi which frequent the open meadows are those that are to be trusted." There is nothing to indicate that the common mushroom *[[agaricus-campestris]]* was used at that time, except possibly we may read this into the information that the mushrooms of the open meadow were regarded as trustworthy.

### The Terfas of Classical Antiquity

The references to subterranean fungi in Theophrastus and other classical authors clearly concern the terfas (Terfeziaceae) of North Africa and the Middle East, not the [[european-truffles]]. The terfas were known to the Greeks but there is apparently no evidence that the European truffles were.

## Food Value of Mushrooms

### Mushrooms as Accessory Food

The cultivated mushroom is properly an **accessory food, or luxury**, deserving particular attention more as a relish or condimentary dish. As such it has a value that cannot adequately be expressed physiologically, much less measured by chemical data. Food values are of little consideration with products that are essentially a matter of taste.

### Price Comparisons

The relationship between food value and market price illustrates the primacy of flavor:

- Truffles may bring **twenty or thirty francs per kilo** in Paris
- Common mushrooms may sell at **two francs**
- Green beans at **less than a franc**

Comparing equal weights, beans have greater actual food value than mushrooms, and the mushroom is nearly as valuable nutritionally as the truffle. Because of their flavor, mushrooms command a special price; the peculiarly attractive flavor and unusual aroma of the truffle stamps it as distinctly a luxury. The same principle applies to fruit varieties: the price difference between a Ben Davis apple and a Jonathan apple — of approximately the same composition — measures our judgment of quality.

### Nutritional Composition

Mushrooms possess a chemical food value equal to that of many vegetables — better than some, poorer than others. However, it is misleading to speak of mushrooms as "vegetable meat" or to imply they possess special food value due to supposedly "animal-like" composition:

- They contain **far less protein than meat**, pound for pound of fresh material
- On the basis of dry substance, the mushroom does contain a relatively high percentage of protein
- Many pounds of mushrooms would be required to directly replace one pound of meat
- Wild mushrooms at European market prices are undoubtedly an important staple food

### The Bottom Line

The chief point for the mushroom is **quality and flavor**, which are quite sufficient. There would be no more reason for eating unsavory mushrooms than for eating tasteless legumes. Full justice will be done to cultivated and desirable native mushrooms when the public has adequate opportunity to utilize them under satisfactory conditions — primarily meaning **savory and appropriate cooking**. Mushrooms may be made delicious, whether for a picnic in the woods or for a dinner party.
