# Mushroom Harvesting and Preservation Methods

Proper harvesting technique is essential for maximizing total yield across
multiple flushes. The way mushrooms are picked can dramatically affect
future flushes. Damage to resting pinheads and disturbance of the casing
soil must be minimized during picking. This page covers harvesting
methods and preservation techniques for cultivated mushrooms.

## Timing the Harvest

The most important factor in harvesting mushrooms is timing. Different
species require different harvesting criteria. [[agaricus-brunnescens]] should
be picked before the veil breaks and the stem elongates. Psilocybe
cubensis, which is morphologically distinct with a longer stem, less
fleshy cap, and more delicate veil, naturally forms tall stands that are
both natural and desirable.

Cultivators of these two species share a common concern: the massive
release of spores from mature mushrooms. Spores often cover the casing
layer and can inhibit further pinhead development. High spore loads also
cause allergic reactions among workers. For these reasons, mushrooms
should be picked at the stage when the veil begins to tear or soon
thereafter.

## Harvesting Technique

The best pickers are meticulous, unhurried, and treat the mushrooms with
care. Carelessness, when multiplied by hundreds of cultures, can be
costly. Equipped with a basket and short-bladed paring knife, the picker
grasps the base of the stem and, with a twisting motion, pulls the
mushroom from the casing layer, being careful not to disturb neighboring
pinheads.

The stem base is then trimmed, removing only flesh to which casing or
substrate is attached. Mushrooms having thin stems are best cleaned
using a knife in a downward scraping motion. All trimmings should be
placed in a sealed plastic bag and removed from the cropping area.

## Handling Clusters

Mushrooms growing in clumps or clusters should be broken apart and
harvested individually when possible. Special care must be taken with
clumps containing both mature and immature mushrooms. Immature mushrooms
should be left attached to the casing layer or substrate to ensure
continued growth. Premature removal of developing pins destroys their
growth potential.

Flushes with mushrooms in varying stages of development are more
difficult to harvest. This is especially true if the primordia formation
period was interrupted by fluctuations in the environment. A phenomenon
common to [[psilocybe-cubensis]] culture in mason jars involves mushrooms
forming between the casing and glass, called "border breaks." These are
due to high humidity pockets and premature light stimulation.

## Managing Between Flushes

To increase flushing speed, the actual harvest period in each flush
should be kept short and concise. Late developing mushrooms are removed
with or on the day after peak production. The sooner the flush is
completely removed, the quicker the next one appears and the shorter the
overall cropping cycle.

Stunted, undeveloped mushrooms should be cleared from the cropping
surface between breaks with care not to disturb the casing. Small dead
pinheads should be left in place and cause little harm. An aborted
mushroom can be removed as long as the casing is not touched in the
process. At no time should the casing be over-handled in an attempt to
clean, as this can spread disease spores and damage subsequent pin
formation.

## Air Drying

If not served within four days, mushrooms can be preserved by drying,
freezing, or canning. Air drying is the method most widely used by home
cultivators and field hunters. Since most mushrooms are 90 percent water,
they must be dried within a few hours or fly larvae and bacteria will
consume them.

Provided mushrooms are placed in a flow of warm, dry air, the water soon
evaporates. Dried mushrooms are smaller, lighter, and less fragrant than
fresh ones. Once dried, they are sealed in airtight moisture-proof
plastic containers and refrigerated. Mushrooms preserved in this manner
will last for years. When needed, they are rehydrated in water before
cooking, regaining much of their original size and flavor.

Commercially available food dehydrators are well suited for drying
mushrooms, though their trays are often too close together, necessitating
cutting large mushrooms into thin slices. A good dryer should dry
mushrooms in 24 to 48 hours using warm air no hotter than 110 degrees F.
Open air drying at room temperature is also feasible using dehumidifiers
combined with air circulation fans.

"Flash" drying at high temperatures should be avoided since mushrooms lose
much of their nutritive value and active compound content.

## Freezing and Freeze Drying

Freezing is another preservation method. Unless the mushrooms are first
dried, frozen mushrooms are soggy and unappealing upon thawing. The
water constituting 90 percent of a mushroom's mass becomes crystallized.
Frozen mushrooms are held together more by ice crystals than their own
cellular structure. Since ice expands upon crystallization, cells break
under the stress. Frozen mushrooms are mostly used in soups or stews.

Freeze drying is the ideal method for preserving flavor, nutrition, form,
and active compound content. Freeze dryers first flash-freeze fresh
mushrooms, which are then placed onto heated trays in a cooled,
high-vacuum chamber. Frozen water transforms directly to vapor, preserving
much of the original cell structure. Since commercial freeze driers are
prohibitively expensive, few home cultivators can afford them. However,
mushrooms placed in a frost-free refrigerator are almost as well preserved.

## Canning

Mushrooms preserved by canning must be carefully cleaned, precooked for
3 to 4 minutes in boiling water, then inserted into glass jars with a
small amount of vinegar and sterilized in a pressure cooker. Sterilization
for mushrooms is usually 30 to 40 minutes at 10 psi. Canned mushrooms,
especially those that have been pickled, are preferred by many epicureans
to those preserved by other means.

## Labeling and Safety

No matter the technique, fresh mushrooms are preferred. Young mushrooms
should be selected over old ones. Each container should be labeled with
species, grower or identifier, date, and place of origin.

A general rule recommended by all mycologists: when eating wild mushrooms
for the first time, always save a specimen in case illness ensues and a
mycologist or doctor needs to be consulted.
