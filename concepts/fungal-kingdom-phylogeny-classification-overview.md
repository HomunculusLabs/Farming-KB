# Fungal Kingdom Phylogeny and Classification Overview

The fungal kingdom (Fungi) represents one of the most diverse and ecologically important groups of organisms on Earth,
with estimated species counts ranging from 2.2 million to 3.8 million — of which only approximately 150,000 have been formally described.
The classification of fungi has undergone dramatic revisions in the molecular era,
as DNA-based phylogenetic analyses have repeatedly revealed that morphological similarity does not reliably reflect evolutionary relationships.
Modern fungal systematics integrates molecular data with traditional morphological,
ecological,
and biochemical characters to produce a classification that reflects evolutionary history.

## Historical Classification Systems

### Traditional Morphological Classification

For most of the [[ectomycorrhizal-morphological-structures]] that provide few characters for classification.
Convergent evolution of similar structures in unrelated lineages was common.
And the morphological characters used for classification often reflected adaptation to ecological niches rather than shared evolutionary ancestry.

### The Molecular Revolution

The introduction of molecular phylogenetic methods in the 1990s,
particularly ribosomal DNA sequencing,  transformed fungal systematics.
Early rDNA-based studies revealed that several traditional taxonomic groups were polyphyletic (containing organisms from multiple evolutionary lineages).
The Zygomycota,  for example,
was found to comprise multiple distinct lineages scattered across the fungal tree of life.
The Chytridiomycota was similarly revealed to be paraphyletic,
with some lineages more closely related to non-chytrid fungi than to other chytrids.

These molecular findings triggered a comprehensive reclassification of the fungal kingdom,
culminating in the multi-author consensus classification published by Hibbett et al.
(2007) in the journal Mycologia.
This landmark paper established a phylogenetically informed framework for fungal classification that has been progressively refined as additional molecular data have become available.

## Modern Phylum-Level Classification

### Basidiomycota

The Basidiomycota (club fungi) is one of the two major phyla of the "higher fungi" (Dikarya) and includes the familiar mushroom-forming fungi,
as well as puffballs,  bracket fungi,  rusts,  smuts,  and jelly fungi.
Basidiomycota are characterized by the production of basidia — specialized cells on which sexual spores (basidiospores) are borne externally.
The phylum is enormously diverse ecologically,
including essential decomposers of wood and litter,
plant pathogens of enormous economic importance (rusts and smuts),
and ecologically critical mycorrhizal symbionts.

Molecular phylogenetics has revealed three major subphyla within Basidiomycota: Pucciniomycotina (rusts and their relatives),
Ustilaginomycotina (smuts and their relatives),
and Agaricomycotina (the mushroom-forming fungi and their allies).
The Agaricomycotina is by far the most species-rich and morphologically diverse,
encompassing everything from the familiar gilled mushrooms and boletes to the coral fungi,
chanterelles,  and polypores.

### Ascomycota

The Ascomycota (sac fungi) is the largest phylum of fungi in terms of described species,
with approximately 64,000 known species.
The defining characteristic of Ascomycota is the ascus — a sac-like cell in which sexual spores (ascospores) are produced following meiosis.
Ascomycota includes yeasts (including the model organism [[biodiversity-powdery-mildews]] and the causative agents of Dutch elm disease and chestnut blight),
lichen-forming fungi,
and endophytic fungi that live within plant tissues without causing disease.

The Ascomycota is divided into three major subphyla: Pezizomycotina (the filamentous ascomycetes,
which produce multicellular fruiting bodies),
Saccharomycotina (the true yeasts),
and Taphrinomycotina (a small group of fission yeasts and plant parasites).
The Pezizomycotina contains the vast majority of described ascomycete species and is further divided into numerous classes and orders.

### Chytridiomycota

The Chytridiomycota (chytrids) are the most basal group of fungi and are unique among fungi in producing flagellated zoospores — motile spores that swim through water films.
This flagellated stage reflects the aquatic ancestry of the fungal kingdom.
Most chytrids are microscopic and found in aquatic or moist terrestrial environments,
where they function as decomposers,  parasites of algae and other fungi,
or in some cases as pathogens of amphibians (the chytrid [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]) was elevated from an order within Zygomycota to its own phylum based on molecular evidence,
reflecting their ancient evolutionary divergence and their critical ecological role as symbionts of the vast majority of land plants.

### Glomeromycota

The Glomeromycota are of particular ecological significance because they form arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) associations with approximately 80% of all land plant species.
In these symbioses,  the fungal hyphae colonize plant root cells,
forming highly branched structures called arbuscules that facilitate the exchange of nutrients and carbon between the partners.
AM fungi are obligate symbionts — they cannot complete their life cycle without a host plant — and they play critical roles in plant nutrient acquisition (particularly phosphorus),
drought tolerance,  and disease resistance.

## The Dikarya and Early-Diverging Fungi

Modern fungal classification distinguishes between the Dikarya (Ascomycota plus Basidiomycota,
united by the shared derived character of dikaryotic hyphae in which each cell contains two genetically distinct nuclei) and the early-diverging fungal lineages (Chytridiomycota,
Blastocladiomycota,  Mucoromycota,  Zoopagomycota,  and Glomeromycota).
The early-diverging lineages are paraphyletic with respect to the Dikarya,
meaning they do not form a natural clade but rather represent a series of successive branches on the fungal tree leading to the Dikarya.

The ecological and morphological diversity of the early-diverging fungi is considerable.
They include aquatic decomposers,  soil fungi,  insect parasites,
and the essential arbuscular mycorrhizal symbionts. However,
they are generally less species-rich and less morphologically complex than the Dikarya,
which contain the vast majority of described fungal species and produce the most elaborate fruiting bodies.

## Ongoing Revision and Future Directions

Fungal classification remains an active and rapidly evolving field.
As genome sequencing becomes increasingly affordable and computational methods for phylogenetic analysis become more sophisticated,
the resolution of the fungal tree of life continues to improve.
Several areas of active research include the resolution of deep phylogenetic relationships among the early-diverging lineages,
the delimitation of species boundaries within morphologically conservative groups,
and the integration of genome-scale data into fungal classification systems.
