# Mycorrhizal Nematode Interactions

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play a significant role in mediating interactions between plants and
soil-borne pests, including plant-parasitic nematodes. These interactions span both
arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) and ectomycorrhizal (ECM) associations, representing an
important dimension of the broader relationship between mycorrhizae and plant pathogens.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Suppression of Nematodes

### Mechanisms of Suppression

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] reduce plant-parasitic nematode damage through several
interrelated mechanisms:

- **Enhanced host plant vigor:** AM colonization improves phosphorus and micronutrient
  uptake, producing plants with greater tolerance to nematode feeding.
- **Reduced nematode reproduction:** AM colonization directly suppresses nematode
  fecundity, reducing egg production and population growth rates.
- **Altered root architecture and chemistry:** AM fungi modify root morphology and
  exudate profiles, reducing nematode attraction and impairing feeding site establishment.
- **Systemic defense priming:** AM colonization can prime systemic plant defense
  responses, activating biochemical pathways that deter nematode invasion.

### Quantitative Evidence: *Glomus intraradices* and *Radopholus similis*

Elsen et al. (2001) demonstrated that *[[glomus-intraradices]]* colonization of carrot
roots significantly suppressed the burrowing nematode *Radopholus similis* (Table 3.24):

| Nematode Stage | AM Absent | AM Present | Reduction |
|---------------|-----------|------------|-----------|
| Juvenile      | 2950      | 1662       | ~44%      |
| Female        | 1739      | 609        | ~65%      |
| Male          | 384       | 117        | ~70%      |

Differences were statistically significant across all life stages. Suppression was
strongest for adults, suggesting AM colonization interferes with nematode maturation
and reproductive success. Overall suppression was approximately 50% — agriculturally
significant and comparable to some conventional nematicide treatments.

## Ectomycorrhizal Biocontrol Against Root Pathogens

Ectomycorrhizal fungi protect host trees through a dense fungal mantle that prevents
direct pathogen–root contact, combined with antibiotic secondary metabolites that
suppress pathogen growth in the rhizosphere.

### Foundational Studies

**Marx (1969, 1980)** first demonstrated that ectomycorrhizal colonization of pine
roots provided significant protection from pathogenic fungi under nursery conditions,
establishing the rationale for deliberate inoculation of nursery stock with ECM fungi.

*Laccaria laccata* is a particularly well-studied ECM biocontrol agent:

- **Sylvia and Sinclair (1983):** *L. laccata* suppressed *Fusarium oxysporum* on
  Douglas fir seedlings, reducing disease incidence and improving growth.
- **Chakravarty and Hwang (1991):** The same fungus protected *Pinus banksiana*
  from *Fusarium*, through both physical barrier and antifungal compound production.

**Branzanti et al. (1999)** tested four ECM species — *Laccaria laccata*, *Hebeloma
crustuliniforme*, *H. sinapizans*, and *Paxillus involutus* — against *Phytophthora
cambivora* and *P. cinnamomi* on chestnut seedlings. All four species provided
significant protection against both pathogens, suggesting biocontrol is a general
feature of the ectomycorrhizal symbiosis rather than a species-specific property.

### Mycorrhizae as Biocontrol Agents

The accumulated evidence has generated substantial interest in mycorrhizal fungi as
biological control agents (Duchesne 1994; Quarles 1999). A single inoculation can
provide both nutritional benefits and protection against multiple pathogen classes.
Commercial application remains limited by mass-production challenges, variable field
results, and competition with resident [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]].

## Fungi in Primary Production: Synopsis and Outlook

### Moderators of Primary Production

Fungi play central roles in moderating rates and patterns of primary production in
terrestrial ecosystems. Mycorrhizal fungi extend root absorbing surface area, facilitating
uptake of phosphorus, nitrogen, and other limiting nutrients. Lichenized fungi contribute
directly to primary production but are notably sensitive to land use change and pollution,
serving as valuable bioindicators of ecosystem health.

### Effects Beyond Nutrient Uptake

Mycorrhizal associations influence [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]], competitive hierarchies,
and interactions with other soil organisms including pathogens and herbivores. Nematode
and pathogen protection represents one facet of this broader ecological influence.

### Inter-Plant Linkages via Mycorrhizal Networks

**Simard et al. (1997a, 1997b)** provided landmark evidence that carbon, nitrogen, and
other resources move between individual plants through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]].
Labeled carbon moved from birch trees into neighboring Douglas fir seedlings, suggesting
mycorrhizal networks facilitate resource transfer between species with different
nutritional strategies. This raises fundamental questions:

- **Resource sharing:** Do mycorrhizal networks facilitate cooperative exchange, or
  primarily serve as conduits for competitive exploitation?
- **Ecosystem stability:** Does inter-plant resource transfer contribute to community
  stability and resilience under stress conditions?
- **Functional redundancy:** How much redundancy exists within mycorrhizal communities?
  Are there critical functions dependent on specific fungal taxa?

### Mycorrhizal Transitions During Development

Some tree species shift from AM to ECM associations with age, reflecting changing
nutritional demands and root architectures. This ontogenetic shift may also alter
defensive relationships with soil pathogens. Some ericaceous plants support multiple
mycorrhizal types simultaneously (ericoid and arbuscular), providing nutritional
flexibility and broadening the range of viable ecological conditions.

## Open Questions

1. **Natural vs. agricultural ecosystems:** Most evidence comes from agricultural
   systems with high pathogen pressure. How important are these protective interactions
   in structuring natural plant communities with diverse mycorrhizal assemblages?

2. **Long-term community manipulation effects:** What are the consequences of
   deliberately introducing mycorrhizal fungi into natural ecosystems? Does introducing
   non-native species alter resident [[fungal-community-dynamics-ecosystem-stability]], and can these changes
   be reversed if unintended consequences arise?

3. **Context dependency:** Given that approximately half of AM–pathogen studies show
   no protective effect or even negative effects, what determines whether a specific
   AM–nematode interaction benefits the host plant?

4. **Integration with soil food webs:** How do mycorrhizal-mediated effects on nematodes
   and pathogens interact with the broader [[soil-food-web]], including bacterial communities,
   fungal-feeding nematodes, and microarthropods that regulate pathogen populations?
