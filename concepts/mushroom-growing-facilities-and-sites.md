# Mushroom Growing Facilities and Sites

## Overview

Mushrooms may be grown in a variety of situations, provided that the key environmental factors — particularly temperature and moisture — can be adequately controlled. The selection of a growing site is fundamentally a question of environmental regulation rather than any mysterious requirement specific to mushrooms.

## The Parisian Cave System

### Origin of the Caves

The French mushroom industry owes its enormous scale to the subterranean limestone quarries beneath and around Paris. Parisian buildings were constructed primarily of limestone mined from beneath the city itself and from adjacent suburbs. Mining operations were conducted to leave vast labyrinthine systems of caves. The caves lying directly under the city proper (the catacombs) are not used for [[champignoniere]]" (mushroom cave) became practically synonymous with "carriere" (quarry).

### Ventilation

The cave systems are ventilated by means of chimneys or chutes. Air circulation is often facilitated by small charcoal fires placed beneath the air chutes, and occasionally by special ventilating devices. This ventilation is critical — without adequate air exchange, the caves would accumulate harmful gases and excess moisture.

### Access

Entrance to the champignonieres may be through an open quarry face that is more or less readily accessible, or through a hole only a few feet in diameter, accessed by ladder with a windlass for lifting and lowering materials.

## Growing Facilities in the United States

### Overview

American mushroom production has been confined to special houses, caves, and cellars. In such places, temperature, moisture, and other environmental factors can be more readily regulated. Near Philadelphia, Chicago, Boston, and other production centers, mushrooms were grown wholly in special mushroom houses, and unquestionably the major part of the American market supply was produced this way.

### Underground Facilities

Some commercial growers in Minnesota, Ohio, Indiana, New York, and Missouri secured underground quarries, caves, or storage cellars of considerable extent. Such places are excellent when readily accessible, provided there is no danger from flooding or seepage water. Natural caves in the limestone sections of Missouri were used successfully, and natural caves also exist in Kentucky, Indiana, Ohio, Virginia, and Arkansas.

### Limitations of Underground Sites

Several practical limitations affect the use of caves and underground sites:
- Poor arrangements for removing old beds can lead to accumulation of spent compost and increased pest problems
- After several years of accumulated bedding material, old cave gardens have too often been abandoned for new ones
- Inaccessible caves may face prohibitive costs for manure delivery
- Adequate ventilation must always be possible

### Advantages of Underground Sites

Caves and deep cellars have the significant advantage of permitting mushroom production during a long period — frequently throughout the entire year — due to their naturally stable temperatures.

## Mushroom House Construction

### Simple Structures

For temporary use, a mushroom house may consist of a very simple boarded shed or unused barn. A heater is necessary since winter is the important season for mushroom work. Many commercial mushroom houses that pay well are not particularly elaborate.

### Permanent Construction

As a permanent investment, the house should be built with due consideration for convenience, durability, and economy. Key construction principles include:

**Drainage**: Perfect drainage is a factor of the first importance. This may be complicated by the fact that for convenience in heating and for more uniform temperature during warmer months, it is often desirable to construct the house over an excavation several feet below the natural surface.

**Tiered Beds**: The greatest economy is attained by constructing houses high enough to accommodate beds in tiers of from two to five levels. Approximately 2.25 feet of free space is needed between tiers, since beds are at least 8 to 10 inches deep, and somewhat more than 3 feet in height must be allowed for each bed level.

**Wall Construction**: The better class of mushroom houses traditionally featured double walls packed with fresh shavings for insulation. More expensive houses may be made of hollow tile or concrete. Hollow tile has much to recommend it because the constant moist atmosphere inside a mushroom house causes moisture-laden air to contact cold walls, producing constant condensation ("sweating").

**Roofing**: A sufficiently sloping roof with an air space corrects much of the condensation difficulty.

**Ventilation**: Ventilation arrangements should always be provided, and it is usually advisable to place ventilators in the roof, as this accomplishes the purpose with less inconvenience from direct drafts.

### Bed Support Construction

When beds are prepared on floors, the matter is simple. But for commercial production using every foot of available space, tier bed construction requires particular attention:

- Upright supports for beds in tiers of four or five should be scantling not less than 2x6 inches, placed about 4 feet apart
- Experienced commercial growers have used large gas piping for supports, which offers great permanency and is ideal from a sanitary standpoint
- Wall beds should seldom be more than 3 feet across
- Beds approachable from either side may be 6 feet across
- Aisles of 3 feet are usually sufficient, with one or more wider aisles in very large houses

## Open Air Culture

### English Practice

In Southern England, where winter temperature extremes are less severe, culture in modified hot beds or cold frames has been practiced. Simple frames of the usual type are employed, but without glass — covered instead with sheet iron or boards, with a layer of straw placed on top if necessary. Fresh manure may be used under the compost to force early spawn growth.

### General Assessment

Open air culture cannot be regarded as having great possibilities of success in most regions. The difficulties include:
- Considerable daily temperature variation
- Drying winds
- Difficulty regulating moisture
- The commercial requirement for relatively uniform temperature

### Potential for California

Sections of California with mean monthly temperatures of 50 to 60 degrees F and without pronounced daily range might support open-air culture. The presence of both *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. arvensis* in parts of California during winter months supports this possibility. A mean temperature somewhat below optimal requirements (48 to 56 degrees F) may actually be desirable, and beds must be protected from drenching rains.

### Field and Pasture Spawning

Inquiries about spawning pastures and lawns to establish natural cultures for increased autumn supply have generally met with inconsistent results. Both American and English experimenters report that success depends entirely on the season and that results are wholly inconstant. Nevertheless, the labor involved is so minimal that spreading spawn about lawns or pastures when it is available may be worth the occasional favorable outcome.
