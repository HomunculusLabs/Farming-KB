# Weed Control With Weeds and the Clover Ground Cover Discovery

## Overview

One of the most counterintuitive yet practically significant innovations in Masanobu Fukuoka's [[natural-farming-animal-husbandry]] system is the principle of weed control with weeds, and the discovery that white clover serves as the ideal living ground cover for both rice and barley fields.

Rather than fighting weeds as enemies to be eradicated, Fukuoka learned to work with them, using competitive plant communities and strategic timing to manage weed populations without herbicides or cultivation. The introduction of white clover into his rotation system was the key breakthrough that made this approach viable on a commercial scale.

## The Weed Problem in No-Tillage Farming

When Fukuoka first abandoned plowing and herbicides, weeds became one of his most serious challenges. In a conventional rice field, weeds are controlled through repeated flooding, cultivation, and herbicide application. In Fukuoka's no-tillage system, none of these methods were available.

The field was not plowed, herbicides were not used, and the water management was designed to mimic natural conditions rather than create the flooded environment that suppresses many weed species.

Initially, weed growth was rampant. Without cultivation to disrupt their life cycle and without herbicides to kill them, weeds proliferated and threatened to overwhelm the crops. Fukuoka observed that in the first years after abandoning conventional practices, weed populations actually increased before they began to decline. This was because the soil seed bank, accumulated over years of conventional farming, contained vast quantities of dormant weed seeds that germinated once the soil was no longer being regularly disturbed.

## The Discovery of Competitive Exclusion

Fukuoka's key insight was that weeds could be controlled not by killing them but by creating conditions in which crop plants had a competitive advantage. He observed that in natural ecosystems, plants coexist in stable communities not because any species has been eradicated but because each species occupies a niche defined by light, nutrients, moisture, and timing.

The goal of natural weed management was not to eliminate weeds entirely but to create a plant community in which crops dominated and weeds were suppressed to tolerable levels.

He noticed that certain weed species actually helped rather than hindered crop growth. Deep-rooted weeds brought nutrients up from subsoil layers and deposited them on the surface when they died. Low-growing weeds protected the soil surface from erosion and maintained soil moisture. Some weeds attracted beneficial insects that preyed on crop pests.

The conventional view that all weeds are harmful was, in Fukuoka's estimation, one of the most damaging misconceptions in modern agriculture.

## The Scientist's Approach versus Nature's Approach

Fukuoka drew a sharp contrast between the scientific and natural approaches to weeds. The scientist planning to cultivate high-yielding rice on a field sees the weeds growing at his feet only as pests that will rob sunlight and nutrients from the rice plants. He believes, understandably, that he will be able to achieve the highest possible yields by totally eradicating such intruders and ensuring that the rice plants monopolize the sun's incident rays.

But removing weeds with herbicides is all it takes to upset the delicate balance of nature. The herbicides destroy the ecosystem of the insects and microorganisms dependent on the weeds, abruptly changing the current of life in the soil bio-community. An imbalance in this living soil inevitably throws all the other organisms there off balance as well. Unbalanced rice is diseased rice, and therefore highly susceptible to concentrated attack by disease and insect pests.

Before pulling the weeds growing at the base of the rice plants, natural farming asks why they are there. Are these grasses the by-product of human action or did they arise spontaneously and naturally? If the latter, then they are without doubt of value and are left to grow. The natural farmer takes care to allow natural plants that protect the natural soil to carry out their mission.

## The Three-Dimensional Energy Argument

Green manure thriving at the foot of the rice plants and, later, algae growing on the flooded field are thought to detract from yields because they directly and indirectly shield the sun, reducing the amount of light received by the rice plants. But we reach a different conclusion if we see this as a nearly natural state.

The total energy absorbed by the rice, green manure, algae, and earth is greater than the energy stored from the sun's rays by the rice plants alone. Energy from the sun is absorbed by the green manure plants. When the field is flooded, these wither and die, passing on their nitrogen to algae, which in turn become a source of phosphate.

Using this phosphate as a nutrient source, microbes in the soil flourish and die, leaving nutrients that are absorbed by the roots of the rice plants. The true value of energy cannot be determined merely by counting the number of calories. The quality of the energy produced within the plant by conversion from absorbed energy must also be taken into account.

## The Principle of Weed Control With Weeds

The principle of weed control with weeds emerged from these observations. Rather than removing all weeds, Fukuoka allowed certain beneficial or harmless weed species to grow alongside his crops while managing more aggressive species through competition and timing.

The rice and barley crops themselves, when healthy and vigorous, were the primary weed control agents. A dense stand of barley shaded the soil surface and prevented weed germination in the spring. A thick rice canopy performed the same function in summer.

Fukuoka also used strategic flooding to control weeds in the rice crop. By flooding the field briefly during critical periods, he could suppress weeds that were sensitive to waterlogging without harming the rice. This was not the continuous flooding of conventional rice cultivation but a carefully timed intervention that worked with the natural tendencies of the crop and weed species involved.

## The Introduction of White Clover

The breakthrough in sustainable weed management came with the introduction of white clover as a permanent ground cover. Fukuoka discovered that white clover possessed a unique combination of characteristics that made it ideal for his system.

As a legume, it fixed atmospheric nitrogen, reducing or eliminating the need for external nitrogen inputs. As a low-growing perennial, it formed a dense mat that suppressed weed germination without competing strongly with taller crop plants for light.

Clover also had the remarkable ability to go dormant under stress. During the rice growing season, when the field was flooded, the clover retreated to its root crown and survived in a dormant state. When the water was drained after the rice harvest, the clover sprang back to life, covering the soil surface and beginning its growth cycle anew. This ability to alternate between active growth and dormancy in response to changing conditions made it perfectly suited to the rice-barley rotation.

## Clover as Living Mulch

The role of clover in Fukuoka's system extended far beyond simple [[weed-suppression-strategies]]. Clover served as a living mulch that performed many of the functions normally requiring human labor or chemical inputs. It fixed nitrogen, reducing the need for fertilizer. It protected the soil from erosion during heavy rains. It maintained soil moisture during dry periods by shading the soil surface. It provided habitat for beneficial insects, including predators of crop pests.

And when it was trampled or mowed to prevent it from overwhelming young crop seedlings, the decomposing clover biomass served as a green manure that fed [[soil-microorganisms-natural-fertility]] and crop plants alike. Fukuoka observed that fields with well-established clover ground cover developed a distinctive soil ecosystem. Earthworm populations increased dramatically, their burrows improving [[soil-structure-and-aggregate-ecology]] and water infiltration.

Beneficial fungi formed mycorrhizal associations with crop roots, extending the effective root system and improving nutrient uptake. The soil surface developed a soft, spongy texture that absorbed rainfall readily and resisted compaction.

## Managing Clover Competition

The primary challenge with clover was preventing it from competing too strongly with crop seedlings, particularly during the establishment phase. Young barley and rice plants were vulnerable to being smothered by vigorous clover growth. Fukuoka developed several techniques for managing this competition.

The most important was timing: by broadcasting crop seeds at the right moment, he could ensure that crop seedlings emerged and established themselves before the clover began its most active growth phase. He also used the weight of rice [[straw-mulch-in-agriculture]] to suppress clover in the areas where crop seeds had been sown.

The straw slowed clover growth while allowing crop seedlings to push through. In some cases, he trampled the clover lightly before sowing, damaging but not killing it, to give crop seedlings a head start. These techniques required careful observation and timing but no special equipment or inputs.

## The Transition Period

Fukuoka was candid about the difficulties of transitioning from conventional weed management to his natural approach. The first two to three years after stopping herbicide use were the hardest. Weed pressure was highest, crop yields were lowest, and the temptation to resort to conventional methods was strongest.

Many farmers who tried his approach gave up during this transition period, concluding that natural farming did not work. Fukuoka insisted that patience through this transition was essential. As the soil ecosystem recovered from years of chemical and mechanical disturbance, natural competitive relationships reestablished themselves.

Clover populations built up to levels where they effectively suppressed weeds. Soil organic matter increased, improving soil structure and water-holding capacity. The weed seed bank in the soil gradually depleted as seeds germinated and were outcompeted by crops and clover. After three to five years, the system stabilized and weed pressure declined to manageable levels.

## Long-Term Results and Ecological Succession

In the long term, fields managed with clover ground cover and weed control with weeds required progressively less intervention. The system became self-regulating, with clover, crops, and a controlled population of weeds coexisting in a dynamic equilibrium. Weeds that remained in the system were those that occupied niches not exploited by crops or clover, and they caused no significant yield reduction.

Fukuoka noted that the weed species composition in his fields changed over time. Aggressive annual weeds that dominated in the early years were gradually replaced by more benign perennial species that integrated smoothly into the plant community. This succession toward a more stable and diverse plant community was, in his view, evidence that nature was restoring its own balance when given the opportunity to do so.

The system required no herbicides, no cultivation, and minimal weeding. The farmer's role shifted from fighting weeds to observing and gently guiding the natural succession of plant communities.

## Practical Weed Identification and Management

Fukuoka did not advocate allowing all weeds to grow unchecked. He distinguished between beneficial weeds, neutral weeds, and truly harmful weeds, and managed each category differently. Beneficial weeds, such as clover and certain leguminous species, were actively encouraged. Neutral weeds that neither helped nor significantly harmed crops were tolerated.

Only truly harmful weeds that threatened to overwhelm crops were actively managed, and even then, the preferred method was competitive suppression rather than physical removal.

Harmful weeds were managed through a combination of crop competition, strategic flooding, mowing, and the creation of unfavorable conditions for their growth. Fukuoka found that most problem weeds could be controlled by adjusting the timing of seeding, the depth of flooding, or the density of the crop stand. These adjustments required knowledge of the specific weed species and their life cycles, knowledge that Fukuoka acquired through years of careful observation.

The practical lesson was that weed management in a natural farming system requires more knowledge of ecology and less knowledge of chemistry than conventional weed management. The farmer must understand how plants interact with each other and with their environment, rather than merely knowing which herbicide kills which weed.
