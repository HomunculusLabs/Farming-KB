# Mushroom Tropisms: Phototropism and Geotropism

Mushroom fruitbody development involves directed growth responses (tropisms) to environmental stimuli. Badham (1982) studied these tropisms in [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] using a controlled wind tunnel, revealing two distinct phases of directed growth with different mechanisms.

## Two-Phase Tropic Model

Badham proposed that P. cubensis exhibits two phases of directed growth:

### Phase 1: Anemotropism (Stages 1-2)

During primary stipe elongation, growth is directed toward the source of greatest evaporative demand:

- Stipes grow **into the wind** (up to Stage 2)
- When rotated perpendicular to wind, growth is upright (negative geotropism)
- Occurs in both light and darkness
- Occurs in both fertile and sterile (sporeless) basidiocarps
- Mechanism: differential evaporative demand creates asymmetric cell expansion — the windward side experiences greater drying, and cells on the leeward side expand faster

The evaporative demand gradient depends on four factors: wind velocity, humidity, light, and temperature. This model is analogous to the avoidance response in Phycomyces blakesleeanus.

### Phase 2: Negative Geotropism (Stage 2+)

At maturity, when spore production begins, a negative geotropic response takes over:

- The pileus turns upward from the direction of wind
- Lamellae face downward for optimal spore dispersal
- **Requires light** — does not occur in darkness
- **Requires spore production** — sporeless mutants did not exhibit this response
- A substance produced in the gills likely modifies the Phase 1 tropism

This connection between geotropism and sporulation suggests that gill-produced hormones regulate the tropic switch.

## Developmental Stages

| Stage | Pileus Description | Dominant Tropism |
|-------|-------------------|------------------|
| 1 | Slight swelling | Anemotropism |
| 2 | Nearly spherical | Transition |
| 3 | Open hemispherical (sporulation) | Negative geotropism |
| 4 | Plane (late maturity) | Negative geotropism |

## Role of the Partial Veil

Plunkett (1961) suggested that the partial veil protects the uppermost unexpanded portion of the stipe from wind or light prior to the final readjustment of the cap before spore release. This is consistent with the two-phase model.

## Phototropism

Light influences tropisms in several ways:
- Required for spore production (and thus Phase 2 geotropism)
- May influence the evaporative demand gradient by heating the mushroom surface
- Exerts some inhibitory effect on stipe elongation
- Necessary for at least two lifecycle phases: [[light-basidiocarp-initiation-psilocybe-cubensis-research]] and spore formation

## Hormonal Basis

Several researchers have postulated mushroom hormones involved in tropisms:
- Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963) linked tropic hormones to the lamellae
- Agar diffusates from the gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus (Hagimoto & Konishi, 1960)
- Indole acetic acid (IAA) occurs in basidiocarp fruitbodies (Konishi & Hagimoto, 1961) but no evidence links it to growth regulation
- Ethylene may be involved (analogous to Phycomyces autochemotropism)

## Adaptive Significance

- **Anemotropism** places caps in favourable positions for spore dispersal by orienting toward airflow
- **Directed growth toward evaporative demand** increases spacing between stipes of cespitose (clustered) fruitbodies
- **Negative geotropism** ensures a horizontal pileus for effective spore release, critical for coprophilous species in unstable habitats

## Comparison with Phycomyces and Other Fungi

The tropic responses of [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] parallel those documented in other fungi:

- **Phycomyces blakesleeanus** exhibits both anemotropism and an avoidance response (Cohen et al., 1975), with wind and water vapor as critical factors (Johnson & Gamow, 1971). The mechanism of stipe curvature in P. cubensis may be analogous.
- **Polyporus brumalis** stipes show a change in tropism during development (Plunkett, 1961), similar to the two-phase model in P. cubensis.
- **Schizophyllum commune** shows phototropic and geotropic responses during normal and mutant fruitbody development (Schwalb & Shanler, 1974).
- **Agaricus bisporus** contains a mushroom growth hormone linked to the lamellae (Gruen, 1963; Hagimoto, 1963), with agar diffusates from gills partially replacing pileus influence (Hagimoto & Konishi, 1960).

## Ethylene and Autochemotropism

Russo et al. (1977) demonstrated that ethylene is involved in autochemotropism of Phycomyces. This finding raises the possibility that ethylene or similar volatile compounds play a role in the tropic responses of basidiomycetes. The gill-produced substance that triggers the Phase 1 to Phase 2 transition in P. cubensis may include ethylene or function through similar signalling pathways.

## Cultivation Implications

Understanding these tropisms has practical value for [[mushroom-cultivation-in-kenya]]:

1. **Airflow direction matters** during early fruiting stages -- stipes grow toward the source of greatest evaporative demand
2. **Uneven lighting** can cause curved or distorted fruitbodies during Stages 1-2
3. **Rotating containers** during early fruiting can promote upright growth
4. **Phase 2 geotropism** ensures self-correction of cap orientation as mushrooms approach maturity, even if early growth was misdirected
5. **Sporeless mutants** will not self-correct at maturity -- useful consideration for [[mushroom-strain-selection-genetics]]

## Anatomical Basis of Stipe Elongation

Heim and Wasson (1958) described the anatomy of early development in P. cubensis: much of the cell division has occurred by the time primordia are a few cm high. Further growth involves acropetal expansion of these cells (Heim & Wasson, 1958; Plunkett, 1961) and possibly some cell division at the upper part of the stipe (Craig, 1977). This means that tropic curvature is largely achieved through differential cell expansion rather than new cell formation.

## Wind Tunnel Conditions

Badham's experimental setup: wind velocity 9 +/- 3 cm/sec, humidity 94 +/- 3% RH, temperature 24.5 +/- 1.5°C, light 0.002 calories/cm^2/min, 12h photoperiod. Primordia were 2cm high when placed in the chamber and removed 72h later at maturity. The wind tunnel had a working area of 43 x 30 x 20 cm with humidity controlled by a spin-type humidifier, temperature by a proportioner, and air filtered through polyester fiber.
