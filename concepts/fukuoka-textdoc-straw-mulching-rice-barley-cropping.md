# Straw Mulching in Natural Rice Barley Cropping

The scattering of uncut rice and barley straw over the field surface is the cornerstone practice of Fukuoka's natural rice-barley succession cropping system.
What appears crude and backward to the scientific eye is, in Fukuoka's view, "quite a bold and revolutionary step in rice farming." The practice simultaneously
promotes seed germination, suppresses winter weeds, enriches the soil, and eliminates the need for composting — making it the single operation that most clearly
distinguishes natural farming from both conventional and organic agriculture.

## The Conventional Wisdom Against Fresh Straw

At the time Fukuoka began experimenting, no farmer spread fresh straw on his paddy fields. Agricultural experts strictly forbade the practice, fearing that rice
straw left on the paddy would harbor and spread disease pathogens. The agricultural technician had always regarded rice straw as nothing but a source of rice
diseases and pests. The common and accepted practice was to apply straw only when fully decomposed as prepared compost.

In some circles, the belief that rice straw must be burned as a primary source of rice blast disease was virtually gospel. Fukuoka noted the burning of rice
straw on an immense scale in Hokkaido under the urging of plant pathologists. Even today, the practice of burning invaluable straw material continues to spread
throughout Japan. At harvest time in early summer, smoke hangs over the plain from burning barley straw. Fukuoka posed a pointed question: "Are there no
farmers, scientists, or agricultural administrators out there who see that whether or not we spread straw over our fields may decide the fate of our national
lands?"

## Fukuoka's Discovery: Fresh Straw Is Safe

Fukuoka deliberately defied the expert consensus because he had earlier confirmed beyond any doubt that rice straw left on the ground during autumn decomposes
entirely by the following spring, leaving no trace of pathogenic microbes. His ground cover experiments began when he sowed ladino clover together with barley
and scattered rice straw over the planted field. This cover of fresh straw showed great promise in weed control from the very first season.

As he combined straw mulching with direct seeding without tillage, Fukuoka grew increasingly certain of the validity of his method. He went from sparse seeding
to dense seeding, then returned again to sparse seeding before settling on broadcasting as his preferred technique. More than ten years of continuous no-tillage
direct-seeded rice-barley succession cropping demonstrated that the practice not only does not degrade the land but actually improves and enriches it. During
this entire period, he never saw any sign of decline in yields due to reduced soil fertility.

## The Straw Mulching Procedure

The straw mulching procedure in Fukuoka's system follows a precise annual cycle integrated with the rice-barley succession:

**After rice harvest (October):** Cut the rice while trampling over the clover and young barley seedlings. After threshing and cleaning, immediately scatter all
the rice straw uncut over the entire field. Apply chicken manure or decomposed organic matter at this time — 650 to 900 pounds per quarter-acre. If rice is to
be overwintered, enclose rice seed in clay pellets and scatter these over the straw before the New Year. This completes the sowing of rice and barley for the
coming year.

**After barley harvest (late May):** Cut the barley, leave it on the ground to dry for three days, gather into bundles, thresh and clean it. Scatter all the
barley straw uncut over the entire field.  Spread a layer of chicken manure (450-900 pounds) before or after returning the barley straw. The trampled rice
seedlings emerge through the barley straw and the clover grows back as well.

**Critical timing for barley straw:** Barley straw must be returned to the field as soon as possible after threshing. Once dried barley straw is wet by rain, it
becomes more than five times as heavy and very difficult to transport, and potassium leaches out of the straw. Fukuoka noted that attempting to do a careful job
can be self-defeating — with all the trouble of getting out cutters and motorized equipment, one is tempted to just leave the straw lying about. If the rice
straw is scattered immediately after threshing, the job is done in just two or three hours. It does not really matter how quick or carelessly it is done.

## Why Straw Mulching Works

Fukuoka identified three primary functions of straw mulching:

**1. Seed germination promotion.** The straw cover creates a microclimate of high humidity and moderate temperature at the soil surface, ideal for seed
germination. When seeds are broadcast over standing rice heads in autumn, the high soil moisture beneath the straw ensures ready germination of both clover and
barley seeds. Winter weeds have not yet appeared at this time, providing an additional weed-control advantage.

**2. Winter weed suppression.** The thick layer of straw physically blocks light from reaching the soil surface, preventing the germination and establishment of
winter weeds. This was the critical breakthrough that made no-tillage direct seeding viable — without herbicides, weed control had been the primary obstacle to
eliminating tillage.

**3. Soil enrichment.** As the straw decomposes in place, it returns organic matter, carbon, and nutrients directly to the field. Combined with the nitrogen
contributed by the clover ground cover, this creates a self-sustaining fertility cycle that eliminates the need for compost preparation and chemical
fertilizers. All of the straw and chaff obtained when harvesting and threshing should be scattered uncut over the entire surface of the field.

## The Elimination of Composting

Fukuoka regarded the composting of organic materials as fundamentally useless. He acknowledged that compost itself is not without value, but argued that all the
trouble taken during preparation — frequent turning of the pile, methods for stimulating aerobic bacteria, the addition of water, nitrogenous fertilizers, lime,
superphosphate, rice bran, and manure — was expended for a net acceleration in decomposition of at most ten to twenty percent. This could hardly be called
necessary, especially since the direct application of straw already achieved outstanding results.

When compost materials such as straw, grasses, trees, or seaweed are applied directly to a field, there is initially a temporary nitrogen deficiency as soil
microbes help themselves to available nitrogen to decompose the material. This is why organic farming ferments these materials into prepared compost first. But
Fukuoka's system circumvents this problem entirely: the nitrogen-fixing clover ground cover supplies the microbial population with nitrogen while the straw
decomposes, so the crops are never starved.

A group of farming specialists and agricultural administrators, most of whom had no first-hand experience of how much hard work preparing compost is, had
started a campaign urging farmers to enrich the soil by composting with straw. But this overlooked the importance of healthy rice and barley production and the
simplicity of direct straw application. Researchers have only just begun to encourage the use of fresh straw by chopping part of the straw with a cutter and
plowing it under — a compromise that misses the point of Fukuoka's method entirely.

## The "Clover Revolution"

Fukuoka referred to his method as the "clover revolution" in rice and barley cropping, deliberately framing it in opposition to modern scientific farming with
its chemicals and large machinery. The complete system combines four elements: no-tillage, direct seeding, straw mulching, and a leguminous green manure ground
cover.

This method was studied at a large number of agricultural testing stations throughout Japan. In almost every instance, researchers found no basic problem with
the no-tillage succession cropping of rice and barley using straw mulch. Weed control remained the persistent difficulty, which Fukuoka addressed through the
ground cover of green manure, mixed seeding of rice and barley, and biennial cultivation modifications.

He called this the basic pattern of natural rice and barley farming because he was certain that this technique enabled the farmer for the first time to farm
without using any pesticides or chemical fertilizers. In 1962, he published his findings in an article entitled "The Truth about Direct-Seeding Rice and Barley
Cultivation." One high-ranking official in the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry called it "research in a class by itself... a guiding light for Japanese
rice cultivation ten years hence."

## Straw and the Self-Sufficient Farm

The significance of straw mulching extends beyond its technical functions. In Fukuoka's vision of the quarter-acre self-sufficient farm, straw represents the
closed-loop nutrient cycling that makes independence possible. The grain grown on the land provides food; the straw from that grain returns to the land to build
soil. No external inputs are needed.

Fukuoka noted that straw produced on Japanese rice fields is of great importance as a source of organic fertilizer and for protecting the fields and enriching
the soil. Yet the modern practice of burning or abandoning this material represents a catastrophic break in the cycle. With large machinery available, all
harvesting gets done at once, and after the grain is taken, the problem for many farmers becomes how to get rid of all the straw — some let it lay and others
burn it.

No matter how conscientious a farmer is in his work, each operation is part of a carefully ordered system. A sudden change in weather or even a small disruption
in the work schedule can upset the timing of an operation enough to lead to a major failure. Straw mulching, simple as it appears, must be performed with
precision as part of the larger system. It is from just such a small matter that the future of Japanese agriculture shall emerge.
