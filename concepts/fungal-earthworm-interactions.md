Moody and colleagues (1995) demonstrated that earthworms feed preferentially on leaf material that has been previously colonized by fungi. They showed that there were broadly similar feeding preferences among three earthworm species, Lumbricus terrestris, Aporrectodea longa, and Allolobophora chlorotica, when offered straw colonized by different saprotrophic fungal species. Straw colonized by Fusarium lateritium and Mucor heimalis was most preferred, while straw colonized by Sphaerobolus stellatus was least preferred. This preference for fungi-colonized substrates likely reflects the nutritional enrichment that fungal metabolism provides: fungi convert recalcitrant plant polymers (cellulose, lignin, hemicellulose) into more digestible forms, concentrate nitrogen and phosphorus from the surrounding environment, and produce vitamins and growth factors that benefit earthworm nutrition.

## Differential Spore Survival

The passage of fungal spores through the earthworm gut has variable effects depending on both the fungal species and the earthworm species involved. Moody and colleagues (1996) showed that there was differential survival of fungal spores on passage through the earthworm gut. Fusarium and Agrocybe failed to survive passage through the gut of Lumbricus terrestris. Both Fusarium and Mucor failed to germinate after passage through the gut of Aporrectodea longa, although the germination of spores of Chaetomium globosum was actually enhanced after passing through this worm species.

In a detailed study on the spores of Mucor heimalis, Moody and colleagues established that the decline in spore germination on passage through an earthworm was caused by the action of intestinal fluid, not by the abrasive action of soil particles as they moved through the gut. In fact, abrasion by soil particles was found to stimulate spore germination, suggesting that the chemical environment of the earthworm gut is the primary factor determining spore viability.

## Effects on Fungal Biomass and Diversity

Tiwari and Mishra (1993) found greater numbers and diversity of fungi in earthworm casts than in surrounding soil. This enrichment of fungal communities in casts may result from the mixing of organic material with mineral soil, the addition of mucus and other earthworm secretions, and the creation of microsites favorable for fungal colonization.

The stimulation of fungal diversity in earthworm casts has been documented across multiple earthworm species and soil types. Studies using culture-based methods typically report 2-5 times more fungal colony-forming units per gram in fresh casts compared to surrounding soil, while molecular methods (DNA extraction and qPCR) show even larger differences because they detect both culturable and unculturable species. The fungal communities in casts are not merely subsets of the surrounding soil community; they include species that are rare or absent in bulk soil but flourish in the nutrient-rich, moist environment of the cast. This "cast effect" on fungal diversity is one reason why earthworm introduction to degraded soils often accelerates the recovery of fungal community structure and function.

The changes in species composition of fungal communities induced by earthworm activity can alter the rates of decomposition of resources. The activity of earthworms often results in greater comminution of leaf litter, increasing the surface area available for attack by saprotrophic fungi. This mechanical breakdown, combined with the selective grazing on certain fungal species, creates a complex interaction that can either accelerate or decelerate decomposition depending on the specific earthworm and fungal species involved.

## Disruption of Mycorrhizal Networks

The physical disturbance caused by earthworm burrowing can have significant effects on mycorrhizal fungal networks. Pattinson and colleagues (1997) demonstrated that in experiments with subterranean clover plants, the presence of the earthworm Aporrectodea trapezoids reduced rather than increased arbuscular mycorrhizal infection of the host plant because of lateral transport of the inoculum. They suggested that the activity of the worms disrupted the extraradical hyphal network of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, preventing interplant infection by mycelial growth.

This disruption is particularly significant in no-till agricultural systems and natural grasslands where [[fungal-grazing-soil-fauna]] extends beyond direct consumption. Earthworm mucus and secretions stimulate fungal growth in their vicinity, creating localized zones of elevated fungal biomass around burrow walls and within fresh casts. These fungal "hotspots" attract fungivorous microarthropods, which in turn become prey for predatory mites and nematodes, creating a concentrated food web in the drilosphere. This trophic cascade means that earthworm activity can increase overall soil biodiversity even in systems where earthworm biomass represents a small fraction of total soil faunal biomass.

## Implications for Soil Management

The interactions between earthworms and fungi have important implications for soil management practices. Minimal-till or no-till agricultural practices reduce the disruption of hyphal networks and earthworm burrow systems, maintaining the beneficial interactions between these organisms. The use of organic amendments that support both earthworm and fungal communities can enhance soil structure and nutrient cycling through the synergistic effects of these ecosystem engineers.

## Vermicomposting and Fungal Dynamics

Vermicomposting — the controlled decomposition of organic matter by earthworms — relies fundamentally on the interplay between earthworm digestion and fungal (and bacterial) decomposition. When organic residues pass through the earthworm gut, they are partially digested, inoculated with gut microorganisms, and deposited as casts that provide a highly favorable substrate for fungal colonization. The succession of fungal communities in vermicompost typically progresses from sugar-loving fast-growing species (Mucor, Rhizopus) early in the process to more cellulolytic and ligninolytic fungi (Trichoderma, various basidiomycetes) as the more recalcitrant compounds become available. This fungal succession drives the maturation of vermicompost from fresh cast material into stable, humus-rich compost. The resulting vermicompost typically contains 10-100 times more microbial activity than the parent material, with fungi playing a central role in humification and the production of plant growth-promoting substances including auxins, cytokinins, and gibberellins.

## Earthworm Burrows as Fungal Highways

Earthworm burrows create unique microenvironments that serve as conduits for fungal colonization of deeper soil layers. The burrow walls (drilosphere) are coated with mucus and organic material that support elevated fungal activity compared to surrounding bulk soil. Fungal hyphae readily colonize these burrow linings, using them as pathways to extend their networks into soil volumes that would otherwise be inaccessible.

## Seasonal Dynamics of Earthworm-Fungal Interactions

The nature and intensity of earthworm-fungal interactions vary seasonally in temperate ecosystems. During spring and autumn — periods of peak earthworm activity and moderate soil moisture — earthworm grazing pressure on fungal biomass is highest. During hot, dry summers, both earthworms and surface-active fungi enter dormancy or retreat to deeper soil layers, reducing interaction intensity.
