# High Yield Theory and Photosynthesis: A Critique

## Overview

Fukuoka presents a detailed critique of the scientific approach to achieving high rice yields through photosynthesis research. He argues that the entire framework of high-yield theory is fundamentally flawed because it confuses preventing crop losses with genuinely increasing productivity. The photosynthesis-centered approach, despite decades of research, has failed to produce the dramatic yield increases it promised.

## The Photosynthesis Framework

Modern high-yield theory, viewed through the lens of plant physiology, defines yield as:

**Yield = starch produced by photosynthesis in the leaves minus starch consumed by respiration**

Proponents claim yields can be increased by maximizing photosynthetic ability while maintaining balance between starch production and starch consumption. The processes involved are:

1. Leaves use photosynthesis to synthesize starch from carbon dioxide and water using chlorophyll and sunlight
2. Starch is broken down to sugar and transported to all parts of the plant
3. Sugar is decomposed by oxidation (respiration), releasing energy that feeds the rice plant
4. A portion of the starch produced is metabolized; the remainder is stored in the grain

## Factors Affecting Photosynthesis and Respiration

**Photosynthesis factors**: carbon dioxide, stomata closure, water uptake, water temperature, sunlight.

**Respiration factors**: sugar, oxygen, strength of wind, nutrients, humidity.

The apparent solution — maximize photosynthesis while minimizing respiration — runs into a fundamental problem: the same conditions that favor photosynthesis (high temperatures, abundant sunlight, good water uptake) also promote respiration. High starch production may be accompanied by equally high starch consumption.

## The Photosynthesis-Respiration Paradox

Fukuoka identifies a counterintuitive truth that undermines conventional thinking: a low starch production does not necessarily mean low yields. If starch consumption is low enough, the amount of stored starch — and therefore yield — may actually be higher than under more vigorous photosynthetic activity.

Farmers and scientists have repeatedly tried techniques that maximize starch production only to find large rice plants that lodge under the slightest breeze. A much easier and more certain path to high yields would be to hold down respiration and grow smaller plants that consume less starch.

The combinations of production factors possible in nature are limitless and may lead to any number of different yields. Fukuoka traces three illustrative pathways:

- **Course 1** (40°C): Root rot reduces water uptake, stomata close, photosynthesis slows, but respiration continues unabated — low yield.
- **Course 2** (30°C): Temperature suits the variety, nutrient absorption is good, photosynthesis and respiration remain in balance — highest yield.
- **Course 3** (low temperature): Conditions are fair but not ideal, yet good root activity maintains normal yield.

## The Impossibility of Combining Optimal Conditions

Even if scientists knew the conditions that maximize photosynthetic activity, they could not design a course that assembles the very best conditions. The best conditions cannot be combined under natural circumstances. Moreover:

- There is no standard for "maximum" and "minimum" — these vary with time, place, rice variety, and cultivation method
- More sunlight does not guarantee higher yields (northern Honshu outproduces sunny Kyushu; Japan outproduces tropical countries)
- Good water uptake invigorates photosynthesis but flooding can hasten root decay
- Nutrient deficiency may at times help maintain root vigor and at other times inhibit growth

The notion of an "optimal temperature" that works under the greatest range of conditions may help raise normal yields but is not the temperature required for high yields.

## The Scientist as Sports Commentator

Fukuoka offers a telling analogy: the rice scientist well-versed in plant physiology is not unlike the sports commentator who can give a good rundown of a tennis match and may even make a respectable coach, but is not himself a top-notch athlete. Understanding the mechanism of starch production does not translate into practical ability to grow high-yielding rice.

## Why Research Has Failed

Despite massive and persistent research efforts, rice yields have remained around 22 bushels per quarter-acre. The possibility of 26 to 28 bushels has been reported by some test centers, but only on a very limited scale using techniques unlikely to gain wide acceptance. The reasons for this failure:

1. **Infinite pathways**: The number of possible combinations of elements and conditions during cultivation is infinite, and each changes on a daily basis over the entire growing season
2. **No starting line or finish line**: Crop cultivation is not a footrace along a clearly marked track
3. **Mutual cancellation**: When multiple improvement measures are combined, they tend to cancel each other out
4. **The wrong foundation**: Taking traditional practices (plowing, transplantation, flooded paddies) as the starting point is a grave error. True progress requires starting from a totally new point — nature itself

## The Mu Perspective on Yield

Fukuoka argues that productivity must be viewed from beyond immediate reality. Nature is one — there is no starting point or destination, only an unending flux. The true essence of nature is "nothingness." The real starting point and destination are found in Mu.

To make nature our foundation is to begin at "nothing" and make this point of departure our destination as well. The present state of agriculture — built on commonsensical notions — has all the stability of a building erected on a boat. High-yield theory is full of holes because it attempts to add floors to a building whose foundation is nature itself, without understanding that the building's capacity is set by the rock on which it stands.

The scientific world of "somethingness" is smaller than the natural world of "nothingness." No degree of expansion can enable the world of science to arrive at the vast, limitless world of nature.
