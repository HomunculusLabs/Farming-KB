# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

Arbuscular [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF) form the most widespread and ancient symbiosis between fungi and plants, associating with approximately 80% of terrestrial plant species. The chapter by Jinyuan Liu, Melina Lopez-Meyer, Ignacio Maldonado-Mendoza, and Maria J. Harrison in *Fungi in the Environment* (Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, 2007) reviews genomic insights into the development and functioning of this critical symbiosis.

## Evolutionary History

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi belong to the phylum Glomeromycota, a monophyletic group estimated to have originated over 400 million years ago. Molecular evidence suggests that the AMF symbiosis predates the colonization of land by plants — the earliest land plants may have been mycorrhizal, with the fungal partner providing mineral nutrition and water in exchange for photosynthetically derived carbon.

Fossil evidence from the Rhynie Chert (approximately 400 million years old) shows structures consistent with arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization of early land plant axes. This ancient origin explains the ubiquity of the symbiosis — it has had hundreds of millions of years to co-evolve with the plant kingdom.

## AMF Biology

### Life Cycle

AMF are obligate biotrophs — they cannot complete their life cycle without a host plant. Their life cycle includes:

1. **Spore germination:** Resting spores germinate in response to [[root-exudates]], producing a short germ tube
2. **Presymbiotic growth:** The germ tube extends limited growth in the absence of a host, sustained by internal lipid reserves
3. **Host recognition and colonization:** Upon contacting a compatible root, the fungus forms an appressorium and penetrates the root cortex
4. **Internal development:** Within the root, the fungus forms intercellular hyphae and characteristic arbuscules within cortical cells
5. **Extraradical network:** An extensive [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] extends from the root into the surrounding soil
6. **Spore formation:** New spores are produced on extraradical hyphae, completing the cycle

### Unique Biological Features

AMF possess several unusual biological characteristics:

- **Coenocytic mycelium:** AMF hyphae are multinucleate without regular septa, a condition called coenocytism. This means individual cells contain hundreds to thousands of nuclei
- **Genetic uniqueness:** AMF are genetically unusual — individual spores can contain genetically different nuclei, challenging traditional concepts of fungal individuals and species
- **Lipid-based nutrition:** Unlike most fungi, AMF receive carbon from their host primarily as lipids rather than sugars. The plant synthesizes lipids that are transported to the fungus and used as its primary carbon and energy source

## Symbiosis Development

### Molecular Signaling

The establishment of the AMF symbiosis requires a precise molecular dialogue between plant and fungus:

**Plant signals:** Root exudates, particularly strigolactones, trigger spore germination and hyphal branching in the fungus. Strigolactones are also plant hormones that regulate shoot branching, revealing an ancient connection between mycorrhizal signaling and plant development.

**Fungal signals:** Mycorrhizal factors (Myc factors) — lipochitooligosaccharides secreted by germinating spores — are recognized by plant receptor kinases, activating the symbiosis signaling pathway in the root. These signals are structurally related to rhizobial Nod factors that trigger legume-rhizobium symbiosis, indicating a shared evolutionary origin.

**Common symbiosis signaling pathway (CSSP):** Both AMF and rhizobial symbioses use a conserved signaling pathway in the plant, involving calcium spiking, the calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase (CCaMK), and the transcription factor CYCLOPS. This shared pathway is a hallmark of the common evolutionary origin of these symbioses.

### Arbuscule Development

The arbuscule is the hallmark structure of AMF symbiosis. It forms when an intracellular hypha branches repeatedly within a root cortical cell, creating a tree-like structure that greatly increases the surface area for nutrient exchange between the fungus and the plant.

Arbuscule development involves extensive remodeling of the plant cell. The host cell reorganizes its cytoskeleton, modifies its vacuole, and increases the surface area of its plasma membrane to wrap around the developing arbuscule branches. This modified membrane — the periarbuscular membrane — contains specialized transporters for nutrient exchange.

Arbuscules are transient structures, typically lasting 4–15 days before degenerating. The plant cell then returns to its normal state, and new arbuscules form in the same or adjacent cells. This continuous turnover allows the symbiosis to respond dynamically to changing conditions.

## Nutrient Exchange

### Phosphorus Uptake

The primary benefit that AMF provide to host plants is enhanced phosphorus nutrition. Phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil, forming a depletion zone around roots. The extensive extraradical mycelial network of AMF extends far beyond this depletion zone, accessing phosphorus from a much larger soil volume.

The process involves:

1. **Phosphorus uptake:** AMF hyphae absorb phosphate from soil solution through high-affinity phosphate transporters
2. **Polyphosphate synthesis:** Phosphate is condensed into polyphosphate chains in the hyphal cytoplasm for transport
3. **Translocation:** Polyphosphate is translocated along hyphae toward the root
4. **Release:** At the arbuscule interface, phosphate is released and transferred to the plant through specific plant phosphate transporters located on the periarbuscular membrane

### Nitrogen Uptake

AMF can also enhance plant nitrogen uptake, though this benefit is generally less dramatic than for phosphorus. AMF hyphae can absorb ammonium and amino acids from soil and transfer nitrogen to the host. Some AMF species can access organic nitrogen sources through protease activity.

### Carbon Transfer to the Fungus

Plants allocate 10–20% of their photosynthetically fixed carbon to AMF. This carbon is primarily transported as lipids (triacylglycerols) from the plant to the fungal partner. The fungus uses this carbon for growth, maintenance, respiration, and production of extraradical mycelium and spores.

## Genomic Insights

### The Rhizophagus irregularis Genome

The sequencing of the *Rhizophagus irregularis* genome revealed several important features:

- **Large genome:** Approximately 150 Mb with a relatively high gene count, reflecting the complexity of the [[holzer-symbiotic-interaction-theory]]
- **Reduced metabolic capacity:** AMF have lost many genes for degrading complex organic polymers, consistent with their obligate biotrophic lifestyle
- **Expanded transporter families:** Genes encoding transporters for lipid uptake, phosphate transport, and sugar transport are expanded, reflecting the central role of nutrient exchange in the symbiosis
- **Effector proteins:** AMF produce small secreted proteins that may modulate plant immune responses and facilitate colonization

### Plant Genomic Responses

Host plant genomes show specific transcriptional responses during AMF colonization:

- **Symbiosis-induced genes:** Hundreds of plant genes are upregulated during AMF colonization, including nutrient transporters, lipid metabolism genes, and cell wall modification enzymes
- **Hormone modulation:** AMF colonization influences plant hormone signaling, including suppression of defense responses and modulation of auxin and cytokinin pathways
- **Transporter regulation:** Plant phosphate transporters in the direct uptake pathway are downregulated during AMF colonization, while mycorrhiza-specific transporters at the arbuscular interface are upregulated

## Ecological Significance

AMF influence [[dighton-mycorrhizal-diversity-ecosystem-function]] at multiple levels:

- **Plant community composition:** Different plant species respond differently to AMF colonization, influencing competitive outcomes and community structure
- **Plant diversity:** AMF generally promote plant diversity by reducing competitive asymmetry — dominant species often benefit less from AMF than subordinate species
- **Soil aggregation:** AMF hyphae and the glomalin they produce contribute to soil [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]] and stability
- **Carbon cycling:** AMF represent a significant carbon sink and contribute to long-term soil carbon storage
- **Ecosystem productivity:** AMF enhance primary productivity in most ecosystems, particularly in phosphorus-limited environments

## Agricultural Applications

AMF inoculants are commercially available and can improve crop phosphorus uptake, reduce fertilizer requirements, and enhance plant stress tolerance. However, the benefits of inoculation are context-dependent — existing AMF communities in agricultural soils may already provide significant benefits, and introduced AMF must compete with indigenous species to establish.

Conservation agriculture practices that minimize soil disturbance and maintain plant cover generally support healthy AMF communities, while intensive tillage and fallow periods reduce AMF abundance and diversity.
