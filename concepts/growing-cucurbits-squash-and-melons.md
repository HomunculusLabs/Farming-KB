# Growing Cucurbits — Squash, Melons, and Cucumbers

Cucurbits are so similar that if you have known one, you know them all. The easiest to start with is squash, because it is the most vigorous, most tolerant of careless planting, and most tolerant of heavy soils. Understanding the cucurbit family's unique sensitivities — especially to cold and wet conditions — is the key to reliable production.

## The Cold Sensitivity Rule

The single most important thing to know about cucurbits:

> Any time the soil turns chilly and damp while cucurbit seeds are sprouting, they may die, or the plant may suffer for weeks after. Should any young seedlings survive this situation, they will grow poorly.

**Implications**:
- Wait until the soil is warm before sowing
- In a chilly, damp spring, a sowing made a week or ten days later will outgrow an earlier one that barely survived
- Be reluctant to water sprouting cucurbit seeds — better to chit them, plant into moist warm soil, and not water at all until they emerge

## Sequential Sowing Schedule

1. **First**: Sow squash and pumpkins after soil has warmed
2. **Second** (after squash has begun to grow well): Sow cucumbers
3. **Third** (after cucumbers are up and growing): Sow melons

Each successive crop needs more warmth. If weather turns chilly after sowing, resow at another location.

## Species Classification

There are three commonly grown squash species — varieties within one species cross-pollinate freely, but different species do not cross:

| Species | Includes |
|---------|----------|
| *Cucurbita pepo* | Summer squash, zucchini, delicata, acorn squash, most pumpkins |
| *Cucurbita maxima* | Hubbard, buttercup, large winter squash, Queensland Blue |
| *[[cucurbita-moschata]]* | Butternut squash |
| *[[cucumis-sativus]]* | All cucumber varieties |
| *Cucumis melo* | Cantaloupes, honeydews (these don't cross with each other) |

## Soil Preparation and Spacing

### Fertility

- Cucurbits grow fast in full sun and fertile soil
- The shallow root system is at least as extensive as the tops are (bush squash roots may spread significantly more than the leaves do)
- The entire area the vines will ultimately cover should be made fertile
- Sow seeds in a hill with extra fertility beneath it to get seedlings off to a fast start

### Watermelon Exception

Watermelons are intolerant of heavy soils.

### Spacing

- Squash: Hills on 4-foot (120 cm) centers minimum
- Bush summer squash: Can be grown on 5-foot (150 cm) centers with great results
- Melons: One vine every 4 feet in rows on black plastic
- Cucumbers: 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) in hills

## The Wilting Warning

> On hot sunny afternoons, gardeners often shrug off temporary wilting of cucurbits as unimportant. This is NOT correct. Any wilting is a big stress and greatly reduces plant health and overall yield.

Cucurbits don't wilt on hot days when only one plant grows in each hill and the plant doesn't share its root zone. So where squash borer is not a problem (and the insect is rarely a problem with cucumbers and melons), start 3 but thin to 1 by the time vines start running.

## Black Plastic for Melons

In maritime climates with cool nights, only the earliest hybrid melons will produce, and only when grown on wide black plastic:
1. Spread a piece about 6 feet (2 m) wide
2. Anchor on edges
3. Put one vine every 4 feet down the row
4. This may be the only way to harvest ripe melons in cool-summer regions

## Harvest and Storage

### Summer Squash and Zucchini

- **Common belief**: Stop yielding much after a month
- **Reality**: If given growing room, plants produce more branches, more flowers, and yield steadily increases until weather turns against the crop
- Remove all overlooked oversized fruit — the burden of forming seed reduces new fruit formation

### Winter Squash

- Don't taste great until fully ripe
- Ripe when the stem attaching fruit to vine has shriveled and become brittle (no longer passing vascular fluid)
- Storage varies by species:
  - *C. pepo* (acorn, delicata): Shorter storage
  - *C. maxima* (Hubbard, buttercup): Medium storage
  - *C. moschata* (butternut): Longest storage
- Ideal storage: 55-60°F (13-16°C), low humidity, good air circulation
- Curing: Leave outside for a week or two of dry weather (not below 45°F/7°C), then bring inside to warm and dry

### Melons

- Cantaloupes slip the vine when ripe — they do NOT ripen after picking
- Watermelons: Test by thumping — this skill must be developed by experience
- Supermarket melons are inevitably harvested unripe and are inferior

## Varieties

### Squash

- **Summer**: Basic Early Crookneck (vining) is by far the best-tasting and resists pests better than most hybrids
- **Winter**: Sweet Meat (from Harris Seeds) or Queensland Blue/Jarrahdale — genuine heirlooms that make the best pumpkin soup and keep over 12 months

### Cucumbers

- Old sprawling "apple" or "lemon" cucumber is best adapted to lower fertility and soil moisture
- Hybrid cucumber seed is affordable; hybrid melon seed isn't

## Saving Seed

- **Isolation**: Half a mile (800 m) prevents enough crossing for home purposes
- **Inbreeding warning**: Saving seed from only a few cucurbits leads to vigor depression within a few generations — seed will barely sprout or grow
- **Minimum population**: 25 plants for maintaining a variety across generations
- **Seed longevity**: Cucurbit seed is long-lasting — 6 years is normal, 10 isn't unusual
- **Practical advice**: Buy fresh seed every other generation, or save from a large enough population
