# Korean Natural Farming Seawater and Fermented Seawater

Seawater is a naturally balanced mineral solution containing all known elements on Earth. In Korean Natural Farming, both raw seawater and fermented seawater are used as mineral supplements for crops and livestock.

## Seawater Characteristics

Seawater contains approximately 80+ minerals and trace elements in proportions similar to those found in healthy soil and living organisms. These include sodium, chloride, magnesium, sulfur, calcium, potassium, bromine, carbon, nitrogen, strontium, boron, silicon, and many trace elements. The mineral composition of seawater is remarkably similar to that of mammalian blood plasma, which has led researchers to propose that the mineral requirements of terrestrial organisms reflect their evolutionary origins in a marine environment. This "sea mineral hypothesis" provides a theoretical basis for the observed benefits of seawater application to crops and livestock.

The key minerals in seawater for agricultural purposes include magnesium (essential for chlorophyll synthesis and enzyme activation), sulfur (component of amino acids and involved in protein synthesis), boron (critical for cell wall formation and pollen viability), and a suite of micronutrients including zinc, copper, manganese, molybdenum, and cobalt that serve as enzyme cofactors. While many of these minerals are present in adequate quantities in healthy soils, decades of intensive cultivation and synthetic fertilizer use have depleted trace mineral reserves in agricultural soils worldwide, making seawater supplementation increasingly valuable for restoring balanced mineral nutrition.

## Functions in Farming

1. **Complete mineral supplementation:** Provides a full spectrum of minerals that may be deficient in depleted soils
2. **Improves crop quality:** Minerals contribute to better taste, texture, nutritional value, and shelf life of produce
3. **Enhances disease resistance:** Well-nourished plants with balanced minerals are more resistant to pests and diseases
4. **Livestock supplement:** When added to drinking water, supports animal health and productivity

## Raw Seawater Usage

- Dilution ratio typically 1:30 to 1:1000 depending on application and crop sensitivity
- Can be mixed with other KNF inputs for foliar spraying
- Used in seed treatment solutions
- Added to IMO and compost production
- Used as a livestock drinking water supplement (highly diluted)

## Fermented Seawater

Fermented seawater is made by combining seawater with a carbon source (brown sugar) and allowing beneficial microorganisms to proliferate, making the minerals more bioavailable.

The fermentation process serves two critical functions: first, it introduces a diverse community of [[knf-imo-four-stage-cultivation]] that will continue to multiply in the seawater medium; second, microbial metabolism partially chelates mineral ions — binding them to organic molecules — which makes them more readily absorbable by plant roots and leaf surfaces. Raw seawater minerals exist primarily as inorganic ions that plants can only absorb slowly through specific transport channels; fermented seawater provides the same minerals in organic forms that bypass these transport limitations.

### How to Make Fermented Seawater

1. Collect clean seawater from an unpolluted source (avoid areas near industrial discharge or river mouths)
2. Mix seawater with brown sugar in approximately equal parts by weight
3. Add IMO #4 to introduce beneficial microorganisms
4. Fill container to 2/3 capacity (leave 1/3 for air circulation)
5. Cover with porous paper and rubber band
6. Ferment at 23-25°C (73-77°F) in a shaded area
7. The fermentation process makes the minerals more readily available to plants
8. Filter and store in airtight container away from direct sunlight

### Storage

- Store in colored glass bottles or PE containers (light-proof)
- Temperature range: 1-15°C (34-59°F)
- No direct sunlight
- Airtight with adequate ventilation
- Longer storage increases concentration and reduces particle size for better absorption

## Application

- **Foliar spray:** Dilute appropriately and spray on leaves during various growth stages
- **Soil drench:** Apply to root zone for mineral uptake
- **Compost additive:** Add to IMO production and mixed compost for mineral enrichment
- **Livestock water:** Diluted in drinking water for poultry and livestock
- **Seed treatment:** Soak seeds before planting for mineral nutrition

## Cautions

- Always dilute seawater — raw seawater will burn plants
- Start with lower concentrations and observe plant response
- Avoid polluted seawater sources
- Do not use during intense sunlight (may cause leaf burn)
- Reduce usage approaching harvest to avoid salt residue on produce

### Managing Sodium Accumulation

The primary risk of seawater application is sodium accumulation in soil. While seawater's mineral balance is beneficial, the high sodium chloride content (approximately 35 g/L in full-strength ocean water) can cause soil structure degradation over time if applied excessively. Sodium ions displace calcium and magnesium from clay particle exchange sites, causing clay dispersion and reducing soil permeability — a process called soil sodification. To mitigate this risk, KNF practitioners recommend applying calcium-rich amendments such as [[water-soluble-calcium-wca]] periodically to maintain favorable calcium-to-sodium ratios on soil exchange sites. Monitoring soil electrical conductivity (EC) can help detect sodium buildup before it becomes problematic. Most KNF practitioners find that the dilution ratios used for fermented seawater (typically 1:500 to 1:1000 for foliar spray) deliver sufficient trace minerals without accumulating sodium to problematic levels, provided applications are not made more frequently than every 2-3 weeks.

## Mineral Synergy and Soil Biology

The minerals in seawater do not act in isolation; they function synergistically with soil biological processes. Trace minerals such as boron, copper, zinc, and manganese serve as essential cofactors for microbial enzymes that drive nutrient cycling. When seawater minerals are introduced to the soil via fermented seawater, they feed not only the plants but also the [[knf-indigenous-microorganism-culture]] that solubilize phosphorus, fix nitrogen, and decompose organic matter. This dual feeding action — minerals for the plant and minerals for the microbial workforce — is why seawater applications often produce visible improvements in plant vigor that cannot be replicated by adding individual mineral salts.

Dr. Maynard Murray's pioneering research in "Sea Energy Agriculture" (1976) demonstrated that crops grown with sea minerals showed significantly lower pest and disease pressure compared to conventionally fertilized controls. He attributed this to the complete mineral spectrum enabling plants to synthesize full complements of defensive compounds — a finding consistent with both KNF practice and [[ingham-foliar-pest-deterrence-aact]] that nutritionally balanced plants are inherently more pest-resistant.

## Seawater in Livestock Management

KNF practitioners use diluted seawater as a livestock supplement for poultry, swine, and cattle. When added to drinking water at ratios of approximately 1:1000, seawater provides trace minerals that support immune function, bone development, and feed conversion efficiency. Korean Natural Farming guidelines suggest that livestock receiving mineralized water produce manure that is richer in nutrients and microorganisms, creating a positive feedback loop when this manure is composted and returned to crop fields. Some practitioners report reductions in livestock mortality and improved coat condition when seawater minerals are consistently provided.

## Fermented Seawater Preparation Details

The fermentation process transforms raw seawater from a simple mineral solution into a biologically active preparation. When brown sugar is added as a carbon source and [[knf-imo-four-stage-cultivation]] is introduced, lactic acid bacteria and yeast populations proliferate, partially chelating the mineral ions and producing organic acids, vitamins, and growth-promoting substances. This biological transformation makes the minerals more readily absorbable by plant roots and leaf surfaces compared to raw seawater. The fermentation process also reduces the sodium chloride concentration relative to other minerals through selective microbial uptake and precipitation, slightly improving the mineral balance for agricultural use.

Quality indicators for finished fermented seawater include a sweet-sour aroma (indicating active lactic acid fermentation), absence of putrid or alcoholic odors (which signal contamination), and a slightly viscous consistency from microbial exopolysaccharides. A pH of approximately 3.5-4.5 indicates successful fermentation. If the preparation develops a white surface mold film, it should be discarded and remade, as this indicates dominance by undesirable aerobic fungi over the intended lactic acid bacteria.
