# Magic Mushrooms in Japan

The history, species, and cultural context of psychoactive mushroom use in Japan, from medieval legends of "laughing mushrooms" to modern mycological discoveries.

## Medieval Legends: The Dancing and Laughing Mushrooms

Tales about the infamous "Laughing Mushroom" date as far back as the Japanese Middle Ages.

A famous account from the 11th century describes several lumberjacks from Kyoto who got lost in the woods and encountered four or five Buddhist nuns.

Instead of meditating in quiet quest for Nirvana, the nuns were dancing and laughing uncontrollably.

The nuns had dealt with their hunger by eating delicious mushrooms they found in the forest.

The lumberjacks ate the same mushrooms and soon succumbed to overwhelming fits of laughter and the urge to dance.

Since that time, the mushrooms have been referred to in Japanese as "maitake" (Dancing Mushrooms) and later as "waraitake" (Laughing Mushrooms).

For a long time, the species thought responsible were identified as Panaeolus papilionaceus and [[gymnopilus-purpuratus-profile]] ("giant laughing mushroom").

However, Panaeolus papilionaceus is a European and North American species containing no psychoactive substances.

Even Japanese authors have been unable since 1980 to confirm the presence of psilocybin in Gymnopilus spectabilis; only inactive substances have been found.

It is likely that the medieval Japanese mentality was a cultural factor that modified the expression of the altered state of consciousness.

While psilocybin initially causes fits of laughter, this phase is generally followed by relaxation and decreased physical activity.

## Mushroom Cultivation History

Japan has a long tradition of mushroom cultivation spanning at least 2,000 years.

Evidence exists that Lentinus edodes (Berk.) Singer was cultivated by collecting naturally grown mycelia and transferring them onto pieces of wood selected to serve as new substrates.

Literary sources from China also attest to knowledge about psychoactive mushrooms, where they were said to cause a temporary "disease of dry laughter."

## Japanese Psilocybin Species

Several psychoactive Psilocybe species can be found in Japan.

The bluing species Psilocybe subcaerulipes Hongo and Psilocybe argentipes Yokoyama have caused intoxications in people who mistook them for honey mushrooms (Armillaria mellea).

These cases led to the isolation of psilocybin in crystalline form from dried fruiting bodies, as well as recognition of the species' wide area of distribution.

Psilocybe argentipes was systematically studied by Yokoyama in 1973, who found no "urge to dance" in his investigations.

### Stropharia caerulescens (Psilocybe venenata)

In 1932, S. Imai described intoxication cases from 1929 and 1931 involving his newly classified species Stropharia caerulescens.

The species was later named Stropharia venenata Imai and grows on top of wood and dung.

Today it is classified within the genus [[psilocybe-genus-classification]] as a close relative of [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]].

On June 21, 1929, a 43-year-old woman collected about 13 oz of mushrooms she erroneously thought were honey mushrooms and served them to her family.

As family members noticed effects, they went to a doctor who found the woman experiencing the most potent effects: stomach pumped without delay, muscle twitches, hallucinations, and a comatose state.

The son, who had eaten only the soup, also experienced hallucinations because the cooking process extracted water-soluble compounds from the mushrooms.

### Psilocybe argentipes Experiments

Yokoyama's systematic experiments with Psilocybe argentipes yielded the following results:

J.H. (24-year-old male): Ingested four cooked mushrooms then three fresh mushrooms 30 minutes later, followed by regurgitation, profuse sweating, accelerated pulse and breathing, visual hallucinations.

K.Y. (31-year-old male): Ate five mushrooms, experienced regurgitation after 30 minutes, sweating, slight paralysis of extremities persisting three hours, depressed mood, and hallucinations of colorful lights flooding from the sky.

M.K. (22-year-old male): Ate just one fresh mushroom with no effects at all.

## Unjustified Reputation

Singer and Smith mistakenly cited the 1929 Japanese incidents in their 1958 monograph as examples of fatal mushroom poisoning.

For that reason, the species was unjustly branded for decades in the literature as a highly dangerous poisonous mushroom.

## Current Status

Much work remains in phytochemistry and taxonomy before knowledge about psychotropic mushroom species in Japan can be considered adequate.

The geographic distribution and ingredients of Japanese Panaeolus species must be studied further.

Panaeolus subbalteatus is known to grow on several Japanese islands.

The body of knowledge about psychotropic mushroom species in Japan continues to grow as more mycological research is conducted.
