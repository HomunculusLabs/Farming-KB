# Fungal Interactions with Bacteria

Competition, cooperation, and coexistence between fungi and bacteria in soil, plant surfaces, and other environments. Based on Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006). See also [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]], [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]], and [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]].

## Overview

Fungi and bacteria coexist in virtually all environments, and their interactions are fundamental drivers of ecosystem processes. These relationships span the full spectrum from antagonism to mutualism and are mediated by chemical signaling, resource competition, physical interactions, and environmental context. Understanding fungal-bacterial interactions is essential for managing soil health, plant diseases, composting systems, and bioremediation.

## Competitive Interactions

### Nutrient Competition

Fungi and bacteria compete directly for limiting resources:

- **Carbon**: In most soils, carbon is the primary limiting resource. Fungi generally outcompete bacteria for complex carbon sources (lignin, cellulose, chitin) due to their enzymatic capabilities, while bacteria dominate on labile substrates (simple sugars, amino acids).
- **Nitrogen**: Both groups compete for ammonium. Fungi typically have higher affinity for ammonium at low concentrations, while bacteria are more efficient at high concentrations. Fungi's higher C:N ratio means they immobilize more carbon per unit of nitrogen assimilated.
- **Phosphorus**: Fungi are often superior phosphorus scavengers due to hyphal exploration and phosphatase production. In low-P soils, mycorrhizal fungi can effectively outcompete bacteria for phosphorus.
- **Iron**: Bacteria produce high-affinity siderophores to scavenge iron. Some fungi also produce siderophores (e.g., *Aspergillus* species produce ferrichrome-type siderophores), and both groups compete for this essential micronutrient.

### Antibiosis

Many fungi and bacteria produce antimicrobial compounds that inhibit each other:

- **Fungal antibacterials**: Penicillin (from *Penicillium*), cycloheximide, and other fungal metabolites inhibit bacterial growth. These compounds are produced as secondary metabolites, often under nutrient-limiting conditions.
- **Bacterial antifungals**: Many soil bacteria produce antifungal compounds. *Streptomyces* species produce numerous antifungal antibiotics. *Pseudomonas* species produce phenazine, pyoluteorin, and 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG), which suppress fungal plant pathogens.
- **Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)**: Both fungi and bacteria emit VOCs that can inhibit competitors. *Muscodor albus*, an endophytic fungus, produces a mixture of volatile compounds lethal to a broad range of fungi and bacteria.

### Physical Interactions

- **Bacterial colonization of fungal hyphae**: Many bacteria colonize the surface of fungal hyphae, forming biofilms. Some bacteria migrate along hyphae using them as "fungal highways" to access new substrates. This can be either beneficial (dispersal) or detrimental (competition, parasitism).
- **Mycophagous bacteria**: Some bacteria are specialized fungal feeders. *Collimonas* species actively lyse fungal hyphae to obtain nutrients. They combine chitinase production with chemical weapons (iron chelation, toxic metabolites) to kill fungi.
- **Bacterial endosymbionts**: Some fungi harbor intracellular bacteria. *Burkholderia* species live inside the hyphae of some arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and their role in fungal nutrition is an active research area.

## Cooperative Interactions

### Decomposition Partnerships

Fungi and bacteria often work synergistically in decomposition:

- **Sequential decomposition**: Fungi break down complex polymers (lignin, cellulose) into simpler compounds, which bacteria then mineralize. This sequential processing is fundamental to the [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]].
- **Cross-feeding**: Bacterial degradation products (amino acids, organic acids) can stimulate fungal growth, while fungal products (sugars from cellulose hydrolysis) feed bacteria. This mutualistic cross-feeding accelerates overall decomposition rates.
- **Fungal-bacterial co-cultures for bioremediation**: Combining white-rot fungi (for initial pollutant degradation) with bacteria (for complete mineralization) is more effective than either group alone. The fungus breaks down complex pollutants (e.g., PAHs, dyes) into intermediates that bacteria can fully mineralize.

### Nutrient Mobilization

- **Bacterial nitrogen fixation with fungal partners**: In some environments, nitrogen-fixing bacteria associate with fungal hyphae. The fungal hyphae provide a carbon source and microsite for bacterial activity, while the bacteria supply nitrogen.
- **Phosphorus solubilization**: Some bacterial-fungal consortia are more effective at P solubilization than either alone. Bacteria may produce organic acids that complement fungal phosphatase activity.
- **Mineral weathering**: Both fungi and bacteria contribute to rock and mineral weathering. Their combined action can be synergistic, with bacterial acids and fungal physical penetration working together to break down minerals.

### Signaling and Communication

Chemical signaling between fungi and bacteria is increasingly recognized as important:

- **Quorum sensing**: Bacteria use quorum sensing (QS) molecules (acyl homoserine lactones, AHLs) for cell-cell communication. Some fungi can detect and respond to bacterial QS molecules, potentially modifying their behavior in response to bacterial population density.
- **Fungal signals to bacteria**: Fungi produce volatile and soluble compounds that influence bacterial gene expression and community composition.
- **Shared signaling in the rhizosphere**: Both fungi and bacteria respond to plant root exudates, creating a shared signaling environment in the rhizosphere.

## Fungal-Bacterial Interactions in the Rhizosphere

### The Rhizosphere Effect

Plant roots create a zone of enhanced microbial activity (the rhizosphere) where fungal-bacterial interactions are particularly intense:

- **Root exudates**: Plant roots release sugars, amino acids, organic acids, and secondary metabolites that select for specific microbial communities. Both fungi and bacteria respond to these signals.
- **Mycorrhizosphere**: The area around mycorrhizal roots supports distinct bacterial communities different from the bulk soil or non-mycorrhizal rhizosphere. AM fungal hyphae create a "hyphosphere" with its own bacterial associates.
- **Helper bacteria**: Some bacteria promote mycorrhizal establishment and function. *Pseudomonas* and *Bacillus* species have been identified as "mycorrhization helper bacteria" (MHBs) that stimulate spore germination, hyphal growth, and root colonization by AM fungi.

### Biocontrol Interactions

Bacterial biocontrol agents often target fungal plant pathogens:

- **Antibiosis**: Biocontrol bacteria produce antifungal compounds that suppress pathogenic fungi
- **Competition**: Biocontrol bacteria compete with pathogens for niches and nutrients on root surfaces
- **Induced systemic resistance (ISR)**: Some rhizobacteria trigger plant defense responses that protect against fungal pathogens. *Pseudomonas fluorescens* and *Bacillus subtilis* are well-studied ISR-inducing bacteria
- **Enzyme degradation**: Bacterial chitinases and glucanases can degrade fungal cell walls, directly attacking pathogen hyphae

## Succession Dynamics

### Primary Succession

During primary succession on new substrates, fungal-bacterial interactions follow predictable patterns:

1. **Pioneer phase**: Bacteria dominate initial colonization, utilizing any available labile organic matter
2. **Fungal establishment**: Fungal spores germinate and hyphae colonize the substrate, beginning to break down complex polymers
3. **Mixed community phase**: Fungal-bacterial interactions intensify as both groups establish. Cross-feeding relationships develop.
4. **Climax community**: A relatively stable community develops where fungal and bacterial abundances fluctuate seasonally but maintain equilibrium

### Compost and Organic Matter Succession

In composting systems (see [[composting-systems]]):

- **Mesophilic phase**: Bacteria dominate, rapidly metabolizing sugars and starches. Temperature rises to 40-45C.
- **Thermophilic phase**: Thermophilic bacteria and fungi (*Thermomyces*, *Myceliophthora*) dominate at 45-70C. Lignin and cellulose degradation accelerates.
- **Cooling and curing phase**: Mesophilic fungi recolonize and complete decomposition. Actinomycetes become prominent.
- **Maturation**: A complex fungal-bacterial community develops, with basidiomycetes often appearing in the final stages.

## Implications for Agriculture and Horticulture

### Soil Health

Managing fungal-bacterial balance is central to soil health:

- **Bacterial-dominated soils**: Typically associated with annual crops, disturbed soils, and high-nutrient conditions. Faster nutrient cycling, less stable soil structure.
- **Fungal-dominated soils**: Associated with perennial systems, no-till, forests, and mature ecosystems. Slower nutrient cycling, more stable aggregates, greater carbon storage. See [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] and [[cannabis-living-soil-guide]].
- **Management**: Practices that favor fungal dominance include no-till, perennial crops, mulching, and reduced synthetic fertilizer inputs. Practices favoring bacterial dominance include tillage, fallow periods, and high nitrogen inputs.

### Plant Disease Suppression

Understanding fungal-bacterial interactions is key to managing plant diseases:

- **Disease-suppressive soils**: Some soils naturally suppress specific fungal diseases. This suppression is often linked to specific bacterial communities (e.g., *Pseudomonas* producing DAPG in Fusarium wilt-suppressive soils).
- **Biocontrol applications**: Inoculating soil with beneficial bacteria (PGPR -- plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria) can suppress fungal pathogens while promoting plant growth.
- **Compost tea**: Aerated compost tea ([[compost-tea-aact]]) aims to introduce beneficial fungal and bacterial communities to plant root zones.

## Key Research Findings

- Fungi typically contribute 60-90% of microbial biomass in forest soils but only 10-30% in agricultural soils
- Bacterial-fungal ratios are a useful indicator of soil health and management history
- Fungal highways: bacteria use fungal hyphae for dispersal, with movement rates 10-100x faster than through bulk soil
- Some AM fungi harbor endosymbiotic *Mollicutes* (mycoplasma-like organisms) whose function remains unclear
- The "Gadgil effect": ECM fungi can suppress bacterial decomposition, slowing nutrient cycling in forest soils
