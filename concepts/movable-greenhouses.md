# Movable Greenhouses

The [[movable-greenhouse]] is described as "the best new gardening idea of the twenty-first century"—or more accurately, a rediscovered idea. By allowing a single greenhouse structure to cover multiple sets of beds throughout the year, movable greenhouses effectively extend greenhouse use from 12 months to the equivalent of 14 months per year. A winter crop can be established two months before it needs protection, and a warm-season crop can continue growing two months beyond what a fixed house would allow.

## The Core Concept

A movable greenhouse slides along rails or skids from one set of growing beds to another. This creates a system where:

- **Winter crops** (spinach, carrots, leeks) are planted outdoors in fall, grow under open sky, and receive greenhouse protection only when freezing weather arrives
- **Summer crops** (tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers) remain under the greenhouse early in the season but can continue ripening after the house moves away to cover winter crops
- **Soil recovery** occurs naturally—each bed section spends time uncovered, exposed to weather, rain, and biological activity

The net effect is dramatically more production per greenhouse dollar invested. Rather than building separate structures for each season, one movable structure serves both.

## Historical Context

The concept is not new. The first recorded movable greenhouse was a large, expensive glasshouse built in England at the end of the nineteenth century. Commercial vegetable growers originally developed the idea to combat **greenhouse soil sickness**—the buildup of pests and diseases in permanently covered soil. At the time, the alternatives were removing and replacing 16 inches of soil or sterilizing with steam, both expensive and disruptive.

The movable greenhouse concept fell into disuse when chemical soil sterilization became widespread. It has been rediscovered by organic growers who cannot or will not use chemical sterilants and who value the soil-restoration benefits of periodic exposure to the open environment.

## Design Evolution

### First Generation: 30 × 96 Feet (1996)

The first houses were deliberately overengineered:
- Each rib reinforced with a crossbar and truss support
- Continuous roof ventilation (no fans)
- Very strong but more expensive than necessary for a winter-harvest greenhouse

Lessons: The truss strength and roof vents were sound decisions, but the cost was higher than justified for winter use.

### Current Design: 48 × 22 Feet

The current standard is smaller and more practical:

**Dimensions**: 48 feet long × 22 feet wide

**Why this size?**
- **Site constraints**: Fits available field locations
- **Ventilation ratio**: End-wall ventilation is adequate when tunnel length is no more than 3× its width (48 ÷ 22 = 2.2, well within the ratio)
- **Mobility**: Shorter houses are significantly easier to move
- **Dead-air prevention**: Beyond the 3:1 ratio, a stagnant air pocket forms in the center of the house

**Structural features**:
- Each hoop has a crossbar with a vertical support (lighter than the original truss design)
- Diagonal braces at all four corners provide rigidity
- Crossbars retained to prevent hoop splaying during moves and to support trellised crops in summer

## Movement Systems

### Pipe Skid System

The 48-foot houses sit on **2-by-3-inch galvanized pipe skids**:

- Three 16-foot lengths of pipe connected to span the full 48-foot house length
- Connections made by milling 2-foot lengths of recycled-plastic decking to fit snugly inside each pipe end, then bolting a base plate across the seam
- Base plates (commercially available hoop-holding plates for concrete foundations) are adapted for bolting to the skids, with hoops attached via these plates

**Moving procedure**:
1. Fit 16-inch "ski tips" made of 4-inch steel under the front of the skids to prevent sinking
2. Place a 12-inch-diameter × 12-inch-long section of round log about 8 feet in front of each ski tip
3. Run pulling cables over the rolling logs to provide upward force
4. Two cables (one per side) extend straight out to pulleys attached to ground anchors at the destination, then redirect to the tractor
5. The straight-line pull moves the house smoothly
6. Secure the house in place by bolting skids to 45-inch-long, 4,000-pound-rated ground anchors screwed into the soil

### Angle Iron Runner Alternative

A Georgia grower simplified and reduced costs by:
- Using lengths of **angle iron** as runners instead of galvanized pipe
- Angle iron lays flat on the soil with the vertical edge on the outside
- Sections bolted together with metal plates, spanning the full greenhouse length
- Hoops bolted directly to the angle iron using U-bolts through holes drilled every 4 feet
- Diagonal braces for stability

This is described as the least expensive sled design encountered.

### Wheel System (Latest Development)

The most recent innovation: a 22 × 48-foot greenhouse on **wheels movable by two strong people—no tractor required**.

**Rail system**: Lengths of 2-inch-diameter round pipe laid on the ground under both sides of the greenhouse

**Wheels**: Metal gate wheels (the curved-flange type used on chain-link fence gates) bolted to the bottom of each greenhouse hoop leg. These roll smoothly along the pipe rails.

**Structural reinforcement** (necessary because the hoops receive no vertical support from the rails):
- Extra diagonal supports on the sides
- Diagonal supports in the superstructure
- Horizontal cross bracing in the upper section of both end walls to prevent racking during movement

**Sealing the gap**: Because the wheeled house sits above the ground, there is a gap between the plastic cover and the soil. This is managed by:
- Leaving a 2-foot plastic flange below the [[wiggle-wire]] channel along both sides when skinning the house
- Placing sandbags (filled with 15 lbs of road gravel) along the flange when a weather-tight seal is needed
- Alternative: burying the flange in the soil

The wheel system represents a significant reduction in the labor and equipment needed to move greenhouses, making the concept accessible to smaller operations.

## Benefits for Organic Growing

Movable greenhouses address several challenges specific to organic production:

1. **Soil health**: Periodic exposure to rain, sun, and weather prevents the soil degradation that occurs under permanent cover
2. **Disease and pest management**: No buildup of soil-borne pathogens in permanently covered soil
3. **No chemical sterilization needed**: The rotation between covered and uncovered periods naturally resets soil biology
4. **Increased productivity**: More harvests per bed per year by extending both ends of the growing season
5. **Flexibility**: The same structure serves winter and summer crops, reducing capital investment

## Soil Sickness Prevention

In permanent greenhouses, soil under continuous cover degrades over time:
- Organic matter breaks down without replenishment from natural inputs
- Soil structure deteriorates
- Salt buildup from fertilization
- Pathogen and pest populations accumulate

Movable greenhouses eliminate these problems by ensuring every bed section spends part of the year uncovered, exposed to:
- Direct rainfall for leaching and rehydration
- Freezing temperatures that kill some soil organisms
- UV radiation from direct sunlight
- Natural incorporation of atmospheric inputs
