# Mycorrhiza Nutrient Exchange

## The Carbon-For-Nutrients Trade

[[bloomfield-mycorrhizal-symbiosis-and-fungal-ecology]] is fundamentally a bi-directional exchange: carbon
flows from plant to fungus while inorganic nutrients move from fungus to
plant. The fungus helps the host plant absorb inorganic nitrogen and
phosphorus from the soil, and in return receives carbohydrates produced
through plant photosynthesis. This exchange is the central mechanism driving
the mutualism and represents a critical linkage between plant roots and the
soil environment.

## Phosphorus Uptake and Mobilization

Phosphorus is often the primary limiting nutrient in terrestrial ecosystems,
and mycorrhizal fungi play a crucial role in its acquisition. Plant roots
alone may be incapable of taking up phosphate ions that are immobilized, for
example, in soils with basic pH. The mycelium of mycorrhizal fungi can,
however, access these phosphorus sources and make them available to colonized
plants.

Mycorrhizal mycelia are much smaller in diameter than the smallest root, and
can explore a greater volume of soil, providing a larger surface area for
absorption. The cell membrane chemistry of fungi differs from that of plants,
further enhancing their nutrient acquisition capabilities. Mycorrhizal fungi
release powerful chemicals into the soil that dissolve hard-to-capture
nutrients such as phosphorus, iron, and other tightly bound soil nutrients.

## Phosphatase Activity

Phosphatase enzymes produced by mycorrhizal fungi play a key role in
releasing phosphate from organic compounds in soil. Both acid and alkaline
phosphatase activities have been documented in arbuscular and
ectomycorrhizal fungi. The ELF-97 fluorogenic substrate method allows
visualization and quantification of these activities in living fungal
tissue.

For ectomycorrhizal fungi such as *Hebeloma cylindrosporum*, acid
phosphatase activity on extramatrical hyphae is typically much more
pronounced than alkaline phosphatase activity. In [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]
fungi, the situation varies by species: *Glomus intraradices* shows higher
alkaline than acid phosphatase activity in extraradical mycelium, while
*Scutellospora calospora* shows the inverse pattern.

Arbuscules show significant phosphatase activity, confirming their role as
primary sites for phosphorus transfer to the host plant. Hyphal coils in
Paris-type AM associations also demonstrate phosphatase activity, suggesting
that coils, like arbuscules, function in phosphorus transfer.

## Nitrogen Uptake

Mycorrhizal fungi significantly enhance plant [[fungal-bacterial-predators-nitrogen-acquisition-soil-ecology]]. Studies
using compartmented culture systems and isotopic tracers have demonstrated
the essential role of AM fungi in nitrogen uptake and its subsequent
translocation to host plants. Microelectrode measurements along
ectomycorrhizal roots have shown enhanced nitrate fluxes when plants are
associated with ECM fungi.

Research on *Pinus pinaster* associated with *Rhizopogon roseolus*
demonstrated that ECM symbiosis greatly helps plants absorb nitrate from
soil. The presence of the ECM fungus increased nitrate uptake rates into
short roots, with the ECM root fraction contributing significantly to total
plant nitrate uptake. The contribution of ECM roots was estimated at 62% of
total plant nitrogen acquisition under certain conditions.

## Compartmented Culture Systems for Transport Studies

Bi-compartmented culture systems have been instrumental in studying nutrient
transport by mycorrhizal fungi. In these systems, roots and mycorrhizal
fungi develop in one compartment (root compartment, RC) while the other
(hyphal compartment, HC) is restricted to the exclusive development of the
extraradical mycelium (ERM). Using isotopic tracers, researchers have
demonstrated the uptake and translocation of phosphorus and other elements
through the fungal mycelium to the host plant.

In vitro monoxenic compartmented systems overcome limitations of soil-based
systems by eliminating undesirable microorganisms, allowing visualization
of both partners, preventing tracer leakage, and facilitating molecular
studies. Modified Strullu-Romand (MSR) and minimal (M) media are widely
used for these in vitro cultures.

## Synergy with Nitrogen-Fixing Bacteria

The interaction between rhizobia and arbuscular mycorrhiza fungi has
received considerable attention because of the relatively high phosphorus
demand of [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]. The two symbioses typically act synergistically,
resulting in greater nitrogen and phosphorus content in combination than when
each is inoculated onto the legume alone. Legumes are typically coarse-rooted
and therefore inefficient in extracting phosphorus from soil. AM fungi
associated with legumes are an essential link for adequate phosphorus
nutrition, leading to enhanced nitrogenase activity.

## Enhanced Nutrient Absorption Mechanisms

The mechanisms of increased nutrient absorption by mycorrhizal fungi are
both physical and chemical:

- **Physical:** Mycorrhizal mycelia are much smaller in diameter than the
  smallest root, can explore a greater volume of soil, and provide a
  larger surface area for absorption.
- **Chemical:** The cell membrane chemistry of fungi differs from that of
  plants, enabling access to different nutrient pools.
- **Enzymatic:** Production of phosphatases, nitrogenase-enhancing
  interactions, and siderophores for micronutrient acquisition.
- **Solubilization:** Release of organic acids and other compounds that
  dissolve tightly bound mineral nutrients.

## Nutrient Conservation in Soil

Mycorrhizal fungi form a complex web that captures and assimilates
nutrients, conserving the nutrient capital in soils. In non-mycorrhizal
conditions, much of this fertility is wasted or lost from the system. The
mycorrhizal mycelium acts as a nutrient retention mechanism, reducing
leaching losses and maintaining [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] within the rooting
zone. This nutrient conservation function is especially important in
nutrient-poor and disturbed ecosystems.

## Carbon Cost to the Plant

The mutualism carries a carbon cost: plants may divert up to 80% or more of
net energy fixed as sunlight to below-ground processes. A high proportion of
this energy feeds mycorrhizal fungi and other soil organisms. However, this
is not energy lost to the plant. Soil organisms in the root zone greatly
influence the ability of plants to establish themselves through effects on
nutrient cycling, pathogen suppression, soil aeration, and water uptake.

Under phosphorus-limiting conditions, the carbon cost per unit of
phosphorus acquired through mycorrhizae is typically lower than through
direct root uptake alone, making the symbiosis economically favorable for
the plant partner.
