# Pattern Understanding and Implicate Order

## Overview

Pattern understanding represents a fundamental way of perceiving the natural world that transcends conventional reductionist thinking. Drawing from physicist David Bohm's concept of "wholeness and implicate order," this framework recognizes that all phenomena are interconnected, enfolded within a single unified process rather than existing as isolated, separate events. In [[james-lovelock]] (1979) combined scientific insights with older tribal beliefs asserting the interdependence of animate and inanimate events. The universe and Earth behave as self-regulating, self-generated constructs akin to a single organism or thought process.

### Birch and Cobb's Liberation of Life

In their 1984 work, Birch and Cobb denied the validity of individual organisms or separate events. All exist in a field of events or as an expression of one life force. Organisms exist only as inseparable parts of their event environments, in continual exchange with surrounding animate and inanimate entities. We are simultaneously acted upon and acting, created and creating, shaped and shaping.

### Capra's Tao of Physics

Theoretical physicist Fritjof Capra (1976) contributed worldviews in conflict with the ethics governing political, educational, and economic systems, contributing to an effort to unify around a common ethic of earth-care.

## Pattern Application in Design

Two aspects of patterning exist in design practice:

1. **Perception of existing patterns**: Understanding how natural patterns function in landscapes, ecosystems, and social systems.
2. **Imposition of pattern on sites**: Applying pattern-based design to achieve specific ends — strategies, harmonious problem resolution, or local resource production.

### Land Division Patterns

A bird's-eye view of centralized societies reveals strictly rectilinear networks of streets, farms, and property boundaries — patterns that serve survey instruments rather than human or environmental needs. Straight-line patterns prevent sensible landscape planning strategies and produce neither aesthetically nor functionally satisfactory landscapes.

**Hawaiian approach**: Villagers used natural ridgelines as boundaries, achieving stable, resource-rich landscapes from dense cloud-forests to outer reefs within a single water catchment. This conic and radial volcanic landscape method allows whole valleys to maintain clean catchments, store and divert mid-slope water, catch lost nutrients in shallow ocean enclosures (converting to algae, then crabs and fish), and preserve offshore reef areas.

**Zulu and American Indian approach**: Adopted circular or zonal modes for plains settlements. These models serve as templates for future bioregional societies.

## The Self as Pattern

We are, in a profound sense, the universe attempting to define its own processes. A Kalahari bushman would say we are the dreams of a dreamer. We can never truly define substance except as process — this may be all it is. Pattern understanding contributes to the continuing evolution of worldviews based on the essential oneness of all phenomena, providing a philosophical and practical basis for ecological design.

## Significance for Permaculture

Pattern understanding shifts the designer's perspective from manipulating individual elements to working with flows, connections, and emergent properties. It encourages designs that:

- Mimic natural pattern forms (spirals, branches, nets, waves)
- Work with existing landscape patterns rather than imposing arbitrary geometries
- Recognize that all design elements are interconnected in ways that may not be immediately apparent
- Create systems with multiple, reinforcing functions rather than single-purpose solutions
- Respect the limits of prediction and design for resilience against unknown perturbations
