# Canker and Gall-Associated Fungi

Canker and gall-associated fungi represent a major group of plant pathogenic organisms that cause localized, often destructive, modifications to host tissues. Cankers are sunken necrotic lesions on stems and branches resulting from localized cambium death, while galls are abnormal growths induced by parasitic organisms. Both types of infections can significantly impact plant health, forest dynamics, and agricultural productivity.

## Foliar Pathogen Recognition

Before examining cankers and galls directly, it is important to understand the broader context of foliar pathogen recognition, as many canker-causing fungi also manifest symptoms on foliage. Foliar pathogens present as water-soaked areas, distortion, chlorotic or necrotic lesions on leaf surfaces. Definitive identification requires examination of reproductive structures including acervuli, pycnidia, perithecia, and apothecia. When these structures are absent in field collections, moist chamber incubation can stimulate their development.

### Parasitic Fungi on Grasses

Temperate grasses are parasitized by a diverse assemblage of fungi:

- **Ascomycetes:** Gaeumannomyces, Gibberella, Monographella, Phyllachora, and Pleospora
- **Hyphomycetes:** Alternaria, Cladosporium, Drechslera, Fusarium, and related genera

Tropical grass parasites include Cercospora, Drechslera, Magnaporthe (anamorph: Pyricularia), Rhynchosporium, and Sphaerulina. Among these, *[[pyricularia-grisea]]* (rice blast) is one of the most important pathogens worldwide, affecting rice production in many countries and capable of parasitizing over 50 additional grass species.

### Needle-Cast Fungi

Conifer foliage is attacked by members of the Rhytismataceae, a family comprising 43 genera and approximately 344 species. Important genera include Lophodermium, Lophodermella, Elytroderma, Hypodermella, and Lirula. Symptoms typically appear during the first season of infection, but fruiting bodies may not sporulate until the infected foliage is one or more years old, complicating field identification.

## Cankers

Cankers are sunken, necrotic lesions on stems and branches caused by localized death of the cambium. They represent one of the most destructive types of fungal damage to woody plants, as they can girdle and kill entire trees and serve as entry points for wood-decay fungi.

### Woody Stem Perennial (Target) Cankers

Perennial or target cankers are characterized by a circular or lens-shaped morphology with concentric ridges formed by seasonal callus production. These cankers persist for years, expanding at approximately the same rate as the tree's radial growth. The alternating layers of callus and necrotic tissue create the distinctive target-like pattern visible in cross-section.

Key fungal groups associated with perennial cankers include:

- **Nectria** and other members of the Hypocreales
- **Ceratocystis** and the Ophiostomatales

### Woody Stem Diffuse Cankers

Diffuse cankers are more aggressive, expanding faster than the tree can produce callus tissue. They are characterized by discolored tissues, a raised margin at the leading edge of infection, and little to no callus formation. Diffuse cankers can kill a tree within a few growing seasons and are particularly common in shade-stressed understory trees.

Major fungal groups causing diffuse cankers include:

- **Diaporthales** (Valsaceae): Valsa, Diaporthe, Gnomonia, Cryptosporella, Cryphonectria, and Endothia
- **Diatrypales:** Eutypa and Leucostoma
- **Leotiales:** Cenangium, Tympanis, and Encoelia
- **Lachnellula** on conifers
- **Hypoxylon** species

### Twig and Shoot Blights

Twig and shoot blights represent the most rapidly developing canker type, completing their development within a single growing season. These infections produce little or no callus, with discolored and sunken tissues bearing conspicuous fruiting bodies. Trees predisposed by frost, drought, or wounding are especially vulnerable.

Causal agents include Melanconis, Sclerophoma, Cytospora, Phoma, Gelatinosporium, Diplodia, and various anthracnose fungi.

### Field Observation of Cankers

Optimal observation conditions vary by host type:

- **Deciduous trees:** Late winter to early spring, before leaf emergence
- **Diffuse cankers:** Look for discolored, sunken bark with a raised leading edge
- **Conifers:** Excessive pitch production at infection sites is a key diagnostic feature

## Gall-Associated Fungi

Galls are abnormal proliferations of host tissue induced by parasitic organisms. Fungal-induced galls range from subtle swellings to dramatic deformations and can occur on stems, branches, leaves, roots, and underground organs.

### Rust-Induced Galls

Several [[rust-fungi]] induce gall formation on their hosts:

- **Endocronartium harknessii:** Causes galls on lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*)
- **Gymnosporangium:** Induces galls on juniper (*Juniperus*) as part of its heteroecious life cycle

### Gall-Inducing Ascomycetes and Deuteromycetes

Multiple fungal taxa cause galls across diverse plant groups:

- **Apiosporina morbosa:** Causes black knot galls on *Prunus* species
- **Phomopsis:** Induces galls on woody temperate angiosperms
- **Nectriella pironii** and **Sphaeropsis tumefaciens:** Affect woody and herbaceous subtropical plants

### Cyttaria

*Cyttaria* is a genus of parasitic discomycetes restricted to *Nothofagus* (southern beech) hosts. The genus includes 12 species that gall branches and twigs, organized into three distinct geographic groups corresponding to the distribution of their host trees in South America, Australasia, and related regions.

### Synchytrium (Chytridiales)

*Synchytrium* is a genus of obligately biotrophic chytrid fungi comprising approximately 120 species. These fungi induce galls on an extraordinarily wide range of hosts including algae, mosses, ferns, and flowering plants. The genus has a worldwide distribution with documented species counts of 106 in North America, 24 in South America, 5 in Central America, 8 in Australia and New Zealand, 16 in Africa, 56 in Europe, and 88 in Asia.

Host specificity data for *Synchytrium* are remarkable:

- Over 1,350 host species across 800+ genera and 165 families
- *S. macrosporum* has been shown to infect over 1,300 species under greenhouse conditions
- Identification is primarily based on host determination due to the obligate biotrophic nature of the genus

### Plasmodiophoromycetes (Protoctista)

Although classified in the Protoctista rather than the true Fungi, plasmodiophoromycetes are obligate endoparasites that cause significant gall-like symptoms and are commonly studied alongside gall-associated fungi. The group comprises 15 genera found in soil and freshwater environments. Above-ground symptoms include stunting, wilting, and chlorosis, while underground symptoms include galls, lesions, warts, and dry rot.

Genera are distinguished by the arrangement of their resting spore cysts within host cells:

- **Plasmodiophora:** Cysts irregularly arranged, free within the host cell
- **Sorosphaera:** Cysts arranged in hollow spheres
- **Spongospora:** Cysts in spongelike clusters
- **Woronina:** Cysts in a single layer
- **Sorodiscus:** Cysts in bilayered disks
- **Octomyxa:** Cysts in groups of eight
- **Tetramyxa:** Cysts in groups of two to four
- **Membranosorus:** Irregular cysts in a single layer

Plasmodiophoromycetes cannot be cultured on artificial media, so identification requires microscopic examination of infected host cells to observe the characteristic cyst arrangements.

## Fungi on Fruits, Nuts, and Flowers

Many leaf-infecting fungi also attack reproductive structures. Blossom blights are commonly caused by members of the Sclerotiniaceae, particularly *Botrytis* and *Monilinia*. *Monilinia* species produce characteristic fruit mummies through brown rot infections. Scab diseases are caused by *Elsinoë*/*Sphaceloma* in tropical regions and *Venturia* in temperate zones. Additional fruit rot pathogens include *Aspergillus*, *Colletotrichum*, *Dothiorella*, *Guignardia*, *Phytophthora*, and *Rhizopus*.

## Ecological and Economic Significance

Canker and gall fungi play critical roles in forest ecosystem dynamics by contributing to stand thinning, creating entry points for secondary colonizers, and influencing species composition. In agricultural and horticultural settings, these fungi cause significant economic losses through reduced yields, tree mortality, and the cost of management interventions. Understanding the diversity, biology, and field identification of these organisms is essential for both forest pathologists and plant disease diagnosticians.
