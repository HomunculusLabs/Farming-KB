## Mechanism of Poisoning

Palmer proposed a theory of absorption that was remarkably prescient for the 1870s.
The alkaloid enters the system by absorption through the villi of the intestinal
lining. Once absorbed, removing every vestige of the mushroom from the stomach
might leave the system "so charged with poison as to refuse to rally."

Critically, Palmer recognized that the poison could enter the body through routes
other than ingestion. He documented three personal experiments demonstrating
percutaneous and inhalation exposure:

1. **Dermal absorption**: Holding an Amanita in a closed, perspiring hand during a
   long walk produced full symptoms including nausea, vertigo, chills, and purging.
2. **Olfactory absorption**: Smelling Amanita specimens in a closed box on his desk
   produced vomiting and stomach oppression three hours later, with livid
   discoloration of the eyes lasting through the following day.
3. **Oral testing**: Chewing a piece the size of a half-dime and ejecting it
   produced throat constriction, vertigo, and sea-sickness-like disgust. Symptoms
   peaked seventeen hours later with tenesmus, then passed.

## The Atropine Discovery

Schmiedeberg and Koppe established the fundamental pharmacological antagonism: the
throbbing heart of a frog is silenced by a drop of muscarine, and action is
restored by a drop of atropine. Animals injected with atropine are protected from
a fatal dose of muscarine. This reciprocal antagonism between a fungal poison and
a plant-derived alkaloid was among the earliest examples of [[nicotine]] (from

## The Diagnostic Key

Palmer provided physicians with a crucial diagnostic tool: the latency of onset
distinguishes Amanita poisoning from other types. Gastrointestinal irritants
from tough or acrid fungi cause immediate symptoms with good prognosis. Amanitine
poisoning manifests only after eight hours or more, indicating systemic absorption
and grave danger. The physician should "inquire carefully how much time has passed
between the suspected meal and the attack. The shorter this elapsed time, the more
hope for success."
