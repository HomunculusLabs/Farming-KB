# Siderophores of Mycorrhizal Fungi Siderophores are low-molecular-weight iron-chelating compounds produced by microorganisms under iron-limiting conditions. Mycorrhizal fungi produce siderophores as a strategy for iron acquisition from soil, and these compounds also play roles in microbial competition, biocontrol, and potentially in plant iron nutrition. The siderophores of mycorrhizal fungi are predominantly hydroxamate-type compounds. ## Importance of Iron in Soil Iron is one of the most abundant elements in soil but is largely unavailable to organisms due to its low solubility under aerobic conditions at neutral to alkaline pH. In well-aerated soils, iron exists primarily as Fe(III) (ferric iron), which forms insoluble hydroxides and oxides. Microorganisms and plants have evolved various strategies to access this limiting nutrient, with siderophore production being one of the most effective. ## Types of Mycorrhizal Fungal Siderophores ### Hydroxamate Siderophores Hydroxamate siderophores are the dominant type produced by mycorrhizal fungi. They contain hydroxamic acid groups (-N(OH)-C=O) that coordinate Fe(III) with very high affinity. Several structural families have been identified: #### Ferrichromes Ferrichromes are hexadentate siderophores forming octahedral complexes with Fe(III). Key members produced by mycorrhizal fungi include: - **Ferrichrome** (Mr ~730): The simplest ferrichrome, with three glycine residues - **Ferricrocin** (Mr ~753): Contains one serine residue; very common in higher fungi - **Ferrichrysin** (Mr ~783): Contains one alanine residue - **Ferrichrome A** (Mr ~999): Contains a longer N-acyl chain (beta-methylglutaconyl), making it more lipophilic - **Ferrirubin** (Mr ~1010): Contains an N-alpha-acetyl group with a specific side chain - **Ferrirhodin** (Mr ~1010): Similar structure to ferrirubin with a different side chain configuration The more lipophilic ferrichromes (A, ferrirubin, ferrirhodin) are generally easier to separate by HPLC due to their longer retention times. #### Coprogens Coprogens are trihydroxamate siderophores with a different structural backbone: - **Coprogen** (Mr ~821): Contains three N(delta)-acyl-N(delta)-hydroxyornithine residues - **Neocoprogen I** (Mr ~): One side chain is the coprogen acyl group, the other is methyl - **Neocoprogen II** (Mr ~): Both side chains are methyl groups - **Dimerum acid**: A linear coprogen-related compound #### Fusarinines Fusarinines are simpler hydroxamate siderophores: - **Fusigen** (Mr ~779): A cyclic trihydroxamate - **Triacetylfusarinine C** (Mr ~905): Also called triacetylfusigen; a highly lipophilic siderophore ### Polycarboxylate Siderophores - **Rhizoferrin** (Mr ~436): Originally isolated from Zygomycetes (Mucorales). ### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi

AMF siderophore production is less well characterized due to their obligate biotrophic nature, which makes in vitro culture difficult. Evidence suggests that AMF may:

- Produce siderophores within roots that contribute to plant iron nutrition
- Rely partially on their associated [[cultivar-endorhiza-bacterial-communities-cannabis]] for siderophore-mediated iron mobilization
- Express genes related to iron metabolism and siderophore transport

### Ericoid Mycorrhizal Fungi

Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi produce siderophores that may be particularly important in the acidic, often iron-rich but poorly available conditions of their native soils. Some ericoid fungi can access iron from insoluble sources, potentially through siderophore-mediated dissolution.

## Detection and Analysis Methods

### Chrome Azurol S (CAS) Assay

The CAS assay is the most widely used method for detecting siderophore production:

- **Principle**: Chrome azurol S forms a blue ternary complex with Fe(III) and a detergent (HDTMA). Siderophores, being stronger chelators, remove Fe(III) from this complex, causing a color change from blue to orange/yellow
- **Solution assay**: Used for quantifying siderophore activity in [[comparison-liquid-culture-vs-grain-spawn]] supernatants
- **Plate assay**: CAS [[query-how-to-make-agar-plates-for-mushroom-cultivation]] allow direct screening of siderophore-producing colonies; colonies that produce siderophores develop an orange halo

#### CAS Dye Solution Preparation

- 6.0 mg chrome azurol S in 5 mL distilled water
- 1 mL Fe(III) solution (1 mM FeCl3 in 10 mM HCl)
- Mix to form CAS-Fe solution
- 7.29 mg hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (HDTMA) in 4 mL water
- Combine HDTMA solution with CAS-Fe solution and sterilize

### HPLC Separation

Individual siderophores are identified and quantified by reversed-phase HPLC:

- **Column**: C18 reversed phase (4.6 x 250 mm, 5 micrometer)
- **Mobile phase**: Gradient of acetonitrile/water (6-40%) with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) or formic acid at pH 2-3
- **Detection**: UV at 220 nm (general) or 435 nm (hydroxamate-specific)
- **Reference compounds**: Available commercially (e.g., EMC Microcollections, Tuebingen, Germany) for co-chromatography confirmation

Semi-preparative HPLC (8 x 250 mm, C18, 7 micrometer) allows purification of siderophores for structural identification.

## Ecological Roles

### Iron Acquisition

The primary function of siderophores is iron acquisition from soil mineral phases. Mycorrhizal fungal siderophores can solubilize iron from:

- Iron oxides and hydroxides
- Iron-containing silicate minerals
- Organic matter-bound iron complexes

### Biocontrol

Siderophore production contributes to biocontrol activity by:

- Sequestering iron from the rhizosphere, creating iron-limiting conditions that inhibit pathogen growth
- Providing a competitive advantage in iron-limited soil environments
- Some siderophores may have direct antimicrobial activity beyond iron deprivation

### Plant Nutrition

Mycorrhizal fungal siderophores may directly contribute to plant iron nutrition:

- Ferric-siderophore complexes may be taken up by plant roots or reduced at the root surface
## Iron Acquisition Mechanisms

Mycorrhizal fungi employ a multi-step process for iron mobilization.
Under iron-limited conditions, fungal hyphae upregulate siderophore
biosynthesis genes and secrete chelating compounds into the soil
matrix. The Fe(III)-siderophore complex is recognized by
specific membrane receptors on the hyphal surface and transported
into the cell via ABC transporter proteins. Inside the hypha, Fe(III)
is reduced to Fe(II) by ferric reductase enzymes, releasing the
siderophore for reuse or degradation. This mechanism is particularly
effective in calcareous soils where iron availability is severely
limited by high pH. The hyphal network extends the depletion zone
far beyond the root surface, effectively mining iron from soil
volumes that plant roots alone cannot access.

Siderophore-producing mycorrhizal fungi show promise for soil
bioremediation beyond nutrient cycling. Their chelating activity
can mobilize heavy metals such as uranium, aluminum, and cadmium
