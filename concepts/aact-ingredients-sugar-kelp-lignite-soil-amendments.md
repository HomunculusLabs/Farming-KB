# AACT Ingredients — Sugar, Kelp, Lignite, and Soil Amendments

Understanding why specific ingredients are added to compost tea — and what happens when they are added to soil — requires understanding the soil foodweb. Each ingredient feeds different organism groups, and the interactions between these groups determine whether the addition helps or harms plant health.

## Simple Sugars as Bacterial Food

Simple sugars (molasses, white sugar, fruit juices, etc.) are **bacterial foods**. When added to soil or tea, they trigger rapid bacterial growth. As bacteria multiply, they consume not just the sugar but all available nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and micronutrients. This creates a zone of nutrient depletion where nothing else can grow. Bacteria win in direct competition when they have sufficient simple carbon because they reproduce so rapidly.

### The Anaerobic Risk

Adding too much sugar drives the soil anaerobic as bacteria grow faster than oxygen can diffuse into the soil. This has several cascading consequences:

- **Compacted or poorly structured soils**: [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] persist indefinitely, severely damaging seed germination and root growth. Seeds may rot before germinating.
- **Well-structured soils**: The impact is temporary — as oxygen returns through well-developed pore spaces, normal organisms revive and life continues with minimal lasting damage.
- **Pest displacement**: Anaerobic conditions can drive away pests like wireworms and cutworms that require some oxygen. However, the pests may simply relocate a short distance and wait for roots to arrive, or may prefer the reduced-oxygen conditions near roots weakened by the anaerobic zone.

### The Sugar-Pest Myth

Claims that molasses or sugar causes root-feeding nematodes to "explode" are unsupported by evidence. Root-feeding nematodes have no mechanism to take up sugars from soil solution — they feed on plant cellular liquid through a stylet (sap-sucking behavior). They have thick cuticles that resist bacterial attack, and they prefer reduced-oxygen conditions.

The observation that sugar addition sometimes reduces pests may relate to alcohol production by fermenting bacteria and yeasts in anaerobic zones — the root-feeding nematodes might become intoxicated. However, this mechanism is speculative and has not been demonstrated experimentally. Sugar addition only appears to work when soil is badly compacted, and claiming it kills pests in all conditions is inappropriate and misleading.

### Proper Sugar Application

If sugar is added carefully enough to benefit [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]] without driving anaerobic conditions, soil structure actually improves. Bacteria produce glues and extracellular polymeric substances, form microaggregates, and enhance oxygen diffusion through improved soil structure. When soil structure improves, the organisms that prey on pests can return to the soil.

The critical question is: how much sugar is the right amount? The answer depends entirely on soil health, compaction level, existing organism populations, and oxygen diffusion capacity. There is no universal formula — testing is required.

## Kelp: Micronutrients and Fungal Surface Area

Kelp serves multiple functions in compost tea and soil:

- **Micronutrients**: Kelp contains a full spectrum of micronutrients for both plants and microorganisms, including boron, iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and molybdenum.
- **Plant growth factors**: Kelp contains cytokinins, auxins, gibberellins, and other growth promoters. However, bacteria and fungi rapidly consume these compounds in the tea, so they likely benefit plants indirectly through the biological community rather than through direct foliar absorption.
- **Surface area for fungi**: Kelp provides physical surface area that fungi can colonize and grow on during the brewing process.
- **Growth foods**: Kelp contains all the foods normally found in plant cytoplasm, making it excellent food for both bacteria and fungi — slightly favoring bacteria in most cases.

Cold water kelp extraction preserves more of the heat-sensitive growth factors and hormones than hot water extraction. The Acadian kelp company has documented extensive trial results showing benefits from their cold-water-extracted product.

### Nutrient Availability Through Biology

Plants cannot directly access nutrients in raw kelp material. The nutrients are locked in [[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]] that plant roots cannot absorb. Protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods must be present to cycle these nutrients into plant-available forms through their feeding activities. Without the full foodweb, kelp nutrients remain locked in organic form and unavailable to plants.

This is why applying kelp alone to degraded soil often shows limited results — the biology needed to unlock kelp's nutrients is missing. Apply compost tea first to restore the foodweb, then kelp becomes far more effective.

## Lignite: Fungal Food Source

Lignite (a form of young coal/leonardite) is a source of **very complex foods that selectively feed fungi**. Only after fungal enzymes begin breaking down the lignite structure are any bacterial food resources released. This makes lignite an excellent ingredient for fungal-dominant teas and for building fungal biomass in soil.

The complexity of lignite means that fungi — with their enzymatic ability to break down complex carbon compounds — get a head start over bacteria. Bacteria must wait for the fungal enzymes to release simpler compounds before they can participate. This temporal separation of fungal and bacterial growth is valuable when trying to establish fungal dominance.

## Fish Products: Bacterial vs. Fungal Foods

- **Fish emulsion**: Feeds mostly bacteria. The oils have been removed during processing, leaving primarily simple proteins and soluble nutrients that bacteria rapidly assimilate.
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Contains fish oil plus simpler proteins, feeding **fungi more than bacteria**. The oil component is particularly important for fungal nutrition — fungi use lipids as energy sources and membrane components. Hydrolysate is produced by enzymatic digestion at low temperatures, preserving more of the original compounds than emulsion.

## Humic Acids

Humic acids are the most selective fungal food available. They are derived from decomposed [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] provide complex carbon compounds that primarily support fungal growth. Humic acids also appear to benefit certain biocontrol fungi like Beauveria, making them a dual-purpose ingredient when biocontrol is a goal.

## High-Nitrogen Ingredients

Several common ingredients provide nitrogen but require careful management due to their rapid effect on oxygen demand:

- **Crab waste (granulated)**: Meat products push rapid bacterial growth — monitor dissolved oxygen carefully.
- **Alfalfa meal (flaked)**: High nitrogen content — add only if plants need nitrogen. Otherwise, you are feeding bacteria at the expense of other organism groups.
- **Blood meal**: Very high nitrogen and a very rich food resource — watch oxygen levels closely. Can rapidly drive tea anaerobic if over-applied.
- **Fish waste (granulated)**: Rapid bacterial growth driver — careful with oxygen concentration.

## Mineral Ingredients

Minerals in compost tea serve as nutrients that microbes help transport into plants. However, before adding minerals to tea or soil, conduct both soil chemistry tests and plant tissue tests. The relationship between soil nutrients and plant uptake is mediated by biology:

- A nutrient may appear **limiting in soil** but be **adequate in plant tissue** because organism-mediated uptake is functioning efficiently.
- Conversely, when a nutrient is **high in soil** but **low in plant tissue**, the biology needed to mobilize and deliver that nutrient is missing — adding more mineral will not help until the biology is restored.

Mineral ingredients include:
- **Greensand** (potassium, iron, magnesium, and trace minerals)
- **Rock phosphate** (phosphorus and calcium)
- **Rock dusts from quarries** (broad spectrum minerals)

## Extract vs. AACT

Some machines produce excellent organism numbers using an extraction method, but this produces a **compost extract**, not an [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-overview]] tea. AACT requires the brewing step — a period of active microbial growth and reproduction that allows the community to develop and multiply. The distinction matters because:

- **Extraction** only removes organisms present in the compost — no growth occurs during the process.
- **Brewing** allows organisms to multiply (often 10–100×), develop community interactions, and reach higher population densities.

## Worm Leachate

Worm leachate (the liquid draining from vermicompost beds) is best applied to root systems. It typically contains plant growth promoters, enzymes, and hormones that benefit plant growth directly. It is distinct from both compost extracts and actively aerated compost tea in its composition and intended use.

## Testing New Ingredients

The only way to determine whether a new ingredient benefits or harms the organisms in a brew is to test for **active organism impacts**. Plate count methods generally cannot detect these changes because they only measure total organisms, not active ones. Direct microscopy or [[biodiversity-of-fungi-pcr-molecular-methods-fungal-diversity]] are required to see whether organisms are actually growing and functioning in response to the ingredient. Active organism counts tell you what the organisms are doing, not just whether they are present.
