# Passive Cooling Strategies for Tropical and Subtropical Houses

Designing comfortable homes in tropical and subtropical regions presents unique challenges. While equatorial climates require only heat reduction, subtropical locations face the dual problem of intense winter cold (with frosts and occasional snow) and very humid, hot summers. The subtropical house must both heat and cool effectively—a design challenge requiring more careful planning than either purely tropical or purely temperate housing.

## Understanding Thermal Comfort

Optimum comfort levels for people occur at dry-bulb temperatures of 20°C (68°F) in still air during winter, and 25°C (77°F) in summer. However, humidity dramatically shifts perceived temperature: above 40% relative humidity, every additional percentage point effectively adds warmth. At 70% humidity—a common condition in the wet-dry tropics—sensible temperature can exceed 30°C (86°F), producing heat stress.

A wet-bulb thermometer (mercury bulb kept damp by a cotton wick in water) provides a useful indicator. Below 18°C (65°F) wet-bulb temperature, people remain fairly comfortable. Above this, active cooling strategies become essential.

Key factors that accentuate heat discomfort include:
- Nearby radiant surfaces (walls or pavement exceeding 38°C/100°F add 1°C per degree radiated)
- Lack of air movement (any airflow above 1 m/second subtracts 1°C from perceived temperature)
- High humidity preventing evaporative cooling through sweating

## The Six Cooling Principles

### 1. Shade

Light and heat are both excluded as incoming radiation in shade. Shade is particularly critical on massive walls (which absorb and re-radiate heat) and over water tanks close to homes. Strategies include wide overhanging eaves, planted shade trees, attached shadehouses, and climbing vine-covered pergolas. The ideal shade trees for houses are palms and tall-trunk species that permit airflow at ground level while blocking overhead radiation.

### 2. Transpiration Cooling

Plants assist cooling by transpiring moisture into the air. Partial shade helps this factor in understory species, except in extremely wet conditions where transpiration may be limited. Gardens, lawns, and climbing plants near and on buildings all contribute to this evaporative cooling effect.

### 3. Cool Bodies (Thermal Mass)

Large heat reservoirs such as water tanks and shaded blocks of stone, concrete, or mud brick absorb heat from the air and from warm bodies, providing a cooling effect. Conversely, hot radiant bodies (sun-heated walls, dark pavement) adversely affect comfort. The key insight is that thermal mass should be shaded during the day and exposed to cool night air to reset its thermal state.

### 4. Air Flows

Even low air flows from shaded areas greatly aid both transpiration and evaporative cooling. Creating cross-ventilation requires developing relatively hotter and colder air sources and providing a clear pathway between them. Essential design features include:

- Clear, straight-through airways with no unnecessary corners
- Large vents for good air volume through workrooms and storage areas
- Vertical shutters and louvres that act as air scoops
- Vented ceiling slopes that allow hot room air to escape while cool air enters

### 5. Removing Heat Sources

All massive cook-stoves and hot-water systems are best placed in a semi-detached kitchen, especially in the tropics. These are commonly reached via a vine-covered shade area, shaded by palms or trees, with wide eaves and ceiling vents for hot air escape. Separating heat-producing functions from living spaces dramatically reduces the cooling load on the main structure.

### 6. Earth-Cooling and Thermal Chimney Systems

Heat can be used productively via metal roof areas, hot-water storages, attached glasshouses, or solar chimneys to vent hot air and create updraught. This updraught acts as a heat engine to draw in cool air from lower or cooler sources.

## Earth-Cooled Air Tunnels

Probably the most effective cooling system for tropical houses uses a hot roof or metal chimney to draw cold air from earth-cooled underground tunnels or pipes. Cool air is dense and naturally flows downhill or sinks to lower levels. It can be drawn into houses via positive exhaust systems or actively fanned into rooms.

### Construction Details

To build an earth-cooled air tunnel:
1. Construct a trench **1 meter deep** and **15–20 meters long** in the earth
2. Drain off condensed water (ideally self-draining to a lower slope)
3. Provide the trench or pipe with a sloping floor
4. Screen the intake end with box screening to exclude mice
5. Shade the intake with plants
6. Connect outlets to floor grills or louvred "cupboard" openings

The cold tunnel solution is very effective and can be combined with evaporative cooling in desert housing. However, it is expensive and difficult to retrofit to existing structures.

## Cross-Ventilation Design

Natural cross-ventilation occurs when a well-sealed room has a roof vent or chimney to create updraught. The essentials of good cross-ventilation include:

- A simple pathway for flowing air (no unnecessary corners)
- Large vents allowing good air volume through workrooms and storage
- Roof vents or chimneys for hot air exit
- Vertical sashes or shutters to each room to scoop air in
- Air scoops as a satisfactory solution on subtropical tradewind coasts

A shadehouse added to the poleward side of a house and cross-ventilated to a well-vented greenhouse on the sun side creates an effective passive cooling system. The greenhouse heats, creating updraught that pulls cool air through the shadehouse and living spaces.

## Practical Retrofit Strategies

For homes that cannot accommodate earth-cooled tunnels, several simpler approaches provide significant relief:

- **Vertical shutters** acting as air scoops on windward walls
- **Shadehouses** on the cooler side of the building
- **White or reflective exterior surfaces** to reduce heat absorption
- **Vented metal roofs** with thin (12 mm) ceilings and permeable soffit lining
- **Ceiling vents** combined with low-level inlet vents to create stack-effect ventilation
- **Climbing vines** on trellises to shade walls without blocking airflow

## Storage Considerations

High humidity causes problems beyond human discomfort: salt will not pour, clothes mildew, food spoils, film and books degrade. Both cool and warm storages are needed, but both must be dry. Earth-cooled air tunnels can open first into a pantry area or a half-buried cool store for root and tuber crops. A cold air tunnel passing through a pantry before entering living spaces provides dual benefit—food preservation and human comfort.

## The Subtropical Challenge

The subtropical house requires a more nuanced design than either equatorial or temperate homes. For heating, it needs an insulated slab floor or trombe wall. For cooling, it needs induced or forced cross-ventilation from a cool or shaded area to an updraught area. The house must work effectively in both modes, with design elements that serve both heating and cooling functions depending on the season. Careful orientation, material selection, and integration of plant systems are essential to meeting this dual challenge.
