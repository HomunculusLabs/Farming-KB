# Super Spawning and Active Mycelium Techniques

## Overview

Super spawning, also called "active mycelium spawning" or the Hunke-Till
process, is a method of inoculating bulk substrates using fully colonized
substrate as the inoculum rather than traditional grain spawn. This technique
allows cultivators to pyramid a small quantity of initial inoculum into a
considerable amount of fully colonized substrate, offering significant
efficiency advantages for certain cultivation scenarios.

## How Super Spawning Works

The process follows a straightforward principle: first, a set amount of
substrate is inoculated and colonized in the normal manner using grain spawn or
another method. Once fully colonized, this substrate is used as inoculum
("super spawn") to inoculate increased amounts of a similar substrate. The
newly inoculated substrate colonizes rapidly and becomes available for fruiting
or for yet another round of expansion.

Theoretically, a cultivator could pyramid a small quantity of inoculum into a
very large volume of fully colonized substrate through successive rounds of
super spawning. However, practical limits exist due to contamination risk and
strain degeneration over multiple generations.

## Critical Requirement: Contaminant-Free Primary

The most important requirement for successful super spawning is that the primary
substrate must be contaminant-free. Because the fully colonized substrate is
broken up and mixed into new substrate, any contamination present will be
propagated and multiplied rather than mycelium. A single contaminated batch can
destroy an entire chain of subsequent inoculations.

This is the fundamental difference from grain-to-grain transfer: grain spawn is
sterilized in sealed containers, whereas bulk substrates used in super spawning
are typically only pasteurized, leaving some thermophilic organisms present but
not competing molds or bacteria.

## When to Use Super Spawning

Super spawning is best suited for outdoor and low-technology cultivation
scenarios where laboratory-grade sterility is impractical. It works particularly
well for species with aggressive colonization that can outcompete contaminants
on pasteurized substrates. Species like Pleurotus ([[query-how-to-grow-oyster-mushrooms-at-home]]) and
Stropharia rugoso-annulata are excellent candidates.

The technique is less appropriate for species that require sterile substrates,
have slow colonization rates, or are being cultivated in indoor environments
where traditional grain spawn methods are already practical. Species requiring
supplemented substrates or specific nutritional profiles may not perform well
with the simple substrate-to-substrate transfer approach.

## Step-by-Step Process

The basic super spawning procedure follows these steps:

1. Prepare and pasteurize the initial substrate (straw, sawdust, or wood chips)
2. Inoculate with grain spawn at the standard rate (typically 10-20% by volume)
3. Incubate at appropriate temperature until fully colonized (7-21 days)
4. Break up the fully colonized substrate into small pieces
5. Mix the colonized pieces with fresh pasteurized substrate at a 1:5 ratio
6. Pack the mixture into growing containers or beds
7. Incubate again until fully colonized (typically faster than the first round)
8. Introduce fruiting conditions once colonization is complete

Each successive round of expansion should use a fresh batch of pasteurized
substrate. The ratio of colonized to fresh substrate can be adjusted based on
the species vigor and the cultivator's confidence in substrate cleanliness.

## Advantages vs Grain Spawn

Super spawning offers several advantages over traditional grain spawn methods.
It eliminates the need for jars, pressure cooking, and sterile laboratory work
for subsequent generations after the initial grain spawn step. This dramatically
reduces equipment costs and labor requirements. A small amount of colonized
material can inoculate large volumes of new substrate rapidly.

Grain spawn, by contrast, offers greater precision and control. Each jar of
grain spawn is individually sterilized and can be quality-checked before use.
Grain-to-grain transfers maintain genetic consistency and allow for storage of
master cultures. The standard Agaricus spawning rate of seven liters per ton of
compost provides a predictable inoculation density that super spawning cannot
easily match.

## Common Mistakes

The most frequent error in super spawning is using a primary substrate that
contains hidden contamination. Even small pockets of mold or bacteria will be
amplified through each generation, eventually overwhelming the mushroom
mycelium. Cultivators should carefully inspect the primary substrate for any
discoloration, off-smells, or unusual textures before using it as super spawn.

Another common mistake is attempting too many successive generations. Each
round of expansion increases the risk of contamination and may select for
less vigorous mycelial strains. Limiting expansion to two or three generations
from the original grain spawn is a prudent practice.

Over-wetting the substrate during mixing is also problematic. The colonized
primary substrate already contains moisture, and adding waterlogged fresh
substrate creates anaerobic conditions that favor bacterial contamination rather
than [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]].

## Best Applications

### Natural Culture on Wood Chips

Super spawning is particularly effective for natural culture methods. The
propagation of [[growing-gourmet-caramel-capped-psilocybe-cyanescens-cultivation]] on wood chips is an excellent example
described by Stamets: locate naturally occurring mycelium on alder, maple, or
fir chips; collect colonized chip pieces as "virgin spawn"; mix with fresh
chips in a 1:5 ratio; store in gallon jars or protective containers indoors;
with sufficient moisture and minimal air exchange, mycelium spreads throughout
the fresh chips within weeks.

### Pasteurized Straw Substrates

For species like Pleurotus and Stropharia rugoso-annulata, super spawning works
well with pasteurized straw. Fully colonize an initial straw substrate using
grain spawn, break up the colonized straw and mix it with fresh pasteurized
straw, and the mycelium rapidly colonizes the new substrate.

### Perennial Production

Once an [[azurescens-outdoor-bed-preparation-woodchips]] is established, it can be likened to a perennial plant;
chips can be drawn annually to expand the growing area by a factor of five.
This self-sustaining expansion is one of the most powerful applications of
the super spawning technique for outdoor cultivation.

## Limitations and Risks

Contamination amplification is the primary risk: any contamination in the
primary substrate is multiplied through each generation. Strain degeneration
from repeated subculturing can lead to loss of vigor, similar to excessive
grain-to-grain transfers. Not all species are suitable; those requiring sterile
substrates or specific nutritional profiles may not perform well.
