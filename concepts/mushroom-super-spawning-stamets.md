# Super Spawning and Active Mycelium Techniques

## Overview

Super spawning, also called "active mycelium spawning" or the Hunke-Till
process, is a method of inoculating bulk substrates using fully colonized
substrate as the inoculum rather than traditional grain spawn. This
technique allows cultivators to pyramid a small quantity of initial inoculum
into a considerable amount of fully colonized substrate, offering
significant efficiency advantages for certain cultivation scenarios.

## How Super Spawning Works

The process follows a straightforward principle:

1. A set amount of substrate is inoculated and colonized in the normal
   manner using grain spawn or another method
2. Once fully colonized, this substrate is used as inoculum ("super spawn")
   to inoculate increased amounts of a similar substrate
3. The newly inoculated substrate colonizes rapidly and becomes available
   for fruiting or for yet another round of expansion

Theoretically, a cultivator could pyramid a small quantity of inoculum into
a very large volume of fully colonized substrate through successive rounds
of super spawning.

## Critical Requirement: Contaminant-Free Primary

The most important requirement for successful super spawning is that the
primary substrate must be contaminant-free. Because the fully colonized
substrate is broken up and mixed into new substrate, any contamination
present will be propagated and multiplied rather than mycelium. A single
contaminated batch can destroy an entire chain of subsequent inoculations.

This is the fundamental difference from grain-to-grain transfer: grain
spawn is sterilized in sealed containers, whereas bulk substrates used in
super spawning are typically only pasteurized, leaving some thermophilic
organisms present but not competing molds or bacteria.

## Best Applications

### Natural Culture on Wood Chips

Super spawning is particularly effective for natural culture methods. The
propagation of Psilocybe cyanescens on wood chips is an excellent example
described by Stamets:

1. Locate naturally occurring mycelium on alder, maple, or fir chips
2. Collect colonized chip pieces as "virgin spawn"
3. Mix with fresh chips in a 1:5 ratio (virgin spawn to fresh chips)
4. Store in gallon jars or protective containers indoors
5. With sufficient moisture and minimal air exchange, mycelium spreads
   throughout the fresh chips within weeks
6. Once one jar is fully colonized, it can inoculate more chips

### Pasteurized Straw Substrates

For species like Pleurotus (oyster mushrooms) and Stropharia rugoso-
annulata, super spawning works well with pasteurized straw:

1. Fully colonize an initial straw substrate using grain spawn
2. Break up the colonized straw and mix it with fresh pasteurized straw
3. The mycelium rapidly colonizes the new substrate
4. This method can be repeated for several expansions

### Non-Sterile Substrates

Super spawning is ideally suited to non-sterile substrates because the
rapidly growing mushroom mycelium can outcompete remaining organisms in
the substrate. The key advantage is that full sterilization of large
volumes of bulk substrate is not required.

## Advantages

1. **No grain spawn needed**: Eliminates the entire grain spawn production
   process for certain species and methods
2. **Lower cost**: Reduces or eliminates the need for jars, pressure
   cooking, and sterile laboratory work
3. **Rapid expansion**: A small amount of colonized material can inoculate
   large volumes of new substrate
4. **Perennial production**: Once an outdoor bed is established, it can be
   likened to a perennial plant; chips can be drawn annually to expand
   the growing area by a factor of five
5. **Simplicity**: The technique is straightforward and requires minimal
   specialized equipment

## Limitations and Risks

1. **Contamination amplification**: Any contamination in the primary
   substrate is multiplied through each generation
2. **Strain degeneration**: Repeated subculturing can lead to loss of
   vigor, similar to excessive grain-to-grain transfers
3. **Not suitable for all species**: Species requiring sterile substrates
   or specific nutritional profiles may not perform well
4. **Lower precision**: Less controlled than traditional sterile inoculation
   methods
5. **Environmental dependency**: Outdoor methods are subject to seasonal
   and climate limitations

## Comparison to Traditional Spawning

Traditional mixed spawning uses grain spawn that has been fully sterilized
and is known to be clean. The standard Agaricus spawning rate is seven
liters per ton of compost or one quart per eight square feet. Super
spawning replaces grain with colonized substrate material, which offers
cost savings but requires confidence in the cleanliness of the primary
culture.

## Supplementation Considerations

When super spawning, delayed release nutrients or supplementation at
casing (SAC) can boost yields. Supplements include soy bean meal,
cottonseed meal, and ground rye or wheat grains at 10% of substrate dry
weight. However, supplementation increases contamination risk and should
only be used with well-established, vigorously colonized cultures.
