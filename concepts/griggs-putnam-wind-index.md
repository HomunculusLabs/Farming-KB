# Griggs-Putnam Wind Index

## Overview

The **[[griggs-and-putnam-index]]** (G) is a field-based scale that assesses wind speed by observing the physical deformation of trees. Because trees permanently record the effects of prevailing winds in their growth form, they serve as living anemometers — providing reliable site-level wind data without instruments.

The index was developed from work by Wade and Hewson (1979) at Oregon State University's Department of Atmospheric Sciences, and correlates tree deformation with annual mean wind speed with approximately **17% accuracy**.

## The Scale

| Index (G) | Effect | Description | Mean Wind Speed (m/sec) | Mean Wind Speed (mph) |
|-----------|--------|-------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| 0 | No Effect | No deformations of branches | <3.3 | <7.5 |
| 1 | Brushing | Branches or needles bent away from prevailing winds. Crown slightly deformed. | 3.3–4.2 | 7.5–9.5 |
| 2 | Slight Flagging | Small branches bent. Tree markedly asymmetric. | 4.3–5.1 | 9.6–11.5 |
| 3 | Moderate Flagging | Larger branches bent away from the wind. | 5.2–6.2 | 11.6–14.0 |
| 4 | Strong Flagging | All branches swept to leeward. Tree becomes a "banner." | 6.3–7.5 | 14.0–17.0 |
| 5 | Partial Throw | Trunk bent to leeward but vertical near ground. | 7.8–8.5 | 17.0–19.0 |
| 6 | Complete Throw | Trunk now quite bent to leeward. | 8.6–11.0 | 19.0–24.0 |
| 7 | Carpeting | Tree becomes a creeping shrub-like layer of prostrate growth. | >11.0 | >24.0 |

**Note:** These effects are standard for trees not affected by salt, ice, or snow load.

## Application in Design

When assessing any site, **older trees are the best guide** to gauge wind effect. Trees indicate local wind direction and intensity, allowing designers to:

- **Place windbreaks** to reduce heat loss in homes
- **Avoid damage** from catastrophic winds by proper placement of structures
- **Steer winds** to well-placed wind machines for energy generation
- **Assess microclimate** without expensive instrumentation

Trees "flag" or "throw" permanently in prevailing strong winds, forming a permanent site record of wind history. This makes them invaluable for permaculture site analysis and design.

## Temperature Effects of Trees

### Evaporative Cooling

Evaporation causes heat loss locally, while condensation causes heat gain. An ordinary elm of medium size transpires approximately **15,000 pounds of water on a clear, dry, hot day.** Evapotranspiration in a 40-inch rainfall area is generally not less than 15 inches per year.

This means tree evaporation by day **cools air in hot weather**, while night condensation of atmospheric water **warms the surrounding air.** Leaves provide condensation surfaces and contact cooling. Leaf surfaces are likely cooler than other objects at evening due to evaporation from stomata by day.

### Leaf Properties and Temperature

Leaves are approximately **86% water**, giving them twice the specific heat of soil — remaining cooler than soil by day and warmer at night. Plants may be **15°C warmer** than surrounding air temperature.

**Color effects on temperature:**
- **Reddish-colored leaves** (some vines and shrubs) reflect chiefly red light rays, producing temperature decreases of up to **20°C (36°F)** compared with green-pigmented plants (Daubenmire, 1974)
- **Whitish plants** (wormwood, birch) may reflect **85%** of incoming light
- **Dark shade-plant leaves** may reflect as little as **2%**

### Practical Applications

White or red-colored **roof vines over tiles** can effectively lower summer temperatures within buildings. Additional cooling comes from fine water sprays and damp mulch systems under trellises, creating cool dense air by evaporation — useful for moderating building heat and providing cool air for cross-ventilation.

Small open water storages or tree clumps upwind of a house have a pleasant moderating effect. Air passing over open water is cooled in summer and warmed with moisture added even in winter. Only water captured by trees has a **dehumidifying effect** in hot, humid tropical areas.
