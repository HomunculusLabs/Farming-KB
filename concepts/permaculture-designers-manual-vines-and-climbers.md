# Permaculture Designers Manual - Vines and Climbers

Vines and climbers play a key role in permaculture design across all climate zones. They serve multiple functions including food production, shade, climate moderation, mulch and forage production, erosion control, and structural material. The Designers' Manual addresses vines primarily in the contexts of dryland gardens, temperate home gardens, and tropical polycultures.

## Vines in Desert Gardens

Vines have a key role in desert gardens. Correctly spaced and pruned, they provide both productive crop and shade cover to mulched and watered gardens. They are also a key element in moderating climate in designed houses, or in retrofitting uncomfortably hot homes.

### Vine Over Garden

Horizontal trellis bars at 1-2 m spacing furnished with grape vines can throw a shade system over the greater part of the vegetable garden, relieving light saturation (50% or more shade helps gardens in hot clear deserts). The sides can be closed with herbaceous vines (beans, climbing tomatoes, yams, cucurbits).

It is more important to defend from the early eastern sun, as dew and guttation moisture is important to plants early in the day. The western side needs fleshy vines, as great heat builds up in late afternoon. Mikania (mile-a-minute) is ideal for western aspects.

### Vines on Houses

Considerable comfort can be brought to over-hot houses by attaching trellis:

- Standing dense evergreen vine crop out from the west wall (Mikania, Dolichos, Pelargonium)
- Growing vines (winter deciduous or summer annual) in a screen fixed out from the eastern sun
- Building extensive closed trellis over the rear door on the shade side to provide a cool air source
- Running a perennial non-invasive vine (Mikania, Pyrostegia) completely across the roof
- Placing a water tank under the vine crop to cool the garden air and water

### Vines as Mulch and Forage

Vines can be vigorous producers of foliage for garden mulch and for feeding small domestic livestock. Shade vines can also provide stick fuel for efficient cook-stoves.

### Desert-Adapted Vines

Several vine crops are desert-adapted, including gourds and yam legumes (ground vines). All thrive better if a few cubic feet of humus is pitted below the plant when set out. Valuable vines grown in large containers can await rains before being placed for permanent field growth.

## Vines in Temperate Climates

Trellis crop in mesothermal (temperate) climates is important. Many peas, beans, and cucurbits will trellis, as will varieties of tomatoes. Trellis over the garden is inappropriate (unlike in deserts). Vertical zigzag, inverted V, or house wall and garden fence trellis is best, aligned north-south where possible for even sun effects.

### Trellis Systems

Various trellis designs are illustrated in the manual:

- Fedge (fence-hedge) using wire and posts 2-2.5 m apart, with straight bearers
- Inter-trellis for beans using strained wire
- Mortlock trellis (pruned tree core)
- Multi-storey trellis on buildings with vine and mulch basket
- Circle gardens with compost centres

On the sides of poultry trellis runs, ephemeral bean and cucurbit crops can be grown on leaning frames of bamboo, or separate bamboo trellis can divide the garden beds. There is no vertical limit to some vine crops, and if water permits, every palm in the clump can eventually carry a high canopy of productive vines.

## Vines in Tropical Systems

In the tropical home garden, vine crops include Lima, velvet, and Dolichos beans trellising on tree legumes. Rattans can be encouraged along waterways and in mangrove edges as structural materials. The range of cucurbits is essential in tropical food production.

In polyculture systems, chayote, kiwifruit, passionfruit, cucurbits, and grapes are carefully chosen vine crops. Chayote is noted as a vigorous vine that can smother invasive species like Lantana.

### Tropical Vine Support

Bamboo on borders provides trellis material, as do rot-resistant timbers. Living trellises using tree legumes, fences, walls, and purpose-built structures all provide support for vine crops. Vine management (pruning, training) is essential to prevent over-shading in temperate climates and to optimise shade patterns in tropical and desert systems.

## Key Vine Species

Tropical: chayote, kiwifruit, passionfruit, Dolichos beans, velvet beans, Lima beans, Mikania (mile-a-minute), Pyrostegia (flame vine), rattans.

Temperate: grape, kiwifruit, peas, runner beans, climbing tomatoes, cucurbits (squash, cucumber, melon).

Dryland: grape, gourds, yam beans, hardy cucurbits, perennial Dolichos.

## Design Considerations

In all climates, vines should be integrated with support structures that serve multiple functions. Walls and fences can be used as trellis, producing food while shading buildings. In drylands, vines over gardens replace the need for artificial shade structures. In temperate zones, north-south aligned wall trellis maximises sun exposure.

The manual emphasises that vines can produce significant mulch biomass, support small livestock, and generate fuel — making them ideal multi-functional elements in integrated designs.
