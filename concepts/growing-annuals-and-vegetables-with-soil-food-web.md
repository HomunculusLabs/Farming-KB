## Growing Annuals and Vegetables with the Soil Food Web

> **Source:** *Teaming with Microbes* by [[jeff-lowenfels]] & [[wayne-lewis]] — Chapter 20

A [[soil-food-web]] approach to annual and vegetable gardening shifts the focus from
feeding plants directly (via synthetic fertilizers) to nurturing the living
ecosystem in the soil. When [[the-soil-food-web]] is healthy and diverse, it
delivers nutrients to plants in the right forms, at the right times, and in
the right amounts — all without chemical inputs.

## Core Principle: Bacterial Dominance

**Rule #2: Annuals and vegetables prefer bacterially dominated soils.**

Unlike perennials and trees — which thrive in fungally dominated soils that
mimic late-succession forests — annuals evolved in disturbed, early-successional
environments where bacteria are the primary decomposers. Bacterial soils cycle
nutrients quickly, making nitrogen available in the short window an annual has
to grow. Fungal soils release nutrients too slowly for fast-growing annuals.

The goal for a vegetable garden is to **encourage bacterial populations** while
maintaining enough fungi and other organisms to keep the full food web
functioning.

## Plant Stress and the Soil Food Web

Healthy, unstressed plants differ from stressed ones in critical ways:

- **Stressed plants emit chemical signals** recognized by pests like aphids.
  Pest infestations are often symptoms of underlying stress, not the cause.
- **Unstressed plants produce extra pitch and sap** — natural defenses that
  deter pests — and attract beneficial microorganisms to their rhizosphere.

A robust soil food web keeps plants unstressed by providing steady nutrition,
pathogen protection, and improved water access.

## The Case Against Rototilling

### Rule #18: Rototilling Destroys the Soil Food Web

Turning soil every spring is one of the most destructive gardening practices:

1. **Chops up [[fungal-hyphae]]** — shredding networks essential to soil structure.
2. **Blows apart worm tunnels** — collapsing natural drainage and aeration.
3. **Kills macro-organisms** — earthworms, beetles, and springtails are
   physically damaged or exposed.
4. **Reverses ecological succession** — pushing developed soil backward and
   destroying fungal networks that took years to establish.
5. **Triggers a compaction spiral** — the first watering after tilling begins
   compaction. Without fungal glues or worm tunnels, soil collapses and
   hardens, leading gardeners to till again — a vicious cycle.

### Historical Note: Jethro Tull"s Mistake

The practice traces back to **Jethro Tull** (1674–1741), who invented the seed
drill. Tull mistakenly believed roots "ate" soil particles and that finer
particles were more nutritious. Modern science shows roots absorb dissolved
minerals fed by soil organisms — not soil particles themselves.

## No-Till Planting Methods

Use tools that disturb soil minimally: **trowels** for transplants, **dowels**
for punching seed holes, and **dibbles** for bulbs or sets. Make only the hole
you need, insert the plant, and leave surrounding soil undisturbed to preserve
fungal networks, worm tunnels, and the microbial community.

## Building Bacterial Dominance with Mulches

### Green Mulches

Green mulches are key to promoting bacterial growth:

- **Finer materials = higher bacterial growth.** Shredded leaves and grass
  clippings provide enormous surface area for bacterial colonization.
- **Lawn clippings are excellent green mulch.** Even after fading to brown,
  they retain a green chemical profile and continue feeding bacteria.
- **Bacteria favor dampness but avoid anaerobic conditions.** Keep mulch moist
  but not waterlogged.

### Autumn Organic Amendments

Add organic matter in autumn for months of breakdown before spring planting:

- **Alfalfa meal** — rich in nitrogen and growth stimulants.
- **Straw** — provides carbon and structure, decomposes slowly.
- **Grass clippings** — nitrogen-rich, feeds bacteria through winter.

Layer materials on the soil surface and let the food web incorporate them
naturally from the top down.

## Monitoring the Soil Food Web

**Earthworms** are the easiest visible indicator of bacterial dominance and
overall soil health. Their presence confirms adequate organic matter, aerobic
conditions, and a functioning food web supporting larger organisms.

For scientific assessment, **Berlese funnel tests** sample microarthropods
(springtails, mites). Their diversity reveals the health of the food web's
middle levels — predators and grazers keeping bacterial and fungal populations
in balance.

## Boosting Nutrient Cycling

Support nutrient cycling actively, especially in new beds or containers:

- **Protozoa soup** — liquid protozoa culture applied to soil to boost grazing
  on bacteria, releasing plant-available nitrogen via the microbial loop.
- **Nematode products** — beneficial nematodes aid nutrient cycling and manage
  soil-dwelling pests.
- **Mycorrhizal inoculants** — even in containers, mycorrhizal fungi extend
  root surface area and improve phosphorus uptake.

## Key Rules Summary

| Rule | Statement |
|------|-----------|
| **#2** | Annuals and vegetables prefer bacterially dominated soils |
| **#18** | Rototilling destroys the soil food web — avoid it entirely |

## Practical Checklist

1. **Stop tilling** — use trowels, dibbles, and dowels for planting.
2. **Apply green mulches** (grass clippings, shredded leaves) to promote bacteria.
3. **Add organic amendments in autumn** — alfalfa meal, straw, clippings.
4. **Check for earthworms** as a quick soil health indicator.
5. **Use protozoa soup or nematode products** to jump-start nutrient cycling.
6. **Inoculate with mycorrhizal fungi**, even in container gardens.
7. **Keep soil moist but aerobic** — bacteria thrive damp but die in anaerobic soil.
8. **Minimize plant stress** — healthy plants attract beneficial organisms and
   repel pests naturally.

By working *with* the soil food web rather than against it, gardeners grow
healthier plants, reduce pest problems, and build soil fertility that improves
year after year — all without synthetic chemicals.
