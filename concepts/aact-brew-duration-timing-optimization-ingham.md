# AACT Brew Duration and Timing Optimization

## Overview

The duration of an [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) brew is one of the most critical factors in determining whether a brew succeeds or fails. Brew too short and the microbial populations haven't had time to multiply to adequate levels; brew too long and the tea shifts into anaerobic conditions, producing metabolites that can harm plants. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide provides detailed guidance on optimal brew timing based on the three phases of AACT extraction: extraction, microbial growth, and maturation.

## The Three Phases of AACT Brewing

### Phase 1: Extraction (0-4 hours)

The initial phase of brewing [[aact-water-quality-chlorine-removal-brewing-standards-ingham]] is primarily an extraction process rather than a growth phase. During this period:

- Soluble nutrients, humic acids, fulvic acids, and [[gibberellins-plant-growth-hormones]] are extracted from the compost and any added ingredients
- Dormant microorganisms are reactivated from their resting states in the compost
- Initial bacterial populations begin to wake up and start consuming the food resources added to the brewer
- Minimal multiplication occurs during this phase; the focus is on getting organisms into solution

The extraction phase is largely mechanical and chemical. Proper aeration during extraction ensures oxygen levels remain high and helps strip organisms from compost particles into the liquid. (see [[circadian-rhythm-in-plants]]).

### Phase 2: Microbial Growth (4-24 hours)

This is the primary growth phase where microorganism populations multiply exponentially:

- Bacteria reproduce rapidly, with generation times as short as 20-30 minutes under ideal conditions
- Fungi begin to grow and extend hyphae, though fungal growth is slower than bacterial growth
- [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] become active and begin feeding on bacteria, cycling nutrients
- The food resources added to the brew (molasses, kelp, fish hydrolysate) drive population growth
- Dissolved oxygen must be maintained above 6 ppm throughout this phase

The growth phase is where the bulk of [[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]] is produced. A well-managed brew can achieve bacterial populations in the range of 10⁸-10⁹ CFU/mL by the end of this phase. (see [[ingham-micronized-compost-aact-brewing-technique]]).

### Phase 3: Maturation (24-36 hours)

During the maturation phase:

- Growth rates begin to slow as food resources are depleted
- [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] stabilize and begin forming more complex food web structures
- Predator-prey relationships mature; protozoa populations peak as they consume accumulated bacteria
- Fungal biomass reaches its maximum extent
- The [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] reaches its most diverse and balanced state

This phase represents the optimal window for applying the tea. The food web is fully developed and the nutrient cycling capacity is at its peak.

## Optimal Brew Duration

### Standard Recommendations

Ingham's standard recommendation for AACT brew duration is:

- **Minimum**: 12 hours — below this, populations haven't reached adequate levels for most applications
- **Optimal range**: 24-36 hours — this window captures the end of the growth phase and the maturation phase
- **Maximum**: 36-48 hours — beyond this, the risk of anaerobic conditions increases substantially
- **Absolute cutoff**: 48 hours — brews exceeding this duration should be discarded rather than applied to plants

### Factors That Affect Optimal Duration

Several variables influence the ideal brew time:

**Temperature**: Warmer temperatures (65-75°F / 18-24°C) accelerate microbial growth, potentially shortening the optimal brew window. In cooler conditions (55-65°F), growth is slower and the brew may benefit from extended time up to 36-48 hours.

**Compost quality**: High-quality, biologically active compost with diverse populations will produce a mature tea faster. Poor-quality or partially finished compost requires longer brew times but may never achieve adequate populations regardless of duration.

**Food resource type and amount**: Simple sugars (molasses) drive rapid bacterial growth and peak earlier. Complex foods (fish hydrolysate, kelp meal) support more gradual, sustained growth over a longer period. Overfeeding can cause rapid population explosions that crash when food is depleted.

**Brewer efficiency**: Well-designed brewers with effective aeration and minimal dead zones maintain aerobic conditions longer, extending the safe brew window. Poorly aerated brewers may go anaerobic within 24 hours.

**Desired organism ratio**: If the goal is bacterial dominance, shorter brews (12-18 hours) may be preferred. If fungal biomass is needed, longer brews (24-36 hours) with fungal foods give fungi time to establish.

## Warning Signs During Brewing

### Odor as an Indicator

The smell of the brew is the most reliable field indicator of brew health and timing:

- **Earthy, sweet, fresh**: Indicates healthy aerobic conditions; the brew is on track
- **Sour, rotten, putrid, septic**: Indicates anaerobic conditions; the brew has gone too long or lacks adequate aeration
- **No smell at all**: May indicate insufficient microbial activity; the brew may need more time or better compost

Any off-odors mean the brew should be discarded, not applied to plants. Anaerobic metabolites including alcohol, phenols, and organic acids can damage plant roots and soil biology.

### Foam Production

Healthy AACT brews typically produce foam due to surfactants created by bacterial activity. Foam indicates active microbial metabolism. However, excessive foam combined with off-odors suggests the brew has entered an undesirable state.
