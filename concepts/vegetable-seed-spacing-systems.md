# Vegetable Seed Spacing Systems

## Overview

The spacing between vegetable plants is one of the most consequential decisions a gardener makes, yet it is also one of the most misunderstood. Steve Solomon identifies four distinct spacing systems, each with fundamentally different assumptions about the relationship between plants, soil, water, and fertility. The choice of spacing system determines not only how much food the garden produces, but also how much water, fertilizer, compost, and labor are required. Solomon advocates for wider spacing than most contemporary gardening authorities recommend, based on decades of variety trials at his seed company that demonstrated well-spaced plants grow larger, taste better, and often yield more per unit of area than crowded plants.

## The Four Spacing Systems

### 1. Intensive / Raised Bed Spacing

This is the dominant system promoted in modern gardening literature, popularized by John Jeavons and others. Plants are grown in closely spaced blocks within deeply dug, heavily amended raised beds. The goal is to maximize production per unit of ground area through high plant density.

**Key assumptions**: Unlimited water and fertility are available. The gardener can supply as much compost, manure, and irrigation as needed. The soil has been double-dug to 24 inches or more. Every square inch of growing area is productive.

**Spacing examples**: Leaf lettuce at 4-inch spacing, carrots at 2-inch spacing, cabbage at 12-15 inches.

**Advantages**: Maximum theoretical yield per square foot. Efficient use of small urban spaces. Good results when all inputs (water, compost, fertilizer) are abundant.

**Disadvantages** (per Solomon):
- Requires enormous quantities of compost and manure that most home gardeners cannot realistically produce or afford
- Demands frequent irrigation — the dense leaf canopy creates a microclimate of high humidity and transpiration demand
- Plants compete intensely for light, water, and nutrients, producing smaller individual vegetables
- Crowded vegetables deteriorate rapidly if not harvested promptly — there is no grace period
- Time and effort required for intensive management often exceeds the modest yield increase over wider spacing
- In hard times, when water, fertilizer, and [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]] reduces disease. Plants have room to develop full-sized root systems. Compatible with mechanized cultivation (wheel hoe, tiller). Graceful — vegetables hold well in the field if harvest is delayed.

**Disadvantages**: Uses more total land area than intensive spacing. Paths between rows consume space that does not directly produce food. ## The Thinning Contract

Solomon frames spacing as a contractual obligation between the gardener and the vegetable. When humans domesticated vegetables, an implicit agreement was formed: the plants agreed to become our food, and we agreed to protect them from competition. When seedlings are allowed to crowd each other — competing for light, water, and nutrients — the gardener has broken this contract.
