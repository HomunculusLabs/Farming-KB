# Magical Illness Categories in the Peruvian Amazon

## Overview

In the urban slum of Belén, Iquitos, Peru, and throughout much of the Amazon basin, illness is understood through a dual framework that distinguishes between "simple" natural ailments and those caused by supernatural or magical forces. When conventional treatments fail — injections from *sanitarios* (poorly trained medical aides), store-bought tonics, or pharmaceuticals — illness is almost invariably reinterpreted as magical in origin. This system of magical illness classification is central to understanding the therapeutic role of ayahuasca and other plant hallucinogens in Amazonian folk medicine.

The fundamental question in Amazonian disease etiology is not "how?" but **"why me?"** — the concern is always to discover exactly why a particular person has been afflicted rather than another, and by whom.

## The Natural/Magical Divide

### Simple, God-Given Illnesses

These are recognized as ordinary ailments that respond to basic treatment:

- Colds and upper respiratory infections
- Some skin disorders
- Simple fevers and aches

These are treated by *sanitarios* (medical aides) who frequent the slums, dispensing penicillin or antibiotic injections for a small fee. Formal medical consultation in the city of Iquitos is prohibitively expensive for most slum residents — an initial visit costs the equivalent of approximately two days' food budget. Moreover, the city hospital has the reputation as "a place one goes in order to die," particularly because of its casual treatment of poor people.

### Magical Illnesses

When simple treatments do not produce rapid recovery, or when sudden pains appear in specific body parts, the illness is reclassified as magical. The transition from natural to magical diagnosis often occurs quickly and is reinforced by the broader cultural framework in which magic, witchcraft, and sorcery function as auxiliaries alongside modern medicine. Twentieth-century medical science has made inroads primarily through pharmaceutical medicines and store-bought tonics, which are used alongside magical treatments without apparent contradiction.

## Major Categories of Magical Illness

### Susto (Soul Loss)

**Susto** is one of the most widespread illness concepts throughout Peru and Latin America. It involves:

- **Definition**: Profound alteration of metabolism or nervous disorder
- **Cause**: Loss of the sick person's soul following an intense psychic trauma provoked by fear
- **Symptoms**: Lack of appetite, lack of energy, profound lethargy
- **Frequency**: One of the most common illnesses treated by Peruvian folk healers
- **Treatment**: Soul retrieval through ritual means, often involving plant hallucinogens

Susto represents a disruption of the person's vital essence rather than a physiological disturbance. The fear that precipitates soul loss may result from a startling encounter, a fall, witnessing something terrifying, or being cursed. The concept links psychological trauma directly to somatic symptoms through the mechanism of soul separation.

### Daño (Magical Harm)

**Daño** is the most frequently cited form of magical illness in Belén and encompasses a broad spectrum of symptoms:

- **Causes**: Envy (*envidia*) or vengeance (*despecho*) directed at the victim by another person
- **Mechanism**: A powerful potion slipped into a drink, or thrown across a doorstep late at night by a paid witch (*brujo*)
- **Symptoms**: Hemorrhaging, muscular pain, loss of consciousness, suffocation, tumors, and consistent bad luck (*saladera*)
- **Key principle**: The cause of the syndrome matters more than its specific manifestation

The *saladera* form of *daño* — a persistent pattern of bad luck affecting all areas of life — is particularly insidious because it cannot be addressed through conventional medicine and requires magical intervention. Witches (*brujos*) may use ayahuasca specifically to inflict *daño*, believing that while intoxicated they can leave their bodies and send harmful spirits or disease-carrying thorns (*chonta*) through the air at their clients' enemies.

Some witches and ayahuasqueros are believed to control special **spirits or familiars** (*espíritus* or *duendes*) whom they can dispatch to do evil or to retaliate against evil magic directed at their own clients.

### Pulsario (Anxiety Ball)

**Pulsario** is a condition marked by psychological and somatic symptoms:

- **Definition**: An infirmity characterized by restlessness, hyperactivity, and free-floating anxiety
- **Somatic presentation**: Described as a painful ball (*bola*) located at the mouth of the stomach that prevents normal digestive action
- **Demographics**: Primarily affects women
- **Emotional component**: Associated with irritability and general unhappiness
- **Alternative interpretation**: The lump may represent repressed pain, sorrow, or anger

Pulsario is notable for its overlap with what Western medicine would classify as generalized anxiety disorder or somatization. The physical symptom (the "ball" in the stomach) provides a culturally legible way to express and treat psychological distress that might otherwise remain unarticulated.

### Mal de Ojo (Evil Eye)

**Mal de ojo** is found throughout Peru and much of Latin America:

- **Cause**: The magical action of one person's glance upon another, not necessarily with evil intentions
- **Motivation**: Often driven by envy, particularly when the victim possesses personal attractiveness or some desirable quality
- **Symptoms**: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, loss of weight, insomnia, and sadness
- **Demographics**: Particularly frequent among children
- **Prevention**: Mothers place charms or amulets on children's wrists or necks, or exorcise them daily with tobacco smoke

The evil eye concept highlights the perceived vulnerability of those who attract attention — especially children, whose personal charm may unintentionally provoke envy. The protective measures (amulets, smoke exorcism) represent an ongoing, daily practice of magical hygiene rather than a response to acute illness.

## Taboo Violation as Illness Cause

In addition to human-directed magical harm, illness may result from **violating taboos** related to natural spirits:

- A menstruating woman who bathes before three days have passed may hemorrhage because of the punishment of an offended forest spirit
- Other taboos involve dietary restrictions, interactions with sacred places, or failure to observe required rituals

In Belén specifically, illness attributed to taboo violation is less common than illness attributed to the willing of evil by others. This reflects the urban context where interpersonal tensions, economic competition, and social fragmentation make interpersonal sorcery a more salient explanatory framework.

## The Role of Witches (Brujos)

Witches occupy a central role in the magical illness framework:

- They are consulted by individuals who wish to cause harm to others
- Some witches specialize in the use of psychedelic drugs (particularly ayahuasca) for evil purposes
- While intoxicated, they are believed capable of leaving their bodies to inflict harm or incurable disease
- They can control familiars (*espíritus*) to carry out magical attacks at a distance
- They use *chonta* (thorns) carrying disease-producing substances, shot through the air at enemies

The relationship between healers (*ayahuasqueros*) and witches (*brujos*) is ambiguous — some practitioners serve both functions, refusing neither healing nor harmful work. Others refuse the harmful trade entirely. The distinction often depends more on reputation and client demand than on any formal separation of roles.

## The Social Context of Magical Illness

The prevalence of magical illness beliefs in Belén reflects the harsh social realities of slum life:

- **Extreme economic insecurity** from chronic underemployment
- **Family breakdown** and fragile interpersonal relationships
- **Crowded living conditions** with flooding four months annually
- **Social fragmentation** from constant migration of forest peoples
- **Limited access to formal healthcare**

In this context, magical illness serves multiple social functions:
1. It provides an explanatory framework for suffering that feels random or unjust
2. It identifies specific agents responsible, converting diffuse anxiety into actionable knowledge
3. It creates a pathway to healing through the ayahuasquero's diagnostic and interventional capabilities
4. It reinforces social norms by punishing taboo violations and expressing communal disapproval of excessive envy or aggression

The magical illness framework, combined with ayahuasca healing, represents what Dobkin de Rios describes as "a very old and time-honored tradition of dealing with psychological problems that predates Freudian analysis by many centuries."
