## Fungal Resource Discovery — Biomass Reallocation and Foraging Decisions

## Overview

When foraging cord-forming basidiomycete mycelia discover new resources, they undergo dramatic, globally coordinated reorganization of their entire network. This response — involving biomass reallocation, changes in nutrient partitioning, and decisions about when to continue searching — mirrors optimal foraging theory from animal ecology. The fungal system integrates information about resource size, quality, spatial distribution, and the carbon/nutrient status of the whole network to make "decisions" about resource exploitation.

## Biomass Reallocation on Resource Discovery

### Sequential Discovery

When mycelia encounter new resources substantially larger than their current resource base:

1. **Cord thickening**: Cords connecting to the newly discovered resource increase in diameter.
2. **Mycelial regression**: Non-resource-connected mycelium regresses, redistributing biomass and nutrients.
3. **Growth cessation**: Radial extension slows or stops.
4. **Delayed egress**: The mycelium pauses before growing out from the newly colonized resource.
5. **Outgrowth**: Eventually, new foraging fronts emerge from the colonized resource.

This sequence ensures that resources are concentrated on exploiting the newly found resource before further exploration.

### Size Matters: Relative Resource Quality

The outcome depends critically on the **relative size** of the original resource and the newly discovered resource:

- **Small → Large**: Complete regression of mycelium from the small original resource; the large new resource becomes the sole centre of activity.
- **Small → Small**: Both resources remain connected; less dramatic regression.
- **Large → Small**: The large resource continues to dominate; the small new resource is incorporated without major reorganization.
- **Large → Large**: Both large resources remain connected with no major regression between them, but connection to the original (small) resource may be lost.

### Simultaneous Multi-Resource Discovery

When *[[phanerochaete-velutina]]* encountered four closely spaced (~3 cm apart) new resources simultaneously, the mycelium responded by producing **fans from points all around the margin** — a response that did not occur with a single resource of equivalent total volume. **Distribution** of resources, not just total quantity, controls mycelial response.

### Signalling Mechanisms

Exactly how local sensing triggers site-specific developmental responses across the whole network remains unknown. Proposed mechanisms include:

- **Changes in intracellular hydrostatic pressure**
- **Intracellular amino acid concentration**
- **Electrical potentials** (action potential-like activity has been detected in fungal mycelium)
- **Proteinase expression** as a marker of propagated stimulus reception

## Delays to Continued Foraging

### The Egress Delay

The time between resource contact and mycelial egress from a newly encountered resource varies dramatically:

| Scenario | Time to Egress |
|---|---|
| Small inoculum (0.2 cm³) → small new resource (0.2 cm³) | ~7 days |
| Small inoculum (0.2 cm³) → large new resource (16 cm³) | ~63 days |
| Large inoculum (8 cm³) → new resource (4 cm³) | ~10 days |
| Small inoculum (0.2 cm³) → new resource (4 cm³) | ~53 days |

Time to egress is governed largely by the **carbon/nutrient status of the whole system**:
- Larger new resources require longer colonization time.
- Smaller inocula leave the whole system more carbon-limited, delaying egress.
- Prior colonization by other fungi delays egress (uncolonized resources are vacated first).

### The Marginal Value Theorem

These delays parallel the **marginal value theorem** of optimal foraging theory (Charnov, 1976):

- A predator arriving at a resource patch acquires resources quickly at first, but the rate of acquisition diminishes with time (resource depression).
- The theorem predicts the optimal time to leave a patch: when the gain rate within the patch drops to the average for the environment.
- If travel time between patches increases, the optimal time to stay in each patch also increases.
- If patches vary in quality, each should be exploited until the gain rate drops to the environmental average.

**Fungal analogy**: The delay before egress from a newly encountered resource depends on the expected quality and distribution of other resources in the environment, integrated with the current carbon/nutrient status of the mycelial system.

## Resource Choice and Competitive Interactions

### Selective Colonization

When mycelia simultaneously encounter resources colonized by different fungi, they preferentially colonize those occupied by **less combative species**:

- *Phan. velutina* preferentially colonized resources occupied by *Kretzschmaria deusta* (weakly combative), while ignoring resources occupied by more combative *[[stereum-hirsutum]]* and *S. rugosum*.
- This reflects ease of colonization rather than attraction.

**Macroorganism parallel**: Predators selecting the weakest prey (e.g., lions preying on gazelle), or selective grazing by sheep that progressively concentrates on the best pasture areas.

### Competition Outcomes

Combat between fungi for territory can result in:

| Outcome | Description |
|---|---|
| **Deadlock** | Neither fungus gains territory |
| **Replacement** | One fungus completely replaces the other |
| **Partial replacement** | Some territory is exchanged |
| **Mutual replacement** | Each gains some of the other's territory |

These outcomes parallel the four theoretical outcomes of two-species competition in macroorganism ecology (Lotka, 1932): competitive exclusion of either species, unstable equilibrium, or stable coexistence through niche separation.

## Division of Labour and Physiological Integration

### Carbon and Nutrient Specialization

Within the mycelial system, different regions specialize:

- **Original resources** (well-colonized) tend to decay more rapidly, supplying carbon/energy.
- **Newly encountered resources** receive priority nutrient allocation, especially when larger.
- **Soil-growing mycelia** scavenge mineral nutrients (N, P) and translocate them to connected resources.
- **Growing fronts** receive translocated carbon and nutrients from resource bases.

### Phosphorus Partitioning

When *Phan. velutina* received multiple new wood resources at 10-day intervals:

- Total ³²P allocated to new resources increased linearly with time.
- The main sink for ³²P was **not the most recently supplied resource** but the **better-colonized** ones.
- This demonstrates **spatial and temporal coordination** of nutrient partitioning.
- The mechanism may involve net removal from a system-wide translocation stream (analogous to a water ring main) at locations of active use/storage, rather than demand-driven translocation.

### Macroorganism Parallel: Clonal Plants

The fungal division of labour directly parallels clonal plant physiology:

- Internal redistribution of resources from acquisition sites to parts of the clone where that resource is scarce.
- Local specialization of ramets enhances acquisition of different resources from sites of greatest abundance.
- Resource sharing results in considerably more growth when resources are heterogeneously distributed vs. homogeneously distributed.
