## The Amanita Argument

The Wassons' argument for Amanita muscaria as Soma rested on several pillars. The
mushroom's bright red cap with white spots is visually striking and could account
for the color descriptions in Vedic texts. The practice of drinking urine after
Amanita consumption — which concentrates the active compounds and removes toxic
metabolites — has been documented in Siberian cultures and could explain the Vedic
descriptions of Soma's purifying properties. The mountainous habitat of Amanita
matches the mountain origin attributed to Soma.

The Wassons noted linguistic connections between Soma and mushroom-related terms in
various Indo-European languages. They also pointed to the absence of any other known
plant that could produce the extraordinary effects described in the Rig Veda while
being available in the geographical region occupied by the Indo-European peoples.

## Siberian Parallels and Fieldwork

The Wassons' fieldwork among Siberian shamans provided the ethnographic foundation
for their theory. The Koryak, Chukchi, and other peoples of the Kamchatka Peninsula
use Amanita muscaria in religious and communal ceremonies. The shaman, acting as
intermediary between the human and spirit worlds, consumes dried fly-agaric caps
and enters a trance state. The Wassons documented the elaborate rituals surrounding
mushroom collection, preparation, and consumption — including the practice of urine
recycling, whereby the shaman's urine, rich in active muscimol, is consumed by others
who then experience the mushroom's effects secondhand.

This urine-drinking practice was critical to the Wassons' argument, as they believed
it paralleled Vedic descriptions of Soma being purified and consumed in successive
rituals. However, McKenna noted that Siberian mushroom users themselves do not
attribute the visionary or transcendent qualities to Amanita that the Vedic poets
attribute to Soma, further weakening the identification.

## Pharmacological Objections

Despite its intellectual appeal, the [[mckenna-wasson-amanita-theory-and-objections-to-fly-agaric]] faces serious pharmacological
problems. The active compounds in Amanita muscaria — ibotenic acid and muscimol —
produce effects that are more accurately described as deliriant than visionary.
Symptoms include confusion, agitation, visual distortions, nausea, and incoordination.
These are not the luminous, inspiring, creativity-enhancing experiences that the Rig
Veda attributes to Soma.

The Vedic hymns describe Soma as producing supernatural power, immortality, healing,
poetic inspiration, and a sense of divine connection. The Amanita experience, by
contrast, is more commonly associated with disorientation and physical discomfort.
While individual responses vary considerably, the typical Amanita experience does not
match the consistent pattern of ecstatic praise found throughout the Rig Veda.

## Wasson's Doubts

Wasson himself came to express doubts about his Amanita theory. He recognized that
the effects of Amanita muscaria did not fully correspond to the visionary qualities
attributed to Soma. In later writings, he considered other fungal candidates and
acknowledged that the question of Soma's identity might never be definitively resolved.

McKenna catalogued Wasson's contradictions: his insistence on Amanita despite its
pharmacological inadequacy, his simultaneous awareness of psilocybin-containing
mushrooms (which he encountered in Mexico), and his reluctance to extend his
ethnomycological analysis to the mushroom species that most closely matches the
effects described in the Vedic texts.
