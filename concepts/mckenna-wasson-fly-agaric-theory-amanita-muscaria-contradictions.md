# The Wasson Fly-Agaric Theory and Its Contradictions

## Source
*Terence McKenna — Food of the Gods (1992)*

## Summary
Gordon and Valentina Wasson, the founders of ethnomycology, proposed that the Vedic
Soma was the fly-agaric mushroom, [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]. Their theory, published in
*Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality* (1968), was based on linguistic analysis,
ethnographic parallels with Siberian mushroom-using cultures, and the mushroom's
distinctive red-and-white appearance in Indian art. While enormously influential,
the theory faces significant pharmacological objections: Amanita's active compounds
(ibotenic acid and muscimol) produce deliriant effects inconsistent with the
luminous, inspiring visions celebrated in the Rig Veda. Wasson himself eventually
expressed doubts, and McKenna argued that [[mckenna-stropharia-cubensis-extraterrestrial-hypothesis-spore-dispersal]] ([[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]])
is a more plausible candidate.

## The Founders of Ethnomycology

Gordon Wasson was a vice president of J.P. Morgan who became fascinated by mushrooms
after his Russian wife Valentina introduced him to the mycophilic traditions of
Slavic culture. Together, they spent decades studying human uses of and lore
concerning mushrooms and other fungi, founding the discipline of ethnomycology.
Their earlier work on the cultural divide between mycophilic (mushroom-loving) and
mycophobic (mushroom-fearing) societies established that [[weil-cultural-attitudes-toward-altered-states]] fungi are
deeply embedded cultural markers.

## The Amanita Argument

The Wassons' argument for Amanita muscaria as Soma rested on several pillars. The
mushroom's bright red cap with white spots is visually striking and could account
for the color descriptions in Vedic texts. The practice of drinking urine after
Amanita consumption — which concentrates the active compounds and removes toxic
metabolites — has been documented in Siberian cultures and could explain the Vedic
descriptions of Soma's purifying properties. The mountainous habitat of Amanita
matches the mountain origin attributed to Soma.

The Wassons noted linguistic connections between Soma and mushroom-related terms in
various Indo-European languages. They also pointed to the absence of any other known
plant that could produce the extraordinary effects described in the Rig Veda while
being available in the geographical region occupied by the Indo-European peoples.

## Siberian Parallels and Fieldwork

The Wassons' fieldwork among Siberian shamans provided the ethnographic foundation
for their theory. The Koryak, Chukchi, and other peoples of the Kamchatka Peninsula
use Amanita muscaria in religious and communal ceremonies. The shaman, acting as
intermediary between the human and spirit worlds, consumes dried fly-agaric caps
and enters a trance state. The Wassons documented the elaborate rituals surrounding
mushroom collection, preparation, and consumption — including the practice of urine
recycling, whereby the shaman's urine, rich in active muscimol, is consumed by others
who then experience the mushroom's effects secondhand.

This urine-drinking practice was critical to the Wassons' argument, as they believed
it paralleled Vedic descriptions of Soma being purified and consumed in successive
rituals. However, McKenna noted that Siberian mushroom users themselves do not
attribute the visionary or transcendent qualities to Amanita that the Vedic poets
attribute to Soma, further weakening the identification.

## Pharmacological Objections

Despite its intellectual appeal, the [[mckenna-wasson-amanita-theory-and-objections-to-fly-agaric]] faces serious pharmacological
problems. The active compounds in Amanita muscaria — ibotenic acid and muscimol —
produce effects that are more accurately described as deliriant than visionary.
Symptoms include confusion, agitation, visual distortions, nausea, and incoordination.
These are not the luminous, inspiring, creativity-enhancing experiences that the Rig
Veda attributes to Soma.

The Vedic hymns describe Soma as producing supernatural power, immortality, healing,
poetic inspiration, and a sense of divine connection. The Amanita experience, by
contrast, is more commonly associated with disorientation and physical discomfort.
While individual responses vary considerably, the typical Amanita experience does not
match the consistent pattern of ecstatic praise found throughout the Rig Veda.

## Wasson's Doubts

Wasson himself came to express doubts about his Amanita theory. He recognized that
the effects of Amanita muscaria did not fully correspond to the visionary qualities
attributed to Soma. In later writings, he considered other fungal candidates and
acknowledged that the question of Soma's identity might never be definitively resolved.

McKenna catalogued Wasson's contradictions: his insistence on Amanita despite its
pharmacological inadequacy, his simultaneous awareness of psilocybin-containing
mushrooms (which he encountered in Mexico), and his reluctance to extend his
ethnomycological analysis to the mushroom species that most closely matches the
effects described in the Vedic texts.

## The Stropharia cubensis Alternative

McKenna proposed Stropharia cubensis (now Psilocybe cubensis) as a more plausible
candidate for the original Soma. This coprophilic (dung-growing) mushroom contains
psilocybin and psilocin, which produce precisely the kinds of visionary experiences
described in the Rig Veda: enhanced visual perception, feelings of timelessness,
encounters with seemingly autonomous entities, and a profound sense of connection to
a larger reality.

The mushroom's association with cattle — it grows on bovine dung — connects it to
the prominent role of cattle in Vedic religion and mythology. McKenna argued that
this cattle connection was central to any attempt to identify Soma. The "mountain
origin" of Soma could refer to the highland pastures where cattle graze and where
the mushroom would be most abundant.

## The Indo-European Migration Context

The identity of Soma became lost as Indo-European migrations carried the tribes far
beyond the environments suitable for the original plant. One possibility is that the
Soma was something the Indo-Europeans did not come in contact with until they
encountered valley-dwelling pastoralists who used mushrooms on the Konya Plain of
Anatolia — the region where McKenna believed the partnership between humans and
psychoactive mushrooms first emerged.

As linguistic differences arose, trade routes became longer, and local substitutes
were experimented with, the original identity of Soma became mingled with myth.
Progressively more esoteric, it became a secret teaching delivered orally and known
to but a few, until finally it was forgotten.

## Legacy of the Wasson Theory

Regardless of its ultimate correctness, the [[mckenna-food-gods-wasson-amanita-theory]] theory transformed the
study of psychoactive plants and their role in human culture. It demonstrated that
ethnobotanical analysis could be applied to ancient religious texts, it legitimized
the study of psychoactive mushrooms within academia, and it inspired generations
of researchers — including McKenna himself — to take seriously the possibility that
psychoactive plants played a central role in the development of human religion,
language, and consciousness.
