# Sacred Groves

updated: 2026-04-18

From: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]

## Overview

While the Designers Manual does not have a dedicated section on sacred groves, Mollison repeatedly references the traditional wisdom of indigenous peoples in protecting forests and placing certain trees in sacred or protected categories. Sacred groves represent a pre-scientific understanding of ecological functions that permaculture seeks to restore through conscious design. The concept of sacred groves -- forest areas set aside from human exploitation for spiritual, cultural, or ecological reasons -- is found in cultures worldwide, from the Devara Kadu of India to the Nemeton of Celtic Europe.

## Traditional Forest Protection

Many tribes and traditional societies evolved beliefs (themselves brought on earlier environmental catastrophes) to "tabu" forest cutting or even to make tracks on high slopes, and to place mountain trees in a sacred or protected category. Now that we begin to understand the reasons for these beliefs, we could ourselves look on trees as our essential companions, giving us all the needs of life, and deserving of our care and protection.

This passage from the Designers Manual explicitly connects traditional sacred forest practices with modern ecological understanding. Indigenous tabu systems prevented the environmental destruction that comes from unrestricted forest clearing. When we cut forests, we must pay for the end cost in drought, water loss, nutrient loss, and salted soils -- costs that traditional societies understood and prevented through sacred prohibitions.

## Sacred Knowledge and Pattern Language

Children of many tribes are taught hundreds of simple chants, the words of which hide deeper, secondary meanings about medicinal, sacred, or navigational knowledge. All this becomes meaningful when the initiate is given the decoding system, or finds it by personal revelation (intuition). A pattern map of the landscape can contain ecological knowledge encoded in sacred art and design.

Designs can combine thousands of bits of information in a single, deceptively simple pattern. The decorative function is incidental to the educational and therefore sacred information function in such patterns. Much of modern art is individualistic and decorative; some "motif" art is plagiarised from ancient origins, but no longer has an educational or sacred function. The primary purposes of sacred or tribal art are carefully assembled to assist the folk records of the function and history of their society.

## Cultural Preservation

Mollison warns that sacred calendars melted down to bullion or objets d'art represent a degradation of generations of human effort and knowledge. The sacred art of tribal peoples hidden in museum storerooms constitutes a form of cultural genocide, removing knowledge from its context and trivialising objects to decorations or loot. To waste energy on resources for the few is a betrayal of the accumulated wisdom of traditional peoples.

## Types of Sacred Groves Worldwide

### Tropical Sacred Groves
- **India** - Devara Kadu (god's groves) preserved by temple communities, protecting patches of original forest in otherwise cleared landscapes
- **West Africa** - Sacred forests preserved around shrines and burial grounds
- **Southeast Asia** - Temple groves and spirit forests protecting watersheds
- **Pacific Islands** - Tabu areas around sacred sites and chief's grounds

### Temperate Sacred Groves
- **Europe** - Celtic Nemeton (sacred groves) serving as places of worship and wildlife refuge
- **Japan** - Shrine forests (Chinju no Mori) protecting old-growth trees around Shinto shrines
- **Middle East** - Olive groves protected by religious tradition

### Arid and Semi-Arid Sacred Groves
- **Australia** - Sacred sites protecting sensitive ecological areas
- **Africa** - Sacred trees (baobabs, figs) serving as meeting places and conservation anchors

## Ecological Functions of Sacred Groves

Sacred groves perform multiple ecological functions that permaculture design seeks to replicate:
- **Watershed protection** -- preventing erosion on slopes and maintaining water quality
- **Biodiversity refuges** -- preserving species that have disappeared from surrounding areas
- **Seed sources** -- providing genetic material for forest restoration
- **Soil conservation** -- maintaining soil structure and fertility under continuous forest cover
- **Microclimate regulation** -- creating cooler, moister conditions than surrounding cleared land
- **Carbon storage** -- sequestering carbon in standing biomass and soil organic matter

## Permaculture Equivalent: Protected Zones

In permaculture design, the functional equivalent of sacred groves is Zone 5 -- the wilderness zone. The zone model specifically reserves areas for natural processes and wildlife, echoing the traditional practice of setting aside sacred forests. The design principle of bringing "wedges" of wilderness zone right to the front door creates wildlife corridors that maintain ecological connectivity.

The six essential design factors include: forests, wildlife refuges, native species reserves, wildlife corridors, aquaculture (not industrial forestry). These functional reserves serve the same ecological purpose as traditional sacred groves -- protecting biodiversity, conserving soil and water, and maintaining the ecological services that support all life.

## Lessons for Permaculture Designers

Key insights from sacred grove traditions applicable to permaculture:
- Certain areas should remain undisturbed as ecological reserves
- Mountain forests and riparian zones deserve special protection
- Traditional ecological knowledge encodes valid scientific understanding
- Cultural practices can effectively enforce conservation without formal regulation
- Protected areas should be connected by corridors for wildlife movement
- The concept of "sacredness" can motivate conservation more effectively than economic arguments alone
- Large areas of forest must be preserved to maintain regional climate and water cycles
