## The Humus Layer: Structure and Capacity

### Humus Depth and Absorption Rate

The forest humus layer is not a thin veneer of decomposing leaves. In mature forests, it constitutes a substantial, living sponge:

- **General rule**: The humus layer can absorb approximately **1 cm of rain per 3 cm of humus depth**
- **Old beech forests** develop humus blankets exceeding **40 cm** in depth, underlain by dense networks of fungal hyphae
- **Rainforests** feature deep moss layers that perform a similar absorptive function
- Throughfall is absorbed by decomposers and living systems across a **40–60 cm** depth profile

## The Root Zone: Living Water Infrastructure

### Root Architecture

The root systems of forest trees constitute an extraordinary [[water-management]] infrastructure:

- **30–40% of a tree's total bulk** exists below ground as roots
- The upper 60 cm of soil contains **thousands of kilometers of root hairs** forming a dense, mat-like network
- Deeper structural roots can **penetrate 40 meters or more** into bedrock fractures

### Fungal and Bacterial Associations

Tree roots are not bare in the soil. They are intimately associated with soil microorganisms:

- Roots are **clothed in fungal hyphae** (mycorrhizae) that extend the effective absorptive surface area enormously
- **Bacterial gels** (biofilms) surround root surfaces, contributing to water retention and nutrient exchange
- The combined root-fungi-bacteria system forms a living membrane that actively absorbs water and dissolved nutrients

### Active Water Uptake

The root mat does not passively sit in wet soil. It **actively absorbs** the water solution and transports it upward through the tree for transpiration. This creates a continuous pull that draws water through the soil profile, maintaining moisture gradients and preventing waterlogging.

## Dryland Water Management by Plants

Certain dryland species have evolved remarkable strategies for building and maintaining local water reserves:

- **Centrosema**, **Gleditsia**, and **Prosopis** species build damp soil micro-environments around their root zones
- These plants **store water as gels** in the soil immediately surrounding their roots
- The gel-like soil matrix holds water against evaporation and makes it available for extended periods during drought

## Soil Water Storage Systems

Mollison identifies three distinct types of water storage in forest soils. Understanding these is essential for [[permaculture-design]].

### Retention Storage

Water held as a thin film around soil particles by **surface tension**. This is the water most readily available to plant roots.

- Typical capacity: **2.5–7 cm of rain equivalent per 30 cm of soil depth**
- Fine organic soils (high humus content) can store **10–30 cm per 30 cm** — a dramatic increase
- Bound water films are available to roots and can be removed by root suction down to approximately **15 atmospheres of pressure**
- **Field capacity** refers to the maximum water a soil can hold against gravity in this retention film

### Interstitial Storage

Water held in **cavities and pore spaces** between soil particles. This water drains more freely under gravity but moves slowly through the soil matrix.

- Provides an additional **0–5 cm of storage** beyond retention capacity
- Percolation through interstitial spaces feeds deeper storage and eventual groundwater recharge
- The rate of percolation depends on soil texture, structure, and organic content

### Humus Storage

Water held within **swollen [[mycorrhizal-networks]]** and **spongy detritus** (partially decomposed organic matter). This is unique to biologically active soils.

- Humus acts like a sponge, absorbing and releasing water through physical swelling and shrinking
- Mycorrhizal hyphae themselves hold significant water within their cellular structure
- This storage type is absent in degraded or compacted soils lacking organic matter

## The Percolation Lag: Forests as Long-Term Reservoirs

Free water that moves beyond the root zone does not reach streams quickly. In forested landscapes, deep percolation through to stream systems can take **1 to 40 years**. This extraordinary lag time means:

- Forests buffer downstream flows across seasons and years
- Peak rainfall events produce muted stream responses compared to deforested catchments
- Dry-season baseflows in streams are sustained by slow release from forest soils
- The forest is effectively a distributed reservoir, releasing water gradually over decades
