## Effects and Visionary Content

When Chacruna is included in the [[ayahuasca-preparation]], the effects are
intensified considerably. In addition to the standard ayahuasca effects
of giddiness, nervousness, profuse sweating, and nausea, the tryptamine
additive produces trembling, convulsive shaking, mydriasis (pupil
dilation), and increased pulse rate. Frequently a show of recklessness
or even aggressiveness marks advanced states of the inebriation.

The visual hallucinations are characteristically brilliant. Partakers
report visions of overwhelming attacks by huge snakes or jaguars —
animals that occupy a central place in Amazonian cosmology due to their
power and stealth. Shamans of the Conibo-Shipibo tribe are said to
acquire great snakes as personal possessions for defense in supernatural
battles against rival shamans.

## Cultural and Ceremonial Significance

Chacruna enters into almost all aspects of the life of Amazonian
peoples who use ayahuasca. The drink is, above all, a medicine — "the
great medicine." Among the Campa of Peru, the ayahuasca leader is a
religious practitioner who maintains shamanistic power through the use
of tobacco and ayahuasca. Under its influence, the shaman acquires an
eerie, distant voice and a quivering jaw, indicating the arrival of
good spirits who sing and dance before him.

The [[metzner-brazilian-ayahuasca-churches-structural-comparison-santo-daime-udv-barquinha]] cult and the Uniao do Vegetal church, syncretic
Brazilian religions, prepare their sacramental drinks using the original
Indian recipe in which Banisteriopsis caapi vine and the leaves of the
Chacruna shrub are boiled together to make an extremely psychedelic
mixture. These churches have expanded to Europe, with congregations in
Germany and the Netherlands.
