Plants do not simply react to light as it arrives. They measure the

## The Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

In 1906, tobacco farmers in the valleys of southern Maryland noticed
something strange. A new strain of tobacco had appeared that never
seemed to stop growing. Dubbed "Maryland Mammoth," this remarkable
plant could reach four and a half metres in height and produce nearly a
hundred leaves. It continued growing until the frosts set in, long
after normal tobacco plants had matured and been harvested. On the
surface, such a vigorous, ever-growing plant would seem like a boon to
farmers. But Maryland Mammoth had a serious drawback: it rarely
flowered. Without flowers, the plant produced no seed, and farmers
could not grow the next year's crop.

The problem was puzzling. Normal tobacco, like other commercial
varieties, was planted in spring and harvested in late summer. Some
plants were left to flower and set seed for the following year. But
Maryland Mammoth ignored this seasonal rhythm entirely, continuing to
produce leaves when it should have been producing flowers.

## Garner and Allard's 1918 Experiments

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard, two scientists at the
US Department of Agriculture, set out to solve the mystery of Maryland
Mammoth. They designed an elegantly simple experiment. They planted the
tobacco in pots and divided the plants into two groups. One group was
left outside in the fields, exposed to the natural long days of summer.
The other group was also placed in the field during the day, but every
afternoon the plants were moved into a dark shed, artificially
shortening their exposure to light.

The results were dramatic. The plants left outside in the long days of
summer continued their relentless [[coleman-persephone-months-winter-day-length]] controls flowering.
They named this phenomenon [[photoperiodism]], and it provided the first
strong evidence that plants actively measure how much light they take
in. Photoperiodism was not a passive response to light intensity or
temperature — it was a precise measurement of the duration of light.

## Short-Day Plants and Long-Day Plants

Further research revealed that photoperiodism is widespread across the
plant kingdom, with different species falling into distinct categories
based on their flowering requirements:

**Short-day plants** flower only when the [[coleman-winter-day-length-and-persephone-months]] is below a certain
threshold — that is, when days are short and nights are long. In
practice, these plants are really responding to long nights. Examples
include chrysanthemums and soybeans. Chrysanthemums naturally bloom in
the autumn as the days shorten, while soybeans flower as summer wanes.

**Long-day plants** flower only when the [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] exceeds a certain
threshold — when days are long and nights are short. Examples include
irises and barley. These plants typically bloom in late spring and
early summer, when the days are at their longest.

This classification gave biologists a powerful framework for
understanding why different species flower at different times of year.
The timing of flowering is not random or determined solely by
temperature — it is a precisely calibrated response to day length,
hardwired into each species' biology.
