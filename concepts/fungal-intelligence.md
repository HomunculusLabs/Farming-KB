# Fungal Intelligence

Fungal intelligence refers to the capacity of fungi — particularly their
mycelial networks — to process information, make decisions, learn, and solve
problems in ways that resemble cognitive behavior, despite lacking a nervous
system, brain, or any conventional center of computation. This concept
challenges traditional definitions of intelligence and cognition, raising
fundamental questions about what it means to process information and adapt
behavior in biological systems. The field sits at the intersection of
mycology, neurobiology, computer science, and philosophy of mind.

The study of fungal intelligence has practical implications beyond theoretical
biology. Mycelial networks have inspired new approaches to computing, including
unconventional computing paradigms that use living fungal networks as
biological processors. Researchers have demonstrated fungal computers capable
of solving logical operations, maze problems, and even simple graph-theoretic
problems by encoding inputs as stimuli and reading outputs from growth
patterns. These "mycocomputers" represent a novel form of biological computing
that operates on fundamentally different principles than silicon-based systems.

In robotics and artificial intelligence, the decentralized, emergent
computation of fungal networks offers a model for distributed autonomous
systems that can adapt to complex environments without central control. Swarm
robotics and edge computing architectures have drawn inspiration from fungal
network organization, applying similar principles of local decision-making
with global coordination.

## Critiques and Limitations

The concept of fungal intelligence remains controversial. Critics argue that
what appears to be intelligent behavior can be explained by simple biochemical
feedback loops and tropisms without invoking cognitive processes. The
optimization of foraging networks may emerge naturally from differential growth
rates in response to local nutrient gradients, rather than from any evaluative
or comparative process. The debate reflects deeper philosophical questions
about the definition of intelligence and the risk of anthropomorphizing
non-neural organisms. Applying the term "intelligence" to fungi may be
metaphorically useful but scientifically misleading if it obscures the actual
mechanisms involved.
