# Fungal Intelligence Fungal intelligence refers to the capacity of fungi — particularly their mycelial networks — to process information, make decisions, learn, and solve problems in ways that resemble cognitive behavior, despite lacking a nervous system, brain, or any conventional center of computation. This concept challenges traditional definitions of intelligence and cognition, raising fundamental questions about what it means to process information and adapt behavior in biological systems. The field sits at the intersection of mycology, neurobiology, computer science, and philosophy of mind. ## Problem-Solving and Optimization The most compelling evidence for fungal intelligence comes from studies of foraging behavior. The study of fungal intelligence has practical implications beyond theoretical
biology. [[mycelial-networks]] have inspired new approaches to computing, including
unconventional computing paradigms that use living fungal networks as
biological processors. Researchers have demonstrated fungal computers capable
of solving logical operations, maze problems, and even simple graph-theoretic
problems by encoding inputs as stimuli and reading outputs from growth
patterns. These "mycocomputers" represent a novel form of [[myconeurological-networks-and-biological-computing]]
that operates on fundamentally different principles than silicon-based systems.

In robotics and artificial intelligence, the decentralized, emergent
computation of fungal networks offers a model for distributed autonomous
systems that can adapt to complex environments without central control. Swarm
robotics and edge computing architectures have drawn inspiration from fungal
network organization, applying similar principles of local decision-making
with global coordination.

## Critiques and Limitations

The concept of fungal intelligence remains controversial. Critics argue that
what appears to be intelligent behavior can be explained by simple biochemical
feedback loops and tropisms without invoking cognitive processes. The
optimization of foraging networks may emerge naturally from differential growth
rates in response to local nutrient gradients, rather than from any evaluative
or comparative process. The debate reflects deeper philosophical questions
about the definition of intelligence and the risk of anthropomorphizing
non-neural organisms. Applying the term "intelligence" to fungi may be
metaphorically useful but scientifically misleading if it obscures the actual
mechanisms involved.

## Decision-Making Mechanisms

Fungal decision-making operates through distributed biochemical signaling
rather than centralized neural processing. [[mycelial-networks]] use cytoplasmic
streaming to transport nutrients, signaling molecules, and organelles across
the network. When a mycelium encounters multiple food sources simultaneously,
it must allocate resources between them, a process that demonstrates genuine
trade-off behavior. Research by Tero et al. (2010) showed that Physarum
polycephalum consistently recreated the efficient Tokyo rail network layout
when presented with the same geographic constraints, suggesting an intrinsic
capacity for [[biological-network-optimization]].

The mechanisms underlying these decisions involve calcium ion waves,
electrical impulse propagation, and [[fungal-fruiting-body-mechanics-osmotic-pressure]] differentials. Recent
research by Adamatzky (2018) demonstrated that mycelial networks produce
action-potential-like electrical spikes, with patterns that change in
response to environmental stimuli. These spikes can propagate at rates of
up to 1 cm per minute along hyphal cords, forming a primitive communication
system analogous to, but fundamentally different from, neural signaling.

## Learning and Memory

Evidence suggests fungi can exhibit habituation, a basic form of learning.
When exposed to repeated non-harmful stimuli, fungal networks reduce their
response over time, indicating a form of sensory adaptation that persists.
Experiments by Fricker et al. demonstrated that mycelial networks could
remember the location of a food source and adjust their growth patterns
accordingly even after the source was removed, suggesting a form of spatial
memory encoded in [[mycelial-network-architecture]].

Mycelial memory may also be encoded epigenetically. Exposure to stressors
such as drought, temperature extremes, or chemical threats can alter gene
expression patterns that persist through subsequent growth cycles. This
trans-generational [[transcendent-other-and-plant-human-information-transfer]] allows fungal colonies to transmit
experience to their descendants without any nervous system whatsoever.

## Common Mycorrhizal Networks

The concept [[wood-wide-web]] Wide Web" describes how [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]]
networks connect individual plants into integrated communication and
resource-sharing systems. Through these networks, plants can transfer
carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water, and chemical alarm signals. Mother
trees have been shown to preferentially funnel resources to their own
offspring through shared mycorrhizal connections. This ecological
intelligence operates at the ecosystem level, with fungal networks serving
as the information and resource infrastructure that connects forest
communities.
