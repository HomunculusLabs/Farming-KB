# Operational Groups in [[gadd-fungal-wood-decay-brown-rot-white-rot-soft-rot-mechanisms]] Survey Design

## Why Not Taxonomic Organization?

Traditional [[biodiversity-of-fungi-leaf-litter-microfungi-survey-methods]] surveys are organized along taxonomic lines.
For fungi, this approach breaks down. Fungal taxa span multiple
kingdoms and are so ecologically diverse that taxonomic organization
creates enormous duplication. An ascomycete on living leaves requires
different methods than an ascomycete in soil or on decaying wood.

## The Operational Group Concept

Mycological survey designers use "operational groups" — assemblages
defined by shared habitat, substrate, ecological function, or [[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]]
method rather than taxonomy. Each group can be surveyed by a specialist
team using appropriate techniques. This recognizes that practical
detection and identification demands drive survey design more than
phylogeny.

## Terrestrial Macrofungi

The most visible and commonly surveyed group: [[bloomfield-mushroom-cells-stem-cells-totipotency-fungal-tissues]]s, bracket fungi,
puffballs, coral fungi, and other large fruiting bodies. Surveys involve
systematic walking of transects or plots during fruiting seasons. Both
ascomycetes (cup fungi, morels, truffles) and basidiomycetes (gilled
mushrooms, boletes, polypores) are included. Specimens are dried and
vouchered for herbarium deposition. Collection is seasonal and requires
repeated visits to capture the full complement of species.

## Wood-Inhabiting Fungi

Includes both macrofungi (visible fruiting bodies on logs and snags)
and microfungi (detected by microscopy or isolation). Divided by decay
stage and wood condition: fresh branches versus decayed logs, bark
versus sapwood versus heartwood. Microfungi are isolated by placing
wood segments onto moist chambers or culture media. Different fungal
[[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] colonize each substrate type.

## Soil and Litter Fungi

Among the most diverse but least surveyed groups. Detection methods
include soil [[particle-filtration-dilution-plating-microfungal-isolation]], direct microscopy, and DNA extraction
from soil cores. Litter fungi are sampled by placing leaf material into
moist chambers and observing emergent structures. The soil [[soil-fungal-community-isolation-survey-methods]]
includes decomposers, mycorrhizal fungi, pathogens, nematode-trapping
fungi, and many unculturable groups detectable only by molecular
methods.

## Root-Associated Mutualists

Mycorrhizal fungi form obligate symbioses with plant roots and are
ecologically critical. Ectomycorrhizal fungi (primarily with temperate
trees) produce visible fruiting bodies but are best surveyed by
examining root tips microscopically for mantle and Hartig net
structures. [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] fungi (with most herbaceous plants)
lack conspicuous fruiting bodies and are detected by examining root
cortex cells or extracting spores from soil. [[biodiversity-of-fungi-pcr-molecular-methods-fungal-diversity]] have
revolutionized these surveys, revealing far greater diversity than
morphological methods.

## Endophytic Fungi

Endophytes live asymptomatically within plant tissues, particularly
leaves and stems. Detected by surface-sterilizing tissue segments,
placing them on nutrient agar, and identifying emerging colonies. Some
plant species host dozens to hundreds of endophyte species. Many are
vertically transmitted through seeds; others horizontally via spores.
Most endophytes' ecological roles remain poorly understood, though
many produce [[cordyceps-cancer-bioactive-compounds]] protecting host plants.

## Plant Pathogens

Fungal pathogens span many taxa and cause diseases from leaf spots to
wilts and rots. Surveyed through symptom [[ingham-soil-food-web-diversity-assessment-dna-molecular-methods]], isolation from
infected tissue onto selective media, and [[biodiversity-fungal-molecular-identification-dna-barcoding]].
Some are host-specific; others have broad host ranges. Monitoring is
important for agriculture and conservation, as introduced pathogens
can devastate native communities (chestnut blight, Dutch elm disease).
