# Integrated House-Garden System Elements

The total design for a [[bill-mollison]]) and adapted for tropical and subtropical conditions, the system demonstrates how a climatically appropriate house of local brick and tiles can amortize in 4–7 years if a productive garden is developed alongside it.

## The House Structure

### Room Layout and Zoning

The house is a simple, easily-heated and cooled structure, preferably on slab or raised pisé floor with edge insulation. Induced cross-ventilation cools and heats the living spaces. Vertical sashes or shutters to each room scoop air inward.

Key zones include:

- **H2: Main living area** — open to the central kitchen (H10) and living axis; during hot periods, outdoor living occurs under a rear porch trellis or front trellis (for societies that prefer street visibility)
- **H3, H4, H11: Bedrooms and study/library** — all open to or oriented toward the central kitchen/living axis for natural light and airflow
- **H5: Bathroom/toilet** — shower water directed to the wet garden area (A) or mulch forage crop (E); toilet and sink water always go to the greywater treatment area (C); handbasin water goes to banana circles (B)
- **H6: Shadehouse** — can extend around the house, with entries, trees or their trunks growing through it, minor crop resting against it, and a crop below the shade structure
- **H7: Summer kitchen** — part-paved outdoor area
- **H8: Outdoor cooking stove** — bulk-cooking stove with ashes directed to the greywater treatment area and garden
- **H9: Mudroom/pantry** — off the kitchen, with outside basin/sink for primary vegetable processing

### Entry and Thermal Design

The entry (H1) in the subtropics functions as a glasshouse fitted with a wide ceiling vent, drawing cool air from the shadehouse (H6). Note the straight-through airway via the living area (H2) and winter kitchen (H10) — this uninterrupted airflow path is essential for effective cross-ventilation.

### Water System

- **Main tank (T)** catches roof water; a small tank (t) on any animal shed supplements supply
- Tanks can be filled from a reticulated water system on a float-valve
- **All tank overflow** goes first to the wet garden bed (A), partially under the house eaves and partially in the open garden
- A path (P) runs around the whole house connecting to the outdoor kitchen and animal house

## Garden Elements

### A: Wet Food Patch

The wet food patch has an earth bank edge and receives clean water (roof runoff, tank overflow). It grows wet crops such as kangkong, watercress, and Chinese water chestnut. The bank is planted to dry staple foods — yam, sweet potato, cassava — which benefit from the wastewater. Some crops grow under partial shade, which taro appreciates along with other sub-aquatics.

### B: Banana Circles

Banana circles receive water from the hand basin and kitchen sink and are mulched from the hedge (E). They can also serve as papaya circles or mixed polyculture circles of papaya/banana/pigeon pea/tomato/yam.

### C: Greywater Treatment Area

The "dry" water patch contains no direct food plants and no root crops. Only vigorous, damp-tolerant, manure-tolerant green forage crops grow here — [[comfrey]], [[banna-grass]], lemongrass, and legumes. These are regularly cut and removed to animal pens as green feed or to garden beds and potato boxes. The area is sunken and surrounded by a bank growing pigeon pea, papaya, banana, or all of these combined.

### D and D*: Avenue Crops

Avenue crops of maize, corn, or other main-value crops are arranged in spiral or zig-zag layouts. They consist of self-mulched, stable intercrop systems such as maize/beans, yam/beans, cassava/beans, or melon/beans, with productive legume hedges (Leucaena, Gliricidia, Sesbania). Sticks from these go to the fire; ash returns to the garden. Hedge clippings from (E) or exotic mulch (paper, carpet wastes) can be added to these beds.

### E: Forage-Mulch Hedge

A low, raggedly-trimmed hedge of forage-mulch species serves three functions: privacy screen, windbreak, and food source. Just outside it, a very dense weed barrier (W) prevents grass invasion. In two places, this hedge connects to chicken pens (Y1 and Y2). Well-chosen clumps of sympodial, edible-shoot, solid bamboos form part of the hedge — providing food, mulch, and trellis repair material.

### F: Front Yard

The front yard contains traditional or showy flowers (where culture demands), a few basic medicinals, pleasant scents, fibre plants for trellis building, ornamental food plants (cactus, agave), and hardy plants that rarely need watering.

### G: Keyhole Garden Beds

Keyhole garden beds (accessible from all sides without stepping on soil) grow preferred vegetables, especially greens. The nearest bed to the kitchen holds bulk culinary herbs, supplemented by a spiral herb bed beside the outdoor stove and wet-area herbs in pots under the trellis in the pond area (mint). Rare dry-area herbs may grow at or near the front door (F).

Garden beds are mulched from the hedge and greywater treatment area (C). Food scraps, ashes, mulch, shredded paper, and plant waste are incorporated in compost pits, along with crop wastes and windbreak cuttings. The beds are always active and full of plants. Water is bucketed or reticulated from mains; shower or bath water can be run along low-pressure leaky pipes.

### H: Fukuoka-Style Grain Plot

A Fukuoka-style grain plot serves both people and partly poultry. It has a low surround of dry-tolerant mulch such as lemongrass and is also mulched from the edge hedge (E) and the palm clump.

### K: Palm Clump

A circle or clump of 6–12 palms of 1–4 species (ideally 2 coconuts, 2 oil palms, 2 dates, and a sugar palm) is mulched with chopped fronds, husks, wastes, ashes, and green hedge material. In this deep mulch, yams or vines grow during the wet season.

### Roof Trellis System

House roof areas are carefully fitted with vine crops spaced to allow approximately 30% light through. Basic vines include chayote, kiwifruit, beans, cucurbits, and grapes. One or two non-bearing vines can sprawl over the roof, significantly cooling the house. Special gutter designs are needed to catch roof runoff when vines cover the roof surface. Under the trellis, shade-tolerant plants like coffee and taro will grow.

### Animal Systems

Two fenced animal runs (Y1, Y2) are alternated — while one is rested it can be cropped if space allows. Both are well-shaded. Fencing can be woven bamboo, palm rib, banna grass stalks, or wire netting. Rabbit hutches, chicken roosts, pigeon lofts, quail houses, and guinea-pig rest areas are placed throughout the system. Each small roof catches water leading to a small tank for the garden. The hedge (E), wet mulch (C), tree herb layer, and spare corners all grow plants chosen specifically for animal fodder.

### Tree Integration

Carefully selected trees overshade the house roof — some may even be placed inside the house with holes in the roof allowing the trunk through. These trees provide light crown shade, fruits that fall or can be harvested from the roof, and can be tough spreading legumes (Prosopis) yielding livestock food crops. Additional trees produce staple fruits — avocado (on mounds 1 m high and 5 m across in wet areas), citrus, nut trees, and oil palm. Soft ground-cover below (nasturtium, soft legumes, comfrey) is sometimes slashed as mulch for trees or fed to livestock.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
Field trials provide essential data for validating theoretical approaches and refining methodologies.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.
Peer-reviewed publications and practitioner reports contribute complementary perspectives.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.

## Integration Strategies
Successful implementation often draws on multiple complementary approaches working in concert.
Scale-appropriate solutions range from backyard gardens to broadacre agricultural systems.
Knowledge sharing between practitioners accelerates collective learning and refinement of methods.
Regional networks and demonstration sites play crucial roles in technology transfer.

## Implementation Notes
Start with small-scale trials before expanding to larger operations.
Maintain detailed records of conditions, inputs, and outcomes for iterative refinement.
Regular review and adjustment of strategies based on observed results ensures continuous improvement.
