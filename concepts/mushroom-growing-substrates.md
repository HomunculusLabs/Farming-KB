# Mushroom Growing Substrates

A substrate is the material on which mushroom mycelium grows and from which it
extracts nutrients to produce fruiting bodies. Selecting and preparing the
right substrate is one of the most important factors in successful mushroom
cultivation. Different mushroom species have evolved to decompose different
materials, so substrate composition must match the target species.

## Substrate Categories

### Grain Spawn Substrates

Grains serve as the initial nutritious medium for expanding mycelium from a
spore or culture sample. Grains are not typically fruited directly (though some
methods do this); instead, colonized grain is used to inoculate bulk
substrates. Key grain options:

- **Rye berries**: Considered the gold standard. Excellent moisture retention,
  good nutritional profile, and individual grains separate easily for even
  distribution into bulk substrates.
- **Whole oats**: Inexpensive and readily available. Slightly more resistant
  to bacterial contamination than rye due to their hull.
- **Millet**: Tiny grains provide enormous surface area for mycelium to grab
  onto, speeding colonization. Often mixed with larger grains.
- **Wild bird seed**: A cheap mix of millet, milo, and sunflower seeds.
  Effective but requires careful preparation to remove floating debris.
- **Brown rice**: Standard for PF Tek. Not ideal for grain spawn due to
  tendency to clump.

### Bulk Substrates

Bulk substrates provide the mass and moisture reservoir needed for large
flushes of mushrooms. They are typically lower in nutrition than grain, which
actually helps resist contamination once fully colonized.

- **Coconut coir**: The most popular bulk substrate base. It is naturally
  resistant to contamination, holds water well, and requires no pasteurization
  when compressed. Available in compressed bricks that expand with water.
- **Vermiculite**: A mineral that improves water retention and aeration. Used
  in combination with coir or as part of PF Tek. Available in various grades.
- **Manure**: Horse or cow manure is excellent for *P. cubensis* and many
  dung-loving species. Must be aged, leached, and pasteurized before use.
  Leached horse manure is considered superior for potency and yield.
- **Straw**: Wheat or oat straw works well for oyster mushrooms and other
  species with aggressive colonization. Straw must be chopped, hydrated, and
  pasteurized (not sterilized, to retain beneficial microorganisms).
- **Hardwood sawdust**: The standard for wood-loving species like shiitake,
  lion's mane, and reishi. Oak, beech, and maple are preferred. Sawdust from
  conifers contains inhibitory compounds.
- **Wood chips**: Larger than sawdust, used for outdoor beds and log
  cultivation. Provides long-term decomposition for perennial mushroom beds.
- **Coffee grounds**: A nutrient-rich supplement that can replace up to 25% of
  a bulk substrate. Attracts contamination quickly; use fresh, pasteurized
  grounds only.

## Supplementation

Adding nitrogen-rich supplements to bulk substrates increases yields but also
raises contamination risk. Supplements must be used after the base substrate
is fully colonized (a technique called "delayed supplementation") or mixed in
small amounts before pasteurization.

- **Gypsum** (calcium sulfate): Added at 5-10% by volume. Improves substrate
  structure, provides calcium and sulfur, and buffers pH. Nearly universally
  recommended.
- **Bran** (wheat or rice): High in nitrogen. Dramatically increases yields
  but raises contamination risk. Typically added at 5-10%.
- **Coffee grounds**: Natural nitrogen source. Use sparingly (10-25%) and
  pasteurize thoroughly.
- **Bone meal or blood meal**: Organic nitrogen sources for outdoor beds.
- **Lime** (calcium carbonate): Raises pH for species that prefer alkaline
  conditions. Used in straw substrates for oyster mushrooms.

## Substrate Preparation Methods

### Sterilization

Full sterilization (15 psi, 90-120 minutes in a pressure cooker or autoclave)
eliminates all living organisms. Used for grain jars, bags, and supplemented
substrates. Sterilized substrates must be inoculated in near-laboratory
conditions because there is no beneficial microflora to compete with
contaminants.

### Pasteurization

Pasteurization (140-170°F / 60-76°C for 1-2 hours) kills most contaminants
while preserving beneficial thermophilic bacteria. These bacteria help protect
the substrate from mold colonization. Pasteurization is used for straw, manure,
and coir-based bulk substrates. Methods include hot water baths, steam
pasteurizers, and hydrated lime treatment.

### Cold Water Lime Pasteurization

A low-tech method where straw or similar material is soaked in water with
hydrated lime (pH raised to 12-13) for 12-24 hours at room temperature. The
high pH kills most mold spores and bacteria. After soaking, the material is
drained to field capacity and used immediately.

## Field Capacity

The moisture content of prepared substrate is measured by the "squeeze test."
A handful of substrate should hold together when squeezed firmly, releasing a
few drops of water but not dripping steadily. Overly wet substrates promote
bacterial contamination and slow colonization; overly dry substrates stall
mycelium growth and produce poor yields.

## Substrate Hygiene

Between uses, all containers, tools, and surfaces should be cleaned with 70%
isopropyl alcohol or a 10% bleach solution. Work areas should be free of dust
and debris. Substrate preparation areas should be separate from colonization
and fruiting areas to minimize cross-contamination.
