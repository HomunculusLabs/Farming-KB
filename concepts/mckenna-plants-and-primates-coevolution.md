# Plants and Primates: Coevolution and the Stoned Ape

In *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna develops the argument that the relationship between early humans and psychoactive plants was not merely one of opportunistic exploitation but a genuine coevolutionary partnership that shaped the emergence of human consciousness, language, and culture. The chapter "Plants and Primates: Postcards from the Stoned Age" presents the empirical foundation for what has become known as the "stoned ape" hypothesis.

## The Magic in Food

McKenna begins by establishing that the ways humans use plants, foods, and drugs cause the values of individuals and whole societies to shift. Eating some foods makes us happy, others sleepy, still others alert. Society tacitly encourages certain behaviors that correspond to internal feelings, thereby encouraging the use of substances that produce acceptable behaviors.

He argues that plant use is "a complex language of chemical and social interactions," yet most people are unaware of the effects of plants on themselves and their reality, partly because humanity has forgotten that plants have always mediated the cultural relationship to the world at large. When thinking about drugs, we tend to focus on episodes of intoxication, but many drugs are normally used in subthreshold or maintenance doses; coffee and tobacco are obvious examples in our culture. The result is a kind of "ambience of intoxication" — like fish in water, people swim in the virtually invisible medium of culturally sanctioned yet artificial states of mind.

Languages appear invisible to the people who speak them, yet they create the fabric of reality for their users. Plant use, McKenna argues, operates similarly — it creates a fabric of consciousness that its users take for granted.

## Early Hominid Dietary Experimentation

The strategy of early hominid omnivores, McKenna suggests, was to eat everything that seemed foodlike and to vomit whatever was unpalatable. This exposed early humans to an ever-shifting chemical equilibrium. An organism may regulate chemical input through internal processes, but ultimately mutagenic influences increase and a greater number of genetically variant individuals are offered up to natural selection.

The results are accelerated changes in neural organization, states of consciousness, and behavior. No change is permanent; each gives way to yet another. All flows. McKenna proposes that this constant exposure to novel plant chemistry was the engine driving the rapid expansion of the human brain during the Pleistocene — each new psychoactive encounter potentially selected for individuals with greater neural plasticity and sensitivity to altered states.

## Primate Self-Medication

A key empirical support for McKenna's thesis comes from primatological research on chimpanzee self-medication. At Gombe Stream National Park in Tanzania, researchers found that a particular species of leaf — *Aspilia* — kept appearing undigested in chimpanzee dung. Rather than eating their usual wild fruit, the chimps would walk twenty minutes or more to a site where *Aspilia* grew, place their lips over a leaf, hold it in their mouths, roll it around for a few moments, then swallow it whole. In this way as many as thirty small leaves might be eaten.

Biochemist Eloy Rodriguez of the University of California at Irvine isolated the active principle — a reddish oil named thiarubrine-A. Neil Towers of the University of British Columbia found that this compound could kill common bacteria in concentrations of less than one part per million. Herbarium records showed that African peoples used *Aspilia* leaves to treat wounds and stomachaches. Of the four species native to Africa, indigenous peoples used only three — the same three species used by the chimpanzees.

Rodriguez and Towers eventually identified nearly a dozen plants in what amounts to a veritable materia medica among chimpanzee populations, demonstrating that primate self-medication with bioactive plants is a real and widespread phenomenon. This research has since been expanded into the field of zoopharmacognosy, with documented examples of self-medication in species ranging from elephants to birds.

## Symbiosis and Human-Plant Relationships

McKenna invokes the concept of symbiosis to describe the relationship between humans and psychoactive plants. As plants influenced the development of humans and other animals, so were the plants themselves affected in turn. He defines this as a relationship conferring mutual benefit — the biological and evolutionary success of each species is linked to and enhanced by that of the other. This is the opposite of parasitism, though happy is the parasite that can evolve into a symbiont.

Symbiotic relationships can be very tightly bound genetically or somewhat more open. While human-plant interactions were symbiotic in their pattern of mutual gain, they were culturally mediated — not genetically fixed. This makes the relationship no less powerful, only more flexible and subject to the vagaries of cultural change and forgetting.

## Alkaloids as Essential Metabolites

McKenna challenges the conventional view that alkaloids — including all the major hallucinogens — are "secondary metabolites" produced by plants as defense mechanisms. He notes that alkaloids are formed in the greatest amounts in plant tissues that are most active in overall metabolism, and they fluctuate in both concentration and rate of metabolic decay. They are not inert end products but are in a dynamic state, suggesting they are essential to the life and survival strategy of the organism in ways not yet fully understood.

One possibility McKenna raises is that some of these compounds function as exopheromones — chemical messengers that do not act among members of a single species but act across species lines, allowing a small group of individuals to affect a community or an entire biome. This concept is explored more fully in his discussion of the [[mckenna-transcendent-other-and-exopheromones]].

The idea that nature might be an organism whose interconnected components communicate through chemical signals was only beginning to be studied when McKenna wrote. He argues that nature tends to maximize mutual cooperation rather than endless competition — the strategy that ensures survival is one of communication and sensitivity to signal processing. These are, in McKenna's view, language skills that plants have practiced far longer than humans.

## The You-Are-What-You-Eat Hypothesis

McKenna's proposed story of human emergence is fundamentally a "you-are-what-you-eat" story. Major climatic change and a newly broadened, hence mutagenic, diet provided many opportunities for natural selection to affect the evolution of major human traits. Each encounter with a new food, drug, or flavoring was fraught with risk and unpredictable consequences. And this is even more true today, when food contains hundreds of poorly studied preservatives and additives.

As an example, he cites sweet potatoes of the genus *Dioscorea*. In much of the tropical world, sweet potatoes provide a reliable and nutritious source of food. Nevertheless, several closely related species contain compounds that can interfere with ovulation — these became the source of raw materials for modern birth control pills. Something close to genetic chaos would descend on a population of primates that settled into feeding upon such species. Many such scenarios must have occurred as early hominids experimented with new foods while expanding their omnivorous dietary habits.

McKenna further notes that in the minds of preliterate people, the lines between drugs, foods, and spices are rarely clearly drawn. The shaman who gorges himself on chili peppers to raise inner heat is hardly in a less altered state than the nitrous oxide enthusiast after a long inhalation. In the human perception of flavor and pursuit of variety in eating, we are markedly different from even our primate cousins. Somewhere along the line, new omnivorous eating habits and an evolving brain were united in the happy notion that food can be experience. Gastronomy was born — born to join pharmacology, which must surely have preceded it.

## The Partnership Paradigm

McKenna's broader argument is that eating a plant or an animal is a way of claiming its power, a way of assimilating its magic. The implication is that the human search for psychoactive experience is not a vice or aberration but a fundamental biological drive — one that predates humanity itself and is shared with our primate cousins. The boundary-dissolving effects of psychedelics, in McKenna's view, represent a return to an earlier mode of consciousness in which the distinction between self and nature was not yet fixed.

This stands in contrast to the dominator model of human-nature relations, in which nature is seen as a resource to be exploited rather than a partner in a communicative relationship. McKenna suggests that the original human-plant partnership was one of mutual benefit and mutual respect, and that its dissolution — through the suppression of mystery religions, the rise of hierarchical religion, and the eventual triumph of scientific materialism — has left humanity in a state of chronic alienation from the natural world.

## Scientific Context and Criticism

The stoned ape hypothesis remains highly speculative and is not widely accepted in mainstream anthropology or evolutionary biology. Critics point out that there is no direct archaeological evidence of psilocybin mushroom use by early hominids, and that the neurological effects McKenna attributes to low-dose psilocybin — enhanced visual acuity, increased sexual arousal — have not been reliably demonstrated in controlled studies.

However, the primate self-medication data that McKenna cites has been substantially confirmed by subsequent research. The field of zoopharmacognosy — the study of how animals self-medicate with plants — has grown considerably since *Food of the Gods* was published, with documented examples of self-medication in species ranging from chimpanzees to elephants to birds. The concept of exopheromones — cross-species chemical signaling — has also gained traction in chemical ecology, though it remains a minority position.

The broader question of whether psychoactive plant use shaped human evolution remains open but has gained renewed attention in evolutionary psychology and ethnobiology. The discovery of psychedelic compounds in mushrooms that grow on the dung of cattle — animals central to human pastoralist cultures across Africa and Asia — continues to lend circumstantial support to the idea that early humans encountered these compounds regularly and that the encounter may have had behavioral and cognitive consequences.

McKenna's synthesis, while speculative, remains valuable for drawing attention to the neglected role of plant chemistry in human evolution and for challenging the assumption that psychoactive drug use is purely a cultural phenomenon with no biological roots.

## Zoopharmacognosy: Modern Developments

The field of zoopharmacognosy — the study of animal self-medication — has expanded
considerably since McKenna wrote. Documented examples now include: elephants in African
savannas selecting specific tree barks to induce labor; woolly spider monkeys in Brazil
consuming leaves of plants with anti-parasitic properties only during the rainy season when
parasite loads peak; capuchin monkeys rubbing millipedes over their fur as a natural insect
repellent; and baboons consuming roots and leaves of plants known to contain compounds
effective against Schistosoma infection.
These findings strengthen McKenna's broader argument that animals possess sophisticated
knowledge of plant pharmacology, even if the specific link to human evolution remains
speculative. The growing body of zoopharmacognosy research suggests that the relationship
between animals and psychoactive/medicinal plants is far more widespread and intentional than
was appreciated when Food of the Gods was published.

## Dietary Broadening and Neural Plasticity

McKenna's emphasis on dietary experimentation as a driver of neural plasticity connects to
contemporary research on the role of dietary compounds in brain development. Omega-3 fatty
acids, flavonoids, and various plant-derived polyphenols are now recognized as important
modulators of neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive function.
The human brain's extraordinary metabolic demands — consuming roughly 20% of the body's
energy while representing only 2% of its mass — required a dietary shift toward energy-dense,
nutrient-rich foods. McKenna's proposal that psychoactive compounds were part of this dietary
revolution is speculative, but the general principle that novel dietary inputs can drive neural
reorganization is well-supported.
The concept of "neuro-nutrition" — the study of how specific dietary compounds affect brain
structure and function — represents a mainstream scientific descendant of the kind of
thinking McKenna was advocating. The recognition that diet shapes cognition at the molecular
level lends indirect support to his broader thesis that the hominid dietary revolution was a
key driver of the cognitive revolution.

## The Exopheromone Hypothesis in Modern Context

McKenna's concept of exopheromones — chemical signals that operate across species boundaries
— has found partial support in the rapidly growing field of chemical ecology. Research on
plant-insect communication has revealed elaborate cross-species signaling systems: plants
under herbivore attack release volatile organic compounds that attract predators of the
attacking insects; mycorrhizal networks transmit chemical warning signals between trees; and
some plants produce compounds that manipulate the behavior of their pollinators.
The idea that psychoactive plant compounds might function as cross-species communication
signals — not merely as plant defense chemicals — aligns with current research on the
ecological functions of secondary metabolites. Whether McKenna's specific claims about the
communicative intent of psychoactive plants are correct remains debatable, but the broader
principle that plant chemistry mediates inter-species relationships in sophisticated ways is
now well-established in chemical ecology.
