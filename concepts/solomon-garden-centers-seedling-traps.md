# Garden Centers and Seedling Buying Traps

[[steve-solomon-evidence-based-organic-gardening]] dedicates an entire chapter of "Gardening When It Counts" to
the pitfalls of buying seedlings at garden centers. His position is blunt:
the first thing he aims to do is wean you off the garden center. This page
covers the common mistakes, deceptive practices, and why growing your own
seedlings is almost always superior.

## The Spring Rush

On spring weekends, garden centers are so busy that people stand in long
checkout lines holding armloads of expensive seedlings and a few seed
packets. Usually these buyers are making several major mistakes at once.
The pattern is familiar: weeks pass while gardeners watch weeds growing in
their yards instead of vegetables. Then, in a burst of spring enthusiasm,
they mob the garden center, buy an instant garden, rush home, start up the
rototiller, and put everything in -- sometimes in one afternoon.

A few weeks later, many seedlings fail to survive. More are purchased and
replanted. This repurchase-replant cycle may repeat several times. A great
deal of money is invested, but at the end of the season, gardeners add up
what was spent and the worth of what was actually eaten, and wonder why
they bothered. Yet next spring, the same compulsion returns.

## Problem One: Crops That Should Not Be Transplanted

People buy seedlings for types of vegetables that should be direct-seeded.
Many gardeners believe it is impossible to [[query-how-do-i-grow-carrots-successfully-and-troubleshoot-common-problems]], beets, radishes,
or corn from seed. The truth is that these crops generally do not
transplant well. Carrots and beets usually fail to make useful roots when
transplanted. Lettuce generally does better from [[comparison-seed-starting-indoors-vs-direct-seeding-techniques]]; the
transplanting process hugely shocks its root system. This is also true of
transplanting corn.

## Problem Two: Mislabeled Varieties

Unless a seedling is obviously mislabeled (such as a red cabbage seedling
labeled as green), you can rarely tell if the label is incorrect. Solomon
asks readers to consider the ethical temptations the seller might face:

- Pretty plastic labels with pictures cost as much as or more than the
  seed itself.
- The garden center operator may have invested in labels for one variety
  whose seed is not available that year. Would it not be tempting to
  substitute a similar variety?
- A seed merchant might be out of stock on a popular variety and
  "accidentally" mislabel a bag. Who could tell?

One species that is not likely to be mislabeled is tomatoes. People look
forward to eating a familiar fruit every year and would quickly notice if
fooled. Green bell peppers and eggplant are a different matter altogether.

## Problem Three: Inappropriate Varieties

The variety offered for sale may not even be a home-garden variety in the
first place. The garden center owner may not be able to get enough
seedlings from a legitimate home-garden seedling supplier, leading to
desperate substitution from commercial sources. Commercial broccoli
varieties, for example, are bred to produce one large central flower all
at once, not to continue producing side shoots for home gardeners.

## Problem Four: Pot-Bound Seedlings

Raising transplants for profit is not easy. Seedlings rapidly go from
being too young to ethically sell to being almost too old in under ten
days. When too young, the root system has not filled the pot; the soil
ball crumbles away during transplanting, damaging delicate root hairs.
Even if the seedling survives, it will not grow well for a week to ten
days.

When the seedling has been in its pot too long, roots overfill the pot.
The pot-bound plant may continue growing above ground, but its roots wrap
around and around inside. When transplanted, the constrained root system
cannot support the top in hot weather unless watered twice daily. Leaves
hardly grow for a week to ten days after transplanting.
