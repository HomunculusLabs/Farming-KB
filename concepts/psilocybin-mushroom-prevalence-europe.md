# Psilocybin Mushroom Prevalence in Europe

## Overview

The use of hallucinogenic (psilocybin-containing) mushrooms in Europe lay
relatively dormant from the late 1950s until availability and prevalence
increased during the late 1990s and early 2000s. The European Monitoring
Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction (EMCDDA) conducted a comprehensive
case study in 2005-2006, gathering data from 12 EU Member States and
Norway to assess the [[emcdda-epod-project-emerging-trend-detection-methodology]]. While overall prevalence estimates
remain considerably lower than for cannabis, lifetime use among young
adults approaches levels comparable to ecstasy in some countries.

## General Population Prevalence

Among young people aged 15-24 years, ever-in-lifetime use of
[[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-emerging-trend-europe]] ranges from less than 1% to 8% across EU Member
States. The highest prevalence estimates were reported in:

- **Netherlands**: Consistently among the highest rates, attributable in
  part to the historical availability through smartshops.
- **Czech Republic**: High prevalence driven by wild [[bloomfield-mushroom-foraging-immigrant-poisonings-paddy-straw-confusion]]
  traditions and domestic species availability.
- **United Kingdom**: Significant use levels, particularly during the
  early 2000s when market stalls and internet shops proliferated.
- **Germany**: Notable prevalence in urban centers.
- **Ireland**: Elevated rates similar to the UK pattern.

The lowest prevalence estimates were reported in Lithuania, Hungary, and
France. These differences likely reflect both actual usage variation and
differences in [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-survey-methodology-data-limitations]] and reporting.

## School Survey Data (ESPAD)

The European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD)
provides data on students aged 15-16. In some countries, ever-in-lifetime
use [[health-risks-of-hallucinogenic-mushrooms]] among school students equals or approaches
prevalence estimates for ecstasy (MDMA), suggesting that mushrooms
represent a significant entry point into hallucinogen use for adolescents.

Key findings from [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-school-surveys-adolescent-use]] include:

- Prevalence is consistently higher among students who have used other
  illegal drugs, indicating that [[health-risks-and-harm-reduction-for-hallucinogenic-mushroom-use]] is strongly
  associated with broader [[emcdda-clubbing-survey-prevalence-data-polydrug-use-patterns-mushrooms]].
- Use among 15-16 year olds who have not used other illegal substances
  is comparatively rare, suggesting mushrooms are not typically a first
  drug but rather one used within an established pattern of substance
  experimentation.

## Clubbing and Festival Surveys

Drug surveys conducted in club settings consistently show that prevalence
of illegal drug use is higher than among general or school populations.
Hallucinogenic mushrooms are more commonly used among young people
frequenting nightlife venues who have used other illegal drugs. This
pattern holds across multiple European countries and suggests that the
nightlife environment is a significant context for mushroom use.

## Trends Over Time

Historically, European drug surveys collected data on LSD consumption or
a general category of [[hallucinogenic-drugs]] rather than specific mushroom
data. This created a significant data gap. More recent surveys have
included specific questions about hallucinogenic mushrooms, allowing for
better trend tracking:

- **1990s**: Use was relatively rare and confined to small subcultures.
- **Late 1990s-early 2000s**: Sharp increase in availability through
  smartshops (Netherlands) and market stalls/internet (UK), driving
  increased prevalence.
- **2001-2006**: Several countries tightened legislation, potentially
  impacting availability and use patterns.
