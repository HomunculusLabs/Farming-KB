# Neurotropic Fungi of the Caribbean and Central America

The Caribbean and Central America form a critical biogeographic bridge between North and South America, and this region hosts a notable diversity of neurotropic fungi. The tropical climate, combined with the introduction of cattle during the colonial period, created ideal conditions for coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) psilocybian species. The region's fungal diversity reflects both native Neotropical elements and species that may have been introduced through historical trade and livestock movements.

## Central America

Central America, stretching from Guatemala to Panama, contains several important neurotropic fungal species, though the region has been less intensively studied than Mexico or South America.

### Guatemala

Guatemala has documented reports of several important neurotropic species:

- **Amanita muscaria** — reported by Lowy (1972, 1974, 1977), Torres (1984), and Wasson et al. (1986); grows in mycorrhizal association with Pinus in highland areas
- **Psilocybe caerulescens** — reported by Singer (1978)
- **Psilocybe cubensis** — documented by Guzmán (1983) and Torres (1984); the common "san isidro" mushroom of lowland cattle pastures
- **Psilocybe mexicana** — reported by Lowy (1977) and Torres (1984); this species, one of the most important sacred fungi of Mexico, extends into Guatemala

### Costa Rica

Costa Rica, despite its extraordinary overall biodiversity, has surprisingly few documented neurotropic fungal records:

- **Amanita muscaria** — reported by Sáenz et al. (1983)
- **Claviceps paspali** — documented on grasses
- **Copelandia cyanescens** — reported by Sáenz et al. (1983)
- **Psilocybe cubensis** — documented by Sáenz et al. (1983) and Guzmán (1995)
- **Psilocybe cf. aztecorum** — an unconfirmed record from Sáenz et al. (1983), suggesting possible highland species yet to be formally documented
- **Psilocybe cf. mexicana** — another unconfirmed record, hinting at species shared with Mexico

The paucity of records from Costa Rica is striking given the country's renowned biodiversity and extensive cloud forest systems, suggesting significant undercollection.

### El Salvador and Honduras

- **Psilocybe subcubensis** — reported from both El Salvador and Honduras by Guzmán (1983); this species is closely related to P. cubensis but is smaller and less commonly collected

### Panama

- **Psilocybe caerulescens** var. caerulescens — reported by Guzmán (1983)
- **Psilocybe dumontii** — a species described by Singer, known from Panama; unusual in its habitat preferences compared to other tropical Psilocybe species

### Belize

- **Copelandia cyanescens** — documented by Gerhardt (1996)
- **Psilocybe cordispora** — reported by Reid (1970)
- **Psilocybe cubensis** — documented by Heim (1956b) and Singer and Smith (1958)

## Caribbean Islands

The Caribbean archipelago presents a fascinating study in island biogeography for neurotropic fungi. The scattered island chains, combined with their tropical climate and livestock economies, support populations of several widespread coprophilous species.

### Cuba

Cuba is notable as the type locality for Psilocybe cubensis, which was originally described by Earle (1906) from Cuban material. The species epithet "cubensis" directly references its Cuban origin:

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — the type species was described from Cuba; additional reports by Heim (1956b, 1978) and Singer and Smith (1958)
- **Psilocybe plutonia** — a distinctive species known from Cuba, described by Berkeley and Curtis, growing on woody substrates rather than dung

### Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico is one of the better-studied Caribbean islands for neurotropic fungi, with several endemic and widespread species:

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — common and widespread (Heim, 1956b; Singer and Smith, 1958)
- **Psilocybe portoricensis** — an endemic species described by Guzmán, Tapia, and Nieves-Rivera (1997b), found only in Puerto Rico
- **Psilocybe guilartensis** — another endemic species from Puerto Rico, described by the same team (1997b), growing in native forests
- **Psilocybe subcubensis** — reported by Navarro and Betancourt (1992)
- **Copelandia cyanescens** — documented by Navarro and Betancourt (1992)

The presence of two endemic Psilocybe species on Puerto Rico suggests that island isolation may have promoted speciation, and that additional endemic species likely occur on other Caribbean islands that have been less thoroughly studied.

### Jamaica

- **Copelandia cyanescens** — reported by Pollock (1976)
- **Psilocybe fuliginosa** — documented by Guzmán (1983)
- **Psilocybe mammillata** — reported by Guzmán (1983) and Stamets (1996)

### Dominican Republic

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — reported by Rodríguez-Gallart (1989) and Guzmán (1995)

### Other Caribbean Islands

- **Bahamas** — Panaeolus papilionaceus (Gerhardt, 1996)
- **Bermuda** — Claviceps paspali and Copelandia cyanescens
- **Guadalupe** — Psilocybe cubensis, P. plutonia, Panaeolus sphinctrinus, P. subbalteatus
- **Martinique** — Psilocybe cubensis, P. caerulescens var. caerulescens, P. plutonia, P. yungensis, Panaeolus sphinctrinus, P. subbalteatus, Claviceps paspali
- **Trinidad** — Copelandia cyanescens (Dennis, 1970) and Psilocybe cubensis (Singer and Smith, 1958)
- **Granada** — Copelandia cyanescens (Gerhardt, 1996)
- **San Vincent Island** — Panaeolus papilionaceus (Pegler, 1983)

## Psilocybe cubensis: A Caribbean Origin

Psilocybe cubensis is arguably the most widely known tropical neurotropic fungus, and its type locality is Cuba (Earle, 1906). This species grows almost exclusively on bovine dung in tropical and subtropical regions. Guzmán (1983) raised the interesting hypothesis that P. cubensis was introduced to the Americas through the slave trade during Spanish colonial times, since cattle were introduced to the Americas by the Spanish in the 16th-17th centuries. The species does not grow natively in Europe despite being common in Mexico, Central America, South America, and tropical regions worldwide. Its presence in Africa (though poorly documented) may represent the original source population.

## Ecological Patterns

The Caribbean and Central American neurotropic mycoflora shows several clear ecological patterns:

1. **Coprophilous dominance**: Psilocybe cubensis, Copelandia cyanescens, and Panaeolus species dominate in lowland pastures where cattle are raised
2. **Elevation stratification**: Amanita muscaria and Psilocybe mexicana-type species occur in highland pine and cloud forests, while P. cubensis and Copelandia species dominate in lowland tropical zones
3. **Endemism on larger islands**: Puerto Rico's two endemic Psilocybe species suggest that larger Caribbean islands may harbor unique species not found on the mainland
4. **Claviceps species**: C. paspali is widely distributed across the Caribbean on grasses, following its host plant distribution

## Conservation and Research Needs

The Caribbean and Central American neurotropic mycoflora remains significantly understudied. Countries like Costa Rica, with their extraordinary biodiversity investment, have paradoxically few records of neurotropic species. Systematic surveys of cloud forests, pine savannas, and lowland pastures throughout the region would almost certainly yield new species and range extensions. The endemic species of Puerto Rico highlight the conservation importance of island mycofloras, which may be threatened by habitat destruction and climate change.
