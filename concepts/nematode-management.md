# Nematode Management

Plant-parasitic nematodes are microscopic roundworms that attack plant roots, causing stunting, yellowing, wilting, and reduced yields. Root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) are the most damaging and widespread group, affecting tomatoes, carrots, peppers, cucurbits, and many other crops. Management requires an integrated approach combining crop rotation, resistant varieties, biological controls, and soil health improvement.

## Understanding Nematodes

### Types of Plant-Parasitic Nematodes

**Root-Knot Nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.)**
- The most destructive group in home gardens and farms
- Cause characteristic galls (knots) on roots that interfere with water and [[plant-nutrient-uptake-mechanisms]]
- Affected plants show stunting, wilting in hot weather (despite adequate moisture), yellowing, and reduced yields
- Species include M. incognita (southern root-knot), M. hapla (northern root-knot), M. javanica, and M. arenaria
- Most active in warm soils (70-85°F); populations decline in cold winters

**Lesion Nematodes (Pratylenchus spp.)**
- Migrate through root tissue, creating lesions that serve as entry points for fungi and bacteria
- Affect a wide range of crops including corn, wheat, soybeans, and fruit trees
- No visible galls; symptoms include darkened root lesions and general decline

**Cyst Nematodes (Heterodera and Globodera spp.)**
- Soybean cyst nematode and potato cyst nematode are economically important species
- Females form visible cysts on roots that protect eggs for years
- Specific to their host crop; soybean cyst nematode only affects soybeans and a few other legumes

**Sting Nematodes (Belonolaimus spp.)**
- Very damaging but relatively localized
- Feed on root tips, causing stubby, swollen root systems
- Affect turfgrass, corn, and many vegetables
- Prefer sandy soils

### Beneficial Nematodes (Entomopathogenic)

Not all nematodes are plant pests. Beneficial (entomopathogenic) nematodes are predators of insect pests. They are used as biological insecticides against soil-dwelling pests like grubs, flea beetles, and fungus gnat larvae. See [[beneficial-insects-guide]] for more details.

- **Steinernema spp.**: Seek and kill various soil insects. Apply as a soil drench.
- **Heterorhabditis spp.**: More aggressive hunters, effective against grubs and beetle larvae.
- These are allies in [[cannabis-pest-management-guide]] and should be encouraged or purchased for soil pest problems.

## Identifying Nematode Problems

### Above-Ground Symptoms

- Stunted growth despite adequate water and nutrients
- Wilting during hot parts of the day (recovering at night, but more severe than expected)
- Yellowing (chlorosis) starting from older leaves
- General decline and reduced yield
- Patchy distribution in the field or garden (nematode populations are not uniform)
- Symptoms often resemble [[cannabis-grow-nutrient-deficiency-problem-solving]] or drought stress

### Below-Ground Symptoms (Root Examination)

- **Root-knot nematodes**: Distinctive galls or knots on roots. These are swollen areas where females are embedded. Roots may also be branched, stubby, or rotten.
- **Lesion nematodes**: Dark brown or black lesions on roots. Roots may be pruned or rotted.
- **Cyst nematodes**: Small (pinhead-sized) white, yellow, or brown cysts attached to roots.
- Healthy comparison: Dig up a healthy plant of the same species nearby and compare root systems.

### Soil Testing

- Collect soil samples from the root zone (4-6 inches deep, 15-20 cores per sample area)
- Submit to a nematode testing laboratory (many university extension services offer this)
- Sample in late summer or early fall when populations are typically highest
- Test before planting susceptible crops to determine if nematodes are present and at what level

## Crop Rotation for Nematode Management

Rotation is the most important long-term strategy. The key is to rotate between susceptible crops and non-host or resistant crops to starve nematode populations.

### Cover Crop Rotation for Nematode Suppression

Certain cover crops suppress nematode populations through natural chemical compounds or by acting as poor hosts that stimulate nematode eggs to hatch without providing food.

- **French marigolds (Tagetes patula)**: Release alpha-terthienyl from their roots, which is toxic to root-knot nematodes. Plant densely as a cover crop for one full [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Varieties with the highest nematicidal effect: 'Nemagone', 'Petite Harmony', 'Tangerine'. See details below.
- **Cereal rye**: Poor host for root-knot nematodes. The rye residues contain allelopathic compounds that suppress nematode populations after incorporation.
- **Sorghum-sudangrass**: Roots release a natural nematicide (sorgoleone). Incorporate the green manure at peak growth for maximum effect.
- **Mustard**: Biofumigant glucosinolates suppress nematodes. See [[green-manure-crops]] for biofumigation protocol.
- **Daikon radish**: Moderate biofumigant effect; breaks compaction, improving root growth of subsequent crops.
- See [[cover-cropping-guide]] and [[crop-rotation-principles]] for comprehensive rotation planning.

## Marigolds for Nematode Control

Marigolds are the most well-known and effective biological nematicide for garden-scale use. Not all marigolds are equally effective — the correct species and variety matter.

### Effective Species and Varieties

- **French marigolds (Tagetes patula)**: Most effective nematicidal species. Alpha-terthienyl is released from roots and accumulated in soil. Recommended varieties: 'Nemagone', 'Petite Harmony', 'Single Gold', 'Tangerine', 'Red Brocade'.
- **African marigolds (Tagetes erecta)**: Less effective than French marigolds but still provide some suppression. Varieties: 'Crackerjack', 'Gold Coin'.
- **Signet marigolds (Tagetes tenuifolia)**: Limited data; may provide moderate suppression.

### How to Use Marigolds

1. **Plant as a cover crop**: Interplant densely throughout the affected bed (6-8 inch spacing) for one full growing season. The more plants, the more root zone nematicidal effect.
2. **Duration**: One full growing season (4-5 months of growth) is needed for significant nematode suppression. Short-term interplanting provides limited benefit.
3. ** incorporation**: At the end of the season, incorporate marigold tops and roots into the soil. The above-ground material also contains nematicidal compounds.
4. **Timing**: Plant after the last susceptible crop is harvested. The marigold crop "cleans" the soil for the next susceptible crop.

### Limitations

- Marigolds suppress nematode populations but do not eliminate them completely
- Effectiveness varies with soil type, temperature, and nematode species
- In heavy infestations, 1-2 years of marigold [[fukuoka-clover-cover-cropping-soil-ecology]] may be needed before replanting susceptible crops
- Not a substitute for rotation — use marigolds as part of a broader nematode management strategy

## Resistant Varieties

Many [[comparison-heirloom-vegetable-varieties-vs-seed-saving-techniques]] have been bred for nematode resistance. The letter "N" in seed catalog descriptions indicates root-knot nematode resistance.

### Nematode-Resistant Tomato Varieties

- 'Better Boy' (VFN), 'Celebrity' (VFFNTA), 'Big Beef' (VFFNTA), 'Mountain Merit' (VFFNTA), 'Solar Fire' (VFFNTA), 'Determinate varieties for processing: 'San Marzano', 'Roma VF'
- The N designation specifically refers to the Mi gene for root-knot nematode resistance
- Resistance breaks down at soil temperatures above 85°F — in very hot climates, resistance may be unreliable

### Other Resistant Crops

- **Peppers**: Some varieties carry the N gene. Check seed descriptions.
- **Soybeans**: Many varieties with resistance to soybean cyst nematode (SCN). Rotate SCN-resistant varieties to prevent race shifts.
- **Cowpeas**: Many southern pea varieties are resistant to root-knot nematodes.
- **Sweet potatoes**: Some varieties show resistance to certain Meloidogyne species.
