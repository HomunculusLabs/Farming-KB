# Two-Tier Selection Model for Root Microbiome Assembly

The two-tier selection model describes how plant root-associated bacterial communities are assembled through two sequential filtering steps: first by soil edaphic factors, then by host genotype. This framework explains why root microbiomes differ from surrounding soil communities while still reflecting their soil of origin, and why different plant cultivars harbor distinct endophytic microbiota even when grown in identical soil.

## The Two Filtering Steps

### Tier One: Soil-Derived Community Shaping

The first selection step is driven by edaphic (soil) factors that structure the baseline microbial community available for root colonization. Bulk soil microbial composition depends on complex interactions between soil type, pH, nutrient content, moisture, temperature, and organic matter. These factors determine which bacterial taxa are present in the local species pool that will serve as recruits for the rhizosphere and endorhiza.

Edaphic factors vary in their relative importance. Across multiple studies, nitrogen concentration has emerged as the strongest structuring factor, followed by salinity, carbon content, water content, and pH. A BEST (BioEnv Stepwise) analysis of pooled Cannabis microbiome data found that three edaphic factors — nitrogen, carbon, and water content — optimally explained community variance (rho = 0.632).

### Tier Two: Host Genotype-Dependent Selection

The second selection step occurs when bacteria migrate from the rhizosphere into root tissues (the endorhiza). At this stage, the plant host exerts genotype-dependent selection, filtering the soil-derived community based on compatibility with root chemistry, immune responses, and exudate profiles. This second tier is primarily responsible for differences in community structure (abundance patterns) within endorhiza samples between cultivars.

Evidence from Cannabis microbiome research demonstrates that soil type had the strongest influence over which OTUs were present (unweighted analysis: 657 significant OTUs), while cultivar had zero significant effect on presence or absence but strongly affected relative abundance (weighted analysis: 71 significant OTUs). This confirms that host genotype fine-tunes community structure without fundamentally changing which taxa are available.

## Empirical Evidence from Cannabis

The model was tested using five Cannabis cultivars across two experiments with contrasting edaphic conditions. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of the second experiment revealed the predicted pattern:

1. **PC1 in unweighted analysis (32.06% variance)** was dominated by soil type, confirming soil as the primary determinant of community composition (which taxa are present).
2. **PC2 (11.34% variance)** was dominated by sample type, with endorhiza samples having high positive values, rhizosphere intermediate, and bulk soil negative values.
3. **PC1 in weighted analysis (34.51% variance)** was dominated by Cannabis strain, demonstrating that cultivar controls community structure (relative abundance) more than composition.

Endorhiza communities shared significantly more OTUs with their own soil (mean = 2,934) than with a different soil in which the same strain was grown (mean = 2,162), confirming the soil-derived origin of endophytic microbes.

## Phylum-Level Shifts Across Compartments

The two-tier model predicts specific taxonomic shifts as bacteria transition from bulk soil through the rhizosphere into the endorhiza:

- **Acidobacteria** dramatically decrease in abundance within the endorhiza. The most significant OTU difference between sample types was the decrease of Acidobacteria order iii1-15 (Bonferroni-corrected ANOVA: p = 1.12e-7).
- **Proteobacteria** and **Actinobacteria** increase in relative abundance within the endorhiza. Of 51 OTUs that increased from rhizosphere to endorhiza, 17 were predominantly Proteobacteria, including several from the Rhizobiales order.
- Mean OTU abundance was highly correlated between bulk soil and rhizosphere (Pearson rho = 0.92), moderately correlated between rhizosphere and endorhiza (rho = 0.63), and lowest between bulk soil and endorhiza (rho = 0.42).

## Alpha Diversity Gradient

Alpha diversity peaks in bulk soil and declines progressively through the rhizosphere into the endorhiza, consistent with sequential filtering. In Cannabis grown in Mo-Bio soil, Chao1 diversity declined from 4,947 in bulk soil to 4,525 in the rhizosphere, then dropped dramatically to 3,321 in the endorhiza. This pattern was consistent across soil types, though the magnitude of the bulk-to-rhizosphere decline varied with soil fertility.

## Rhizosphere as Intermediate Zone

Beta diversity comparisons reveal that rhizosphere and bulk soil communities are more similar to each other than either is to endorhiza communities. Beta distances between rhizosphere and bulk soil were significantly lower than distances between rhizosphere and endorhiza for both weighted and unweighted analyses. However, rhizosphere-to-endorhiza distances were not significantly different from bulk-soil-to-endorhiza distances, providing mixed evidence for the first filtering step and suggesting the transition from soil to root interior may be more abrupt than gradual.

## Cultivar-Specific Enrichment

Different Cannabis strains enriched for distinct bacterial groups within their endorhiza. Significant OTU abundance differences between strains were composed mostly of Proteobacteria, notably from the Pseudomonadales, Burkholderiales, Sphingomonadales, and Rhizobiales orders. Bacteroidetes from Sphingobacteriales and Flavobacteriales also contributed to strain-specific differences. One notable finding was the prevalence of Sphingomonas wittichii in the Maui Wowie strain, a bacterium capable of metabolizing phenazine-1-carboxylic acid and implicated in enhanced soil survival.

## Shared Core Microbiome

Despite cultivar-specific differences in community structure, a core microbiome of taxa consistently associated with Cannabis roots was identifiable. When pooling samples by strain and analyzing the core microbiome within each, certain bacterial groups recurred across all cultivars regardless of soil type. The core was dominated by Proteobacteria, particularly Alpha- and Beta-proteobacteria, along with Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes. This core likely represents taxa with generalist adaptations to the rhizosphere niche: the ability to utilize root exudates as carbon sources, tolerance of plant defense compounds, and capacity for biofilm formation on root surfaces.

The existence of a shared core alongside cultivar-specific enrichment patterns is consistent with the two-tier model: the first tier (soil) determines the available species pool from which the core is drawn, while the second tier (host genotype) modulates the relative abundances of specific taxa within that pool. This means that even genetically distinct cultivars will share a baseline set of root associates, while differing in which of those associates become dominant community members.

## Comparison with Other Plant Systems

The two-tier selection model was initially developed from studies of Arabidopsis thaliana and Populus deltoides before being validated in Cannabis. In Arabidopsis, Bulgarelli and colleagues (2012) demonstrated that root-inhabiting bacterial communities were distinct from the surrounding soil and structured by host genotype. In Populus, Gottel and colleagues (2011) showed that distinct microbial communities existed within the endosphere and rhizosphere across contrasting soil types, with the endosphere harboring significantly reduced diversity compared to the rhizosphere.

The Cannabis findings extend these observations to a crop plant that produces an unusually diverse array of secondary metabolites (cannabinoids, terpenes, flavonoids), raising the question of whether these bioactive compounds serve as additional selection pressures within the second tier.

The fact that cultivar-specificity was detectable in Cannabis endorhiza communities even eight weeks after harvest — when root exudation had ceased and roots were undergoing senescence — suggests that the plant's structural and chemical legacy within root tissues persists as a selection pressure longer than expected. This durability of the genotype signal has implications for understanding how long root-associated communities remain stable after plant death, a factor relevant to crop rotation and soil health management.

## Experimental Design Considerations

The Winston et al. study highlighted several methodological factors important for microbiome studies:

- **Sampling timing:** Post-harvest sampling introduced confounding effects from root decay, detectable through elevated Cellvibrio abundance. Pre-harvest sampling from living plants provided cleaner signals of cultivar-specificity.
- **Edaphic variation:** Experiments with minimal edaphic variation (first experiment, single location) failed to show rhizosphere cultivar effects, while experiments with significant edaphic differences (second experiment, two locations) revealed strong cultivar-specific structuring.
- **Pseudoreplication:** Triplicate samples from different roots on the same plant served as pseudoreplicates rather than true biological replicates, a distinction important for statistical power analysis.
- **Sequencing depth:** Rarer OTUs in shallow-sequenced samples may be missed, affecting alpha diversity estimates and potentially obscuring low-abundance but functionally important community members.

## Limitations and Complications

Several factors complicate the clean application of the two-tier model:

- **Cannabinoid-microbiome correlation:** THC concentration was significantly correlated with endorhiza community structure (Mantel test: r-stat = 0.863, p = 0.001), but THC also covaried with soil edaphic factors, making it difficult to disentangle host chemistry from soil effects. One soil type produced plants with both higher THC and distinct microbial communities, confounding the two variables.
- **Post-harvest sampling effects:** When root samples were taken eight weeks post-harvest, root decay signatures (particularly high Cellvibrio abundance at 16.9% vs. 0.095% in living plants) could obscure the living plant's cultivar-specific signal. Despite this, cultivar-specificity remained detectable, suggesting a durable structural imprint within root tissues.
- **Growth stage dependence:** Plant growth stage strongly influences microbiota composition, meaning that the strength and nature of both selection tiers may shift throughout the growing season. Seasonal and diel temperature changes, water content fluctuations, and CO2 concentration all affect rhizosphere dynamics.
- **Fungal interactions:** Mycorrhizal fungi and fungal saprotrophs play important roles in the rhizosphere that were not fully captured in this bacterial-focused study. The interplay between bacterial and fungal selection tiers remains an open question.

## Implications for Agriculture

Understanding the two-tier selection model has practical implications for Cannabis cultivation and agriculture more broadly. If cultivar-specific microbiota can be shown to improve plant fitness, suppress disease, or augment desired metabolite production, then breeding programs could select for cultivars that recruit beneficial microbial communities. The model also suggests that soil management practices that shape the tier-one community will have cascading effects on the tier-two endophytic community, making soil health a fundamental lever for optimizing plant-microbe partnerships.

### Biocontrol Applications

Endorhiza bacteria support plant growth and suppress disease by providing phytohormones, low molecular weight compounds, and enzymes involved in regulating growth and metabolism. They also assist host plants in tolerating phytotoxic effects of environmental toxicants through mechanisms including ACC deaminase activity, which lowers plant ethylene concentrations under stress conditions. If specific Cannabis cultivars selectively recruit bacteria with biocontrol properties, this could be leveraged to reduce dependence on chemical pesticides and fertilizers, particularly in organic cultivation systems where regulatory constraints on synthetic inputs are strict.

### Terroir and Crop Quality

The concept of microbial terroir — first documented in wine grapes through work by Bokulich, Gilbert, and colleagues — extends naturally to Cannabis. Endophytes and epiphytes may play a role in localized crop characteristics, including terpene and cannabinoid profiles.

The two-tier model predicts that even the same cultivar grown in different soils will develop distinct endorhiza communities, which may in turn influence the plant's secondary metabolite production through complex signaling pathways between host and microbe. This has implications for the geographic origin labeling and quality consistency of Cannabis products, paralleling the appellation systems used in viticulture.

The terroir concept is further supported by observations that microbial communities in the Cannabis rhizosphere and endorhiza are strongly influenced by soil nitrogen, carbon, and salinity levels — the same variables that vary naturally across agricultural regions and impart distinct chemical signatures to crops.

### Future Research Directions

Key unanswered questions include: which aspects of host genotype drive the observed community structure differences (root exudate chemistry, immune receptor diversity, cell wall composition)? How does the relative importance of each tier shift across the plant life cycle? Can targeted inoculation with beneficial strains overcome the soil-derived first tier to establish desired endophytic communities? Time-series sampling of endorhiza communities across multiple cultivars and growth stages will be essential to address these questions. Additionally, metagenomic and metatranscriptomic approaches could reveal the functional gene content and active metabolic pathways that distinguish cultivar-specific communities, moving beyond taxonomic composition to understand the ecological roles of individual community members.
