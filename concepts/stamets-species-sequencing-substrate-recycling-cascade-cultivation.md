## Species Sequencing: Maximizing Substrate Potential

Species sequencing is a cultivation strategy developed by [[lentinula-edodes]]), for example, is a
primary decomposer of hardwood lignin and cellulose but leaves behind significant
undigested fiber. Once its life cycle is complete, a secondary decomposer such as

remaining wood fiber and also consume the dead mycelium of the Shiitake.

After the second species has run its course, a third species can be introduced,
and so on. No single fungal species possesses the complete enzymatic repertoire
needed to fully decompose complex wood substrates. By sequencing multiple species
with complementary enzyme systems, the cultivator extracts a far greater
proportion of the substrate's nutritional value.

Chang and Miles (1989) demonstrated that net available nitrogen in waste
substrate actually increased proportionally through sequential cropping. Using
cotton waste, total nitrogen of fresh compost waste was 0.63%. After the Paddy
Straw mushroom ([[grifola-frondosa]]), and Reishi (Ganoderma
lucidum) are the first to colonize fresh, [[coprinus-comatus]]) occupy the final
indoor stage, growing on heavily degraded substrate with minimal or no
nutritional supplementation.

Each species in the cascade prepares the substrate for the next by increasing
surface area through partial digestion, liberating bound nutrients, and creating
a more hospitable physical structure. The dead mycelium of each predecessor
serves as a nutrient base for the next species, recycling biological material
that would otherwise be waste.

## A Practical Sequencing Model: The Shiitake Pathway

The Shiitake-to-Oyster-to-King Stropharia sequence is the most thoroughly
documented pathway in Stamets' work. After Shiitake mushrooms stop producing on
supplemented sawdust blocks, the now-blackened blocks are broken apart until they
resemble sawdust in texture.

Calcium sulfate and/or calcium carbonate are added at roughly one cup per twenty
blocks to enhance particle separation, improve drainage, and adjust pH to the
6.5 to 7.5 range. Water is slowly added to achieve a moisture content of 60 to
65%, intentionally lower than the ideal 75% to reduce fermentation and spoilage
risk during the transition.

The reconstituted substrate is filled into polypropylene bags and sterilized.
Waste substrates harbor far more microorganisms than fresh sawdust, so
sterilization times may need to be prolonged to ensure complete kill of resident
contaminants. Oyster mushrooms grow prolifically on spent Shiitake substrate with
no amendment, though King Oyster and Maitake benefit from 10% supplementation
with rice bran or corn.
