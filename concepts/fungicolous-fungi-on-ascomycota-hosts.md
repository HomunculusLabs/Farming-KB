# Fungicolous Fungi on Ascomycota Hosts

Fungicolous fungi — fungi that grow on other fungi — represent a vast yet poorly documented component of fungal biodiversity. Their interactions range from obligate parasitism to facultative colonization, with many species exhibiting highly specific host relationships. This page covers fungicolous associations involving ascomycete sporocarps, myxomycete plasmodia, dung communities, sequestrate ascomycetes, and anamorphic ascomycete hosts.

## Myxomyceticolous Fungi (Continued)

The myxomycetes (slime molds) serve as substrates for a diverse array of fungicolous fungi. Several species exhibit striking host specificity or ecological breadth:

- **Nectriopsis violacea** — found on *Fuligo* species, producing distinctive violet-colored perithecia on the plasmodial or aethalial stages of its host.
- **Blistum tomentosum** — colonizes members of the Trichiales (an order of myxomycetes), demonstrating specificity at the order level.
- **Nectriopsis exigua** and **Verticillium rexianum** — these two species are among the most ubiquitous myxomyceticolous fungi, encountered across a wide range of myxomycete hosts and substrates, suggesting generalist ecological strategies.
- **Stilbella byssiseda** — a synnematous fungus commonly observed on various myxomycete fruiting bodies.
- **Dipodascus macrosporus** — an unusual zygomycete acting as a facultative parasite within the plasmodium of its myxomycete host, representing one of the few documented intracellular fungal parasites of slime molds.

Other zygomycetes also colonize myxomycetes, expanding the taxonomic range of organisms capable of exploiting these substrates beyond the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota. The diversity of myxomyceticolous fungi suggests that slime mold fruiting bodies and plasmodia serve as significant but underappreciated ecological niches, particularly in moist forest floor environments where both groups co-occur abundantly.

## Fungi on Discomycetes (Cup Fungi)

Discomycetes — the cup fungi and their allies — host a remarkable diversity of fungicolous species. The following associations are among the best documented:

### Key Parasite–Host Pairs

- **Hypomyces cervinigenus** and its anamorph **Mycogone cervina** — parasitize *Mycolachnea*, a genus of discomycetes associated with decaying plant material.
- **Hypomyces stephanomatis** and **Stephanoma** species — recorded on *Helvella*, one of the most frequently parasitized genera of cup fungi.
- **Stephanoma tetracoccum** — specifically associated with members of the Geoglossaceae (earth tongues).
- **Hypomyces papulasporae** and **Papulaspora** species — also found on Geoglossaceae, indicating this family as a hotspot for fungicolous interactions.
- **Sphaeronaemella helvellae** — colonizes *Gyromitra*, a genus containing some of the most toxic mushrooms known.
- **Hormiactis** species — recorded on both *Morchella* (morels) and *Peziza* (cup fungi), two economically and ecologically important genera.
- **Hypomyces leotiicola** and its anamorph **Sepedonium leotiarum** — specialize on *Leotia* (jelly babies).
- **Nectriopsis discophila** — found on *Lachnum*, a common genus of minute cup fungi on dead plant material.
- **Nectria sepultariae** and **Nectria discicola** — two distinct Nectria species associated with discomycete hosts.
- **Trichonectria albidopilosa** — characterized by its white hairy perithecia, growing on cup fungi.
- **Exochalara** species — recorded on *Bulgaria*, a gelatinous discomycete genus.

### Additional Discomycete Associates

- **Xenolachne** — parasitizes *Cudoniella* and *Disciniella*, replacing host tissue with its own fruiting structures.
- **Helicogonium** — an unusual genus that replaces the entire ascal hymenium of its host, effectively hijacking the reproductive machinery of the discomycete.
- **Cystobasidium** — a basidiomycetous yeast genus found on *Lasiobolus*, representing a cross-phylum fungicolous interaction.
- **Exidiopsis** — a basidiomycete genus recorded on *Mollisia*, another cross-phylum association on discomycete apothecia.
- **Clitocybe sclerotoidea** — a basidiomycete that colonizes *Helvella*, producing sclerotium-like structures on the host.

## Dung Fungi Interactions

Dung ecosystems support intense fungal competition where fungicolous interactions can be lethal:

- **Coprinus heptemerus** — exerts lethal hyphal interference against *Ascobolus* and *Pilobolus*, two common dung-inhabiting ascomycetes, effectively eliminating competitors through chemical warfare mediated by hyphal contact.
- **Penicillium glabrum** — has been investigated as a biocontrol agent in dung communities, capable of suppressing target fungi through antibiosis.

These interactions underscore the importance of interference competition in structuring dung fungal communities, where space and resources are severely limited. The dung habitat represents a natural laboratory for studying fungal antagonism, with succession proceeding rapidly and fungicolous interactions playing a key role in community assembly and turnover.

## Sequestrate Ascomycetes

Sequestrate (truffle-like) ascomycetes harbor distinctive fungicolous communities due to their subterranean, enclosed fruiting bodies:

- **Tuber** (true truffles) — colonized by **Acremonium**, **Verticillium**, and **Mortierella** species; **Nodulisporium tuberum** is named for its host genus.
- **Sphaerodes** and **Battarrina** — additional associates of truffle-like ascomycetes, with some species potentially influencing truffle quality and marketability.
- **Elaphomyces** (deer truffles) — serves as a substrate for **Cordyceps** species, representing one of the most iconic fungicolous associations and a potential model system for studying host specificity in hypocrealean parasites.

The enclosed, subterranean nature of sequestrate fruiting bodies creates a stable microenvironment that may facilitate specialized, long-term fungicolous relationships not commonly observed on exposed above-ground sporocarps.

## Coriaceous Discomycetes (Phacidiales and Rhytismatales)

The tough, leathery fruiting bodies of Phacidiales and Rhytismatales present unique challenges and opportunities for fungicolous fungi:

- **Sirotrema** — parasitizes *Lophodermium*, a cosmopolitan genus of needle endophytes and saprobes on conifers.
- **Hendersonia** — exhibits a competitive (rather than strictly parasitic) relationship with its coriaceous discomycete hosts.
- **Hemiphacidium** — another genus colonizing the fruiting bodies of Phacidiales.
- **Cosmospora ganymede** — found on members of this group, extending the known host range of the cosmopolitan genus *Cosmospora*.

## Stromata of Pyrenomycetes

Pyrenomycete stromata — the dense, carbonaceous or woody structures enclosing perithecia — are colonized by specialized fungicolous taxa:

- **Cosmospora** species — frequently recorded on Xylariaceae and Diatrypaceae, two of the most species-rich families of pyrenomycetes, representing a major host group for this hyperdiverse genus.
- **Capronia parasitica** — parasitizes *Diatrype* and *Hypoxylon*, entering through cracks in the stromatal surface and replacing host tissue.
- **Tubeufia cerea** — found on pyrenomycete stromata, adding to the ecological diversity of Tubeufiaceae.
- **Didymosphaeria** — grows inside the ascomata of pyrenomycetes, representing an endo-fungicolous lifestyle.
- **Ascodichaena** — itself a pyrenomycete that becomes parasitized by secondary fungicolous colonizers.
- **Chaetosphaerella** — specifically targets diatrypaceous fungi, demonstrating family-level host specificity.
- **Epicoccum** and **Coniothyrium** — recorded as biocontrol agents against pyrenomycete pathogens, illustrating the applied significance of fungicolous interactions.

## Soft Perithecia

Soft-textured perithecia of Nectriaceae and related fungi are readily colonized:

- **Nectria cinnabarina** and its anamorph **Tubercularia** — frequently overgrown by **Tympanosporium parasiticum**, a specialist hyperparasite.
- **Gabarnaudia** — another fungicolous genus targeting nectriaceous fungi.
- **Nitschkia parasitans** — as its epithet suggests, an obligate parasite on its fungal host.
- **Circinoniesslia** and **Valetoniella** — two genera specifically recorded on *Nectria* species, indicating convergent evolution of fungicolous lifestyles within diverse ascomycete lineages.
- **Nematogonum** — parasitizes *Neonectria*, a genus of major plant pathogens.
- **Sphaerodes** on **Hypomyces** represents a three-trophic-level interaction (fungus on fungus on fungus), illustrating the complexity of fungicolous food webs. Such multi-level parasitic chains are increasingly recognized as common in fungal communities but remain very difficult to study in situ.

## Anamorphic Ascomycota as Hosts

Anamorphic (asexual) ascomycetes serve as hosts for numerous fungicolous species, many with biocontrol relevance:

- **Calcarisporium** — grows on **Hirsutella**, a genus of entomopathogenic fungi, potentially disrupting biological control programs.
- **Debaryomyces** — a yeast genus recorded on **Penicillium** and **Rhizopus**, representing yeast–mold fungicolous interactions.
- **Sphaeronaemella** and **Trichoderma** — parasitize **Fusarium** and **Cochliobolus**, two major genera of plant pathogenic fungi.
- Biocontrol attempts targeting **Verticillium dahliae** — a devastating vascular wilt pathogen — have explored fungicolous fungi as biological control agents.
- **Hansfordia** (syn. **Dicyma pulvinata**) — hyperparasitizes **Cercospora** and **Passalora**, two important genera of leaf-spot pathogens; this interaction has been studied for biological control of Cercospora leaf diseases.
- **Penicillium funiculosum** — recorded as a fungicolous species with potential biocontrol applications.
- **Fusarium udum** — employs a diffusible toxin to suppress competing fungi, illustrating chemical interference competition.
- **Letendraea** and **Bisporella pallescens** — associated with **Bispora**, a dematiaceous hyphomycete common in damp environments.
- **Arachniotus** and **Aspergillus** — recorded on **Macrophomina**, a broad-host-range plant pathogen.
- **Acremonium sordidulum** — colonizes **Colletotrichum**, another major genus of anthracnose-causing plant pathogens.

The study of fungicolous fungi on anamorphic hosts is particularly relevant to applied mycology, as many plant pathogenic fungi have anamorphic stages and their suppression by fungicolous agents offers a promising avenue for sustainable disease management.

## Summary

Fungicolous fungi on ascomycete hosts represent an extraordinarily diverse ecological guild spanning all major fungal phyla. These interactions range from highly specific obligate parasitism to generalist saprotrophy on dead fungal tissue. Many of these associations have practical significance for plant pathology, biological control, and understanding fungal community dynamics. The vast majority of fungicolous species remain poorly studied, suggesting that the unknown biodiversity of fungicolous fungi is substantial.

The study of fungicolous fungi on ascomycete hosts is still in its infancy. Many of the associations documented here are known from only a handful of collections, and molecular systematic studies are urgently needed to confirm host specificity, resolve phylogenetic relationships, and uncover cryptic diversity. As sequencing technologies become more accessible, it is likely that the described fungicolous biodiversity will expand dramatically.
