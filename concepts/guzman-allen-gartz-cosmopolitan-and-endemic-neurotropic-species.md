# Cosmopolitan and Endemic Neurotropic Species

The [[global-distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] fungi spans the full range of biogeographic
patterns, from truly cosmopolitan species to highly localized endemics.
Guzmán, Allen and Gartz (1998/2000) applied Hongo's (1978) nine types of
fungal distribution to the neurotropic fungi, revealing patterns that
illuminate evolutionary history, dispersal mechanisms, and the influence of
human activity on fungal ranges.

## Hongo's Nine Distribution Types Applied

Hongo (1978) proposed nine distribution types for Japanese fungi that
Guzmán, Allen and Gartz found applicable worldwide: 1) Cosmopolitan, 2)
Northern hemisphere, 3) Eurasian, 4) North American and Eastern Asiatic,
5) Far Eastern, 6) Southeastern Asiatic, 7) Tropical and subtropical, 8)
Arctic and alpine, and 9) Endemic. Each type is represented among the
neurotropic fungi, though some patterns are more strongly expressed than
others.

## Cosmopolitan Species

[[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]] and almost all Panaeolus species are cosmopolitan.
P. foenisecii is common worldwide but poorly documented in some regions; it
is not recorded from Central America and the Caribbean, and in Japan was
reported only by Hongo (1986) despite being very common there. Claviceps
purpurea and allies are also broadly distributed in temperate regions.
The cosmopolitan distribution of these coprophilous and parasitic fungi
reflects the global distribution of their hosts (herbivorous mammals and
grasses, respectively).

mycorrhizal association with Pinus and Betula in northern forests, and has

Colombia, Tanzania, Australia, and New Zealand. This introduction pattern
demonstrates how human [[mycelium-running-forestry-practices-and-mushrooms]] can extend natural distribution
ranges of [[psilocybe-cubensis]], P. subcubensis, and Copelandia spp. are primarily
tropical and subtropical, with some exceptions. C. cyanescens sometimes
grows in disturbed zones of temperate regions. The tropical concentration
of psilocybin-containing species aligns with the broader pattern of higher
fungal diversity in tropical forests. Mexico, the Caribbean, Mesoamerica,
and Colombia show particularly high concentrations of tropical neurotropic
species, reflecting both genuine biodiversity and relatively intensive
collecting efforts.

## Arctic and Alpine Species

Psilocybe aztecorum var. aztecorum is exclusively subalpine and alpine,
growing in the high mountains of Mexico at elevations up to 4,000 m. It is
related to P. baeocystis from the Pacific Northwest and P. quebecensis from
Quebec, Canada, all belonging to Section Aztecorum. Panaeolus moellerianus
and P. olivaceus from the Faroe Islands represent northern species adapted
to cold, maritime environments.

## Endemic Species: Geographic Concentrations

Endemic neurotropic species are concentrated in specific regions. Mexico
has the highest number of endemics, including [[guzman-conocybe-siligineoides-lost-sacred-mushroom-oaxaca-never-recollected]],
Hypholoma naematoliformis, Psilocybe muliercula, P. chiapanensis, and P.
laurae. Colombia has P. columbiana, P. guatapensis, and P. pintonii. Brazil
has P. brasiliensis and P. paulensis. Spain has P. hispanica, Central Europe
has P. serbica, and Puerto Rico has P. portoricensis. Japan and India each
have endemic Panaeolina species described by Hongo and by Natarajan and
Raman, respectively.

## Amphiatlantic and Amphipacific Species

[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] has the widest temperate
distribution, known from Europe, India, Russia, Canada, USA, Chile, Peru,
New Zealand, Australia, and Tasmania, but is surprisingly absent from Mexico.
