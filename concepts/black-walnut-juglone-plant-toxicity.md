# Black Walnut Juglone Plant Toxicity

Black walnut trees (Juglans nigra) produce a naturally occurring chemical
compound called juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone) that is allelopathic,
meaning it inhibits the growth of certain other plants. Juglone is present
in all parts of the black walnut tree, including the roots, leaves, bark,
nut hulls, and wood, though the highest concentrations are found in the
roots and nut hulls.

## What Is Juglone

Juglone is a phytotoxic compound that acts as a defense mechanism for the
black walnut tree, reducing competition from neighboring plants. It is
released into the soil primarily through root exudation, though decomposing
leaves and nut hulls also contribute. Rainfall can carry juglone from leaves
and branches down to the soil surface, extending the affected zone.

The compound disrupts cellular respiration in sensitive plants by inhibiting
mitochondrial enzymes, particularly those involved in the electron transport
chain. This effectively starves sensitive plant cells of energy, leading to
wilting, yellowing, stunted growth, and eventually plant death.

## Symptoms of Juglone Toxicity

Plants sensitive to juglone exhibit a characteristic pattern of decline.
Symptoms typically develop gradually and may include:

- Yellowing of leaves (chlorosis), particularly between veins

- Wilting that is not explained by drought or disease

- Stunted growth and reduced vigor

- Eventual death of the plant if exposure is sustained

Symptoms often appear first on one side of the plant and may be confused with
other problems like fusarium wilt or verticillium wilt. The key diagnostic
clue is proximity to a black walnut tree or an area where one was recently
removed.

## Juglone-Sensitive Plants

Many common garden vegetables and ornamentals are highly sensitive to juglone.
These plants should not be grown within the root zone of a black walnut tree,
typically considered to extend to the drip line or about 50 feet from the
trunk.

The most sensitive garden vegetables include:

Tomato is among the most severely affected vegetables and serves as a
bioindicator plant. Even small amounts of juglone cause rapid wilting and
death. See [[tomato-growing-guide]].

Potato is also highly sensitive and will show stunting and yellowing.
See [[potato-growing-guide]].

Other highly sensitive vegetables include eggplant, pepper, cabbage,
broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, and kohlrabi. Members of the
Solanaceae (nightshade) and Brassicaceae (mustard) families are generally
the most affected.

## Juglone-Tolerant Plants

Several vegetables and ornamentals can be grown near black walnut trees
without significant adverse effects. These include:

Beans (lima and snap), beets, carrots, corn, melons, onions, parsnips,
radishes, squash, and sweet potatoes have shown good tolerance. Cherry
trees, black raspberry, and quince are among the tolerant fruit species.
See [[companion-planting-deterrents]] for companion planting strategies.

Among ornamentals, hostas, zinnias, begonias, and many native woodland
plants tolerate juglone well. Grass and many weeds are generally unaffected.

## Safe Planting Distance

The recommended minimum distance between a black walnut tree and sensitive
garden plants is 50 feet. However, the actual root zone can extend well
beyond the drip line of the tree, especially in older, well-established
specimens. The root zone of a mature black walnut may extend two to three
times the crown radius.

Critically, walnut roots remain active and continue to release juglone even
after the tree is cut down. Stumps and root systems can persist and remain
toxic for several years after removal. Simply cutting the tree does not
immediately make the area safe for sensitive plants.

## Soil and Environmental Factors

Juglone toxicity is influenced by several environmental factors:

Well-drained soils with good microbial activity tend to break down juglone
more quickly than poorly drained, compacted soils. Organic matter additions
can help speed decomposition. Sandy soils may show less toxicity than clay
soils because juglone moves through sand more readily, reducing
concentrations at the root zone.

Soil pH affects juglone availability. Under acidic conditions, juglone
persists longer in the soil. Liming to raise soil pH may help reduce the
toxicity but is not a reliable solution on its own.

## Mitigation Strategies

Several approaches can reduce juglone impact on gardens near black walnuts:

Raised beds with imported soil can provide a buffer zone, though roots from
the walnut tree may eventually penetrate raised beds if they are shallow.
Thick barriers of landscape fabric or plastic sheeting at the base of raised
beds can slow root intrusion.

Removing all black walnut debris, including leaves, nut hulls, and twigs,
from garden areas helps reduce the local juglone load. Do not use black
walnut leaves or hulls in compost intended for sensitive plants, as juglone
may persist through the composting process.

If removal of the tree is necessary, the entire root system should be
removed to the extent possible. Even then, waiting a year or more before
planting sensitive crops in the area is advisable.

Planting juglone-tolerant vegetables and ornamentals in affected areas is
the simplest and most reliable approach. This avoids the ongoing challenge
of managing toxicity in a living root zone.

## Black Walnut in Permaculture and Homesteading

Despite its allelopathic properties, black walnut has significant value.
The wood is highly prized for furniture and woodworking. Walnuts are a
nutritious food crop, and the trees provide valuable shade and wildlife
habitat. Juglone itself has documented antifungal and nematicidal
properties. See [[permaculture-walnut-guide]] and [[permaculture-designers-manual-plant-toxins-dryland]].
