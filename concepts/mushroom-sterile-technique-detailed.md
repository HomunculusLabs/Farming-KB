## Overview

Sterile technique is the set of practices used to prevent [[mushroom-contamination-identification]] during mushroom cultivation. Contamination by molds, bacteria, or competing fungi is the primary cause of cultivation failure. Proper sterile technique reduces contamination rates from potentially 50%+ for careless work down to 1-5% for experienced cultivators working with good equipment. This page covers the practical, hands-on techniques used at every stage of the cultivation process.

## Sources of Contamination

Understanding where contaminants come from helps you avoid them:

- **Airborne spores**: Mold spores (Trichoderma, Aspergillus, Penicillium) are ubiquitous indoors and outdoors. They settle on exposed surfaces constantly.
- **Your body**: Skin, hair, breath, and clothing carry bacteria and spores. A single sneeze can release millions of particles.
- **Surfaces**: Countertops, tools, jar lids, and work surfaces harbor microorganisms.
- **Water**: Tap water can contain bacteria and mold spores. Distilled or boiled water is preferred for sensitive work.
- **Substrates and materials**: Coco coir, grain, vermiculite, and other materials arrive with dormant organisms.

## The Still Air Box (SAB)

The still air box, also called a glove box or still air box (SAB), is the entry-level sterile workspace. It is a simple transparent container (usually a large plastic storage bin) with two arm holes cut in the sides.

### Building a SAB

1. Obtain a clear plastic storage bin (minimum 54-quart, 90-110 quart preferred).
2. Cut two 6-inch diameter holes on opposite long sides, positioned about 4-6 inches from the bottom.
3. Cover the hole edges with duct tape to smooth any rough plastic.
4. Wipe the interior with 70% isopropyl alcohol before each use.

### Using a SAB Effectively

The SAB works not by providing positive pressure sterile air (like a flow hood), but by creating a still-air environment where airborne particles settle out of suspension. Proper technique is critical:

- **Spray down the interior** with 70% isopropyl alcohol or a 10% bleach solution before working. This knocks down dust and spores.
- **Wait 15-20 minutes** after spraying before opening the SAB. This allows airborne particles to settle and the alcohol mist to dissipate (alcohol fumes can damage mycelium).
- **Wipe all items** that go into the SAB with alcohol before inserting them: jars, syringes, tools, gloves.
- **Move slowly and deliberately** inside the box. Fast movements create air currents that disturb settled particles.
- **Work in the center** of the box, away from the arm holes where unfiltered air enters.
- **Keep the SAB on a clean surface** at a comfortable working height.
- **Minimize time with lids off**. Plan your workflow before starting.
- **Do not put your head inside the box**. Your breath is a major contamination source.

### SAB vs. Flow Hood

A SAB is an excellent starting point and can achieve very low contamination rates with good technique. However, a laminar flow hood (see [[mushroom-laminarar-flow-hood]]) provides superior protection and much more comfortable working conditions. The choice depends on your budget and commitment level.

## Flame Sterilization

Flame sterilization is used to sterilize metal tools (scalpels, inoculation loops, needle tips) between uses. The intense heat of a flame kills all microorganisms on contact.

### Technique

1. Use a butane torch or alcohol lamp (not a lighter — lighters produce a sooty, uneven flame).
2. Pass the tool through the hottest part of the flame (the inner blue cone of a butane torch) until the metal glows red-hot.
3. Allow the tool to cool for 10-15 seconds before using it on cultures. A hot tool will kill mycelium on contact.
4. Keep the sterile tool within the sterile field (inside the SAB or in front of the flow hood) after flaming.
5. Re-flame between every transfer.

### Needle Sterilization

For [[mushroom-spore-syringe-preparation]] s, flame the needle tip until red-hot, then squirt a small amount of liquid through the needle to cool it. Wipe with an alcohol wipe between flame and use. The alcohol wipe is for cleaning the outside of the needle, not for sterilization — only the flame achieves true sterilization.

## Isopropyl Alcohol Use

Isopropyl alcohol is the primary surface disinfectant in mushroom cultivation.

### Concentration

- **70% isopropyl alcohol** is the standard. It is more effective than 91% or 99% because the water content allows it to penetrate cell walls. Higher concentrations evaporate too quickly and do not penetrate as effectively.
- Use in spray bottles for surface disinfection.
- Use alcohol-soaked paper towels for wiping jar lids, tools, and work surfaces.

### Limitations

- Alcohol disinfects surfaces but does not sterilize them. It kills bacteria and some spores on contact but has no residual effect.
- Alcohol does NOT penetrate substrates or materials. Soaking grain in alcohol does not sterilize it.
- Alcohol fumes can damage mycelium. Allow surfaces to dry before introducing cultures.
- Alcohol does not kill bacterial endospores — only pressure cooking achieves this.

## Sterile Workflow: Inoculating Grain Jars

This is the most common sterile procedure for beginners:

1. **Preparation (30 minutes before)**: Clean the SAB interior with alcohol spray. Arrange all items inside: grain jars (with filtered lids), spore syringe or LC, lighter, alcohol wipes. Close the SAB.
2. **Wait**: Allow 15-20 minutes for particles to settle.
3. **Personal preparation**: Wash hands thoroughly with antibacterial soap. Wear clean clothes. Consider wearing a face mask.
4. **Enter the SAB**: Insert hands through the arm holes. Wipe gloved hands with alcohol.
5. **Flame the needle**: Remove the syringe cap, flame the needle until red-hot. Squirt a tiny amount of liquid to cool the needle.
6. **Wipe jar injection ports**: Wipe the self-healing injection port (or tape-covered hole) on each jar with an alcohol wipe.
7. **Inoculate**: Insert the needle through the injection port at an angle (pointing toward the glass). Inject 1-2 cc of spore solution or 5-10 cc of liquid culture. Distribute the liquid along the inner glass surface for maximum spread.
8. **Flame between jars**: Re-flame the needle between each jar.
9. **Exit and store**: Remove hands, seal the SAB. Store jars at 70-78F in a clean location.

## Sterile Workflow: Agar Transfers

1. Prepare the SAB or flow hood as above.
2. Arrange: donor agar plate, receiving plate(s), flame-sterilized scalpel, alcohol wipes.
3. Flame the scalpel until red-hot, allow to cool.
4. Open the donor plate lid just enough to access the mycelium. Cut a small wedge (roughly 5mm x 5mm) from the leading edge of the mycelium.
5. Transfer the wedge to the receiving plate, placing it mycelium-side down on the fresh agar surface. Position it near the edge for directional growth.
6. Close both plates immediately.
7. Wrap plate edges with micropore tape or parafilm to prevent contamination while allowing gas exchange.
8. Re-flame scalpel between each transfer.

## Clean Room Practices

For growers working at scale or with a flow hood, additional clean room practices reduce contamination risk:

- **HEPA air purifier**: Run a standalone HEPA purifier in the room for at least 30 minutes before working.
- **Close windows and doors**: Minimize air currents and outside air introduction.
- **No fans or HVAC running**: Air currents disturb laminar flow and stir up settled particles.
- **Mop floors**: Wet-mop the room before working.
- **Shower and wear clean clothes**: Reduces personal contamination load.
- **Reduce clutter**: Fewer surfaces means fewer places for contaminants to hide.

## Common Mistakes

- Working too fast — slow down and be deliberate.
- Touching the inside of jars or lids with unsterilized tools or hands.
- Removing jar lids entirely instead of cracking them open minimally.
- Talking, breathing, or coughing over open cultures.
- Reusing tools without re-flaming.
- Skipping the alcohol wipe on jar surfaces before opening.
- Not shaking spore syringes before use (spores settle to the bottom).
