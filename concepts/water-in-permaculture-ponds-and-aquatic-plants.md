## Water in Permaculture — Ponds and Aquatic Plants

Water is the foundation of productive [[permaculture]] systems. Understanding the global water cycle, designing effective water storage, and managing productive pond systems are essential skills. Permaculture treats ponds not merely as cattle watering points but as highly productive integrated systems yielding food, fuel, fertilizer, and ecological services simultaneously.

## Global Water Context

The vast majority of Earth's water is unavailable for human use: it resides in oceans and ice caps. Of fresh water, roughly 75% is locked away. Only tiny fractions exist in lakes, ponds, soils, rivers, and the atmosphere — and less than 1% of all water is actively moving through the cycle.

The world mean average rainfall is 33.8 inches annually. Of atmospheric water, 77% falls on oceans and only 23% on land. Of that land rainfall, 16 parts are lost to transpiration and evaporation, leaving only 7 parts to run off to the ocean. The land also receives 7 parts of its water from horizontal advection — water vapor movement that forests intercept and precipitate.

The practical applications for [[bill-mollison]] are clear: establish surface storage and soil storage of water at greater elevations than usual, reduce runoff through [[food-forest-establishment]], and construct swales throughout urban and rural landscapes.

## Water Storage Principles

### The Critical Importance of Water in Establishment

It is pointless to recommend plants or design orchards without first securing the water supply. Plants must be able to access water at least twice during summer in their establishment phase. Water storage must be designed into every site — either from off-site sources or through on-site capture — to carry plants through establishment.

Seaweed and seaweed concentrates assist water storage by working as gel in soils. In very dry soils, dried seaweed, powders, and concentrates greatly improve water retention and plant resistance to wilt. They work on the soil surface film itself.

### Swales and Soil Storage

Water can be stored in soils treated with the Wallace plow and by constructing swales. Swales are particularly appropriate throughout urban areas. The goal is to reduce runoff and maximize infiltration, storing water where plants can access it.

## Pond Design and Construction

### Ideal Pond Structure

The ideal pond has:

- **Sloping or stepped floor**: Allows complete drainage
- **Drainage capability**: Preferably draining into a second pond for nutrient transfer
- **Gley layer**: A fermented organic seal that prevents seepage into sandy or porous soils
- **Lime treatment**: The base of new ponds should be limed; most pond organisms prefer pH 6-8, which is 100-200 times less acidic than most [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]]

### Gleying Ponds

Gleying is the process of creating an impermeable biological seal at the bottom of a pond. Once established, the gley penetrates the soil deeply and perpetuates itself. The fermenting process must be initiated but does not need continuous maintenance. A pond sitting on a sand dune should not be disturbed after gleying.

Ponds can also be deliberately drained by punching through the gley layer at sufficient depth. Even perfectly stable lagoons can be drained this way by digging post holes through the seal.

### Pond Cycling

Intensively cultivated ponds should cycle between aquatic and dryland phases:

1. **Aquatic phase**: Several years of pond production
2. **Drainage**: Transfer animals and water to a forage pond
3. **Dryland phase**: 3-4 years of terrestrial crops without additional manuring
4. **Re-flooding**: Roll down residues and reflood

The pond mud and mud surface have fantastic ability to fix passing nutrients from water. Diatoms (invisible) and freshwater mussels are key agents — mussels filter approximately 200 gallons of water per day per individual, pumping nitrogen and phosphorus into the mud floor. Mussels are the superior phosphorus fixer among all pond organisms and should only be harvested modestly for chicken grit.

### Pond Longevity

Shallow ponds made from loose fill material are most prone to obliteration through sediment accumulation. Well-constructed ponds on stable substrates can persist for millennia. Even commonly constructed ponds on appropriate sites will outlast their builders' lifetimes. When ponds do fill with detritus, the accumulated muck becomes excellent field fertilizer.

## Aquatic Plant Zones

### The Marginal Zone (Water's Edge)

Plants at the water's edge grow in soaked soils. This zone includes:

- **Bamboo**: Must not live more than 20 feet from water's edge but never in the water; sends feeder roots down to the water table
- **Poplar and willow**: Thrive where the water table is only 3-4 feet down
- **Swamp trees**: Produce some of the world's most durable and lightest woods; many have air cells throughout stems and root systems for oxygen storage
- **Mints**: Extremely productive (black mint can generate $70,000 from 2-3 acres through [[menthol]] distillation), but laterally invasive — contain with dense buffer bushes
- **Blueberries**: Pond edges are excellent blueberry habitat

### The Shallow Water Zone

Several feet from shore, in water 1-3 feet deep:

- **Arrowheads and duck potatoes** (*Sagittaria*): At least 80 species with wide climate range; important wildfowl food; can be treated like leeks
- **Wild rice**: Requires seed to be thrown into mud balls and placed in ponds; seeds have limited air storage life; grows in still lagoons and slow rivers at 18 inches to 3 feet depth
- **Cattails**: Among the most productive aquatic vegetables; keep in small annual garden ponds and cut tops to prevent seeding
- **Chinese water chestnuts**: Form large tubers in shallow water
- **Water lilies**: Best planted in bags of manure inside old tires (note: tires contain toxic cadmium — alternative containers should be used)
- **Lotus**: Some species have popcorn-like seeds that can be gathered and popped

### The Deep Water Zone

Beyond 6-9 feet depth:

- **Duckweed and water fern** (*[[azolla]]*): Continue production on the surface at depths where other plants cannot root
- **Water hyacinth**: Can be used in restricted locations to clear pollution; dangerous in large slow rivers in warm climates
- **Algae**: Production practically ceases below 12 feet deep; the primary purpose of deeper water (12-15 feet) is to allow fish to escape low oxygen and temperature extremes

### The Bog Zone

Rather than always thinking in terms of ponds, designers should consider creating bogs. A hundred square yards of bog can support specialized crops like:

- **Thatch** (*Typha*): Superb thatching material lasting 40-60 years, rivaling or exceeding slate and turf roofs
- **Cattail distillation**: The plant can be processed for oils

### Intertidal and Seasonal Zones

Between water levels, plants that die down seasonally (either in summer or winter depending on species) persist as rhizomata or tussock systems. Fly-catching plants like Venus fly traps, pitcher plants, and sundews occupy the very wet margin, capturing tiny insects from the air.

### Floating and Submerged Species

- **Water hyacinth**: Useful for pollution cleanup in contained systems
- **Papyrus**: Grows in the reed zone, excellent for paper and fodder
- **Reeds**: Used for mats and thatching; varied species serve different purposes

## Pond Productivity Assessment

The Secchi disk test measures pond fertility through water clarity:

- **Disk disappears at 2.5 feet** (soft green water): Well-manured, productive pond
- **Disk blacks out at 1 foot**: Over-manured — reduce nutrient input
- **Disk visible at 5 feet**: Under-manured — add chicken manure or other nutrients

For production of shrimp, prawns, yabbies, and fish, a well-manured pond with soft green bloom is ideal.

## Integrated Pond Animals

### Mussels

Freshwater mussels are the most important decomposers in the pond ecosystem. They filter 200 gallons per day, fixing nitrogen and phosphorus into the mud floor. They lower phosphorus throughout the whole system and should be preserved, harvesting only modestly.

### Crawfish and Crayfish

Crawfish are fantastic inland resources that thrive in ponds about 3 feet deep with brush piles for shelter. Different species have different burrowing behaviors:

- **Restricted burrowers**: Prefer beer cans as habitat; can be trapped by attaching cans to corks and drawing them when crawfish enter
- **Deep burrowers** (like the yabbie): May tunnel 25 feet and emerge on the wrong side of dam walls

A Scottish biologist estimated that 30 quarter-acre ponds in marine culture could sustain a family with $20,000-$30,000 annual income (1981 dollars). In Australia, hundreds of acres of previously non-productive flat land are now under crawfish aquaculture.

### Fish Selection

- **Catfish**: Ideal pond fish — very low on the trophic chain, good eating, undemanding
- **Trout**: Prefer extensive (large, unmanaged) ponds; intensive trout production requires fine adjustments and is considered a nuisance
- **Brown trout**: Lack the gill rakers that allow rainbow trout to collect algae and zooplankton

### Ducks

Approximately eight ducks per [[fukuoka-agriculture-tomorrow-quarter-acre-vision]] is a workable [[savory-stock-density-vs-stocking-rate]]. Ducks provide:

- Manure input to the pond
- Eggs as an additional crop
- Energy turnover in the pond system
- Synergy with wild rice (which grows above duck height during [[cannabis-vegetative-stage]])
