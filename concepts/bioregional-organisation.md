# Bioregional Organisation

## Overview

A bioregional association is an association of the residents of a natural and identifiable region. The region may be defined by a watershed, remnant or existing tribal or language boundaries, town boundaries, suburban streets, districts, or some combination of these factors. Many people identify with their local region or neighbourhood and intuitively know its boundaries. The acid test of a bioregion is that it is recognised as such by its inhabitants.

## Balancing Local and Global

There is an inherent tension between the need to live in a region in a responsible way (bioregional centrality) and the need to integrate with other people in other places (global outreach). The permaculture approach advocates not only "thinking globally and acting locally" but "acting and thinking globally and locally."

The region serves as the home address --- the place where culture develops and bioregional networks form. Through global associations and families of common interest, people cross regional, state, and national borders to establish multicultural alliances. Just as bioregions need periodic federal congresses, they also occasionally need global congresses. Societies and families need global meetings to break down the idea of defended regional boundaries to humanity.

## Historical Precedent

Tribal maps often defined bioregions very well. Totems and "skins" (clan groups) of tribes might take as their totemic mothers a particular tree or animal, which was itself limited in distribution by the sum of topographic and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-climatic-factors-in-design]]. Other groups occupied ecologies of grasslands, stony deserts, swamps, or mountain ridges. Today, minority language groups such as the Saamen, Basque, and Pitjatjantjara claim territories that are ancient and specific to their life mode.

## Optimal Scale

Ideally, a bioregion can be limited to that occupied by 7,000 to 40,000 people. Of these, perhaps only a hundred will be initially interested in any regional association, and even fewer will be active in it. The work of the bioregional group is to assess the natural, technical, service, and financial resources of the region, and to identify areas where leakage of resources --- water, soil, money, and talent --- leaves the region. This assessment quickly points the way to local self-reliance strategies.

## Core Functions

People can be called on to write accounts of their specialties as they apply to the region, and regional news sheets can publish results as they become available. Once areas of action have been defined, regional groups can be formed into associations dealing with specific areas:

- **Food**: Consumer-producer associations, gardening or soil societies
- **Shelter**: Owner-builder associations
- **Energy**: [[appropriate-technology]] associations
- **Finance**: An "earthbank" association

Additional associations can form around crafts, music, markets, livestock, nature study, and any other shared interest.

## The Regional Office

The bioregional office is a complex coordination hub requiring 4--6 people to act as consultants and coordinators, with others on call when needed. All other associations can use the office for registration, address, phone, and newsletter services, paying a fee for usage.

### Key Services

- **Land access centre**: Operating strategies for connecting people with land
- **Leasehold and title register**: Servicing agreements for clubs and societies
- **Community self-funding schemes**: Collecting monies for trusts and societies
- **Contact centre**: Serving as a trade and coordination link to other regions
- **Educational hub**: Inviting craftspeople or lecturers to address interest groups locally

Having one regional office makes it very easy for any resident or visitor to contact all services and associations available in the region, and greatly reduces communication costs for all groups. An accountant on call can readily contract to service many groups. The regional group can also share income from educational enterprises by inviting visiting experts.

## Cultural Exchange

Intermarriage, visits, mutual trade and aid, skills exchange, and educational exchange between regions of very different cultures enriches both parties. This is the antithesis of "integration" --- bureaucratic genocide promulgated by majority groups who disallow language use and cultural life to minorities. Reciprocal education values both sets of knowledge and world concepts and respects others' lifestyles.

## Resource Index for Bioregions

A comprehensive resource index compiled for bioregional use covers nine primary categories:

1. **A. Food and food support systems** --- Plant and animal resources, pest management, food processing, markets, support functions
2. **B. Shelter and buildings** --- Construction materials, energy systems, waste and recycling
3. **C. Livelihoods and support services** --- [[community-finance-and-alternative-economies]], skills banks, manufacturing services
4. **D. Information, media, communication, and research** --- Regional radio, newspapers, computer services, libraries, maps
5. **E. Community and security** --- House and livestock security, emergency services, communications
6. **F. Social life** --- Introductory services, think tanks, expeditions, work groups
7. **G. Health services** --- Medical services, ecological midwifery, morbidity assessment
8. **H. Future trends and potential** --- Threats and opportunities for the region
9. **I. Transport services**

Each section can contain case histories of successful strategies. The criteria for inclusion are: practical resources (people, skills, machinery, services, biological products) essential to the functioning of a small region, and assisting the conservation of resources, regional cash flow, the survival of settlement, employment, and community security.

## Governance Principles

[[permaculture-ethics-and-principles]] of self-governance, interdependence, and [[voluntary-simplicity]] or restriction of human numbers on earth apply at both regional and global outreach levels. The bioregional model represents a fundamentally different approach to governance --- one that is decentralised, ecologically grounded, and based on voluntary association rather than coercive [[holmgren-bioregional-governance-and-political-structures]].

## Mechanisms and Processes

The systems involved in bioregional organisation operate through interconnected biological and ecological pathways. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for effective implementation and optimization of related practices.

## Practical Applications

Practical implementation of bioregional organisation varies by context, scale, and local conditions. Key considerations include site assessment, resource availability, and integration with existing systems.

## Research and Evidence

Contemporary research continues to explore the underlying principles and effectiveness of bioregional organisation. Studies range from controlled laboratory experiments to long-term field observations, contributing to an evolving understanding of best practices.

## Limitations and Considerations

Several limitations and considerations apply to bioregional organisation. These include environmental constraints, scalability challenges, and potential interactions with other systems. Practitioners should evaluate local conditions and adapt approaches accordingly.

## Future Directions

Emerging research and technological advances continue to shape the understanding and application of bioregional organisation. Areas of active investigation include improved methodologies, integration with complementary approaches, and long-term sustainability assessments.

## Key Principles

The core principles underlying bioregional organisation emphasize working with natural systems rather than against them. Understanding these foundational concepts enables practitioners to adapt techniques to diverse conditions while maintaining ecological integrity and long-term productivity.

## Comparative Analysis

Comparative evaluation of bioregional organisation alongside alternative approaches reveals distinct advantages and trade-offs. Factors such as resource efficiency, scalability, environmental impact, and practical feasibility all inform the selection of appropriate methods for specific contexts.

## Integration Strategies

Effective integration of bioregional organisation requires careful planning and adaptive management. Successful approaches combine theoretical knowledge with hands-on observation and iterative refinement based on site-specific feedback.

The application of bioregional organisation requires site-specific adaptation and ongoing monitoring to ensure optimal results.
Observation and iterative adjustment remain central to successful implementation across diverse environments.
