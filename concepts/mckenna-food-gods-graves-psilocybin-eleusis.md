# Graves Psilocybin at Eleusis Theory in Food of the Gods

In *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna presents the poet and classical
scholar Robert Graves as a crucial but underappreciated figure in the
history of psychedelic scholarship. Graves advanced the controversial
thesis that the sacred potion consumed at the [[eleusinian-mysteries]]
was not, as most scholars have assumed, an ergot-based preparation, but
rather a psilocybin-containing mushroom. McKenna takes this possibility
seriously and argues that it deserves far more scholarly attention than
it has received.

## The Eleusinian Question

The Eleusinian Mysteries, conducted at Eleusis near Athens for nearly
two thousand years, were the most important religious initiation
ceremony of the ancient Greek world. Initiation involved the
consumption of a sacred potion called the *kykeon*, which produced
profound visionary experiences described by participants as life-
transforming. The precise identity of the psychoactive ingredient in
the kykeon has been debated for centuries, with the ergot hypothesis
proposed by Albert Hofmann, R. Gordon Wasson, and Carl Ruck being the
most widely discussed in modern scholarship.

McKenna acknowledges the strength of the ergot hypothesis but raises
the possibility that psilocybin mushrooms may have been the true
sacrament. He notes that if future research were to rule out ergot as
the active ingredient, then Graves's insistence on psilocybin mushrooms
would need to be taken very seriously.

## Graves's Argument for Psilocybin

Robert Graves, best known for *The White Goddess* and his
reconstructions of pre-Greek mythology, argued that the religious
traditions of the ancient Mediterranean were rooted in a mushroom cult
centered on the worship of a Great Goddess. He suggested that knowledge
of psychoactive mushrooms, particularly what McKenna calls the "Ur-plant
of the Goddess," *Stropharia cubensis*, survived from paleolithic and
neolithic times into the Minoan-Mycenaean civilization and was
maintained at Eleusis until the final destruction of the Mysteries by
Christian authorities in the late fourth century CE.

Graves's argument drew on several strands of evidence: the persistence
of mushroom imagery in Minoan art, the association of mushrooms with
divine beings in Greek mythology, the description of visionary
experiences in the accounts of initiates that are more consistent with
psilocybin than with the ergot alkaloids, and the broader pattern of
mushroom use in the ancient Near East that McKenna traces throughout
*Food of the Gods*.

## The Ur-Plant of the Goddess

Central to Graves's theory, as McKenna presents it, is the concept of
the "Ur-plant of the Goddess," a single psychoactive mushroom species
that served as the original sacrament of the Goddess-worshipping
partnership cultures of the ancient world. McKenna identifies this Ur-
plant as *Stropharia cubensis*, the same species he argues played the
central role in hominid evolution through his [[mckenna-food-gods-stoned-ape-hypothesis]].

The continuity from the African grasslands where McKenna places the
origin of the mushroom-religion to the temples of Minoan Crete and
finally to Eleusis represents, in this view, an unbroken tradition of
psychoactive mushroom use spanning tens of thousands of years. The
destruction of Eleusis thus marks not merely the end of one religious
institution but the final extinction of a tradition that McKenna
describes as "the great broad river of partnership, Goddess worship,
and hallucinogenic ecstasy that had flowed for over ten thousand years."

## Pindar and the Promise of Eleusis

McKenna quotes the Greek poet Pindar to convey the transformative
power attributed to the Eleusinian experience: "Happy is he who, having
seen these rites, goes below the hollow earth; for he knows the end of
life and he knows its god-sent beginning." This promise of knowledge of
both death and rebirth, of the cyclical nature of existence, is
precisely the kind of insight that psilocybin is known to produce, and
McKenna argues that it is more consistent with the effects of
psilocybin than with those of the ergot alkaloids.

## The Historical Watershed

Whether the Eleusinian sacrament was ergot or psilocybin, McKenna sees
the destruction of Eleusis as a decisive historical turning point. With
the eclipse of Minoan Crete and its Mysteries, humankind crossed what
McKenna calls a "watershed into the progressively more vacant, more
ego-dominated world, whose energies were coalescing into monotheism,
patriarchy, and male domination." The great society-shaping plant
relationships of the Old World declined to the status of "mysteries,"
esoteric pursuits available only to the wealthy, the obsessed, and
eventually, as McKenna dryly notes, cynical intelligence operatives.

The phonetic alphabet, which emerged during this same period, helped
move consciousness away from what McKenna calls "gestalt pictographic
awareness" toward a world emphasizing linear, analytical thought. These
developments reinforced the emergence of what he calls the "antivisionary
dominator style of culture," and "the dark night of the planetary soul
that we call Western civilization began."

## Graves's Broader Contribution

McKenna values Graves not only for the specific Eleusinian hypothesis
but for his broader insight that the mythological and religious
traditions of Europe preserve, in encoded form, the memory of a
mushroom-based religion that predates all of the historical
civilizations of the Mediterranean. This insight is central to
McKenna's own project in *Food of the Gods*, which seeks to recover
the history of humanity's relationship with psychoactive plants from
the fragments preserved in myth, art, and archaeology.
