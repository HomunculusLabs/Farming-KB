# Earth Constructs and Raised Banks

Wherever earth is dug, banks are raised. In [[permaculture-design]], earth constructs — banks, mounds, walls, and raised platforms — serve as multi-functional landscape elements that provide shelter, control water, create growing surfaces, and shape microclimates. Unlike conventional engineering that treats excavated material as waste ("spoil"), [[permaculture]] regards earth moved during construction as a resource to be carefully sorted, stockpiled, and redeployed for permanent beneficial ends.

## Principles of Earth Construction


The fundamental principle is that every excavation creates material for construction. When earth is moved, topsoil must first be removed and set aside, then returned to cover the spoil after construction is complete. This preserves the living soil layer and allows vegetation to establish rapidly on new earthworks.

## Eleven Functions of Raised Banks


### 1. Shelter for Houses and Fields

An earth crescent bowed into cold winds is the fastest way to create warmth and shelter on its lee side. Even a few blade sidecasts along a field boundary provide cover for hedgerow and windbreak species. High banks of 2.5–3.5 m (9–12 feet) behind house sites create instant shelter that can be reinforced with trees. This represents a long-term and considerable heat energy saving in cold climates, and provides shading and cooling wind direction in warm climates. On flatland sites, a single earthbank and the pond formed by its excavation can simultaneously address cold, hot, and cooling wind patterns.

### 2. Plant Sites for Windbreaks

Low sidecasts heap up topsoil, create shelter, and delay runoff. This reduces root competition for establishing windbreak species for the first year or two. In wetter ground, sidecast banks allow establishment of willows, poplars, and tamarisk above waterlogged conditions. Sidecasting is recommended for long windbreak sequences because it provides an elevated, well-drained planting position.

### 3. House and Barn Walls

Machines exist that can raise and compact a complete house or barn wall in a morning's work. Adding floor and roof (another two days' work) produces a long-term, fireproof, silent, and energy-conserving structure. This technique suits open-space situations, cheap barns, and large outbuildings. Almost any earth will work, provided the compacted rest angle is monitored. For sandy soils, wall corners must be bagged — stabilized with cement-filled sandbags.

### 4. Containments and Fire Protection

Earth walls can surround tanks containing inflammable fluids and provide effective protection against radiant heat from wildfire. Above-ground wildfire radiation refuges in bushland can be earth-covered, built off forested roads and near isolated bush houses. Earth is one of the most effective materials for fire protection due to its low thermal conductivity and high thermal mass.

### 5. Noise Deflectors and Absorbers

Traffic noise is effectively blocked from housing by earth walls. Plants growing on these banks further decrease noxious fume effects. Deflection is most effective for long-wavelength (low-frequency) noise; high-frequency noise may require additional treatment. Earth berms alongside highways represent a low-cost, low-maintenance noise mitigation strategy.

### 6. Tracks and Plant Sites in Marshes

Earth banks, islands, and mounds in marshes create multiple opportunities for access, tree planting, and structural placement. Mounds in ponds isolate useful but rampant species (thorny blackberries, runner bamboo), while banks allow foot or vehicle access to service duck nest boxes, harvest fruit, and attend to fish ponds. The best tool for raising banks in marshes is a light "swamp" tractor with wide tyres or tracks and a swivel bucket excavator. In very soft ground, timber matting (three wooden gratings) allows the machine to progressively extend its reach.

### 7. Wind and Water Deflection

Earth constructs can be designed to channel wind to energy systems (such as vertical-axis wind machines) or direct water to storages. Ridge wind tunnels for mill power, based on older Afghan designs, use walls or trenches on hillslopes to steer wind to mills as durable, low-maintenance systems.

### 8. Flood and Tide Control (Polders and Levees)

Well-maintained earth banks are the only practical protection for houses and villages in flood plains and below tide level. These are essentially reverse dams — inhabitants live inside and waters are contained outside. Mounds can serve as flood refuge islands in deltaic areas, containing storage barns and refuges. In fire-prone sites, villages can be made safe on lakes or moated islands. Causeways of earth provide access to low mounds or islands near shore. Bangladesh and other deltaic regions critically need wooded refuge mounds for human survival during floods.

### 9. Rail, Canal, and Road Grades

These common applications of earth banks and cuttings are well established in civil engineering. Permaculture adds the dimension of integrating productive plantings and water harvesting into every transportation earthwork.

### 10. Earth Ramps and Stands

Earth ramps serve multiple permanent functions: unloading trucks from the side or back, loading cattle at various levels, unloading hay into upper barn floors, and loading/unloading boats into water. Once built, a single loading/unloading ramp can serve an entire district. The loading face must be stabilized with stone, concrete, or beams.

### 11. "Ha-Ha" Fences

The ha-ha is a below-grade ditch acting as a fence, used in classical landscape gardens where uninterrupted views are desired, and in zoos for direct viewing of dangerous animals. It also serves as a stock barrier where wire fencing is unavailable but labor or machinery can be obtained. A ha-ha is essentially a deep pit (dry or wet) with one steep wall faced by stone, scaled to the species to be excluded.

## Road Enhancement


Roads represent an enormous missed opportunity in conventional design. Kilometers of black bitumen are laid without installing heat pump pipes that could heat the towns the roads pass through, or harvesting road runoff for local irrigation and wetlands. Roadsides can preserve bunchgrasses, sagebrushes, rangeland plants, and remnant forests, with pull-over areas displaying vegetation maps, geological features, and archaeological remains. This planning must precede construction. Underpasses and guide fences allow migrating wildlife to cross roadways without accidents.
