# Permaculture Zones and Sectors

[[permaculture-zones]] and sectors are the foundational spatial planning tools of [[permaculture-design]]. Zones organize elements by frequency of human use and maintenance needs. Sectors map external energies (sun, wind, fire, water, wildlife) flowing through a site. Together they enable efficient, resilient landscape design that minimizes energy input while maximizing yield.

## Zone Analysis

Zones are concentric rings radiating outward from the center of human activity (typically the home). They are numbered 0 through 5, with lower numbers indicating higher frequency of interaction.

### Zone 0 — The Home

The house or primary dwelling. This is the center of activity where people spend the most time. Includes:

- Indoor living spaces
- Kitchen and food preparation areas
- Indoor herb gardens, sprouting setups
- [[passive-solar-design]] elements
- Water harvesting from rooftops
- Composting toilet or worm bin in attached greenhouse

Zone 0 design focuses on energy efficiency, thermal comfort, and integrating growing systems into daily living spaces.

### Zone 1 — Intensive Garden

Immediately surrounding the home, visited daily. Contains high-maintenance, high-yield elements:

- Annual vegetable beds
- Salad gardens and culinary herbs
- Seedling nursery and propagation area
- Small greenhouse or [[cold-frame]]
- Compost bins (close for kitchen scrap delivery)
- Mushroom logs or small cultivation blocks
- intensively managed fruit bushes (blueberries, currants)
- Worm farm / vermicompost system

Elements here need daily attention: watering, harvesting, pest scouting. Soil is typically the most amended and fertile on the property.

### Zone 2 — Orchard and Food Forest

Visited every few days. Lower maintenance than Zone 1 but still actively managed:

- Fruit and nut trees (semi-dwarf and standard)
- Berry patches (raspberries, blackberries, strawberries)
- [[perennial-vegetables]] (asparagus, rhubarb, artichokes)
- Larger composting systems
- Poultry housing and forage areas
- Pond or small water feature
- Guild plantings around fruit trees
- Beehives

[[forest-garden-layers]] are fully expressed here, with canopy trees, understory, shrubs, herbs, ground covers, roots, and vines all working together.

### Zone 3 — Broadacre Cropping and Pasture

Visited weekly or seasonally. Main production zone for staple crops and livestock:

- Field crops (grains, beans, potatoes)
- Large orchards or vineyards
- Grazing pasture for goats, cattle, or sheep
- Large-scale [[permaculture-earthworks]] (swales, ponds, terraces)
- Windbreaks and shelterbelts
- Cover cropping and green manure rotations
- Fallow areas for soil restoration

This zone relies on perennial systems and self-maintaining designs wherever possible.

### Zone 4 — Managed Wild

Visited occasionally for foraging and observation. Semi-wild productive landscape:

- Foraging for wild mushrooms, nuts, berries
- Timber and firewood production
- Hunting and fishing zones
- Wildlife corridors
- Seed collection from wild plants
- Minimal intervention — observe and harvest only

Management is limited to selective harvesting, invasive species control, and maintaining access paths.

### Zone 5 — Unmanaged Wilderness

Left entirely to nature. No human intervention except observation:

- Provides habitat for wildlife
- Serves as a reference ecosystem for understanding natural patterns
- Protects watershed and old-growth forest
- Genetic reservoir for native species
- Spiritual and recreational value

Zone 5 teaches the designer how natural systems self-organize without human input.

## Sector Mapping

Sectors are directional energies that pass through a site regardless of zone placement. Mapping them reveals opportunities and hazards for design placement.

### Sun Sector

Track the sun's path across seasons:

- Summer solstice sun angle determines shade patterns
- Winter solstice sun angle reveals where passive solar gain is possible
- South-facing slopes (Northern Hemisphere) receive maximum light
- Deciduous trees can provide summer shade while allowing winter sun through

Key design response: Place greenhouses, solar panels, and cold-sensitive plants to capture maximum sun. Use deciduous trees to shade structures in summer.

### Wind Sector

Map prevailing winds and seasonal storm patterns:

- Cold winter winds from the north/northwest (Northern Hemisphere)
- Cooling summer breezes
- Desiccating hot winds
- Channeling winds through conifer windbreaks

Key design response: Plant dense evergreen windbreaks on the windward side of sensitive areas. Use wind as a design feature for ventilation, pollination, and drying.

### Fire Sector

Identify fire risk directions and create defensible space:

- Clear fuel breaks along high-risk edges
- Zone fire-resistant plantings (broadleaf deciduous, high moisture content)
- Position water storage upslope from vulnerable areas
- Access roads doubled as firebreaks

### Water Sector

Map all water flows onto the property:

- Natural drainage patterns and seasonal streams
- Rainfall distribution and intensity
- Flood risk areas
- Springs, seeps, and groundwater indicators
- Potential sites for ponds, swales, and catchment

Key design response: [[permaculture-earthworks]] such as swales and keyline systems capture, store, and slowly release water across the landscape. See [[keyline-design]] for detailed water management techniques.
