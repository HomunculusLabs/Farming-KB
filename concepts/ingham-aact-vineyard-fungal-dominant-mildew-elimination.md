## Why Fungal-Dominant Teas for Vineyards

Grape vines evolved in forest-edge ecosystems where fungal-dominated soils are the
norm. The vineyard environment, with its perennial woody rootstock and perennial
canopy, closely mirrors this natural habitat. Ingham consistently recommends that all
teas for vineyards should be as fungal as possible from the very beginning of the
season. While bacterial teas will help early on, fungal biomass becomes critical in
late season when mildew pressure peaks and protection is most needed.

## SARE Grant Results: Copper and Sulfur Reduction

The Western SARE-funded grant that Ingham collaborated on produced two seasons of
data at Paso Robles vineyard with grower Roger Hanson. The results were striking
and represent some of the most compelling evidence for compost tea efficacy in a
high-value crop setting:

- Chemical sprays were reduced from 10-14 per season down to a single application

## Interaction with Wine Fermentation Yeasts

A common concern among viticulturists is whether compost tea applications might
disrupt the wild yeasts involved in natural wine fermentation. Ingham reports no
negative effect from compost tea with respect to desirable fermentation yeasts. If
anything, the tea helps establish the historically normal set of organisms on the
grape skin surface, which is exactly what winemakers seeking terroir expression want.

Keeping the tea aerobic during brewing is critical for this reason: aerobic brewing
ensures that beneficial aerobic bacteria and fungi are placed onto the grape
surfaces rather than problem anaerobic bacteria that could interfere with
fermentation. While compost tea does contain some yeasts, aerobic brewing conditions
keep their numbers low and prevent anaerobic contamination.

## Pest Management Integration

Compost tea applications in vineyards also support [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]] species in properly brewed teas can be repugnant to insects,
reducing feeding by leafhoppers, whiteflies, and aphids on grape foliage. Some
beneficial bacterial species encourage the growth of beneficial fungi that occupy
the space disease fungi would otherwise use, providing a dual protective mechanism
against both disease and pest pressure. For vineyards using any pesticide
applications, Ingham recommends applying compost tea three days after each pesticide
treatment to help restore the beneficial biology that was damaged by the chemical.
