# Chicken Husbandry in Permaculture

Chickens are the most productive and manageable livestock for a [[permaculture-zones-system]]. The goal is to feed chickens from Zone 2 and profit from them in Zone 1. They provide eggs, meat, fertilizer, pest control, and soil cultivation — making them one of the most versatile elements in [[permaculture-animal-integration]].

## Zone Placement

- Chickens are located on the border between [[permaculture-climatic-zones-design]] and Zone 2
- They can run in the garden at certain times of year
- Do NOT let them into mulched gardens or orchards — they will scratch up mulch and eat crops
- In winter, chickens can be allowed into harvested garden beds to clean up and fertilise
- Coop with attached pen along the border of Zone 1 gardens and Zone 2 orchard-management|orchard
- A second pen for chicks should be surrounded by spiny shrubs for hawk protection
- Fence should be at least 5 feet high
- 50–100 chickens per acre can be sustained in an unmulched orchard; 300 per acre on dedicated forage pasture as a cash crop

## Chicken Tractor System

A chicken tractor is a movable coop without a floor:

- Allows chickens to forage on fresh ground daily
- Provides natural fertilisation of soil
- Controls insects and weeds in the tractor's path
- Can be moved across fallow beds for soil preparation
- Protects chickens from predators while allowing access to pasture
- Each chicken needs a minimum of 3 sq ft including pen space
- Floor is open so birds eat directly off the ground and manure falls through

### Tractor Designs

- **A-frame**: Triangular house with pen enclosed in chicken wire — simple and portable
- **Rectangular pen**: House occupies 1/3 of space with a square cutout for the run
- **Wheeled coops**: Wheels set very low to the ground, extra wood barrier around bottom edge
- **Large mobile coops**: On wheels with removable ramp, no attached pen — best for [[permaculture-community-scale-design]]

## Feeding

- Kitchen scraps reduce feed costs significantly
- Chickens eat insects, seeds, greens, and small vertebrates
- Grain supplement needed when forage is insufficient
- duck-keeping|Ducks and chickens should be kept separately — ducks are messier
- Oyster shells or crushed eggshells provide calcium for strong eggshells
- 25 light-breed hens eat roughly 1/4 lb of food per day; heavy breeds require more
- Cottage cheese can supplement protein
- Without grit, chickens will eat their own eggs — always provide gravel, sand, or pebbles in a container

### Chicken Forage Plants

Pasture planting for chickens should include pigeon pea, berries, fruits, acacias, clovers, grasses, chicory, comfrey, and dandelions. Insects from manure piles and mulches supplement diet. You can grow your own crickets as additional protein.

**Comprehensive chicken plant list**: Alfalfa, Amaranth, Autumn olive, Barley, Buckwheat, Chickweed, Chicory, Clover, [[comfrey-multifunctional-guild-plant]], Corn, Cucumber, Currant, Dandelion, Elderberry, Fava beans, Fennel, Fruit/nut trees, Honey locust, Oak, Oats, Quinoa, Russian olive, Rye, Siberian pea shrub, Stinging nettle, Sunflower, Swiss chard, Vetch, Wheat.

## Housing

- Minimum 4 sq ft per bird in the coop, 10 sq ft in the run
- Roosts should be 2 feet off the ground, 18 inches from wall, low enough for chickens to fly up to
- Nesting boxes: 1 per 3-4 hens, 12x12 inches, attached outside coop with lid, 18 inches from ground
- Ventilation is critical — more chickens die from poor ventilation than cold
- Predator protection: wire mesh buried 12 inches underground, secure latches
- Chicken door should be 12 inches high in the roof peak for ventilation and predator deterrence
- Ladder should be 5 feet wide with rungs 3 feet apart (difficult for foxes to climb)
- Coop can be raised on stilts so manure drops through floor slats into a swale or wheelbarrow
- Cold climate: add extra insulation and hay bedding
- A 7x7 ft coop (49 sq ft) comfortably houses 16 chickens

## Egg Production

- Heritage breeds: 150-250 eggs per year
- Commercial hybrids: 280-320 eggs per year
- Peak production in first 2 years, declining after
- Hens need 14-16 hours of light for peak laying
- Hens molt once per year (typically fall), triggered by decreasing sunlight — they stop laying during molt
- A light on a timer can maintain production through winter
- Summer molting signals stress: food/water shortage, disease, cold, or lighting changes
- [[passive-solar-and-biomass-energy]] lighting can extend winter production
- Typical yield: ~300 eggs per year per hen

## Breed Selection

- **Light breeds**: Fly short distances, not good mothers, excellent foragers, often don't need supplementary food
- **Heavy breeds**: Don't fly, better mothers, lay brown eggs, hardier, lay for more years
- **Orpington**: Popular dual-purpose breed, laid-back temperament, good for beginners
- Heritage breeds are more likely to go broody for natural breeding

## Health and Maintenance

- Disinfect coop thoroughly before introducing new chickens
- Clean bedding weekly; disinfect annually
- Fresh water daily — 1 gallon per 50 chicks at room temperature
- Separate old birds from young birds
- Provide crushed shells daily for calcium
- Grit in the diet helps prevent worms
- Adequate space and nutrition prevents cannibalism
- Vitamin D supplementation (cod liver oil) is important in northern winters
- Check for predators regularly and reinforce weak points in fencing

## Breeding and Raising Chicks

A rooster is needed for breeding (note: roosters are typically not allowed in city limits). Heritage breeds are more likely to go broody. To encourage a hen to set:

1. Build a private nest: 15x15x15 inch wood box with roof on ground, filled with straw
2. Place a dummy egg to trigger brooding behavior
3. Once the hen is warm and laying daily, she will begin setting — do not disturb her
4. After 21 days, chicks will hatch
5. Remove hatched chicks to a brooder box until all have hatched (a hen will be torn between sitting and caring for chicks)

### Brooder Setup

- Box with red heat lamp and thermometer, starting at ~95°F
- Decrease temperature 5°F per week until reaching 70°F at 6 weeks
- Bedding: burlap or cloth rags for the first week, then newspaper, hay, or wood shavings
- Food: egg carton at eye level for the first week, then a proper container
- Small grit mixed on feed from the start
- 1 inch feeding space per chick until 30 days, then 3 inches
- Transfer to chick house at 4 weeks, remove heat box at 6 weeks
- Integrate with the main flock at 10–12 weeks (pullets)

## Integration with Other Systems

- Chicken manure is high nitrogen — valuable for [[faires-composting-and-vermiculture]]
- Chickens process food scraps that would otherwise be wasted
- Feathers contribute nitrogen when composted
- Chickens control pest insects in orchards (when supervised)
- Used in [[permaculture-designers-manual-chicken-tractors]] for garden bed preparation
- Wire mesh around fruit trees protects roots from scratching
- Garden waste can be thrown over the fence into the chicken run
- Runs can be divided into pens planted in succession for rotational grazing
- Each pen can include a rotting log that can be flipped to reveal pill bugs and worms
