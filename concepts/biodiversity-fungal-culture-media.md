# Fungal Culture Media

Culture media are the foundational tools for isolating, cultivating, and
identifying fungi from environmental samples. The choice of medium profoundly
influences which species are recovered, making media selection critical in
any fungal biodiversity assessment. No
single medium recovers the full spectrum of fungal diversity, so multiple
media are typically used in combination.

## General-Purpose Media

Several standard media are widely used for routine fungal isolation and
subculturing. These are often described as "nonselective," though in reality
they impose strong nutritional biases:

- **Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA):** Among the most commonly used fungal media.
  Made from potato infusion and dextrose, PDA supports robust growth of a
  wide range of fungi. It is frequently employed for subculturing, maintaining
  stock cultures, and characterizing isolates. PDA can be amended with
  antibiotics or adjusted in pH for selective purposes.

- **Malt Extract Agar (MEA):** Prepared at 1 to 2% concentration, MEA is the
  most commonly used medium for primary isolation of endophytic fungi and for subculturing. It is sometimes
  combined with yeast extract (0.1 to 0.2%) to enhance growth of nutritionally
  demanding species. A mixture of PDA and MEA has been recommended to broaden
  the range of fungi recovered.

- **Czapek Agar:** A synthetic medium containing sodium nitrate as a nitrogen
  source. Czapek agar with yeast and malt extracts (Czapek-Yeast Extract
  Agar, CYA) is widely used in taxonomic studies, particularly for
  [[biodiversity-ascomycete-diversity-taxonomy]] such as
  *Penicillium* and *Aspergillus* species.

- **Oatmeal Agar and Cornmeal Agar:** Both promote sporulation in many
  groups, including coelomycetes, dematiaceous hyphomycetes, and
  [[biodiversity-zygomycota-diversity]].

- **Water Agar (WA):** A minimal medium used where low nutrient levels
  reduce contamination, though colonies may be diffuse and harder to
  recognize on weak media.

## Selective Media and Antimicrobial Additives

Selective media incorporate chemical agents that suppress certain groups of
microorganisms while permitting the growth of target fungi. This is essential
for recovering slow-growing or rare species that would otherwise be
overgrown:

- **Cycloheximide (Actidione):** Extremely toxic to most filamentous fungi
  at concentrations of 0.25 to 0.5 g/L. Used to isolate oomycetes selectively
  or to restrict growth of common molds. MEA or PDA amended with 200 ppm
  cycloheximide is a standard formulation.

- **Antibacterial Antibiotics:** Streptomycin and chlortetracycline (50 ppm
  each) are commonly added to suppress bacterial growth during primary
  isolations. These are particularly important when isolating from soil or
  plant tissues with high bacterial loads.

- **Benomyl:** Toxic toward most ascomycetous fungi but less so toward
  [[biodiversity-basidiomycete-diversity-taxonomy]],
  Pleosporales, and Mucorales. Incorporated into media for selective
  isolation of basidiomycetes from soil.

- **Pimaricin (Natamycin):** An antifungal polyene antibiotic toxic to
  most true fungi. Used in formulations such as P10VP for selective isolation
  of filamentous oomycetes including *Aphanomyces*, *Phytophthora*, and
  *Pythium*.

- **Dichloran-Rose Bengal-Chloramphenicol Agar (DRBC):** Combines colony-
  restricting agents with antibacterial antibiotics. Recommended for general
  inventories of saprobic soil fungi to increase detection of slow-growing
  species.

- **Cyclosporin A:** Used at 2 to 10 ppm to inhibit fast-growing
  filamentous fungi, enabling detection of slower species. Malt
  cyclosporin medium is one such formulation.

## Media for Specific Applications

Different ecological contexts and fungal groups require specialized media:

- **Endophyte Isolation:** Malt extract agar (1 to 2%) with or without yeast
  extract is used most commonly. Weak media reduce contamination by
  epiphytic fungi. Bills and Polishook found that 1% malt extract plus 0.2%
  yeast extract with antibiotics gave the highest species richness for
  endophyte isolations from twigs and leaves.

- **Soil Fungi:** Low-nutrient media such as soil extract-rose bengal medium
  or carboxymethyl cellulose medium simulate oligotrophic soil conditions.
  These are recommended for general inventories of saprobic soil fungi.

- **Thermophilic Fungi:** Incubation of plates at temperatures above 40
  degrees Celsius on suitable media reveals thermotolerant and thermophilic
  species that would be missed at standard incubation temperatures.

- **Psychrophilic Fungi:** Cool incubation (0 to 10 degrees Celsius) recovers
  cold-adapted species, including terrestrial aquatic hyphomycetes.

- **Sporulation Promotion:** Cornmeal agar, oatmeal agar, V8-juice agar,
  SNA, and Weitzman-Silva-Hutner agar induce sporulation in sterile
  isolates. Extended incubation at 5 to 10 degrees Celsius may be needed
  for some species.

## pH Adjustment and Media Optimization

Adjusting the acidity of culture media can serve both selective and
diagnostic purposes. Acidified PDA (APDA, pH 4) has been used to isolate
specific mycoparasites. Some *Penicillium* species are identified by pH
shifts in media containing creatine and sucrose. pH adjustments should be
made before autoclaving, and the effects of pH on both target organisms and
contaminants should be considered.

## Practical Considerations

The description of any fungal community using culture-based methods will be
incomplete, even with a range of nutrient media and incubation regimens.
Investigators are encouraged to experiment with several media and incubation
conditions before initiating detailed biodiversity surveys. Media that
appear similar may yield very different species lists, and the effects of
light, temperature, and incubation duration on medium performance should be
evaluated. Sealing plates with Parafilm prevents desiccation but may inhibit
sporulation, while slow desiccation often promotes sporulation in
coelomycetes.
