# War as Vector of Addiction: [[morphine]], Soldiers, and the Drug Trade

One of the most striking patterns identifiedterence-mckenna-[[tryptamine]]-hypothesisenna in *Food of the Gods* is
the recurring role of warfare as a mechanism for spreading drug addiction through
populations. McKenna argues that war functions as a "vector of addiction" — a biological
metaphor that captures the way military conflict systematically introduces addictive
substances to new populations, creating dependent users who then sustain demand long after
the conflict has ended. This pattern recurs from the American Civil War through the Opium
Wars to the World Wars of the twentieth century.

## The Hypodermic Syringe and the Civil War

1853. Before this invention, physicians had used the hollow stems of lilac plants to
introduce drugs inside the body. Wood's innovation made possible the intravenous injection
of morphine, transforming it from an oral medicine into a drug capable of producing

iatrogenically induced by the medical establishment itself.

## The Pattern Repeats Across Conflicts

McKenna shows that this pattern is not an isolated incident but a recurring structural
feature of the relationship between warfare and drugs. Each major conflict creates
conditions that favor the spread of addictive substances: the need to treat wounds and
pain, the psychological trauma of combat, the disruption of normal social structures, and
the presence of military supply chains that can be diverted to black markets.

The connection between war and drugs operates at multiple levels simultaneously. At the
medical level, wounded soldiers receive addictive painkillers. At the psychological level,
traumatized combatants seek chemical relief from what would later be called post-traumatic
stress. At the economic level, the disruption of normal commerce creates opportunities
for drug traffickers. And at the political level, governments that control drug supply
chains during wartime find it difficult to relinquish that control afterward.

## Japanese Opium as a Weapon in Manchuria

McKenna extends the pattern to the Pacific theater of World War II. During the occupation
of Manchuria, the Japanese military took what he calls "a page from the book of British
colonial oppression of a century earlier" and produced vast amounts of opium and heroin
for distribution inside China.

The intent was explicitly political: to create so many addicts that the will of the
Chinese people to resist the occupation would effectively be destroyed. This was not a
profit-driven enterprise, as in the British case, but a deliberate weaponization of
addiction as a tool of imperial control. McKenna notes the bitter irony that the Japanese,
who had themselves been subjected to the British opium trade in the nineteenth century,
adopted the same methods when they achieved imperial power.

This example illustrates McKenna's larger point about the relationship between drugs and
political power. Drug addiction is not merely a public health problem but a tool of
political control that has been used repeatedly by imperial powers to subjugate
populations. The "war on drugs," in McKenna's analysis, is itself a form of this same
pattern — a political instrument that serves the interests of state power rather than the
populations it claims to protect.

## Prohibition and the Windfall for Hard Narcotics

The prohibition of alcohol in the United States (1920-1933) created what McKenna
describes as a "vast windfall market for hard narcotics." When alcohol was made illegal,
the criminal syndicates that had been built to supply illegal alcohol naturally expanded
into the trade in morphine, heroin, and cocaine. The infrastructure of organized crime —
the distribution networks, the corrupt officials, the money-laundering operations — was
already in place.

From 1914 until World War II, drug distribution was largely in the hands of the gangsters
who directed other illicit operations: prostitution, loan sharking, and various rackets.
The prohibition era had created the organizational template. After World War II, the
relationship between intelligence agencies and drug trafficking became increasingly direct.

McKenna's point is that drug prohibition does not eliminate drugs but merely shifts the
market from legal to illegal channels, enriching criminal organizations and creating the
conditions for wider addiction. The pattern observed with morphine during the Civil War
repeats at the societal level: prohibition creates the very conditions it claims to
address.

## Governments and the Drug Trade

McKenna's most provocative argument in this section concerns the direct involvement of
governments in the drug trade. He argues that as drug addiction gave the trade a
blackened reputation, governments that had "dealt drugs with impunity for centuries
suddenly found themselves, in the new atmosphere of temperance and social reform, forced
to legislate this lucrative trade out of the realm of ordinary commerce and into the
status of an illicit activity."

The result was not the elimination of government involvement in drugs but its
transformation. Governments would now make their drug money through kickback schemes and
arrangements in which they would be paid to "look the other way." The modern solution,
in McKenna's analysis, is for drug cartels to operate as proxies for national governments
in supplying addictive narcotics. Legitimate governments prefer to have their intelligence
agencies cut secret deals with drug traffickers while "the visible machinery of diplomacy
seems all aflutter over the 'drug problem.'"

McKenna notes that it is significant that the major production areas of hard narcotics
are "tribal zones." Modern imperialists would have the world believe that they have never
been able to overrun and control these areas, in Pakistan and Burma for example, where
major production of opium occurs. Consequently, faceless tribal leaders, ever changing
and with unpronounceable names, can be held responsible. The true dynamics of production,
distribution, and profit-sharing remain obscured behind this convenient narrative.

## Heroin: The Perfect Drug for Damaged People

McKenna's discussion of heroin synthesizes these themes. Heroin was invented as a cure for
morphine addiction — a pattern he notes recurs throughout drug history, where each new
synthetic compound is hailed as a cure for the addiction caused by the previous one.
Heroin quickly replaced morphine as the opiate of choice among addicts and has retained
that position throughout the twentieth century.

Drawing on William S. Burroughs, McKenna describes heroin as "the perfect drug for anyone
who has been damaged by lack of self-esteem or traumatized by historical upheaval." It is
"a drug of battlefields, concentration camps, cancer wards, prisons, and ghettos. It is
the drug of the resigned and the dissolute, the surely dying and the victims unwilling or
unable to fight back."

Burroughs's description of the "Algebra of Need" — "The face of 'evil' is always the
face of total need... A dope fiend is a man in total need of dope. Beyond a certain
frequency need knows absolutely no limit or control" — captures the logic of addiction
that McKenna sees as the end product of the dominator culture's relationship to
psychoactive substances.
