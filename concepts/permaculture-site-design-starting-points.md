## Overview

The greatest difficulty in any [[permaculture-design]] is determining where to start. [[bill-mollison]] provides a clear, ordered sequence for beginning a site design that has been refined through approximately 800 completed designs. The approach prioritizes the most consequential and irreversible decisions first, then works outward from the dwelling core.

## The Design Sequence

### Step One: Property Boundaries

On a large property, boundaries may need to be flown. [[maps]] and photography are useful for records and orientation but are no substitute for walking the land. The most important tool is the designer's own body — senses for judging wind, temperature, evaporation, and slope. Observation is the best tool; experience is the second best.

### Step Two: Experience the Site

The designer must physically experience the site before making any decisions. This means walking the land in different conditions, at different times of day and season. The local weather station is unreliable — it sits in a flat location far from hills and makes abstract observations that do not apply to specific sites. The site itself tells you what happens there through evidence of climatic long-time influences: wind-flagged trees, frost pockets, erosion patterns, and vegetation indicators.

### Step Three: Identify Resources

Before any design work begins, catalog every resource on and around the site:

- **Earth resources**: Clay, minerals, stone, topsoil
- **Plant resources**: Existing vegetation, timber, forage
- **Animal resources**: Wildlife, insect life, [[permaculture-domestic-animals]]
- **Energy resources**: Wind, water, wood, oil, gas, solar potential
- **Water**: Springs, streams, runoff patterns, good-quality water for sale
- **Social resources**: Rights to collect seaweed, driftwood, shells; mining rights; water rights

A critical but frequently missed category is **resources extrinsic to the site** — market demands, waste products from the district, unfulfilled needs (soil conditioners, tanks, [[permaculture]] equipment). The designer who identifies and connects these resources earns their fee before any design work begins.

### Step Four: Advise on Legal Protections

Ensure the client stakes out rights to resources on their property before others discover them. Wind power sites can be claimed by multinationals under mining law. Clay deposits, mineral rights, and water rights should be secured. Clients on the seashore need special rights to collect [[building-with-natural-materials]]. A good lawyer should check for covenants before purchase.

### Step Five: Water Layout

Identify water resources and flows first. Lay out water systems, access, and house sites as a single entity. If this triad is placed correctly, everything else fits naturally. There is no need to worry about details if the water-access-house complex is right.

### Step Six: Access and Roads

**Placing access is the first design move.** A misplaced road costs more over time than almost anything else, including the house itself. Design access according to keyline principles — running very gently across slope with little turns on plateaus. Access roads should run up the center of ridges for drainage. Valley roads need higher maintenance.

Always finish access upgrade to the dwelling, even if the road must drop slightly to run upgrade to the house. Roads that descend to the house carry water around the foundation. In winter climates, position roads in the sun to reduce shoveling. Roads can simultaneously protect against fire and stop snow drifting.

### Step Seven: House Site Selection

For community designs, stress that free selection of house sites is unacceptable. A designer should select and mark sites connected by access roads. Without guidance, people make two catastrophic errors: heading for ridge-tops (fire tunnels, cold, wind) or retreating into the bush (conflict with wildlife, isolation).

### Step Eight: Zonation and Land Use

Around the house site, begin the wheels and spokes of zoned land use. Define zones by visit frequency, not by rigid circles. Reserve areas for vital future uses (pond sites, access routes) so they are not planted to trees or otherwise preempted.

## Type-One Errors to Avoid

Two errors are so severe they can destroy a design and ruin the client's experience:

### Error One: Ridge-Top or Wind Tunnel Placement

A house on a ridge in a valley is very badly situated — the valley funnels wind and the ridge catches it. Ridge-top houses are exposed, losing 60% of energy efficiency to wind. Such places change hands every four years because nobody can stand the misery and expense. The correction is to place the client just below the ridge, where they lose very little view but gain enormous shelter.

The ideal site is a minor ridge between two major ridges, backing into mountains and facing the sun — the typical Appalachian site.

### Error Two: Bush Isolation

Placing a client in a tiny clearing in the bush creates terrible conflict. Conservation-minded clients are then forced to kill woodchucks, wallabies, and possums to protect their garden. This transforms gentle vegetarians into what Mollison calls "rednecks" — people hardened by constant killing. The guilt drives them back to town. The solution is mid-slope positioning with adequate clearing and proper fencing.

### Additional Hazards

Also verify the site is not subject to mud flows, volcanoes, or other catastrophic events. A check-list should be maintained for [[permaculture-site-assessment-report-writing]].

## Working with Rich Clients

A practical warning: keep your mouth shut around wealthy clients until you have things together and can present your report properly. A casual suggestion about a [[diversion-drain-mollison]] can result in a rich client immediately bulldozing a catastrophically bad canal uphill. Present complete recommendations at once, with clear instructions.

## Retrofit vs. New Design

The designer may face two positions:

1. **Things already in place**: A retrofit design, where the most difficult work is often around existing dwellings where most energy is burned. You might save a client more money by starting around their house than by redesigning their farm.

2. **Nothing has yet happened**: A very happy state of affairs where the designer has maximum freedom.

## The Multi-Functional Design Rule

For every element placed, it must work in as many functions as possible. Anyone should be able to point to any element and ask "Why did you put that there?" and receive multiple answers: "Because it collects manure, because it keeps the grass down, because it defends against fire." A designer who cannot answer these questions is not a designer.

## Setting Priorities

No client has unlimited resources. Set criteria for priorities:

1. **First**: Install energy-productive systems
2. **Second** (or concurrently): Energy-conserving systems
3. **Last**: Systems that consume energy

Design for [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-livestock-caloric-efficiency]]. Work with architects when possible, as interaction between designer and architect is fruitful. Plan for evolution in the design over time.
