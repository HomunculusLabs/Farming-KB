## Volatile Organic Compounds as Chemical Signals

Plants release hundreds of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the
atmosphere. These include:

- **Terpenoids** — the largest class of plant VOCs, including monoterpenes
  (C10), sesquiterpenes (C15), and diterpenes (C20). Examples: [[limonene]],
  pinene, linalool
- **Green leaf volatiles (GLVs)** — C6 aldehydes, alcohols, and esters
  released from damaged leaves. Examples: hexenal, hexanol, hexenyl acetate
- **Phenylpropanoids/benzenoids** — derived from phenylalanine. Examples:
  methyl salicylate, eugenol
- **Fatty acid derivatives** — including [[jasmonic-acid-plant-defense-signaling]] and its volatile
  methyl ester (methyl jasmonate)
- **Ethylene** — the simplest plant hormone (C2H4), a gaseous signal
  molecule involved in fruit ripening, senescence, [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]] through several mechanisms:

1. **Direct ligand-receptor binding** — some plant receptors on the cell
   surface or within cells directly bind specific VOCs, triggering downstream
   signaling cascades.

2. **Membrane permeation and intracellular sensing** — many small volatile
   molecules are lipophilic and can diffuse across the plasma membrane, where
   they interact with intracellular sensors or modify protein function.

3. **Enzymatic conversion** — some VOCs are converted to active forms upon
   entering tissues. Methyl salicylate → [[comparison-jasmonate-vs-salicylic-acid-defense-signaling]] is one example.
4. **Membrane modulation** — certain volatiles alter membrane fluidity or
   ion channel function, triggering rapid physiological responses.

### Ethylene Perception: The Best-Characterized System

Ethylene perception is the most thoroughly understood [[plant-olfaction-and-volatile-signaling]] system
in plants. Ethylene receptors are membrane-bound histidine kinases related to
bacterial two-component systems. In the absence of ethylene, these receptors
actively suppress the ethylene response pathway. When ethylene binds, receptor
activity is inhibited, releasing the suppression and allowing downstream
signaling through CTR1 (Constitutive Triple Response 1), EIN2 (Ethylene
Insensitive 2), and EIN3/EIL transcription factors.

This "repression relief" mechanism means ethylene does not activate a cascade
so much as release the brake — fundamentally different from animal olfactory
receptor activation.

## Inter-Plant Communication

### Eavesdropping on Neighbors

Plants can detect volatile cues from neighboring plants and adjust their own
defenses accordingly. When a plant is attacked by herbivores, it releases a
bouquet of VOCs that serve multiple functions:

- **Direct defense** — some compounds are directly toxic or repellent to
  herbivores
- **Predator attraction** — VOCs recruit natural enemies of the herbivore
  ([[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides]], [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]])
- **Neighbor warning** — undamaged neighboring plants perceive the VOCs and
  preemptively activate their own defense pathways

The "talking tree" hypothesis — that plants warn their neighbors of attack
through volatile signals — has been demonstrated in multiple species including
sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata), lima beans, and poplar.

### Kin Recognition

Some evidence suggests plants can distinguish VOCs from kin vs. non-kin
individuals. Sagebrush responds more strongly to cues from genetically related
individuals, potentially directing altruistic warning signals toward kin.

## Plant-Pathogen Interactions

### Methyl Salicylate and Systemic Acquired Resistance

When a plant detects pathogen infection, it produces salicylic acid (SA) as a
defense signaling molecule. SA can be converted to methyl salicylate (MeSA),
a volatile form that travels through the air or through the phloem to
systemic tissues. Upon reaching distant organs, MeSA is converted back to
SA by methyl salicylate esterase, reactivating the defense signal.

This volatile conversion allows the defense signal to move through the plant
without relying solely on vasculature, providing a parallel communication
channel for systemic acquired resistance (SAR).

### Methyl Jasmonate and Herbivore Defense

Methyl jasmonate (MeJA), the volatile derivative of jasmonic acid, is released
in large quantities during herbivore damage. Neighboring plants that perceive
MeJA activate jasmonate-dependent defense responses including protease inhibitor
production, extrafloral nectar secretion, and volatile emission — creating a
chain reaction of defensive priming through the plant community.

## Fruit Ripening Coordination

Ethylene is the primary hormone coordinating fruit ripening. "One bad apple
spoils the bunch" reflects real biology: ripening fruit produces ethylene that
accelerates ripening in neighbors. This positive feedback loop can rapidly
synchronize ripening across an entire crop, which is why fruit is often harvested
before the climacteric ethylene rise and shipped under controlled atmosphere.

Commercial applications exploit ethylene sensitivity:
- Bananas are gassed with ethylene to synchronize ripening
- Ethylene scrubbers and 1-MCP delay ripening in storage
- Modified-atmosphere packaging reduces ethylene accumulation

## Ecological Significance

Plant olfaction operates at every ecological scale:

- **Individual level** — detecting tissue damage and activating defenses
- **Population level** — coordinating flowering, ripening, and dormancy
- **Community level** — mediating competitive and mutualistic interactions
- **Ecosystem level** — influencing [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] through VOC effects on soil
