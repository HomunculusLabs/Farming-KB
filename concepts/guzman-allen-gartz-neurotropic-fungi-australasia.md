# Neurotropic Fungi of Australia and New Zealand: Biogeography and Endemism

Australasia, comprising Australia, New Zealand, Tasmania, and the Pacific island nations, represents one of the most distinctive biogeographic regions for neurotropic fungi. The long geological isolation of the Australian continent and New Zealand archipelago, combined with their unique flora dominated by Eucalyptus and Nothofagus, has resulted in a neurotropic mycoflora that includes both cosmopolitan species and notable endemics. The work of Allen et al. (1991), Johnston and Buchanan (1995), Chang and Mills (1992), and others has revealed a surprisingly diverse psilocybian mycoflora in the region.

## Australia

Australia possesses a diverse neurotropic mycoflora despite its relatively arid climate. The concentration of species in the wetter southeastern and southwestern corners, as well as the tropical northeast (Queensland), reflects the continent's rainfall patterns.

### Documented Species from Australia

**Psilocybe species:**
- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa** — perhaps the most iconic Australian neurotropic fungus; first reported by Picker and Richards (1970), subsequently documented by Hall (1973), Southcott (1974), Cleland (1976), Chilton (1978), Margot and Watling (1981), Guzmán (1983), Chang and Mills (1992), and Johnston and Buchanan (1995). This species is closely related to the European P. cyanescens but is a distinct taxon. It grows on wood chips, mulch, and in gardens, and is particularly abundant in the Melbourne area during autumn.
- **Psilocybe cubensis** — common in tropical Queensland pastures; documented by Hall (1973), Southcott (1974), Margot and Watling (1981), Guzmán (1995), Gartz (1996), and Stamets (1996).
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** — the "liberty cap," also found in Australia; documented by Margot and Watling (1981), Dickinson and Lucas (1983), Redhead (1989), Allen et al. (1991), Guzmán (1995), and Stamets (1996). Its presence in the Southern Hemisphere, far from its European center of distribution, is biogeographically significant.
- **Psilocybe cyanescens** — documented by Margot and Watling (1981) and Guzmán (1995); found in wood chip mulch gardens, similar to its habitat in the Pacific Northwest of North America.
- **Psilocybe australiana** — an endemic Australian species described by Margot and Watling (1981); also documented by Guzmán (1983), Chang and Mills (1992), and Stamets (1996).
- **Psilocybe eucalypta** — another endemic species, described by Margot and Watling (1981) and Guzmán (1983); named for its association with Eucalyptus, reflecting the unique Australian mycological context.
- **Psilocybe tasmaniana** — a Tasmanian endemic described by Guzmán (1983), documented by Chang and Mills (1992) and Stamets (1996).
- **Psilocybe collybioides** — reported from Australia by Hall (1973) and Southcott (1974); however, Guzmán (1983) noted this is not the same species as P. collybioides reported from Argentina, and that P. collybioides is likely a synonym of P. zapotecorum.
- **Psilocybe subcubensis** — documented by Margot and Watling (1981), Guzmán (1983, 1995).

**Other genera:**
- **Amanita muscaria** — introduced to Australia and now naturalized; documented by Hongo (1959), Cleland (1976), Southcott (1974), Hongo and Yokoyama (1978), and Allen et al. (1991).
- **Copelandia cyanescens** — documented by Pollock (1976), Southcott (1974), Allen et al. (1991), Stijve (1992), Gerhardt (1996), and Gartz (1996).
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis** and **G. purpuratus** — documented by Hongo (1959), Dickinson and Lucas (1983), and Allen et al. (1991).
- **Panaeolus subbalteatus** — documented by Hall (1973).
- **Panaeolina foenisecii** — widely distributed in Australia, documented by Hongo (1959), Southcott (1974), Cleland (1976), and Dennis (1986).
- **Claviceps paspali** and **C. purpurea** — documented on grasses.
- **Gerronema fibula** — documented by Hongo (1959, 1974).

### Tasmania

Tasmania, the island state south of mainland Australia, has a distinctive subset of the Australian neurotropic mycoflora:

- **Psilocybe australiana** — documented by Guzmán (1983), Chang and Mills (1992), and Stamets (1996)
- **Psilocybe cubensis** — documented by Guzmán (1983)
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** — documented by Guzmán (1983, 1995), Allen et al. (1991), Chang and Mills (1992), Samorini (1992), and Stamets (1996)
- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa** — documented by Picker and Rickards (1970), Southcott (1974), Guzmán (1983), Chang and Mills (1992), Johnston and Buchanan (1995), and Stamets (1996)
- **Psilocybe tasmaniana** — a Tasmanian endemic
- **Copelandia cyanescens** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)

## New Zealand

New Zealand has a remarkably rich neurotropic mycoflora for its small land area, with several endemic and distinctive species:

### Endemic and Notable Species

- **Psilocybe aucklandii** — endemic to New Zealand, described by Guzmán, King, and Bandala (1991, 1993b); documented by Johnston and Buchanan (1995) and Stamets (1996). Named for the Auckland region.
- **Psilocybe makarorae** — another endemic species, described by Johnston and Buchanan (1995) and Stamets (1996). Named after the Makarora region of New Zealand's South Island.
- **Psilocybe eucalypta** — also found in New Zealand, documented by Guzmán et al. (1993b) and Allen et al. (1991).

### Widespread Species in New Zealand

- **Psilocybe australiana** — documented by Allen et al. (1991) and Guzmán et al. (1993b)
- **Psilocybe cubensis** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** — documented by Allen et al. (1991), Guzmán et al. (1993b), and Stamets (1996)
- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa** — documented by Allen et al. (1991) and Johnston and Buchanan (1995)
- **Psilocybe subcubensis** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Psilocybe tasmaniana** — interestingly present in New Zealand despite being named for Tasmania; documented by Allen et al. (1991), Chang and Mills (1992), and Stamets (1996)
- **Psilocybe collybioides** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Psilocybe kumaenorum** — originally described from New Guinea (Heim et al., 1967), also found in New Zealand according to Allen et al. (1991)

**Other genera:**
- **Amanita muscaria** — introduced and naturalized in New Zealand (Hongo and Yokoyama, 1978; Allen et al., 1991)
- **Copelandia cyanescens** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Gymnopilus purpuratus** and **G. spectabilis** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Panaeolus subbalteatus** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Panaeolina foenisecii** — documented by Allen et al. (1991)
- **Claviceps paspali** and **C. purpurea** — documented on grasses

## Biogeographic Patterns

The Australasian neurotropic mycoflora reveals several important biogeographic patterns:

### Endemism
New Zealand and Tasmania each harbor endemic Psilocybe species (P. aucklandii and P. makarorae for New Zealand; P. tasmaniana for Tasmania). Australia has its own endemics including P. australiana and P. eucalypta. This pattern of endemism is consistent with the general biogeographic isolation of the Australian continent and New Zealand archipelago.

### Gondwanan Connections
The presence of Psilocybe subaeruginosa in both Australia and New Zealand, and its absence from other continents, suggests a Gondwanan origin. This species is closely related to P. cyanescens from the Pacific Northwest of North America, raising interesting questions about trans-Pacific dispersal.

### Cosmopolitan Species
Several species found in Australasia are genuinely cosmopolitan:
- Psilocybe cubensis occurs throughout the tropics worldwide
- Panaeolina foenisecii is nearly global in temperate regions
- Amanita muscaria has been introduced and naturalized in both Australia and New Zealand
- Claviceps purpurea follows cereal agriculture worldwide

### Northern Hemisphere Connections
Psilocybe semilanceata, a classic Northern Hemisphere temperate species, also occurs in both Australia and New Zealand. This disjunct distribution could reflect recent introduction or ancient vicariance.

### Eucalyptus Association
Psilocybe eucalypta is named for its apparent association with Eucalyptus, reflecting the unique ecological context of Australian mycology. The relationship between Eucalyptus ecosystems and neurotropic fungal diversity warrants further investigation.

## Oceanic and Pacific Islands

The Pacific islands have very sparse records of neurotropic fungi:

- **Fiji** — Copelandia cyanescens (Ola'h, 1969) and Psilocybe cubensis (Wasson, 1959b)
- **Bononi Islands** — Copelandia tropicalis (Hongo, 1977a)
- **Hawaii** — Amanita muscaria (Merlin and Allen, 1993; Allen, 1998), with Psilocybe species also documented

The extreme paucity of records from Pacific islands likely reflects collection effort rather than actual absence, as the tropical climate and livestock presence would support coprophilous species.

## Conservation and Research Priorities

The Australasian neurotropic mycoflora faces several conservation and research challenges:

1. **Habitat loss**: Urbanization and agricultural expansion threaten the natural habitats of endemic species, particularly in southeastern Australia and New Zealand
2. **Underdocumentation**: Large areas of Australia (Western Australia, Northern Territory, arid interior) have virtually no records of neurotropic fungi
3. **Taxonomic uncertainty**: Several Australian Psilocybe species need modern taxonomic revision using molecular techniques
4. **Indigenous knowledge**: Aboriginal Australian and Maori knowledge of psychoactive fungi remains largely undocumented and could provide important ethnobotanical information
5. **Climate change**: Changes in rainfall patterns and temperature could significantly affect the distribution of moisture-dependent species in Australia's already marginal climate for fungal growth
