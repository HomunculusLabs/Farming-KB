# Designer's Checklist

> From: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 3, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13

Each chapter of the Designers' Manual concludes with a "Designers' Checklist" -- a set of questions and assessments specific to that domain. Mollison's checklists serve as practical guides for the systematic evaluation of any site and design. A comprehensive permaculture assessment draws on all of these domain-specific checklists to create a holistic site plan.

## General Site Assessment

A property design from one-quarter to 50 hectares needs first a clear assessment of client or occupier needs, and stated aims or ideas from all potential occupiers (including children). A clear idea of the financial and skill resources of occupants is necessary so that the plan can be financially viable.

The following areas should be assessed:

### Access and Earthworks
- Existing roads, tracks, and pathways.
- Slope analysis and contour mapping.
- Soil suitability for proposed earthworks.
- Erosion risk assessment and prevention priorities.
- Placement of access ways in relation to slope and landforms.

### Water Systems
- Water supply sources (rainfall, streams, bores, springs).
- Water quality and testing results.
- Irrigation needs and methods.
- Dam and pond sites, clay availability.
- Flood risk and drainage patterns.
- Greywater and sewage disposal requirements.

### Climate and Microclimate
- Temperature ranges (frost dates, heat extremes).
- Rainfall data (annual total, seasonal distribution, drought frequency).
- Wind patterns (prevailing direction, strength, seasonal variation).
- Solar access and shading patterns.
- Microclimate variation across the site.

### Soil Assessment
- Soil types and depth across the property.
- pH, mineral content, and trace element levels.
- Drainage characteristics and water-holding capacity.
- Erosion evidence and stability of slopes.
- Prior land use (chemical residues, biocides, fertilisers).
- Organic matter content and biological activity.

### Vegetation
- Existing native vegetation and its condition.
- Weedy or invasive species present.
- Useful exotics already established.
- Indicator plants for soil and site conditions.
- Fire risk assessment of existing vegetation.

### Structures
- Existing buildings and their condition.
- Possible retrofit for energy efficiency.
- Sites for new structures (house, sheds, animal housing).
- Legal requirements for buildings, easements, permits.
- Proximity to services (power, phone, water).

## Bioregional and Community Assessment

Based on traditional and modern villages, a complex can be built from a comprehensive checklist including:

- A well-arranged array of housing, often grouped around a compound.
- School, seminar, and workshop facilities.
- Food processing centre, cafe, and commercial areas.
- Noisy/oily work areas (woodwork, metalwork, vehicle service).
- Retail shops including plant nursery.
- Dairy and dairy processing centre.
- Domestic livestock housing (chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, rabbits).
- Energy systems and biomass conversion.
- Glasshouses, commercial crop areas.
- Camp area for visitors.

## Design Goals Assessment

Over a relatively short evolution of three to six years, a sound design might well achieve:

- Reduction in the need to earn (conservation of food and energy costs).
- Repair and conservation of degraded landscapes, buildings, soils, and species at risk.
- Sustainable product in short-, medium-, and long-terms.
- A unique, preferably essential, service or product for the region.
- Right livelihood (good work) for occupants in services or goods.
- Sound and safe legal status for the occupiers.
- A harmonious and productive landscape without wastes or poisons.
- A cooperative and information-rich part of a regional society.

## Domain-Specific Checklists

Mollison provides detailed checklists for each climate and topic:

- **Chapter 5 (Climate):** Temperature, precipitation, radiation, wind, landscape effects, latitude effects.
- **Chapter 8 (Soils):** Soil indicators, erosion, rehabilitation, soil in house foundations, life in earth, respiration of earth.
- **Chapter 10 (Humid Tropics):** Coast stabilisation, shelterbelts, low island and coral cay strategies.
- **Chapter 11 (Drylands):** Water systems, forestry, grazing, desertification, salting of soils, cold and montane deserts.
- **Chapter 12 (Cold Climates):** House and settlement, orchard and farm, lawn, grasslands, rangelands, wildfire.
- **Chapter 13 (Aquaculture):** Pond design, species selection, water quality, edge planting.

## Design Methodology

A good site map makes any landscape design much easier, and far more visual. A good map indicates sensible options and hypotheses (dam sites, soil/crop suitability) which can later be checked with actual site conditions. The restrictions on site use must first be ascertained before a plan is prepared or approved.
