# Grass Seed Jar Colonisation, Metabolite Stress, and Casing Transition

The colonisation phase of grass seed jar cultivation is a critical period that determines the success or failure of the entire grow cycle. Understanding the dynamics of mycelial growth through the substrate, the warning signs of metabolic stress, and the proper timing for transitioning from colonisation to casing is essential for consistent results with [[psilocybe-tampanensis-entity]], [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]]. Grass seed offers several advantages over other grain substrates: it is widely available, inexpensive, and its small kernel size provides numerous inoculation points per unit volume.

## Colonisation Dynamics

After inoculation, jars are placed in a clean, draft-free location at room temperature (20 degrees Celsius) or slightly higher. The speed of colonisation depends on three primary variables: temperature, species, and method of inoculation. Under optimal conditions, grass seed can be completely colonised in as few as five days, while slower colonisation may take up to twenty days. The range reflects both biological variability between species and the practical reality that different [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]].

The yellowish metabolites are distinct from other discolorations that may indicate contamination. Metabolite exudate tends to be a uniform yellowish hue, often accumulating at the bottom of the jar or in areas of dense mycelial growth. The exudate is typically translucent and may appear as droplets or as a diffuse yellowing of the mycelium itself. Green, black, or pink discolorations are more likely indicators of mold or bacterial contamination and require different responses, typically immediate disposal of the affected jar.

## Jar Cooling and Lid Management

After sterilisation in the pressure cooker, the jars must be allowed to cool before inoculation. A critical but often overlooked detail is that the lids must remain loose during cooling. If the lids are tightened while the jars are hot, the cooling process will pull a vacuum inside the jar, creating a seal that cannot be easily broken for inoculation and that may crush delicate grain substrates. The vacuum effect can be strong enough to deform flexible substrate containers or to draw contamination inward through any microscopic gaps in the seal.

The loose lid also serves an ongoing function during colonisation. The mycelium requires gas exchange, particularly oxygen intake and carbon dioxide release, to sustain its [[fruiting-initiation]]. Smooth, perfectly level casing surfaces produce fewer pins and less even flushes than roughened surfaces.

## Casing Soil Preparation and Sterilisation

The casing soil recipe used in this method consists of three ingredients: 10 parts peat, 5 parts vermiculite, and 2 parts limestone (marl). The ingredients are mixed in dry form while water is gradually added. The goal is to achieve maximum water content without turning the mixture into mud. If the casing becomes too wet, additional dry ingredients are added to correct the moisture balance.

A notable aspect of this approach is the use of full sterilisation for casing soil, rather than pasteurisation. Many authors advise against sterilising casing soil, arguing that it kills beneficial organisms. However, the authors of this method report only bad experiences with untreated or pasteurised casing soils. The casing soil is filled into oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags (polypropylene), or jars and sterilised for one hour in the pressure cooker. After cooling to room temperature, the soil is ready for use.

The decision to fully sterilise rather than pasteurise reflects a pragmatic assessment of the contamination risks in the specific cultivation context described. While pasteurisation preserves some [[psilocybe-cubensis]] is generally the fastest coloniser under standard conditions, often completing full jar colonisation within seven to ten days. Psilocybe tampanensis and [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] occupies an intermediate position, typically completing colonisation within ten to fourteen days.

These differences reflect the natural ecology of each species. P. cubensis is a subtropical coprophilous species adapted to rapid colonisation of nutrient-rich substrates, while P. tampanensis and P. mexicana are species adapted to more stable soil environments where slower, more thorough colonisation is advantageous. Understanding the expected colonisation timeline for each species helps cultivators distinguish between normal variation and problematic delays that may indicate contamination or poor inoculant viability.

## Troubleshooting Slow or Stalled Colonisation

When colonisation is significantly slower than expected for a given species, several factors should be investigated. Insufficient inoculum is the most common cause of slow starts; a spore syringe with low spore concentration or an old agar culture with reduced vigour will produce fewer initial colonisation points. Substrate moisture content that is too low can slow or halt mycelial growth, while excess moisture can create anaerobic conditions that favour bacterial contamination over fungal growth.

Temperature is another frequent variable. While grass seed colonises at room temperature, temperatures below 18 degrees Celsius significantly slow growth for most species. Conversely, temperatures above 27 degrees Celsius can stress the mycelium and trigger metabolite exudation. The optimal range for most Psilocybe species is 22 to 25 degrees Celsius, which can be achieved without dedicated incubation equipment in most indoor environments.
