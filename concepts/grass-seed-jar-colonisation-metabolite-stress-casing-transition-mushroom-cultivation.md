# Grass Seed Jar Colonisation, Metabolite Stress, and Casing Transition

The colonisation phase of grass seed jar cultivation is a critical period that determines the success or failure of the entire grow cycle. Understanding the dynamics of mycelial growth through the substrate, the warning signs of metabolic stress, and the proper timing for transitioning from colonisation to casing is essential for consistent results with [[psilocybe-mexicana]], [[psilocybe-tampanensis-entity]], [[psilocybe-cubensis]], and [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]]. Grass seed offers several advantages over other grain substrates: it is widely available, inexpensive, and its small kernel size provides numerous inoculation points per unit volume.

## Colonisation Dynamics

After inoculation, jars are placed in a clean, draft-free location at room temperature (20 degrees Celsius) or slightly higher. The speed of colonisation depends on three primary variables: temperature, species, and method of inoculation. Under optimal conditions, grass seed can be completely colonised in as few as five days, while slower colonisation may take up to twenty days. The range reflects both biological variability between species and the practical reality that different [[inoculation-methods]] deliver different quantities of viable inoculum to different locations within the jar.

A key technique for accelerating colonisation is the redistribution shake. When mycelium begins to grow in only a few spots within the jar, shaking the jar redistributes the colonised kernels throughout the substrate. Each colonised kernel becomes a new point of inoculation, dramatically increasing the rate of substrate colonisation. This simple step can reduce total colonisation time by a significant margin compared to allowing the mycelium to spread outward from the initial inoculation points without intervention.

The redistribution shake is most effective when performed early in the colonisation process, as soon as visible colonization is detected in multiple locations. Shaking too early, before mycelium has established a foothold on several kernels, may not achieve the desired redistribution because the colonised material is insufficiently developed to serve as effective inoculation points. Shaking too late, when the substrate is already mostly colonised, is unnecessary and risks damaging the established mycelial network, potentially setting back the colonisation progress.

Multiple shakes are sometimes employed for difficult-to-colonise species or for jars with slow initial growth. A first shake distributes the initial inoculum, and a second shake, performed when the redistributed kernels have established new growth, can further accelerate the process. However, each shake introduces a small risk of contamination if the jar lid is removed or loosened during the operation, so the number of shakes should be minimised consistent with achieving full colonisation in a reasonable timeframe.

## Metabolite Exudation as a Stress Signal

One of the most important diagnostic signs during colonisation is the appearance of yellowish metabolites excreted by the mycelium. When jars are incubated too long or at too high a temperature, the mycelium begins to exude these yellow compounds, which are indicators of metabolic stress. The mycelium is essentially signalling that conditions are suboptimal and that it needs to move to the next phase of its life cycle.

The appearance of metabolites does not necessarily mean the culture is lost, but it does mean the jar should be cased as soon as possible. Continuing to incubate under stress conditions risks further degradation of the mycelium and increases the probability of contamination. The metabolites themselves can become a substrate for competing organisms if the situation is not corrected. In advanced cases of metabolite stress, the mycelium may become visibly weakened, taking on a translucent or slimy appearance that indicates loss of vigour.

Metabolite stress is typically caused by excessive heat, excessive incubation time, or a combination of both. A common mistake among novice cultivators is to incubate at higher temperatures than necessary, under the assumption that warmer conditions will speed growth. While moderate warmth accelerates colonisation, excessive heat shifts the metabolic balance of the mycelium into stress territory. The optimal approach is to maintain stable temperatures at or slightly above room temperature and to monitor the jars regularly for signs of [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]].

The yellowish metabolites are distinct from other discolorations that may indicate contamination. Metabolite exudate tends to be a uniform yellowish hue, often accumulating at the bottom of the jar or in areas of dense mycelial growth. The exudate is typically translucent and may appear as droplets or as a diffuse yellowing of the mycelium itself. Green, black, or pink discolorations are more likely indicators of mold or bacterial contamination and require different responses, typically immediate disposal of the affected jar.

## Jar Cooling and Lid Management

After sterilisation in the pressure cooker, the jars must be allowed to cool before inoculation. A critical but often overlooked detail is that the lids must remain loose during cooling. If the lids are tightened while the jars are hot, the cooling process will pull a vacuum inside the jar, creating a seal that cannot be easily broken for inoculation and that may crush delicate grain substrates. The vacuum effect can be strong enough to deform flexible substrate containers or to draw contamination inward through any microscopic gaps in the seal.

The loose lid also serves an ongoing function during colonisation. The mycelium requires gas exchange, particularly oxygen intake and carbon dioxide release, to sustain its [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]]. A loosely fitted lid allows this exchange while still providing sufficient protection against contamination from airborne spores. Some cultivators add a filtered breathing patch to the lid, but the simple approach of leaving the lid slightly loose has been proven effective for grass seed substrates.

Always check the jars for cracks before shaking after sterilisation. Thermal stress during pressure cooking can occasionally cause glass jars to crack, and shaking a cracked jar will spread the contaminated contents and potentially expose the cultivator to microbial hazards. If using a substrate that has been sterilised with added water, the presence of water outside the jar after sterilisation is a strong indicator of a crack.

## Inoculation Technique

When the jars have cooled to room temperature, they are ready for inoculation. Several types of inoculant can be used: spore syringes, mycelium syringes, agar squares, or other inoculant forms. Regardless of the inoculant type, the paramount concern is cleanliness. The inoculation area should be as clean as practical, and all tools and surfaces that will contact the sterilised substrate must be sterilised.

When using syringes, the needle must be flame-sterilised before each jar is inoculated. The flame should heat the needle until it glows red, after which it is allowed to cool for a few seconds before insertion. When using agar squares, the scalpel must be similarly flame-sterilised. The authors issue a specific safety warning: alcohol and spray Lysol are highly flammable, and flame sterilisation must be performed with awareness of any flammable vapours in the inoculation area.

Even a simple hood made from a cardboard box can significantly reduce contamination by preventing drafts that carry airborne spores. The sterilised grain should not be exposed to open air longer than absolutely necessary. The jar lid is opened just a crack, the inoculant is introduced, and the lid is closed quickly. After inoculation, the jar is shaken to distribute the inoculant and the lid is loosened again for gas exchange during colonisation.

## Transition to Casing

When the grass seed in the jars is fully colonised, the substrate must be transferred to a casing arrangement for fruiting. The procedure involves opening the colonised jar, shaking the grain loose, and pouring it into a 1-litre disposable plastic tray. Any lumps within the seed should be broken up using the clean rim of the jar. The surface of the grain is levelled evenly using a large spoon and a fork.

The grain is then covered with a thin layer of casing soil, approximately 1.5 to 2.0 cm deep. The casing surface should be kept rough, with small valleys and hills, while maintaining an overall even distribution. This roughness increases the surface area available for pin formation and creates microclimates that promote [[fruiting-initiation]]. Smooth, perfectly level casing surfaces produce fewer pins and less even flushes than roughened surfaces.

## Casing Soil Preparation and Sterilisation

The casing soil recipe used in this method consists of three ingredients: 10 parts peat, 5 parts vermiculite, and 2 parts limestone (marl). The ingredients are mixed in dry form while water is gradually added. The goal is to achieve maximum water content without turning the mixture into mud. If the casing becomes too wet, additional dry ingredients are added to correct the moisture balance.

A notable aspect of this approach is the use of full sterilisation for casing soil, rather than pasteurisation. Many authors advise against sterilising casing soil, arguing that it kills beneficial organisms. However, the authors of this method report only bad experiences with untreated or pasteurised casing soils. The casing soil is filled into oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags (polypropylene), or jars and sterilised for one hour in the pressure cooker. After cooling to room temperature, the soil is ready for use.

The decision to fully sterilise rather than pasteurise reflects a pragmatic assessment of the contamination risks in the specific cultivation context described. While pasteurisation preserves some [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]], it also preserves potential contaminants. For cultivators working without laminar flow hoods or dedicated clean rooms, the reduced contamination risk of fully sterilised casing soil may outweigh any benefits of microbial diversity in the casing layer.

## Species-Specific Colonisation Characteristics

Different species colonise grass seed at markedly different rates. [[psilocybe-cubensis]] is generally the fastest coloniser under standard conditions, often completing full jar colonisation within seven to ten days. Psilocybe tampanensis and [[psilocybe-mexicana]] tend to colonise more slowly, sometimes requiring fifteen to twenty days for complete colonisation. [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] occupies an intermediate position, typically completing colonisation within ten to fourteen days.

These differences reflect the natural ecology of each species. P. cubensis is a subtropical coprophilous species adapted to rapid colonisation of nutrient-rich substrates, while P. tampanensis and P. mexicana are species adapted to more stable soil environments where slower, more thorough colonisation is advantageous. Understanding the expected colonisation timeline for each species helps cultivators distinguish between normal variation and problematic delays that may indicate contamination or poor inoculant viability.

## Troubleshooting Slow or Stalled Colonisation

When colonisation is significantly slower than expected for a given species, several factors should be investigated. Insufficient inoculum is the most common cause of slow starts; a spore syringe with low spore concentration or an old agar culture with reduced vigour will produce fewer initial colonisation points. Substrate moisture content that is too low can slow or halt mycelial growth, while excess moisture can create anaerobic conditions that favour bacterial contamination over fungal growth.

Temperature is another frequent variable. While grass seed colonises at room temperature, temperatures below 18 degrees Celsius significantly slow growth for most species. Conversely, temperatures above 27 degrees Celsius can stress the mycelium and trigger metabolite exudation. The optimal range for most Psilocybe species is 22 to 25 degrees Celsius, which can be achieved without dedicated incubation equipment in most indoor environments.
