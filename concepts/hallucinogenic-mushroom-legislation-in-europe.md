# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Legislation in Europe

## Overview

The legal status [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]]) and the fact that mushrooms containing these substances grow wild across much of the continent. Between 2001 and 2006, six EU member states tightened legislation specifically targeting mushrooms, each responding to rising prevalence but using different legal mechanisms to address the unique regulatory challenges posed by a living organism that contains [[tryptamine-chemistry-psilocin-and-psilocybin-synthesis]] are controlled under the **1971 UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances**, listed in Schedule I under the names "psilocybine" and "psilocine" (or "psilotsin"). All EU member states control these substances at the national level accordingly.

However, the Convention controls the [[psilocybe-cubensis]] or other fungi/spores containing psilocin or psilocybin, including grown, dried or processed in any other manner" as a controlled substance.

### Netherlands (November 2002)

The Dutch Opium Act prohibited psilocin, psilocybin, and "preparations" containing these substances. The Dutch Supreme Court clarified that the Act applied to mushrooms that had been "prepared" by being dried, mashed, or processed into food, but **not to fresh mushrooms**. This ruling created the famous loophole where fresh mushrooms remained legal while dried mushrooms were controlled. Smartshops exploited this by selling fresh mushrooms, which stayed viable for 7 to 10 days. Fresh mushroom sales continued until the Netherlands further tightened regulations in subsequent years.

### Germany (March 2005)

Germany addressed the taxonomic ambiguity by changing the definition of a controlled substance from "plant" to "organism." Since mushrooms are fungi rather than plants, the previous wording did not clearly encompass them. The new terminology of "organic substances" closed this loophole, ensuring mushrooms were included under drug control legislation.

### Estonia (May 2005)

Clarified that cultivation of hallucinogenic mushrooms constitutes a criminal offence. This legislation targeted the supply chain directly, addressing grow operations and commercial cultivation.

### United Kingdom (July 2005)

The UK had previously controlled mushrooms only "if treated or prepared." A 2004 argument that even packaging constituted "preparation" was ultimately superseded by comprehensive new legislation. The Drugs Act 2005 added "Fungus (of any kind) which contains psilocin or an ester of psilocin" as a Class A controlled substance, removing any reference to the state of the mushroom. This was the most sweeping approach, covering fresh, dried, and living fungi indiscriminately.

The UK Treasury had been collecting approximately £175,000 per year in sales tax on an estimated £1 million annual turnover from mushroom sales prior to prohibition.

### Ireland (February 2006)

Ireland's legislation criminalised possession or sale of "fungus of any kind or description, which contains psilocin or an ester of psilocin." The law included an exemption for mushrooms "growing uncultivated," reflecting the challenge of criminalising naturally occurring organisms. The change was reportedly prompted by increasingly commonplace sales and the death of a young man after consuming [[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]], [[cervantes-grow-room-security-law-enforcement]].
