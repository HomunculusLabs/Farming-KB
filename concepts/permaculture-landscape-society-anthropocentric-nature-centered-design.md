# Permaculture in Landscape and Society: Anthropocentric and Nature-Centered Design

## Permaculture as Beneficial Design

[[permaculture]] is fundamentally a design system. As the basis of permaculture is beneficial design, it can be added to all other ethical training and skills and has the potential to take a place in all human endeavors. Mollison emphasizes that permaculture arranges what was always there in a different way, so that it works to conserve energy or generate more energy than it consumes. What is novel — and often overlooked — is that any system of total commonsense design for human communities is revolutionary.

The system deals primarily with already-settled areas and agricultural lands, almost all of which need drastic rehabilitation and rethinking. The central thesis is that integrating food supply and settlement — catching water from roof areas, placing nearby zones of fuel forest that receive wastes and supply energy — would free most of the globe for the rehabilitation of natural systems that need never be looked upon as "of use to people" except in the broadest sense of global health.

## Anthropocentric vs. Nature-Centered Systems

Mollison draws a critical distinction between cultivated (designed) ecosystems and natural systems. The real difference is one of intent:

- In a **natural system**, only a small fraction of species and biomass is directly available to humans; the system serves itself
- In a **cultivated ecology** (garden), almost every plant is selected to provide or support some direct yield for people; the system is human-centered or **anthropocentric**

This anthropocentric aim is valid for settlement design — household design relates principally to the needs of people. However, Mollison insists that a complementary **nature-centered ethic** is needed for wilderness conservation. The two ethics must operate in parallel.

## The Recycling Responsibility

In natural systems, recycling of nutrients and energy is a function of many species. Decomposers, fungi, bacteria, detritivores, and countless other organisms process wastes and return them to the soil. In gardens, this responsibility falls to humans — we must actively return wastes via compost or mulch to the soil and plants. We actively create soil in our gardens, whereas in nature many other species carry out that function.

Similarly, around homes people can catch water for garden use, but they rely on natural forested landscapes to provide condenser leaves and clouds that keep rivers running with clean water, maintain the global atmosphere, and lock up gaseous pollutants. This creates an unavoidable dependency: even anthropocentric people would be well-advised to pay close attention to and assist in the conservation of existing forests and the rehabilitation of degraded lands. Our own survival demands that we preserve all existing species and allow them a place to live.

## Three Parallel Responses to Environmental Crisis

In a world losing forests, species, and whole ecosystems, Mollison identifies three concurrent and parallel responses:

### 1. Care for Surviving Natural Assemblies
Leave the wilderness to heal itself. This means implacable and uncompromising opposition to further disturbance of any remaining natural forests where most species are still in balance. These remnants of intact ecosystem represent irreplaceable libraries of genetic and ecological information.

### 2. Rehabilitate Degraded or Eroded Land
Using complex pioneer species and long-term plant assemblies — trees, shrubs, and ground covers — restore damaged landscapes to stable, self-sustaining states. This is active intervention, not passive protection, requiring knowledge of succession, soil building, and [[water-management]].

### 3. Create Our Own Complex Living Environment
With as many species as can be saved or have need for, from wherever on Earth they come. This is the anthropocentric design component — building productive human settlements that are rich in biodiversity and largely self-sustaining.

## The Global Species Perspective

Mollison takes a deliberately global view of species and ecosystems. While local flora and fauna are recognized as "native," all life can logically be seen as "native to Earth." This perspective has practical implications:

- Jet travel has merely accelerated a process already well-established by continental drift, bird migration, wind transport, and the rafting of debris by water
- New adapted hybrids are already arising — palms, sea grasses, snails, and microorganisms from many continents are meeting, mixing, and producing new accommodations to their environments
- The chemistry of the air, soil, and water is in flux: metals, chemicals, isotopes, gases, and plastics are present on Earth that have never before existed, or never in such form and quantity

Rather than fighting this reality, Mollison argues that people should build new or recombinant ecologies from global resources, especially to stabilize degraded lands. While trying to preserve systems that are still local and diverse, they should also use all the species they need or can find in their own settlement designs, providing those species are not locally rampant and invasive.

## The Practical Conservation Imperative

Mollison insists that truly responsible conservationists have gardens which support their food needs and are working to reduce their energy needs to a modest consumption, or to that which can be supplied by local wind, water, forest, or solar power. The practical approach beyond the home garden involves:

- Forming or subscribing to institutes or organizations whose legal charter commits them to conservation activities
- Reserving even a few square meters of insect, lizard, frog, or butterfly habitat in the smallest garden
- Fencing off forest and wetland areas of critical value to local species in larger gardens and farms
- Such conservation areas should be exclusively for the preservation of local species

## The Design Framework

Mollison presents [[permaculture-design]] as the result of a unique assembly of constructs, species, and social systems into a pattern suited to a specific site and set of occupants. The design process involves:

- **Selection** of inorganic elements, organic-derived elements, and socio-legal elements
- **Assembly** into guilds, communities, technologies, and constructs
- **Site-specific consideration** of earth-shaping, water supply, and planting patterns
- **Elemental control** for best flow, function, and yield while conserving resources
- **Feedback loops** through observation, evaluation, and control

The result is a total design in evolution toward maturity — a living system that continues to develop and refine itself over time.

## Wilderness and Settlement: The Grand Bargain

The ultimate promise of [[permaculture-in-landscape-and-society]] is what might be called a grand bargain: if people can design their settlements to supply their own food, energy, and material needs from a minimal land area, they can withdraw from the majority of the agricultural landscape and allow natural systems to flourish.

This is not utopian idealism but ecological arithmetic. Dense, diverse, perennial polycultures produce far more usable yield per unit area than broadscale monocultures. When settlements are designed to catch and store their own water, generate their own energy from biomass and sunlight, and recycle their own wastes, the area needed for human support shrinks dramatically — freeing the rest of the landscape for the sole use of wildlife and re-occupation by endemic flora.

The end result of adopting permaculture strategies in any country or region will be to dramatically reduce the area of agricultural environment needed by households and settlements, and to release much of the landscape for wildlife and endemic species recovery. Respect for all life forms is not just a basic ethic but an essential one for all people.
