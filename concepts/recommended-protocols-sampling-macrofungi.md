# Recommended Protocols for Sampling Macrofungi Standardized sampling protocols are essential for producing comparable data across sites and studies. Three complementary approaches are recommended for comprehensive macrofungal inventories. ## 1. Opportunistic Sampling Carefully walking through a study site and collecting conspicuous sporocarps across as many habitats as possible. This technique does not yield quantitative data but is an important adjunct to plot-based methods because additional species will be seen "off plot" due to the patchy distribution of sporocarps. ## 2. Fixed-Size Plot Sampling for Conspicuous Macrofungi ### Plot Setup 1. Select representative areas for permanent plots in important forest or grassland types 2. Each plot: 10 transects, 100 m long, parallel at 10-m intervals 3. Transects marked every 5 m with flags/stakes (lettered A-J, numbered 1-20) 4. Total: 200 subplots of 5 m2 each = 0.1 ha per plot ### Sampling - Circumscribe 5-m2 circular subplots around each flag using a 1.262-m rope - Collect all macrofungi in each subplot - Label with transect letter and subplot number - Note substratum (soil, leaf litter, wood) for each specimen - Transport to field station for sorting, describing, photographing, and drying ### Sampling Frequency - Ideally every 2 weeks during fruiting season - If resources limited, determine peak fruiting times and adjust schedule accordingly - Based on Chicago area data, peak fruiting times can be determined from one year of intensive sampling plus herbarium records ## 3. Microlot Sampling for Small Ascomycetes ### Purpose Not to cover a lot of area but to obtain quantitative samples of microfungi growing on small substrata, especially rarely collected species. ### Protocol 1. Establish microplots adjacent to macrofungal subplots 2. Use 0.56-m or 1.128-m pipe to circumscribe 1-m2 circular subplots 3. Collect ALL microfungi on twigs, branches, and leaves within the plot 4. Sort under stereomicroscope; determine fertility 5. Describe, divide, and dry specimens 6. Note substratum (branch, twig, leaf) for each ### Sampling Intensity - Most individuals can sample only 2-3 plots per week due to time involved - Minimum: sample twice per year (middle and end of fruiting season) ## 4. Fixed-Number Log Sampling For fungi on large woody substrata (>20 cm diameter, >2 m length): 1. Select 30 logs per decay class per site (3 classes) 2. Mark, number, map, measure, and identify host species 3. Collect 2-3 sporocarps of common species; multiple of rare species 4. Section large polypores radially (1.0-1.5 cm width) for drying 5. Sample 4 times per year in tropical areas ## Data Management and Analysis - Extrapolate site richness from samples - Complementarity tests to evaluate sampling efficiency - Archive voucher specimens in recognized herbaria - Standardized data enables comparison across studies and sites ## Key Constraints The greatest constraints on macrofungal diversity studies are: 1. Paucity of fungal taxonomists 2. Limited identification resources 3. No region has a complete mycota equivalent to a vascular-plant flora

## Field Collection Methods

Field collection of macrofungi requires careful planning and
appropriate equipment. Collectors should carry a field kit
including wax paper or aluminium foil for wrapping specimens,
a hand lens (10-20x), GPS device, camera, collecting bags,
and a waterproof field notebook. Specimens should never be
placed in sealed plastic bags, as condensation promotes rapid
bacterial degradation. Each collection event should record the
date, location (GPS coordinates), habitat description, host
species (if on wood or associated with plants), substratum,
and collector name. When collecting agarics and boletes, the
entire [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] including the base of the stipe should be
excavated to preserve critical identification features such as
mycelial strands, rhizomorphs, or sclerotia that may be
present underground. For resupinate and corticioid fungi,
collect the substratum (bark, wood) with the fungus attached.

## Specimen Documentation Standards

Thorough documentation at the time of collection is essential
for later identification and ecological analysis. Photographs
should be taken in situ before disturbance, capturing both the
upper and lower surfaces of pileate fungi, habit on the
substratum, and any associated organisms. Notes should include
fresh colour descriptions using standard colour charts (e.g.,
Methuen, Munsell, or Ridgway), odour (when present), latex
colour and colour changes in Lactarius and Mycena, [[spore]]
print colour, bruising or staining reactions, and taste (for
edible groups only). Macroscopic features such as cap size,
stipe dimensions, pileus surface texture, gill attachment, and
presence or absence of an annulus or volva should all be
recorded. After transport to the field station, specimens are
sorted, fresh descriptions finalised, and [[macrofungal-documentation-photography-spore-prints]] made
on glass slides or white and black paper before drying.

## Preservation Techniques

Proper preservation ensures specimens remain useful for
[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] and molecular work. Air-drying is the
standard method for most macrofungi: specimens are placed on
wire mesh drying racks or in food dehydrators at 35-40°C with
adequate airflow. Thin-fleshed specimens dry within 12-24
hours, while thick polypores may require several days. For
molecular studies, small tissue samples should be collected
into CTAB buffer or placed directly into [[aminopropyl-silica-gel-chromatography-indole-alkaloid-purification]] at the
time of collection, before drying begins. Specimens for DNA
work should never be freeze-dried or exposed to UV light,
which fragments nucleic acids. Dried specimens should be stored
in sealed containers with silica gel desiccant packets to
prevent moisture absorption and pest damage.

## Taxonomic Identification Workflow

Initial identification proceeds from macroscopic features to
microscopic characters and increasingly to molecular data.
Begin with gross morphology: cap and stipe characteristics,
spore-bearing surface, and ecological context. Prepare thin
sections of the lamellae, pileipellis, and stipe tissue for
microscopic examination by mounting in Melzer's reagent,
Congo Red, or KOH (5-10%). Measure basidiospore dimensions
from at least 20 mature spores, noting shape, ornamentation,
and amyloidy or dextrinoidy. For difficult groups (Inocybe,
Cortinarius, Russula, Entoloma), microscopic features of the
pileipellis, cheilocystidia, and caulocystidia are often
definitive. [[biodiversity-fungal-molecular-identification-dna-barcoding]] using the ITS region has become the
standard for confirming identifications, especially for
species complexes and cryptic taxa.

## Voucher Specimen Management

Voucher specimens are physical records that anchor species
identifications and enable future verification. Each voucher
should be assigned a unique accession number and deposited in
a recognised herbarium (registered in Index Herbariorum).
Duplicates may be distributed to multiple herbaria for
broad accessibility. Labels must include the full taxonomic
name (with author citation), collection date, geographic
