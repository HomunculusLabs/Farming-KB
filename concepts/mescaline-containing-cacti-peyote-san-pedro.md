# Mescaline-Containing Cacti
## Overview
Mescaline-containing cacti represent some of the most important
hallucinogenic plants of the New World, with a history of ritual and
ceremonial use spanning thousands of years. The two principal species are
peyote ([[lophophora-williamsii]]), a small spineless cactus of the Mexican
and Texan deserts, and San Pedro ([[trichocereus-pachanoi]]), a large
columnar cactus cultivated throughout the Andes. Both produce the
phenylethylamine alkaloid mescaline, which causes spectacular colored
visual hallucinations, though the cacti are not closely related
botanically.
The Golden Guide to Hallucinogenic Plants describes peyote as "the most
spectacular hallucinogenic plant of the New World" and one of the
earliest known. The Aztecs used it, calling it peyotl, and Spanish
chroniclers documented its effects as early as the 1500s. San Pedro, by
contrast, has received less scientific attention despite its widespread
cultivation and use in Andean folk healing ceremonies that combine
indigenous ritual with imported Christian elements.
The cultural significance of these cacti extends far beyond their
pharmacology. Peyote is the central sacrament of the Native American
Church, claiming 250,000 members who consider it a medium through which
God manifests. San Pedro serves as a catalyst for divinatory healing
sessions in Peru, where it is said to guard houses like a dog and
whistle with such unearthly force that intruders flee in terror.
Archaeological finds of peyote buttons in Texas date back approximately
1,000 years.
## Key Concepts
### Peyote Botany and Distribution
Peyote (Lophophora williamsii) is a small, fleshy, spineless cactus
with a rounded gray-green top, tufts of white hair, and a long
carrot-like root. It rarely exceeds 7.5 inches in length or 3 inches
across. Two species are recognized: the widespread L. williamsii and the
local L. diffusa in Queretaro. Peyote is native to the Rio Grande valley
of Texas and northern and central Mexican plateau. When the top is
severed, the plant often sprouts new crowns, making many-headed
peyotes common in the wild.
### Mescal Button Preparation
The dried crowns of peyote are called "mescal buttons" - brown,
discoidal objects that last long periods and can be shipped to distant
points for ceremonial use. These buttons are moistened and swallowed
during rituals, with participants consuming from 4 to 30 or more
buttons in a typical ceremony. The preparation method has remained
essentially unchanged for centuries, preserving the plant's
psychoactive alkaloids through simple sun-drying.
### San Pedro Botany and Cultivation
San Pedro (Trichocereus pachanoi) is a large columnar cactus cultivated
throughout the Andes of Peru, Ecuador, and Bolivia, also called
aguacolla or giganton. Several "kinds" are recognized differing mainly
in rib count, with seven being most common. Often planted along fields
as a fence row, Trichocereus comprises about 40 species of columnar
cacti growing in subtropical and temperate parts of the Andes.
### Cimora Preparation
An intoxicating drink called cimora is made from San Pedro by slicing
short lengths of stem like bread loaves and boiling them in water for
several hours. The brew may include superstitious additions such as
cemetery dust and powdered bones, as well as other plants: Neoraimondia
macrostibas (an Andean cactus of undetermined chemistry), Pedilanthus
tithymaloides, Isotoma longiflora, and occasionally Datura. The
significance of these secondary plant ingredients in altering the
hallucinogenic effects of the brew is not fully understood.
### Effects and Experience
Peyote intoxication divides into two periods: initial contentment and
extrasensitivity, followed by artificial calm and muscular sluggishness.
Before visions appear, approximately three hours after ingestion, there
are flashes and scintillations in colors of depth and saturation that
defy description. The visions often follow a sequence from geometric
figures to unfamiliar and grotesque objects that vary with the
individual. Hallucinations of hearing, feeling, and taste often occur as
well. The same alkaloid, mescaline, is responsible for visual
hallucinations from both peyote and San Pedro.
### Native American Church Ceremony
The peyote ritual as practiced by Plains Indians takes place weekly in
an all-night teepee ceremony. Worshipers sit in a circle around a
half-moon altar of sand with a large "Father Peyote" specimen and a
sacred fire. The ceremony, led by a "roadman," consists of chanting
with rattle and drum, alternating with prayers, lessons, testimonies,
and occasionally curing rituals. Dried peyote tops are moistened and
swallowed throughout the night, with the ritual ending at dawn with
breakfast.
### Aztec and Colonial History
Spanish chroniclers reported peyote gave the Chichimeca Indians courage
to fight without fear, hunger, or thirst. Church authorities likened
its use to cannibalism and by 1720 prohibited it throughout Mexico.
Despite four centuries of persecution, peyote use spread and
strengthened, anchored so firmly in native lore that even Christianized
Indians believe a patron saint walks on hills where peyote grows.
### Huichol Peyote Pilgrimage
The Huichol peyote-collecting trip is a highly religious pilgrimage
requiring participants to forego adult experiences. It reenacts the
first peyote quest of divine ancestors. Pilgrims must confess to become
spirit and enter the sacred country through the gateway of clashing
clouds, a journey repeating the soul's passage to the underworld.
## Applications
### Ethnopharmacological Research
Mescaline was among the first hallucinogenic compounds isolated. The
chemistry of Trichocereus remains incompletely studied, and additional
alkaloids may yet be found in T. pachanoi. Research into mescaline's
mechanism of action has contributed to understanding serotonin receptor
pharmacology and remains relevant to modern neuroscience.
### Cultural Preservation
The Native American Church and Andean healing traditions represent
living practices integrating psychoactive plant use within ceremonial
frameworks. Legal protections for religious peyote use exist in some
jurisdictions, though conflicts continue.
### Horticultural Interest
Both species are valued horticulturally. Peyote grows extremely slowly
in cultivation while San Pedro is fast-growing. Legal status varies
by jurisdiction.
