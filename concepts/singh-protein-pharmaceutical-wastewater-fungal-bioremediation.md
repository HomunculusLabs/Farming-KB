# Fungal Bioremediation of Protein and Pharmaceutical Wastewaters

Pharmaceutical and protein-containing wastewaters present unique treatment
challenges due to their complex chemical composition, variable toxicity,
and persistent organic content. Fungi offer effective biological treatment
options that can simultaneously reduce waste strength, remove toxicity,
and produce valuable biomass as a byproduct.

## Pharmaceutical Wastewater

### Characteristics and Challenges

Pharmaceutical wastes are biologically active substances exposed to the
environment that can be recognized as environmental micropollutants.
These substances have specific biological effects, and antibiotics
employed as growth promoters or feed additives for animals often end up
in the environment. Few studies report results of medical substances in
field samples other than sediment or treated wastewater.

Pharmaceutical plant wastewaters vary considerably in composition. A
plant at Snackviken, Sweden, discharges approximately 1000 cubic meters
per day of wastewater with a COD of 3 to 4 g/l. Another plant at
Gartuna, Sweden, produces approximately 100 cubic meters per day with a
COD of 0.8 to 1.5 g/l. The overall BOD of pharmaceutical waste ranges
from about 44 to 752 mg/l. Production of the majority of drugs leads to
large variations in wastewater composition, requiring stable and
cost-effective treatment methods.

### Treatment Approaches

Activated carbon is used to treat the most toxic portion of
pharmaceutical wastewater. Swedish Environmental Protection Agency
investigations revealed that wastewaters from pharmaceutical plants were
highly toxic and contained large amounts of persistent organic material
and phosphorus. Previous treatment included evaporation combined with
reverse osmosis; current practice favors incineration.

Process steps include pH control, 12-hour holding in an equalization
basin, primary clarification, trickling filtration, and secondary
clarification. Sludges are removed, dewatered, and disposed of in a
landfill. The BOD of the final effluent is reduced to 30 mg/l.

### Fungal Process Development

A multistage biofilm process demonstrated the elimination of 90% of COD
and 95% of total organic carbon (TOC) with better removal of toxicity in
pilot-scale tests. The disadvantage is that pH must be adjusted to 4 in
the fungal stages and then raised to 7. Methanogenic activity tests have
been used for determining anaerobic biodegradability of concentrated
pharmaceutical wastes, including waste biomass from threonine production
and mycelium after Penicillium extraction.

Organic material, phosphorus, and toxicity can be removed to a higher
degree by incorporating fungal treatment. Treatability studies for the
degradation of specific compounds in batch and continuous laboratory-scale
processes are recommended before application in pilot-scale tests. When
researching new drugs and synthetic methods, it is important to consider
the biodegradability of discharges to wastewaters.

## Protein and Fat-Containing Wastewater

### Sources and Characteristics

Large amounts of protein- and fat-containing wastewaters released from
slaughterhouses and tanneries are discharged to the environment.
Wastewater from a Swedish company contained approximately 6 to 8 g/l of
fat and 10 to 13 g/l of protein. Slaughterhouse wastewaters contain
about 0.05% protein and 0.03% fat. The fat content includes 90% free
fatty acids, with a composition similar to tallow: 43% oleic acid, 23%
palmitic acid, 14% stearic acid, 7% linoleic acid, 4% palmitoleic acid,
2% myristic acid, and 2% linolenic acid.

### Bioconversion Process

Biodegradation of fat into yeast biomass meets two beneficial objectives:
(1) removal of fat from wastewater with easier handling of remaining waste
for subsequent processing such as methane generation, and (2) use of the
yeast biomass as a feed supplement.

Candida tropicalis S001 grows on protein-containing wastewater in a
bioconversion process involving pH lowering, heat treatment, yeast
culture at pH 4.0 with 10% CO2 in the gas phase, and sedimentation to
separate soluble protein and yeast biomass. The process yields both
soluble protein (for methane production) and yeast biomass (for feed).

### Bioreactor Performance

Cultures are performed in fermentors with batch and continuous
operations. The maximum specific growth rate of C. tropicalis S001
increases in the order triolein, glycerol, wastewater, and glucose. The
highest biomass yield (0.97 g dry weight/g fat) occurs on glycerol,
followed by triolein (0.68 g dry weight/g fat) and glucose (0.57 g dry
weight/g fat). The bioreactor reduces 86% of COD using a feed of 40 kg
COD per cubic meter per day.

A 500-ml fluidized-bed bioreactor is employed for methane production
from the protein phase of the wastewater. The rate of methane production
is 10.8 cubic meters per cubic meter reactor per day, constituting a
yield of 0.31 cubic meters methane per kg COD reduced.

### Yeast Growth at Low pH

The growth of yeasts at low pH generally provides a sterilizing effect
in continuous systems, allowing yeast domination. Most bacteria cannot
grow or compete for growth at low pH. Several yeasts are well recognized
to grow on lipids between pH 3.5 and 4.0, including Candida lipolytica,
Saccharomycopsis lipolytica, and Candida tropicalis. This pH tolerance
provides a significant operational advantage for fungal treatment of
protein- and fat-containing wastewaters.

### Substrate Utilization

Since the 1970s, researchers have focused on production of yeast cells
using fat- or protein-containing wastewater. Oil from Atlantic menhaden
has been used to cultivate several types of yeasts. Fish oil and
stickwater (a byproduct of the fish meal and oil industries) are employed
as substrates for Candida lipolytica and Geotrichum candidum,
respectively. Saccharomycopsis lipolytica can grow on tallow or lard as
a sole source of carbon and nitrogen.
