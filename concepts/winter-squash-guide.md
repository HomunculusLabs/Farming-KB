## Winter Squash Guide

Winter squash (Cucurbita species) are warm-season vining crops harvested at maturity when the rind is hard and the seeds are fully formed. Unlike summer squash, winter squash store for months and are a staple homesteading crop for winter food security. This guide covers varieties, growing, curing, and storage.

## Popular Varieties

### Butternut (Cucurbita moschata)
- Tan, bell-shaped, orange flesh.
- Sweet, nutty, dense — excellent for roasting, soups, and purees.
- Storage: 3-6 months.
- Varieties: Waltham Butternut, Honeynut (mini), Early Butternut.
- Vines are vigorous but can be trellised.

### Acorn (Cucurbita pepo)
- Dark green, ribbed, acorn-shaped (also gold and white varieties).
- Mild, slightly sweet, good for stuffing and roasting.
- Storage: 1-2 months (shorter than most winter squash).
- Varieties: Table Queen, Carnival, Honey Bear (bush type).
- More compact vines; good for small gardens.

### Spaghetti Squash (Cucurbita pepo)
- Yellow, oval, flesh separates into spaghetti-like strands when cooked.
- Mild flavor, used as a low-carb pasta substitute.
- Storage: 2-3 months.
- Varieties: Vegetable Spaghetti, Small Wonder, Hasta La Pasta.

### Delicata (Cucurbita pepo)
- Cream-colored with green stripes, oblong.
- Sweet, tender skin (edible), excellent roasted.
- Storage: 2-3 months.
- Varieties: Delicata, Honey Boat, Zeppelin.
- Compact vines; can be grown on trellises.

### Kabocha (Cucurbita maxima)
- Japanese squash, dark green or orange, dense and sweet.
- Dry, flaky, sweet flesh — exceptional for soups and roasting.
- Storage: 4-6 months.
- Varieties: Red Kuri, Buttercup, Sunshine, Black Futsu.

### Hubbard (Cucurbita maxima)
- Very large, bumpy, blue-grey or orange.
- Excellent storage (up to 6 months).
- Dense, rich flesh; good for pies and mashing.
- Varieties: Blue Hubbard, Golden Hubbard, Baby Blue Hubbard.

### Other Notable Varieties
- **Sweet Dumpling** — small, sweet, green-striped (C. pepo).
- **Red Kuri** — teardrop-shaped, nutty flavor, stores well.
- **Tromboncino (Zucchetta)** — C. moschata, can be eaten as summer or winter squash.
- **Musquee de Provence** — large, flat, ribbed, French heirloom.

## Growing Requirements

### Soil
- Well-drained, fertile soil, pH 6.0-6.8.
- Work 3-4 inches of compost into the bed before planting.
- Squash are heavy feeders — rich soil is essential for good production.
- Avoid fresh manure — increases risk of disease.

### Planting
- Direct sow after all frost danger has passed and soil is at least 65°F.
- Can start indoors 2-3 weeks before last frost (sensitive to root disturbance — use peat pots or soil blocks).
- Plant seeds 1 inch deep.
- **Spacing**: Bush types 2-3 feet apart; vining types 4-6 feet apart (rows 6-8 feet apart).
- In square foot gardens: 1 plant per 2 square feet (bush) or 1 per 4 square feet (vining with trellis).

### Sun and Heat
- Full sun (8+ hours daily).
- Warm-season crop — thrives in 75-90°F.
- Does not tolerate frost.
- Black plastic mulch can warm soil 2-3 weeks earlier.

## Care During Growth

### Watering
- 1-2 inches per week.
- Critical during flowering and fruit development.
- Drip irrigation ideal; avoid wetting foliage ([[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]]).
- Mulch heavily to conserve moisture.

### Fertilizing
- Heavy feeder: side-dress with compost or balanced [[complete-organic-fertilizer]] when vines begin to run.
- Additional side-dress when fruits begin to set.
- Too much nitrogen = lush vines, few fruits.

### Pest Pressure

| Pest | Damage | Control |
|------|--------|---------|
| Squash bugs | Suck sap, wilt and kill plants | Hand-pick eggs (bronze clusters under leaves), neem oil, row covers early |
| Squash vine borer | Larvae bore into stem, wilt plant | Row covers until flowering, slit stem and remove larva, Bt, wrap stem bases with foil |
| Cucumber beetles | Strip leaves, spread bacterial wilt | Row covers, kaolin clay, [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] |
| Powdery mildew | White coating on leaves, reduces yield | Good airflow, neem oil, potassium bicarbonate spray, resistant varieties |

### Pollination
- Squash have separate male and female flowers on the same plant.
- Male flowers appear first; female flowers have a miniature squash at the base.
- Pollination requires bees — avoid insecticides during flowering.
- Poor pollination = misshapen or aborted fruit.
- Hand pollination: transfer pollen from male to female flower with a small brush in early morning.

## Harvesting

### When to Harvest
- Harvest when the rind is hard and cannot be easily pierced with a fingernail.
- The stem should be dry and corky.
- The fruit should sound hollow when tapped.
- Harvest before hard frost — frost damages the rind and reduces storage life.
- Leave 2-3 inches of stem attached — no stem = entry point for rot.

### How to Harvest
- Cut (do not pull) fruit from the vine with pruners or a sharp knife.
- Leave stem attached — this is critical for curing and storage.
- Handle gently — bruising reduces storage life.
- If harvested before full maturity (before frost threat), squash will continue to ripen in storage but won't store as long.

## Curing

Curing thickens the skin and heals minor cuts, dramatically improving storage life.

- **Cucurbita moschata and C. maxima** (butternut, kabocha, hubbard): Cure 10-20 days at 75-85°F, 60-75% humidity. These benefit most from curing.
- **Cucurbita pepo** (acorn, spaghetti, delicata): Cure 7-10 days at 75-80°F. These have thinner skins and benefit less from curing — some gardeners skip it for acorn squash.
- Place in a single layer, not touching, in a warm dry place.
- Ensure good airflow.

## Storage

### Ideal Conditions
- Temperature: 50-60°F (cool but NOT refrigerator-cold).
- Humidity: 50-70% (drier than root cellar conditions).
- Well-ventilated, dark, single layer or on shelves (not stacked).
- Check weekly — remove any squash with soft spots or mold immediately.
