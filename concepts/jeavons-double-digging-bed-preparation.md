# Double-Digging Bed Preparation

The goal of bed preparation is to develop soil structure so the plants will have a "living sponge cake" in which to thrive. Double-digging loosens the soil to a depth of 24 inches below the surface.

## Raised Beds vs. Rows

Traditional planting rows are only a few inches wide with wide spaces in between. Plants have difficulty growing in these rows due to extreme penetration of air and greater fluctuations in [[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]]. During irrigation, water floods the rows, immerses the roots, and washes soil away. The beneficial microbiotic life — concentrated in the upper 6 inches of soil — is destroyed.

Raised beds (3 to 6 feet wide, of varying lengths) provide a curved surface area with more surface area for penetration and interaction of natural elements. When raised beds are prepared in friable soil, the edges are included in the mini-climate effect created by closely spaced plants.

## Tools

- **D-handled flat spade** — Digs equally deep all along its edge rather than in a pointed V pattern. The blade goes into the soil at less of an angle.
- **D-handled spading fork** — For loosening subsoil.
- **Digging board** — A 3/8-inch-thick plywood board, 2 to 3 feet long by 3 to 5 feet wide, to stand on and avoid compacting the soil.
- **Rake** — For leveling soil.

People with back problems should check with their physician before proceeding with the physically active process of double-digging.

## Soil Moisture for Digging

Only dig when the soil is evenly moist. Digging hard, dry soil breaks down the structure. Wet soil is heavy and easily compacted.

- **Too dry:** Loose and will not hold its shape after being squeezed in the palm (sands/loams), or hard and cannot be penetrated (clays).
- **Too wet:** Sticks to the spade as you dig.
- **Just right:** Forms a ball when squeezed, holds shape, breaks apart with slight pressure.

## The Initial Double-Dig Process (Step by Step)

1. **When soil is lightly moist**, loosen the top 12 inches of the entire area to be dug with a spading fork, and remove any weeds.

2. **Spread a 1-inch layer of compost** over the entire area to be dug.

3. **Remove the soil** from the upper part of the first trench (1 foot deep and 1 foot wide across the width of the bed), and place it in a soil storage area for use in making compost and flat soil. A 5-foot-wide bed fills about 7 five-gallon buckets.

4. **Loosen the soil an additional 12 inches** with a spading fork by digging the tool in to its full depth and pushing the handle downward so the fork tines lever through the soil, loosening and aerating it. For compacted soil, lift out tight soil sections on the fork and allow them to break apart as they fall back onto the tines.

5. **Dig out the upper part of the second trench** (1 foot deep and 1 foot wide), and move each spadeful of soil forward into the first trench, mixing the soil layers as little as possible.

6. **Loosen the lower part of the second trench** another 12 inches.

7. **Continue the double-digging process** (repeating steps 3, 4, 5, and 6) for the remaining trenches. Rake after every 3 to 4 trenches to ensure even bed height.

8. **Shape the bed by raking it.** Then evenly spread any needed fertilizers over the entire area, and sift them in 2 to 4 inches deep with a spading fork.

## Key Principles

- **Do not turn over the top 12 inches of soil.** Most of the microbiotic life lives in the upper 6 inches. The natural layering of the soil should be preserved.
- **Slide soil forward** from one trench into another. Some of the compost layer slides 3 to 6 inches down into the trench, creating a small mound — approximating how nature adds leaves and decaying vegetation to the top of the soil.
- **Use Aikido-like economy of motion.** Conserve energy — virtually just shift balance and weight rather than digging.
- **The first year**, you may only reach 15 to 18 inches. Be satisfied. Nature, worms, and plant roots will further loosen the soil with each crop, increasing depth 3 to 6 inches annually.

## Types of Deep Soil Preparation

Ecology Action uses four basic types:

1. **Initial Double-Dig** — The standard first-time preparation described above.

2. **Ongoing Double-Dig** — Performed after the initial dig. Follows the same process but the soil is easier to work.

3. **Complete Texturizing Double-Dig** — Developed to improve soil quality more rapidly. Used one time only, usually in place of the initial double-dig. Further loosens and aerates the subsoil.

4. **U-Bar Dig** — Used in soil that is in reasonably good shape (after one or more normal double-digs). The U-bar has a 18-inch-wide blade with 4 to 6 tines. It aerates the soil approximately 10 inches deep, more quickly than double-digging with a spade and fork. Do a normal double-dig as often as increased compaction requires.

## Timing

- Perform double-digging at least once a year, normally at the beginning of the main [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].
- For autumn crops, the practice is often to only single-dig and add no compost or fertilizers.
- A skilled gardener can complete a double-dig in about 2 hours for a 100-square-foot bed.
- For a beginner, allow 4 to 6 hours per 100-square-foot bed.

## The Living Sponge Cake: Soil Structure Goals

The fundamental goal of all bed preparation is to develop soil structure so plants have a "living sponge cake" in which to thrive. This sponge-like structure is created and maintained by the interaction of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] (especially humus from cured compost), microbial life, plant roots, air, and water. The double-digging process initiates this structure by loosening compacted soil to a depth of 24 inches, and the ongoing addition of compost sustains it.

In a well-prepared bed, the soil should be friable — crumbly and loose rather than hard or compacted. When you squeeze a handful of moist soil and release it, it should hold its shape briefly, then break apart gently. This texture allows roots to penetrate easily, water to infiltrate and be retained, air to circulate, and microbial populations to thrive.
