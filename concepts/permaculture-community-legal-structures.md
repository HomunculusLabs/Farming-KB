## [[permaculture-community-enterprises-and-self-reliance|Permaculture]] Community Legal Structures

The legal and financial architecture supporting [[permaculture]] communities is as important as their [[gaias-garden-ecological-design-process-checklist]]. [[bill-mollison]] developed a sophisticated organizational model that uses standard legal instruments—companies, trusts, and non-profit structures—to create insulated, tax-efficient, and internationally scalable systems for community-based [[integrated-land-management-and-polyculture-design]] and trading. This model, tested through the Tagari community in Australia, demonstrates how [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]] can be applied to social and financial systems.

## Core Principles

The foundation of any [[permaculture-community-legal-structure]] rests on agreement to three [[holmgren-ethical-principles-care-earth-people-fair-shares]]:

1. **Earth care** — stewardship of ecological systems
2. **People care** — responsibility toward human welfare
3. **Non-profiteering** — not accumulating wealth beyond one"s needs

A community can be defined as a group of designers, people sharing a house, or individuals scattered across the globe—all connected by agreement to these principles.

## The Three-Entity Structure

### Level 1: The Institute (Public Trust Company)

The community forms an ordinary company that acts as a trustee. This company, which Mollison called the [[permaculture-institute]], does not trade and takes no cash flow in or out. It issues shares of one dollar to each member, with all members eligible to serve as directors.

**Functions:**
- Acts in areas of health, education, and agriculture
- Provides a broad legal basis for public-benefit activities
- Exists as a publicly oriented trust "for the good of all [citizens]"

**Advantages:**
- **Immunity from land taxes and rates** and local government charges
- **Automatic membership** in networks like the World Free Seed Exchange (accessing 4,600 institutes and 680 botanical gardens worldwide at no cost)
- **Joint research capacity** with any similar institute, including shared funding, staff arrangements, and knowledge exchange
- **Insulated legal status** — the same protections that shield schools, churches, and public institutions from government interference apply to this structure
- **Tax-exempt donations** — contributions to the Institute are typically tax deductible

Mollison noted that raiding or dissolving such a trust is "uncommon, almost unheard of" and pointed out that wealthy families structure their own money flow systems using identical mechanisms, with the Ford Foundation as a prominent example.

### Level 2: The Trading Trust (Non-Profit Trading Corporation)

The second trust is a non-profit trading corporation that is legally separate from the Institute but under the same company"s trusteeship. This is where all business activity occurs.

**Key [[metzner-shamanic-ceremony-versus-psychotherapy-structural-features]]:**
- Occupies structures **loaned at no cost** by the Institute (which it funds)
- **Staffed free** by community members
- **Owns no buildings** and employs no staff formally
- Borrows moveable fixtures, furnishings, and equipment from an external entity (such as a political party)
- Carries **no chattels** of its own

This structure mirrors merchant banking systems worldwide, where the trading entity exists without physical assets that could be at risk. Political parties in Australia enjoy unique privileges—they need not declare income, membership, or pay traffic fines, and because they are not corporate entities, they pay no income tax.

**Appropriate businesses for the Trading Trust include:**
- **Travel agencies** — generating 15% discounts, free tickets, and potentially free international flights
- **Publishing companies** — information distribution as a core community function
- **Consultancies** — global knowledge transmission through trained agents
- **Real estate operations** — property management within the community network
- **Seed companies** — as in Tagari's case, owned directly by the Trading Trust

The Trading Trust can also hold shares in consumer co-ops, workers' co-ops, and unemployment co-ops, and can participate internationally in trading cooperation.

### Level 3: External Support

The third element is an external body—typically a political party or small external society—that loans all moveable fixtures, furnishings, vehicles, tractors, typewriters, desks, and chairs to the Trading Trust. This separation ensures that the trading operation carries no asset risk.

## Money Flow Systems

### Revenue Sources

Money enters the system through multiple channels:

1. **Trading operations** — the primary source, generated by businesses run under the Trading Trust
2. **Government business aids** — automatic, non-application-based support for exporters
3. **Public donations** — occasional substantial gifts, often for specific projects
4. **Agent networks** — international agents generating remission of travel and accommodation costs

### Government Export Incentives (Australian Model)

Australia's export incentive system provides a powerful illustration of how community structures can leverage government policy:

- All businesses engaged in exporting automatically receive remission of portions of overseas travel fares
- Consultancy and knowledge are classified as exports
- Export remission can reach approximately 90% of costs
- Agents appointed in any country receive the same fare remissions plus full accommodation cost remission
- If the community also operates a travel agency, additional airline discounts may apply, potentially making a world trip profitable

The annual cycle works as follows: an accountant prepares accounts in June, reports to the Department of Trade and Industry, and refunds are issued in November. This is described as "normal channels" of capital flow from government to business—no applications, no bureaucrats, no forms for clients.

### Government Invention Funding

The Australian government fully funds and refunds the development cost of any salable invention or device. This includes all productivity development costs and is automatic rather than application-based.

## Profit Distribution

The Trading Trust, as a non-profit corporation, must distribute all profits in four ways:

1. **To the Research Institute** — the largest portion, creating double tax exemption (not taxable as income, and tax deductible as a donation)
2. **To public charities** — including ethnic and aboriginal groups, providing a second tax deduction
3. **To a political party** — donations exactly equaling the cost of typewriters, desks, vehicles, and tractors (the party then purchases these items for the community"s use)
4. **To workers and dependents** — what Mollison calls a "thin miserable stream" (at Tagari, $21 per week per person regardless of age or gender)

## Worker Economics

Community workers in the Trading Trust system live below the minimum income level, paying no income tax. At Tagari, every person received identical compensation—$21 per week—covering clothes and incidentals. The community provided transport, seeds, and (formerly) bulk food. Clothing came from charitable organizations like the Vincent de Paul Society.

Mollison described this group as "the most heavily self-taxed group" in Australia, since virtually all their money flows to the public. Workers at the seed company who were community members took only $21 per week and returned all additional earnings to the Trading Trust.

## Land Management

### Land Acquisition

Land enters the system exclusively through the Trading Trust, not the Institute, because the Institute cannot take risks. The process follows strict protocols:

1. **Trading departments** handle initial deposits on property
2. **The Trading Trust pays all transfer costs**
3. **Part-owned properties** remain in the risk area (Trading Trust)
4. **Fully owned properties**, once all costs are paid off, pass into the Institute as a gift

Land can come from multiple sources:
- Public donations
- Government transfers (including unwanted local government land)
- Cooperative shares in enterprises (exchanged for design work)
- Copyrights and intellectual property

### Property Types

Properties accumulated include warehouses, office buildings, inner urban sites, suburban lots, rural acreage, and preservation areas. These can be used for:
- Trading activities sponsored by the Trust
- Health and retreat purposes
- Educational facilities and schools
- Distribution to communities, cooperatives, or individuals

### Tenancy Arrangements

Land distributed from the Institute can be held under various tenancy types:
- **Limited tenancy**
- **Life tenancy** (inheritable or non-inheritable)
- **Transferable and inheritable tenancy**
- **Public land** (open to all)

Houses not yet fully paid off remain in the Trading Trust and are rented to community members. Rent is remitted for those working within the Trust; those who leave incur full rent.

### Insulation from Risk

The Institute cannot accept properties with any attachments, claims, or encumbrances. Land must be fully owned and heavily insulated from outside claims. The Trading Trust has stopped accepting land that does not "pay its own way" in terms of the time and money required to process the transfer (sometimes $4,000-5,000 per property).

### Scale of Land Accumulation

Mollison claimed there is "absolutely no problem" in acquiring several million dollars worth of land—hundreds of square miles—within 12 months, provided a group is organized to handle the logistics. Offers have been received from Great Britain, France, Africa, islands, and all Australian states. The limiting factor is finding people willing to live on and manage the land.
