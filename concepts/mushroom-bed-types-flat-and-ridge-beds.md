# Mushroom Bed Types — Flat and Ridge Beds

## Overview

Nearly every experienced mushroom grower develops preferences regarding the small details of making beds and caring for them during the growing period. However, all bed types can be referred to two fundamental systems: the **flat bed** (commonly known as the English type) and the **ridge bed** (the favorite with the French). Each system has distinct advantages and is suited to different growing situations, and the choice between them significantly affects labor requirements, space utilization, yield, and mushroom quality.

## The Flat Bed (English Type)

### Description and Application

The flat bed is the system more generally employed in the United States. It may be used in ordinary cellars or mushroom houses where beds are made on the floor only, and is equally applicable in mushroom houses or deep cellars where it is desirable to use floor space while simultaneously arranging shelves in tiers.

### Advantages

- **Less labor required** in construction compared to ridge beds
- **Simpler technique**: More accessible to beginners and less skilled workers
- **Versatility**: Adaptable to both single-tier floor installations and multi-tier shelf systems

### Multi-Tier Shelf Systems

The shelf-bed variant of the flat system is particularly important for commercial economics. Shelf beds can multiply available growing space **four to five times** compared to a single-tier floor layout. This multiplication of bed space is essential for operating on a paying basis, since a single tier of beds would make it necessary to invest perhaps twice as much in the plant (building, heating, infrastructure) for the same amount of bed space when arranged in three tiers.

The trade-off is that multi-tier arrangements involve some sacrifice of convenience in bed installation, maintenance, and harvesting. Working conditions are more cramped, and material handling requires more planning.

### Dimensions

- **Depth**: About **8 to 10 inches** after suitable compression
- **Width (wall beds)**: Seldom more than 3 feet across (accessible from one side only)
- **Width (center beds)**: Up to 6 feet across (approachable from either side)
- **Aisles**: At least 3 feet wide; one or more wider aisles may be used in very large houses

### Sided vs. Unsided Beds

To the American commercial grower, the sort of flat bed that is almost invariably preferred is the **sided bed**—a bed with wooden boards forming retaining walls on the sides. In the author's opinion, this type of bed—other conditions being equal—encourages the formation of heavier mushrooms. Unsided flat beds require a gentle slope to prevent collapse, similar to the ridge bed principle.

### Compression

The compost for flat beds is pitched into the designated area and immediately arranged and somewhat compressed with blows from a shovel, or "firmed" with a compressing board, to prevent it from becoming loose and dry. Compression should not be so severe as to involve hammering with a mallet, but the bed should be firm enough even before spawning to support a person without sinking more than 1 or 2 inches.

## The Ridge Bed (French Type)

### Description and Application

In the relatively low-roofed caves of Paris, the [[ridge-bed-system]] is exclusively practiced, and there it has its distinct advantages. The ridge bed takes its name from its cross-sectional shape—broad at the base and tapering to a narrow crest.

### Advantages

- **More effective sanitation**: The increased surface area and exposed slopes facilitate air circulation and reduce conditions favorable to disease
- **Increased floor space**: Slightly more growing surface area per unit of floor space than flat beds
- **Improved yield potential**: Appears to increase yield possibilities overall

### Disadvantages

- **More labor** both in preparation of the bed and in subsequent care
- **Variable yield distribution**: The first and last parts of the crop harvested from ridge beds tend to run lighter in weight compared with the product from continuous flat beds
- **Steepness sensitivity**: If the slope is too steep, mushrooms will be smaller (subject to more rapid drying and clusters pulling away from their attachment to the spawn below)

### Dimensions

- **Base width**: About **20 to 24 inches**
- **Crest width**: Not more than **6 inches** across
- **Height**: **12 to 14 inches**
- **Grouping**: Arranged in groups of two, with 10 to 12 inches of floor space between each pair (reduced to about 9 inches after casing)
- The two beds in each group may be in contact throughout their length

### The Slope Principle

The dimensions indicated give ridge beds a gentle slope from crest to floor, which permits the production of heavy mushroom clusters. This slope principle applies equally to unsided flat beds. A gentle slope allows the mushrooms to develop against a supportive surface while maintaining adequate moisture retention and structural attachment to the [[mycelium]] below. If the slope is too steep, the mushrooms develop smaller because they dry out more rapidly and the clusters pull away from their spawn attachment before reaching maximum size.

## Depth and Bottom Heat

The author saw no necessity for giving beds such depth that bottom heat may be temporarily maintained. It is preferable that, with uniform conditions, the temperature of the beds should very soon approximate that of the air. However, in special situations:

- **Cold frames or exposed locations**: Where temperature is likely to fall below requirements, greater depth and a small amount of fresh manure at the bottom may help maintain slight and fairly uniform bottom heat
- **Open-air cultures in England**: This method of using fresh manure for bottom heat is commonly employed

## Species-Specific Bed Requirements

The desirability of growing all varieties of mushrooms in the same type of bed is frequently questioned. There are undoubtedly different requirements to be met between such species as *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. fabaceus*, or *A. arvensis* and *A. silvicola*. Much experimental work was required before any definite rules could be laid down governing the practices with these diverse forms.

## Choosing Between Systems

The choice between flat and ridge beds depends on:

1. **Growing environment**: Low caves favor ridge beds; purpose-built houses favor flat beds
2. **Labor availability**: Ridge beds require more skilled labor
3. **Economic considerations**: Multi-tier flat beds maximize return on building investment
4. **Market requirements**: Sided flat beds tend to produce heavier mushrooms preferred by the hotel trade
5. **Regional tradition**: American growers overwhelmingly preferred flat beds; French cave growers exclusively used ridge beds

The flat bed's dominance in American commercial practice reflected its better adaptation to the purpose-built mushroom houses that characterized the American industry, as well as its lower labor requirements and suitability for the tiered shelving systems that maximized space utilization.
