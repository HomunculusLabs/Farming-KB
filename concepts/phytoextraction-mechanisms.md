# Phytoextraction Mechanisms and Applications

Phytoextraction is a phytoremediation technology that uses plants to absorb contaminants from soil, concentrate them in aboveground tissues, and remove them from the site through harvest of the contaminated biomass.

This approach is most commonly applied to [[rhizofiltration]].

## Mechanisms of Metal Uptake and Accumulation

Metal phytoextraction involves a sequence of physiological processes: mobilization of metals from soil to the rhizosphere, uptake across root cell membranes, translocation from roots to shoots, and sequestration within leaf and stem tissues.

Each step in this sequence must function efficiently for effective phytoextraction, and the rate-limiting step varies depending on the plant species and the metal involved.

[[rhizofiltration]] provides detailed coverage of known hyperaccumulator species and their metal specificities.

Nickel hyperaccumulation is the most common type, with over 400 known nickel hyperaccumulators found primarily on ultramafic (serpentine) soils in tropical and subtropical regions.

Alyssum species (Brassicaceae) are well-known nickel hyperaccumulators that have been tested in field-scale phytoextraction trials.

Zinc and cadmium hyperaccumulation is exemplified by Thlaspi caerulescens (Noccaea caerulescens) and Arabidopsis halleri, which can accumulate zinc at concentrations exceeding 30,000 mg/kg in their leaves.

Arsenic hyperaccumulation by Pteris vittata (Chinese brake fern) is unique among fern species and has been extensively studied for arsenic phytoextraction from contaminated soils.

Lead phytoextraction is challenging because lead has low solubility and bioavailability in most soils, but Indian mustard (Brassica juncea) and sunflower (Helianthus annuus) have demonstrated moderate lead accumulation when soil amendments enhance lead availability.

## Chelator-Assisted Phytoextraction

Chelator-assisted phytoextraction involves the application of synthetic chelating agents to soil to enhance metal solubility and bioavailability for plant uptake.

EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) is the most commonly studied chelating agent for this purpose, effectively solubilizing lead, cadmium, zinc, and other metals from soil.

EDTA forms stable water-soluble complexes with metal ions that are readily taken up by plant roots and translocated to shoots.

However, the use of EDTA raises environmental concerns because the metal-EDTA complexes are highly mobile and can leach to groundwater, potentially spreading contamination beyond the treatment zone.

Alternative chelating agents including EDDS (ethylenediamine-N,N'-disuccinic acid), DTPA (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid), and NTA (nitrilotriacetic acid) have been evaluated as more biodegradable alternatives to EDTA.

Natural chelating agents including citric acid, malic acid, and other organic acids can also enhance metal phytoextraction, though their effects are generally less pronounced than synthetic chelators.

The timing and rate of chelator application must be optimized to maximize metal uptake while minimizing leaching risk.
