**Village Size and Social Scale** — Human settlements vary in their capacity to
provide resources, develop self-reliance, and foster neighbourly behaviour
according to population size and function. At approximately 100 income-producing
people, a significant financial institution can be village-based. At around 500,
all residents can know each other if social affairs are properly organised.
Beyond 2,000 people, theft and competitiveness become more common, and sects
form in opposition to one another, eroding the ecumenical alliances that sustain
social cohesion. Mollison recommends starting small at roughly 30 adults,
building gradually to 200–300 people, and proceeding slowly by choice to 500, at
which point the village should "calve" into new neighbourhoods or daughter
villages. Alliances of 200–500 household-size hamlets can form viable
manufacturing or trading confederations while maintaining a safe genetic base.
Many traditional tribes of roughly 200 people confederated into alliances of
3,000–7,000, sharing special products by trade and arranging out-marriages,
providing a model for pioneer village networks.

**The Mondragon Cooperative Model** — The [[mondragon-cooperatives]] of Spain
initially grew to 3,000–5,000 members but later deliberately restructured into
cooperatives of 300–500 to preserve the identity and recognition of every
individual. Nevertheless, a confederation of such smaller cooperatives can
manufacture any vehicle or machine if each unit produces a component, and this
is in fact the organisational pattern used within the Mondragon system. The
model demonstrates that small-scale units confederated into larger networks can
achieve industrial capacity without sacrificing community cohesion.

**The Neighbourhood Factor** — Knowledge of each other"s names and circumstances
is identified as a primary factor in community survival. This was assessed in
events such as the 1967 Hobart fires, where casualties in "anonymous" commuter
suburbs were many times higher than in neighbourhoods where people knew and
cared for one another. The implication for [[integrated-land-management-and-village-design]] is clear: settlements should be sized and structured to ensure
that residents can recognise and maintain relationships with all other members
of the community.

**Infrastructure Reservations** — A self-reliant village must reserve land and
areas for future priority development of both structures and [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]]. The following categories of infrastructure
should be planned from the outset: school, seminar, and workshop rooms including
computers, library, artwork, and craft areas; food processing centres, cafés,
coffee shops, home-baked goods, and commercial surplus sale facilities; noisy or
oily work areas including woodwork, metalwork, machine shops, repairs, bulk
fuels and oils, and vehicle service; retail shops including plant nursery and
visitor reception; dairy and [[faires-dairy-processing-cheese-and-butter]]
centres; domestic livestock housing for chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, and
rabbits; methane generators and ferment tanks, sewage and sullage systems,
biomass conversion, and water clean-up; glasshouses and commercial or special
crop areas; and camp areas for casual visitors with toilets and showers,
potentially serving as a youth or student hostel.

**Community Tools and Equipment** — Essential site-wide tools include a tractor
with slasher for fire control, a posthole borer for fencing, a trailer, a chisel
plough or soil conditioner, a chipper for biomass and mulch production and fire
control, a tow truck for mulch and goods transport, a van, and a back-pack
slasher for blackberry and brush control. In workshops, essential tools such as
a drill press, lathe, radial-arm saw, welder, planer or thicknesser, and router
can be made available on lease or time-share. Trust B can charge for replacement
cost, wear and tear, fuel or power, and a service fee for tool use.

**Planning Priority Sequence** — The correct planning sequence for
[[permaculture-village-development]] is: first design for water and energy
systems; then establish access routes; then plan dwellings and other structures;
then indicate land use; and finally discuss legal, social, and financial systems
appropriate to the place and time. This sequence ensures that the most
fundamental and site-dependent elements are addressed before the more flexible
and adaptable components.

**Dwelling Diversity** — Villages need a mix of dwelling types to serve
different needs and life stages: family homes with individual or trust ownership
and two to five bedrooms; singles quarters as flats with strata title, some
guest accommodation, and one to two bedrooms; elderly and hospital quarters with
strata and community trust title in single and double bedroom configurations;
and terrace housing where appropriate. A mix of housing types provides far more
for varying needs and age differences than the traditional single-family home
model. Every village should maintain at least one empty strata title for
emergencies such as family breakups or to stage people into permanent housing.

**Energy-Efficient [[mollison-designers-passive-solar-heating-building-design]]** — Modern buildings can approach 100 percent space heating efficiency
through design alone, at no extra construction cost. Solar hot water systems are
routine, and photovoltaics are approaching 30 percent efficiency. The key
principle is that energy needs are solved primarily in the home through a
combination of good passive design and appropriate hardware. Some American towns
such as Davis, California, have enacted energy-conserving legislation requiring
60 percent self-sufficient space heating, encouraging non-polluting home
businesses, and providing bikeways throughout settled areas.

**Integrated Live-Work-Recreation** — Wherever possible, life, work, and
recreation should be integrated within a dwelling and its immediate
surroundings. This integration yields better-informed households, less alienated
children, and less isolated adults, while eliminating the need for complex
transport systems. Historical examples include Florence, Vienna, older Berlin,
and virtually every pre-1930 village, where cultural, crafts, trades,
commercial, and domestic functions coexisted. Old Berlin blocks featured housing
over street-level shops on the sunny side, trades in accessible interior
courtyards, and market depots nearby. In Istanbul and India, trades are grouped
by street or market area so that materials, assembly, and sale are all
facilitated within the total market neighbourhood.

**Land Allotment Categories** — Land not attached to individual dwellings should
be allotted across these categories: land for public buildings and services;
land for recreation, trails, and walks; water storages, reserves, and energy
sites; productive land for food including livestock, orchards, nurseries, and
greenhouses; forested areas for fuels and timbers; reserves for wildlife and
flora conservation; easements, wayleaves, roads, and public utility allotments;
and commercial areas for retail, manufacturing, offices, professional services,
and common markets.

**Shared Facilities and Vehicle Leasing** — Public access service centres owned
by the village — such as food processing, freezer, and laundrette facilities —
provide part-time income for a resident while sharply reducing per-household
energy needs. A modest tractor-tool-truck hire service, where Trust B leases
infrequently used assets to residents as needed, yields even greater savings. A
total vehicle leasing system with fleet purchase and insurance, local
maintenance, and bulk fuel supply should be closely investigated by any sensible
village development group.
