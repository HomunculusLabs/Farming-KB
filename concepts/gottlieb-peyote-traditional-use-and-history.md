The Navajo peyote ritual makes wise use of the different phases of the peyote
experience:

- **First part of ceremony**: Participants submit to the feeling and let the peyote
  teach them (corresponding to the initial phase of rapid thought flow and altered
  consciousness)
- **Latter part of ritual**: The mind turns to thoughtful contemplation and
  understanding with the conscious intellect what the peyote has taught the
  subconscious mind (corresponding to the later phase of more ordered thought)

passed there is no comedown. One is likely to feel pleasantly relaxed and much at

### Wound Healing

For ages the Huichol Indians have rubbed the juices of fresh peyote into wounds to
prevent infection and to promote healing. This practice is supported by the finding
that [[hordenine]] and tyramine (alkaloids present in peyote) possess antibacterial
activity due to their phenolic function.

### Two Sacred Forms

The Huichol and other tribes recognize two forms of peyote:

- **Tzinouritehua-hikuri** (peyote of the Gods): Larger, more potent, more bitter
- **Rhaitoumuanitari-hikuri** (peyote of the goddesses): Smaller, more palatable,
  milder

### Pilgrimage Tradition

The Huichol make long pilgrimages to the sacred places where peyote grows in search
of the sacrament. They believe that if a person has not been properly purified, the
spirits will lead them to the False Peyote (Ariocarpus retusus, known as tsuwiri),
and if they partake of it they will suffer madness or at least a [[EMCDDA]].

## Tarahumara Indian Uses

### Endurance Running

The Tarahumara Indians consume small amounts of peyote to combat hunger, thirst and
exhaustion, especially while hunting. They have been known to run for days after a
deer with no food, water or rest.

### Folkloric Medicine

Peyote has many uses in Tarahumara folkloric medicine including treatment of:

- Arthritis
- Consumption (tuberculosis)
- Influenza
- Intestinal disorders
- Diabetes
- Snake bites
- Scorpion bites
- Datura poisoning

## Peyote Substitutes of Central Mexico

Several cacti are used by the Tarahumares and other tribes of central Mexico as
substitutes for peyote:

### Peyotillo (Pelecyphora aselliformis)

Also known as the hatchet cactus because of its oddly flattened tubercles. Found in
San Luis Potosi. Contains traces of [[mescaline]] too minute to have any effect, plus
small amounts of several other alkaloids.

### Tsuwiri / False Peyote (Ariocarpus retusus)

Known as Chautle or Chaute among some tribes. Contains hordenine, N-methyltryamine,
and traces of other alkaloids. The amounts appear insufficient for psychopharmacological
response.
