### Phase 2: Harvest and Transition (October–November)

The rice is harvested by hand while walking carefully on the young shoots of the winter grain that were broadcast over the field before the rice harvest. After the rice is cut, it is dried for three days, then threshed. All of the rice straw is scattered uncut over the entire field, creating a thick mulch layer. If chicken droppings are available, they are scattered over the straw as a supplementary nutrient source (though this is optional).

### Phase 3: Winter Grain Growing Season (November–May)

The winter grain (barley or wheat) grows through the straw mulch and the recovering clover. During the winter, the clover enters a period of vigorous growth, building its root system and fixing nitrogen that will become available to subsequent crops. The grain matures through the winter and early spring, drawing nutrients from the decomposing rice straw and the clover-fixed nitrogen.

### Phase 4: Winter Grain Harvest and Rice Planting (May–June)

The winter grain is harvested, dried, and threshed. All straw is again scattered uncut over the field. Before the New Year, clay pellets containing rice seed have been scattered over the straw mulch. These pellets protect the rice seed until conditions are right for germination. In late May, the rice shoots are visible emerging from the clay pellets through the clover cover.

### Phase 5: Flooding for Transition (June)

The field is flooded for four to five days. This temporary flooding weakens the clover enough to give the rice seedlings a competitive advantage, but does not kill the clover — it quickly recovers once the water is drained. The flooding also creates conditions favorable for rice growth and suppresses certain weeds that cannot tolerate waterlogging.

### Phase 6: Minimal Summer Management (June–August)

After the transition flooding, the field is drained. For the remainder of the growing season, water is run through the drainage ditches once every week or ten days. No standing water is maintained (unlike conventional paddy rice, which is kept flooded throughout the growing season). This minimal irrigation is sufficient because the clover mulch and the straw cover maintain soil moisture.

## The Straw Mulch Cycle

The return of all crop straw to the field is essential to the system's sustainability. In conventional rice farming, straw is often removed and burned, exported from the field as a waste product. In Fukuoka's system, straw is the primary vehicle for nutrient cycling:

- Rice straw returned in autumn feeds the winter grain crop and the soil microbiome.
- Barley/wheat straw returned in spring feeds the rice crop and the soil microbiome.
- The continuous cycle of straw addition and decomposition maintains soil organic matter levels and supports the diverse [[fukuoka-scattering-straw-technique]] in autumn
