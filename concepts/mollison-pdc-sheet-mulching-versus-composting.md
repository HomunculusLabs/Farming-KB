# [[permaculture-design]]

## Overview

In Bill Mollison's Permaculture [[permaculture-design-course-methodology]] (Pamphlet IX: Permaculture
Techniques), he presents a strongly argued case for sheet mulching over
traditional composting as the primary soil-building strategy for home
gardens and small-scale food systems. Mollison contends that composting,
while useful in specific contexts, fundamentally destroys nutrients and
represents unnecessary labor compared to the simpler, more effective
approach of sheet mulching directly on garden beds.

## The Nutrient Loss Problem with Composting

Mollison argues that every time you compost materials, you dramatically
decrease the nutrients available to plants. He estimates that you get only
about one-twelfth of the nutrient value from compost that you would obtain
from the same material applied as mulch. The composting process consumes
nutrients rather than preserving them. When organic material is
concentrated in a [[mushroom-cultivator-compost-pile-management]] and allowed to break down under hot
conditions, much of the nutrient content is lost to the atmosphere through
volatilization. The result is a nutrient-poor end product compared to the
original inputs.

The practical implication is that gardeners who rely primarily on compost
are working hard to create a substance that is significantly less valuable
than the raw materials they started with. Mollison states plainly: "We
want to get right out of composting. We want to get back into sheet
mulching."

## How Sheet Mulching Works

Sheet mulching spreads organic materials directly across the soil surface
where decomposition occurs slowly and in situ. Any leach loss goes
directly into the soil rather than being lost to the air. The general
level of biological activity spreads across the entire mulched area
rather than being concentrated in a small pile. By the time the mulch has
fully broken down and incorporated into the soil, most of the nutrient
release activity has already occurred at the root zone where plants can
access it.

Key materials for sheet mulching include straw, seaweed, pine needles,
oyster shells, bark (up to two-inch thick branches), lawn clippings, and
kitchen wastes. Hay should go through chicken runs first to eliminate
seeds.

## The Mulch Stratification Effect

Over two to three years of consistent mulching, Mollison observes that a
stratification of pH develops naturally in the mulch layers. Different
materials decompose at different rates and create distinct microzones.
Some layers become highly alkaline while others become acidic. Plants
respond by putting out feeder roots at completely different levels,
accessing the specific pH conditions they prefer. The result is that
alkaline-loving and acid-loving plants can grow side by side in the same
bed, each finding its preferred root zone.

When asked "What pH have you got?" Mollison's answer is "Everything." This
natural pH stratification is one of the key advantages of sheet mulching
over composting, which homogenizes materials into a single product.

## Worm Castings: The Highest Value Output

Mollison identifies worm manure (vermicast) as the highest tonnage per
acre of any manure and considers it the best soil amendment available.
Rather than concentrating materials in a [[query-how-do-i-start-a-worm-compost-bin-for-my-garden]] where they break down
through thermophilic bacterial action, spreading materials as mulch
allows earthworms to process them across the entire garden surface. Worms
consume [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] and produce castings that are rich in nitrogen,
potash, and phosphate. Worm castings also tend to test alkaline, which can
be useful for modifying soil chemistry.
