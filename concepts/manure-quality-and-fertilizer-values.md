# Manure Quality and Fertilizer Values

## Overview

Manure is one of the oldest and most widely used garden fertilizers, but its value varies enormously depending on source, animal diet, handling, and storage. Understanding this variability is critical for gardeners who rely on manure as a primary fertility input, because poor-quality manure can be worse than none at all.

## The Core Problem: Unpredictable Quality

The fundamental challenge with manure is that you rarely know what you're getting. Unlike bagged fertilizer with a guaranteed analysis, manure's nutrient content depends on factors entirely outside the gardener's control. Two loads of "horse manure" can differ by 200-300% in nitrogen content alone.

### Factors Affecting Manure Quality

1. **Animal diet**: The single largest variable. Animals fed premium feeds (alfalfa, grain supplements, oilseed meals) produce far richer manure than animals eating poor-quality pasture or bedding straw.
2. **Bedding material**: Straw, sawdust, wood shavings, or other bedding mixed with manure dilutes nutrient concentration. The carbon from bedding also ties up nitrogen during decomposition.
3. **Species**: Different animals metabolize feeds differently, producing manures with distinct nutrient profiles.
4. **Age and freshness**: Fresh manure has the highest available nutrient content but may contain pathogens and weed seeds. Composted manure is safer but has lower immediately available nutrients.
5. **Storage method**: Manure exposed to rain loses nutrients through leaching. Manure stored under cover retains more value.
6. **Animal health and breed**: Well-maintained animals in good health produce more consistent, higher-quality manure.

## The Horse Manure Example

Solomon illustrates the variability problem with two neighboring horse manure sources:

**Neighbor 1 (Ken):** Feeds his horses like endurance athletes — premium hay from knowledgeable farmers, oilseed meal supplements, abundant vitamins and minerals. His manure grows an excellent garden (though Solomon notes the nutritional quality may be questionable without mineral balancing).

**Neighbor 2:** Keeps horses in a back pasture with pathetic vegetation despite heavy urine deposition. She must buy most of the horse food and feeds mainly low-quality local grass hay that better farmers would reject as bedding straw. Her manure is so poor it barely improves rose bushes.

Both are "horse manure." Both are free or cheap. One grows food; the other barely sustains ornamentals.

## Solomon's Recommendation: Minimal Manure, Maximum COF

Given the unpredictability of manure quality, Solomon recommends:

1. **Use only the minimum amount of manure necessary** to supply the soil ecology's requirement for organic matter
2. **Rely on COF ([[complete-organic-fertilizer]])** for the plants' mineral nutrient requirements
3. **Do not assume manure provides balanced nutrition** — it almost certainly does not
4. **If you must rely primarily on manure**, get it from the best possible source: animals fed premium diets with known supplements

## Special Warnings

### Chicken Manure Burns

Chicken manure is the hottest (highest nitrogen) commonly available manure. It is easy for inexperienced gardeners to poison or burn plants with it. Apply sparingly, compost it first, or use it only as a minor component of a blend.

### Pathogen Concerns

Fresh manure from any animal can contain pathogens (E. coli, Salmonella, parasites). Solomon notes that concern about disease transmission from tankage (rendered animal products) has led to regulations requiring that it be sterilized, but this primarily affects its nitrogen content (requiring more volume) rather than its safety.

### The Myth of "Natural" Superiority

Natural manure is not inherently superior to chemical fertilizers just because it's natural. If the manure is mineral-imbalanced — which it almost certainly is — it will produce nutritionally inferior vegetables compared to crops grown with properly balanced COF. The key is mineral balance, not the source.

## Manure vs COF: A Clear Framework

| Aspect | Manure | COF |
|--------|--------|-----|
| Nutrient balance | Unpredictable, usually imbalanced | Formulated for balance |
| Organic matter | Excellent source | Not a significant source |
| Trace minerals | Variable, depends on animal diet | Guaranteed through kelp/basalt |
| Cost | Often free or cheap | Moderate upfront, very economical per season |
| Storage | Requires composting, bulky | Compact, stores indefinitely when dry |
| Consistency | Highly variable | Consistent from batch to batch |
| Primary role | Soil ecology food | Plant mineral nutrition |

## Practical Guidelines

- **Compost manure before use**: Reduces pathogens, weed seeds, and the risk of burning plants
- **Apply in fall or early winter**: Allows decomposition before spring planting
- **Don't exceed 1-2 inches of composted manure per year**: More is not better and can cause nutrient imbalances (especially potassium excess)
- **Test if possible**: A simple lab analysis costs $20-40 and reveals exactly what you're working with
- **Source matters more than quantity**: One load from well-fed animals is worth several loads from poorly fed ones
