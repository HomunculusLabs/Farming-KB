## Algae and Slime Molds

Algae and slime molds are often-overlooked members of the soil food web. Though
neither fits neatly into the categories of "plant" or "animal," both play
important roles in soil formation, nutrient cycling, and soil structure. This
page draws from *Teaming with Microbes* by Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis.

---

## Algae

### Overview

Algae are photosynthetic organisms found in marine, freshwater, and terrestrial
environments. They range from single-celled forms to thread-like filamentous
structures. Despite being photosynthetic, algae are **not** plants — they lack
true roots, leaves, and stems and have no vascular system for transporting
water and nutrients.

As **photoautotrophic primary producers**, algae capture sunlight and convert
carbon dioxide into [[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]], forming the base of many food chains.

### Terrestrial Algae in Soil

Terrestrial algae live on or near the **soil surface**, where sunlight is
available. They are not typically found near plant roots, which are in the
darker regions of the soil profile. Three major groups of algae occur in soil:

- **Green algae (Chlorophyta)** — The most common soil algae, thriving in
  moist, sunlit surface soils.
- **Yellow-green algae (Xanthophyta)** — Less abundant than green algae, found
  in similar surface habitats.
- **Diatoms (Bacillariophyta)** — Unicellular algae encased in intricate
  **silica cell walls**. When diatoms die, their glassy frustules accumulate
  over geologic time to form **[[diatomaceous-earth]]**, used in filtration,
  pest control, and soil amendments.

### Pioneer Organisms and Soil Formation

Algae are among the earliest colonizers of bare rock and mineral surfaces. As
pioneer organisms, they played a critical role in the origin of soils
themselves. Through photosynthesis and respiration, algae release **carbonic
acid**, which slowly dissolves and weathers rock into mineral particles — the
raw material of soil.

**[[lichens]]** — symbiotic partnerships between algae (or cyanobacteria) and fungi
— accelerate this weathering process even further. The fungal partner anchors
the organism to rock surfaces and expands the area of contact, while the algal
partner supplies sugars from photosynthesis. Many lichens also fix atmospheric
nitrogen, adding a key nutrient to the developing soil.

### Nitrogen Fixation by Blue-Green Algae

Blue-green algae, more accurately classified as **cyanobacteria (Cyanophyta)**,
possess the enzyme **nitrogenase**, which allows them to convert atmospheric
nitrogen (N₂) into ammonium — a form usable by plants. This [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]]
fixation is ecologically significant: it explains how **rice** grown in flooded
paddies receives substantial nitrogen without [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

### Role in Soil Structure

Beyond photosynthesis and nutrient input, algae contribute directly to **soil
physical structure**. They excrete polysaccharides, mucilage, and slimes that:

- **Bind soil particles** together into stable micro-aggregates.
- **Form air passageways** within the soil, improving aeration and water
  infiltration.

These exudates act as biological glues, enhancing soil tilth and resistance to
erosion — particularly important in the surface layers where algae are most
active.

## Slime Molds

### Overview

[[slime-molds]] are amoeba-like organisms found in damp, decaying organic matter —
rotting wood, leaf litter, manure, and moist forest soil. Though historically
classified as fungi, they belong to the kingdom Protista. They are heterotrophic,
consuming bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and [[dighton-fungal-dissolved-organic-carbon-production]] matter.

### Two Major Groups

Slime molds in soil fall into two groups, differing in cellular organization:

- **Cellular slime molds (Dictyosteliomycota)** — Individual amoeboid cells
  that remain separate throughout most of their life cycle but aggregate
  temporarily under stress to form a mobile, multicellular "slug" that
  migrates toward light before forming a fruiting body.
- **Plasmodial slime molds (Myxomycota)** — Exist as a single, giant,
  multinucleate cell (a **plasmodium**) that streams over surfaces, engulfing
  food as it goes. This is the classic, brightly pigmented slime mold often
  spotted on decaying logs.

### Life Cycle

The slime mold life cycle is remarkable:

1. **Spore germination** — Spores released from fruiting structures germinate
   into **myxamoebae**, small amoeba-like cells.
2. **Feeding phase** — Myxamoebae crawl through soil [[mollison-humus-and-organic-matter-in-soils]],
   consuming bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and other microorganisms.
3. **Swarming and aggregation** — When food becomes scarce, myxamoebae follow
   **chemical attractant trails** (cyclic AMP in many species) laid down by
   other cells, swarming together into a dense mass.
4. **Plasmodium formation** — The aggregated cells fuse (in plasmodial species)
   to form a single large plasmodium — a visible, creeping sheet of
   protoplasm.
5. **Migration and feeding** — The plasmodium moves at roughly **1 mm/hr**,
   engulfing food by phagocytosis. If cut into pieces, each fragment can
   regenerate into a complete plasmodium.
6. **Fruiting** — Under the right conditions, the plasmodium forms
   **sporangia** (fruiting structures) that release new spores, completing the
   cycle.

### Role in the Soil Ecosystem

Slime molds contribute to soil health in several ways:

- **Nutrient cycling** — By consuming bacteria and fungi, slime molds mineralize
  nutrients, making them available to plants and other soil organisms.
- **Soil structure** — Like algae, slime molds secrete slime that helps **bind
  soil particles**, contributing to aggregation and stability.
- **Food web dynamics** — They serve as both predators of microbes and prey for
  larger soil organisms, linking trophic levels in [[the-soil-food-web]].

## Summary

| Feature | Algae | Slime Molds |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrition | Photoautotrophic | Heterotrophic |
| Habitat | Soil surface (needs light) | Damp organic matter |
| Key groups | Chlorophyta, Xanthophyta, Bacillariophyta | Dictyosteliomycota, Myxomycota |
| Soil role | [[ectomycorrhizal-biocontrol-fungal-balance-primary-production]], N fixation, structure | Nutrient cycling, soil binding |

Together, algae and slime molds illustrate the remarkable diversity of life in
soil — organisms that are neither plant nor animal, yet essential to the living
system beneath our feet.
