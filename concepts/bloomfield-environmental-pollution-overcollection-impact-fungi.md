## Environmental Pollution and Overcollection Impact on Fungi

Fungi, though often overlooked in conservation discussions, are among the
organisms most sensitive to environmental degradation. Air pollution,

mycorrhizal to saprotrophic dominance has cascading effects on plant

nutrient uptake are disadvantaged relative to plants that can absorb

Soil acidification, driven by sulfur and nitrogen deposition, further
stresses fungal communities by mobilizing toxic aluminum and manganese
ions. The combined effects of nitrogen enrichment, acidification, and

## Habitat Destruction

Forests, the primary habitat of most macrofungal species, are being
cleared, fragmented, and degraded worldwide. Old-growth forests,
which harbor the highest diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi, are
particularly vulnerable because their complex soil ecosystems
require centuries to develop. When old-growth forest is cleared, the

in species like porcini, chanterelles, and matsutake, has raised
concerns about overcollection. In Europe and North America, some
popular foraging sites have experienced significant declines in
mushroom abundance that correlate with increased foraging pressure.

The impact of harvesting on fungal populations depends on the
harvesting method and the species' biology. Picking mushrooms by
cutting the stem above ground is generally considered less harmful
than uprooting, which damages the mycelium. However, even careful
picking removes the fruiting body and its spores, potentially
reducing the fungus's capacity for sexual reproduction and dispersal.

Long-term climate shifts may eliminate suitable habitat for
temperature-sensitive species, particularly those restricted to
high-altitude or high-latitude environments. Alpine fungi, which
occupy narrow ecological niches near mountaintops, have nowhere to
go as temperatures rise and are among the most vulnerable fungal
groups to climate change.

## Conservation Challenges

Fungal conservation faces unique challenges. Most fungal species
cannot be seen for most of their life cycle, making population
assessment difficult. There are far fewer mycologists than botanists
or zoologists, and fungal taxonomy remains incomplete. Conservation
laws and regulations rarely mention fungi explicitly. And public
awareness of fungal conservation issues is minimal compared with the
attention given to plants and animals.

The Red List of the International Union for Conservation of Nature
(IUCN) includes relatively few fungal species, reflecting the
difficulty of assessing conservation status for organisms that are
poorly known and hard to census. Expanding fungal conservation efforts
will require more trained mycologists, better public education, and
explicit inclusion of fungi in conservation legislation.
