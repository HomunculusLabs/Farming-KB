The hardness of the tallow directly affects candle quality. Harder tallow (from suet) produces taller, straighter candles that burn more slowly and drip less. Softer tallow produces candles that may bend, sag, or drip excessively, especially in warm conditions.

## Rendering Tallow for Candles

Before making candles, raw fat must be rendered into clean tallow:

1. **Prepare the fat**: Trim suet or fat into small pieces (1-2 cm cubes). Remove any meat, membrane, or blood spots — these cause odors and reduce quality
2. **Wet rendering method (preferred for beginners)**: Place fat pieces in a large pot, add just enough water to prevent sticking (about 1 cup water per 1 kg fat). Heat on low to medium heat (200-250°F / 95-120°C). Simmer gently for 2-4 hours, stirring occasionally. Do not boil vigorously — this can cause splattering and scorching
3. **Strain**: Pour the rendered fat through a fine mesh strainer lined with cheesecloth into a clean container. The liquid tallow will rise to the top as it cools, with the water and impurities settling to the bottom
4. **Cool and separate**: Allow the tallow to cool and solidify at room temperature, then refrigerate until firm. The solid tallow cake can be lifted off the water layer underneath. Scrape any discolored residue from the bottom of the tallow cake
5. **Double render (optional)**: For the cleanest, whitest tallow, repeat the rendering process one more time. Melt the tallow, strain through cheesecloth again, and allow to solidify
6. **Store**: Rendered tallow can be stored at room temperature for several months or refrigerated/frozen for longer storage. In a cool environment, it remains stable for a year or more

## Candle Wicking

The wick is a critical component that determines burn rate, flame size, and smoke production:

- **Cotton wicking**: The most common choice for tallow candles. Flat-braided cotton wicks work well for dipped and molded tallow candles. The wick must be sized appropriately for the candle diameter — too thin and the flame is small and the tallow pools without burning; too thick and the flame is large, smoky, and consumes tallow too quickly
- **Wick sizing**: For tallow candles 2-3 cm in diameter, use a medium (#2) flat braid. For larger candles (4-5 cm), use a large (#3) flat braid. For very small candles (1-2 cm), use a small (#1) wick
- **Pre-treating wicks**: Soak cotton wicks in a strong salt solution (1 tablespoon salt per cup of water) and dry thoroughly. Salt-treated wicks burn more slowly, produce less smoke, and curl into the flame as they burn (self-trimming). This technique was historically significant and is still recommended
- **Wick length**: The wick should extend approximately 6-10 mm (1/4-3/8 inch) above the tallow surface when the candle is ready to burn. Trim to this length before each use

## Candle Making Methods

### Dipped Tallow Candles

The traditional method, producing elegant, tapered candles:

1. Melt tallow in a tall, narrow container (a coffee can or tall pitcher works well). The container should be tall enough to allow full-length dipping. Maintain temperature at 150-160°F (65-70°C) — hot enough to remain liquid but cool enough to build layers quickly
2. Cut wicks to the desired candle length plus 10 cm (for hanging). Tie a small weight (a metal nut or washer) to the bottom of each wick
3. Dip the wick into the tallow and withdraw slowly. The first dip will coat the wick thinly. Hang to cool for 2-3 minutes until the tallow layer is firm
4. Repeat the dipping process, allowing 2-3 minutes of cooling between dips. Each dip adds a thin layer of tallow, gradually building up the candle diameter. Expect 20-40 dips for a standard-sized candle
5. For straight candles, alternate the hanging direction (flip 180°) every few dips. This promotes even thickness on all sides
6. When the desired diameter is reached, hang the candles to cool completely (several hours or overnight). Roll the warm candles on a smooth surface to straighten them if needed
7. Trim the bottoms flat and the wicks to 6-10 mm

### Molded Tallow Candles

Faster and more consistent than dipping, requiring candle molds:

1. Prepare candle molds by cleaning thoroughly and applying a thin coat of mold release (vegetable oil or silicone spray)
2. Thread pre-cut wicks through the mold, securing at the bottom with a wick holder or sealant (rubber putty, mold sealer, or a small piece of tallow pressed into the wick hole)
3. Melt tallow to 160-170°F (70-75°C) and pour slowly into the mold to avoid air bubbles
4. Allow to cool undisturbed at room temperature for 4-6 hours, or refrigerate for faster setting (2-3 hours)
5. Tallow shrinks slightly as it cools, creating a depression around the wick. Poke relief holes near the wick with a needle or thin wire during cooling, then fill with additional melted tallow for a smooth top surface
6. Once fully cooled and solid, remove from molds. If stubborn, briefly place the mold in warm water or a warm oven (100°F / 38°C) to loosen the candle
7. Trim the bottom flat and the wick to length

### Container Tallow Candles

The simplest method, suitable for small candles and emergency lighting:

1. Melt tallow and pour into heat-safe containers (glass jars, ceramic cups, metal tins)
2. Place a pre-tabbed wick in the center (or hold a wick in place with a clothespin across the top of the container)
3. Allow to cool and solidify at room temperature
4. Trim the wick and use directly from the container

## Tallow Candle Performance Characteristics

- **Burn time**: Tallow candles burn approximately 6-8 hours per 100g of tallow, depending on wick size and candle design. This is comparable to beeswax and longer than many paraffin candles
- **Flame quality**: Produces a warm, slightly yellow flame. Well-made tallow candles with properly sized wicks burn cleanly with minimal smoke and no more soot than beeswax
- **Scent**: Clean-rendered tallow from suet is essentially odorless when burning. Lower-quality tallow may produce a faint beefy aroma, which some people find objectionable. Adding [[comparison-steam-distillation-vs-co2-extraction-for-essential-oils]]**: Add 10-30 drops of essential oil per 500g of melted tallow, stirring gently. Lavender, cedar, cinnamon, clove, and pine are traditional choices. Add the oil just before pouring, as heat degrades some essential oils
- **Natural colorants**: Add small amounts of [[building-with-natural-materials]] for color. Turmeric for yellow, paprika for orange, beetroot powder for pink/red, spirulina for green, cocoa powder for brown. Use sparingly — too much colorant affects burning quality
- **Beeswax blend**: Adding 10-30% beeswax to tallow hardens the candle, raises the melting point, improves drip resistance, and adds a natural honey scent. This blend was historically common and produces a superior candle

## Historical Context

Tallow candles were the primary source of artificial light in European households from Roman times through the 18th century. The word "candle" itself derives from the Latin "candela," and tallow chandlers (candle makers) were essential tradespeople in every town and city. In medieval Europe, tallow candles were the standard lighting for common households, while beeswax candles were reserved for churches and the wealthy due to their higher cost and cleaner burn. The advent of whale oil lamps in the 18th century and the development of paraffin wax from petroleum in the 1850s gradually displaced tallow from the lighting market. The transition from tallow to kerosene and then electric lighting represented one of the most significant quality-of-life improvements in human history, as tallow candles produced relatively dim light, considerable smoke, and a characteristic odor that today would be considered unpleasant by most people. Modern homesteaders and traditional skills practitioners have revived tallow [[candle-making]] primarily for its self-sufficiency value rather than as a primary light source.
