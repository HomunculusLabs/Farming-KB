# Climatic Factors in Permaculture Design

## Overview

Climatic factors are the primary determinant of species selection, technology choices, and the overall plant, animal, and structural assemblies that can be used on a [[permaculture]] site. Understanding climate is essential because it dictates sector strategies and interacts intimately with site features such as slope, valley configuration, proximity to coasts, and altitude. Fire and wind effects are both site-related and weather-related, making local climate the ultimate arbiter of what is possible in any design.

## The Challenge of Climatic Prediction

Long-term weather prediction beyond about 10 days is essentially impossible because the atmosphere forms a chaotic system. The surface of the earth absorbs heat and warms the atmosphere from below, while the upper atmosphere loses heat, creating a roughly hexagonal cellular array of vortices with ascending warm air feeding descending cool air. This chaotic nature means designers must plan for variability rather than relying on averages.

### Factors Increasing Unpredictability

Several long-term influences make climate even more unpredictable:

- **Orbital interactions**: The relative interaction of the orbits of Earth, Sun, and Moon trigger longer-term trends
- **Atmospheric composition**: Changes from vulcanism, industrial pollution, agriculture, and forestry activities alter gaseous composition
- **Extra-terrestrial factors**: Meteors, perturbations in high-level atmospheric jet streams, oceanic circulation, fluctuations in Earth's magnetic field, and solar flares all contribute

## Key Design Principles for Variable Climates

### Fail-Safe Mixed Cropping

Because climatic prediction may forever remain inexact, designers should always spread the risk of crop failure by using mixtures of crop species, varieties, and strategies. This fail-safe system of mixed cropping is fundamental to regional self-reliance. Departure from buffering diversity leads to the feast-or-famine regime that currently affects world markets.

### House Design Strategies

The interaction of thermal mass (heat storage) and insulation (buffering for temperature extremes), combined with sensible siting, permits the design of efficient and safe housing across broad climatic ranges. Additional strategies include:

- **Water storage** to modify extreme effects
- **Windbreaks** to reduce wind damage
- **Species selection** using locally-adapted cultivars

### Focus on Extremes, Not Averages

Permaculture designers are as interested in climatic extremes as in means. Measures such as "average rainfall" have very limited relevance to specific sites. Far more valuable data includes:

- **Seasonal fluctuation** patterns
- **Dependability** of precipitation
- **Intensity** of weather events
- **Recorded ranges** (maximum and minimum values)

These factors determine the practical limits that need to be incorporated into any design.

## Importance of Local Knowledge

There is no substitute in any design for:

- **Local observation** of weather patterns
- **Anecdotal evidence** from long-term residents
- **Detailed [[maps]]** of local factors
- **Lists** of locally successful plant and animal species
- **Analysis** of local soils

Every designer is obligated to study regional long-term human and agricultural adaptations to climate. Native agricultural and pastoral management practices are often finely tuned to survival, sometimes very productive, and above all independent of outside aid.

## The Classification of Broad Climatic Zones

### Koppen Classification

Most global climatic classifications are based on precipitation-radiation interactions as formulated by Vladimir Koppen (1918), subsequently modified by authors such as Trewartha (1954). This system categorizes climates based on temperature and precipitation patterns.

### Holdridge Life Zone Matrix

The Holdridge Life Zone matrix enables more closely-defined plant lists by providing concise climatic keys. This system is particularly useful for annotating plant lists with precise climatic requirements.

### Hardiness Zones

Hardiness zones (commonly used in the USA) are defined by average annual minimum temperatures:

| Zone | Min Temp (°F) | Min Temp (°C) | Description |
|------|--------------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | Below -50 | Below -45 | Arctic tundra |
| 2 | -50 to -40 | -45 to -40 | Cold prairie and conifers |
| 3 | -40 to -30 | -40 to -34 | Conifers and mixed forests |
| 4 | -30 to -20 | -34 to -29 | Cold interiors of continents |
| 5 | -20 to -10 | -29 to -23 | Mixed forests, cool prairies |
| 6 | -10 to 0 | -23 to -18 | Broadleaf and deciduous forests |
| 7 | 0 to 10 | -18 to -12 | Broadleaf forests |
| 8 | 10 to 20 | -12 to -7 | Arid grasslands, savannah |
| 9 | 20 to 30 | -7 to -1 | Semi-arid coasts and basins |
| 10 | 30 to 40 | -1 to 4 | Sub-tropical, palms, coasts |
| 11 | 40 to 50 | 4 to 10 | Tropical forests, deserts |
| 12 | Over 50 | Over 10 | Equatorial rainforests, monsoon |

### Broad Zone Definitions

- **Tropical**: No month under 18°C (64°F) mean temperature
- **Subtropical**: Coolest months above 0°C (32°F) but below 18°C (64°F) mean; effectively frost-free areas
- **Temperate**: Coldest months below 0°C (32°F), warmest above 10°C (50°F) mean temperature
- **Polar**: Warmest month below 10°C (50°C) or in perpetual frost (8°C or less) mean
- **Arid**: Mean rainfall 50 cm (19.5 inches) or less
- **Desert**: Mean rainfall 25 cm (10 inches) or less; includes sub-humid areas where evaporation exceeds precipitation

### Modifying Factors on Broad Classifications

Several factors modify the broad climatic zones:

1. **Special mountain conditions** — altitude creates microclimates that deviate significantly from the surrounding lowland classification
2. **Coastal effects** — coasts moderate temperatures and modify the extremes of continental interiors
3. **Energy transfer** — winds and oceanic currents redistribute heat and moisture across regions
4. **Long-term cyclic factors** — periodic shifts in climate patterns that can persist for years or decades

### Challenges in Classification

Several practical problems complicate accurate climatic classification:

1. **Instrument limitations**: Accurate measurement instruments are expensive and often specific to a narrow range of effects
2. **Incomplete data**: Averages for precipitation and radiation often refer only to one part of the total spectrum. Few long-term records exist for fog precipitation, dew, long-wave radiation, ultraviolet incidence, or gaseous atmospheric composition
3. **Dynamic interaction**: Rain, sun, and wind interact continuously, so averages mean little to a plant or animal subject to the normally changeable effects that may cover wide ranges

## Design Implications

The critical takeaway for permaculture designers is that climate must be understood not as a set of static averages, but as a dynamic, variable system with both predictable patterns and unpredictable extremes. Designs should:

- Incorporate diversity as a buffer against climate variability
- Focus on local adaptation rather than imported techniques
- Account for extreme events alongside average conditions
- Recognize the interaction between all climatic factors rather than treating them in isolation
- Prioritize water storage and conservation strategies given the potential for precipitation changes
