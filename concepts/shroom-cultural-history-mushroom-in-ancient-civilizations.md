# Mushrooms in Ancient Civilizations

## From "Shroom: A Cultural History of the Magic Mushroom" by [[andy-letcher]]

## The Aztecs and Mesoamerica

Perhaps uniquely in the world, there exists in the region of Mexico a genuine history of intentional psilocybin mushroom consumption that extends back at least five hundred years to the Spanish conquest, and may go back much further.

When Cortés landed on the Mexican coast in 1519, the Spanish invaders encountered a civilisation that employed a range of psychoactive plants and fungi in religious, secular, and prophylactic contexts. The Spanish quite literally demonised these practices, attributing divinatory powers to the work of demons or the Devil, and began imposing Christian values upon the indigenous peoples.

### Teonanacatl: "God's Flesh"

The mushrooms were known in the local Nahuatl language as *teonanacatl*, meaning "God's flesh." Bernardino de Sahagún, who spent some sixty years among the indigenous populations, recorded that the mushrooms:

- Were found growing in grassland, fields, moors, and waste places
- Were so bitter as to hurt the tongue
- Caused palpitations of the heart, excited lust, and induced laughter and tears
- Were eaten at "the hour of blowing conches and flutes"
- Produced visions in which participants might see their destiny, or even the manner of their death

### Royal Mushroom Use

The Dominican friar Diego Durán described a coronation feast for the Aztec sovereign Tizoc in 1481 where inebriating mushrooms were provided. Aztec royalty rarely consumed alcohol but preferred mushrooms which they ate raw, "with which they would grow merry and become somewhat tipsy." Moctezuma II employed priests who consumed mushrooms for divinatory purposes, especially predicting the outcome of battles.

### The "Feast of Revelations"

Moctezuma appeased traditional enemies with a mushroom feast, the "Feast of Revelations," originating at his coronation in 1502. Spies were captured, pardoned, and magnanimously plied with mushrooms. According to one account, "they all lost their senses and ended up in a state worse than if they had drunk much wine."

### Pre-Columbian Evidence

Mesoamerican codices portray mushrooms. The Vienna Codex shows several gods, including Quetzalcóatl, clutching mushrooms. Throughout Central America, approximately three hundred stone and pottery "mushroom" effigies have been uncovered, originating mainly in the highlands of Guatemala. The earliest date from the pre-classic period — the first millennium BCE — suggesting the practice extended back thousands of years.

## Siberia and the Fly-Agaric

The fly-agaric (Amanita muscaria) has been consistently used as an intoxicant in only two relatively small regions of Siberia. Knowledge of this practice began filtering into Western society in the eighteenth century, through travelogues describing:

- Shamans consuming dried fly-agaric mushrooms to enter trance states
- The remarkable discovery that human urine is as potent as the fungus itself (the active ingredients pass through unmetabolised)
- Reindeer becoming intoxicated after eating fly-agaric and actively seeking it out

### The Cultural Context Problem

Letcher argues that for any psychoactive plant to become legitimised, three conditions must obtain: the plant must be available in sufficient quantities; the culture must know it causes altered states; and there must exist a cultural context in which those alterations can be meaningfully apprehended and socially integrated. Many cultures that had access to psychoactive plants nevertheless shunned them.

## Prehistoric Europe: The Archaeology of Ecstasy

### The Evidence Problem

Two fundamental problems confront the search for prehistoric mushroom use: mushrooms require no preparation paraphernalia (unlike other drugs), leaving nothing in the archaeological record; and mushrooms themselves are highly putrescible and almost never preserve.

### Stone and Pottery Evidence

Archaeological evidence for prehistoric European mushroom use is largely absent. Not a single magic mushroom has been preserved anywhere in the archaeological record. Proposed evidence includes:

- **Stonehenge carvings**: Laser scans revealed Bronze Age carvings that some suggested depict mushrooms in cross-section; the more widely accepted theory identifies them as Bronze Age axes
- **Tassili plateau rock art**: A Neolithic image from Algeria, popularised by [[terence-mckenna]], appears to show a figure clutching mushrooms. However, Kat Harrison's widely circulated drawing subtly reinforced a shamanistic interpretation not clearly present in the original
- **Gundestrup Cauldron**: Tentatively suggested to depict a psychoactive plant, possibly the Liberty Cap, though equally likely to be entirely decorative
- **Hildesheim Cathedral doors**: A panel that looks like a giant Liberty Cap is actually a stylised fig-tree from a coherent biblical narrative

### The Three Stages of Trance

David Lewis-Williams and Thomas Dowson argued that some Palaeolithic rock art was an attempt to represent hallucinations obtained in trances. Their model proposes: (1) light trance with entoptic hallucinations (grids, zigzags, dots); (2) deeper trance where these become culturally specific iconic forms; (3) the deepest trance, where subjects feel in the presence of deities.

### Letcher's Critical Assessment

Letcher argues that the popular narrative of an unbroken tradition of mushroom use stretching from Palaeolithic shamans through the Neolithic builders of Stonehenge, the ancient Greeks at Eleusis, Iron Age Druids, and medieval witches is an "essentialist view" that is "problematic." Drug experiences, whatever essential elements they may contain, are "always culturally bound, culturally mediated."

## Ancient China

Magic mushrooms have been known about in China since at least the Chin Dynasty (265-420 CE), when Chang Hua wrote about them in his *Po-wu-chih* (Record of the Investigation of Things). But for Chang Hua and later compilers of herbals, the mushrooms that "made you laugh unceasingly" were poisons, to be avoided or treated with herbal remedies.
