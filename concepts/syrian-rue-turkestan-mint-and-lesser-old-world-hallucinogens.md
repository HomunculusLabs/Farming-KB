## Syrian Rue, Turkestan Mint, and Lesser Old World Hallucinogens

Beyond the well-known fly agaric, cannabis, and [[lycanthropy-solanaceous-hallucinogens]], the Old World harbors several lesser-known psychoactive plants that have played important roles in traditional medicine and indigenous practices. This page covers Syrian rue, Turkestan mint, kanna, agara, kwashi, and galanga — minor Old World hallucinogens documented in the ethnobotanical literature.

## Syrian Rue (Peganum harmala)

- **Distribution:** Mediterranean region through northern India, Mongolia, and Manchuria
- **Family:** Zygophyllaceae (caltrop family), approximately two dozen genera in dry tropical and subtropical regions of both hemispheres
- Seeds have been employed as a spice, source of red dye, and source of oil throughout its range
- Seeds contain known hallucinogenic alkaloids, especially **harmine** and **harmaline** (β-carboline alkaloids)
- Harmine and harmaline are potent **MAO inhibitors** (monoamine oxidase inhibitors)
- The extraordinary esteem in which Asian peoples hold the plant may indicate former religious use as a hallucinogen
- Purposeful visionary use has not yet been established through literature or field work
- Widely used in folk medicine across its range for numerous ailments
- The plant's MAO-inhibiting properties make it a key ingredient in [[schultes-ayahuasca-analogs-dmt-plants]] when combined with DMT-containing plants
- Commonly burned as incense or brewed as a tea in Middle Eastern and Central Asian traditions
- Syrian rue represents the pharmacological bridge between Old World and [[mckenna-food-gods-new-world-hallucinogen-clustering-americas-psychedelic-continent]] traditions
- Harmine was first isolated in 1847 and was one of the earliest plant alkaloids to be chemically characterized

## Turkestan Mint (Lagochilus inebrians)

- **Distribution:** Dry steppes of Turkestan in central Asia
- **Family:** Lamiaceae (mint family); approximately 34 other species in the genus Lagochilus
- **Native range:** Central Asia through Iran and Afghanistan
- Used for centuries as an intoxicant among Tajik, Tartar, Turkoman, and Uzbek tribesmen
- Leaves gathered in October, toasted, sometimes mixed with stems, fruits, and flowers
- [[psilocybin-mushroom-drying-and-storage-methods]] increase aromatic fragrance; honey and sugar added to reduce intense bitterness
- Valued as a folk medicine; included in the 8th edition of the Russian Pharmacopoeia
- Medicinal uses: treating skin disease, checking hemorrhages, and providing sedation for nervous disorders
- A crystalline compound isolated and named **lagochiline** has been identified as a diterpene
- Whether lagochiline alone produces the psychoactive effects of the whole plant remains unknown
- Representing a distinct class of psychoactive compound (diterpene, not alkaloid), it is pharmacologically unique among Old World hallucinogens
- The plant highlights how many psychoactive species remain incompletely characterized chemically
- The species name "inebrians" (inebriating) directly reflects its traditional psychoactive use

## Kanna (Mesembryanthemum expansum and M. tortuosum)

- **Distribution:** Drier parts of South Africa
- **Family:** Aizoaceae (carpetweed family), mainly South African; related to pokeweed, pink, and cactus families
- About 1,000 species of Mesembryanthemum in South Africa, many of bizarre form (including the ice plant)
- Two dozen species including these two are considered by some botanists to represent a separate genus, **Sceletium**
- Strong evidence that one or both species were used by Hottentots (Khoekhoe people) as vision-inducing narcotics
- Hottentots chewed the root, keeping the chewed material in the mouth for extended periods
- Reported effects from a 17th-century account: "animal spirits awakened, eyes sparkled, faces manifested laughter and gaiety"
- "Thousands of delightsome ideas appeared, and a pleasant jollity which enabled them to be amused by simple jests"
- Excessive use caused loss of consciousness and "terrible delirium"
- Some botanists have suggested the true hallucinogenic kanna may have been cannabis or Sclerocarya species (cashew family)
- The two Mesembryanthemum species do contain alkaloids with sedative, cocaine-like properties capable of producing torpor
- Modern commercial Sceletium products (Kanna) have renewed interest in this plant's psychoactive properties
- The Sceletium genus name refers to the skeleton-like appearance of the dried plant material

## Agara (Galbulimima belgraveana)

- **Distribution:** Malaysia and Australia (Papua New Guinea region)
- **Family:** Himantandraceae, a rare family related to the magnolias
- One of four species of Galbulimima; a tall forest tree of tropical rainforests
- Papua natives make a drink by boiling leaves and bark together with leaves of ereriba (Homalomena sp.)
- Consumers become violently intoxicated, eventually falling into a deep sleep accompanied by visions and fantastic dreams
- Some 28 alkaloids have been isolated from this tree species
- Despite the number of alkaloids, the specific psychoactive principle remains unidentified
- All isolated alkaloids are biologically active but none has been definitively identified as the primary inebriant
- Agara represents one of the most pharmacologically puzzling psychoactive plants in the Old World
- Recent pharmaceutical interest has investigated Galbulimima alkaloids for potential neuroactive drug development

## Ereriba (Homalomena sp.)

- **Distribution:** Tropical Asia and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]] (undetermined species)
- **Family:** Araceae (aroid family); approximately 140 species of Homalomena native to tropical regions
- A stout herb reported to have narcotic effects when its leaves are taken with the leaves and bark of agara
- Always used in combination with agara, never alone — suggesting a synergistic or potentiating relationship
- The active chemical constituent is unknown
- This combination use parallels the New World practice of adding plant admixtures to primary hallucinogenic preparations

## Kwashi (Pancratium trianthum)

- **Distribution:** Dobe region of Botswana; used by San Bushmen
- **Family:** Amaryllidaceae (amaryllis family)
- The bulb is rubbed over incisions made in the scalp to induce visual hallucinations
- This method represents a kind of primitive counterpart to the modern hypodermic technique
- Chemical constitution is entirely unknown
- Of the 14 other Pancratium species (mainly in Asia and Africa), many contain psychoactive principles, mostly alkaloids
- Some Pancratium species are potent cardiac poisons — extreme caution warranted for any internal use
- The scalp-incision method of administration is unique among documented hallucinogen practices
- The Amaryllidaceae family is known for producing diverse alkaloid classes including galanthamine and lycorine

## Galanga or Maraba (Kaempferia galanga)

- **Distribution:** New Guinea and Southeast Asia
- **Family:** Zingiberaceae (ginger family); about 70 species in the genus Kaempferia
- An herb rich in essential oils; natives in New Guinea eat the rhizome as a hallucinogen
- Valued locally as a condiment in cooking and used in traditional folk medicine
- Medicinal uses: bringing boils to a head and hastening the healing of burns and wounds
- Phytochemical studies have revealed no confirmed psychoactive principle despite its reputed hallucinogenic use
- Effects may be attributable to essential oil components acting as pseudohallucinogens rather than true hallucinogens
- The discrepancy between traditional reputation and [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] remains unresolved
- Ethyl cinnamate and ethyl p-methoxycinnamate are among the major essential oil constituents identified

## Pharmacological Themes Across Lesser Old World Hallucinogens

- Syrian rue's harmine/harmaline are the best-characterized chemically (β-carboline MAO inhibitors with well-documented pharmacology)
- Turkestan mint's lagochiline represents a rare diterpene psychoactive compound, a class seldom associated with hallucinogenic effects
- Kanna's mesembrine alkaloids have cocaine-like (local anesthetic and serotonergic) properties rather than classic hallucinogenic activity
- Agara's 28 alkaloids remain incompletely characterized, making it a target for future pharmacological investigation
- Several of these plants may act as **pseudohallucinogens** — upsetting body functions enough to induce delirium rather than producing true hallucinations
- Pseudohallucinogenic effects are often secondary to toxicity, making these plants dangerous at visionary doses
- The chemical diversity across these species (β-carbolines, diterpenes, mesembrine alkaloids, Amaryllidaceae alkaloids, essential oils) underscores that hallucinogenic activity arises from many unrelated chemical classes
