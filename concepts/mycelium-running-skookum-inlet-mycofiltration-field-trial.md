# The Skookum Inlet Mycofiltration Field Trial

## Overview

One of the most compelling empirical demonstrations in *Mycelium Running* is Paul
Stamets' personal field trial at Skookum Inlet, Washington, where he installed
mycofiltration beds using *Stropharia rugoso-annulata* (the garden giant mushroom)
to address fecal coliform pollution threatening the local shellfish industry. This
experiment provided dramatic, real-world evidence that [[mycelium-running-rhizomorphic-mycelium-structural-strength-erosion-control]] that aggressively penetrates wood-chip
substrates. It is also tolerant of a wide range of environmental conditions,
making it well-suited to outdoor mycofiltration applications where temperature,
moisture, and nutrient availability fluctuate considerably.

The installation consisted of beds of wood chips inoculated with *Stropharia*
mycelium, positioned to intercept surface and subsurface water flow from the
property before it entered drainage pathways leading to the inlet. The wood chips
served both as a growth substrate for the mycelium and as a physical filter matrix.

## Results: The 100-Fold Coliform Reduction

The results of the mycofiltration installation were dramatic. After the mycelial
beds became fully established, fecal coliform counts in water leaving the property
dropped by approximately two orders of magnitude — a roughly 100-fold reduction.
This improvement occurred despite the fact that Stamets had simultaneously doubled
the number of livestock on the property, which should have increased, not decreased,
the bacterial load entering the drainage system.

The timing of the improvement correlated with the maturation of the mycelial beds,
suggesting that the fungal network was directly responsible for the coliform
reduction rather than other environmental variables. The counterintuitive result —
cleaner water with more animals — provided strong evidence that the mycofiltration
system was functioning as designed.

## Mechanism: How Mycelium Consumes Bacteria

Stamets explains the mycofiltration mechanism as a combination of physical capture
and biological destruction. As water percolates through the mycelial mat, bacteria
are physically trapped in the dense network of hyphae and the surrounding substrate.
Once trapped, the bacteria encounter a hostile biochemical environment: the mycelium
secretes a range of antibacterial and antifungal compounds, including enzymes and
[[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] of the filtered water. Stamets describes a
process in which the dominant bacterial populations downstream of the mycelial
filter change as the fungal network establishes itself, with pathogenic or indicator
species being suppressed and replaced by less harmful organisms.
