# Critique of Agricultural Science Laws

updated: 2026-04-18

Fukuoka systematically critiqued the foundational laws of agricultural science — the laws of diminishing returns, equilibrium, adaptation, compensation and cancellation, relativity, and the law of minimum (Liebig's law). He argued that all these laws are scattered fragments of a single, unnamed principle of nature.

## The Laws of Modern Agriculture

### Law of Diminishing Returns
Scientific technology proves effective up to some upper limit on a given plot, but exceeding this limit diminishes yields. Such limits change with time and circumstance, so agricultural technology constantly seeks to break through them.

### Equilibrium
Nature works constantly to strike a balance. When balance breaks down, forces act to restore it. All phenomena in the natural world act to maintain equilibrium.

### Adaptation
Animals and crops show the ability to adapt to changes in growing conditions, another activity aimed at restoring equilibrium.

### Compensation and Cancellation
When rice is planted densely, plants send out fewer tillers; when sparsely, more stalks grow per plant. Larger number of stalks results in smaller heads of grain.

### Relativity
Factors determining yield are associated with other factors and change constantly in relation to each other — planting period, seed quantity, fertilizer timing, seedling spacing.

### Law of Minimum (Liebig)
Yield is determined by the one nutrient element in shortest supply. No matter how abundant other nutrients, the scarcest sets the upper limit — illustrated by Liebig's barrel.

## All Laws Are One

Fukuoka's conclusion: nature is an indivisible whole; all laws emanate from one source and return to [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]], or nothingness.

- The law of diminishing returns reflects a force striving to maintain equilibrium by opposing gradual increases
- Compensation and cancellation are mutually antagonistic forces maintaining balance
- Equilibrium and adaptability protect the balance, order, and harmony of nature
- If there is a law of the minimum, there must also be a law of the maximum — plants have aversion to deficiencies and excesses of anything

## The Blind Men and the Elephant

Scientists have examined nature from every conceivable angle and have seen this unity as a thousand different forms. Each law is nothing other than a manifestation of the great harmony and balance of nature, springing from a single source. When the same law emanates in different directions, man perceives each image as a different law.

Fukuoka compared this to the blind men and the elephant — one touches the trunk and calls it a snake, another touches a leg and calls it a tree. Man believes he knows the whole of nature by touching a part of it, but what he has understood is nothing more than the elephant as seen by the blind men.

## The Dharmic Law That All Things Return to One

If this fundamental law had a name, Fukuoka would call it "The Dharmic Law That All Things Return to One." All things fuse into a circle, which reverts to a point, and the point to nothing. Nothing is ever created or destroyed — not the same as the scientific conservation of matter, which ventures no further.

## Scientific Laws Cannot Improve Farming

As long as [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] stands on this unique law, it is guaranteed truth and possesses eternal life. The laws of scientific farming are useful only in examining the status quo — they cannot develop better cultivation techniques, cannot boost rice yields beyond those attainable by present methods, and are useful only in preventing reduced yields.

## The Critique of Liebig's Law

Implicit in Liebig's law is the notion that yield can be increased by supplying the nutrient in shortest supply. But providing a sufficient amount of a scarce nutrient does not increase yield — it merely maintains it. A nutrient in short supply decreases yield, but providing enough of it does not increase yield beyond natural capacity.

The [[von-liebig-law-of-the-minimum-and-fertilizer-history]] treats the soil as an inert container of nutrient building blocks. It ignores the living soil ecosystem of microbes, organic matter, physical structure, and biological relationships that actually determine plant growth.

## Science Leads Further From Nature

Human knowledge deepens and expands through study, but man is deceived in thinking he draws closer to understanding nature as he learns more. He actually draws further from nature with each new discovery and each fresh bit of knowledge.

The fragments of natural law, if collected together, can never add up to the great source principle. Scientific farming based on these partial laws is vastly different from natural farming, which observes the basic principle of nature itself.

## No Cause and Effect in Nature

Behind each apparent cause lies another cause, and behind that yet another. What we view as a cause can also be seen as the result of another cause. When examined from a broader spatial and temporal perspective, nature shows a tangled confusion of causal relationships that defy unraveling.

There is nothing to get ahold of, so all measures are futile. Nature has neither beginning nor end, before nor after, cause nor effect. Causality does not exist — this is Fukuoka's principle of non-causality.

This critique extends beyond agriculture into a broader epistemological challenge to the scientific method itself. Fukuoka argued that reductionist analysis — breaking nature into isolated variables — fundamentally misapprehends how natural systems function. The scientist who studies nitrogen uptake in isolation misses the living soil ecosystem that makes such uptake possible.

The practical implication of this philosophy is radical: if all agricultural laws are fragments of one unified principle, then attempting to optimise farming by manipulating individual variables (fertilizer rates, planting density, pesticide timing) is not merely incomplete — it is actively counterproductive. Each manipulation creates new imbalances that require further manipulations, producing an endless cycle of technological intervention that moves farming further from nature rather than closer to it.
