# Amanita

Amanita is one of the most recognized and ecologically significant genera of
mushroom-forming fungi, containing approximately 600 species worldwide. The
genus includes some of the most deadly [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]].
Modern phylogenetic studies have confirmed that Amanita is monophyletic and
most closely related to the genus Limacella. The genus is divided into several
sections based on morphological and molecular characteristics.

## Morphological Features

Members of Amanita share several characteristic features that aid
identification:

- **Universal veil**: A membrane enclosing the young mushroom. Remnants
  persist as a cup-like volva at the stem base or as patches on the cap.
- **Partial veil**: A membrane protecting developing gills, persisting as a
  ring or annulus on the stem after expansion.
- **Free gills**: Not attached to the stem, distinguishing Amanita from
  superficially similar genera.
- **White spore print**: Nearly all species produce a white spore print, a
  critical diagnostic feature.
- **Bulbous stem base**: Many species have an enlarged base surrounded by
  remains of the universal veil.

The combination of volva at the stem base and white gills is the hallmark of
the genus, though these features can vary in prominence across species.

## Deadly Species

Several Amanita species contain amatoxins, particularly alpha-amanitin, among
the most potent natural toxins known. These inhibit RNA polymerase II, halting
protein synthesis and leading to progressive liver and kidney failure. Poisoning
is characterized by a deceptive latency period of 6-24 hours after ingestion,
followed by severe liver damage that is often fatal without prompt medical
intervention.

**Amanita phalloides** (death cap) is responsible for the majority of fatal
mushroom poisonings worldwide. Originally native to Europe, it has been
introduced to [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] equivalent, with similar
  appearance and edibility when thoroughly cooked.
- **Amanita fulva** (tawny grisette) and species in section Vaginatae are
  edible when properly identified. However, many mycologists recommend
  avoiding the genus entirely for foraging given the lethal consequences of
  misidentification.
