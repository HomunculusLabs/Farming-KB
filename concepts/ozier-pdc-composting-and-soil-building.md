# Composting and Soil Building (Ozier PDC)

**Source:** [[william-ozier-williams]], *William's PDC: A [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] Course* (2011)

## Composting as a Permaculture Element

Composting is the technique of accelerating the [[fukuoka-microbes-scavengers-natural-decomposition-biosystem]] process to
convert organic waste into nutrient-rich humus. In Permaculture systems, composting
serves as the primary method for closing the nutrient loop—returning [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]]
to the soil so that plants can access the nutrients they need. Ozier positions
composting as an integral part of any [[comparison-sepp-holzer-vs-permaculture-design]], essential for maintaining
soil health, fertility, and biological activity.

## Why Compost Matters

In natural ecosystems, nutrients are recycled with remarkable efficiency. Studies of
temperate forests show that 98% of calcium and other minerals are retained within the
system, with only 2% lost annually to runoff. In contrast, conventional agricultural
systems lose 25-60% of nutrients annually. Composting is the primary tool Permaculture
designers use to approach nature's recycling efficiency.

Composting transforms waste materials—kitchen scraps, yard debris, animal manures,
crop residues—into a stable, humus-rich amendment that feeds soil life and plants.
The process is driven by microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes) and
macroorganisms (worms, insects) that break down complex organic molecules into
plant-available forms.

## The Composting Process

Composting involves four key factors that must be managed:

### Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio (C:N)

The balance between carbon-rich ("brown") materials and nitrogen-rich ("green")
materials is critical. An ideal C:N ratio for active composting is approximately
25-30:1. Too much carbon and decomposition slows dramatically; too much nitrogen
and the pile becomes anaerobic, producing foul odors and losing nitrogen as ammonia
gas.

Common brown materials: dry leaves, straw, wood chips, cardboard, sawdust
Common green materials: kitchen scraps, grass clippings, fresh manure, green weeds

### Moisture

The compost pile should be as damp as a wrung-out sponge—approximately 50-60%
moisture content. Too dry and microbial activity stops; too wet and anaerobic
conditions develop. Covering the pile helps maintain consistent moisture.

### Aeration

Composting organisms need oxygen. Regular turning of the pile introduces air and
prevents anaerobic conditions. Turning also mixes materials, ensuring even
decomposition. A well-aerated pile decomposes faster and produces higher quality
compost.

### Temperature

Active composting generates heat as microorganisms metabolize organic matter.
Temperatures of 130-160°F (55-71°C) are ideal for rapid decomposition and pathogen
kill. The pile should be large enough to retain heat (minimum 3x3x3 feet) but not
so large that air cannot penetrate.

## Types of Composting Systems

### Hot Composting (Berkshire Method)

Active management of C:N ratio, moisture, and aeration to produce finished compost
in 2-6 weeks. Requires regular turning and careful material selection. Produces the
highest [[ingham-brix-testing-plant-quality-compost-tea-response]] and effectively kills weed seeds and pathogens.

### Cold Composting

Passive accumulation of organic materials in a pile or bin with minimal management.
Decomposition is slower (6-12 months) but requires less labor. Weed seeds may
survive. Suitable for situations where labor is limited.

### Vermicomposting

Using red worms (Eisenia fetida) to process kitchen scraps and organic waste.
[[ingham-vermicompost-worm-castings]] are among the finest soil amendments available, rich in plant-available
nutrients and [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]]. Vermicomposting can be done indoors in small
bins, making it ideal for urban settings.

### Sheet Mulching (Composting in Place)

Rather than creating a separate compost pile, organic materials are layered directly
on garden beds where decomposition occurs in situ. This method avoids the labor of
moving materials and preserves soil structure. Sheet mulching typically consists of:
- A weed-suppressing base layer (cardboard or newspaper)
- A nitrogen-rich layer (manure or green materials)
