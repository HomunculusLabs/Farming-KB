## Radiotropism and Chernobyl Fungal Communities

The Chernobyl nuclear disaster of 1986 created an unprecedented natural laboratory for studying fungal responses to intense ionizing radiation. Research over the following decades revealed remarkable fungal adaptations including radiotropism (directional growth toward radiation sources), melanin-mediated radiation protection, and the ability to decompose radioactive debris.

## Community Structure Changes Under Radiation

Monitoring fungal communities around Chernobyl over 15 years yielded approximately 2,000 strains encompassing 180 species from 92 genera. Community structure correlated strongly with radiation dose:

- **Decreased diversity**: Fungal community complexity declined with increasing radioactive dose, as measured by pleiads (constellations of points representing species, with connecting lines indicating community linkage complexity).
- **Increased melanization**: The proportion of melanin-containing (pigmented) fungal species increased with dose rate. Melanin appears to protect fungal tissue against ionizing radiation damage, analogous to its protective role against ultraviolet light in lichen fungi.
- **Simplified structure**: Intense radiation doses led to simpler community organization dominated by melanized species.

### Bioindicator Species

Specific fungal species serve as indicators of contamination levels:

- **High contamination**: *[[chaetomium-aureum]]* and *[[paeciliomyces-lilacinus]]* indicate high radionuclide levels in woodland soils.
- **Moderate contamination**: Pleiad analysis showing intermediate linkage complexity.

## Radiotropism

Perhaps the most intriguing discovery was the evolution of radiotropism — the ability to direct growth actively toward sources of radioactivity:

- Fungi isolated from high-radiation zones directed growth toward collimated radiation sources in culture.
- The same species isolated from low-radiation areas did not exhibit this behavior.
- This represents a genuine behavioral adaptation, though the triggers and physiological mechanisms remain poorly understood.
- The phenomenon was first documented by Vember et al. (1999) and Zhdanova (1994).

The adaptive significance may relate to melanin's ability to convert gamma radiation into chemical energy, potentially providing a metabolic advantage in irradiated environments. This hypothesis parallels discoveries of melanized fungi growing inside the Chernobyl reactor itself.

## Decomposition of Radioactive Debris

Several microfungi demonstrated the ability to grow into and decompose carbon-based radioactive debris ("hot particles") from the reactor:

- *[[cladosporium-cladosporioides]]* and *Penicillium roseo-purpureum* could overgrow hot particles with activity less than 1,147 Bq and destroy them within 50–150 days.
- This decomposition capability suggests potential applications for bioremediation of radioactive contamination.

## Reactor Room Fungal Colonization

The most extreme environment studied was the reactor room itself, where radiation levels ranged from 1.5 to 800 mR h⁻¹ in the presence of multiple alpha, beta, and gamma emitters including ²³⁹Pu, ²⁴⁰⁺²⁴¹Pu, ²⁴¹Am, ²⁴⁴Cm, and ¹³⁷Cs. Thirty-seven species from 19 genera were isolated from the walls, with isolation frequency generally increasing at higher radiation levels.

*Cladosporium sphaerospermum* was by far the most frequently isolated species, appearing in nearly 100% of samples at higher radiation levels. The sustained radiation has also shifted genetic composition within some fungal species, with Mironenko et al. (2000) documenting genetic changes in populations from the reactor zone.

## Lichen Radionuclide Accumulation

The fungal component of lichen symbiosis is responsible for elevated radionuclide content. After Chernobyl, Austrian lichen ¹³⁷Cs levels rose from approximately 0.4 kBq kg⁻¹ to over 50 kBq kg⁻¹:

- *Pseudovernia furfuracea* exceeded natural ⁴⁰K activity by 430 times.
- Biological half-lives: ~1.3 years for ¹³⁷Cs and 1.2–1.6 years for ⁹⁰Sr in foliose arboreal lichens; ~2.5 years for ¹³⁷Cs in the terricolous *Cetraria islandica*.

Norwegian lichens showed extreme contamination after Chernobyl, with *Pseudephebe pubescens* reaching 54,080 Bq kg⁻¹. These long biological half-lives enable extended trophic transfer to grazing animals, as lichens constitute approximately 40% of annual food intake for reindeer at densities of about four per hectare.

## Trophic Transfer and Ecological Implications

The transfer of radionuclides through fungal-based food webs has significant ecological consequences:

- Reindeer in Norway graze lichen-contaminated ranges with mean activities of 6,700–24,000 Bq kg⁻¹.
- Roe deer in Sweden consume 2,500–3,000 Bq d⁻¹ from fungal fruit bodies during autumn.
- Fungal-feeding beetles accumulated mercury at concentrations 7–9× higher than other feeding guilds.
- Ectomycorrhizal fruit bodies contribute significantly to radiocesium and radiostrontium transfer into higher trophic levels through organic horizon accumulation in steppe soils.

## Summary of Key Principles

1. **Fungal resistance**: Fungi are highly resistant to environmental radionuclides, with melanin providing protection against ionizing radiation.
2. **Efficient absorption**: Extensive hyphal networks efficiently absorb radionuclides, particularly radiocesium, which mimics potassium in biochemical pathways.
3. **Internal translocation**: Radionuclides move between sources and physiological sinks like essential nutrients, enabling long-term retention in fungal biomass.
4. **Adsorption capacity**: Ion-exchange sites on hyphal walls contribute to radionuclide binding, exploitable for industrial effluent cleanup.
5. **Knowledge gaps**: The mechanisms of radiotropism, quantitative immobilization potential across radionuclide types, and optimal remediation strategies require further investigation.
