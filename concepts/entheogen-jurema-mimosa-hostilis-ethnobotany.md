# Jurema Mimosa hostilis Ethnobotany

Jurema (Mimosa hostilis) is a poorly understood shrub of the dry
regions of eastern Brazil whose roots provide the "miraculous jurema
drink," known locally as ajuca or vinho de jurema. The beverage was
traditionally consumed by several indigenous tribes in Pernambuco
state, including the Kariri, Pankaruru, Tusha, and Fulnio, in
ceremonies usually connected with warfare. Despite its long history
of indigenous use, the hallucinogenic properties of jurema remain
incompletely understood by modern science.

## Botanical Description

Mimosa hostilis is a shrub native only to the dry regions of eastern
Brazil, where it grows in the caatinga -- a semi-arid tropical biome
characterized by thorny scrub vegetation. The genus Mimosa is closely
allied to Acacia and Anadenanthera, and comprises some 500 species
of tropical and subtropical herbs and small shrubs. The mimosas
belong to the subfamily Mimosoideae of the bean family, Leguminosae
(also known as Fabaceae). Most Mimosa species are American in origin,
although some occur in Africa and Asia.

Several species of Mimosa are locally called jurema in different
parts of Brazil, which has created some confusion in the ethnobotanical
literature regarding the precise identity of the psychoactive plant.
The taxonomic complexity of the genus, with hundreds of similar-
looking species, makes field identification challenging. Other species
of Mimosa may also possess entheogenic properties, but M. hostilis
is the best-documented hallucinogenic member of the genus.

## Traditional Use and Ceremony

The jurema drink was consumed in preparation for warfare by now-extinct
tribes of eastern Brazil. Participants would "pass the night navigating
through the depths of slumber" just prior to sallying forth to battle.
The visionary content of the experience was vividly described: warriors
would see "glorious visions of the spirit land" or "catch a glimpse of
the clashing rocks that destroy souls of the dead journeying to their
goal" or "see the Thunderbird shooting lightning from a huge tuft on
his head and producing claps of thunder."

These descriptions suggest that the jurema experience was highly
visionary and culturally meaningful, providing access to spiritual
realms and supernatural beings that validated the warriors' courage
and mission. The connection between warfare and entheogenic use is a
pattern found across many indigenous cultures worldwide, where
psychoactive plants serve to prepare warriors both psychologically
and spiritually for combat, and where visions of protective spirits
or foreknowledge of battle outcomes are sought through plant-induced
states of consciousness.

## Chemistry

Early chemical studies of Mimosa hostilis indicated the presence of
an active alkaloid that was given the name nigerine, but this was
later shown to be identical with N,N-dimethyltryptamine ([[dmt-and-ayahuasca-history]] is a potent short-acting [[mckenna-tryptamine-hallucinogen-classification-four-groups-plants]] that is also
found in [[psychotria-viridis]]
and in the resin of Virola species used as snuff.

A significant pharmacological puzzle surrounds the jurema drink: since
tryptamines are not active when taken orally unless in the presence
of a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI), it is obvious that the
traditional jurema drink must contain ingredients other than M.
hostilis itself, or that the plant must contain an endogenous MAOI
in its tissues. The [[iboga-root-bark-preparation]] of M. hostilis has been shown to be
exceptionally rich in DMT, with concentrations that make it one of
the most potent natural sources of this compound known, but the
full complement of its alkaloids has not been fully characterized
by modern analytical methods.

## The Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitor Question

The mystery of how orally ingested jurema produces psychoactive effects
remains unresolved. Several possibilities exist: the drink may have
included admixture plants containing beta-carboline MAOIs similar to
those found in [[peganum-harmala]]; the root
bark itself may contain as-yet-unidentified MAOI compounds; or the
preparation method (possibly involving fermentation) may produce MAOI
activity through chemical transformation of plant constituents.
