# Soil Health and Management

Soil health refers to the capacity of soil to function as a living ecosystem
that sustains plants, animals, and humans. Healthy soil supports plant growth,
regulates water, filters pollutants, and cycles nutrients. Understanding and
maintaining soil health is foundational to successful gardening and agriculture.

## Soil Composition

Mineral soil consists of three particle types: sand, silt, and clay. The
relative proportions determine soil texture, which influences drainage,
water-holding capacity, nutrient retention, and ease of cultivation. Sandy soils
drain quickly but retain few nutrients. Clay soils hold nutrients and water but
drain poorly and can become compacted. Loam, a balanced mixture of all three
particles, is considered ideal for most garden plants.

## Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

Soil pH, measured on a scale from 0 to 14, determines the chemical availability
of nutrients to plant roots. Most [[companion-cultivation-of-mushrooms-with-garden-vegetables]] and ornamentals prefer a
slightly acidic pH between 6.0 and 6.8. At this range, macronutrients like
nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, plus most micronutrients, are readily
available.

When soil pH is too low (acidic), elements like aluminum and manganese can
become toxic, while phosphorus becomes less available. When pH is too high
(alkaline), iron, manganese, zinc, and boron become deficient. Liming materials
(agricultural lime, dolomitic lime) raise pH, while elemental sulfur or
aluminum sulfate lower it. A professional soil test should always guide pH
adjustment decisions.

## The Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is the nutrient most frequently limiting to plant growth. The nitrogen
cycle describes how nitrogen moves through the atmosphere, soil, and living
organisms. Atmospheric nitrogen is fixed by bacteria (both free-living and
symbiotic rhizobia in legume root nodules) into forms plants can absorb.

In the soil, organic nitrogen in plant residues and [[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]] is
converted to ammonium by mineralization. Nitrification, carried out by
specialized bacteria, converts ammonium to nitrate, the form most readily
taken up by plants. Nitrogen can be lost through leaching (especially nitrate),
volatilization as ammonia gas, and denitrification in waterlogged conditions.

## Phosphorus and Potassium

Phosphorus is essential for energy transfer (ATP), root development, flowering,
and [[berry-fruit-production-cool-humid-climates]]. It is relatively immobile in soil and tends to accumulate
near the surface. [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]], for example,
extend root networks and improve nutrient and water uptake in exchange for
carbon from the host plant.

Beneficial soil microbes are supported by minimizing soil disturbance, keeping
soil covered with plants or mulch, maintaining living roots year-round through
[[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]], and maximizing plant diversity. These four principles form the
foundation of [[no-till-farming-principles]] and regenerative soil management.

## Composting

Composting is the controlled decomposition of organic materials into a stable,
humus-rich product. Effective composting requires four elements: carbon-rich
materials (browns like leaves, straw, paper), nitrogen-rich materials (greens
like grass clippings, kitchen scraps), adequate moisture, and oxygen. A carbon-
to-nitrogen ratio of roughly 30:1 produces the best results.

A properly managed [[mushroom-cultivator-compost-pile-management]] heats to 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit within
days, killing weed seeds and most pathogens. Turning the pile every one to two
weeks maintains aerobic conditions and accelerates decomposition. Finished
compost is dark, crumbly, and has an earthy aroma. It can be applied to garden
beds as a soil amendment, used as a mulch, or incorporated into potting mixes.
