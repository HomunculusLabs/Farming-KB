# Agar Culture Technique

Agar culture is the practice of growing mushroom [[mycelium]] on a solid,
gelatinous nutrient medium in petri dishes. It is the cornerstone of
advanced [[oyster-mushroom-cultivation-economics]], enabling isolate selection, contamination
identification, long-term storage, and clean inoculum production.

## What Is Agar?

Agar is a polysaccharide derived from red algae that forms a firm gel when
dissolved in water and cooled. It serves as a solid support matrix for
nutrient solutions, allowing mycelium to grow on the surface where it can be
observed and manipulated. Agar melts at approximately 85°C and solidifies at
around 40°C, allowing it to be poured into dishes at a comfortable working
temperature.

## Agar Media Formulations

### Malt Extract Agar (MEA)

The most widely used medium for mushroom cultivation:
- Malt extract: 10-20 g
- Agar: 15-20 g
- Water: 1 liter

MEA provides a balanced nutrient profile that supports growth of most
cultivable mushroom species. At 20 g/L malt extract, it is considered a
"rich" medium suitable for vigorous growth.

### Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)

Another common formulation:
- Potato infusion (from 200g peeled, boiled potatoes): 1 liter
- Dextrose (glucose): 20 g
- Agar: 20 g

PDA is widely available commercially in pre-mixed powder form. It supports
good growth for most species.

### Dog Food Agar (DFA)

A budget-friendly alternative:
- Plain dog food kibble: 40-60 g
- Agar: 20 g
- Water: 1 liter

Boil the kibble for 30 minutes, strain, add agar to the liquid. DFA provides
excellent nutrition at a fraction of the cost of commercial media.

### Modified Agar Recipes

Cultivators modify basic recipes for specific purposes:
- **Lowered nutrition** (5-10 g/L malt extract): Slower growth but reduces
  bacterial contamination and encourages rhizomorphic morphology
- **Antibacterial additives**: Gentamicin, chloramphenicol, or streptomycin
  to suppress bacterial growth (does not affect fungal growth)
- **Activated charcoal**: Added at 1-2 g/L to darken the medium, making
  contaminant colonies easier to spot
- **Wood-based agar**: Sawdust or wood chip tea replaces malt extract for
  wood-loving species

## Pouring Plates

1. Dissolve agar and nutrients in water, stirring thoroughly
2. Pour into a flask or bottle (fill no more than 2/3 full)
3. Cover the opening with aluminum foil
4. Sterilize at 15 PSI for 20-30 minutes (larger volumes need longer)
5. Allow to cool to approximately 50°C (warm to touch but not hot)
6. In a [[sterile-cultivation-technique]] environment (SAB or flow hood),
   pour 20-25 mL per plate
7. Swirl gently to distribute evenly
8. Cover immediately and allow to solidify
9. Store plates upside down to prevent condensation dripping onto the surface
10. Let plates sit for 24-48 hours before use to check for contamination

### Spore Print/Streak

Spores are transferred from a print or syringe to the agar surface using a
sterile loop or swab. The goal is to deposit spores sparsely so that
individual germination points can be identified and isolated.

### Tissue Culture

A small piece of interior mushroom tissue (from a fresh, healthy specimen)
is transferred to the agar surface. This captures the exact genetics of the
fruiting body, unlike spore germination which produces new genetic
combinations. The tissue piece should come from the stem base or cap interior,
not the outer surface.

Procedure:
1. Flame-sterilize a scalpel and allow to cool
2. Tear (do not cut) the mushroom open to expose clean interior tissue
3. Excise a small piece (2-3 mm) of clean tissue
4. Place tissue-side-down on the agar surface
5. Seal the plate and incubate

### Wedge Transfer

A small wedge of colonized agar is cut from a clean plate and transferred to
a fresh plate. This is the standard method for expanding cultures and moving
between plates.

## Isolation and Strain Selection

After spore germination, multiple sectors of mycelium with different genetics
will be visible. Each represents a unique dikaryotic strain with potentially
different characteristics (growth rate, yield, fruiting quality, disease
resistance).

Strain selection process:
1. Transfer individual sectors to separate plates
2. Grow each isolate to full colonization
3. Test each isolate on grain and through fruiting
4. Select the best-performing isolates for continued cultivation

This process of selecting from a multispore germination to a single,
high-performing isolate is called strain isolation or strain development.

## Sectoring and Morphology

Healthy mycelium on agar can display different growth patterns:
- **Rhizomorphic**: Thick, rope-like strands radiating outward. Generally
  indicates vigorous, fruiting-capable mycelium
- **Tomentose**: Fluffy, cottony growth. Can indicate less vigorous genetics
  or overly rich media
- **Sectoring**: Visible boundaries between genetically distinct mycelial
  zones within the same culture

## Storage

Cultures can be stored short-term at room temperature (sealed plates last
2-4 months) or long-term in refrigeration (2-4°C, sealed with parafilm,
lasting 6-12 months). For indefinite storage, cultures can be preserved on
sterilized wooden dowels (slant cultures) or in glycerol solution at -20°C
or in liquid nitrogen.
