# THC - Tetrahydrocannabinol

Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC, specifically delta-9-THC) is the primary psychoactive constituent of cannabis and the most studied cannabis-derived cannabinoid. Under a microscope, THC appears as a sticky liquid crystal when warm and a glass-like solid when cool. The chemical structure of THC was discovered in 1964 by Israeli scientists Raphael Mechoulam and Yechiel Gaoni, a breakthrough that launched the modern era of cannabinoid research. Despite decades of study, THC continues to reveal new aspects of its immense influence on human physiology and psychology.

## Chemical Properties

- **Chemical name**: (−)-trans-Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol
- **Molecular formula**: C₂₁H₃₀O₂
- **Classification**: A terpenophenolic compound; specifically, a cannabinoid with a dibenzopyran core and a pentyl side chain
- **Physical state**: Sticky, viscous oil or resinous solid at room temperature
- **Solubility**: Highly lipophilic (fat-soluble); poorly soluble in water
- **Boiling point**: Approximately 157°C (315°F); this is relevant for vaporization and smoking

## Biosynthesis in the Plant

In the living cannabis plant, THC does not exist in its active form. Instead, it is produced as tetrahydrocannabinolic acid (THCA), the non-psychoactive carboxylated precursor:

1. **THCA biosynthesis**: Cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) is converted to THCA by the enzyme THCA synthase in the glandular trichomes of the cannabis flower
2. **Decarboxylation**: When cannabis is heated (through smoking, vaporizing, or cooking), THCA loses its carboxyl group (-COOH) and converts to active THC. This process is called decarboxylation
3. **Time-dependent conversion**: THCA also gradually converts to THC through aging, though the process is much slower than heat-induced decarboxylation

The THCA-to-THC conversion is critical for understanding cannabis potency: raw, unheated cannabis contains primarily THCA (non-psychoactive) rather than THC (psychoactive). Laboratory potency testing typically measures total THC, which includes both THC and the THCA that would convert to THC upon heating.

## Receptor Pharmacology

THC exerts its effects primarily through interaction with [[metzner-ayahuasca-therapeutic-potential]] of THC for inflammatory and [[blesching-cannabis-multiple-sclerosis]] patients (approved as Sativex in many countries)
- May protect neurons from damage (neuroprotective properties via CB1)
- Investigational for epilepsy, [[cannabis-for-multiple-sclerosis]] spasticity and cancer pain
- **Epidiolex**: CBD-dominant extract (not THC, but often discussed alongside THC preparations)

A notable limitation of pharmaceutical THC preparations is that they contain only isolated THC and lack the other cannabinoids, terpenes, and plant compounds found in whole-plant cannabis that may contribute to therapeutic effects through the "entourage effect."

## Psychoactive Effects

The psychoactive effects of THC are mediated primarily through CB1 receptor activation in the brain:

- **Euphoria and relaxation**: The primary subjective effects sought by recreational users
- **Altered perception**: Changes in visual, auditory, and temporal perception; enhanced sensory appreciation
- **Increased appetite**: Often described as "the munchies," mediated by hypothalamic CB1 receptors
- **Impaired memory**: Short-term memory impairment through hippocampal CB1 activation
- **Motor effects**: Reduced coordination and reaction time through cerebellar and basal ganglia CB1 activation
- **Cognitive effects**: Altered thinking patterns, difficulty with complex tasks, impaired attention

The intensity and character of psychoactive effects depend on dose, individual sensitivity, set (mindset), setting (environment), and the ratio of THC to other cannabinoids (particularly CBD, which can modulate THC's effects).

## Adverse Effects and Safety

Understanding the safety profile of THC is essential for both medical and recreational use:

- **Acute adverse effects**: Anxiety, paranoia, tachycardia (increased heart rate), dry mouth, red eyes, impaired coordination. These effects are dose-dependent and typically resolve as THC is metabolized.
- **Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome**: Paradoxical cyclic vomiting in chronic heavy users, relieved by hot showers and abstinence.
- **Dependency potential**: Cannabis use disorder affects approximately 9% of users who begin in adulthood; the rate is higher (17%) for those who begin in adolescence.
- **Psychiatric risk**: THC may trigger psychotic episodes in vulnerable individuals, particularly those with a personal or family history of schizophrenia. CBD appears to have anti-psychotic properties that may partially counteract this risk.
- **Lethal dose**: No confirmed human deaths from cannabis overdose alone; the therapeutic index of THC is extremely high compared to most pharmaceutical agents.

## Strain Variation and THC Content

THC content varies significantly among cannabis strains and growing conditions:

- **Strain genetics**: Some strains are bred specifically for high THC content (20-30%+ in modern varieties), while others contain only trace amounts (hemp strains, defined as <0.3% THC)
- **Growing conditions**: Indoor cultivation typically produces higher THC content than outdoor crops due to more controlled environmental conditions
- **Harvest timing**: THC content changes during flowering; harvesting at the optimal time maximizes potency
- **Degradation**: THC content decreases over time and is affected by UV light, heat, and oxidation. Proper storage (cool, dark, airtight) preserves potency

## Strain Selection

Choosing appropriate cultivars depends on intended use, local
climate, and growing conditions. Indica-dominant varieties
typically flower faster and stay compact. Sativa-dominant
varieties require longer flowering but produce different profiles.
## Common Problems

Nutrient deficiencies, pest infestations, and environmental
stress are the most frequent cultivation challenges. Early
detection through regular inspection prevents most issues.
Maintaining proper pH and watering schedules reduces problems.
## Harvest and Processing

Timing harvest correctly requires monitoring trichome
development. Clear trichomes indicate immaturity, cloudy
indicates peak potency, and amber signals degradation.
Proper drying and curing preserves quality significantly.
