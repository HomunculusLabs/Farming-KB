## Overview

Apples are among the most rewarding fruit trees for home orchards. With proper planting, pruning, and pest management, a single tree can produce fruit for 50-100+ years. This guide covers planting, central leader pruning, fruit thinning, pest management, and variety selection.

## Planting

### Timing
- Plant bare-root trees in early spring as soon as soil is workable
- Container-grown trees can be planted spring through early fall
- Fall planting works in mild climates where winter kill is not a concern
- Avoid planting during the heat of midsummer

### Site Selection
- Full sun (6-8+ hours direct) for maximum fruit production
- Good air circulation — avoid low frost pockets and wind-sheltered corners
- Well-draining soil — standing water kills roots quickly
- [[soil-ph]] 6.0-7.0
- Space standard trees 20-25 feet apart, semi-dwarf 12-15 feet, dwarf 8-10 feet
- Consider mature tree size when planting near structures, power lines, or other trees

### Planting Steps
1. Dig hole 2-3 times wider than the root system and as deep as the root flare
2. The graft union (bulge near the base of the trunk) must be 2-4 inches above the soil line — burying the graft causes the scion to root, negating the rootstock's benefits
3. Do not amend the backfill soil excessively — tree roots need to adapt to native soil
4. Spread roots out in the hole; do not ball, fold, or crowd them
5. Backfill partially, water to settle, finish backfilling
6. Tamp gently — do not compact heavily
7. Water thoroughly after planting
8. Apply 3-4 inches of mulch in a ring extending to the [[drip-line]], keeping mulch 3-4 inches away from the trunk

### Pollination
- Most apple trees require cross-pollination from a different variety
- Plant at least two different varieties that bloom at the same time
- Triploid varieties (Bramley's Seedling, Mutsu, Jonagold) cannot pollinate other trees — need two other varieties
- Crabapples bloom over a long period and make excellent pollinizers
- Honeybees are the primary pollinators — avoid spraying insecticides during bloom
- See [[backyard-orchard-design]] for pollination group planning

## Pruning: Central Leader System

The central leader system is the standard training method for apple trees, creating a strong, conical shape.

### First Year (Whip/Unbranched Tree)
- Cut the leader back to 24-30 inches above the ground at planting
- This forces lateral (scaffold) branches to develop
- Select 3-4 well-spaced scaffold branches during the first growing season
- Remove any branches below 18-24 inches

### Second Year
- Select the strongest upward-growing shoot as the new central leader
- Retain 3-5 scaffold branches evenly spaced around the trunk
- Scaffold branches should be at wide angles (45-60 degrees from vertical)
- Narrow-angled branches are weak and prone to splitting under fruit load
- Use spreaders or weights to widen branch angles on narrow-angled shoots
- Remove competing shoots growing too close to the leader

### Years 3-5: Building the Framework
- Continue establishing the central leader and scaffold branches
- Target 5-7 primary scaffolds by year 5
- Maintain a conical shape — lower branches spread wider than upper ones
- Remove water sprouts (vigorous upright shoots from the trunk and scaffolds)
- Remove suckers from the base of the tree

### Mature Tree Pruning (Year 5+)
- Prune annually in late winter (February-March) before bud break
- Remove dead, damaged, and diseased wood first
- Remove crossing branches and those growing toward the center
- Thin crowded areas to improve light penetration and air circulation
- Maintain the central leader dominance
- Remove water sprouts and suckers annually
- Goal: sunlight reaches the interior of the canopy — fruit needs light to color and sweeten
- Remove no more than 20-25% of the canopy in any single year

### Thinning Cuts vs Heading Cuts
- **Thinning cuts**: Remove entire branch at its point of origin. Preferred method — opens the canopy without stimulating excessive regrowth
- **Heading cuts**: Cut a branch back to a bud or side branch. Stimulates dense regrowth near the cut — use sparingly, mainly on young trees for training

## Thinning Fruit

Thinning excess fruit is essential for quality apples and tree health.

### Why Thin
- Prevents biennial bearing (alternating heavy/light years)
- Produces larger, better-colored, sweeter fruit
- Reduces branch breakage from excessive weight
- Prevents limb damage that invites disease
- Reduces pest pressure (insects target clustered fruit)

### When to Thin
- Begin when fruit is marble-sized (4-6 weeks after petal fall)
- Complete thinning within 6-8 weeks after petal fall
- Earlier thinning has greater impact on fruit size

### How to Thin
- Hand-thin to one fruit per cluster
- Space clusters 6-8 inches apart on the branch
- Remove the "king fruit" (center, largest fruit in the cluster) if it is misshapen or damaged
- Leave the most perfectly shaped fruit
- Twist fruit off gently rather than pulling to avoid damaging the spur

## Pest Management

### Codling Moth
- Primary pest of apples — the proverbial "worm in the apple"
- Larvae tunnel into fruit, leaving frass (excrement) at the entry hole
- Management:
  - Pheromone traps to monitor flight and disrupt mating
  - Apply spinosad (Entrust) or Bt ([[bacillus-thuringiensis]]) at petal fall and repeat every 10-14 days for 3-4 applications
  - Remove dropped fruit regularly (breeding ground for larvae)
  - Bag individual fruit clusters with nylon footies or paper bags (very effective on small trees)
  - Trunk banding with corrugated cardboard to trap overwintering larvae

### Cedar Apple Rust
- Fungal disease requiring two hosts: cedar/juniper trees and apple trees
- Causes orange spots on apple leaves and fruit; deforms fruit
- Galls on cedar trees release spores that travel to apples in spring
- Management:
  - Remove nearby cedar trees if practical (within 2 miles, ideally)
  - Apply sulfur or myclobutanil at pink bud stage and repeat per label
  - Resistant varieties: Liberty, Enterprise, Freedom, Williams Pride
  - Rake and destroy fallen leaves in autumn

### Apple Scab
- Most common apple disease in humid regions
- Olive-green to dark spots on leaves, causing premature defoliation
- Velvety brown spots on fruit
- Management:
  - Choose resistant varieties (Liberty, Enterprise, Freedom, scab-immune selections)
  - Apply sulfur or lime-sulfur as a preventive spray starting at green tip
  - Remove fallen leaves in autumn (overwintering inoculum)
  - Good air circulation through pruning reduces disease pressure

### Other Pests
- **Apple maggot**: Flies lay eggs under fruit skin; larvae tunnel through flesh. Red sticky traps, [[kaolin]] clay sprays, clean up fallen fruit
- **Plum curculio**: Small weevil that scars fruit and causes premature drop. Surround WP (kaolin clay) spray, pyrethrin at petal fall
- **Aphids**: Curl leaves and produce honeydew; encourage ladybugs, use insecticidal soap
- **Mites**: Cause bronzing of leaves; dormant oil spray, encourage predatory mites

### Organic Spray Schedule
- **Dormant season**: Horticultural oil (smother overwintering pests and eggs)
- **Green tip**: Sulfur (scab prevention)
