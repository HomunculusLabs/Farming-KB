# Passive Solar Energy Systems

Passive energy works in most places most of the time. There are two kinds: solar and mass. There are also two kinds of mass: biomass and thermal mass. All are low-cost, no-energy solutions that often work together to heat or cool.

## Types of Passive Energy

**Passive Solar:** Utilizing the sun's rays without special equipment — simply allowing sunlight to enter and warm a space.

**Thermal Mass:** Non-organic material with good insulating properties used to regulate temperature. Usually very thick with lots of mass — stone, brick, tile, concrete. Wood, carpet, and furniture do NOT work efficiently as thermal mass.

**Biomass:** Organic material piled to create heat through bacterial decomposition. Most supply comes from food waste and composting toilets.

## The Passive Solar Home

The solar home must be well-insulated with an open floor plan for air circulation. Key principles:

- Long side of house faces south (northern hemisphere) or north (southern hemisphere)
- Main living area on the sunny side with floor-to-ceiling double-glazed windows
- Cover windows at night during cold weather; during the day in hot weather
- Solar energy should fall upon thermal mass after entering through windows

**Thermal mass strategies:**
- Build the far wall or living room floor from stone, brick, or tile
- A Trombe wall directly behind windows with small openings radiates heat into the room behind it
- The mass absorbs solar energy during the day and releases it at night

## Solar Hot Water

One of the most efficient ways to harvest solar energy. Five major types of systems:

1. **Batch:** Heats a water tank directly from the sun
2. **Thermosyphon:** Flat panel collector with piping leading to a storage tank. Cold water enters pipes, warms, moves to the top, and flows by gravity to the backup heater. Most popular option; can be DIY-built.
3. **Open-loop direct:** Direct circulation through panels
4. **Pressurized glycol:** Uses antifreeze in a closed loop through a heat exchanger. Good for cold climates. Needs a pump.
5. **Closed-loop drainback:** Uses distilled water in a closed loop. Good for cold climates. Needs a pump.

### Sizing a Solar Hot Water System

| Climate | Collector Size | Family of 4 |
|---------|---------------|-------------|
| South and sunny | 1 sq ft per 2 gal | 40 sq ft |
| Temperate | 1 sq ft per 1-1.5 gal | 80-120 sq ft |
| Rainy/cold north | 1 sq ft per 0.75 gal | 140 sq ft |

**Key considerations:**
- Don't expect 100% solar hot water. Summer: ~90% solar. Winter: ~40% solar.
- Overall savings: about 7/8 of previous energy use for water heating.
- Pre-conservation is essential: fix drips, install low-flow heads, turn tank to 125°F (52°C), insulate pipes.
- Average American uses 15-30 gallons (57-113L) of hot water per day.

## Biomass Hot Water

Compost reaches 140°F (60°C) and can maintain that temperature for 6 months to a year. To harvest this heat:

1. Place compost heap inside a building you want to heat
2. Run plastic piping (thin-walled PVC or garden hose) through the compost
3. The heated water can be sent to the hot water tank

**Compost coil heater method:**
- A ring of chicken wire contains the compost pile
- A coil of plastic pipe wraps around it
- A second ring is set 4+ feet (1m) outside the first
- More compost fills the outer ring
- As it heats, it warms water running through the piping

This is most commonly used in greenhouses but can supply household hot water.

## Biomass and Biogas Considerations

Biogas (methane from manure decomposition) requires about 240 lbs of fresh manure/day to fuel a stove. A single cow produces 100 lbs/day. However, permaculture principles argue against this on small properties:

- Manure collection requires confining animals (taking them off forage)
- Breaks the nitrogen cycle by removing fertilizer from gardens
- Methane + oxygen is explosive — systems must be foolproof
- Small-scale human/food waste doesn't produce enough gas

Biogas makes more sense for city-scale systems where composting isn't possible for many people.
