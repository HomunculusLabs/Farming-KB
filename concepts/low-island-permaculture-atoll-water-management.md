# Low Island Permaculture and Atoll Water Management

## Overview

Low islands — atolls and cays — present some of the most constrained and fragile environments for human habitation. With finite freshwater lenses sitting atop saltwater, limited catchment area, and a [[calcium-phosphate]] soil chemistry that creates an impermeable subsurface layer, these islands demand exceptionally careful design. [[bill-mollison]]'s approach to [[low-island-permaculture]] integrates geology, water physics, traditional Polynesian practices, and innovative gardening techniques to create productive systems on these marginal lands.

## Island Classification

### Low Islands (Atolls and Cays)
- **Origin**: Residual islands composed of coral, sand, and accumulated sea bird biomass
- **Composition**: Primarily calcium — ground shell, coral, or both
- **Formation**: The sea makes the island; calcareous sands accumulate with phosphate deposits from sea bird manure
- **Character**: These are temporary geological events, appearing and disappearing over time

### High Islands
- **Origin**: Volcanic activity or ocean bed folding bringing up granites
- **Character**: More permanent, with greater elevation and freshwater capacity
- **Note**: Coconut palms establish naturally on high islands but nearly always require human intervention on low islands

## The Freshwater Lens

The freshwater system on a low island is a thin lens floating on saltwater:

- **Top of freshwater**: Often only 3-5 feet below the surface
- **Bottom of freshwater**: Only 4-5 feet below the top
- The actual volume can be calculated from the island's surface area
- Drawing down the freshwater lens allows saltwater intrusion, killing plants
- **Pollution prevention is critical** — even 2-3 septic tanks can contaminate the entire water table
- Dry toilets become a necessity on small islands

## The Platen Problem

The combination of phosphates (from sea bird manure) and calcareous sands creates a unique soil problem:

### Formation
- Calcium triphosphate forms a **"concrete" layer** approximately 18 inches below the surface
- This **platen** varies in depth from 9 inches to 2-3 feet thick
- It is often level on top but variable in depth below
- Rain penetrates very little — water follows the platen layer laterally and runs off

### Consequences
- Only shallow-rooted agriculture is possible without intervention
- The platen prevents rainwater from reaching the freshwater storage below
- A new platen layer reforms above mulch within 12 months if the old one is removed
- The platen can actually be harvested and used as a building material (it makes good stone walls)

## Breaking the Platen: Tree Establishment

The primary strategy for establishing productive vegetation on low islands:

### The Hole-and-Humus Method
1. **Shovel away** the surface sand layer
2. **Break up the platen** beneath it to reach brackish groundwater
3. **Fill the hole with humus** — everything organic available, brought in by canoe if necessary
4. **Plant a tree** — preferably coconut — in the filled hole
5. The tree **keeps the platen open** — down from the trunk and around its circumference, the platen cracks and becomes permeable to rain
6. The tree establishes its own groundwater system
7. **Repeat** the process to establish more trees
8. Within **20 years**, trees are in production

### Critical Insight
It is no good simply removing the platen and replacing it with mulch under sand — a new platen reforms within 12 months. The solution is **permanent penetration** through living tree roots that continuously break up the platen layer.

## The Ring Garden (Circle Garden)

The magical ring garden is Mollison's signature technique for low island vegetable production:

### Construction
1. **Break up the platen** and remove it from a circular area
2. **Heap sand around the edge** to form a raised ring
3. **Fill the circle with mulch** — creating a deep organic matrix
4. **Install a single drip system** at the center
5. The drip creates a **"wick watering" system** — fibrous organic material carries water laterally to all plant roots

### Geometry and Efficiency
- Drip water extends laterally approximately 2-2.5 feet
- Maximum effective diameter: approximately 4-5 feet
- Within a 4-foot circle, it's possible to pack **12 running feet of row crop** reachable from a single drip point
- If the hole is deep enough, it touches the water table at the base, adding **capillary wicking** to the system

### Planting Scheme
- **Inner edge**: Cabbages, peas, and beans
- **Outer edge**: More arid-tolerant plants
- **Vertical element**: Circle of reinforcing wire for climbing cucumbers and beans
- **Center**: Leave a few empty spots — a pleasant place to sit surrounded by plants

### Why Not Sprinklers
Sprinklers are actively harmful on coral atolls:
- Water is lost to hot wind before reaching the ground
- Surface evaporation is excessive
- Sprinkler systems accelerate platen formation below the surface
- The wick-drip system delivers water directly to root zones with minimal loss

### Design Philosophy
The main objectives are to **decrease pathway area** and **concentrate linear feet of crop** around the minimum number of drip points. Whether circular or linear patterns are superior remains an open question — either way, the principle of hose efficiency applies.

## Water Storage and Management

### Gley Ponds
Small ponds can be lined with fast-fermenting leaves:
- **Pawpaw, grape, banana** leaves — anything that ferments very quickly
- Fill with water pumped from a ground well
- This effectively **doubles** the water available in the profile
- **Coconut logs** line sandy banks to prevent collapse until vegetation stabilizes them

### Roof Catchment
All small roofs provide water:
- Storage in above-ground tanks, placed high for gravity feed
- Even small roof areas contribute meaningfully to island water budgets

### Septic Tank Placement
If clients insist on septic systems:
- Place them at the **perimeter of the island**
- General water flow is outward from center
- Excessive water use causes reverse pollutant flow — sea enters edges, signified by tree death from salt

## Settlement Pattern: Peripheral Housing

The optimal settlement pattern places housing on the island periphery:

- Water is drawn from central areas where it is freshest
- Waste is kept away from main water lanes
- Building a large village at the center of the island is described as a **disastrous technique**
- This arrangement keeps the freshwater lens intact while allowing peripheral waste management

## Aquaculture on Atolls

### Pond Strategy
A question Mollison found intriguing: what to grow in a pond on a coral atoll when the fringing reef already provides abundant fish and shellfish?

- **Aquatic plant food** — things not common to the islands
- **Exotic freshwater species** such as prawns
- **Ducks** to feed prawns via the algae cycle
- **Semi-aquatics** at the water's edge with varying root penetration depths
- **Tubers** on the bank above the waterline

### Self-Sealing Ponds
It is possible for a pond to grow its own gley lining:
- Allow a pond to fill gradually
- Grow crops within it that roll down and decompose
- Grade banks up as the organic material builds
- Transition from a dry hole to a sealed pond through biological processes

## Shoreline Defense and Erosion

### The Vulnerability of Low Islands
- Waves never cease to attack island shorelines
- From the air, Mollison has observed waves actively wiping out islands in bays
- The process accelerates when deflecting vegetation is removed

### Wind Patterns
All oceanic islands have two sets of wind and two periods of calm:
- Spring-autumn calm periods
- Winter-summer wind periods
- Typically northeast-southwest or northwest-southeast

### Vegetation as Coastal Defense
Vegetation can build an island up to approximately **6 feet** through sediment trapping. Front-line species include:

- **Pemphis, Tournefortia, Scaevola** — stand directly in the sea spray zone, growing to ~15 feet
- **Second line**: Tall plants including palms; date palms work surprisingly well on islands
- **Third line** (within 50 feet): Normal yield begins
- **Coprosma, bayberries, Prunus maritima, Rosa rugosa** for colder climates

### Critical Rules
- Never remove coastline vegetation without having alternative defenses ready
- Removing trees for an airstrip allows the sea to march inland — contain airstrips within vegetation, preferably at an angle to winds
- Keep coastline vegetation intact at the ends of any airstrip

## Mangroves: The Island Builder

### Importance for Nutrient Cycles
Mangroves are perhaps the highest biomass turnover system of any on Earth:

- **Genera**: Sonnerata, Rhizopodia, Aegiceras, Nipa, Avicenna (reaches latitude 40)
- **Nipa palms** are true mangroves
- All have twisty stems and leathery leaves, standing in quiet tidal waters
- They lay thick leaf mulch in the sea among their roots, creating rich loose mud
- The whole food chain starts within mangrove systems — shrimp, anthropods, diatoms
- **Aegiceras** is a superb honey plant, responsible for most tropical honey
- Mangrove jacks and specific fish are associated with mangrove stands
- The system is responsible for most offshore shrimp fisheries

### Mangrove Products
- Useful fruits (some edible if not delicious)
- Honey yields (Aegiceras)
- Durable woods and timbers
- Stem products

### Degradation
Mangroves are everywhere being degraded, filled in, drained, and chopped. When they are removed, people get washed away — then wonder why fish are getting scarce.

### Island Extension
Mangroves can carry an island out into the lagoon:
- Place mangroves along raised barriers made from logs and sand
- These form fish-trap enclosures that may increase fish populations
- The mangrove root systems build land outward

## Island Soil Biology

Starting from a potentially sterile environment:

- **Bring in worms** — they may not be present
- **Bring a handful of compost** — introduce bacterial, fungal, and algal forms
- **Bring soil with each plant** — plants have soil associates
- **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** for legumes may not be present
- Some tree varieties won't grow from seed unless started in their own soil
- Many **island legumes** are available — vines, ground vines, small trees, and tall trees
- The **horse-radish tree (Moringa)** provides edible leaves, flowers, and pods as a vegetable hedge growing 20-30 feet tall

## Animal Integration

Islands can support a variety of useful animals:

- **Giant tortoises**: Excellent lawn mowers, convert table scraps to fertilizer, clean up fallen coconuts
- **Land crabs**: Compost shredders and insectivores, consume wood beetle larvae, reduce infestation
- **Pigs**: Useful and fairly easily controlled
- **Ducks**: Do well in island systems
- **Chickens**: Standard integration

## Key Design Principles

1. **Protect the freshwater lens** — it is the island's most precious and fragile resource
2. **Break the platen permanently** — use living tree roots, not one-time mechanical breaking
3. **Use wick-drip systems** — never sprinklers on coral atolls
4. **Settle on the periphery** — keep waste away from central freshwater
5. **Maximize surface water storage** — every roof, every pond, every tank counts
6. **Preserve coastline vegetation** — it is your primary erosion defense
7. **Encourage mangroves** — they build land, support fisheries, and cycle nutrients
8. **Introduce soil biology** — bring worms, compost, and soil associates with every plant
9. **Think about nitrogen** — plant legume species throughout the system
10. **Use every linear foot efficiently** — in ring gardens, maximize row crop per drip point
