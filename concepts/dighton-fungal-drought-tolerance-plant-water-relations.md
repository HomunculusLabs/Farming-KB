# Fungal Drought Tolerance and Plant Water Relations

[[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] and [[fungi-in-the-environment-fungal-endophytes-plant-communities]] can significantly influence
plant water relations and help alleviate drought stress in host plants. This
role may be as ecologically important as the nutritional benefits that fungi
provide, and in some cases may represent the primary rationale for the
persistence of mycorrhizal symbioses in natural ecosystems.

## Mechanisms of Drought Protection

Mycorrhizal associations improve plant water relations through several
mechanisms: direct water flow through fungal hyphae, improvement in plant
phosphate nutrition, and altered hormonal balance in the host plant. These
mechanisms can operate independently or synergistically to enhance plant
survival during periods of water deficit.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Effects on Drought

Aug (2001) reviewed the effects of arbuscular [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] of
roots in relation to drought, concluding that root colonization by arbuscular
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] increased water relations of plants under both drought
conditions and during periods of adequate water supply. This suggests that the
mycorrhizal effect on plant water status is not merely a stress-response
phenomenon but represents a fundamental alteration of plant hydraulic
properties.

The effect of arbuscular mycorrhizal infection of [[mollison-designers-moringa-and-multipurpose-tropical-trees]] Acacia
nilotica and [[leucaena-leucocephala]] benefited Leucaena most in the presence of
drought conditions. The addition of phosphorus improved the growth of both
species, but the effect of mycorrhizae was greater than the effect of P
addition in Leucaena under drought stress (Michelsen and Rosendahl, 1990).
This indicates that [[mycorrhizal-drought-and-temperature-tolerance]] protection operates beyond simple
phosphorus-mediated improvements.

Cruz et al. (2000) demonstrated mycorrhizal drought protection in papaya,
where the decrease in leaf water potential was less in arbuscular mycorrhizal
plants during drought than in nonmycorrhizal plants. This direct effect on
leaf water status suggests that hyphal water transport contributes
meaningfully to plant hydration.

## The Nutritional vs. Hydraulic Debate

Fitter (1985) suggested that because of the observed lack of nutritional
benefit of mycorrhizal association in many natural ecosystems, other benefits
such as drought tolerance may be more likely the rationale for the existence
of mycorrhizae. This perspective challenges the traditional view that
mycorrhizae primarily enhance plant nutrition and proposes that water
relations may be the more universally significant function.

Sanches-Diaz and Honrubia (1994) further documented the significant impact of
mycorrhizal associations on plant water relations and drought stress
alleviation, supporting the idea that mycorrhizal drought protection is a
widespread and ecologically important phenomenon.

## Endophytic Fungi and Drought Tolerance

Protection against drought is not restricted to mycorrhizal [[endophytic-fungal-diversity]]
species. Leaf endophytes of grasses have been shown to confer drought tolerance
through the production of loline alkaloids, which act as osmoregulators
(Belesky and Malinowski, 2000). These fungal alkaloids help maintain cellular
turgor and metabolic function under water deficit conditions.

However, the effect of [[fungal-endophytes]] on drought tolerance is not
universal. Cheplick et al. (2000) found no benefit of endophytes in Lolium
perenne for drought tolerance; in fact, growth under both droughty and normal
conditions was lower in the presence of the endophyte. This indicates that the
effect of fungal endophytes is dependent upon a variety of environmental
conditions, including the specific host-endophyte combination and the
severity and timing of drought stress.
