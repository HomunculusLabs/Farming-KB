# Evaporative Demand Gradient and Stipe Elongation Mechanism in Basidiomycetes

## Overview

The evaporative demand hypothesis proposes that differential water loss across the surface of a mushroom stipe drives directional growth and curvature. This mechanism, articulated by Edmond R. Badham in his 1982 study of *[[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]* tropisms, provides a unifying explanation for anemotropism (wind response), phototropism (light response), and aspects of geotropism [[anemotropism-in-basidiomycete-mushrooms]]. The core idea is that stipe cells individually respond to local evaporative gradients, causing asymmetric elongation and resulting in the characteristic bending observed during [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]].

## The Boundary Layer Redistribution Model

When a mushroom stipe is exposed to airflow, the boundary layer of still air surrounding it is redistributed asymmetrically. The windward side of the stipe experiences a thinner boundary layer, while the leeward side retains a thicker boundary layer. This asymmetry has direct consequences for the local microenvironment at the cell surface:

- **Windward side**: Thinner boundary layer means water vapor and other gases are more readily removed from the surface. The local humidity at the cell wall is effectively lower, creating greater evaporative demand.
- **Leeward side**: The thicker boundary layer acts as an insulating layer, trapping water vapor near the surface. Evaporative demand is reduced relative to the windward side.

This differential in evaporative demand across the stipe circumference is the proposed driver of anemotropic curvature. The stipe bends toward the wind because cells on the windward side, experiencing greater drying, elongate at different rates than cells on the protected leeward side. The result is that the mushroom grows into the airflow, orienting its developing cap toward the direction of greatest evaporative potential.

## Four Factors Governing Evaporative Demand

Badham identified four [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] that interact to determine the magnitude and direction of the evaporative demand gradient:

### 1. Wind Velocity

Wind is the primary factor redistributing the boundary layer. In Badham's wind tunnel experiments, a velocity of 9 ± 3 cm/sec was sufficient to induce clear anemotropic bending in *P. cubensis* primordia. Higher wind velocities would presumably increase the boundary layer asymmetry and strengthen the tropic response. The wind velocity threshold for initiating anemotropic curvature in different species remains an area requiring further investigation. Importantly, the effect was observed in both fertile and sterile basidiocarps, suggesting the mechanism operates independently of reproductive development.

### 2. Humidity

Relative humidity directly modulates the evaporative demand gradient. Badham maintained conditions at 94 ± 3% RH, which is close to the saturation point that mushrooms typically require for normal development. At lower humidity, the overall evaporative demand increases, potentially amplifying any gradient. At very high humidity approaching 100%, the evaporative demand approaches zero on both sides of the stipe, which would diminish the tropic response. The interplay between absolute humidity and wind-driven boundary layer redistribution determines whether a meaningful gradient exists to drive directional growth.

### 3. Light

Light influences evaporative demand through surface heating. When photons strike the mushroom surface, they convert to thermal energy, raising the local temperature and increasing the capacity of air to hold water vapor at the cell surface. Badham noted that if the light flux density were strong relative to wind and humidity, light could become the dominant factor in determining evaporative demand. In his experiments, the light intensity was approximately one-tenth of natural field conditions, so the wind effect dominated. However, under natural conditions with full sunlight, phototropic curvature may be primarily mediated through this same evaporative mechanism rather than through a separate photoreceptor pathway. Light also exerts some inhibitory effect upon stipe elongation, adding complexity to its role.

### 4. Temperature

Temperature affects evaporative demand by altering both the saturation [[cervantes-vapor-pressure-deficit-transpiration]] at the cell surface and the capacity of the surrounding air to absorb moisture. Badham maintained a constant temperature of 24.5 ± 1.5°C in his wind tunnel. Temperature gradients across the stipe surface—whether caused by directional lighting, radiant heat sources, or convective cooling—would contribute to the evaporative demand gradient and could independently drive tropic responses. The four factors do not operate in isolation; their combined effect determines the net evaporative gradient.

## Parallel with Phycomyces

Badham drew explicit parallels between the proposed mechanism in *Psilocybe cubensis* and the well-studied anemotropic responses of *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*, a zygomycete fungus:

- *Phycomyces* exhibits both anemotropism (directed growth toward air flow) and an avoidance response (bending away from solid obstacles).
- Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated that wind and water vapor are both necessary for these responses in *Phycomyces*.
- Russo et al. (1975) suggested that ethylene may be involved in the autochemotropic (self-directed chemical) component of the *Phycomyces* avoidance response.

The similarity suggests that evaporative demand-mediated tropisms may be a conserved mechanism across divergent fungal lineages, rather than a peculiarity of basidiomycetes. The involvement of ethylene as a signaling molecule is particularly intriguing, as ethylene is a plant hormone that could theoretically play a role in fungal development, though this remains speculative for *Psilocybe cubensis*. If ethylene were involved, it would suggest a deeper evolutionary connection between fungal and plant hormonal signaling systems.

## Individual Cell Response Hypothesis

A key feature of Badham's evaporative demand model is the proposal that stipe cells may respond individually to local evaporative conditions rather than being directed by a centralized hormonal signal. This is supported by several observations from the wind tunnel experiments:

- Anemotropic bending occurs in both fertile and sterile basidiocarps, suggesting the mechanism does not require gill-produced hormones.
- The response occurs in both light and darkness, indicating it is not dependent on photoreceptor-mediated pathways.
- When cultures are placed with stipes directed away from the wind, the stipes still grow into the airflow, consistent with a purely physical (evaporative) stimulus rather than a gravity-sensing mechanism.
- Primordia between Stage 1 and Stage 2 consistently showed the response regardless of orientation.

This individual cell response model contrasts with the second phase of directed growth in *P. cubensis*, where a substance produced in the gills (likely a hormone) modifies the primary tropism and shifts the mushroom's orientation toward a negative [[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]. The two-phase model thus integrates both local physical stimuli and hormonal regulation.

## Alternative: Mechanical Force Hypothesis

Badham also considered an alternative explanation—that the mushroom is responding to the physical force of the wind itself rather than to evaporative effects. Studies on *Phycomyces* sporangiophores (Dennison, 1961) have shown that growth responds to both gravitational and centrifugal forces. A mechanical force model would predict that:

- [[windward-leeward-boundary-layer-redistribution-stipe-curvature-badham-1982]] should be proportional to wind velocity (force).
- The response should depend on the mechanical rigidity of the stipe tissue.
- Thinner, more flexible stipes should show greater curvature at equivalent wind speeds.

Badham's experiments did not explicitly distinguish between the evaporative demand hypothesis and the mechanical force hypothesis, and both mechanisms may contribute simultaneously. The involvement of water vapor in *Phycomyces* responses (Johnson and Gamow, 1971) provides indirect support for the evaporative model, but the mechanical contribution cannot be ruled out without further experimentation using controlled humidity at zero wind velocity.

## Hormonal Considerations

Several workers prior to Badham had postulated the existence of [[mushroom-hormones-gill-directed-tropism]] involved in tropisms (Gruen, 1963; Hagimoto, 1963). Decapitation experiments linked these hormones to the lamellae (gills), and agar diffusates from gills could partially replace the influence of the pileus (Hagimoto and Konishi, 1960). While some mushrooms contain hormones of higher plants, no evidence at the time suggested these regulate mushroom growth (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961; Jeffereys and Greulach, 1956; Pegg, 1973). The evaporative demand hypothesis provides an alternative, non-hormonal mechanism for the first phase of stipe elongation, reserving hormonal regulation for the second, geotropic phase.

## Implications for Cultivation

Understanding the evaporative demand mechanism has practical implications [[grass-seed-substrate-for-mushroom-cultivation]]:

- **Air circulation patterns** in [[fruiting-chambers-and-environmental-control]] affect the directionality of stipe growth. Even gentle air movement from fans or ventilation can induce anemotropic bending.
- **[[pf-tek-dual-chambered-terrarium-design-humidity-management]]** interacts with air circulation to modulate the strength of tropic responses. Very high humidity may reduce directional bending but promotes overall growth.
- **Lighting placement** can either reinforce or counteract wind-induced tropisms through its contribution to the evaporative demand gradient.
- **Clustered (cespitose) fruiting** bodies may use anemotropic growth to increase spacing between individual stipes, which would improve air circulation around caps and enhance spore dispersal.
