# Fungal Antibiotics

## Overview

Fungi have been one of the most important sources of antibiotics in human medicine. The discovery of penicillin from the mold [[penicillium-notatum]] in 1928 launched the antibiotic era and fundamentally transformed medicine. Fungi continue to be a rich source of antimicrobial compounds, and ongoing research seeks to identify new fungal-derived antibiotics to combat the growing crisis of antimicrobial resistance.

## Penicillin: History and Impact

### Alexander Fleming's Discovery (1928)

In September 1928, [[alexander-fleming]], a bacteriologist at St. Mary's Hospital in London, observed that a contaminating mold colony on a Staphylococcus culture plate had created a zone of inhibited bacterial growth. He identified the mold as Penicillium notatum and named the active antibacterial substance "penicillin." Fleming published his findings in 1929, noting penicillin's activity against staphylococci, streptococci, and other Gram-positive bacteria, as well as its apparent non-toxicity to human cells.

### Development into a Drug (1939-1941)

Fleming's discovery remained largely unexploited for a decade until Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and their team at Oxford University's Sir William Dunn School of Pathology took up the challenge of mass-producing penicillin. Key achievements:

- Chain purified penicillin and demonstrated its chemical structure (a beta-lactam compound)
- Florey's team conducted the first clinical trials in 1941, showing dramatic efficacy in severe bacterial infections
- Mass production was developed in the United States through fermentation technology, using corn steep liquor and deep-tank fermentation

### Wartime Production and Global Impact

Penicillin production was prioritized during World War II. By D-Day (1944), American pharmaceutical companies were producing enough penicillin to treat all severe Allied casualties. Penicillin saved hundreds of thousands of lives during the war and millions in the decades that followed. Fleming, Florey, and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945.

### Mechanism of Action

Penicillins are beta-lactam antibiotics that inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis:

- The beta-lactam ring structurally resembles the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of bacterial peptidoglycan precursors
- Penicillin binds to and irreversibly inhibits **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)** — transpeptidase enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan strands
- Without cross-linking, the bacterial cell wall is weakened, leading to cell lysis (particularly in actively dividing cells)
- Human cells lack peptidoglycan, explaining the selective toxicity

### Penicillin Classes

Multiple semi-synthetic penicillins have been developed from the natural product:

- **Natural penicillins**: Penicillin G (IV), Penicillin V (oral) — narrow spectrum, primarily Gram-positive
- **Aminopenicillins**: Ampicillin, Amoxicillin — broader spectrum including some Gram-negatives
- **Antistaphylococcal penicillins**: Methicillin, Nafcillin, Oxacillin — resistant to staphylococcal penicillinase
- **Extended-spectrum penicillins**: Piperacillin, Ticarcillin — active against Pseudomonas and other resistant Gram-negatives
- **Beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations**: Amoxicillin-clavulanate, Piperacillin-tazobactam — overcome bacterial beta-lactamase enzymes

## Cephalosporins

### Discovery

Cephalosporins were discovered in 1948 by Giuseppe Brotzu in Cagliari, Sardinia. Brotzu isolated a fungus, Cephalosporium acremonium (now classified as Acremonium chrysogenum), from a sewage outfall and observed that it produced antibacterial substances. Edward Abraham and Guy Newton at Oxford purified the active compounds — cephalosporin C — in the 1950s.

### Structure and Mechanism

Cephalosporins share the beta-lactam core structure with penicillins but have a six-membered dihydrothiazine ring instead of a five-membered thiazolidine ring. They also inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to PBPs, but generally have greater resistance to beta-lactamases.

### Generations

Cephalosporins are classified into five generations based on their antimicrobial spectrum:

1. **First generation**: Cephalexin, Cefazolin — primarily Gram-positive (staphylococci, streptococci)
2. **Second generation**: Cefuroxime, Cefoxitin — expanded Gram-negative coverage, some anaerobic activity
3. **Third generation**: Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime, Ceftazidime — potent Gram-negative activity, some cross blood-brain barrier
4. **Fourth generation**: Cefepime — broad spectrum including resistant Gram-negatives and some Gram-positives
5. **Fifth generation**: Ceftaroline, Ceftobiprole — activity against MRSA (methicillin-resistant [[staphylococcus-aureus]]) and resistant Gram-negatives

## Other Fungal-Derived Antibiotics

### Fusidic Acid

Produced by Fusidium coccineum (a fungus, despite its name), fusidic acid inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by blocking elongation factor G (EF-G). Used topically for skin infections caused by Staphylococcus aureus, including MRSA.

### Griseofulvin

Produced by Penicillium griseofulvum, griseofulvin binds to tubulin and inhibits microtubule function. It is not a true antibacterial but an antifungal used to treat dermatophyte infections of skin, hair, and nails. It works by inhibiting fungal cell mitosis.

### Pleuromutilins

Although originally isolated from the basidiomycete fungi Clitopilus passeckerianus and Pleurotus mutilus, pleuromutilins were developed primarily as veterinary antibiotics. Retapamulin (topical) and lefamulin (systemic, approved 2019) are derivatives used in human medicine. They inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the peptidyl transferase center of the 50S ribosomal subunit.

## Antimicrobial Resistance

### The Resistance Crisis

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing global health threats:

- An estimated 4.95 million deaths were associated with bacterial AMR in 2019, including 1.27 million directly attributable to AMR
- Without intervention, AMR could cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050
- Resistant strains: MRSA, VRE, ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas, multidrug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis

### Resistance Mechanisms

Bacteria have evolved multiple strategies to resist beta-lactam antibiotics:

- **Beta-lactamase production**: Enzymes that hydrolyze the beta-lactam ring. Hundreds of beta-lactamases exist, including extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs), AmpC enzymes, and carbapenemases (KPC, NDM, OXA-48)
- **PBP modification**: Altered PBPs with reduced affinity for beta-lactams (e.g., PBP2a in MRSA)
- **Efflux pumps**: Active transport of antibiotics out of the cell
- **Reduced permeability**: Mutations in porin channels that limit antibiotic entry
- **Biofilm formation**: Communities of bacteria embedded in extracellular matrix are inherently resistant to antibiotics

## Future Fungal Antibiotics

### Untapped Potential

The vast majority of fungal species have never been screened for antibiotic activity. With an estimated 2.2-3.8 million fungal species (of which only ~150,000 have been described), the potential for discovering new antibiotics is enormous. Approaches being pursued include:

### Genomic Mining

Modern techniques allow researchers to identify biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in fungal genomes that encode for potential antibiotic compounds, even when the compounds are not expressed under laboratory conditions. Techniques to activate "silent" BGCs include:

- Co-culturing fungi with bacteria to trigger defensive compound production
- Epigenetic modulators (histone deacetylase inhibitors, DNA methyltransferase inhibitors) to activate silent genes
- Heterologous expression of BGCs in model fungal hosts ([[aspergillus-oryzae]], [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]])

### Environmental Sampling

Underexplored environments are being targeted for novel fungal isolates:

- Deep-sea sediments and hydrothermal vents
- [[endophytic-fungi]] living inside plant tissues
- Fungi associated with insects (especially social insects with sophisticated antimicrobial defenses)
- Cave and mine environments
- Polar and extreme environments

### Novel Compound Classes

Promising fungal-derived compounds in development:

- **Sclareol derivatives**: Modified from fungal sources, showing activity against drug-resistant Gram-positives
- **Malbrancheamide**: From Malbranchea species, a novel indole alkaloid with antibiotic activity
- **Cyclodepsipeptides**: From various fungal sources, disrupting bacterial membranes

## Challenges

- **Rediscovery problem**: Many screening programs isolate known compounds rather than novel ones
- **Cultivation difficulty**: Many fungi are unculturable with standard techniques
- **Drug development cost**: Bringing a new antibiotic to market costs $1-2 billion, with poor return on investment compared to chronic disease drugs
- **Regulatory hurdles**: Stringent requirements for safety and efficacy testing
