# Aquaculture Temperature and Salinity Management

Temperature and salinity are species-specific factors that define which organisms can thrive in a given water body. Managing these parameters—through site selection, structural design, and active heating or dilution systems—is essential for maintaining productive aquaculture systems, especially when working with selected or exotic species outside their natural range.

## Temperature Management

Higher temperatures stimulate plant growth, algae production, and general biological turnover. It is advantageous to maintain winter temperatures at optimum levels for the selected species mix, as gains in yields of 2 to 5 times are common where ponds are regulated to optimise feeding activity and food growth.

### Heating Strategies

**Solar ponds** can be included within or adjacent to any fish pond as a passive heating system. Water pumped over a dark roof, rocks, or cement areas extracts solar heat at close to 100% efficiency. Sections of bitumen road can serve this purpose, or can have pipes buried under them at 1–5 cm depth as thermal siphoning systems for pond heating in winter.

**Waste heat** from industries such as power stations, salt works, or food processing plants provides a free, continuous heat source that can dramatically extend [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]].

**Structural insulation** includes glazing over canals and refuges, building raft columns insulated from the earth and colder waters, and providing heat refuges as earth-covered pipes or shaded canals. Glasshouses holding small ponds, [[compost]] heat, collectors, and trough reflectors are all proven strategies.

**Deep kettles** of 5 metres or so in shallow ponds provide natural heat refuges where fish can kettle (crowd together) during cold extremes or wait for warmer conditions to develop.

**Passive collector systems** are ideal and cheap to run, using a system of [[thermosiphon]] and non-return valves when heat sources are placed below the pond to be heated. Heat pumps from canals can deliver heat to ponds in the same way, shortening maturation times of fish or plants to about one-third that of cool, open pond areas.

### Yield Impact of Heating

Heating devices can raise water temperatures by 3°C or more (glasshouses). Although modest-sounding, this can extend growing periods of fish by 30–40% (New Scientist, 30 June 1977). Collectors depend on the area of collection relative to the pond. Adapted local fish generally need little assistance to survive, but selected stock or exotic fish may require refuges for a week or more of [[windbreak-effects-on-soil-moisture-and-extreme-weather]].

## Salinity Management

### Salinity Classifications

| Classification | Salinity (ppt) | Notes |
|---------------|----------------|-------|
| Fresh water | <1 | Very little dissolved salt |
| Freshwater limit for most fish | ~8 | Few freshwater species tolerate more |
| Coastal lagoons | 7–9 | Safe upper limit for human consumption; few frogs breed above this |
| Brackish detection | 11–12 | Detectably salty; oysters begin appearing |
| Estuarine | 11–27 | Rich transitional zone |
| Marine organisms | ~27 | Many shellfish and marine organisms found |
| Full sea water | 33–35 | Open ocean |
| Hyper-saline | 40–60 | Marine organisms seldom spawn above 40–50 ppt; desert organisms can live to 60 ppt |

### Species Salinity Tolerance

Fish that live their entire lives in freshwater rarely do well above 8 ppt. However, a great many fish and shrimp migrate between fresh and salt water—some spawning in rivers (trout), some in the sea (eels). These are called **euryhaline** species. Many mullet, salmonids, and some oysters and shrimp can be kept across a range from fresh to saline waters.

Saltwater fish rarely tolerate salinity below 28 ppt. Changes in salinity can kill pond fish not adapted to estuaries. Salt or freshwater immersion is also used deliberately to kill external parasites and disease in fish.

### Estuarine Dynamics

Estuaries and brackish waters generally contain more fish species than the river itself or the sea offshore, although the sea is far richer in molluscs and crustaceans.

In deep lagoons or rivers with barways, a **halocline** (salt-fresh interface) can develop, with denser salt water at depth or pushing up under the surface as a tidal wedge below fresh water. Marine forms live at depth, freshwater fish at the surface—sometimes for many miles inland.

As tides push into and recede from large river estuaries, extreme changes in temperature and salinity can occur. At low tide, a mud-living shellfish can be in fresh water at 4°C; at high tide, in seawater at 20°C. Many species in this zone burrow, migrate with the tide, or return to the river at high tide.

### Salinity Hazards

A hazard of marine pond culture in the humid tropics is torrential rain. Even with flood [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]], the sea itself may become so dilute as to kill lobsters, prawns, or milkfish in marine impoundments. Conversely, in areas of high evaporation and restricted tide flow, hyper-saline conditions can develop where fish and crabs occur as one-sex populations, one-age groups, or gigantic forms.

### Brackish-Water Polycultures

Very rich brackish-water polycultures are possible in estuarine and desert or atoll impoundments. Species assemblages can include algae, crabs, mullet, sea bass, milkfish, eels, and trout. Salinity is controlled by flood by-pass canals and sea weirs or locks. Fish are graded to suit pond conditions, or a salinity mosaic is established to mix species as diverse as carp and mullet.

Where tide and streams interact, as in some Hawaiian fish ponds, both freshwater and marine species can be cultivated in one pond series.

Brief immersion in salt water is an old remedy for reducing fungal disease in freshwater fish.

## Flow Management

Even very modest flow stimulates [[soil-gaseous-exchange-mollison]], photosynthesis, and therefore growth in plants, up to a level where mechanical damage by turbulence can occur. Flow in food-rich waters increases yields far beyond what is possible in still ponds.

### The Flow Balance

The balance to achieve is maintaining sufficient flow for aeration and vigorous plant growth while restricting flow to a level where soluble nutrients and calcium are not rapidly leached. For this reason, flow is often piped or channelled to fish and terrace areas rather than working in the stream itself, where floods can cause fish losses, structural damage, and wipe-out of balanced fertiliser levels.

### Flow Strategies

- **Holding back flow** for a few days allows fertiliser uptake, then resuming flow for aeration
- **Flowforms and aerators** oxygenate water through vortex patterns in the stream bed
- **Rippled falls or weirs** provide aeration at each drop
- **Herbivore stocking** — in fertilised or manured streams, weed clogging can be controlled by nutria, grass carp (White Amur carp), or other efficient herbivores

### Combined Oxygenation and Feeding

It is often ideal to combine the feeding station with the oxygenated area so that food wastes are also oxidised and waste control simplified. For eels, the aerated section is built with a narrow pass to the main pond to prevent aerated water escaping.

The combination of managed flow, [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]], and salinity regulation allows pond designers to create precise environmental conditions for diverse species assemblies, dramatically increasing total system yields while reducing the risks inherent in monoculture approaches.
