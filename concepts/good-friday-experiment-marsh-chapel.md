## Good Friday Experiment (Marsh Chapel)

The Good Friday Experiment, also known as the Marsh Chapel Miracle, stands as one of the most significant and controversial psychological studies ever conducted.

It demonstrated that a naturally occurring compound could reliably induce genuine [[psilocybin-compound-profile]] could produce experiences meeting the criteria for genuine mystical experience as defined by the philosophy of [[psychedelic-therapy-set-and-setting]] principles.

## The Experience

As the psilocybin took effect, the behavior of the two groups diverged markedly.

The placebo group experienced a relatively ordinary Good Friday service, with some noting the flushing sensation from the nicotinic acid but little else.

The psilocybin group, however, underwent what several participants later described as the most significant spiritual experience of their lives.

Timothy Leary, who was present, observed that the ten psilocybin subjects began behaving unconventionally almost immediately.

Some wandered the chapel murmuring prayers, one lay prostrate on the floor, others lounged on benches in states of apparent rapture, and another began playing strange haunting music on the chapel organ.

The most profound effects were internal, invisible to outside observation but vividly captured in the post-experience questionnaires.

## Measurement and Questionnaires

Pahnke developed a 147-item questionnaire designed to systematically assess various dimensions of the mystical experience.

The questionnaire was based on a typology of mystical experience derived from the work of philosopher W.T. Stace, who had identified common features across mystical traditions including a sense of unity, transcendence of time and space, deeply felt positive mood, sense of sacredness, objectivity and reality, and paradoxicality.

Independent raters who were naive to which group subjects belonged to were asked to compare the written descriptions of the psilocybin experiences with accounts of mystical experiences drawn from religious scriptures.

They were unable to distinguish between the two, confirming that the psilocybin-induced experiences met the established criteria for genuine mystical states.

## Key Findings

The results were striking and remain significant decades later.

All ten members of the psilocybin group reported experiences rated as either complete or nearly complete mystical experiences according to the questionnaire criteria.

The placebo group, by contrast, reported only mild religious feelings consistent with normal meditative states during a religious service.

The psilocybin group scored significantly higher on every dimension measured, including the sense of profound unity with all existence, the feeling of encountering an ultimate reality or divine presence, the dissolution of the sense of separate selfhood, and the conviction that the experience was more real and more true than ordinary waking consciousness.
