# Livestock Integration Systems

In a permaculture system, livestock are not separate from the garden -- they are integrated into every zone, performing multiple functions: fertilization, pest control, weed management, and food production. Animals are introduced gradually as the food forest matures.

## Sequential Animal Introduction

Animals should only be introduced after food forest establishment, as they may eat or destroy young plants:

| Wait Time | Animal Introduced |
|-----------|-------------------|
| 1-3 years | Chickens |
| 3-7 years | Pigs |
| 7 years | Sheep |
| 15 years | Cattle |

This timeline reflects how long different systems take to mature and become resilient enough to withstand animal impact.

## Multiple Functions of Livestock

Every animal should serve at least three purposes:

- **Chickens**: Eggs, meat, manure, pest control, weed management, compost turning
- **Ducks**: Eggs, meat, feathers, slug/snail control, pond fertilization
- **Geese**: Eggs, meat, grass mowing, guarding, weeding
- **Rabbits**: Meat, fur, manure (directly into worm bins)
- **Goats**: Milk, meat, brush clearing, manure
- **Pigs**: Meat, land clearing, compost turning, rooting/tilling
- **Bees**: Honey, wax, pollination, garden productivity
- **Cattle**: Milk, meat, manure, grass management

## Grazing Management

### Goats

Goats browse rather than graze -- they prefer woody plants, brush, and weeds. They control invasive species and clear land.

**Care**: Need good fencing (they climb and escape). Shelter in bad weather. Hoof trimming every 6-8 weeks. Vaccination program. Worming as needed. Fresh water daily.

**Breeding**: Breed at 7-8 months (75 lbs). Gestation 150 days. Watch for ketosis in late pregnancy (sweet feed daily). Separate pregnant does 2 months before kidding. Wean kids at 8-10 weeks. Milk twice daily after kidding.

**Housing**: Simple three-sided shelter, dry and draft-free. 15-20 sq ft per goat indoor; 200 sq ft per goat outdoor.

### Pigs

Pigs are exceptional land clearers and composters. They root and till soil, eat kitchen waste, and produce rich manure.

**Integration**: Can be rotated through garden areas between crops to clear and fertilize. In a food forest, they process fallen fruit and prevent pest buildup. Can be combined with duck/chicken systems for layered processing.

## Composting Toilet Livestock Integration

A composting toilet's liquid output (urine separated from solids) is high in nitrogen and can be fed to plants or used as fertilizer tea base. The separated liquid output feeds the garden system. Combined with animal manure, this closes the nutrient loop entirely on-site.

## Forage Systems

Permanent pasture requires management:

- **Forage forest**: Trees and shrubs that produce animal food (mulberry, honey locust, acorns, mesquite)
- **Strip grazing**: Move electric fencing daily to prevent overgrazing
- **Pasture cropping**: Direct-seed annual crops into perennial pasture
- **Silvopasture**: Trees integrated with pasture for shade, fodder, and timber

The key principle: let animals do the work. Chickens spread compost by scratching. Pigs turn piles by rooting. Geese mow grass. Ducks eat pests. The more functions each animal performs, the less human labor required.

## Rotational Grazing Design

Effective rotational grazing is the single most important technique for integrating livestock
into a permaculture system sustainably. The principle is simple: move animals frequently
enough that they graze plants down to the optimal height (typically 3-4 inches for most
pasture grasses) and then remove them before those plants are grazed again.
Recovery periods vary by season — 30-60 days in spring, 60-90 days in summer, and
potentially longer during drought. This rest-recovery cycle builds soil organic matter,
improves root depth, increases species diversity, and breaks parasite lifecycles.
In permaculture design, rotational grazing plans are integrated with the broader site layout:
grazing cells are sized to match the herd's daily forage needs, and paddock boundaries are
designed to follow land contours, minimize fencing costs, and maximize access to water and
shade. Electric fencing has made rotational grazing accessible even on small properties,
allowing paddocks to be reconfigured as conditions change.

## Poultry Integration in Garden Systems

Chickens are the most versatile livestock for small-scale permaculture integration due to
their small size, rapid reproduction, and multiple functions. The
[[permaculture-chicken-tractors]] is the classic integration method: a mobile enclosure
that is moved daily across garden beds, where chickens scratch, manure, and eat pests while
preparing the soil for the next planting.
Beyond the chicken tractor, chickens can be integrated into orchard systems as "chicken
platoons" that follow behind harvest to clean up fallen fruit and break pest cycles, or kept
in deep-litter systems in greenhouses where their body heat extends the growing season and
their manure fertilizes beds.
Ducks serve a complementary role — their preference for slugs, snails, and aquatic insects
makes them invaluable in gardens plagued by these pests, and their webbed feet do not scratch
and damage plant roots the way chickens do. Integration of [[faires-duck-and-goose-keeping]]
with pond systems creates additional nutrient cycling pathways.

## Manure Management and Composting

Manure is both the most valuable product and the most significant management challenge of
integrated livestock systems. Fresh manure can harbor pathogens, generate ammonia that burns
plants, and create odor problems if mishandled. The permaculture approach treats manure as a
resource to be cycled through biological decomposition before use.
Hot composting (achieving temperatures above 55°C for at least 15 days) destroys most
pathogens and weed seeds while preserving nutrients. Vermicomposting — using red wiggler
worms to process manure — produces a high-quality castings product with excellent nutrient
availability and beneficial microbial communities.
Rabbit manure is unique in that it can be applied directly to gardens without composting,
as it is relatively low in nitrogen compared to poultry and livestock manure and does not
burn plants. The integration of manure management with composting systems, worm farms, and
[[faires-rabbit-and-small-poultry-keeping]] creates closed nutrient loops that eliminate the
need for imported fertilizers.

## Health and Biosecurity Considerations

Successful livestock integration requires attention to animal health and biosecurity. Regular health checks, vaccination programs appropriate to local disease pressures, and quarantine protocols for new animals prevent costly outbreaks. [[livestock-feed-management]] should emphasize on-farm forage and [[livestock-feed-management]] to reduce input costs and close nutrient loops. Parasite management through rotational grazing and mixed-species stocking (different animals have different internal parasites, so cross-grazing reduces parasite loads) is more sustainable than continuous chemical deworming. Water access must be clean and reliable -- animals consume significantly more water than plants per unit of biomass, making [[mollison-designers-dam-construction-and-water-storage]] infrastructure a critical design element in any integrated livestock system.
