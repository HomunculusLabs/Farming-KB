# Chelated Nutrients

## Overview

When metal ions combine with organic compounds, a chelate is formed. The term comes from the Greek word for "claw" -- chelating molecules literally grab and surround metal ions, preventing them from participating in unwanted chemical reactions. This process makes otherwise unavailable nutrients soluble and mobile in soil, and is particularly important for [[micronutrient-deficiency-symptoms|micronutrient]] delivery.

## How Chelation Works

Chelating agents are organic molecules that form ring-shaped structures around metal ions, holding them in a soluble, stable complex. The chelate:

- Prevents the metal from reacting with other soil components (like hydroxides at high pH)
- Keeps the nutrient in solution and available for plant uptake
- Protects the nutrient from precipitation and fixation

This is especially critical for iron (Fe2+), which becomes unavailable at higher pH values. Many essential nutrients have specific chelate counterparts, but iron is the most important because its availability is most affected within the normal range of soil pH.

## Natural Chelation in Soil

### Siderophores

Certain bacteria and fungi in soil produce exudates called siderophores when soluble iron is in short supply. These bind to insoluble iron through chelation, making it much more soluble and mobile in the soil. In times of iron stress, plant [[root-exudates|root exudates]] can increase these microbial populations. The siderophore-producing microbes chelate iron primarily for themselves, but plants benefit from the increased availability.

### Organic Acids

Plant roots exude organic acids that can chelate metals, gathering them up and making them available (or in some cases deliberately making them unavailable to limit uptake of nonessential or toxic elements). Plants have developed systems to minimize uptake of nonessential elements by chelating metals in the soil before they can be absorbed.

### Glomalin

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi secrete glomalin, a compound that ties up metals (including heavy metals) in the soil, sequestering them so they won't harm their symbiotic plant partner.

### Humic and Fulvic Acids

Humic substances naturally present in compost and organic matter act as natural chelators. This is one reason compost is so effective at making micronutrients available.

## Synthetic Chelating Agents

### EDTA (Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid)

EDTA is the most common synthetic chelating agent used in commercial micronutrient fertilizers. It forms stable complexes with iron, zinc, manganese, and copper. EDTA-chelated nutrients remain soluble across a wider pH range than unchelated forms.

### Other Chelators

- **DTPA** (Diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid): More stable than EDTA at higher pH
- **EDDHA** (Ethylenediamine-N,N'-bis(2-hydroxyphenylacetic acid)): Most stable for iron at high pH (alkaline soils)
- **Citric acid**: A natural chelator used in some foliar sprays

## Foliar Application

Chelated micronutrients are commonly applied as [[foliar-feeding-plants|foliar sprays]] because:

- They are readily soluble in water
- They can be absorbed through leaf surfaces
- They bypass soil fixation issues entirely
- They are particularly effective for iron and zinc, which are mobile in plants once absorbed

Chelated iron sprays are a standard remedy for iron chlorosis (interveinal yellowing) caused by high pH soils where iron is otherwise locked up.

## Biological vs Synthetic Approaches

The biological approach to chelation -- maintaining healthy soil biology through compost, mulches, and minimal soil disturbance -- supports the microbes that naturally produce siderophores and other chelating compounds. This provides slow, steady micronutrient availability. Synthetic chelated fertilizers offer a quick fix but do not build long-term soil health.

Compost, mulches, and manures are effective at maintaining sufficient micronutrient levels because they support the microbial communities that chelate nutrients, greatly reducing or eliminating the need for supplemental applications.

## Key Points

- Chelation makes micronutrients soluble and available by preventing unwanted chemical reactions
- Iron is the nutrient most affected by pH and most commonly applied in chelated form
- Soil microbes naturally produce chelators (siderophores) -- healthy biology reduces need for supplements
- Humic acids in compost act as natural chelators
- Chelated nutrients are most useful as [[foliar-feeding-plants|foliar sprays]] for quick correction of deficiencies
