# Mushroom Garden Design

Outdoor mushroom gardens integrate fungal cultivation into landscapes, creating productive food systems that build soil, manage water, and support plant health. From Cotter's "Organic Mushroom Farming and Mycoremediation."

## Design Principles

- Integrate mushroom cultivation into existing gardens, pathways, and landscape features
- Use multiple mushroom species in complementary roles (primary decomposers, brown rot fungi, mycorrhizal partners)
- Design for the specific climate, available materials, and target species
- Plan for seasonal fruiting by selecting species with different temperature windows
- Consider the full lifecycle: inoculation, colonization, fruiting, and spent substrate recycling

## Key Species for Garden Cultivation

### Easy Outdoor Species (Difficulty 1)
- **King stropharia** (Stropharia rugoso-annulata): Garden giant, wine cap; grows in wood chip beds
- **Oyster mushrooms** (Pleurotus spp.): Logs, stumps, straw bales, cardboard
- **Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes): Hardwood logs and stumps
- **Reishi** (Ganoderma spp.): Stumps and buried logs; medicinal polypore
- **Turkey tail** (Trametes versicolor): Logs and stumps; medicinal
- **Wood ear** (Auricularia spp.): Hardwood logs and stumps; low insect pressure
- **Black poplar** (Agrocybe aegerita): Hardwood logs and wood chips
- **Elm oyster** (Hypsizygus ulmarius): Stumps and wood chips; large clusters
- **Nameko** (Pholiota nameko): Hardwood logs; slimy cap, popular in Asian cuisine

### Intermediate Outdoor Species (Difficulty 2)
- **Lion's mane** (Hericium erinaceus): Hardwood logs, prefers to fruit from cavities
- **Brick top** (Hypholoma sublateritium): Buried logs and wood chip beds
- **Maitake** (Grifola frondosa): Oak stumps, slow but large fruiting bodies
- **Chicken of the woods** (Laetiporus spp.): Large stumps and rounds; brilliant orange
- **Blewit** (Clitocybe nuda): Composted leaves and manure; cold-weather fall fruiting

## Wood Chip Bed Method

The most versatile outdoor cultivation method for garden integration:

1. Obtain fresh hardwood chips (harvested within 4 weeks)
2. Source chips from power-line companies, college campuses, or municipal tree services
3. Lay chips 4-8 inches deep over the target area
4. Crumble sawdust spawn and sprinkle over the surface
5. Rake gently to distribute spawn through the top 2 inches
6. Add more chips and spawn in layers for deeper beds
7. Water gently every few days if no rain; moisture is critical for colonization
8. Cover with cardboard or straw to retain moisture during colonization (optional)
9. Full colonization: 3-6 months depending on species and conditions
10. Mushrooms typically fruit after rain events during appropriate temperature windows

## Log Cultivation in Gardens

Hardwood logs serve dual purposes as mushroom hosts and landscape elements:

### Log Selection
- Fresh logs cut within 1 month of inoculation
- Diameter: 4-8 inches; length: 3-4 feet
- Oak preferred for most species, but many hardwoods work
- Match species to wood type: each strain prefers specific wood chemistry

### Inoculation Methods
- Plug spawn: Drill 5/16-inch holes, 1-1.5 inches deep, 6 inches apart; insert and wax-seal
- Sawdust spawn: Pack into drilled holes, seal with wax
- Wafering: Insert colonized cardboard into machete cuts in logs
- Stem base method: Bury stem bases of harvested mushrooms at log junctions

### Log Placement
- Stack logs in shaded areas; avoid direct sun
- Keep off ground using pallets, rocks, or log racks
- Place logs along garden borders, pathways, or as natural fencing
- Use as landscape elements around patios and seating areas
- Bury logs partially (half-buried rounds) for species that prefer ground contact

### Combining with Chicken Coops
- Place inoculated logs around chicken coop perimeters
- Logs serve as fencing and produce medicinal mushrooms
- Chickens benefit from the fungal ecosystem; mycelium remediates waste
- Use as roosting posts for dual function

## Trenched and Raft Methods

### Trench Method
1. Dig a trench 6-8 inches deep in a shaded area
2. Place inoculated logs or rounds in the trench
3. Cover with soil and leaf litter
4. Species like brick top and blewit fruit at the soil-wood interface
5. Fruiting occurs on shadier sides of the trench

### Raft Method
1. Stack logs horizontally in a crisscross pattern
2. Inoculate each layer with sawdust spawn
3. Cover with leaves or straw
4. Works well for nameko, brick top, and shiitake
5. Maintains higher humidity than single log placement

## Companion Planting

### Mushroom-Plant Synergies
- Brown rot fungi (Hypsizygus, Laetiporus) bond with nitrogen-fixing bacteria that collaborate with mycorrhizae around vegetables and fruit trees
- Myceliated wood chips as mulch improve water retention and soil structure
- Spent mushroom substrate as topdressing provides nutrients and beneficial microbes
- CO2 from mushroom fruiting benefits plant growth in enclosed spaces

### Integrated Systems
- CO2 from indoor fruiting rooms circulated to rooftop greenhouses benefits plants
- Water hyacinth biofiltration systems can be harvested for oyster/paddy straw substrate
- Spent substrate used for soil building completes nutrient cycling
- Mycelia serve as biofilters in aquaponics systems
- Chicken coops with fungal filter beds produce sweet-smelling compost and mushrooms

## Mycoremediation in Gardens

- Place myceliated wood chip barriers at downslope property edges to filter runoff
- Use king stropharia beds to capture and remediate pet waste
- Myceliated cardboard under chicken roosts captures manure for composting
- Install fungal filters around compost piles to reduce coliform bacteria in runoff
- Fungi help manage erosion on slopes and disturbed habitats

## Seasonal Planning

- Spring: Cold-weather oysters, shiitake, morels (if you have the skill)
- Summer: King stropharia, warm-weather oysters, paddy straw
- Fall: King stropharia (second flush), blewits, cold-weather oysters
- Winter: Planning, log inoculation, indoor projects

## Maintenance

- Keep wood chip beds moist during dry periods
- Replenish wood chips annually; old chips become rich compost
- Harvest colonized chips from old beds to inoculate new beds (perpetual system)
- Replace chicken coop fungal filters annually; use spent material as garden compost
- Remove competing vegetation from log areas
- Monitor for pests and disease; isolate problems quickly
