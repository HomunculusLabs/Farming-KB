# Mycorrhizal Fungi and Plant Symbiosis

Mycorrhizae ("fungus-roots") are symbiotic associations between fungi and the
roots of plants. In these mutualistic relationships, the fungal partner
sustains the plant by extracting scarce inorganic nutrients from soil and rock,
while the plant provides carbohydrates produced through photosynthesis.
Neither partner can thrive without the other in many ecosystems, making
mycorrhizae among the most ecologically significant biological partnerships
on Earth.

## Rock Penetration and Soil Formation

Some mycorrhizal fungi perform the remarkable feat of growing inside granite
bedrock. These fungi penetrate rock by secreting organic acids, including
citric acid and oxalic acid, that dissolve minerals in the granite. They absorb
the leached minerals and intrude deeper, pushing forward at their tips and
prying apart rock particles.

By infiltrating granite, the fungi provide pathways for water percolation.
Subsequent crystallization of ice accelerates weathering, yielding smaller
rock fragments incorporated into developing soil. In this way, mycorrhizal
fungi are not merely participants in soil ecology but active agents of soil
creation.

## Evolutionary Significance

Rock-penetrating mycorrhizal fungi may have been major players in the early
evolution of life on land. The earliest soils on the planet were probably thin
sludges derived from cyanobacteria and other simple microorganisms, offering
poor conditions for plant growth. By mining nutrients from underlying rocks
and transferring them to plants, mycorrhizal fungi may have played a crucial
role in facilitating the colonization of land by plants.
