# Teaming with Microbes: Soil Minerals and Cation Exchange

**Source:** Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis, *Teaming with Microbes*

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

Understanding soil mineral composition and how nutrients are held and exchanged is fundamental to understanding how the [[teaming-with-microbes-humic-and-fulvic-acids]] particles are small enough to have abundant negatively charged anions that attract positively charged cations.

### Key Cations (Plant Nutrients)
The cations absorbed by clay and humus include:
- Calcium (Ca++)
- Potassium (K+)
- Sodium (Na+)
- Magnesium (Mg++)
- Iron (Fe+)
- Ammonium (NH4+)
- Hydrogen (H+)

These are all major plant nutrients held in the soil by clay and humus. The attraction is so strong that only about 1% of cation nutrients remain in soil solution at any time.

### Key Anions (Easily Leached)
Anions include chloride (Cl-), nitrate (NO3-), sulfate (SO4-), and phosphate (PO4-)—all plant nutrients. Unfortunately, soil anions are repelled by the negative charge on clay and humus particles and stay in solution. They are easily leached away when it rains or soil is watered, which is why these nutrients are often missing from garden soils.

### How Plants "Eat"
Root hair surfaces have their own electrical charges. When a root hair enters the soil, it exchanges its own hydrogen cations (H+) for those attached to clay or humus particles, then absorbs the nutrient cation. This gives up one hydrogen cation for every cation nutrient absorbed, maintaining charge balance.

## CEC and Soil Fertility

The number of exchange sites on soil particles measures the soil's capacity to hold nutrients—the Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC). CEC is measured in milligram equivalents per 100 grams (meq/100g). The higher the CEC number, the more nutrients a soil can hold and the more fertile it is.

CEC depends on soil texture:
- **Sand and silt**: Low CEC (particles too large for electrical charge)
- **Clay**: Higher CEC (small particles with many charges)
- **[[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]/humus**: Highest CEC per unit weight

This is why gardeners add organic matter: more humus means more nutrient storage capacity. However, too much clay without sufficient humus creates high CEC but poor drainage due to clay's platy structure blocking pore space.

## pH and Nutrient Availability

Every time a plant root tip exchanges a hydrogen cation for a nutrient cation, hydrogen ion concentration increases, raising pH (making soil more alkaline). Root surfaces also take up anions using hydroxy (OH-) ions as exchange medium, lowering pH (making soil more acidic). Fungi and bacteria also carry charges on their surfaces, influencing soil pH through their [[plant-nutrient-uptake-mechanisms]] and release.

The pH created by nutrient-ion exchanges influences what types of microorganisms live in the soil, which in turn affects nitrification and other biological activities. Each plant has an optimum soil pH, but this has more to do with the needs of certain fungi and bacteria important to those plants than with chemistry alone.

## Soil Horizons

Soil develops distinct layers (horizons) over time. For gardeners, the O and A horizons matter most:
- **O horizon**: Contains organic material—fibric (identifiable plant matter), humic (decomposed but recognizable), and sapric (fully decomposed, unidentifiable origin)
- **A horizon**: Below the O horizon, where humus accumulates. This has the highest organic matter content, highest biological activity, and is where roots grow
