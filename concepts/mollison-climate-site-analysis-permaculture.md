# Climate and Site Analysis for Permaculture

Climate and site analysis is the foundation upon which all permaculture
design is built. Permaculture Two provides a systematic framework for
reading landscapes, understanding their hydrological and thermal
characteristics, and designing interventions that work with rather than
against natural forces.

## Broad Landscape Profile Analysis

Mollison presents a universal landscape profile model applicable from
humid tropical to cool temperate climates. The profile is divided into
four zones, each demanding specific management strategies:

**High plateau (A):** The vast catchment roof where rain and snow
gather. Prof. W.D. Jackson estimates that perhaps 85% of precipitation
condenses from night air on the myriad leaf surfaces of plateaus.
This area is the most critical to national or continental well-being.
Management requires careful husbandry, guarding from over-grazing and
soil loss, maintaining trees, shrubs, and [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-ground-cover-cultivation]] to trap and
hold moisture. Vandalism by ski-resort developers and high-country
graziers should be reduced to a minimum.

**Steep upper slopes (B):** Trees occupying these areas are groundwater
pumps preventing the rise of salted water to the surface downslope.
Upper-slope forests reduce cold air flow and the erosion that fills
lower valleys. A moratorium on all clearing or grazing of slopes of
18 degrees or more should be an international concern of as much
importance as a moratorium on arms.

**Lower slopes (C):** Potentially very productive mixed agricultural
areas, well suited to human structures, domestic animals, and
implements. Here the keyline system allows high runoff to be led to
midslope storage dams at the "keypoint." Using the high slopes as
watershed and diversion catchment drains, water is conserved for
frugal use in fields and buildings.

**Foothills and plains (D):** Where cheap water storage is available
as large shallow dams, and extensive cropping can take place.

## Sector Planning

Sector planning overlays external energy factors onto the zone map as
wedge-shaped sectors. The factors to sketch on a ground plan include:

- Fire danger sector
- Cold winds
- Hot, salty, or dusty winds
- Screening of nearby undesirable views
- Winter and summer sun angles
- Reflection from ponds

With zones and sectors sketched in, [[mollison-designers-slope-analysis-keyline-design]] can proceed. High
and low access roads can be placed -- the former for heavy cargo or
mulch, the latter for fire control. Provision for attached
glasshouse, hot air collector, reflection pond, solar pond, and
shadehouse should be made at all homestead sites.

## Placement Rules for Climate Response

Mollison gives a concrete example of climate-responsive placement:
a pine tree belongs in Zone IV (infrequent visits), away from the fire
danger sector (it accumulates fuel and burns intensely), toward the
cold wind sector (pines are hardy windbreaks), and should bear edible
nuts as forage.

A poultry shed should border Zone I (for frequent visits), be away
from the fire sector, border the annual garden (for easy manure
collection), back onto the forage system, possibly attach to a
greenhouse, and form part of a windbreak system.

## Thermal Belts and Microclimate Creation

Forests on upper slopes create thermal belts that protect lower
settlements from cold air drainage. The keyline water system
integrates with this by placing water storage at midslope, where
stored water moderates temperature extremes for adjacent crops and
structures.

Mollison describes creating microclimates through earth-sheltered
housing: vegetation and earth insulation, moated water supply, and
frost-clear roofs functioning as indoor glasshouses. The Rudolf
Doernach "biostructure" uses climbing plants on light frames to
create insulated, living buildings.

## Slope Advantages for Settlement

Mollison emphasises that slope gives immense planning advantages and
that hardly a viable human settlement exists that is not positioned to
draw from at least two environments. Any settlement that fails to
preserve natural benefits -- for example, clearing all forest from a
ridge -- impoverishes itself. Slopes allow filtration of wastes,
inseparable from large populations, through natural biological systems.

## Water as Central Design Element

Water is both the chief agent of erosion and the source of life. The
[[hemenway-garden-design-process-observation-and-visioning]] begins with water: covered tanks for drinking water,
high dams for bulk domestic water, waste water ponding systems for
algae, ducks, and fish production, and high slope access roads with
integrated drainage.

Mollison follows Yeomans' keyline principles: store water high in
the landscape, use gravity for distribution, and integrate water
flow with every other element of the design. The closer to the
source that intervention occurs, the greater the network of uses
that can be established.

## Fire as a Design Factor

Fire may try to get in (to a house or town) or out (from a public
fireplace). Orientation of structures, placement of water bodies,
selection of fire-resistant plant species, and maintenance of green
firebreaks are all integrated into the [[mollison-designers-sector-analysis-wind-sun-fire]]. Mollison
notes that the only moral use of steep slopes is permanent forests
as buffers to productive lower areas, which also serve as fire
protection.

## The Design Process Sequence

The recommended sequence for [[holmgren-site-design-as-cellular-design]] is:

1. Analyse the broad landscape profile and water systems
2. Map sectors (sun, wind, fire, wildlife, views)
3. Establish zones based on visit frequency
4. Conduct slope analysis and keyline planning
5. Place structures according to zone, sector, and slope criteria
6. Design water storage and distribution
7. Integrate plant and animal systems
8. Plan for edge effects and microclimate creation
