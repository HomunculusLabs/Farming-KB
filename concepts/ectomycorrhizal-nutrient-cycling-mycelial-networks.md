## Ectomycorrhizal Nutrient Cycling and Mycelial Networks

## Overview

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi form extensive [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] that connect individual host plants and drive critical nutrient cycling processes in forest ecosystems. These networks facilitate interplant carbon and nutrient transfer, mobilize nutrients from organic matter, and interact with other soil organisms to shape ecosystem function. Understanding these belowground networks is essential for comprehending forest productivity, stability, and resilience.

## Mycelial Interconnections Between Plants

### Carbon Transfer Between Plants

One of the most significant discoveries in mycorrhizal ecology is the demonstration that ectomycorrhizal mycelia can interconnect different plant individuals, allowing carbon transfer between them. Finlay and Read (1986a) used ¹⁴C-labelled carbon to show translocation between plants interconnected by a common mycelium. This groundbreaking work established that:

- ECM [[fungal-hyphae]] form physical connections between adjacent plants
- Carbon fixed by one plant can be translocated through the shared mycelium to another plant
- This interplant linkage creates a belowground resource-sharing network

### Phosphorus Distribution Through Shared Mycelium

Finlay and Read (1986b,c) further demonstrated that mycelial strands interconnecting host plants mediate the uptake and distribution of phosphorus. This showed that the mycelial network functions not only for carbon sharing but for bidirectional nutrient exchange between plant partners.

### Interplant Cycling Specificity

Eason et al. (1991) investigated the specificity of interplant cycling of phosphorus and the role of mycorrhizas in mediating this process. Their work addressed whether nutrient sharing occurs indiscriminately or is preferentially directed toward plants with greater nutrient demand.

### Carbon and Nutrient Cycling from Dying to Living Roots

Newman and Eason (1989) documented the cycling of nutrients from dying roots to living plants, including the critical role of mycorrhizas in this process. When roots die, the mycorrhizal mycelium colonizing them can absorb and redistribute nutrients to living roots, reducing nutrient losses from the ecosystem.

## Interactions Between Mycorrhizal and Saprotrophic Fungi

### ³²P Translocation Between Mycelia

Lindahl et al. (1999) demonstrated translocation of ³²P between interacting mycelia of a wood-decomposing fungus and [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] in microcosm systems. This finding revealed direct nutrient exchange between fundamentally different fungal functional groups — decomposers and mutualists — suggesting a previously unrecognized pathway for nutrient flow in forest soils.

### Litter Decomposition and Mycorrhizae

The relationship between mycorrhizae and litter decomposition is complex and sometimes antagonistic:

- Gadgil and Gadgil (1971, 1975) demonstrated that mycorrhizal roots of *Pinus radiata* suppressed litter decomposition — the "Gadgil effect"
- Berg and Lindberg (1980) asked whether litter decomposition is retarded in the presence of mycorrhizal roots
- Bending and Read (1995a,b) documented the foraging behavior of ECM mycelium and its translocation of nutrients from exploited litter
- Dighton et al. (1987) investigated interactions between tree roots, mycorrhizas, a saprotrophic fungus, and organic substrate decomposition in microcosms

### Mycorrhizal Mat Soils

ECM fungi form dense mycelial mats in some forest soils that have distinct chemical and biological properties:

- Griffiths et al. (1990) documented microbial dynamics and nitrogen chemistry in Douglas fir forest soils colonized by ECM mats, showing seasonal variation in nitrogen cycle transformation rates
- Aguilera et al. (1993) compared nitrogen in ECM mat and non-mat soils of different-age Douglas fir forests
- Cromack et al. (1979) described calcium oxalate accumulation and soil weathering in mats of the hypogeous fungus *Hysterangium crassum*
- Cromack et al. (1988) studied interactions between soil animals and ECM fungal mats

## Enzymatic Nutrient Mobilization

### Phosphatase Activity

ECM fungi produce extracellular phosphatases that release phosphate from organic compounds:

- Antibus et al. (1992) documented phosphatase activities and phosphorus uptake from inositol phosphate by ECM fungi
- Antibus et al. (1997) measured root surface phosphatase activities and ³²P-labelled inositol phosphate uptake in field-collected gray birch and red maple roots
- Dighton (1983) documented phosphatase production by mycorrhizal fungi generally
- Kroehler et al. (1988) studied the effects of organic and inorganic phosphorus concentration on acid phosphatase activity of ECM fungi
- Häussling and Marschner (1989) examined organic and inorganic soil phosphates and acid phosphatase activity in the rhizosphere of 80-year-old Norway spruce

### Phosphate Solubilization

Lapeyrie et al. (1991) demonstrated phosphate-solubilizing activity of ECM fungi in vitro, showing that some ECM species can dissolve mineral phosphates, making them available for plant uptake.

### Polyphenol Oxidase Production

Giltrap (1982) documented production of polyphenol oxidases by ECM fungi, with special reference to *Lactarius* species. These enzymes are important for breaking down phenolic compounds in soil organic matter.

### Nitrogen Mineralization

Jayachandran et al. (1992) showed that VAM fungi can mineralize organic phosphorus, and related work by Bending and Read (1996) demonstrated nitrogen mobilization from protein–polyphenol complexes by ericoid and ectomycorrhizal fungi.

## Nitrogen Dynamics in Mycorrhizal Systems

### Nitrogen Source Utilization

ECM fungi vary in their ability to utilize different nitrogen sources:

- Anderson et al. (2001) documented variation in nitrogen source utilization by *Pisolithus* isolates in axenic culture
- Dickie and Koide (1998) studied tissue density and growth response of ECM fungi to nitrogen source and concentration
- Hobbie et al. (1999) used isotopic evidence to gain insights into nitrogen and carbon dynamics of ECM and [[saprotrophic-fungi]]
- Norton and Firestone (1996) examined nitrogen dynamics in the rhizosphere of *Pinus ponderosa* seedlings

### Nitrogen Competition

Kaye and Hart (1997) reviewed competition for nitrogen between plants and soil microorganisms, highlighting the central role of mycorrhizal fungi in mediating this competition.

### Mycorrhization Helper Bacteria

Garbaye and colleagues pioneered the concept of [[mycorrhization-helper-bacteria]] (MHB) — specific bacterial populations associated with mycorrhizal roots that stimulate mycorrhizal formation:

- Garbaye (1991, 1994) described biological interactions in the mycorrhizosphere and the helper bacteria concept
- Garbaye and Bowen (1987, 1989) showed effects of different microflora on ECM inoculation success of *Pinus radiata*
- Garbaye and Duponnois (1992) demonstrated specificity and function of MHB associated with the *Pseudotsuga menziesii*–*Laccaria laccata* symbiosis
- Duponnois and Garbaye (1990, 1991) identified mechanisms involved in growth stimulation of ECM fungi by bacteria

### Soil Animal Interactions

Cromack et al. (1988) documented interactions between soil animals and ECM fungal mats, showing that soil fauna influence mycelial growth and nutrient cycling within mats. Brussard et al. (2001) further examined relationships between nematodes, mycorrhizal fungi, and plants, emphasizing the functional composition of species and its effect on plant performance.

## Phosphorus Dynamics

### Phosphorus Depletion Zones

The ability of mycorrhizal hyphae to extend the phosphorus depletion zone beyond the root surface has been well documented:

- Li et al. (1991a,b) studied the extension of the phosphorus depletion zone in VA-mycorrhizal white clover in calcareous soil and phosphorus depletion at the root–soil and hyphae–soil interfaces
- Owusu-Bennoah and Wild (1979) used autoradiography to visualize the phosphate depletion zone around onion roots in the presence of VAM
- Jakobsen et al. (1992a,b) documented hyphal spread, phosphorus inflow, and hyphal transport of ³²P over defined distances by VAM fungi

### Phosphorus Sources

Mycoorrhizal fungi access diverse phosphorus sources:

- Mousain and Salsac (1986) documented phytate utilization and acid phosphatase activities in *Pisolithus tinctorius*
- Clark and Zeto (2000) reviewed mineral acquisition by arbuscular mycorrhizal plants
- Goh et al. (1997) showed VAM-mediated uptake and translocation of P and Zn by wheat in calcareous soil
- Polglase et al. (1992) studied nitrogen and phosphorus cycling in relation to stand age of *Eucalyptus regnans*, including phosphatase activity and labile soil P pools

## Mycorrhizal Diversity and Physiological Variation

### Intraspecific Variation

Cairney (1999) reviewed intraspecific physiological variation and its implications for understanding functional diversity in ECM fungi. Cairney and Burke (1996) emphasized physiological heterogeneity within fungal mycelia as an important concept for understanding the ECM symbiosis.

### Functional Diversity

Miller (1995) addressed functional diversity in fungi more broadly, establishing frameworks for understanding how species differences translate into ecosystem-level differences in nutrient cycling.

## Bacterial Community Structure in the Mycorrhizosphere

Mogge et al. (2000) used fluorescence in situ hybridization and confocal laser scanning microscopy to determine bacterial community structure and colonization patterns of *Fagus sylvatica* ectomycorrhizospheres. This molecular approach revealed the spatial organization of bacterial communities on and within mycorrhizal roots.
