# Cannabis and Culture: McKenna's Historical Survey
Chapter 10 of [[terence-mckenna-stoned-ape-hypothesis]]'s *Food of the Gods*, "The Ballad of the Dreaming Weavers: Cannabis and Culture," presents a sweeping account of cannabis through human history.
McKenna frames cannabis not merely as a commodity or intoxicant but as a deeply entwined partner in human cultural evolution.
His central thesis is that cannabis embodies the values of partnership society and that its persecution reflects dominator culture's fear of boundary-dissolving experience.
## Ancient Origins and Global Spread
Cannabis is native to Central Asia and was spread by humans throughout Eurasia, Africa, and eventually the New World.
It appears in the earliest habitation strata wherever archaeologists have looked.
The sheer diversity of its names testifies to its cultural ubiquity across millennia.
Known as *kunubu* to the Assyrians (685 BC), *kannapu* in Greek and Latin (the root of "cannabis"), *bang*, *ganja*, *ganga*, and thousands of other local terms, the plant has been woven into the fabric of virtually every civilization it touched.
McKenna notes that resin collection for hashish production is thousands of years old, though smoking as a method of consumption reached Europe relatively late, arriving after Columbus brought tobacco-smoking practices from the New World.
An archaeological site at Non Nak Tha in Thailand, dating to roughly 15,000 BP, may contain evidence of prehistoric cannabis smoking.
The traditional Indian *chelum* represents one of the oldest continuously used smoking instruments in human history.
## The Scythians and European Contact
The Scythians, around 700 BC, were the vector that brought cannabis to Europe.
Herodotus left a vivid account of their [[scythian-cannabis-steam-bath-ritual]]: Scythians threw cannabis seeds onto hot stones inside enclosed tents, then shouted with the vapor that filled the space.
They also burned cannabis fruit in campfire circles, dancing and singing around the flames.
These descriptions, preserved in one of the foundational texts of Western historiography, establish cannabis use in Europe far earlier than is commonly assumed.
## India, China, and Religious Use
In China, the legendary emperor Shen-Nung (28th century BC) is credited with teaching hemp cultivation, establishing cannabis as one of humanity's oldest agricultural companions.
The physician Hoa-tho (~220 AD) used hemp as an anaesthetic, demonstrating sophisticated pharmacological knowledge.
In India, cannabis was known by 1000 BC as both a euphoriant and a remedy.
By the 10th century AD, it had become widespread in religious contexts, closely associated with esoteric Hindu spirituality, yogic saddhus, and the pursuit of direct transcendent experience.
Shivites and Sufis both relied on cannabis for religious inspiration, placing the plant at the heart of devotional practice across religious traditions.
This connection between cannabis and [[cannabis-spiritual-ritual-use]] is central to McKenna's argument about the plant's cultural significance.
## Cannabis as Cultural Style
McKenna advances one of his most provocative claims: entire cultural aesthetics may be derivative of hashish visions.
He points to Mughal Delhi and Isfahan as examples of civilizations whose art, architecture, music, and social life bear the imprint of cannabis-influenced consciousness, just as alcohol fundamentally shaped feudal European culture.
The argument rests on the plant's unique pharmacological properties:
- Cannabis is a boundary-dissolving substance that softens the rigid categories of dominator thinking
- The plant occurs in male and female forms, with narcotic resin produced exclusively by female plants
- Caring for female plants reinforces feminine values and a partnership-oriented worldview
- Cannabis deconditions users from dominator values by reducing competitiveness and promoting intuitive, less goal-oriented behavior
This last point — that cannabis actively undermines the psychological foundations of dominator culture — is, for McKenna, the key to understanding why the plant has faced such unrelenting persecution despite evidence of its benignity.
The dominator paradigm recognizes cannabis as a genuine threat to its values.
## The Language of Story and Cordage
McKenna draws a fascinating linguistic connection between the vocabulary of spoken discourse and cordage-making — the primary use of hemp fiber.
Terms like "weave a story," "spin a yarn," and "thread of a story" all derive from the language of working with hemp.
He suggests that cannabis was the plant that replaced sacred psilocybin mushrooms in Near Eastern cultures as the Great Mother was displaced by the Sky Father.
Its continued presence in Vedic society and Islam may have slowed the advance of dominator values in those civilizations, preserving vestiges of partnership thinking that might otherwise have been extinguished.
## Classical References and the Near East
Pliny (AD 23-79) reproduced Democratus's account of a cannabis-like plant that caused visionary delirium and prophetic powers, suggesting classical awareness of the plant's psychoactive properties.
Dioscorides described cannabis but curiously omitted its intoxicating effects.
In the Near East, cannabis became the intoxicant of choice for ecological and cultural reasons: the climate favored hemp cultivation, and Islamic prohibition of alcohol created space for alternative psychoactives.
## European Discovery and the Club des Hachichins
Marco Polo's account of the "Old Man of the Mountain" and his hashishin (from which the word "assassin" derives) introduced cannabis into the European imagination as an instrument of oriental intrigue.
Napoleon's Egyptian expedition of 1798 brought French soldiers into direct contact with hashish, and though French administrators attempted to ban it, Greek smugglers ensured a steady supply.
The monumental *Description d'Egypte* (1809-1813) stimulated European fascination with the plant and the cultures that used it.
This "orientomania" reached its apex in 1840s Paris, where Théophile Gautier, Charles Baudelaire, Gérard de Nerval, Alexandre Dumas, and Honoré de Balzac formed the legendary Club des Hachischins at the Hôtel Luzan.
The psychiatrist J.J. Moreau de Tours provided *dawamesc* — a jellied preparation of Algerian hashish — to the assembled writers.
Moreau's 1845 publication, *Du Hachisch et de l'Alienation Mentale*, was the first serious psychiatric study of cannabis intoxication.
Simultaneously, W.B. O'Shaughnessy introduced ganja to English medicine in 1842, establishing a Western medical tradition of cannabis use.
## 19th Century American Literature
Bayard Taylor's 1854 account in the *Atlantic Monthly* of hashish eating produced some of the most vivid visionary descriptions in American literature — pyramids, mother-of-pearl barges, and rainbow vistas cascading through altered consciousness.
Fitz Hugh Ludlow expanded this literary tradition with his own cannabis writings, inaugurating what McKenna calls a "pharmo-picaresque" genre that would later be continued by William S. Burroughs and Hunter S. Thompson.
## 20th Century Prohibition and the Archaic Revival
The 20th century brought systematic suppression.
Harry J. Anslinger's crusade in the 1930s, abetted by William Randolph Hearst's popularization of the term "marijuana" to link the plant to mistrusted underclass populations, transformed cannabis from America's largest agricultural product into a prohibited substance.
McKenna frames this persecution within his broader theory of dominator culture's assault on all boundary-dissolving plants.
Yet cannabis also became a vehicle for what McKenna calls the [[archaic-revival-philosophy]] — the innate human drive to restore partnership society values and reconnect with the plant-based spiritual practices of our ancestors.
## Recreational vs. Ritual Use
McKenna draws a crucial distinction between recreational cannabis use, which he sees as trivializing the plant's potential, and Archaic ritual use, which is genuinely transformative.
When employed in a ritual context with proper [[psychedelic-set-and-setting]], McKenna argues that cannabis is capable of producing "nearly the full spectrum of psychedelic effects."
The difference lies not in the substance but in the intention and cultural container surrounding its use — a distinction with profound implications for how societies choose to integrate or suppress psychoactive plants.
## Legacy and Significance
McKenna's survey of cannabis history in Chapter 10 is more than archaeology; it is an argument about the relationship between psychoactive plants and social organization.
By demonstrating cannabis's deep roots in partnership-oriented cultures and its systematic suppression by dominator institutions, he reframes the cannabis question from a matter of public health or criminal justice to a civilizational choice about which values to cultivate.
The chapter stands as one of McKenna's most historically grounded contributions to the ethnobotanical literature, grounding his larger theory of cultural evolution in specific archaeological, linguistic, and literary evidence.
