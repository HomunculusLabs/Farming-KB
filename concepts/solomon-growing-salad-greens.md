# Solomon on Growing Salad Greens: Lettuce, Spinach, and More

From [[solomon-gardening-when-it-counts-index]] (2005) by Steve Solomon.

## The Demand Level System

Solomon classifies vegetables by their fertility needs. Most salad greens fall into the **low-demand** category, making them among the easiest and most rewarding crops for new gardeners, especially those building soil fertility from scratch.

## Lettuce

### Growing Details

Lettuce needs cool conditions. It germinates poorly in warm soil (above 75 degrees F). For summer crops, Solomon suggests starting seed indoors or in a shaded area, then transplanting. For spring and autumn, direct-seed outdoors.

Lettuce is generally self-pollinating, making seed saving straightforward. Sow tiny seeds barely covered, pressing the soil down to restore capillarity. Thin progressively.

### Varieties

Many types exist: leaf (loose-leaf), butterhead (Bibb), romaine (cos), and crisphead (iceberg). Leaf lettuces are the easiest and can be harvested by the cut-and-come-again method. Solomon notes that some garden center seedlings are not worth buying; lettuce is better direct-seeded.

### Spacing

For full-head lettuce, thin to 10-12 inches apart. For cut-and-come-again, closer spacing (4-6 inches) works well.

## Spinach

### Growing Details

Spinach is a cool-season crop that bolts (goes to seed) quickly in warm weather or long days. Sow in early spring as soon as soil can be worked, and again in late summer/early autumn for a fall crop. Spinach tolerates considerable cold and can survive mild winters.

Spinach needs more calcium than many vegetables. If not using COF on rain-leached soil, apply lime before sowing.

### Varieties

Bloomsdale is a classic savoyed variety. Smooth-leaf types are easier to clean. Some varieties are bred for bolt resistance, which extends the spring harvest window.

### Spacing

Sow about three-quarters of an inch deep, thin progressively to 4-6 inches apart. Spinach roots go surprisingly deep for a leaf crop.

### Seed Saving

Spinach is wind-pollinated and dioecious (separate male and female plants). Isolation of at least a half mile is needed for pure seed. It is easier to buy fresh seed each year.

## Other Salad Greens

### Endive and Chicory

These become sweeter after frost, similar to kale. Endive needs blanching (covering the inner leaves to exclude light) for 2-3 weeks before harvest to reduce bitterness. Chicory (including radicchio) is more tolerant of poor conditions. Solomon notes radicchio as a crop discussed in his book.

### Radicchio

Radicchio is a type of chicory that forms a tight, red-and-white head. It is bitter but valued in salads. It tolerates cold and can be grown as a late-season crop.

### Oriental Greens

Many Asian greens (mizuna, mibuna, tatsoi, bok choy, Chinese cabbage) are fast-growing, cool-season crops. They are generally low to medium demand and excellent for autumn gardens. Chinese cabbage may need protection from cabbage worms.

## General Salad Green Principles

### Succession Planting

For a continuous supply, sow small amounts of salad greens every 2-3 weeks throughout the growing season. In hot weather, switch to heat-tolerant varieties or grow in partial shade.

### Winter Gardens

Where the snow lies thickly, Solomon refers to Eliot Coleman's Four-Season Harvest for techniques. In milder climates, many salad greens can be grown year-round with minimal protection. Kale, endive, spinach, and a few other greens can provide fresh produce throughout the winter.

### Sowing Depth

Tiny seeds (celery, basil, sorrel, most herbs) should fall into tiny cracks of newly raked soil, barely covered with a sprinkling of fine compost. Press the earth down gently to restore capillarity. Ordinary small seeds (brassicas, carrots, parsley) are sown about half an inch deep. Larger small seeds (spinach, beet, chard, radish) about three-quarters of an inch deep.

### The Importance of Root Systems

Root systems that don't compete produce healthier, more nutritious plants. Solomon includes Weaver's root system drawings to show that roots never turn back toward the center. Each plant extends into new soil to find untapped moisture and nutrition. Crowding causes stress that shows up as poor growth, insect attack, and disease.
