# Belladonna Plant

Atropa belladonna (deadly nightshade) is a perennial herbaceous plant of the
Solanaceae (nightshade) family native to Europe, North Africa, and western
Asia. One of the most notorious poisonous plants in European history, belladonna
was also a principal ingredient of the witches' brews of medieval Europe and
has a long history of medicinal, cosmetic, and psychoactive use. Its name
derives from the Italian "bella donna" (beautiful woman), referring to the
cosmetic use of its juice to dilate the pupils.

## Botanical Description

Atropa belladonna is a bushy perennial herb growing 3-5 feet tall, with ovate,
entire leaves and dull purple, bell-shaped flowers approximately 1 inch long.
The fruit consists of ripe black berries that are extremely poisonous, about
the size of small cherries. The entire plant has a characteristic unpleasant
odor, particularly when the leaves are bruised.

The plant grows in shaded, moist woodland areas throughout central and southern
Europe, and has been naturalized in parts of North America and Asia. It prefers
calcareous soils and is often found at the edges of forests, in clearings, and
in disturbed ground. The name Atropa derives from one of the three Fates in
Greek mythology, Atropos, who cuts the thread of life -- a reference to the
plant's lethal properties.

## Historical Use

### Medicinal Use

Belladonna has a long and distinguished history of use in European medicine.
Extracts of the plant were used to dilate pupils (mydriasis) for eye
examinations, a practice that continues to the present day in ophthalmology.
The plant was also used to reduce smooth muscle spasms, to reduce secretions
in respiratory conditions, and as an antispasmodic for digestive and urinary
complaints. See [[nightshade-plants-entheogen]] for related species with similar properties.

The tropane alkaloids derived from belladonna -- particularly atropine and
scopolamine -- remain important in modern medicine. Atropine is used in
emergency cardiac care, ophthalmology, and as an antidote to organophosphate
poisoning. Scopolamine is used for motion sickness and as a pre-anesthetic
medication.

### Cosmetic Use

The name "belladonna" (beautiful woman) refers to the practice, fashionable in
Renaissance Italy, of applying juice of the berries to the eyes to dilate the
pupils, producing a striking, doe-eyed appearance considered beautiful at the
time. This practice was dangerous, as the tropane alkaloids are readily
absorbed through mucous membranes, and accidental poisoning from cosmetic use
was not uncommon.

### Use in Witchcraft

Deadly nightshade was one of the main ingredients of the witches' brews of
medieval Europe, along with [[mandrake-plant]], [[henbane-plant]], and
[[datura-stramonium]]. These plants of the nightshade family were the major
plants involved in European witchcraft and divination, long exerting a great
cultural and historical influence. See also [[witches-fungi-and-flying-ointments]] for the broader context of psychoactive substance use in European witchcraft.

The tropane alkaloid intoxication -- with its sensations of flying, encounters
with supernatural beings, vivid hallucinations, and complete dissociation from
the body -- provided the experiential basis for many of the legends
surrounding European witchcraft. Witches "persecuted during the Inquisition
were often accused of using hallucinogenic plants of the Nightshade family, in
particular Henbane and Mandrake. For this many were tortured, murdered, and
burned."

The application of tropane alkaloid-containing ointments to the skin
(particularly in the armpits and other hairy areas) could produce psychoactive
effects through transdermal absorption, leading to the sensations of flying and
transformation that characterized witches' Sabbaths in popular imagination.

## Chemistry

The tropane alkaloids of Atropa belladonna are anticholinergic agents that
block the action of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors:

- **Atropine**: The racemic mixture of D- and L-hyoscyamine; the principal
  alkaloid and the most important medicinally. Causes pupil dilation,
  increased heart rate, and reduced secretions.
- **Hyoscyamine**: The biologically active enantiomer of atropine; the
  naturally occurring form in the plant
- **Scopolamine (hyoscine)**: A deliriant compound present in smaller
  amounts; more potent as a hallucinogen than atropine
- **Aporphine and other minor alkaloids**: Trace constituents contributing to
  the overall pharmacological profile

All parts of the plant contain tropane alkaloids, with the highest
concentrations in the roots and leaves. The berries, while attractive and
sweet-tasting, contain sufficient alkaloids to be fatal, especially to
children.

## Pharmacological Effects

The effects of belladonna alkaloids are characteristic of anticholinergic
(parasympatholytic) poisoning and affect both the central nervous system and
the peripheral autonomic nervous system:

- **Mydriasis**: Extreme dilation of the pupils with sensitivity to light
  (photophobia)
- **Tachycardia**: Rapid heartbeat, sometimes exceeding 150 beats per minute
- **Dry mouth and skin**: Loss of secretions, including saliva, sweat, and
  tears
- **Cycloplegia**: Paralysis of the ciliary muscle, causing blurred near
  vision
- **Delirium**: Confusion, agitation, visual and auditory hallucinations,
  inability to distinguish reality from hallucination
- **Hyperthermia**: Elevated body temperature due to loss of sweating
- **Urinary retention**: Loss of bladder tone, inability to urinate
- **Amnesia**: Memory loss during and after the experience

The hallucinations produced by belladonna are "true" hallucinations -- the user
cannot distinguish them from reality. This distinguishes tropane alkaloid
delirium from the perceptual alterations produced by classical hallucinogens
like LSD or psilocybin, where the user typically retains insight into the
drug-induced nature of the experience.

## Toxicity

Belladonna is one of the most toxic plants in the European flora. Fatal
poisoning can result from ingestion of the berries, leaves, or roots. Children
are particularly susceptible due to their smaller body size and the attractive
appearance of the berries. A dose of as few as 2-5 berries can be lethal to a
child, while 10-20 berries may be fatal to an adult.

## Cultural Significance

Belladonna holds a unique place in European cultural history at the
intersection of medicine, cosmetics, religion, and folklore. Its psychoactive
properties fueled centuries of witchcraft accusations and trials, while its
medicinal alkaloids remain important in modern pharmacology. The plant
represents the dual nature of many psychoactive species -- simultaneously a
medicine, a poison, a cosmetic, and a tool of spiritual practice. See [[entheogen-culture]] and [[hallucinogenic-plants-history-ethnobotany]] for broader context.
