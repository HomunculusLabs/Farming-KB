# Fungal Strain Crossability and Resin Disease Experiment

masanobu fukuoka conducted a notable mycological experiment demonstrating that two supposedly distinct species of resin disease fungi — one attacking citrus in the United States and another in Japan — were in fact capable of interbreeding. This finding has significant implications for understanding [[plant-disease-management-solomon]] strategies that treated the two pathogens as unrelated threats. Fukuoka's experiment would overturn this assumption entirely.

## The Crossing Experiment

Fukuoka, working from his orchard laboratory in Ehime Prefecture, attempted to cross the American and Japanese resin disease fungi. The procedure involved bringing mycelial cultures of both fungi into contact under controlled conditions to test whether [[spore-dispersal]] or through human-mediated transport of plant material, the genetic connectivity of fungal populations has implications for disease spread and the emergence of new virulent strains.

### Rapid Evolution Under Pesticide Pressure

When combined with Fukuoka's broader concerns about pesticide-driven mutation, the demonstrated crossability of these fungi becomes particularly alarming. If fungi from different continents can exchange genes, and if pesticide applications are simultaneously selecting for resistant genotypes and potentially inducing mutations, the conditions exist for the rapid evolution of new, more dangerous pathogen strains.

## The Octopolar Botrytis Comparison

Fukuoka drew a parallel between the resin disease fungi and the botrytis (gray mold) fungus involved in the loquat blossom rot outbreak. The botrytis ascus also contains eight genetically distinct spores and, if octopolar, may be capable of producing more genetic variants than even the tetrapolar shiitake fungus. This comparison highlights the vast evolutionary potential of plant pathogenic fungi. While higher organisms require many generations and large populations for significant genetic change, fungi can produce enormous numbers of genetically novel individuals from a single reproductive event. A single sclerotium, no larger than a poppy seed, can give rise to a mushroom that releases thousands of genetically distinct spores, each a potential new strain capable of infecting new hosts or resisting new pesticides.
