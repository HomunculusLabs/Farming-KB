# Potting Compost Recipes and Growing Media

Choosing and making the right potting compost is essential for success with
container-grown plants, greenhouse crops, and propagation. Plants grown in
containers have different requirements from those cultivated in open ground,
depending entirely on the gardener for nutrients, water, and a suitable
[[cervantes-green-algae-growing-medium]]. Several approaches to potting compost are available to the
organic gardener, ranging from commercially produced mixes to homemade
recipes.

## Commercial Organic Composts

A number of organic potting and seed composts are commercially available,
either peat-free or peat mixed with [[animal-manure-guide]], worm-worked material, or
kelp. Because commercial products vary greatly in quality and performance,
trial and error is the only way to evaluate them. Once you find a compost
that suits you and your plants, it is worth staying with it.

## Soil-Based Potting Composts

Many gardeners prefer soil-based composts, and there are clear advantages.
Soil retains moisture longer than peat and is much easier to rewet if it
does dry out. Most good [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] also contain [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]] that are
absent from peat-based products. Soil-based composts are particularly
recommended for plants grown in containers outside, where they may receive
less frequent attention, and for plants like chrysanthemums and fuchsias
that seem to prefer the stability of loam-based media.

The challenge is that not all garden soil is suitable. The loam must be
fibrous and crumbly to be useful, a type only obtained from freshly
cultivated grassland or from stacked turf. However, it is possible to make
your own loam over a period of about three years.

### Seed Compost Recipe (Soil-Based)

- 2 parts loam made from stacked turf
- 2 parts sphagnum peat (or peat-free substitute)
- 1 part coarse sand
- Per 2-gallon (9-litre) bucketful: 2oz (60g) bone meal and 1oz (30g)
  garden lime

### Potting Compost Recipe (Soil-Based)

- 7 parts loam
- 3 parts sphagnum peat (or peat-free substitute)
- 2 parts coarse sand
- Per bucketful: 1oz (30g) garden lime and 5oz (150g) blood, fish, and
  bone meal

## Making Loam from Stacked Turf

Making fibrous loam for soil-based potting compost is straightforward but
requires patience. Use the space between and around rows of raspberries,
[[holzer-soft-fruit-bushes-and-berries]], or apple trees. Sow the ground with grass and use it as
a path for at least a year. Then strip off the grass with a 1in (2.5cm)
layer of soil and stack it, grass side down, for a year.

Replace the removed soil with spent compost from finished greenhouse crops
and resow immediately with grass. The process takes three years in all, so
you need three plots to provide a continuous succession. As a safeguard
against soil-borne [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]], the loam can be sterilized by
heating to 212 degrees F (100 degrees C) for 15 minutes in an oven or
electric sterilizer.

## Peat-Free Potting Compost Recipes

For gardeners who wish to avoid peat entirely, several homemade recipes
produce excellent results:

### Peat-Reduced Compost

Mix peat 50:50 with green compost. Add 1oz (30g) of [[cervantes-dolomite-lime-ph-buffering]] per
2 gallons (9 litres) to balance the acidity. Remember to add both trace
and major nutrients, as both components contain little or no plant food.
A combination of pelleted chicken manure mixed into the compost, with
liquid kelp feed applied when needed, provides good nutrition.
