# AACT pH and Anaerobic Conditions

Managing pH and preventing anaerobic conditions are two of the most critical aspects of successful [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) brewing. Both parameters directly influence which organisms thrive and whether the final product benefits or harms plants.

## pH in Compost Tea Brewing

### Optimal pH Range

Most AACT brewers aim for a pH between **6.0 and 7.0**, which supports the broadest range of beneficial aerobic organisms. However, pH is not a parameter to aggressively adjust — it is primarily an indicator of the biological and chemical processes occurring within the brew.

### pH and Fungal Growth

Fungi generally prefer slightly more acidic conditions than bacteria. A pH range of **5.5–7.0** favors fungal growth, while bacteria are more comfortable in the **6.5–8.0** range. When brewing tea with the goal of increasing fungal biomass (for perennials, trees, and other fungal-dominated systems), allowing the pH to drift slightly lower can be beneficial.

### Buffering Capacity

Compost itself acts as a pH buffer, helping to stabilize the tea's chemistry. Well-made, mature compost typically has a pH near neutral and contains humic acids and other compounds that resist rapid pH shifts. This natural buffering is one reason compost quality is so important — poor compost lacks buffering capacity and allows pH to swing unpredictably.

### pH Dynamics During Brewing

During a 24-hour brew, pH typically follows a predictable pattern:

1. **Initial (0–4 hours):** pH reflects the starting water and compost chemistry.
2. **Mid-brew (4–16 hours):** Bacterial metabolism produces organic acids that may lower pH. Bacterial foods like simple sugars cause more rapid acidification.
3. **Late brew (16–24 hours):** As bacteria consume organic acids and more complex compounds are metabolized, pH may stabilize or rise slightly.

### Adjusting pH

Deliberate pH adjustment is generally discouraged in AACT brewing. The organisms themselves regulate pH through their metabolic activity. If pH drifts significantly outside the desired range, the root cause is usually:

- **Low pH (<5.5):** Excessive simple sugar foods feeding rapid bacterial growth, or contamination from acidic compost. Reduce food sources or improve compost quality.
- **High pH (>8.0):** Alkaline water source, or anaerobic conditions producing ammonia. Check water quality and aeration.

## Anaerobic Conditions

### What Is Anaerobic Brewing?

Anaerobic means "without oxygen." When dissolved oxygen drops below the threshold needed by aerobic organisms (typically below 5.5 mg/L), facultative and obligate anaerobes begin to dominate. These organisms produce metabolites that are harmful to plants, including:

- **Alcohols** — methanol, ethanol, butanol
- **Organic acids** — acetic, propionic, butyric, valeric acids
- **Hydrogen sulfide** — rotten egg smell
- **Ammonia** — in high concentrations
- **Phenols and terpenes** — plant-toxic compounds

### Indicators of Anaerobic Conditions

Recognizing anaerobic conditions early is essential:

1. **Smell:** The most reliable field indicator. Properly aerobic tea smells earthy, sweet, or like fresh mushrooms. Anaerobic tea smells sour, putrid, septic, like vomit, manure, or sulfur.
2. **Visual:** Foaming that is thick, persistent, and colored (especially dark or brown foam) may indicate anaerobic conditions. Surface scum or films are warning signs.
3. **Dissolved oxygen reading:** Below 6 mg/L is concerning; below 5 mg/L is actively anaerobic.
4. **pH shift:** Rapid pH drops often accompany anaerobic metabolism.

### The 14–20 Hour Danger Zone

Many brewers experience anaerobic conditions developing between hours 14 and 20 of the brew cycle. This typically occurs because:

- Bacterial populations peak and consume available food, then begin dying and decomposing, consuming oxygen in the process.
- Biofilm buildup on equipment reduces effective aeration.
- Temperature rises accelerate metabolism beyond the aeration system's capacity.

**Additional factors contributing to late-brew anaerobes:**
- Too much food added at once
- Compost overload relative to water volume
- Insufficient aeration capacity
- High ambient temperature
- Clogged diffusers or air lines

### The Smell Test

A simple but effective quality check: if the tea doesn't smell good, don't apply it. Anaerobic compost tea can cause more harm than no tea at all, potentially damaging plant roots, foliage, and soil biology.

### Foam as an Indicator

Some foaming is normal in AACT due to surfactants produced by bacteria and proteins from compost. However:

- **Light, white foam that dissipates quickly:** Normal, indicates healthy bacterial activity.
- **Thick, colored, persistent foam:** May indicate excess protein breakdown, dead organism decomposition, or anaerobic conditions.
- **No foam at all:** May indicate insufficient biological activity.

### Cleaning and Maintenance

Preventing anaerobic conditions starts with equipment hygiene:

- **Clean tanks between brews:** Remove all biofilm and residue.
- **Wipe slime from surfaces:** Biofilm buildup reduces effective aeration.
- **Check diffusers regularly:** Clogged diffusers drastically reduce oxygen transfer.
- **Inspect air lines:** Kinked or blocked lines reduce airflow.

### Harmful Pathogens in Anaerobic Tea

Anaerobic conditions create an environment where human and plant pathogens can proliferate:

- **Milk pathogens:** *Listeria*, *Salmonella*, and *E. coli* can multiply in poorly aerated tea.
- **Plant pathogens:** *Pythium*, *Phytophthora*, *Rhizoctonia*, and *Fusarium* species are facultative anaerobes that thrive when oxygen is limited.
- **Coliforms:** While not all coliforms are harmful, elevated coliform counts indicate fecal contamination risk and anaerobic conditions.
- **Fecal E. coli:** A specific indicator of contamination; its presence means the tea is unsafe for food crops.

### Citric Acid and Chlorine

Citric acid is sometimes mentioned in compost tea discussions for pH adjustment, but its use is generally not recommended. It can feed undesirable microorganisms and does not address the root cause of pH imbalance. Regarding chlorine, all municipal water chlorine must be removed before brewing — either by pre-aeration or chemical neutralization — as even low levels are lethal to beneficial organisms.

### Holding Time for AACT

Once brewing is complete, AACT should be applied as soon as possible — ideally within **4–6 hours**. During holding:

- Oxygen levels decline without active aeration.
- Bacterial populations begin to shift.
- Food resources are depleted.
- Risk of anaerobic conditions increases with each hour.

If tea must be held, continue aeration at reduced levels and maintain temperature control.
