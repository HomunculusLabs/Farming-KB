# Mushroom Contaminant Identification

## Overview

Contamination is the most common cause of failure in amateur [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]].
Contaminant organisms — molds, bacteria, and yeasts — compete with the desired
mycelium for substrate resources and can completely overrun a culture if not identified
and addressed early. The PF TEK incorporates several design features that reduce
contamination risk, but understanding how to identify and prevent contaminants remains
essential for consistent success. Contaminant invaders appear in various colors from
pastels to black, and if they appear, the culture is generally doomed.

## Types of Contamination

### Mold Contamination

Molds are the most visible and easily identified contaminants. They appear as fuzzy or
powdery growths on the substrate surface, often in colors that contrast sharply with
the white mycelium of the desired mushroom:

- **Green molds** (Trichoderma, Aspergillus) — the most common contaminants, appearing
  as green or blue-green patches. Trichoderma is particularly aggressive and can
  spread rapidly, overtaking a jar within days. Green mold often indicates
  insufficient sterilization or a breach in the dry vermiculite barrier layer.

- **Black molds** — appear as dark black or very dark green patches. These can be
  particularly dangerous as some species produce harmful mycotoxins. Black mold
  contamination warrants careful handling and disposal.

- **Pink molds** — appear as pinkish or salmon-colored growth, often indicating
  contamination by bacteria of the genus Serratia or by certain Neurospora species.

- **White molds** — can be difficult to distinguish from mushroom mycelium.
  Contaminant white molds may appear more rhizomorphic (stringy) or powdery compared
  to the cottony growth of healthy mushroom mycelium. Some white molds have a
  different texture or growth pattern that becomes apparent with experience.

### Bacterial Contamination

Bacterial contamination is detectable through the top dry vermiculite layer as a sour,
foul odor within two days after inoculation, often accompanied by no visible spore
germination. Bacteria thrive in wet conditions and can spread rapidly if the substrate
is too moist. The characteristic rancid or rotting smell is unmistakable — if you
detect this odor when loosening a jar lid, the jar is bacterially contaminated.

Bacterial contamination can be dangerous to handle. When cleaning contaminated jars,
maintain a safe distance from the substrate, do not inhale the bacteria, and wash
hands thoroughly after contact. Bacterial contaminants can cause respiratory infections
and other health issues in humans.

### Yeast Contamination

Yeasts may appear as slimy or wet-looking patches on the substrate. They often produce
a slight alcoholic or fermented odor. Yeast contamination is less common than mold or
bacterial contamination but can occur when [[pf-tek-substrate-moisture-diagnosis-and-correction]] is excessive.

## The Control Jar Technique

The control jar technique is a diagnostic method used to determine the source of
contamination. After jars are steam sterilized, let them cool, tighten the lids, and
let them sit uninoculated for several days. Watch for any colored growths or changes
in the appearance of the substrate. Check for the telltale rancid odor of bacteria by
loosening the jar lid.

**If contamination appears in the uninoculated control jars:**
- The [[boil-a-bag-pressure-sterilization-technique]] needs to be checked.
- The most likely cause is insufficient sterilization time — lengthen the steaming
  duration.
- Possible causes include: pot lid not fitting tightly, water sloshing into jars,
  or insufficient steam time (less than one hour).

**If the control jars remain clean but inoculated jars show contamination:**
- The spore syringe was contaminated.
- The dry vermiculite barrier layer was breached during inoculation (needle disturbed
  the barrier and exposed the wet substrate to air).
- Inoculation was performed in a non-sterile environment (drafts, airborne particles).

## Sources of Contamination

Contaminants enter the cultivation process through several vectors:

1. **Airborne contamination** — the most common source. Spores of mold and bacteria
   are ubiquitous in the air. The dry vermiculite barrier layer is the primary
   defense against this vector.

2. **Contaminated inoculant** — spore syringes that are not properly prepared or
   stored can carry bacterial or fungal contaminants directly into the substrate.

3. **Insufficient sterilization** — steam sterilization must be thorough and
   sustained. A tightly covered pot with a gentle boil for a full hour is the
   minimum recommended duration.

4. **Post-inoculation breaches** — disturbing the dry vermiculite layer during or
   after inoculation exposes the wet, nutritious substrate to airborne contaminants.

5. **Tool contamination** — unsterilized needles, forks, or other tools can
   introduce contaminants directly to the substrate.

## Prevention Strategies

### The Dry Vermiculite Barrier

The dry vermiculite layer on top of the substrate is the primary contamination defense
in the PF TEK. This layer was discovered by [[pf-tek-psilocybe-fanaticus]] and serves as both a
physical barrier and a moisture buffer. It protects the wet substrate from airborne
contaminants and absorbs and regulates moisture transpiration and condensation within
the jar. Maintaining the integrity of this layer during inoculation is critical.

### Sterile Technique During Inoculation

- Work in a clean, draft-free environment.
- Flame-sterilize the needle between uses if it contacts anything.
- Use an alcohol flame rather than butane for cleaner sterilization.
- Do not touch the needle to any non-sterile surface.
- Minimize the time the jar is open during inoculation.

### Proper Sterilization

- Use a tight-fitting pot lid for steam retention.
- Steam at the lowest possible gentle boil for at least one hour.
- Do not overheat (dries substrate) or under-heat (insufficient sterilization).
- Ensure jar lid bands are loose during steaming to allow steam penetration.

### Moisture Management

- Follow the substrate formula precisely — too much water promotes bacterial growth.
- Use the correct type of vermiculite (finer grade holds more water; adjust formulas
  accordingly).
- Keep jar lids tight during incubation if substrate is on the dry side; loose if
  substrate is wet to prevent water accumulation at the bottom.

## When to Discard

If contamination is detected, the affected jar should be discarded. There is no
reliable way to rescue a contaminated culture — attempting to do so risks spreading
contaminants to other jars and the [[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]]. Discard contaminated jars
carefully, especially bacterial contamination, which can be harmful to humans.
