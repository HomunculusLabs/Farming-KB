# Historical Intensive Gardening Literature

## Overview

The study of historical intensive gardening literature provides essential context for modern winter-harvest and year-round vegetable production. Many foundational techniques used today — including [[coleman-protected-cultivation]], intensive soil management, and [[succession-planting]] — were developed and refined by 19th and early 20th century European growers, particularly the French *maraîchers* (market gardeners) of Paris. [[eliot-coleman]]'s [[eliot-coleman-and-the-winter-harvest-handbook]] draws extensively on this body of work, providing an annotated reading list that serves as both a scholarly resource and a practical guide for growers seeking to deepen their understanding of these time-tested methods.

## The French Intensive Tradition

### Origins in Parisian Market Gardening

The [[parisian-market-gardening]] system (*la culture maraîchère*) that developed in the 18th and 19th centuries was the most sophisticated intensive vegetable production system of its era. Operating within the city limits of Paris, these growers (known as *maraîchers*) produced remarkable quantities of fresh vegetables year-round using horse manure for soil fertility and heat, cold frames, cloches (glass bells), and careful succession planting. The system was built on three pillars: **manure, water, and labor**.

### Key Historical Sources

- **Aguatias, P. — *Intensive Culture of Vegetables on the French System* (London: L. Upcott Gill, 1913)**
  This foundational text explicitly states that "manure, water, and labor constitute the backbone of intensive culture" and provides detailed instructions on applying these three elements for successful year-round vegetable production. It remains one of the most comprehensive English-language descriptions of the French intensive system.

- **Courtois-Gérard, M. — *Manual pratique de culture maraîchère***
  A practical manual from a working French grower, providing firsthand documentation of the techniques and systems used in Parisian market gardens.

- **Curé, J. — *Ma pratique de la culture maraîchère ordinaire et forcée***
  Another practitioner's manual documenting both ordinary and forced (protected) [[cannabis-sativa-indoor-cultivation-techniques]].

- **Moreau, J.G., and Daverne, J.J. — *Manual pratique de la culture maraîchère de Paris***
  A comprehensive practical manual covering the full scope of Parisian market gardening practice.

- **Gressent, Vincent Alfred — *Le potager moderne***
  Documents the modernized version of the traditional French garden system, bridging classical and 20th-century techniques.

- **Robinson, William — *The Parks and Gardens of Paris* (London: John Murray, 1883)**
  A wonderfully written observational account of Parisian gardens, now available online through Google's digital library project. Robinson was instrumental in bringing French growing successes to English attention.

- **Weathers, John — *French Market-Gardening* (London: John Murray, 1909)**
  While not as complete as some other sources, this book is valuable because of Weathers' extensive experience. The introduction by [[william-robinson]] provides important [[ghouled-field-guide-historical-context]].

## British Market Gardening Literature

### The Transfer of French Techniques to Britain

British growers and writers recognized the value of French intensive methods and produced their own literature adapting these techniques to British conditions. Several key figures stand out:

- **Thomas Smith — *French Gardening* and *The Profitable Culture of Vegetables* (London: Longmans, Green and Co., 1919)**
  Joseph Fels, a wealthy American soap manufacturer who had been involved with providing gardens for workers in Philadelphia, purchased land in England to help small-scale intensive vegetable producers get established. Thomas Smith served as his farm manager. These books present a clear, practical account of the production systems they developed, blending French techniques with British conditions.

- **Nussey, Helen, and Olive Cockerell — *A French Garden in England* (London: Stead's Publishing House, 1909)**
  A particularly valuable account because it records the successes and failures of two beginners — young women graduates of Lady Warwick's Gardening School — during their first year of intensive cultivation. This realistic beginner's perspective makes it especially relatable.

- **Newsome, T. — *Gold Producing Soil* (Stroud: Frederick Steel & Co., 1908)**
  An enthusiastic and reasonably complete presentation of the French system, reflecting the excitement British growers felt about these productive methods.

- **McKay, C.D. — *The French Garden in England* (London: The Daily Mail, 1909)**
  One of the early small books attempting to popularize French growing practices for a wider British audience.

- **Macself, A.J. — *French Intensive Gardening* (London: W. H. & L. Collingridge Ltd., 1932)**
  Good, hard data from a competent practitioner. Notable for its excellent drawings and photographs clearly illustrating every aspect of the craft.

## Foundational American Works

### Peter Henderson — *Gardening for Profit* (New York: Orange Judd Company, 1907)

First published in 1867, this is described as "the classic of classics" — the first American book devoted to market gardening. It inspired generations of growers to try commercial vegetable production and remains a valuable information source more than a century later.

### Dreer, Henry A. — *Dreer's Vegetables Under Glass* (Philadelphia: Henry A. Dreer, 1896)

Dreer was an obviously skilled practitioner who had traveled extensively and could comment knowledgeably on the techniques of other growers. His easy writing style and clear, concise information make this "a real gem" among historical greenhouse gardening references.

### Bailey, L.H. — *The Forcing Book* (New York: The Macmillan Company, 1897)

A classic from [[liberty-hyde-bailey]], one of America's most important horticultural writers. The bulk of his information on protected cultivation remains just as valuable today as when it was first published.

### Nisley, Charles H. — *Starting Early Vegetable and Flowering Plants Under Glass* (New York: Orange Judd, 1929)

A very professional book covering plants, horticultural techniques, and the construction of glasshouses and frames. Particularly valuable for its numerous black-and-white photographs documenting the field and greenhouse vegetable industry of the 1920s.

## Philosophical and Ecological Context

### Kropotkin, Peter — *Fields, Factories and Workshops* (New York: G.P. Putnam's Sons, 1901)

Prince Peter Kropotkin was a perceptive observer of the agricultural realities of his time. This small volume has been described in *The Ecologist* as "one of the canonical texts of the ecological tradition." Kropotkin's work provides important philosophical grounding for small-scale, intensive food production as a viable alternative to industrial agriculture.

## Modern Technical References

### Winter-Harvest Specific Literature

The annotated bibliography also includes modern technical works essential for winter harvest production:

- **Coleman, Eliot — *The New Organic Grower*, Revised edition (White River Junction, VT: Chelsea Green, 1995)**
  Provides indispensable background on soil-fertility management and cultural techniques for both field and greenhouse vegetable growers. The information in The Winter Harvest Handbook complements and updates the winter-harvest chapters in this earlier work.

- **Gerst, Jean-Jacques — *Légumes sous abris* (Paris: CTIFEL, 1993)**
  Translating as "Vegetables Under Covers," this professional technical manual for French growers covers every possible vegetable and every imaginable combination of high tunnels, low tunnels, reflective covers, floating covers, and heated and unheated greenhouses. Written for more [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] than Maine, but no less valuable for its comprehensive coverage.

- **Lawrence, William J.C. — *Science and the Glasshouse* (Edinburgh: Oliver and Boyd, 1948)**
  Lawrence is noted as a favorite old-time greenhouse writer because he was tireless in trying to pin down what worked, what didn't, and why. His experiments are engaging to read and his conclusions have great practical value. His earlier work, *Seed and Potting Composts* (1939), served as an indispensable guide for formulating potting mixes.

- **Artiss, Percy — *Market Gardening* (London: W. H. & L. Collingridge Limited, 1948)**
  A little-known book by a competent grower who was "almost organic without saying so." His insistence that "the soil must be biologically healthy" aligns with modern organic principles, and he provides thorough coverage of every aspect of field and greenhouse cultivation.

- **Larkcom, Joy — *Oriental Vegetables* (New York: Kodansha International, 1991)**
  The definitive reference consulted when exploring the vast potential of Asian crops for winter harvest systems. Covers everything one could want to know about Oriental vegetables.

### Grower Guide Series (Grower Books, London)

A series of small professional volumes from England's leading growers' magazine, covering specific crops in detail:

- *Blocks for Transplants* (Grower Guide No. 10, 1980)
- *Cucumbers* (Grower Guide No. 15, 1983)
- *Lettuce Under Glass* (Grower Guide No. 21, 1981)
- *Peppers and Aubergines* (Grower Guide No. 3, 1986)
- *Vegetables Under Glass* (Grower Guide No. 26, 1982)

While written for chemical growers using mostly heated greenhouses, the basic cultural information in these guides is described as "system-neutral and very complete."

### Greenhouse History

- **Van den Muijzenberg, Edwin W.B. — *A History of Greenhouses* (Wageningen, Netherlands: Privately published, 1980)**
  Traces greenhouse development from the earliest recorded examples through modern Dutch glasshouses. Fascinating reading for understanding the evolution of protected cultivation.

## Key Themes Across the Literature

Several recurring themes emerge from this body of historical and modern literature:

1. **Soil biological health as the foundation** — Nearly every author, from the 19th century French *maraîchers* to modern organic growers, emphasizes that productive intensive cultivation depends on maintaining living, biologically active soil.

2. **Protected cultivation as a multiplier** — Cold frames, cloches, greenhouses, and row covers have been used for centuries to extend growing seasons and protect crops, with the basic principles remaining constant even as materials have evolved from glass to plastic.

3. **Succession and rotation as essential practices** — The intensive systems described in these works all rely on careful planning of crop successions and rotations to maintain continuous production and soil health.

4. **Integration of fertility and protection** — The original French system combined manure's soil-building properties with its heat-generating capacity for hotbeds, demonstrating the interconnected nature of fertility management and protected cultivation.

5. **Small-scale viability** — A persistent theme across three centuries of literature is that small-scale, intensive production can be both commercially viable and ecologically sound — a message that remains central to the local food movement today.
