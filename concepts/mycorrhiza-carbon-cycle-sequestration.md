# Mycorrhiza and the Terrestrial Carbon Cycle

Mycorrhizal fungi are among the most significant biological drivers of the terrestrial carbon cycle. Over 80% of plant species invest substantial amounts of photosynthetically-fixed carbon into these fungal symbionts. At the global scale, the annual carbon flux through soil respiration is ten times greater than fossil fuel combustion and recycles approximately 10% of atmospheric CO₂. Roots and their associated mycorrhizas are the single most important component of this flux.

## Three Major Mycorrhizal Types and Their Biomes

The major biomes are dominated by plants with one of three kinds of mycorrhiza, each adapted to particular vegetation and soil characteristics.

**Ericoid mycorrhizas (ERM)** dominate heathlands and northern tundra, associated with ericaceous plants such as Cassiope, Vaccinium, Erica, Calluna, and Rhododendron. These cold, nutrient-poor environments accumulate the most soil carbon per unit area, primarily in peat and raw humus.

**Ectomycorrhizas (EM)** dominate the boreal coniferous forest — the world's largest vegetation system — stretching as a continuous circumpolar belt 1,000–2,000 km wide around the Northern Hemisphere. EM also occurs in temperate deciduous forests with trees such as Fagus, Quercus, and Betula.

**Arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM)** dominate temperate grasslands, sub-tropical and tropical savannas, and rainforests. AM plants typically produce less recalcitrant litter that decomposes more rapidly.

The most important biomes for soil carbon sequestration are the heathland and boreal forest regions, which together cover approximately 70% of the vegetated land mass of the Northern Hemisphere and contain the majority of global soil carbon stocks.

## Direct Carbon Inputs from Mycorrhizal Mycelium

Mycorrhizal mycelia are major components of soil microbial biomass, supported by receiving carbohydrate directly from plants at rates unparalleled among soil organisms. They are functionally distinct from saprotrophs because they add new carbon to soil rather than merely decomposing existing detrital stocks.

Mycorrhizal carbon is chemically distinct from that of their host plants — cell walls of chitin rather than cellulose, melanin rather than lignin — producing different stable δ¹³C signatures compared to saprotrophic fungi.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizal Carbon Fluxes

The hair roots of ericaceous plants contain extensive fungal coils of their mycorrhizal symbionts, typically occupying 10%–80% of root volume. Because fungal structures turn over faster than roots, the relative importance of the fungus as a carbon sink is much larger than suggested by biomass measures alone.

Field ¹⁴C pulse-labelling studies in subarctic tundra have shown that approximately 50% of assimilated carbon reaching hair roots passes to the fungi. At seasonal peaks, allocation to mycorrhiza accounts for up to 4.8% of net fixation. ERM fungi are highly adapted to decompose the toxic, polyphenolic litter produced by their host plants, mobilizing nitrogen and phosphorus while leaving very incomplete decomposition products that accumulate as humic compounds.

## Ectomycorrhizal Carbon Fluxes

Carbon passing into ectomycorrhizas typically ranges from 10% to 30% of net photosynthate in many forest ecosystems. Because EM entirely sheath root tips — over 90% of which are normally symbiotic — virtually all labile carbon released from living roots in boreal forests passes through the fungi.

A conservative estimate places the EM contribution to soil microbial biomass in boreal forest soil at 32%. EM external mycelium production has been estimated at 420–590 kg ha⁻¹ year⁻¹, with approximately 80% of EM biomass existing as extra-radical mycelium. Some studies attribute over 70% of total soil microbial biomass to EM fungi.

## Carbon Persistence and Sequestration

The quality and persistence of carbon inputs determine the extent of sequestration. EM mycelium has very high C:N ratios (18.5–21.9), similar to or exceeding boreal forest organic matter. Many EM fungi produce robust hydrophobic multicellular hyphal cords persistent for months, and heavily melanized species such as ectomycorrhizal-ecology|Cenococcum geophilum produce sclerotia directly implicated in soil carbon sequestration.

Mycorrhizal colonization has been shown to result in 30% lower decomposition rates of root carbon compared to non-mycorrhizal roots, thereby increasing short-term soil carbon storage.

## Glomalin and Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Sequestration

AM fungi contribute to carbon sequestration through a unique mechanism: the hydrophobic protein glomalin, secreted into soil by hyphae. Glomalin has a residence time of 6–42 years and contributes up to 15% of total stable soil organic carbon in grasslands and 4%–5% in tropical rainforest soils. AM hyphae and glomalin also stabilize soil macroaggregates, which protect entrapped organic matter from decomposition.

Total carbon allocation to AM external mycelium in temperate grassland exceeds 9% of net carbon fixation in summer — significantly more than typical carbon inputs from herbivore dung — with most carbon transferred within the first 3 days after photosynthesis.

## Indirect Effects on Decomposition

ERM and EM fungi compete with saprotrophs for limiting nutrient sources, restricting mineral nutrient supplies to decomposer communities and thereby slowing decomposition of accumulated organic matter. EM mycelia can territorially exclude [[fungal-enzyme-wood-decay]], the organisms best adapted for decomposing lignocellulose. This antagonism further slows organic matter decomposition and enhances carbon storage.

These positive feedback mechanisms mean that mycorrhizal type directly influences soil carbon storage capacity: ERM and EM systems accumulate more carbon than AM systems, largely because of the recalcitrant nature of the litter and the competitive suppression of decomposer communities.

## Mycoheterotrophs: Plants That Cheat Fungi

The magnitude of carbon flux through mycorrhizal networks has driven the evolution of over 400 achlorophyllous plant species — mycoheterotrophs — that depend exclusively on fungi for carbon. These plants exploit mycorrhizal fungi co-infecting the roots of adjacent green plants, effectively stealing carbon through shared mycelial connections.

About 10% of all plant species depend on exploiting fungal carbon during at least part of their life cycle. The orchid family (Orchidaceae), with approximately 17,500 species, is the most prominent example, producing dust-seeds that require fungal carbon for germination and establishment.

## Climate Change Implications

Knowledge of the mycorrhizal contribution to the carbon cycle is of increasing importance because this component is likely to be among the most sensitive to anthropogenic disturbance of both carbon and nitrogen biogeochemical cycles. Changes in mycorrhizal community composition, driven by nitrogen deposition, warming, or land-use change, could have cascading effects on global soil carbon storage.
