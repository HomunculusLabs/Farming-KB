The guide notes that it is very difficult to give explicit directions on a watering regime — the cultivator must develop a "feeling" for it based on visual and tactile assessment of the casing surface moisture. As a general guideline: the casing surface should glisten with fine droplets after misting but should not appear wet or puddled. If the casing surface appears dull and dry between misting sessions, misting frequency should be increased. If water pools on the surface or the casing appears waterlogged, frequency should be decreased. Over-watering is generally more problematic than under-watering, as waterlogged casing can become anaerobic and promote bacterial growth.

## Temperature Considerations

The guide recommends fruiting temperatures of **20–25 °C** for most species. Temperature affects both the speed of mushroom development and the risk of contamination:

- **Lower temperatures (18–20 °C):** Slower growth but denser, meatier mushrooms with better shelf life. Lower contamination risk because bacterial and mould growth rates are also reduced.
- **Optimal range (20–25 °C):** Good balance of growth speed and quality. Most *Psilocybe* species fruit well within this range.
- **Higher temperatures (25–28 °C):** Faster growth but thinner-fleshed mushrooms that may mature too quickly and have reduced potency. Significantly elevated risk of bacterial contamination and *Trichoderma* mould outbreaks.

## Air Exchange

Passive air exchange is provided by **holes melted in the sides of the bins**. These holes serve several functions:

- **CO₂ removal:** Mycelium and developing mushrooms produce CO₂ through respiration. Elevated CO₂ levels (>2000 ppm) can suppress normal fruiting and cause elongated, stunted stems. The holes allow CO₂ to diffuse out of the bin.
- **O₂ supply:** Fresh air enters through the holes, providing oxygen for mycelial respiration.
- **Pressure equalisation:** Without holes, temperature fluctuations would create pressure differentials that could pull unfiltered air through gaps in the covering, potentially introducing contamination.

The holes should be small enough that the mesh or filter covering them is effective at excluding insects, but large enough to provide adequate gas exchange. This species-specific approach is important: applying cold shock to species that do not require it wastes time and may stress the mycelium, while failing to cold shock species that benefit from it can result in delayed or absent fruiting.
