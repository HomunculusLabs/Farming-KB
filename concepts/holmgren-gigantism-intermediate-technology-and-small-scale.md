# Gigantism, Intermediate Technology and Small Scale

David Holmgren's analysis of scale draws extensively on E.F. Schumacher's
work and extends it into the context of energy descent. He argues that
the cultural bias toward growth in scale and speed is the primary obstacle
to sustainable design.

## Schumacher's Intermediate Technology

E.F. Schumacher, the English economist and author of "Small is Beautiful,"
demonstrated that modern technologies and organisation were ineffective,
counterproductive and destructive when applied to Third World development.
Schumacher's Intermediate Technology Development Group promoted technology
and development methods that:

- Are small scale
- Are simple to apply and maintain
- Are labour-intensive rather than capital- or energy-intensive
- Use local resources
- Support local markets

These intermediate technologies have been far more effective in
achieving economic, social and environmental benefits than conventional
development technology pushed by corporations and most government aid.

## The Scale Imbalance

Holmgren emphasises small physical scale and slow speed because of the
built-in cultural bias in modern society towards growth in scale and
speed as indications of what is good, effective and powerful. The
sustainable balance between small/large and slow/fast polarities is not
achieved when equal amounts of both are present. Rather, a "beam
balance" model shows we need to weight the small and slow end
disproportionately to compensate for the massive cultural momentum toward
giantism.

## Corporate Gigantism and Its Failures

Despite evidence for the climaxing of energy, corporations and banks are
getting bigger faster to pursue remaining economies of scale. The degree
to which globalisation obtains any remaining real economies of scale is
debatable. Critics point out that ordinary people everywhere are the
losers in the rush to giantism led by big banks and corporations.

Most corporations have shorter lifespans than humans -- a fact that
surprises many because big, powerful systems "should" be slow-changing
with long lifespans. World affairs are dominated by enormous institutions
driven by intelligence and planning horizons shorter than individual
humans are capable of. This mismatch of scale and lifespan is close to
the heart of industrial culture's unsustainability.

## Gigantism Creates Open Niches

New economic activities begin at micro scale and grow into the vacuum
left by the rush to giantism. Boutique breweries and bakeries have
emerged alongside national and multinational ones. As Monsanto and other
chemical giants bought out seed companies, new companies sprouted to
provide non-hybrid and non-GMO seeds. The explosive growth of the
personal computer and Internet -- despite corporate plans for centralised
supercomputer dominance -- is the most potent symbol of "small is
powerful."

## The Ethics of Size

The permaculture ethic of recognising limits provides a clear foundation
for the "small and slow" principle. "Big is better" is a form of greed.
The slogan "live simply so others may simply live" sums up this idea.

Traditional societies had boundless examples of ethics and taboos that
constrained growth. The Congo Pygmies' story of ancestors who lived in
cities destroyed by technological hubris -- their own "Atlantis" story
-- suggests that understanding the dangers of excessive scale may be
embedded in collective human memory.

## House Size as Ecological Indicator

In the ecological housing movement, early optimism about zero-impact
houses capturing all their own energy from the sun has been tempered by
acceptance that a small house is an ecological house. Larger houses
create cascading problems: higher debt, more space for consumer goods,
indoor lifestyles, and higher heating, cleaning and maintenance costs.

## Agricultural Research and Small-Scale Innovation

The failure of agricultural research to tackle the myriad of small-scale
and situation-specific opportunities illustrates the difficulties of
changing from macro to micro scale. Because most sustainable agriculture
solutions are small-scale, they fall through the net of large-scale
research systems.

Holmgren's own research on tagasaste in central Victoria suggests it
may be economically advantageous on specific stony, well-drained ridges
covering perhaps 4000 hectares -- an amount "chickenfeed" in terms of
national industry development but addressing urgent salinity issues
while increasing farm productivity. This scale of benefit is ignored by
large-scale systems.

## Some Progressive Corporate Recognition

Some progressive manufacturing companies have sacrificed engineering
economies of scale for the human productivity and creativity gains of
small working groups where workers control complete manufacturing tasks.
This recognition that small size allows reallocation of resources to
flexibility is widely acknowledged in relation to small business.
