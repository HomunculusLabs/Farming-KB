# The Name 'Jesus' as a Mushroom Epithet

Perhaps the single most provocative claim in John Marco Allegro's "The
Sacred Mushroom and the Cross" is that the name "Jesus" itself was not a
personal name at all, but an epithet for the sacred mushroom. According to
Allegro's philological analysis, the Greek name Iesous (and its Hebrew
equivalent Yehoshua or Joshua) derived from Sumerian compound words that
described the [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] -- its shape, its
growth habits, and its psychoactive properties. The entire Christian story
of Jesus of Nazareth was, in this reading, a personification of the sacred
fungus.

## Etymology: From Sumerian to Greek

Allegro traced the name Jesus/Iesous through a complex chain of linguistic
development. The Greek Iesous was a standard transliteration of the Hebrew
Yehoshua (Joshua), meaning "Yahweh is salvation." But Allegro argued that
this Hebrew meaning was itself a secondary interpretation imposed on a much
older word whose original Sumerian significance had been forgotten.

The Sumerian roots that Allegro identified beneath the name involved
compounds describing the mushroom's physical form: words for "mound,"
"cone," "erect," "weep" (referring to the mushroom's exudation of liquid
when cut), and "dew" (referring to the moisture associated with its
fruiting). The name, in its original form, was a description of the
mushroom's characteristic appearance and behavior, not a reference to a
deity or a person.

## The Chrestus/Christos Distinction

Allegro drew attention to a significant detail in Roman historical sources.
The historian Suetonius, writing in the early second century, described the
emperor Claudius expelling Jews from Rome because they were making a
disturbance "at the instigation of Chrestus." The name Chrestus (meaning
"useful" or "good" in Greek) was distinct from Christos (meaning
"anointed"), yet both forms appeared in early sources referring to the
object of Christian worship.

For Allegro, this distinction was highly significant. He connected Chrestus
to the Greek word Chreston, which Pliny used to describe a plant whose
juice, when mixed with oil and used as an anointing substance, made people
"more popular" and helped them "obtain their wishes more easily." The act
of anointing with this substance was called khristos in Greek. Thus the
sequence was: the sacred plant was called Chreston ("the good, the useful");
its juice was applied by anointing (khristos); and the anointed one became
the Christ.

The identity of this anointing plant was, Allegro argued, the mushroom
itself -- specifically the [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]], whose juice, when extracted and mixed with oil, served as the original chrism or holy anointing oil. This connection between the plant called Chreston and the practice of chrism (anointing) explained how a botanical epithet became a divine title.

## The "Jesus" Mushroom in Classical Sources

Allegro pointed to several classical descriptions of plants that, when read
with an awareness of mushroom nomenclature, appeared to describe the
psychoactive fungus. Pliny's account of the Halicacabum was particularly
telling: its root was taken by those who wished to "display the inspired
prophet" and to be "seen publicly raving in unpretended madness." This
description of deliberate, ritualized madness induced by consuming a plant
root matched exactly the pattern of [[amanita-muscaria-cultural-history]] use in shamanic and cultic contexts worldwide.

Pliny's further observation that the Halicacabum was "antipathetic to the
nature of asps" -- that it could counteract snake venom -- connected it to
the broader Near Eastern symbolism of the mushroom as an antidote to the
serpent's poison. Allegro linked this to the Old Testament story of Moses'
brazen serpent, on which Jesus explicitly modeled himself: "As Moses lifted
up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up"
(John 3:14).

## The Mushroom as Healer and Savior

The salvific language of the New Testament -- Jesus as savior, healer,
redeemer, the one who gives eternal life -- took on a literal meaning in
Allegro's interpretation. The mushroom was called "savior" because consuming
it produced an experience of ego death and rebirth that was interpreted as
salvation from the limitations of ordinary consciousness. It was called
"healer" because its psychoactive effects were experienced as the curing of
spiritual blindness and the restoration of visionary capacity. It was called
the one who "gives eternal life" because the mushroom experience provided a
direct, unmediated encounter with what was experienced as the divine realm
-- a realm perceived as eternal and unchanging.

The phrase "the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) was, in Allegro's
reading, a direct reference to the inward visionary journey produced by the
mushroom. The "Kingdom" was not a future political entity or a postmortem
heaven but the immediate experiential reality accessed through the
sacrament. The "Key of the Kingdom" given to Peter was the knowledge of the
mushroom's identity and preparation.

## Joshua and the Mushroom conquest

Allegro extended his analysis to the Old Testament figure of Joshua
(Yehoshua), whom he saw as an earlier personification of the same mushroom
deity. The conquest of Canaan described in the Book of Joshua was read as an
allegory for the spread of the mushroom cult across the Near East. The
falling walls of Jericho represented the collapse of competing religious
systems before the power of the mushroom experience. The Jordan River
crossing symbolized the passage from ordinary to non-ordinary consciousness.

The naming of the New Testament figure as Joshua/Iesous was, Allegro argued,
a deliberate echo of the Old Testament prototype, signaling to initiates
that the same divine reality was at work in both narratives. The "second
Moses" was the second Joshua -- the renewed manifestation of the mushroom
deity for a new era.

## Non-Christians and the Christ

Allegro noted that several early sources described non-Christians using the
name Chrestus rather than Christos to refer to the object of the sect's
adoration. This suggested to him that the original designation was
botanical rather than theological: the cult worshipped a plant called
Chreston, the "good" or "useful" one, and only later was this botanical
name theological into a divine title. The "Christ" was not a messianic
expectation fulfilled by a historical person but a botanical epithet
personified through mythology.

This interpretation, while extreme, highlights an important question about
early Christian vocabulary that continues to be debated: the extent to which
Christian theological language was shaped by, or borrowed from, the
religious vocabulary of the [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]]
and its [[amanita-muscaria-cultural-history]] mystery cults.
