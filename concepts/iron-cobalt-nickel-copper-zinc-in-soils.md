# Iron, Cobalt, Nickel, Copper, and Zinc in Soils

## Overview

Iron, cobalt, nickel, copper, and zinc are essential micronutrients and trace elements that play vital roles in plant and animal metabolism. Their availability is highly pH-dependent, and both deficiency and toxicity can cause serious problems in agricultural and natural systems. Understanding their behaviour in soils is crucial for [[permaculture-design]] and sustainable land management.

## Iron (Fe)

### Soil Availability

Iron is normally plentiful in soils and is a critical element for plants and animals. Plants can show iron deficiency symptoms in alkaline drylands or on heavily limed garden soils. Sulphur in gardens, iron chelates, or small amounts of ferric sulphate remedy this situation.

### Deficiency Symptoms

Iron deficiency causes interveinal leaf yellowing in plants and low blood iron in people (anaemia). Ferrous iron spicules in soil assist the formation of ethylene. Ferric iron (oxides) are "unavailable" (Fe₂O₃). Iron concretions and iron staining in sands are often associated with microbial action along plant root traces.

### Human Health

Iron-deficiency anaemia in people is a common problem in deserts, or after blood losses in menses or dysentery. In humans, blood iron is as important in energy transfers as is copper in invertebrates. Deficiencies are normally rare but must be watched in women of reproductive age, when lack of iron causes ill-health and lassitude.

### Dietary Sources

Meats such as veal, liver, and fish eaten with soya beans, lettuce, parsley, maize, etc. can provide dietary iron. Meat and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) increase iron absorption from food. Iron in bone marrow is needed for new blood cells. Clinically, ferrous salts ingested can be used in cases of excessive blood loss.

### Iron Excess

People brewing beer and wine or cooking cereals in iron pots often ingest excess iron. Alcoholism and diabetes exacerbate iron excess, although excessive iron is an unusual condition.

## Cobalt (Co)

### Deficiency Conditions

Copper and cobalt can be deficient on poor coastal sands. Cobalt is necessary to many legumes and is especially needed by livestock, who become "unthrifty" if it is deficient, as it can be in highly alkaline areas.

### Application Rates

Only an ounce or two per acre is needed, and this can be supplied as a cobalt "bullet" placed in the rumen of sheep — a common veterinary practice.

### Vitamin B12 Connection

Cobalt is necessary for synthesis of vitamin B12 in ruminants. If deficient, further metabolic problems then occur. Often diagnosed via B12 tests on blood levels. Deficiency can cause anaemia. Toxicity can occur if excess is ingested. Radioactive cobalt is a serious poison.

## Nickel (Ni)

Nickel is not noted as a plant nutrient or poison in normal quantities, but with copper can intensify poisoning in people, for example in office rooms using urns, or in mining areas. Many people demonstrate skin reaction (rash) if nickel is used as bracelets, indicating individual sensitivity.

## Copper (Cu)

### Essential Micronutrient

Copper is a necessary micronutrient which can become a poison at higher concentrations. In plants, copper is an enzyme activator and is concentrated in the chloroplasts of leaves.

### Deficiency Conditions

Copper is deficient on shell-grit dunes, acid peaty soils, coastal heaths over poor quartzite sands, and in deeply weathered basalts. Black sheep are good indicators: if deficient they develop whitish or brown wool, or white-coloured "steely" wool.

### Remediation

Copper sulphate at 1 kg/ha every 5 years is a cure. Old stockmen traditionally put a few crystals into water-holes. Deficiency (with iodine) can lead to goitre (thyroid imbalance) and anaemia in animals. Levels above 12 micromoles/l are excessive in drinking water. High blood copper levels in deserts are often a result of zinc deficiency.

### Copper Toxicity

While copper deficiency is rare or absent in adults, copper toxicity is of more concern, especially where copper sulphate is used to clear algae from drinking water, and from foods or water standing in or boiled in copper utensils and pipes. Copper poisoning is serious, with liver deterioration (cirrhosis) and brain effects (tremors, personality changes). Once hopeless, such cases can now be somewhat alleviated by chelating agents.

## Zinc (Zn)

### Deficiency Conditions

Zinc is deficient on leached sands, dunes, and alkaline sands. It is supplied by zinc sulphate spray on plants or zinc salts in seed pellets. It should be tested for in all desert gardens.

### Critical Importance

Zinc is critical for both tree establishment in many deserts or dunes and to human health, and is cheap to supply. In plants, zinc is essential to growth control hormones.

### Human Health Effects

Zinc deficiency causes gross metabolic imbalances and stunting of growth. People with malnutrition often have low zinc blood levels and poor wound healing. Diabetes is often linked with zinc deficiency. Excessive zinc acts as an emetic, causing vomiting and weakness.

### Metabolic Role

Zinc is necessary to several enzymes in the body. Prostate (hence seminal) fluids are high in zinc. Diets of coarse grains, unleavened bread, and low meat intake contribute to zinc deficiency in poor people, especially in high-calcium soils. Oral zinc sulphate can be given clinically.

### Factors Lowering Zinc

Alcoholism and diabetes, feverish sweating, and stress all lower zinc levels. Severe deficiency causes hair loss, moist eczema of the mouth area, impotence, apathy, and diarrhoea.

## Heavy Metal Pollutants

### Arsenic (As)

Sometimes seems to be needed by horses and is included in chicken pellets, but is a poison to animals even at slight concentrations. Polluting sources come often from gold processing areas, or from chickens fed on pelleted foods containing high arsenic levels.

### Selenium (Se)

Selenium deficiency in lambs causes "white muscle" or "still lamb" disease, cured by selenium and vitamin E injection. It also causes "thrift" in animals in many countries where a severe dietary deficiency occurs. Five mg/year is sufficient for sheep; more can cause toxicity. Seaweeds may supply sufficient selenium to gardens.

### Strontium (Sr)

Strontium-90 is becoming, as a long-term and very poisonous radioactive element, a widespread danger from atomic plants. It now pollutes many areas and milk. It causes cancer at absorption sites, leukaemia in children, and is a "hidden cost" of the atomic age. It is excreted in urine of breast-fed babies and concentrates 4–8 times in cows' milk, thus levels in cows' milk must be monitored after atomic fallout. There are no safe levels. Ordinary strontium is a trace element.

### Cadmium (Cd)

Cadmium is a poison concentrated by green leafy plants and shellfish. It is derived from traffic (tyres) and superphosphate. It may already be at very high levels in acid soils of market gardens using artificial fertilisers. Cadmium causes painful human disease itai-itai (Japan) and permanent deformations.

### Tin (Sn)

Ores and wastes create plant establishment problems. It is not noted as toxic at low levels in diets, but can become toxic at high levels in canned food.

### Antimony (Sb)

Similar considerations to arsenic apply.

### Iodine (I)

Deficiencies occur in weathered basalts, causing growth problems and goitre. Remedied by fish and shellfish, seaweed diet. I-131 is now a common radioactive fall-out from atomic plants and tests; it poisons milk over wide areas and affects thyroid function. Iodine can cause cancer, death of children from hyperthyroidosis — a real risk from atomic establishment and tests.

### Mercury (Hg)

A common poison released by mines, metal processes, and acid rain. It is more active in organically polluted areas. It creates very serious coordination and sanity problems, central nervous system malfunction, bone deformity, and insanity.

### Lead (Pb)

A common poison from petrol, old paints, and battery burning. It is a serious urban soil pollutant needing heavy organic soils to block uptake, or removal of lead-concentrating vegetation for disposal. Earthworms may concentrate lead to lethal levels in polluted soils. Adding salt to cooking waters can block uptake of some heavy metals on plant proteins if lead pollution is suspected.

### Radon (Rn)

A gas from the decay of uranium that seeps up in most soils, especially over volcanic areas and igneous rock. It can pollute super-insulated buildings if the air is not exchanged. Several million homes in the UK and USA have high levels of radon. Avoid building poorly ventilated homes in areas of radioactive ores, granites, and basalts, or the use of such rocks or crushed metals in buildings. If houses are to be super-insulated or draught-proofed, adequate heat exchangers must be fitted to bring in clean air.
