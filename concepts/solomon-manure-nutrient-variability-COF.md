# Manure Nutrient Variability and Complete Organic Fertilizer

One of the central arguments in Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts" is that animal manure is an unreliable source of plant nutrients, despite its widespread reputation as a garden panacea. Solomon documents the enormous variability in manure quality and advocates for Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) — a precisely formulated blend of organic nutrient sources — as a more dependable approach to supplying plant nutrition. Organic matter from compost and manure should be used for soil ecology, while mineral nutrition should come from measured COF applications.

## The Problem with Manure Quality

### Source Variability
The nutrient content of animal manure varies enormously depending on the animal species, diet, age, bedding material, storage method, and degree of decomposition. Two bags of "steer manure" purchased from different suppliers can have dramatically different fertilizing value. Solomon's core insight is that the gardener cannot know what they are actually getting when they purchase or obtain manure, making it impossible to apply nutrients with any precision.

### Sacked Steer Manure: Low Value
Solomon is particularly critical of sacked steer manure commonly sold at garden centers and supermarkets. This product often contains semi-decomposed sawdust as a bedding material, which has little fertilizing value and may actually tie up nitrogen as it decomposes (the sawdust-carbon draws nitrogen from the soil to support decomposition). While it does feed soil microbes and improve soil structure, its direct nutrient contribution to plants is minimal. Solomon describes it as useful for soil texture improvement but warns against relying on it for nutrition.

### Chicken Manure: Better but Variable
Composted chicken manure is significantly more potent than steer manure — Solomon references a product with an NPK of 4-3-2, which is genuinely useful for fertilizing vegetables. However, he cautions against overuse, as excessive chicken manure can lead to nutrient imbalances, particularly phosphorus accumulation. The NPK of chicken manure varies depending on the bird's diet, the presence and type of bedding, and the composting process.

### The Manure Trust Problem
Solomon frames the fundamental issue as a "trust problem": the gardener must trust that the manure they obtain contains adequate nutrients, but has no practical way to verify this without laboratory analysis. Even fresh manure from a known source varies seasonally as the animals' diet changes. This unpredictability makes manure fundamentally unsuitable as a primary nutrient source for gardeners who need reliable, productive harvests.

## Solomon's Nutrient Supply Hierarchy

Solomon proposes a clear separation of functions:

1. **Organic matter** (from compost, manure, or green manures) feeds the soil ecology — the bacteria, fungi, earthworms, and other organisms that create and maintain soil structure, cycle nutrients, and support plant health. This is the biological dimension of soil fertility.

2. **Mineral nutrients** (from COF or other balanced fertilizers) feed the plants directly — the nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and trace minerals that plants require for growth. This is the chemical dimension of soil fertility.

By separating these two functions, the gardener can apply organic matter at whatever rate is available (knowing it supports soil biology without worrying about precise nutrient content) while supplying plant nutrition through measured COF applications with known nutrient profiles.

## Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) Formula

Solomon's COF is a blend of organic and mineral ingredients that provides balanced nutrition for vegetable crops. The formula is designed to be mixed by the gardener from readily available ingredients:

### Basic COF Recipe (per 10 square feet)
- 4 quarts seed meal (cottonseed, canola, or soybean meal) — primary nitrogen and phosphorus source
- 1 quart agricultural lime or 1 quart dolomite lime (depending on soil pH) — calcium and magnesium, pH adjustment
- 1/2 quart bone meal or 1/2 quart rock phosphate — additional phosphorus and calcium
- 1/2 quart kelp meal — trace minerals and growth hormones

### Ingredient Selection
- **Seed meal**: The backbone of COF, providing slow-release nitrogen and phosphorus. Cottonseed meal is most common; canola or soybean meal are alternatives. Seed meals release nutrients over 3-6 months as soil microbes decompose them.
- **Lime**: Agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) is used on acid soils; dolomite lime (calcium magnesium carbonate) is used when magnesium is also needed. Solomon emphasizes not exceeding recommended lime rates, as excessive liming is harmful to soil.
- **Bone meal**: Provides concentrated phosphorus and calcium. Rock phosphate is a slower-release alternative.
- **Kelp meal**: Supplies a full spectrum of trace minerals (iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum) plus natural plant growth hormones (cytokinins, auxins) that may enhance root development and stress resistance.

### Application Rates by Crop Demand
Solomon categorizes vegetables by nutrient demand and adjusts COF application accordingly:

- **Low-demand vegetables** (root crops, beans, peas): 4-6 quarts COF per 100 square feet, plus organic matter
- **Medium-demand vegetables** (most common vegetables): 6-8 quarts COF per 100 square feet, plus organic matter
- **High-demand vegetables** (tomatoes, corn, brassicas): 8-10 quarts COF per 100 square feet, plus organic matter

## Why COF Outperforms Manure as a Nutrient Source

### Predictability
Every ingredient in COF has a known and relatively consistent nutrient analysis. The gardener knows exactly how much nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals they are applying. This predictability allows for rational adjustment based on crop requirements and previous years' results.

### Balance
COF provides nutrients in approximately the ratios that vegetable crops require. Manure, by contrast, often provides excess phosphorus relative to nitrogen and potassium, leading to imbalanced nutrition over time. Repeated manure applications can build up soil phosphorus to levels that inhibit the uptake of other essential nutrients (iron, zinc, copper) — a condition called phosphorus-induced zinc deficiency.

### Completeness
COF supplies all major nutrients (NPK) plus calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals. Manure supplies whatever nutrients the animal's diet contained, which may not match the needs of vegetable crops. Trace mineral deficiencies are common in gardens fertilized primarily with manure from animals fed mineral-poor diets.

### Efficiency
Because COF ingredients release nutrients at predictable rates, the gardener can time applications to match crop demand. Nutrients are available when plants need them most, reducing waste and leaching. Manure releases nutrients unpredictably depending on decomposition conditions — too fast in warm, moist conditions (potentially leaching), too slow in cool, dry conditions (potentially limiting early growth).

## Manure's Legitimate Role

Despite his criticism of manure as a nutrient source, Solomon acknowledges that manure has an important role in the garden. Its primary value is as a source of organic matter that feeds soil organisms, improves soil structure, and enhances water-holding capacity. Manure is also valuable as a mulch and as a carbon source for composting. Solomon's recommendation is to use the minimum amount of manure necessary to supply the soil ecology's requirement for organic matter, and to use COF to supply the plant's requirement for nutrients.

## Practical Recommendations

For gardeners who have access to free or inexpensive manure, Solomon's gradient approach suggests:

1. Use manure primarily for organic matter and soil structure improvement
2. Apply COF at recommended rates for plant nutrition
3. Do not exceed double the recommended amount of manure or compost, to avoid unbalancing soil minerals
4. If vegetables aren't growing well enough, fix the problem with additional COF rather than more manure
5. Have manure tested if possible to understand its actual nutrient content

For gardeners without access to manure, COF alone — combined with ley rotation or green manures for organic matter — can produce excellent results. This is Solomon's preferred approach and the one he uses in his own garden.

## Economic Analysis

Solomon frames the cost-benefit calculation clearly: spending five to ten cents to grow a dollar's worth of food is entirely reasonable and doable. COF ingredients, while requiring an initial investment, are used in small quantities per square foot. A single batch of COF can last an entire growing season for a modest garden, and the ingredients have long shelf lives when kept dry. Compared to purchasing equivalent nutrition through repeated manure applications (especially bagged steer manure with low actual nutrient value), COF is often more economical on a per-nutrient basis.

The cost comparison shifts for gardeners with free access to large quantities of high-quality manure from well-fed animals (such as horse or cattle operations with known feeding programs). In these situations, the labor of hauling and spreading manure may be offset by the zero material cost. However, the unpredictability of nutrient content remains a limitation even when the manure is free.

## Common Mistakes with Manure

### Over-Application
The most common error is assuming that "more is better" with manure. Excessive manure application, particularly of poultry manure, can burn plants with ammonia, create nutrient imbalances, contaminate groundwater with nitrates, and suppress beneficial soil organisms. Solomon's recommendation to not exceed double the suggested organic matter rate is a practical upper limit.

### Using Incomplete or Raw Manure
Raw manure (not composted) can contain viable weed seeds, pathogenic organisms (E. coli, Salmonella), and may produce ammonia that burns plant roots. Fresh manure should be composted for at least 3-6 months before application, or applied in fall so it decomposes over winter before spring planting.

### Ignoring pH Effects
Some manures (particularly poultry manure) are acidic and can lower soil pH over time. Others, if containing lime-based bedding, can raise pH. Without knowing the manure's composition, the gardener cannot predict its effect on soil pH, which affects nutrient availability. COF allows pH management through deliberate lime selection (agricultural vs. dolomite) applied at known rates.

## Sourcing COF Ingredients

Solomon provides guidance on finding COF ingredients through feed stores, farm suppliers, and garden centers. Seed meals are typically available at feed stores where they are sold as animal feed supplements. Agricultural lime is available at any garden center. Bone meal and rock phosphate are common garden amendments. Kelp meal, while more expensive, is used in small quantities and a single bag lasts multiple seasons. Online suppliers have made all COF ingredients widely accessible, even in urban areas without agricultural supply stores.

## The Bigger Picture: Input vs. Output Efficiency

Solomon's emphasis on COF over manure reflects a broader philosophy about garden efficiency. The goal is to produce the maximum amount of nutritious food with the minimum of purchased inputs and labor. Manure, with its unpredictable nutrient content and bulky physical nature, represents an inefficient input — the gardener hauls and spreads large volumes of material without knowing how much actual nutrition they are delivering. COF, by contrast, delivers known quantities of nutrients in a concentrated, easy-to-apply form. This efficiency matters most for gardeners who are growing food for sustenance rather than as a hobby, and who need reliable harvests from limited space.
