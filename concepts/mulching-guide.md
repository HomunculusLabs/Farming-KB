# Mulching Guide

Mulch is any material spread over the soil surface to conserve moisture, suppress weeds, regulate temperature, and feed soil biology. Proper mulching is one of the highest-impact, lowest-cost practices in a regenerative garden.

## Mulch Types

### Wood Chips
- Best for: perennial beds, pathways, fruit trees, around established shrubs.
- Depth: 3-6 inches (settles to 2-4 in over time).
- Pros: Long-lasting (1-3 years), excellent moisture retention, feeds fungal networks.
- Cons: Can tie up nitrogen at soil surface if mixed in; slow to break down.
- Source: Arborist chips (mix of wood, bark, and leaves) are ideal. Avoid dyed/colored chips.
- Note: Apply on top of soil only — do NOT till wood chips into soil.

### Straw
- Best for: vegetable gardens, annual beds, garlic, strawberries.
- Depth: 4-6 inches (settles significantly).
- Pros: Light, easy to spread, breaks down in one season, seeds-friendly.
- Cons: Can harbor slugs; may contain weed seeds if not clean straw.
- Important: Use seed-free straw (not hay). Hay contains seed heads and will germinate.
- Wheat straw and oat straw are most common; rice straw in warmer climates.

### Leaves (Shredded)
- Best for: General garden mulch, compost ingredient, overwintering.
- Depth: 2-4 inches after shredding.
- Pros: Free, nutrient-rich, feeds earthworms and microbes, excellent for soil building.
- Cons: Can mat and repel water if not shredded; blows away if whole.
- Tip: Run over leaves with a lawnmower to shred. Oak leaves are slightly acidic but fine in moderation.

### Grass Clippings
- Best for: Quick summer mulch around heavy feeders (corn, squash, tomatoes).
- Depth: 1-2 inches (apply thin layers, let dry between applications).
- Pros: Very high nitrogen, breaks down fast, free.
- Cons: Can mat, go anaerobic, and smell if applied too thickly. Must be from untreated lawns (no herbicides).
- Warning: Nitrogen tie-up is NOT an issue with grass clippings — they are nitrogen-rich.
- NEVER use clippings from lawns treated with broadleaf herbicides (clopyralid, 2,4-D, dicamba).

### Cardboard / Newspaper
- Best for: Sheet mulching, killing sod, establishing new beds, pathways.
- Depth: 2-3 layers cardboard or 5-10 sheets newspaper.
- Pros: Excellent weed suppression, breaks down within a season, smother grass.
- Cons: Can block water if overlapping seams are not soaked; may contain glue/tape.
- Rules: Remove tape, staples, and glossy/colored ink. Use plain brown cardboard only.
- Always cover with 2-4 inches of organic mulch on top for aesthetics and moisture.

### Pine Needles
- Best for: Acid-loving plants (blueberries, strawberries, rhododendrons).
- Depth: 2-4 inches.
- Pros: Lightweight, interlock well, slightly acidic, long-lasting.
- Cons: Can increase soil acidity over time (modest effect — not as strong as myth suggests).
- Note: Do not use excessively around plants that prefer neutral pH.

## Seasonal Mulching Schedule

### Spring
- Pull back winter mulch to let soil warm.
- Apply thin layer (1-2 in) of compost, then light mulch (2 in straw or shredded leaves).
- Avoid heavy mulching too early — it insulates soil and delays warming.
- Wait until soil reaches 60°F before heavily mulching heat-loving crops.

### Summer
- Replenish mulch to 3-4 inches as it decomposes.
- Grass clippings are ideal for summer top-ups around heavy feeders.
- Ensure mulch is pulled back slightly from plant stems (prevent rot).

### Fall
- Apply heavy mulch (4-6 in leaves, straw) after cleanup to protect soil over winter.
- Sheet mulch new beds with cardboard + leaves for spring planting.
- Do NOT remove fall mulch in spring — soil life depends on winter insulation.

### Winter
- Leave all mulch in place — it protects soil biology, prevents erosion, and reduces frost heaving.
- Add extra leaves or straw around tender perennials for insulation.

## Nitrogen Tie-Up

When carbon-rich mulches (wood chips, sawdust, straw) decompose on the soil surface, soil microbes consume nitrogen to break down the carbon. This temporarily reduces available nitrogen at the interface layer.

Key points:
- Surface mulch causes MINIMAL nitrogen tie-up — the effect is concentrated at the mulch-soil interface, not in the root zone.
- The problem occurs when carbon materials are TILLED into soil, not when laid on top.
- If nitrogen deficiency appears (yellowing lower leaves), side-dress with blood meal, composted manure, or fish emulsion.
- Mixing nitrogen sources (grass clippings, compost) with carbon mulch reduces tie-up risk.

## Application Rules

1. Never pile mulch against plant stems or tree trunks (crown rot, bark rot). Leave a 2-3 inch gap.
2. Keep mulch 6-12 inches away from building foundations (termites, moisture).
3. Replenish annually as mulch decomposes — a maintained 3-4 inch layer is the goal.
4. Water new mulch thoroughly after application to settle it and prevent wind displacement.
5. Avoid "volcano mulching" around trees — shallow root flare must remain exposed.
