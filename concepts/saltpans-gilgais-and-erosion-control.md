# Saltpans, Gilgais, and Erosion Control in Drylands

## Overview

Dryland landscapes present a suite of interrelated degradation problems—saltpans, gilgai formations, gully erosion, and flood-outs—that require specific techniques for rehabilitation. [[water-management]] to restore productive function to degraded arid and semi-arid lands.

## Saltpans and Salt Lakes

Saltpans and salt lakes are depressions that hold many metres of water during flood events. As this water evaporates, great depths of salt accumulate, sometimes capped by a thin layer of clay. Vegetation of salt-tolerant plants occurs only on the margins.

### Sources of Salt Contamination

Salt enters the freshwater zone in sandy deserts and atolls from several key sources:

- **Infiltration**: Salt is washed down by water infiltrating through saline surface sands.
- **Deep diffusion**: Salt diffuses upward from deep, heavily-salinated groundwaters (often saltier than seawater).
- **Advective transport**: Salt is carried inland by rains originating from the sea or saltpans after rainfall. This effect is most pronounced where freshwater has been overdrawn from groundwater reserves.
- **Pumping-induced pollution**: Salt is drawn up and inward by forceful local pumping, then pollutes surface soils.

### Rehabilitation of Saline Areas

Non-saline eroded claypans can be revegetated using several techniques:

- **Pitting**: Creating small depressions to collect water and seed.
- **Flood retention banks**: Low banks of 0.5–1 m sufficient to support adapted trees in saltpan areas. Once established, trees assist in desalination.
- **Chequerboard ridging**: A pattern of ridges that traps water and organic material.
- **Ploughing and ripping**: Breaking compacted clay allows faster water infiltration. Vegetation keeps the initial crevices open if dry-leaf mulch species are avoided, and herbaceous or needle-leaf groups are substituted instead.

## Gilgais

Gilgais are "puff and hollow" micro-relief features formed by patches of clay 3–5 m across that expand into 6–20 cm deep hollows on a "swell-shrink" cycle driven by wetting and drying. The hollows occur in solonetic (sodic) clay soils which shed water into the depressions. Hollows can link up in chains or patterns.

### Ecological Significance

Where gilgais are intact, the area is typically rich in plant species. The micro-topography creates diverse micro-habitats with varying moisture conditions, supporting a wider range of species than uniform terrain.

### Degradation

When the area is overgrazed, gilgais become silt-filled and the solonetic clays wash away. A series of sandy mounds replaces the original pattern. The area loses most of its plant diversity and becomes unstable.

## Gully Erosion and Flood-Outs

Flood-outs (also called flat-outs or run-ons) are the ever-widening, flattish floors of valleys as they leave the hills. These "streams" are only 2–8 cm deep and 20 m or more wide—braided and stable under natural conditions. Water absorbs over a wide area of such plains. However, when overgrazed, the streams commonly erode deeper channels that then develop into steep gullies.

### Causes of Gully Formation

Fragile soils, loose shaley ground, and recent sediments exposed to sudden overland flows may gully in just a few downpours. In deserts, gully formation most frequently occurs where:

- Foothill flood plains are overgrazed
- Rabbits tunnel into loose subsoils
- Vehicles or cattle create downhill tracks
- Badly built roads direct sheet flow to culverts that concentrate flow

### Gully Control Strategies

Controlling gullies is essentially a two-part process:

1. **Relieving the causes**: Usually those of overuse and lack of water control
2. **Preventing further cut-back**: Stabilising existing gully headwalls and floors

Specific techniques include:

- **Fencing out**: Excluding livestock from degraded areas
- **Pest control**: Managing rabbit and other burrowing animal populations
- **Spreader banks**: Distributing concentrated flow across broader areas
- **Water diversion**: In valleys above gullies, stream flow can be diverted to flood spreader channels
- **Rip-lines**: Lines drawn downhill from the gully bank towards the ridges (sloping at 1:1000) to redirect sheet flow away from gully heads
- **Interceptor drains**: Made across small gullies to cut off flow
- **Direct filling**: Small gullies can be bulldozed full

### Weirs and Gabions

A series of weirs 0.5 m high traps sediment so that tree establishment can stabilise run-off. Weirs can be constructed from:

- Well-braced wire-mesh fences across gully floors
- Low stone bunds across gully floors
- Stone-filled wire baskets (gabions) placed to spread the flow of floodwater

**Design principles for weirs:**

- If weirs exceed 0.5 m in height, a splash apron must be provided at the foot where plunge pools form
- If interceptor banks are made above the gully-head to divert water, weirs of 1.0–1.5 m become feasible without scour damage
- All weirs start at the gully outlet and are built in sequence upstream
- Where no local boulder supply exists, lower-height weirs of wire mesh alone are used
- Stone seepage weirs are very satisfactory and double as roads across gullies
- Weirs must extend well up the gully sides above any flood level
- All weirs need [[cajanus-cajan]]*) providing food and ground cover
- Formation of cooperatives for food production and marketing
- Wells and galleries to tap groundwater for tree and vegetable crops
- Silt from erosion building terraces 6–8 m deep and ~200 m wide across eroded valleys
- Replacement of goats and sheep with rabbits and pigs to reduce overgrazing
- Integration of government revolving loans, community employment, education, and transition from export crops to local nutritional food and tree crops

## General Principles for Dryland Erosion Rehabilitation

1. **Small, frequent, well-made structures** are more effective than large, infrequent ones
2. **Vegetation is essential** for long-term stability—earthworks alone are insufficient
3. **Start at the outlet** and work upstream when building weirs or check dams
4. **Address root causes** (overgrazing, inappropriate land use) alongside physical interventions
5. **Community involvement** in design and maintenance is critical for long-term success
6. **Integrated biosocial strategies** that address poverty, education, and nutrition alongside ecological restoration produce the most durable outcomes
