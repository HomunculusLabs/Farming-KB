# Neurotropic Fungi Classification

Neurotropic fungi, also referred to as hallucinogenic, psychotropic, magic,
sacred, psychedelic, or entheogenic mushrooms, encompass a diverse array of
fungal species distributed across multiple genera, families, and even
subdivisions of the fungal kingdom. In their comprehensive 1998 review, Guzmán,
Allen, and Gartz catalogued 216 accepted species of neurotropic fungi, organized
into four major groups based on their active chemical constituents and their
ethnobotanical significance. Understanding this classification is essential for
mycologists, ethnobotanists, and anyone studying the pharmacology of
psychoactive fungi.

## Group 1: Psilocybin and Indole Alkaloid-Containing Fungi

The largest and most well-studied group comprises fungi containing psilocybin
and related indole compounds. This group spans 13 genera with a total of 183
species. The primary genera and their species counts are as follows:

### Psilocybe (116 species)

Psilocybe is by far the dominant genus, accounting for more than half of all
known neurotropic species. The genus contains 116 species divided into several
sections based on morphological and chemical characteristics. The bluing
species, which contain confirmed indole alkaloids, belong to sections
Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae,
Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum. Species that do not
exhibit the bluing reaction, such as P. atrobrunnea and P. coprophila, are
generally excluded from the neurotropic list unless chemical analysis confirms
the presence of active compounds.

### Panaeoloideous Fungi (29 species)

The panaeoloid group includes three genera: Panaeolus (13 species), Copelandia
(12 species), and Panaeolina (4 species). Copelandia species are particularly
important as tropical and subtropical neurotropic fungi, with C. cyanescens
being among the most widely distributed and potent species in this group. Many
species in Panaeolus and Panaeolina have uncertain chemical status, with
conflicting reports in the literature about whether they contain active indole
compounds.

### Other Genera in Group 1

Several additional genera contain confirmed or probable psilocybin-like
compounds. Gymnopilus (14 species) includes G. spectabilis (G. junonius) and
G. purpuratus, widely distributed in temperate regions. Inocybe (6 species)
includes I. aeruginascens and I. haemacta. Hypholoma (6 species), formerly
classified under Naematoloma, includes species transferred from Psilocybe.
Pluteus (6 species) includes P. salicinus and P. cyanopus, wood-inhabiting
temperate species. Conocybe (4 species) includes C. cyanopus and C. smithii,
confirmed to contain psilocybin. Gerronema (2 species), Agrocybe (1), Galerina
(1), and Mycena (1) complete the list, though some chemical status remains
debated.

## Group 2: Ibotenic Acid-Containing Fungi (Amanita)

This group consists of three species in the genus Amanita, section Amanita,
which contain the excitatory amino acid ibotenic acid and its decarboxylation
product muscimol. Unlike the psilocybin-containing fungi, the Amanita species
are mycorrhizal associates of trees, particularly Pinus and Betula species.

### Amanita muscaria

The fly agaric (Amanita muscaria) is the most iconic neurotropic fungus,
recognizable by its bright red cap with white spots. It has an extremely
broad distribution across the northern hemisphere, forming mycorrhizal
associations with Pinus and Betula. Several subspecies and varieties are
recognized (ssp. muscaria, ssp. americana, ssp. flavivolvata, ssp.
kamtschatica). It has been documented as a sacred fungus among the Chukchee
and Koryak peoples of Siberia (Wasson and Wasson, 1957).

### Amanita pantherina

The panther cap (Amanita pantherina) is a widely distributed species
containing ibotenic acid, with several recognized varieties including var.
mutisquamosa, var. pantherinoides, and var. velatipes. Reports of
intoxication from this species are common in central Europe.

### Amanita regalis

Amanita regalis, sometimes treated as a variety of A. muscaria, is a northern
species found in Scandinavia and Alaska. It is closely related to A. muscaria
but distinguished by its yellowish-brown cap.

## Group 3: Ergot Fungi (Ascomycotina)

The ergot group includes species of Claviceps and Cordyceps, which produce
ergot alkaloids rather than indole compounds. These fungi are ascomycetes
rather than basidiomycetes.

### Claviceps (5 species)

Claviceps purpurea, the most famous ergot species, parasitizes rye and
produces sclerotia containing ergotamine and related alkaloids. Other species
include C. nigricans, C. paspali, C. rolfesii (a synonym of C. paspali),
and C. tripsicii. These species are distributed in temperate regions
worldwide.

### Cordyceps (2 species)

The Cordyceps group includes two species used by indigenous peoples in Mexico,
particularly in the Nevado de Toluca region, where they are employed in
nocturnal ceremonies with Psilocybe muliercula. Cordyceps is taxonomically
related to Claviceps, and both produce ergot-like alkaloids. The Trique
Indians place Elaphomyces specimens (the Cordyceps host) at the center of
ceremony rooms as a "director" of the ritual.

## Group 4: Fungi Used as Sacred Without Confirmed Chemistry

The fourth group comprises fungi used as sacred or divinatory mushrooms by
indigenous peoples but without confirmed chemical studies. This includes 20
species across several genera.

### Boletaceae (8 species)

Boletus species used as sacred fungi in New Guinea include B. flammeus, B.
kumaeus, B. manicus, B. nigerrimus, B. nigroviolaceus, and B. reayi,
described by Heim from New Guinean collections. Heimiella anguiformis and H.
retispora are also included. Ott (1993) noted these boletes probably lack true
neurotropic properties, their use involving symbolic rather than
pharmacological significance.

### Russulaceae (6 species)

Six species of Russula are reported as sacred fungi, all described from New
Guinea by Heim and Singer. These include R. agglutinata, R. kirinea, R.
maenadum, R. nondorbingi, R. pseudomaenadum, and R. wahgiensis.

### Gasteromycetes (6 species)

Puffballs and stinkhorns used as sacred fungi include Lycoperdon candidum, L.
oblongiosporum, Vascellum pratense, V. intermedium, V. qudenii, and
Dictyophora indusiata (three varieties). Ott et al. (1975) concluded that the
Mixtec "narcotic puffballs" were a mixture of at least nine species with no
confirmed neurotropic properties, likely used through confusion with
psilocybin-containing species.

## Problems in Classification

The classification of neurotropic fungi faces persistent challenges including
species misidentification, lack of taxonomic verification in chemical studies,
and the degradation of active compounds in stored specimens. A single species
may yield different chemical results from different researchers, as
illustrated by the controversial case of Panaeolina foenisecii.
