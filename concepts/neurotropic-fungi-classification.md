# Neurotropic Fungi Classification

## Overview

Neurotropic fungi, also referred to as hallucinogenic, narcotic, magic, sacred,
psychedelic, or entheogenic mushrooms, represent a diverse assemblage of fungi
with psychoactive properties that affect the central nervous system. In the
seminal paper by Guzman, Allen and Gartz (1998), 216 species of neurotropic
fungi are documented across both Ascomycotina and Basidiomycotina divisions,
spanning 20 genera and distributed on every continent except Antarctica (with
one record from Macquarie Island near the Antarctic).

## Four Major Groups

The authors divide all known neurotropic fungi into four principal groups based
on their chemical constituents and cultural usage:

### Group 1: Psilocybin and Related Indole Species

This is by far the largest group, containing fungi with psilocybin, psilocin,
baeocystin, and norbaeocystin. The bluing reaction upon bruising is a key
morphological indicator of psilocybin content in many species. This group
includes the genera Psilocybe (116 species), Gymnopilus (14), Panaeolus (13),
Copelandia (12), Hypholoma (6), Pluteus (6), Inocybe (6), Conocybe (4),
Panaeolina (4), Gerronema (2), and single species each of Agrocybe, Galerina,
and Mycena.

### Group 2: Ibotenic Acid Species

This small group contains fungi with ibotenic acid and muscimol as their
primary psychoactive compounds. It comprises three species: Amanita muscaria
(fly agaric), A. pantherina (panther cap), and A. regalis. These species are
chemically distinct from the psilocybin-containing fungi and produce different
neurotropic effects, including both hallucinations and toxic symptoms.

### Group 3: Ergot Fungi

This group includes the well-known ergot fungi producing ergot alkaloids such
as lysergic acid derivatives. Five species of Claviceps are recognized: C.
nigricans, C. paspali, C. purpurea, C. rolfesii, and C. tripsicii, along with
two species of Cordyceps (C. capitata and C. ophioglossoides) which are
taxonomically related to Claviceps and used traditionally in Mexico.

### Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Chemical Confirmation

This group encompasses fungi used as sacred or narcotic mushrooms by
indigenous peoples for which no reliable chemical studies confirming
neurotropic substances have been found. It includes boletes in the genera
Boletus (6 species) and Heimiella (2 species), Russula (6 species), and
gasteromycetes (6 species in Lycoperdales and Phallales).

## Taxonomic Considerations

Several taxonomic complexities affect the classification of neurotropic fungi.
The concept of the genus Psilocybe followed is that of Guzman (1983, 1995),
which modifies Singer's (1986) treatment by excluding Section
Chrysocystidiatae. The Kuhner and Romagnesi concept, which merges Psilocybe,
Hypholoma, and Stropharia into a single genus, is not followed. Similarly,
species of Psilocybe sensu Noordeloos (1995) such as P. aeruginosa and P.
albonitens are excluded from the neurotropic list.

## Criteria for Neurotropic Classification

Following Singer and Smith (1958) and Guzman (1983), species are considered
neurotropic if they exhibit the bluing feature, contain indolic substances, or
morphologically suggest the presence of such substances. Within Psilocybe,
bluing species belong to sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae,
Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and
Zapotecorum.

## Species Excluded Despite Reports

Several species reported as hallucinogenic in the literature are excluded from
the neurotropic list. [[psilocybe-atrobrunnea]], P. coprophila, and P.
pseudobullacea lack confirmed neurotropic properties despite erroneous reports.
Psilocybe antillarum is frequently misidentified as Copelandia by recreational
users, though it does not blue and is edible. Panaeolus foenisecii shows
contradictory chemical results across studies. Stijve and Kuyper (1988) did
not find psilocybin in several species previously reported as neurotropic,
including [[gerronema-fibula]], Gymnopilus fulgens, G. spectabilis, and
Hygrocybe psittacina, highlighting the need for rigorous chemical
verification. Bresinsky and Besl (1990) considered studies on
Stropharia cyanea, Mycena pura, and Amanita gemmata as doubtful.

## Confusion with Non-Neurotropic Species

Significant confusion exists between neurotropic and non-neurotropic species
in the literature. Laetiporus sulphureus and Meripilus giganteus were
considered psychotropic by Oldridge et al. (1989) due to hordenine and
tyramine content, but L. sulphureus is a common edible species in Mexico and
elsewhere. Polypores such as Polyporus tuberaster, [[poria-cocos]], and
[[ganoderma-lucidum]] have been used in religious ceremonies, though without
confirmed neurotropic properties. Chlorophyllum molybdites from Africa
bears the Yoruba name meaning "eat and hear voices from heaven" yet is
generally regarded as poisonous rather than hallucinogenic.

## Historical Periods of Study

The history of neurotropic fungi study has progressed through five periods: (1)
ancient use by indigenous peoples in Mesoamerica, Siberia, and New Guinea,
(2) early uncertain studies of the early 20th century by Schultes (1939) and
Singer (1949), (3) rigorous scientific investigations beginning with Wasson,
Heim, and Singer in the 1950s, (4) recreational use and commercialization in
the 1960s leading to legal restrictions worldwide, and (5) the current period
of taxonomic recapitulation, description of new species, and advanced chemical
analyses involving new chromatographic and spectroscopic methods.
