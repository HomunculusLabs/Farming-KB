# Far-Red Light as Phytochrome Antagonist in Floral Regulation

## Overview

Far-red light (wavelengths 700-750 nm, centred around 730 nm) acts as a biological antagonist to red light in the phytochrome [[phytochrome-signaling-plant-light-perception]] signalling system that controls flowering in plants. Discovered by Borthwick, Hendricks, and colleagues in the early 1950s, the red/far-red reversibility of photoperiodic responses revealed that a single photoreceptor could exist in two interconvertible forms, each responding to a different wavelength and producing opposite biological effects.

Far-red light converts the active phytochrome form (Pfr) back to the inactive form (Pr), effectively cancelling any flowering signal initiated by red light. This antagonistic relationship is one of the most elegant examples of molecular signal switching in plant biology, and it has profound practical implications for agriculture, horticulture, and [[cannabis-sativa-indoor-cultivation-techniques]].

## The Discovery

### Serendipitous Observation

The discovery of far-red antagonism was unexpected. Researchers studying how red light affected flowering in short-day plants noticed that plants exposed to red light during the night (which should have prevented flowering) sometimes flowered anyway. Investigation revealed that these plants had been exposed to a brief flash of far-red light immediately after the red light, and this far-red exposure completely reversed the red-light effect.

### The Butler and Siegelman Experiment

In 1959, Warren Butler and Sterling Siegelman identified the molecule responsible for both red and far-red sensitivity. They extracted a blue-green pigment from dark-grown corn seedlings that could be reversibly converted between two forms by alternating red and far-red illumination. This molecule was named **phytochrome**, and its two forms designated:

- **Pr (P660)**: Absorbs red light maximally at ~660 nm; biologically inactive
- **Pfr (P730)**: Absorbs far-red light maximally at ~730 nm; biologically active

The conversion is reversible: red light converts Pr → Pfr, far-red light converts Pfr → Pr. This molecular switch forms the basis of photoperiodic flowering control.

## Mechanism of Antagonism

### The Night-Break Reversal

The most dramatic demonstration of far-red antagonism is in night-break experiments. When a short-day plant such as chrysanthemum or soybean receives a brief flash of red light in the middle of a long dark period, flowering is suppressed because Pfr is restored. However, if a far-red flash immediately follows the red flash, Pfr reverts to Pr and the plant flowers normally, as if neither light treatment had occurred.

This reversibility can be cycled multiple times: red → far-red → red → far-red produces no net effect if the last flash is far-red. The plant's response depends entirely on the final state of the phytochrome system, not on the total number or sequence of light treatments. This demonstrated that phytochrome acts as a true biological switch, not as an accumulating signal.

### Quantitative Aspects

The antagonism is not perfectly efficient—each red/far-red cycle results in some loss of phytochrome due to photochemical side reactions. After approximately 20-30 cycles, the system begins to degrade. However, within the range of natural light conditions (where plants rarely experience rapid red/far-red alternation), the system operates with high fidelity.

The conversion quantum yield is approximately 0.2 for both directions (red→Pfr and far-red→Pr), meaning about 20% of the pigment molecules convert per photon absorbed. This relatively low quantum yield explains why photoperiodic responses require sustained light exposure of at least a few minutes to be effective.

## Natural Occurrence of Far-Red Antagonism

### Sunset Signal

In nature, the far-red antagonism serves an important [[phytochrome-ecological-function-dusk-dawn-red-far-red-light-switch]]. At sunset, the ratio of red to far-red light drops dramatically because sunlight passes through a longer atmospheric path and because chlorophyll in vegetation preferentially absorbs red while transmitting far-red. This natural far-red enrichment at dusk converts remaining Pfr to Pr, providing a reliable daily signal that night is beginning.

### Shade Detection

Plants growing beneath a canopy receive light with a reduced red-to-far-red ratio because the chlorophyll in upper canopy leaves absorbs most red light while transmitting far-red. Phytochrome detects this spectral shift and triggers [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]] responses: accelerated stem elongation, reduced leaf expansion, and accelerated flowering. The far-red component effectively acts as an "antagonist" to the red-light signal of full sunlight, informing the plant that it is being shaded.

### End-of-Day Far-Red Treatment

Growers exploit the far-red antagonism deliberately through end-of-day (EOD) far-red treatments. Applying far-red light for 5-15 minutes at the end of the photoperiod promotes stem elongation in many species. This technique is used in ornamental horticulture to increase stem length in crops like poinsettia and Easter lily without extending the actual photoperiod. The EOD far-red treatment converts residual Pfr to Pr, mimicking the onset of shade conditions.

## Far-Red and Long-Day Plant Flowering

For long-day plants (which flower when nights are short), the antagonism works in the opposite direction. Red light promotes flowering in long-day plants by establishing Pfr at the end of the day. If far-red light is applied at the end of the day, it removes Pfr and prevents flowering even under long-day conditions. This means the same red/far-red system controls flowering in both short-day and long-day plants, but the interpretation of the signal is inverted between the two groups.

In Cannabis, which is a short-day plant, the natural decline in Pfr during long nights triggers the transition to flowering. Indoor growers must ensure that dark periods are truly dark—any red-light contamination (even from indicator LEDs on equipment) can restore Pfr and delay flowering.

## Molecular Consequences of Pfr

When Pfr is present in sufficient quantities, it translocates to the cell nucleus and interacts with transcription factors called PIFs (Phytochrome Interacting Factors). Pfr binding causes phosphorylation and degradation of PIFs, releasing their repression of light-responsive genes. When far-red converts Pfr to Pr, this nuclear signalling ceases, PIFs accumulate, and dark-adapted gene expression patterns resume. The [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] depends on the cumulative time Pfr is absent from the nucleus.

## Phytochrome Gene Families

Plants typically possess multiple phytochrome genes (PHYA through PHYE in Arabidopsis), each with distinct roles in far-red and red [[darwin-phototropism-experiments-plant-light-sensing]]. PHYA is particularly sensitive to far-red light and is responsible for the "far-red high irradiance response" (FR-HIR), which operates at very low light levels. PHYB is the primary mediator of the red/far-red reversible responses described above, including shade avoidance and photoperiodic flowering control. PHYC, D, and E have more specialised roles in specific developmental contexts. The diversity of phytochrome genes allows plants to respond to a broader range of light conditions than would be possible with a single photoreceptor type.

## Far-Red in Indoor Cultivation

Understanding far-red antagonism is critical for indoor growers, particularly those cultivating Cannabis or other photoperiod-sensitive species. [[cmh-grow-lights]] vary in their far-red output: high-pressure sodium (HPS) lamps emit significant far-red, while some LED fixtures emit very little. The far-red content of the light source affects plant morphology—HPS-grown plants tend to be taller than LED-grown plants, partly because of the far-red component.

Some advanced LED fixtures include dedicated far-red LEDs that can be programmed for end-of-day treatments, allowing growers to manipulate plant height without changing the photoperiod. Conversely, growers who want compact plants must ensure their lighting does not include excessive far-red, as this would trigger unwanted stem elongation through the phytochrome shade-avoidance pathway.
