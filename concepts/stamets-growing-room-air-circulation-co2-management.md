## Functions of Fresh Air Exchange

Fresh air serves several critical functions in of [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]]:
- Supplying oxygen to the [[falconer-growing-mushrooms-in-sheds]]
- Carrying away CO2 produced by metabolism
- Facilitating moisture evaporation from the cropping surface

The exact amount of air needed depends on the CO2 requirements of the species
being grown. Fresh air requirements can also be measured in terms of air
changes per hour, a common metric mushroom growers use to size the fan.

## Fan Selection and Sizing

Axial flow and centrifugal fans are the two most commonly used types in
mushroom houses. Both operate well against high static pressure, which is a
measure of resistance to forced air flow. Static pressure is measured in inches
of water gauge and is caused by filters, heating and cooling coils, and other
obstructions to free air flow. Fans are rated by their output in cubic feet
per minute (CFM) at varying static pressures.

### Fan Sizing Guidelines

- Agaricus growers use fans capable of 4 to 6 air changes per hour, or 0.5
  CFM per [[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]] of cropping surface.
- For most small growing rooms, an axial flow fan of 6 to 10 inches diameter
  delivering 100 to 500 CFM at up to 0.5 inches of static pressure is
  sufficient.
- A variable speed motor control allows precise air velocity adjustment during
  different phases of the cultural cycle.

### Calculating Required Fan CFM

The minimum fan output for a given room can be calculated from the air changes
per hour required by the species. First, calculate the volume of the room in
cubic feet (height x width x length), then subtract the volume occupied by
trays, shelves, substrate, and other fixtures to get the free air space.

The formula is:

    CFM = (air changes per hour x free air space) / 60

Increased substrate amounts generate heat and CO2 beyond the handling capacity
of the ventilation system. A large free air space acts to buffer these changes.
A ventilation system matched to a room with only a 3:1 air-to-bed ratio would
need to move so much air that evaporation from the cropping surface would be
uncontrollable and excessive. Growing on thin grain layers (1 to 3 inches)
produces less CO2 than growing on 8 inches of compost, allowing lower
air-to-bed ratios.

## Air Ducting Design

Ducting is standard inflatable polyethylene tubing, sized to conform to the fan
diameter. PVC pipe can be substituted if ducting is unavailable. Ducts run the
length of the room at ceiling level. Configurations include central mounting
that discharges toward both walls or directly down the center aisle, and wall
mounting that directs air across the room width.

### Outlet Hole Sizing

The outlet holes are designed so the airstream reaches walls and floor without
directly hitting top containers. Two sizing guidelines apply:

1. The total surface area of the holes equals the cross-sectional area of the
   duct.
2. The spacing between holes equals one-quarter the distance between the duct
   and the wall or floor.

## Entrainment and Dead Air Elimination

The discharge of air at sufficient velocity to draw in surrounding room air is

air circulation system. Proper hole sizing and spacing create a flow pattern
of even air that directly benefits the growing mushrooms. Eliminating dead-air
pockets through entrainment is the fundamental goal of air management in the
growing room.

## Ventilation Filtration

Fresh air filters screen out atmospheric dust particles including smoke,
silica, soot, and decayed biological matter. Atmospheric dust also contains
spores, bacteria, and plant pollen, some of which are detrimental to mushroom
culture. Agaricus growers commonly use high efficiency, extended surface, dry
filters with pleated or deep fold designs. These filters remove 0.3 micron
particles at 90 to 95% efficiency and 5.0 micron particles at even higher
rates. Some mushroom farms also filter recirculated air to counteract the
spread of spores and microorganisms originating within the cropping room.
