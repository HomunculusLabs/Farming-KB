# Mollison Permaculture Design Philosophy

*Permaculture Two* (1979) expands on the design philosophy of permaculture, emphasising that permaculture is primarily a consciously designed agricultural system combining landscape design with perennial plants and animals. The word permaculture — originally "permanent agriculture" — came to mean "permanent culture" because the idea encompassed much more than just agriculture, including community systems, cultural ideologies, business, art, and every facet of human life.

## The Core Distinction

Permaculture differs from other agricultural systems (except keyline concepts) in that it is a consciously designed system. Many traditional systems have the appearance of design, but are in fact an unconscious result of the limitations of their community in its landscape, not conscious designs chosen for their relevance to society and energy savings. The main reasons for designing a plant system:

- Save energies within the system
- Cope with energies entering from outside (sun, wind, fire)
- Arrange plants to assist each other's health and survival
- Place all units in the best possible landscape arrangement
- Suit climate and site through specific design
- Integrate with human society, saving heating fuel and cooking energy
- Provide a wide range of necessities in a way every person can achieve

## Design as Aikido vs. Karate

Mollison distinguishes two approaches to landscape:
- **Aikido**: Rolling with the blows, turning adversity into strength, using everything positively. Cooperation with sun, wind, people, buildings, stones, sea, birds, and plants. "I have spoken of using aikido on the landscape, of rolling with the blows, turning them to advantage."
- **Karate**: Trying to make the landscape yield by exerting strength, striking hard blows. "If we attack nature we attack (and destroy) ourselves."

The aikido approach is indeed a low-energy system of agricultural strategy. Traditional "high farming" of the West is an example of conscious design in space and time, using plants and animals to assist each other, but it still relies on high external energy inputs.

## Three Models of Permanence

1. **Feudal permanence** (old China): Man bound to landscape by unremitting toil in service to a landlord. Labour-intensive annual grain culture in terraces, pits, pathways, and forests. Leads eventually to famine and revolution.

2. **Baronial permanence** (modern western farms): Large holdings, few people, vast grazing for a single species. Pasture is the least productive land use. Mechanisation destroys landscapes and soil. Near-regal properties of immense extent but few people.

3. **Communal permanence** (forests): Needs generations of care and knowledge, tribal or communal reverence. "To be able to plant a pecan or citrus when we are old, and to know it will not be cut down by our children's children." This is the communal permanence many seek.

The further one departs from communal permanence, the greater the risk of tyranny, feudalism, and revolution — and the more work for less yield. Any error or disturbance can bring disaster.

## Energy Dependency

The characteristic of all permanent agricultures: the system's energy needs are provided by that system. Modern crop agriculture is totally dependent on external energies — hence the oil problem. "Without permanent agriculture there is no possibility of a stable social order." Mollison traces how overpopulation itself is a response to increased energy needs in annual peasant crops — it was never a problem in forested areas, nor where energy excess is locally available.

## Design Criteria

Good design should:
- Leave options open for further refinements
- Not cut out the sun as an energy source
- Keep water running downhill
- Store water in the soil and release it clean
- Let heated air and water rise naturally (no pumps forcing reverse of natural flows)
- Apply principles of whole-system energy flows as devised by Odum
- Apply the principles of good ecologies as enunciated by common sense

Design for specific climate and site, not generic solutions. Provide for food self-sufficiency on site. Ensure adequate climate control on site through structure, tree placement, and water storage.

## Evolution of Design

Once design begins, ongoing evolution requires:
1. Observation of results
2. Control or steering toward desired end, or new evolution

"Quite small adjustments to the system may have the effect of turning disaster into triumph, or invasion into retreat."

Understanding of plant nutrition led to the evolution of crop rotation, and then to the traditional "high farming" of the west — conscious design in space and time using plants and animals to assist each other. Permaculture continues this evolution by reducing external energy dependence.

## The Crop-Master

Mollison references Aboriginal ecology where the most important person in any tribe was the crop-master — "not the chief (of warfare), not the doctor (of spirit and flesh), but the living computer, one of a long line of accurate memories, who orchestrated the taking of food." This role ensured tribal permaculture endured. "We lack crop-masters today."

## Connection to Fukuoka

Mollison references [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] work extensively: "Perhaps Fukuoka, in his book The One Straw Revolution, has best stated the basic philosophy of permaculture." Until reading Fukuoka, there was no satisfactory basis for including grain and legume crops in tree systems. Fukuoka seems to have solved the problems of no-dig grain cultivation, and both Yeomans and Fukuoka demonstrate practical applications of low-energy permanent agriculture.
