# Fungal Interactions with Metals

## Overview

Fungi interact with metals through a diverse array of mechanisms that include mobilization, immobilization, transformation and tolerance. These interactions are central to biogeochemical cycling of elements, soil formation, rock weathering, and have significant implications for bioremediation of contaminated environments. Fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at higher pH values and over a wider redox range more efficiently than bacteria. Toxic metal ions can disrupt cellular and organellar membranes, but fungi have evolved multiple strategies for metal resistance.

## Metal Mobilization Mechanisms

Fungi solubilize minerals and insoluble metal compounds through three primary biochemical mechanisms:

### Acidolysis (Proton-Promoted Dissolution)

Fungi acidify their microenvironment through excretion of protons and organic acids, and through formation of carbonic acid from respiratory CO2. Acidolysis is the main mechanism when fungi are grown with ammonium as a nitrogen source, since ammonium assimilation generates protons. Many fungi are able to excrete large quantities of organic acids including citric, oxalic, gluconic, malic, succinic and formic acids. This [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]] dihydrate (weddelite) and calcium oxalate monohydrate (whewellite) are the most common forms. Precipitation of calcium oxalate acts as a reservoir for calcium in the ecosystem and influences phosphate availability. Toxic metal oxalate formation provides a mechanism for tolerating environments with high metal concentrations. Beauveria caledonica, an oxalate over-excreting fungus, transforms cadmium, copper, lead and zinc from various toxic metal minerals into oxalates and tolerates all tested minerals. Oxalate excretion is enhanced with nitrate as nitrogen source (versus ammonium), and by the presence of bicarbonate, calcium, and some toxic metals (Cu, Al) or minerals (pyromorphite, zinc phosphate).

## Mycorrhizal Metal Interactions

Mycorrhizal fungi are directly involved in metal transformations:

- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi** filter toxic metals in the hyphal sheath or Hartig net by sorption, restrict metal mobility due to sheath hydrophobicity, and complex metals with released organic acids.
- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi** reduce toxic metal (e.g., Zn) uptake by plants.
- **Ericoid mycorrhizal and ectomycorrhizal fungi** dissolve cadmium-, copper-, zinc- and lead-bearing minerals including metal phosphates.

The ectomycorrhizal association of Paxillus involutus with Scots pine demonstrated that zinc phosphate dissolution, phosphorus acquisition, and zinc accumulation depend on mycorrhizal status, fungal zinc tolerance, and phosphorus status. Under phosphorus-replete conditions, zinc-tolerant ectomycorrhiza employed a metal avoidance strategy restricting metal entry. Under phosphorus deficiency, zinc-tolerant ectomycorrhiza mobilized the highest amount of zinc. Metal-resistant mycobionts are most efficient at ameliorating toxicity to host plants, and such associations can be used in re-vegetation, re-forestation and phyto- and rhizo-remediation strategies.

## Redox Transformations and Metal Speciation

Fungi mediate important redox transformations of metals in the environment. Reduced forms of metals and metalloids (elemental Ag, Se, Te) are precipitated within and around fungal cells. The reductive ability is manifested by characteristic coloration: black for Ag or Te, red for Se. Desert varnish, an oxidized metal layer a few millimetres thick on rocks in arid and semi-arid regions, is believed to be of microbial origin with proposed fungal involvement. Fungi oxidize manganese and iron from minerals such as siderite (FeCO3) and rhodochrosite (MnCO3), precipitating them as oxides, and form dark Fe(II)- and Mn(II)-patinas on glass surfaces. Fungi with Cr(VI)-reducing activity are useful for treatment of Cr-polluted soils.

## Secondary Mineral Precipitation

Formation of secondary organic and inorganic minerals by fungi occurs through metabolism-independent and -dependent processes. Precipitation, nucleation and deposition of crystalline material on and within cell walls is influenced by environmental pH and cell wall composition. Precipitation of carbonates, phosphates and hydroxides increases soil aggregation. Cations released through dissolution (Si4+, Fe3+, Al3+, Ca2+) stimulate precipitation of compounds acting as bonding agents for soil particles. Hyphae enmesh soil particles, alter alignment and release organic metabolites that enhance aggregate stability. Secondary mycogenic minerals include birnessite, MnO, FeO, ferrihydrite, iron gluconate, calcium formate, forsterite, goethite, halloysite, hydroserussite, todorokite, moolooite and montmorillonite.

## Bioremediation Applications

The ability of fungi to transform toxic metals and metalloids has potential for treatment of contaminated land. Fungal metal immobilization via biosorption, accumulation and precipitation turns metals into chemically more inert forms, resulting in detoxification. Heterotrophic leaching by fungi can be used to clean up soils, solid industrial wastes, low-grade ores and metal-bearing minerals. However, fungal mineral-solubilizing activity may also compromise in situ chemical remediation methods: phosphate-induced metal stabilization (formation of hopeite, pyromorphite) may be undermined by fungal dissolution of these stabilized products. Assessment of long-term environmental consequences of chemical remediation technologies should account for the biogeochemical activity of soil fungal and bacterial communities.

## Fungal Interactions with Radionuclides

Fungi interact with radioactive elements through the same mechanisms that govern their interactions with stable metals, but the radionuclide dimension adds significant ecological and practical importance. Fungi can accumulate uranium, cesium-137, strontium-90, and other radionuclides from contaminated environments through biosorption to cell walls and intracellular uptake. At the Chernobyl exclusion zone, melanized fungi have been documented growing on and within the concrete structures of the sarcophagus, apparently using the radiation itself as an energy source through the process of radiosynthesis — melanin may act as a radiation shield and energy transducer, converting ionizing radiation into chemical energy for growth. This remarkable capability has implications for the bioremediation of radioactive waste sites and the design of biological shielding materials for nuclear applications.

## Fungal Transformations of Metalloids

In addition to metals, fungi interact with several metalloids including arsenic, selenium, antimony, and tellurium. These interactions have both environmental and biotechnological significance. Fungal reduction of selenium and tellurium produces elemental forms that precipitate within and around fungal cells, visible as characteristic red (selenium) or black (tellurium) coloration. This bioreductive precipitation has been explored for the recovery of selenium from industrial waste streams and for the bioremediation of selenium-contaminated agricultural drainage water. Arsenic transformations by fungi include oxidation, reduction, methylation, and volatilization, with the production of volatile trimethylarsine representing a potential mechanism for arsenic removal from contaminated soils. The ability of fungi to transform metalloids makes them valuable agents in [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]], which can extend for meters from individual root systems, represent enormous surface areas for metal exchange with soil solution. A single gram of soil may contain 10-100 meters of fungal hyphae, creating a vast interfacial zone where metal transformations occur. The fungal necromass (dead fungal biomass) that accumulates in soil contributes significantly to the soil's metal-binding capacity — up to 50% of the total metal-binding capacity of some soils has been attributed to dead fungal hyphal material. This long-term metal sequestration by fungal necromass represents an important sink for toxic metals in terrestrial ecosystems and has implications for the long-term stability of contaminated sites following remediation.

## Fungi in Phytomining and Bio-Ore Processing

Fungal metal interactions have potential applications in phytomining (extracting metals from low-grade ores using biological systems) and bio-ore processing. Certain fungi can bioaccumulate metals from mineral matrices, concentrating them in their biomass to levels that exceed the original ore concentration. Fungal leaching of low-grade ores using organic acid production is commercially practiced for copper, gold (as a pretreatment step), and some rare earth elements. The concept of "fungal bio-ore" involves cultivating metal-accumulating fungi on contaminated substrates, harvesting the biomass, and processing it as a concentrated metal source. This approach is particularly promising for recovering valuable metals from electronic waste, industrial slag, and mine tailings that are too low in concentration for conventional metallurgical processing.
