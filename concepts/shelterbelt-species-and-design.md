# Shelterbelt Species Selection and [[cover-crops]] yield (e.g., forage trees in pasture). This ensures the area occupied by shelterbelts adds to total crop yields rather than deducting from them.

### Yield-Positive vs. Yield-Negative Shelterbelts

- **Yield-positive shelterbelts** use nitrogen-fixing and browse-edible species that actively contribute to the farming [[araucaria-heterophylla]]* ([[norfolk-island-pine]])

Yields are little affected by strong winds. These species have evolved to thrive in the harshest wind-exposed positions.

### Exposure-Tolerant Species

These species can withstand significant wind but show some yield reduction:

- Barley
- Some Brassicas
- *Casuarina* (she-oak)
- *[[coprosma-repens-entity]]* (mirror bush)

Yields are reduced in strong winds, but dry matter yield is less affected than in wind-sensitive plants.

### Wind-Sensitive Species

These are many important crop species that require intensive shelterbelt systems:

- Citrus
- Avocado
- Kiwifruit vines
- Many deciduous fruits
- Corn (maize)
- Sugar cane
- Bananas

Both plant height and yields rapidly decrease with increases in wind speed. For these species, very intensive shelterbelt systems are essential.

## Morphological Adaptations of Wind-Resistant Plants

Many fire-resistant plants are also wind resistant. In addition, some drought-resistant but fire-prone species (pines) will withstand wind. What wind-resistant plants have in common are ways of resisting desiccation and sandblast through specific morphological features:

- **Fibrous stems**: Palms resist wind through flexible, resilient trunks that bend rather than break
- **Fleshy leaves**: Aloes, agaves, and Euphorbias store water in thick leaves, reducing transpiration stress
- **Hard, needle-like leaves or stems**: Pines, tamarisks, *Casuarina*, and some *Acacias* present minimal surface area to drying winds
- **"Furry" or hairy (tomentose) leaf covers**: Create a [[boundary-layer-redistribution-fungal-tropism]] of still air that reduces moisture loss
- **Waxy leaves**: *Coprosma*, eucalypts, some pines, and some *Acacias* have cuticular wax coatings that reduce transpiration

## Shelterbelt Permeability

The permeability of a windbreak is critical to its effectiveness. A solid, impermeable barrier creates strong turbulence on the lee side that can actually increase damage. The optimal design allows **40–60% wind penetration** through the barrier, which:

- Reduces wind velocity without creating damaging eddies
- Provides a more extensive sheltered zone on the lee side
- Allows the wind to deposit rather than scour material

A less permeable hedgerow needs to be wider than a more open one to achieve the same shelter effect, as shown in Figure 5.16 of the source. The profile of the windbreak should be carefully streamlined, especially where space is ample and winds are strong.

## Shelterbelt Succession and Establishment

### Staged Planting

Shelterbelt is planted as a succession from a tall grass to a taller legume to a long-term, tall, windfast hedge. Species might include *Casuarina*, poplar, willow, eucalypt, oak, or chestnut. All this complex can be set out at once and managed as it evolves to maturity.

Quickset (by cuttings) hedges of poplar and *Erythrina* are popular because of their fast windbreak effect, but species must be chosen to suit a particular climate.

### Multi-Row Windbreaks

Where space is ample and winds strong, up to **six rows of tree and tall grass lines** can be established, giving a mixed yield of:

- Forage
- Timber
- Fuel
- Mulch
- Honey
- Shelter

In more constricted areas, a matrix of single-tree lines is usual and effective if close-spaced. However, there is no such thing as a standard shape or windbreak — very different configurations are needed for different sites, functions, accessory species to the enterprise sheltered, wind strength, and wind load (salt, sand, dust).

### Interplanting with Crops

Trees like avocado and crops like papaya can be grown on sub-tropical frosty sites provided there is a high canopy of hardy palms or light-crowned legumes (e.g., *Butia* palm, *Jacaranda*, *[[tipuana-tipu]]*). Such sites do not frost because:

- There is no bare ground radiation at night (the canopy traps heat)
- Advected frost is impeded by the canopy barrier

## Water Use and Shelterbelt Interactions

For isolated trees or trees whose canopy lifts above the general forest level, wind of even low speeds may increase the transpiration rate, sometimes **doubling water use**. The effect is greatest on water-loving plants and much less on dry-adapted species which have:

- Impermeable leaf cuticle
- Good control of stomata
- A cover of spines and hairs

### Root Competition Issues

Problems arise when plants used for shelterbelts (e.g., poplar) are themselves heavy water-use species with invasive roots. An **annual root-cutting or rip-line** may be necessary along such windbreaks to permit the sheltered crop to obtain sufficient water, but it is best to choose more suitable species in the first place.

### Yield and Water Use Balance

Research shows that hedgerows increase yields on both sides but also increase total water use (Figure 5.17 in the source). The net effect is generally positive because the [[farm-strategies-for-yield-increase]] outweighs the additional water consumption, but in water-limited environments this trade-off must be carefully managed.

## Photosynthetic Efficiency and Wind Damage

Hot, dry winds and winds laden with salt have the most damaging effect on plant yields, although at high wind speeds mechanical damage can occur regardless of humidity or salt content. Damaged crop plants such as corn or bananas suffer **photosynthetic inefficiency ranging from 20–85%** when the leaf laminae are torn or frayed, or the midribs are broken (Chang, 1968).

This highlights the critical importance of shelterbelts not just for preventing outright plant death but for maintaining the photosynthetic efficiency that underpins all crop productivity. Even sub-lethal wind damage that tears leaves or breaks stems can dramatically reduce harvestable yields.

## Summary of Integrated Shelterbelt Design

A well-designed shelterbelt system integrates multiple functions:

1. **Wind protection** for crops, livestock, and structures
2. **Soil [[cervantes-mulch-outdoor-moisture-conservation]]** through reduced evaporation and increased precipitation capture
3. **Soil building** through [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-and-green-manure]] leaf litter
4. **Biodiversity support** through habitat for predatory insects and birds
5. **Productive yield** from forage, timber, fuel, mulch, and food species
6. **Frost mitigation** through canopy heat trapping
Ongoing developments continue to expand practical applications and theoretical understanding.
