# Brassinosteroid Growth Promotion in Psilocybe Cubensis

Brassinosteroids are a class of steroidal plant growth regulators that were first
recognized as a distinct group of phytohormones in the late 1970s.

By 1990, approximately 30 members had been detected across a wide variety of higher
plants, including both Angiospermae and Gymnospermae, as well as in a green alga.

Their strong growth-promoting activity involves complex physiological effects,
including increases in cell elongation and cell division.

Promising results in agricultural applications to produce higher crop yields and
anti-stress effects had been reported, but no studies had examined the occurrence
and activity of brassinosteroids on fungi until this pioneering work by Gartz,
Adam, and Vorbrodt.

## The Discovery

Published in Naturwissenschaften in 1990, the study by J. Gartz (Institut fur
Biotechnologie der AdW der DDR, Leipzig) alongside G. Adam and H.M. Vorbrodt
(Institut fur Biochemie der Pflanzen der AdW der DDR, Halle/Saale) was the first
to document a strong growth-promoting effect of a brassinosteroid on mycelial
growth and fruiting of a fungus.

The subtropical fungus [[Psilocybe cubensis]] was selected as the test organism,
using a strain characterized by high stability in growth and fruiting with various
substrates.

The significance of this discovery extends beyond the specific findings because it
demonstrated for the first time that a class of compounds previously considered
exclusive to plant physiology could exert powerful biological effects on fungi.

This cross-kingdom activity challenged the assumption that fungal growth regulation
was mediated solely by endogenous fungal hormones and nutrients, opening an entirely
new avenue of research into the hormonal regulation of fungal development.

## Historical Context of Brassinosteroid Research

The discovery of brassinosteroids began with the isolation of brassinolide from
rapeseed pollen in 1979 by Grove et al. at the United States Department of
Agriculture.

Since that initial discovery, the field expanded rapidly as researchers identified
numerous related compounds across the plant kingdom.

Brassinosteroids were recognized as a sixth class of plant hormones, alongside
auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, and ethylene.

Their role in promoting cell elongation and division, enhancing stress resistance,
and increasing agricultural yields made them a subject of intense research interest
throughout the 1980s.

The research by Gartz and colleagues represented a conceptual leap, testing whether
a plant hormone could affect fungal physiology.

This cross-kingdom approach was novel and raised fundamental questions about the
conservation of steroid signaling mechanisms.

The study was conducted in East Germany shortly before reunification, at
institutions of the Academy of Sciences of the DDR, representing some of the last
major mycological research from that scientific tradition.

## Experimental Design

The cultivation system employed two parallel approaches.

Mycelium was grown on 6% malt agar as well as on a horse dung and rice grain
mixture in water for fruiting production.

Both substrates contained 5 g of KH2PO4 per liter.

The brassinosteroid used was synthetic 22S,23S-homobrassinolide, prepared from
stigmasterol.

The concentration tested was 10 to the negative 2 ppm (0.01 ppm), with ten
experimental runs conducted for each cultivation system, alongside control series
without steroid.

The choice of two complementary cultivation systems was methodologically important.

Malt agar provided a chemically defined substrate that allowed assessment of the
brassinosteroid's effect on mycelial growth without the confounding variables
present in complex organic substrates.

The dung and grain mixture, by contrast, represented a more ecologically relevant
and commercially realistic substrate that could reveal effects on fruiting body
production that would not be apparent on simple agar media.

## The Brassinosteroid Compound

22S,23S-homobrassinolide is a brassinosteroid with a specific stereochemistry at
the 22 and 23 positions, both in the S configuration.

The compound was synthesized from stigmasterol, a plant-derived sterol, through
established chemical routes documented by Adam and Marquardt in their 1986
Phytochemistry publication.

This particular brassinosteroid was chosen for its documented potency in plant
growth promotion assays and its availability through synthetic production.

The synthetic preparation from stigmasterol is noteworthy because it demonstrates
that the active compound does not need to be extracted from natural sources, making
potential commercial application more feasible.

Stigmasterol is itself abundant in plant tissues, particularly in legumes and
unrefined vegetable oils, providing an inexpensive starting material for
brassinosteroid synthesis.

## Results: Mycelial Growth on Agar

On malt agar, the brassinosteroid-treated cultures exhibited dramatically
accelerated mycelial growth, expanding two to three times faster than the control
series without steroid.

This represents one of the most pronounced growth-promoting effects ever documented
for a phytohormone applied to a non-plant organism.

The speed differential was readily observable and consistent across all ten
experimental runs, providing strong statistical confidence in the result.

The fact that the growth enhancement was observed on a simple defined medium (malt
agar) suggests that the brassinosteroid is not merely improving nutrient
availability or substrate utilization but is directly influencing the fungal
metabolic or regulatory machinery responsible for hyphal extension.

This distinction is important because it points to a direct biological mechanism
rather than an indirect nutritional effect.

## Results: Fruiting on Dung/Grain Substrate

The effects on the dung and grain substrate were equally striking.

The first flush of fruit bodies appeared remarkably earlier in the
brassinosteroid-treated cultures.

In the control group, the first sporocarps were produced within 4 to 5 weeks.

With the addition of brassinosteroid, fruiting of the mycelia occurred in just 3
to 3.5 weeks, representing a 25 to 30% reduction in the time to first harvest.

For commercial mushroom cultivation, where time to harvest directly impacts
profitability, this acceleration would represent a significant economic advantage.

The time reduction is particularly valuable because it shortens the period during
which cultures are vulnerable to contamination.

Faster colonization means less exposure to competing microorganisms, potentially
reducing crop losses in commercial settings where contamination rates can be a
major factor limiting production efficiency.

## Increased Fruiting Body Production

The variation in the number of fruit bodies formed across the five flushes during
the fructification period showed significant enhancement.

With brassinosteroid treatment, four to seven mushrooms were produced in the first
flush compared to only one to three in the control.

This increase in early yield was consistent and substantial, suggesting that the
brassinosteroid may influence the regulatory mechanisms that trigger the transition
from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting.

The increase in first-flush yield is particularly important in mushroom cultivation
because the first flush typically produces the largest and highest-quality fruit
bodies.

Enhancing this initial harvest while reducing overall crop cycle time would compound
the economic benefits.

The total yield across all five flushes was also higher in treated cultures, though
the differential was most pronounced in the earliest flushes.

## Enhanced Dry Mass

The steroid-treated cultures also afforded a remarkably higher dry mass compared to
the control cultivations.

Treated cultures yielded 3.4 to 3.9 grams of dry material per 10 grams of substrate,
while control cultures produced only 2.3 to 2.9 grams per 10 grams of substrate.

This represents approximately a 40 to 50% increase in dry mass yield, which is a
commercially significant improvement for mushroom cultivation.

The dry mass increase indicates that the brassinosteroid does not merely accelerate
water uptake or create a visual impression of larger growth, but actually increases
the biosynthetic output of the fungal metabolism.

More fungal tissue is being produced per unit of substrate consumed, suggesting
improved metabolic efficiency or enhanced access to substrate nutrients facilitated
by the hormone treatment.

## Suppression of Incomplete Fruit Bodies

One of the most notable observations was that the formation of incomplete fruit
bodies, which are normally present in mushroom cultivation and represent wasted
biological effort, was almost completely suppressed by the brassinosteroid.

This suggests that the phytohormone helps coordinate the developmental program more
effectively, ensuring that initiated fruit bodies complete their development to
maturity rather than aborting partway through.

The suppression of abortive fruit body formation has both biological and practical
significance.

Biologically, it implies that brassinosteroids may stabilize the developmental
commitment of primordia, reducing the sensitivity of developing fruit bodies to
environmental fluctuations that might otherwise trigger abortion.

Practically, it means that more of the biological resources invested in fruit body
initiation are carried through to harvest, improving overall cultivation efficiency.

## Morphological Changes

Interestingly, the added brassinosteroid also influenced the morphology of the
formed fruit bodies.

The treated specimens lacked the typical membranous annulus on the stems, which is
a characteristic feature of [[Psilocybe cubensis]].

This morphological alteration suggests that brassinosteroids may interact with fungal
developmental pathways in ways that differ from their effects on plant cell
elongation, potentially affecting the expression of genes involved in fruit body
tissue differentiation.

The loss of the annulus could be related to changes in the timing or pattern of
tissue differentiation during fruit body maturation.

In untreated specimens, the annulus forms as a remnant of the partial veil that
connects the cap to the stem during early development.

If brassinosteroid treatment accelerates the developmental timeline, the veil may
rupture and deteriorate more completely, or the cellular differentiation that
produces the annular tissue may be altered.

## Implications for Fungal Biology

The results of this study suggest that brassinosteroids could play a physiological
role in higher fungi, expanding the known biological range of these compounds beyond
the plant kingdom.

The fact that a plant-derived steroidal hormone can so dramatically enhance fungal
growth and development raises fundamental questions about the evolutionary
conservation of steroid signaling pathways across kingdoms.

The cross-kingdom activity of brassinosteroids also raises the possibility that fungi
might produce or respond to endogenous brassinosteroid-like compounds.

If fungal genomes encode steroid receptors or biosynthetic pathways analogous to
those in plants, this would represent a previously unrecognized layer of fungal
regulatory biology.

The original authors noted that further studies, including commercially important
species, were in progress.

## Relevance to Commercial Cultivation

The practical implications for mushroom cultivation are significant.

A 40 to 50% increase in dry mass yield, coupled with earlier fruiting and reduced
abortive fruit body formation, would translate to substantially improved efficiency
in commercial operations.

The extremely low concentration required (0.01 ppm) means that treatment costs would
be negligible.

However, the morphological changes observed, particularly the loss of the annulus,
might affect the marketability of the product for certain applications where species
identification features are important.

For gourmet mushroom cultivation of species such as Agaricus bisporus, Pleurotus
ostreatus, or Lentinula edodes, brassinosteroid treatment could potentially offer
similar benefits without the legal complications associated with psilocybin-containing
species.

## Mechanism of Action

The mechanism by which brassinosteroids promote fungal growth remains unclear.

In plants, brassinosteroids are known to bind to specific cell surface receptors and
activate a signaling cascade involving phosphorylation events that ultimately modify
gene expression.

Whether fungi possess analogous receptor systems or whether the brassinosteroid
interacts with existing fungal steroid signaling pathways is an open question that
the original authors noted deserved further investigation.

One possibility is that brassinosteroids interact with fungal ergosterol-based
membrane systems, since both brassinosteroids and ergosterol are sterol derivatives.

Another possibility is that brassinosteroids influence the fungal oxidative
metabolism or stress response pathways in ways that redirect resources toward
growth and away from other cellular processes.

The extremely low effective concentration suggests a receptor-mediated mechanism
rather than a nonspecific membrane effect.

## Comparison with Other Fungal Growth Promoters

The brassinosteroid effect can be compared with other known fungal growth promoters.

Classical fungal growth stimulants include plant extracts, peptone supplements, and
various organic amendments that improve nutrient availability.

Unlike these nutritional supplements, brassinosteroids act at concentrations orders
of magnitude lower and appear to function as signaling molecules rather than nutrient
sources.

This places them in a fundamentally different category of growth promoters, more
analogous to plant hormones than to fertilizers or supplements.

Other steroidal compounds have been reported to affect fungal growth, including
certain animal steroid hormones and synthetic anabolic steroids.

However, the specificity and magnitude of the brassinosteroid effect on
[[Psilocybe cubensis]] appears unusual even among steroidal growth promoters,
suggesting a particularly strong interaction between the brassinosteroid structure
and the fungal regulatory machinery.
