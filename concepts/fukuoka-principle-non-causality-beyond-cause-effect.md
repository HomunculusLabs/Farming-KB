## Rice Blast Disease: A Case Study

Fukuoka's most extended analysis of non-causality examined rice blast
disease, one of the most common and destructive diseases of rice in
Japan. The scientific view was straightforward: rice blast bacteria
infiltrate the plant, causing disease; therefore, applying bactericides
controls the disease. Fukuoka showed that this analysis, while not
incorrect at a surface level, was dangerously incomplete.

identified as a cause, understanding how it relates to bacterial attack
required understanding photosynthesis, which science had not yet fully
explained. Root rot might make plants susceptible, but defining what
constituted a healthy root-to-shoot balance led to further questions
about silica content, stalk hardness, physiology, and genetics. "In the
end, we gradually fail to understand even those causes that appeared
clear at first, and completely lose sight of the true cause."

## Disease as Natural Process

Fukuoka then made his most provocative argument: the rice plant itself
might perceive blast disease not as an attack but as a protective
mechanism. "The disease might even be regarded as a means by nature for
preventing the overly dense growth of rice plants, thus aiding
photosynthesis and assuring the full production of seed." In this view,
disease was not a problem to be solved but a process to be understood
within the larger context of natural balance.

This reframing had profound practical implications. If disease was a
symptom of imbalance rather than an invasion by a pathogen, then the
appropriate response was not to kill the pathogen but to restore balance
— and the most effective way to restore balance was to stop the practices
that created the imbalance in the first place. "Without a grasp of the
true cause of the disease, his solution cannot be a real solution."
