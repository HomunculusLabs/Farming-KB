# Mushroom Growing Conditions

The successful cultivation of [[agaricus-campestris]] depends upon the
careful regulation of three principal environmental factors: temperature,
moisture, and light. The main factors to be regulated are temperature
and moisture, and consequently any situation in which these conditions
may be controlled to accord with the requirements should be satisfactory
for mushroom growing.

## Temperature Requirements

### Optimum Range

The optimum temperature for commercial mushroom growing is from about 54
to 56 degrees F. If a broader good range is considered, the limits may
be extended from 50 to 65 degrees F. Growing at a temperature more or
less continuously above 60 degrees F, or below 50 degrees F, is not
commercially successful; yet much depends upon other factors.

### High Temperature Effects

A high temperature long continued, such as 72 degrees F for 24 hours,
is fatal to the pin heads and injurious to the spawn. However, this
injury is not due to the mushroom [[cotter-mushroom-life-cycle-mycelium-biology]] being
directly killed by the temperature; rather, it results from the great
stimulus given to competing organisms at the higher temperature. These
include insects, fungi, and bacteria that either crowd out or directly
attack the mushroom mycelium.

Pure cultures of [[agar]]icus campestris grow well up to 85 degrees F so
long as sufficient moisture is present. This proves that conditions for
growth in pure cultures differ from those in the mushroom bed, the
difference being due in large part to the elimination of certain
biological factors and enemies. Sporophores have been produced in Mason
jar cultures at a temperature constantly at or above 70 degrees F.

### Low Temperature Effects

Mushroom beds will withstand being frozen for considerable periods. A
bed spawned in November, with the temperature never above 52 degrees F
through winter and frozen on several occasions, bore abundantly when
continuously higher temperature arrived in March. The mushrooms produced
were of unusual size, though the period of production was relatively
brief.

### Quality Considerations

The grower who wishes to produce heavy mushrooms should not push the
beds by regulating to the highest permissible temperature. At higher
temperatures the sporophores expand and elongate so rapidly that the
large, heavy buttons making up the highest market quality are not
produced. This effect is partially due to decreased humidity as
temperature rises.

## Moisture Requirements

### Compost Moisture

The compost must remain moist throughout the growing process. A simple
test for sufficient moisture is to compress some compost in the hand:
under proper pressure it will not be easy to squeeze out drops of water,
yet the hand will be distinctly moistened. This usually denotes a water
content of about 65 percent.

### Watering After Casing

When the casing material is applied, it should be well moistened by
repeated sprinkling, not by a sudden drenching. The amount of water
subsequently should be as little as possible to keep the bed merely
moist. It is usually best to water frequently rather than heavily,
applying sufficient water each time to slightly more than counterbalance
evaporation.

When mushrooms begin to appear, more water may be given. The exact
amount varies with evaporation conditions. A dry room may require
sprinkling twice a day; a favorable cellar no oftener than once a week.
Surface indications are generally satisfactory criteria if followed
closely.

### Air Humidity

A high temperature has an unfortunate effect upon mushroom form partly
due to a decrease in atmospheric humidity. Proper humidity helps
produce the large, heavy buttons that command the highest prices. A
sufficiently sloping roof with air space helps correct precipitation of
moisture on walls and ceilings.

## Light

Mushrooms require no light for growth. In fact, the underground quarries
of Paris and its suburbs, where the mushroom industry reached enormous
proportions, demonstrate that complete absence of light is perfectly
satisfactory. The suburban caves lie from a few feet to 50 feet beneath
the surface, varying from 5 to 25 feet in width and 5 to 20 or more
feet in height. These caves contain upwards of 2,000 kilometers (about
1,200 miles) of mushroom beds.

## Suitable Growing Situations

### Caves and Cellars

The French mushroom industry has fallen heir to the subterranean
quarries which constitute a very extensive array of artificial caves.
These caves are responsible for the enormous development of the
industry in France. In the suburbs of Montrouge, Vitry, Chatillon,
Issy, Nanterre, and others, mushroom growing is one of the really
important industries.

### Mushroom Houses

For situations where natural caves are unavailable, purpose-built
mushroom houses should provide perfect drainage, sufficient height for
beds in tiers of two to five, and ventilation arrangements. Double
walls with fresh shavings between them provide insulation. Hollow tile
or concrete are suitable materials. About 2.25 feet of free space
between beds in tiers is required.

### Open Air Culture

Open air culture for mushrooms cannot be regarded as having great
possibilities of success in most regions. There are many difficulties:
daily temperature variation, drying winds, and difficulty regulating
moisture. Successful open air beds have been observed in Southern
England during late autumn and winter, and parts of California with mean
monthly temperatures of 50 to 60 degrees F may also be suitable.

## Spawning Temperature

Spawning should not be carried out until the bed temperature falls to
70 to 75 degrees F, and spawning at 75 degrees is permissible only when
the temperature is unquestionably declining. Recent tests indicate that
65 to 70 degrees F is generally preferable. Below 50 degrees growth will
be very slow. Earlier advice recommending spawning at 85 to 90 degrees
F was incorrect; that temperature is fatal if maintained for some time.
