# Fungi on Living Plant Substrata

Fungi that colonize living plant tissues, including leaves, stems,
fruits, and flowers, represent a major component of fungal biodiversity.
These fungi range from obligate biotrophs that require living host cells
to necrotrophs that kill host tissue before colonizing it, and
endophytes that inhabit plant tissues asymptomatically. The diversity
of fungi on living plants is enormous, with many species remaining
undescribed, particularly in tropical regions where plant species
richness is highest.

## Major Groups on Living Plant Surfaces

### Rust Fungi (Uredinales)

Rust fungi are obligate biotrophic pathogens that cause some of the
most economically significant plant diseases worldwide. They produce
distinctive spore-bearing structures (spermogonia, aecia, uredinia,
and telia) on living host tissue. Their taxonomy and life cycles are
discussed in [[biodiversity-fungi-tropical-fungal-diversity]]. Rust fungi are found on a wide range of
host plants from ferns to angiosperms and gymnosperms.

### Smut Fungi (Ustilaginales)

Smut fungi are biotrophic pathogens characterized by the production of
dark, powdery teliospore masses in host tissues, particularly in
inflorescences, leaves, and stems. Major genera include Ustilago
(species causing loose smuts of cereals), Tilletia (bunt and dwarf
bunt of wheat), and Thecaphora. The monograph by Zundel (1953) remains
an important reference for the group. Smaller tropical genera include
Dermatosorus, Erratomyces, Georgefischeria, and Pericladium, many of
which are poorly known and likely represent undescribed diversity.

### Powdery Mildews (Erysiphales)

Powdery mildews are obligate biotrophic ascomycetes producing
characteristic white powdery colonies on aerial plant surfaces. They
are among the most recognizable and easily collected plant-pathogenic
fungi. Their taxonomy and diversity are covered in [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]].
Species are highly host-specific, making them useful indicators of
plant host presence and distribution.

### Foliar Fungi

Beyond the major pathogen groups, a vast diversity of other fungi
colonize living leaf surfaces (phylloplane) and internal leaf tissues.
These include endophytic fungi that asymptomatically inhabit leaf
tissues, discussed in [[biodiversity-of-fungi-endophytic-fungi]]. The phylloplane mycobiota
includes both saprotrophic species that colonize senescing tissue
and latent pathogens that persist on leaves before causing disease
when conditions become favorable.

### Canker-Associated Fungi

Canker-causing fungi infect living bark and cambium of woody plants,
producing localized necrotic lesions (cankers) that can girdle and kill
branches or entire trees. Important canker fungi include species of
Nectria, Cryptostroma, Hypoxylon, and Ceratocystis. The
[[biodiversity-fungi-plant-mutualisms]] relationship often involves a complex interaction
between the fungus, the host tree, and environmental stress factors.

### Gall-Associated Fungi

Several groups of fungi induce gall formation on their host plants.
Species of Exobasidiales cause hypertrophic growth on leaves, stems,
and flowers of Ericaceae and other plant families. The gall tissue
provides nutrition and protection for the fungus. Other gall-inducing
fungi include some smut species and Taphrina species, which cause
leaf curl and pocket galls on various tree species.

## Sampling Methodology

### Disease Assessment

Two primary measurements are used in assessing fungal diseases on
living plants: disease incidence (the proportion of plants or plant
organs affected) and disease severity (the proportion of tissue
affected on individual plants). Both measurements are essential for
quantifying disease impact and tracking changes over time or across
geographic gradients.

Sample size requirements for disease surveys can be estimated using
statistical methods described by Kranz (1988). Factors influencing
sample size include the expected disease level, the desired precision
of estimates, and the spatial distribution pattern of infected plants.

### Canopy Access Methods

Studying fungi on living plant substrata in forest canopies presents
significant logistical challenges. Several methods have been developed
for canopy access: (1) ropes and pulleys (single-rope technique),
(2) spiked foot griffes (climbing irons) for ascending tree trunks,
(3) shotguns for collecting branches from tall trees, and (4) trained
monkeys for collecting canopy material in some tropical research
programs. Each method has advantages and limitations regarding safety,
specimen quality, accessibility of different canopy levels, and
levels, and potential impact on the trees being studied.

### Collection Protocols

Specimens should be collected with adequate host material to allow
plant identification. For rust and smut fungi, this means including
leaves, stems, or inflorescences bearing spore-bearing structures
along with vegetative features. Pressing specimens in standard plant
presses with drying paper preserves both fungal structures and host
morphology. Field notes should record host species, plant part
affected, geographic location, habitat, collector, and date.

## Fungal Diversity on Fruits

Fungi colonizing living and harvested fruits include both pre-harvest
pathogens that infect fruit on the tree and post-harvest pathogens
that cause decay during storage and transport. Major post-harvest
pathogens include Penicillium digitatum and P. italicum on citrus,
Botrytis cinerea on various fruits (gray mold), Monilinia species
on stone fruits (brown rot), and Colletotrichum species (anthracnose)
on a wide range of tropical and subtropical fruits. The diversity of
fungi on fruit surfaces is influenced by fruit chemistry, cuticle
thickness, environmental conditions, and the presence of natural
antimicrobial compounds.

## Geographic and Host Distribution Patterns

The diversity of fungi on living plants follows patterns of host plant
diversity, with tropical regions harboring far more species than
temperate zones. However, some fungal groups show different patterns:
powdery mildews, for example, are often more diverse in temperate
regions. Elevational gradients in fungal diversity on plants have been
documented, with some fungal groups showing peak diversity at mid-
elevations. Seasonal patterns of fruiting and sporulation influence
detection rates, requiring sampling across multiple seasons for
comprehensive surveys.
