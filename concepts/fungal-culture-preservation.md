# Fungal Culture Preservation — Methods and Protocols

## Overview

Long-term preservation of fungal cultures is essential for maintaining living reference collections, supporting taxonomic research, and ensuring reproducibility in [[fungal-zoospore-biology-and-flagellate-motility]]. Different preservation methods vary in their effectiveness, cost, and suitability for different fungal groups. Choosing the appropriate method depends on the culture's characteristics, intended duration of storage, and available infrastructure.

## Mineral Oil Preservation

Mineral oil overlay is one of the simplest and most widely used short-to-medium-term preservation methods:

- Cover the slant culture completely with sterile mineral oil
- Seal the culture tube with Parafilm to prevent evaporation and contamination
- Store at **5°C** (refrigeration) or **-20°C** (freezer)
- Lower temperatures increase survival rates by **2–3 times** compared to room temperature storage

Mineral oil preservation is suitable for many common molds and yeasts but may not adequately preserve fastidious species or cultures with high metabolic activity.

## Low-Temperature Freezing

Standard freezing provides effective preservation for many fungal cultures:

- Most cultures can be stored at temperatures between **-20°C and -80°C**
- The addition of cryoprotectants (e.g., glycerol at 10–15% final concentration) improves viability
- Ultra-low freezers (-80°C) offer better long-term stability than standard freezers (-20°C)

## Cryopreservation

Cryopreservation at or below **-139°C** (typically in liquid nitrogen at -196°C) is the gold standard for long-term culture maintenance:

- Indicated for:
  - **Non-sporulating cultures** that cannot be preserved by other means
  - Species with **large or delicate spores** that lose viability under other conditions
  - **Genetic stocks** and mutant strains of research importance
  - **Dangerous pathogens** requiring secure, long-term containment
- Controlled-rate freezing (slow cooling at ~1°C per minute) minimizes ice crystal damage
- Cultures are stored in vapor phase or liquid phase of liquid nitrogen

## Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying)

Lyophilization combines rapid freezing with sublimation of water under vacuum:

- A **menstruum** (protective matrix) is used — typically **5–10% nonfat dry milk** or **bovine serum**
- Suitable for many yeasts and sporulating fungi
- Produces a dry powder sealed under vacuum in glass ampoules
- Ambient temperature storage is possible, reducing infrastructure costs

### Viability Assessment

Viability after lyophilization is categorized based on colony-forming units per ampoule:

| Category | Colonies per Ampoule | Expected Performance |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Poor** | 10–50 | Marginal survival; may require careful rehydration |
| **Moderate** | 50–100 | Acceptable for many routine purposes |
| **Good** | 100–1,000 | Excellent survival; preferred for valuable cultures |

## Shipping Cultures

Transport of living fungal cultures must follow strict containment protocols:

- **Triple containment** is the standard:
  1. **Primary container** — Culture tube or vial with absorbent material to cushion and contain any leakage
  2. **Secondary container** — Watertight, leak-proof bag or container
  3. **Outer container** — Sturdy, insulated packaging capable of withstanding transport stresses

### Regulatory Requirements

- **International shipments** are governed by **CFR Part 122.2** (U.S. Code of Federal Regulations)
- **USDA/APHIS PPQ Form 526** is required for importing fungal cultures into the United States
- Additional permits may be required by the destination country
- Shippers must comply with IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations for certain categories of organisms

## DNA Preservation from Specimens

Preserved specimens and cultures can yield DNA for molecular studies, but preservation method affects DNA quality:

- Some preservation methods maintain DNA satisfactorily for downstream applications
- **Chemical fixation** (e.g., formalin) alters and fragments DNA, often rendering it unsuitable for PCR and sequencing
- Air-dried and silica-gel-dried specimens generally preserve DNA well
- Cryopreserved cultures typically yield high-quality genomic DNA

Integration with [[dna-barcoding-fungal-identification]] requires careful attention to preservation method from the point of collection.

## Long-Term Best Practices

- **Liquid nitrogen storage** with **slow drying before freezing** provides the best combination of viability and genetic stability
- **Propagules exposed directly to liquid nitrogen do not survive** — vapor phase storage is essential
- Regular viability testing of preserved collections identifies failing cultures before they are lost
- Duplicate storage at geographically separate facilities provides insurance against catastrophic loss

Proper culture preservation supports all aspects of [[fungal-species-recognition-genetics]], [[biodiversity-fungal-culture-preservation]], and [[fungal-species-estimates]], ensuring that living reference material remains available for future study.
