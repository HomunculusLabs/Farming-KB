# Pasteurization Techniques

Pasteurization is a heat treatment process that reduces microbial contaminants
in bulk substrates without sterilizing them completely. Unlike sterilization,
which kills all micromushroom-cultivationation selectively eliminates
competitors while preserving beneficial thermophilic organisms that help
protect the substrate from subsequent contamination. This makes it the
preferred treatment for bulk substrates [[mushroom-cultivation]].

## Why Pasteurize Instead of Sterilize

Bulk substrates such as straw, wood chips, and compost are too large and
voluminous to practically sterilize. More importantly, complete sterilization
of bulk substrates is actually undesirable. Sterilized bulk substrate with no
microbial defenders is extremely vulnerable to contamination from airborne
spores, especially Trichoderma and other aggressive molds.

Pasteurization achieves the right balance: it kills most competitor organisms
while leaving beneficial thermophiles alive. These thermophilic bacteria and
actinomycetes continue to occupy the substrate, competing with potential
contaminants and giving the mushrmyceliumlium time to fully colonize before
invaders can establish.

## Temperature and Duration

The target temperature for effective pasteurization is 60–80°C (140–176°F) held
for 30–120 minutes. The most common protocol is 65–70°C (149–158°F) for 60–90
minutes. Within this range, most competitor fungi and mesophilic bacteria are
killed, while thermophilic organisms survive.

Temperatures above 82°C (180°F) begin killing beneficial thermophiles and may
caramelizing sugars in the substrate. Temperatures below 55°C (131°F) are
insufficient to kill key competitors. Precise temperature control is important
for achieving reliable results.

## Hot Water Pasteurization

The most straightforward method for straw-based substrates. The process:

1. Chop or shred straw to 2–5 cm (1–2 inch) lengths. Shorter pieces hydrate
   more evenly and pack more uniformly.
2. Submerge straw in water at 65–70°C for 60–90 minutes. Use a large vessel
   such as a barrel, stockpot, or cooler. A propane burner is the most common
   heat source for barrel setups.
3. Maintain temperature throughout. Insulated vessels help conserve heat and
   reduce fuel consumption.
4. Drain thoroughly after pasteurization. Straw should be at field capacity —
   squeeze a handful firmly and only a few drops of water should emerge.
5. Cool to below 30°C (86°F) before inoculation. Spread thinly or use fans to
   accelerate cooling.

This method works well for wheat straw, oat straw, and similar materials. It
is less suitable for substrates that break down or become waterlogged with
prolonged immersion, such as sawdust.

## Steam Pasteurization

Steam pasteurization uses injected steam rather than hot water immersion. It is
the standard method for commercial operations due to its efficiency and
scalability.

The substrate is loaded into a pasteurization chamber — often a modified
barrel, shipping container, or purpose-built room — and steam is injected until
the internal substrate temperature reaches the target range. Temperature probes
placed at multiple locations ensure uniform heat penetration.

Advantages over hot water pasteurization:

- No need to drain excess water afterward, simplifying handling.
- More energy-efficient at scale — steam transfers heat more effectively than
  hot water.
- Better for dense substrates like sawdust and wood chips that would become
  waterlogged in immersion.
- Easier to automate and monitor.

A common DIY steam pasteurization setup uses a 55-gallon drum with a steam
source (wallpaper steamer, electric kettle element, or propane burner)
connected at the bottom. The substrate sits on a raised rack above the water
line, and steam rises through the material.

## Lime Bath Pasteurization

A cold or warm pasteurization method that uses hydrated lime (calcium
hydroxide) rather than heat to create an inhospitable environment for
contaminants. Commonly used for straw substrates in tropical regions where
fuel for heating is expensive.

The process:

1. Prepare a bath of water with hydrated lime at a ratio of approximately 1
   cup lime per 50 gallons of water. The pH should reach 12–13.
2. Soak chopped straw in the lime bath for 12–24 hours. The high pH kills
   most competitor organisms.
3. Drain and optionally rinse the straw before inoculation. Some cultivators
   skip rinsing, noting that the high pH dissipates naturally.
4. Inoculate within 24 hours of draining.

This method is simple and requires no heat, but it is less reliable than
proper heat pasteurization and can leave residual alkalinity that slows
colcomposting It is best suited for warm-climate cultivation where its
simplicity outweighs the lower reliability.

## Compost Pasteurization

For compost-based substrates (pragarily Agaricus species), pasteurization
occurs as part of the composting process itself. The compost is brought
through two phases:

1. **mushroom cultivator phase**: Raw materials are mixed and undergo a natural
   thermophilic fermentation. Internal temperatures reach 70–80°C, pasteurizing
   the compost from within. This phase lasts 7–14 days with regular turning.
2. **Phase II pasteurization**: The compost is moved to a controlled
   environment where temperature is carefully managed. It is held at 57–60°C
   for 6–8 hours to kill pests and pathogens, then gradually cooled to
   allow beneficial microbial recolonization.

## Verification

Regardless of method, verify pasteurization effectiveness by checking:

- **Temperature uniformity**: Use multiple probes. Cold spots are the most
  common cause of contamination in pasteurized substrates.
- **Smell**: Properly pasteurized substrate should smell earthy and pleasant.
  Sour, ammonia, or rotten odors indicate insufficient pasteurization or
  anaerobic conditions.
- **Visual inspection**: No visible mold growth should be present at the time
  of inoculation.
