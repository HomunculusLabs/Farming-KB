# Geomycology — Fungi in Geological Processes

Geomycology is the study of the roles fungi play in fundamental geological
processes, including mineral transformations, metal cycling, rock weathering,
soil formation, and biogeochemical cycling. Despite fungi's enormous
importance in terrestrial ecosystems — where they can comprise the largest
pool of biomass in soil — their geological roles have historically been
neglected in favor of bacterial and plant-focused research.

## Why Fungi Matter in Geology

Fungi occupy a unique position at the interface of biology and geology:

- They are the primary decomposers of complex organic polymers (lignin,
  cellulose, chitin) in terrestrial ecosystems
- They form [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] and
  organic acids that chemically transform minerals
- They accumulate, immobilize, and transform metals and radionuclides
- They maintain soil structure through hyphal networks and exopolymer
  production

## The Geomycological Framework

Gadd and colleagues organize [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]] lower pH
- **Complexolysis**: Metal-chelating metabolites (oxalate, citrate,
  siderophores) pull metals from mineral lattices
- **Redoxolysis**: Redox reactions change metal oxidation states

These processes release trapped nutrients (phosphorus, potassium, calcium,
trace metals) from otherwise unavailable mineral forms.

### Mineral Formation (Biomineralization)

Fungi don't just dissolve minerals — they create them. Fungal-induced
mineral precipitation includes:
- **[[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]] precipitation
  through metabolic CO2 production
- **Iron and manganese oxides**: Oxidation products of fungal redox activity

### Metal Transformations

Fungi transform metals through:
- **Oxidation/reduction**: Changing metal oxidation states (Fe²⁺ ↔ Fe³⁺,
  Mn²⁺ ↔ Mn⁴⁺), altering solubility and bioavailability
- **Methylation**: Converting metals like mercury, arsenic, and tin into
  organometallic compounds with different mobility and toxicity
- **Volatilization**: Converting metals to volatile forms (e.g., selenium
  to dimethyl selenide), effectively removing them from the local environment
- **Accumulation**: Concentrating metals in biomass through biosorption
  to cell walls and intracellular uptake

### Aggregate Formation and Soil Structure

Fungal hyphae physically bind soil particles together, while extracellular
polymeric substances (EPS) act as biological glue. This aggregation is
essential for:
- Soil water-holding capacity
- Resistance to erosion
- Root penetration and aeration
- Carbon stabilization in soil

## Biogeochemical Cycling

Fungi participate in all major biogeochemical cycles:

### Carbon Cycle
- Decomposition of organic matter releases CO2
- Mycorrhizal fungi receive 10-20% of plant photosynthate as carbon
- Fungal biomass represents a significant soil carbon pool
- Calcium oxalate crystals serve as a long-term carbon sink

### Nitrogen Cycle
- Fungi decompose nitrogen-containing organic compounds (proteins, chitin)
- Some fungi fix atmospheric nitrogen (rare but documented)
- Mycorrhizal fungi enhance plant nitrogen uptake
- Fungal biomass stores nitrogen in chitin-rich cell walls

### Phosphorus Cycle
- Fungal oxalate solubilizes rock phosphate
- Mycorrhizal hyphae extend the phosphorus depletion zone far beyond
  root reach
- Fungal phosphatase enzymes mineralize organic phosphorus compounds

### Metal Cycles
- Fungi weather metal-bearing minerals, releasing elements into soil
- Mycorrhizal fungi accumulate metals and deliver them to host plants
- Fungal transformations alter metal mobility and bioavailability

## Fungi as Environmental Agents

### Bioremediation

Fungi's metal-accumulating and mineral-transforming abilities make them
valuable for bioremediation of contaminated sites. Applications include:

- **Mycofiltration**: Using fungal mycelium to capture metals from water
- **Mycorestoration**: Applying fungi to rehabilitate degraded soils
- **Biosorption**: Dead fungal biomass as metal-absorbing material for
  industrial waste treatment

### Biomining

Fungi can be used to extract metals from low-grade ores and waste materials
through bioleaching. While bacterial bioleaching is more established,
fungal contributions to metal solubilization are increasingly recognized.

### Conservation

Understanding fungal roles in stone weathering informs the conservation of
cultural heritage sites, monuments, and buildings affected by biodeterioration.

## Research Frontiers

Gadd and colleagues highlight several emerging areas in geomycology:

- **Molecular tools**: DNA-based characterization of lithic fungal
  communities, revealing far greater diversity than culture-based methods
- **Imaging advances**: Real-time visualization of nutrient dynamics in
  mycelial networks using fluorescent probes and confocal microscopy
