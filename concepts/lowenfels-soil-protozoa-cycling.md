# Soil Protozoa and Nutrient Cycling

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that play a critical role in
the soil food web as the primary consumers of bacteria. In "Teaming With
Microbes," Lowenfels and Lewis highlight protozoa as essential players in
nutrient cycling, particularly through their role in [[fungal-nitrogen-mineralization-pathways]].
Without protozoa, nutrients locked in bacterial biomass would remain
unavailable to plants for extended periods. Protozoa are the mechanism by
which the microbial loop returns nutrients to the plant-available pool.

## Types of Soil Protozoa

Soil protozoa are classified into three major groups based on their method of
locomotion, each occupying slightly different ecological niches:

**Flagellates:** The smallest and most numerous soil protozoa, flagellates
range from 5 to 20 micrometers in length. They move using one or more
whip-like flagella and are the dominant protozoan group in most soils.
Flagellates prefer water-filled soil pores and thrive in moist conditions.
They are the first protozoa to respond to increases in bacterial populations.

**Amoebae:** Larger than flagellates, amoebae range from 20 to 40
micrometers and move using temporary extensions of cytoplasm called
pseudopodia. Amoebae are further divided into naked amoebae and testate
amoebae, the latter enclosed in a protective shell. Their larger size allows
them to consume bigger bacteria and even small fungal hyphae. Amoebae can
move through thin water films on soil particles, accessing bacteria in pores
too small for larger predators.

**Ciliates:** The largest soil protozoa, ciliates range from 30 to 200
micrometers and are covered in hair-like cilia used for locomotion and
feeding. They require water-filled pores to move effectively and are
therefore most abundant in wet soils. Ciliates are voracious bacterial
consumers and are sensitive indicators of soil anaerobic conditions; their
presence in high numbers often suggests waterlogged or compacted soils.

This process, called the microbial loop or protozoan grazing pathway, is one
of the most efficient natural mechanisms for nitrogen mineralization. A
single protozoan can consume thousands of bacteria per day, and the collective
grazing activity of protozoan populations can mineralize significant
quantities of nitrogen. In healthy soils, protozoan grazing may supply 30 to
80 percent of plant-available nitrogen, far exceeding the contribution from
abiotic decomposition processes alone.

## Soil Moisture Effects on Protozoan Activity

Protozoa are highly sensitive to soil moisture conditions, and their activity
follows a pattern closely tied to water availability. In dry soils, most
protozoa encyst, forming dormant resting stages that can survive for months
or years. When soil moisture increases, cysts germinate rapidly and protozoan
populations can explode within hours to days. This rapid response means that
protozoan-mediated nitrogen mineralization spikes after rainfall or irrigation,
delivering a pulse of plant-available nitrogen precisely when plants are best
able to use it.

## Supporting Protozoan Populations

Practices that support bacterial populations naturally support protozoa.
Applying [[lowenfels-cover-crops-soil-biology]], creates favorable habitat.
Avoiding excessive tillage preserves the soil pore structure protozoa depend
on for movement and feeding. The key principle from Lowenfels and Lewis is
that protozoa thrive when the entire soil food web is intact and functioning.
