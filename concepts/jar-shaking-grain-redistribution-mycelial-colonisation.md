# Jar Shaking and Grain Redistribution for Mycelial Colonisation

Shaking colonised grain jars to redistribute mycelium-bearing kernels among
uncolonised grain is one of the simplest yet most impactful techniques in
small-scale [[brassinosteroids-in-fungal-cultivation]]. The practice dramatically accelerates full
substrate colonisation, reduces the window of vulnerability to contamination,
and produces more uniform spawn for subsequent cultivation steps including
casing and fruiting.

## Mechanism of Action

When a spore syringe, [[comparison-agar-vs-liquid-culture]], or agar inoculant is introduced to a jar
of sterilised grain, colonisation begins from discrete inoculation points. On
substrates such as grass seed ([[lolium-perenne]]), rye grain, or other cereal-based
media, the mycelium must spread from these initial points of contact throughout
the entire volume of grain. This process proceeds at the rate of mycelial
extension through the substrate, which is constrained by the need to colonise
each kernel sequentially from the original inoculation site.

Shaking the jar physically disperses colonised kernels throughout the substrate
mass. Each redistributed kernel becomes a new point of colonisation,
effectively multiplying the number of actively growing fronts. Rather than a
single mycelial front advancing through the grain, dozens or hundreds of fronts
expand simultaneously. The reduction in total colonisation time is dramatic,
often cutting the time to full colonisation by fifty percent or more.

The technique exploits the fact that [[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]] is resilient to physical
disruption. When a colonised kernel is separated from its neighbouring mycelial
network by shaking, the mycelium on that kernel does not die but instead
resumes growth from its existing hyphal tips upon contact with fresh, uncolonised
substrate. Each colonised kernel thus functions as a miniature inoculation point.

## Timing of the First Shake

The optimal timing for the first shake depends on several interacting factors:

**Species**: Fast-colonising species like [[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]] establish visible
mycelium within a few days of inoculation, while slower species may take a week
or more to develop sufficient colonisation to justify shaking. Tropical species
generally colonise faster at warm temperatures than temperate species.

**[[boil-a-bag-long-needle-inoculation-method-jarrold]]**: Spore syringes, which introduce thousands of individual
spores that must germinate and mate before colonisation proceeds, typically
require longer colonisation before shaking than liquid culture or agar
inoculation, which deposit already-mated mycelium directly onto the grain. The
genetic compatibility window required for [[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]] adds days or weeks
to the initial phase.

**Substrate**: Grass seed substrates, with their relatively small kernel size
and high surface area to volume ratio, tend to colonise faster than whole grain
substrates like rye or wheat berries. The increased number of individual kernels
per unit volume means more potential inoculation points per shake.

The general principle is to shake when mycelium has visibly colonised a small
but clear proportion of the grain, typically when white [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] is
apparent at multiple inoculation points. Shaking too early, before colonisation
is well established, risks distributing uncolonised kernels without
meaningfully increasing the number of growth fronts.

## Shaking Technique

Proper shaking technique maximises redistribution while minimising the risk of
contamination or jar damage:

1. Ensure the jar lid is securely fastened but not airtight. Lids on grain
   jars are typically fitted with a filter or gas exchange mechanism that
   allows respiration while excluding contaminants.

2. Strike the jar firmly against the palm of the hand or a padded surface to
   break up colonised grain clumps. The goal is to separate individual kernels,
   not to pulverise them.

3. Shake vigorously in all directions, rotating the jar to ensure thorough
   mixing. The goal is to distribute colonised kernels as evenly as possible
   throughout the substrate.

4. After shaking, the mycelium may appear to have "disappeared" as colonised
   kernels are buried among uncolonised grain. This is normal; visible growth
   will resume from the redistributed kernels within a day or two.
