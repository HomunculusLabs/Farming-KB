# Jar Shaking and Grain Redistribution for Mycelial Colonisation

Shaking colonised grain jars to redistribute mycelium-bearing kernels among
uncolonised grain is one of the simplest yet most impactful techniques in
small-scale fungal cultivation. The practice dramatically accelerates full
substrate colonisation, reduces the window of vulnerability to contamination,
and produces more uniform spawn for subsequent cultivation steps including
casing and fruiting.

## Mechanism of Action

When a spore syringe, liquid culture, or agar inoculant is introduced to a jar
of sterilised grain, colonisation begins from discrete inoculation points. On
substrates such as grass seed (Lolium perenne), rye grain, or other cereal-based
media, the mycelium must spread from these initial points of contact throughout
the entire volume of grain. This process proceeds at the rate of mycelial
extension through the substrate, which is constrained by the need to colonise
each kernel sequentially from the original inoculation site.

Shaking the jar physically disperses colonised kernels throughout the substrate
mass. Each redistributed kernel becomes a new point of colonisation,
effectively multiplying the number of actively growing fronts. Rather than a
single mycelial front advancing through the grain, dozens or hundreds of fronts
expand simultaneously. The reduction in total colonisation time is dramatic,
often cutting the time to full colonisation by fifty percent or more.

The technique exploits the fact that fungal mycelium is resilient to physical
disruption. When a colonised kernel is separated from its neighbouring mycelial
network by shaking, the mycelium on that kernel does not die but instead
resumes growth from its existing hyphal tips upon contact with fresh, uncolonised
substrate. Each colonised kernel thus functions as a miniature inoculation point.

## Timing of the First Shake

The optimal timing for the first shake depends on several interacting factors:

**Species**: Fast-colonising species like Psilocybe cubensis establish visible
mycelium within a few days of inoculation, while slower species may take a week
or more to develop sufficient colonisation to justify shaking. Tropical species
generally colonise faster at warm temperatures than temperate species.

**Inoculation method**: Spore syringes, which introduce thousands of individual
spores that must germinate and mate before colonisation proceeds, typically
require longer colonisation before shaking than liquid culture or agar
inoculation, which deposit already-mated mycelium directly onto the grain. The
genetic compatibility window required for spore germination adds days or weeks
to the initial phase.

**Substrate**: Grass seed substrates, with their relatively small kernel size
and high surface area to volume ratio, tend to colonise faster than whole grain
substrates like rye or wheat berries. The increased number of individual kernels
per unit volume means more potential inoculation points per shake.

The general principle is to shake when mycelium has visibly colonised a small
but clear proportion of the grain, typically when white mycelial growth is
apparent at multiple inoculation points. Shaking too early, before colonisation
is well established, risks distributing uncolonised kernels without
meaningfully increasing the number of growth fronts.

## Shaking Technique

Proper shaking technique maximises redistribution while minimising the risk of
contamination or jar damage:

1. Ensure the jar lid is securely fastened but not airtight. Lids on grain
   jars are typically fitted with a filter or gas exchange mechanism that
   allows respiration while excluding contaminants.

2. Strike the jar firmly against the palm of the hand or a padded surface to
   break up colonised grain clumps. The goal is to separate individual kernels,
   not to pulverise them.

3. Shake vigorously in all directions, rotating the jar to ensure thorough
   mixing. The goal is to distribute colonised kernels as evenly as possible
   throughout the substrate.

4. After shaking, the mycelium may appear to have "disappeared" as colonised
   kernels are buried among uncolonised grain. This is normal; visible growth
   will resume from the redistributed kernels within a day or two.

## Colonisation Timeline

On grass seed substrates inoculated with Psilocybe species and maintained at
approximately 20 degrees Celsius, full colonisation can be achieved in five to
twenty days depending on species, strain, temperature, and inoculation method.
With timely shaking, the faster end of this range is typical. Without shaking,
colonisation time is substantially extended, particularly in larger jars where
the distance between inoculation points and the far reaches of the substrate is
greatest.

## Signs of Colonisation Problems

Several visual indicators can signal problems during the colonisation phase:

**Yellowish metabolite excretion**: If jars are incubated too long or at too
high a temperature, the mycelium will excrete yellowish metabolites into the
substrate. This fluid is a stress response and indicates that the mycelium is
struggling, often due to elevated temperatures or depleted substrate. Jars
showing metabolite production should be cased or fruited as soon as possible to
prevent further degradation.

**Stalled colonisation**: If growth stops or dramatically slows before the
substrate is fully colonised, contamination is a likely cause. Bacterial
contamination may produce a sour or putrid smell, while mould contamination
typically produces visible growth of a colour other than white.

**Excessive moisture**: Grass seed substrates are particularly sensitive to
water content. Too much water produces a slimy, anaerobic clump that cannot be
shaken and supports bacterial growth. Too little water produces dry substrate
that supports little or no mycelial growth. The correct moisture content allows
individual kernels to be separated by shaking while remaining plump and
hydrated. Different varieties of grass seed and even batches of the same variety
can differ greatly in their ability to absorb water.

## Application to Sclerotia Production

The jar shaking technique is also relevant to sclerotia production in species
such as Psilocybe mexicana and Psilocybe tampanensis. These species can produce
sclerotia while still colonising grain in the jar, without the need for casing
or fruiting. Sclerotia will continue to enlarge until approximately four months
after inoculation. While shaking during early colonisation is beneficial for
establishing complete substrate colonisation, excessive disturbance of
developing sclerotia should be avoided once they have begun to form.

## Contamination Prevention During Shaking

Shaking necessarily introduces air movement within the jar and temporarily
disrupts the mycelial network. To minimise contamination risk during and after
shaking:

- Shake in a clean environment, ideally in front of a laminar flow hood or
  inside a still air box constructed from a simple cardboard box.
- Wash hands thoroughly or wear gloves before handling jars.
- Ensure jar filters are intact and properly seated before shaking.
- Minimise the time that jars are open or unfiltered.
- After shaking, return jars to a clean, draft-free incubation location
  promptly.

The speed advantage conferred by proper shaking technique is itself a
contamination prevention measure: faster colonisation means less time during
which the substrate is vulnerable to competing organisms that might be
introduced during inoculation or through filter breaches.
