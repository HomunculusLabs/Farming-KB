Phyllotaxy is the fixed law governing the arrangement
of leaves and branches on a plant. Every species follows
a specific pattern of divergence and spacing that ensures
no two leaves or branches overlap, and all receive equal
ventilation and sunlight. [[fukuoka-mandarin-orange-citrus]], orange, and cherry trees is
always located directly above the first leaf, and the
eleventh leaf is always directly above the sixth. When the
distance along the branch between two consecutive buds is
one inch, the distance from one leaf to the next leaf
directly above it is always five inches. Two leaves will
not overlap, and two branches will not emerge, within any
five-inch length along the branch.

This regularity is not approximate. It is absolute and
unerring. The direction, angle, and divergence of every
shoot and branch follows this fixed law. Lower and upper
branches maintain the same distance over their entire
length, never overlapping one another.

## What Phyllotaxy Means for Natural Form

The consequence of phyllotaxy is that the branches and
leaves of natural plants all receive equal ventilation
and sunlight. Not a single leaf is wasted, not a single
branch is lacking. This is what Fukuoka calls "the true
form of a plant," a form of perfect efficiency that arises
without any human intervention.

This principle is most clearly observed in the mountain
pine. The central trunk rises straight and true, putting
out branches at equal vertical spacings in a radial
arrangement. One can clearly make out the chronology of
branch emergence. The spacing and angle of the branches
are regular and orderly. Never does one branch grow too
long or cross with another branch. The same law operates

Had the persimmon, the peach, and the citrus tree been
left to grow of their own accord, it would never have
been necessary to cut the trunk with a saw or lop off
branches to control erratic growth. Just as no person is
so foolish as to strike his left hand with his right, no
persimmon or chestnut tree has branches on the right that
compete with those on the left and eventually have to be
cut back because they grow too long. A branch on the east
side of the tree does not wander over to the south side,
cutting off light. The whole premise of pruning as a
necessary practice rests on a misunderstanding of what
the natural form actually is.
