# Saddle Dams and High Water Storages

Saddle dams and high water storages are foundational elements of [[permaculture]] [[water-management]] strategy. They represent the principle of capturing and storing water at the highest possible point in the landscape, where it can be distributed by gravity to serve multiple functions throughout the system below.

## What Are Saddle Dams?

A saddle dam is built on a **ridge saddle** — a low point along a ridgeline between two higher points. This position makes saddle dams the **highest water storages possible** on a property. By siting dams at these elevated positions, permaculture designers exploit gravity to deliver water wherever it is needed downstream without any pumping energy.

Saddle dams are a direct application of the permaculture principle: **store water at the highest point you can, and let gravity do the work.** This simple positioning strategy eliminates the need for mechanical pumping and provides reliable water pressure for domestic use.

## Advantages of High-Position Water Storage

Storing water high in the landscape provides multiple compounding benefits:

- **Gravity-fed distribution:** Water flows to all lower zones without energy input.
- **Hydraulic head for electricity generation:** The elevation difference (head) between the dam and the point of use can drive micro-hydro turbines, producing clean electricity.
- **Fire protection:** High storages can feed fire protection sprinkler systems. During [[wildfire]] events, a dam on the ridge can supply sprinklers that wet building roofs and surrounding vegetation, potentially saving structures.
- **Emergency reserve:** Even during extended dry periods, high dams retain water that can be gravity-fed to critical uses.
- **Wildlife habitat:** Elevated dams create edge habitat and drinking water sources for wildlife across ridge and slope ecosystems.

## Water Quality Cascade

A critical design principle in [[permaculture-water-systems]] is the **water quality cascade**. The cleanest water collected — typically rainfall or clean runoff from forested ridge tops — is stored at the **highest point** in the landscape. As water descends through the system, it may pick up sediments, nutrients, or contaminants from lower-elevation uses.

The design sequence follows:

1. **Ridge-top storage (saddle dams):** Holds the purest water for the most sensitive uses.
2. **Mid-slope storage:** Receives water that may have passed through or been used by higher elements.
3. **Valley floor storage:** Contains the most nutrient-rich or sediment-laden water, suitable only for irrigation or stock.

This cascading approach ensures that water quality is matched to use. You do not waste pristine high-elevation water on low-value purposes, and you do not expose domestic water supplies to contamination from agricultural runoff.

## Contour Dams

Contour dams are **shallow dams with very large surface areas**, constructed along contour lines on gently sloping land. They are among the cheapest and easiest dams to build because:

- They require minimal earthworks — only shallow embankments.
- They flood flat or gently sloping land, capturing large volumes of water per dollar spent.
- They are well-suited to broadacre and farm-scale applications.

Mollison's key principle for dam economy: **"The flatter the floor that you flood, the more water you get for dollars spent."** A shallow dam across a broad flat area stores vastly more water than a deep, narrow dam in a steep valley for the same construction cost.

### Water Table Recharge

Contour dams serve a critical hydrological function beyond surface storage. Their large surface area allows significant volumes of water to **percolate into the soil and recharge the groundwater table**. This recharge effect:

- Maintains baseflows in streams and springs downslope.
- Keeps vegetation productive during dry periods by maintaining accessible soil moisture.
- Reduces the severity of drought across the broader landscape.

### Flood Moderation

By capturing runoff across broad contour lines, these dams act as distributed flood control. Rather than allowing water to concentrate rapidly into valley streams during heavy rain, contour dams spread the capture across the slope, slowing runoff velocity and reducing peak flows in downstream waterways. This mitigates flooding and prevents erosion in valleys below.

## Spillway Design

Every dam must have a properly designed **spillway** — a safe overflow path for water when the dam reaches full capacity. Key design principles:

- **Contoured away from the dam wall:** The spillway must direct overflow water away from the dam embankment itself. If water overflows directly against or alongside the dam wall, it can erode and eventually breach the structure.
- **Planted with shrubs and vegetation:** Spillways should be stabilized with deep-rooted shrubs and ground covers. The vegetation slows water flow, dissipates energy, prevents erosion of the spillway channel, and filters sediment from overflow water.
- **Sized for extreme events:** Spillways must handle the maximum expected rainfall event, not just average conditions. Undersized spillways are the most common cause of dam failure.

## Valley Dam Site Selection

Valley dams — dams built across valleys rather than on saddles — require careful site selection. Key considerations include:

- **Narrow valley with broad flat area behind:** This geometry minimizes the wall length while maximizing storage volume.
- **Impervious subsoil:** Clay or rock foundations reduce seepage losses.
- **Suitable catchment area:** The upstream catchment must generate sufficient runoff to fill the dam reliably.
- **Stable banks and slopes:** The valley sides must be geologically stable to avoid landslides into the dam.

Valley dams typically sit lower in the landscape than saddle dams and serve different purposes — larger volume storage for irrigation, stock, and broad landscape hydration rather than gravity-fed domestic supply.

## Setting Water Priorities

Mollison establishes a clear hierarchy for water allocation, ensuring that the most critical needs are met first:

1. **Domestic water first** — Drinking, cooking, bathing, and household use. This water must come from the cleanest, most reliable source (highest storage).
2. **Garden water second** — Intensive food production close to the home. Garden water comes from the next tier of storage.
3. **Extensive agriculture third** — Broadacre crops, pasture, and orchards. These can tolerate lower water quality and less reliable supply.
4. **Landscape and ecosystem hydration** — The remaining water supports the broader landscape, wildlife, and hydrological health.

This priority cascade mirrors the physical cascade of water from ridge to valley and ensures that limited water resources are allocated where they create the greatest benefit.

## Large-Scale Water Storage

On a grand scale, Mollison advocates for putting **13–15% of the landscape under water**. This proportion represents the amount of surface water storage needed to fully hydrate a landscape, recharge groundwater, moderate climate (through increased evaporation and humidity), support diverse ecosystems, and buffer against drought and flood. This is not wasteful — it is a design target for long-term landscape resilience.

## Key Principles Summary

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **Highest storage first** | Capture and hold water at the highest elevation possible |
| **Gravity-fed distribution** | Let elevation do the work of moving water |
| **Quality cascade** | Cleanest water at the top; allow degradation downward |
| **Flat floor economy** | Flood the flattest land for maximum storage per dollar |
| **Contour dams for recharge** | Shallow, broad dams recharge groundwater and moderate floods |
| **Spillway safety** | Always contour spillways away from the dam wall and vegetate them |
| **Domestic water priority** | Allocate the best water to the most critical human uses first |
| **13–15% under water** | Target this proportion for full landscape hydration and resilience |
