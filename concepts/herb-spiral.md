# Herb Spiral

The herb spiral is a classic [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] element that creates a vertical, spiral-shaped garden bed for growing herbs and culinary plants. By building upward in a spiral, it creates multiple microclimates within a small footprint — from a dry, sunny, well-drained top to a moist, shaded, water-retentive bottom. A single herb spiral can replace a 20-30 foot row of conventional garden bed while providing ideal growing conditions for a diverse range of herbs.

## Design Principles

The herb spiral embodies several permaculture principles:

- **Multiple functions**: Produces herbs, creates beauty, provides habitat, demonstrates microclimates
- **[[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]]**: Vertical design stacks multiple growing zones in the space of one
- **Edge effect**: The spiral shape maximizes the edge between different microclimates, creating the most productive zone
- **Small-scale intensive**: Maximum production in minimum space, ideal for Zone 1 placement
- **[[mollison-spiral-patterns-in-nature-and-design]]**: The spiral is a universal natural pattern found in shells, flowers, galaxies, and DNA

## Microclimate Zones

A well-built herb spiral creates distinct growing conditions from top to bottom and from the north-facing side to the south-facing side (in the Northern Hemisphere):

### Top of Spiral (Zone 1 — Hot and Dry)

- **Conditions**: Full sun exposure, best drainage, soil dries fastest, warmest position
- **Ideal plants**: Rosemary, thyme, oregano, lavender, sage — [[query-best-mediterranean-herbs-for-beginner-herb-garden]] that love heat and excellent drainage
- **Soil**: Sandy, well-draining, slightly alkaline

### Upper Middle (Zone 2 — Warm and Moderate)

- **Conditions**: Good sun, moderate drainage, warm but not as extreme as the top
- **Ideal plants**: Tarragon, marjoram, cilantro (in cooler seasons), chives, savory
- **Soil**: Loamy with moderate drainage

### Lower Middle (Zone 3 — Moderate to Moist)

- **Conditions**: Partial shade from upper coils, retains more moisture, moderate temperature
- **Ideal plants**: Basil, parsley, dill, mint (if contained), lemon balm
- **Soil**: Rich, moisture-retentive loam

### Bottom of Spiral (Zone 4 — Cool and Moist)

- **Conditions**: Most shade, collects water runoff from above, stays coolest, highest moisture
- **Ideal plants**: Mint, watercress, mizuna, Vietnamese coriander, shiso — moisture-loving herbs
- **Soil**: Rich, moisture-retentive, can be slightly acidic

### North Side (Shade)

- **Conditions**: Shaded by the spiral mass itself, cooler, slower to dry
- **Ideal plants**: Shade-tolerant herbs like sweet woodruff, violet, parsley, mint

### South Side (Sun)

- **Conditions**: Maximum sun exposure, warmer, dries faster
- **Ideal plants**: Heat-loving herbs: rosemary, thyme, oregano, basil

## Construction

### Materials

- **Structural material**: Stone (best thermal mass), brick, concrete block, or untreated wood
- **Fill**: Topsoil, compost, sand, and gravel in varying ratios depending on zone
- **Cardboard**: For weed suppression at the base
- **Water source**: A small pond or basin at the bottom is traditional but optional

### Dimensions

- **Diameter**: 5-6 feet across (fits in a small yard or courtyard)
- **Height**: 3-4 feet at the center/top
- **Path width**: 18-24 inches between coils for easy access
- **Spiral turns**: Typically 1.5 to 2.5 complete turns from bottom to top

### Step-by-Step Construction

1. **Choose the site**: Zone 1 location near the kitchen door. Full sun to partial shade. Level ground is best.

2. **Mark the footprint**: Use a stake at the center and a 2.5-3 foot string to draw a circle. Mark the spiral path on the ground with chalk or sand.

3. **Lay cardboard**: Cover the entire footprint with overlapping cardboard to suppress weeds. Water thoroughly.

4. **Build the first (bottom) coil**: Place your stone or brick wall along the outside of the spiral path, one layer high. Fill the interior with gravel or rubble for drainage. Add a layer of soil mixed with compost on top.

5. **Build upward**: Continue adding coils, each one slightly smaller and higher than the last. The wall should angle slightly inward (batter) for stability. Each coil rises about 6-8 inches above the previous one.

6. **Vary the fill**: As you build upward, modify the soil mix:
   - Bottom: moisture-retentive soil with extra compost
   - Middle: standard loamy garden soil
   - Top: sandy, well-draining soil with extra grit or perlite

7. **Finish the top**: Cap the center with a flat stone or create a small planting pocket at the peak.

8. **Plant immediately**: Fill the spiral with herbs according to the microclimate zones. Water thoroughly after planting.

9. **Mulch**: Apply a 1-2 inch layer of appropriate mulch for each zone. See [[mulching-guide]] for mulch options.

### Optional Pond

The traditional design includes a small pond or basin at the base of the spiral:
- Provides water for irrigation (dip and pour)
- Creates [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects and amphibians
- Enhances the moisture zone for water-loving herbs
- Adds aesthetic and ecological value
- Can integrate with [[pond-and-aquaculture]] principles

## Planting Guide

### Mediterranean Herbs (Top, Sunny, Dry)
- Rosemary, thyme, oregano, lavender, sage, bay laurel

### Culinary Herbs (Middle, Moderate)
- Basil, parsley, cilantro, dill, chives, tarragon, marjoram

### Moisture-Loving Herbs (Bottom, Shady, Wet)
- Mint (always contain — it will take over), watercress, lemon balm, Vietnamese coriander

### Companion Flowers
- Nasturtiums (edible flowers, pest deterrent), calendula (medicinal, attracts pollinators), borage (edible flowers, bee forage), chamomile

### Integration with Permaculture

The herb spiral integrates with broader permaculture design:
- Place near the kitchen for daily harvest access (Zone 1 principle from [[permaculture-zones-in-practice]])
- Use herbs from the spiral in [[food-fermentation-guide]] recipes and [[herbal-medicine]] preparations
- Include insectary flowers to support [[pollinator-habitat]]
- Connect to [[irrigation-systems]] for automated watering during dry periods
- Use culinary herbs in [[food-preservation-canning]] and cooking
- Stone walls provide thermal mass that moderates temperature extremes — a principle from [[edge-effect-and-microclimate]]

## Advantages Over Flat Beds

- **Space efficiency**: A 6-foot diameter spiral provides the growing area of a 20-30 foot row
- **Reduced bending**: Vertical design brings plants to a comfortable working height
- **Microclimate diversity**: Grow herbs with conflicting needs (dry/sunny vs. moist/shady) in one structure
- **Drainage control**: Create the exact drainage each herb prefers by zone
- **Aesthetic appeal**: Spirals are visually striking and serve as landscape focal points
- **Thermal mass**: Stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night, moderating temperature
- **[[cannabis-pest-management-guide]]**: The diversity of plants and microclimates disrupts pest cycles
