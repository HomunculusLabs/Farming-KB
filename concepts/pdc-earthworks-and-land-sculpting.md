## The Scale of Earthworks

Williams establishes a clear hierarchy of design permanence and impact:

### Relative Permanence Scale
- **Most permanent**: Earthworks and land sculpting (changing the shape of the land itself)
- **Intermediate**: Structures, fences, orchards, food forests
- **Least permanent**: Annual gardens, annual crops, mulches

Because earthworks are the most permanent element in any design, they must be planned with extreme care. Mistakes in earthworks are difficult and expensive to correct.

### The Foundation Principle
Earthworks should be planned FIRST in the design process because:
1. They are the most permanent design element
2. They establish the water and nutrient flow patterns for everything else
3. Other design elements (plantings, structures, access) are placed in response to the earthwork-created landscape
4. They create the foundational conditions (soil moisture, microclimate) that determine what can grow

## Types of Earthworks

### Swales

Swales are one of the most important earthwork tools in permaculture. Williams describes them in detail:

**Definition**: A swale is a level trench dug along the contour of a slope. It is NOT a waterway -- water that enters a swale stays in the swale and is absorbed into the soil. Water that overflows the swale must be managed by spillways.

**Function**: Swales capture runoff water and allow it to infiltrate into the soil profile rather than running off the site. This:
- Reduces erosion on slopes
- Recharges groundwater
- Creates a moist planting zone downslope of the swale berm
- Reduces the need for irrigation
- Builds soil over time through organic matter accumulation in the moist zone

**[[keyline-design]]

Williams references Keyline design as a foundational earthworks approach for broadacre landscape management:

**Origin**: Developed by P.A. Yeomans in Australia in the 1950s, Keyline is a system for the planned development of agricultural land that maximizes water absorption and soil development.

**Core principle**: Identify the "keypoint" (the point in a valley where the slope changes from convex to concave) and design contour-based cultivation and water management systems that spread water from valleys to ridges. This reverses the natural tendency of water to concentrate in valleys and erode them.

**Application**: Keyline cultivation creates a pattern of plowing or subsoiling parallel to the keyline contour, gradually moving water from wet valleys to dry ridges. Over time, this creates more uniform soil moisture and fertility across the landscape.

### Soil as the Foundation

The single most important thing you can do for a design is to create and maintain a healthy soil. Earthworks are one of the primary tools for achieving this because they:
- Manage water infiltration, which is critical for soil biology
- Reduce erosion, preserving topsoil
- Create conditions for organic matter accumulation
- Enable the establishment of deep-rooted plants that build soil structure

### The [[soil-food-web]]

Earthworks create the physical conditions for soil biology to thrive. Williams emphasizes that healthy soil is alive -- it contains billions of microorganisms per teaspoon. Earthworks that manage water and reduce compaction support this biological community.

## Tools and Equipment

### Manual Earthworks
- Shovels, spades, mattocks for small-scale work
- A-frame levels and bunyip levels for contour finding
- Wheelbarrows for material movement
- Broadforks for subsoiling without inversion

### Mechanical Earthworks
- Excavators for large swales and ponds
- Tractors with implements for keyline cultivation and terracing
- Bulldozers for major land reshaping (use with caution -- they can compact soil severely)
- Laser levels and GPS-guided equipment for precision work

### The Designer's Role

Williams emphasizes that the designer must understand earthworks principles even when hiring equipment operators. The designer creates the plan; the operator executes it. Without a proper design, heavy equipment can cause more damage than benefit.
