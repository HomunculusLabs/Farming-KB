# Growing Okra

Okra is a heat-loving crop that produces best in hot, humid conditions. The plants can get quite large and will continue bearing as long as they can continue growing roots and accessing moisture. It's a perfect crop for periodic fertigation and an excellent choice for southern gardens.

## Climate Requirements

- Needs heat — sow only when soil has warmed to at least 70°F (21°C)
- Prefers humid conditions
- Does not tolerate frost or cold soil

## Soil and Fertility

Okra makes a deeply penetrating taproot but also develops surface feeder roots. This dual root system allows it to survive drought, but to keep bearing heavily it needs more than minimum moisture.

### Fertilizer Strategy

- Okra does best in moderately fertile soil
- Too much nitrogen makes overly leafy plants with fewer pods
- A bit of extra phosphorus helps pod set
- An aggressive root system can find nutrients in the subsoil
- **Perfect crop for periodic fertigation** — apply liquid fertilizer at intervals through the growing season

## Seed Germination

Okra seeds are difficult to germinate, so it's wise to:
1. Sow 4 seeds about 3/4 inch (2 cm) deep at every planting point
2. Plant only after soil has warmed to at least 70°F (21°C)
3. Thin progressively to a single plant as competition develops

## Planting and Spacing

- Sow 4 seeds per station, thin to 1
- Spacing: 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) in rows 3-4 feet (90-120 cm) apart
- Give each plant enough room for its extensive root system

## Crop Rotation

Follow the crop on a patch that hasn't seen it for many years. Okra is subject to root-knot nematodes, so do not follow it after other nematode-susceptible crops. The best crop to precede okra is corn, which discourages these insects.

## Pest Management

- When plants are small, spraying [[rotenone]]/[[pyrethrum]] or Bt (for hoppers) may get them past the danger point
- Once established, okra is fairly pest-resistant
- Root-knot nematodes are the main soil-borne concern

## Harvest

### Timing Is Critical

Initially the pods form rapidly. While enlarging fast, they are tender. After the initial growth spurt slows, they become woody.

**Important**: Size does not indicate the state of the pod — if the plant is growing lustily, pods over 5 inches (13 cm) long may still be tender.

### Harvesting Method

- Pick pods before they start getting crunchy
- Best done by cutting them off with a sharp knife
- Some people are sensitive to the plant's tiny spines — wear gloves
- **Must be done almost daily**

### Storage Warning

- Chilled below 45°F (7°C), pods rapidly deteriorate
- If not cooled, they rapidly become woody
- Best to eat them promptly

### Eating Quality

Okra is inevitably overcooked. Try eating a raw pod in the garden the instant it is picked — in a good okra patch, hardly a pod ever makes it past the harvester and into the kitchen.

## Saving Seed

- If you have a large patch (over 25 plants), save seeds
- Okra is bee-pollinated — pure variety requires isolation of over 1/2 mile (800 m)
- On every plant with mostly desirable characteristics, allow one unharvested pod to mature and dry out
- Do not let more than one pod per plant form seed unless you are growing a seed crop — unharvested pods greatly reduce formation of new pods
- Seed develops rapidly
