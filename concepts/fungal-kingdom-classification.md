# Fungal Kingdom Classification and Major Phyla

## Overview

Fungi (Kingdom Fungi) and fungus-like groups encompass an astounding breadth of taxa, morphologies, ecologies, and life history strategies. Only about 74,000 to 120,000 fungal species have been described, but estimates suggest 1.5 million or more species exist — meaning fewer than 5% of extant fungi are known to science. Taxa in three kingdoms comprising 11 phyla are treated in fungal biodiversity surveys, reflecting the phylogenetic complexity of the group.

## Phylogenetic Context

Kingdom Fungi is a monophyletic group well supported as "true fungi," a kingdom-level taxon. Molecular phylogenetic analyses indicate that members of Kingdom Fungi are most closely related to animals, not plants, possibly through a choanoflagellate-like ancestor. Microsporidia are a sister group to Fungi, and together with Metazoa (animals) form a monophyletic grouping. Fewer than 800 fungi have been included together in any single phylogenetic reconstruction — less than 1% of described species — meaning our understanding of fungal phylogeny remains incomplete.

Historically the monophyletic Fungi were classified in four phyla: Chytridiomycota, Zygomycota, Basidiomycota, and Ascomycota. Current molecular studies have shown that this simple classification does not accurately represent phylogeny — Chytridiomycota and Zygomycota in particular do not emerge as monophyletic groups, requiring fundamental rethinking of fungal classification.

## Major Phyla of True Fungi

### Chytridiomycota (Zoosporic Fungi)

Defined traditionally by the presence of a single posteriorly inserted smooth flagellum, chytrid groups are basal in phylogenetic trees, consistent with a flagellated ancestor. The Chytridiomycota as historically defined (about 800 species) is not monophyletic. The phylum includes Blastocladiales, Monoblepharidales, and core Chytridiomycota (Chytridiales, Neocallimastigales, Spizellomycetales). A resurgence of interest in chytrid diversity has followed the discovery that chytrids may be an important factor in global amphibian population declines. Chytrids are distributed widely in soils from the Arctic to the tropics, with species diversity increasing toward equatorial regions. See [[chytrid-fungi-guide]] for further detail.

### Zygomycota

A group of more than 1,000 species defined by the presence of a meiospore (zygospore) and absence of a flagellum. As currently defined, zygomycetes are polyphyletic — they do not form a natural evolutionary group. Major orders include Mucorales, Entomophthorales, and Trichomycetes. Some genera like Basidiobolus have uncertain phylogenetic placement. The coenocytic mycelium and formation of zygospores through sexual reproduction characterize this diverse group. See zygomycete phylum guide for expanded treatment.

### Ascomycota

The largest phylum of fungi, containing the majority of described species. Classes include Archiascomycetes, Saccharomycetes (yeasts), and Euascomycetes (filamentous ascomycetes). Members range from single-celled yeasts (Saccharomyces) to complex cup fungi (Peziza) and truffles (Tuber). Many economically important fungi (Penicillium, Aspergillus, Fusarium) and plant pathogens belong to this phylum. Lichenized Ascomycota represent approximately one-fifth of all described species in the phylum. See [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] for detailed coverage.

### Basidiomycota

Includes the familiar mushrooms, boletes, puffballs, and bracket fungi. Classes include Ustilaginiomycetes (smuts), Urediniomycetes (rusts), and Hymenomycetes (typical mushroom-forming fungi). Basidiomycota form a monophyletic grouping with Ascomycota as "crown" or terrestrial fungi. Approximately 3,500+ species of ectomycorrhizal fungi belong to this phylum. Thermophily is notably absent from Basidiomycota, contrasting with its strong development in Ascomycota. See [[basidiomycete-phylum-guide]] for further detail.

### Glomales (Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi)

Formerly placed in Zygomycota, molecular evidence now supports Glomales as a distinct ancient lineage. These are the arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, critical for plant nutrient uptake worldwide. About 163 species in 7 genera have been described, divided into two suborders (Glomineae and Gigasporineae). They are obligate symbionts that cannot be cultured apart from plant hosts and have co-evolved with plants over approximately 400 million years.

## Detailed Ascomycota Classification

The classification of Euascomycetes is currently in flux. Key clades include Sordariales, Xylariales, Hypocreales, Diaporthales, and Pyrenomycetes. The Pyrenomycetes clade encompasses numerous perithecial fungi. Lichenization occurred early in Ascomycota evolution, and lichenized forms are found across multiple clades. Saccharomycetes (yeasts) form a separate well-supported clade distinct from filamentous ascomycetes. Approximately 23,000+ described species of non-lichenized Ascomycota are recognized. Archiascomycetes includes Taphrinales (about 100 species) and represents a basal lineage within the phylum.

## Detailed Basidiomycota Classification

Within Basidiomycota, three major classes are recognized:

- **Ustilaginiomycetes** (smuts): Plant pathogens producing teliospores
- **Urediniomycetes** (rusts): Obligate plant pathogens with complex life cycles; includes Septobasidiales (parasitic on scale insects) and a mix of yeast-like and filamentous forms
- **Hymenomycetes** (mushroom-forming): The most conspicuous class, including Tremellales, Auriculariales, and typical mushroom-forming orders (Agaricales, Boletales, Russulales). The cantharelloid and gomphoid-phalloid clades are also recognized within this class

Approximately 3,500+ ectomycorrhizal species belong to Basidiomycota. Basidiomycetous yeasts are polyphyletic, having evolved from multiple filamentous ancestors. See [[fungal-phylogeny-systematics]] and [[ectomycorrhizal-ecology]] for further detail.

## Kingdom Straminipila

Several groups traditionally studied by mycologists are NOT true fungi but are historically important in mycology. Kingdom Straminipila includes:

- **Oomycota**: Water molds including *Phytophthora* species; *Phytophthora infestans* caused the Irish potato famine. Oomycetes are among the most destructive plant pathogens known.
- **Hyphochytriomycota**: Small group of aquatic organisms
- **Labyrinthulomycota**: Including thraustochytrids, marine organisms of ecological and biotechnological interest

These groups are characterized by heterokont flagellation (two unequal flagella, one with tripartite tubular hairs). See [[oomycetes-water-molds]] for expanded treatment.

## Slime Molds

Slime molds are a polyphyletic assembly of organisms that are not true fungi but are included in fungal biodiversity surveys due to their convergent morphologies and ecological roles:

- **Myxomycetes** (plasmodial slime molds): Form large, multinucleate plasmodia that creep over surfaces engulfing food particles
- **Dictyosteliomycota** (cellular slime molds): Individual amoebae aggregate to form multicellular fruiting structures
- **Acrasiomycota**: Similar cellular aggregation behavior but taxonomically distinct
- **Plasmodiophoromycota**: Obligate intracellular plant parasites causing diseases such as clubroot of cabbage
- **Protostelids**: The simplest slime molds, with minimal fruiting structures

See [[myxomycetes-slime-molds]] for further detail.

## Classification Challenges and Future Directions

The tension between classification stability and phylogenetic accuracy continues to challenge mycologists. Some researchers advocate using clade names rather than formal taxon designations until relationships are better resolved. Taxonomic coverage across higher taxa is highly uneven, with most molecular studies focusing on easily cultured or economically important species. As DNA barcoding and environmental sequencing become routine, the gap between described and actual diversity will likely narrow dramatically.

His personal wealth, independence from academic institutions, and sharp tongue made him a unique and formidable figure in the history of [[mycophiles-and-mushroom-culture]]. ## Merged: Tulasne Brothers
Louis-Rene Tulasne (1815-1885) and Charles Tulasne (1816-1884) were two French bachelor brothers who revolutionized mycology with their recognition that individual fungi could produce different types of spores in different phases of their life cycle -- a phenomenon termed pleomorphy. Their masterwork, Selecta Fungorum Carpologia (1861-1865), introduced concepts that remain fundamental to fungal taxonomy and to our understanding of [[truffle-evolution-and-hypogeous-fungi]]. The [[ingoldian-hyphomycetes-spore-dispersal-in-water]] provide a striking modern example of pleomorphy: the same organism produces star-shaped spores in water and entirely different sexual spores on land. - fungal kingdom classification
