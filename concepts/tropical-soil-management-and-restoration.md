# Tropical Soil Management and Restoration

Tropical soils present unique and formidable challenges for agriculture and land management. Unlike temperate soils that are periodically renewed by glaciation, tropical soils are ancient, deeply weathered, and subject to intense leaching from high rainfall and heat. Understanding their characteristics and implementing appropriate management strategies is essential for sustainable food production in the humid, wet-dry, and monsoon tropics.

## The Nature of Tropical Soils

In the wet tropics, approximately 80–90% of available nutrients are locked up in the living biomass of plants and animals rather than in the soil itself. The soils are predominantly infertile, especially when cleared of vegetation, and tend to erode rapidly, leaching down to insoluble oxides of iron and aluminum (ferricrete or silcrete). Only terraces, floodplains, and areas near recent volcanic activity maintain reasonable fertility.

The clay mineralogy of tropical soils is dominated by kaolinite and oxides of iron and aluminum. In these oxidic kaolinitic soils, the [[rock-phosphate]] releases more slowly and provides better long-term benefits. Returning crop wastes as mulch is essential and can reduce the pH of coral sands (pH 8–9) to a level nearer pH 6.5–7, which is suitable for gardens.

### Volcanic Soils

In areas of recent vulcanism or volcanic dust deposits, soils are sufficiently rich to sustain intensive agriculture without special amendments, but constant cropping will eventually exhaust even these soils. Alkaline volcanic soils of Indonesia support rich terrace and palm polyculture systems, and tropical high-island soils with dolomite or recent volcanics respond well to mulch-manure mixtures with only minor nitrogen deficiencies correctable through legume intercrop and manures.

## Deep Granitic Sands

Soils over rotted granites, found on high islands of the Indian Ocean, the Deccan in India, and around inselbergs (domed hills), present a peculiar challenge: open, coarse, granitic sands that are often very deep will not retain mulch beyond one growing season. Two approaches address these free-draining, low-nutrient soils:

### Broadscale Approach

Palms, Albizzia, Inga, and Acacia species, along with other adapted leguminous trees and native vegetation, establish a light canopy of leaves when small amounts of nutrients are added at regular intervals. Palms planted in mulch-filled pits establish high crown cover, and legume detritus supports more valuable fruit trees (mango, cashew, pomegranate, persimmon, citrus, tamarind, lychee, custard apple, avocado) while maintaining a complete root web of legumes. Palm trunks serve as trellises for vanilla and passionfruit. Niches in the granite mass itself hold pockets of soil and mulch for valuable trees, and vines establish on granite slabs that maintain heat and ripen crops effectively.

The mycelial web of pioneer legume roots enables retention of applied nutrients, reduces water use, and establishes fruiting trees in sparse, drought-prone soils. Palms suited to these sites include date, coconut, doum, and Borassus palms.

### Garden-Scale Approach

Long trenches excavated in loose sand (1–2 m wide, 1–1.5 m deep) can be lined with sheet plastic (upturned at one end only) or thickly lined with cardboard, paper, carpet, and leaf material, then backfilled with sandy loam. The deep sealed layer holds water and leached mulch, and household greywater can be directed into these trenches for root irrigation. Alternatively, log-boxes built above ground level, carpeted at the base with plastic or thick paper, and filled with humus-sand mixes work well for green crops and vegetables.

Large domestic water tanks, gleyed ponds, solid granite dams, and underground plastic-lined cisterns back-filled with sand provide water storage.

## The Long-Term Imperative

In the long term, tree and ground legumes are essential for maintaining soil health in the tropics. Destructive approaches—combining forest clearing, bare-soil cropping, and careless water runoff management—create desolate, brick-like, hostile baked clays from once-rich tropical forests. These practices represent what may prove to be some of the worst crimes against nature.

## Key Species for Tropical Soil Building

- **Tree legumes**: Leucaena, Gliricidia, Acacia albida, Albizzia, Inga, Prosopis, Sesbania
- **Ground legumes**: Lablab bean, cowpea, soya bean, Desmodium, Centrosema, vetch, Haifa clover
- **Mulch producers**: Bamboo, Casuarina, palms, comfrey, lemongrass, Hibiscus
- **Pioneer species**: Castor oil bean, tamarind, Vitex, Sapium
- **Fruiting trees for poor soils**: Mango, cashew, pomegranate, persimmon, citrus, tamarind, lychee, custard apple, avocado
