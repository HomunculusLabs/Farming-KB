# Supplemented Sawdust Block Cultivation

Supplemented sawdust blocks represent the standard
method for commercial oyster mushroom production
and are increasingly adopted by advanced home
cultivators seeking higher yields and better
quality fruiting bodies. This approach produces
significantly higher yields compared to straw-
based methods, at the cost of requiring pressure
sterilization equipment and more controlled
environmental conditions throughout the growing
cycle from inoculation through final harvest and
post-harvest handling.

## Substrate Formulation

### Base Materials
Hardwood sawdust forms the structural backbone of
the substrate. Oak, beech, maple, alder, and birch
sawdust all perform well for oyster mushroom
cultivation. The sawdust should be from kiln-dried
lumber or well-aged chips; fresh sawdust from
green wood contains volatile compounds that can
inhibit mycelial growth and reduce colonization
rates. Sawdust particle size affects colonization
speed: fine sawdust packs too densely limiting air
exchange, while coarse material provides excellent
aeration but lower nutrient density. A mix of
coarse chips (30-40%) and fine sawdust (60-70%)
provides optimal balance for most commercial and
hobby growing operations.

### Supplements
Nutritional supplements dramatically increase
yield by providing readily available nitrogen,
vitamins, and minerals that accelerate mycelial
growth and support larger fruiting bodies. Common
supplements and typical addition rates include
wheat bran at 10-20% by dry weight (the most
popular supplement globally), rice bran at 8-15%
(preferred in Asian cultivation systems), soybean
hulls at 10-25% (increasingly popular with very
high reported yields), and cottonseed meal at
5-10% (high protein content but use sparingly to
avoid contamination issues). Supplementation
levels above 20% require extended sterilization
times and increase contamination risk
substantially.

### Moisture Adjustment
The final substrate mixture should be hydrated to
60-65% moisture content. This typically requires
adding water at approximately 1.5-2 times the dry
weight of substrate materials. The mixture should
pass the squeeze test (firmly squeezed material
releases only a few drops of water) and feel
uniformly moist without any wet spots or dry
pockets throughout the mass before loading into
bags for sterilization.

## Mixing, Bagging, and Sterilization

Dry ingredients (sawdust and supplements) are
thoroughly mixed first to ensure even supplement
distribution before water is added gradually while
mixing continues. The mixed substrate is loaded
into autoclavable polypropylene bags (typically
5-10 kg capacity for commercial blocks, 2-3 kg for
home cultivation). Supplemented sawdust substrates
must be sterilized at 121 degrees C (15 PSI) for
90-120 minutes because the nutrient-rich
supplements support rapid contaminant growth that
would overwhelm mushroom mycelium. The large
thermal mass means center temperatures lag
significantly behind surface temperatures;
temperature probes at the load center verify
adequate heat penetration throughout the entire
batch.

## Inoculation and Colonization

After cooling to below 30 degrees C, grain spawn
is added at 5-10% by weight through the filter
patch opening or dedicated inoculation port. The
spawn is mixed through the substrate by shaking
and massaging the bag thoroughly until grain
kernels are evenly distributed. Supplemented
sawdust blocks are colonized at 22-26 degrees C
over 14-21 days for most oyster species, with king
oysters requiring a longer 21-30 day colonization
period before being ready for the fruiting phase.

## Fruiting and Yield Expectations

When fully colonized, blocks are removed from bags
(or bags are opened) for fruiting. The exposed
surface is misted and maintained at high humidity
(90-95%) for pinning. Some cultivators score the
block surface with a sterilized knife to increase
surface area for pin formation. Cold shock
(refrigeration at 2-4 degrees C for 12-24 hours)
can initiate more uniform pinning in some oyster
species. Supplemented sawdust blocks typically
achieve 75-150% biological efficiency,
significantly exceeding straw-based methods. First
flush yields of 30-50% of substrate dry weight are
common, with 2-4 total flushes possible. Oyster
mushrooms command premium prices (8-20 USD per kg
retail) making this method profitable at modest
scales.

## Block Disposal After Harvest

After 2-3 flushes, spent blocks are nutrient-
depleted but contain residual enzymatic activity
from the fungal mycelium. Spent blocks can be
composted, used as garden mulch, or further
processed. Some cultivators use spent oyster
blocks to inoculate outdoor log stacks, extending
their productive life while reducing waste
disposal costs. The spent substrate also makes
excellent worm bedding for vermicomposting
systems.
