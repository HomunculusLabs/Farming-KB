# Integrated Home and Garden Design

A permaculture home and garden forms a single, self-regulating system in which the house,
garden, animals, and waste streams are woven together so that every output serves as an
input elsewhere. Bill Mollison's total-design model treats the dwelling not as a separate
structure placed on a landscape but as the central node of a productive ecosystem.

## The House

The house is sized to the family and kept simple — a structure that is easy to heat and
cool. Floors may be slab-on-grade or raised pisé (rammed earth), both edge-insulated to
prevent thermal bridging. Induced cross-ventilation, aided by vertical sashes or shutters
on each room, scoops prevailing breezes through the interior for passive cooling and
heating. During hot periods the main living area shifts outside — to a rear deck or under
a porch trellis — with side trellises carrying productive vine crops. Materials are
sourced locally; the only manufactured inputs are glass, stone or ceramic pipes and
drains, and a water storage tank.

## How the Garden Works

The garden is designed to accept virtually all household water and organic waste streams.
Only plastics, glass, and most metals are excluded; even some metal cans are deliberately
buried to release zinc and iron slowly into the soil. The garden supplies its own mulch
and most of the fodder needed for rabbits, guinea pigs, poultry, and a small pig. Layout
follows a strict visit-and-tend frequency: the most-used elements are closest to the
kitchen door, while lower-maintenance zones radiate outward. The overall aesthetic is
natural in both appearance and function. Where no septic tank exists, a dry toilet
system composts humanure in pits beneath fruit trees. Toilet paper is replaced by
soft-leaf hedge species such as *Nicotiana* and *Leucaena*.

## Garden Elements

### Element A — Wet Food Patch

A shallow, earth-banked basin receives clean household greywater or roof runoff. The
waterlogged zone produces aquatic or semi-aquatic food crops: kangkong, watercress, and
Chinese water chestnut. The raised earth bank is planted with dryland staple root crops
— yam, sweet potato, and cassava — which benefit from the moisture gradient at the
bank's edge.

### Element B — Banana Circles

Banana circles (often interplanted with papaya or mixed fruit species) sit downslope of
the house and are directly irrigated by hand-basin and kitchen-sink effluent. Hedgerow
prunings supply the heavy mulch bananas demand. The circular pit design traps organic
matter and moisture, creating a fertile micro-site that sustains rapid, continuous
production.

### Element C — "Dry" Water Patch

A sunken, damp zone reserved for vigorous forage and mulch species rather than direct
food crops. Comfrey, banna grass, lemongrass, and various legumes tolerate the wet feet
and provide cut-and-come-again mulch material. The surrounding bank supports pigeon pea,
papaya, and banana, linking this element into the broader food system.

### Element D / D* — Avenue Crops

The main staple crops — maize, cassava, yam, melons — are grown in avenue-style strips
with spiral or zig-zag planting patterns that increase edge and disrupt pest spread. Each
strip is a self-mulching polyculture: maize/beans, yam/beans, cassava/beans, melon/beans.
Hedgerows of *Leucaena*, *Gliricidia*, and *Sesbania* border the avenues, supplying
nitrogen fixation, chop-and-drop mulch, and animal fodder.

### Element E — Forage Hedge and Animal Integration

A low, raggedly-trimmed hedge of multi-purpose forage-mulch species provides privacy,
windbreak, and food. Bamboo clumps are interspersed for building material and edible
shoots. A dense weed barrier strip (W) runs outside the hedge. Two fenced animal runs
(Y1 and Y2) are rotated through the hedge zone, allowing chickens and other poultry to
scratch, fertilise, and control pests before being shifted to the fallow pen.

### Element F — Front Yard

The public-facing zone is planted with traditional flowers, medicinal herbs, aromatic
plants, fibre crops, and ornamental food species. This element satisfies cultural
expectations for a "garden" while still contributing edible and useful yield.

### Element G — Keyhole Garden Beds

Nearest the kitchen, keyhole beds hold the household's preferred vegetables — especially
leafy greens. The closest bed is dedicated to bulk culinary herbs for daily harvest.
Seasonal vegetables rotate through remaining beds. All beds are mulched from the hedge
and from Element C's forage pit, closing the nutrient loop.

### Element H — Fukuoka-Style Grain Plot

A no-till grain plot (after Masanobu Fukuoka) grows cereals for both people and poultry.
A low surround of dry-tolerant mulch species protects the grain and provides additional
fodder, reducing the need for external feed inputs.

### Element K — Palm Circle

A clump of six to twelve palms — coconut, oil palm, date, or sugar palm — occupies a
dedicated zone. Chopped fronds provide deep, self-renewing mulch. During the wet season,
yam or climbing vines use the palm trunks as living trellises, stacking yet another
yield into the same ground.

## Trees Over the House

Carefully selected trees with light, open canopies overhang the roof. Criteria include:
fruits that fall (so nothing heavy strikes the roof), the ability to pass through or
around roof structures, and dense-enough foliage to shade the roof surface. Vine crops
on trellis systems between house and trees add further production and passive cooling.

## Animal Integration

Rabbit hutches, chicken roosts, pigeon lofts, and quail houses are designed as
elevated, slatted-floor structures that allow manure to fall directly into garden beds
or collection pits below. Each structure's roof catches rainwater to a small tank,
multiplying its function. The two fenced animal runs (Y1, Y2) are alternated so that
one rests and regenerates while the other is in active use, both well-shaded by
overstory trees or trellises.

## House Trellis Systems

Trellises on and around the house support productive vine crops: chayote, kiwifruit,
climbing beans, cucurbits, and grapes. Some non-bearing, vigorous vines can be allowed
to cover sections of the roof entirely, providing a living insulation layer that
significantly reduces indoor temperatures during hot seasons.

## Accessories and Off-Grid Systems

- **Hot water**: Solar collector or simple black pipe on the roof provides heated water
  without fuel inputs.
- **Lighting**: Oil lamps or a small photovoltaic array supply evening illumination.
- **Cooking and preservation**: A solar oven and solar food drier harness sun energy for
  cooking and preserving surplus harvest, reducing dependence on stored fuels and
  extending the usefulness of seasonal abundance.

## Design Principles

The integrated home-garden system embodies several core permaculture principles:
every element performs multiple functions, every function is supported by multiple
elements, and energy flows (water, nutrients, heat, food) are cycled locally rather
than imported and exported. The result is a resilient, low-input household that meets
most of its own needs from a compact, intensively-managed landscape.
