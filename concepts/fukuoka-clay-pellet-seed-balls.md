Fukuoka emphasized that the clay used for the pellets should be relatively pure and not
overly rich in topsoil. If the pellets crumble too early in the spring, the seed inside will
be exposed and devoured by rodents and other pests before it has a chance to germinate. The
ideal pellet is hard enough to withstand the elements and animal predation until rainfall
softens it sufficiently for the seed inside to emerge. For those who prefer what Fukuoka
called a "scientific method of convenience," the seeds may alternatively be coated with a
synthetic resin such as styrofoam containing the necessary pesticides and fungicides,
though Fukuoka himself advocated for the clay-only approach as part of his commitment to
farming without chemicals.

germinating in the spring. When enclosed in clay pellets and scattered over the field in
mid-November or later, the seeds remain protected through the cold season. In the spring, as
temperatures rise and moisture increases, the pellets gradually soften and the rice seedlings
begin to emerge from beneath the cover of clover and straw mulch. Fukuoka found that this
method eliminated the need for chemical seed treatments containing fungicides and

This proposal, which he acknowledged some would call outlandish, was inspired by a
realization he had while standing in an American desert: that rain does not fall from the
heavens but issues forth from the ground. Fukuoka came to believe that deserts do not form
because there is no rain; rather, rain ceases to fall because the vegetation has
disappeared. The loss of plant cover disrupts the local water cycle, and once this cycle is
broken, the land dries out progressively. Building a dam in the desert, in his view, is an
attempt to treat the symptoms of the disease rather than a strategy for increasing rainfall.
The only effective approach to restoring rainfall is to restore the vegetation that sustains
the water cycle.

For his desert revegetation proposal, Fukuoka described a specialized form of clay pellet
containing a mixture of seeds chosen for their hardiness and ability to establish themselves
in arid conditions. The pellets would be prepared by first mixing the seeds of green manure
trees such as black wattle, which can grow in areas with an annual rainfall of less than two
inches, with the seeds of clover, alfalfa, bur clover, and other types of green manure, as
well as grain and vegetable seeds. This mixture of seeds would be coated first with a layer
of soil and then with a layer of clay to form what Fukuoka called microbe-containing clay
pellets. The inclusion of soil in the pellet serves to introduce [[roger-heim-scientific-study-psilocybin]] to determine
why the deserts are spreading in the first place. He argued that even if such a study were
undertaken, the causes would recede into an endless chain of interwoven events and factors
beyond man's powers of comprehension. Suppose that man were able to learn which plant had
been the first to die off in a land turned to desert, Fukuoka wrote. He would still not know
enough to decide whether to begin by planting the first type of vegetation to disappear or
the last to survive. The reason, he argued, is simple: in nature, there is no cause and
effect. He believed that the only effective approach to revegetating barren land is to leave
things largely up to nature, providing only the initial spark of seeds and microorganisms
encased in clay pellets and trusting the natural processes of succession to take over from
there.

Fukuoka extended this reasoning to the broader problem of global desertification and
vegetation loss. He held that human civilization and the misguided methods of crop
cultivation that arose from man's arrogance are largely responsible for the global plight of
drying arable lands. Overgrazing by large animal herds kept by nomadic peoples had reduced
vegetation variety and denuded the land, while agricultural societies, with their shift to
[[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] and heavy reliance on petroleum-based chemicals, had confronted the
problem of rapid debilitation of the land. His visit to the United States in 1979
reinforced these convictions, as he witnessed firsthand how grazing had destroyed the ecology
of natural grasses and devastated the land, driving American farmers toward large-scale
mechanized operations totally reliant on petroleum energy.

Once people accept that nature has been harmed by human knowledge and action, and renounce these instruments of chaos and destruction, Fukuoka
believed that nature will recover its ability to nurture all forms of life. The clay pellet
technique, in his vision, was a practical means of initiating this recovery on a global
scale, requiring minimal resources and no industrial infrastructure beyond the labor of
human hands scattering seed over the barren earth. The technique embodies the essence of
Fukuoka's philosophy: that nature, given the slightest opportunity, possesses an inherent
capacity to restore itself to wholeness and abundance.
