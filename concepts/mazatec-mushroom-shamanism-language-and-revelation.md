## Mazatec Mushroom Shamanism — Language and Revelation

## Overview

Among the Mazatec Indians of the Sierra Mazateca in northeastern Oaxaca, Mexico, psychoactive mushrooms of the genus *Psilocybe* are used in nocturnal healing ceremonies that are fundamentally linguistic acts. The mushrooms—known as "the holy ones" (*los santos*)—are not merely hallucinogens but are understood as medicines that liberate the capacity for inspired speech. The shaman, or *curandero*, functions as an oral poet, seer, and oracle whose chanting during mushroom sessions constitutes both diagnosis and treatment of illness.

## Ceremonial Setting

The Mazatecs eat the mushrooms only at night, in absolute darkness. They believe that consuming them in daylight causes madness. The darkness is considered the time most conducive to visionary insights. Typically, several members of a family participate together—a father, mother, children, uncles, and aunts—sharing in what the essayist Henry Munn describes as a "transcendental subjectivity" grounded in kinship.

The mushrooms are eaten in pairs, symbolizing the dual principle of procreation and creation—man and woman. Participants sit together in darkness, often near a fire, breathing the incense of copal. The shaman presides, and the experience unfolds within an authoritarian context: rather than being encouraged to speak, participants are enjoined to keep silent and listen while the shaman speaks for them.

## The Shaman as Speaker

The central figure in Mazatec mushroom ceremonies is not a silent guide but an active speaker. The shaman is called "the man of language" (*hombre de palabra*) and "the man of wisdom." His primary function is to speak on behalf of those present—to chant, pray, sing, and utter the truths that the mushrooms reveal.

When asked where their imagery comes from, shamans consistently reply: "I didn\'t say it; the mushrooms did." This attribution reflects a core understanding: the mushrooms do not literally speak, but they endow the person who consumes them with an inspired capacity for language. The words that issue forth during sessions are experienced as coming from outside oneself—as dictation rather than invention.

### The Rhythmic "Tzo"

A distinctive feature of Mazatec shamanic speech is the repetitive utterance of *tzo* ("says" in the Mazatec language) at the end of each phrase. This serves as rhythmic punctuation—*tzo, tzo, tzo*—emphasizing that the words are being spoken *through* the shaman rather than *by* him. As Munn describes it: "Says, says, says. It is said. I say. Who says? We say, man says, language says, being and existence say."

### Comparison with Surrealist Automatic Writing

The linguistic phenomenon produced by the mushrooms bears comparison with the surrealist technique of automatic writing, but with a critical difference. While surrealist automatic dictation tends toward disconnected flow, the mushroom-inspired speech tends toward coherence—a "rational enunciation of meanings." The spontaneity liberated is not only perceptual but linguistic: the spontaneity of fervent, lucid discourse.

## The Concept of Poesis as Healing

The Mazatec shaman operates with a concept of *poesis* in its original Greek sense—as an action, a making, a transmutation. Words themselves are understood as medicine. To enunciate and give meaning to the events and situations of existence is considered life-giving in itself.

Claude Levi-Strauss\'s observation is directly relevant: "The psychoanalyst listens, whereas the shaman speaks." In the psychoanalytic transference, the patient puts words into the analyst\'s mouth. In shamanic incantation, the shaman speaks *for* the patient—questioning and providing answers that correspond to the interpretation of the patient\'s condition. The shaman provides the sick person with a language by means of which unexpressed and otherwise inexpressible psychic states can be immediately expressed.

This transition to verbal expression—making it possible to undergo in an ordered and intelligible form a real experience that would otherwise be chaotic and inexpressible—is what induces the release of the physiological process and reorganizes it in a favorable direction.

## The Medicine Woman\'s Discourse

Detailed ethnographic records exist of mushroom sessions led by a Mazatec medicine woman, Mrs. Irene Pineda de Figueroa. Her discourse, recorded and translated from the Mazatec tonal language, reveals several characteristic themes:

### Diagnostic Invocation

The session begins with a search for the origin of illness. The medicine woman chants prayers asking to discover "from where comes sickness, from where comes pain and affliction." She invokes a systematic examination: "Let\'s go searching for the tracks of her feet to encounter the sickness that she is suffering from."

### The Footprint Metaphor

A recurring metaphor in Mazatec shamanic discourse is that of footprints (*huellas*). Footprints represent meaning—traces of a going-to-and-coming-from, sedimented clues of significance to be followed. The hunt for meaning is temporal, carried into the past and projected into the future: "What happened? What will happen?" The method of looking "from the right side to the left side" represents the articulation of intuition, fact, feeling, and wish.

### Communal Concern

The medicine woman speaks not for one individual but for the entire community. Her concerns encompass physical illness, mental troubles, ethical problems, economic poverty, and the welfare of children. The daily diet of the Mazatecs consists of black beans and tortillas with chili sauce; meat is eaten only at festivals. White spots from malnutrition splotch their faces.

### Syncretism

The mushroom ceremonies synthesize indigenous beliefs with Catholicism. The mushrooms are called "the Flesh of God" and "the Blood of Christ." The medicine woman invokes the Virgin of Conception, the Virgin of the Nativity, Christ, the Holy Spirit, and "Fifty-three Saints, Fifty-three Saintesses." This reflects a long history of religious syncretism in Mesoamerica, where the indigenous legend of Quetzalcoatl was transformed into the story of Christ walking through the miraculous mountains.

## Therapeutic Mechanisms

The mushrooms serve both chemical and spiritual functions in healing:

1. **Chemical medicine**: The Mazatecs report that syphilis, cancer, epilepsy, stomach disorders, and skin irritations have been alleviated by mushroom use. Tumors are said to have been cured. These claims have some pharmacological basis in the known compounds of *Psilocybe* species.

2. **Psychological catharsis**: For psychosomatic complaints and psychological troubles, the liberation of spontaneous activity provoked by the mushrooms is most remedial. Given to the depressed, they awaken a catharsis of the spirit; to those with problems, a vision of their existential situation.

3. **Somatic healing**: The shaman\'s intoxicated state of intense, vibrant energy gives him strength for physical healing through massage and suction, working directly over the sick person\'s body.

4. **Spiritual diagnosis**: The shaman eats the mushrooms to "see into the spirit of the sick, to disclose the hidden, to intuit how to resolve the unsolved." The mushroom experience provides visionary empathy that guides the healing process.

## Vocation and Initiation

Shamanic vocations typically begin with personal illness. Many shamans, including the well-known curandera Maria Sabina, discovered their calling when they were themselves cured by the mushrooms. The pattern is consistent: illness, unsuccessful treatment by conventional medicine, mushroom cure, and acceptance of shamanic vocation.

Not everyone has a "predilection for such extreme and arduous experiences." Those who repeatedly eat the mushrooms and develop expertise are recognized by their community as the shamans—the masters of psychological adventure. When others eat the mushrooms, they always call one of these recognized experts to guide the session.

## Words as Medicine

The most profound insight of Mazatec mushroom shamanism may be its understanding that language itself is therapeutic. The mushrooms are called not "mushrooms" but "prayer," "well-being," and "wisdom." The experience they produce is understood as synonymous with language, communication, perception, insight, and knowledge.

The medicine woman\'s most frequently repeated words are *freshness* and *tenderness*—emotions of activated, perceptualized being. For the Mazatecs, the mushrooms do not produce madness; they produce "good and happiness." They are "a remedy for decent people."

## Contemporary Relevance

Munn argues that the rediscovery of psilocybin\'s effects in Western society has been distorted by association with madness and the pejorative label "drugs." The shamans who used these substances spoke of them as medicines and described their effects as putting one into communication with the spirit. The value of studying their use in indigenous societies lies in finding a path "beyond the superficial to a more essential understanding of phenomena which we, with our limited conception of the rational, have too quickly, perhaps mistakenly, termed irrational."
