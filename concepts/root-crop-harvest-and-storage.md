# Root Crop Harvest and Storage

Detailed harvest timing and storage instructions for root vegetables
in the home garden, based on Purdue Extension guidelines. Root crops
store best under cold, moist conditions (32 to 40 degrees F, 90 to
95 percent relative humidity). See [[vegetable-garden-harvest]], [[vegetable-watering-and-thinning]], [[vegetable-storage-conditions-by-temperature-and-humidity]] — Full temperature/humidity reference for all crops
[[winter-gardening-season-extension]] — Extending the growing season into cold months
[[stone-cellar-building]] for construction guidance), or an outdoor pit. Perforated plastic bags help
maintain proper humidity levels while allowing some gas exchange.

The key to successful root crop storage is harvesting at the right
time, handling carefully to avoid bruising, and maintaining consistent
cold, moist conditions throughout the storage period.

## Beets

Begin harvest when the beet is 1 inch in diameter. Beet tops at this
stage make excellent tender greens for cooking. For the main harvest,
pull beets when they reach 2 to 3 inches in diameter.

Harvest spring-planted beets before hot weather arrives (typically by
July). Hot weather causes beets to become tough, fibrous, and
strong-flavored. Harvest fall beets before the first moderate freeze.

For storage, wash roots to remove soil, trim tops to 1/2 inch, place
in perforated plastic bags, and store in the refrigerator, a cold
moist cellar, or an outdoor pit. Storage life: 2 to 4 months under
ideal cold, moist conditions.

## Carrots

Harvest spring carrots before hot weather (July). Heat causes carrots
to become bitter and woody. Fall-planted carrots should be harvested
before the first moderate freeze, though light frosts can improve
sweetness. Handle carefully to avoid bruising, which shortens
storage life.

For storage, wash roots, trim tops to 1/2 inch, place in perforated
plastic bags, and store in the refrigerator, cold cellar, or pit.
Storage life: 2 to 4 months.

## Irish Potatoes

Harvest when the tops have yellowed and/or died. Do not leave tubers
in the ground because high soil temperatures will accelerate
over-ripening and reduce storage quality.

Cure for about a week in a shaded, well-ventilated place such as a
barn, shed, or garage. Remove excess soil from potatoes and discard
any that are diseased or damaged.

Avoid exposing tubers to light. Potatoes will turn green with even
small amounts of light exposure. Green potatoes contain solanine, a
toxic compound. Store in as cool a place as possible above 40 degrees
F. Cool basements are probably the best storage available in late
summer. Keep humidity high and provide good ventilation. Storage
life: 2 to 4 months.

## Radishes

Harvest when 1/2 to 1 inch in diameter. Radishes become pithy and
overspicy if left too long. Harvest spring radishes promptly for best
quality.

Wash roots, trim both tap root and tops, and store in plastic bags in
the refrigerator for up to 1 month. Winter or black radishes are
stored the same as carrots and have a longer storage potential.

## Turnips

Turnips can be harvested from the time they are 1 inch in diameter.
They are best as a fall crop and can withstand several light freezes,
which actually improve their flavor. Smaller turnips tend to be more
tender and mild-flavored.

Store turnips the same as carrots: wash, trim tops to 1/2 inch,
place in perforated plastic bags, and store in the refrigerator, cold
cellar, or pit. Storage life: up to 4 months for fall-harvested
turnips.

## Parsnips

Harvest in late fall after several moderate freezes. Exposure to cold
weather develops the sweet flavor that parsnips are known for. Unlike
many root crops, parsnips improve with frost exposure and should not
be harvested early if the sweetest flavor is desired.

Same storage requirements as for carrots: wash, trim tops to 1/2
inch, perforated plastic bags, refrigerator or cold moist cellar.
Storage life: 2 to 4 months.

## Horseradish

Harvest after several severe freezes. The roots continue to develop
flavor through cold exposure. Horseradish is one of the most
cold-tolerant root crops available to home gardeners.

Store in the ground all winter by mulching heavily with straw or
leaves, and dig roots as needed throughout the cold months. This is
often the simplest and most effective storage method. Alternatively,
horseradish can be stored much like carrots in cold, moist conditions
for 2 to 4 months.

## Salsify (Oyster Plant)

Same harvest and storage approach as for parsnips. Harvest after
several moderate freezes for the best flavor development. Salsify
improves with cold exposure similar to parsnips. Store in perforated
plastic bags under cold, moist conditions for 2 to 4 months.

## General Root Crop Tips

- Handle all root crops carefully to avoid cuts and bruises, which
  invite rot during storage
- Do not wash potatoes before storage; brush off excess soil only
- Remove tops promptly before storage to prevent moisture loss from
  the roots
- Check stored roots periodically and remove any showing signs of
  decay or shriveling
- Perforated plastic bags maintain humidity while allowing some gas
  exchange, extending storage life
