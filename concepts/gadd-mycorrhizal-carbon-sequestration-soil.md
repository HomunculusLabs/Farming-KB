# Mycorrhizal Fungi and Soil Carbon Sequestration

## The Scale of Mycorrhizal Carbon Flow

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the vast majority of
terrestrial plants, and the carbon that flows through these associations
represents one of the largest carbon fluxes in the terrestrial biosphere.
Plants allocate a substantial fraction of their photosynthetically fixed
carbon, typically 10 to 20 percent and sometimes up to 30 percent, to their
mycorrhizal fungal partners. This carbon is used by the fungus to build and
maintain its extensive [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]], which explores the soil for mineral
nutrients (particularly phosphorus) and water. The sheer scale of this carbon
allocation means that mycorrhizal fungi play a major role in the global carbon
cycle, influencing both the rate at which carbon enters soil and the form in
which it is stored.
## Mechanisms of Carbon Stabilization

Mycorrhizal fungi contribute to soil carbon sequestration through several
mechanisms. First, a portion of the plant-derived carbon allocated to
mycorrhizal fungi is incorporated into fungal biomass, including hyphae,
spores, and extracellular structures. This fungal biomass has a different
chemical composition than plant litter, with higher concentrations of chitin
and glomalin-related proteins that are relatively resistant to decomposition.
Second, mycorrhizal fungi produce extracellular polymeric substances that bind
soil particles into stable aggregates, physically protecting organic carbon
from microbial decomposition. Third, the extensive [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-graph-theory]] physically
stabilizes soil structure, creating microenvironments that favor carbon
preservation.
## Glomalin and Recalcitrant Carbon Compounds

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] fungi that was
first identified by Sara Wright in 1996. It is operationally defined as the
protein fraction extracted from soil by hot citrate buffer and is remarkably
resistant to microbial decomposition, persisting in soil for decades. Glomalin
contributes significantly to soil organic carbon pools, with estimates
suggesting it may account for 27 percent of total soil carbon in some soils.
The recalcitrance of glomalin and related compounds means that carbon
allocated to mycorrhizal fungi can be stabilized in soil for much longer
periods than carbon in plant litter or root exudates, making mycorrhizal fungi
important contributors to long-term carbon sequestration.
## Hyphal Turnover and Carbon Input

The mycelial network [[query-what-are-the-different-types-of-mycorrhizal-fungi]] is not permanent; hyphae have
limited lifespans and are constantly being produced and decomposed. This
hyphal turnover represents a continuous input of carbon into the soil. Because
mycorrhizal hyphae extend far beyond the root zone, they deposit carbon in
soil volumes that would not otherwise receive significant organic inputs. The
fine, branching hyphae of mycorrhizal fungi also have a high surface-area-to-
volume ratio, which promotes interaction with soil minerals and the formation
of organo-mineral complexes that protect carbon from decomposition. The rate
of hyphal turnover depends on environmental conditions, fungal species, and
plant host, but can represent a substantial annual carbon input in many
ecosystems.
## Mycorrhizal Influence on Decomposition Pathways

Mycorrhizal fungi can influence soil carbon dynamics not only through direct
carbon inputs but also by modifying the decomposition of existing organic
matter. Some studies suggest that mycorrhizal fungi can suppress the activity
of free-living saprotrophic decomposers, slowing the decomposition of plant
litter and promoting the accumulation of soil organic matter. This effect,
sometimes called the Gadgil effect, may occur because mycorrhizal fungi
compete with saprotrophs for nutrients or because they alter the soil
environment in ways that favor carbon preservation over decomposition. The net
effect of [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] on soil carbon storage depends on the
balance between these competing influences.
## Land Use and Mycorrhizal Carbon Sequestration

Land use changes that affect mycorrhizal communities can have significant
impacts on soil carbon storage. Converting natural ecosystems to agricultural
land typically reduces mycorrhizal abundance and diversity, particularly when
tillage disrupts hyphal networks and when non-mycorrhizal crops are grown. The
loss of mycorrhizal carbon inputs can contribute to the soil carbon depletion
that commonly accompanies agricultural intensification. Conversely, practices
that support mycorrhizal fungi, such as [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], cover cropping, and
the use of mycorrhizal host crops, can enhance soil carbon sequestration. The
potential to manage mycorrhizal fungi for carbon sequestration is an active
area of research with implications [[query-why-are-mangroves-critical-for-climate-change-adaptation-and-coastal-protection]] mitigation.
