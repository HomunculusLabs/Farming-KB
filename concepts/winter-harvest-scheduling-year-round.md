## Winter Harvest Scheduling for Year-Round Vegetable Production

## Overview

The winter harvest system developed by Eliot Coleman is built on a counterintuitive insight: winter harvesting is primarily about **planting at the right time in fall**, not about growing through the deepest cold. The key realization is that most cold-hardy vegetables achieve near-full maturity by the time daylight hours drop below ten per day (around mid-November in the northeastern United States). After that point, growth essentially stops, and the plants enter a state of dormancy or near-dormancy. ### Crop Rotation Within the Greenhouse

Even within the confined space of a winter greenhouse, rotation matters. Coleman follows a simple four-year rotation:

1. **Legumes** (peas, fava beans) — fix nitrogen
2. **Leaf crops** (spinach, lettuce, asian greens) — moderate nitrogen demand
3. **Root crops** (carrots, beets, turnips) — low nitrogen, need loose soil
4. **Brassicas** (broccoli raab, radishes, mustard) — heavy feeders, benefit from preceding legume bed

## Harvest Techniques for Winter

### Cut-and-Come-Again

For leafy greens, the primary winter harvest method is cutting individual outer leaves while leaving the growing point intact. This allows the plant to continue producing new leaves, albeit very slowly during Persephone Days. Key techniques:

- Use scissors or a sharp knife to cut leaves 1 inch above the crown.
- Never harvest more than one-third of the foliage from any single plant in one cutting.
- Harvest in the late morning after the greenhouse has warmed, when plants are turgid and least stressed.
- After cutting, lightly water the bed if the soil surface is dry.
