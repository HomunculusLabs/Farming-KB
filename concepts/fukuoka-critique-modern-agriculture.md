# Fukuoka Critique of Modern Agriculture

Masanobu Fukuoka's critique of modern agriculture is one of the most thorough and penetrating in the literature of natural farming. Drawing on thirty to forty years of direct farming experience and a career as a plant pathologist, he systematically dismantles the assumptions underlying scientific agriculture. His approach is grounded in the [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]].

## The Kochi Prefecture Lesson

Fukuoka's turning point came during WWII when he was assigned to increase food production in Kochi Prefecture. Despite the warm climate, Kochi had the second lowest rice yields in Japan. The solution -- a massive post-season cultivation program to eradicate the yellow rice borer -- represented a cross-section of the most advanced agricultural technology of the time.

The result: "This program, which represented the most advanced agricultural technology of the time, succeeded only in preventing further losses." It demonstrated that scientific agriculture always aims at convenience to man and "no matter how large and complete the technology amassed, it will never amount to more than a temporary expedient."

Disease and pest outbreaks in Kochi were nature's method of suppressing excessive growth; man applied a near-sighted interpretation, seeing damage rather than natural regulation.

## Critique of Analytic Science

Scientific farming breaks rice production into constituent elements (germination, tillage, seeding, transplanting, fertilizing, weeding, pest control), researches improvements in each, then reassembles them. Fukuoka compares this to "carrying a single lantern to guide one's way through a pitch-dark night" -- blind, directionless progress. Unlike one who walks without a lantern toward a faraway light, this approach has no unity of purpose.

Techniques developed through research on rice yielding 15 bushels per quarter-acre cannot be applied to rice giving 30 or 40 bushels. The approach is inherently fragmented.

## Pesticides: The Biosystem Disruption

Fukuoka's most detailed critique concerns pesticides. Having observed their effects directly:

- After WWII, powerful organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides destroyed all insect pests -- but it was not long before massive mite outbreaks recurred uncontrollably
- The pesticides had destroyed the natural predators (ladybugs, predaceous mites, spiders) that kept mites in check
- New pesticides developed to control resistant pests simply created new problems
- Reports of major outbreaks of pesticide-resistant pathogens and pests involved dozens of organisms: mites, leafhoppers, rice borers, beetles

### The Botrytis Case Study
When a loquat orchard was hit by botrytis disease (initially misdiagnosed as cold damage), a village-wide pesticide spraying program brought it under control. But Fukuoka traced the outbreak's origin to:
1. Interplanting of citrus in loquat groves
2. Rapid transition from clean cultivation to mulching/sod cultivation (increased humidity)
3. Discontinuation of Bordeaux mixture in favor of new pesticides
4. The original Bordeaux mixture had been keeping the fungus in check

### Mutation Risk
Spraying pesticides with high residual toxicity and mutagenic chemicals onto easily mutated microbes creates risks scientists do not fully appreciate. Fukuoka personally demonstrated that fungal strains from different countries could cross and produce new variants with eight genetically distinct spores capable of producing more variants than even shiitake fungi.

## Critique of Organic Farming

Fukuoka explicitly states that organic farming is "just another type of scientific farming." It involves moving organic materials, processing and treating them, with only local and temporal gains. From a broader perspective, many efforts to protect the natural ecology through organic methods are actually destructive. For more on this distinction, see [[fukuoka-compost-unnecessary-philosophy]] for a detailed treatment:
- Scattering fresh straw directly over fields is more effective than preparing compost
- The decomposition of straw on the ground involves a vast natural succession of organisms
- Human composting efforts "just disturb the rapid and perfect natural processes"
- The "microbial" and "enzymatic" farming methods are unnecessary

## Critique of Genetic Engineering

Fukuoka was writing before modern genetic engineering but addressed its precursors:
- Radiation breeding experiments give no thought to changes in soil and airborne microbes
- Dousing fields with radiation may create monstrous microbial mutants undetectable to the unaided eye
- "Scientists believe they are wrestling in earnest with the problem of pollution when in fact they are just sowing the seeds for future pollution"
- Breeding experiments for new plant varieties through radiation are particularly dangerous

## Critique of Soil Science

- Soil management as normally practiced turns living soil into mere mineral matter
- Adjusting soil acidity is no easy matter -- correcting for manganese deficiency can lead to manganese or zinc deficiency in alkaline conditions
- No criteria exist for judging whether soil contains the right number of microbes, organic matter, or percentage of water and air
- In 30 years of natural farming without lime or micronutrients, Fukuoka noted no deficiencies

## Critique of Mechanization

- Energy requirements escalate with each level of mechanization: traditional with animals uses 10x the energy of natural farming; small-scale mechanization doubles that again; large-scale doubles it yet again
- Modern farming methods multiply energy input while not proportionally increasing yields
- Concrete levees and vinyl sheeting represent false economies that create worse problems in 2-3 years

## The Fundamental Error

The ultimate error of modern agriculture is anthropocentrism -- the belief that nature exists to serve man. "Man is the sole heretic in the natural order. Only he acts as he pleases." Scientific farming is centripetal and divergent, breaking away from nature with expanding human desires. Natural farming is centrifugal and convergent, seeking to return to nature.
