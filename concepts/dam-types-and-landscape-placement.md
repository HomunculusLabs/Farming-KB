## Overview

In permaculture water design, small dams and earth tanks are the primary infrastructure for storing surplus runoff. Bill Mollison outlines a systematic approach to dam placement within the total landscape, identifying specific dam types for each topographic position from hilltop to valley floor. The guiding principle is that well-placed small storages, integrated with forests, swales, and soil conditioning, can make a landscape drought-proof while simultaneously producing food, wildlife habitat, and energy.

## Placement Philosophy

Rather than the engineering tradition of placing dams primarily as valley barriers on streams, Mollison advocates distributing storages across the full range of landscape positions. Dams placed anywhere but as barriers on streams **effectively add to stream flow** in the long term by slowing water, allowing infiltration, and releasing stored water gradually.

The ideal target for humid lowland or foothill landscapes is to fit **10–15% of the landscape with small earth storages**. Yeomans (pers. comm., 1978) stated this proportion could eliminate floods, drought, and fire threat entirely.

## Slope Constraints

- Almost every type of dam is **cost-effective** on slopes of 5% or less
- Essential dams can be built at higher grades if constructed from concrete, rock, or by excavation
- **Contour dam walls** are practical on slopes of 8% or less
- Areas above 16% slope are generally unsuitable for open water storages (best left to forest)
- Every dam needs careful soil and level surveys and planning for local construction methods

## Not All Landscapes Can Store Surface Water

Some areas cannot cost-effectively store free surface water due to:

- **Free-draining soils** or deep/coarse sands
- **Rocky terrain** or fissured limestone
- **Steep or unstable** slopes

However, virtually every landscape will store more soil water if humus, soil treatment, or swales are applied. The soil itself is the largest water storage system available if allowed to absorb.

## The Dam Type Hierarchy

Dams are arranged in a hierarchy from highest to lowest positions in the landscape:

### 1. Saddle Dams (Highest)

**Position:** Saddles or hollows in the skyline profile of hills.

**Construction:** Fully excavated below grade, or walled on one or both sides. Shapes include circular, oblong, or "shark egg" with horn extensions at either end.

**Water source:** Diversion drains cut at 1:250–500 grade collect runoff from hills and direct it to the saddle dam. Additional drains feed the next dam downhill via a person drain.

**Uses:** Wildlife, stock water, high storage, fire control. Limited irrigation due to elevation.

### 2. Ridgepoint (Horseshoe) Dams

**Position:** Sub-plateaus of flattened ridges on a descending ridgeline, below saddle dams.

**Construction:** Below grade or walled by earth banks. Typically horse-hoof shaped.

**Uses:** Same as saddle dams. Valuable for runoff collection and pumped storages. Can act as energy storage for pumped water generation systems.

### 3. Keypoint Dams

**Position:** Valleys of secondary streams in humid landscapes, at the highest practical construction point where the stream profile changes from **convex to concave**.

**Construction:** A descending contour from the first keypoint picks up all other keypoints on the main valley. Multiple series can be installed below the primary series. Spillway of the last dam returns surplus to streams.

**Uses:** Primarily irrigation water for lower slopes. The keyline (linking keypoints) defines the gravity-flow irrigation layout.

### 4. Contour Dams

**Position:** On contour wherever slope is 8% or less.

**Construction:** Dam walls follow contour lines. Walls can be concave or convex to the fall line across the slope, depending on the shape of the land.

**Uses:** Irrigation, aquaculture, flood-flow basins in semi-arid areas. Practical as part of a general series across farm slopes.

### 5. Barrier Dams

**Position:** Across flowing or intermittent stream beds.

**Construction:** Require the most careful engineering — ample spillways, fish ladders on important streams, and consideration of downstream effects.

**Uses:** Energy generation (most frequent), irrigation (when placed above main valley floors). Works best as part of a keyline series.

**Caution:** Barrier dams are "the engineer's dam" — they can affect fish migration, are difficult to spill safely, and may deprive downstream ecosystems of flush-water and silt deposits.

### 6. Turkey's Nest (Ring) Dams

**Position:** Flatlands, above grade.

**Construction:** Water must be pumped in, typically by windmill or solar pump. Circular ring-shaped earth banks.

**Uses:** Stock water tanks, low-head irrigation. Common in flat terrain where gravity-fed options don't exist.

### 7. Check Dams

**Position:** Small stream beds.

**Construction:** 1–3 metre walls not intended for storage but to regulate flow. May have a base pipe allowing manageable flow downstream while banking flood crests.

**Uses:** Provide head for hydraulic rams, waterwheels, stream diversion to contoured irrigation canals, and flood buffering.

## Dam Safety

Earth dams or weirs with retaining walls of **6 metres (19 feet) or less** and large oversized stable spillways are no threat to life or property if well-made. They need not:

- Displace populations
- Stop flow in streams
- Create health problems
- Fill with silt
- Block fish migrations

Low barrier dams of 1–4 metres can assist stream oxygenation, provide permanent pools, be stepped for fish ladders, and provide modest power generation sites.

## Integration with Other Systems

The full permaculture water storage system integrates:

- **High dams** (saddle, ridgepoint) for fire control and wildlife
- **Mid-slope dams** (keypoint, contour) for irrigation via gravity
- **Low dams** (barrier, check) for energy and stream regulation
- **Swales and soil conditioning** for infiltration between dam sites
- **Forests on slopes >16°** for condensation, erosion control, and water cycling
- **Convex slopes** above the keyline for forest and absorption
- **Concave slopes** below the keyline for irrigation and production

## Design Principles

1. **Work from the top down** — place saddle and ridgepoint dams first, then work downhill
2. **Link dams with diversion drains** at 1:250–500 grade for gravity-fed filling
3. **Use the keyline** as the primary planning reference for irrigation layout
4. **Keep barrier dams small** (under 6m) with oversized spillways for safety
5. **Match dam type to position** — each topographic position has an optimal dam type
6. **Include biological design** from the start — aquaculture, wildlife habitat, and irrigation integration
7. **Aim for 10–15% water surface** in humid landscapes to eliminate drought and flood risk
