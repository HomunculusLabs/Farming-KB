## Terpenoids: The Aromatic Medicine

Terpenoids (also called terpenes) are the aromatic compounds responsible for
the distinctive fragrance and flavor profiles of cannabis and countless other
plants. These volatile organic compounds serve the plant's [[beta-caryophyllene-dietary-cannabinoid]] ((E)-BCP), a dietary cannabinoid that activates CB2 receptor
sites and initiates potent anti-inflammatory actions and protection from
oxidative stress. The FDA has approved (E)-BCP as a dietary cannabinoid, making
it a legally accessible form of cannabinoid therapy available through common
spices.

Research has shown that (E)-beta-caryophyllene protects against microbes,
inflammation, oxidative stress, pain, and cancer. Blesching notes that the
efficacy of ancient healing practices using spices as medicine has been
confirmed by recent scientific discoveries related to this compound. When used
in conjunction with cannabis, a synergy of beneficial effects may result.

### Terpenoid-Rich Spices and Their Properties

Blesching documents a comprehensive catalog of terpenoid-containing spices and
their therapeutic properties, many of which overlap with the effects attributed
to cannabis itself:

**Clove (Syzygium aromaticum):** Contains almost 4% (E)-beta-caryophyllene.
The essential oil of clove has demonstrated analgesic, anti-inflammatory,
antioxidant, antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral (including against herpes
simplex and hepatitis C), antibacterial (including multi-drug-resistant
strains), anticancer, cancer protective, and antidiabetic properties. Clove
oil cream works as an effective treatment for chronic anal fissures.

**Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis):** Contains 1-8% (E)-beta-caryophyllene.
Research from Taiwan concluded that rosemary is "an excellent multifunctional
therapeutic herb" with potent antiglycative bioactivity useful in preventing
and treating diabetic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative diseases.
Medicinal uses include antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, circulatory promotion,
radioprotection, and potential skin cancer prevention.

**Black Pepper (Piper nigrum):** Contains significant (E)-beta-caryophyllene.
Piperine, another compound in black pepper, enhances the bioavailability of
numerous pharmaceuticals and nutrients, demonstrating how terpenoids can
amplify the therapeutic effects of other compounds.

**Basil (Ocimum species):** Contains 5% (E)-beta-caryophyllene. Basil extracts
including apigenin, linalool, and ursolic acid exhibit broad-spectrum antiviral
activity. Basil has shown protective gastrointestinal and antibacterial
properties.

**Cinnamon and Oregano:** Cinnamon contains significant (E)-beta-caryophyllene
with well-documented anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties. Mexican
oregano (5% (E)-BCP) inhibits acyclovir-resistant herpes virus in traditional
healing practice.

**Cardamom and Black Caraway:** Cardamom contains cineole, a potential treatment
for vaginal herpes, and protects platelets from aggregation. Black caraway (8%
(E)-BCP) demonstrates potent antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal
properties.

## Flavonoids: Beyond Basic Nutrition

Flavonoids are polyphenolic compounds found in many plants, including cannabis.
Blesching's work references several flavonoid-containing herbs that complement
cannabinoid therapy:

### Apigenin and Ursolic Acid

Found in basil and numerous other herbs, apigenin showed the highest activity
against HSV-2 among basil's antiviral compounds. Ursolic acid showed the
strongest activity against HSV-1. Both are flavonoid-related compounds with
anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and potential anti-cancer properties.
