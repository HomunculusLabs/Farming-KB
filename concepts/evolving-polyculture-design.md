# Evolving Polyculture Design

Polyculture design is the art and science of assembling many plant and animal species into productive, self-maintaining systems. The process of evolving a polyculture begins with thorough site planning and earthworks, proceeds through strategic species placement, and matures into a complex [[cover-crop]] mix: nasturtium, Dolichos, Haifa clover, broad bean (fava), buckwheat, Umbelliferae (dill, fennel), lupin, vetch, dun peas, chilies, pigeon pea, or any useful species suitable to the climate. The aim is to completely carpet and overshade the ground within the first 18-20 months of growth.

Dense plantings should be grass or hay mulched using monsoon grasses and swamp grasses initially, later replaced by tops of arrowroot, comfrey, banana, and Acacia. Shade-loving species such as coffee and dry taro can then be placed in any open spots. Paths for access, openings for annual crops, bee plants on edges, flowers, and fire-resistant ground covers such as comfrey, Tradescantia, Impatiens, and succulents are added over time, while fruit trees are kept grass-free and mulched by cutting crowded Acacia and banana.

It is far better to occupy a quarter hectare thoroughly than to scatter trees over 2 hectares — production is higher, maintenance is less, moisture is conserved, and frost is excluded.

## Species Richness and Zonation

Species richness can be very complex and dense near the village or home, simplifying to well-tested high-yield species as distance increases. In a complex village situation, the potential species inventory spans 195-360 total species:

## The Fiscal vs. Nutritional Optimum

A critical insight for polyculture design is that the optimal number of species depends on the evaluation criterion. Fiscal (dollar) return peaks at approximately 6-8 species in a system, while nutritional-total yield peaks at 50-100 species well-distributed over all seasons. These two factors — extensive-intensive and fiscal-nutritional — must be defined by the designer for their specific needs and will profoundly affect design outcomes.

What emerges is a sensible zonation: species richness concentrated close to the home, with fewer species of high cash value as the system extends outward. Gardens serve as trial areas for the outer zones, testing new species and techniques without sacrificing large amounts of energy and capital.

## Seven Factors Determining Polyculture Complexity

1. **Labor**: The number of people responsible for managing one or two crops or animal species
2. **Zoning**: The proximity of the complex to village or settlement
3. **Fiscal economics**: The relative cost-benefit balance of increasing inputs to optimum levels
4. **Plant guilds**: The need for effective harmonious ecological assemblies
5. **Marketing and processing**: Whether systems can cope with complex products
6. **Total area controlled**: Larger areas demand increasing simplicity at cost to guild complexity
7. **System maturity**: Older systems provide more niches; younger systems produce more regular product

## Planning the Whole Site

Even in established polycultures, regular re-survey with attention to key factors is valuable:

- Main access and harvesting ways
- Earth-shaping for rainwater harvest and specific crops
- Sufficiency of mulch
- Best water and irrigation strategies
- Better village planning
- Improved or more sophisticated site processing for market

On a new site, the priority sequence is: (1) Assess market, future, prices, processing potential, labor, legal systems, social necessities, and local self-reliance needs; (2) Analyse soils and necessary nutrients; (3) Plan ground layout, windbreaks, access, and water; (4) Plan and carry out essential earthworks; (5) Establish nursery with selected varietal forms; (6) Commence broadscale placements with or after windbreak and nurse crop; (7) Continue with constant assessment, consultation, feedback, and innovative trials, filling niches as they evolve.

## Pest and Disease Management

Permaculture pest management relies on adding species to the polyculture that regulate problem organisms. Powerful natural biocides derived from Chrysanthemum species (pyrethrins), Derris species (rotenone), and the neem tree ([[azadirachta-indica]]) are harmless, short-lived, and fully biodegradable. A few of these plants in home gardens and small clumps in crop provide ready insect control for both terrestrial and aquatic systems.

Broadscale mosquito control can combine fats or oils (lecithin), neem oil as poison, and [[bacillus-thuringiensis]] as an infective agent, all assisted by small fish and insect predators such as notonectids (backswimmers) in open water systems.

Ground foragers — chickens, pigs, cattle, large tortoises — eat fallen fruit and larval insect infestations. Leaf foragers — birds, frogs — attend to canopy infestations, as do small skink lizards. Some lizards (Tiliqua) forage for snails and slugs at ground level, as do ducks. Tropical land crabs seek larval insects in mulch. Even problematic kikuyu grass is controlled by domestic guinea pigs on range, freeing trees from grass competition while providing manures.

For soil nematodes in tree crops, marigolds (Tagetes) reduce or eliminate populations, while Crotolaria as a leguminous green crop traps nematodes in its root mycelia. Mulch and green manures buffer fungal pest effects by hosting fungal predators.

## Animal Integration in Polycultures

The main function of animal species in the tropics is to recycle plant wastes and help control rampant ground cover growth. Usual domestic species include pigs, chickens, geese, guinea pigs, rabbits, pigeons, milking goats, cattle, and buffalo, with horses and bullocks for draught. In palm groves, chickens control rhinoceros beetle larvae, guinea pigs and geese reduce grass competition, pythons control rats and mice, owls serve as the best rodent predators, and bees provide pollination — all while contributing manure and additional yields.

Where predators threaten poultry, special housing or pigeons in safe elevated roosts may be the only solution. Electric fences in tropical areas are often nullified by rank grass growth and become inoperative against python, pig, bandicoots, and foxes. Fence lines must therefore be planted with dense perennial ground carpets to exclude grasses, or overshaded by tree canopies.

## Coconut and Palm-Dominant Polycultures

Planning a coconut or palm-dominant polyculture involves several themes: structure and zoning, species selection, patterning, economics, reworking old plantations, and the effects of plantation monoculture. Palm groves provide sheltered and shaded aspects for both intercrop and livestock, creating a layered production system where every vertical niche contributes to total yield.

## Key Principles

- **Start with earthworks**: Water storage and soil loosening precede all planting
- **Dense initial planting**: Carpet the ground within 18 months to suppress weeds and conserve moisture
- **Small area, thorough occupation**: A quarter hectare well-managed outperforms 2 hectares scattered
- **Know your optimum**: 6-8 species for cash, 50-100 for nutrition — design accordingly
- **Zone for complexity**: Rich polycultures near home, simplified high-value systems further out
- **Use biological pest control**: Neem, pyrethrum, derris, and integrated livestock replace synthetic chemicals
- **Integrate animals at every level**: Chickens, pigs, geese, and guinea pigs recycle waste and control pests
- **Gardens are test grounds**: Try new species close to home before extending to outer zones

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
Field trials provide essential data for validating theoretical approaches and refining methodologies.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.
Peer-reviewed publications and practitioner reports contribute complementary perspectives.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.

## Integration Strategies
Successful implementation often draws on multiple complementary approaches working in concert.
Scale-appropriate solutions range from backyard gardens to broadacre agricultural systems.
Knowledge sharing between practitioners accelerates collective learning and refinement of methods.
Regional networks and demonstration sites play crucial roles in technology transfer.

## Implementation Notes
Start with small-scale trials before expanding to larger operations.
Maintain detailed records of conditions, inputs, and outcomes for iterative refinement.
Regular review and adjustment of strategies based on observed results ensures continuous improvement.
