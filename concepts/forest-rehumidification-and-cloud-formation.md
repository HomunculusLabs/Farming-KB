# Forest Rehumidification and Cloud Formation

## How Forests Recycle Water

As clouds move inland from the sea, they carry water **mostly evaporated from forests** rather than the ocean. Forests are cloud-makers from both water vapour evaporated from leaves by day and water transpired as part of life processes.

On high islands, standing clouds cap forested peaks — but **disappear when forests are cut.** The great bridging cloud that once reached from the forests of Maui to the island of Kahoolawe vanished as cutting and cattle destroyed the upper forests on Maui, lifting the cloud cap from Kahoolawe and leaving it a desert island.

### Water Return Rates

A forest can return **75% of its water to air** — in large enough amounts to form new rain clouds. Key comparisons:

| Surface Type | Moisture Returned to Air |
|-------------|------------------------|
| **Forests** | ~75% of water received |
| **Grasslands** | ~37% of water received |
| **Bare ground** | ~7.5% of water received |

Forested areas return **ten times as much moisture as bare ground** and **twice as much as grasslands.** The release of water from trees and other plants accounts for **half or more of all moisture returned to air** globally.

A large evergreen tree such as *[[eucalyptus-globulus]]* may transpire **3,600–4,500 litres of water per day.** With sixty or so trees per hectare, many tens of thousands of litres are returned to air to become clouds. It was this mechanism that Mussolini exploited to pump dry the Pontine marshes of Italy.

### The Amazon Example

The Amazon rainforest demonstrates the water recycling principle at continental scale:

- **75% of water** returned by trees to air (25% evaporated from leaf surfaces, 50% transpired)
- **25% of rainfall** infiltrates soil and eventually reaches streams
- The Amazon discharges **44% of all rain falling**, meaning the remainder is locked in forest tissue or returns to air
- **Twice as much rain falls** over the forests than is available from incoming air
- The forest produces **50% of its own rain** through continuous recycling

### Cloud Chemistry

Clouds forming above forests are mixtures of oceanic and forest water vapour, distinguishable by **isotope analysis.** Forest water vapour contains more organic nucleii and plant nutrients than pure oceanic water. Oxygen isotopes are measured to determine a forest's contribution to any cloud system.

## Sap Flow Mechanics

Vogel (1981), applying the principle of continuity of fluids to trees, calculates that sap may rise in a young oak **fifty times as fast as leaves transpire** — needing only 7% of total trunk area as conductive tissue, with actual sap speed of **1 cm/sec.** This means only about one-fiftieth of the xylem is conducting sap at any time, with most xylem cells containing either air or stationary sap. The tree likely moves water in **pulsed stages** rather than continuous streamflow.

## Effects on Snow and Meltwater

Although trees intercept some snow, their net effect is overwhelmingly positive for water storage:

- Shrubs and trees **entrap snow at clump edges** and hold **75–95% of snowfall in shade**
- Melting is **delayed 2–10 days** compared with bare ground, producing gradual meltwater release
- Of trapped snow within trees, most melts into groundwater; on open ground, snow may **sublime directly to air** without productive effect

On high cold uplands (continental interiors of the USA, Mt. Ararat region in Turkey), thin skeins of winter snow either blow off bald uplands or sublime directly to water vapour — in neither case producing streams. Even a thin belt of trees entraps large quantities of driven snow in drifts, creating a protracted release of meltwater to river sources.

### Case Study: Pyramid Lake, Nevada

When forests were cleared for mine timber in 1846 at Pyramid Lake, Nevada, the streams **ceased to flow** and lake levels fell. Combined with river diversion and irrigation, whole lakes rich with fish and waterfowl became dustbowls (including Lake Winnemucca). The Cuiuidika'a (Paiute) Indians lost their fish, waterfowl, and freshwater in less than 100 years.

## Implications for Design

These findings make clear that:

- **Drought in one area may relate directly to deforestation upwind**
- Saving all remaining forest is urgent
- Planting trees on hills facing the sea increases condensation and precipitation
- The relationship between trees and weather is definitively established — forests are essential climate infrastructure, not optional scenery

## Ecological Significance
The principles described in relation to forest rehumidification and cloud formation
are fundamental to understanding how natural systems maintain equilibrium.
Forests and vegetation play critical roles in water cycling, temperature
regulation, and atmospheric processes that sustain regional climate patterns.

## Practical Applications
These concepts apply directly to [[permaculture-design]], agroforestry systems,
and watershed management. Understanding the interactions between vegetation,
water, and atmosphere enables designers to create more resilient and productive
landscapes that work with natural processes rather than opposing them.
