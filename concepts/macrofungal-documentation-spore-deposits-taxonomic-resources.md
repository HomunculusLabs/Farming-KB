# Macrofungal Documentation, Spore Deposits, and Taxonomic Resources

## Describing Macromorphological Features (continued)

Accurate documentation of macromorphological features is fundamental to [[fungal-taxonomy]] and biodiversity assessment. Proper equipment ensures consistent, reproducible descriptions.

### Essential Equipment

The minimum field and laboratory kit for describing macrofungal features includes:

- **Writing instruments**: pencil or pen with permanent ink (pencil preferred for wet conditions)
- **Cutting tools**: pocket knife with a sharp thin blade; single-edge razor blades for fine sectioning
- **Optical aids**: hand lens (10×–20×) or dissecting microscope for detailed surface examination
- **Measurement**: millimeter rule for recording dimensions of sporocarps and their parts
- **Chemical tests**: spot plate and reagents for macrochemical tests (see below)

### Key Reference Works

1. **Dictionary of the Fungi** (Kirk et al. 2001) — comprehensive nomenclatural and taxonomic reference
2. **How to Identify Mushrooms to Genus: Macroscopic Features** (Largent 1986) — systematic guide to macroscopic characters used in generic-level identification
3. **Modern Genera** (Largent and Baroni 1988) — updated treatment of genera with emphasis on modern classification

### Standardized Color Notation

Consistent use of standardized color names is essential for meaningful communication of macromorphological descriptions. Common color guides include:

- **Ridgway (1912)** — *Color Standards and Color Nomenclature*; historically important
- **Kornerup and Wanscher (1978)** — *Methuen Handbook of Colour*; widely used in [[mycology]]
- **Munsell (1966)** — *Munsell Book of Color*; the most comprehensive system, but expensive
- **Rayner (1970)** — *A Mycological Colour Chart*; designed specifically for fungal specimens
- **Maerz and Paul (1950)** — *A Dictionary of Color*; general reference
- **Kelly (1965)** — *Universal Color Language and Dictionary of Names*

Most guides are out of print. The Munsell system is most comprehensive but costly; select one system and use it consistently.

## Color Photography

Photographs — whether slides, prints, or digital images — are extremely valuable for documenting macromorphological features and supplementing written descriptions. Field photography is time-consuming and subject to variable lighting and background conditions; a laboratory or studio setup provides greater control and is often a practical alternative.

### Magnification and Equipment

- A minimum of **1:2 magnification** is required for adequate documentation
- **1:1 magnification or greater** is necessary for very small taxa; achieved using bellows or extension tubes
- For digital cameras, **macro capability** and **aperture priority control** are the most important features

### Lighting and Composition

Daylight-type film with sunlight or electronic flash produces the most accurate color. Ring flash, multiple flash units, or bounce flash eliminates the harsh "shot-at-night" appearance of single on-camera flash. Photograph specimens against a **black or neutral gray background** to highlight diagnostic features. Each specimen should be photographed to include:

- **All developmental stages** (buttons, immature, mature, senescent)
- **Sporocarp cut lengthwise** to reveal internal anatomy (context, lamellae attachment, stipe interior)
- **Top view of the pileus** showing surface features
- **Underside view** showing lamellae or pore edges
- **Pie-shaped sections** for large sporocarps that cannot be photographed whole

## Testing for Macrochemical Color Reactions

Spot tests employing chemical reagents have been used for decades in fungal identification and classification. They provide rapid, field-applicable data that can distinguish otherwise similar taxa.

### Particularly Useful Groups

- **Ramaria** (coral fungi) — reagent responses are important diagnostic characters
- **Russula** — color reactions to iron salts, KOH, and other reagents aid species delimitation

Key references: Romagnesi (1967), Marr et al. (1986), Singer (1986b).

### Methodology and Practical Considerations

1. Place reagents on **detached pieces** of the sporocarp (never on the intact specimen)
2. Note **color changes** immediately and over time
3. Use **no more than one reagent per sporocarp piece** to avoid confounding results
4. Record **both positive and negative results** — a negative reaction is itself informative

Testing every specimen in a large biodiversity program is extremely time-consuming; prioritize key taxa where reagent responses are taxonomically informative. Recipes and procedures are available in Marr et al. (1986) and Singer (1986b). **Safety measures** are essential: many reagents are corrosive, toxic, or otherwise dangerous.

## Obtaining Spore Deposits

The primary purpose of obtaining a [[spore]] deposit is to determine **spore color**, a critical character in fungal classification.

### Materials and Setup

- White paper, clean glass slides, or aluminum foil as deposition surfaces
- Humid chamber (cigar box, plastic container, or aluminum foil wrap)
- Temperature range: **10–15°C**
- A moistened paper towel can increase humidity; the sporocarp should **not** be in water

### Techniques by Fungal Group

**Coral fungi (Ramaria and allies):** Place white paper directly beneath the branches before wrapping the specimen for transport. Spores shed naturally during handling.

**Agarics:** Remove the cap from the stipe and place it gill-side down on the deposition surface; alternatively, cut a hole or X-slit in the paper and insert the stipe until lamellae contact the paper. For large basidiomes, only a portion is needed.

### Quality Assurance

- Only **mature sporocarps** produce a spore deposit, confirming maturity
- **Label each spore deposit** with the corresponding collection number

## Micromorphological Features

A **compound microscope** is required for examining spore characteristics, pileipellis anatomy, cystidia, and other microscopic structures.

### Fresh vs. Dried Specimens

Assessment in fresh condition is ideal to capture features lost on drying, but complete fresh micromorphological description is impractical during active collecting trips. Well-preserved dried specimens yield data comparable to fresh material; study is normally conducted between trips and off-season. Key references: Watling (1968), Largent et al. (1977), Singer (1986b).

### Ephemeral Features

Some features are lost irreversibly upon drying and must be assessed fresh, e.g., the **fluorescent ascus ring** in *Valsaria*, visible only with Calcafluor stain on fresh material.

## Taxonomic Resources for Identification

### General Strategy

The first step in identifying an unknown fungus is **assignment to family**, which directs the researcher to appropriate specialized monographs and keys. General references include **Fungus Flora of Venezuela** (Dennis 1970), **The Fungi** (Ainsworth et al. 1973a,b), and **Fungi of Switzerland** (Breitenbach and Kränzlin). **Dictionary of the Fungi** (Kirk et al. 2001) provides references to taxa at all ranks.

### Basidiomycetes: Identification Resources

**Nonagaric fungi on wood:** Eriksson and Ryvarden (1973–1976), Eriksson et al. (1978, 1984), Gilbertson (1974), Jülich and Stalpers (1980), Gilbertson and Ryvarden (1986). Burt (1914–1926, reprinted 1966) covers resupinate/erect forms of North America and the Caribbean but uses outdated concepts. **Temperate keys do not always work for tropical species.**

**Tremellales:** McNabb (1964–1973) for North America; Breitenbach and Kränzlin (1984) for Europe; Dennis (1970), Lowy, and Ryvarden for the Neotropics.

**Polypores:** Gilbertson and Ryvarden (1986) for North America/neotropical; Ryvarden and Johansen (1980) for East Africa; Domanski (1972, 1973); Ryvarden (2000); Lodge et al. (2001).

**Pleurotoid fungi:** Pegler (1983b).

**Gasteromycetes:** Breitenbach and Kränzlin (1986), Coker and Couch (1928), Miller and Miller (1988), Pegler et al. (1995), Smith and Smith (1973); Clathraceae: Dring (1980); Nidulariaceae: Brodie and Dennis (1954).

**Agaricales:** The most extensive identification literature of any fungal order. Annotated catalog: Watling and Watling (1980). North American field guides: Arora (1987), Bessette et al. (1997, 2000), Lincoff (1981), McKnight and McKnight (1987), Miller (1972), Phillips (1991). European: Bas et al. (1988–1995), Breitenbach and Kränzlin (1991–2000), Courtecuisse and Duhem (1995), Moser (1978). Regional: Horak (1979) for Tierra del Fuego; Teng (1996) for China; Nishida (1989) for Neotropics.

**Key strategy:** Begin with artificial keys (Dennis 1970; Largent and Baroni 1988), then natural keys in Singer (1986b) if unsatisfactory.

**Tropical agaric species:** Corner (1994), Dennis (1970), Pegler (1977–1989), Singer (1970–1989).

### Ascomycetes: Identification Resources

**General works:** Ainsworth et al. (1973a), Breitenbach and Kränzlin (1984), Dennis (1970, 1978).

**Loculoascomycetes and pyrenomycetes:** von Arx and Müller (1954, 1975), Barr (1987, 1990), Hanlin (1990), Luttrell (1973), Müller and von Arx (1962, 1973), Sivanesan (1984).

**Wood-inhabiting ascomycetes:** Aptroot (1991, 1995), Barr (1972–1990c).

**Xylariaceous fungi:** Dennis (1956–1970), Ju and Rogers (1996), Laessøe et al., Petrini (1992), Rappaz (1995), Rogers et al. (1987–1988), San Martín-González and Rogers (1989–1993).

**Discomycetes:** Breitenbach and Kränzlin (1984), Dennis (1970, 1978), Gamundi (1979), Le Gal (1953, 1959), Korf (1973), Rifai (1968), Seaver (1942, 1951).
