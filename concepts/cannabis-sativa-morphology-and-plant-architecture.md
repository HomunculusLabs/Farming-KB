# Cannabis Sativa Morphology and Plant Architecture

## Overview

Cannabis sativa L. exhibits a distinctive morphological profile that
sets it apart from other Cannabis subspecies. Understanding its
structural characteristics is fundamental to successful cultivation,
breeding, and strain identification. The sativa phenotype expresses
itself through tall stature, narrow leaflet morphology, extended
internodal spacing, and a characteristic flowering pattern that demands
specific growing approaches.

## Growth Habit and Stature

Sativa-dominant plants are renowned for their considerable vertical
growth. Under optimal conditions, pure sativa varieties can reach
heights of 3-6 meters outdoors, with some equatorial landraces
stretching even taller. This vigorous upward growth is driven by
extended internodal spacing between branch pairs, creating an open,
airy plant structure.

Indoors, sativa genetics present a significant challenge due to their
height. Plants can double or even triple in size during the stretch
phase after switching to a flowering photoperiod. Growers must plan
canopy management carefully, often employing techniques like Screen of
Green (SCROG), super-cropping, or strategic topping to control
vertical expansion.

The main stem of a sativa plant tends to be flexible rather than rigid.
This flexibility is an adaptation to windy tropical and subtropical
environments. The branches likewise exhibit a more pliant character,
bending and swaying rather than snapping under wind stress. This is
particularly relevant for outdoor growers in exposed locations.

## Leaf Morphology

Sativa leaflets are characteristically narrow and long, often described
as "finger-like." A typical sativa leaf may have 7-13 long, slender
leaflets per fan leaf, compared to the broader, shorter leaflets of

## Flower Structure

Sativa flowers are typically elongated, less dense, and more airy than
indica flowers. Individual calyxes tend to be larger but more loosely
packed, giving sativa buds a "foxtail" or "spindly" appearance. This
loose structure is actually advantageous in humid climates, as it
allows better airflow through the bud and reduces the likelihood of
[[query-how-do-i-prevent-and-treat-bud-rot-on-cannabis]] (botrytis cinerea).

The reduced flower density means sativa buds often weigh less per unit
volume compared to indica buds. However, the extended flowering time
(10-16 weeks for many pure sativas) allows for sustained resin
production, and the total trichome coverage can be exceptional.

Pistil coloration in sativa varieties often progresses from white
through orange to reddish-brown as flowers mature. The extended
flowering window means growers must monitor [[cannabis-trichome-development-cannabinoid-biosynthesis-sites]]
carefully to harvest at the optimal window.

## Trichome Distribution

Sativa plants tend to produce abundant capitate-stalked trichomes,
particularly on the calyxes and small sugar leaves surrounding flower
sites. The trichome density can be extraordinary in well-grown
specimens, with a frosty appearance developing during mid-to-late
flowering. Sativa resin often has a more fluid, less viscous
consistency than indica resin, which some hashish makers find produces
a different character in solventless extracts.

## Environmental Adaptations

The morphological features of Cannabis sativa reflect millions of years
of adaptation to tropical and subtropical environments. The tall
stature, narrow leaflets, open branching, loose flower structure, and
deep root system all serve survival functions in warm, humid, often
windy climates with well-drained soils and distinct wet and dry
seasons.
