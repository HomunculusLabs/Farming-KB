# Mushroom Bioremediation and Mycofiltration

[[paul-stamets]]' Growing Gourmet and Medicinal Mushrooms introduced
two groundbreaking concepts for using fungi in environmental
management: bioremediation (using fungi to detoxify contaminated
environments) and mycofiltration (using mycelial mats as biological
filtration membranes). Both concepts have since grown into major
fields of applied mycology.

## Mushrooms and Toxic Wastes

In heavily industrialized areas, soils are often contaminated with
a wide variety of pollutants, particularly petroleum-based
compounds, polychlorinated biphenols (PCBs), heavy metals,
pesticide-related compounds, and even radioactive wastes. Mushrooms
grown in polluted environments can absorb toxins directly into their
tissues. As a result, mushrooms grown in these environments should
not be eaten.

### Cautionary Example

Mushrooms picked near Chernobyl were radioactive enough to set off
Geiger counter alarms at customs. Unfortunately, most toxins are
not so readily detected. Mushrooms grown from toxic wastes are best
not eaten as residual toxins may be concentrated within the
mushroom tissues.

## Bioremediation with Fungi

A number of fungi can be used to detoxify contaminated environments,
a process called "bioremediation." The [[white-rot-fungi]] (particularly
[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]]) and brown rot fungi (notably Gloeophyllum
species) are the most widely used. Most of these wood-rotters produce
lignin peroxidases and cellulases which have unusually powerful
degradative properties.

These extracellular enzymes have evolved to break down plant fiber,
primarily lignin-cellulose, the structural component in woody plants,
into simpler forms. By happenstance, these same enzymes also reduce
recalcitrant hydrocarbons and other man-made toxins. Given the number
of industrial pollutants that are hydrocarbon-based, fungi are
excellent candidates for toxic waste cleanup.

### Current and Prospective Applications

Current and prospective future uses include:
- Detoxification of PCBs (polychlorobiphenols)
- Degradation of PCP (pentachlorophenol)
- Oil spill remediation
- Pesticide/herbicide residue breakdown
- Ameliorating the impact of radioactive wastes

### Advantages of [[fungal-bioremediation]]

Bioremediation of toxic waste sites is especially attractive because:
- The environment is treated in situ (soils do not have to be
  hauled away)
- Extraordinary expense of handling, transportation, and storage
  is eliminated
- Complex hydrocarbons are reduced into elemental compounds that
  pose no threat and could even be considered "fertilizer"

### The Higher Fungi Advantage

Dozens of bioremediation companies have formed, but most look to
imperfect fungi. The higher fungi should not be disqualified for
bioremediation just because they produce fruitbodies. The most
vigorous rotters described in the book include Ganoderma species
and [[growing-gourmet-phoenix-oyster-pulmonarius-cultivation]] mushrooms.

## Mycofiltration

### The Mycelial Network as a Filtration Membrane

The mycelium is a fabric of interconnected, interwoven strands of
cells. A colony can be the size of a half-dollar or many acres. A
cubic inch of soil can host up to a mile of mycelium. This organism
can be physically separated and yet behave as one.

The exquisite lattice-like structure of the mushroom mycelium,
often referred to as the mycelial network, is perfectly designed as
a filtration membrane. Each colony extends long, complex chains of
cells that fork repeatedly in matrix-like fashion, spreading to
geographically defined borders. The mushroom mycelium, being a
voracious forager for carbon and nitrogen, secretes extracellular
enzymes that unlock organic complexes. The newly freed nutrients
are then selectively absorbed directly through the cell walls.

### How Mycofiltration Works

In the rainy season, water carries nutritional particles through
this filtration membrane, including bacteria, which often become a
food source for the mushroom mycelium. The resulting downstream
effluent is cleansed of not only carbon/nitrogen-rich compounds but
also bacteria, in some cases nematodes, and legions of other
micro-organisms.

### Nematode-Trapping by Oyster Mushrooms

Only recently has the classic saprophyte, the voracious Oyster
mushroom, been found to be parasitic against nematodes (Thorn and
Barron, 1984). The extracellular enzymes act like an anesthetic,
stunning the nematodes and allowing the mycelium to invade directly
into their immobilized bodies. This predatory behavior is remarkable
for a saprophytic mushroom.

### Agricultural Applications

The use of mycelium as a mycofilter is being studied for removal of
biological contaminants from surface water passing directly into
sensitive watersheds. By placing sawdust implanted with mushroom
mycelium in drainage basins downstream from farms raising livestock,
the mycelium acts as a sieve which traps fecal bacteria and ameliorates
the impact of a farm's nitrogen-rich outflow into aquatic ecosystems.

This concept is incorporated into an integrated farm model and
explored in the

## Catastrophia: Nature as a Substrate Supplier

Many saprophytic fungi benefit from catastrophic events in the
forests. When hurricane-force winds rage across woodlands, enormous
masses of dead debris are generated. Every time catastrophes occur
-- hurricanes, tornadoes, volcanoes, floods, even earthquakes -- the
resulting dead wood becomes a stream of inexpensive substrate
materials. The cost of mushroom production is underwritten by natural
disasters.
