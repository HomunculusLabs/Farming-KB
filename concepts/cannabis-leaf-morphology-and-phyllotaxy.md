# Cannabis Leaf Morphology and Phyllotaxy

Cannabis leaf morphology is one of the most visually distinctive features of the
genus and serves as a primary identifier for the plant. The leaves of Cannabis
sativa are compound and palmate, meaning they consist of multiple leaflets
radiating from a single point at the end of the petiole (leaf stem). This
characteristic fan shape is immediately recognizable and varies in complexity
throughout the life cycle of the plant. Understanding leaf morphology and
phyllotaxy (the arrangement of leaves on the stem) is essential for cultivators
and breeders who wish to identify [[cannabis-phenotype-and-genotype-guide]] and predict plant behavior.

## Cotyledons and Early Leaf Development

The first leaves to emerge from a germinating Cannabis seed are the cotyledons
(seed leaves). These are slightly unequal in size, narrowed at the base and
rounded or blunt at the tip. The hypocotyl (embryonic stem) ranges from 1 to 10
centimeters in length and straightens to push the cotyledons above the soil
surface. About 10 centimeters above the cotyledons, the first true leaves
arise. These appear as a pair of oppositely oriented single leaflets, each with
a distinct petiole, rotated one-quarter turn from the cotyledons.

Subsequent pairs of leaves arise in opposite formation and a characteristically
patterned leaf sequence develops. The second pair of leaves typically has three
leaflets, the third pair has five, and so on up to a maximum of eleven
leaflets. Occasionally the first pair of true leaves will produce three leaflets
each rather than one, and the second pair will produce five leaflets each. This
progressive increase in leaflet number is one of the defining developmental
patterns of Cannabis.

## Compound-Pinnate Leaf Structure

The mature Cannabis leaf is described as digitate or palmately compound,
meaning that all leaflets attach at a single point rather than along an
extended rachis (as would be seen in a pinnately compound leaf like a rose or
ash). Each leaflet has serrated (toothed) margins and a prominent central
vein with branching lateral veins. The leaflets are typically lanceolate to
elliptical in shape, tapering to a point at the tip. The upper surface of
the leaflets is darker green and may be slightly glossy, while the lower
surface is lighter with visible venation.

The serrations along the leaflet margins vary in depth and frequency among
different [[cannabis-landrace-strains]]. Some strains produce very
coarsely serrated leaflets while others have fine, closely spaced teeth. This
trait can be used as a diagnostic character in [[cannabis-sex-expression-hermaphrodite-breeding-clarke]]
programs, as leaf serration pattern is often genetically determined.

## Phyllotaxy: Leaf Arrangement Patterns

Phyllotaxy refers to the geometric arrangement of leaves on the stem. In
Cannabis, phyllotaxy changes dramatically at the onset of flowering, providing
a reliable indicator of the plant's developmental stage.

### Vegetative Phase: Decussate Arrangement

During the vegetative growth phase, Cannabis exhibits a decussate (opposite)
phyllotaxy. This means that pairs of leaves arise at each node, positioned
directly across from one another on opposite sides of the stem. Each successive
pair is rotated approximately 90 degrees from the pair below, creating a
spiral pattern when viewed from above. This arrangement maximizes light
interception by minimizing overlap between successive leaf pairs.

### Transition to Flowering: Phyllotactic Reversal

Before the start of flowering, the phyllotaxy reverses and the number of
leaflets per leaf decreases. The leaves become progressively simpler, with
fewer leaflets, until a small single leaflet appears below each pair of calyxes.
Simultaneously, the arrangement shifts from decussate (opposite) to alternate
(staggered), and usually remains alternate throughout the floral stages
regardless of sexual type. This phyllotactic reversal is among the earliest
visible signs that [[clarke-marijuana-botany-flowering-and-photoperiodism]]
has been initiated.

## Leaf Shape Genetics: Webbed vs. Compound-Pinnate

Variation in leaf shape among Cannabis strains provides one of the clearest
examples of Mendelian inheritance in the genus. Robert Connell Clarke
documented a classic breeding experiment involving two true-breeding
populations: one with webbed leaves and one with normal compound-pinnate
leaves.

In this system, the gene for compound-pinnate leaves (designated W) is
dominant over the gene for webbed leaves (designated w). When a cross is made
between a true-breeding compound-pinnate parent (WW) and a true-breeding
webbed-leaf parent (ww), all first-generation (F1) offspring exhibit
compound-pinnate leaves because the dominant W gene masks the recessive w gene.

When two F1 offspring (Ww) are crossed, the second generation (F2) shows the
classic Mendelian ratio of approximately 3:1, with three plants showing
compound-pinnate leaves for every one showing webbed leaves. This demonstrates
that leaf shape is controlled by a single gene pair with complete dominance.

If incomplete dominance were involved, the F1 generation would show
intermediate leaf forms, and the F2 ratio would shift to 1:2:1
(compound-pinnate : intermediate : webbed). The back-cross technique, crossing
an F1 plant back to one of the parental (P1) types, produces a predictable 1:1
ratio that helps breeders determine the genotype of parental plants.

## Leaf Traits as Breeding Markers

Leaf morphology serves several practical purposes in Cannabis breeding and
cultivation. Leaf shape, size, serration pattern, and color are all visible
phenotypic markers that can be tracked alongside more subtle traits like
potency, aroma, and maturation time. Because leaf traits are often controlled
by single gene pairs with clear dominance relationships, they provide
accessible entry points for understanding the [[cannabis-genetics-mendelian-breeding]] of more complex traits.
