## Clearing Uncultivated Ground

Starting a brand new garden, taking over a weed-infested plot, or
incorporating a new area all require making the ground as clean as
possible from the outset. There is no such thing as an organic weedkiller,
and the prolonged use of chemical weedkillers has a very damaging effect
on soil organisms. At one research station, soil that had been regularly
treated with paraquat/diquat was found to contain no earthworms and most
other beneficial soil organisms were either reduced or absent. The organic
approach uses physical methods that protect [[comparison-mulch-types-and-effects-vs-landscape-fabric-alternatives]] allows water through while
blocking light. For ornamental areas, a layer of coarse gravel at least
2in (5cm) thick provides an attractive and effective weed barrier. Bark
chippings also work well and last for several years.

## Step Three: Sowing the Lawn Area

If you plan to establish a lawn, the area can be sown immediately after
digging. Even if weeds reappear, regular mowing will eventually eradicate
all but rosetted types like dandelions and daisies. These can be
controlled by dropping a pinch of table salt into the centre of each
rosette or by digging them out with a penknife. Several applications may
be needed, but persistence pays off.

## Step Four: Planting a Cleaning Crop

Areas planned for ornamental borders, vegetable plots, or fruit gardens
should be planted in the first year with a "cleaning crop." Potatoes are
ideal because they help clean the soil of weeds while producing a useful
harvest. Potatoes have two great virtues as a cleaning crop.

First, their cultivation requires turning over the soil three times:
once at planting, again when earthing up, and finally at harvest. This
repeated cultivation brings weed roots to the surface and disturbs
established perennial weeds. Second, their dense canopy of leaves excludes
light from any weeds attempting to compete. The combination of light
starvation and soil disturbance encourages most weeds to give up.

## Dealing with Persistent Weeds

Some weeds survive even a potato crop. Climbing weeds like bindweed can
reach sunlight through the densest canopy by twining around competing
plants. Their root systems store enormous food reserves. In the second
year, these will need targeted attention. Systematic digging to remove
every piece of root, combined with continued cultivation and never
allowing the weed to produce top growth, will eventually exhaust the
root reserves.
