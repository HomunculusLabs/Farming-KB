# Truffle Hunting Methods and Truffle Culture

## Overview

Truffle hunting is one of the most distinctive and picturesque practices in all of [[mycelium]] is growing — by several indicators:

- **Reduced herbage** under the borders of trees or around the edges of clumps of bushes — the herbage is noticeably less abundant than elsewhere, possibly because the truffle mycelium is destructive to many plants
- **Soil moisture** — conditions are most favorable for detection when the soil is merely moist; very dry weather or drizzling rain reduce the number found
- **Soil type** — calcareous, clay-rich soils in association with appropriate tree species

## Truffle Culture and Exploitation

### Artificial Establishment

Many of the finest truffle areas of France were at one time barren of these fungi, and it is through the perseverance and intelligence of the owners that truffle production was made possible:

1. Trees known to shelter truffles were **planted**
2. **Drainage** was provided
3. **Predatory animals** were shut out
4. **Soil from truffle regions** was brought and scattered upon the land

The old adage, **"plant acorns if you would have truffles,"** has been handed down through several generations, but other conditions must also be considered.

### Reforestation and Government Programs

The growth of the truffle industry in certain departments, particularly Vaucluse, encouraged the government to proceed on a large scale with reforesting many large and barren areas. The work has often yielded handsome returns through the **spontaneous appearance of truffles** in such regions. The reforested areas are typically owned by the communes or villages, and under certain restrictions (or with a tax imposed where truffles are abundant), any villager may become a truffle hunter in the village domain.

### The Market and Rural Life

At wholesale markets such as Carpentras, truffle selling follows a distinctive social rhythm. Hunters troop in at nine o'clock with sacks, baskets, and pouches. Before and after each trade, visits are made to the cafés, and wares are disposed with little loss of time. In the Department of Vaucluse, a regulation limits truffle hunting to four days each week (frequently only three in smaller villages): Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday for hunting; Thursday for market preparation; Friday is market day; Saturday is for recovery.

### The Decline of Mont Ventoux

The Périgord region is a famous truffle ground, yet Carpentras in Vaucluse was for many years foremost in the truffle trade. The village of Bédouin, about eight miles from Carpentras under the shadow of Mt. Ventoux, is the center of one of the chief truffle sections of France. Through some unknown cause, truffles have begun to fail in this region — a matter of regret, as a thorough study of the conditions has not been made by botanists and mycologists.
