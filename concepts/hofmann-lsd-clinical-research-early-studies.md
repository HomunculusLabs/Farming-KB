# Early LSD [[maitake-cancer-clinical-research]] in Hofmann's LSD: My Problem Child

Following [[hofmann-lsd-discovery-bicycle-day]] in April 1943, a remarkably productive period of clinical and psychiatric research unfolded over the subsequent two decades. Hofmann's account in [[hofmann-lsd-discovery-bicycle-day]] documents the key early studies, the development of therapeutic approaches, and the scientific investigations that established LSD as both a research tool and a pharmaceutical agent before its adoption as a recreational substance.

## The First Systematic Human Investigation: Werner A. Stoll (1947)

The first systematic investigation of LSD on human subjects was conducted at the psychiatric clinic of the University of Zurich by Dr. Werner A. Stoll (son of Professor Arthur Stoll). Published in 1947 in the Schweizer Archiv fur Neurologie und Psychiatrie under the title "Lysergsaure-diathylamid, ein Phantasticum aus der Mutterkorngruppe," this foundational study tested both healthy research subjects and schizophrenic patients.

The dosages used -- 0.02 to 0.13 mg -- were substantially lower than Hofmann's 0.25 mg self-experiment, producing predominantly euphoric emotional states rather than the terrifying experience Hofmann had suffered from overdose. The publication classified LSD as a "phantasticum" and provided the first scientific description of all basic features of LSD inebriation.

Stoll also raised the possibility of therapeutic application and noted LSD's extraordinarily high activity, comparable to endogenous trace substances "considered to be responsible for certain mental disorders." He proposed LSD as a research tool for studying the nature of psychoses.

### Stoll's Own Self-Experiment

Stoll also published the first self-experiment by a psychiatrist, describing his experience with 60 micrograms (0.06 mg). After twenty minutes, he felt heaviness in the limbs and ataxia, followed by euphoria. During the "dark experiment" (eyes closed in a darkened room), he experienced:

> "An unprecedented experience of unimaginable intensity that kept increasing in strength... characterized by an unbelievable profusion of optical hallucinations that appeared and vanished with great speed, to make way for countless new images. I saw a profusion of circles, vortices, sparks, showers, crosses, and spirals in constant, racing flux."

The images progressed from elementary forms (rays, bundles, rain, rings, vortices) to organized visions (Gothic vaults, skyscrapers, a Spanish city under a pale blue evening sky, a garden with brilliant colored lights). All images consisted of "countless repetitions of the same elements." Stoll reported feeling "one with all romanticists and dreamers," experiencing "the euphoria and exultation of an artistic vision."

## Animal Research and Toxicology

Before clinical trials could proceed, extensive animal studies were conducted by Dr. Aurelio Cerletti under Professor Rothlin at Sandoz. Key findings across species:

- **Mice**: Motor disturbances and alterations in licking behavior
- **Cats**: Piloerection, salivation, and behavior suggesting hallucinations -- staring anxiously in the air, leaving mice alone, or standing in fear before them
- **Dogs**: Behavior indicating hallucinatory experiences
- **Chimpanzees**: A single dosed individual disrupted the entire cage's "finely coordinated hierarchic tribal order," throwing the community into uproar
- **Spiders**: Web-building altered; at low optimum doses webs were better proportioned than normal, while higher doses produced rudimentary, poorly built webs

### Toxicity Data

The toxicity data revealed an extraordinarily safe substance by pharmaceutical standards:

| Species | LD50 (mg/kg, i.v.) | Effective human dose ratio |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| Mouse | 50-60 | ~50,000-100,000x overdose needed |
| Rat | 16.5 | -- |
| Rabbit | 0.3 | ~300-600x overdose needed |
| Elephant (single case) | 0.06 | -- |

The human effective dose is 0.0003-0.001 mg/kg. Most animals die from lethal doses by respiratory arrest. To Hofmann's knowledge, no human fatalities have resulted directly from LSD poisoning; attributed deaths were accidents or suicides occurring during the mentally disoriented state of intoxication. Chromosome damage and fetal deformity allegations were not supported by subsequent large-scale statistical investigations.

## Sandoz Product Delysid and Its Indications

Sandoz introduced LSD under the trade name Delysid with two formal indications:

### Analytical Psychotherapy
To elicit release of repressed material and provide mental relaxation, particularly in anxiety states and obsessional neuroses. Starting dose: 25 micrograms (1/4 ampoule or 1 tablet), increased by 25 micrograms per weekly session until the optimum dose (usually 50-200 micrograms) was found. Effects set in after 30 to 90 minutes and generally lasted 5 to 12 hours.

### Experimental Studies on Psychoses
By self-administering Delysid, psychiatrists could gain insight into the world of mental patients. It could also induce short-duration "model psychoses" in normal subjects. Normal subjects required 25-75 micrograms (approximately 1 microgram/kg); certain psychoses and chronic alcoholism required 2-4 micrograms/kg.

The antidote was specified as 50 mg chlorpromazine administered intramuscularly.

## Pharmacological Properties

Key findings about LSD's pharmacology:

- Absorbed easily and completely through the gastrointestinal tract
- Distributed rapidly throughout the organism; lowest concentration found in the brain
- Concentrated in midbrain centers regulating emotion
- Maximum organ concentrations at 10-15 minutes post-injection, falling rapidly
- Eliminated primarily (up to 80%) through intestine via liver and bile
- Only 1-10% eliminated as unaltered LSD; remainder as transformation products
- Psychic effects persist even after LSD is no longer detectable, suggesting it triggers ongoing biochemical and neurophysiological mechanisms
- Stimulates sympathetic nervous system centers: pupillary dilatation, increased body temperature, elevated blood sugar
- Serotonin-blocking effect discovered by J. H. Gaddum, though insufficient alone to explain hallucinogenic properties
- Also influences dopamine-connected neurophysiological functions

## Two Therapeutic Approaches

### Psycholytic Therapy

Developed in European clinics, this approach used moderately strong doses of LSD in several successive sessions at regular intervals. The term was coined by Ronald A. Sandison, an English Jungian therapist and pioneer of clinical LSD research. The root "-lysis" signifies dissolution of tension or conflicts in the psyche. LSD experiences were subsequently worked out in group discussions and expression therapy through drawing and painting.

### Psychedelic Therapy

Favored in the United States, this method administered a single very high dose (0.3 to 0.6 mg) after intensive psychological preparation. The goal was to induce a mystical-religious experience through LSD's shock effects, which could then serve as a starting point for personality restructuring. The term "psychedelic" (mind-manifesting or mind-expanding) was introduced by [[humphry-osmond]].

## Key Therapeutic Mechanisms

LSD's therapeutic value derived from properties diametrically opposed to tranquilizers: whereas tranquilizers covered up problems, LSD made them "more exposed and more intensely experienced," leading to clearer recognition and greater susceptibility to treatment. Specific mechanisms included:

- **Ego dissolution**: The accustomed worldview undergoes deep transformation, loosening the I-you barrier and releasing patients from egocentric fixations
- **Reviviscence**: Forgotten or suppressed experiences from earliest childhood reappeared not as ordinary recollection but as true reliving (Jean Delay's distinction between reminiscence and reviviscence)
- **Enhanced suggestibility**: Improved rapport with the therapist
- **Treatment acceleration**: The speed of unconscious material surfacing could shorten therapy duration, though some psychiatrists considered this a disadvantage

## LSD and the Terminally Ill

A particularly significant application was LSD's use with terminally ill cancer patients whose pain no longer responded to conventional medication. The diminution of pain occurred not through analgesic effect but through psychological dissociation from the body: "patients under the influence of LSD are psychologically so dissociated from their bodies that physical pain no longer penetrates their consciousness."

Pioneers including S. Grof, J. Halifax, E. Kast, S. Cohen, and W. A. Pahnke documented patients who "gained meaningful insights about life and death on their deathbeds as, freed from pain in LSD ecstasy and reconciled to their fate, they faced their earthly demise fearlessly and in peace." This work was summarized in Grof and Halifax's The Human Encounter with Death (1977).

## Key Pioneers

Hofmann names the front-rank researchers in LSD-assisted psychotherapy:

- **United States**: A. K. Busch, W. C. Johnson, S. Cohen, B. Eisner, H. A. Abramson, H. Osmond, A. Hoffer
- **England**: R. A. Sandison
- **Germany**: W. Frederking, H. Leuner
- **Czechoslovakia**: G. Roubicek, S. Grof

## The Transition from Remedy to Inebriant

Hofmann notes that for more than ten years, LSD research proceeded productively within medical and scientific channels. The first non-medicinal self-experiments were conducted by "writers, painters, musicians, and other intellectuals," producing psychedelic art and new insights into creative processes. But by the late 1950s, LSD was "swept up in the huge wave of an inebriant mania" spreading across the Western world, becoming "the number-one inebriating drug" and -- to Hofmann's dismay -- his "problem child."
