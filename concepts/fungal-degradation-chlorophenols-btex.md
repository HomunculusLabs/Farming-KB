# Fungal Degradation of Chlorophenols and BTEX Compounds

Chlorinated monoaromatic compounds and BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene) represent important classes of environmental pollutants. Fungi, particularly [[white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization]], have demonstrated significant ability to degrade these compounds through [[cellulase-enzyme-systems-in-fungi]] that evolved to break down the complex aromatic polymer lignin.

## Chlorophenols as Priority Pollutants

Chlorophenols are widespread in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems and are listed as priority pollutants. Pentachlorophenol (PCP) has been widely used as a wood preservative and biocide, while other chlorophenols serve as precursors for herbicide synthesis. A variety of microorganisms can degrade PCP, primarily through pathways involving dechlorination, hydroxylation, and [[fungal-methylation-oxidative-coupling]].

Fungi are particularly notable because they often tolerate and detoxify chlorophenols at concentrations that are toxic to indigenous bacterial populations. White rot fungi can tolerate PCP at concentrations as high as 500-1000 mg/l, which correspond to levels found in wood treatment plant effluents and contaminated soils. Unlike bacteria, fungi do not normally use PCP as a carbon and energy source but degrade it through fortuitous [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] using wood components like lignocellulose as their primary carbon source.

## PCP Degradation Pathways

The [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] for PCP involves multiple steps and has been extensively studied in Phanerochaete chrysosporium. The pathway involves two parallel routes with cross-pathway steps:

1. **Lignin peroxidase (LiP) oxidation** -- PCP is converted to tetrachlorobenzoquinone through oxidative dechlorination reactions catalyzed by [[fungal-enzyme-systems-xenobiotic-degradation]]

2. **Intracellular reduction** -- Tetrachlorobenzoquinone undergoes intracellular reductive dechlorination reactions

3. **Complete dechlorination** -- Through the combination of extracellular hydroxylation and intracellular reduction, PCP was shown to be completely dechlorinated by purified enzymes from P. chrysosporium

4. **Methylation pathway** -- P. chrysosporium can also methylate PCP to pentachloroanisole (PCA) using its lignin-degrading system. PCA, though less toxic than PCP, is more lipophilic and may bioaccumulate. PCA can subsequently become coupled to humic materials through co-polymerization.

A dual degradation pathway has been proposed in which PCP is converted to 2,3,5,6-tetrachloro-2,5-cyclohexadiene-1,4-dione (THCD) by LiP, which is then degraded by mycelial enzymes, while PCP breakdown also occurs through an initial non-peroxidative step involving intracellular quinone reductases.

## Enzyme Systems in Chlorophenol Degradation

Multiple [[cellulase-enzyme-systems-in-fungi]] participate in chlorophenol degradation:

- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)** -- catalyzes oxidative dechlorination of PCP and other polychlorinated phenols, producing p-benzoquinones. LiP from P. chrysosporium was used to convert PCP to THCD.

- **Manganese peroxidase (MnP)** -- participates in the oxidative steps of PCP degradation, particularly the extracellular hydroxylation reactions

- **Laccase** -- Trichoderma versicolor laccases have demonstrated dechlorination of chlorophenols. Purified laccase from T. versicolor removed PCP from solution, producing acid-stable polymers of molecular weight approximately 80,000

- **Quinone reductases** -- intracellular NAD(P)H-dependent quinone oxidoreductases participate in reductive dechlorination steps. These include 1,4-benzoquinone reductase from P. chrysosporium

- **Phenol O-methyltransferase** -- an S-adenosylmethionine-dependent enzyme that methylates chlorinated phenols, converting them to less toxic but more lipophilic anisole derivatives

Trametes and Phellinus species have shown particular potential as PCP degraders and may be better candidates than Phanerochaete because they are more resistant to PCP and do not accumulate toxic intermediates like pentachloroanisole.

## Other Chlorophenol Congeners

Beyond PCP, several other chlorophenols are considered priority pollutants:

- **2,4,5-Trichlorophenol** -- a precursor for the herbicide 2,4,5-T. P. chrysosporium rapidly degrades this compound under secondary metabolite conditions through cycles of peroxidase-catalyzed oxidative dechlorination and quinone reduction, yielding 1,2,4,5-tetrahydroxybenzene as the key intermediate, which is then ring-cleaved to carbon dioxide

- **2,4-Dichlorophenol** -- a breakdown product of the herbicide 2,4-D. Ectomycorrhizal fungi have demonstrated degradation of 2,4-DCP in both axenic culture and in symbiosis with pine, with better transformation occurring in the symbiotic state

- **2,4,6-Trichlorophenol** -- LiP-catalyzed oxidative dechlorination produces p-benzoquinone intermediates

The fungal degradation pathway for 2,4,5-TCP contrasts with bacterial pathways because all three chlorine atoms are removed before ring cleavage occurs. In bacteria, ring cleavage of chlorocatechol intermediates could generate toxic acylhalide intermediates.

## BTEX Compounds

BTEX compounds (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene) are monoaromatic hydrocarbons found in petroleum-contaminated sites and gasoline spills. While bacterial degradation of BTEX is well documented, fungal contributions are less well characterized but potentially significant.

Key points about BTEX degradation by fungi include:

- The mycoremediation-enzymology|non-specific nature of ligninolytic enzymes allows some white rot fungi to attack monoaromatic compounds

- Degradation of m-toluate has been observed in Pinus sylvestris--Suillus bovinus rhizospheres inoculated with Pseudomonas fluorescens possessing a toluene-degrading plasmid, suggesting that mycorrhizal fungi may facilitate BTEX degradation through [[singh-fungal-consortia-synergistic-biodegradation]]

- The broad substrate specificity of fungal [[fungal-enzymes-degradation]] means that aromatic pollutants need not be structurally similar to lignin to be transformed

- BTEX degradation in the ectomycorrhizosphere may be enhanced by the diverse [[fungal-communities-extreme-environments]] supported by mycorrhizal associations

## Environmental Factors Affecting Degradation

Several factors influence the rate and extent of chlorophenol and BTEX degradation by fungi:

- **Soil type** -- the percentage of PCP converted to pentachloroanisole versus soil-bound products depends on the type of soil. Sorption to soil organic matter affects [[staycare-bioavailability-pollutants-fungal-bioremediation]].

- **Temperature** -- L. edodes remains active at lower temperatures typical of temperate soils, while P. chrysosporium has a high optimum of 37-40C. Field trials at temperatures as low as 8C posed problems for P. chrysosporium.

- **Moisture content** -- L. edodes was more effective at lower soil moisture while P. chrysosporium performed better at higher moisture levels

- **pH** -- both fungi showed optimal transformation at pH 4

- **Contaminant concentration** -- fungi can tolerate PCP at 500-1000 mg/l, but higher concentrations inhibit fungal growth and enzyme production

- **Nutrient status** -- nitrogen limitation triggers ligninolytic enzyme production in P. chrysosporium, but excessive nitrogen suppresses it

## Field Applications

Field trials using P. chrysosporium and P. sordida in contaminated soils have shown approximately 80% conversion of PCP within 6 weeks, primarily to pentachloroanisole and non-extractable soil-bound products. The inoculum consisted of inexpensive solid substrates such as wood chips or grain-sawdust mixtures supporting fungal hyphae, mixed into contaminated soil with periodic tilling.

A silicone membrane biofilm reactor was used to study fungal LiP production and PCP degradation, achieving PCP disappearance at 10.5 mg/l per day -- five times faster than in conventional flasks. This demonstrates the potential for [[fungal-bioreactor-effluent-treatment]] to enhance chlorophenol degradation rates.
