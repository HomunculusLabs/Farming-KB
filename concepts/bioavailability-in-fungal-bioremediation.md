# Bioavailability in Fungal Bioremediation

Bioavailability is one of the most critical factors determining the success or failure of [[fungal-bioremediation]]. In the context of persistent organic pollutants (POPs), bioavailability refers to the acquisition and subsequent transformation and/or metabolism of an organic pollutant by a living organism. Even the most efficient [[mycoremediation-enzymology]] are useless if pollutants are inaccessible to the fungal mycelium or its extracellular enzymes.

## Sorption Mechanisms and Pollutant Sequestration

The interactions of POPs with soil are complex but essentially involve removal of pollutants from solution by sorption to soil constituents, resulting in decreased bioavailability. Multiple sorption mechanisms operate simultaneously:

- **Hydrophobic partitioning** -- hydrophobic organic compounds such as pentachlorophenol (PCP) generally sorb to soil organic matter. The extent of partitioning depends on the organic carbon content of the soil and the octanol-water partition coefficient (Kow) of the pollutant.

- **Covalent bonding** -- some POPs form covalent bonds with soil organic matter, particularly through reactions catalyzed by fungal [[fungal-enzyme-systems-xenobiotic-degradation]]. This creates bound residues that are resistant to extraction and biological attack.

- **Electrostatic interactions** -- charged pollutant species can interact with charged surfaces of clay minerals and organic matter. pH significantly affects the charge state of both pollutants and soil surfaces.

- **Pore entrapment** -- slow diffusion of POPs into soil micropores can physically sequester pollutants, making them inaccessible even to extracellular enzymes that can penetrate soil aggregates.

The extent of sorption is largely dependent on the chemical nature of the pollutant and the amount of organic matter or clay in the soil. Common soil organic materials such as humic and syringic acid have been found to decrease levels of PCP transformation in liquid culture by Phanerochaete chrysosporium.

## Factors Affecting Pollutant Bioavailability

Multiple factors determine whether pollutants are available for fungal transformation:

- **Chemical nature of the pollutant** -- hydrophobic compounds like PAHs and PCP sorb strongly to organic matter, while water-soluble pollutants may be rapidly transformed (unless extremely toxic) because of higher bioavailability

- **Soil organic matter content** -- higher organic matter generally increases sorption and decreases bioavailability of hydrophobic pollutants

- **Soil clay content** -- clay minerals provide surfaces for sorption and can create micropores that physically trap pollutants

- **pH** -- pH affects both the charge state of pollutants and soil surfaces. PCP becomes a water-soluble salt at high pH, increasing its mobility and bioavailability

- **Contact time (aging)** -- longer periods of contact allow more time for sorption reactions and slow migration into micropores

- **Soil moisture** -- water content affects diffusion rates of pollutants and oxygen availability for fungal metabolism

- **Temperature** -- higher temperatures generally increase desorption rates and biological activity

## Aged Contamination and Pore Restrictions

Longer periods of contact between POPs and soil constituents allow more time for sorption reactions to occur, and subsequent slow migration and/or diffusion of POPs into soil micropores renders pollutants unavailable for microbial transformation. This phenomenon is known as "aging" and has profound implications for [[fungal-bioremediation]].

Evidence for pore-size restrictions on pollutant transformation includes:

- Carbon turnover rates were lower when organic substrates were located in smaller soil pores at low soil matric potential

- Nitrifying bacteria were found to be restricted to soil pores of 136 to 214 micrometers in size

- Evidence exists for restriction of pollutant transformation in pores less than 1 micrometer in diameter

These pore restrictions affect even extracellular enzymes, which are large protein molecules that cannot easily penetrate soil micropores. The result is that however efficient a fungal soil inoculant may be in transforming POPs in the laboratory, chemical and physical restrictions in the heterogeneous soil environment will prevent complete pollutant transformation.

## The Desorption Question

A critical question in [[fungal-bioremediation]] is whether fungi transform sorbed pollutants directly or require pollutants to be in solution before transformation. This relates to the mechanisms of pollutant transformation by fungi:

- **Membrane-based transformations** -- intracellular enzymes may require pollutants to be dissolved and transported across the cell membrane

- **Extracellular enzyme action** -- [[fungal-enzyme-systems-xenobiotic-degradation]] are secreted into the soil environment and could potentially act on sorbed pollutants at the soil-solution interface

- **Mycelial contact** -- fungal hyphae can grow into soil aggregates and potentially access pollutants through direct contact, even in soil micropores too small for bacteria

Understanding how sorption affects these different mechanisms could allow development of more effective and long-lasting soil remediation strategies. The [[fungal-enzyme-systems-xenobiotic-degradation]] of fungi may provide a unique advantage in accessing pollutants that are physically inaccessible to smaller organisms.

## Surfactant Strategies to Enhance Bioavailability

Surfactants represent a promising approach to increase POP transformation by fungi, although they have not been widely used in fungal bioremediation studies. From available data, surfactants have shown positive effects:

- **PAH transformation** in both liquid culture and soil was increased by surfactants

- Non-ionic surfactants such as Tween-80 (polyoxyethylene sorbitan monooleate) may serve as growth substrates for microorganisms while simultaneously increasing pollutant solubility

- High concentrations of synthetic surfactants may increase the proportion of PAHs accessible to microorganisms in the aqueous phase, enhancing degradation

- However, some surfactants, especially ionic surfactants, may be toxic to microorganisms

- Microbially produced biosurfactants may prove more useful than synthetic surfactants for bioremediation of contaminated soil and water

The positive effect of surfactants on PAH transformation indicates that bioavailability is a major factor controlling PAH degradation rates. Surfactants work by solubilizing hydrophobic pollutants into micelles, increasing their effective concentration in the aqueous phase where they can interact with fungal enzymes.

## Fungal Strategies to Overcome Bioavailability Limitations

Fungi have several inherent strategies that may help overcome bioavailability limitations:

- **Extracellular enzyme systems** -- [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] diffuse away from the hyphae into the soil matrix, extending the zone of influence beyond the immediate hyphal surface. This is particularly important for POPs with low soil mobility.

- **Hyphal exploration** -- the [[fungal-enzyme-systems-xenobiotic-degradation]] can grow through soil aggregates, accessing pollutants in microenvironments that are inaccessible to bacteria. This is especially relevant for [[ectomycorrhizal-fungal-ecology]] fungi, which can increase root surface area by up to 47-fold.

- **[[fungal-methylation-oxidative-coupling]]** -- rather than fully mineralizing pollutants, fungi can catalyze the coupling of pollutant molecules to soil humic material, reducing their bioavailability and toxicity. While not complete degradation, this represents an important detoxification pathway.

- **Solubilizing agents** -- some fungi produce organic acids and other metabolites that can alter soil pH and increase pollutant desorption from soil particles

- **Enzyme immobilization** -- extracellular enzymes can become associated with soil particles, potentially maintaining activity at pollutant sorption sites

## Bioavailability and Bound Residue Formation

An important aspect of bioavailability in fungal bioremediation is the formation of bound residues. When fungi transform pollutants, some products become covalently bound to soil organic matter through [[fungal-methylation-oxidative-coupling]] reactions catalyzed by laccases and peroxidases. This can appear as pollutant disappearance without the appearance of identifiable transformation products.

Bound residue formation has both positive and negative implications:

- **Positive** -- reduces the concentration of freely available toxic pollutant, effectively detoxifying the soil

- **Negative** -- the ultimate fate and stability of bound residues is uncertain; pollutants could potentially be remobilized if soil conditions change

The long-term stability of fungal-remediated soils needs assessment to satisfy potential concerns about release of pollutants from humic materials, although the risk appears low unless the soil receives subsequent chemical additions causing pollutant mobilization.

## Implications for Remediation Design

Understanding bioavailability is essential for designing effective [[mycoremediation-field-applications]] strategies:

- It may be possible to make residual amounts of POPs available by chemical or physical means, but the extra cost must be balanced with the level of cleanup required and the residual toxicity of the soil after remediation

- [[fungal-bioreactor-effluent-treatment]] that physically separate contaminated soil from fungal cultures can overcome bioavailability limitations by extracting pollutants from soil before treatment

- Soil amendments like straw can sorb pollutants and decrease their toxicity while providing a growth substrate for fungi

- The rate of bioremediation depends on bioavailability; it is much slower when there are problems in mass transfer of pollutant molecules to microorganisms
