## The Urban Food Waste Problem

Mollison observes that cities produce enormous quantities of organic waste that is
neither composted nor fed to animals. This wasted food becomes the energy source
for urban pest populations: rats, mice, cockroaches, pigeons, and gulls all
thrive on the edible garbage that cities discard. These pests, in turn, are
stalked by cats and other predators, creating complex urban food webs based on
human waste.

The critical insight is that the biomass of these pests is roughly equivalent to
the biomass of domesticated animals—pigs, chickens, guinea pigs, rabbits—that
could be productively converting the same waste into food, fertilizer, and other
useful products. The choice is not between feeding animals or not; it is between
feeding useful animals or feeding pests.

## Cultural Prejudices and Food Resources

Mollison argues that cultural prejudices grossly reduce available food resources
in cities. He notes several examples of productive waste-stream animals:

- **Pigs**: Traditionally used throughout Asia and Europe to process household
  and restaurant waste into high-quality protein.
- **Guinea pigs**: Kept in large numbers in South American households, they
  efficiently convert kitchen scraps to protein with minimal space requirements.
- **Chickens**: Fed on household wastes, they would eliminate the food source
  for gulls while producing eggs and meat.
- **Pigeons**: Already kept by Mediterranean farmers as "phosphate factories,"
  pigeons convert grain into valuable manure for gardens.

He provocatively suggests that a few good Asian restaurants could deal with the
cats and dogs that have become urban pests—highlighting how cultural food
taboos prevent productive use of existing animal biomass.

## The Pigeon Loft as Phosphate Factory

Mollison uses the example of Mediterranean pigeon keepers to illustrate the
principle that every thinking farmer can build their own phosphate factory. A
pigeon loft converts grain (a relatively low-value input) into two valuable
outputs: squabs (young pigeons for food) and nitrogen- and phosphorus-rich manure
for garden fertilization.

The pigeon system demonstrates several [[composting-systems]] for all household organic waste.

### The Backyard Integration Model

A typical urban backyard can produce a significant portion of a household's food
needs through:
- Vegetable beds replacing lawn areas
- Fruit trees and berry bushes along fences
- A small chicken coop processing kitchen scraps
- Compost bins converting all organic waste to fertilizer
- Rainwater harvesting for irrigation

## The Ecological Argument

Mollison's argument is fundamentally ecological. The biomass that currently exists
as urban pests (rats, cockroaches, gulls) will exist regardless of human
preferences. The question is whether that biomass serves useful purposes or
remains a nuisance and disease vector.

Every peasant farmer who keeps pigeons in the Mediterranean knows the truth:
here, every thinking farmer builds their own phosphate factory, as a pigeon loft.
The alternative—wasting the nutrients in landfills or feeding them to
pests—is objectively less productive and ecologically harmful.

## Design Implications

For permaculture designers working in urban contexts, Mollison's analysis
suggests:

- **Audit waste streams**: Map all organic waste production in the target area.
- **Match waste to consumers**: Design animal systems appropriate to the waste
  types and quantities available.
- **Overcome cultural barriers**: Education about the productivity and hygiene
  of integrated animal systems.
- **Design for cycling**: Place animal housing where their outputs (manure, heat)
  serve adjacent systems.
- **Measure total yield**: Account for all products of the cycling system, not
  just the primary food product.
