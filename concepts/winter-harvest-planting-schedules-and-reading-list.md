# Winter-Harvest Planting Schedules and Reading List

## Planting Schedules for Fall and Winter Harvesting

Eliot Coleman's [[winter-harvest-system]] depends on precise succession planting across four distinct growing environments. Each site offers progressively more protection, extending the harvest season deeper into winter.

### The Four-Site Planting System

1. **Outdoor Fields** — Crops are harvested until early November in USDA Zone 5 (coastal Maine, latitude 44°N). No protection beyond normal field conditions.

2. **Early Greenhouses** — The first wave of [[mollison-designers-glasshouse-protected-cropping]]. These houses are planted earlier and harvested before the later houses reach peak production.

3. **Later Greenhouses** — Planted after the early houses, these provide mid-winter harvests during the coldest months.

4. **Cool Greenhouses** — The most protected environment, used for the latest sowings and the most cold-sensitive greens. These are minimally heated or unheated but benefit from the thermal mass and wind protection of the greenhouse structure.

### Key Crops and Sowing Windows

| Crop | Outdoor | Early House | Late House | Cool House |
|------|---------|-------------|------------|------------|
| **Arugula** | 9/4–9/16 | 9/22–10/2 | 9/10 | 10/20–12/10 |
| **Lettuce** | 8/29–9/6 | 9/13–9/17 | 9/19–10/12 | 10/24–11/11 |
| **Mizuna** | 8/26–8/30 | 9/2–9/6 | 9/10 | 10/1–10/5 |
| **Scallions** | 8/10–8/16 | 8/20 | — | 10/6–11/5 |

### Succession Planting Principles

- **Sow often and keep notes** — Coleman emphasizes that these dates are based on decades of refinement at [[four-season-farm]] in Harborside, Maine (Zone 5). Growers at different latitudes and hardiness zones must adjust accordingly.
- **Bold dates** indicate sowings planned for multiple harvests ([[cut-and-come-again]]), while regular-weight dates are for single harvests.
- **Staggered planting** across sites ensures continuous availability. As outdoor production winds down in November, early greenhouse crops begin, followed by later and cool houses.
- **No dates are given for crops where recommendations are uncertain** — an honest acknowledgment that some crops need further experimentation.

### Scheduling Philosophy

The planting schedule is intimately tied to **daylight hours** and the **Persephone Period** (days under 10 hours of daylight). Crops sown before the Persephone Period arrives will reach harvestable size and then essentially hold in a dormant state through the shortest days, ready for harvest as daylight increases. This biological reality drives the entire scheduling system.

## Historical Reading List

Coleman provides an annotated bibliography of foundational texts on intensive vegetable cultivation, primarily drawn from the [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] tradition (*la culture maraîchère*) and British market gardening.

### Foundational European Texts

- **Aquatias, P. (1913). *Intensive Culture of Vegetables on the French System*.** States that "manure, water, and labor constitute the backbone of intensive culture." A comprehensive guide to applying these three inputs for year-round vegetable production.

- **Bailey, L. H. (1897). *The Forcing Book*.** A classic [[liberty-hyde-bailey]] text. The bulk of the information on greenhouse forcing remains valuable today.

- **Dreer, Henry A. (1896). *Dreer's Vegetables Under Glass*.** Written by a skilled practitioner with broad travel experience. Noted for its clear, concise information and easy writing style.

- **Henderson, Peter (1907). *Gardening for Profit*.** First published in 1867 — "the classic of classics" of American market gardening. Inspired generations to take up commercial vegetable production.

- **Kropotkin, Peter (1901). *Fields, Factories and Workshops*.** Described in *The Ecologist* as "one of the canonical texts of the ecological tradition." Kropotkin was a perceptive observer of agricultural realities.

- **Macself, A. J. (1932). *French Intensive Gardening*.** Hard data from a competent practitioner, with excellent drawings and photos illustrating every aspect of the craft.

- **McKay, C. D. (1909). *The French Garden in England*.** One of the early books attempting to popularize French intensive practices for English growers.

- **Newsome, T. (1908). *Gold Producing Soil*.** An enthusiastic and reasonably complete presentation of the French system applied to English soils.

- **Nussey, Helen, and Olive Cockerell (1909). *A French Garden in England*.** A realistic first-year account by two young women graduates of Lady Warwick's Gardening School — valuable for its honest reporting of successes and failures.

- **Robinson, William (1883). *The Parks and Gardens of Paris*.** Available online via Google Books. Robinson was the first to bring French growers' successes to English attention.

- **Smith, Thomas (1909, 1919). *French Gardening* and *The Profitable Culture of Vegetables*.** Sponsored by Joseph Fels, an American soap manufacturer who bought English land to help small-scale intensive producers get established.

- **Weathers, John (1909). *French Market-Gardening*.** Though not the most complete, valuable for Weathers' extensive experience. Introduction by [[william-robinson]].

## Winter-Harvest Reading List

### Technical Grower Guides

- **Anonymous Grower Guides (1980–1986).** A series published by Grower Books (England's leading grower's magazine): *Blocks for Transplants*, *Cucumbers*, *Lettuce Under Glass*, *Peppers and Aubergines*, *Vegetables Under Glass*. Written for chemical growers using mostly heated greenhouses, but the basic cultural information is system-neutral and very complete.

### Practitioner Texts

- **Artiss, Percy (1948). *Market Gardening*.** A competent grower who was "almost organic without saying so" — insisted that "the soil must be biologically healthy." Thorough coverage of field and greenhouse cultivation.

- **Coleman, Eliot (1995). *The New Organic Grower* (Revised edition).** Indispensable background on soil-fertility management and cultural techniques. The [[eliot-coleman-and-the-winter-harvest-handbook]] complements and updates the winter-harvest chapters in this earlier work.

- **Gerst, Jean-Jacques (1993). *Légumes sous baches* (Vegetables Under Covers).** A professional French technical manual covering every possible vegetable and every combination of high tunnels, low tunnels, reflective covers, floating covers, and heated/unheated greenhouses. Written for more [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] than Maine but extremely valuable.

### Crop-Specific References

- **Larcom, Joy (1991). *Oriental Vegetables*.** The definitive English-language reference for the vast trove of potential Asian crops. Everything you ever wanted to know about growing Asian greens.

### Greenhouse Science

- **Lawrence, W. J. C. (1948). *Science and the Glasshouse*.** Coleman's favorite old-time greenhouse writer — tireless in testing what worked, what didn't, and why. His experiments are engaging to read and his conclusions have great practical value. His earlier *Seed and Potting Composts* (1939) was Coleman's guide for formulating potting mixes.

- **Nisley, Charles H. (1929). *Starting Early Vegetable and Flowering Plants Under Glass*.** A professional reference with information on plants, horticultural techniques, and building glasshouses. Includes extensive black-and-white photos of the 1920s vegetable industry.

- **Van den Muijzenberg, Edwin W. B. (1980). *A History of Greenhouses*.** The story of greenhouse development from earliest recorded examples through modern Dutch glasshouses — fascinating reading for anyone interested [[crop-rotation-in-protected-cultivation]] history.

- **Wittwer, S. H., and S. Honma (1979). *Greenhouse Tomatoes, Lettuce, and Cucumbers*.** A solid introduction to greenhouse techniques, though some material is dated.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Historical depth matters** — The French intensive tradition dating back to the 1800s provides proven principles that modern growers can adapt: manure-based fertility, close spacing, protected cultivation, and year-round production.

2. **Schedules are site-specific** — Coleman's dates work for Zone 5 coastal Maine. Every grower must adapt based on latitude, microclimate, and local weather patterns.

3. **Succession planting across multiple environments** is the key to continuous winter harvest — not any single technique or structure.

4. **The reading list bridges centuries** — From 19th-century French practitioners to 1980s technical manuals, the accumulated knowledge base for protected cultivation is deep and accessible.
