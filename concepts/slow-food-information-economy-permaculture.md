## Slow Food and the Information Economy in Permaculture

The tension between slow, deliberate processes and the accelerating pace of modern life extends beyond agriculture into food culture, information technology, and the fundamental paradox of political identity. This concept explores how the Slow Food movement, the information economy, and the structural paradox between radicalism and conservatism all reflect the deeper [[permaculture]] principle that "slow is sane."

## The Slow Food Movement

The Slow Food movement emerged as a direct cultural response to the acceleration of food production and consumption. It celebrates the loving [[magic-mushroom-preparation-and-consumption-methods]] of food as an alternative to what Holmgren calls "the aesthetic, physiological, and ecological obscenities of fast food." Founded in Italy by Carlo Petrini in the 1980s as a protest against a McDonald"s opening near the Spanish Steps in Rome, the movement had grown to approximately 60,000 members worldwide by the time of Holmgren's writing.

Slow Food represents a signpost toward cultural turnaround — a recognition that speed in food production and consumption sacrifices nutrition, flavor, cultural meaning, ecological health, and social connection. The movement embodies several [[permaculture-principles]] simultaneously:

- **Use [[small-and-slow-solutions]]** — favoring local, artisanal production over industrial scale
- **Apply self-regulation and accept feedback** — listening to the body's nutritional signals rather than engineered cravings
- **Use [[permaculture-principle-use-and-value-diversity]]** — celebrating regional food traditions [[cannabis-landrace-and-heirloom-varieties]]
- **[[holmgren-produce-no-waste-waste-hierarchy-refuse-reduce-reuse-repair-recycle]]** — traditional cooking uses whole ingredients with minimal processing

## The Information Economy and Accelerating Culture

The acceleration in the material world has been dwarfed by the acceleration in information, media, and culture. Several trends illustrate this:

### Publishing and Media
- The throughput of authors and titles by publishers began accelerating in the 1970s, astonishing those who remembered a slower pace
- New information is generated and becomes redundant almost as soon as it is produced
- TV news item duration has contracted from minutes to seconds, measuring both increasing speed and collapsing attention spans

### Communication Technology
- The mobile phone, within just 10 years of widespread adoption, severely damaged people's ability to make travel plans and communicate them without continuous contact
- It has fundamentally redefined what it means to be alone
- As artificial memory systems (computers) grow exponentially, there is a corresponding general loss of historical memory and ability to understand older perspectives

### Presentation Over Substance
- The Microsoft PowerPoint presentation became a symbol of "all show, no substance" in the 1990s
- Consultant reports follow technology escalation while content focus shrinks
- High-level conferences give earnest attention to content-less presentations

## The Myth of Dematerialization

The information economy has been called the "weightless economy" because buying and selling often involves no direct materials or energy. However, this view ignores the long chains of embodied energy (EMERGY) in producing information technology infrastructure.

### The Paperless Office That Never Was
Despite promises of reduced paper use, office paper consumption has **quadrupled** over a decade. The increasing capacity and reduced cost of printing electronic documents, combined with new habits and expectations, produced the opposite of the predicted outcome.

### E-Commerce and Transport
E-commerce, touted as reducing shopping travel, instead generated an explosion in individual door-to-door delivery. In the United States, private logistics corporations replacing postal services became among the fastest-growing companies, stimulated by e-commerce and subsidized by cheap oil and publicly funded highways. Many high-level planners remain unaware of the linkage between e-commerce and increased transport costs.

## Permaculture and Information Technology

The value of information technology is a contentious issue within permaculture. Two broad positions exist:

### The Pragmatic View
Information technology is simply a tool that can be used for good or ill. Rejecting it is like refusing to use two arms when everyone else is. Some enthusiasts see the self-organizing, networking, and evolving nature of IT as reflecting natural [[mollisonian-permaculture-principles]], believing in the promise of reduced materials and energy use.

### The Critical View
Others mistrust information technology for:
- **Reducing connection** to, and comprehension of, slow-moving natural processes
- **Accelerating consumption** of materials and energy despite promises of waste reduction
- **Increasing centralization** of power despite promises of devolution
- **Increasing dependency** on complex technology that cannot be locally produced

### Holmgren"s Personal Guidelines

As someone who has used computers for both consulting and writing, Holmgren offers practical guidelines for a balanced relationship with information technology:

**Avoid:**
- Operating at the "bleeding edge" of new hardware and software — you become an unpaid beta tester
- High costs and depreciation rates that require full-time commitment
- Rewarding corporations for producing new products rather than refining existing ones
- Letting the "gee-whiz" factor distract from the task at hand

**Instead:**
- Update with proven second-hand hardware and software
- Accept that fully integrated, compatible systems may never exist while rapid growth continues
- Assess all functions for whether computerization is truly worthwhile, including realistic estimates of learning time and downtime
- Value the local over the global — use global networks to strengthen local resource use
- Always maintain non-computerized backup systems for [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]]
- Balance screen time with time spent with people and nature
- Minimize computer exposure for children under 12
- Remember: the map is not the territory; the virtual world is never reality

## The Paradox of the Radical and the Conservative

As energy availability peaks and begins to decline, the rate of change slows in many material domains. However, the change of direction — from growth to contraction, from materialistic to more spiritual values — is so fundamental that it creates an experience of incomprehensible, chaotic change, an intensification of current turbulence.

### The Structural Paradox

This creates a structural paradox inherent in permaculture and the broader counterculture:

- **Radical and revolutionary ideas** suggest change from a stable norm, a break with tradition
- **When the norm itself is one of continuous radical change**, advocating for durability, permanence, persistence, and sustainability becomes a contradiction in terms
- **The conservative becomes the radical**, and the radical advocates a new conservatism

This paradox underlies much confusion in environmental and political debates. The term "conservationist" epitomizes it: conserving what exists is now the radical position against a status quo of constant destruction and innovation.

### Fundamentalism vs. Radical Critique

Both radical critics of accelerating change and reactionary fundamentalists who want to return to traditional values share critiques of modernity. However, they differ fundamentally:

- **The reactionary** seeks to rebuild the past piece by piece
- **The radical** aspires to construct or evolve utopian possibilities from present conditions
- For those in the mainstream, unable to imagine anything beyond the present instant, these critiques are indistinguishable

### The Speedboat Metaphor

The experience of energy descent can be likened to a speedboat making a tight 180-degree turn: even though the boat slows down during the turn, the maneuver itself intensifies the thrill and turbulence. We are habituated to the speed and exhilaration of continuous acceleration; the reversal of direction, while slower in absolute terms, feels more chaotic and disorienting.

## Conclusion

The evidence that "small is beautiful" and "slow is sane" is pervasive. As energy peak and descent reshape civilization, giant centralized systems reveal themselves as dinosaurs of the fossil-fuel era. The emergence of Slow Food, the questioning of information technology"s benefits, and the paradox of radical conservatism all point toward a cultural paradigm shift — from adolescent immortality and values of speed, novelty, and endless growth toward maturity, persistence, and acceptance of limits.

The conclusion Holmgren offers is both warning and invitation: when an adolescent sense of immortality defines a civilization, we may be close to its demise and the birth of something new. The task is to "watch it slowly unfold."
