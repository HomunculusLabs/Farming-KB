## Overview

Mycoremediation of plastics is an emerging field investigating fungi capable of degrading synthetic polymers, particularly polyurethane (PUR). Plastics persist in the environment for centuries, and fungal enzymes offer a potential biological pathway for breaking them down. Research is still largely in laboratory stages, but promising results suggest fungi could become a tool in managing plastic waste.

## The Plastic Problem

Global plastic production exceeds 400 million tonnes annually. Key challenges:
- Polyurethane: Used in foam insulation, furniture, footwear, automotive parts
- Polyethylene (PE): Most common plastic; packaging, bags, containers
- Polyethylene terephthalate (PET): Bottles, textiles, food packaging
- Polystyrene (PS): Foam packaging, disposable containers
- Polypropylene (PP): Auto parts, textiles, consumer goods

## Pestalotiopsis microspora -- The Breakthrough Species

### Discovery
In 2011, Yale University researchers led by Scott Strobel discovered Pestalotiopsis microspora in the Ecuadorian Amazon rainforest. This endophytic fungus was found to degrade and consume polyurethane (PUR) -- the first documented case of a fungus breaking down this persistent plastic.

### Mechanism
- Produces a serine hydrolase enzyme that cleaves polyurethane bonds
- Degrades PUR under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions
- Can survive on polyurethane as its sole carbon source
- Enzyme secretion occurs extracellularly, breaking down plastic outside the fungal cells

### Significance
- Anaerobic degradation means potential application in landfills (low-oxygen environments)
- Endophytic lifestyle suggests it could be applied without disrupting plant communities
- Demonstrates that rainforest biodiversity harbors undiscovered biodegradation capabilities

## Other Fungi with Plastic-Degrading Potential

### Aspergillus spp.
- **A. fumigatus**: Degrades polyester polyurethane; produces esterases and ureases
- **A. niger**: Breaks down low-density polyethylene (LDPE) films
- **A. flavus**: Degrades polycaprolactone (PCL), a biodegradable polyester used as model polymer

### Penicillium spp.
- **P. simplicissimum**: Isolated from soil, degrades polyester-type polyurethane
- **P. chrysogenum**: Produces cutinase-like enzymes that attack ester bonds in plastics
- Multiple species show activity against polystyrene and polyethylene

### White-rot Basidiomycetes
- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Degrades polystyrene when pre-treated or co-metabolized with lignocellulosic material
- **Trametes versicolor**: Laccase enzymes partially oxidize bisphenol A (BPA) and phthalate plasticizers
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: Lignin peroxidase attacks aromatic structures in some plastics
- **Schizophyllum commune**: Degrades polyethylene under pre-oxidation conditions

### Other Notable Species
- **Cladosporium cladosporioides**: Isolated from marine plastic debris; degrades polyethylene
- **Engyodontium album**: Breaks down polypropylene in laboratory settings (2022 discovery)
- **Xepiculopsis sp.**: Found degrading plastic in mangrove environments

## Mechanisms of Plastic Biodegradation

### Enzymatic Attack
- **Esterases/Ureases**: Break ester and urethane bonds in polyurethane
- **Laccases**: Oxidize phenolic groups in plasticizers and additives
- **Cutinases**: Attack ester bonds; originally evolved to degrade plant cutin (structurally similar to synthetic polyesters)
- **Manganese peroxidases**: Generate reactive radicals that fragment polymer chains

### Pre-treatment Requirements
Many plastics require pre-treatment before fungal degradation:
- UV exposure creates reactive sites on polymer surfaces
- Thermal oxidation introduces carbonyl and hydroxyl groups
- Chemical pre-treatment (acid, alkali, ozone) increases surface hydrophilicity
- Mechanical grinding increases surface area

### Co-metabolism
Fungi often degrade plastics more effectively when alternative carbon sources are available. Co-metabolism with agricultural waste, straw, or wood chips provides energy for enzyme production while fungi simultaneously attack plastic polymers.

## Research Status and Challenges

### Laboratory vs. Field Results
- Most studies conducted in controlled laboratory conditions
- Degradation rates in natural environments are significantly lower
- Temperature, moisture, and microbial competition affect field performance
- Typical laboratory degradation: 10-40% mass loss over 30-90 days (PUR)
- Field degradation rates: poorly documented; likely much slower

### Polymer Complexity
- Commercial plastics contain additives (plasticizers, stabilizers, colorants) that may inhibit fungal growth
- Different plastic types require different enzymatic approaches
- Mixed plastic waste streams are harder to treat than single-polymer materials
- Crystallinity affects degradation -- amorphous regions degrade faster

### Scale and Economics
- No commercial-scale fungal plastic remediation system exists yet
- Enzyme production costs remain high
- Processing times are long compared to mechanical recycling or incineration
- Regulatory frameworks for biological plastic treatment are undeveloped

## Potential Applications

### Waste Treatment
- Landfill bioreactors inoculated with plastic-degrading fungi
- Composting facilities processing biodegradable plastics alongside organic waste
- Industrial pre-treatment of plastic waste before recycling

### Environmental Cleanup
- Inoculation of plastic-polluted soils (agricultural film residues)
- Marine plastic debris treatment in controlled facilities
- Microplastic degradation in wastewater treatment

### Material Design
- Understanding fungal degradation pathways to design more easily biodegradable plastics
- Bio-inspired enzyme design for industrial plastic recycling

## Future Directions

- Metagenomic surveys to discover novel plastic-degrading fungi from extreme environments
- Genetic engineering to enhance enzyme expression and activity
- Consortium approaches combining multiple fungal and bacterial species
- Life cycle assessment comparing fungal degradation to conventional disposal methods
