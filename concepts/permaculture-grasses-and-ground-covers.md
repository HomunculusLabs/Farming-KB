# Permaculture Grasses and Ground Cover Species

> Drawing from Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual* (Appendix Plant List), this page catalogues key grass and ground cover species used across tropical and temperate permaculture systems.

## The Role of Grasses in Permaculture

Grasses are among the most functionally important plants in any permaculture design. They serve multiple simultaneous purposes: erosion control on slopes and waterways, biomass production for mulch and compost, livestock fodder, soil stabilization and organic matter accumulation, and microclimate regulation through ground-level shading and wind reduction. Mollison emphasized that grasses should be viewed not as monoculture lawns but as productive, multi-functional elements integrated into polycultural systems.

In a mature permaculture design, grasses occupy the herbaceous layer, forming the transition between canopy trees and the soil surface. Their dense fibrous root systems bind soil particles, prevent surface runoff, and create channels for water infiltration. Above ground, their rapid growth captures sunlight efficiently and converts it into biomass that can be cut and dropped as mulch around tree crops, fed to livestock in integrated animal systems, or composted on-site.

### How Grasses Build Soil

Grasses contribute to soil formation through several mechanisms. Their root systems exude carbohydrates that feed soil microorganisms, particularly mycorrhizal fungi. As roots die back and regrow — a continuous process in perennial grasses — they leave channels of organic matter throughout the soil profile. This process, combined with the decomposition of cut biomass on the surface, steadily increases soil carbon levels. In systems designed for soil rehabilitation, fast-growing grasses like sudan grass and elephant grass can produce several tons of organic matter per hectare per year, accelerating the transition from degraded land to productive soil.

### Biomass Production for Mulch

The "chop and drop" method is central to permaculture ground management. Tall, productive grasses are grown in dedicated mulch strips or as understory plants and periodically cut, with the cut material laid directly on the soil surface as mulch. This practice suppresses weed germination, moderates soil temperature, retains moisture, and slowly releases nutrients as the material decomposes. Species selected for mulch production should combine high annual biomass yield with ease of cutting and relatively fast decomposition — elephant grass, banana grass, and guinea grass are among the best tropical choices, while tall wheatgrass and perennial rye grass serve this role in temperate climates.

## Categories of Grasses

### Tropical Pasture Grasses

Tropical pasture grasses are the backbone of tropical and subtropical livestock systems and play a critical role in ground cover establishment during farm establishment phases.

- **Banana grass** (*Pennisetum purpureum*) — A tall, vigorous perennial that produces enormous biomass. Excellent for mulch production and erosion control on steep tropical slopes. Can be harvested multiple times per year. Palatable to cattle when young.
- **Elephant grass** (*Pennisetum* spp.) — Closely related to banana grass, reaching heights of 3–4 meters. One of the highest-yielding tropical grasses for biomass and fodder. Used extensively in cut-and-carry systems and as a windbreak around garden plots.
- **Guinea grass** (*Panicum maximum*) — A productive perennial pasture grass adapted to humid and subhumid tropics. Tolerates partial shade, making it suitable for integration under tree canopies in agroforestry systems. Good drought tolerance once established.
- **Rhodes grass** (*Chloris gayana*) — A spreading perennial that forms dense mats, making it excellent for erosion control and pasture. Highly drought-tolerant and establishes readily from seed. Useful on degraded land rehabilitation projects.
- **Signal grass** (*Brachiaria* spp.) — Widely adapted tropical pasture grass with good shade tolerance and palatability. Forms a dense sward that suppresses weeds. Several cultivars available for different soil and climate conditions.
- **Sudan grass** (*Sorghum halepense*) — A vigorous summer-growing grass with deep roots that break compacted subsoil layers. Produces abundant biomass for green manure or mulch. Can become invasive if not managed; best used in controlled rotations.
- **Purple pigeon grass** (*Setaria porphyrantha*) — A tufted perennial useful in tropical pastures, providing both fodder and ground cover. Tolerates seasonal waterlogging and a range of soil fertility levels.

### Temperate Pasture Grasses

Temperate systems rely on a different suite of grasses, many of which form the basis of permanent pastures and meadow polycultures.

- **Perennial rye grass** (*Lolium perenne*) — One of the most widely used temperate pasture grasses globally. High palatability and rapid establishment make it ideal for grazing systems. Works well in mixtures with clovers for balanced nutrition.
- **Wimmera rye** (*Lolium rigidum*) — An annual ryegrass adapted to dry Mediterranean climates. Useful as a self-seeding winter-spring pasture component in areas with hot, dry summers.
- **Cockfoot / Orchard grass** (*Dactylis glomerata*) — A hardy perennial grass that thrives in shaded conditions, making it ideal for orchard understories and silvopastoral systems. Tolerates drought better than many other temperate grasses.
- **Tall wheatgrass** (*Agropyrum elongatum*) — A deep-rooted, salt-tolerant perennial suited to saline or alkaline soils where few other grasses thrive. Valuable for rehabilitation of degraded agricultural land.
- **Barley** (*Hordeum marinum*) — Sea barley is a salt-tolerant annual grass useful for coastal and saline site rehabilitation. Can serve as a nurse crop during establishment of permanent ground covers.
- **Bent grass** (*Agrostis* spp.) — Fine-leaved grasses forming dense, low-growing turf. Useful for stabilizing soil surface, pathways, and living ground cover between garden beds. Tolerates close mowing and foot traffic.
- **Buffalo grass** (*Buchloe dactyloides*) — A native North American prairie grass requiring minimal water and maintenance. Forms a dense mat ideal for low-input ground cover, lawns, and erosion control on disturbed sites.

### Erosion Control Specialists

- **Beach grass** (*Ammophila arenaria*) — A pioneering dune grass with extensive rhizome systems that trap sand and build dune structure. Essential for coastal stabilization and the first stage of dune succession in permaculture coastal designs.
- **Blady grass** (*Imperata cylindrica*) — A rhizomatous grass with strong erosion control capacity. Mollison notes its utility with caution — while effective at soil stabilization, it can become aggressively invasive in tropical systems. Best managed through regular cutting and integration with competitive legumes.
- **Rice grass** (*Oryzoides hymenopsis*) — A wetland grass useful for riparian and waterway stabilization. Its dense root network holds stream banks and pond edges against erosion from water flow, making it valuable in aquaculture and dam edge plantings.

## Vetiver Systems

**Vetiver** (*Vetiveria zizanioides*) deserves special attention as one of the most versatile and important plants in the permaculture toolkit. Vetiver hedges planted on contour lines form living barriers that dramatically reduce sheet erosion, trap sediment, and slow water runoff across slopes. The dense, deep root system (reaching 2–3 meters) anchors soil on steep grades, road embankments, and drainage lines.

Beyond erosion control, vetiver provides mulch material, thatch for building, and its roots yield essential oil used in perfumery. It is sterile (does not spread by seed), non-invasive, and tolerant of a wide range of soil conditions including acidity, salinity, and heavy metals. Mollison championed vetiver as a cornerstone technology for land restoration, particularly in the tropics and subtropics.

In practical application, vetiver hedges are typically planted at 10–15 cm spacing along contour lines, with multiple rows spaced 1–2 meters apart on steep slopes. The hedges are maintained by cutting to 30–50 cm height several times per year, with the cut material used as mulch. Over time, sediment builds up behind the hedges, creating natural terraces that further improve water retention and arability.

## Ground Cover Legumes

Leguminous ground covers fix atmospheric nitrogen, improve soil fertility, suppress weeds, and provide fodder — making them essential companions to grasses in any polycultural ground cover system.

- **Greenleaf desmodium** (*Desmodium intortum*) — A vigorous tropical legume that climbs over grasses and forms a dense mat. Excellent nitrogen fixer and high-protein fodder. Its intercropping with maize has been shown to repel stemborer pests through a push-pull mechanism, a technique that aligns closely with permaculture principles of integrated pest management.
- **Haifa white clover** (*Trifolium* spp.) — A low-growing perennial clover ideal for temperate pasture mixes and living mulches beneath orchard trees. Fixes significant nitrogen, attracts pollinators, and tolerates regular mowing or grazing.
- **Haole koa** (*Leucaena leucocephala*) — A fast-growing nitrogen-fixing tree/shrub used as a fodder, green manure, and shade tree. Leaves are high in protein. Contains mimosine, which can be toxic to non-ruminants at high levels; ruminants develop tolerance. Useful in alley cropping systems.
- **Lablab** (*Lablab purpureus*) — A dual-purpose climbing legume producing both edible beans and high-quality fodder. Excellent green manure crop. Deep taproot brings nutrients from subsoil layers. Performs well in tropical and subtropical climates with distinct wet and dry seasons.

## Additional Ground Cover Species

- **Gorse** (*Ulex* spp.) — A nitrogen-fixing shrub often considered a weed, but valued in permaculture for its ability to thrive on poor, acidic soils and fix nitrogen. Provides excellent windbreak hedging and wildlife habitat. Must be managed aggressively as it can dominate landscapes.
- **Glyricidia** (*Gliricidia sepium*) — A fast-growing nitrogen-fixing tree used extensively in tropical alley cropping, live fencing, and fodder systems. Leaves decompose rapidly, providing quick-release green manure. Coppices readily for sustained biomass production.
- **Lippia** (*Lippia nodiflora*) — A low-growing, mat-forming ground cover tolerant of heavy foot traffic and compacted soils. Useful for pathways, play areas, and as a living mulch between crops in no-till garden systems.
- **Lupin** (*Lupinus* spp.) — Temperate nitrogen-fixing legumes with deep taproots that break compacted soil layers. Used as green manure crops, ornamentals, and grain producers. Blue lupins are particularly valued for acid soil rehabilitation.
- **Kniphofia** (*Kniphofia* spp.) — Red hot pokers, valued in permaculture for their ability to attract nectar-feeding birds and beneficial insects. Provide ornamental value and fire-resistant landscaping. Useful in edge plantings and wildlife corridors.

## Integration into Permaculture Design

### Alley Cropping and Hedgerow Systems

Grasses and legumes integrate naturally into alley cropping systems where rows of nitrogen-fixing trees or shrubs (such as glyricidia or leucaena) alternate with alleys of productive grasses and ground covers. The tree rows provide mulch, nitrogen, and microclimate modification, while the grass alleys produce fodder, control erosion, and build soil organic matter.

### Guild Planting

In food forest guilds, grasses and ground covers occupy the lowest herbaceous stratum. Beneath fruit trees, a diverse mix of clovers, desmodium, and low grasses creates a living mulch that retains soil moisture, cycles nutrients, and provides habitat for ground-dwelling beneficial insects. Vetiver can be planted on the uphill side of tree plantings to intercept runoff.

### Contour Strip Planting

Planting grass strips along contour lines is a fundamental permaculture earthworks strategy. Perennial grasses like vetiver, rhodes grass, or napier grass planted on the contour slow runoff, filter sediment, and create terracing effects over time. These strips can be integrated with swales and keyline systems for maximum water harvesting efficiency.

### Successional Use

Grasses serve an important role in ecological succession. Pioneer grasses such as beach grass, sudan grass, and annual ryegrasses are used to stabilize disturbed land and begin the process of soil building. As the system matures, these are gradually replaced or supplemented with perennial grasses, legumes, and eventually woody species in a managed succession toward productive polyculture.

### Fodder Systems and Animal Integration

In integrated animal systems, grasses and legumes form the productive base. Rotational grazing through diverse pasture mixes of rye grass, clover, cocksfoot, and other species mimics natural herbivore patterns, stimulating root growth and nutrient cycling while preventing overgrazing. The manure returned to pasture closes nutrient loops. Cut-and-carry systems using elephant grass, guinea grass, and lablab allow fodder production on steep or erosion-prone land where grazing animals would cause damage.

## Selecting Species for Site Conditions

Mollison's plant list provides species for a wide range of environmental conditions. When selecting grasses and ground covers, consider the following factors:

| Condition | Recommended Grasses | Recommended Legumes |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Wet tropics / high rainfall** | Guinea grass, signal grass, elephant grass | Greenleaf desmodium, lablab |
| **Dry tropics / seasonal drought** | Rhodes grass, buffalo grass | Haole koa, lablab |
| **Temperate / humid** | Perennial rye grass, cocksfoot | Haifa white clover, lupin |
| **Saline / coastal soils** | Tall wheatgrass, barley, beach grass | Lupin (some species) |
| **Steep slopes / erosion** | Vetiver, rhodes grass, beach grass | Desmodium (trailing) |
| **Shaded understory** | Cocksfoot, guinea grass | Desmodium, clover |
| **Degraded / compacted soil** | Sudan grass, tall wheatgrass | Lupin, lablab |

The key principle from Mollison's approach is to use polycultures rather than monocultures. A mixed sward of grasses and legumes outperforms any single species across multiple functions — producing more total biomass, fixing more nitrogen, supporting more diverse soil biology, and providing more resilient ground cover against weather extremes and pest pressures.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Match species to microclimate** — Use the herbaceous layer beneath existing canopy to identify shade-tolerant species versus full-sun pasture grasses.
2. **Always include legumes** — Every grass planting should include a nitrogen-fixing companion to maintain soil fertility without external inputs.
3. **Plan for succession** — Use fast-growing pioneer grasses for initial site stabilization, then transition to perennial polycultures as the system matures.
4. **Design for harvest** — Locate high-biomass mulch grasses where their cut material can be easily transported to garden beds and tree crop areas.
5. **Prevent invasives** — Species like blady grass and sudan grass require active management; prefer sterile or clumping species like vetiver where invasion risk is a concern.
