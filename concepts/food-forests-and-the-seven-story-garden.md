# Food Forests and the Seven-Story Garden

## Overview

A food forest, also called a forest garden, is a multi-layered edible landscape that mimics
the structure and function of a natural forest. It produces food, fuel, fiber, medicine, and
habitat for people and wildlife within a self-sustaining ecosystem.

The concept arises from [[dynamic-accumulators]].

### 5. Ground-Cover Layer

Low spreading plants form a living mulch that suppresses weeds, retains moisture, and builds
soil. Strawberries, clover, creeping thyme, miner's lettuce, vinca, and sweet woodruff
protect the soil surface and create favorable conditions for the layers above.

### 6. Vine Layer

Climbing plants exploit vertical surfaces that would otherwise go unused. Grapes, kiwi, hops,
passionfruit, climbing beans, hardy kiwi, honeysuckle, and scarlet runner beans use trees,
shrubs, or trellises for support, adding yield without consuming ground area.

### 7. Root-Crop Layer

Plants harvested for edible roots occupy the vertical soil zone beneath everything else.
Potatoes, sweet potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, garlic, onions, daikon radish, carrots, and
burdock draw nutrients from different depths while loosening compacted soil.

## Design Considerations

Proper tree spacing is the most critical design decision — it determines whether light reaches
all seven layers. Shade-tolerant plants go under taller elements; sun-lovers occupy bright
openings and south-facing edges.

Regional climate matters significantly. Northern gardens need more open spacing because the
sun's angle is lower. Southern gardens can support denser planting without sacrificing
understory productivity.

On hilly land, [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]], amphibians, and other wildlife
return to the layered habitat, providing natural pest control and pollination services.

Production spans the full calendar. Cool-season greens provide winter harvests, berries arrive
in spring, and fruit peaks in summer and fall. Shade-grown shrubs require less irrigation but
still produce well, making food forests especially resilient in drought-prone regions.

## Historical Context

Tropical food forests have existed for millennia across Africa, Asia, and South America. Many
indigenous cultures developed sophisticated multi-layered polycultures long before modern
agriculture. Early European anthropologists often did not recognize these as gardens because
they looked "untamed" compared to conventional row-cropped fields.
