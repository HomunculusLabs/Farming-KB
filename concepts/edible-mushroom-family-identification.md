## Identification of Major Edible Mushroom Families

Julius A. Palmer provided detailed descriptions of the principal edible mushroom
families accessible to American foragers in the late 19th century. His approach
was practical and field-oriented, emphasizing visible characteristics that could
be recognized without microscopic equipment. Each family presented its own
challenges and rewards for the amateur mycologist.

## The Meadow Mushroom and Horse Mushroom

The two most celebrated edible fungi — *[[agaricus-campestris]]* (meadow mushroom)
and *[[agaricus-arvensis]]* (horse mushroom) — share enough characteristics that
Palmer often ignored any difference between them. A rational explanation for
their similarity was the theory that the horse-mushroom was propagated from
spores of the meadow mushroom after passing through the digestive system of
horses, hence its common name and frequent occurrence in pastures.

### Growth Stages

At first growth, these mushrooms resemble a puffball — an oval, button-like
form with the cap tightly pressed to the stem. As they develop, a veil or web
appears beneath the cap, which soon ruptures to expose the gills. The gills
begin as a faint shade of pink, deepening progressively through purple into
black as the spores mature.

### Key Characteristics

- **Ring**: When pink-gilled, the ring around the stem is quite perfect, though
  older specimens may lose all trace of ring or veil.
- **Cap color**: Every shade from pure white to deep brown.
- **Taste and smell**: So peculiar to the species that Palmer considered there
  was not much danger of deception once familiar with them.

Palmer advised the amateur to have one identified by an expert if possible,
acknowledging that these mushrooms are difficult to describe despite being
the most widely recognized edible fungi.

## The Maned Mushroom (Coprinus comatus)

The most easily recognized agaric, Palmer called the maned mushroom (*Coprinus
comatus*) his first successful independent identification. Its distinctive
appearance makes confusion with other species virtually impossible.

### Physical Description

When fit for food, the maned mushroom is shaped like a turtle"s egg — ovate but
alike at each end. The top is brown and smooth, but toward the earth the outer
skin ruffles up, creating a white mane or fringe of concentric layers. The stem
is hollow, and when cut appears like macaroni as it cooks. A ring around the
stem is movable in well-developed specimens.

### Lifecycle and Deliquescence

Very soon after acquiring full growth, the gill tips begin turning black,
sometimes running through pink. The mushroom then opens umbrella-like and
melts into an inky fluid — a process called deliquescence. This natural
dissolution is how the Coprinariae release their spores. Palmer noted that the
manuscript of his original article was written with ink produced by this very
process.

### Culinary Notes

The maned mushroom should never be eaten when stale or when the gills have
turned black. It has no poisonous counterpart, making it one of the safest
choices for beginners. It is excellent both broiled and stewed.

## The Elm-Tree Mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius)

Growing exclusively in autumn on elm trees, the elm-tree mushroom was Palmer's
second successful experimental identification. It typically appears in clefts or
where limbs have been sawn off, often reappearing yearly in the same spot.

### Key Features

- **Stem**: Thick and solid, sometimes set eccentrically to one side of the cap.
- **Gills**: Broad and white, turning yellow in older specimens.
- **Flesh**: Firm and white throughout.
- **Cap**: Light yellow at first, smooth, becoming spotted and cracked with
  age, eventually very leathery.

Palmer knew of no other large fungus with gills and white flesh growing from
elm, making identification relatively straightforward once the habitat was noted.

## The Russulae Family

Palmer considered the Russulae quite safe for beginners because their distinctive
brittle texture provided a reliable diagnostic feature absent in most other
families.

### Brittle Texture as Identifier

Unlike the common mushroom, whose gills are pliable and whose flesh can be bent
without breaking, the milk-mushrooms and Russulae are very brittle. The gills do
not mat down like those of the horse-mushroom or elm-tree mushroom — they break
into small pieces, and the whole fungus snaps suddenly when bent. This
characteristic brittleness immediately distinguishes the Russulae from other
[[pleurotoid-mushroom-morphology-gilled-fungi]].

### The Milk Test

If milk or juice exudes from cut gills, Palmer advised amateurs to reject the
specimen outright, as handling milky Lactarii required more experience. For
non-milking Russulae, he recommended tasting a small piece of stem. Non-edible
varieties would bite and sting the tongue, while all esculent varieties of the
genus were tasteless, mild, or had the flavor of chestnuts.

### Dangerous Look-alikes

Palmer highlighted *[[russula-alutacea]]* (bright red top, buff-colored gills, stout
short stem) as a common edible species, but noted that *[[russula-emetica]]* and
*[[russula-rosacea]]* resembled it so closely that he invariably tasted a small piece
of each stem while washing them to avoid any possibility of mistake.

## The Boleti

Among pore-bearing fungi, Palmer distinguished carefully between edible and
potentially dangerous species:

- **Edible Boleti**: Brown, yellowish, or greenish pores with a russet or brown
  cap, and flesh that remains white when cut.
- **Warning signs**: Brilliant coloration (red or yellow), blue discoloration
  when bruised, or red pore mouths indicating probable toxicity.
- **Separation from Polypori**: Edible Boleti grow from the ground with distinct
  stems and easily separable tubes, while Polypori generally grow on wood with
  abortive stems and fused tube structures.

## The Honey-Colored Mushroom (Agaricus melleus)

A controversial species in Palmer's time, the honey-colored mushroom illustrated
the uncertainty surrounding many fungal species. Authorities disagreed: Greville
noted it was "freely eaten on the Continent" according to Fries, yet Paulet
reported a dog died twelve hours after eating it. Palmer himself ate it plentifully
and considered it a favorite, concluding it might be noxious raw but rendered
safe by cooking.

## Puffballs as Beginner Fungi

Palmer reserved his strongest safety endorsement for puffballs. The giant
puffball (*[[lycoperdon-giganteum]]*) in its infant state — smooth-skinned and
perfectly white inside — was considered unmistakable. Any yellow stain from a
knife indicated the specimen was too old. Several smaller varieties existed,
which Palmer ate indiscriminately. He believed all white puffballs could be safely
used if cooked promptly, and noted that no poisonous fungus resembles them — a
claim that has largely held up to modern scrutiny.
