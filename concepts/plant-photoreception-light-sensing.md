# Plant Photoreception and Light Sensing

Plants possess sophisticated light-sensing capabilities that, while fundamentally different from animal vision, allow them to perceive and respond to an extraordinary range of light information. Plants detect light color (wavelength), intensity, direction, and duration — information that governs virtually every aspect of their growth, development, and behavior.

## Light as Electromagnetic Radiation

Light is electromagnetic radiation within a specific range of wavelengths. The visible spectrum that humans perceive spans approximately 400-700 nanometers (nm), from violet/blue at the short-wavelength end to red at the long-wavelength end. However, plants detect light across a much broader range:

- **Ultraviolet (UV-B, 280-320 nm)**: Detected by the UVR8 photoreceptor; triggers protective responses including flavonoid production
- **UV-A/Blue light (320-500 nm)**: Detected by phototropins and cryptochromes; controls phototropism, stomatal opening, and [[plant-circadian-rhythms]]
- **Green light (500-600 nm)**: Can penetrate deeper into canopy; detected by cryptochromes and potentially other receptors
- **Red light (600-700 nm)**: Detected by phytochromes; controls germination, [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]], and flowering time
- **Far-red light (700-800 nm)**: Detected by phytochromes; signals proximity of neighboring vegetation

Plants can detect light levels as low as moonlight and as high as full sunlight — a dynamic range far exceeding that of human vision.

## Major Plant Photoreceptors

Plants use several families of photoreceptor proteins to detect different aspects of light:

### Phytochromes
Phytochromes are the most extensively studied plant photoreceptors. They exist in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr (red-light absorbing form)**: The inactive form that absorbs red light (~660 nm) and converts to Pfr
- **Pfr (far-red absorbing form)**: The biologically active form that absorbs far-red light (~730 nm) and reverts to Pr

This red/far-red reversible switching allows phytochromes to act as a molecular switch that detects the ratio of red to far-red light in the environment. Under direct sunlight, the R:FR ratio is approximately 1.2. When a plant is shaded by neighboring vegetation, the ratio drops because chlorophyll in the shading leaves absorbs red light but transmits or reflects far-red light. This drop in R:FR ratio triggers shade avoidance responses — stem elongation, upward leaf movement (hyponasty), and accelerated flowering — allowing the plant to grow toward light.

Phytochromes control numerous developmental processes including:
- Seed germination (many seeds require red light to break dormancy)
- De-etiolation (the transition from dark-grown to light-grown morphology)
- Shade avoidance and canopy position sensing
- Photoperiodic flowering (measurement of day length)
- [[cervantes-vegetative-phase]] change

### Phototropins
Phototropins are blue-light receptors that control directional growth responses:

- **Phototropism**: The bending of plant organs toward (stems) or away from (roots) light. Phototropins in the shoot tip detect light direction and trigger asymmetric auxin distribution, causing the shaded side of the stem to elongate faster.
- **Chloroplast movement**: In low light, phototropins cause chloroplasts to move to cell surfaces perpendicular to light to maximize absorption. In intense light, chloroplasts move to side walls to avoid photodamage.
- **Stomatal opening**: Blue light detected by phototropins in guard cells triggers ion fluxes that drive stomatal opening for CO₂ uptake.

### Cryptochromes
Cryptochromes are blue-light receptors with roles in:

- **Circadian clock entrainment**: Cryptochromes help synchronize the plant's internal clock with external light/dark cycles
- **De-etiolation**: Work alongside phytochromes to inhibit stem elongation and promote leaf expansion when seedlings emerge into light
- **Flowering time**: Cryptochromes contribute to photoperiodic sensing and influence the transition to flowering
- **Magnetoreception**: Some evidence suggests cryptochromes may enable plants to sense magnetic fields, though this remains controversial

### UVR8
The UV Resistance Locus 8 (UVR8) protein detects UV-B radiation:

- **Triggers photoprotection**: UVR8 activation leads to production of UV-absorbing flavonoids and other protective compounds
- **Regulates gene expression**: Activates defense genes and suppresses growth under high UV conditions
- **Monomerization mechanism**: UVR8 exists as a homodimer in the dark; UV-B absorption causes it to split into monomers, which then interact with COP1 protein to initiate signaling

## Photomorphogenesis vs. Skotomorphogenesis

Plants follow fundamentally different developmental programs depending on light availability:

### Skotomorphogenesis (Dark-Grown)
- Long, spindly stems with extended internodes
- Small, unexpanded cotyledons (yellow because chlorophyll hasn't formed)
- Apical hook (protects the shoot tip as it pushes through soil)
- Minimal leaf development
- Energy invested in reaching light as quickly as possible

### Photomorphogenesis (Light-Grown)
- Short, sturdy stems with compact internodes
- Expanded green cotyledons and true leaves with developed chloroplasts
- Open apical hook
- Active chlorophyll synthesis
- Energy invested in photosynthetic capacity

The transition from skotomorphogenesis to photomorphogenesis (de-etiolation) is one of the most dramatic developmental switches in biology, involving rapid changes in gene expression, hormone signaling, and organelle development.

## Light Intensity Sensing

Plants can detect and respond to an enormous range of light intensities:

- **Very low light (moonlight, ~0.1 µmol/m²/s)**: Cryptochromes and phytochromes can still detect light at these levels, influencing circadian rhythms
- **Low light (shade, 10-100 µmol/m²/s)**: Plants maximize light capture through leaf expansion, chloroplast positioning, and shade avoidance responses
- **Moderate light (typical indoor, 200-600 µmol/m²/s)**: Optimal range for photosynthesis in many species
- **High light (full sun, 1000-2000 µmol/m²/s)**: Maximum photosynthetic rate but risk of photoinhibition
- **Extreme light (sunflecks, >2000 µmol/m²/s)**: Triggers photoprotective mechanisms including non-photochemical quenching and chloroplast avoidance movement

Plants acclimate to different light intensities through changes in leaf anatomy (thicker leaves in high light), chlorophyll composition (adjusting chlorophyll a:b ratios), and photosystem stoichiometry.

## Light Duration: Photoperiodism

Many plants use day length (photoperiod) as a critical cue for timing developmental transitions:

- **Long-day plants**: Flower when days exceed a critical length (e.g., spinach, lettuce, many [[landrace-cannabis-sativa-strains]])
- **Short-day plants**: Flower when days fall below a critical length (e.g., chrysanthemums, Cannabis indica-dominant strains)
- **Day-neutral plants**: Flower regardless of photoperiod (e.g., tomato, rice)

The molecular basis of photoperiodism involves phytochromes and cryptochromes measuring the length of the light period, with the circadian clock gating the sensitivity to light signals. In the model plant Arabidopsis, the CONSTANS protein integrates light and clock signals to activate the flowering gene FT (FLOWERING LOCUS T).

## Applications in Horticulture

Understanding plant photoreception has direct applications in controlled environment agriculture:

- **Light spectrum manipulation**: LED grow lights can be tuned to provide specific wavelengths optimized for [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] (more blue) or flowering (more red/far-red)
- **Photoperiod control**: Indoor growers manipulate day length to trigger flowering in photoperiod-sensitive species like Cannabis
- **Shade avoidance management**: Understanding far-red signaling helps growers optimize plant spacing to avoid excessive stem elongation
- **Supplemental lighting**: Knowledge of action spectra allows efficient use of supplemental lighting to fill gaps in solar spectrum
- **End-of-day far-red treatment**: Brief far-red exposure at the end of the light period can accelerate flowering in some species
