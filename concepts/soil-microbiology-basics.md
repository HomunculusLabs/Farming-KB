# Soil Microbiology Basics

The soil food web is the community of organisms living in soil, from bacteria and fungi to protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms. Dr. Elaine Ingham's work demonstrates that these organisms are the primary drivers of soil fertility, plant health, and disease suppression -- not chemical nutrients alone.

## The Five Functions of a Healthy Soil Food Web

1. **Out-compete disease organisms**: Beneficial organisms occupy all infection sites and consume all available food, leaving nothing for pathogens. "This is NOT a pesticide effect. There are no toxic chemicals involved."
2. **Retain nutrients**: Bacteria and fungi immobilize nutrients in their biomass, preventing leaching and runoff
3. **Make nutrients available at rates plants require**: Protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria and fungi, releasing plant-available forms of nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients
4. **Decpose toxins**: Bacteria and fungi break down chemical residues and organic toxins
5. **Build soil structure**: Microbial glues create microaggregates (bacteria) and macroaggregates (fungi); larger organisms create pore spaces

## Bacteria

### Role
- Primary decomposers of simple organic compounds (sugars, proteins, amino acids)
- Make the glues that form microaggregates (the basic building blocks of soil structure)
- Retain nutrients in their biomass
- Some species fix atmospheric nitrogen (Rhizobium, Azotobacter)
- Some species produce antibiotics that suppress pathogens
- Dominant in annual crop systems, early successional ecosystems, and disturbed soils

### Key Facts
- A single gram of healthy soil contains 300,000 to 1,000,000 species of bacteria (by molecular identification)
- They reproduce rapidly (20-30 minutes under ideal conditions)
- Most soil bacteria are aerobic; anaerobic conditions kill beneficial species
- Simple sugars (molasses, fruit juice) selectively feed bacteria
- Excess bacterial growth can deplete oxygen, driving soil anaerobic

### In Compost Tea
- Minimum threshold: 300 ug/ml for adequate leaf surface coverage
- "Bacteria are the most variable" component of compost tea
- Active bacteria are more important than total bacteria

## Fungi

### Role
- Primary decomposers of complex organic compounds (lignin, cellulose, humic materials)
- Make the glues that form macroaggregates (crumbs and clumps that give soil structure)
- Hold nutrients (especially calcium, nitrogen, phosphorus) in their biomass
- Hyphal networks transport nutrients over distances
- Critical for disease suppression of many foliar and root diseases
- Dominant in perennial systems, forests, and late successional ecosystems
- [[teaming-with-fungi-mycorrhizal-inoculation-techniques]] form symbiotic associations with plant roots

### Key Facts
- Grow as hyphae (thread-like filaments) that can extend meters through soil
- Grow much more slowly than bacteria
- Extremely sensitive to oxygen: "Any time oxygen concentration falls below 5.5 to 6 ppm oxygen, the beneficial fungi will be lost"
- Humic acids, fish hydrolysate, and lignin selectively feed fungi
- "Fungi must be brought back in order to suppress root rot diseases, to hold onto calcium, nitrogen, phosphorus, and to build soil structure"
- Can produce 30-50% water use reduction through improved soil structure

### Good vs. Bad Fungi
- **Beneficial**: Thick white strands/hyphae growing through compost; produce enzymes that decompose complex materials
- **Undesirable**: Gray fuzzy aerial growth, dust-like spore clouds (often Fusarium, Phytophthora, or other pathogens)
- "Strands of hyphae through the compost, not dust clouds" is the visual indicator

## Protozoa

### Role
- Primary nutrient cyclers: consume bacteria and release plant-available nitrogen
- Three types: **flagellates** (smallest, most numerous), **amoebae** (medium), **ciliates** (largest)
- Flagellates and amoebae are the main nutrient cyclers in healthy soil
- Key to making nitrogen available: bacteria immobilize N, protozoa eat bacteria, N is mineralized

### Key Facts
- All protozoa require aerobic conditions
- Ciliates are indicator organisms: high ciliate numbers indicate anaerobic or compacted soil conditions
- Protozoa are lost when compost tea goes anaerobic
- Worm compost is the best source for protozoa

## Nematodes

### Role
- Four functional groups based on what they eat:
  - **Bacterial-feeders**: Cycle nutrients by consuming bacteria; second most important nutrient cyclers after protozoa
  - **Fungal-feeders**: Regulate fungal populations; cycle nutrients
  - **Root-feeders**: Plant parasites; indicate soil health problems
  - **Predators**: Eat other nematodes; regulate populations
- Important nutrient cyclers in perennial systems
- Some species trap and consume pathogenic fungi

### Key Facts
- Visible to the naked eye (0.1-2mm long)
- Require consistent moisture; dry conditions kill them
- "Worm compost is the best place" for beneficial nematodes
- Forest soil is a good source for collecting beneficial nematodes
- Thermal compost can have good numbers but "not consistently" -- most composters don't manage for biology
- Root-feeding nematodes have thick cuticles that resist bacteria; they prefer reduced-oxygen conditions

## Nutrient Cycling: The Key Mechanism

The fundamental nutrient cycle in soil food webs:

1. Organic matter is decomposed by bacteria and fungi
2. Nutrients are immobilized (locked up) in bacterial and fungal biomass
3. Protozoa and bacterial-feeding nematodes consume bacteria and fungi
4. Nutrients are mineralized (released) in plant-available forms (ammonium, nitrate, phosphate)
5. Plants take up mineralized nutrients
6. If plants don't take up nutrients, other bacteria and fungi re-immobilize them

This cycle is why adding compost or compost tea is more effective than adding soluble fertilizer: the biology holds nutrients until plants need them, preventing leaching.

## Anaerobic Conditions: The Enemy

When soil goes anaerobic (oxygen drops below 5.5-6 ppm):

- Beneficial fungi die
- Protozoa die
- Beneficial nematodes die
- Anaerobic bacteria produce toxic metabolites (hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, organic acids)
- Human pathogens (Clostridium, E. coli, Salmonella) can grow
- Roots are damaged or killed by anaerobic metabolic byproducts
- Soil structure is destroyed as bacterial glues break down

"Lack of oxygen results in growth of anaerobic bacteria, and the products of anaerobic metabolism kill roots."

updated: 2026-04-18 Even if all the right plant nutrients are present in unlimited quantities in your soil, there are other factors that affect their availability to plants. A gardener must understand not only what nutrients plants need and how to supply them, but also the special conditions required to ensure those nutrients are most efficiently taken up by plants. Temperature has a direct effect on the level of microbial activity in the soil. Problems with pH plague chemical gardeners more often than organic gardeners who use [[compost-building-guide]] and are building humus. Organic gardeners should occasionally test for pH but normally need not worry too much -- just continually add compost and use mulches to increase organic matter, which increases [[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]], food web populations, and buffering capacity.
