# Phototropism

Phototropism is the directional growth response of a plant toward or away from a
light source. It is one of the most fundamental and widely observed behaviors in
the plant kingdom, exhibited by nearly all plant species. The term derives from
the Greek "photo" (light) and "tropos" (turn). Charles Darwin, in his 1880 book
*The Power of Movement in Plants*, wrote that "there are extremely few [plants],
of which some part does not bend towards lateral light." In modern molecular
terms, phototropism results from the asymmetric redistribution of the plant
hormone auxin, mediated by blue-light photoreceptors called phototropins.

## Darwin's Landmark Experiments

The mechanistic understanding of phototropism was pioneered by Charles Darwin
and his son Francis in a series of elegant experiments published in 1880. For
the twenty years following the publication of *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin
had conducted botanical research that continues to influence plant science to
this day. The Darwins were fascinated by the effects of light on plant growth,
as was their contemporary Julius von Sachs.

The Darwins grew canary grass (*Phalaris canariensis*) seedlings in total
darkness for several days, then exposed them to a dim gas lamp placed twelve
feet (3.5 meters) away, so faint that "they could not see the seedlings
themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper."

After only three hours, the seedlings had visibly curved toward the light, with
the bending consistently occurring about an inch or two centimeters below the
tip.

To determine which part of the plant perceived the light, the Darwins tested
five conditions on identical seedlings:

1. **Untreated control**: bent toward light normally
2. **Tip removed**: no bending occurred, the plant went "blind"
3. **Tip covered with an opaque cap**: no bending, confirming the tip was needed
4. **Tip covered with a clear glass cap**: bending occurred, proving light
   transmission through the tip was sufficient
5. **Middle section covered with an opaque tube**: bending occurred normally,
   showing the bending region did not need to perceive light

This single experiment demonstrated that phototropism results from light
detection at the shoot tip, which then transmits a signal downward to cause
differential elongation in the stem. The Darwins had successfully demonstrated
rudimentary sight in plants, published in 1880, nearly fifty years before the
photoreceptor molecules themselves were identified.

## Blue Light as the Primary Trigger

In 1864, Julius von Sachs discovered that blue light is the primary color
inducing phototropism in plants, while other colors have little effect on
directional bending. This specificity was later attributed to photoreceptor
proteins called phototropins, which are most sensitive to blue wavelengths
(approximately 450 nm).

Phototropins contain two LOV (Light, Oxygen, or Voltage) domains that bind
flavin mononucleotide (FMN) as a chromophore. Upon absorbing blue photons, the
FMN chromophore undergoes a conformational change that activates the phototropin
kinase domain, triggering downstream signaling.

Phototropins activate auxin transporters (PIN proteins) on the plasma membrane,
causing auxin to accumulate on the shaded side of the stem. The resulting
differential cell elongation on the shaded side produces the characteristic
curvature toward the light source. This mechanism operates across vascular
plants, from ferns to angiosperms, underscoring its ancient evolutionary
origin.

Two phototropins exist in Arabidopsis:

- **phot1** mediates responses to low-intensity blue light
- **phot2** handles high-intensity responses including the chloroplast
  avoidance response

## Phototropism Versus Photosynthesis

A critical insight from the Darwin experiments was that phototropism is
independent of photosynthesis. The dim gas lamp used in their experiments
provided insufficient light for photosynthesis, yet the plants still bent
toward it. This proved that phototropism arises from a dedicated light-sensing
system rather than from the energy-gathering process of photosynthesis.

Plants possess multiple, distinct photoreceptor systems that serve different
biological functions, much as the human eye has both rods (for low-light vision)
and cones (for color vision). The human retina contains about 125 million rods
and 6 million cones in an area the size of a passport photo, equivalent to
roughly 130 megapixels.

Plants similarly deploy multiple photoreceptor families, each tuned to different
wavelengths and serving distinct physiological purposes:

- Phototropins for directional blue-light responses
- Phytochromes for red/far-red light measurement
- Cryptochromes for blue-light circadian entrainment
- UVR8 for ultraviolet-B perception
- Zeitlupe family for circadian timing

## Phototropism in Fungi

While phototropism is most commonly associated with plants, many fungi also
exhibit directional growth responses to light. Certain mushroom species direct
their fruiting body growth toward light sources, an adaptation that aids spore
dispersal by orienting the cap for optimal spore release.

The phototropic responses of fungi utilize different photoreceptor systems than
those of plants, often involving white collar proteins (WC-1 and WC-2) and
cryptochromes rather than phototropins. The white collar complex in
Neurospora crassa was one of the first fungal photoreceptors characterized at
the molecular level and has served as a model for understanding fungal light
perception.

The study of fungal phototropism intersects with cultivation science, as light
direction and intensity can significantly influence the morphology and yield of
cultivated mushroom species.

## Negative Phototropism

While most above-ground plant organs (stems, leaves, petioles) exhibit positive
phototropism (growing toward light), roots typically show negative phototropism,
growing away from light. This response helps roots maintain their orientation
within the soil matrix and is mediated by the same basic auxin redistribution
mechanism but with inverted polarity.

Root phototropism is generally weaker than shoot phototropism and may be
masked by the dominant gravitropic response of roots, which causes them to grow
downward regardless of light direction. However, under conditions where gravity
is removed or altered, root phototropism becomes more apparent, as has been
observed in spaceflight experiments.

## Time-Lapse Evidence

Time-lapse photography has revealed that phototropic responses in plants are not
simple, linear bending events. Plants exhibit oscillatory movements called
circumnutation even in uniform light, and the phototropic response involves
complex tracking behavior.

When a light source is moved, the plant gradually reorients its growth
direction to follow it. The speed of the phototropic response varies
considerably among species and is influenced by light intensity, temperature,
and the physiological state of the plant. Some plants can complete a
phototropic bend within hours, while others require days to achieve full
reorientation.

## Agricultural Relevance

Understanding phototropism has practical implications for agriculture and
horticulture. In greenhouse production, uniform lighting from above promotes
straight, upright growth, while lateral lighting can cause undesirable leaning.

In forestry, seedlings grown in dense nurseries may develop pronounced
phototropic curvature that affects subsequent field performance. In space
agriculture, where gravitational cues are absent, phototropism becomes one of
the primary directional cues available to plants, making it critical for
designing growth chambers for orbital or lunar habitats.
