# Permaculture Dryland Strategies

updated: 2026-04-18

Drylands cover approximately 40% of the Earth's land surface and are home to over 2 billion people. Bill Mollison's permaculture design approach provides specific strategies for productive land use in arid and semi-arid regions, drawing from both traditional desert agriculture and modern ecological design.

## Core Principles for Drylands

### Water as the Limiting Factor
In drylands, water is the primary limiting factor. Every design decision must prioritize water capture, storage, and efficient use. The permaculture approach inverts the conventional view of deserts as wastelands -- deserts are productive if designed correctly.

### Swales and Earthworks
- **Swales**: Level ditches on contour that capture runoff water and allow it to infiltrate into the soil
- **Keyline design**: A system of water management using the natural topographic keypoint to distribute water across the landscape
- **Gabions**: Wire cages filled with rock that slow water flow in arroyos and gullies
- **Check dams**: Small dams across watercourses that slow runoff and build soil behind them

### Microcatchments
Small-scale water harvesting structures for individual plants:
- **Negarim**: Diamond-shaped microcatchments for individual trees
- **Semi-circular bunds**: For shrubs and grasses on slopes
- **Contour bunds**: For rows of trees or crops
- **Vallerani system**: Mechanical creation of microcatchments for large-scale reforestation

## Wind and Shade

### Windbreaks
Wind is as critical as water in drylands. Windbreaks:
- Reduce evapotranspiration by 20-30%
- Protect crops from desiccation
- Create microclimates for understory plantings
- Provide biomass for mulch and fodder

### Shade Structures
- Use shade to reduce evaporation from soil and water surfaces
- Shade houses for nurseries and seedling protection
- Deciduous trees provide shade in summer, allow light in winter

## Plant Strategies for Drylands

### Drought-Adapted Species
- Deep-rooted perennials that access groundwater
- Succulents that store water in tissues
- Waxy-leaved plants that reduce transpiration
- Plants with small or needle-like leaves (reduced surface area)
- Dormancy strategies (die back to roots during dry season)

### Zone-Stacking
In drylands, zones are compressed. What might be Zone 2 in a temperate climate becomes Zone 1 in a desert. Intensive management is concentrated near water sources.

### Pioneer Species
First-phase species that prepare the soil for later, more productive species:
- Nitrogen-fixing trees (mesquite, acacia, carob)
- Deep-rooted plants that break hardpan
- Species that provide shade and leaf litter for soil building

## Soil Building in Drylands

### The Challenge
Desert soils are typically:
- Low in organic matter (slow decomposition due to low moisture)
- High in mineral content (nutrients are present but unavailable)
- Compacted or crusted (prevents water infiltration)
- Saline (evaporation concentrates salts at surface)

### Strategies
- **Mulch**: Critical for reducing evaporation, moderating soil temperature, and building organic matter. Use stone mulch (traditional in desert agriculture) as well as organic mulch.
- **Compost teas**: Liquid organic matter that can be applied to plants and soil
- **Biochar**: Charcoal added to soil improves water retention and nutrient availability
- **Green manure cover crops**: During brief wet seasons, fast-growing legumes add organic matter
- **Grazing management**: Managed grazing with hoof action breaks soil crust and incorporates organic matter

## Traditional Desert Agriculture

### Nabatean and Roman Desert Farming
The Nabateans ( Petra, Jordan) and Romans developed sophisticated water-harvesting systems in the Negev desert:
- Diversion channels from distant watersheds
- Terraced hillsides for runoff farming
- Underground cisterns for water storage
- These systems supported agriculture in areas receiving less than 100mm annual rainfall

### Persian Qanats
Underground water channels that tap groundwater and transport it by gravity to settlements and farms, with minimal evaporation loss.

### Afghan Karez
Similar to qanats, these underground aqueducts have sustained agriculture in arid Afghanistan for millennia.

## Livestock in Drylands

### Appropriate Stocking Rates
Overgrazing is the primary cause of desertification worldwide. Permaculture emphasizes:
- Stocking rates appropriate to carrying capacity (which varies seasonally)
- Holistic grazing management (Allan Savory's planned grazing)
- Goats and camels as more appropriate than cattle in arid regions
- Poultry in portable coops for manure distribution

### Integration with Tree Systems
Silvopasture -- combining trees with grazing -- is particularly appropriate for drylands:
- Trees provide shade and reduce evapotranspiration
- Leguminous trees fix nitrogen for pasture grasses
- Livestock control undergrowth and recycle nutrients
- Carob, mesquite, and acacia pods provide fodder

## Climate Adaptation

### Hot Arid vs. Cold Arid
Design strategies differ between:
- **Hot arid** (Sahara, Arabian, Australian): Emphasis on shade, evaporative cooling, heat-tolerant species
- **Cold arid** (Gobi, Patagonia, high mountain): Emphasis on wind protection, frost pockets, thermal mass, short-season varieties

### Monsoon Drylands
Areas with pronounced wet/dry seasons (India, Africa, northern Australia):
- Capture maximum water during wet season for dry-season use
- Short but intense growing season requires efficient planning
- Storage (water, food, seed) is critical for survival through dry months
