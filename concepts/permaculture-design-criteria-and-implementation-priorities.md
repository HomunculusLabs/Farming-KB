## Permaculture Design Criteria and Implementation Priorities

[[permaculture-design]] criteria provide the framework for making placement decisions about every element in a landscape. Mollison insists that any design must satisfy these criteria or it fails as [[permaculture]], regardless of how aesthetically pleasing or commercially successful it may appear.

## Why Design?

Mollison states that the main reasons for designing a plant system are:

- To save our energies in the system
- To cope with energies entering the system from outside (sun, wind, fire)
- To arrange plants so that they assist the health and survival of other plants
- To place all units (plants, earthworks, artifacts such as houses) in the best possible arrangement in landscape
- To suit climate and site (specific design)
- To integrate with man and society, and save heating fuel and cooking energy
- To provide a wide range of necessities for man, in a way every man can achieve

## Critique of Conventional Landscape Design

Mollison is scathing about what passes for landscape design in [[mycophobia-in-western-culture]]:

### The Taj Mahal Example
"The formal lawns outside the courts of the Taj are maintained by a crouching group of 20–30 widows, equipped with small knives to cut the grass, and forced to this undignified labour by the need to maintain the status of (deceased) nobility."

### The British Hedge Example
"The patient hedger and gardener of Britain tugs his forelock and clips away at the hedges of the nouveaux riches, and the council employee tends the public parks and gardens for their public status value only."

### The Home Gardener
"The home gardener, in his front garden, tries to keep up a pale imitation of the same high status. But in this case, of course, he is the schizoid serf as well as the feudal lord, following his lawnmower and wielding his hedge clippers, contorting roses and privet into fanciful and meaningless topiary, as a witness to a deprived and stunted education."

### The Cabbage Incident
Mollison recounts a man in Burnie, Tasmania who planted cabbages on his "nature strip"—the formal area in front of his house. The local council sent trucks and men to uproot the vegetables "which were merely useful, therefore of no aesthetic or design value." By 1979, the council had begun tentatively planting fruit and nut species in public parks.

## The Two Elements of Good Design

1. **Principles**: The management of natural elements to the advantage of man and the environment (where we are going)
2. **Procedures**: Practical gardening and [[holzer-alpine-farming-techniques]] (how we get there)

Windbreak shape, species, and placement fall under principles; mulching, manuring, and soil improvement fall under procedures.

## Ongoing Evolution

Once design begins, the essentials for ongoing evolution are:
- **Observation of the result**
- **Control or steering** to the desired end, or to a new evolution

"In complex systems, where animals and plants interact, even computers fail to cope with the number of variables, or account for the worm, the robin, the soil, and their total relationship." Only the careful human observer can assess such systems. "Quite small adjustments to the system may have the effect of turning disaster into triumph, or invasion into retreat."

Mollison distinguishes masters: "Yeoman is a master of design; Fukuoka is a master of strategy."

## Design Criteria Checklist

### Energy Criteria
- **Passive energy systems**: The site must work with natural energy flows
- **Minimal external energy needs**: The system should be self-sustaining
- **Adequate [[mollison-pdc-trellis-systems-climate-control]] on site**: Without mechanical intervention
- **Future developments planned**: Space reserved for future energy systems

### Self-Sufficiency Criteria
- **Provision for food self-sufficiency on site**: Total food provision or the system risks failure
- **Water supply assured, conserved**: Clean water as a foundation of health

### Waste Criteria
- **Wastes safely disposed of on site**: No pollution exported to surrounding environment

### Maintenance Criteria
- **Low-maintenance structures and grounds**: High maintenance costs may exceed initial costs by 10x or more

### Protection Criteria
- **Fire, cold, excess heat, and wind factors controlled and directed**: External energies managed, not ignored

## Implementation Priorities

### Planning Priority
1. First priority: **Planning**
2. Second: **Human needs**
3. Third: **[[desert-house-water-energy-conservation]]**
4. Almost as a result: **Environmental modification by designs and structures**

### Construction Priority
1. **First**: Structures and designs that **generate** energy
2. **Second**: Those which **save** energy
3. **Third**: Those which **consume** energy

### The Reservation Principle
"Provision for future energy conservation systems must be left open, so that the whole site is marked out for wind, tide, water, or sun systems. Even if these cannot be implemented in the first few years, the space is "reserved' under annual crop or short term use."

## The Synergy Principle

"In all our planning, the main aim should be to achieve a synergy, so that everything that can be together is put together, works together, and helps each other."

The permaculture motto captures this: **"Every unit has many uses; every use is achieved in many ways"—a fail-safe system.**

## The Two Fundamental Rules

1. **"No placement without the element (plant, animal, or structure) serving at least two or more functions."**
2. **"Every function (water collection, fire protection) served in two or more ways."**

These two rules, combined with the criteria above, constitute the complete methodology for permaculture design decisions.

## Practical Design Decisions

### Where to Build a Glasshouse?
Ranked by energy efficiency:
1. Against dwellings as heat sources and stores, and to grow food
2. Against non-dwelling structures as heat sources
3. As part of [[permaculture-designers-manual-animal-housing-detailed]], with heat, manure, and gas exchange
4. Only finally, or perhaps never: as free-standing, all-glazed structures

### How to Deal with Preventive Wind?
1. Plant any tree or shrub that is cheap/free locally, grows very quickly, survives (wormwood, pampas, pine, coprosma, thorn)
2. Structures: trellis, loose or dry-stone wall, ditch, bank, small hedgerow
3. Broadscale quickset or seedling planting of hardy species
4. Useful permanent hedge planted under the protection of the above

### What is Worth Main-Cropping?
Three main considerations:
1. Needs little attention after establishment (potatoes, corn, pumpkin)
2. Easy to harvest, store, and use
3. May form a staple in diet

Commercial additions:
4. High economic value (strawberries, raspberries)
5. Hard to keep (melons, peaches)
6. Rare but in wide demand (ginseng, asparagus)
7. Particularly suited to site (cactus, fig, water chestnut, cranberry)

### Tree Crop Selection (Same Criteria)
1. Plums, hardy fruits, vines
2. Nuts and easily dried fruits
3. Nuts again, high food value fruits
4. Cherries, crocus for saffron
5. Local soft fruits (paw-paw, raspberries)
6. Spices, medicines, dyes, oils
7. Sugar maple, cider gum, pistachios

## Yield: Beyond Product

Mollison identifies four components of total yield:
1. **Product yields**: Variety and amount of crop
2. **Design yields**: Channelling and conservation of energy
3. **Interaction yields**: Energy transfer between permaculture and other systems (dwellings)
4. **Social and health yields**

The total yield depends more on design—knowledge and intellect—than on available energy. Design determines edge amount, species arrangement, and energy flow—all of which multiply productivity beyond what any single crop analysis would predict.
