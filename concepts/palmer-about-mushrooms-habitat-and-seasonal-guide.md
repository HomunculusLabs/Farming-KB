# Habitat and Seasonal Guide

## The Myth of Overnight Growth

One of Palmer's most emphatic corrections is that mushrooms do NOT spring up in a single night. Most of the development is subterranean, in the spawn or mycelium. A heavy dew or evening shower straightens the stem and expands the cap, breaking the earth during the night -- so the gatherer finds white buttons in the morning where nothing was visible the day before.

But days and even weeks are necessary for full production, just as for a rose. The common mushroom requires about ten days from a pin-head button to a disk three or four inches in diameter. This is the average period for other kinds, although the Coprinariae consume less than half that time. "Popular error has made mushroom growth proverbial for a superficiality which by the fungi, at least, is undeserved."

## The Spawn (Mycelium)

The hidden part of the fungus -- a white, branching, vine-like substance permeating the soil -- is called "spawn." It corresponds to the vine of a grape, while the visible mushroom represents the fruit or blossom.

Palmer witnessed the excavation opposite the Brunswick Hotel in Boston, where "as the pick of the workman broke up the soil, a white substance ran through every piece. Starting with large branches, it divided and sub-divided like the veins on the back of the hand." This was the spawn of [[coprinus-comatus-cultivation]]. The smell was very strong and noticeable across the street.

Key properties of spawn:
- Is as real and distinct for each variety as the hop-vine from the woodbine, the ivy from the virgin's bower.
- Will produce the same variety in the same spot year after year, with the same certainty as a farmer's wheat crop.
- On the very spots where Palmer gathered mushrooms in 1874, he found the identical variety in 1879.
- Can pass a year or even become extinct under difficult conditions, just as any crop may fail.
- Interlaces and twines with the spawn of other species beneath the soil, so wholesome food and mortal poison may grow side by side from adjacent matrices.
- The means of reproduction from spore to spawn remains one of the great unsolved mysteries of biology.

## Seasonal Succession

The various kinds of fungi "succeed each other in rotation just as the bloodroot and anemones of spring are followed by the roses of summer and the cardinal or gentian of fall." The main season runs from **May to November**, with some varieties producing more than one crop during this period.

As Palmer wrote poetically: "When, as now, the autumn leaves are falling, soon to be covered with the winter's snows, and no vestige of your growth remains... by some creative process hidden below the moss of the bank or under the grass of the lawn, the face of the earth, even if apparently barren of root or germ, will in the coming of the spring be once more smiling with these 'commas in sweet nature's book, strewn up and down her emerald-tinted page.'"

The mushroom gatherer can establish regular haunts, returning year after year to the same spots for favorite varieties, with "just that degree of certainty, no more and no less" as the farmer who expects his wheat crop.

## Specific Habitat Preferences by Species

### The Meadow / Pasture Mushroom (Agaricus campestris)
- Found where cattle graze; always associated with pastures and open fields.
- Follows the horse through every climate; can be raised from horse manure.
- Reproduction likely aided by passage through the horse's intestinal canal.
- Rich lawns and well-kept gardens produce the finest specimens.
- Never has a volva and is seldom found in woods.

### The Elm-tree Mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius)
- Grows ONLY in the fall, exclusively on elm trees.
- Most common in a cleft or where a limb has been sawn off.
- Reappears yearly in the same spot.
- Palmer famously gathered them from the elms on Boston Common using a long pole with a knife at the end.

### The Maned Mushroom (Coprinus comatus)
- Found in lawns (delicious when from clean grass) but also in dust-heaps, brick-yards, and city offal (no flavor and potentially deleterious from polluted locations).
- The quality of the specimen depends entirely on its environment -- same species, vastly different edibility.

### The Fairy-ring Champignon (Marasmius oreades)
- Grows in great profusion in rich, grassy lawns.
- Forms the classic "fairy rings" in turf.

### The Russulae
- Found in woods; they sometimes choose open spots but never venture far from the shade of trees.
- Side by side with these grow the beautiful but deadly Amanitas -- a dangerous proximity requiring careful discrimination.

### The Boleti
- Grow from the ground with a distinct stem (unlike the Polyporei, which grow mostly on wood with abortive stems).
- Found in forests; Palmer notes meeting Italians "making a harvest here of like varieties to those they eat at home."
- Abundant in wooded areas of New England.
- The Boletus sub-tomentosus (olive-red Boletus) was Palmer's favorite until he was made ill by specimens in a waterlogged, fermented state.

### The Amanita Family
- Found everywhere in the vicinity of Boston (ten varieties identified).
- Grow in woods, uncultivated ground, on margins of pastures near woods, and in fence corners.
- Resemble the common mushroom when young, frequently leading to fatal mistakes.
- Appear after heavy rains at certain periods of the year.
- Common in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and throughout the northeastern states.

### The Clavaria (Coral Fungi)
- Found in forests during fall.
- Abundant around Boston; enough for days can be gathered in an hour.

### The Hydnei (Teeth-bearing Fungi)
- Forest dwellers; the spreading hydnum is found on the forest floor.

## Geography and Abundance

- New England contains about 3,000 species of fungi, of which roughly 1,000 are of sufficient size and substance to be noticed by the forager (the balance being microscopic, gelatinous, or otherwise unsuitable for food).
- Palmer believed "tons of rich, wholesome food rot yearly in our woods and pastures."
- America is "very rich in the production of esculent fungi."
- Among other nations, the Chinese, Russians, Germans, French, and Italians use mushrooms very extensively. Palmer speaks from personal experience of Chinese habits acquired in San Francisco, Honolulu, and Hong Kong.
- The Truffle, which grows entirely underground (both spawn and "blossom" beneath the surface), has been reported in isolated American specimens but is not established as indigenous. It is "a delicacy of the epicure's table" that might be discovered in American oak plantations if trained swine or dogs were imported from France.

## Cultivation

Only one species is susceptible of artificial cultivation: the horse-mushroom (Agaricus arvensis) and its wild relative, the meadow mushroom (Agaricus campestris). This fact alone explains why this single variety became "the mushroom" of commerce while all others are dismissed as "toadstools."

### Cultivation Method
1. Prepare a bed two-thirds horse manure, one-third from other grazing animals or rich loam.
2. Break and turn until a thermometer shows 60-80 degrees Fahrenheit.
3. Insert blocks of spawn (preferably from an old mushroom bed rather than purchased -- much commercial spawn "contains not a trace of the fungus").
4. Plant in sections (like a Pullman car) so different ends bear at different times.
5. In about three weeks, the bed fills with the white vine; in ten days more, mushrooms appear.

### Common Causes of Failure
1. Lack of good spawn.
2. Spawning the entire bed at one time instead of in sections.
3. Failure to maintain atmospheric moisture -- water must come from beneath (like dew), not from surface watering. "Watering the ground is worse than useless."
4. The fungi are very susceptible to atmospheric change and cannot grow without attention to humidity.
5. Steam pipes in greenhouses work best for maintaining the required dog-days atmosphere.

This explains the high price of cultivated mushrooms ($1-2 per pound in Palmer's time) and the fact that many beds produce only a single picking before going barren.

## Best Practices for Gathering

- **Time of day**: Best gathered in the **afternoon when dry**. Morning dew is irrelevant; the idea that mushrooms must be gathered with dew on them is "vulgar error."
- **Condition**: Specimens should be young, fresh, and free from maggots (which infest them in large quantities as soon as they become stale).
- **Quantity**: A large lot of the same kind is preferable to a miscellaneous collection for identification purposes.
- **Storage for transport**: Lay in damp grass after gathering.
- **Regular haunts**: The forager can establish regular locations, returning year after year to the same spots for favorite varieties.
- **Identification**: Any specimens brought for identification should be young, fresh, and free from maggots.
