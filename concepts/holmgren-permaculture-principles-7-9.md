Source: David Holmgren, *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002)

## Principle 7: Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback

### Top-Down Thinking, Bottom-Up Action
Permaculture design begins with the most concrete and mundane aspects of material existence through self-audit. The garden provides a laboratory for exploring change processes because we can exercise great power if and when we choose.

### The Jevons Paradox (Problems of Success)
William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that improvements in steam engine efficiency led to increased coal consumption, not reduced. This paradox -- where efficiency gains lead to increased consumption -- is central to understanding why technological solutions alone cannot solve sustainability problems.

Applications:
- Fuel-efficient cars encourage more driving
- Water-efficient appliances encourage more water use
- Insulated houses encourage higher internal temperatures
- Communication technologies increase the volume of trivial communication
- The "prosperous road down" is Holmgren's proposed solution: deliberately choosing to use less

### Organisational Change: Semco Example
Semco, a Brazilian company under Ricardo Semler, demonstrated radical organisational redesign:
- Flattened hierarchy to maximum of 7 layers
- Worker share ownership
- Human-scale workplaces (150-200 people)
- Workers set their own hours and choose their own managers
- Productivity and profits increased dramatically

This illustrates that permaculture design principles apply equally to organisations and social systems as to landscapes.

## Principle 8: Integrate Rather Than Segregate

### Zoning as Integration Tool
Permaculture zones (0-5) represent an integration of elements based on frequency of use and management intensity:
- Zone 0: House (centre of activity)
- Zone 1: Intensive garden, daily attention
- Zone 2: Less intensive, regular attention
- Zone 3: Broadacre crops, occasional attention
- Zone 4: Managed forest, seasonal attention
- Zone 5: Wilderness, minimal management

### Traditional Land Use Regulation
Nepalese villages maintained a 1:7 ratio of arable land to common forest. The caste system in India can be read as permaculture zoning: Brahmins (Zone 0-1 garden), Kshatriyas (Zone 2-3 arable/pasture), Vaishyas (Zone 3-4 trade), Shudras (Zone 4-5 forest management).

### Integration in Built Environment
- Multi-purpose buildings easily retrofitted internally
- Earth and wood construction
- Minimised pumps and active devices
- Buildings change over time by organic processes
- Stewart Brand's Six S's: Site, Structure, Skin, Services, Space Plan, Stuff (most to least permanent)

### Melliodora Design Example
Holmgren's own property demonstrates integration:
- Earth and stone terraces, dams, paths
- Minimal fences and outbuildings
- Multi-purpose buildings
- Living fences of wild cherry plum seedlings
- Seedling fruit trees for fruit and rootstock given away to others
- Goats to eat blackberry and coarse pasture, encouraging richer species for geese

## Principle 9: Use Small and Slow Solutions

### Scale and Appropriate Technology
"The bigger they are, the harder they fall" applies to systems as well as individuals. Large-scale systems are more vulnerable to catastrophic failure, less adaptable to change, and require more energy to maintain.

### Agricultural Scale
- Small farms are more productive per unit area than large ones
- Diverse smallholdings are more resilient than monocultures
- Local food systems are more resilient than global supply chains
- Human-scale workplaces (150-200 people) are more effective and satisfying

### Retrofitting Design Criteria
For suburban retrofitting, Holmgren advocates:
- Modest scale
- Long-life and renewable materials
- Simple maintenance requirements
- Multi-purpose and adaptable designs
- Low capital investment

### Successional Approach to Gardens
Most people design gardens following the "initial floristic composition model" and are disappointed when results don't match expectations. Understanding succession is essential:
- Wide-spaced shelterbelts struggle with competing grass
- Sheet mulch gardens have limited lifespan without constant mulch input
- Food forests take years to mature
- Pulsing systems (cultivation followed by recovery) must respect the rebuilding phase

### The Slow Food Connection
Slow food, slow money, slow cities -- all reflect the principle that "small and slow" solutions build more resilient, adaptable systems. Holmgren argues this is not romantic nostalgia but practical recognition that energy descent will force a return to human-scale systems.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

### Ecosystem Services
The value of environmental services exceeds total economic output:
- Water purification via reed beds, living machines, and swamps
- Climate regulation
- Soil building and erosion prevention
- Pollination
- Pest regulation

### Traditional Commons
Common property resource management systems (forests, grazing, water) demonstrate that communities can self-regulate resource use without either private ownership or state control. The "tragedy of the commons" is better understood as a tragedy of open access (lack of regulation) rather than of common property itself.

### "To Change the World We Need to Change Ourselves"
Holmgren emphasises that permaculture is not just a design system for landscapes but a framework for personal and social transformation. The most powerful changes start with the individual, in the garden, and radiate outward through demonstration and example.
