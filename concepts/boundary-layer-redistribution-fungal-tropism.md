# Boundary Layer Redistribution and Evaporative Demand Hypothesis for Fungal Tropism

## Overview

The evaporative demand hypothesis proposes that fungal tropisms, including
anemotropism (wind-directed growth) and phototropism (light-directed growth),
are mediated through a common mechanism: differential evaporative demand
across the surface of the stipe. When wind flows past a cylindrical stipe,
the boundary layer of still air that clings to the surface is redistributed,
becoming thicker on the leeward (downwind) side and thinner on the windward
(upwind) side. This asymmetric boundary layer creates differential rates of
water evaporation from the stipe surface, which in turn drives differential
cell expansion rates that produce curvature toward or away from the stimulus.
This hypothesis unifies multiple tropic responses under a single physical
mechanism.

## Boundary Layer Physics on Cylindrical Stipes

Every solid object immersed in a flowing fluid develops a boundary layer, a
region of reduced flow velocity adjacent to the surface where viscous forces
dominate. For a cylindrical mushroom stipe in a wind stream, the boundary
layer is thinnest on the windward side (facing into the flow) where the
freestream velocity is highest and the pressure gradient drives air close to
the surface. On the leeward side, flow separation creates a wider region of
recirculating air with lower velocity. The thicker leeward boundary layer
acts as an insulating layer that reduces the rate of heat and mass transfer
between the stipe surface and the surrounding air. Water vapour diffusing
from the stipe surface must traverse this boundary layer to reach the bulk
air, so a thicker boundary layer means slower evaporation on the leeward
side and faster evaporation on the windward side.

## Evaporative Demand as the Growth Signal

The evaporative demand hypothesis posits that the differential evaporation
rate across the stipe creates a differential water potential within the
tissue. Cells on the windward side, experiencing greater evaporative water
loss, develop lower turgor pressure than cells on the leeward side. Since
cell expansion in growing fungal tissue is driven by turgor pressure acting
against the cell wall, cells with lower turgor expand more slowly while
cells with higher turgor expand more rapidly. The result is that the
leeward side of the stipe grows faster than the windward side, causing the
stipe to curve toward the wind. This is exactly the anemotropic response
observed [[cap-and-stem-alkaloid-distribution-in-psilocybe-cubensis-strains]] and Phycomyces. The same mechanism can
explain phototropism if light heats the illuminated side of the stipe,
increasing evaporative demand on that side and producing curvature toward
the light source.

## The Four-Factor Evaporative Demand Model

Badham identified four factors that determine evaporative demand at the
stipe surface: wind velocity, humidity, light intensity, and temperature.
Each factor influences the rate of water evaporation from the stipe and
thus the magnitude of the tropic response. Wind increases evaporation by
thinning the boundary layer. Low humidity increases the vapour pressure
gradient that drives evaporation. Light increases evaporation by heating
the surface and increasing the saturated vapour pressure at the surface
temperature. Temperature affects both the water viscosity (influencing
tissue water movement) and the vapour pressure gradient. These four
factors interact: the effect of wind on evaporation is greater at low
humidity than at high humidity, and the effect of light is greater when
wind is present to carry away the heated, moisture-laden boundary layer.
The relative importance of each factor determines which tropic response
dominates in any given environment.

## Evidence from Wind Tunnel Experiments

The wind tunnel experiments with Psilocybe cubensis provide several lines
of evidence supporting the evaporative demand hypothesis. When primordia
were placed with the stipe perpendicular to the airflow, growth was
upward regardless of wind direction, suggesting that gravitational
evaporative differences (the stipe tip being farther from the moist
substrate) dominated when wind-induced asymmetry was minimised by
rotation. When stipes faced into the wind, growth curved into the flow
(anemotropism). When stipes faced away from the wind, they still curved
toward the airflow, indicating that the response is to the wind itself
rather than a simple mechanical bending. The fact that anemotropism
occurred in both light and darkness and in both fertile and sterile
strains argues against a hormonal mechanism specific to reproduction.

## Comparison with Phycomyces Avoidance Response

The zygomycete fungus Phycomyces blakesleeanus exhibits a well-studied
avoidance response where the sporangiophore grows away from nearby objects,
including other sporangiophores. Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated
that wind and water vapour are necessary for this response, and Cohen et
al. (1975) characterised the anemotropic component. The mechanism in
Phycomyces is thought to involve the same boundary layer redistribution
and evaporative demand differential proposed [[miniculture-rye-grain-cultivation-method-for-psilocybe-cubensis]]. Russo
et al. (1975) suggested that ethylene gas may mediate the avoidance
response in Phycomyces, providing a possible molecular signalling pathway
that could translate the physical evaporative demand signal into a
biochemical growth response. If ethylene or a similar volatile signalling
molecule is involved in Psilocybe tropisms, it would connect the physical
boundary layer hypothesis to known hormonal signalling pathways.

## Alternative Explanations

Two alternative explanations for fungal tropisms deserve consideration.
The first is a direct mechanical response to wind force: the physical
pressure of airflow against the stipe could trigger differential growth.
Dennison (1961) showed that Phycomyces sporangiophore growth responds to
both gravity and centrifugal force, demonstrating that physical forces can
directly influence growth. However, the wind velocities used in Badham's
experiments (9 ± 3 cm/sec) are quite low and unlikely to exert
significant mechanical force on a rigid stipe. The second alternative is
a hormonal mechanism where gill-derived substances differentially
accumulate on one side of the stipe due to wind-displaced diffusion
gradients. This hypothesis is supported by decapitation experiments in
other species showing that gill removal eliminates [[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]], but
it does not easily explain why anemotropism occurs in sterile (gill-less)
basidiocarps.

## Adaptive Significance of Anemotropism

The evaporative demand hypothesis suggests a clear adaptive function for
anemotropism. By growing into the wind, the mushroom places its cap in
the position of maximum airflow, which optimises spore dispersal after
the cap opens. Spores released into a steady airflow travel farther than
spores released into still air. Additionally, directed growth toward the
evaporative demand could increase spacing between the stipes of
caespitose (clustered) fruiting bodies, reducing competition for light,
air, [[ingoldian-aquatic-fungi-and-spore-dispersal]] routes. This dual function, optimising both
individual spore dispersal and cluster architecture, provides a
compelling evolutionary explanation for why the evaporative demand
mechanism is conserved across distantly related fungal lineages.
