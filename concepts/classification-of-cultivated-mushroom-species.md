## Classification of Cultivated Mushroom Species

## Overview

The taxonomy of cultivated mushrooms presents unique challenges because cultivation conditions can dramatically alter the morphological characteristics that botanists traditionally use to distinguish species. The early 20th century understanding of cultivated Agaricus species, as documented by Benjamin Minge Duggar, reveals a complex picture in which several distinct species are grown commercially under the generic label "mushroom," with considerable intergradation and confusion between forms.

## The Basidiomycetes

The fleshy fungi used as food mushrooms belong predominantly to the **Basidiomycetes**, characterized by the development of a sporophore or fruit-body (the "mushroom" or "toadstool") frequently of large size and diverse form. When sectioned and examined microscopically, the fruiting surface consists of a close layer from which arise basidia — clavate branches each producing, as a rule, four spores. The major families of fleshy Basidiomycetes include:

- **Agaricaceae** (gill fungi) — the largest group, including most cultivated and wild edible mushrooms
- **Polyporaceae** (pore-bearing fungi) — including boletes and bracket fungi
- **Hydnaceae** (tooth fungi) — with tooth-like spore-bearing structures
- **Clavariaceae** (coral fungi) — often much-branched forms
- **Lycoperdaceae** (puffballs) — enclosed spore-producing structures

The **Ascomycetes**, another large class characterized by asci or spore sacs, include the saddle fungi and morels (Helvellaceae), truffles (Tuberaceae), and terfas (Terfeziaceae).

## The Agaricaceae

The Agaricaceae are characterized by gills — usually blade-like structures on the under side of the pileus (cap). More than 5,000 species have been described. Within this family, species are grouped by spore color: **black** (purple-black or brown-black), **ochraceous**, **pink**, or **white**.

## Agaricus: The Primary Cultivated Genus

### The Cultivation Problem

It is generally maintained that the only species in cultivation is *[[agaricus-arvensis]]** — The Horse Mushroom. Typically larger and stouter than the Field Mushroom, distinguished by a **double ring**. Descriptions differ regarding pileus color, but several cultivated varieties may properly be referred to this species. Under cultivation, A. arvensis has not been found inferior to A. campestris in flavor.

**[[agaricus-placomyc]]** — A woodland species with a large, flat, thin cap that appears smoky from numerous small dark scales. The veil is double (like A. arvensis) but not so stout, with an enlarged stem base.

**Agaricus silvicola** — An inhabitant of woods, almost pure white except as to gills, sometimes tinged with yellow. A rather small summer species not intentionally cultivated but possibly propagated unintentionally through commercial spawn.

### The Effect of Cultivation Conditions

Cultivation conditions dramatically affect morphological characteristics:

- Spawn derived from typical field A. campestris may yield specimens indistinguishable from A. arvensis under vigorous cellar conditions
- Specimens produced when spawn is just beginning to "run" may more closely resemble the typical wild form
- Mushrooms at the end of the season may again seem more typical
- **Light and wind exposure** intensify cap color up to a certain point
- Forms producing perfectly white mushrooms in moist, protected places may produce unrecognizably different-colored specimens near doors or in the open
- A. campestris grown on composted leaves exhibits very little pink in the gills, with color changing rapidly from pale brown to gray-brown

### Trade Classification

A practical trade classification was recommended:

- **"Alaska"**: The smaller, practically pure white form
- **"Columbia"**: The large cream-colored form (sometimes grayish)
