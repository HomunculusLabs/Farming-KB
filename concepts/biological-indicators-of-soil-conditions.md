# Biological Indicators of Soil and Site Conditions

## Overview

In any local area, the composition, shape, size, and distribution of plants provide many clues to soil type, depth, and extrinsic factors. By learning to read these biological indicators, land managers can assess conditions without expensive laboratory testing. The specific factors indicated by biological indicators include soil depth, water reserves, pH, mineral status, fire frequency, frost exposure, drainage, mineral deposits, overgrazing, and macrofaunal effects.

## 1. Soil Depth

Shallow soils dry out quickly and hold few nutrients. The best indicator of soil depth is to observe one species of tree (such as [[acacia]], Prosopis, or honey locust) across a range of sites. A height and spread comparison will reveal areas of deeper soils, where the largest specimens grow. The same species will be dwarfish on shallow soils of the same derivation or rock type.

This simple observational technique — comparing the same species across sites — is one of the most powerful tools available for rapid soil assessment without excavation.

## 2. Water Reserves

Deep-rooted trees that need water serve as natural indicators of subsurface moisture. Large nut trees and candlenuts (*Aleurites*) occur naturally only in well-drained but water-conserving sites. They often show water-lines not associated with valleys and stand over springs or aquifer discharge areas.

In sands, a great variety of deep-rooted shrubs and trees indicate where a clay base lies at 1–2 m depth — a common situation on desert borders and hills in drylands. The principle is simple: large tree stems reveal well-watered sites, small stems reveal drier sites. Armed with these observations, designers can create sites by water diversion and select appropriate sites for large trees or shrubs.

## 3. Soil pH

Several plant species serve as reliable pH indicators:

**Acid indicators:**
- Sorrel and oxalis in pastures may indicate compact or acid conditions

**Alkaline indicators:**
- Several fen and limestone species establish in alkaline areas
- Large snails and dense snail populations occur only over alkaline soils or in alkaline water
- No snails or only minute species occur in acid water (pH < 5.0)

**pH Preferences of Cultivated Plants:**

*Alkaline intolerant (pH 4.5–6):*
Blueberry, chicory, chestnut, endive, potato, fennel, tea, shallot, coffee, watermelon, rhubarb

*Acid intolerant (pH 7.0–8.5):*
Cauliflower, cabbage, asparagus, green peas, bush beans, celery, leek, beet, onion, chard, parsnip, spinach

*Acid tolerant:*
Lupin, oats, white clover, rye, lucerne, broccoli

*Alkali tolerant:*
Oats, kale

## 4. Mineral Status

Plants can serve as indicators of specific mineral deposits. Davidov (Sputnik, 1979) provided data on plant systems over mineral deposits in Russia. Analysis of plant residues often indicates concentration of ores in underlying soil or rock.

**Plant-ore associations:**
- Birch, cherry, honeysuckle, St. John's Wort, wormwood, juniper, heather: reveal lead, copper-molybdate, tungsten, and tin concentrates
- Rue of Violets: zinc
- Catchfly: cobalt
- Astragalus: selenium
- Milk vetch: selenium and uranium
- Russian thistle: boron
- Alyssum: nickel
- Honeysuckle: silver and gold
- Horsetail: gold and silica

### Biological Mining and Phytoremediation

Plants have the ability to both concentrate and tolerate unusually high levels of specific minerals. This has implications for:

- **Biological concentration and removal of metallic soil pollutants** such as lead or uranium
- **Mining locally rare trace elements** using concentrator plants
- Some patents have been granted for mining gold deposits using banana or citrus plants deprived of common elements (potash, phosphate), causing their leaves to concentrate sparse gold deposits

### Bioaccumulation in Animals

- Oysters concentrate zinc to 11% dry weight (an emetic dose)
- Abalone concentrate cadmium
- Several large fish concentrate mercury and biological poisons from corals to inedible levels

These relationships have obvious implications for public health monitoring of lead, cadmium, and mercury levels in fish or plants.

## 5. Fire Frequency

East-west ridges often reveal abrupt species changes at the ridge wherever fire occurs. Fire produces:

- Dry, scrabbly, summer-deciduous, thick-seeded species

Lack of fire develops:

- Broadleaf, winter-deciduous, small-seeded plants with thin seed capsules and deep litter fall

Cross-sectional cuts of trees reveal fire scars as gum pockets or charred sections. If tree stem sections are marked for directions before sampling, the direction of fires can also be determined. Counting fire scars provides the "fire frequency" of the site.

## 6. Frost

Many tree and plant species indicate frost-lines. Observing local flora, or planting frost-susceptible species down a hill profile, measures frost intensity. Tomatoes, bananas, and potatoes are all frost-sensitive and will reveal frost-lines on hills in subtropics and deserts.

## 7. Drainage

- **Poor drainage:** Indicated by mosses, sundews, and fine-leaved heaths
- **Good drainage:** Indicated by large trees such as chestnuts, which require 2 metres of well-drained soil

These biological indicators assist survey before pits are dug or drainage measured.

## 9. Overgrazing and Soil Compaction

Both the level of grasshopper and pasture grub activity (high on overgrazed landscapes) and the presence of patches of poisonous, inedible, thorny, and unpalatable plants indicate overstocking or range mismanagement. Common indicator weeds include Sodom apple, oxalis, and capeweed.

The effect is a synthesis between:
- Changing soil conditions
- Plant stress
- Heavy selection by livestock of palatable species, favouring survival and spread of spiny or inedible species

**Common misdiagnosis:** Too often, pastoralists blame the weeds and seek a chemical solution rather than a management solution. A better approach combines:

- Sensible utilisation of grasshoppers and grubs as a dried-protein supplement for fish or food pellets
- Soil conditioning, slashing, and de-stocking or re-seeding to restore species balance

## 10. Macrofaunal Effects

The site of a sea-bird rookery, rabbit warren, ground nest of a goose or eider clutch, pellet-pile of an owl, or decay of a large carcass will cause sudden and often long-term change in immediate vegetation, as will termite mounds and harvester-ant colonies.

Once such sites are recorded and the plant assembly identified, similar sites can be located and recognised. The data can be used for:
- Conservation
- Indication of soil drainage (rabbits choose good drainage)
- Assessment of specific nutrient supply (guano on seabird rookeries)
- Establishing tree clumps following natural indicators

A large proportion of wind-blown, nitrogen-loving, and inedible plants — or plants carried by birds as seed — depend on the specific habits of birds or mammals, on their dung, or on soil disturbances. The role of animals in plant seed distribution and root associations is well-recognised; their role in soil change is less commonly noted but equally important.

## Practical Application

The comprehensive use of biological indicators allows [[permaculture]] designers and land managers to make informed decisions about site selection, soil treatment, plant species choice, and management strategies without relying solely on laboratory analysis. These living indicators are always present, continuously updated, and provide site-specific information that generalised soil maps and tests cannot match.
