# Dryland Water Yield and Run-off Estimation

## Overview

Accurate estimation of water yield and run-off is fundamental to designing water harvesting, storage, and spreading systems in arid and semi-arid lands. Understanding how much water can be captured from different surfaces under various rainfall intensities determines the sizing of tanks, swales, dams, channels, and flood bypass systems. This concept presents the quantitative framework for run-off estimation in dryland environments.

## Rainfall-Run-off Relationships

### The 88% Loss Problem

In Australia's arid zones, 88% of rainfall runs off or is evaporated from land and water surfaces. Other hot deserts worldwide show similar losses, with only 12% of rainfall available to plants. This stark ratio underscores why every design decision must maximise infiltration and capture.

### Run-off Percentages by Substrate

At a baseline rainfall intensity of 10 mm/day, the following run-off percentages can be expected from various substrates:

| Run-off % | Substrate Type |
|-----------|---------------|
| 0% | Coarse sands, active dunes, fixed dunes in dune seas |
| 5% | Forested areas |
| 10% | Wooded and grassed areas, tussock grassland |
| 15% | Cultivated sands and sandy loams |
| 20% | Approximate average for semi-arid and arid areas |
| 25% | Ungrazed steppe of mixed shrubs, forbs, and grasses |

**Key insight**: The difference between 5% run-off (forested) and 25%+ (degraded rangeland) represents a five-fold increase in water loss from the same rainfall event. Vegetation cover is the single most powerful factor influencing run-off.

### Higher Run-off Surfaces

For deliberately compacted or sealed surfaces used in water harvesting:
- Concrete, bitumen, or plastic-sealed surfaces: 50–80% run-off
- Well-compacted clay: 40–60% run-off
- Roaded catchments (graded, compacted earth): 30–50% run-off
- Bare rock slabs: 60–90% run-off

## Rainfall Intensity and Its Effects

### Daily Precipitation Categories

Rainfall intensity directly determines whether water infiltrates or runs off, and at what rate:

| mm/day | Category | Typical Effects |
|--------|----------|----------------|
| 0–10 | Light | Light dews (~0.8 mm), light rains in semi-arid areas (~4 mm), prairie rains (~6 mm). Smallest rills commence flow at 6–10 mm |
| 10–20 | Moderate | All-day drizzle (~12 mm), sheet erosion begins at 13 mm, gully erosion can develop at 15 mm. First and second order stream diversion drains begin to flow |
| 21–50 | Heavy | Monsoon cloud streets on coasts, light rain all day in western maritime climates, periods of convective rain in deserts |
| 51–100 | Intense | Condensation drip from onshore fogs (~60 mm), tropical rains exceed 100 mm approximately once per year |
| 100–200+ | Extreme | Heavy rain all day, exceeded once per year on monsoon and tradewind coasts, approximately once per century in temperate maritime areas |

### Design Implications of Intensity

The range from light to extreme rainfall means that storage and channel systems must handle enormous variation in flow volume. A system designed for average conditions will fail during extreme events, while over-designing for rare extremes wastes resources. ## Practical Estimation for Design

### Calculating Tank Capacity

For domestic water supply from roof catchment:
- 1 cm of rain on 100 m² of roof = 1,000 litres
- 1 cm of rain on 1 hectare of sealed surface = 100,000 litres

A family requiring 150 litres per day (drinking, cooking, basic hygiene) needs approximately 55,000 litres per year of storage capacity to bridge dry seasons.

### Calculating Swale Capacity

Swales should be sized to hold 200% of village run-off plus any intercepted overland flow from surrounding areas. For a village of 100 hectares with 20% average run-off in 100 mm of rain:

- Total rainfall = 100,000 m³
- Total run-off = 20,000 m³
- [[swale]] capacity needed = 40,000 m³ (200% of run-off)
- Plus additional capacity for intercepted overland flow from surrounding areas

### Estimating Flood Peaks

For channel and flood bypass design, use the run-off percentages combined with catchment area and rainfall intensity:

```
Peak flow (m³/s) = Catchment area (km²) × Rainfall intensity (mm/hr) × Run-off coefficient ÷ 3.6
```

This simplified formula gives order-of-magnitude estimates suitable for initial design. Site-specific factors (soil type, antecedent moisture, vegetation) require field verification.
