# Straw Decomposition and Ecological Succession




## Overview

Fukuoka provides a detailed account of how a single piece of rice straw, casually dropped onto the earth, undergoes a complex succession of biological transformations involving dozens of organism species. This natural decomposition process, he argues, is far more efficient and complete than any human-managed composting operation. The account serves as both a practical guide to understanding soil biology and a philosophical argument for why human intervention in natural processes is counterproductive.




## The Five Stages of Natural Decomposition

### Stage 1: Initial Colonization

The straw draws a lot of flies and other small insects that lay eggs, from which maggots and other larvae soon emerge. Before this happens, rice blast disease, leaf blotch, and rot-causing fungi already present on the rice leaves spread rapidly over the straw. Spider mites are soon crawling over this fungal growth. This initial stage establishes the first wave of organisms that begin breaking down the straw's surface structure.

### Stage 2: Fungal Proliferation

Different microbes begin to proliferate at once. The most common include yeasts, blue mold, bread mold, and trichoderma fungi, which destroy the pathogens and begin decomposing the straw. These fungi represent nature's first major wave of decomposition agents, breaking down cellulose and other structural carbohydrates that make up the bulk of the straw material.

### Stage 3: Multi-Trophic Cascade

As decomposition progresses, the number and types of organisms drawn to the straw increase dramatically. Nematodes feed on the fungi. Bacteria feed on the nematodes. Mites consume the bacteria. Predaceous mites feed on these mites. Spiders, ground beetles, earwigs, mole crickets, and slugs join the community. These organisms mingle and live in the straw, which undergoes a succession of "tenants" as it gradually decomposes. Each organism plays a specific role in the decomposition food web, creating a self-regulating ecosystem.

### Stage 4: Anaerobic Transition

Once the fibrin-decomposing fungi run out of food, they stop growing and are supplanted by lipoid- and lignin-decomposing bacteria which feed on the fungi and the scraps left over by the fungi. Before long, parasitism and cannibalism sets in among the aerobic bacteria, and these are gradually replaced by anaerobic bacteria. This transition marks a fundamental shift in the decomposition process, from oxygen-dependent to oxygen-independent breakdown.

### Stage 5: Final Fermentation

Lactic acid bacteria round off the process with lactic acid fermentation, at which point all trace of the straw disappears. The end product is fully humified organic matter indistinguishable from the surrounding soil. This stage represents the completion of the biological transformation from complex plant material to simple soil constituents.




## Fukuoka's Compost Experiment at Kochi

While at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing Center, Fukuoka searched for a beneficial bacterium with which to prepare compost from straw and brush. He collected refuse from garbage sinkholes as well as cattle, hog, chicken, rabbit, and sheep droppings. From these, he isolated and cultured microorganisms, obtaining samples of many different bacteria, fungi, slime molds, and yeast. He then inoculated samples into straw in test tubes and within concrete enclosures and observed the rotting rates.

Later, however, he realized that such an experiment was really quite worthless. To one concerned with how long things take, such an investigation may seem useful, but a closer look reveals that nature makes use of far better methods of treating garbage and preparing compost. Rather than isolating beneficial microbes and inoculating straw with a "fermentation promoter," all he had to do was scatter a handful of chicken droppings or clumps of soil over the straw. This was both the quickest method and the one giving the most completely rotted compost.




## The Critique of Human-Managed Composting

There is no call for making a lot of fuss over "microbial" and "enzymatic" farming methods. Microbiologists are well aware of how rapidly and perfectly the processes of decomposition and rotting break down garbage in the natural world. Yet man, believing that he has to make intensive use of beneficial microbes to speed up putrefaction or that he must raise the temperature to promote bacterial growth, prepares compost. He should stop and consider how worthless and undesirable such efforts are. Anything he does just disturbs the rapid and perfect natural processes.

People must not forget, in looking at the rotting of a straw, at the fertilizer response, at soil improvement, and at all the other processes that take place in nature, that what man knows is only the most minute, infinitesimal part of the natural order. In addition to the very visible lead roles are an infinite number of supporting roles that perform important yet unknown functions.




## The Director Metaphor

If man jumps onto center stage and begins giving out directions like a know-nothing director, the play will be ruined. When something goes wrong in nature, the biosystem changes course. Unlike in a factory where the damage may consist of only a broken gear, in nature a disruption gives rise to an unending series of repercussions. This is why pesticides, which target specific pests, set off cascade effects throughout the entire biological community.




## Implications for Soil Management

This understanding of natural decomposition is central to Fukuoka's practice of scattering uncut rice straw over fields after harvest. The straw serves not as a conventional mulch but as the catalyst for an entire ecological succession that builds soil structure, feeds microorganisms, attracts beneficial insects, and ultimately returns all organic matter to the earth in a form available to plants. No compost preparation, no turning, no temperature management is required. Nature accomplishes in days what human composting takes months to achieve, and does so more completely and with no pollution.
