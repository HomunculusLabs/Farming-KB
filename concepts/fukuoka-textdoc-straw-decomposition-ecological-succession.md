# Straw Decomposition and Ecological Succession

Masanobu Fukuoka describes the decomposition of a single piece of rice straw on the ground as a marvel of ecological engineering that far surpasses any human-managed composting system. His detailed account of the succession of organisms that break down straw reveals the extraordinary complexity and efficiency of nature's recycling processes, and serves as a foundational argument against human intervention in natural decomposition.

## Nature's Composting vs. Human Effort

While at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing Center, Fukuoka searched for beneficial bacteria capable of quickly decomposing straw and coarse plant material. This was similar to what scientists today call for when they seek beneficial bacteria to convert garbage and sludge into artificial manure for use as fertilizer.

He collected refuse from garbage sinkholes as well as the droppings of cattle, hogs, chickens, rabbits, and sheep. From these he isolated and cultured microorganisms, obtaining samples of many different bacteria, fungi, slime molds, and yeasts. He was able in this way to collect a large number of microbes suited to preparing compost. He then inoculated samples of each of these into straw in test tubes and within concrete enclosures and observed the rotting rates.

He later realized that this entire experiment was really quite worthless.

To one concerned with how long things take, an investigation such as this may seem useful, but a closer look reveals that nature makes use of far better methods of treating garbage and preparing compost. Rather than going to all the trouble of isolating beneficial microbes and inoculating straw with a "fermentation promoter," all one needs to do is scatter a handful of chicken droppings or clumps of soil over the straw.

Not only was this the quickest way, it also gave the most completely rotted compost. The laboratory isolation of specific organisms and their application as inoculants represents a fundamental misunderstanding of how nature's decomposition systems actually work.

## The Five Stages of Ecological Succession

When a piece of rice straw is casually dropped onto the earth, the following ecological succession unfolds over a period of several days. Each stage involves increasingly complex interactions between organisms, demonstrating the self-organizing capacity of natural systems.

### Stage 1: Insect Attraction and Pathogen Spread

The straw draws a large number of flies and other small insects that lay eggs, from which maggots and other larvae soon emerge. Before this happens, however, rice blast disease, leaf blotch, and rot-causing fungi already present on the rice leaves spread rapidly over the straw. Spider mites are soon crawling over this fungal growth.

This initial stage establishes the straw as a substrate for colonization by a diverse community of organisms. The pathogens that were on the living rice plant continue their activity on the dead straw, serving as the first wave of decomposers.

### Stage 2: Microbial Proliferation

Different microbes begin to proliferate at once. The most common include yeasts, blue mold, bread mold, and trichoderma fungi. These organisms destroy the pathogens that spread in the first stage and begin decomposing the straw itself.

At this point, the number and types of organisms drawn to the straw increase dramatically, transforming the straw into a thriving micro-ecosystem teeming with life. The trichoderma fungi are particularly important as antagonists of the pathogenic fungi from stage one.

### Stage 3: Complex Food Web Development

The organisms that arrive and interact with the straw include a diverse array of life forms across multiple trophic levels:

- **Nematodes** that feed on the fungi
- **Bacteria** that feed on the nematodes
- **Mites** that consume the bacteria
- **Predaceous mites** that feed on these mites
- **Spiders, ground beetles, earwigs, mole crickets, and slugs**

These organisms mingle and live in the straw, which undergoes a succession of "tenants" as it gradually decomposes. Each organism plays a specific role, and the interactions between predator and prey regulate populations naturally without any external management.

### Stage 4: Fungal Succession and Anaerobic Transition

Once the fibrin-decomposing fungi run out of food, they stop growing and are supplanted by lipoid- and lignin-decomposing bacteria which feed on the fungi and the scraps left over by them.

Before long, parasitism and cannibalism set in among the aerobic bacteria, and these are gradually replaced by anaerobic bacteria. This transition from aerobic to anaerobic conditions represents a fundamental shift in the decomposition chemistry and marks the beginning of the final breakdown phase.

### Stage 5: Final Fermentation

Lactic acid bacteria round off the process with lactic acid fermentation, at which point all trace of the straw disappears.

This is just the briefest of looks at the total decomposition of a single piece of straw on the ground over a period of several days. The process is biologically, physically, and chemically perfect in every sense.

## The Futility of Microbial and Enzymatic Farming Methods

Fukuoka strongly criticizes what he calls "microbial" and "enzymatic" farming methods. There is no call for making a lot of fuss over such approaches. Microbiologists are well aware of how rapidly and perfectly the processes of decomposition and rotting break down garbage in the natural world.

Yet man, believing that he has to make intensive use of beneficial microbes to speed up putrefaction or that he must raise the temperature to promote bacterial growth, prepares compost. He should stop and consider how worthless and undesirable such efforts are.

Frankly, anything that he does just disturbs the rapid and perfect natural processes. Nature employs not isolated species but entire communities working in concert, and any attempt to select and amplify individual organisms necessarily diminishes the overall effectiveness of the system.

## The Biosystem as a Stage

People must not forget, in looking at the rotting of a straw, at the fertilizer response, at soil improvement, and at all the other processes that take place in nature, that what man knows is only the most minute, infinitesimal part of the natural order.

In addition to the very visible lead roles are an infinite number of supporting roles that perform important yet unknown functions.

If man jumps onto center stage and begins giving out directions like a know-nothing director, the play will be ruined. When something goes wrong in nature, the biosystem changes course. Unlike in a factory where the damage may consist of only a broken gear, in nature a disruption gives rise to an unending series of repercussions.

## Implications for Natural Farming

This understanding of natural decomposition underpins Fukuoka's entire approach to soil fertility. Rather than managing compost piles or applying microbial inoculants, the natural farmer allows organic matter to decompose in place through the activity of the full ecosystem.

The scattered straw mulch on rice fields is not merely a weed suppressant or moisture conservator; it is the substrate for an entire ecological community that builds soil fertility through natural succession.

The principle extends beyond straw to all organic matter on the farm. Branches, leaves, roots, and crop residues all undergo similar ecological processing. The farmer's role is not to manage this decomposition but to avoid disrupting it with chemicals, excessive tillage, or removal of organic material from the land.

By allowing nature's own succession of decomposers to work, the farmer harnesses a system that is both faster and more complete than any human-managed composting operation. This insight also explains why the no-fertilizer principle works: the soil already contains all the microorganisms needed for complete nutrient cycling.

## Temperature and Moisture in Natural Decomposition

An important aspect of natural decomposition that human composting methods often overlook is the role of temperature gradients. As the straw decomposes on the ground surface, the microclimate within the straw layer creates a range of conditions that support different organisms at different stages.

The upper surface of the straw, exposed to sun and air, favors aerobic fungi and bacteria that require warmth and oxygen. The lower layers, in contact with the soil, maintain higher humidity and support different microbial communities. This natural stratification allows multiple decomposition pathways to operate simultaneously.

Human composting, by contrast, often homogenizes conditions through turning and watering, creating uniform environments that favor only certain organisms. The result is incomplete decomposition and the loss of many of the intermediate products that feed the soil food web.

## The Role of Predation in Decomposition

Fukuoka's account highlights the critical role of predation in driving the decomposition process forward. The nematodes that feed on fungi, the mites that consume bacteria, and the predaceous mites that eat other mites are not merely bystanders in the decomposition of straw.

They are active participants that regulate populations and accelerate the breakdown of organic matter. Each act of predation releases nutrients in forms that can be utilized by the next organism in the chain. Without this predatory regulation, certain decomposer populations could explode, leading to imbalances that slow decomposition.

This is why simply adding bacteria or fungi to a compost pile, as some commercial products recommend, often fails to produce the expected results. The organisms are introduced without the predator-prey community that regulates their activity and channels their products into useful pathways.

## What Modern Science Confirms

Contemporary soil science has largely validated Fukuoka's observations about the complexity of decomposition. Modern molecular techniques have revealed that a single gram of soil may contain thousands of species of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and archaea, far more than were known in Fukuoka's time.

The recognition of the soil microbiome as a complex, self-regulating community mirrors Fukuoka's description of the "biosystem" at work in decomposing straw. Scientists now speak of "soil food webs" in terms very similar to Fukuoka's account of trophic cascades in decomposing straw.

However, modern agricultural practice has largely ignored the practical implications of these findings, continuing to rely on synthetic fertilizers and isolated microbial products rather than working with the full community of soil organisms.
