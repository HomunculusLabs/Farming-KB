# Mycoforestry: Forest Management with Fungi

## Overview

Mycoforestry is the use of fungi to sustainably manage forest ecosystems, a concept developed and popularized by mycologist Paul Stamets. The approach recognizes fungi as essential partners in forest health -- not merely decomposers but active ecosystem engineers that mediate nutrient cycling, protect trees from disease, filter water, and create underground networks connecting forest plants. Mycoforestry integrates fungal biology into every stage of forest management, from planting to harvest to restoration.

## Fungi in Forest Ecosystems

Forests are fungal systems as much as they are tree systems. The forest floor is underlain by vast mycelial networks that:

- Decompose woody debris and recycle nutrients back to plant-available forms
- Form mycorrhizal associations with tree roots, extending the effective root zone by 10-100x
- Connect individual trees into shared resource networks (the "wood wide web")
- Produce enzymes that break down complex organic compounds (lignin, cellulose, toxins)
- Filter pathogens and pollutants from water moving through the soil profile

Understanding these roles is the foundation of mycoforestry. See [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] for how these same capabilities apply to environmental cleanup.

## Mycorrhizal Inoculation for Tree Planting

Most forest trees depend on mycorrhizal fungi for nutrient uptake, drought tolerance, and disease resistance. When planting trees -- especially in degraded or disturbed sites -- inoculation dramatically improves survival and growth rates.

### Ectomycorrhizal vs. Endomycorrhizal

- **Ectomycorrhizal (ECM)**: Associated with most conifers, oaks, beeches, birches, and pines. Fungi form a sheath around root tips (Pisolithus, Rhizopogon, Suillus, Amanita species).
- **Endomycorrhizal (Arbuscular Mycorrhizal / AMF)**: Associated with most hardwoods, shrubs, and understory plants. Fungi penetrate root cells (Glomus, Rhizophagus species).

### Inoculation Methods

1. **Spore slurries**: Collect mature mushrooms of target species, blend with water, and apply to planting holes and root dips
2. **Mycorrhizal inoculant products**: Commercial preparations of spores and propagules available from forestry suppliers
3. **Soil transfer**: Move small amounts of soil from healthy forest stands to planting sites to transfer native mycorrhizal communities
4. **Root dips**: Dip bare-root seedlings in mycorrhizal slurry before planting

### When to Inoculate

- Always inoculate when planting on disturbed sites (mined land, clear-cuts, urban tree plantings)
- Inoculate nursery stock before outplanting for best establishment
- Re-inoculate after site disturbances (fire, flooding, heavy equipment traffic)

## Stump Inoculation for Mushroom Cultivation

After timber harvest, tree stumps represent both a waste disposal problem and a cultivation opportunity. Inoculating stumps with edible or medicinal mushroom species converts waste into food while accelerating decomposition.

### Suitable Species

- **Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)**: Inoculate oak, alder, maple, beech stumps. Drill holes and plug with spawn. Harvest begins in 6-12 months.
- **Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)**: Very aggressive colonizer; works on most hardwood stumps. See [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]] for cultivation details, and [[gourmet-mushroom-cultivation]] for broader species coverage.
- **Lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus)**: Inoculate hardwood stumps, especially oaks and beeches. Slower colonizer but high-value harvest.
- **Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)**: Grows on stumps of oaks and other hardwoods. Medicinal rather than culinary.

### Inoculation Technique

1. Cut stumps as close to ground level as practical
2. Drill holes (5/16 inch diameter, 1-2 inches deep) into the stump top and sides
3. Insert spawn plugs or pack with sawdust spawn
4. Seal holes with wax to prevent desiccation
5. Keep stump moist; best results when inoculated within weeks of cutting

## Mycofiltration of Forest Runoff

Forests naturally filter water through soil and fungal networks, but human disturbance (logging roads, clear-cuts, development) creates concentrated runoff carrying sediment, nutrients, and pollutants. Mycofiltration uses strategically placed fungal substrates to intercept and treat this water.

### Applications

- **Road crossings**: Straw or wood chip berms inoculated with oyster mushroom mycelium placed across forest road ditches to filter sediment and hydrocarbons from road runoff
- **Stream buffer enhancement**: Inoculate riparian buffer zones with mycorrhizal fungi to improve nutrient uptake and prevent excess nitrogen and phosphorus from reaching waterways
- **Landslide restoration**: Inoculate disturbed slopes with mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi to stabilize soil and accelerate revegetation

### Species Selection

- Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.) for general filtration and hydrocarbon breakdown
- King stropharia (Stropharia rugosoannulata) for filtering bacteria and nutrients from agricultural runoff
- Mycorrhizal species for long-term soil stabilization and plant community health

## Species Selection for Timber Stand Improvement

Mycoforestry can actively improve timber quality and forest productivity:

- **Pre-inoculate seedling stock**: Mycorrhizal inoculated seedlings show 20-50% improved growth rates in the first 5 years
- **Species matching**: Pair ECM species with their preferred tree associates (Suillus with pines, Laccaria with Douglas fir, Pisolithus with eucalyptus)
- **Competition management**: Certain fungal species can suppress root pathogens (Armillaria, Phytophthora) that damage timber stands
- **Nutrient cycling acceleration**: Introduce aggressive decomposer fungi to speed breakdown of slash and logging residue, returning nutrients to the soil faster

## Creating Fungal Corridors

Fungal corridors are connected networks of woody debris, inoculated substrates, and living mycelium that span the forest landscape. They function as underground highways for fungal colonization, connecting isolated forest patches and enabling mycelial networks to spread across disturbed areas.

### Design Principles

- Place woody debris (logs, branches, wood chips) in continuous lines along contours and water flow paths
- Inoculate key nodes with target species (edible, medicinal, or mycorrhizal fungi)
- Maintain connectivity between forest stands through hedgerows, riparian buffers, and woody debris placement
- Protect existing fungal corridors from disturbance during timber operations

## Integration with the Soil Food Web

Mycoforestry works within the broader [[soil-food-web-structure]] -- fungal networks interact with bacteria, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and higher organisms. Healthy mycoforestry practices support the entire soil food web, which in turn supports tree health, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem resilience.

## Limitations and Considerations

- Mycoforestry is not a replacement for sound silvicultural practices -- it enhances, not replaces, good forest management
- Inoculation success depends on environmental conditions (moisture, temperature, substrate quality)
- Some introduced fungal species may become invasive; prefer native species when possible
- Regulatory restrictions may apply to intentional fungal introductions in some jurisdictions
- Long-term monitoring is needed to assess the effectiveness of mycoforestry interventions
