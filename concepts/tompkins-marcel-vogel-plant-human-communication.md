Marcel Vogel was a research chemist at IBM in Los Gatos, California,
whose rigorous scientific background and personal openness to
unconventional phenomena led him to become one of the most credible
investigators of plant-human communication. His work provided
independent confirmation and significant extension of [[plant-perception]].

## Background at IBM

Vogel"s scientific career began with boyhood curiosity about
bioluminescence in fireflies and glowworms. By his twenties, he had
co-authored a book on luminescence with Chicago University's Dr. Peter
Pringsheim. He founded Vogel Luminescence in [[fukuoka-textdoc-post-season-cultivation-kochi-turning-point]] came when Vogel was asked to teach a course on
"creativity" for IBM engineers. A student handed him an Argosy magazine
article about Backster"s work titled "Do Plants Have Emotions?" Vogel
initially dismissed it as charlatanism, but something about the idea
gnawed at him. A few days later he retrieved the article from the
wastebasket and completely reversed his opinion. He challenged his
class to replicate Backster"s results. While three student teams failed
entirely, Vogel himself succeeded, demonstrating that plants anticipated
having their leaves torn and reacted with greater alarm to threats than
to actual damage.

## The Leaf Preservation Experiments

A spiritually gifted friend, Vivian Wiley, conducted a remarkable
experiment: she placed two saxifrage leaves in separate rooms, directing
focused attention and will toward one while ignoring the other. After a
month, the attended leaf remained radiantly green and vital, while the
ignored leaf was flaccid, brown, and decaying. Vogel replicated the
experiment with elm leaves, finding the same result within a week. The
stems of the living leaves appeared to have healed their wounds. Vogel
concluded he was witnessing "psychic energy" in action.

## Refined Electrode Technique

Vogel recognized that Backster"s midnight-to-dawn experiments were
necessitated by electromagnetic noise from household appliances. He
developed a mucilaginous paste of agar, karri gum, and salt that he
brushed onto leaves before applying polished stainless-steel electrodes.
When the agar hardened, it sealed the pickups into a moist interior,
eliminating variability from pressure and producing a perfectly straight
baseline on the chart recorder.

## The Communication Protocol

Vogel found that individual leaves had unique "personalities." Fleshy
leaves with high water content responded best. Plants went through
phases of activity and inactivity, being highly responsive at certain
times and "sluggish" or "morose" at others. His protocol for establishing
communication involved quieting his sensory responses, becoming aware of
an energetic relationship with the plant, and achieving a state of
bioelectrical balance between himself and the plant. Once attuned, the
plant responded only to Vogel, becoming insensitive to noise,
temperature, and other plants.

## Distance Experiments

Like Backster, Vogel demonstrated that the plant-human bond operated
across distance. In a botanical nursery, he identified sensitive plants
by feeling a cooling sensation followed by electrical pulses in his
hands. Increasing his distance, he got similar reactions from outside
the house, down the block, and from his laboratory eight miles away.

He wired two plants to the same recorder and snipped a leaf from the
first. The second responded, but only when Vogel was paying attention
to it. If he cut a leaf while ignoring the second plant, there was no
response, suggesting that Vogel himself was an integral part of the
circuitry required to monitor the phenomenon.
