# Straw Bale Construction

Straw bale construction uses baled agricultural straw (wheat, rice, oats, barley, rye) as building blocks for walls. The bales provide excellent insulation (approximately R-2.4 to R-3.0 per inch, or R-30 to R-40 for a typical 18-inch bale wall) and are covered with plaster for weather protection and structural integrity.

## Load-Bearing vs. Post-and-Beam (Infill)

### Load-Bearing (Nebraska Style)

In load-bearing construction, the straw bale walls carry the weight of the roof directly. This is the original method, developed by settlers in the Sandhills of Nebraska in the late 1800s when timber was scarce.

- **How it works:** Bales are stacked in a running bond pattern (like bricks), pinned together with rebar, bamboo, or wooden stakes, and compressed with a top plate and threaded rod. The roof bears on the top plate, which distributes load evenly across the bale wall.
- **Pre-compression:** Essential — bales must be compressed before the roof load is applied. Target 10-15% compression from the original bale height.
- **Window and door openings:** Framed with rough buck frames (wooden frames) that are integrated into the bale wall and pinned securely.
- **Height limit:** Typically limited to one story; two-story load-bearing requires engineering.
- **Pros:** Simpler framing, fewer materials, lower cost
- **Cons:** Limited design flexibility, code approval can be difficult, requires careful plaster application for structural strength

### Post-and-Beam (Infill)

In post-and-beam construction, a timber or steel structural frame carries all loads. The straw bales serve purely as insulation and are stacked within the bays of the frame.

- **How it works:** The structural frame is erected first, then bales are stacked as infill between posts and beams. The frame may be on the interior, exterior, or center of the bale wall.
- **Settling:** Less critical than load-bearing since the frame carries structural loads, but bales should still be pre-compressed or allowed to settle before plastering.
- **Design flexibility:** Much greater — large openings, complex rooflines, multiple stories are straightforward.
- **Pros:** Structural certainty, easier code approval, design freedom, can build in phases
- **Cons:** Requires more timber/steel, higher cost for the structural frame

## Bale Selection and Preparation

### What to Look For

- **Straw, not hay:** Hay contains seed heads and is attractive to pests; straw is the hollow stalk after grain harvest
- **Dry bales:** Moisture content below 14%. Insert a moisture meter probe into the center of several bales before purchase.
- **Density:** Bales should be tight and heavy. Target density of 7-10 lbs per cubic foot. Pick up a bale — it should feel solid, not spongy.
- **Length:** Consistent length is important for planning. Measure 10-20 bales from the same source.
- **String ties:** Polypropylene or wire ties are standard. Avoid bales with broken ties.

### Storing Bales on Site

- Keep bales elevated off the ground on pallets or a gravel base
- Cover with tarps, ensuring good airflow underneath
- Stack in a manner that allows air circulation
- Do not leave exposed to rain for any extended period

## Plastering Straw Bale Walls

Plaster is critical for straw bale buildings — it provides weather protection, fire resistance, structural rigidity, and pest resistance. A properly plastered straw bale wall has a 2-hour fire rating.

### Types of Plaster

- **Earthen plaster:** Clay-based, most common for natural builders, breathable, easy to work with, see [[earth-plaster-and-natural-finishes]]
- **Lime plaster:** More weather resistant, good for exteriors in wet climates, takes longer to cure
- **Cement-lime stucco:** Most code-friendly but least breathable; use only with a drainage plane between bales and stucco

### Application Process

1. **Prep:** Trim bales flush with a weed-whacker or chainsaw; mist surfaces; stuff any gaps with loose straw and clay slip
2. **Keying:** Scratch the bale surface or apply a clay slip coat for plaster adhesion
3. **Scratch coat:** 3/8 to 1/2 inch thick, scored or notched for the next coat
4. **Brown coat:** 1/4 to 3/8 inch, smooth and level
5. **Finish coat:** 1/8 inch thin, can be colored and textured

### Mesh Requirements

- **Earthen and lime plaster:** May not require mesh if applied correctly to dampened, keyed bale surfaces
- **Cement stucco:** Usually requires metal lath or mesh fastened to the bale wall with pins
- **Code compliance:** Many codes require mesh regardless of plaster type — check local requirements

## Moisture Control

Moisture is the primary enemy of straw bale construction. Design and build to manage water at every stage.

### Design Principles

- **Large roof overhangs:** Minimum 24 inches, preferably 36 inches on all sides
- **Raise bales above grade:** Foundation should elevate the first course at least 12 inches (18 inches preferred) above finished grade
- **Splash protection:** Gravel or paved areas at the base of walls to prevent rain splash
- **No sprinkler contact:** Position irrigation to avoid wetting walls
- **Good drainage:** Slope ground away from foundation; use French drains if needed

### Building Details

- **Foundation:** Concrete, stone, or rubble trench with a moisture barrier (gravel, asphalt emulsion, or lime wash on the top course)
- **Window sills:** Sloped outward with drip edges to shed water away from walls
- **Roof-to-wall connection:** Cap the bale wall with a top plate that extends past the wall plane, or use a raised-heel truss to keep insulation continuous

### Monitoring

- Install moisture meters at critical locations during construction and periodically after
- Check for discoloration or musty smells — signs of moisture issues
- Ensure the building dries completely before enclosing walls

## Code Compliance

Straw bale building codes exist in several jurisdictions and can guide design:

- **International Residential Code (IRC):** Appendix S includes prescriptive straw bale construction details (adopted in some US states)
- **ASTM standards:** ASTM E119 for fire testing of plastered straw bale walls (2-hour rating achieved)
- **Local permits:** Some areas have no experience with straw bale — provide engineering documentation and reference existing code sections
- **Tips:** Work with an engineer familiar with [[natural-building]]; use prescriptive code sections where available; document moisture management strategies thoroughly

## Common Mistakes

- Using hay instead of straw — pest and mold problems
- Failing to pre-compress load-bearing walls — settling cracks plaster
- Inadequate roof overhangs — water damage to walls
- Plastering before bales are dry — trapped moisture causes [[decomposition]]
- Using impermeable finishes — vapor barriers trap moisture in walls
- Ignoring foundation moisture protection — wicking from ground rots bottom courses
