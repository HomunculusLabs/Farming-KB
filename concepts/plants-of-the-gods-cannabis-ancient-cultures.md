# Cannabis in Ancient Cultures

## Overview

In *Plants of the Gods*, cannabis (Hemp) is presented as the most
widespread of all narcotics and one of the oldest psychoactive plants
known to humanity. As Marijuana, Maconha, Daggha, Ganja, Charas, and
many other names, the drug and its use have spread nearly throughout
the world. The book traces its use from ancient shamanic practices in
Eurasia through its spread to nearly every culture on Earth.

## Eurasian Origins

Cannabis is the home hallucinogen of Eurasia. The book notes that the
hallucinogenic use of Hemp can be traced far back into history, and it
is possible that the ingestion of this plant was responsible for the
wild dances of the Mongolian shaman. In India, the plant has been used
for millennia in religious and medicinal contexts. At the Shiva Temple
of Pashupatinath near Kathmandu, Indian yogis smoke Marijuana in
preparation for arduous body practice and meditation.

The Scythians, ancient nomadic warriors of the Eurasian steppes, used
cannabis in ritual steam-bath ceremonies. Herodotus described how they
threw hemp seeds onto hot stones in enclosed tents, producing a vapor
bath that induced euphoria and shouting. This practice represents one
of the earliest documented ritual uses of cannabis.

## African Dispersal

Cannabis spread to Africa through multiple routes, becoming deeply
embedded in various cultural practices. The book places cannabis among
the most significant hallucinogenic plants that dispersed from their
Eurasian homeland to nearly every continent, adapted by each receiving
culture to local religious, medical, and social practices.

## Albert Hofmann's Classification

Albert Hofmann classified psychoactive drugs based on Louis Lewin's
older arrangements, dividing them into: analgesics and euphorics
(Opium, Coca); sedatives and tranquilizers (Reserpine); hypnotics
(Kava-kava); and hallucinogens or psychedelics (Peyote, Marijuana,
etc.). While cannabis was grouped with hallucinogens, the authors note
that its effects differ somewhat from classical hallucinogens, often
producing a mixture of effects including euphoria, altered perception,
and sedation rather than purely visionary experiences.

## The Hallucinogen Debate

The book addresses the complex question of whether cannabis qualifies
as a true hallucinogen. While it may induce several kinds of
hallucinations — visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, and gustatory —
its effects are variable and dose-dependent. The definition by Hoffer
and Osmond is cited: "Hallucinogens are chemicals which, in non-toxic
doses, produce changes in perception, in thought and in mood, but
which seldom produce mental confusion, memory loss or disorientation."

Modern brain research has shown that hallucinogens trigger brain
activity entirely different from that apparent with true psychoses.
The authors acknowledge that cannabis produces deep changes in the
sphere of experience, in perception of reality, in space and time, and
in consciousness of self. Without loss of consciousness, the subject
enters a dream world that often appears more real than the normal
world.

## Sacred and Shamanic Use

The book emphasizes that indigenous cultures have considered
hallucinogenic plants as gifts of the gods, if not the gods themselves.
Cannabis is no exception. Its use in shamanic contexts across Eurasia
involved communication with the spirit world, divination, and healing.
The Scythian cannabis steam-bath ceremony and the Hindu sadhu's use of
charas in devotion to Shiva exemplify this sacred dimension.

Among the many definitions offered for this class of substances, the
German toxicologist Louis Lewin first used the term "phantastica," and
while he admitted it "does not cover all that I should wish it to
convey," the book employs the terms hallucinogen and psychotomimetic
as the most widely understood designations.

## Cultural Significance

While most hallucinogens are of plant origin, a few derive from the
Animal Kingdom (toads, frogs, fish) and some are synthetic (LSD, TMA,
DOB). Their use goes back so far into prehistory that it has been
postulated that perhaps the whole idea of the deity could have arisen
as a result of the otherworldly effects of these agents. Cannabis, as
one of the most ancient and widely distributed, played a unique role in
this prehistoric and historic relationship between humans and
psychoactive plants.

## Legal and Social Context

The book notes that in the United States, a substance must be included
in the Harrison Narcotic Act to be considered legally a narcotic. Thus
Marijuana is not legally a narcotic, although it is a controlled
substance. The term "narcotic" has been interpreted as referring to
dangerously addictive agents such as opium and its derivatives
(morphine, codeine, heroin) and cocaine. This legal classification
has had profound social consequences for cannabis users worldwide.

## Modern Spread

From its Eurasian origins, cannabis has become the most widely used
illicit psychoactive substance globally. The book documents how its
spread followed trade routes, colonial expansions, and cultural
exchanges, adapting to local practices wherever it traveled. Despite
this ubiquity, the authors note that the number of hallucinogenic
species employed in indigenous societies is far greater in the New
World than the Old, even though cannabis dominates Old World usage.
