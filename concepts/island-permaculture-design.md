## Low Islands

Low islands are residual landforms composed of coral, sand, and calcareous material, with an accumulation of biomass from sea birds and migratory animals. The critical feature is the **platen** -- a layer of calcium triphosphate (a natural concrete) that forms 9 inches to 3 feet below the surface. This platen is nearly impermeable to rain, causing water to run off laterally rather than infiltrating. Only shallow-rooted agriculture is possible without intervention.

### The Fresh Water Lens

On low islands, fresh water sits as a lens floating on salt water. The top of the fresh water table is typically 3-5 feet below the surface, with only another 4-5 feet of fresh water below. The volume can be calculated from the island's area. Pollution of this lens is catastrophic -- even two or three septic tanks can contaminate the entire supply. Dry toilets are a necessity.

### Grow-Hole Technology

The primary strategy for establishing productive systems on low islands is "grow-hole technology":
1. Shovel away the surface sand and break through the platen
2. Fill the hole with all available humus and organic material
3. Plant a tree (preferably coconut) in the enriched hole
4. The tree's roots keep the platen cracked and permeable, creating a local fresh water system
Grow-hole technology mimics the natural process by which coconut palms establish on atolls. In nature, coconuts wash ashore, germinate on the surface, and send roots down through any crack in the platen. The tree's root system gradually expands the crack, allowing more water infiltration and creating favorable conditions for other species to establish nearby. Mollison recommends planting 100-200 grow holes per hectare initially, focusing on multipurpose trees that provide food, timber, mulch, and wind protection. Each successive generation of planted trees improves conditions for the next.

Within twenty years, a productive forest canopy can be established through repeated grow-hole planting.

### Ring Gardens

The "magical ring garden" is a circular growing system designed for atoll conditions:
- Break up platen in a circle, heap sand around the edge
- Fill the circle with mulch, plant vegetables in the mulch
- Install a single drip point at center (drip water extends laterally about 2-2.5 feet)
- Within a 4-foot diameter circle, 12 running feet of row crop can be achieved

The ring geometry maximizes growing edge relative to pathway area and water delivery points. Sprinkler systems are avoided because wind causes rapid evaporation before water reaches the ground and accelerates platen formation.

### Gley Ponds

Gley ponds (lined with rapidly fermenting organic material like papaya, grape, or banana leaves) can double available water storage. Coconut logs line the banks to prevent collapse. Once vegetation is established, bank stability improves.

### Settlement Pattern

Settlements should be arranged on the periphery of the island, drawing water from central areas. This keeps human waste away from the main water lens. Building a large village at the center of a small island "could be a disastrous technique."

## High Islands

High islands are volcanic or granitic, with more complex topography, deeper soils, and generally greater water storage capacity. They often lack the platen layer that constrains low islands. Design strategies include:
- **Keyline systems** for water distribution across slopes
- **Ridge protection** to prevent erosion of the volcanic cone
- **Coastal vegetation preservation** to protect against wave erosion
- **Multiple zone systems** taking advantage of elevation changes

### High Island Zonation

High islands create natural elevation-based climate zones that allow diverse production in a small area:
- **Coastal zone (0-100m)**: Tropical species, salt-tolerant crops, coconut, breadfruit, pandanus, and mangrove systems for fish and shellfish
- **Lower slopes (100-400m)**: Bananas, taro, sweet potato, and tree crops adapted to warm humid conditions
- **Mid slopes (400-800m)**: Coffee, citrus, avocado, and subtropical fruits; this zone often has the most reliable rainfall
- **Upper slopes (800m+)**: Temperate species, cloud forest vegetation, and watershed protection forest
- **Ridge crests**: Must be maintained as intact forest to protect watershed function; deforestation causes rapid erosion of the volcanic cone and loss of freshwater springs downslope

## Wind and Erosion

All oceanic islands experience two sets of wind (typically northeast-southwest and northwest-southeast) and two periods of calm. Windbreak design is critical. Coastline vegetation -- plants like Pemphis, Tournefortia, and Scaevola that grow directly in the surf zone -- must be preserved. These plants can build island elevation by up to six feet through sand accumulation. Removing them for airstrips or development allows waves to march inland and destroy the island.

Mollison has "observed in these bays that waves are in the process of wiping out islands." The solution is to maintain and extend shoreline vegetation. Airstrips should be contained within vegetation, preferably at an angle to prevailing winds.
Windbreak species for islands must be salt-tolerant and multi-stemmed: Casuarina equisetifolia (ironwood) is the most widely used tropical island windbreak, growing rapidly to 30-60 feet with deep taproots that resist windthrow. In the understory, Hibiscus tiliaceus (sea hibiscus) and Thespesia populnea (miro) provide secondary wind protection and yield useful timber and fiber. Windbreaks should be planted in multiple staggered rows perpendicular to prevailing winds, with the tallest species on the windward side.

## Soil Biology

Islands may start from sterile environments. Mollison recommends bringing in worms, compost, and a handful of soil with every introduced plant. "These plants have soil associates. The nitrogen-fixing bacteria for legumes are not necessarily going to be there."
The biological impoverishment of isolated islands is a well-documented phenomenon. Darwin's observations on volcanic island colonization showed that new islands receive initial biological input from oceanic deposition (seaweed, driftwood, bird droppings) and atmospheric fallout (dust, spores, bacteria). In permaculture design, accelerating this natural colonization process is essential — introducing mycorrhizal inoculants, compost tea, and diverse soil organism communities can establish functional soil food webs within 2-3 growing seasons that would otherwise take decades to develop naturally.

## Species Selection for Islands

Island environments demand salt-tolerant, wind-resistant species with multiple functions:
- **Coastal pioneers**: Pandanus (screw pine), Casuarina (ironwood), Scaevola, Pemphis, Tournefortia — stabilize sand and build elevation
- **Food trees**: Coconut, breadfruit, banana, papaya, mango, citrus, avocado — most productive per unit area in tropical islands
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Gliricidia, Leucaena, Casuarina, Sesbania — essential for building soil fertility on nutrient-poor island soils
- **Ground covers**: Sweet potato, beach morning glory (Ipomoea pes-caprae), Canavalia — prevent erosion and provide food
- **Aquatic species**: Taro (Colocasia), water chestnut, water spinach — productive in freshwater lens margin areas

## Waste Management on Islands

Island ecosystems have zero tolerance for pollution due to limited dilution capacity:
- **Composting toilets**: Essential for low islands; prevent nitrogen and phosphate pollution of the freshwater lens
- **Greywater systems**: Constructed wetlands with appropriate tropical plants (canna, papyrus, water hyacinth in contained systems) treat household wastewater
- **Organic waste recycling**: All organic material must cycle back into the system; island food production depends on returning nutrients to the soil
- **Plastic and inorganic waste**: The greatest long-term threat to island ecosystems; reduction and proper disposal are design priorities from the outset
- **Zero-discharge design**: Every output should become an input for another system — this is not optional on islands but a survival imperative

## Cross-Application

Mollison suggests that coastal species should be tried in desert environments and desert species on coasts, as the basic conditions (salinity, wind exposure, limited water) are often similar. The accidental establishment of date palms on the Hawaiian coast from discarded pits illustrates this principle.
This cross-application extends to design techniques: swales developed for tropical islands are applicable in arid lands where water capture is equally critical; gley pond technology works in any climate where plastic pond liners are unavailable; and the ring garden concept translates to container gardening in urban environments where space and water are limited. The island design mindset — treating every resource as precious and every output as a potential input — is the foundation of all resilient permaculture systems regardless of scale.
