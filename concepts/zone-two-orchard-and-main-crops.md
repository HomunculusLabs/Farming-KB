## Overview

Zone two sits just beyond zone one in the [[permaculture]] zonation system. It is less intensively managed but still visited regularly — perhaps daily or several times per week during growing seasons. This zone contains the orchard, main crop gardens, spot-mulched rather than heavily mulched areas, lightly rather than heavily manured crops, and the foods that comprise the bulk of storage goods for the client group. Zone two also houses products from small domestic animals and serves as the primary nutrient collection zone feeding back into zone one.

## The Nutrient Funnel

Zone two functions as a **biological funnel**, deliberately creating a nutrient vortex. Materials are gathered from the outer zones and recycled through animals for use in zone one. When acorns are collected and fed to chickens, they produce high-value manure and high-value mulch. Nut husk mulch is of high value — typically alkaline and high in calcium. The outer zone materials flow inward through this animal-mediated recycling system.

## Crop Placement in Zone Two

### Distinguishing Zone One from Zone Two Crops

The allocation of crops between zones follows a strict visit-frequency logic. A quick scan through any plant list allows assignment to functional zones:

- **Zone one**: Lettuce, spinach, green beans, pole beans, zucchini, patty-pan squash, parsley, soft herbs — things picked every day
- **Zone two**: Tomatoes (50+ plants visited 2–3 times then pulled wholesale), winter-keeping squash, asparagus (short season, few visits), raspberries (40 feet away, visited daily in season), strawberries (some in zone one, bulk in zone two), main crop vegetables

### Orchard Systems

Zone two contains the orchard — the primary tree crop production area. Orchard trees receive less intensive management than zone one elements but still benefit from regular attention. Poultry housed at the zone one/two boundary can range through the orchard, controlling pests and fertilizing trees.

### Cultivation Methods

Unlike zone one, zone two is **not fully mulched**. It may contain row-cropped main crop gardens. If abundant free mulch is available, mulch it; otherwise, clean cultivation is acceptable. The zone two garden need not maintain the strict weed-free standards of zone one.

## Animal Integration

### Chickens and Ducks

Chickens and ducks can be contained within zone two, housed at the edge of zone one. They require daily attention, so their housing is placed as close as possible to the dwelling. The design exploits the larger zone two system to enrich the smaller zone one through the medium of an animal collector. Expanded-mesh flooring in chicken and goat houses allows manure to collect dry underneath, readily transferred to the garden.

### Milk Goats

For milk goats on hillsides, a corridor into zone two with a range in zone three can be created. The corridor is edged with plants that serve as goat forage, planted just outside a coarse mesh fence so the goats perform pruning as they pass through.

### Pigs

Pigs are typically stocked toward the back of zone two. In confined quarters they can become messy, but on grass they are clean animals and can bed close to zone one. Feeder pigs can be released into zone one and two gardens in fall, confined by temporary electric fencing, to clean up crop residue and eat grubs, slugs, and pest larvae. Pigs are the quickest way to eradicate [[jerusalem-artichoke]] and can destroy asparagus, so fencing must be judicious. In cool fall weather, their odor is reduced, making it convenient to move them closer to the dwelling.

### Milk Cows

A milk cow may extend zone two out to two acres, but this remains a limited area. Anybody who is truly controlling an acre is feeding many people. Two acres fully developed — with apples, eggs, chickens, orchard, and main crops — constitutes a very productive unit.

## Self-Sufficiency on Small Areas

Mollison emphasizes that very small areas can produce remarkable yields:

- Some people achieve financial self-sufficiency on **one-eighth of an acre**
- Others need 500 acres, depending on their approach
- One man near Melbourne provides all the parsley for a city the size of Boston from a very small area, harvesting two boxes daily year-round
- Mollison himself plowed 12 acres and found that only one acre was ever effectively managed — the rest produced weed problems and wasted resources

The lesson: **intensive development of a small area outproduces extensive management of a large area**. As designers, stress the nuclear approach. Almost everything done in a small, intensively managed area works and survives, while broad-scale plantings see decreasing success with extension.

## The Biological Funnel Concept

Zone two is where the permaculture designer creates what Mollison calls a "biological funnel" — a nutrient vortex. Animals do the same thing natural systems do: the beaver, the emu, and other species are among the first agriculturists. Everything farms for itself. The designer bends these rules by deliberately orchestrating the flow of nutrients from outer zones through animals back to the intensive inner zone.

## Integration with Wild Elements

Zone two can incorporate corridors bringing wild elements close to the house. Birds are easily attracted through gardens, shrubs, and trees. Wallabies can serve as lawnmowers, deer as sumac pruners, and squirrels as acorn gatherers. The designer's task is to make the marriage between what the client wants and what the environment offers — protecting the site while providing for the client.

Guarded corridors allow wild animals to coexist with food production. Clients who learn to value these other species — rather than warring against them — discover that the system works more productively and with less labor than constant defensive killing.

## Companion Cropping in Zone Two

Asparagus benefits from ducks and chickens, which eat the asparagus beetle and add fertilizer. Ducks keep mulch relatively undisturbed on sloped beds, while chickens shred and scratch mulch on flat beds, suppressing weeds. If the asparagus bed is placed just sunward of a plum orchard or thicket, poultry can be held in the plums until asparagus cutting is over and shoots have toughened. Poultry also control plum pests, especially early in the season. Raspberries, as edge species, work well in poultry forages — red raspberries suit dry, sunny edges while black raspberries prefer partially shaded edges in well-drained soil.

## The Key Design Rule

The purposes of the zones are to **design distances properly**. When this is done, it pays hundreds of times over. Every time a designer breaks the zone rules, there are consequences — neglected crops, unreached harvests, uncollected manure. The zone system is not about rigid circles but about optimizing the relationship between human attention frequency and the needs of each element in the design.
