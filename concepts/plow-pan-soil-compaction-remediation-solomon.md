## How Rototillers Create Compaction

A walk-behind rototiller may appear to loosen soil eight inches (20 cm)
deep, but this is an illusion. After settling, actual loosened depth is
typically no more than five inches (12 cm). A heavy tractor-mounted
cultivator might reach seven inches (18 cm). This is insufficient for
most vegetables.

If the soil contains clay, the tiller's bent-over tines compress a layer
at their farthest reach. This compressed layer becomes denser than the
original undisturbed soil, forming a barrier to root penetration. To
detect it: push away the loose surface soil after tilling and look for a
shiny, polished layer below — that is the plow pan. The rototiller has
created the illusion of loose soil while actually capping [[fukuoka-textdoc-crops-grow-own-accord-independence-fertilizer]] only in the top seven inches. The existence of deeper
soil below the pan is of little use to the crop. Solomon references
Edward Faulkner's Plowman's Folly, a classic critique of moldboard
plowing, and notes that a plow pan may persist for half a century after
plowing has ceased. It will not go away on its own.

## Why Plow Pans Matter for Gardeners

Solomon asks why he discusses plow pans in a garden book: many home
gardens occupy former farm field. If so, a plow pan almost certainly
exists and will not disappear on its own. The only way to eliminate it is
to dig through it with a shovel or fork, breaking the hard layer and
allowing roots to penetrate deeper soil.

Many vegetables develop root systems three to four feet deep when given
the opportunity. When restricted to seven inches by a plow pan, they
cannot access subsoil moisture during dry periods or tap deeper nutrient
reserves. The difference between a garden with and without a plow pan can
be dramatic.

## Remediation: Spade and Fork Double-Digging

The only way to eliminate a plow pan on a garden scale is with a spade
or fork. After using a rototiller for initial sod destruction (or
skipping it entirely), the gardener should switch to [[garden-hand-tools]]. By this
point the tiller has done its useful work: grass is mostly destroyed and
surface soil is broken. The rest — working to a full foot deep and making
seedbeds — is faster by hand.

For breaking through compaction, Solomon recommends a two-pass method.
First, dig the area by turning chunks of sod upside down to damage grass
roots. Wait a week for the grass to weaken. Then make a second pass,
cutting through chunks to reduce size, killing remaining grass and
blending in amendments. On the second turn, digging goes deeper with
less effort. After another week, enough sod decomposes for fine, crumbly
soil nearly to the shovel blade's depth. Any preexisting plow pan is
eliminated.
