# Mushroom Substrate Preparation and Sterilization

Substrate preparation is the foundational step in mushroom cultivation. The
substrate provides the nutritional base from which fungal [[mycelium]] colonizes and
eventually fruits. Proper preparation and sterilization determine whether a grow
succeeds or falls to contamination.

## What Is a Substrate

A substrate is any organic material that supports fungal growth. In nature,
mushrooms decompose dead wood, leaf litter, or composted soil. In cultivation,
growers replicate these conditions using formulated mixes of agricultural
byproducts supplemented with nitrogen and mineral sources.

The ideal substrate balances carbon, nitrogen, moisture, and structure. Carbon
comes from cellulose and lignin in straw, sawdust, or wood chips. Nitrogen is
added through bran, grain, or composted manure. Moisture content typically sits
between 55 and 65 percent by weight.

## Common Substrate Materials

**Straw** is the classic substrate for oyster mushrooms (*Pleurotus* spp.).
Wheat or oat straw is chopped to short segments, soaked in water, then drained
to field capacity. Straw is inexpensive and widely available, making it the
first choice for beginners.

**Hardwood sawdust or wood chips** suit shiitake, lion's mane, maitake, and
reishi. Oak, beech, and maple are preferred. Softwoods contain resins and
terpenes that inhibit mycelial growth and should be avoided or thoroughly
leached.

**Supplemented sawdust** combines hardwood sawdust with 10 to 20 percent wheat
bran or rice bran. The bran boosts nitrogen content, accelerating colonization
and increasing yields. This is the industry standard for gourmet mushroom
production.

**Composted substrates** are used for button mushrooms (*Agaricus bisporus*)
and some specialty species. Composting involves a controlled thermophilic
[[decomposition]] phase that breaks down raw materials into a selectively nutritive
medium.

**Master's mix**, popularized by gourmet cultivators, blends hardwood sawdust
and soy hulls at a 1:1 ratio. Soy hulls provide both nitrogen and readily
available carbohydrates, producing impressive yields with minimal supplementation
complexity.

## Hydration and Field Capacity

Substrates must be hydrated to the correct moisture level before sterilization.
The term "field capacity" describes the moisture content at which the substrate
holds maximum water without dripping freely. For most bulk substrates, this is
achieved by soaking or steeping, then draining thoroughly.

A practical field-capacity test: squeeze a handful of substrate firmly. A few
drops of water should escape, but the material should not stream water. Too dry
and colonization stalls; too wet and anaerobic pockets form, encouraging
bacterial contamination.

## Pasteurization vs. Sterilization

These two terms are often confused but describe fundamentally different
processes with different goals.

**Pasteurization** heats substrate to 140 to 170 degrees Fahrenheit (60 to 76
degrees Celsius) for 1 to 2 hours. This kills most competitors and pathogens
while preserving beneficial thermophilic microorganisms. Pasteurization is used
for straw and compost substrates where residual microbes help defend against
contamination.

Common pasteurization methods include hot water baths, steam pasteurization
chambers, and the "lye bath" technique where straw is soaked in hydrated lime
solution for 12 to 18 hours, raising the pH to inhibit contaminants.

**Sterilization** heats substrate to 250 degrees Fahrenheit (121 degrees
Celsius) at 15 PSI for 60 to 120 minutes. This kills all living organisms
including bacterial endospores. Sterilization is required for supplemented
substrates because the added nutrients (bran, soy hulls) would otherwise feed
contaminants as readily as they feed mushroom mycelium.

Pressure canning is the standard sterilization method. Substrate is packed into
autoclavable bags or jars, fitted with filter patches for gas exchange, and
processed in a pressure canner or autoclave.

## The Importance of Filter Patches

Sterilized substrate bags must breathe. Mycelium generates carbon dioxide during
colonization, and if gas cannot escape, the bag inflates and eventually bursts.
Filter patches made of microporous polypropylene allow gas exchange while
blocking airborne spores larger than 0.3 microns.

## Common Mistakes

Overpacking the pressure vessel restricts steam circulation and creates cold
spots where substrate is not fully sterilized. Bags should not be stacked
tightly against each other or against the vessel walls.

Insufficient hydration before sterilization leads to dry substrate that
colonizes poorly. Substrate should be hydrated 12 to 24 hours before loading to
allow even water distribution.

Skipping the cool-down phase and inoculating while substrate is still hot will
kill or severely stress the spawn. Substrate must cool to below 90 degrees
Fahrenheit (32 degrees Celsius) before inoculation.

## Bulk Substrate vs. Spawn Substrate

Spawn substrate (grain) is sterilized because grain is extremely nutritious and
contamination-prone. Bulk substrate (straw, sawdust) can often be pasteurized
because its lower nutrient density makes it less attractive to competitors. The
distinction matters because pasteurization is simpler and cheaper than
sterilization.

## Scaling Up

Small-scale growers use pressure canners holding 4 to 10 substrate bags.
Commercial operations use retort autoclaves processing hundreds of bags per
cycle. Regardless of scale, the principles remain identical: clean materials,
proper hydration, adequate sterilization time and temperature, and sterile
handling post-process.
