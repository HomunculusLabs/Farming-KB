## Fungal Genomics and Comparative Approaches

Fungal genomics has undergone rapid expansion since the sequencing of
[[bloomfield-chytrid-fungi-early-fungal-evolution]].

## Landscape of fungal genome sequencing

As of early 2006, 13 completed fungal genomes were published, including
Ashbya gossypii, [[neurospora-crassa]], Phanerochaete
chrysosporium, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Schizosaccharomyces pombe,
and Yarrowia lipolytica. An additional 158 large-scale fungal sequencing
projects were ongoing, including 136 full genome projects through the
Broad Institute's Fungal Genomes Initiative.

## Genome size and gene content variation

Fungal genomes vary substantially in size and complexity. S. cerevisiae
has 6,591 predicted genes, N. crassa has 10,082, and M. grisea has
11,109. The greater complexity of filamentous ascomycete genomes
compared to unicellular yeasts likely reflects their multicellular
nature and more diverse life histories and ecologies. Genome sizes range
from approximately 10 Mb in microsporidia to over 40 Mb in some
basidiomycetes.

## The S. cerevisiae legacy

S. cerevisiae was the first eukaryotic genome sequenced (1996). Its
comprehensive databases (MIPS Yeast Genome Database, Saccharomyces
Genome Database at Stanford) incorporate predicted ORFs, deletion mutant
phenotypes, microarray data, and proteomic analyses. This provides a
template for what could be achieved for other fungi, though improvements
in technology for generating whole-genome knockout collections and
inter-laboratory collaboration are needed.

## COGEME and EST resources

The COGEME (Consortium for the Genomics of Microbial Eukaryotes)
EST database serves the phytopathogen community. EST sequences are
clustered into unisequences to reduce redundancy, annotated by homology,
and classified by function. As of 2006, the database contained 59,765
unisequences from 18 species of fungal and oomycete phytopathogen and
three non-pathogenic fungi. ESTs confirm gene expression and enable
intron positioning and UTR definition.

## Comparative genomics of pathogenicity

Comparing gene inventories between pathogenic and non-pathogenic fungi
addresses what makes a pathogen. Three mechanisms may account for
pathogenicity evolution: (1) acquisition of novel genes via horizontal
transfer or gene duplication; (2) co-option of existing genes for new
roles in pathogenic species through regulatory changes; (3) gene loss.
M. grisea has 122 cytochrome P450 genes versus 37 in saprotrophic
N. crassa, and nine cutinase genes versus none, reflecting gene family
expansion associated with plant infection.
