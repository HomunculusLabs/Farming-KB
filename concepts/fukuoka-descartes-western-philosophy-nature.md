## Descartes and the Western Philosophy of Nature

## Overview

In *The Road Back to Nature*, [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophical-foundation]] of the Western attitude toward nature
that has led to environmental destruction. He contrasts this anthropocentric worldview with
Eastern philosophies of unity between humanity and nature, arguing that the West"s error was
not merely technological but fundamentally philosophical.

## The Cartesian Worldview

Descartes' statement "I think, therefore I am" reflects the Western viewpoint as Fukuoka
understood it.

What it says, in essence, is that nature exists only because the self-reflecting "I" exists to
perceive it. Nature has no independent existence apart from human consciousness.

This means nature may be used and refashioned as man pleases to serve human purposes.

The natural world becomes raw material for human ambition, lacking intrinsic value or rights
of its own.

This philosophical stance provided the starting point for the development of sciences that
serve man rather than nature.

## Science as the Instrument of Dominion

Using the Cartesian framework, Westerners developed sciences that allowed them to control the
world and other races.

Technology became the means by which humanity asserted its dominance over the natural world.

Agriculture was transformed from a partnership with nature into an industrial process of
extraction and production.

Forests were cleared, rivers dammed, soils mined, and species driven to extinction — all in
service of human progress defined in purely material terms.

Nature was not something to be understood on its own terms but something to be taken apart
and reconstructed according to human designs.

## The Growing Western Doubt

By the 1980s, Fukuoka observed that Westerners had begun to realize something was wrong.

They had started to notice the fallacies of Western philosophy.

They could see environmental degradation, resource depletion, and spiritual emptiness but did
not know which direction to turn.

They said: "We see that something"s wrong, but we have no idea which way to go from here."

This crisis of confidence was not merely about specific environmental problems but about the
entire philosophical framework that had produced those problems.

## The Turn Toward Eastern Philosophy

Fukuoka detected that Westerners had begun to question the road taken by Christianity and
Western philosophy.

They noticed new possibilities in Eastern philosophy and Buddhism.

They were not saying that Eastern philosophy was unconditionally good, or that they would
abandon Christian beliefs for the Buddhist faith.

They had noticed something valuable in Eastern thought — a different way of understanding the
relationship between humanity and nature.

Westerners were beginning to mine forgotten Eastern wisdom and use it to progress in a new
direction.

## The Eastern View of Unity

The Eastern view that Fukuoka championed holds that man should seek to become one with nature
rather than standing apart from it.

In this framework, nature is not an object to be studied and controlled but a living whole of
which humanity is an inseparable part.

Knowledge of nature comes not from dissection and analysis but from participation and
intuition.
