# Psilocybin Synthesis

The chemical synthesis of psilocybin and its dephosphorylated analog psilocin
has been pursued since Albert Hofmann first isolated these compounds from
Psilocybe mushrooms in the late 1950s. Isolating psilocybin and psilocin
directly from fungal biomass on a gram scale is impractical because psilocin
readily decomposes and psilocybin has high polarity that complicates
chromatographic purification.

Shirota, Hakamata, and Goda (2003), working at the National Institute of
Health Sciences in Tokyo, developed a concise, large-scale synthesis route that
avoids chromatographic purification entirely, making it suitable for producing
analytical reference standards needed for forensic and regulatory identification
of psilocybin-containing mushrooms.

## Chemical Background

Psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, or 4-HO-DMT) and psilocybin
(4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, or O-phosphoryl-psilocin) are
tryptamine alkaloids that closely resemble the neurotransmitter serotonin
(5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT).

Psilocybin is the phosphorylated prodrug of psilocin; it is pharmacologically
inactive until dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatases in the body, converting
it to the active psilocin. Both compounds act primarily as partial agonists at
the 5-HT2A serotonin receptor in the brain, which underlies their psychedelic
effects.

Baeocystin (4-hydroxy-N-methyltryptamine) and norbaeocystin
(4-hydroxytryptamine) are often present as minor constituents in Psilocybe
mushrooms and are thought to contribute to the overall pharmacological profile.

The structural similarity of psilocin to serotonin is significant: both
possess an indole ring system and an ethylamine side chain, differing primarily
in the substitution pattern on the indole ring (4-hydroxy in psilocin versus
5-hydroxy in serotonin). It is likely that the hallucinogenic effect of
psilocybin-containing mushrooms is caused by their interference with normal
brain serotonin actions, a mechanism that may also apply to LSD.

## Historical Synthesis Efforts

The first total synthesis of psilocybin was reported by Troxler, Seemann, and
Hofmann in 1959, followed by an alternative route from Hofmann, Frey, and
colleagues in 1958. These early syntheses suffered from low overall yields,
particularly in the phosphorylation step, and required chromatographic
purification.

Nichols and Frescas (1999) improved the phosphorylation methodology using
dibenzyl phosphate chemistry but still relied on anion-exchange resin
purification and achieved only a 47% isolated yield for the phosphorylation
step. The Shirota route represents a significant practical advance by
eliminating all chromatographic steps.

## The Shirota Synthesis Route

The synthesis begins with commercially available 4-hydroxyindole and proceeds
through six steps without requiring any chromatographic purification.

### Step 1: Protection as 4-Acetylindole

4-Hydroxyindole is acetylated using acetic anhydride in pyridine and
dichloromethane at 0 degrees Celsius to room temperature, yielding
4-acetylindole. Acetylation was chosen over benzyl ether protection because
benzyl protection requires a separate deprotection step, adding complexity.

The 4-acetylindole crystallizes as ivory-white crystals in quantitative yield
(34 g from 185 mmol starting material) and is used directly without further
purification. The 1H NMR spectrum shows characteristic signals at delta 8.27
(broad singlet, NH) and 2.39 (singlet, acetyl methyl), confirming the acetyl
protection.

### Step 2: Acylation with Oxalyl Chloride

The acetyl-protected indole is treated with oxalyl chloride in cold diethyl
ether, followed by addition of n-hexane and storage in a freezer overnight.
This produces 3-glyoxalyl-4-acetylindole as yellow crystals, isolated by
filtration.

The crystalline nature of this intermediate simplifies purification compared
to the 4-O-benzyl derivative used by Nichols and Frescas, which was described
as somewhat unstable and difficult to isolate in crystal form without careful
reaction control. The yellow crystals are separated from solution, dissolved in
THF, and carried directly to the next step.

### Step 3: Amidation with Dimethylamine

The glyoxalyl chloride intermediate is reacted with a 2 M solution of
dimethylamine in THF, with pyridine as a base, yielding
3-dimethylaminooxalyl-4-acetylindole as an ivory-white crystalline powder in
80% yield (22.0 g from 100 mmol).

This two-step acylation-amidation sequence is efficient because each
intermediate crystallizes and can be isolated by simple filtration and washing,
avoiding column chromatography entirely. Additional diethyl ether is added
during the reaction to prevent solidification.

### Step 4: Reduction to Psilocin

The oxalylamide is reduced with lithium aluminum hydride (LiAlH4) in THF under
argon atmosphere. The solution of the oxalylamide in THF is added dropwise to a
suspension of LiAlH4 in THF over 2 hours, then refluxed for 2 hours.

After cooling, careful quenching with anhydrous Na2SO4 powder followed by
saturated Na2SO4 solution is performed. The product is collected by filtration
and washed with ethanol to afford psilocin as white crystals in 87.5% yield
(14.3 g from 80 mmol).

The melting point of 169 to 174 degrees Celsius (decomposing) matches
literature values of 173 to 176 degrees Celsius. The ESI mass spectrum shows
the molecular ion at m/z 205 [M+H]+, and high-resolution MS confirms the
molecular formula C12H17N2O.
