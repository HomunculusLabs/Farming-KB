## Learning from Established Forest Gardens

[[hemenway-accelerated-succession]] presents several real-world food
forest gardens that demonstrate the principles of
ecological design in practice. Each case study
offers unique lessons about adapting mollison designers temperate to different climates, sites, and goals.
Together, they prove that the food forest concept
is not theoretical but practical, productive, and
achievable for home gardeners.

## The Bullock Brothers' Food Forest (Orcas Island)

Perhaps the most impressive example in the book,
the Bullock brothers' several-acre food forest on
Orcas Island, Washington, demonstrates what is
possible with patient, observant ecological design.

### Background

Joe, Douglas, and Sam Bullock moved to a brambled,
overgrown field and systematically transformed it
into a verdant, productive landscape. They
understood that wetlands are among the most
productive ecosystems on the planet, teeming with
more and animals than any farm. When they
found that the lowest part of their property had
been drained and farmed by a previous owner, they
tore out the dikes and restored the wetland.

### The Muskrat Lesson

The restored wetland attracted muskrats, which
initially decimated the cattail plantings. Rather
than declaring war on the animals, the Bullocks
observed the natural cycle: muskrat populations
rose as food became abundant, then crashed when
they ate themselves out of house and home. The
cattails returned, and a natural balance emerged.
This illustrates Hemenway's principle of trusting
ecological processes rather than fighting them.

### Results

In less than a decade, the property leapt from
overgrown field to a landscape where walnut trees
and bamboo groves cast dappled shade on glowing
nasturtium blossoms, nut trees shelter a bamboo
grove, and vegetable beds wind into the woods.
Doug Bullock described the benefits: "Sheer
biodiversity is part of it. We get birds and
other animals here that no one else has seen for
years. But the best part is the food -- it's
unreal! Every summer we have about twenty students
here for three weeks. And there's enough fruit
right here to feed them."
Every month of the year brings fresh fruit or vegetables: cool-season salad greens in midwinter,
berries in late spring, and branch-breaking loads
of fruit in summer and fall. Even in December,
their exotic medlar trees bear a fruit that tastes
like cinnamon-spiced pear butter.

### Key Techniques

- Wetland restoration for biodiversity and
  [[water-management]]
- Dense, diverse plantings in every niche
- Integration of bamboo, nuts, fruit, and
  vegetable production
- Use of swales for water harvesting
- Patience with natural population cycles

## Jerome Osentowski's Mountain Food Forest (Colorado)

Jerome Osentowski's garden at 7,400 feet in Aspen,
Colorado, demonstrates that food forests can
thrive even in harsh mountain climates where
frosts can occur during any month.

### The Transition from Annuals to Perennials

After years of growing organic salad greens for
upscale Aspen markets, Jerome was ready for a
change. The food forest was a natural evolution:
"After years of growing annual vegetables, a lot
of fertility had leached down to where the short
roots of salad greens couldn't get it. So I went
to fruit trees, with their deep roots, to get at
all those nutrients."

### The Companion Plant System

Jerome calls his guild companion-plants
"companieros." The rich array includes:
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Pea shrubs, fava beans,
  clovers, fenugreek, alfalfa
- **Bee plants**: Borage and comfrey
- **Insectary species**: Fennel, celery, dill,
  coriander
- **Pest confusers**: Horseradish, Mexican
  marigold, garlic mustard, walking onions

### Species Palette

The mountain food forest includes:
- **Trees**: Apples (multi-grafted), apricots,
  plums, Douglas firs, New Mexico locusts
- **Shrubs**: Black and white currants,
  gooseberries, bush cherries, cranberries,
  Siberian pea shrubs
- **and climbers**: Grapes, scarlet runner
