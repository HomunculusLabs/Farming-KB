The stipe curves upward so the pileus turns away from the
wind direction, opening with lamellae facing directly downward.
This behavior occurs under both overhead and under-lighting
conditions, indicating that gravity is the primary driver
rather than light direction.

## The Spore Production Link

A critical finding from Badham (1982) is that sporeless
mutants of *Psilocybe cubensis* did not exhibit the
negative geotropic response at all.
This directly links geotropic sensitivity to spore production,
suggesting that the physiological changes accompanying
sporogenesis activate the tropism mechanism.
Light is necessary for this geotropic response, but this may
simply be because light is itself required for spore formation.
The causal chain likely runs from light enabling sporulation,
and sporogenesis in turn activating the geotropic curvature
machinery.

## Horizontal Rotation Experiments

When mushrooms were rotated horizontally on a clinostat
(equalizing the gravitational stimulus) under constant
illumination, growth and pileus expansion proceeded
horizontally rather than upward.
This confirms that gravity perception is the dominant
orienting force once the geotropic response has been
activated at Stage 2.
Without a consistent gravitational vector, the mushroom
cannot determine which direction constitutes "up,"
and the pileus expands in whatever orientation growth
happens to take.

## Role of the Lamellae and Tropism Hormones

Decapitation experiments by Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963)
provided evidence that tropism-regulating hormones are
produced in or associated with the lamellae (gills).
Removing the pileus abolished the geotropic response in
the remaining stipe tissue, pointing to the gills as a
source of tropism signals.
Agar diffusates collected from gills were shown by
Hagimoto and Konishi (1960) to partially replace the
pileus influence on [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]].
Some mushrooms have been found to contain higher plant
hormones such as auxins and gibberellins, but there is
no evidence that these regulate mushroom growth or
tropic responses.
The tropism hormones of basidiomycetes remain an open
question in fungal physiology.
