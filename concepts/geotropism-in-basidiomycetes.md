# Geotropism in Basidiomycetes

[[negative-geotropism-in-mushroom-fruiting]] basidiomycetes is the upward curvature
response of mushroom stipes that positions the pileus
horizontally for effective spore dispersal.
This response is one of several tropisms studied in
[[mushroom-tropisms]].

## Overview

In *[[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]*, negative geotropism manifests at
Stage 2 of development, when the pileus becomes spherical
and [[spore-production-geotropic-sensitivity-coupling-basidiomycete-badham-1982]] begins.
The stipe curves upward so the pileus turns away from the
wind direction, opening with lamellae facing directly downward.
This behavior occurs under both overhead and under-lighting
conditions, indicating that gravity is the primary driver
rather than light direction.

## The Spore Production Link

A critical finding from Badham (1982) is that sporeless
mutants of *Psilocybe cubensis* did not exhibit the
negative geotropic response at all.
This directly links geotropic sensitivity to spore production,
suggesting that the physiological changes accompanying
sporogenesis activate the tropism mechanism.
Light is necessary for this geotropic response, but this may
simply be because light is itself required for spore formation.
The causal chain likely runs from light enabling sporulation,
and sporogenesis in turn activating the geotropic curvature
machinery.

## Horizontal Rotation Experiments

When mushrooms were rotated horizontally on a clinostat
(equalizing the gravitational stimulus) under constant
illumination, growth and pileus expansion proceeded
horizontally rather than upward.
This confirms that gravity perception is the dominant
orienting force once the geotropic response has been
activated at Stage 2.
Without a consistent gravitational vector, the mushroom
cannot determine which direction constitutes "up,"
and the pileus expands in whatever orientation growth
happens to take.

## Role of the Lamellae and Tropism Hormones

Decapitation experiments by Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963)
provided evidence that tropism-regulating hormones are
produced in or associated with the lamellae (gills).
Removing the pileus abolished the geotropic response in
the remaining stipe tissue, pointing to the gills as a
source of tropism signals.
Agar diffusates collected from gills were shown by
Hagimoto and Konishi (1960) to partially replace the
pileus influence on [[windward-leeward-boundary-layer-redistribution-stipe-curvature-badham-1982]].
This suggests that a diffusible substance — likely a
growth-regulating hormone — is produced by the lamellae
and transported downward through the stipe to regulate
differential growth and curvature.
However, the specific mushroom hormones involved in
tropism have been postulated but never conclusively
identified by [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]].
Some mushrooms have been found to contain higher plant
hormones such as auxins and gibberellins, but there is
no evidence that these regulate mushroom growth or
tropic responses.
The tropism hormones of basidiomycetes remain an open
question in fungal physiology.

## Ecological Significance

The negative geotropic response is particularly important
for lignicolous (wood-dwelling) and coprophagous
(dung-dwelling) mushroom species.
These species often inhabit unstable substrates where
the orientation of the fruiting body at emergence may
be far from horizontal.
Effective spore dispersal demands a horizontal pileus
so that basidiospores can drop freely from the lamellae
and be carried away by air currents (Plunkett 1961).
Without geotropic correction, spores would be shed at
suboptimal angles and dispersal efficiency would drop
significantly.
The evolutionary pressure to orient the pileus horizontally
is therefore strong in species growing from substrates
that shift, tilt, or topple.

## The Veil and Developmental Timing

The partial veil may serve a protective function during
early development, shielding the upper stipe from wind
and light before the final cap readjustment occurs.
By restricting exposure before Stage 2, the veil could
prevent premature activation of the geotropic response
before spores are ready for dispersal.
Once the veil ruptures and the pileus expands, the full
geotropic correction takes place, ensuring the cap
reaches its optimal horizontal orientation for spore
release.

## Summary

Negative geotropism in basidiomycetes is a tightly
coupled developmental response that activates at
sporogenesis and orients the pileus for effective
dispersal.
Evidence from sporeless mutants, decapitation studies,
and horizontal rotation experiments collectively point
to a lamellae-originating tropism signal that depends
on active spore production.
The exact chemical identity of this signal remains
unknown, representing a significant gap in our
understanding of [[mushroom-tropisms]] and fungal
developmental biology.
