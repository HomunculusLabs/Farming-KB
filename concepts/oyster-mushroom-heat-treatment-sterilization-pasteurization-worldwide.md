# Oyster Mushroom Heat Treatment Methods: Worldwide Comparison

## Overview

Substrate must be treated before spawning to kill or reduce pests and
microorganisms. Two categories exist: **sterilisation** (kills all
microorganisms, harmful or beneficial) and **pasteurisation** (reduces
microorganism count while preserving some beneficial organisms). The choice
depends on substrate type, available equipment, scale, and budget. Methods
vary dramatically across countries, from autoclaves to simple oil drums.

## Method Categories

### Normal Pressure Sterilisation

Bags are placed in an autoclave or steriliser at 90–95°C for 5–8 hours, or at
100°C for 4 hours (measured from when the inside temperature reaches target).
Substrates are then moved to a cooler room.

**Advantages**: Minimal substrate moisture loss, nutrients conditioned for
increased mycelial absorption, easy and low-cost setup.
**Disadvantages**: Insufficient sterilisation for some substrates, time
consuming, fuel consuming.

### High Pressure Sterilisation

Under pressure of 1.5 kg/cm² (20 psi), the steriliser temperature reaches 121°C.
Maintained for 60–90 minutes. Pressure is released first; after steam escapes,
bags are moved to a cooler place.

**Advantages**: Time saving, fuel saving, complete sterilisation possible.
**Disadvantages**: Considerable substrate moisture loss, nutrient loss possible,
high set-up cost.

### Oil Drum Pasteurisation

Oil drums are cheap, easy to obtain, and simple to install. They are mounted on
bricks, rocks, or other heat-resistant material. The assembly consists of the
drum(s), metal or wooden grates, lids with steam escape holes, and a heat source.

**Procedure**:
1. First grate fitted into a notch in the drum
2. Water filled about 15 cm below the grate
3. Grate surface and drum wall lined with linen to avoid bag burning
4. Prepared bags stacked in layers on grates
5. Lid placed and rim sealed
6. Water boiled; heating maintained for 4–6 hours from when vapour starts rising
7. Pasteurisation time depends on bag size and substrate material

### Outdoor Bulk Pasteurisation

Bags in stack or nest containers are placed on shelving, covered with plastic
sheet, insulation, and tarpaulin. Steam from a boiler is fed through the
tent-like structure for about 10 hours. Bags then cool to 25°C. This method
suits larger operations; the key challenge is uniform temperature distribution.

## Country-Specific Practices

### India

**Hot water method**: A gunny bag of straw is placed in boiling water for 15–25
minutes, removed, and left for 8–10 hours to drain excess water and cool. The
bag is not opened until block-making time to prevent contamination. Moisture
content is tested by the "palm method" (a handful squeezed should yield a few
drops without dripping).

**Steam method**: A small hole is punched in the drum lid. While boiling straw,
the lid is sealed with a rubber tube. Stones placed in the drum with water to
their level. Wetted straw is steamed in a bamboo basket over the stones. After
boiling, straw is transferred to a sterilised gunny bag for 8–10 hours cooling.

### Nepal

The wetted substrate is steamed in a 200-litre drum for 1–2 hours at 90°C. The
steamed straw is then allowed to cool. At this point some growers supplement the
substrate with protein-rich material such as wheat or rice bran.

### Vietnam

There is no special pasteurisation — fermentation is performed outdoors instead.
This low-tech approach relies on natural microbial succession during fermentation
to prepare the substrate, avoiding the need for fuel-intensive heat treatment.

### South Africa

Methods range from hot water treatment to pasteurisation depending on equipment
availability. Chopped wheat straw is mixed with lime, gypsum, and water in
rotatable pasteurisation vessels. The substrate is pasteurised with live steam
for 2 hours at 70–75°C and cooled overnight.

**Key feature**: Rotatable vessels for even mixing, lime and gypsum amendments,
moderate temperature pasteurisation sufficient for straw substrate.

### Bangladesh

Substrate is sterilised at 121°C for 15 minutes in an autoclave and cooled at
room temperature for 24 hours. This is the most intensive method, using full
high-pressure sterilisation despite the relatively simple substrate (sawdust
with wheat bran).

## Choosing the Right Method

Several factors determine the optimal heat treatment:

1. **Substrate composition**: Straw-based substrates tolerate pasteurisation;
   sawdust-based substrates often require sterilisation for complete pathogen
   elimination. Supplemented substrates (with bran, lime, or gypsum) are more
   susceptible to contamination and may need higher treatment intensity.

2. **Equipment availability**: Oil drum methods require only basic materials;
   autoclaves represent significant capital investment. The choice often
   reflects economic reality rather than biological optimality.

3. **Fuel costs**: High-pressure sterilisation is faster but requires more
   sophisticated equipment; normal-pressure methods are slower but accessible.

4. **Scale of operation**: Bulk pasteurisation under tarps suits medium to
   large operations; individual bag sterilisation in drums suits smallholders.

5. **Climate**: In tropical regions like Vietnam, outdoor fermentation can
   replace heat treatment entirely; in cooler climates, fuel-based methods are
   necessary.

## Temperature Targets

| Method | Temperature | Duration |
|--------|------------|----------|
| High pressure sterilisation | 121°C | 60–90 min |
| Normal pressure (hot) | 100°C | 4 hours |
| Normal pressure (warm) | 90–95°C | 5–8 hours |
| Drum pasteurisation | ~100°C (steam) | 4–6 hours |
| Bulk steam pasteurisation | 70–75°C | 2 hours |
| Nepal drum steam | 90°C | 1–2 hours |
