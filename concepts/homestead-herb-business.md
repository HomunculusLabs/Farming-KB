# Homestead Herb Business

A homestead herb business transforms homegrown herbs into value-added products: dried herbs, tinctures, salves, teas, and culinary blends. Herbs require minimal space, have low startup costs, and yield high margins. This guide covers production, processing, labeling, and selling.

## Herb Selection

### Culinary Herbs (High Volume, Steady Demand)
- Basil: top seller fresh and dried, annual
- Rosemary: perennial, easy to dry, versatile
- Thyme: perennial, easy to dry, staple culinary herb
- Sage: perennial, strong flavor, popular for stuffing and smudging
- Oregano: perennial, easy to grow and dry
- Parsley: biennial, high volume, fresh and dried
- Cilantro/coriander: annual, leaves (cilantro) and seeds (coriander) both sellable
- Lavender: perennial, flowers and leaves, crosses into crafts and medicine
- Mint: perennial, spreads aggressively (contain it), popular for tea

### Medicinal Herbs (Higher Margin, Niche Market)
- Chamomile: calming tea, easy to grow, high demand
- Echinacea: immune support, popular tincture ingredient
- Lemon balm: calming, easy to grow, perennial
- Calendula: skin healing, salves, infused oils, annual
- Peppermint: digestive, tea, tincture
- Yarrow: wound healing, fever reduction, perennial
- Holy basil (tulsi): adaptogen, growing popularity
- Valerian: sleep aid, root harvest, perennial
- Elderberry: syrup, immune support, shrub

### Best for Beginners
Start with 5-8 varieties. Recommended starter mix:
1. Basil (culinary, high demand)
2. Rosemary (culinary, perennial, easy)
3. Chamomile (medicinal tea, high demand)
4. Lavender (culinary + craft crossover)
5. Calendula (medicinal, easy, prolific)
6. Peppermint (culinary + medicinal, perennial)
7. Thyme (culinary staple, perennial)
8. Sage (culinary, perennial, easy to dry)

## Growing and Harvesting

### Growing Methods
- Direct sow or start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost
- Most herbs prefer well-drained soil and full sun (6+ hours)
- Perennial herbs: plant once, harvest for years
- Annual herbs: succession plant every 2-3 weeks for continuous harvest
- Container growing: excellent for mint (containment) and annuals
- Integrate into [[food-forest-design|food-forest]] and [[food-forest-design|plant-systems]] designs

### Harvest Timing
- Harvest culinary herbs just before flowering (peak oil content)
- Harvest flowers (chamomile, calendula, lavender) when fully open
- Harvest medicinal roots (valerian, echinacea) in fall after 2+ years growth
- Harvest in the morning after dew dries but before heat of day
- Cut stems 2-3 inches above ground for most herbs; this encourages regrowth

### Yield Expectations
- Dried herb yield: 4-8 lbs fresh = 1 lb dried (varies by herb)
- Basil: 2-3 lbs fresh per plant per season
- Rosemary: 1-2 lbs fresh per established plant per season
- Chamomile flowers: 0.5-1 lb dried per 50 sq ft bed
- Calendula flowers: 0.5-1 lb dried per 50 sq ft bed

## Processing Methods

### Drying Herbs
- Air drying: bundle small bunches (1-inch stems), hang upside down in warm, dark, well-ventilated area. 1-3 weeks.
- Screen drying: spread herbs on mesh screens in single layer. 3-7 days.
- Dehydrator: 95-115F for leaves, 125F for roots. 4-12 hours.
- Oven drying: lowest setting with door propped open. Less ideal -- easy to over-dry.
- Herbs are dry when stems snap and leaves crumble.
- Store in airtight glass jars away from light and heat.
- Shelf life: 1 year for leaves, 2 years for roots/seeds.

### Tinctures
- Alcohol tincture: pack dried herb in jar, cover with 80-100 proof vodka or everclear
- Ratio: 1:4 or 1:5 herb to alcohol (by weight)
- Shake daily for 4-6 weeks
- Strain through cheesecloth or fine mesh
- Bottle in amber dropper bottles
- Shelf life: 3-5 years

### Salves and Balms
- Create herb-infused oil first: slow-cook dried herbs in carrier oil (olive, coconut, jojoba) on low heat for 2-4 hours or solar infuse for 2-4 weeks
- Strain oil
- Combine infused oil with beeswax (1:4 to 1:5 beeswax to oil ratio by weight)
- Heat gently until beeswax melts
- Pour into tins or jars
- Add essential oils (optional) after cooling slightly
- Shelf life: 1-2 years

### Tea Blends
- Mix dried herbs in proportions that taste good
- Package in compostable tea bags, loose-leaf tins, or muslin bags
- Label with ingredients and brewing instructions
- Popular blends: sleep blend (chamomile + lavender + lemon balm), immune blend (echinacea + elderberry + rosehip), digestion blend (peppermint + fennel + ginger)

## Labeling Requirements

### FDA/Cottage Food Label Requirements
- Product name
- Net weight/volume
- Ingredients list (descending order by weight)
- "This statement has not been evaluated by the FDA" (for medicinal products)
- Business name and address
- Allergen statement if applicable

### Medicinal Herb Disclaimer
- "These statements have not been evaluated by the FDA. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."
- Required on all products making health claims
- Some states restrict therapeutic claims more than others

## Pricing Guide

### Dried Herbs
- Loose dried herbs: $3-6 per 1oz bag
- Dried herb jars (2-4 oz): $6-12
- Culinary herb blends: $6-10 per 2oz jar

### Tinctures
- 1 oz dropper bottle: $8-14
- 2 oz dropper bottle: $14-24
- Cost of goods: $2-5 per 2oz bottle

### Salves
- 1 oz tin: $6-10
- 2 oz tin: $10-16
- Cost of goods: $2-4 per tin

### Tea Blends
- Tea bags (per bag): $1-2
- Loose-leaf tins (2-4 oz): $8-14
- Sample sizes: $3-5 for 1-2 servings
