## Overview

In *What a Plant Knows*, [[daniel-chamovitz]] explores the biochemistry of
animal vision — specifically rhodopsin and photopsins — and draws striking
structural parallels to the photoreceptor systems that plants evolved
independently. Both kingdoms solve the same fundamental problem: converting
electromagnetic radiation into a biochemical signal, using proteins whose
three-dimensional structure selectively absorbs particular wavelengths.

## Rhodopsin and the Chemistry of Vision

The human retina contains two classes of photoreceptive cells: **rods**
and **cones**. Rods mediate low-light vision via a single pigment,
**rhodopsin**. Cones mediate color vision using related pigments called
**photopsins** (cone opsins), each tuned to a different spectral band.

Every visual pigment has two components:

1.  **Opsin** — a membrane-embedded G-protein coupled receptor whose
    amino-acid sequence determines the pigment's absorption maximum.
2.  **Retinal** — a vitamin-A-derived chromophore that undergoes *cis-trans
    isomerisation* upon absorbing a photon, triggering the opsin to
    activate a G-protein and launch a signal cascade.

The geometry of the retinal-binding pocket — shaped by the opsin's
sequence — shifts each pigment's peak absorption wavelength. Small
differences in side-chain interactions produce the entire palette of cone
sensitivities in trichromatic humans.

## Wavelength Selectivity and Human Color Vision

Chamovitz highlights the absorption patterns underlying human color
perception:

| Wavelength   | Absorbed By                                         |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| Blue light  | Rhodopsin **and** blue photopsin                    |
| Red light   | Rhodopsin **and** red photopsin                     |
| Purple light| Rhodopsin, blue photopsin, and red photopsin        |
|             | *(but **not** green photopsin)*                     |

Each cone type is tuned to a different wavelength range; the brain infers
color by comparing relative activation across the three populations.
Purple light stimulates the red and blue pathways while leaving green
quiescent — a combinatorial code the visual cortex decodes as purple.

Every rod or cone responds only to light focused upon it. The retina
functions as a dense detector array — Chamovitz likens it to "rows and
rows of LEDs in flat-screen TVs or sensors in digital cameras." Each
pixel fires independently, and the aggregate pattern forms a visual scene.

## When Vision Fails

Chamovitz categorizes blindness into three classes:

1.  **Structural defects** — physical retinal damage ([[blesching-cannabis-age-related-macular-degeneration]],
    detachment) that destroys the detector array.
2.  **Pigment defects** — mutations impairing rhodopsin or photopsin
    function. Over 150 rhodopsin (*RHO*) mutations cause retinitis
    pigmentosa. Missing photopsins produce color blindness: no red cones
    yields red color blindness (protanopia); no green cones yields green
    color blindness (deuteranopia).
3.  **Signal-transfer defects** — failures in the neural pathway from
    retina to visual cortex (optic-nerve damage, cortical lesions).

## Plant Photoreceptors: Phytochrome and Cryptochrome

Plants lack eyes yet possess light-sensing machinery chemically analogous
to [[rhodopsin-photopsins-animal-visual-pigments-plant-photoreceptor-parallels]]. Chamovitz identifies two major families:

-  **Phytochrome** — a biliprotein detecting red and far-red light. It
    toggles between Pr and Pfr conformations upon photon absorption,
    regulating germination, [[phototropism-and-shade-avoidance]], and flowering.
-  **Cryptochrome** — a flavoprotein absorbing blue light, influencing
    [[plant-circadian-rhythms]], de-etiolation, and stomatal opening. Structurally
    homologous to photolyase DNA-repair enzymes but co-opted for signalling.

Like rhodopsin, both undergo light-induced conformational changes in a
chromophore-binding domain, triggering downstream cascades (kinase
activity, protein-protein interactions) that alter gene expression.

## Cross-Kingdom Structural Parallels

The convergent features between animal and plant photoreceptors are
remarkable:

-  **Protein + chromophore architecture** — a protein scaffold holds a
    small chromophore (retinal in animals; bilin or flavin in plants),
    with protein structure dictating spectral sensitivity.
-  **Conformational switching** — photon absorption drives isomerisation
    or redox changes, mechanically deforming the protein into an active
    signalling state.
-  **Signal amplification via cascades** — a single activated photoreceptor
    triggers multi-step enzymatic cascades (G-proteins in animals; kinase
    cascades in plants), ensuring even dim light produces a response.
-  **Tunable spectral sensitivity** — gene duplication produces pigment
    families with shifted absorption peaks, sampling multiple spectral bands.

Chamovitz argues these parallels reflect **fundamental biophysical
constraints** on detecting electromagnetic radiation. The basic design —
a chromophore tuned by a protein pocket, coupled to a signal cascade —
emerged independently in plants and animals as an optimal solution.

## Broader Implications

Visual pigment chemistry bridges molecular biology, evolutionary biology,
and biophysics. Widely separated lineages converge on the same biochemical
strategies when confronting the same physical problem. For Chamovitz,
these shared mechanisms undermine the notion that plants are fundamentally
different from animals in perceiving environmental signals. Both kingdoms
*see* — they do so with different toolkits assembled under the same
biophysical constraints.
