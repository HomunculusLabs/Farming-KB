# Neurotropic Fungi in New Guinea

## Overview

New Guinea occupies a singular position in the global ethnomyological landscape as one of
only two documented centers of indigenous ceremonial use of psilocybin-containing mushrooms,
the other being Mexico. This parallel, first brought to scientific attention by R. Singer
(also cited as Reinger in some literature) in 1965, represents one of the most significant
findings in the study of psychoactive fungi and raises profound questions about biogeography,
cultural transmission, and the evolutionary [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]], though many remain understudied phylogenetically.

## Unique Scope of Use: Beyond Psilocybe

A distinctive feature of New Guinean ceremonial mushroom use is that it extends well beyond
the genus *Psilocybe*, which accounts for the vast majority of psychoactive mushroom use
worldwide. Indigenous communities have been documented consuming species from at least three
genera:

- **Psilocybe** (Hymenogastraceae) — Several undescribed or poorly known species,
  typically bluing-staining, found in humus-rich forests and open highland grasslands.
- **Russula** (Russulaceae) — Certain *Russula* species consumed for psychoactive
  properties. The use of Russulaceous fungi as psychoactive agents is essentially unique
  to New Guinea with no parallel in any other ethnomyological tradition.
- **Boletus and allied Boletaceae** — Boletaceous species (pored mushrooms) are reported
  among fungi used ceremonially. This is extraordinary because the Boletaceae are not
  generally associated with psilocybin or similar tryptamine alkaloids. The possible
  presence of psilocybin or novel psychoactive compounds in these Boletaceae remains an
  important open biochemical question.

This broad taxonomic range makes New Guinea the most mycologically diverse center of
indigenous psychoactive mushroom consumption known, suggesting either independent discoveries of psychoactive properties across multiple genera
or a deep, multi-generational tradition of experimentation with fungal resources.

## Species Known from the Region

New Guinea's mycoflora remains significantly underexplored. Among neurotropic species
reported or suspected:

- Several undescribed *Psilocybe* species from highland and lowland forests, some allied
  with Southeast Asian taxa and others showing Australasian affinities.
- *Psilocybe* spp. from subalpine grasslands, possibly representing southern extensions
  of Southeast Asian lineages.
- *Russula* species consumed ceremonially, with chemical constituents poorly characterized.
- Boletaceous species tentatively identified as psychoactive by indigenous informants;
  biochemical verification remains an open research need.

Guzman, Allen, and Gartz (1998) included New Guinea in their worldwide survey
but noted the paucity of collections and the likelihood that many species remain
undocumented.

## Biogeographical Significance

New Guinea sits at the biogeographic crossroads of Australasia and Southeast Asia. E. Horak,
in 1983, drew attention to striking similarities between the agaric floras of temperate
South America and Melanesia, suggesting ancient Gondwanan connections or trans-Pacific
dispersal pathways — observations directly relevant to [[powell-mayan-civilisation-and-psilocybin-mushroom-use]] in Australia despite geographic proximity and shared geological
history. Australia and New Guinea were connected by a land bridge for much of the Pleistocene,
yet the ethnographic record from Australia contains no confirmed reports of ceremonial use by
Aboriginal peoples, despite indigenous Australian species such as *Psilocybe subaeruginosa*
in the southeast. Possible explanations include: (1) knowledge may have been lost or never
developed in Australia for cultural reasons; (2) relevant species may not have been present
during human habitation; (3) the ethnographic record may be incomplete; or (4) cultural
practices may have been specific to New Guinea highland cultures. The Australian-New Guinean
disjunction remains one of the most intriguing unsolved problems in Australasian ethnomyology.

## Cultural Context of Ceremonial Use

Among aboriginal groups of the New Guinea highlands, psychoactive mushrooms were used in
ceremonial contexts involving divination, communication with ancestral spirits, and healing.
Ceremonies varied between groups but generally involved ingestion in a ritual setting
accompanied by chanting, singing, and guidance of a ritual specialist. The integration of
diverse taxa — *Psilocybe*, *Russula*, and Boletaceae — into ceremonial practice points to
a sophisticated ethnomycological knowledge system of considerable depth and antiquity.
