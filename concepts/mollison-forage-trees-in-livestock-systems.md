# Forage Trees in Livestock Systems

Throughout Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Mollison consistently
advocates for the integration of forage trees into livestock systems. He
argues that basic changes to agriculture involve replacing animal forage
grains with tree crops, and that shelterbelt species must be carefully
selected to give multiple uses, either assisting the crop yield or
adding to the end-use yield (e.g. forage trees in pasture).

## The Case for Tree Forage

Mollison frames forage trees as essential for bridging the seasonal feed
gaps that characterise all open grassland systems. The net product of a
grassland in a cool humid climate has a bimodal growth curve with two
deficit periods: the late summer deficit from grass seeding and drought,
and the winter deficit from slow or no growth. For the summer period,
green leaf from forage tree plantations largely overcomes this shortage.

Forage trees provide foliage, food sugars, seed carbohydrates, or
fruits. They can be integrated into grazing systems as belts at 30-100
metre strip spacing, under palms, or between large tree crops. Each
system needs assessment and planning for soil type, location, rainfall,
slope, and main crop requirements.

## Key Forage Tree Species

### Leucaena (Leucaena leucocephala)

One of the most important forage tree legumes. A long-term forage system
evolves when Leucaena is planted, providing replanting or rest periods
are given for seedlings to re-establish. It combines well with elephant
grass (Pennisetum purpureum); the combination can carry 7 milk cows/ha
if cut and hand-fed. Leucaena provides abundant seeds for chickens and
honey for bees, making it a multi-purpose forage crop.

### Tagasaste (Chaemocytisus palmensis)

Tolerant and hardy from tropics to cool areas, widely used in New
Zealand in dry areas for cut forage, as a pioneer, for mulch, and as a
nurse crop. Unirrigated tagasaste produces the equivalent forage weight
of irrigated lucerne. For cut forage, spacing at 1.0 to 1.5 m is ideal
(keeping stems cut at 1 m high), while for seed and bees, spacing at 4-5
m produces better forage.

Tagasaste needs mature leaf to carry into frost periods, so it should
not be winter-lopped. Seed is most cheaply obtained from the soil under
mature trees, which can be thrown on a series of shaker trays to clean
seed from the grass sod and soil.

Matthew Carpenter, on the cold Canterbury Plain of southern New Zealand,
has successfully field-drilled tagasaste for summer sheep forage. One
unforeseen benefit is that grasses between tagasaste strips dry out much
later and produce more growth than grasses in the open field situation.

### Honey Locust (Gleditsia triacanthos)

Selected trees bear heavy loads of pods in dry subtropics and are
frost-hardy. Thornless forms exist. Deep soil moisture is required in
the dry season, but the tree is soil-tolerant and wind-hardy. Best trees
are thornless, high sugar types.

### Kiawe (Prosopis pallida)

A staple pod forage on dry savannah sites in subtropics. Produces dense
wood that is excellent firewood and termite-resistant posts. About 20%
thornless trees on Hawaii. Non-invasive.

### Albizia Species

Used extensively as nurse crops and forage trees. Albizia saman (rain
tree) is a very fast-growing large tree of the tropics and subtropics
with sugary pods; grass grows well beneath it. Wood is valuable and
durable. A. auriculiformis is an important pioneer for exhausted
savannah soils.

### Acacia Species

Many Acacia species provide edible seed (most species) and serve as
browse. Acacia auriculiformis and A. mangium are key pioneer species for
defeating grasses and restoring fertility. Acacia, tagasaste, baobab,
and Prosopis are listed as primary forage tree choices for shelterbelts.

### Samanea saman (Rain Tree)

A very fast-growing large tree of the tropics and subtropics with sugary
pods that serve as stock feed. Grass grows well below the canopy, making
it ideal for silvopastoral systems.

### Other Forage Trees

Mollison lists additional species including Inga, Glyricidia, Casuarina,
mulberry (fruit for poultry), Caragana, boxthorn (Lycium spp.), pigeon
pea, most Solanum species with berries, and oaks (especially white oak
group, cork oak, turkey oak). In the tropics, mulberry, Bunya pine, fig,
Inga edulis, and various palms provide forage for pigs.

## Shelterbelt Forage Design

Mollison emphasises that shelterbelt species must be carefully selected
to give multiple uses, either to assist crop yield or add to end-use
yield. Forage trees in pasture ensure that the area occupied by
shelterbelts adds to total crop yields rather than deducts from them. In
general, nitrogen-fixing and browse-edible shelterbelt species increase
crop and pasture yield, while high water-demand, non-leguminous, and
inedible shelterbelt species decrease it.

## Fodder Trees for Poultry Range

On poultry range, full nutrition can be obtained by providing seed from
abundant trees and shrubs including Coprosma repens, Caragana, tagasaste,
boxthorn (Lycium spp.), pigeon pea, most Solanum species with berries,
mulberry of 5-6 varieties, and normal fruits. Acacias with edible seed,
Leucaena, clovers, grasses, and "weeds" such as cleavers, chicory,
comfrey, Oxalis, and dandelion supply the rest of the diet together with
insects and their larvae.

## Integration with [[mollison-chicken-tractor-and-animal-tractor-systems]]

Forage trees are essential infrastructure for animal tractor systems.
Chickens, Mollison believes, should be a permanent forage system in all
mixed orchards. The combination of forage trees providing year-round
leaf, pod, and seed drop with poultry foraging beneath creates a largely
self-maintaining system that produces eggs, meat, fruit, and improved
soils simultaneously.
