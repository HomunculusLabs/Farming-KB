The nitrogen excreted by protozoa and nematodes is primarily in the

to nitrate (NO3-) depends on soil conditions. In bacterially dominated
soils, where bacterial biofilm maintains a pH above 7, nitrifying
bacteria (Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter) convert ammonium through
nitrite to nitrate. In fungally dominated soils with lower pH, nitrogen
tends to remain in ammonium form.

## The Grazing-Mineralization Cycle

The mineralization cycle operates as a continuous loop driven by
grazing pressure:

- Plants produce exudates (sugars, proteins) through photosynthesis
- Exudates attract bacteria and fungi to the rhizosphere
- Bacteria and fungi multiply, incorporating nutrients into their bodies
- Protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi
- Grazing releases excess nutrients as plant-available mineral waste
- Plants absorb these minerals and produce more exudates
- The cycle repeats

As much as 80% of the nitrogen a plant needs comes from the wastes
produced by bacteria- and fungi-eating protozoa and nematodes. This
remarkable efficiency means that, in a healthy soil food web, plants
are essentially fed by the predators of their microbial partners.

## Nematode Contributions to Mineralization

Nematodes are arguably the most important mineralizers in many garden
soils. They need less nitrogen than protozoa do -- their carbon-to-
nitrogen ratio is higher -- so when they consume bacteria or fungi,
they release proportionally more nitrogen as waste. Key points about
nematode mineralization:

- Bacterivorous nematodes eat bacteria with hollow tube mouthparts,
  consuming thousands of bacteria per hour
- Fungivorous nematodes use stylets to puncture fungal hyphae,
  digesting and releasing fungal nutrients
- Their waste products contain ammonium that is immediately
  available to plant roots
