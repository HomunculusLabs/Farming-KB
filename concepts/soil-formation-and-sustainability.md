# Soil Formation and Sustainability

## Overview

Soil is one of the most complex and dynamic materials on Earth. It defies precise treatment because its structure, permeability, organic content, gaseous components, minerals, pH, and water status change from hour to hour with soil depth and treatment. Understanding how soils form—and how they can be sustained or rehabilitated—is fundamental to [[permaculture-design]].

## The State of Global Soils

Topsoil is being lost at an ever-increasing rate worldwide. In Australia, perhaps only 30% of original soils remain in fair condition. The rest have been washed or blown away, or are sadly depleted in structure and yield. This pattern holds true for most countries where extractive agriculture and forestry occur.

The closer soils are defined, the less likely we are to truly know them. Despite soil services, soil analysis, and the best attempts of people to care for land, the net trend is one of degradation. However, there are sustainable approaches to soil maintenance and rehabilitation.

## Soil Formation Processes

Soils can be **residual** (resting in place over their parent rock) or **transported** by water, ice, gravity, and wind. The key factors in soil formation are:

1. **Rock type**: The parent material determines the mineral composition of the resulting soil
2. **Climate**: Temperature, rainfall, and seasonal patterns drive weathering rates
3. **Topography/landform**: Slope, aspect, and elevation affect water movement and erosion

### The Role of Water in Rock Breakdown

Water plays a central role in decomposing rock into soil:

- **Hydration**: Water combines with common minerals such as felspars in rock, causing them to swell and fracture, then hydrates the felspars to kaolin, clay, and potassium carbonate
- **Carbonic acid formation**: Carbonates released during weathering make soil water a stronger carbonic acid than rain itself
- **Oxidation**: Atmospheric oxygen dissolves in rain to oxidise iron minerals (pyrites), forming haematite and sulphuric acid, which further dissolves metals
- **Ice action**: The expansion of water as ice fractures rock mechanically
- **Glacial grinding**: Glaciers grind rock to flour-like particles

### The Role of Plants

Plants are active agents in soil formation:
- Wedging open rocks and mineral particles through root growth
- Recreating acids at root zones that dissolve mineral particles
- Transporting minerals in their sap to other locations
- Contributing organic matter that becomes humus

### Rate of Soil Formation vs. Loss

Topsoil builds at approximately 2-4 tonnes per hectare per year as uncompacted topsoil. However, we remove soil at a rate of 40-500 tonnes per year in cropping and soil tillage. Even the most ideal tillage barely keeps pace with the most ideal conditions of soil formation.

In the worst cases, 2,000 years of soil can be lost in a single erosion season—one sequence of flood or strong wind over cultivated soils.

## Where Soils Are Conserved or Increased

Soils are only conserved or increased in four situations:

1. **Uncut forest**: The forest canopy and understory protect soil from erosion while root systems hold it in place and leaf litter builds organic matter
2. **Under quiet water**: Lakes and ponds allow sediments to accumulate
3. **Prairies and meadows**: Permanent plant cover with deep root systems
4. **Mulched or non-tillage systems**: Where soil disturbance is minimized and organic matter is maintained

These are the core subjects of sustainable societies. They are notably not the subjects most taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses.

## Values in Soil Management

### Health (Human and Plant)

A conservative approach is essential, especially regarding biocides and high levels of artificial fertilisers. Our ancestors lived to a ripe old age on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilisers. Beyond diet, gardening contributes to health through:

- **Mild but regular exercise**: Gardening as a steady, non-stressing form of physical activity
- **Meditation**: The opportunity to sit, observe, and reduce stress
- **Meaning**: A conscious sense of worthwhile work directly valuable to others
- **Life interest**: Constant change, new observations, and continuous learning

### Yield

There is no doubt that a ploughed and fertilised monoculture supported by chemicals and large energy inputs can out-yield almost every other production system on a once-off basis. But the critical questions are: at what public cost? For how long maintained? With what improvement in nutrition? With what guarantee of sustainability?

Abundant proof now exists that such forced yields are temporary and that plough cultures destroy soils and societies.

### Life in Soil

Soil organisms are a major soil factor with myriad profound effects on pH, mineral content and availability, soil structure, and erosion. The living component of soil is what distinguishes productive soil from inert mineral substrate.

### Ethics

The purpose of people on earth is not to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens. Permaculture soil strategies refer primarily to the 4% of the earth needed for food production, with wider implications for the 60% of agricultural soils that have been ruined by the plough or polluted by emissions.

The largest job is the restoration of soils and forests for the sake of a healthy earth itself—not to clear or ruin any more land, but to put in order what has been destroyed.

## Land Restoration Potential

[[colin-tudge]] estimated that perhaps 60% of the British Isles could be given back to nature. John Jeavons estimated, on the basis of gardens, that we could return perhaps 94% of land to its own purposes. A reduction of the ecological deserts created by agriculture is long overdue.

## Science vs. Practice

Soil science concentrates on classification but not on how to evolve soil. Often it is left to gardeners and farmers—amateurs in the academic sense—to create good soil through water control, modest aeration, and plant and animal management. Farmers and gardeners are frequently the practical, innovative, and successful group, while often ignored by academics.

Science is good at explaining why things work and making skills teachable. It is not so good at initiating field work or training people already in the field to work effectively. The most effective knowledge transfer happens through farmer field days and local educational sessions.
