# Earth Resources — Materials Revealed by Excavation

Whenever earth is moved, it seldom reveals a uniform composition. Most excavation reveals materials already sorted by nature, each of specific use. To mix them up indiscriminately is to set back the clock a few thousand years. The supervising designer or property owner should follow every excavator (animal or technological) and carefully set aside the valuable materials revealed.

## Topsoil

Topsoil is the often dark, root-filled living surface of the earth — the most precious material on any site.

### Handling Protocol

- **Carefully strip off** before any excavation begins
- **Pile aside** separately from subsoil and other materials
- **Later return** over other material onto banks, over paddocks or fields, and even below water in ponds

### Characteristics

- Usually no more than **6-18 cm deep**
- Can be very thin and rare in many landscapes
- Where deeper deposits exist, surplus can be **spread on poorer soils** to improve productivity
- Can be **pocketed** (placed in localized pits) for trees in poor soil areas

Topsoil is the foundation of all terrestrial productivity. Its careful preservation during earthworks is the single most important material management practice.

## Peat

Excavation in marshes, bogs, or lowlands may reveal **1-9 m (3-30 feet)** of semi-compacted fibrous plant material.

### Handling Protocol

- Most can be stockpiled for later use
- Approximately **0.6 m (2 feet)** should be returned to the surface as for topsoil
- The peat stockpile is extremely valuable and versatile

### Uses

- **Soil amendment** — mixing with sands and loams creates additional topsoil
- **Nursery growing medium** — an excellent fine growing medium for plant propagation
- **Building insulation** — peat's insulating properties serve in construction
- **Fuel** — only in desperation, as those who burn peat are "near the base of life on earth; the next step is into barrens and rocks"

### Ecological Importance

Peatlands throughout the world now urgently need preservation as threatened habitats of unique vegetation. Peat preserves timber, animals, and unexpected treasure-troves such as hoards of acorns and firkins of beech butter from the forests that preceded the bogs.

### Archaeological Value

A whole archaeology may lie in peat deposits:

- **Pollen records** reveal past climate and vegetation history
- **Preserved artifacts** — tools, bridges, butter, and evidence of forest life
- **Buried trees** provide growth rings telling stories of older climate, air quality, and dating their own burial
- At the base of Irish bogs, the Fir Bolg (the "little people") and their axes, bridges, butter, and forest life are well preserved

## Clay

Good clay is very useful stuff, with deposits potentially extending **0.3-6 m (1-20 feet)** deep.

### Handling Protocol

- **Stockpile** separately, preferably covered with plastic sheets to prevent drying and cracking

### Uses

- **Brick making** — both dried (adobe) and fired brick
- **Dam and pond sealing** — clay's impermeability makes it ideal for water retention
- **Pottery** — pots shaped and fired for containers and cooking vessels
- **Specialty applications:**
  - Some clays make good cricket pitches
  - Other types make fine porcelain
  - Special clays serve as filters and insulators

### Clay Composition

- **Fireclay**: 58-75% silica, 25-36% alumina, 0.25-2% iron oxide
- **Brick clay**: ~95% silica, no alumina, <2% lime

## Sand

Clean sand has multiple specialized uses depending on its color and composition.

### Types and Uses

| Sand Type | Characteristics | Uses |
|--------|----------------|------|
| **White sand** | Reflects light like snow | Reflects heat to houses and walls |
| **Fine black sand** | Dark, heavy | Casting metals; black-sand solar collectors |
| **Yellowish sand** | Contains calcium | Counters [[soil-ph|soil]] acidity |
| **Fat sand** | Contains some clay (<20%) | Makes ovens and mortar |
| **Sharp sand** | Mostly silica | Makes glass and cement |
| **Sieved sand** | Specific particle sizes | Grit or polish in mortars and grinders |

### General Uses

- **Clean sand** — grinding powder or silica chips
- **Fat sand** — oven construction and mortar
- **Sharp sand** — glass and cement manufacture
- **Sand mixed with peat** — one of the best nursery growing media for trees and other plants
- **Sieved sand** — specialized grit sizes for mortars and grinders

## Gravels

Gravels are among the most versatile earth materials.

### Uses

- **Heaped up** — good roads, drains, and soft sun-bathing patches (better than lawns, with no green stains)
- **Angular gravel** — good concrete aggregate
- **Smooth gravel** — good water filters or enzyme columns
- **[[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs|Tree]] [[mulching-guide|mulch]]** — as are cinder and crushed pumice
- **Heat storage** — 19 mm-size angular gravels are the best for heat stores, where air from solar attics or glasshouses is blown into a heat store wall or tank filled with such gravels
- **Underfloor heating** — heat stoves placed under floors to heat gravel beds

## Shingle

Shingle (coarser than gravel) serves specific functions:

- **Under gravels on roads** — provides drainage base
- **Drains** — coarse drainage medium
- **Coarse filters** — for water treatment
- **Desert water condensation** — excellent mulch for condensing water in deserts or dry places
- **Wildlife habitat** — refuge for snails and decomposers
- **[[natural-swimming-pools]]** — filter bed for pools au naturel

## Slate

Any rock that can be split can be utilized extensively:

- **Floors** — durable, attractive flooring
- **Tablets** — writing surfaces and signage
- **Billiard tables** — precision flat surfaces
- **Roofs** — roofing material
- **Walls and houses** — building stone
- **Garden paths** — paving
- **Floor tiles** — interior finishing

## Boulders

Boulders serve multiple practical functions:

- **Coarse mulch** — around trees and plantings
- **Wildlife refuge** — habitat for reptiles, insects, and small mammals
- **Walling and [[windbreak-design-and-configuration|windbreak]] material** — gains and radiates heat
- **Tools** — some boulders serve as pounders, others as excellent mortar and pestles
- **Weights** — anchors and ballast in boats
- **Thermal mass** — large boulders absorb heat during the day and release it at night

## The Excavator as Source of Information

A bulldozer or excavator is a source of information — the making of a [[water-wise-vegetable-gardening|pond]] is a rare chance to explore, explain, and store useful information and material relating to the site. On a complex site (old volcano, shoreline, swamp, or desert pediment), excavation can:

- **Confirm or deny theories** on formation, geology, and geological history
- **Build predictive capacity** — experience on many sites gives confidence in finding the right sites for ponds, excavated houses, silos, or silage pits
- **Discover earth resources** — materials carefully set aside during excavation provide long-term value

Excavation can pay for itself on any one of the factors listed, and the client can benefit long after the water storage is built. It would be uncommon for excavation NOT to pay for itself through the materials discovered.

### Critical Advice

> If you are wise, you never leave the site when earth is being moved: it is very expensive to bring machines back for fine touches, to adjust the work, or to sort out mixed materials.

## Tracks and Signs

Newly moved earth and puddled clays reveal a section of earth life, making imprints of secretive and nocturnal birds and mammals — from beetles to elephants, mice to plovers. After rain is a good time to observe footprints, scrapes, and tracks on any site where earth was freshly moved.

### Value of Track Observation

- **Species inventory** — a list of species obtained this way adds to basic information resources
- **Early warning** — may warn of troubles from ground foragers
- **Cryptic animal detection** — many cryptic animals avoid traps but can be detected by their tracks
- **Underground analysis** — tunnels, nests, and food stores of mice, gophers, and larger mammals are revealed, along with root penetration and spread patterns

The earth hides a great variety of life forms, from secretive larvae and worm-like legless lizards to redolent fungi revealed only by their garlic-like scent or fruiting bodies.

## Shards and Fossils

Under the earth, almost everywhere, lie the mute stories of prior life. These have more lessons for us than do futurologists, who after all can only look forward by looking back first.

### What Excavation Reveals

- **Shell middens and tool fragments** — camps of ancestors revealed by shells, tools, shards, ashes, and bones (datable)
- **Buried trees** — growth rings tell stories of older climate, air quality, and accurately date their own burial
- **Pottery shards** — may encode astronomical or technological data
- **Bones** — reveal past diseases and wars

The archaeological record preserved in earth is irreplaceable. As civilizations deposit their remains — the Ganges River bears its children's bodies, clothes, adornments, and possessions to the sea — future people may want to know about us, how we lived and what we valued. Every excavation is simultaneously an act of creation and an act of archaeological responsibility.
