# Fukuoka European Travels and Natural Farming Spread

Fukuoka's travels through Europe in the 1980s, documented extensively in [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]], reveal both the growing international interest in natural farming and Fukuoka's observations about European agriculture, culture, and the contrast between Western and [[fukuoka-western-vs-japanese-attitudes]].

## The Yakuso Farm in Holland

The most detailed European case study is Thomas's farm in Holland, which Fukuoka calls "Yakuso" (the Dutch pronunciation of "hyakusho," the Japanese word for farmer):

### Farm Layout
- **75 acres total**, divided into three equal thirds
- One third: enormous apple and pear trees
- One third: wheat and naked barley fields in clover cover
- One third: Japanese vegetables (daikon, Chinese cabbage, sweet potato, squash, cucumber)
- Thomas had "succeeded in growing the wheat and barley in a cover of clover"

### Farm Life
- Over a dozen hardworking young helpers
- Japanese futons being made and sold in Europe
- Natural bread from farm-ground wheat baked at a traditional windmill -- "a commercial success"
- Plans for miso and tofu production
- Barn converted to sleeping quarters and meeting hall for natural farming students

### Teaching
- Fukuoka gave three days of morning lectures to two hundred people from across Europe
- Afternoon practical instruction sessions
- Thomas called Fukuoka "the father of European nature"

### Fukuoka's Parting Words
"Even without me, you have Mother Earth. If you obey the will of the Virgin Mary who gives birth to all things, then even without me, you will without fail become a center of natural farming in Europe."

## The Dutch Windmill

Fukuoka's visit to a traditional windmill where Thomas's wheat was milled provides a powerful metaphor for the conflict between traditional and modern:

- The old miller, operating a four-story brick structure with enormous wooden gearwheels, declared: "You can't make real flour except with a windmill"
- "True flour can't be made except in stone mortars because these do not generate heat"
- The miller lamented that "there just aren't any strong oaks like this one left anymore" and "no craftsman today could ever get just the right angles on these gearwheels"
- Computerized mills produce "pure white" flour but "that flour is not real flour"
- Becoming a top-rate miller requires "the training and experience of three successive generations"

## Observations Across Europe

### Switzerland
- Fukuoka began his European tour here
- Farmers practiced careful stewardship of mountainous terrain
- The contrast between Swiss precision and natural farming's apparent disorder

### Italy
- Natural farming "took root" in Italy (chapter title: "Natural Farming Takes Root in Italy")
- Milan-area brown rice grown by natural farming was "especially prized"
- Italian farmers showed strong interest in natural methods

### Austria
- Lectures in Vienna
- Interest in natural diet and farming methods

### Holland
- The most developed European natural farming community
- Dutch government research on vegetation for coastal protection -- Fukuoka observed that wild vines and shrubs on sandy beaches were healthier than transplanted saplings
- Below-sea-level geography requiring careful water management

### Germany
- Natural bread from Holland was being shipped to West Germany
- Interest in Eastern philosophy and natural living

## Fukuoka's Analysis of European Agriculture

### Scale and Specialization
- European farms were large and specialized compared to Japanese small-scale mixed farming
- The emphasis on single crops contrasted with Fukuoka's polycultural approach
- Heavy machinery and chemical inputs were standard

### Dying Traditions
- Windmills, traditional crafts, and diversified farming were all declining
- "The Dutch windmill will probably soon vanish"
- The loss of traditional food processing (stone-ground flour, natural bread)

### Hope in the Young
- Young people across Europe were drawn to natural farming
- Communal living experiments integrating natural agriculture
- Willingness to adopt simple lifestyles and vegetarian diets

## [[fukuoka-western-vs-japanese-attitudes]]

Fukuoka observed the paradox that while Japan was rapidly Westernizing its agriculture and diet, Europeans were adopting Japanese food and farming practices:

- Europeans followed natural diet with total commitment once decided
- Japanese drifted "from right to left and back again" without commitment
- Europeans arrived at Zen temples "as tabulae rasae" (blank slates), while Japanese claimed to understand Zen after minimal meditation
- The "simplicity of heart" of Westerners pursuing Eastern practices, "knowing full well that what they are doing is little more than mere imitation," impressed Fukuoka deeply
