# Centrifugal and Centripetal Directions of Natural and Scientific Farming

## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka describes the fundamental divergence between [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] and
scientific farming as a movement in opposite directions within the same dimension.
Natural farming proceeds centripetally inward toward a state of nothingness (Mu) by
the elimination of human labor; it compresses and freezes time and space. Modern
scientific farming proceeds centrifugally outward toward somethingness, expanding and
developing as it goes. Although the two may appear identical at a given point, they
move in opposite directions — the one headed for zero and the other for infinity.

## The Intertwining Spiral

The relationship between natural farming and scientific farming can be depicted as
an intertwining spiral. Narrowly defined natural farming, which includes organic
farming, proceeds inward. Scientific farming seeks to appropriate time and space
through complex and diverse means, proceeding outward. Both exist in a relative
relationship in the same dimension or plane.

Seen relatively and discriminatively, the two readily appear to be in opposition, yet
they are intimately intertwined — neither approaching nor moving away from one
another, advancing together and complementarily through time. However, because
natural farming condenses inward, seeking ultimately a return to the true world of
nature that transcends the world of relativity, it is in irreconcilable conflict with
scientific farming, which expands forever in the relative world.

## Sericulture as Illustration

Fukuoka uses the example of sericulture (silk production) to illustrate this
centrifugal-centripetal dynamic. From natural farming, silk production moved to
scientific farming, then appeared to move a step back in the direction of natural
farming. However, once scientific farming begins to get under way, it does not
regress or turn back but rushes madly onward along a path that takes it away from
nature.

Modern sericulture has reached an age in which natural silk is something produced in
factories. Silkworms are raised in plants fully outfitted with heating and air
conditioning equipment; lighting and ventilation are adjusted automatically. Feed
is carried in and droppings carried out on a belt conveyor. The room can be
hermetically sealed and disinfected with gas. With all feeding and cocoon collection
operations fully automated, the starting material — mulberry leaves — will probably
be replaced by a totally synthetic feed prepared from petrochemicals.

## The Mahayana and Hinayana Ways

Fukuoka extends this directional metaphor to Buddhist philosophy, drawing a parallel
between the two major branches of Buddhism and the two directions of farming. The
Hinayana way, like scientific farming, proceeds outward through accumulation of
knowledge and technique. The Mahayana way, like natural farming, seeks to return
inward to the source.

In the same way that Hinayana Buddhism accumulates precepts and rules, scientific
farming accumulates data, techniques, and interventions. Mahayana Buddhism seeks
direct awakening by transcending all rules, just as natural farming seeks to work
with nature by abandoning all human knowledge and intervention. Both approaches
address the same reality but from fundamentally different directions.
