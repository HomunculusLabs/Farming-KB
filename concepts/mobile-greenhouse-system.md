# Mobile Greenhouse System

## Overview

The mobile greenhouse system is an innovative approach to season extension that
solves several persistent problems in [[coleman-protected-cultivation]]. Rather than
keeping crops in a fixed structure year-round, the greenhouse itself is moved
between growing plots on a rotational schedule. Adapted and popularized by
Eliot Coleman, this concept allows the same structure to serve different
functions in different seasons — covering summer crops during warm months and
winter crops during cold months — while providing the uncovered land with a
full year of exposure to natural weather cycles. The result improves soil
health, breaks [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] cycles, reduces operating costs, and maximizes
the productive utility of every square foot of growing space.

## Historical Origins

The concept of moving greenhouse structures is not new. The earliest recorded
mobile greenhouse was built in 1898 in England, where glasshouses were mounted
on railroad wheels and rolled along tracks to follow seasonal needs. These
Victorian-era movable structures solved the problem of soil exhaustion in
permanent glasshouse culture. By moving the glasshouse off a plot after a
cropping cycle, the soil beneath was exposed to cleansing rain, beneficial
frost, and natural biological processes that restored fertility. While the
railroad-wheel greenhouses of 19th-century England were expensive and complex,
they established the principle that the greenhouse should serve the soil.

## Coleman's Adaptation

Eliot Coleman adapted the mobile greenhouse concept for modern small-scale
market farming, scaling it to his standard 30-foot-wide by 96-foot-long
gothic-style hoop houses. Rather than railroad wheels, Coleman's greenhouses
sit on wooden or steel skids that allow the entire structure to be dragged
from one site to an adjacent one. The two sites are prepared side by side,
each measuring the same footprint as the greenhouse, with a smooth, level
surface that permits the structure to slide without catching. The moving
process typically requires a tractor or draft animals. A properly designed
mobile hoop house can be relocated between adjacent sites in a matter of
hours, making it practical for a small farm crew.

## The Two-Site Rotation System

The core of Coleman's system is a two-site rotation aligned with the seasons:

### Phase 1: Winter Position

In late summer and early fall, cold-hardy winter vegetables are planted
directly in open ground on Site A — outdoors, with no greenhouse cover. The
crops grow through autumn in open air, reaching near-mature size by the time
cold weather arrives. Meanwhile, the mobile greenhouse sits on Site B,
covering warm-season crops such as tomatoes, peppers, or basil that were
planted in spring and are maturing through summer.

### Phase 2: The Move

In late October or early November, the warm-season crops on Site B have been
harvested and removed. The greenhouse is moved from Site B to Site A, where
winter crops are already established. Once the greenhouse covers Site A, the
double-layer protection system (inner row cover plus greenhouse) is deployed,
and the winter harvest season begins. Site B is now exposed to the elements.

### Phase 3: Summer Return

In spring, the greenhouse remains on Site A as winter crops finish. Summer
crops are started on Site B, which has benefited from a full winter of rain,
snow, frost, and sun exposure. In late spring or early summer, the greenhouse
is moved back to Site B to cover the emerging summer crops, and the cycle
begins again with new winter vegetables planted on the now-exposed Site A.

## Advantages of the Mobile System

### Avoiding Cooling Costs in August

A stationary greenhouse becomes intolerably hot in August, requiring extensive
ventilation or active cooling to prevent heat stress. By moving the greenhouse
off the summer crop site and onto the winter crop site at the appropriate
time, each site receives the greenhouse only when its moderating effect is
beneficial — not when it is detrimental.

### Breaking Pest and Disease Cycles

Many common greenhouse [[mushroom-pests-and-diseases]] depend on continuous cultivation of
host plants in a protected environment. The mobile system interrupts these
cycles by removing the protected environment from any given plot for a full
year. Soil-borne pathogens, overwintering insect eggs, and persistent weed
seeds are exposed to lethal winter cold, desiccating wind, and biological
predation that cannot occur under greenhouse shelter. This natural sanitation
reduces or eliminates the need for chemical controls.

### Soil Restoration Through Exposure

When soil is continuously covered, it is deprived of direct rainfall,
atmospheric [[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]], freeze-thaw action that breaks up compaction,
and ultraviolet radiation that helps sterilize the surface. The mobile system
ensures each plot receives a full year of open-air exposure, allowing these
restorative processes to occur naturally. Rain leaches accumulated salts,
frost fractures compacted layers, and sun kills surface pathogens and seeds.

### Long-Term Green Manure Crops

The uncovered period provides an extended window for growing green manure
crops — soil-building cover crops that fix nitrogen, add organic matter, and
[[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]]. Because the plot is uncovered for approximately 10 to
13 months, there is ample time to establish and incorporate a substantial
green manure crop. Leguminous cover crops such as clover, vetch, and field
peas fix atmospheric nitrogen and contribute it to the soil when incorporated,
eliminating the need for imported nitrogen fertilizers.

## Practical Considerations

- **Site preparation**: Both sites must be level, well-drained, and free of
  obstacles. A smooth transition zone between sites is essential.
- **Structural integrity**: The greenhouse must withstand the stresses of
  being dragged. Reinforced baseboards, secure skid attachments, and sturdy
  end-wall construction are critical.
- **Moving frequency**: The greenhouse is moved only twice per year, keeping
  labor manageable and minimizing structural wear.
- **Irrigation**: Uncovered plots receive natural rainfall, reducing
  irrigation needs. The covered plot requires dedicated irrigation.
- **Crop timing**: Success depends on precise scheduling of planting dates to
  ensure crops are at the right growth stage when the greenhouse arrives or
  departs.
