# Mobile Greenhouse System

## Overview

The mobile greenhouse system is an innovative approach to [[cervantes-season-extension-cloches-row-covers]] that
solves several persistent problems in [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] cycles, reduces operating costs, and maximizes
the productive utility of every square foot of growing space.

## Historical Origins

The concept of moving greenhouse structures is not new. The earliest recorded
mobile greenhouse was built in 1898 in England, where glasshouses were mounted
on railroad wheels and rolled along tracks to follow seasonal needs. These
Victorian-era movable structures solved the problem of soil exhaustion in
permanent glasshouse culture. By moving the glasshouse off a plot after a
cropping cycle, the soil beneath was exposed to cleansing rain, beneficial
frost, and natural biological processes that restored fertility. While the
railroad-wheel greenhouses of 19th-century England were expensive and complex,
they established the principle that the greenhouse should serve the soil.

## Coleman's Adaptation

Eliot Coleman adapted the mobile greenhouse concept for modern small-scale
market farming, scaling it to his standard 30-foot-wide by 96-foot-long
gothic-style hoop houses. Rather than railroad wheels, Coleman's greenhouses
sit on wooden or steel skids that allow the entire structure to be dragged
from one site to an adjacent one. The two sites are prepared side by side,
each measuring the same footprint as the greenhouse, with a smooth, level
surface that permits the structure to slide without catching. The moving
process typically requires a tractor or draft animals. A properly designed
mobile hoop house can be relocated between adjacent sites in a matter of
hours, making it practical for a small farm crew.

## The Two-Site Rotation System

The core of Coleman's system is a two-site rotation aligned with the seasons:

### Phase 1: Winter Position

In late summer and early fall, cold-hardy [[coleman-cold-hardy-winter-vegetables]] are planted
directly in open ground on Site A — outdoors, with no greenhouse cover. The
crops grow through autumn in open air, reaching near-mature size by the time
cold weather arrives. Meanwhile, the mobile greenhouse sits on Site B,
covering warm-season crops such as tomatoes, peppers, or basil that were
planted in spring and are maturing through summer.

### Phase 2: The Move

In late October or early November, the warm-season crops on Site B have been
harvested and removed. The greenhouse is moved from Site B to Site A, where
winter crops are already established. Once the greenhouse covers Site A, the
double-layer protection system (inner row cover plus greenhouse) is deployed,
and the winter harvest season begins. Site B is now exposed to the elements.

### Phase 3: Summer Return

In spring, the greenhouse remains on Site A as winter crops finish. Summer
crops are started on Site B, which has benefited from a full winter of rain,
snow, frost, and sun exposure. In late spring or early summer, the greenhouse
is moved back to Site B to cover the emerging summer crops, and the cycle
begins again with new winter vegetables planted on the now-exposed Site A.

## Advantages of the Mobile System

### Avoiding Cooling Costs in August

A stationary greenhouse becomes intolerably hot in August, requiring extensive
ventilation or active cooling to prevent heat stress. By moving the greenhouse
off the summer crop site and onto the winter crop site at the appropriate
time, each site receives the greenhouse only when its moderating effect is
beneficial — not when it is detrimental.
