# Mycoremediation of Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs)

PAHs are a group of over 100 different organic compounds containing two or more fused benzene rings. They are among the most widespread and hazardous environmental contaminants, produced by incomplete combustion of organic matter (fossil fuels, wood, tobacco) and found at creosote sites, gasworks, and former industrial facilities. See [[mycoremediation-basics]] for the general framework.

## What Are PAHs?

PAHs range from simple 2-ring compounds (naphthalene) to complex 5+ ring structures (benzo[a]pyrene). Key characteristics:

- **Hydrophobic** — low water solubility, high lipid solubility, bioaccumulate in organisms
- **Persistent** — resistant to chemical and biological degradation, especially high-molecular-weight PAHs
- **Toxic and carcinogenic** — many are known or suspected human carcinogens (EPA lists 16 as priority pollutants)
- **Ubiquitous** — found in air, water, soil, and sediment worldwide

### EPA Priority PAHs

## Fungal Degradation Pathways

Fungi degrade PAHs through two main metabolic routes:

### 1. Ligninolytic Enzyme System (Extracellular)

White-rot fungi secrete nonspecific oxidative enzymes that attack PAHs:

- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)** — one-electron oxidation of aromatic rings, producing aryl cation radicals that react with water to form hydroxylated products
- **[[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] (MnP)** — generates Mn(III)-organic acid chelates that act as diffusible oxidants; degrades PAHs to quinones and ring-fission products
- **Laccase** — oxidizes phenolic PAH metabolites; can be enhanced with mediators (ABTS, HBT) to attack non-phenolic PAHs
- **Key advantage**: These enzymes are extracellular and nonspecific — they do not require the PAH to enter the cell

### 2. Cytochrome P450 Monooxygenase System (Intracellular)

Many non-ligninolytic fungi metabolize PAHs via cytochrome P450:

- Initial epoxidation of the aromatic ring
- Epoxide hydrolase converts epoxides to trans-dihydrodiols
- Further oxidation to phenols, quinones, and conjugated metabolites
- Similar to mammalian liver metabolism (which is why PAHs are carcinogenic)
- Key species: [[staycare-cunninghamella-elegans-in-bioremediation]], Penicillium spp., Fusarium spp., Aspergillus spp.

## Key Species and Their PAH Capabilities

### Cunninghamella elegans (Zygomycete)
- One of the most studied PAH-degrading fungi
- Degrades phenanthrene, anthracene, fluoranthene, pyrene, benzo[a]pyrene, and chrysene
- Cytochrome P450 pathway: epoxidation to dihydrodiols, then conjugation (sulfate, glucoside)
- Metabolizes benzo[a]pyrene to 7,8-dihydrodiol (precursor to the ultimate carcinogen)
- Produces sulfate conjugates of hydroxylated metabolites

### Penicillium spp.
- P. janthinellum SFU403: metabolizes pyrene via P450 to 1-pyrenol, 1,8-pyrenequinone, and pyrenesemiquinone
- P. simplicissimum: degrades polyethylene (plastic) in addition to PAHs
- P. terrestre > P. simplicissimum > P. funiculosum > P. janthinellum in pyrene oxidation capacity
- P. ochrochloron: produces mycelial pellets in presence of pyrene
- Among 41 micromycete isolates tested, Penicillium strains from the subgenus Furcatum were most common in highly contaminated soils

### White-Rot Basidiomycetes
- **[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]]**: Model organism; mineralizes PAHs under nitrogen-limited conditions
- **[[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]**: Degrades phenanthrene, anthracene, pyrene; high laccase production
- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Oyster mushroom; used in oil spill remediation; degrades 3-4 ring PAHs
- **Bjerkandera adusta**: Produces manganese peroxidase; effective against fluoranthene
- **Irpex lacteus**: Removed nearly 40% pyrene after 2 weeks; produces lignin and manganese peroxidases
- **Crinipellis stipitaria**: Oxidizes pyrene to quinones and hydroxylated derivatives; mineralizes [14C]pyrene to 14CO2

### Yeasts
- **Rhodotorula glutinis**: Utilizes pyrene as sole carbon source
- **Rhodotorula minuta**: 35% consumption of pyrene at 40 mg/L; high 14CO2 evolution
- **Candida maltosa**: Degrades alkanes and PAHs; studied for petroleum bioremediation

## Metabolism of Specific PAHs

### Naphthalene (2-ring)
- Metabolized by Fusarium, Penicillium, Aspergillus, and Cunninghamella species
- Initial oxidation to 1,2-dihydrodiol via P450
- Further oxidation to salicylaldehyde, salicylic acid, and catechol
- Ring cleavage of catechol by catechol dioxygenase

### Anthracene (3-ring)
- Degraded by P. chrysosporium, T. versicolor, and several Penicillium species
- Initial attack at 1,2- and 3,4- positions
- Produces anthraquinone as a major metabolite
- Can be used as sole carbon and energy source by some fungi

### Phenanthrene (3-ring)
- Well-studied model compound for 3-ring PAH degradation
- C. elegans metabolizes to trans-1,2-dihydrodiol, 3,4-dihydrodiol, and 1-hydroxy-2-naphthoic acid
- P. chrysosporium mineralizes phenanthrene to CO2 under nitrogen limitation
- More readily degraded than higher-ring PAHs due to greater water solubility

### Pyrene (4-ring)
- Used as an indicator for monitoring PAH-contaminated wastes
- Not genotoxic itself, but quinone intermediates (1,8-pyrenequinone) are mutagenic
- P. janthinellum: 5 metabolites account for 20% of metabolism; optimized conditions achieve ~100% biotransformation
- Pyrenequinones bind irreversibly to cells via semiquinone radical intermediates
- Zygomycetes (especially Mucor racemosus) found to be among most efficient PAH degraders
- Basidiomycetes (Crinipellis, Marasmius, Marasmiellus) also oxidize pyrene

### Benzo[a]pyrene (5-ring)
- Potent human carcinogen (IARC Group 1)
- C. elegans metabolizes via P450 to 7,8-dihydrodiol-9,10-epoxide (the ultimate carcinogenic metabolite)
- Also produces 3-hydroxybenzo[a]pyrene, 1,6-quinone, 3,6-quinone, and 6,12-quinone metabolites
- White-rot fungi degrade it via ligninolytic enzymes rather than P450
- Degradation rate decreases with increasing molecular weight

### Fluoranthene (4-ring)
- Metabolized by C. elegans to five metabolites after 120 hours
- White-rot fungi degrade via MnP and laccase systems
- Classified as a possible human carcinogen (IARC Group 2B)

### Chrysene (4-ring)
- Weak carcinogen, genotoxic
- C. elegans produces sulfate conjugates of 2,8-dihydroxychrysene and 2-hydroxychrysene (33% of total metabolism)
- Penicillium janthinellum and Syncephalastrum racemosum also metabolize chrysene
- Produces trans-1,2-dihydroxy-1,2-dihydrochrysene as a minor metabolite

## Fungal-Bacterial Co-Cultures

Synergistic degradation occurs when fungi and bacteria work together:

- Fungi break down high-molecular-weight PAHs via [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]], producing more soluble metabolites
- Bacteria then mineralize these fungal metabolites to CO2
- Co-cultures of P. chrysosporium with Pseudomonas showed enhanced mineralization rates
- Fungal mycelium also provides surface area for bacterial biofilm formation

## Factors Affecting PAH Degradation

- **Molecular weight**: Lower MW PAHs (2-3 rings) degrade faster than higher MW (4-5 rings)
- **Bioavailability**: PAHs adsorb to soil organic matter; bioavailability decreases with aging
- **Nutrient limitation**: White-rot fungi produce ligninolytic enzymes under nitrogen limitation (C:N ratio > 40)
- **Oxygen**: Aerobic conditions required; ligninolytic enzymes need H2O2
- **Surfactants**: Addition of surfactants (Tween 80) can increase PAH bioavailability
