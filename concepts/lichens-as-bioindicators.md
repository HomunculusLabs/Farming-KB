## [[gadd-lichens-water-relations-symbiosis|Lichens]] as Bioindicators of Atmospheric Pollution

The use of lichens as biological monitors dates back to the mid-nineteenth century, when European naturalists first noticed that certain lichen species disappeared from urban areas while others persisted. This early observation laid the groundwork for systematic lichen biomonitoring, which has since been adopted worldwide as a standard component of environmental assessment programs.

Lichens are among the most sensitive and widely used [[biological-indicators-of-soil]] air quality. Because they lack roots and absorb water and nutrients directly from the atmosphere through their entire surface area, they are exquisitely sensitive to atmospheric pollutants — particularly sulfur dioxide (SO₂), [[acid-rain]], ozone, and heavy metals. The distribution, species composition, and health of lichen communities provide a living record of air pollution history and current conditions.

Across Europe, North America, and Asia, lichen surveys have been used to map SO₂ deposition patterns, identify areas of heavy metal contamination, and track the effectiveness of emission control regulations. National and international agencies, including the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE), have incorporated lichen-based methods into their air quality monitoring protocols.

## Why Lichens Make Effective Bioindicators

Lichens are symbiotic organisms composed of a fungal partner (mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (photobiont), typically a green alga or cyanobacterium. Several characteristics make them ideal bioindicators:

- **No protective cuticle or stomata** — they absorb everything the atmosphere delivers, including pollutants dissolved in rain and dew.
- **Slow growth rates** — pollutant effects accumulate over long periods, making them integrative monitors rather than snapshot samplers.
- **No roots** — they depend entirely on atmospheric deposition for mineral nutrients, so terrestrial contamination sources do not confound readings.
- **Wide geographic distribution and taxonomic diversity** — different species exhibit a spectrum of tolerances, allowing gradient analysis.
- **Lack of excretory mechanisms** — absorbed pollutants tend to accumulate rather than being shed, producing measurable concentration gradients.

The combination of these traits means that a single lichen thallus acts as a passive, long-term air sampler. Where instrumental monitors measure conditions at a point in time, lichens record cumulative exposure over months to years. This temporal integration is especially valuable for detecting chronic, low-level pollution that may escape detection by intermittent monitoring programs.

In practical terms, a researcher collecting lichen samples from a forest can reconstruct not only current pollution levels but also recent pollution history. The outer layers of a slow-growing lichen thallus reflect recent exposure, while inner layers preserve a record of older conditions. This natural archiving capability has been compared to tree-ring analysis, though lichen growth rates are typically even slower — often less than 1 mm per year for crustose species.

The cost-effectiveness of lichen biomonitoring is another significant advantage. A comprehensive lichen survey requires only basic field equipment and taxonomic expertise, in contrast to the substantial capital and operational costs of instrumental air quality monitoring stations. This makes lichens particularly valuable for baseline assessments in [[low-cost-mushroom-growing-house-construction-developing-countries]] or remote regions where monitoring infrastructure is limited.

## Sensitivity to Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)

Sulfur dioxide from fossil fuel combustion has historically been the most damaging atmospheric pollutant for lichen communities. The spatial distribution of lichen species across landscapes shows clear correlations with areas of high industrialization and elevated SO₂ levels. De Wit (1976) demonstrated strong statistical correlations between air pollutant loading and epiphytic lichen community classifications, establishing lichens as quantitative tools for pollution mapping.

The SO₂ sensitivity of lichens operates through several physiological mechanisms. Sulfur dioxide dissolves in the moist lichen surface to form sulfite (SO₃²⁻) and bisulfite (HSO₃⁻) ions, which are directly toxic to both the fungal and algal partners. Sulfite disrupts chlorophyll structure, inhibits photosynthetic electron transport, and damages cell membranes. At the ecosystem level, SO₂ deposition also acidifies bark surfaces, altering substrate chemistry and indirectly affecting epiphytic communities. The cumulative effect of these direct and indirect toxicity mechanisms is a progressive simplification of lichen communities, with sensitive species eliminated first and only the most tolerant persisting in heavily polluted areas.

Understanding these physiological mechanisms helps explain why lichen community response is not simply proportional to SO₂ concentration. Factors such as moisture availability (which controls dissolution of gaseous SO₂), temperature (which affects metabolic rates and thus vulnerability), and the presence of other co-occurring pollutants all modulate the relationship between ambient SO₂ levels and observed lichen damage.

### Differential SO₂ Tolerance Among Species

Different lichen species tolerate markedly different concentrations of atmospheric SO₂, forming a sensitivity gradient that can be used as a biological scale:

| Species | SO₂ Tolerance (mg/m³) |
|---|---|
| *[[lecanora-conizaeoides]]* | 150 |
| *Parmelia caperata* | 40 |
| *Usnea* spp. | 30 |
| *Ramalina fastigiata* | 10 |

*Ramalina fastigiata* is among the most sensitive, declining at concentrations as low as 10 mg/m³, while *Lecanora conizaeoides* can survive in environments with SO₂ concentrations up to 150 mg/m³ — fifteen times higher. This dramatic range allows researchers to infer ambient SO₂ levels from which species are present or absent in a given area.

The species at the sensitive end of this spectrum serve as early warning indicators. When *Ramalina fastigiata* disappears from a forest, it signals that SO₂ concentrations have reached levels that may also begin to affect vascular plant health and soil chemistry. Conversely, the presence of highly tolerant species like *Lecanora conizaeoides* in the absence of sensitive species is itself an indicator of degraded air quality.

### The Paradox of *Lecanora conizaeoides*

*Lecanora conizaeoides* occupies a unique ecological niche as a pollution-tolerant species that actually requires elevated SO₂ to outcompete more sensitive species. Historically abundant in polluted urban and industrial areas across Europe, it is now found only in remaining sites with above-ambient SO₂ pollution. As air quality has improved in many regions, *L. conizaeoides* populations have declined — a counterintuitive but ecologically significant signal of successful pollution control.

The decline of *L. conizaeoides* as air quality improves is a positive environmental indicator, but it also illustrates the competitive release that occurs when pollution-tolerant species lose their advantage. In recovering landscapes, *L. conizaeoides* is gradually replaced by a more diverse suite of species, including previously extirpated sensitive taxa. Monitoring this species replacement sequence provides insight into the pace and trajectory of ecosystem recovery.

## Substrate Effects on Pollution Sensitivity

The sensitivity of epiphytic lichens to acidifying pollutants is not determined solely by atmospheric chemistry. The substrate on which a lichen grows significantly modifies its exposure and tolerance. Bark chemistry, moisture retention, buffering capacity, and leaching rates all influence the pollutant dose a lichen actually receives.

Key substrate-mediated sensitivity differences include:

- ***[[evernia-prunastri]]*** and ***Parmelia physodes*** are less sensitive when growing on oak bark compared to poplar or elm. Oak bark has higher buffering capacity and lower bark pH variability, which moderates the acidifying effects of atmospheric pollutants.
- ***Physcia tenella*** shows its lowest sensitivity when growing on elm, suggesting elm bark provides some protective quality — possibly related to bark texture, moisture dynamics, or chemical composition that reduces effective pollutant exposure.

The practical implication of substrate effects is that biomonitoring protocols must be standardized with respect to substrate. Most formal lichen biomonitoring programs specify a preferred substrate tree species (often oak or ash in European studies) and exclude samples from other trees unless substrate-specific calibration data are available. Without such controls, apparent differences in lichen diversity between sites may reflect differences in tree species composition rather than differences in air quality.

These substrate interactions mean that lichen biomonitoring studies must account for tree species composition when interpreting community data. A lichen diversity index calibrated for oak-dominated forests may not be directly comparable to one from elm or poplar stands, even under identical atmospheric conditions.

The physical structure of bark also matters. Rough, fissured bark retains moisture and particulate deposition longer than smooth bark, potentially increasing pollutant exposure for lichens growing there. However, this same moisture retention may buffer against the drying effects of polluted air, creating complex trade-offs that vary among species and pollutant types. Researchers typically control for substrate effects by sampling from a single tree species or by applying substrate-specific correction factors to their diversity indices.

## Cyanobacterial Lichens: Acid Rain Sensitivity and Heavy Metal Tolerance

Lichens containing cyanobacteria as their photobiont display a distinctive pollution response profile that differs markedly from green-algal lichens. Their dual sensitivity — extreme vulnerability to acidification combined with relative heavy metal tolerance — makes them particularly informative bioindicators for specific pollution regimes.

### Acid Rain Sensitivity

Cyanobacterial lichens are especially sensitive to acid rain (pH depression from SO₂ and NOₓ deposition) — far more so than green-algal lichens. This heightened sensitivity is likely related to the cyanobacterial nitrogen metabolism, which is strongly pH-dependent; acidification disrupts [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-and-green-manure]] can collapse the symbiosis. The nitrogenase enzyme complex that cyanobacteria use to fix atmospheric nitrogen operates optimally near neutral pH and is rapidly inactivated under acidic conditions.

The disappearance of cyanobacterial lichens from an area can therefore serve as a specific indicator of acid deposition, distinct from general SO₂ toxicity. In regions where acid rain is the primary pollutant stressor, cyanobacterial lichen loss may occur even at relatively low SO₂ concentrations, providing an early signal of ecosystem acidification before widespread vascular plant damage becomes apparent.

### Heavy Metal Tolerance

Paradoxically, cyanobacterial lichens show relatively high tolerance to heavy metals such as copper, zinc, lead, and cadmium. This tolerance is sufficiently strong that cyanobacterial lichens have been found as the sole inhabitants of abandoned heavy-metal-contaminated mire and mining sites — environments too toxic for most other organisms, including green-algal lichens.

The ecological significance of this finding extends beyond biomonitoring. The ability of cyanobacterial lichens to colonize and persist in contaminated sites suggests they may play a role in the initial stages of ecological succession on post-industrial land. By establishing a biological cover, they may facilitate soil stabilization, organic matter accumulation, and the eventual establishment of vascular plant communities — a process known as primary succession.

The mechanisms underlying this metal tolerance may involve:

- Extracellular polymeric substances that bind and sequester metal ions before they reach cell membranes.
- Intracellular sequestration by metallothionein-like proteins in both the mycobiont and cyanobiont.
- The ability of the mycobiont to immobilize metals in the cell wall matrix, preventing translocation to the sensitive photobiont layer.
- Metal efflux pumps that actively transport accumulated metals out of sensitive cellular compartments.

This tolerance profile makes cyanobacterial lichens natural sentinels for heavy metal contamination. Their persistence in metal-contaminated environments, combined with measurable tissue metal concentrations, provides a dual signal — presence indicates community resilience, while tissue analysis quantifies exposure levels.

## Lichens as Bioindicators of Ozone Pollution

Tropospheric ozone is an increasingly important air pollutant, and lichens respond to elevated ozone concentrations with visible injury, reduced photosynthetic efficiency, and community shifts. Research has established a sensitivity hierarchy among lichen species for ozone damage:

> *Parmotrema austrosinense* > *P. tinctorum* > *Canterkua braunsiana* > *Ramalina yasudae*

Species at the top of this hierarchy (most sensitive) develop symptoms at lower ozone concentrations, making them early warning indicators. Those at the bottom are more resistant and may persist even in ozone-enriched environments. This graded response mirrors the approach used for SO₂ and allows the construction of ozone-specific lichen community indices.

Ozone damage to lichens manifests as bleaching of the photobiont layer, reduced chlorophyll content, decreased photosynthetic oxygen evolution, and in severe cases, necrosis of the thallus. Because ozone concentrations vary seasonally (peaking in summer) and diurnally (peaking in afternoon), lichens integrate this variability in a way that a single instrumental measurement cannot.

The ozone sensitivity hierarchy among lichens also has implications for forest health monitoring. Since ozone damages vascular plants as well as lichens, the lichen response can serve as an early indicator of ozone stress on the broader forest ecosystem. Forest managers can use lichen surveys to identify areas where ozone levels may be approaching thresholds harmful to tree growth and productivity.

It is worth noting that the interaction between ozone and other pollutants can produce synergistic effects. Lichens exposed simultaneously to elevated ozone and SO₂, or ozone and nitrogen deposition, often show greater damage than would be predicted from either pollutant alone. These interactive effects complicate the interpretation of field data but also provide a more realistic picture of the multi-pollutant environments that characterize most industrialized landscapes.

## Recovery of Lichen Communities After Pollution Reduction

Perhaps the most encouraging finding in lichen biomonitoring is the evidence that lichen communities can recover when pollution pressures are reduced. Bates et al. (2001) documented the revival of lichen species along a 70-km transect extending from central London outward, following decades of declining industrial SO₂ emissions:

- ***[[hypogymnia-physodes]]*** reappeared in areas where it had been absent for decades.
- ***Lepraria incana*** colonized new substrates in the recovery zone.

These observations confirm that lichen recolonization is a reliable indicator of improving air quality. The London transect study is particularly valuable because it spans a strong pollution gradient — from the urban core (still relatively polluted) to the rural periphery (cleaner) — allowing researchers to track community recovery as a function of distance from pollution sources and elapsed time since emission controls were implemented.

However, recovery is typically slower than initial decline. Dispersal limitations (many lichens reproduce primarily by vegetative fragments that travel short distances), substrate availability (suitable bark surfaces may be limited in recovering forests), and the time required for thallus development all constrain the rate at which communities reassemble. Full recovery to pre-industrial diversity baselines may take decades or longer, and some particularly sensitive species may never return without assisted reintroduction.

The London recovery data also demonstrate that lichen community response is not uniform across species. Early colonizers like *Lepraria incana* (a powdery, fast-growing species) appear first, followed by slower-growing foliose and fruticose species. This successional sequence provides additional information about the stage of recovery and the residual pollution pressure at a given site.

## Fungal Roles in Pollutant Processing

Beyond their utility as passive indicators, fungi (including the mycobionts of lichens) play active roles in controlling and processing environmental pollutants. Fungi can immobilize, translocate, release, and alter the chemical state of heavy metals through several mechanisms. This active participation in pollutant cycling means that fungal communities — including lichens — are not merely victims of pollution but also agents of environmental remediation.

### Biosorption

Biosorption is the primary mechanism by which fungi remove metals from solution. It involves ion exchange between dissolved metal cations and reactive functional groups (carboxyl, phosphate, amino, and hydroxyl groups) on the fungal cell wall. This process is largely passive, does not require metabolic energy, and can occur even in dead biomass.

Dead mycelium of *[[rhizopus-arrhizus]]* has proven highly efficient at adsorbing heavy metals from aqueous solutions, though it shows poor adsorption of alkali metals (Na⁺, K⁺). This selectivity makes fungal biomass potentially useful for targeted bioremediation applications where specific metals must be removed from mixed waste streams.

The biosorption process is influenced by pH, temperature, initial metal concentration, biomass particle size, and the presence of competing ions. Optimal biosorption typically occurs at pH values where metal cations are soluble but cell wall functional groups are deprotonated and available for binding. Pretreatment of biomass — including autoclaving, acid washing, or chemical cross-linking — can significantly enhance biosorption capacity by exposing additional binding sites.
