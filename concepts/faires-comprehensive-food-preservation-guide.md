# Faires Comprehensive Food Preservation Guide

Food preservation is the critical link between seasonal abundance and year-round food security. In a permaculture system, the goal is to extend the growing season through season extension techniques and preserve food through multiple methods to ensure a continuous food supply. The Faires guide presents preservation as part of a broader strategy that includes freezing, drying, fermentation, salting, smoking, pickling, and no-energy storage.

## The Preservation Philosophy

In a colder, temperate climate it is probably not possible to grow enough food all year to feed an entire family. The strategy has two components:

1. Extending the growing season through succession planting, cold frames, greenhouses, and variety selection
2. Preserving the surplus from peak harvest times for use during lean periods

Every preservation method has trade-offs in terms of energy input, equipment cost, nutritional retention, flavour, and shelf life. A resilient food system uses multiple methods, not relying on any single approach.

| Freezing | High (electricity) | Freezer | 6-12 months | Excellent |

## Freezing and Blanching

Freezing is a quick and healthy method of preserving food. The best temperature is -5 degrees F (-20 degrees C), but to save energy 0 degrees F (-17 degrees C) is acceptable, though that is pushing the limit for keeping meat safe.

### Freezer Management

- Keep a few plastic jugs of water in the bottom of the freezer -- if electricity goes out, the food will last longer and you will have an emergency water supply
- Keep the freezer in the coolest room of the house, but not where it could freeze (freezing temperatures kill the motor)
- Freeze foods in usable portion sizes -- food cannot be safely refrozen after thawing
- Label everything with contents and date
- Rotate stock: first in, first out

### Blanching

Many vegetables must be blanched before freezing. Blanching involves briefly immersing vegetables in boiling water, then immediately cooling in ice water. This stops enzyme activity that would cause deterioration during frozen storage. Typical blanching times range from 1-5 minutes depending on the vegetable. See [[faires-freezing-and-blanching-guide]] for detailed instructions.

## Drying and Dehydration

Drying is one of the oldest preservation methods and works well with a wide range of foods. See [[faires-food-drying-and-no-energy-storage]] for comprehensive instructions.

### Fruits for Drying

Apples, pears, plums, apricots, peaches, berries, and grapes all dry well. Over-ripe or even fermenting fruit can still make good fruit leather. Wash, peel, remove seeds and pits, and grind up by mashing or blending. The puree must be thin enough to pour but not too thin to be watery.

### Vegetables for Drying

Tomatoes, peppers, onions, garlic, green beans, peas, corn, and root vegetables all dry well. Vegetables are dry when they are brittle and break when bent. Properly dried vegetables should be conditioned for a week in a covered bowl, stirred two to three times daily, before packing in airtight containers.

### Meat for Drying (Jerky)

Choose lean cuts of beef, buffalo, goat, or deer. Do not use pork, which is too fatty. Cut into trimmed strips about one inch wide and half an inch tall, cut along the grain. Sprinkle with ground pepper and salt. Dry in a dehydrator at 160 degrees F (71 degrees C) or in the sun on a tray four feet above a slow fire using non-resinous hardwood.

## No-Energy Storage

Live storage -- storing food without any processing -- is the most energy-efficient preservation method. See [[faires-no-energy-food-storage]] for detailed storage conditions and durations.

### Key Principles

1. Leave the dirt on root vegetables -- it helps protect from decay
2. Use plastic buckets or enamel cans that are rodent and decay-proof
3. Store fruits away from vegetables -- gas from apples causes vegetables to sprout
4. Pack root vegetables in damp sawdust, sand, or moss
5. Keep potatoes in complete darkness -- light turns them green and poisonous
6. Monitor temperature daily using thermometers inside and outside the cellar
7. Maintain 32 degrees F with ventilation management
8. Keep humidity at 60-75% using pans of water or dampened floors
9. Remove spoiled food immediately to prevent spread
10. Check regularly for insects and rodent activity

## Lacto-Fermentation

Fermentation preserves food while actually improving its nutritional profile. See [[faires-food-fermentation-methods]] and [[faires-pickling-and-advanced-fermentation]] for detailed recipes.

### What Can Be Fermented

Almost any vegetable can be lacto-fermented:
- Cabbage (sauerkraut, kimchi)
- Cucumbers (pickles)
- Carrots, radishes, turnips, beets
- Green beans, peppers, onions
- Tomatoes (whole or as salsa)
- Garlic, ginger, horseradish
- Mixed vegetable combinations

### Fermentation Principles

- Use unchlorinated water (chlorine kills beneficial bacteria)
- Use sea salt or mineral salt (not iodised table salt)
- Keep vegetables submerged below the brine
- Ferment at cool room temperature (60-70 degrees F)
- Allow adequate time (days to weeks depending on the food)
- Store finished ferments in cool conditions

## Salting and Curing

Salt preservation works by creating an environment where bacteria cannot survive. See [[faires-meat-processing-curing-and-smoking]] for detailed meat curing instructions.

### Salted Vegetables

Vegetables can also be preserved with salt:
- Salted green beans (a traditional French method)
- Salted eggplant (Middle Eastern preservation)
- Salted radish (Asian preservation)
- Salted herbs (preserving fresh herb flavour)

## Clarified Fats and Oils

Fat preservation is an often-overlooked method. Rendered tallow, lard, and clarified butter (ghee) store for extended periods at room temperature. See [[faires-meat-processing-curing-and-smoking]] for rendering instructions.

## Building a Preservation Schedule

A well-organised preservation practice follows the harvest calendar:

### Spring
- Freeze or ferment asparagus
- Dry or freeze early greens
- Begin lacto-fermentation of radishes and early cabbage
- Process and freeze or dry herbs

### Summer
- Freeze berries and stone fruits
- Make jam from summer fruits
- Dry tomatoes, peppers, and summer squash
- Begin pickling cucumbers
- Freeze green beans, peas, and corn
- Make fruit leather from surplus fruit

### Autumn
- Ferment large batches of sauerkraut and kimchi
- Cure and smoke meat
- Dry apples, pears, and plums
- Set up root cellar storage for root vegetables and winter squash
