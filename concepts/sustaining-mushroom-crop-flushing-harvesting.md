# Sustaining the Mushroom Crop — Flushing, Watering, Harvesting

Chapter X of The Mushroom Cultivator covers the critical environmental
factors that determine success once [[mushroom-pinning-primordia-formation-strategies]] is underway. Temperature, humidity,
airflow, watering, and harvesting technique all influence total yield and
quality.

## Temperature During Cropping

Once the generative phase begins, air temperature becomes the
controlling factor (rather than substrate temperature). Each species has
an optimum for fruitbody development within a broader growing range:

-   **Lower temperatures** — postpone or lengthen the harvesting period,
    allowing maximum quality control.
-   **Higher temperatures** — shorten cropping by promoting rapid, intense
    flushes, but risk CO2 buildup, heat stress, and faster insect and
    contaminant reproduction.

Commercial Agaricus growers commonly lower air temperature 2°F for 48
hours before peak of the first and second flushes, then run subsequent
flushes hotter to speed the crop to completion.

## The Flushing Pattern

Mushroom crops grow in cycles called **flushes** or **breaks**, normally
in seven to ten day intervals with each successive flush bearing fewer
mushrooms. The manner of flushing is determined during the pin
initiation period:

-   Even pinning sets up a uniform flushing pattern throughout the crop.
-   Uneven flushing creates difficult situations for watering and
    environmental control.
-   Early-forming pinheads ("volunteers") should be picked off as
    buttons unless they constitute the flush itself.

To increase flushing speed, keep each harvest period short and concise.
Late-developing mushrooms are removed on or the day after peak
production. The sooner a flush is completely removed, the quicker the
next appears.

Poor first flushes indicate faulty pinning procedures and lead to lower
total yields. Since competing contaminants progressively build up, the
goal is to maximize early flushes.

## Air Movement and Fresh Air

Even airflow across all cropping levels counteracts temperature
stratification and dead air pockets. During pinning, fresh air removes
metabolic gases; during cropping, maturing mushrooms create more CO2.

**Fresh air rates:**
-   *Agaricus brunnescens*: 4-6 air changes per hour.
-   *Agaricus bitorquis*: half the fresh air of *A. brunnescens*.
-   *Psilocybe cubensis*: 2-3 changes per hour (more CO2 tolerant).

High CO2 environments produce long stems and small underdeveloped caps
in Agaricus and Pleurotus. Too much fresh air is preferable to
insufficient, though preconditioning fresh air avoids overworking heating
and humidification systems.

## Evaporation Rate Balance

An important effect of air circulation is moisture evaporation from the
cropping surface:

-   Excessive humidity without adequate air movement retards development.
-   Stagnant air pockets breed [[mushroom-cultivator-contaminants-identification]] and Bacterial Blotch.
-   If evaporation is too high (humidity below 85%), mushrooms become
    stunted with cracked, scaly caps.
-   A dry cropping surface is difficult to recondition.

Reaching a balance between air circulation, fresh air, and
humidification is "the Art of mushroom culture."

## Watering Technique

Mushrooms on [[stamets-casing-layer-design]] draw
moisture from both substrate and casing; uncased substrates draw only
from the substrate and require 90-95% RH with periodic misting.

**Key watering principles:**
-   Before button stage: only light mistings — substantial watering
    damages pins.
-   At button stage: begin building casing moisture back to pre-pinning
    levels through daily light-to-moderate waterings with a fine mist
    nozzle.
-   Goal: reach peak moisture just before the main harvest.
-   Water 2-4 times per day rather than once — prevents panning.
-   After each flush harvest: keep casing moist with light mistings
    until the next flush reaches button stage.
-   Never allow the casing to dry out completely.

**Bacterial Blotch prevention:** Lower humidity and increase air
circulation after watering to dry mushroom caps quickly. Stop watering
once caps reach adolescence. Do not water contaminated areas.

## Harvesting Technique

The picking method dramatically affects future flushes:

1.  Grasp the base of the stem with a twisting motion, pulling from the
    casing without disturbing neighboring pinheads.
2.  Trim stem base — remove only flesh with attached casing or substrate.
3.  Clusters should be broken apart and harvested individually when
    possible; leave immature mushrooms attached for continued growth.

**Timing:** Pick before the veil tears and stem elongates (Agaricus) or
when the veil begins to tear (Psilocybe). Mature mushrooms release
massive spore loads that can inhibit further pinning and cause allergic
reactions.

Stunted mushrooms between flushes should be cleared without disturbing
the casing. Small dead pinheads cause little harm if left in place.

## Preserving Mushrooms

If not served within four days, mushrooms can be preserved by:

-   **Air drying** — most common method. Most mushrooms are 90% water.
    Dry with warm air no hotter than 110°F over 24-48 hours. Seal in
    airtight containers and refrigerate; preserved for years.
-   **Freezing** — without pre-drying, frozen mushrooms become soggy on
    thawing; best for soups and stews.
-   **Freeze drying** — ideal for preserving flavor, nutrition, and
    form, but equipment is prohibitively expensive for home growers.
-   **Canning** — another option for long-term preservation.
