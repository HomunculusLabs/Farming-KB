# Microfungal Species Diversity Over Large Areas

Understanding fungal species diversity at landscape and regional scales is essential for conservation planning and biodiversity estimation, yet the data remain remarkably sparse.

## The Best-Studied Sites

The most complete information on fungal species numbers for specific areas comes from three sites in southern England:

| Site | Area | Total Species | Survey Duration |
|------|------|---------------|-----------------|
| Slapton Ley | 211 ha | 2,500 | 25+ years |
| Esher Common | ~400 ha | 2,700 | Long-term |
| Mickleham Down | 4 ha | 1,380 | Long-term |
| Warwickshire County | 250,000 ha | 2,700 | County-wide |

### Key Observations

- Only ~30% of species are common to both Slapton Ley and Esher Common
- Large numbers of microhabitats at both sites remain unexamined
- An estimate of 3,000 species for either site does not seem unreasonable
- Slapton Ley (~500 plant species) corresponds well with Hawksworth's 6:1 fungal-to-plant ratio
- The relatively small increase from 4 ha to 250,000 ha suggests extrapolation from large areas may not be a good way to estimate fungal diversity

## Taxonomic Composition

Across English sites, the proportional breakdown is roughly:
- Basidiomycota: 32-46%
- Ascomycota: 28-43%
- Mitosporic fungi: 8-24%
- Mycetozoa: 3.5-6%
- Oomycota/Zygomycota: 1-6%

The large variation in proportions between sites indicates that historical sampling procedures have been biased and that the proportion of species remaining to be sampled is significant.

## Tropical vs. Temperate Diversity

### Evidence for Greater Tropical Diversity
- Many circumstantial data support the view that overall fungal diversity is greater in tropical regions
- Leaf epiphytes and inhabitants of living leaves are significantly more diverse in tropical than temperate regions
- Batista and co-workers frequently observed 10+ microfungus species on single leaves in Brazil
- As many as 26 fungal species recorded from a single foliicolous lichen community

### Quantitative Evidence
For the Phyllachoraceae (Ascomycota):
- Species between latitudes 20N and 20S: baseline
- Species between 40-60N and 40-60S: only 38% of tropical figure
- Species above 60N: only 6% of tropical figure

The true proportions are likely even more extreme given under-recording of tropical species.

## Factors Influencing Site Diversity

### Plant Diversity
The taxonomic diversity and structural variety of plant taxa exert the greatest influence on microfungal diversity. Fungal diversity is affected by variations in host life form; lignified tissues (wood, bark, bamboo) decay more slowly and exhibit more extensive succession of fungal species.

### Temperature and Water
- Most fungi grow and sporulate best above 15-20C
- Humidity plays a more prominent role than temperature alone
- Aquatic environments harbor considerable fungal diversity, indicating water's importance
- Some species are adapted to xeric environments

### Seasonality
Significant seasonal variation in temperature or water availability results in different fungal communities compared to constant conditions.

## Under-Sampling Problem

Bills and Polishook (1994) detected 78-134 species per collection from Costa Rican litter, with rarefaction curves showing no sign of leveling off. Reanalysis found only 15-28% overlap of named species between samples, underscoring the enormity of under-sampling.

# Coprophilous Fungi Succession Defense Mechanisms

Updated: 2026-04-18

Coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi exhibit one of the best-documented successional sequences in mycology, alongside an array of remarkable defense mechanisms adapted to their challenging substrate.

## Successional Sequence

The classical succession on herbivore dung proceeds through distinct fungal groups:

### Phase 1: Zygomycetes (Days 5-14)
Pioneer colonizers that rapidly exploit easily available sugars and starches. These fast-growing fungi are the first to fruit on fresh dung, capitalizing on the brief window before competitors arrive.

### Phase 2: Ascomycetes
As the easily metabolized compounds are depleted, Ascomycetes colonize the dung, specializing in cellulose decomposition. Many produce distinctive fruiting bodies (apothecia, perithecia) on the dung surface.

### Phase 3: Basidiomycetes
Late-stage colonizers that decompose more recalcitrant compounds. Coprinus and other Basidiomycetes produce characteristic ink-cap mushrooms on aging dung.

### Phase 4: Myxomycetes (Up to 2 Months)
Slime molds appear on very old dung as the final successional stage, feeding on remaining organic matter and the dead remains of earlier fungal colonizers.

## Overlapping Timing

Lodha (1974) documented considerable overlap in succession timing. The sequence is not strictly linear but modified by competition and nutrient availability (Webster 1970). Different substrates (carbohydrates, cellulose, lignins) within the same dung pat support simultaneously active fungal communities.

## Environmental Influences

### Temperature
Temperature affects succession timing significantly. Wicklow and Moore (1974) found that *Thelebolus* and *Preussia* dominate in cold spring conditions, while other species peak in summer. Seasonal patterns shift the entire successional trajectory.

### Light
Light influences succession through phototropic responses. *Ascobolus* and *Podospora* show positive phototropism — their fruiting structures orient toward light, affecting spore dispersal direction and efficiency.

### Herbivore Species
Most coprophilous studies have focused on cattle dung, but Eliasson and Lundqvist (1979) sampled 25 herbivore species and found distinct fungal communities on the dung of different animals, reflecting differences in diet, digestive physiology, and dung chemistry.

## Defense Mechanisms

### Cleistothecial Ascomycete Dispersal
Cleistothecial Ascomycetes produce fruiting bodies with modified appendages designed for attachment to mammal fur. This dispersal mechanism is advantageous for rodent dung fungi under arid conditions, where spores must be transported to new dung deposits by animals rather than wind.
