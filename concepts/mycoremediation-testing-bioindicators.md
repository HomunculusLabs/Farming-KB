# Mycoremediation Testing with Bioindicators

## Overview

Verifying the effectiveness of a mycoremediation project requires practical, accessible [[ingham-aact-testing-methods]]. While professional [[solomon-soil-testing-laboratory-analysis-low-input-gardening]] is valuable for confirming results, low-tech biological assays using organisms with extremely low thresholds for toxin exposure provide accurate, cost-effective ways to monitor remediation progress. The three primary bioindicator approaches—cell counting for biological contaminants, the bean test for chemical contaminants, and the worm assay for soil toxicity—are accessible to everyone from students and home cultivators to commercial operators.

## Dosing Mycelial Biomass for Metabolite Harvest

Before testing remediation outcomes, cultivators may want to intentionally dose established mycelial biomass with biological or chemical triggers to elicit metabolic responses. This technique can produce novel antibiotics or enzymes specific to the mechanisms needed in response to the stimulus.

The process involves inserting small sterile, open-ended tubing into the biomass at an angle, accessible from the exterior, where solutions can be added to infuse into the mycelial block. The tube should sit for one week in the mycelial block before adding any solution, allowing the mycelium to heal and seal off any holes, making the system watertight. When done correctly, the solution should not flow through but rather maintain a level throughout the experiment.

## Cell Counting for Biological Contaminants

Cell counting is critical for tracking bacterial growth rates and calculating the efficiency of a mycofiltration system. The process involves several key steps:

1. **Gram stain validation**: Perform a gram stain test on a small sample of bacteria to validate identity and purity of the culture. Gram stain kits are inexpensive (under $30) and sort bacteria into classes based on cell membrane composition, shape, and metabolic functions.

2. **[[dilution-to-extinction]] plating**: Prepare a series of dilutions of the bacterial culture using the dilution-to-extinction method, spreading each across three agar plates and incubating overnight. This produces individual colonies that can be counted.

3. **Colony counting**: Bacterial colonies appear mostly as clear, yellow, or rose-colored dots on the agar surface. Focus on plates with thirty or fewer colonies for accurate counting. Record the dilution rate for future reference.

4. **Calculation**: Count colonies on the selected plates, calculate the average across the three replicate plates for each dilution, and multiply by the inverse of the dilution to determine the number of bacterial cells in the original culture volume.

**[[easygel-coliform-detection]] kits** offer a convenient alternative that doesn't require autoclaving and can be performed anywhere. The coliforms are stained in different colors depending on type and are easily counted without microscopes.

### Sampling Intervals

Bacteria such as E. coli can double in population every 20 minutes at optimum incubation temperatures. Recommended sampling intervals include: initiation, 30 minutes, 1 hour, 3 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, and every 6 hours thereafter until the experiment concludes. After pulling each sample, dilute to the appropriate level, plate it, incubate overnight, and perform a gram stain test to confirm you are tracking the pathogen of interest.

## The Bean Test for Chemical Contaminants

The bean test uses [[seed-germination-and-stratification]] growth as a bioindicator for chemical contamination levels. It establishes a spectrum of response that can later be used to evaluate water or soil from mycofiltration systems.

### Materials

- At least 70 bean seeds of known viability from the same stock
- Fourteen small jars or large test tubes
- Distilled water
- The chemical compound being tested
- Sterile soil and growing containers
- Gloves and eye protection

### Procedure

1. **Setup**: Label containers as "control," 0, and 1 through 12. Add five beans to each container.

2. **Control**: Add 10 ml distilled water to the control container.

3. **Dilution series**: Add 9 ml distilled water to each remaining container. Prepare a 1% solution of the test chemical and add enough to cover the beans in container 0.

4. **Serial transfer**: Using a sterile pipette, transfer 1 ml from container 0 to container 1, shake well, then repeat sequentially through container 12. Each transfer creates a tenfold dilution: container 0 is 1 part per hundred, container 1 is 1 part per thousand, container 2 is 1 part per ten thousand, and so on.

5. **Soaking**: Soak beans for 24 hours, then strain and save the water for watering seedlings.

6. **Planting**: Plant beans from each dilution group separately in sterile soil, labeled with their dilution number. Water with the saved dilution solution.

7. **Observation**: Over days and weeks, record sprout emergence rates, growth rates, and any abnormalities including leaf tissue damage, color variations, and wilting compared to the control group.

### Interpreting Results

The "safe" concentration level is the highest dilution at which bean seeds grow normally, behaving identically to the control group in all measurable ways. Water harvested from a mycofiltration system can then be run through the bean test without dilution and compared against the established spectrum to determine contaminant concentration and filtration efficiency.

### Additional Applications

- **Foliar spray testing**: For herbicide remediation projects, dilutions can be administered as foliar sprays rather than seed soaks.
- **Soil and compost testing**: Plant herbicide-sensitive seeds (beans, corn, tomatoes) into soil or compost samples to detect persistent herbicides like clopyralid, which can survive four to six years even after composting.
- **Composted manure screening**: Many [[teaming-with-microbes-the-organic-gardeners-guide-to-the-soil-food-web]] have experienced total crop loss from persistent herbicides in composted manures. Testing before bulk purchase is strongly recommended.

## The Worm Assay for Soil Toxicity

Worm assays provide complementary information to bean tests because worms ([[eisenia-fetida]] or Lumbricus terrestris) are highly sensitive to a broad range of hydrocarbons and other toxins that beans may tolerate. Worm toxicity data is available on manufacturer product information materials and EPA registration documents online.

### Procedure

1. **Setup**: Fill sanitized perforated plastic cups (~250 ml) with collected soil samples moistened with distilled or deionized water. For contamination experiments, hydrate soil with solutions based on parts-per-million dilutions of the target compound. Prepare five replicate cups for each test concentration plus controls.

2. **Inoculation**: Add twenty cultivated worms (not wild-harvested) of the same species and approximate size to each cup. Record original color, reaction to light, and sensitivity to touch—healthy worms twitch when touched in the middle.

3. **Incubation**: Cover cups with perforated lids, incubate at room temperature (65–75°F / 18–24°C), and place a small light above to discourage escape. Open containers once daily to observe and record changes in behavior, color, or mortality.

4. **Final assessment (14 days)**: On the last day, dissect soil samples and sift through to find and count surviving worms. Note final color, light reaction, and touch sensitivity. Compare against original observations and control worms.

### Applications

Once the effect of contaminants at different concentrations on worms is established, this data serves as a reference for evaluating contaminated soil that has undergone mycoremediation. Worms can also be analyzed for coelomic fluids and blood to determine how and where bioremediation or chemical tolerance took place.

## Follow-Up Professional Testing

After initial bioindicator testing, professional laboratory analysis of remediated water or soil is recommended to determine the fate of pollutants. Key questions include:

- Did mycoremediation achieve complete inactivation or degradation of the contaminant?
- Did the contaminant undergo biotransformation into different compounds?
- Is it possible the results are worse than the original contamination (biotransformation into a more toxic form)?

All collected data—whether from lab work, cell counting, bean tests, or worm assays—guides recalibration of subsequent trials. Good data collected upfront allows fine-tuning of different project aspects before performing full-scale field installations, where more is at stake.
