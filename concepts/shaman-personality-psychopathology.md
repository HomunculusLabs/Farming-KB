# Shaman Personality Structure and Psychopathology

## Overview

The scholarly investigation of shaman personality structure represents one of the most enduring intersections between anthropology and psychiatry. Drawing from psychoanalytic, projective, and cross-cultural frameworks, researchers have sought to understand whether shamans are psychologically abnormal, uniquely gifted, or something more culturally contextual. The literature assembled in *Hallucinogens and Shamanism* (Harner, ed.) — particularly the Boyer et al. study of Mescalero Apache shamanism and the transitional essay by Marlene Dobkin de Rios on ayahuasca — traces a paradigm shift from individual psychopathology toward the cultural shaping of altered states of consciousness.

This concept page synthesizes the key references and arguments found in the Boyer et al. bibliography and the opening sections of the Dobkin de Rios article, examining how the field moved from diagnosing the shaman to understanding the cultural systems that give shamanic experience its form and function.

## Boyer's Longitudinal Study of Mescalero Apache Shamanism

L. Bryce Boyer and his collaborators conducted the most sustained psychodynamic study of shaman personality in the anthropological record, spanning roughly a decade (1961–1972). Their work focused on the Mescalero Apache of the American Southwest and examined how personality development, cultural socialization, and the aggressive drive intersect in the formation of shamanic practitioners.

Key publications in the Boyer corpus include:

- **Boyer & Boyer (1972)**: Examined the effects of acculturation on the vicissitudes of the aggressive drive among Mescalero Apache, published in *Psychoanalytic Study of Society*. This work linked changes in shamanic practice to the broader stresses of cultural contact and assimilation, situating personality dynamics within the historical pressures facing Native communities in the mid-twentieth century.
- **Boyer (1969)**: "Shamans: To Set the Record Straight" in *American Anthropologist*, defending methodological rigor in shamanic personality research against what Boyer saw as romanticized or reductive portrayals. This brief but pointed article underscored the need for systematic, clinically informed approaches to the study of shamanic practitioners.
- **Boyer, Klopfer, Brawer, and Kawai (1964)**: A landmark Rorschach comparison of shamans and pseudoshamans on the Mescalero Indian Reservation, published in the *Journal of Projective Techniques and Personality Assessment*. This multi-author study represented the most empirically ambitious effort in the Boyer program.

Ruth M. Boyer's 1962 doctoral dissertation at UC Berkeley on the social structure and socialization of the Mescalero Apache provided the ethnographic foundation upon which the psychodynamic analyses were built, grounding clinical inference in rich cultural context.

## Rorschach Studies: Shamans vs. Pseudoshamans

The most methodologically distinctive contribution of the Boyer group was their use of the Rorschach inkblot test to compare genuine shamans with pseudoshamans — individuals who claimed or were accorded shamanic status without authentic training or recognized ability. This distinction between shaman and pseudoshaman is itself culturally significant: Mescalero Apache society recognized a category of fraudulent practitioners, suggesting that not all who claimed spiritual power were accepted as legitimate.

**Klopfer and Boyer (1961)**, in "Notes on the Personality Structure of a North American Indian Shaman: Rorschach Interpretation" (*Journal of Projective Techniques*), offered an initial personality profile drawn from projective testing. This was expanded in the 1964 multi-author comparison, which attempted to identify measurable psychological differences between authentic practitioners and impostors.

The Rorschach approach was significant because it provided a standardized, culture-sensitive (though still debated) instrument for probing the inner life of shamans — something purely observational ethnography could not easily achieve. Findings suggested that genuine shamans displayed certain integrative personality features and creative-affective capacities that distinguished them from pseudoshamans, though the interpretation of these results has remained contested within both anthropology and psychology.

## The Devereux Framework: Normal vs. Abnormal in Psychiatric Anthropology

A foundational theoretical touchstone for the study of shaman psychopathology is George Devereux's 1956 essay, "Normal and Abnormal: The Key Problem of Psychiatric Anthropology," published in *Some Uses of Anthropology: Theoretical and Applied* (Anthropological Society of Washington).

Devereux argued that the categories of "normal" and "abnormal" cannot be transplanted wholesale from Western psychiatric nosology into other cultural contexts. What appears as pathology in one society may serve an adaptive, even essential, social function in another. For shamanism, this meant that traits such as visionary experience, trance states, apparent hallucinations, or unusual affective expression — which might be classified as psychotic in a Western clinical setting — could be normative, valued, and functionally integrated within Indigenous cosmologies.

Devereux's framework pushed researchers to ask not "Is the shaman mentally ill?" but rather "How does this culture organize and interpret experiences that Western psychiatry might label pathological?" This reframing deeply influenced Boyer's approach and the broader field of psychiatric anthropology, establishing a principle of cultural relativism that continues to shape debates about cross-cultural concepts of mental health.

## Psychoanalytic Dimensions

Several psychoanalytic works cited in the Boyer bibliography illuminate specific facets of shamanic personality:

- **Freud (1915)**, "The Unconscious" (*Standard Edition*, Vol. 14): Provided the theoretical architecture for understanding how repressed material, drives, and unconscious processes surface — relevant to shamanic visionary experience and the controlled access to normally inaccessible psychic content.
- **Erikson (1950)**, *Childhood and Society*: Erikson's developmental framework informed understanding of how early socialization experiences on the reservation shaped personality trajectories, including the resolution (or fixation) at stages that might predispose individuals toward shamanic callings.
- **Greenacre (1958)**, "The Imposter" (*Psychoanalytic Quarterly*): Greenacre's analysis of impostor psychology was directly relevant to the Rorschach comparison of shamans and pseudoshamans, offering a psychodynamic model for understanding those who assume healing roles without genuine capacity or initiation.
- **Kris (1952)**, *Psychoanalytic Explorations in Art*: Kris's work on regression in the service of the ego — the idea that creative individuals can access primary-process thinking and return to adaptive function — offered a model for understanding how shamans might use altered states productively rather than destructively.

## Shamanism and Acute Schizophrenia

The question of whether shamans suffer from acute schizophrenia has been one of the most provocative in the literature. **Julian Silverman (1967)**, in "Shamans and Acute Schizophrenia" (*American Anthropologist*), directly confronted this issue, comparing the phenomenology of shamanic trance with the symptomatology of acute schizophrenic episodes.

Silverman argued that while there are superficial similarities — visual and auditory hallucinations, altered sense of self, visionary content — the social context, course, functional outcome, and cultural interpretation of the two phenomena differ fundamentally. Shamans operate within a culturally sanctioned role that provides structure, meaning, and social support for their experiences; schizophrenic patients, by contrast, typically lack such scaffolding, and their experiences are experienced as fragmenting rather than integrative.

This distinction echoes Devereux's broader argument: the same raw experiential material can lead to radically different outcomes depending on the cultural matrix in which it is embedded.

## Peyote Use Among the Apache

The broader context for Mescalero shamanism includes the ritual use of peyote, documented extensively in the references:

- **La Barre (1938)**, *The Peyote Cult*: The foundational ethnographic and historical study of peyotism in Native North America, situating the Mescalero practice within a continent-wide religious movement.
- **Opler (1936, 1938, 1940, 1945)**: Morris Edward Opler's series of articles documented the influence of aboriginal patterns and white contact on the Mescalero peyote rite, the use of peyote among Carrizo and Lipan Apache tribes, and the associated myths and legends. His work showed how peyote use was not a static tradition but one continuously reshaped by historical and intercultural forces.
- **Slotkin (1956)**, *The Peyote Religion*: Provided a comprehensive synthesis of peyotism as an institutionalized religious form.
- **Castetter and Opler (1936)**, *The Ethnobiology of the Chiricahua and Mescalero Apache*: Documented the material base of plant use, including peyote, in Apache subsistence and ceremonial life.

## Transition: From Individual Psychopathology to Cultural Variables

The transition from the Boyer et al. study to Marlene Dobkin de Rios's essay on ayahuasca marks a significant shift in the field's orientation — from the individual personality structure of the shaman to the cultural variables that shape the hallucinogenic experience itself.

Dobkin de Rios begins by noting that while plant hallucinogens in non-Western societies have received "widely scattered attention," there has been little systematic focus on the cultural variables — belief systems, attitudes, and values — that determine and structure the subjective experience. Her fieldwork in Iquitos, Peru, examined ayahuasca (a drink prepared from the *Banisteriopsis* vine, boiled for several hours) as used in Amazonian psychotherapy sessions.

She draws a sharp contrast between clinical and anthropological approaches to hallucinogens:

- **Clinical studies** attempt to specify antecedent variables, consequent events, and their relations as objectively as possible, focusing on individual pathology in controlled hospital settings.
- **Anthropological studies** work in "natural laboratory" settings where variables cannot be controlled, but where the investigator can delineate the cultural antecedent variables that structure the experience itself.

The anthropological contribution, as Dobkin de Rios frames it, lies in mapping the **corpus of beliefs** surrounding group use of hallucinogens — the cosmological frameworks, ritual practices, and communal expectations that give meaning to what would otherwise be mere pharmacological effects.

This shift represents a move from asking "What kind of person becomes a shaman?" to asking "What kind of cultural world makes shamanic experience meaningful, therapeutic, and socially functional?" It builds on Devereux's insight while redirecting analytic attention from individual psychopathology to the collective symbolic systems within which altered states are embedded.

## Key Synthesis

The literature surveyed in this chunk supports several overarching conclusions:

1. **Shaman personality cannot be reduced to Western diagnostic categories.** Devereux's framework and Silverman's schizophrenia comparison both demonstrate that the same experiential phenomena have fundamentally different meanings, courses, and outcomes in different cultural contexts.
2. **Projective testing can complement ethnographic observation.** The Boyer group's Rorschach studies represented an ambitious attempt to bring standardized psychological measurement to bear on shamanic personality, with suggestive — if debated — results differentiating genuine practitioners from impostors.
3. **Psychoanalytic theory provides useful but limited models.** Freud, Erikson, Greenacre, and Kris each offered concepts — the unconscious, developmental stages, impostor dynamics, regression in service of the ego — that illuminate facets of shamanic experience, but none fully accounts for the cultural embeddedness of the phenomenon.
4. **Cultural variables are the essential missing dimension.** Dobkin de Rios's call to examine belief systems, attitudes, and values as determinants of hallucinogenic experience points toward a more holistic, culturally grounded approach — one that studies not just the shaman's psyche, but the world of meanings in which that psyche operates.
5. **Hallucinogenic plants are culturally mediated technologies.** Peyote among the Apache and ayahuasca in the Amazon are not simply psychoactive substances; they are elements embedded in complex ritual, cosmological, and therapeutic systems that determine their effects and social functions.
