# Tryptamine Hallucinogens and Consciousness

Tryptamine hallucinogens, particularly DMT (N,N-dimethyltryptamine), psilocybin, and the ayahuasca complex, occupy a unique position in pharmacology and consciousness studies because of their structural relationship to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), their endogenous presence in the human brain, and the extraordinary intensity of the experiences they produce. Terence McKenna's 1983 lecture at Esalen Institute provides a detailed phenomenological account of these compounds and their implications for understanding consciousness.

## The Tryptamine Family

The tryptamine hallucinogens are tryptophan-derived compounds sharing a common indole ring structure with serotonin, the major neurotransmitter in the human brain. This structural similarity is central to their mechanism of action and their relationship to consciousness:

- **DMT**: 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (when phosphorylated, becomes psilocybin). Endogenous to the human brain. Rapid onset (15 seconds when smoked), brief duration (5 minutes), extreme intensity.
- **Psilocybin**: 4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine. Metabolized to psilocin (4-hydroxy-DMT) in the gut. Slower onset (30 to 60 minutes), longer duration (4 to 6 hours), similar phenomenology to DMT.
- **Ayahuasca**: Oral preparation combining DMT-containing plants with beta-carboline MAO inhibitors, enabling 3 to 4 hours of intoxication.

The rapid onset and brief duration of DMT suggest the brain is "at home" with this compound, in contrast to synthetic hallucinogens like LSD that are retained in the body for extended periods.

## Phenomenology of the DMT Experience

### Onset and Initial Effects

The DMT experience begins approximately 15 seconds after smoking. The onset is characterized by:

- An auditory phenomenon described as "ripping cellophane" or crumpling plastic
- An ascending tone
- Shifting geometric surfaces of migrating, changing colored forms (the "generic hallucinogenic modality")
- A complete mode shift occurring over approximately 30 seconds as all available receptor sites become occupied

### The Hyperspace Experience

Following the mode shift, the experiencer arrives in what McKenna describes as a space with the feeling of being underground, insulated, and domed. This space has been called the "merry go raum" (from the German "raum," meaning space), a rotating room-like environment. Key characteristics include:

- The sensation of becoming a child in eternity
- Encounters with what McKenna calls "self-transforming machine elves," described as dynamically contorting topological modules distinct from the surrounding background, which is itself undergoing continuous transformation
- These entities are described as fractal reflections of a previously hidden and suddenly autonomous part of one's own psyche
- The entities communicate through sounds that pass from "things heard to things beheld," a translinguistic object that cannot be translated into ordinary language

### The Logos

Both psilocybin and DMT are described as invoking the Logos, meaning they work directly on language centers of the brain. An important aspect of the experience is interior dialogue, and the experiencer must decide whether to engage with the incoming signal. The experience is described as a confrontation with an alien intelligence that presents translinguistic information complexes.

### Emotional Quality

The experience includes:
- A touch of terror that marks it as "existentially authentic"
- Profound astonishment that persists and persists
- The entities reassure: "Don't be alarmed. Remember, and do what we are doing."
- The entities urge the experiencer to control their wonder and focus on understanding

## Glossolalia and Primal Language

Under the influence of tryptamines, a phenomenon distinct from classical glossolalia occurs. Rather than the unconscious speaking-in-tongues of religious traditions, this is described as a brain state allowing expression of the "assembly language" behind ordinary language. McKenna draws parallels to Robert Graves' concept of a primal language in "The White Goddess" and the Kabbalistic language described in the Zohar, an "Ursprache" that emerges from within.

## Quantum Mechanical Correlates

McKenna argues that quantum mechanical phenomena are central to hallucinogenesis. The observation that moving a single atom on the molecular ring of an inactive compound can render it highly psychoactive demonstrates, in his view, a "dynamic linkage at the formative level between quantum mechanically described matter and mind." The tryptamine experience is characterized by information presented three-dimensionally and deployed fourth-dimensionally, coded as light and evolving surfaces.

## The Stoned Ape Hypothesis

McKenna proposed that the Stropharia cubensis (Psilocybe cubensis) mushroom may not have evolved on Earth. Within the mushroom trance, he reports being informed that it is a symbiotic organism that desires deeper relationship with humanity. It achieved early symbiosis by associating with domesticated cattle and through them, human nomads, spreading wherever human genes traveled. The mushroom's "re-engineering" involves a mycelial network strategy on planetary surfaces and spore dispersion for galactic radiation.

## Shamanism and the Tryptamine Experience

### Shamanic Selection

In archaic societies, potential shamans are identified by unusual traits: epilepsy, survival of lightning strikes, recovery from near-fatal illness, or hypersensitivity to trance states. McKenna observes that experienced shamans are distinguished by extraordinary centeredness, intellectual sophistication, and deep knowledge of plant chemistry (which they describe as knowledge of "spirits" or energy fields).

### The Black Hole Effect

McKenna describes a "black hole effect" surrounding DMT: once one knows about it, it becomes extremely difficult to communicate the experience to others. The more precisely one articulates the experience, the less others are able to understand it. He compares this to the silence of those who attain enlightenment.

### Absence in Cultural Traditions

A notable observation is the absence of DMT-specific motifs in familiar cultural and artistic traditions. While LSD experiences correlate with Mahayana Buddhist iconography, the unique phenomenology of DMT appears absent from all major artistic and religious traditions known to McKenna. He speculates this may relate to Borges' "Sect of the Phoenix," a hypothetical universal secret cult.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Destruction of Mushroom Cults

The classic mushroom cults of Mexico were destroyed by the Spanish conquest. The Franciscans, who assumed a monopoly on theophagy (eating the body of God), encountered indigenous peoples calling a mushroom teonanacatl ("flesh of the gods"). The Inquisition drove the practice into the mountains of Oaxaca, where it survived in a few villages until rediscovered by Valentina and Gordon Wasson in the 1950s.

### The Logos in Hibernation

Drawing on Philip K. Dick's novel "Valis," McKenna draws parallels between the mushroom consciousness and a "creature of pure information" that was buried with the Nag Hammadi library circa 370 AD and became active again when the texts were translated and read in 1947. Like mushroom consciousness, this entity was imagined as a thing of pure information.

## Implications for Consciousness Studies

McKenna argues that:
- Dreams and hallucinations are major quantum mechanical phenomena that everyone experiences
- The tryptamine state may be transtemporal, representing a "stepping out of the moving image of time and into eternity"
- Human cerebral cortex is the most richly organized material in the universe
- The experience should be "constellated outward from the perceiving self" as the primary datum
- Science and mathematics may be culture-bound, making the contents of shamanic experience inadmissible to mainstream science despite being "the source of novelty and the cutting edge of the ingression of the novel into the plenum of being"

## Cautionary Notes

McKenna emphasizes that all tryptamine compounds are potentially dangerous. He advises building up to experiences gradually, moving carefully, reflecting deeply, and always attempting to map experiences onto the broader history and philosophical achievements of the species. "The library is the first place to go when looking into taking a new compound."
