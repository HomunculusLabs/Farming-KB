# Falconer Growing Mushrooms in Greenhouses

Chapter V of William Falconer's *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891)
explores the cultivation of mushrooms within greenhouses, describing how
this crop could be profitably integrated into existing floriculture and
vegetable-forcing operations. The chapter demonstrates the Victorian
market gardener's principle of "doubling up" crops wherever possible.

## Availability of Greenhouse Space

Any greenhouse could accommodate mushrooms -- fruit houses, flower houses,
or vegetable houses. One of the chief advantages was that mushrooms grew
to perfection in parts of the greenhouse nearly worthless for other
purposes, particularly under the stages where nothing else grew well,
though rhubarb and asparagus might be forced there, and witloof chicory
and dandelion blanched.

## Cool vs. Hot Houses

Cool greenhouses were always better for mushrooms than hothouses. Cool
houses were seldom kept below 45 to 50 degrees in winter, while hothouses
ran 60 to 70 degrees at night with a ten to twenty degree rise by day --
too hot for mushrooms. It was relatively easy to keep mushroom beds warm
in a cool house by covering with hay or boxing over, but very difficult
to keep them cool enough in a hothouse.

## On Greenhouse Benches

Beds formed directly on greenhouse benches were made one board deep (eight
to ten inches) of short, fresh [[falconer-manure-for-mushroom-beds]].
After spawning and casing with soil, a layer of straw litter or hay
prevented surface drying, supplemented by light sprinkling every day or
two (not enough to soak through). As young mushrooms appeared, the
mulching was replaced with shutters raised above the bed, or strong calico
hung curtain-fashion over the beds.

Falconer's principal objection to bench beds was their susceptibility to
frequent changes of temperature and moisture, and to drying out. In
midwinter they were manageable, but as spring advanced and sun brightness
increased, the beds became increasingly prone to drying.

## Mr. Gardner's Frame Method

Mr. J. G. Gardner, who managed a 900-foot range of glasshouses for
forcing fruit and vegetables, grew mushrooms in hotbed frames inside his
greenhouses. By attending to ventilating, keeping close, covering, or
leaving bare, he regulated the mushroom bed temperature regardless of the
greenhouse atmosphere. The frames were common hotbed box frames seven feet
wide carrying three-and-a-half-foot sashes, run along the middle of the
greenhouses.

A temporary staging was erected over these frames for growing peas,
beans, or other light-loving crops above, while mushrooms occupied the
dark quarters below. The beds were prepared with New York stable manure
brought direct from the cars, with no preliminary preparation. A layer
of loam was spread over the surface and forked into the bed to form an
earthy mat. Spawn was sown in drills and covered with loam. The beds,
made in October, bore in December, and by March 14 Gardner reported "large
basketfuls of the finest mushrooms I have ever seen, some of them measuring
five inches in diameter before being fully expanded."

## Critical Temperature Control

Gardner emphasized one vital point for all who grew mushrooms in frames
or under greenhouse benches: sudden changes of temperature must be
avoided. While light was good for mushrooms, it caused temperature rises
that must be guarded against. All glass exposed to light or heat should be
covered with a non-conducting material such as rye straw. Neglect of this
simple matter was "the cause of many failures in growing in frames and
greenhouses."

## Under Greenhouse Benches

Open spaces under greenhouse stages were good places for beds provided
the floor was dry. A wet floor soaked and chilled the beds. Beds should
not be built close to hot-water pipes, steam pipes, or smoke flues, as
the heat would bake and render unproductive the parts nearest them, and
crack and spoil nearby mushroom caps. The middle bench was best adapted
because it had more headroom and was typically free from pipes.

## Among Other Plants

Mushrooms sometimes sprang spontaneously among roses, carnations, violets,
and other bench crops, from natural [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]] in the loam or manure. These were generally the common
brick-spawn variety, but occasionally a larger "horse" mushroom appeared.
A fair crop could be obtained by planting spawn pieces between plants and
filling holes with fresh dry horse droppings containing a piece of spawn,
covered with an inch of loam.

## In Rose Houses

George Savage grew mushrooms successfully under rose house benches at Mr.
Kimball's greenhouses in Rochester, New York. In early fall the rose house
was kept cool, benefiting mushroom beds that got warmth from fermenting
manure. As November advanced and bed heat waned, the rose houses were
"started" with artificial warmth that benefited the growing mushrooms.
