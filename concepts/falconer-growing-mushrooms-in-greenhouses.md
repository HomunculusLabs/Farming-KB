## Mr. Gardner's Frame Method

Mr. J. G. Gardner, who managed a 900-foot range of glasshouses for
forcing fruit and vegetables, grew mushrooms in hotbed frames inside his
greenhouses. By attending to ventilating, keeping close, covering, or
leaving bare, he regulated the mushroom bed temperature regardless of the
greenhouse atmosphere. The frames were common hotbed box frames seven feet
wide carrying three-and-a-half-foot sashes, run along the middle of the
greenhouses.

A temporary staging was erected over these frames for growing peas,
beans, or other light-loving crops above, while mushrooms occupied the
dark quarters below. The beds were prepared with New York stable manure
brought direct from the cars, with no preliminary preparation. A layer
of loam was spread over the surface and forked into the bed to form an
earthy mat. Spawn was sown in drills and covered with loam. The beds,
made in October, bore in December, and by March 14 Gardner reported "large
basketfuls of the finest mushrooms I have ever seen, some of them measuring
five inches in diameter before being fully expanded."

## Critical Temperature Control

Gardner emphasized one vital point for all who grew mushrooms in frames
or under greenhouse benches: sudden changes of temperature must be
avoided. While light was good for mushrooms, it caused temperature rises
that must be guarded against. All glass exposed to light or heat should be
covered with a non-conducting material such as rye straw. Neglect of this
simple matter was "the cause of many failures in growing in frames and
greenhouses."

## Under Greenhouse Benches

Open spaces under greenhouse stages were good places for beds provided
the floor was dry. A wet floor soaked and chilled the beds. Beds should
not be built close to hot-water pipes, steam pipes, or smoke flues, as
the heat would bake and render unproductive the parts nearest them, and
crack and spoil nearby mushroom caps. The middle bench was best adapted
because it had more headroom and was typically free from pipes.
