# Leaf-Based Photoperiodic Perception

## Overview

In most flowering plants, the organ responsible for measuring day length and
triggering the transition to flowering is not the shoot tip where flowers form,
but the leaf. This discovery, which overturned assumptions derived from
phototropism research, revealed that leaves function as the primary
photoperiodic sensors. Leaves use phytochrome photoreceptors to detect light
quality and duration, then communicate this information via a mobile protein
signal called florigen. The finding that leaves are the "eyes" of photoperiodism
is one of the most counterintuitive discoveries in plant biology.

## Darwin's Misleading Analogy

The initial expectation that the shoot tip would serve as the photoperiodic
sensor arose directly from Darwin's phototropism experiments. In those landmark
studies, Charles Darwin and his son Francis demonstrated that the coleoptile
tip was the light-sensing organ for directional bending: when the tip was
covered with a lightproof cap, the seedling went completely blind to
directional light.

But when the middle section was covered with a lightproof tube, bending
proceeded normally. This established the paradigm that the apex was the
plant's primary sensory organ for light-mediated responses.

Given that flowering occurs at the shoot apical meristem (the growing tip),
it was natural to assume the meristem also sensed the light conditions
regulating flowering timing. This assumption was reinforced by the anatomical
logic that perception and response sites should be co-located. This persisted
for decades after Garner and Allard's discovery of photoperiodism in 1920.

## The Critical Illumination Experiments

The leaf's role was established through night-break illumination experiments in
the mid-twentieth century. Scientists directed a narrow beam of red light onto
different parts of a plant during the middle of the night and observed which
illumination sites were sufficient to regulate flowering.

When a beam of red light was shone on a single leaf during a long night,
flowering was regulated in the entire plant just as effectively as if the
entire plant had been illuminated. The single illuminated leaf could trigger
the full flowering response at every meristem.

Conversely, when all leaves were surgically removed and only the stem and
apical meristem were illuminated, the plant remained completely insensitive
to the light treatment regardless of intensity or duration.

These experiments established three fundamental principles:

1. A single leaf is sufficient to perceive the photoperiodic signal and
   communicate it to the entire plant.

2. The shoot apical meristem, despite being the site where flowering is
   initiated, cannot perceive photoperiodic light signals.

3. The leaf must produce a mobile signal that travels to distant meristems
   to trigger flowering.

This was particularly surprising because phytochrome photoreceptors are present
throughout all plant tissues. The specificity lies in the downstream signalling
context: only leaf cells express the full complement of genes needed to convert
the phytochrome signal into the mobile flowering stimulus.

## The Florigen Hypothesis and Its Resolution

The mobile signal was hypothesised for over seventy years before its molecular
identity was determined. Named "florigen" (flower-inducing substance) by
Soviet physiologist Mikhail Chailakhyan in 1937, it was demonstrated through
grafting experiments where a flowering-inducing signal transmitted across a
graft junction from an inductive donor to a non-flowering recipient.

The molecular identity of florigen was established in 2005 when several groups
independently demonstrated that FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), a small protein of
approximately 20 kDa encoded in leaf cells under inductive photoperiods, is
the primary mobile signal.

FT belongs to the phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein (PEBP) family and
moves through the phloem from source leaves to the apical meristem. At the
meristem, FT interacts with the bZIP transcription factor FD and 14-3-3
proteins to form a florigen activation complex (FAC) that activates floral
identity genes such as APETALA1 (AP1), converting the vegetative meristem
into an inflorescence meristem.

## Phytochrome Function in Leaf Cells

The phytochrome photoreceptors mediating photoperiodic perception are
localised in leaf cells, particularly in the mesophyll parenchyma and vascular
bundle tissues. In leaf cells, phytochrome absorbs red and far-red light and
undergoes the characteristic Pr/Pfr interconversion.

During daylight, phytochrome accumulates in the active Pfr form. When darkness
falls, Pfr gradually reverts to inactive Pr through thermal dark reversion.
The plant measures continuous darkness duration by monitoring the phytochrome
pool state.

If the dark period exceeds a critical length (short-day plants) or falls
below a critical length (long-day plants), the photoperiodic pathway is
activated in the leaf, leading to FT production and export to the meristem.

## The Circadian Clock as Photoperiodic Timer

The signalling cascade involves the circadian clock as an essential timing
component. The clock generates an endogenous approximately 24-hour rhythm that
gates photoperiodic gene expression, ensuring correct timing relative to dawn
and dusk.

CONSTANS (CO) is a central component. CO mRNA is expressed with a circadian
rhythm, peaking 12 to 16 hours after dawn. However, CO protein is unstable in
darkness and degraded by COP1 and SPA proteins. In light, photoreceptors
including phytochrome and cryptochrome stabilise CO by inactivating COP1.
Only when CO accumulates during light does it activate FT expression.

## Short-Day Versus Long-Day Leaf Sensing

The same leaf-based mechanism operates in both plant types, but regulatory
wiring is inverted:

In **long-day plants** such as Arabidopsis and barley, CO protein is
stabilised by light. Under long summer days, CO accumulates during the
extended light period and activates FT in the evening. Under short winter days,
CO is degraded before it can activate FT.

In **short-day plants** such as chrysanthemums and soybeans, genes including GIGANTEA repress flowering under long days. Under short days, phytochrome-mediated detection of extended darkness relieves this repression, allowing FT homologues to be expressed and triggering flowering.

## Grafting Evidence for Systemic Signalling

Classic grafting experiments provide the most compelling evidence. A single photoinduced leaf grafted onto a multi-branched recipient plant can induce flowering at every meristem on the recipient, including those far distant from the graft site. The signal's ability to move systemically through the phloem and trigger flowering simultaneously at all meristems underscores the leaf's role as the master regulatory organ for photoperiodic responses.

## Agricultural Applications

Understanding leaf-based photoperiodic perception has direct horticultural applications. Since any single leaf can regulate flowering, even minimal light exposure to leaves during the dark period can disrupt photoperiodic control. In greenhouse chrysanthemum production, night-break lighting must reach leaves to prevent premature flowering. For long-day crops such as lettuce, supplemental lighting must ensure leaves perceive sufficient day length to prevent premature bolting. Defoliation can delay flowering by removing photoperiodic sensors, a principle exploited in pruning strategies.
