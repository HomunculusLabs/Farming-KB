## Livestock Movement Systems
Access design must accommodate livestock movement between paddocks, shelters,
water, and handling facilities. Mollison emphasises designing laneway systems
that allow animals to move without stress or handler effort. Key principles:
- Laneways should connect all paddocks to central handling facilities without
  requiring animals to pass through other paddocks (reducing disease
  transmission and social disruption)
- Gate placement should allow flexible grazing rotation, enabling any paddock
  combination without complex animal movements
- Laneway width should suit the species and herd size -- cattle require wider
  laneways than sheep or poultry
- Shade trees along laneways reduce heat stress during movement, especially
  in hot climates where livestock must travel between distant paddocks
## Integration with Water Management
Roads and paths are inseparable from water management design. Every road is a
water catchment surface, and every path is a potential drainage line. Mollison
recommends designing road crowns, culverts, and energy dissipators as integral
components of the site water management system. Road runoff can be directed to
stock tanks, [[query-how-do-i-build-and-manage-a-greywater-system-for-garden-irrigation]], or wetland systems. In urban contexts,
permeable paving materials allow rainwater infiltration rather than generating
stormwater runoff. The most elegant access designs make water management
invisible -- the road appears simply as a road, while simultaneously
performing sophisticated hydrological functions.
