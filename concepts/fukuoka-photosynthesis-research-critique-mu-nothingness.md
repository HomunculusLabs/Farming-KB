## Photosynthesis Research as Empty Theorizing

The standard trajectory of photosynthesis research follows a predictable pattern of increasing abstraction from reality. It begins with morphological observation of the rice plant, proceeds to dissection and analysis, expands into plant ecology, and culminates in laboratory, pot, and field experiments. At each step, the conditions grow more controlled, more artificial, and more removed from the complex, irreducible conditions of a natural field.

The results obtained under these special conditions, Fukuoka argues, have little relevance to what actually happens in a living, breathing ecosystem. The entire enterprise rests on what he calls the "current high-yield theory," expressed as a simple equation:

> Yields = starch produced by photosynthesis − starch consumed by respiration

Proponents of this theory claim that yields can be increased by maximizing photosynthesis while carefully balancing production against consumption. The logic appears sound on paper. Yet the practical results tell a different story. A yield of 22 bushels per quarter-acre is already quite good by any reasonable standard, while the vaunted 26–28 bushels achieved through scientific methods are attainable only on a very limited scale under carefully managed conditions.

## The Catch — Photosynthesis and Respiration Are Linked

The fatal flaw in the high-yield theory is that it treats photosynthesis and respiration as independent variables that can be separately optimized. In reality, the same conditions that favor photosynthesis — abundant sunlight, high temperatures, adequate water and nutrients — simultaneously promote respiration.

This means that high photosynthesis combined with high respiration does not yield maximum starch storage. Conversely, lower photosynthesis paired with lower respiration can actually produce higher net yields. The relationship is not a simple arithmetic balance but a dynamic, interconnected process that resists compartmentalization.

Fukuoka poses a pointed question born of hard experience:

> "How often have farmers and scientists tried techniques that maximize starch production only to find large rice plants that lodge?"

The answer, of course, is: repeatedly. The pursuit of maximum photosynthetic output tends to produce lush, overgrown plants structurally unable to support their own weight. Fukuoka offers a radically different path:

> "A much easier path to high yields would be to hold down respiration and grow smaller plants that consume less starch."

He illustrates the complexity with three courses of development:

- **Course 1 (40°C):** High temperatures cause root rot, leading to poor water uptake. Stomata close to conserve moisture, reducing CO₂ intake. Photosynthesis slows, but respiration continues unabated. The result is low yield.

- **Course 2 (30°C):** Good growing conditions prevail. Photosynthesis and respiration remain in balance. This produces the highest yield — not through maximization of either process, but through their harmonious interaction.

- **Course 3 (low temperatures):** Conditions are merely fair, but root activity remains healthy. The result is a normal, dependable yield.
