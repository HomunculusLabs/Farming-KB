# Fungal Cultivation Methods

## Overview

Cultivation and preservation of fungal cultures are essential elements of [[fungal-phylogeny-systematics]] and biodiversity studies. Because fungi are extremely diverse, multiple methods of cultivation and preservation are required to ensure the viability and integrity of cultures over time. The methods range from simple serial transfer to sophisticated cryopreservation, each with distinct advantages and limitations for different fungal groups. Cultures serve as the foundation for [[fungal-phylogeny-systematics]], [[fungal-genetics]], ecological, physiological, genetic, and conservation research.

## Isolation Techniques

The method most commonly used to detect and quantify fungi from natural substrata is isolation from surface-sterilized tissue fragments onto nutrient agar media. Surface sterilization typically uses sequential immersion in ethanol (30-70%) and sodium hypochlorite (1-2%) solutions, with sterile water rinses between treatments. The specific sterilization protocol must be adjusted for the substratum type: delicate leaf tissue requires milder treatment than woody stem segments.

After surface sterilization, tissue fragments are plated on nutrient media. Common media include potato dextrose agar (PDA), malt extract agar (MEA), corn meal agar (CMA), and oatmeal agar. Selective agents are often added to suppress unwanted organisms: antibiotics such as streptomycin and chloramphenicol suppress bacterial growth, while specific fungicides suppress competitor fungi. See [[biodiversity-fungal-culture-media]] for detailed medium formulations.

## Direct Isolation and Baiting Techniques

Beyond direct tissue plating, several additional isolation methods are widely used in [[fungal-biodiversity-sampling-design]]:

- **Dilution plating**: Substratum samples are suspended in sterile water, serially diluted, and plated to isolate individual colonies. Useful for quantifying fungal propagules in soil or water samples, including [[biodiversity-fungi-freshwater-aquatic-habitats]].
- **Baiting techniques**: Selective substrata are placed in environmental samples to capture specific fungal groups. Common baits include hair, snake skin, and insect exoskeletons for coprophilous-fungi|coprophilous and keratinophilic fungi; pollen or cellulose baits for aquatic hyphomycetes; and plant material for saprotrophic-fungi|saprotrophs. Baiting is particularly valuable for [[bloomfield-chytrid-fungi-early-fungal-evolution]] and other microfungal groups difficult to isolate by direct plating.
- **Moist chamber methods**: Substrata (leaf litter, dung, wood fragments) are placed in sealed humid chambers and monitored over days to weeks for fungal sporulation. This technique is effective for documenting [[fungal-communities-extreme-environments]] on natural substrata and often reveals species not recovered by plating methods.

## Culturing from Sporocarps

For [[macrofungal-sampling-design-plots-transects]], tissue culture from fresh sporocarps is a primary isolation approach. Inner tissue is excised from the pileus or stipe under sterile conditions and transferred to [[biodiversity-fungal-culture-media]]. Specimens should be collected with a range of developmental stages in mind, as younger, actively growing tissue typically establishes more readily in culture.

Maintaining diagnostic features in culture is critical: cultures should be preserved in ways that allow researchers to connect the isolated strain to the original specimen and its [[fungal-species-concept-and-taxonomy]]. Voucher specimens and detailed collection notes are essential links between field observations and living cultures.

## Culture Conditions

Incubation temperature, light regime, and atmosphere affect both growth rate and the ability of fungi to produce diagnostic structures. Standard incubation is at 20-25°C in darkness or with alternating light/dark cycles. Some fungi require specific conditions:
- **Thermophilic species**: Incubation at 35-50°C
- **Psychrophilic species**: Incubation at 4-15°C
- **Light-requiring sporulation**: UV or near-UV exposure to induce fruiting
- **Endophytic and entomopathogenic species**: Specific media requirements (Bacon 1990; Singleton et al. 1992; Humber 1994)

[[endophytic-fungi-in-grasslands]] often require specialized isolation conditions and selective media to separate them from fast-growing surface contaminants.

## Preservation Methods

### Serial Transfer

The simplest method: periodic transfer of mycelium to fresh agar. Cultures are transferred every 1-6 months depending on the taxon. Oomycetes and some basidiomycetes (Boletus, Coprinus, Cortinarius, Mycena) may require monthly transfers at 16°C. The major disadvantages are labor intensity, risk of contamination, and potential loss of sporulation capacity or genetic stability through repeated subculturing.

### Oil Overlay

Cultures grown on agar slants are covered with ~10 mm of sterilized mineral oil or liquid paraffin. Viable for several years, or up to 32 years in exceptional cases, at room temperature or 15-20°C. Appropriate for mycelial or nonsporulating cultures not amenable to freezing. Oil overlay reduces mite infestations but has the disadvantage that fungi continue to grow slowly, potentially selecting for mutants adapted to the adverse conditions.

### Drying and Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying)

Spore-producing fungi can be preserved by lyophilization. Spores are collected, suspended in a protective medium (often containing skim milk or sucrose as cryoprotectants), frozen, and dried under vacuum. This method preserves genetic stability well and is space-efficient. However, many basidiomycetes and nonsporulating fungi cannot be preserved this way.

### Cryopreservation

Storage in liquid nitrogen (-196°C) or mechanical freezers (-80°C) is the gold standard for long-term [[fungal-culture-preservation]]. Cryoprotectants (glycerol, DMSO) are added to prevent ice crystal damage. This method maintains genetic stability and viability for decades. Most fungal groups can be cryopreserved, though some sensitive taxa require optimization of cooling rates and cryoprotectant concentration. See [[fungal-culture-preservation]] for protocols.

### Distilled Water Storage

A simple, low-cost method: agar plugs or sclerotia are stored in sterile distilled water in screw-cap vials at room temperature or 4°C. Suitable for many sporulating fungi. Cultures remain viable for years in some cases.

## Quality Control

All preservation methods require periodic viability testing. Cultures should be checked for contamination, genetic stability (particularly important for type specimens and reference strains), and retention of diagnostic morphological characters. Some characters are particularly prone to loss: the ability to produce sexual fruiting structures (teleomorphs) may be lost after repeated transfers, and spore production, pigmentation, or colony morphology may drift from the original description. Such character loss compromises the utility of cultures for [[fungal-species-recognition-genetics]] and [[fungal-rdna-primers-molecular-identification]].

## Molecular Considerations

For [[fungal-phylogeny-systematics]] and [[dna-barcoding-fungal-identification]] studies, cultures must be preserved in ways that maintain DNA integrity. Cryopreservation is preferred for long-term storage of cultures intended for molecular work. Cultures maintained by serial transfer may accumulate mutations or lose plasmids over time, compromising their utility as reference material.

## Role in Biodiversity Studies

Living cultures are indispensable for the full range of fungal biodiversity research. They provide the material for [[fungal-phylogeny-systematics]], [[fungal-bioremediation-case-studies]], physiological experiments, and [[formulation-and-delivery-of-fungal-inocula]]. Major culture collections such as ATCC, CBS, and regional repositories maintain reference strains that serve as standards for [[fungal-species-concept-and-taxonomy]] and enable reproducible science. Cultures complement [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] by preserving living representatives of documented biodiversity, forming a dual preservation strategy essential for long-term [[molecular-methods-fungal-taxonomy-and-diversity]] and conservation work.
The interplay between living cultures and dried voucher specimens is critical: without a herbarium voucher, a culture loses its taxonomic context, and without a living culture, a specimen cannot provide physiological or genetic data beyond what DNA extraction from preserved material allows.
