Because fungi are extremely diverse, multiple methods of cultivation and preservation are required to ensure the viability and integrity of cultures over time. The methods range from simple serial transfer to sophisticated cryopreservation, each with distinct advantages and limitations for different fungal groups. Cultures serve as the foundation for [[fungal-genetics]], ecological, physiological, genetic, and conservation research.

## Isolation Techniques

The method most commonly used to detect and quantify fungi from natural substrata is isolation from surface-sterilized tissue fragments onto nutrient agar media. Surface sterilization typically uses sequential immersion in ethanol (30-70%) and sodium hypochlorite (1-2%) solutions, with sterile water rinses between treatments. The specific sterilization protocol must be adjusted for the substratum type: delicate leaf tissue requires milder treatment than woody stem segments.

After surface sterilization, tissue fragments are plated on nutrient media. Common media include potato dextrose agar (PDA), malt extract agar (MEA), corn meal agar (CMA), and oatmeal agar. Selective agents are often added to suppress unwanted organisms: antibiotics such as streptomycin and chloramphenicol suppress bacterial growth, while specific fungicides suppress competitor fungi. See [[fungal-biodiversity-sampling-design]]:

- **Dilution plating**: Substratum samples are suspended in sterile water, serially diluted, and plated to isolate individual colonies. Useful for quantifying fungal propagules in soil or water samples, including [[bloomfield-chytrid-fungi-early-fungal-evolution]] and other microfungal groups difficult to isolate by direct plating.
- **Moist chamber methods**: Substrata (leaf litter, dung, wood fragments) are placed in sealed humid chambers and monitored over days to weeks for fungal sporulation. This technique is effective for documenting [[macrofungal-sampling-design-plots-transects]], tissue culture from fresh sporocarps is a primary isolation approach. Inner tissue is excised from the pileus or stipe under sterile conditions and transferred to [[fungal-species-concept-and-taxonomy]]. Voucher specimens and detailed collection notes are essential links between field observations and living cultures.

## Culture Conditions

Incubation temperature, light regime, and atmosphere affect both growth rate and the ability of fungi to produce diagnostic structures. Standard incubation is at 20-25°C in darkness or with alternating light/dark cycles. Some fungi require specific conditions:
- **Thermophilic species**: Incubation at 35-50°C
- **Psychrophilic species**: Incubation at 4-15°C
- **Light-requiring sporulation**: UV or near-UV exposure to induce fruiting
- **Endophytic and entomopathogenic species**: Specific media requirements (Bacon 1990; Singleton et al. 1992; Humber 1994)
