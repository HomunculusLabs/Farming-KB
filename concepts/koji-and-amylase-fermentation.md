## Koji and Amylase Fermentation

Koji is one of the most important and sophisticated fermentation technologies ever developed. It is the cultured growth of the mold Aspergillus oryzae on cooked grain, most commonly rice or barley. Koji produces powerful amylase enzymes that convert starch to sugar, enabling the fermentation of grains into sake, miso, soy sauce, shochu, amazake, and many other traditional Asian foods and beverages. Understanding koji opens the door to a fascinating world of enzyme-driven fermentation.

## What Is Koji?

Koji (in Japanese; qu in Chinese; nuruk in Korean) is the product of inoculating cooked, cooled grain with spores of Aspergillus oryzae (or related species) and allowing the mold to grow under controlled conditions. The growing mold produces a dense network of hyphae that penetrate the grain and secrete enzymes -- primarily amylases for starch conversion and proteases for protein breakdown.

The result is grain that has been pre-digested by fungal enzymes. When koji is mixed with water and additional cooked grain, the amylase enzymes rapidly convert starches to sugars, creating a sweet mash that can then be fermented by yeast or bacteria.

## The Science: Amylase Enzymes

Amylase enzymes are the key to koji"s function. There are two main types relevant to fermentation:

### Alpha-Amylase

Alpha-amylase randomly cleaves starch molecules at internal bonds, producing shorter chains of sugar called dextrins. This is called "liquefaction" because it breaks down the thick starch paste into a thinner liquid. Alpha-amylase works quickly and produces a range of sugar chain lengths.

### Beta-Amylase

Beta-amylase works from the ends of starch chains, cleaving off maltose units (a two-sugar molecule) one at a time. This is called "saccharification" because it produces sweet, fermentable sugar. Beta-amylase is slower but more precise than alpha-amylase.

Koji produces both alpha and beta amylase in quantities that far exceed what occurs naturally in barley malt. This is why koji can convert rice starch to sugar so efficiently -- the enzyme concentration is very high.
