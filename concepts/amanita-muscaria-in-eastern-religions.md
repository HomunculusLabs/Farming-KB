# Amanita Muscaria in Eastern Religions

The [[amanita-muscaria-herb-of-immortality-soma-hypothesis]] source argues that [[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]] theory, first proposed by R. [[gordon-wasson-comparative-method-ethnomycology]] in "Soma: [[edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]] of Immortality" (1968), identifies the [[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]] as the original Soma plant.

The Herb of Immortality author extends Wasson's argument by providing:

- Detailed preparation methods that match Vedic descriptions
- Evidence that Soma was a living, renewable sacrament (not a simple plant tea)
- Linguistic connections between Soma and Amanita-related terminology
- Pharmacological evidence that the described effects match Amanita muscaria's known properties

### Vedic Preparation Descriptions

The [[rig-veda-soma-hymns-amanita-muscaria]] describes Soma as being:

- Pressed or pounded from a plant
- Filtered through wool
- Mixed with milk or barley
- Capable of producing immortality in those who consume it
- Described as "the king of plants"
- Associated with the moon and with immortality

### The Indo-European Connection

Soma (Vedic) and Haoma (Avestan) are linguistically cognate, both deriving from the Proto-Indo-European root *sauma-. This shared terminology reflects a shared sacramental practice that predates the split between the Indo-Aryan and Iranian branches of the Indo-European family -- a practice the author identifies as Amanita muscaria consumption.

## Hinduism and the Mushroom

### The Immortality Theme

The central promise of Hinduism -- liberation from the cycle of death and rebirth (moksha) -- is interpreted by the author as originating in the mushroom's apparent ability to "resurrect" after being dried and stored:

- The mushroom appears to die when dried
- It returns to life when rehydrated
- It produces a "living" sacrament from "dead" material
- This resurrection cycle mirrors the Hindu concept of reincarnation and ultimate liberation

### Amrita

The Hindu concept of Amrita -- the nectar of immortality that emerged from the churning of the cosmic ocean -- is linguistically connected to Amanita muscaria through the Indo-European root meaning "not-death":

- **a-mrita** = "not-death" (Sanskrit)
- **am-brosia** = "not-death" (Greek)
- **am-arta** = "not-death" (Persian)

All three terms refer to a substance that confers immortality, and all three, the author argues, originally referred to preparations of Amanita muscaria.

### The Churning of the Ocean

The Hindu myth of the churning of the ocean (Samudra Manthan) -- in which gods and demons cooperate to churn the cosmic ocean to obtain Amrita -- is interpreted as:

- A mythologized description of the preparation process
- The "ocean" represents the nutrient liquid (milk, juice, water)
- The "churning" represents the mixing/stirring process
- The Amrita that emerges is the finished sacrament

## The Feathered Serpent and Phoenix

### Quetzalcoatl

The [[amanita-muscaria-feathered-serpent-phoenix]] (Quetzalcoatl in Mesoamerica) is connected by the author to Amanita muscaria through:

- The serpent's association with transformation and rebirth
- The feathered appearance (resembling the white spots on the red cap)
- The association with knowledge and civilization
- The promise of return (the mushroom's ability to resurrect)

### The Phoenix

The Phoenix -- the mythical bird that dies in fire and is reborn from its own ashes -- parallels the mushroom's:

- Death (drying) and resurrection (rehydration)
- The fire association (mushrooms fruit after fire in some ecosystems)
- The eternal cycle of renewal
- The production of a fragrant substance (the dried mushroom's aroma)
