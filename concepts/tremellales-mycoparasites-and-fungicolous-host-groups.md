## Tremellales Mycoparasites and Fungicolous Host Groups

## Overview

Mycoparasitism — the parasitic relationship between fungi — represents one of the most ecologically significant yet understudied interactions in fungal biology. Among the major groups of mycoparasitic fungi, the order **Tremellales** within the subclass **Tremellomycetidae** stands out for its diversity of parasitic strategies, host ranges, and morphological adaptations. This page synthesizes the biology, taxonomy, and ecological roles of Tremellales mycoparasites and related [[fungicolous-fungi]] across major host groups.

## Tremellomycetidae: The Tremellales

### Dimorphic Life Cycle and Core Biology

Tremellales fungi exhibit a characteristic **dimorphic life cycle**, alternating between a yeast phase and a filamentous parasitic phase. This dimorphism is central to their ability to colonize living fungal hosts. Key structural features include:

- **Tremelloid basidium**: A deeply longitudinally septate basidium, often cruciate or transversely divided, that is the defining morphological trait of the group. This unique structure facilitates spore production during the sexual stage.
- **Septal pore apparatus**: Tremellales possess a specialized septal pore structure that regulates cytoplasmic flow between cells. This apparatus differs from those of other basidiomycete groups and is important for maintaining cellular integrity during host penetration.
- **Haustorial branches**: The primary tool of parasitism. Haustoria in Tremellales typically consist of a **bulbous base** that attaches to or penetrates the host cell wall, connected to a slender **tubular appendage** that interfaces with the host cytoplasm. Through these structures, the parasite draws nutrients from the living host cell.
- **Restricted dikaryotic growth**: Unlike many other basidiomycetes, Tremellales often exhibit limited dikaryotic mycelial development, with the parasitic phase frequently being predominantly monokaryotic or showing short-lived dikaryotic segments.
- **Host penetration and cytoplasmic degeneration**: Upon haustorial contact, host cells often undergo progressive cytoplasmic degeneration, indicating an active and destructive parasitic process rather than a benign biotrophic relationship.

### Micropore Openings in Specialized Genera

Genera such as **Syzygospora** and **Phragmoxenidium** possess unique **micropore openings** in their cell walls. These minute pores facilitate direct cytoplasmic exchange or nutrient uptake and represent a specialized adaptation distinct from the haustorial system seen in other Tremellales.

## Tremellales Families and Key Genera

### Tremellaceae

The largest and most diverse family within the order:

- **Tremella**: With over **170 described species**, *Tremella* is the most speciose genus. Its members parasitize an extraordinarily diverse range of hosts, including polypores, corticioid fungi, agarics, and even lichens. The jelly-like basidiocarps of many species are conspicuous on dead wood but originate from mycoparasitic activity.
- **Holtermannia**: A genus with club-shaped basidiocarps, associated with wood-inhabiting fungi.
- **Sirotrema**: An anamorphic or minimally differentiated genus linked to mycoparasitic lifestyles.
- **Trimorphomyces**: Characterized by polymorphic yeast states and filamentous parasitic phases.
- **Xenolachne**: A poorly known genus associated with corticioid hosts.
- **Bulleromyces**: Named after the mycologist A.H.R. Buller, known for its yeast-like state and association with basidiomycete hosts.
- **Itersonilia**: A dimorphic fungus notable for its role as a plant pathogen (e.g., causing leaf spot on chrysanthemums) but also exhibiting fungicolous tendencies.
- **Biatoropsis**: A genus primarily known from lichenicolous associations, forming galls or deformations on lichen thalli.

### Syzygosporaceae

This family has undergone significant taxonomic revision:

- **Syzygospora / Christiansenia / Carcinomyces**: These genera have been **synonymized** under a single conceptual framework, reflecting their shared biological features and morphological overlap. The group is best known for inducing **gall-like deformations** on host fungi.
- Hosts include **Phanerochaete**, **Gymnopus**, and **Marasmius**, among other basidiomycetes. The resulting galls are conspicuous, swollen, and often colorful structures on the host hymenophore or stipe.
- **Zygoconidia**: A distinctive type of asexual spore produced by these fungi, formed through the conjugation of adjacent cells — a rare reproductive strategy among fungi.
- ***Syzygospora bachmannii*** and ***S. physciacearum*** are **lichenicolous** species, attacking lichen fungi rather than free-living mushrooms or polypores, expanding the known ecological range of the family.

### Filobasidiaceae

A family containing genera of medical and ecological importance:

- **Filobasidium**: Characterized by long-chain basidia, found as epiphytic or weakly parasitic associates of other fungi.
- **Filobasidiella / [[cryptococcus-neoformans]]**: The teleomorph (*Filobasidiella*) and anamorph (*Cryptococcus neoformans*) complex is one of the most medically significant fungi globally, causing cryptococcosis in immunocompromised patients. Its ability to produce **haustoria** during the yeast phase on fungal hosts links it to the mycoparasitic heritage of the Tremellales.
- **Cystofilobasidium**: A genus of cold-adapted yeasts, some of which show weak mycoparasitic activity.

### Other Families

- **Rhynchogastremaceae**: A small, poorly characterized family with uncertain host associations.
- **Phragmoxenidiaceae**: Known for species like **Phragmoxenidium**, which produce micropore openings and associate with corticioid fungi.
- **Tetragoniomycetaceae**: Another understudied family within Tremellales, with limited documentation of mycoparasitic behavior.

## Mycoparasitism Beyond Tremellales

### Hymenomycetidae — Aphyllophorales as Mycoparasites

Members of the Aphyllophorales (polypores and corticioid fungi) include several **destructive necrotrophic mycoparasites**:

- **Bjerkandera**, **Lenzites**, **Trametes**, **Schizophyllum**, and **Pleurotus**: These well-known wood-decay genera also exhibit mycoparasitic activity, aggressively colonizing and destroying other fungi competing for the same substrate.
- **Hyphal interference**: The primary mechanism involves direct hyphal contact, where aggressive species secrete enzymes or toxic metabolites that lyse the hyphae of competitors, leading to rapid necrosis of the rival mycelium.
- ***Geotrichopsis mycoparasitica***: A specialized mycoparasite with a name reflecting its [[ecological-niche]], attacking other fungi in soil and wood substrates.
- ***Athelia arachnoidea***: A **lichenicolous** species that overgrows and kills lichen thalli, forming white, web-like patches on the surface.
- ***Marchandiomyces corallinus***: Another notable lichenicolous fungus producing coral-like fruiting structures on its hosts.

### Boletales and Agaricales — Fungicolous Members

Certain mushrooms have evolved to be obligate or facultative parasites of other mushrooms:

- **Asterophora**: Species such as *Asterophora parasitica* grow almost exclusively on decaying agaric sporocarps, producing chlamydospores instead of normal basidiospores — an adaptation to their fungicolous niche.
- **Squamanita**: This remarkable genus contains species that are **obligate mycoparasites**, replacing the fruiting bodies of other agarics (notably *Hebeloma* and *Inocybe* species) with their own mushrooms. The host's mycelium is co-opted entirely.

## Fungicolous Fungi by Host Group

Fungicolous (fungus-colonizing) fungi can be arranged into **five major groups** based on host specificity:

1. **Fungi on mushroom sporocarps (SCIF — Sporocarp-Inhabiting Fungi)**: The largest group, including Tremellales, Hypocreales, and many other taxa that colonize the cap, gills, stipe, or mycelial cords of agarics and boletes.
2. **Fungi on polypore basidiocarps**: A diverse assemblage colonizing the pore surface, context, and margins of bracket fungi.
3. **Fungi on corticioid fungi**: Species growing on resupinate (crust-like) basidiomata on wood and bark.
4. **Fungi on lichens (lichenicolous fungi)**: A specialized guild that includes Tremellales, Ascomycetes, and coelomycetes.
5. **Fungi on slime molds (myxomyceticolous fungi)**: Fungi colonizing the fruiting bodies or plasmodia of Myxogastria.

## Polyphagous Fungicolous Fungi

Some fungicolous species have exceptionally broad host ranges, attacking multiple fungal groups:

- ***Clonostachys rosea*** (syn. *Gliocladium roseum*): A hyperparasite and biocontrol agent known to attack a wide range of plant-pathogenic fungi, including *Botrytis*, *Fusarium*, and *Rhizoctonia*.
- ***Calcarisporium arbuscula***: A common fungicolous species found on diverse mushroom hosts, forming branching, tree-like conidiophores.
- ***Acremonium strictum***: A ubiquitous soil fungus with broad fungicolous capabilities, isolated from numerous mushroom and polypore hosts.
- ***Verticillium luteo-album***: A polyphagous species colonizing sporocarps of many agarics and polypores, often appearing as white mycelial patches.
- ***Sistotrema brinkmannii***: A corticioid basidiomycete that overgrows and destroys other wood-inhabiting fungi.
- ***Athelia arachnoidea*** / ***Fibulorhizoctonia carotae***: These taxa represent teleomorph–anamorph connections of polyphagous species active on lichens and soil fungi respectively.

## Fungi on Sporocarps (SCIF)

Sporocarp-Inhabiting Fungi (SCIF) constitute the single largest ecological guild of fungicolous organisms. They colonize the **sporocarps** (fruiting bodies) of agarics, boletes, polypores, and other macrofungi. SCIF include:

- Tremellales yeasts and jelly fungi (e.g., *Tremella* spp.)
- Hypocrealean perithecial fungi (e.g., *Hypomyces* spp.)
- Various hyphomycetes and coelomycetes
- Basidiomycetes such as *Asterophora* and *Squamanita*

SCIF can be **biotrophic** (drawing nutrients without killing the host), **necrotrophic** (actively killing host tissue), or **saprotrophic** (colonizing already dead or senescing sporocarp tissue). The ecological impact ranges from negligible surface colonization to complete destruction and replacement of the host fruiting body.

## Fungi on Slime Molds (Myxogastrea)

A specialized but underappreciated group of fungicolous fungi colonizes **myxomycetes** (plasmodial slime molds):

- **Sporangia colonization**: Many fungicolous fungi specifically target the sporangia (fruiting structures) of slime molds, using the spore mass as a nutrient substrate.
- ***Nectriopsis violacea***: A striking species that colonizes members of the **Physarales**, producing violet-colored perithecia on the surface of slime mold sporangia. It represents a highly host-specific association.
- ***Stilbella byssiseda***: Found on a variety of slime mold sporangia, producing synnematous conidiomata that arise from the decaying spore mass.
- ***Nectriopsis exigua***: Another nectrioid fungus associated with myxomycete sporangia, demonstrating the recurring pattern of Hypocrealean fungi as myxomyceticolous colonists.

The diversity of fungi associated with slime molds remains poorly documented, and many species likely await discovery.

## Summary

The Tremellales and their relatives represent a major radiation of mycoparasitic fungi characterized by dimorphic life cycles, haustorial feeding structures, and diverse host associations spanning mushrooms, polypores, corticioid fungi, lichens, and even slime molds. Beyond Tremellales, mycoparasitism has evolved independently in multiple fungal lineages including the Aphyllophorales, Boletales, Agaricales, and various Ascomycete groups. The polyphagous fungicolous species — such as *Clonostachys rosea* and *Acremonium strictum* — demonstrate that host generalism is a viable and widespread strategy, while highly specialized species like *Squamanita* spp. and *Nectriopsis violacea* illustrate the extreme end of host specificity. Together, these fungi form a complex web of inter-fungal interactions that profoundly shapes fungal community structure, succession, and biodiversity in virtually all terrestrial ecosystems.
