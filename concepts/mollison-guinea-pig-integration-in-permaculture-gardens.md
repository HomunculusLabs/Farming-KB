# Guinea Pig Integration in Permaculture Gardens

In *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, Bill Mollison identifies the
guinea pig (Cavia porcellus) as a uniquely valuable small domestic
animal for tropical and subtropical permaculture systems. The edible
guinea pig occupies a niche that no other domestic animal fills:
controlling competitive grasses beneath young trees while producing
manure and protein in a compact, low-impact system.

## The Guinea Pig as Garden Ally

Mollison states that the edible guinea pig lives towards people, a
behavioural trait that makes it ideal for integration near human
settlements. In urban and suburban systems, guinea pigs would abolish
much of the nuisance of organic waste, converting food scraps to
manure while requiring minimal space and management.

The guinea pig aids small tree establishment by chipping the base of
young grasses that compete with tree seedlings. This grass-suppression
function is critical in tropical systems where rampant grasses such as
kikuyu can smother young trees and prevent orchard establishment.

## Specific Design Examples

Mollison documents several specific applications of guinea pigs:

### Malanda, Queensland (Tropical System)

At Bob Kingwell's property in tropical Queensland, 3-4 guinea pigs
are housed in small shelters beneath young litchee trees. They forage
below the canopy and are fed food pellets. The guinea pigs manure the
trees and keep kikuyu grass from choking young saplings. Owls and
pythons eat excess populations, providing natural predator control.

As an alternative where guinea pigs are not available, Desmodium (a
leguminous ground cover) can restrain kikuyu grass, though this uses
more water and does not provide the food production benefit of the
guinea pig system.

### Gangamma's Mandala Garden

In the integrated [[mandala-garden-design]], guinea pig shelters are
built into the garden infrastructure alongside rabbit hutches and
chicken houses. The roofs of these structures double as pigeon
roosts, quail houses, or additional guinea pig rest areas, stacking
multiple functions in a single structure.

### Tropical Polyculture Systems

In the established tropical polyculture, guinea pigs are listed
among the standard domestic species (alongside pigs, chickens, geese,
rabbits, pigeons, and milking goats) that recycle plant wastes and
help control the rampant growth of ground cover.

## Guinea Pigs for Kikuyu Grass Control

Mollison specifically addresses the problem of kikuyu grass (Pennisetum
clandestinum), an aggressive runner grass that overwhelms gardens and
young tree plantings. The problem of kikuyu grass is eased by domestic
guinea pigs on range in small houses. These free trees from grass
competition and provide manures.

The guinea pig approach is superior to chemical or mechanical control
because it:
- Produces manure as a by-product of grass control
- Requires no fossil fuel inputs
- Provides food (protein) as a yield
- Maintains continuous pressure on regrowth
- Can be managed in small, targeted areas

## Guinea Pigs in Arid Systems

Mollison notes that guinea pigs do well in deserts, alongside chickens
and ducks. In arid environments, guinea pigs survive in sheltered
locations with access to supplementary food and water. Their small
size means they require minimal water and feed compared to larger
livestock, making them appropriate for water-scarce conditions.

## Integration with Zone 1 Gardens

In the zone planning framework, guinea pigs are placed in Zone 1,
within 6 metres of the home. Their quiet nature and small size make
them ideal backyard animals. They join fish, rabbits, pigeons, and
culinary herbs in the intensive home garden zone, where householders
can produce most of the food necessary to existence.

The total design garden model specifies that guinea pigs are part of
a system that provides most mulch and forage for a small household.
When bulked out by house scraps, the garden should feed rabbits,
guinea pigs, some poultry, and even fatten a small pig.

## Nutrient Cycling Function

Perhaps the main function of animal species in the tropics is to
recycle plant wastes and to help control the rampant growth of ground
cover. Guinea pigs perform this function efficiently, converting
grass and vegetable waste into concentrated manure that can be applied
directly to garden beds and tree root zones.

Their manure is particularly valuable because:
- It is high in nitrogen and phosphorus
- It is produced in small, manageable quantities
- It can be applied directly without composting (being relatively
  mild and non-burning)
- It is produced exactly where it is needed (beneath tree canopies)

## Housing and Management

Guinea pig housing in permaculture systems is simple and low-cost:

- Small portable shelters that can be moved between tree rows
- Protection from predators (owls, pythons, cats, dogs)
- Access to forage areas beneath tree canopies
- Supplementary feeding with food pellets or kitchen scraps
- Adequate ventilation and shade

In the documented Malanda design, guinea pig shelters are small
structures placed among young fruit trees, allowing the animals to
move freely between the shelter and the forage zone beneath the
canopy.

## Broader System Integration

Guinea pigs complement the full suite of small domestic animals:

  weeds and snails in water features
- [[mollison-geese-grass-control-and-integrated-poultry]] control
  larger grasses in orchards and along water edges
- [[mollison-rabbits-in-permaculture-design-and-soil-preparation]]
  prepare soil and contribute manure in zone 1
- [[mollison-pigeons-and-squab-production-in-permaculture-systems]]
  recycle phosphorus and disperse seeds
