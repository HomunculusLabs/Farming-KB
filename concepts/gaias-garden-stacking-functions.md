# Stacking Functions and Redundancy in Ecological Design

Toby Hemenway's explanation of two complementary permaculture design principles: stacking functions and redundancy. From Gaia's Garden, Chapter 2.

## Stacking Functions: Two Rules

### Rule 1: Each Element Performs Multiple Functions

Nothing in nature has only one function. A shrub doesn't just cast shade — it feeds birds with berries, offers shelter, mulches soil with leaves, provides browse for deer and porcupines, blocks wind, holds soil with roots, collects and channels rainwater.

Nature stacks functions because every living thing represents a big investment in matter and energy, which nature husbands with immense stinginess. She gets the most bang for her buck by squeezing every erg of energy out of each organism and tying it into many cycles.

### Rule 2: Each Function Is Served by Multiple Elements

Always have backups in place. Gardeners already follow this unconsciously — planting several vegetable varieties in case one fails, or maintaining sprinklers, drip irrigation, soaker hoses, and watering cans all for the single purpose of delivering water.

## Why Redundancy Matters

### Disaster Protection

Most important functions in organisms and ecosystems have backups, often several layers deep. The human body uses three independent methods for balance (eyes, inner ear, muscles/tendons). Any organism or system with backups survives longer. If soil is well mulched, plants survive when irrigation fails during vacation.

### Increased Yield

The digestive tract filters nutrition through multiple passes: small intestine, large intestine, gut bacteria. This multilayered approach ekes out nearly all available sustenance from food. A garden with several layers of water-conserving, frost-protecting, disease-proofing, wind-deflecting, or soil-building strategies gains cumulative benefit from the multiple techniques.

### The "Tell Me Three Times" Strategy

Nature devotes energy and organs to having multiple systems for every critical function. This "tell me three times" strategy means a big investment in not falling over — and it is worth it because if any single system fails, others compensate.

## The Grape Arbor Example

Hemenway's concrete example of stacking functions:

A 5,000-gallon rainwater tank next to the kitchen had an ugly concrete lid. The solution stacked multiple functions:

- **Water storage** — the cistern's primary function
- **Deck** — cedar deck hid the concrete
- **Arbor** — built over the deck for shade
- **Seedless grapes** — trained on the arbor for food
- **Jasmine** — trellised on the tank's side for fragrance
- **Summer shade** — grape leaves shade the deck and cool the house
- **Winter sun** — deciduous leaves drop, letting sun reach the deck and kitchen window
- **Mulch** — fallen leaves go to compost or garden beds as mulch
- **Propagation stock** — winter prunings provide cuttings for friends
- **Houseplant vacation** — houseplants summer on the light-dappled corner
- **Irrigation** — cistern overflow waters nearby plantings
- **Outdoor dining** — a pleasant spot to eat lunch

By combining the water tank, grapevine, and deck in the right arrangement, each element's usefulness increased, producing benefits that none alone would provide.

## Contrast with Conventional Design

Most human designs are "prodigiously wasteful." We hurry goods from source to landfill in an arrow-straight stream, while nature bends and re-bends that stream into a zigzag course, extracting benefit at every turn and recycling what is left.

## The Ecological Design Criterion

For a garden to be considered ecological, it should:

- Require few outside inputs, especially once mature
- Create rather than destroy wildlife habitat
- Enhance air, water, and soil quality
- Eventually result in less work instead of more for the human occupants

## Nature as Model for Interconnectedness

A natural landscape harvests energy (sun, wind, heat) and matter (water, nutrients) that flow through it, acting as a net to catch these resources and transform them into more life. Nearly everything that enters is captured and used. One creature's waste is another's food. The linking of one species' "outputs" to another's "inputs" is what ecological design seeks to recreate.
