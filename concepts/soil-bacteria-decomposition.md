# Soil Bacteria and Decomposition

Bacteria are the **primary decomposers** in soil ecosystems. They are the first organisms to break down organic matter — everything from fallen leaves and dead plant roots to animal remains — converting complex biological materials into forms that other [[soil-life]] and plants can use. Understanding bacterial decomposition is essential for anyone who wants [[query-how-to-build-healthy-garden-soil]], living soil.

## Bacteria as Primary Decomposers

When organic material enters the soil, bacteria are typically the first to colonize it. They break down plant and animal material to access the nitrogen, carbon, and other nutrients locked inside. Through this process, nutrients become temporarily **immobilized** — held within the bacterial cells themselves. These nutrients are not available to plants until the bacteria are consumed by predators (such as [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]]) or killed and their cellular contents released back into the soil.

This cycle of bacterial growth, predation, and nutrient release is one of the most fundamental processes driving soil fertility.

## Food Sources and Preferences

Not all bacteria eat the same things. Different species have evolved to thrive on different food sources, which is why a diverse soil supports a diverse bacterial community. Some key points about bacterial diets:

- **Most soil bacteria prefer young, fresh "green" material** — the kind of organic matter rich in simple sugars and amino acids. Think of fresh grass clippings, young leafy greens, or newly sloughed root cells.
- **[[root-exudates]] are a favorite food**. Plants actively secrete sugars, amino acids, and other compounds from their roots to feed the microbial community around them. This creates a concentrated zone of bacterial activity called the **rhizosphere** — the thin layer of soil immediately surrounding living roots.
- **Sloughed root cells** — as roots grow and shed older cells, these become an additional food source for bacteria living in the rhizosphere.

This is why living roots in the soil at all times (via [[cover-crops]], perennials, or no-till systems) are so important for maintaining a thriving bacterial population.

## How Bacteria Feed: Enzymes and Transport

Bacteria cannot swallow large pieces of organic matter. Instead, they use a two-step process:

1. **External digestion**: Bacteria secrete enzymes outside their cell walls that break down complex organic chains — long carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids — into simpler molecules: simple sugars, fatty acids, and amino acids.
2. **Active transport**: Once food molecules are small enough, they must cross the bacterial cell membrane. This happens through **active transport**, a process that requires energy. Specialized proteins embedded in the cell membrane act as pumps, passing molecules through one to the next in a kind of "fire bucket brigade." This is an energy-intensive process, which is why bacteria need a steady supply of carbohydrates to fuel their work.

## Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Bacteria

Soil bacteria can be broadly divided by their relationship with oxygen:

### Aerobic Bacteria
- Require oxygen to function
- Are the dominant decomposers in well-aerated, healthy soils
- Produce mostly harmless by-products like carbon dioxide and water
- Preferred in garden and farm soils — encouraged by proper aeration, drainage, and reduced compaction

### Anaerobic and Facultative Anaerobic Bacteria
- **Anaerobic bacteria** function without oxygen
- **Facultative anaerobes** like *Clostridium* and *E. coli* can switch between [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-aerobic-and-anaerobic-composting]] metabolism depending on conditions
- Common in compacted, waterlogged, or poorly aerated soils
- Produce undesirable by-products, including:
  - **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)** — the rotten egg smell
  - **Butyric acid** — rancid, vomit-like odor
  - **Ammonia**
  - **Vinegar (acetic acid)**
- These compounds can be toxic to plant roots and indicate soil health problems

**Takeaway for gardeners**: If your soil smells sour, rotten, or like sewage, [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] are likely present. Improve drainage, reduce compaction, and add organic matter to restore aerobic conditions.

## Actinomycetes: The In-Between Decomposers

Actinomycetes (particularly the genus *Streptomyces*) occupy a unique niche — they have characteristics of both bacteria and fungi:

- They grow in **filamentous structures** similar to [[fungal-hyphae]], allowing them to reach into organic matter
- They are responsible for the **characteristic "earthy" smell** of healthy soil (the compound geosmin)
- They can decompose tough materials including **cellulose and chitin** (insect exoskeletons, fungal cell walls)
- They tolerate a **wider pH range** than many other bacteria, making them important decomposers in both acidic and alkaline soils
- They become especially active in later stages of decomposition, breaking down materials that earlier bacterial waves could not

## Specialized Decomposers

### Cellulomonas
The genus *Cellulomonas* specializes in breaking down **cellulose** — the main structural component of plant cell walls. Cellulose is a tough, fibrous carbohydrate that many bacteria cannot handle, but *Cellulomonas* produces specific cellulase enzymes to dismantle it.

### The Lignin Limit
**Lignin** — the complex polymer that gives wood its rigidity — is generally too complex and chemically resistant for most bacteria to decompose. This material is primarily left for **fungi**, which produce powerful lignin-degrading enzymes (like [[lignin-peroxidase]]). This is one reason fungi become more dominant in later decomposition stages and in woody, mature compost.

## Element Cycling by Bacteria

Bacteria drive several critical nutrient cycles in soil:

### Carbon Cycle
- Bacteria consume organic carbon compounds and release **carbon dioxide (CO₂)** through cellular respiration
- This CO₂ can dissolve in soil water to form carbonic acid, which helps weather minerals and release rock-bound nutrients
- Bacterial biomass itself represents a significant carbon pool in soil

### Sulfur Cycle
- **Sulfur-oxidizing bacteria** (chemoautotrophs) convert sulfur compounds into plant-available sulfate forms
- These bacteria obtain energy from chemical reactions rather than from sunlight or organic matter
- Sulfur cycling is essential for protein synthesis in plants

### Nitrogen Cycle
The nitrogen cycle is perhaps the most well-known nutrient cycle, and bacteria play starring roles at every stage:

**Nitrogen Fixation** — converting atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into biologically usable forms:
- *Azotobacter* — free-living, aerobic nitrogen fixer
- *Azospirillum* — associates closely with plant roots
- *Clostridium* — anaerobic nitrogen fixer
- *Rhizobium* — forms symbiotic nodules on legume roots

**Nitrification** — converting ammonium to nitrate (the form most plants prefer):
- **Nitrosomonas** converts ammonium (NH₄⁺) to nitrite (NO₂⁻)
- **Nitrobacter** converts nitrite (NO₂⁻) to nitrate (NO₃⁻)
- This two-step process is sensitive to soil conditions — nitrification **diminishes significantly below pH 7**, meaning acidic soils can accumulate ammonium while lacking nitrate

## Biofilms: Bacterial Communities

Bacteria in soil do not typically live as isolated, free-floating cells. Instead, they organize into **biofilms** — structured communities encased in a self-produced matrix of:

- **Sugars (exopolysaccharides)**
- **Proteins**
- **DNA**

Biofilm characteristics relevant to soil health:

- The biofilm matrix creates a **slightly alkaline** micro-environment around the bacterial colony
- Biofilms **protect bacteria from desiccation** (drying out), helping them survive dry periods
- They also provide **protection from antibiotics** and other [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]] produced by competing microorganisms
- Biofilms concentrate [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]], making decomposition more efficient than it would be with scattered individual cells

## Key Takeaways for Soil Managers

- Bacteria are the **first responders** in decomposition, breaking down simple organic matter rapidly
- Keep soil **aerated** to support beneficial aerobic decomposers and suppress anaerobic by-products
- Maintain **living roots** year-round to feed bacteria through root exudates and rhizosphere activity
- A diverse organic matter input (green and brown materials) supports a diverse bacterial community
- Monitor **soil pH** — nitrification and many other bacterial processes work best near neutral (pH 7)
- Biofilms show that bacteria work **as communities**, not individuals — building soil biology is about building ecosystems
