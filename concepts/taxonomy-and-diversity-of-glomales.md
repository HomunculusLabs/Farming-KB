# Taxonomy and Diversity of Glomales

The order Glomales contains all arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, organisms that form symbiotic associations with the roots of most terrestrial plants. Despite their ecological importance and ancient evolutionary origins (~400 million years), Glomales is a relatively species-poor order with only 163 described species across seven genera — a striking contrast to the more than 3,500 species of ectomycorrhizal fungi (Law and Lewis 1983).

## Classification System

### Suborders and Genera

Glomales is divided into two suborders based on characteristics of the mycorrhizae:

**Suborder Glomineae** — Form arbuscules and vesicles:
- *Glomus* — 91 species (disproportionately speciose)
- *Acaulospora* — 35 species
- *Entrophospora* — 4 species
- *Archaeospora* — 2 species
- *Paraglomus* — 2 species

**Suborder Gigasporineae** — Form arbuscules and auxiliary cells:
- *Gigaspora* — 5 species
- *Scutellospora* — 28 species

### Taxonomic History

The classification of Glomales has undergone significant revision:

1. **Original erection**: Morton and Benny (1990) established the order with six genera
2. **Sclerocystis transfer**: Almeida and Schenck (1990) transferred most *Sclerocystis* species to *Glomus* based on shared morphological traits; the remaining species, *S. coremioides*, was placed in *Glomus* based on 18S rDNA sequences (Redecker et al. 2000a)
3. **Archaeosporaceae and Paraglomaceae**: Some *Acaulospora* and *Glomus* species were found to be more closely related to *Geosiphon pyriformis* — a fungus involved in mutualistic symbiosis with photosynthetic *Nostoc* cyanobacteria — than to other AM fungi (Redecker et al. 2000b). These phylogenetically ancient species were placed in two new families with one genus each: *Archaeospora* (family Archaeosporaceae) and *Paraglomus* (family Paraglomaceae) (Morton and Redecker 2001)

## Why So Few Species?

The low morphological diversity of Glomales is predictable given:

- The relatively simple designs of taxonomically important structures in arbuscular mycorrhizae and spores
- The lack of complexity in spore or sporocarp organization (Morton 1999)
- Clonal reproduction via mitospores, which limits the generation of genetic diversity through sexual recombination

### Spore Wall Characters as Primary Taxonomic Characters

Developmental evidence indicates that properties of the **spore wall** resolve species primarily, while other traits group species into higher taxa:

- *Glomus*: Species produce spores with the greatest variety of spore wall layers
- *Acaulospora* and *Scutellospora*: Have only a bilayered spore wall (outer layer + laminate layer), with co-varying ornamentation
- *Gigaspora*: Low species diversity attributed to developmental constraints — the only genus where the terminal event in spore wall differentiation is germination (Bentivenga and Morton 1995)
- *Entrophospora*: Spore morphologies identical to "sister" species in *Acaulospora*, except for spore position relative to a "sporiferous saccule"; likely polyphyletic (each species evolved from a different *Acaulospora* ancestor)

## Physiological and Ecological Diversity

The low taxonomic diversity is offset by considerable **physiological adaptation** of spatially separated populations to local environmental conditions. Adaptations include:

- Tolerance to soil pH, heavy metal ion concentrations, and salinity levels
- Growth responses to different soil-fertility regimens
- Responses to changes in habitat or growing conditions (Brundrett 1991; Weissenhorn et al. 1994)
- Indirect evidence suggests adaptation to particular host species (McGonigle and Fitter 1990; Johnson et al. 1992; Bever et al. 1995)

The physiological and ecological diversities among populations of all globally distributed species likely far exceed the taxonomic diversity.

## Specimen Repositories

### Living Collections

- **INVAM** (International Culture Collection of Vesicular Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi), West Virginia — the oldest and largest centralized collection, with nearly 1,100 accessions of more than 86 species (as of 2001)
- **BEG** (Bank of European Glomales) — organized as a network with a central coordinator; accessions registered centrally but cultured at individual researcher institutions
- Regional collections exist in Australia, Cuba, Taiwan, and Colombia

### Nonliving Specimens

Major repositories for type specimens include:
- Oregon State University Mycological Collections
- Farlow Herbarium, Harvard University
- Royal Botanic Gardens Kew Herbarium

## Geographic Distribution

### Global Patterns

Endemism has not been conclusively established for any AM fungal species. Some species appear to have limited distributions, with temperature being an important limiting factor (Koske 1987). However, several species — both ancient and highly derived — are distributed globally:

- *Glomus intraradices*: Found on all continents except Antarctica, in tropical and temperate latitudes, arid and mesic climates, and a wide range of habitats
- *Paraglomus occultum*, *Acaulospora scrobiculata*, *Archaeospora leptoticha*, *Gigaspora gigantea*, and *Scutellospora calospora*: Distributed widely on most continents

### Habitat Patterns

AM fungi have been found in nearly every habitat where plants grow, but show reduced abundance in:
- Coniferous forests (largely ectomycorrhizal)
- Arctic and tundra regions
- High-altitude environments

Highest diversity occurs in:
- Maritime and lacustrine sand dunes
- Prairie grasslands
- Temperate and tropical forests
- Deserts, salt marshes, and agricultural fields

At the generic level, no clear-cut habitat preferences are discernible. The apparent dominance of *Glomus* in most communities (3:1 ratio of species) may reflect the greater number of described species rather than genuine ecological or geographic preference.

## Future Taxonomic Directions

Additional species presently included in other genera will likely be transferred to *Archaeospora* and *Paraglomus* as new molecular analyses are conducted. New species likely will fill ecological gaps between existing species, but this will create greater difficulty in defining species boundaries from morphological traits alone (Morton 1990b).
