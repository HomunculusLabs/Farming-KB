# Woronin Bodies and Septal Pore Organisation in Ascomycete Fungi

## Overview

**Woronin bodies** are dense, membrane-bound organelles found exclusively in the **Euascomycetes** (Pezizomycotina), a major subphylum of the Ascomycota. They are strategically positioned near **septal pores**—openings in the cross-walls (septa) that separate hyphal compartments—and function as emergency plugs that can rapidly seal the pore in response to cellular damage. Woronin bodies represent one of the key evolutionary innovations that enable the complex multicellular organisation observed in filamentous ascomycete fungi, allowing a syncytial mycelium to maintain cellular autonomy when necessary.

## Fungal Phylogeny and Septal Evolution

### Major Fungal Groups

Phylogenetic analysis based on 18S ribosomal RNA reveals four major groups of fungi:

| Group | Vegetative Septa | Multicellular Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| **Chytridiomycota** | Infrequent | Generally unicellular or simple |
| **Zygomycota** | Infrequent | Coenocytic hyphae |
| **Ascomycota** | Regular, perforate | Highly diverse, complex fruiting bodies |
| **Basidiomycota** | Regular, perforate | Complex fruiting bodies, extensive mycelia |

The Ascomycota and Basidiomycota are sister clades that evolved more recently. Both produce regular perforate septa in vegetative hyphae, a trait inferred to have been present in their common ancestor. The Ascomycota are further divided into three subgroups:

- **Archiascomycetes** (early-branching)
- **Hemiascomycetes** (includes yeasts like *Saccharomyces*)
- **Euascomycetes** (Pezizomycotina, the largest group, including most filamentous ascomycetes)

Woronin bodies are diagnostic of the Euascomycetes, and the **HEX1 gene**—which encodes the major structural protein of the Woronin body core—has been identified in all Euascomycete genomes examined to date, including the earliest-branching lineages, but is absent from all other known fungal genomes.

## Structure of the Woronin Body

### Morphology

Woronin bodies are characterised by two distinctive features:

1. **Dense core**: An electron-dense, osmophilic proteinaceous matrix composed primarily of HEX1 protein that self-assembles into a crystalline or paracrystalline lattice.
2. **Surrounding unit membrane**: A closely associated lipid bilayer that envelopes the core.

Two morphological forms are commonly observed:

- **Oval (spherical) form**: The typical shape, approximately 0.1–1.0 µm in diameter.
- **Hexagonal form**: A less common variant with a hexagonal cross-section, likely reflecting the symmetry of the underlying HEX1 protein lattice.

Woronin bodies are tethered near septal pores by **filamentous structures** that position them within striking distance of the pore opening, enabling rapid occlusion when triggered.

### Peroxisomal Origin

Woronin bodies are **peroxisome-derived organelles**. They originate from the peroxisomal compartment and share biogenesis machinery with conventional peroxisomes. Electron microscopy revealed an early association with microbodies (now recognised as peroxisomes), and molecular work confirmed that Woronin bodies bud from a specialised peroxisomal compartment. The HEX1 protein, while unique to Woronin bodies, is targeted to peroxisomes via peroxisomal targeting signals (PTS1), and Woronin body biogenesis requires the general peroxisomal import machinery.

## Function: Emergency Septal Pore Plugging

### The Syncytium Problem

The fungal mycelium forms a **syncytium**—a multinucleate mass of cytoplasm interconnected through septal pores. This arrangement permits:

- **Protoplasmic streaming**: Rapid cytoplasmic flow between compartments, facilitating distribution of nutrients, organelles, and nuclei.
- **Intercellular communication**: Coordinated responses to environmental stimuli across the colony.
- **Resource reallocation**: Directional movement of materials towards growing tips or reproductive structures.

However, this continuity creates vulnerability: damage to a single hyphal compartment could allow catastrophic loss of cytoplasm through the open septal pores, potentially killing the entire connected mycelium.

### The Cellularisation Solution

Woronin bodies solve this problem by enabling rapid **cellularisation**—the sealing of septal pores to isolate damaged compartments:

1. When a hypha is severed or damaged, the resulting pressure differential or biochemical signal triggers Woronin body deployment.
2. The tethered Woronin body is drawn into the septal pore, where its dense core physically occludes the opening.
3. Cytoplasmic contents are retained in the undamaged compartments, preventing exsanguination.
4. The sealed compartment is effectively sacrificed while the rest of the mycelium survives.

This mechanism is analogous to platelet plugging in animal blood vessels and represents an elegant solution to the fundamental problem of maintaining a syncytial lifestyle in the face of environmental hazards.

## Comparative Septal-Pore-Associated Organelles

Different fungal lineages have evolved distinct organelles associated with septal pores:

### Septal Pore Cap (Parenthosome) — Hymenomycetes

The Hymenomycetes (a major group within the Basidiomycota) produce a **[[septal-pore-cap]] (SPC)** or parenthosome:

- Barrel-shaped swelling of the cell wall surrounds the septal pore.
- The SPC sits atop the pore apparatus like a cap, consisting of an electron-dense core with a unit membrane.
- Membrane continuity with the **nuclear envelope** suggests the SPC is a specialised domain of the endoplasmic reticulum, distinguishing its origin from the peroxisomal Woronin body.
- Associated with cytoplasmic filaments and osmophilic material that can occlude the pore.

### "Woronin-Body-Like" Organelles — Urediniomycetes

The Urediniomycetes (rust fungi and allies) produce perforate septa with associated organelles that superficially resemble Woronin bodies:

- Appear peroxisome-like with unit membranes.
- May contain electron-dense polyhedral structures in their lumen.
- However, the core often shows fine **striations** not seen in true Woronin bodies, suggesting distinct core composition.
- These organelles appear to have evolved independently (convergent evolution) rather than sharing a direct evolutionary relationship with Euascomycete Woronin bodies.

## Evolutionary Significance

The distribution of septal-pore-associated organelles across the fungal phylogeny supports the hypothesis that complex multicellular organisation in fungi is facilitated by specialised mechanisms for controlling intercellular continuity:

- The fungi with the most prominent and complex septal-pore organelles—the **Hymenomycetes** and **Euascomycetes**—also produce the largest and most complex multicellular fruiting bodies.
- The ability to seal septal pores allows compartmentalisation during **cellular differentiation** (e.g., during fruiting body development) and in response to **stress or damage**.
- The evolution of Woronin bodies in the ancestral Euascomycete, as inferred from the ubiquity of HEX1, may have been a key innovation that enabled the radiation of filamentous ascomycetes into diverse ecological niches requiring robust multicellular development.

## Historical Context

Woronin bodies were first discovered by **Mikhail Stepanovich Woronin** in 1886 in the cells of *Ascobolus pulcherrimus* (= *Lasiobolus pulcherrimus*). He observed "certain highly refractive particles" near septa. In 1900, **Charlotte Ternetz** further defined basic features of Woronin body cell biology. The structures were formally named "Woronin bodies" by the eminent mycologist **A. H. R. Buller** in his seminal work *Researches on Fungi*.
