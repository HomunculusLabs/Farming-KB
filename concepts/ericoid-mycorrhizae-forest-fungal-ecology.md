# Ericoid Mycorrhizae and Forest Fungal Ecology

## Overview

[[ericoid-mycorrhizae]] represent a specialized symbiotic association between fungi and plants in the order Ericales, enabling these plants to thrive in nutrient-poor, acidic soils. Alongside ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal systems, ericoid mycorrhizae form a critical component of forest [[fungal-ecology]], contributing to nutrient cycling, plant community dynamics, [[fungal-rhizosphere-and-ecosystem-resilience]]. This concept page synthesizes research on ericoid mycorrhizal physiology, forest mycorrhizal community ecology, and the broader ecological roles of fungi in woodland ecosystems.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizal Physiology

### Nitrogen Acquisition from Organic Sources

Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi are exceptionally capable of accessing nitrogen from complex organic sources unavailable to most plants:

- **Peptide utilization**: Bajwa and Read (1985) demonstrated that peptides serve as nitrogen sources for the ericoid endophyte and for mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal plants in the Ericaceae
- **Protein degradation**: Leake and Read (1989, 1990b) characterized the extracellular proteinase activity of the ericoid endophyte *Hymenoscyphus ericae*, including the effect of extracellular pH on enzyme production and activity
- **Chitin degradation**: Kerley and Read (1995) documented chitin degradation by *H. ericae* and transfer of chitin-derived nitrogen to the host plant; Leake and Read (1990a) confirmed chitin as a nitrogen source for mycorrhizal fungi
- **Protein–polyphenol complex mobilization**: Bending and Read (1996) showed that ericoid and ectomycorrhizal fungi can mobilize nitrogen from protein–polyphenol complexes, a particularly recalcitrant nitrogen pool in acidic soils

### Phosphorus Acquisition

Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi access phosphorus from organic sources through specialized [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]]:

- Mitchell and Read (1981) documented utilization of inorganic and organic phosphate by mycorrhizal endophytes of *Vaccinium macrocarpon* (cranberry) and *[[rhododendron-ponticum]]*
- Leake and Miles (1996) demonstrated phosphodiesterase production and utilization of DNA as a phosphorus source by *Hymenoscyphus ericae*
- Myers and Leake (1996) extended this work to show that ericoid mycorrhiza of *Vaccinium macrocarpon* can utilize nuclei as both phosphorus and nitrogen sources

### Phosphorus Relations in Natural Settings

Dighton and Coleman (1992) studied phosphorus relations of roots and mycorrhizas of *[[rhododendron-maximum]]* in the southern Appalachians of North Carolina. Their work revealed complex phosphorus dynamics in the rhizosphere of ericaceous plants in natural forest understories, where phosphorus availability is often limiting due to acidic soil conditions and competition from organic matter.

### Heavy Metal Resistance

Bradley et al. (1982) documented the role of mycorrhizal infection in heavy metal resistance in ericaceous plants. This finding has implications for understanding plant colonization of metalliferous soils and the potential use of ericoid systems in phytoremediation.

## Multiple Mycorrhizal Associations

### Ericaceous Plant Flexibility

Some ericaceous plants support multiple types of mycorrhizal associations simultaneously or at different life stages:

- Dighton and Coleman (1992) found evidence that *Rhododendron maximum* supports both ericoid and possibly ecto- and arbuscular mycorrhizae
- Smith et al. (1995) documented ericoid, ecto-, and arbuscular mycorrhizae on ericaceous plants in northern California (Largent et al., 1980)
- Chilvers et al. (1987) observed ectomycorrhizal vs. endomycorrhizal fungi within the same root system

The functional significance of supporting multiple mycorrhizal types remains an open question with potentially important ecosystem consequences. Different mycorrhizal types may provide complementary benefits — ericoid mycorrhizae for organic nitrogen access, ectomycorrhizae for phosphorus mining, and arbuscular mycorrhizae for broad nutrient uptake.

## Forest Mycorrhizal Community Ecology

### Succession and Stand Development

Mycorrhizal fungal communities undergo predictable changes during forest stand development:

- Dighton and Mason (1985) reviewed mycorrhizal dynamics during forest tree development, establishing the concept of mycorrhizal succession
- Dighton et al. (1986) documented changes in basidiomycete fruit body occurrence during stand development, with mycorrhizal species showing distinct patterns of appearance and disappearance
- Last et al. (1987) proposed that successions of sheathing mycorrhizal fungi are a general feature of forest development
- Cullings et al. (2001) examined defoliation effects on the ectomycorrhizal community of mixed *Pinus contorta/Picea engelmannii* stands in Yellowstone Park, showing that disturbance affects mycorrhizal community composition

### Species Identification and Classification

Accurate identification of ECM morphotypes has been essential for community ecology studies:

- Agerer (1987–) produced the Colour Atlas of Ectomycorrhizae, the standard reference for morphological identification
- Ingleby et al. (1990) published Identification of Ectomycorrhizas as a practical guide
- Goodman et al. (1996–2000) compiled A Manual of Concise Descriptions of North American Ectomycorrhizae
- Egli et al. (1993) highlighted discrepancies between macroscopic and microscopic classification of *Picea abies* ectomycorrhizas

### Community Composition and Continuity

Jonsson et al. (1999a) studied continuity of ectomycorrhizal fungi in self-regulating boreal *Pinus sylvestris* forests by comparing mycobiont diversity on seedlings and mature trees. They found evidence for persistent fungal populations across tree generations, suggesting that old-growth forests maintain reservoirs of ECM fungal diversity critical for regeneration.

Jonsson et al. (1999b) further documented ectomycorrhizal fungal communities in late-successional Swedish boreal forests and their composition following wildfire. Post-fire communities differed significantly from pre-fire communities, with some species appearing only after fire disturbance.

### Species Richness and Ecosystem Function

Leake (2001) asked whether diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi is important for ecosystem function, reviewing evidence for functional redundancy vs. complementarity among ECM species. Jonsson et al. (2001) showed context-dependent effects of ECM species richness on tree seedling productivity, finding that the relationship between diversity and function depends on environmental conditions.

### Repeated Disturbance Effects

Mahmood et al. (1999) investigated the effects of repeated harvesting on the ectomycorrhizal community in a Swedish spruce forest. Their findings have implications for sustainable forestry practices, as repeated disturbance may progressively alter the mycorrhizal community in ways that affect long-term forest productivity.

## Fungal Foraging and Mycelial Organization

### Cord-Forming Saprotrophic Fungi

Boddy (1999) reviewed saprotrophic cord-forming fungi and their ability to meet the challenge of heterogeneous environments. These fungi form organized mycelial cords that ramify through soil and litter, foraging for nutrients in a manner analogous to — and potentially competing with — ectomycorrhizal mycelial networks.

### ECM Mycelial Structure and Function

The structure and function of the vegetative mycelium of ectomycorrhizal plants was systematically studied by Read and colleagues:

- Bending and Read (1995a) documented foraging behavior and translocation of nutrients from exploited litter
- Bending and Read (1995b) measured activities of nutrient-mobilizing enzymes in birch litter colonized by *[[paxillus-involutus]]*
- Cairney (1992) reviewed translocation of solutes in ectomycorrhizal and saprotrophic rhizomorphs
- Cairney and Burke (1996) emphasized physiological heterogeneity within fungal mycelia as an important concept

### Mycelial Individuality

Rayner (1991, 1993, 1998) developed the concept of the individualistic mycelium, emphasizing that fungal mycelia are not homogeneous units but show internal differentiation and developmental patterning. Rayner (1998) described trees and fungi as "fountains of the forest," highlighting their fundamental interconnectedness.

## Competition and Interactions Among Fungal Functional Groups

### Negative Associations Between AM and ECM Fungi

Lodge and Wentworth (1990) documented negative associations between vesicular-[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] and some ectomycorrhizal fungi inhabiting the same root system. This antagonism suggests competition between mycorrhizal types for root colonization sites.

### Mycorrhizal–Saprotrophic Interactions

Dighton et al. (1987) investigated interactions between tree roots, mycorrhizas, a saprotrophic fungus, and organic substrate decomposition in microcosms. Conn and Dighton (2000) showed that litter quality influences decomposition, ectomycorrhizal community structure, and mycorrhizal root surface acid phosphatase activity.

### Litter Quality Effects

Baar et al. (1994) found both stimulatory and inhibitory effects of needle litter and grass extracts on the growth of some ectomycorrhizal fungi. This indicates that leaf litter chemistry can directly influence which ECM species dominate in a particular microsite.

## Water Relations and Drought Stress

### Mycorrhizae and Plant Water Status

Augé (2001) comprehensively reviewed water relations, drought, and vesicular-[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-symbiosis]], documenting that mycorrhizae can improve plant water status through both direct (hyphal water uptake) and indirect (improved root morphology and nutrient status) mechanisms.

Cruz et al. (2000) demonstrated that [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] affected tree growth, leaf water potential, and levels of the ethylene precursor ACC in papaya roots under water-stress conditions.

### Drought and Endophytes

Belesky and Malinowski (2000) described abiotic stresses and morphological plasticity in *Neotyphodium*-infected tall fescue plants. Cheplick et al. (2000) examined drought effects on *Lolium perenne* genotypes with and without fungal endophytes, showing that endophyte presence can alter drought response.

### Dryland Tree Establishment

Osonubi et al. (1990) studied effects of different fungal inoculation periods on performance of *Gmelina* seedlings under dry soil conditions, demonstrating practical applications of mycorrhizal inoculation for tree establishment in semi-arid regions.

## Fungi and Environmental Change

### Pollution Effects

Chen et al. (2000) reviewed weathering of rocks induced by lichen colonization, documenting how fungi contribute to soil formation and mineral weathering. This process has implications for understanding ecosystem responses to atmospheric pollution and acid deposition.

### Forest Litter Dynamics

Hughes and Fahey (1994) documented litterfall dynamics and ecosystem recovery during forest development, showing how fungal-mediated decomposition processes change with stand age and affect nutrient availability for mycorrhizal plants.

Dighton et al. (2000) studied determinants of leaf litter patchiness in mixed species New Jersey pine barrens forests and its possible influence on soil biota, linking aboveground litter patterns to belowground fungal and microbial communities.
