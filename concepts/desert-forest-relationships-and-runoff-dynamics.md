## Desert Forest Relationships and Runoff Dynamics

## Overview

The relationship between forests and [[water-management]] systems in arid areas.

### Catchment Size

Small catchments discharge greater peak flood flows than large catchments. Foothill areas will peak to floods more rapidly than plains. This means that headwater areas are where intervention is most critical — small earthworks in the headwaters can prevent large flood problems downstream.

### Stream Gradient

Steep streams discharge more rapidly than downstream areas of gentle gradient. The smaller headwater streams are usually steeper than the larger streams in the plains, which are often sand-clogged with wide flood plains. Design interventions must account for this gradient transition.

### Storm Size and Distribution

Large storms may totally wet a small catchment, causing local floods, whereas the same storm has little downstream effect. In deserts, floods commonly disappear a few kilometres from the headwaters except during very widespread rains. This means that:

- Local flood protection is more important than regional flood protection in most cases
- Water harvesting should focus on capturing local runoff rather than distant floodwaters

### Storages and Retardation

Dams, retention banks, [[interceptor-banks]], swales, natural swamps, and created wetlands all reduce or delay flood peaks. Local soil absorption is much more efficient in small streams of high density, but large catchments have greater dampening capacity when retardation areas are preserved. Many retardation areas are unfortunately drained, filled, or built upon, eliminating their flood control function.

### Catchment Form

Normal dendritic (tree-like) drainage patterns, where branches join at small angles, are subject to faster flooding due to greater streamlining. Fold-mountain or fault-area grid catchments with their abrupt turns, constricted valleys, and long runs of middle-order streams tend to moderate flood peaks. Designers should work with natural catchment form rather than against it.

### Drainage Density

The actual length of stream per square kilometre determines how efficiently water is removed from the landscape. Very dense streamlines create higher and faster flood peaks. Sheet flow across flat interfluves or braided broad streams of flat desert areas retards floodwaters. Increasing drainage density (through channelisation or clearing) accelerates water loss; decreasing it (through infiltration structures) retains water in the landscape.

### Vegetation Cover

Runoff increases progressively as vegetation is cleared. The less crown interception, humus storage, and ground interference encountered by rain, the more water runs off. The transition from diverse shrubland to tussock grassland, and from grassland to bare soil, each step increases runoff and decreases the landscape's water-holding capacity.

## Strategic Planning for Water in Arid Settlements

### Settlement Siting Considerations

- Avoid locations in wadis, upstream of restricted gaps in hills, or in hollows near dry watercourses
- Design for oversize channels, drains, infiltration, and diversion systems to cope with high-intensity storms
- Plan for 2–3 day rain events of 2.5 cm/day, which produce severe local flooding

### Water Quality Gradient

A general profile of water depth and quality from intake to salt pan should decide the strategies of settlement placement, water systems, water quality management, and disposal. Wells and windmills should be located close to hills in the loose sediments of alluvial fans, where freshwater is most accessible and least saline.

### Integration of Water Strategies

Effective arid land design integrates multiple water strategies:

- Roof catchment for drinking water (highest quality, highest priority)
- Swale systems for tree irrigation and garden water
- Interceptor banks for landscape-scale water harvesting
- Greywater recycling for supplementary garden irrigation
- Groundwater pumping (via windmill or solar) for peak demand periods
