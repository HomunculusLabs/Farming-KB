# Oss & Oeric: Psilocybe Cubensis Strain Guide

The 1976 guide focused exclusively on Stropharia cubensis (now
Psilocybe cubensis Earle ex Singer), which the authors identified
as the most easily cultivated of all psilocybin-containing mushrooms.
The mycologist Rolf Singer had recently reclassified the species
from the genus Stropharia into Psilocybe, a taxonomic change the
authors acknowledged. The guide provides detailed morphological
descriptions and discusses the species' global distribution, genetic
complexity, and relationship to other psychoactive fungi.

## Species Description

The botanical description in the guide is taken from Orson K.
Miller's *Mushrooms of North America*. Key identifying features
include:

**Cap:** 1.5-8 cm broad, conic to bell-shaped when young, becoming
convex and eventually flat with upturned edges in age. Surface is
viscid and hairless, whitish to pale yellow, light brownish in age.
The flesh is firm and white, bruising blue when damaged. The cap
stains bluish with age, a characteristic enzymatic oxidation of
indole substrates (tryptophane, serotonin, or psilocybin).

**Gills:** Adnate to adnexed (attached to notched), close spacing,
grey becoming violet-grey with white edges in maturity.

**Stem (Stipe):** 4-15 cm long, 4-14 mm thick, slightly enlarging
toward the base. Surface is dry and hairless, white, staining blue
when bruised. A persistent membranous annulus (veil remnant) is
present on the upper stem.

**Spores:** 10-17 x 7-10 micrometers, elliptical to oval in side
view, thick-walled with a large pore at the apex. Spore print is
purple-brown. Cystidia on the gill edge are club-shaped with
rounded heads.

**Chemical Tests:** The blue-staining reaction can be confirmed with
metol (p-methyl aminophenol), a photographic darkroom compound that
dissolves in 20 times its weight of water and turns deep purple
within 1-30 minutes when applied to crushed stem tissue. This test
distinguishes psilocybin-containing species from non-psychoactive
blue-staining genera like Boletus and Russula.

## Global Distribution

The authors note the species has a circumtropical distribution
spanning the Southeastern United States (Texas, Louisiana, Alabama,
Mississippi, Arkansas, Florida, Tennessee, Georgia), coastal Mexico,
and equatorial regions of South America. They also mention its
presence in Cambodia, Australia, and Colombia. The preferred
habitat is cow dung, and the authors wryly observe that the
species' worldwide distribution was "doubtless encouraged, if not
caused, by the world cattle industry," making it a "weed" on
high-technology cattle-raising cultures.

## Genetic Complexity

The guide provides a thorough explanation of the species' mating
system. Members of the family Strophariaceae are heterothallic
and tetrapolar, meaning sexual reproduction requires fusion of
two compatible monokaryotic mycelia that differ in both the A and
B mating factors (e.g., AB x ab). This tetrapolar system generates
a large number of mating types from a single basidiocarp, providing
substantial genetic diversity.

The dikaryotic mycelium, containing two nuclei per cell, is the
most prolonged stage of the life cycle and serves as the main
assimilative stage. It can propagate vegetatively indefinitely
without sexual reproduction, but given appropriate conditions,
it differentiates into the spore-bearing basidiocarp (mushroom).

## Related Genera and Species

The guide notes that "magic mushrooms" include members of the
genera Psilocybe, Stropharia, Conocybe, Panaeolus, and Copelandia,
all containing psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine)
and psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) as active agents.
These compounds share the basic indole structure characteristic of
natural hallucinogens. The authors suggest that with appropriate
adaptations, their methods could potentially be applied to other
species, though they found Stropharia cubensis the most reliable.

## Strain Selection

The authors recommend isolating multiple dikaryotic strains from
a single spore germination plate and comparing them for both
growth vigor on agar and fruiting ability over several generations.
Through observation and trial-and-error, the most vigorous strain
can be identified and used exclusively for future cultivation.

## Species Identification and History

The guide acknowledges the taxonomic confusion surrounding the
species. Originally described as Stropharia cubensis by Earle in
1906, the species was later transferred to the genus Psilocybe
by Singer and Smith in 1958. The guide uses the original name
throughout, reflecting the nomenclature current at the time of
writing (1976). The name P. cubensis is now widely accepted.

The species was first described from specimens collected in Cuba,
hence the specific epithet "cubensis." It is pan-tropical and
subtropical in distribution, commonly found in the wild growing
on bovine dung in warm, humid climates. The species has been
reported from tropical and subtropical regions worldwide,
including Central and South America, Southeast Asia, Australia,
and sub-Saharan Africa.
