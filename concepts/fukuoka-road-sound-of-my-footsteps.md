# The Sound of My Footsteps: Walking Through Europe

Walking through Europe in geta (Japanese wooden clogs),
masanobu-fukuoka [[fukuoka-road-one-straw-revolution-continuation]]|Fukuoka discovered that the sound of his footsteps became a
medium for philosophical and cultural observation. The
rhythmic clatter of geta on European cobblestone streets
opened windows into national character, the relationship
between people and the land, and the nature of Eastern
philosophy. See also [[fukuoka-road-roots-of-clothing]].

## The Music of Cobblestone Streets

As Fukuoka walked, his geta rang out on the cobblestones.
He discovered just how good a sound Europe's cobblestone
streets give off. Japan's roads are paved with asphalt,
which prompted him to reflect on what type of road is good
for walking. Europe's streets, both in large cities and
small country villages, are still paved with stone as in
the past.

He observed the strong attachment of West Europeans to
stone pavement when watching them repair their roads.
Workers wearing leather leggings dig up and replace the
pavement stones one at a time. Laid down strategically long
ago, town roads twist and wind and are almost impassable by
car. These are left unchanged because people do not wish to
destroy the stone pavement. As a result, no straight roads
run through the towns.

## Each Stone Creates Its Own Music

The top surface of a paving stone is about six inches
square, and the stone runs about a foot deep. Carriage
tracks and the sound of horses' hooves are said to be
carved into these. Because each stone is different, walking
over the road creates music with a rhythm all its own.
Whether you like it or not, as you walk, you constantly
hear the sound of your own footsteps.

Fukuoka felt that the sound of the Orient echoes within the
geta. Whether it has a ringing cadence ("ke-tang, ke-tong"),
an even beat ("clippety, cloppety"), or a lonely
"picky-pocky" depends on the weather and climate. And when
one feels rushed inside as one walks, the geta call out:
"What are you in such an awful hurry for?" The geta always
give warning, whether you walk quickly, slowly, or
absentmindedly.

## Reading National Character Through Walking

Listening to the sound of his geta put Fukuoka in the mood
for writing haiku. He composed several poems about his geta
journey. More significantly, he found he could tell
something about the national characters of people by their
reaction to the geta.

Children were always the first to notice. They would stare
intently from far up the road and continue watching until
he had passed. Sometimes he even teased them playfully. But
adults did not notice. Englishmen, Austrians, and Germans
strutting along self-importantly, with their heads held
high in a pompous manner, were very slow to take notice.

The self-deprecating Italians, who claim to be the most
ridiculed people of Europe, notice quickly. This is because
of their low posture, apparent even from the way they walk.
The Dutch, who themselves wear clogs or sabots, were also
quick to notice.
