# Mycorrhizae, Plant Fitness, and Community Structure

## Overview

Mycorrhizal associations influence not only plant growth but also plant fitness—the ability of a plant to survive, reproduce, and contribute offspring to the next generation. When mycorrhizal benefits are asymmetric across plant species in a community, they can shift competitive balances and alter plant community composition. This page examines the evidence for mycorrhizal effects on plant fitness, the continuum of plant responses to mycorrhizal colonization, and the mechanisms by which mycorrhizae shape plant communities.

## Mycorrhizae and Reproductive Fitness

### Enhanced Reproductive Output

Beyond improving vegetative growth through enhanced nutrient acquisition, mycorrhizal associations significantly increase reproductive potential:

- **Sanders et al. (1995)**: Plants with arbuscular mycorrhizae showed improved phosphate nutrition, significant increases in flower bud production, and enhanced seed production
- **Koide et al. (1988)**: Documented mycorrhizal enhancement of reproductive potential
- **Stanley et al. (1993)**, **Lewis and Koide (1990)**, **Bryla and Koide (1990)**, **Koide and Lu (1992)**: All demonstrated increased reproductive potential in mycorrhizal plants, leading to improvements in offspring vigor

### Transgenerational Effects

The fitness benefits of mycorrhizal colonization can extend across generations:

- **Heppell et al. (1998)** showed that offspring of arbuscular mycorrhizal-infected *Abutilon theophrasti* were significantly larger than offspring of nonmycorrhizal parents
- Under high-density conditions, mycorrhizal offspring improved even more due to effects of early self-thinning
- This fitness advantage transferred to the next generation in terms of total seed production:

| Offspring Age (days) | Fitness Parameter | Mycorrhizal Parent | Nonmycorrhizal Parent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | Shoot height (cm) | 12.5 | 9.4 |
| 20 | Shoot dry mass (g) | 61.2 | 30.9 |
| 20 | Leaf number | 3.6 | 3.0 |
| 47 | Shoot height (cm) | 30.6 | 19.8 |
| 47 | Shoot dry mass (g) | 521 | 154 |
| 47 | Leaf number | 4.4 | 3.4 |
| 94 | Survivors per box | 59.1 | 26.6 |
| 94 | Seeds per survivor | 17.9 | 10.6 |

These transgenerational effects mean that mycorrhizal colonization can have lasting impacts on plant population dynamics that extend well beyond the lifetime of the originally colonized individual.

### Mechanisms of Fitness Enhancement

Mycorrhizal enhancement of offspring vigor operates through several mechanisms:
- Increased seedling germination rates
- Greater leaf area development
- Improved root:shoot ratios
- Enhanced root enzyme production
- Better competitive ability under high-density conditions

## Continuum of Plant Responses to Mycorrhizae

### The Francis and Read Framework

Francis and Read (1994, 1995) reviewed the effects of mycorrhizae on plant community composition and proposed that arbuscular mycorrhizal effects exist along a continuum:

### K-Selection vs. Ruderal Species

The response to mycorrhizae correlates with plant life history strategy:
- **K-selected species** (slow-growing, competitive, resource-conserving) generally benefit most from mycorrhizal associations
- **Ruderal species** (fast-growing, disturbance-adapted) may be adversely affected by mycorrhizal colonization
- This pattern suggests that mycorrhizae can differentially regulate plant communities by favoring certain life history strategies over others

### Implications for Community Structure

Janos (1980) suggested that mycorrhizal effects can be a significant factor determining plant species composition, particularly in the tropics. The asymmetric nature of mycorrhizal benefits means that:

1. **Competitive displacement**: Species that benefit strongly from mycorrhizae can outcompete species that benefit less or are harmed
2. **Successional direction**: Mycorrhizal dependence can influence which species dominate at different successional stages
3. **Coexistence mechanisms**: The continuum of responses may allow coexistence of species with different mycorrhizal dependencies in the same community

## Seasonal Dynamics of Mycorrhizal Benefits

### Bluebell (*Hyacinthoides non-scripta*) Case Study

Mycorrhizal benefits are not constant throughout the year. Research on bluebells in natural ecosystems demonstrated:

- Greatest phosphate uptake occurs at specific times of the year when nutrient demand is highest
- The temporal pattern of mycorrhizal benefit corresponds to the plant's phenological cycle
- This seasonal variation means that mycorrhizal effects on competitive interactions may shift throughout the growing season

Sanders and Fitter (1992a, 1992b) documented seasonal patterns of mycorrhizal occurrence and morphology in co-existing grassland species, showing that mycorrhizal function varies both within and between species across the growing season.

## Driver vs. Passenger Hypothesis

Hart et al. (2001) proposed two competing hypotheses to explain successional changes in arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal communities:

### Passenger Hypothesis
- Mycorrhizal communities are determined by the plant community
- Changes in mycorrhizal species composition are passive responses to changes in plant species and environmental conditions
- Fungi are "passengers" along for the ride as plant communities change

### Driver Hypothesis
- Mycorrhizal fungi determine plant species composition through interspecific differences in colonization and persistence potential
- Fungi actively "drive" plant community composition by differentially benefiting certain plant species
- The mycorrhizal community is the causal agent, not the consequence

### Evidence and Reconciliation

Both hypotheses likely operate to some degree, with the relative importance varying across:
- **Environmental conditions**: Nutrient availability, soil moisture, and pH may shift the balance
- **Successional stage**: Early succession may be more passenger-driven, while later stages may be more driver-driven
- **Plant community diversity**: More diverse plant communities may provide stronger passenger effects

## Population and Community Regulation

Population and community regulation by fungi results from either promotion or reduction in growth, fitness, or reproduction potential of organisms. When fitness effects are asymmetric among species in a community, selective pressures shift the relative abundance and standing of community members across successive generations.

The variety of direct and indirect effects through which fungi alter populations and communities includes:
- **Mycorrhizal-mediated nutrient competition**: Differential nutrient acquisition advantages among plant species
- **Pathogen-driven selection**: Preferential pathogen attack on certain species
- **Endophyte-conferred resistance**: Selective protection of host plants from herbivory
- **Faunal-mediated spore dispersal**: Non-random distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum

## Research Implications

Understanding mycorrhizal effects on plant fitness has practical applications:
1. **Restoration ecology**: Inoculation strategies for revegetation projects should consider which plant species will benefit most from mycorrhizae
2. **Agriculture**: Crop selection and rotation can be optimized based on mycorrhizal responsiveness
3. **Invasive species management**: Invasive plants that are highly mycorrhizal-responsive may gain competitive advantages in novel environments
4. **Climate change predictions**: Shifts in mycorrhizal communities under changing conditions will cascade through plant communities
