# Mycoremediation of PAHs and PCBs

## Overview

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are among the most persistent and toxic environmental pollutants. Both are recalcitrant aromatic compounds that resist conventional degradation, but white-rot fungi and their ligninolytic enzyme systems offer effective biological remediation pathways. Based on Singh's *Mycoremediation*, *Fungi in Bioremediation*, and Gadd et al.'s *Fungi in the Environment*.

## Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs)

### What Are PAHs?
PAHs are organic compounds composed of fused aromatic rings. They are produced by incomplete combustion of organic materials and are widespread environmental contaminants:
- **Sources**: Vehicle emissions, coal tar, creosote, petroleum spills, wood burning, industrial processes
- **Properties**: Hydrophobic, low water solubility, tendency to bind to soil organic matter and particulates
- **Toxicity**: Many PAHs are carcinogenic, mutagenic, and teratogenic. Benzo[a]pyrene is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen.
- **Environmental persistence**: High molecular weight PAHs (4-6 rings) can persist in soil for decades

### Fungal Degradation Mechanisms

The primary pathway is through ligninolytic enzymes:

**Laccase (EC 1.10.3.2)**:
- Copper-containing oxidase; does not require H2O2
- Oxidizes phenolic PAHs directly
- With mediators (e.g., ABTS, HBT, 1-hydroxybenzotriazole), can oxidize non-phenolic PAHs
- Most versatile for PAH degradation among fungal enzymes

**Manganese Peroxidase (MnP) (EC 1.11.1.13)**:
- Generates Mn(III)-organic acid complexes as diffusible oxidants
- Degrades PAHs with 2-5 rings
- Important in soil environments where Mn is abundant

**Lignin Peroxidase (LiP) (EC 1.11.1.14)**:
- Most powerful oxidant of the ligninolytic system
- Degrades high molecular weight PAHs (5-6 rings)
- Limited by H2O2 availability and sensitivity to environmental conditions

**Cytochrome P450**:
- Intracellular enzyme system for initial oxidation
- Important for fungi that lack strong ligninolytic systems
- Works on lower molecular weight PAHs

### PAH Degradation by Ring Number

| Ring number | Example compounds | Degradability | Primary enzymes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 rings | Naphthalene | High | Cytochrome P450, laccase |
| 3 rings | Anthracene, phenanthrene | Moderate-High | Laccase, MnP, P450 |
| 4 rings | Pyrene, chrysene, benzo[a]anthracene | Moderate | Laccase (with mediators), MnP |
| 5 rings | Benzo[a]pyrene, dibenzo[a,h]anthracene | Low-Moderate | LiP, laccase (with mediators) |
| 6 rings | Coronene, benzo[ghi]perylene | Low | LiP |

### Key Fungal Species for PAH Degradation
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: Most studied white-rot fungus; effective on 3-5 ring PAHs
- **[[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]]**: Strong laccase producer; effective on diverse PAHs; tolerant to environmental stress
- **[[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]**: Oyster mushroom; effective PAH degrader; edible (potential for combined remediation and cultivation)
- **Bjerkandera adusta**: Produces MnP and laccase; effective in soil systems
- **[[ganoderma-lucidum-reishi-comprehensive-profile]]**: Laccase producer; shows promise for contaminated soil
- **Non-white-rot fungi**: Some ascomycetes (Cunninghamella, Penicillium) degrade PAHs through cytochrome P450 systems

## Polychlorinated Biphenyls (PCBs)

### What Are PCBs?
PCBs are synthetic chlorinated aromatic compounds once widely used in electrical equipment, hydraulic fluids, and other industrial applications:
- **Production**: Manufactured from 1929-1979 (US); over 1.5 million tons produced globally
- **Properties**: Extremely stable, lipophilic, bioaccumulative
- **Toxicity**: Endocrine disruption, neurotoxicity, carcinogenicity; classified as persistent organic pollutants (POPs) under the Stockholm Convention
- **Environmental persistence**: Half-lives in soil range from years to decades; 209 possible congeners with varying toxicity

### Fungal Degradation Mechanisms

PCB degradation by fungi proceeds through two main pathways:

**Aerobic degradation (ligninolytic enzymes)**:
- Laccase can directly oxidize lower chlorinated PCBs (di- and tri-chlorinated)
- With redox mediators, laccase degrades higher chlorinated congeners
- MnP indirectly oxidizes PCBs through Mn(III) intermediates
- Degradation products include hydroxylated PCBs and ring-opened compounds

**Anaerobic dechlorination (by fungal-mediated conditions)**:
- Some fungi create microaerophilic or anaerobic zones in their mycelial mats
- Reductive dechlorination removes chlorine atoms, making congeners more susceptible to subsequent aerobic degradation

### Key Fungal Species for PCB Degradation
- **Trametes versicolor**: Most effective white-rot species; strong laccase production
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: Degrades lower chlorinated PCBs
- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Effective with mediator-enhanced systems
- **[[lentinula-edodes]]**: Shiitake mushroom; degrades PCBs in contaminated soil
- **Aspergillus niger**: Non-ligninolytic but produces laccase under certain conditions

## Field Applications and Case Studies

### Creosote-Contaminated Sites
- Former wood treatment facilities often have PAH concentrations exceeding 10,000 mg/kg
- White-rot fungal inoculation combined with nutrient amendments has achieved 50-80% PAH reduction over 6-12 months
- Pleurotus ostreatus showed particular effectiveness in field trials

### Manufactured Gas Plant Sites
- Coal tar contamination with mixed PAHs and other aromatic compounds
- Fungal inoculation in biopiles achieved significant degradation of 3-4 ring PAHs
- Higher ring PAHs required longer treatment times and mediator supplementation

### PCB-Contaminated Soils
- Laboratory and pilot-scale studies show 30-70% PCB degradation by white-rot fungi
- Field results are more variable due to bioavailability constraints and environmental factors
- Combined fungal-bacterial approaches show promise

## Enhancing Fungal Degradation

### Strategies to Improve Performance
1. **Mediator addition**: ABTS, HBT, or natural mediators (syringaldehyde, vanillin) extend laccase substrate range
2. **Nutrient optimization**: Nitrogen limitation induces ligninolytic enzyme production
3. **Surfactant addition**: Biosurfactants increase PAH/PCB bioavailability
4. **Co-substrate addition**: Straw, sawdust, or other lignocellulosic materials support fungal growth and enzyme production
5. **Combined approaches**: Sequential anaerobic-aerobic treatment; fungal-bacterial consortia
6. **Genetic improvement**: Strain selection and genetic modification to enhance enzyme production

### Bioavailability Constraints
The primary limitation in field remediation. PAHs and PCBs strongly adsorb to soil organic matter, reducing the fraction available for fungal degradation. Approaches include:
- Cyclodextrin addition to increase aqueous solubility
- Biosurfactant production by fungi or co-inoculated bacteria
- Gentle soil mixing to increase contact
- Use of organic amendments that compete for sorption sites

## Monitoring and Assessment

- **Chemical analysis**: GC-MS quantification of parent compounds and metabolites
- **Toxicity testing**: Bioassays (Microtox, earthworm, plant) to assess ecotoxicological improvement
- **Enzyme assays**: Laccase, MnP, and LiP activity measurements in soil
- **Molecular methods**: qPCR and metagenomics to track fungal populations and gene expression
- **Mineralization assays**: Radiolabeled compounds to confirm complete mineralization vs. partial transformation

## Limitations

- Incomplete mineralization: Some PAH metabolites may be more toxic than parent compounds
- Scale-up challenges: Laboratory conditions (sterile, controlled) differ greatly from field conditions
- Competition with native microorganisms
- Enzyme sensitivity to environmental conditions (temperature, pH, heavy metals)
- Regulatory and public acceptance issues for deliberate fungal release
- Long treatment times compared to excavation or chemical treatment
