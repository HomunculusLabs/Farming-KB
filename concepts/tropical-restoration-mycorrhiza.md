## AMF in Tropical Soil Biology

### Indigenous AMF Communities

Native [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]] harbor diverse AMF communities that are adapted to local conditions:

- AMF diversity varies with successional stage, soil type, and vegetation composition
- Early-successional and late-successional AMF species differ in their ecological strategies
- Fragmented landscapes create heterogeneous AMF inoculum potentials across the landscape
- Soil disturbance (clearing, compaction, erosion) dramatically reduces AMF propagule density

### Mycorrhizal Dependency in Tropical Species

Tropical plant species show a range of mycorrhizal dependencies:

- **Obligate mycotrophs**: Require AMF for survival and normal growth
- **Facultative mycotrophs**: Benefit from AMF but can grow without them
- **Non-mycotrophs**: Do not form [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]]
- The magnitude of RGR enhancement varies with species and AMF inoculum source

### Mycorrhizal Dependency

- Quantified as the relative biomass difference between mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal plants
- Pioneer species often show higher dependency than late-successional species
- Dependency is influenced by soil [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-in-tropical-forest-restoration]] in Mexico

## Landscape-Level Considerations

### Forest Fragmentation

Tropical rain forest fragmentation affects AMF-mediated restoration:

- Small fragments have reduced AMF diversity and inoculum potential
- Large fragments serve as AMF reservoirs for surrounding degraded areas
- Edge effects create gradients in AMF [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]]
- Fragment connectivity influences the recolonization potential of AMF in restored areas
