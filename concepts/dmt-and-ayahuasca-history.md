# DMT and Ayahuasca History

[[ayahuasca-brewing-guide]] represent what McKenna called "the most profound of the indole-hallucinogen-induced ecstasies." The story of DMT spans from indigenous Amazonian shamanism to modern psychopharmacology, revealing a pharmacological sophistication that Western science did not match until the 1950s.

## Ayahuasca: The Vine of Souls

The word ayahuasca is Quechua, roughly translating as "vine of the dead" or "vine of souls." It refers both to the prepared hallucinogenic beverage and to one of its main ingredients, the woody liana now classified as Banisteriopsis caapi.

Ayahuasca has continued to the present day as a central element in the spiritual life of many tribes in the montane rain forest of South America. Immigrants into the Amazon basin—including mestizo populations—have also adopted it, creating their own ethnobotanical-medical systems for using the psychedelic visions to promote healing. In native areas of use, ayahuasca is regarded as a general-purpose healing elixir and is called *la purga*—the purge—because of its purgative effects. Its effectiveness in combating intestinal parasites has been scientifically verified, and its effectiveness in killing the malaria organism is now being investigated.

## The Pharmacological Miracle

The tissues of Banisteriopsis caapi are rich in beta-carboline alkaloids, primarily [[shipibo-icaros-healing-songs]]—magical curing songs. The experience induced by ayahuasca produces visual hallucinations particularly susceptible to being "driven" by vocally produced sound. Consequently, one of the legacies of the ayahuasca-using cultures is a large repository of these songs. The effectiveness and sophistication of an ayahuasquero is predicated upon how many magical songs they have effectively memorized. In curing sessions, both patient and healer ingest ayahuasca, and the singing becomes a shared, largely visual experience.

McKenna's own experiences among mestizo populations of Amazonas convinced him that "the long-term effect of ayahuasca use is an extraordinary state of health and integration," with methods that "exhibit startling parallels to the techniques of modern psychotherapy."

## DMT in Human Metabolism

DMT occurs as part of ordinary human neurometabolism. McKenna called it "the most powerful of the naturally occurring indole hallucinogens." The ease with which DMT "utterly destroys all boundaries and conveys one into an impossible-to-anticipate and compellingly Other dimension" is, he argued, one of the miracles of life itself—followed by a second miracle: the speed with which brain enzymes recognize, bind, and harmlessly inactivate DMT molecules, restoring ordinary amine levels within minutes.

McKenna saw this as evidence of "a long co-evolutionary association between human beings and hallucinogenic tryptamines." The fact that the most powerful hallucinogen known is also a naturally occurring human metabolite, processed with such efficiency by the brain's own enzymes, suggests that this relationship is not accidental but evolutionary. As McKenna concluded: "This first miracle is followed by a second: the utter ease and simplicity with which enzyme systems in the human brain recognize the DMT molecules at the synapses."

## McKenna's Own Ayahuasca Experience

McKenna included a vivid personal account of his first deep-forest ayahuasca experience in *Food of the Gods*. Traveling to an unnamed location on the Rio Cumala, he drank the "black, slow-flowing liquid" prepared by an old woman—Dona Catalina—whose face was "startlingly hideous" in the firelight. The brew tasted "warm and salty, chalky and bittersweet... like the blood of some old, old thing."

After losing consciousness, McKenna experienced "surfing on the inner curl of a wave of brightly lit transparent information several hundred feet high." Exhilaration gave way to terror, then to a feeling of being a shipwrecked sailor washed onto a tropical shore. At this point the old woman began to sing an icaro—"a magical curing song that in our intoxicated and ecstatic state seemed more like a tropical reef fish or an animated silk scarf of many colors than a vocal performance. The song is a visible manifestation of power, enfolding us and making us secure."

This experience anchored McKenna's conviction that shamanism was not metaphorical but real—that "the world of shamanism, of allies, shape shifting, and magical attack are far more real than the constructs of science can ever be, because these spirit ancestors and their other world can be seen and felt, they can be known, in the nonordinary reality."
