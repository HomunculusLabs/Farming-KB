## Vertebrate Soil Organisms and Plant Benefits of the Soil Food Web

## Overview

While microorganisms and microarthropods form the vast numerical majority of the soil food web, vertebrate organisms — reptiles, mammals, and birds — play an essential mechanical and ecological role. Their burrowing, feeding, and movement physically reshape soil structure, redistribute organic matter, and create pathways for water and air. The soil food web also provides plants with critical benefits: nutrient retention, nutrient cycling, soil structure maintenance, disease suppression, pH-regulated nitrogen availability, and pollutant degradation. Understanding these benefits and how they relate to the fungal-to-bacterial ratio is central to applying soil food web principles in gardening.

---

## Vertebrate Organisms in the Soil Food Web

### Key Species

A wide range of vertebrate animals interact directly with soil ecosystems:

- **Rodents and small mammals:** squirrels, mice, groundhogs, rabbits, chipmunks, voles, moles, prairie dogs, and gophers
- **Reptiles:** snakes and lizards
- **Large browsers:** moose and deer
- **Birds:** ground-foraging and burrowing species whose dung enters the soil system

### Mechanical Contributions to Soil

Vertebrate soil organisms contribute to soil health primarily through physical disturbance:

- **Burrowing** creates channels and tunnels that serve as pathways for water infiltration and air exchange, improving drainage and reducing compaction
- **Movement through soil** mixes organic and mineral layers, distributing decomposing material deeper into the profile
- **Organic matter deposition** occurs through dung, shed skin, hair, feathers, and eventually the animals' own bodies — all becoming food for the decomposer community
- **Creation of reservoirs** — burrow systems act as temporary water storage, improving moisture availability

### Ecological Context

Despite their visible impact, vertebrate soil organisms are vastly outnumbered by microarthropods (mites, springtails) and microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes). These smaller organisms form the functional foundation upon which vertebrate contributions rest. Without microbial decomposers, organic matter deposited by vertebrates would accumulate rather than being recycled.

### Dung as a Food Web Resource

The dung of reptiles, mammals, and birds is a significant organic input, providing a concentrated carbon and nutrient source for bacteria and fungi, food for coprophagous insects like dung beetles, and a substrate that kickstarts localized microbial activity [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]].

## Benefits of the Soil Food Web to Plants

### 1. Nutrient Retention

Soil organisms hold nutrients in biological forms, preventing leaching. Bacteria and fungi assimilate nutrients into their biomass; when consumed by [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]], nutrients are released in plant-available forms at the root zone. This biological retention is far more efficient than relying on the [[cation-exchange-capacity]] of mineral particles alone.

### 2. Nutrient Cycling

The food web converts organic matter — dead plant and animal material, dung, [[root-exudates]] — into inorganic forms plants can absorb. Decomposer fungi and bacteria break down complex organic compounds; protozoa and bacterial-feeding nematodes consume them and excrete ammonium (NH₄⁺). This **microbial loop** is the primary natural pathway for organic nitrogen to become plant-available.

### 3. Maintaining Soil Structure

- **Aggregation:** Bacterial glues (slimes) and fungal hyphae bind soil particles into stable aggregates
- **Pore creation:** [[soil-burrowing-organisms]] and root channels create macropores for air and water movement
- Well-aggregated soil resists erosion, supports deeper root growth, and maintains favorable moisture

### 4. Suppression of Disease

A diverse [[soil-food-web]] protects plants through multiple mechanisms:

- **Predation:** Beneficial nematodes, protozoa, and fungi consume or parasitize pathogens
- **Competition:** Beneficial organisms compete with pathogens for nutrients, space, and oxygen
- **Physical barriers:** Fungi and bacteria colonize root surfaces, forming protective biofilms that block herbivores and invaders
- **[[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]]:** Some beneficial organisms trigger plant immune responses for above-ground protection

### 5. Rhizosphere pH Regulation

Soil organisms influence rhizosphere pH, determining nitrogen form availability:

- **Bacterial-dominated soils** have higher pH (>7) due to bacterial slime, favoring nitrifying bacteria that convert ammonium to nitrate (NO₃⁻)
- **Fungal-dominated soils** have lower pH (<7) due to fungal organic acids, suppressing nitrifying bacteria and retaining ammonium (NH₄⁺)

### 6. Pollutant Degradation

Soil organisms can metabolize or sequester lawn/garden chemicals, air and water pollutants, and other contaminants. The principle that "something in soil eats almost anything you can find there" reflects the extraordinary metabolic diversity of [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]].

## Rules for Applying the Soil Food Web in Gardening

### The Nitrogen Preference Framework

| Form | Chemical | Behavior in Soil | Preferred By |
|------|----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ | Anion; dissolves in soil water; does not bind to humus/clay; prone to leaching | Annuals, vegetables, grasses |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ | Cation; binds to soil colloids; less mobile; retained in root zone | Trees, shrubs, perennials |

### Rule #1: Plants Prefer Specific Soil Food Web Dominance

The fungal-to-bacterial ratio determines which nitrogen form predominates:

- **Bacterially dominated soils** produce nitrate nitrogen — suited to fast-growing, short-lived plants
- **Fungally dominated soils** retain ammonium nitrogen — suited to long-lived, woody plants

### Rule #2: Vegetables, Annuals, and Grasses Prefer Bacterial Soils

These plants are adapted to nitrate-dominant nutrition and perform best where bacteria outnumber fungi. Seasonal growth cycles align with rapid bacterial nutrient cycling.

### Rule #3: Trees, Shrubs, and Perennials Prefer Fungal Soils

Longer-lived plants are adapted to ammonium-dominant nutrition and perform best with significant fungal biomass. Fungal hyphae extend the effective root zone, and slower nutrient release matches steady perennial growth demands.

### Plant Lifespan Determines Nitrogen Preference

- **Seasonal plants** (lifespan < 1 year) → prefer nitrate nitrogen → bacterial soils
- **Perennial plants** (lifespan ≥ 1 year) → prefer ammonium nitrogen → fungal soils

### Plant Succession and the Fungal-to-Bacterial Ratio

Ecological succession follows a predictable shift in soil food web composition:

1. **Pioneer weeds** — earliest colonizers; strongly bacterial
2. **Grasses** — bacterial dominance continues
3. **Shrubs** — fungal biomass begins to increase
4. **Soft hardwoods** — fungal dominance grows
5. **Expanding saplings** — continued fungal increase
6. **Mature hardwoods** — strongly fungal
7. **Old-growth forest** — peak fungal biomass

Throughout succession, **fungal biomass increases steadily** while **bacterial biomass remains roughly constant**. The fungal-to-bacterial ratio is the key metric tracking successional stage.

### The Prairie Exception

Prairie ecosystems represent a special case where plants prefer a **balanced ratio** of fungi to bacteria, similar to lawn grasses. Prairie soils are neither strongly bacterial nor fungal, reflecting the mixed perennial and annual composition of prairie communities.

### Caution: Inorganic Soluble Nitrogen Fertilizers

Conventional inorganic nitrogen fertilizers deliver nitrate directly but are detrimental to soil food webs:

- They bypass the microbial loop, starving protozoa and nematodes that depend on consuming bacteria and fungi
- Nitrates, as anions, do not bind to humus or clay and readily leach into groundwater
- Excess soluble nitrogen disrupts fungal-bacterial balance, often favoring bacterial overgrowth and reducing fungal network protections
- Long-term use degrades soil structure, reduces organic matter, and increases chemical dependence

## Key Takeaways

- Vertebrate soil organisms physically engineer soil through burrowing, mixing, and organic matter deposition, but their impact depends on microbial communities that process their contributions
- [[the-soil-food-web]] provides plants with nutrient retention, cycling, soil structure, disease suppression, pH-regulated nitrogen, [[staycare-lignin-peroxidase-and-pollutant-degradation]]
- The fungal-to-bacterial ratio determines whether nitrogen is available as nitrate (bacterial soils) or ammonium (fungal soils)
- Matching plant type to soil food web composition is foundational: annuals/vegetables for bacterial soils, trees/perennials for fungal soils
- Plant succession naturally shifts soils from bacterial to fungal dominance, and a plant's lifespan reliably predicts its nitrogen preference
