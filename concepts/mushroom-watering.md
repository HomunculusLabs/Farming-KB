## Mushroom Watering

Watering is one of the most critical aspects of mushroom
cultivation. Too much water destroys [[mushroom-growing-houses]]. Even leafy plants need little
water when shut from bright light, and mushrooms are even
more vulnerable without leaves.

## When to Begin Watering

Cuthill established clear timing rules:

- Never water until the bed comes into bearing
- Not until the sixth week after spawning at earliest
- Ideally, tops should not appear sooner than two months
  after the bed is made

He observed that fine crops appear in fields after dry
summers, never wet ones. This underpinned his conservative
approach to [[azurescens-outdoor-bed-preparation-woodchips]].

## Liquid Manure Water

Cuthill generally used liquid manure-water and was not very
particular about its strength, noting that "mushrooms are
fond of good things." His standard mixture was half liquid
manure and half plain water. This supplemented the rich salts
and ammonia preserved in well-compacted manure, supporting
sustained bearing over the bed's productive life.

## Seasonal Watering

### Winter

During winter, the key was taking the chill off water and
avoiding excess moisture. Lower temperatures meant slower
evaporation, and enclosed conditions meant moisture lingered
longer. Watering had to be even more conservative than in
milder seasons.

### Summer

For summer cultivation, Cuthill developed an innovative
technique: rather than watering the mould directly, he
sprinkled the mats and straw coverings. This extracted heat
from the bed surface through damp coverings, keeping
[[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]] low enough to prevent maggot breeding. He
illustrated this with an analogy: butter under a basin with
a water-soaked cloth would harden regardless of hot weather.

In the hot summer of 1863, beds in glass houses exposed to
the sun had shade temperatures of 80 to 90 degrees, yet
produced fine maggot-free crops using this technique. But if
watering was neglected, failure resulted. Too much water
would drown the bed.

### September

New September beds required minimal watering. A slight hay
covering prevented surface cracking, but had to be removed
periodically to dry, preventing mould growth. The hay was
replaced after drying, maintaining consistent conditions
through the critical early weeks of [[mushroom-spawn]]
development.

## After Watering

After watering, the hay covering was put out to dry, or dry
hay replaced once water had penetrated. This cycle prevented
the cover from becoming mouldy while ensuring adequate
hydration. All cracks should be filled with fine mould before
bearing begins, as cracks channel water to lower levels,
creating uneven moisture distribution across the bed.
