# Phytohormone Cross-Kingdom Effects on Fungi

The Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt (1990) study documenting brassinosteroid
effects on Psilocybe cubensis was the first published report of a
brassinosteroid affecting fungal growth. This finding highlighted a broader
and often overlooked phenomenon: phytohormones (plant hormones) can have
significant biological effects on fungi, blurring the traditional boundary
between plant and fungal hormonal signaling systems. Understanding these
cross-kingdom interactions has implications for both basic mycology and
applied mushroom cultivation.

## Classes of Phytohormones

The major classes of phytohormones include auxins (indole-3-acetic acid and
related compounds), gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, ethylene,
brassinosteroids, jasmonates, salicylic acid, and strigolactones. Each class
regulates distinct aspects of plant growth and development, from cell
division and elongation to stress responses and senescence.

Several of these hormone classes have known effects on fungi, though the
extent and significance of these effects varies considerably. Gibberellins,
ironically, were first discovered as fungal metabolites produced by
Gibberella fujikuroi, the causal agent of bakanae disease in rice. This
historical precedent established that the same compounds can function as
both fungal secondary metabolites and plant hormones.

## Brassinosteroids and Fungi

Prior to the 1990 Gartz study, no research had been published on the
occurrence or activity of brassinosteroids in fungi. Despite extensive
investigation of brassinosteroids in plant systems, the fungal kingdom had
been entirely overlooked as a potential domain of brassinosteroid biology.

The Gartz study demonstrated that synthetic 22S,23S-homobrassinolide at
10^-2 ppm dramatically increased mycelial growth rate (2-3 times faster on
agar) and enhanced fruiting (earlier onset, higher yield, improved quality)
in P. cubensis. The authors concluded that brassinosteroids "could also
play a physiological role in higher fungi," suggesting the possibility of
endogenous fungal brassinosteroid production.

## Auxins in Fungal Systems

Auxins, particularly indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), are the most extensively
studied phytohormones in fungal contexts. Many fungi produce IAA, and it
has been detected in mycelial cultures of numerous species. IAA production
by fungi has been implicated in plant pathogenicity, as fungal-derived auxin
can manipulate host plant growth to benefit the pathogen.

In mycorrhizal fungi, IAA production may facilitate the establishment and
maintenance of symbiotic associations with plant roots. The fungal auxin
stimulates lateral root formation, increasing the surface area available
for nutrient exchange between fungus and host. This represents a clear
example of a phytohormone mediating plant-fungal communication.

## Ethylene and Fungal Interactions

Ethylene, a gaseous plant hormone, plays important roles in plant defense
against fungal pathogens. Many fungi produce ethylene, and plant-derived
ethylene can either stimulate or inhibit fungal growth depending on the
species and concentration. The ethylene signaling pathway in plants
interacts with jasmonic acid signaling to coordinate defense responses
against necrotrophic fungal pathogens.

Some fungal pathogens have evolved mechanisms to manipulate plant ethylene
signaling, either suppressing ethylene production to avoid detection or
stimulating excessive ethylene to promote symptom development that benefits
fungal spread. This molecular arms race illustrates the complexity of
phytohormone-mediated plant-fungal interactions.

## Gibberellins: A Fungal Origin Story

Gibberellic acid (GA3) and related gibberellins were first isolated from
Gibberella fujikuroi (now reclassified as Fusarium fujikuroi), a fungus
that causes excessive elongation of rice seedlings. The discovery that a
fungal metabolite could cause dramatic plant growth effects led to the
identification of gibberellins as a major class of plant hormones.

Gibberellins are now known to be produced by both plants and fungi through
independently evolved biosynthetic pathways. The fungal pathway involves
distinct enzymes and intermediates compared to the plant pathway, but
converges on structurally identical end products. This remarkable case of
convergent evolution parallels the potential situation with brassinosteroids
suggested by the Gartz study.

## Implications for Mushroom Cultivation

The cross-kingdom activity of phytohormones on fungi has practical
implications for mushroom cultivation. If commercially available plant
growth regulators can enhance fungal growth and fruiting, they represent
potential additives for improving yield and efficiency in mushroom farms.

The extremely low effective concentrations of brassinosteroids (nanomolar
range) make them economically attractive, as very small quantities would be
needed to treat large volumes of substrate. However, regulatory approval
for the use of plant hormones in food production would need to be addressed
before commercial application could proceed.

## Evolutionary Considerations

The existence of cross-kingdom phytohormone activity raises evolutionary
questions. Do plants and fungi share common signaling pathways inherited
from a common ancestor? Or have these interactions evolved independently
through convergent evolution? The answer likely differs for different
hormone classes. Steroid signaling, for instance, is ancient and widespread
across eukaryotes, making shared ancestry plausible for brassinosteroid
responses in plants and fungi.

An alternative explanation is ecological: phytohormones may have evolved
as signaling molecules in one kingdom and been co-opted by the other through
ecological interactions such as pathogenesis, symbiosis, or competition.
In this view, fungal sensitivity to brassinosteroids might reflect an
evolutionary response to plant-produced compounds encountered in natural
habitats.
