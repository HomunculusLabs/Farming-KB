[[sacred-mushroom-and-the-cross]] ceremonies represent one of humanity's oldest
religious practices, involving the ritual consumption of
psychoactive fungi to achieve altered states of consciousness,
divine visions, and spiritual healing. These ceremonies are
found across multiple cultures and continents, from Mesoamerican
veladas to Siberian Amanita rituals, reflecting a universal human
recognition of mushrooms as conduits to the sacred.

## Mesoamerican Mushroom Ceremonies

The modern mushroom ceremony of the Mazatec Indians of
northeastern Oaxaca illustrates the importance of the ritual in
present-day Mexico and how the sacred character of these plants
has persisted from pre-conquest times. The divine mushrooms are
gathered during the new moon on hillsides before dawn, often by
a virgin. They may be consecrated on the altar of the local
Catholic church, reflecting the syncretic blending of indigenous
and Christian traditions.

The Mazatec call the mushrooms "nti-si-tho," meaning "worshipful
object that springs forth." They believe the mushroom appears
miraculously and may be sent from outer realms on thunderbolts.
As one Indian expressed it: "The little mushroom comes of
itself, no one knows whence, like the wind that comes we know
not when or why."

## The Velada Ceremony

The all-night Mazatec ceremony, called a velada, is typically
led by a woman shaman (curandera). It comprises long,
complicated, and curiously repetitious chants, percussive
beats, and prayers. The ceremony often serves as a curing rite
during which the practitioner, through the "power" of the
sacred mushrooms, communicates and intercedes with supernatural
forces on behalf of the patient.

Maria Sabina, the famous Mazatec curandera who guided R. Gordon
Wasson in 1955, became one of the most widely known figures in
the history of entheogenic exploration. Her veladas became
prototypical of the mushroom ceremony for Western audiences,
though she later expressed regret about the attention brought to
her tradition. See [[entheogenic-ceremony-preparation]] for
guidance on respectful ceremonial practice.

## Siberian Amanita Shamanism

In northeast Asia, the home of what anthropologists term
"classic" shamanism, a close relationship exists between the
psychoactive mushroom [[amanita-muscaria]] (fly [[agar]]ic) and the
shamanistic act. Amanita muscaria contains muscarine and
muscimol, both with demonstrated hallucinogenic properties. In
Siberia, it was used by the Koryak, Chukchi, Yukagir, Yakut,
Ostyak, Samoyed, and Kamchadal tribes.

Jochelson, who traveled among the Koryak in 1900-1901,
documented that fly agaric "produces intoxication,
hallucinations, and delirium." Many shamans consumed it
before seances to enter ecstatic states. Under strong
intoxication, "the senses become deranged; surrounding objects
appear either very large or very small, hallucinations set in."
Remarkably, the Koryak practiced urine recycling to prolong
the effects, as the active alkaloids are excreted largely
unchanged.

## Amazonian Ayahuasca and Mushroom Combinations

In the Amazon basin, [[ayahuasca-botany-and-preparation]]
represents a different but related tradition of plant-based
visionary practice. While ayahuasca is primarily a vine-based
brew, some Amazonian traditions incorporate psychoactive
mushrooms into healing practices. The Jivaro and other
Amazonian peoples use a variety of psychoactive plants in
shamanic contexts, with the shaman typically consuming the
substance to diagnose and treat illness rather than the
patient directly consuming it.

## The Shaman as Psychotherapist

Contemporary anthropology tends to view the shaman as a
psychotherapist, though the cultures in which shamans operate
believe them to be in direct contact with an invisible spirit
world. The use of hallucinogens appears to be the easiest and
fastest technique for achieving trance states conducive to
contacting the supernatural. As Harner notes, "the use of
psychedelic agents is only one of the ways of achieving the
trance-like states," alongside fasting, flagellation, sensory
deprivation, breathing exercises, and ritual dancing.

The widespread utilization of hallucinogens in a population to
obtain direct revelations tends to compete with the authority
of priestly hierarchies. Shamanic authority has been threatened
by the general adoption of peyote in some societies, suggesting
that the social implications of [[entheogen-culture]] are complex
and culturally specific.

## Ceremonial Elements Across Cultures

Despite vast geographical separation, sacred mushroom ceremonies
share common elements: the collection of mushrooms during
specific lunar phases, the role of a specialized practitioner,
the use of music (chanting, drumming, icaros), the presence of
a supportive community, and the interpretation of visions for
healing and divination. These cross-cultural parallels suggest
that the ceremonial use of psychoactive fungi addresses
fundamental human needs for spiritual experience and community
healing that transcend individual cultural traditions.

## The New World as a Center of Diversity

The New World is overwhelmingly emphasized as a region of
research in hallucinogen studies. As Schultes has pointed out,
the Americas are unusually rich in hallucinogenic plants, a
factor which undoubtedly facilitated their use by North,
Middle, and South American Indians. For reasons not entirely
clear, American Indian cultures have preserved an emphasis on
shamanism, perhaps because the majority were untrammeled by a
state religion. Today similar circumstances are usually found
only in the most remote parts of the Old World.
