# Decomposition and Wood Decay by Fungi

Fungi have fundamental importance as [[fungal-interactions-organisms]] in terrestrial ecosystems. [[basidiomycete-phylum-guide]] are the major agents of decomposition and nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems, occurring as both saprotrophs and mycorrhizal symbionts. Their ability to decompose [[lignin-degradation-mechanisms-wood-rot-fungi]], [[fungal-cellulose-decomposition-fungal-enzymes]], and other recalcitrant plant polymers makes them essential drivers of carbon and nutrient flow.

## The Decomposer Role

Fungi are of fundamental importance as decomposers, responsible for the breakdown of complex organic matter including plant litter, wood, and other organic residues. Free-living fungi have major roles in decomposition and are primary agents of organic matter turnover in most terrestrial ecosystems. They decompose organic matter, relocate nutrients between different organic resources, and contribute to soil formation.

## Wood Decay Processes

### Types of Wood Decay

[[fungal-enzyme-wood-decay]] employ different strategies for breaking down woody substrates:

- **White rot fungi**: Capable of degrading all major components of wood, including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. They use ligninolytic enzyme systems to break down the lignin matrix
- **Brown rot fungi**: Primarily degrade cellulose and hemicellulose while modifying but not completely removing lignin. They utilize carbohydrates but degrade lignin to only a limited extent

The distinction between these decay types has significant implications for carbon cycling and the fate of carbon in forest ecosystems. Brown rot fungi tend to leave behind a lignin-rich residue that contributes to long-term soil carbon storage.

### Decay Dynamics

The rate and pattern of wood decay is influenced by multiple factors:
- Wood resource quality (size, state of decay, nutrient content)
- Fungal species identity and interactions
- Temperature, moisture, and other environmental conditions
- Invertebrate activity

When wood is well decayed at the point of colonization, newly encountered resources tend to decay more rapidly. The presence of antagonistic decay fungi affects partitioning of decay between competing species. In decomposing litter, enzymes associated with ligninolysis are switched on in response to resource conditions.

## Lignocellulose Decomposition

[[fungal-decomposition-ecology]] is the most abundant organic polymer on Earth and the primary structural component of plant cell walls. Its decomposition requires a complex suite of fungal enzymes:

- **Cellulases**: Break down cellulose into glucose units
- **Hemicellulases**: Degrade hemicellulose polymers
- **Ligninolytic enzymes**: Including laccases, peroxidases, and manganese peroxidases that attack the complex lignin polymer

Wood-decay saprotrophs (primarily white-rot Basidiomycota) are the organisms best adapted for decomposition of lignocellulose. Their ability to produce extracellular ligninolytic enzymes sets them apart from most other organisms.

## Nutrient Translocation During Decomposition

Non-resource-unit restricted saprotrophic fungi can grow out of one resource in search of others, interconnecting fallen branches, trunks, and stumps. These mycelial networks enable translocation of nutrients from resource-rich to resource-poor areas. Radiotracer studies have shown that nutrients are scavenged at foraging fronts and translocated from the resource base to support growth fronts.

The mycelial networks are continuously remodelled in response to local nutritional cues. Regressing mycelium allows redistribution of biomass and nutrients for further exploration. This translocation capability is a key advantage of filamentous fungal growth over unit-restricted decomposers.

## Interaction with Invertebrates

Wood-boring beetles inoculate wood with spores of wood-decay fungi during the process of egg laying. The fungal spores germinate and the mycelium rapidly degrades the wood. Insects may benefit from consuming partially decayed woody tissue and/or fungal mycelium, creating a mutualistic relationship between insects and decomposer fungi.

## Isotope Signatures of Decomposition

Natural abundance of stable isotopes (15N and 13C) can be used to study decomposition processes. As organic matter decomposes, 13C content tends to increase from the litter layer downwards into more decomposed material. Within plants, cellulose is enriched in 13C compared with lignin. Fungal species utilizing more decomposed material tend to have higher 15N signatures, reflecting the progressive enrichment that occurs during decomposition.

## Decomposition and Ecosystem Carbon

The interaction between mycorrhizal fungi and decomposition processes significantly affects ecosystem carbon storage. Mycorrhizal fungi can reduce litter decomposition rates in some systems through various mechanisms including nutrient competition with decomposer communities and production of recalcitrant compounds. In contrast, the presence of certain saprotroph communities accelerates decomposition.

Rates of soil organic matter decomposition tend to be higher in ecosystems dominated by arbuscular mycorrhizal plants than in those dominated by plants with ectomycorrhizal or ericoid mycorrhizas, due to differences in litter quality and the competitive interactions between mycorrhizal and decomposer communities. Brown rot decay leaves behind a lignin-enriched residue that contributes to long-term soil carbon storage, while white rot can mineralize lignin completely, releasing carbon as CO2.

## Fungal Enzyme Systems for Decomposition

The enzymatic machinery employed by decomposer fungi is remarkably diverse:

- **Laccases**: Copper-containing oxidases that degrade phenolic compounds and are widespread among both white-rot fungi and non-wood-decomposing species
- **Peroxidases**: Including lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese peroxidase (MnP), which require hydrogen peroxide for activity and are primarily produced by white-rot basidiomycetes
- **Cellulases**: Multi-enzyme complexes including endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and beta-glucosidases that act synergistically to break down crystalline cellulose
- **Hemicellulases**: Including xylanases, mannanases, and other enzymes that degrade the heterogeneous hemicellulose polymers
- **Chitinases and proteases**: For degradation of fungal and insect biomass within decomposing material

The regulation of these enzyme systems is tightly controlled by nutrient availability, with ligninolytic enzymes typically induced under nitrogen limitation while cellulolytic enzymes are repressed by readily available carbon sources.

## Succession During Decomposition

Fungal communities undergo predictable successional changes during decomposition of wood and litter. Early colonizers tend to be fast-growing opportunistic species with broad substrate ranges. As decomposition progresses and resource quality changes, the community shifts toward species specialized on more recalcitrant materials. This succession is driven by changing substrate chemistry, moisture dynamics, and competitive interactions between fungal species.

## Fungi as Decomposers in Different Ecosystems

- **Forest ecosystems**: Basidiomycetes dominate wood decomposition, while ascomycetes are important in litter decomposition
- **Grassland ecosystems**: Saprotrophic fungi decompose fine plant litter and root material, with decomposition rates influenced by litter quality and fungal community composition
- **Salt marshes**: Specialized ascomycete communities decompose salt marsh grasses, with laccase genes adapted to intertidal conditions
- **Agricultural soils**: Fungal decomposer communities process crop residues, cover crop biomass, and organic amendments

## Farming Relevance

Understanding fungal decomposition is valuable for agriculture:
- Composting and organic matter management depend on fungal decomposition processes
- [[dighton-fungal-succession-resource-decomposition]] affect nutrient release from organic amendments
- Wood chip mulch decomposition influences soil biology and weed suppression
- Managing crop residues to optimize fungal decomposition improves nutrient cycling
- Understanding white-rot enzyme systems informs development of fungal-based pest control agents
- Fungal decomposition of cover crops and green manures releases nutrients for subsequent crops
