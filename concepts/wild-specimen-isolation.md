# Wild Specimen Isolation

Wild specimen isolation is the process of obtaining pure [[spore-syringe-production]].

Specimens collected at the right maturity stage -- with the partial veil just beginning to tear -- offer the best combination of spore viability and cleanliness. Overly mature specimens with exposed gills have accumulated environmental contaminants on their spore surfaces, while immature specimens may not have produced sufficient spores.

## Spore Germination

Dehydrated spores benefit from soaking in sterilized water for 6-12 hours before inoculation. When first produced, spores are moist, inflated cells with high germination rates; as they dry and collapse, germination becomes more difficult. At least three media dishes should be inoculated to improve success chances.

Different media formulations can improve germination rates for difficult species. Adding a small amount of wood extract or specific sugars to the agar can trigger germination in species that resist standard malt extract agar. Some species require heat shocking or cold shocking to break spore dormancy before germination will proceed.

## Tissue Culture Protocol

1. Select a fresh, healthy mushroom less than 24-48 hours old

2. Split the stem longitudinally with a sterilized scalpel to expose the interior tissue

3. Cut a small piece of flesh from the inner stem tissue using a cooled, flame-sterilized scalpel

4. Transfer the tissue fragment to a prepared agar media plate

The advantage of tissue culture is genetic consistency -- the resulting mycelium is a clone of the parent mushroom, preserving its exact characteristics.

## Tissue Culture Best Practices

Successful tissue culture from wild specimens requires careful attention to technique:

- Select specimens that are young, firm, and free of insect damage or decay

- Work quickly to minimize exposure of the interior tissue to airborne contaminants

- Use the innermost tissue from the stem base, as this region is best protected from environmental contamination

- Always flame-sterilize tools between specimens to prevent cross-contamination

- Transfer tissue to the center of the agar plate, where nutrients are most accessible

- Incubate plates at the appropriate temperature for the target species

## Contamination Challenges

In tissue culture, bacteria are the most commonly encountered contaminants. An overall contamination rate of 10% is tolerable for most cultivators. In primary cultures isolated from wild specimens, rates of 25% are not unusual. Diverse and colorful contaminants often appear near the transfer point, their numbers depending on the cleanliness of the tissue or spores and the hygienic state of the laboratory.

If sporulating mold colonies develop adjacent to growing mycelium, the culture should be promptly isolated by transferring mycelium away from contaminants. However, strains isolated from contaminated dishes may harbor hidden spores that only become evident when the mycelium is later inoculated into sterile grain.
