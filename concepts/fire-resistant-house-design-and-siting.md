# Fire-Resistant House Design and Siting

## Overview

In fire-prone regions, the design and placement of buildings is the single most important factor in determining whether structures — and their occupants — survive [[wildfire]]. [[permaculture-design-principles]] provide a comprehensive framework for fire-resistant construction, building siting, and landscape modification that dramatically reduces fire risk without sacrificing the benefits of living in forested or rural landscapes.

## Principles of Fire-Resistant Construction

### Roof and Wall Design
Buildings in wildfire zones should feature simple roof and wall outlines. Complex rooflines with internal valleys or re-entrant wall corners accumulate incandescent ash and embers. Tarpaper roof linings projecting into gutters are a major vulnerability — roof gutters should have either a leaf-free profile or be pluggable and water-fillable during fire events (plugs chained to gutters near downpipes). Roof spaces frequently catch alight from leaves accumulated in gutters.

### Ventilation and Openings
All unscreened windows, underfloor spaces, and wall cavity vents must be fitted with fine-mesh metal screens to reduce spark entry. Embers can ignite beds, and cellars or underfloor spaces often store dry firewood or fuel liquids. Inflammable door mats, wood piles, and shrubs against house walls must be removed. Fuel cans and explosive materials should be stored in a separate shed, tightly lidded.

### Cladding and Insulation
Thatch and shingle roofing must be replaced by tile or metal cladding in fire-prone areas (mandated by law in some districts). Fire-proof or slow-to-burn insulation materials — mineral wool, seagrass, sawdust, feathers, wool — keep interiors cool and assist fire control. Wooden panelling transmits relatively little heat compared to stone, brick, and mud, which may convey heat indoors unless of sufficient thickness to absorb and disseminate it.

### Color and Reflectivity
White-painted walls and roof areas reduce radiation absorption and sun heat. A white-painted brick wall serves as an ideal radiation shield.

## Siting Houses for Fire Safety

### The Slope Factor
Houses are at greatest danger from upslope fire. Few houses survive wildfire on sharp ridgetops or in hill saddles with diverging ridgelines that create a wind (fire) funnel effect. The same funneling is created by planting inflammable trees (eucalypts) or grasses (pampas grass) along house driveways — these create a blowtorch effect even on concrete and steel structures.

### Optimal Sites
The safest house sites are:
- Damp valley mouths
- Well-tended built-up areas
- Farms with flood-flow or Keyline irrigation
- Irrigated areas
- Peninsulas in dams and lakes
- Downslope plateaus away from ridges

### Excavated Sites
Rather than raising houses on stilts or stumps on downslopes, sites should be excavated into the hillside. A house nested into a shelf on the hill is protected from radiation, has no open underfloor area, and can incorporate a rim wall, pond, or earth bank on the plateau edge for additional protection. Such houses can more easily develop a cave or dugout refuge behind the shelter of the house itself — fully earthed over with a wet blanket door and a dogleg or curved entrance to escape direct radiation.

## The 30-Metre Defensible Zone

Around house and building sites, forest and grass fuel must be reduced to a distance of 30 metres (100 feet). This does not mean tree removal — rather, planting fire-resistant species:
- **Trees**: Coprosma, deciduous fruits, figs, willows, poplars (not olives, pines, eucalypts)
- **Lilies and bulbs**: Agapanthus, spring bulbs, arum, Canna
- **Summer-green [[coastal-ground-covers-hedges-permaculture]]**: comfrey, iceplants, Tradescantia, Impatiens, shortgrass sward

## Radiation Shields

Radiation shields are solid or reflective objects that reflect or harmlessly absorb radiant heat from the fire front. Radiant heat — not direct flame — is what quickly kills plants, animals, and people. Most human casualties are either smothered by toxic smoke from burning furniture or plastics, or killed (unburnt) by radiation.

### Types of Radiation Shields
- Houses (particularly white-painted brick)
- Stone walls
- Thick tree trunks
- Hollows or caves
- Hedgerows
- Car bodies

### Shield Geometry
A fire shadow tapers to about 4–5 times the height and width of the solid radiation shield. Therefore, shields of trees or walls must extend well past the house to provide complete coverage.

## Sacrificial Hedges and Fire Barriers

Downslope lines of deciduous or fern-leaved (not hard-leaved) trees can serve as sacrificial fire barriers. Willows and certain Acacia species may be fire-killed above ground but rejuvenate from the roots when cut down. These hedges dampen ground fires — even willow leaves may not combust but shrivel and produce steamy ash as flames approach. Such barriers provide critical protection while requiring minimal maintenance.

## Real-World Survival Example

[[bill-mollison]]'s own family survived the 1967 Hobart firestorm — a dense eucalypt [[fungi-forest-fire-succession-litter-dighton]] that destroyed 1,100 houses and killed 90 people in the area — through prior preparation:
- Removal of all lower limbs, loose bark, twigs, fallen leaves, dry brush, dry grass, and dead stumps (annually)
- Chopping material into hollows and swales, weighted with stones, or burnt as cooking fuel
- Tall tree stems saved the house from fire wind and regenerated after fire
- Downslope willow and fern-leaved Acacia hedge acted as sacrificial fire barrier
- House was white-painted with fitted screens
- Ample bucket water stored indoors and out for spot fires after the front passed

The house was blistered and zinc roof coating flaked, but close-fitted boards were unburnt. In Mollison's street alone, 70 houses burned to the ground.

## Fire Refuges

Each fire or radiation refuge should incorporate:
- A small permanent water tank (approximately 270 litres)
- Old blankets
- Buckets of water

These are described as absolute "fire insurance" for those caught at home — often women and young children. The same principles apply to barns, livestock shelters, and outbuildings.

## Personal Survival in Fire

The main survival factor for people is to cover the body with wool or cotton clothing and shelter from fire-front radiation behind a tree, car, house, or in a trench. A wet blanket cape provides additional protection. After flames pass, move cautiously onto burnt ground.

**Critical warning**: In firestorms, oxygen is periodically exhausted and the fire briefly goes out. People faint from lack of oxygen and should never take refuge in small water tanks, dams, or rivers — they will drown while unconscious. Only the sea and large rivers (100+ metres across) are safe.
