## Curing with Ayahuasca in Urban Iquitos

## Overview

The use of ayahuasca (*Banisteriopsis caapi* and related species) as a healing tool in the urban setting of Iquitos, Peru, represents one of the most extensively documented cases of plant hallucinogen use in folk medicine. Marlene Dobkin de Rios's year-long fieldwork (1968–1969) in the Belén slum of Iquitos revealed a sophisticated system in which cultural beliefs, expectations, and values fundamentally structure the hallucinogenic experience and its therapeutic outcomes. Unlike clinical psychedelic therapy in Western settings, ayahuasca healing operates within a magical framework where the drug serves primarily diagnostic and revelatory purposes rather than as a direct curative agent.

## The Ayahuasca Brew

Ayahuasca is prepared by cutting and boiling the woody vine for several hours. The brew often contains additives beyond the primary *Banisteriopsis* species (including *B. caapi*, *B. quitensis*, *B. inebriens*, and *B. rusbyana*). Common additives include:

- **Toe** — identified as *Datura suaveolens* (Schultes)
- **Chacruna** — *Psychotria viridis* (referred to as *B. rusbyana* in the source), whose leaves contain dimethyltryptamine (DMT)

The optimal dose identified by Rios Reategui was approximately **7 mg/kg of body weight**, though healers varied dosages based on their assessment of the patient's physical strength and body weight, typically giving larger doses to those suffering from psychosomatic illnesses.

## The Healing Session

### Structure and Setting

Healing sessions always occur at **night** in forest clearings at the edge of urban centers. The typical structure:

1. **Gathering** (approximately 10:00 p.m.): Patients (5–25 people) assemble with the healer (*ayahuasquero*)
2. **Distribution**: The healer recites orations and whistles protective invocations while passing a communal cup. Dosage is individually calibrated.
3. **Onset period** (~30 minutes): Participants sit smoking and chatting while waiting for effects. Vomiting and diarrhea (the "purge") are common and not hidden.
4. **Active session** (4–5 hours): The healer moves around the circle, contacting each person with a *schacapa* (rattle made from dried forest leaves), blowing cigarette smoke over patients, sucking at painful areas, and extracting believed magical objects (spines, thistles).
5. **Conclusion** (2:00–3:00 a.m.): Patients return home or sleep in a *tambo* (wallless shelter).

### Accompanying Practices

Healers employ a rich repertoire of techniques alongside the ayahuasca:

- **Quechua drug songs** and **whistling incantations** throughout the ceremony
- **Cigarette smoke blowing** (*soplada*) over the patient's body
- **Sucking** at afflicted body regions to extract harmful objects
- **Counseling and ritual exorcism** during the session
- **Dietary proscriptions**: No salt, lard, or sweets for at least 24 hours before and after — to propitiate the vine's jealous guardian spirit
- **Sexual abstinence** may be demanded before sessions

## Patient Selection

Ayahuasqueros carefully **screen their patients** and tend to eliminate individuals suffering from severe psychotic disorders. The drug is not used for simple illnesses — herbs, plants, and store-bought medicines are prescribed for those. Ayahuasca is reserved primarily for illnesses classified as **psychosomatic** — those with physical symptoms believed to originate from magical harm.

## The Belén Slum: Research Setting

Belén is a community of approximately 12,000 people living in houses built on rafts (*balsas*) on the Amazon River, flooded at least four months each year. The slum is characterized by:

- Exceedingly high unemployment
- Excessive malnutrition
- Family breakdown and fragile relationships
- Prostitution, vandalism, and chronic illness
- Extreme economic insecurity
- Primary occupations: *rematistas* (small-scale wholesalers of forest produce) and *regatones* (river traders with motor boats)

Belén serves as a receiving area for vast numbers of forest migrants seeking work and education. Its residents have little shared community tradition, and even the "amoral familiarism" described in other societies does not apply, given the fragility of family unions.

## Disease Etiology in Belén

### Natural vs. Magical Illness

Belén residents distinguish between two categories of illness:

1. **Simple, God-given illnesses**: Colds, respiratory infections, some skin disorders — treated by *sanitarios* (poorly trained medical aides) with penicillin or antibiotic injections.

2. **Magical illnesses**: When simple treatments fail, illness is attributed to the malice of another person or the punishment of a natural spirit. This is the primary domain of ayahuasca healing.

The central question in disease is not "how?" but **"why me?"** — the concern is to discover exactly why a particular person is afflicted, not the mechanical pathology.

### Categories of Magical Illness

- **Susto**: Profound metabolic alteration or nervous disorder caused by loss of the soul following an intense psychic trauma of fear. Includes lack of appetite and energy.
- **Daño**: Caused by envy (*envidia*) or vengeance (*despecho*). Manifests as hemorrhaging, muscular pain, loss of consciousness, tumors, or consistent bad luck (*saladera*). Believed to be caused by potions slipped into drinks or thrown across doorsteps.
- **Pulsario**: Marked by restlessness, hyperactivity, and free-floating anxiety. Described as a painful ball at the mouth of the stomach preventing normal digestion. Primarily affects women and may represent repressed pain, sorrow, or anger.
- **Mal de ojo** (evil eye): Caused by the magical action of one person's glance, often motivated by envy. Common among children whose attractiveness attracts notice.

## Mechanisms of Healing

### Diagnostic and Revelatory Function

Ayahuasca is **not conceptualized as a curative agent** per se. Rather, it serves as a powerful means to a diagnostic end:

1. It gives the healer entry into the culturally important area of **disease causality**
2. It enables identification of the nature and source of the illness
3. It allows the healer to deflect or neutralize evil magic deemed responsible

The drug transforms **generalized, immobilizing anxiety** present in the sociocultural milieu into **specific fear** placed on the shoulders of an identified evil-doer, facilitated by the healer's omnipotent presentation.

### Vision Content and Interpretation

Common visionary patterns include:

- **Forest creatures**: Boa constrictors and viperous snakes — believed to be the reincarnated spirit of the vine itself, bringing messages of healing. Patients who withstand the fright may spontaneously learn the vine's songs.
- **The bewitcher**: The person responsible for causing illness may appear, expressing their innermost thoughts (sexual desire, vengeance, hate).
- **Scenes of magical harm**: Potions being manufactured, objects being thrown across doorsteps.

Healers cleverly utilize even frightening visions ("bad trips") as therapeutic tools. The presence of a belief system frames these experiences within an ongoing magical framework where they become controllable and meaningful rather than threatening.

### The Placebo Question

Dobkin de Rios argues that ayahuasca **does not operate primarily as a placebo** because:
- It is not used to gain verbal insight or work through psychodynamic material
- It serves a diagnostic function rather than a direct curative one
- The healing mechanism depends on the identification and neutralization of specific magical causes, not mere faith in the drug

### Magical Conditioning and Doctrinal Compliance

Healers spend up to **two weeks in preparatory exorcism** before allowing some patients to take ayahuasca. This serves as a **learning experience** to bring patients into alignment with culturally held expectations. The concept of **doctrinal compliance** (Ehrenwald, 1966) — where patients unconsciously conform to the healer's theoretical framework — is highly relevant. Unlike conscious suggestion, doctrinal compliance operates at an unconscious level in both magical and modern therapeutic settings.

## Field Methodology

Dobkin de Rios employed several innovative approaches:
- **Fortune-telling cards (naipes)** to establish rapport and cross-check data on illness beliefs
- **Direct participation**: She took both LSD (100 micrograms Sandoz) and ayahuasca in ritual context to understand subjective experience
- **Thematic Apperception Test** administration compared with over 200 naipes readings
- Cross-checking interview data with card-reading clients who had no direct ayahuasca involvement

## Significance

The Iquitos case demonstrates that the efficacy of hallucinogenic healing depends critically on cultural variables — the shared belief system, expectations, healer authority, and ritual context — rather than on the pharmacological properties of the drug alone. This has implications for understanding psychedelic therapy across cultures and for the design of therapeutic protocols in Western settings.
