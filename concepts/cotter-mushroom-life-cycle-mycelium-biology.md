# Mushroom Life Cycle and Mycelium Biology

## Overview

Most mushrooms share the same basic stages of development, although there are
a few variations and exceptions. Understanding all stages of development helps
cultivators become more intuitive growers. Each mushroom species has specialized
needs at every stage, and observing natural cycles in the wild provides clues
about the ecological connections that trigger fruiting.

## The Four Stages

### Sporulation

Fungi produce spores rather than seeds. Spores are microscopic packets of
fungal DNA packaged in a hard chitin coating that protects them from heat,
drought, and damage. They are produced on the surface of gills or pores through
meiosis, a form of sexual reproduction where each spore carries only half the
genetic information needed for reproduction. When a mushroom matures, it
forcibly ejects its spores in a process known as sporulation.

To collect spores, take a spore print by placing the stem of a mature mushroom
through a slit in paper or aluminum foil. The mushroom will forcibly discharge
spores onto the surface within hours if left undisturbed in a draft-free area.
Spores remain viable for several years when stored folded in a plastic bag in
the refrigerator.

### Spore Germination and Cell Mating

When a spore germinates, it is monokaryotic (one nucleus per cell) and carries
only half the genetic information needed for sexual reproduction. The hyphae
explore their immediate environment, looking for compatible hyphae from another
germinated spore. When compatible hyphae meet, they fuse through karyogamy,
combining genetic material. The fused cell becomes dikaryotic (two nuclei per
cell), and growth explodes as hyphae branch out rapidly.

### Colonization

Also called "nutrient capture," colonization is the stage where fungi secure
their territory, competing for nutrients in their environment. The mycelium
pushes through wood, compost, and soil until encountering a competitor or
barrier. Once blocked, the mycelium branches heavily to increase surface area
and begins aggressively digesting the substrate, recharging its biological
battery in preparation for fruiting. Cotter describes this as paving a major
highway, then filling in all the side roads and pathways until colonization is
complete.

### Complete Colonization and Primordia Formation

The fruiting cycle begins when colonization is complete and environmental
changes stimulate the fungus. Nutrients and water stream to spots the mycelium
has chosen as good fruiting locations, based on favorable gas exchange,
consistent humidity, and light availability. Primordia -- small knots -- are the
first observable sign that mushrooms are starting to fruit.

## Fungal Metabolism: By-Products

Fungi require a carbon source (feedstock) such as wood chips, sawdust, coffee
grounds, or agricultural wastes. They excrete digestive enzymes into the
environment, which break down food using oxygen. The fungi then absorb dissolved
nutrients directly through their cell walls. Like animals, fungi produce heat,
carbon dioxide, and water as metabolites.

### Heat

Fungal enzymes produce heat when combined with available oxygen. The enzymes
cleave molecules into smaller, bioavailable compounds. Temperature increases
due to digestion. Cultivators must account for this heat: packing too many
mushrooms together can overheat and "cook" the mycelium during colonization,
creating dead pockets and anaerobic zones where molds and thermophilic bacteria
thrive.

### Carbon Dioxide

Fungi consume oxygen and release carbon dioxide. Mushrooms need fresh air
constantly, especially when forming fruitbodies. Excessive CO2 levels cause stem
elongation -- an undesirable result since caps are typically more valuable than
stems. Since CO2 is heavier than oxygen, it pools in undisturbed pockets and
depressions, causing developing mushrooms to stretch with reduced cap size.

### Other By-Products

Every fungus is chemically unique. Compounds can be embedded in the mycelium or
floating in fungal metabolites. Fungi produce different compounds in varying
amounts in reaction to environmental changes. An observant cultivator can
customize growing parameters to optimize levels of desirable by-products,
including medicinal compounds.

## Four Key Growth Requirements

### Food

Fungi are heterotrophs that consume food like animals. Mushrooms fall into two
categories for cultivation purposes: primary decomposers (breaking down raw
wood and plant debris) and secondary decomposers (consuming partially
decomposed matter). Supplements increase the risk of contamination, so use
them sparingly.

### Water

Mushrooms need high moisture and humidity (90-95 percent) to sustain growth and
supply the mycelium with enough water to spread. The "sweating" of enzymes
lubricates the paths of spreading mycelium. Be aware that mushrooms can
hyperaccumulate heavy metals, so test water sources for lead, mercury, and
arsenic.

### Gas Exchange

During colonization, very little oxygen is needed. The outer edges of the
mycelium in contact with fresh air transport oxygen inward and channel CO2
out. Colonization rooms should maintain around 5,000 ppm CO2, while fruiting
rooms should be ventilated to below 1,000 ppm to promote primordia formation.

### Light

Only a small handful of species (Agaricus species) can mature in complete
darkness. All other mushrooms require diffuse natural or fluorescent light,
particularly in the blue-green spectrum. These wavelengths regulate metabolic
pathways for energy, growth, protein production, vitamin D, and medicinal
properties. Lack of light creates the same effect as elevated CO2: long stems
and small caps.

## Integration with Plant Systems

Because fungi produce heat, CO2, and water, they are perfect biological
companions for enclosed plant production systems such as greenhouses. Adding
plants to growing rooms helps offset CO2. The synergy between fungal and plant
metabolism can improve growing conditions for both organisms.
