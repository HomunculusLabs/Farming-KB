## The Chemistry of Flesh Colour Change
The colour change in boletes is a chemical phenomenon. Certain
substances called chromogens (for example, boletol in Boletus species)
occur in the flesh together with oxidising enzymes such as lactase and
tyrosinase. When the flesh is broken and exposed to oxygen and moisture,
these enzymes act upon the chromogens, producing new, sometimes vividly
coloured compounds. On continued contact with air, the flesh may undergo
a series of colour changes as the chromogen is gradually altered through
successive oxidation states.
Ramsbottom explicitly stated that "none of these colour changes is of
any significance in denoting the presence of poisonous substances." The
bright blues, though disconcerting during food preparation, are not
danger signals. Even the Field Mushroom (Psalliota campestris) and the
Horse Mushroom (Psalliota arvensis) show slight colour changes: the
flesh of the first becoming slightly reddish, that of the second
yellowish, when cut.
## Boletus Satanas — Devil"s Bolete
Boletus satanas is among the most handsome of European boletes, with a
dirty white or greyish cap often tinged with olive, and a stem that is
yellow above and below with a bright red middle covered in a close
network of red veins. The tubes are yellow, and the pores turn from
yellow to bright crimson when mature. All parts of the fungus turn blue
when rubbed, a trait that contributed to its fearsome reputation.
When first described over a century before Ramsbottom's 1945 account, B.
satanas had caused sickness and diarrhoea in several people who ate it,
tasted it, or were merely near specimens being examined. Ramsbottom,
however, concluded that "its powers of evil have been much overrated."
He considered it probably very indigestible and possibly containing some
irritant substance, but noted that it was eaten in Czechoslovakia and
parts of Italy, with the general agreement that it should not be
consumed raw. This assessment aligns with modern understanding: B.
satanas contains bolesatine, a glycoprotein that is destroyed by
cooking, making properly prepared specimens edible though not
recommended.
## Boletus Luridus — Lurid Bolete
Boletus luridus is frequently mistaken for B. satanas, but the cap is
some shade of brown (never whitish) and the pores are orange-red or red-
brown rather than crimson. The cap varies from olive-brown when young to
umber, reddish brown, or buff. The tubes are yellow, becoming blue or
green when old, with small rounded pores that change from yellow to red-
brown and turn blackish-blue when bruised. The stem is yellow at the
summit, washed with red elsewhere, ornamented with a blood-red network.
The flesh is thick and soft, yellow except for a reddish zone under the
tubes and at the base of the stem. When broken, it rapidly becomes blue
after a momentary violet-red stage, later turning yellowish-green.
Ramsbottom stated that B. luridus is edible, and that "the rapid change
of colour when the flesh is broken is doubtless responsible for the
widespread belief that they are very poisonous." The flesh becomes
yellow during cooking.
## Boletus Erythropus — Dotted Stem Bolete
Boletus erythropus is very similar to B. luridus, but its stem is
covered with reddish dots rather than a reticulate network, the flesh is
clear yellow under the tubes, and the cap is usually darker brown and
finely tomentose. It grows typically in coniferous woods. Like B.
luridus, B. erythropus is edible despite its dramatic blue-staining
reaction. The reddish dots on the stem distinguish it from the net-
patterned B. luridus in the field, though both share the alarming blue
colour change on bruising.
## Boletus Felleus — Bitter Bolete
[[boletus-felleus]] presents a different kind of hazard: not poisoning, but
overwhelming bitterness. When young, with tubes still white, it can be
confused with the prized [[boletus-edulis]] (cep or porcini). The network on
the stem is more marked than in B. edulis, and the taste has been
described as "unforgettable" in its bitterness. Mature specimens are
readily distinguished by their pink tubes and pores.
The cap is pale yellowish, honey- or chestnut-coloured, hemispherical
then flattened, slightly pubescent. The tubes are adnate, first white
then pink. The cylindrical stem is paler than the cap with a very fine
whitish network at the apex that widens and darkens to chestnut-brown
and finally blackish towards the swollen base. The white flesh becomes
pinkish when cut. It grows in woods, especially on calcareous soil, in
summer and autumn.
Ramsbottom noted that though not poisonous, "its intensely bitter taste
renders it inedible; a single specimen will ruin a meal." This is a
practical warning that remains relevant: a single bitter bolete in a
basket of edibles can contaminate an entire dish through cross-contact
during cooking.
## The Colour-Change Myth in Context
The belief that blue-staining indicates poison is one of the most
persistent mushroom folklore myths. Ramsbottom placed it alongside other
discredited rules such as the [[silver-spoon-test]], the peeling test, and
the notion that poisonous fungi grow only in woods. He emphasised that
"to know whether a species is edible or poisonous it is necessary to be
able to recognise it and to learn its reputation. No rule-of-thumb
method is safe."
This message remains central to modern mycological education. The
[[boletus-genus]] provides a compelling case study: among the blue- staining
species, some are excellent edibles (B. edulis stains slightly), some
are edible but alarming (B. luridus, B. erythropus), some are edible
with caveats (B. satanas when cooked), and some are merely bitter (B.
felleus). The colour change itself is irrelevant to edibility.
## Practical Identification Notes
Several field characters help distinguish the species discussed:
- **Cap colour**: B. satanas has a whitish to greyish-olive cap;   B.
luridus is brown; B. erythropus is darker brown and tomentose;   B.
felleus is honey to chestnut-coloured.
- **Pore colour at maturity**: B. satanas turns crimson; B. luridus
and B. erythropus turn orange-red to red-brown; B. felleus turns   pink.
- **Stem ornamentation**: B. satanas has a red reticulate network;   B.
luridus has a blood-red network; B. erythropus has reddish dots;   B.
felleus has a fine network darkening to blackish.
- **Taste**: B. satanas is mild; B. luridus and B. erythropus are
mild; B. felleus is intensely bitter. Taste should only be tested   on a
small piece that is spat out, and never on raw specimens of   uncertain
identity.
## Seasonal and Habitat Notes

All four species fruit in late summer to autumn in Britain. Boletus
satanas favours chalky soils in woods, heaths, and pastures. Boletus
luridus grows in woods, heaths, and pastures more broadly. Boletus
erythropus is associated with coniferous woods. Boletus felleus
prefers calcareous soils in deciduous woods. Their overlapping
habitats and fruiting periods mean that collectors may encounter
multiple species in a single foray, making careful identification
essential.
