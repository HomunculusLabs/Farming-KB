# Nutrient Deficiencies and Disorders in Cannabis

## Overview

Cannabis plants require 16 essential elements for healthy growth. Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are absorbed from air and water; the remaining 13 elements (nutrients) must be supplied through the growing medium. These nutrients are categorized as macronutrients (primary and secondary) and micronutrients (trace elements). Understanding whether a nutrient is mobile or immobile within the plant is the single most important diagnostic key — it determines where [[knf-calcium-deficiency-symptoms]] appear first and guides corrective action.

Research from Canna's scientific laboratory in Breda, Netherlands, has produced rigorously tested data on cannabis nutritional needs over 20 years of controlled experimentation.

## Mobile vs Immobile Nutrients

### Mobile Nutrients (deficiency appears on OLDER, lower leaves)

Mobile nutrients can translocate — move from one portion of the plant to another as needed. When a deficiency develops, the plant mobilizes these nutrients from older tissue to supply new growth. Consequently, deficiency symptoms appear first on older, lower leaves.

- **Nitrogen (N)** — most common deficiency
- **Phosphorus (P)** — common, often misdiagnosed
- **Potassium (K)** — present but often locked out
- **Magnesium (Mg)** — critical for photosynthesis
- **Zinc (Zn)** — needed for P utilization

### Immobile Nutrients (deficiency appears on NEW, upper leaves)

Immobile nutrients do not translocate to new growing areas. They remain deposited in their original location in older leaves, so new growth suffers first when supply is inadequate.

- **Calcium (Ca)** — cell wall integrity
- **Boron (B)** — cell division, pollen viability
- **Chlorine (Cl)** — osmosis, ion balance
- **Cobalt (Co)** — nitrogen fixation (minor)
- **Copper (Cu)** — enzyme cofactor
- **Iron (Fe)** — chlorophyll synthesis
- **Manganese (Mn)** — enzyme activation
- **Molybdenum (Mo)** — nitrate reduction
- **Selenium (Se)** — antioxidant function
- **Silicon (Si)** — cell wall strength
- **Sulfur (S)** — protein synthesis

## Nitrogen (N) — Mobile

**Practical**: Cannabis loves nitrogen during vegetative growth but needs much less during flowering. Excess nitrogen in harvested plants causes poor burning quality.

**Technical**: Nitrogen regulates protein synthesis, hormone production, chlorophyll formation, and enzyme activity. It is responsible for leaf and stem growth, overall size, and vigor. Ammonium (NH₄⁺) is the most readily available form but can burn plants; nitrate (NO₃⁻) is slower but safer.

**Deficiency progression**: Lower leaves yellow between veins (interveinal chlorosis) → entire older leaves turn yellow → progressively younger leaves affected → severe leaf drop → stems and leaf undersides may turn reddish-purple.

**Toxicity**: Excessively lush, soft foliage susceptible to pests and disease. Weak stems fold easily. Roots develop slowly and darken. Flowers are smaller and sparse. Treat by flushing with three times the medium volume of water; withhold nitrogen for one week.

## Phosphorus (P) — Mobile

**Practical**: Highest demand during germination, seedling, cloning, and flowering stages. "Super Bloom" fertilizers are high-P formulations.

**Technical**: Necessary for photosynthesis and energy transfer (ATP). Component of DNA, enzymes, and proteins. Associated with vigor, resin, and seed production. Highest concentrations in root tips, growing shoots, and vascular tissue.

**Deficiency progression**: Stunted growth with dark bluish-green leaves and blotches → stems and petioles turn reddish-purple on undersides → leaf tips curl downward → large purplish-black necrotic blotches develop → leaves become bronzish-purple, dry, and contort.

**Key diagnostic**: P deficiency is aggravated by clay soils, acidic soils (below 5.8), soggy soils, and pH above 7.0. Excess iron and zinc lock out phosphorus. Zinc is also necessary for proper P utilization.

**Toxicity**: Manifests as deficiencies of other nutrients — zinc, iron, magnesium, calcium, and copper (zinc most commonly). Symptoms take weeks to surface if pH is stable.

## Potassium (K) — Mobile

**Practical**: Used at all growth stages. High K levels increase resistance to bacteria and mold.

**Technical**: Essential for sugar, starch, and carbohydrate production and movement. Increases chlorophyll, regulates stomata, accumulates carbohydrates, augments oil content and flavor. Encourages strong root growth and disease resistance.

**Deficiency progression**: Plants initially appear healthy with dark green foliage → leaves lose luster → branches increase but are weak and scrawny → leaf margins turn grey, progress to rusty-brown, curl up and dry → yellowing with rust-colored blotches → rot sets in, leaves drop → flowering retarded and diminished.

**Key insight**: [[knf-potassium-deficiency-and-excess]] causes internal foliage temperature to climb and protein cells to burn. Evaporation is highest at leaf edges — that is where burning appears. K is usually present in soil but locked out by high salinity.

**Toxicity**: Difficult to diagnose because it manifests as deficiency of magnesium, manganese, zinc, and iron.

## General Treatment Protocols

### Deficiency Treatment

1. Identify whether symptoms appear on older (mobile) or newer (immobile) leaves
2. Check pH — adjust to 5.5-6.2 (hydroponic) or 6.0-7.0 (soil)
3. Apply appropriate nutrient supplement
4. Expect visible recovery in 4-5 days for mobile nutrients
5. Organic fast sources: seabird guano, fish emulsion, blood meal (N); bat guano, bone meal (P); wood ash / soluble potash (K)

### Toxicity Treatment

1. Flush the growing medium with three times its volume of water
2. Use a very mild, complete fertilizer in the flush solution
3. Do not add the problematic nutrient for one week
4. Monitor for secondary deficiencies caused by the excess

### Foliar Feeding

Foliar feeding provides rapid nutrient uptake through leaf stomata but carries risks: stomata become clogged with residue, temporarily reducing photosynthesis. Not recommended for potassium deficiency. Avoid spraying seedlings or unrooted cuttings. Never spray within two weeks of harvest.

## pH and Nutrient Availability

The pH of the growing medium is the master variable controlling nutrient availability:

- **Hydroponic ideal**: 5.5-6.2
- **Soil ideal**: 6.0-7.0
- **Potting soil ideal**: 5.5-6.5
- **Below 6.5 in soil**: May cause [[query-how-do-i-fix-calcium-deficiency-in-plants]]
- **Above 7.0 in any medium**: Phosphorus becomes unavailable
- **High salinity**: Locks out potassium and other nutrients

A stable pH with adequate buffer capacity prevents the rapid fluctuations that trigger [[cannabis-nutrient-lockout-and-ph]] deficiency cascades. Test pH with every watering for best results.

pH imbalance is the most common root cause of apparent nutrient deficiency. Always check and correct pH before supplementing individual nutrients.
