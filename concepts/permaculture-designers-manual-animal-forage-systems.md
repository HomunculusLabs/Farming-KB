# Animal Forage Systems

updated: 2026-04-18

From: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]

## Overview

Permaculture forage systems replace conventional grain-based animal feed with tree crops, perennial pastures, and integrated grazing systems. The transition from conventional to permaculture farming involves replacing animal forage grains with tree crops, increasing forest cover, and adopting low-to-no tillage on remaining croplands. Free-range forage systems are a critical element of self-reliant homestead design.

## Principles of Forage Design

To rear animals on range, three factors must be studied:
1. Social behaviour of the animals: preferred sex ratios, ranging behaviour, preferred flock or herd size, and adaptations of special breeds to soils, pH, site factors, and foraging
2. Nutrition over a full year: adequate provision of energy foods, vitamins, and minerals essential to animal health
3. Interactions of ranging animals with crops, forests, other animals, and people

## Forage Trees and Shrubs

Key forage tree species mentioned throughout the Designers Manual include:

### Leguminous Forage Trees
- **Leucaena** (Leucaena leucocephala) - high-protein fodder, nitrogen fixer
- **Tagasaste** (Chamaecytisus palmensis) - excellent temperate forage shrub
- **Glyricidia** (Gliricidia sepium) - tropical forage and living fence
- **Albizia** - nitrogen-fixing shade and forage tree
- **Prosopis** - arid-zone forage pod producer
- **Acacia** species - browse and windbreak
- **Inga** - tropical shade, mulch, and forage tree
- **Sesbania** - fast-growing green manure and forage

### Non-Leguminous Forage Trees
- **Mulberry** (Morus spp.) - outstanding poultry and livestock forage
- **Baobab** (Adansonia spp.) - drought-resistant fodder
- **Fodder palms** - multiple species for tropical forage
- **Casuarina** - windbreak and forage (nitrogen-fixing)

## Forage in Windbreak Systems

Shelterbelt species should be selected to give multiple uses, either assisting crop yield or adding to end-use yield (e.g. forage trees in pasture). Permeable low hedgerows of Acacia or legumes provide good effects on grass and crop growth while allowing air movement to reduce frosts.

The "incrop" windbreak configuration uses open-spaced, light-crowned trees in crop or pasture, creating an excellent forage situation in arid areas, especially if trees provide fodder crop and the pasture is protected from drying winds.

## Savannah Forestry

If Leucaena, Samanea, Prosopis, and Inga are planted, a long-term forage system will evolve, provided replanting or rest periods are given for seedlings to re-establish. The only thing preventing or delaying savannah forestry is a lack of tree nurseries and seed supply.

## Arid Zone Forage

In drought years, all choice or essential livestock need to be penned in or near the basic survival hectare for survival feeding. This forage system is cut and managed in good years. As no crops can be planted in drought, farmers and their families can tend cattle on rotation. The survival hectare is established along roads, bunds, swales, and ditches.

## Chicken Forage in Orchards

Chickens should be a permanent forage system in all mixed orchards. They provide pest control, manure, and soil aeration while obtaining much of their food from orchard wastes, pests, and interplants of woodlot or forest components. Branches of legumes and forage trees may also be used for poultry bedding or strawyards.

## Bee Forage Systems

Bees form a special forage system covering about 2.5 km radius from the hive. They need:
- Nectar producers (clumped hedgerows are more efficient than scattered plants)
- Pollen sources, especially early in the season (willows, Acacias, pines, vine crop)
- Cold wind protection from hedgerows of rosemary, Acacia, or native plants
- Adequate water supplies near hives

## Pigs in Permaculture Systems

Pigs serve multiple functions in permaculture forage systems beyond meat production.
Their rooting behavior can be directed as a biological plow for site preparation, converting compacted pasture or brushy land into worked ground suitable for planting.
Mollison describes using pigs to prepare orchard sites: their rooting opens soil, incorporates organic matter, and disrupts pest cycles.
In tropical systems, pigs forage on fallen fruit, waste produce, and crop residues.
The key design principle is controlling pig access through rotational paddocks and electric fencing to prevent over-concentration damage.
Pigs can be integrated with [[permaculture-designers-manual-grazing-management]] systems by following cattle — pigs will root through cattle dung to access fly larvae, reducing parasite loads while processing manure into the soil.
Their wallowing behavior creates small ponds that benefit amphibians and wildlife.
Pig forage crops include Jerusalem artichokes, comfrey, fodder beets, and acorns from oak trees in silvopasture systems.

## Sheep and Goat Forage Design

Sheep and goats offer complementary grazing capabilities in permaculture systems.
Sheep preferentially graze grasses and forbs at ground level, while goats are browsers that prefer shrubs, vines, and lower tree branches.
This complementary feeding behavior makes mixed sheep-goat herds effective for managing diverse vegetation types without overgrazing any single layer.
Goats are particularly valuable for controlling invasive brush species (blackberry, gorse, multiflora rose) that other livestock cannot or will not eat.
Mollison emphasizes that goats, being browsers adapted to steep, rocky terrain, are ideal forage animals on marginal land where cattle and sheep cannot effectively graze.
Both species benefit from [[permaculture-designers-manual-windbreak-species]] that provide shelter and supplemental forage.
Fodder shrubs including Tagasaste, Lespedeza, and Sericea lespedeza provide protein-rich browse during periods when pasture quality declines.

## Duck and Geese Forage Systems

Waterfowl integrate valuable forage functions in permaculture designs, particularly in orchard and rice-paddy systems.
Ducks forage on snails, slugs, insects, and weed seeds, providing pest control in orchards and gardens.
The traditional Japanese integrated duck-rice system (aigamo method) demonstrates how ducks control rice pests and weeds while fertilizing the paddy with their manure, eliminating the need for pesticides and herbicides.
Muscovy ducks are particularly effective for slug and snail control in vegetable gardens and orchards.
Geese are efficient grazers of grass pastures and can maintain lawns and orchard understories without damaging trees if managed appropriately.
Both ducks and geese produce eggs and meat as yield.
Their forage systems require water access, which can be designed as productive ponds integrated with [[composting-systems]] and nutrient cycling.
Waterfowl manure is high in nitrogen and phosphorus, benefiting adjacent plantings when managed properly.

## Rabbit Forage Systems

Rabbits are among the most efficient converters of forage to protein, with a feed conversion ratio superior to cattle, sheep, or goats.
In permaculture systems, rabbits can be raised on forage alone, including fresh greens (comfrey, dandelion, plantain, clover), tree fodders (mulberry leaves, Siberian pea shrub), and agricultural byproducts (straw, vegetable trimmings).
Rabbit manure is a "cold" fertilizer that can be applied directly to plants without composting, unlike poultry and livestock manures which require decomposition to avoid nitrogen burn.
Rabbit housing can be stacked vertically in rabbitries, making efficient use of limited space.
The deep-litter system, where rabbits are housed over carbon-rich bedding that absorbs manure and urine, produces valuable worm-rich compost.
Rabbit manure supports [[soil-food-web-structure]] by feeding earthworms and beneficial microorganisms.
Breeding stock requires 12-16% protein in their diet, achievable through a combination of leguminous forages and supplemental grain.

## Fodder Production and Conservation

Successful year-round forage systems require planning for seasonal production gaps.
Mollison emphasizes the importance of fodder conservation including haymaking, silage, and fodder banks.
Hay production from mixed pastures (grass-legume combinations) provides winter feed storage.
Tree hay — branches cut and dried from leguminous trees like [[permaculture-designers-manual-legumes]] (Leucaena, Tagasaste, Albizia) — provides protein-rich winter browse.
Fodder banks, densely planted stands of fast-growing forage species (Sesbania, Pennisetum purpureum, Cajanus cajan), provide cut-and-carry feed during dry seasons.
Seed storage of drought-resistant forage crops ensures that emergency feed can be established when needed.
Fodder beet, mangels, and turnips provide winter root fodder in temperate climates.
The permaculture approach to fodder conservation emphasizes diversity of storage methods and forage species to build resilience against climate variability and crop failure.

## Integration with Zone Planning

Animal forage systems are integrated into Mollison's zone planning framework based on frequency of management.
Poultry in [[permaculture-designers-manual-chicken-tractors]] operate primarily in Zone 1-2 (intensive garden areas) where daily management occurs.
Dairy animals, sheep, and goats operate in Zone 2-3 (orchard and pasture), requiring daily or twice-daily attention.
Beef cattle and extensive grazing systems occupy Zone 3-4, needing less frequent management.
Bee forage systems span across all zones due to the 2.5 km foraging radius.
Pig systems for site preparation operate in specific zones during transition phases.
Wild and semi-wild forage systems (deer, game birds, fish) function in Zone 4-5 with minimal management.
The zone-based placement ensures that labor inputs match management intensity, and that animals are positioned where their forage needs can be met by the surrounding vegetation.
