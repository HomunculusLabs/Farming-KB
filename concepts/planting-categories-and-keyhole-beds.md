# Planting Categories and Keyhole Beds

## Overview

In desert and dryland gardens, the method of establishing plants depends on their category. Different types of planting stock — advanced perennials, seedlings, tubers and bulbs, and seeds — each require specific techniques for successful establishment in mulched garden beds. Understanding these categories and their associated planting methods is fundamental to productive dryland horticulture.

## Four Planting Categories

### 1. Advanced Perennials and Trees in Pots

Shrubs, flowers, and advanced tomatoes fall into this category. The establishment protocol is:

- Keep plants under shade until rains have fallen
- Transplant in wet weather
- Dig holes to accept the full root system
- Soak the hole and add a small handful of fertiliser or soil amendment:
  - Lime in acid areas
  - [[compost]]: dibble a hole for the seedling
- If in thick mulch: pour one-quarter of a bucket (about 1 litre) of good soil into a hole made in the mulch, then place a flat stone upwind
- Plant in the soil hole

### 3. Tubers and Bulbs

Potato, yam, sweet potato, flower bulbs, and sunroot:

- Green and if possible sprouted tubers and tuber shoots can be pushed down into mulch
- Alternatively, bury them and then mulch (in damp soils)

### 4. Large or Fine Seeds

Two sub-families of seed planting exist:

**Method A — Scatter on fine tilth:**
- Scatter seeds over a fine-tilth bed, ideally of sieved compost
- Wet and press down
- Sometimes hessian (burlap) is laid above the bed before watering
- Remove the hessian on day 3 or 4 after the first signs of germination appear
- This method suits larger crops

**Method B — Soil lens over mulch:**
- A lens of fine soil 4–5 cm thick is patted down over a thick (12 cm) base mulch
- Fine seeds are sprinkled on this, forming patches of crop in a general mulch
- This suits repetitive sowings in home gardens

## Instant Gardens

The techniques above enable the establishment of "instant gardens" — gardens that can be commonly established in just a few hours. They give a satisfactory result in the first year, but as with any garden, good garden soils do not form until a few years of continuous gardening have passed, when:

- The biological content of the soil is stabilised
- Crumb structures in the soil have formed

## Specific Crop Bed Designs

### Carrots and Onions

For carrots and onions, 3–6 beds (4 m long × 1 m wide) are recommended depending on family size. An additional 2–3 beds should be allotted to chives, garlic, and shallots. Keen gardeners would include beds for leeks, sand leeks, and multiplier onions.

**Bed construction:**
- All beds can be built raised but dead level by levelling with a straight-edge
- Either sunken below a built-up rim, or heaped and levelled off
- Both onions and carrots prefer free sandy soils with moderate drainage and modest fertility (pH 5.5–7.5)
- Old compost and dry fine organic material (such as powdered grazing-animal manures) are mixed at a ratio of 1:9 or 1:8 (organic material to soil)
- The bed is levelled and pressed flat
- Seed is sprinkled over it (previously mixed with sawdust or coloured sand to show seed coverage)
- The bed is flooded to the rim with water not exceeding 1,100 ppm salt

**Germination care:**
- Cover beds with hessian and inspect daily for germination
- A light daily flood has good effects on germination
- Remove burlap as seeds start to sprout

**Growing season:**
- As crop tops grow, reduce irrigation to once every 2–3 days
- Carrots: 3 beds allotted, planted mid-summer and just before and after the cool season
- Surplus carrots keep well when picked and layered in cool sand in pits 0.5–1 m deep (green tops removed to 1 cm from the crown, pitted without washing or removing roots)
- Onions: hung in dry airy plaits

**[[keyhole-bed-design]]:**
- An efficient bed is a "keyhole" system holding 20–50 plants in 3 rows deep (1.5 m wide)
- Both benefit from Tagetes interplant and a wind shelter of sunflower, sunroot, or Crotalaria
- A culinary accompaniment is basil
- These keyhole beds provide easy access with minimum path:bed ratio and wasted space
- Both plants do well when set out as strong seedlings in soil pockets in deep mulch

### Root Crops

Broad beds, keyholes, or long rows are all suitable. The one essential is thick coarse mulch to:

- Shield the tubers
- Cool the root runs
- Keep pH buffered

**Preferred conditions:**
- Potato and sweet potato prefer organic dry soils (pH 5.0–6.8) achieved by compost, perhaps some sulphur, and a thick damp mulch
- Subsurface water is ideal
- A few marigold interplants aid root health
- A family needs 2–3 successions of each, with areas of 10 square metres or so in each bed
- These are staple crops, so 20–30 square metres of garden needs to be devoted to staple food

## Irrigation Substitutes

Where plastic irrigation pipes are absent or expensive, adequate substitutes can be made of:

- Unglazed pots (for seepage irrigation)
- Pipe and gravel beds
- Hand-moulded concrete on or below the surface, covered with tiles or any flat material
- Bamboo pipes, drilled to leak
- Water flagons or bottles suspended and perforated to drip
- Simple bucket systems (albeit with more labour)

## Planning Returns

Planning a garden repays itself quickly. A good gardener expects to amortise establishment costs (fences, beds, seed, mulch, micronutrients) in 6–8 months, thereafter gaining a profit in health, food, and cash. Detailed planning guides exist for spacing, micronutrients, pH, and yields under good conditions for many common vegetable crops.
