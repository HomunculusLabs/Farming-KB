# Metal Biotransformation by Fungi

Updated: 2026-04-18

Fungi possess remarkable abilities to transform metals and metalloids through diverse mechanisms of mobilization, immobilization, accumulation, and precipitation. These activities have profound implications for biogeochemical cycling, soil formation, and bioremediation of contaminated environments.

## Overview of Fungal Metal Interactions

Fungi can interact with metals through both active and incidental mechanisms contributing to tolerance and survival (Gadd, 1993). The main processes include:

- Mobilization of metals from minerals and solid substrates
- Extracellular and intracellular accumulation (bioaccumulation)
- Biosorption to cell walls, pigments and extracellular polysaccharides
- Extracellular precipitation of secondary minerals
- Reduction and oxidation of metal species

Fungi can be highly efficient bioaccumulators of soluble and particulate forms of metals (Ni, Zn, Ag, Cu, Cd, Pb), especially from dilute external concentrations (Gadd, 1993, 2000). Metal binding can be an important passive process in both living and dead fungal biomass.

## Mechanisms of Metal Solubilization

Three synergistic mechanisms operate for mineral dissolution:

1. **Acidolysis**: Fungi acidify their microenvironment through proton excretion, organic acid excretion, and formation of carbonic acid from respiratory CO2. Acidolysis was the main mechanism of fungal dissolution of toxic metal phosphates when ammonium was the nitrogen source (Fomina et al., 2004)

2. **Complexolysis**: Metal-complexing metabolites include carboxylic acids (oxalic, citric, malic, succinic, formic acids), amino acids, siderophores, and phenolic compounds. When fungi excrete large amounts of a strong chelator like oxalate, the mechanism shifts to ligand-promoted dissolution (Fomina et al., 2004, 2005a)

3. **Redoxolysis**: Fungi reduce and oxidize metals, precipitating elemental forms. Reduced forms of metals/metalloids (Ag, Se, Te) are precipitated within and around fungal cells, visible as black (Ag, Te) or red (Se) coloration of colonies (Gharieb et al., 1999)

## Secondary Mineral Precipitation

Fungi precipitate a variety of secondary minerals:

- **Carbonates**: Calcite (CaCO3) precipitation coupled with silicate weathering provides an important CO2 sink. Chitin in fungal cell walls acts as a nucleation substrate for calcite (Manoli et al., 1997)

- **Oxalates**: Fungi produce metal oxalates with Ca, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Sr, Zn, Ni and Pb (Gadd, 1999; Sayer et al., 1999; Fomina et al., 2005a). Calcium oxalate dihydrate (weddelite) and monohydrate (whewellite) are the most common forms. Oxalate excretion is enhanced by NO3- as nitrogen source (vs NH4+), and by HCO3-, Ca2+, Cu, Al, and toxic metal minerals

- **Other mycogenic minerals**: Birnessite, MnO, FeO, ferrihydrite, iron gluconate, calcium formate, forsterite, goethite, halloysite, todorokite, moolooite (copper oxalate), glushkinskite (MgC2O4.2H2O) and montmorillonite (Gadd et al., various)

## Beauveria caledonica: A Model Metal-Tolerant Fungus

The oxalate-over-extracting fungus Beauveria caledonica transforms cadmium, copper, lead and zinc from toxic metal minerals into oxalates and tolerates all tested minerals (Fomina et al., 2005a). This demonstrates that oxalate precipitation provides a mechanism for fungal metal tolerance while simultaneously transforming toxic mineral phases.

## Overview

Fungi have been components of microbial communities of terrestrial environments including deserts, metal-rich and hypersaline habitats, since the Ordovician period (480-460 MYBP) (Heckman et al., 2001; Burford et al., 2003). The fungal explorative growth habit and high surface-area-to-mass ratio, together with nutrient translocation ability and hydrated mucilaginous sheaths, ensure that fungal processes are an integral component of biogeochemical change (Gadd, 2004, 2006).

Fungal bioweathering of basaltic outcrops in sub-polar areas is believed to be chronologically the first process of weathering, followed by cryogenic processes (Etienne and Dupont, 2002). Fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at higher pH values and over a wider redox range more efficiently than bacteria (Gu et al., 1998; Castro et al., 2000; Burford et al., 2003).

## Rock-Dwelling Fungal Communities

Rock- and stone-dwelling microorganisms are classified as (a) epilithic (on rock surfaces), (b) hypolithic (associated with pebbles), (c) endolithic (inhabiting rock sub-surface), (d) chasmolithic (growing in pre-existing cracks and fissures), (e) cryptoendolithic (growing inside cavities among crystal grains), and (f) euendolithic (capable of actively boring into rock) (May, 2003).

Diverse microbial communities have been found in pore spaces of exposed rock from the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica (Torre et al., 2003), hot and cold deserts (Staley et al., 1982; Sterflinger, 2000), and in rock substrates buried several kilometres below the Earth's surface (Kerr, 1997; Banfield et al., 1999).

Fungi have been found in many rock types: limestone, soapstone, marble, granite, sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite and quartz. Alkaline rocks appear more susceptible to fungal attack than acidic rocks (Eckhardt, 1985; Kumar and Kumar, 1999).

## Mechanisms of Weathering

### Biomechanical Weathering

Direct biomechanical degradation occurs through extensive hyphal penetration into decayed rocks and tunnelling into otherwise intact mineral matter along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks and grain boundaries. Fungal hyphae can exert considerable mechanical force deriving from osmotically generated turgor pressure (Money, 2004).

Biomechanical penetration is facilitated by thigmotropic reactions and lubrication with mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating metabolites (Burford et al., 2003). Indirect biomechanical weathering also occurs from shrinking and swelling of hydrated mucilage (Warscheid and Krumbein, 1994).

### Biochemical Weathering

Biochemical processes are believed to be much more important than mechanical mineral degradation (Kumar and Kumar, 1999; Burford et al., 2003). Three synergistic mechanisms operate:

1. **Acidolysis (proton-promoted dissolution)**: Fungi acidify their microenvironment through proton excretion, organic acid excretion, and carbonic acid formation from respiratory CO2. Acidolysis was the main mechanism of fungal dissolution of toxic metal phosphates when ammonium was the nitrogen source (Fomina et al., 2004, 2005b).

2. **Complexolysis (ligand-promoted dissolution)**: Fungi excrete metal-complexing metabolites including carboxylic acids, amino acids, siderophores and phenolic compounds (Manley and Evans, 1986; Gadd, 1999). Organic acids with strong chelating properties (oxalic and citric acid) perform aggressive attack on mineral surfaces.
