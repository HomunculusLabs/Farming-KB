# Metal Biotransformation by Fungi

Updated: 2026-04-18 Fungi possess remarkable abilities to transform metals and
metalloids through diverse mechanisms of mobilization, immobilization,
accumulation, and precipitation. These activities have profound implications for
[[mycorrhizal-fungi-in-biogeochemical-cycling]], soil formation, and bioremediation of contaminated
environments.

## Overview of Fungal Metal Interactions

Fungi can interact with metals through both active and incidental mechanisms
contributing to tolerance and survival (Gadd, 1993). The main processes include:

- Mobilization of metals from minerals and solid substrates
- Extracellular and intracellular accumulation (bioaccumulation)
- Biosorption to cell walls, pigments and extracellular polysaccharides
- Extracellular precipitation of [[gadd-mycogenic-mineral-precipitation-secondary-minerals]]
- Reduction and oxidation of metal species

Fungi can be highly efficient bioaccumulators of soluble and particulate forms of
metals (Ni, Zn, Ag, Cu, Cd, Pb), especially from dilute external concentrations
(Gadd, 1993, 2000). Metal binding can be an important passive process in both
living and dead fungal biomass.

## Mechanisms of Metal Solubilization

Three synergistic mechanisms operate for mineral dissolution:

1. **Acidolysis**: Fungi acidify their microenvironment through proton
   excretion, organic acid excretion, and formation of carbonic acid from
   respiratory CO2. Acidolysis was the main mechanism of fungal dissolution of
   toxic metal phosphates when ammonium was the nitrogen source (Fomina et al.,
   2004)

2. **Complexolysis**: Metal-complexing metabolites include carboxylic acids
   (oxalic, citric, malic, succinic, formic acids), amino acids, siderophores,
   and [[ingham-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds]]. When fungi excrete large amounts of a strong chelator
   like oxalate, the mechanism shifts to ligand-promoted dissolution (Fomina et
   al., 2004, 2005a)

3. **Redoxolysis**: Fungi reduce and oxidize metals, precipitating elemental
   forms. Reduced forms of metals/metalloids (Ag, Se, Te) are precipitated
   within or around fungal cells, frequently as extracellular nanoparticles

## Overview

Fungi have been components of microbial communities of terrestrial environments
including deserts, metal-rich and hypersaline habitats, since the Ordovician
period (480-460 MYBP) (Heckman et al., 2001; Burford et al., 2003). The fungal
explorative growth habit and high surface-area-to-mass ratio, together with
[[dighton-fungal-nutrient-translocation-element-redistribution]] ability and
hydrated mucilaginous sheaths, ensure that fungal processes are an integral
component of biogeochemical change (Gadd, 2004, 2006).

Fungal bioweathering of basaltic outcrops in sub-polar areas is believed to be
chronologically the first process of weathering, followed by cryogenic processes
(Etienne and Dupont, 2002). Fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at
higher pH values and over a wider redox range more efficiently than bacteria
(Gu et al., 1998; Castro et al., 2000; Burford et al., 2003).

## Rock-Dwelling Fungal Communities

Rock-dwelling microorganisms are classified as epilithic (on surfaces),
hypolithic (associated with pebbles), endolithic (inhabiting rock sub-surface),
chasmolithic (growing in cracks), cryptoendolithic (in cavities among crystal
grains), and euendolithic (actively boring into rock) (May, 2003). Diverse
communities have been found from Antarctic dry valleys to hot deserts and rock
substrates kilometers below the Earth's surface. Fungi colonize many rock types:
limestone, marble, granite, sandstone, basalt, gneiss, and quartz. Alkaline
rocks are more susceptible to fungal attack than acidic rocks (Eckhardt, 1985).

## Mechanisms of Weathering

### Biomechanical Weathering

Direct biomechanical degradation occurs through extensive hyphal penetration
into rocks and tunnelling along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks and grain
boundaries. Hyphae exert considerable mechanical force from osmotically
generated [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] (Money, 2004).
Penetration is facilitated by thigmotropic reactions and lubrication with
mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating metabolites.

### Biochemical Weathering

Biochemical processes are believed to be much more important than mechanical
mineral degradation (Kumar and Kumar, 1999; Burford et al., 2003). Three
synergistic mechanisms operate:

1. **Acidolysis (proton-promoted dissolution)**: Fungi acidify their
   microenvironment through proton excretion, organic acid excretion, and
   carbonic acid formation from respiratory CO2. Acidolysis was the main
   mechanism of fungal dissolution of toxic metal phosphates when ammonium was
   the nitrogen source (Fomina et al., 2004, 2005b).

2. **Complexolysis (ligand-promoted dissolution)**: Fungi excrete
   metal-complexing metabolites including carboxylic acids, amino acids,
   siderophores and phenolic compounds (Manley and Evans, 1986; Gadd, 1999).
   Organic acids with strong chelating properties (oxalic and citric acid)
   perform aggressive attack on mineral surfaces.

## Metal Tolerance Mechanisms

Fungi employ multiple strategies to tolerate elevated metal concentrations.
Intracellular sequestration involves binding metals to metallothionein proteins
and phytochelatin-like peptides. Extracellular complexation occurs through
excretion of organic acids and siderophores that bind metals outside the cell.
Efflux transporters actively pump metals out, maintaining internal concentrations
below toxic thresholds. Melanized species (Cladosporium, Aureobasidium)
incorporate metals into cell wall melanin pigments. Vesicular compartmentalization
sequesters metals within vacuoles. These mechanisms can operate simultaneously.

## Oxalate-Mineral Interactions

Fungal oxalic acid production plays a central role in metal biotransformation.
Oxalate forms strong soluble complexes with many metals (Fe, Al, Ca, Cu, Zn),
enhancing their mobility. When concentrations are sufficient, insoluble metal
[[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]] (weddellite, whewellite) can precipitate, immobilizing metals
in stable crystalline forms. Calcium oxalate crystal formation contributes
significantly to calcium cycling in forest soils. In ectomycorrhizal
associations, oxalate excretion by fungal mantles contributes to mineral
weathering and influences [[phosphorus-availability]] through dissolution of
phosphate minerals.

## Fungal Biomineralization and Nanoparticle Synthesis

Fungi can precipitate metal-bearing minerals both intracellularly and
extracellularly, with applications in bioremediation and nanomaterial synthesis.
[[fusarium-oxysporum]] synthesizes silver nanoparticles extracellularly when exposed
to silver nitrate, with nitrate reductase reducing silver ions to elemental
silver. Similar processes occur with gold and platinum. Fungal biomineralization
of uranium (as uranyl phosphate minerals) has been investigated for immobilizing
radionuclides at contaminated sites. These biomineralized products are often
