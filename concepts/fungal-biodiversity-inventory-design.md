# [[fungal-biodiversity]] Inventory Design

## Overview

Designing effective fungal biodiversity inventories is one of the most challenging tasks in mycology. Fungi cannot be trapped or fogged en masse like insects. They may produce fruit bodies only at intervals of many years, structures may persist for only hours, and different types of fungi demand entirely different detection and identification procedures. No single site in the world has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi.

This page synthesizes principles from "Biodiversity of Fungi" (Mueller, Bills, and Foster) for designing fungal inventory and monitoring programs.

## Fundamental Challenges

- **Temporal variability**: Fruiting is episodic; many fungi fruit only under specific conditions
- **Spatial variability**: A single underground mycelium may produce fruit bodies over a wide area
- **Cryptic species**: Many fungi live inside plants, arthropods, or soil and are detectable only by special methods
- **Pleomorphy**: Many fungi have multiple sporing stages with different scientific names
- **Method dependence**: Isolated species depend on methods used; many fungi do not grow readily in culture
- **Resting spores**: Many fungi isolated from soil may have been present only as dormant propagules
- **Quantification difficulty**: Distinguishing individuals and measuring abundance is problematic

## Inventory Scale and Duration

### Three Levels of Inventory

1. **Rapid (1-year) inventory**: Survey of large regions. Designed for broad characterization of species composition across many sites. Lower resolution per site but wider geographic coverage.

2. **Medium-term (3-5 year) inventory**: Balanced approach covering regions with moderate intensity per site. Allows detection of seasonal and interannual variation in fruiting patterns.

3. **Intensive inventory of selected sites**: Long-term (25+ years) study of specific sites. This is the only approach that approaches comprehensiveness. Lindner et al. found that in temperate areas, 2,500-3,000 species can be found in about 200 hectares if various specialists study the site for more than 25 years.

### Temporal Scale Considerations

- Fungal inventory programs must be designed with temporal scale as a primary consideration
- Fruiting phenology varies dramatically among groups
- Some fungi are perennial ([[lichens]], many polypores); others ephemeral
- Seasonal and interannual variation in fruiting must be accounted for
- Climate factors (rainfall, temperature) drive fruiting patterns

## Spatial Organization

### Site Selection

Sites should be selected to represent:

- Major habitat types in the region of interest
- Environmental gradients (elevation, moisture, substrate)
- Representative vegetation communities
- Both disturbed and undisturbed areas
- Replication where possible for statistical comparison

### Plot-Based Sampling

Standardized plot designs are essential for comparability:

- **Macrofungi**: Fixed-area plots with timed searches; or transect-based searches
- **Microfungi**: Defined substrate samples (wood, litter, soil volumes)
- **Mycorrhizae**: Root samples from defined plots or individual trees
- **Aquatic fungi**: Water samples and substrate baits from defined points

### Data Categories for Comparison

1. **Within-site**: Presence list for all species; abundance measures
2. **Within-site class**: Comparisons across habitat or vegetation types
3. **All sites combined**: Average frequency and variation in occurrence

## Operational Groups

Fungal inventories are designed around operational groups based on habitat, substratum use, or ecological habits rather than strict taxonomy. Distinct protocols are necessary for each:

- Single-celled aquatic species ([[zoosporic-fungi]])
- Other aquatic fungi (Ingoldian, aero-aquatic)
- Soil-associated saprobic fungi
- Obligate root mutualists (arbuscular mycorrhizae, ectomycorrhizae)
- [[endophytic-fungi]] (inside plant tissues)
- Plant pathogens ([[biodiversity-powdery-mildews]], rusts, smuts)
- Lichenized fungi
- Wood-inhabiting macrofungi and microfungi
- Terrestrial macrofungi (mushrooms, puffballs, etc.)
- [[coprophilous-fungi]] (dung-inhabiting)
- [[arthropod-associated-fungi]]
- [[fungicolous-fungi]]
- [[sequestrate-fungi]] (truffles, false truffles)

## Species Richness Estimation

### Quantitative Approaches

Several indices and estimators are available:

- **Species accumulation curves**: Plot cumulative species against sampling effort
- **Rarefaction**: Standardize species counts by sample size
- **Chao1 and Chao2 estimators**: For estimating total species from sample data
- **Jackknife estimators**: First-order and second-order jackknife
- **Bootstrap estimators**: Resampling-based approaches

### Power Analysis

Before initiating monitoring programs, power analysis should determine:

- Minimum detectable change in species composition
- Required sample sizes for statistical significance
- Number of plots or transects needed
- Number of sampling visits per year

### Extrapolation to Total Richness

Various methods extrapolate from observed to total species:

- Parametric estimators (log-normal, Poisson-log-normal)
- Nonparametric estimators (Chao, jackknife, bootstrap)
- Species-richness estimators from incidence data

## Standardization Requirements

For meaningful comparisons across sites and studies:

- Use standard protocols for each operational group
- Document all deviations from standard methods
- Calibrate sampling effort precisely
- Use standard data fields and formats
- Deposit voucher specimens in recognized herbaria
- Deposit cultures in recognized culture collections
- Submit molecular data to public databases
- Report methods in sufficient detail for replication

## Products of Fungal Surveys

### Scientific Contributions

- Baseline data for measuring environmental change
- Species composition and distribution data
- Host and substratum association records
- Phenological data
- New species descriptions

### Applied Products

- Bioindicator data for ecosystem health assessment
- Data for land management decisions
- Educational materials and field guides
- Checklist for parks and nature areas
- Natural product discovery (secondary metabolites)
