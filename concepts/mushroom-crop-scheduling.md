# Mushroom Crop Scheduling and Rotation
Mushroom crop scheduling is the practice of planning and timing the various 
stages of cultivation, from culture preparation through harvest, to maximize 
yield, efficiency, and facility utilization. Unlike green plants that follow 
seasonal cycles driven by light and temperature, cultivated mushrooms can be 
grown year-round indoors, but they still require careful timing of each phase 
to ensure continuous production and optimal use of growing space.

## The Cultivation Timeline
A typical mushroom cultivation cycle includes several sequential phases, each 
with a characteristic duration. Agar culture work requires 7-14 days for 
mycelial growth across a petri dish. Grain spawn preparation requires 3-5 
days for grain hydration and sterilization, plus 10-21 days for mycelial 
colonization. Bulk substrate preparation requires 1-2 days for pasteurization 
or sterilization and cooling. Spawn running on bulk substrate requires 7-21 
days depending on species and conditions. The casing application and 
colonization period adds 7-14 days. Pinning initiation requires 5-14 days. 

Each flush of mushrooms develops over 5-10 days, with 1-3 flushes typical for 
most species.
From start of agar work to final harvest, a complete Psilocybe cubensis cycle 
takes approximately 8-14 weeks. Agaricus brunnescens, with its composting 
requirements, takes 12-16 weeks. Understanding these timelines allows the 
cultivator to plan overlapping crops that keep growing spaces continuously 
productive.

## Staggered Cropping
The key principle of efficient crop scheduling is staggering the start of 
successive crops so that different growing spaces are occupied by substrates 
at different stages of the cultivation cycle. While one set of substrates is 
fruiting in the growing room, the next set is in spawn running, and another 
set of grain spawn jars is colonizing in the incubation area. This approach 
maximizes facility utilization by ensuring that no growing space sits empty.

For a cultivator with two fruiting chambers, an ideal stagger might be two 
weeks between chamber start dates. When the first chamber enters its peak 
fruiting period, the second chamber is in pinning initiation. When the first 
chamber is harvested and cleaned, the second chamber is at peak fruiting, and 
a new set of substrates is ready to enter the first chamber for spawn running 
or pinning.

## Species Rotation
Different mushroom species have different environmental requirements, which 
can be exploited for sequential use of the same growing space. For example, a 
growing room maintained at 75-80°F can produce Psilocybe cubensis in spring 
and summer, while the same room cooled to 55-62°F can produce Agaricus 
brunnescens or Pleurotus ostreatus in autumn and winter. This seasonal 
rotation takes advantage of natural ambient temperature changes to reduce 
heating and cooling costs.
Some cultivators rotate between compost-based species and non-composted 
substrates in the same growing room. After harvesting a compost-based crop, 
the room is thoroughly cleaned and the next crop on straw or sawdust is 
introduced. This rotation reduces the buildup of species-specific pests and 
diseases that can occur when the same substrate type and species are grown 
continuously.

## Facility Capacity Planning
Effective crop scheduling requires understanding the capacity constraints of 
the facility. The most common bottlenecks are incubation space (for 
colonizing grain jars and bulk substrates), fruiting space (for the actual 
mushroom production), and sterilization equipment capacity (pressure cooker 
or autoclave throughput). If the pressure cooker can only process 10 jars per 
cycle, and each grow requires 20 jars of spawn, two sterilization cycles are 
needed, which adds a day to the timeline.

Growing room capacity determines how many substrates can be fruiting 
simultaneously. The air-to-bed ratio of the growing room limits the total 
substrate mass that can be managed. Exceeding this capacity results in 
elevated CO2, poor temperature control, and reduced yields. Planning crop 
scheduling around these capacity constraints prevents overloading the system 
at any stage.

## Spawn Bank Management
A critical component of crop scheduling is maintaining a reliable supply of 
spawn. Cultivators who depend on a single agar plate or liquid culture for 
each grow are vulnerable to contamination events that can halt production 
entirely. Maintaining a spawn bank, a collection of multiple agar plates, 
slant cultures, and liquid cultures of each desired strain, provides 
insurance against individual culture failures.

The spawn bank should be sufficient to support continuous production even if 
several cultures are lost to contamination. A minimum of 3-5 backup cultures 
per strain is recommended. Slant cultures under refrigeration provide 
medium-term storage (6-12 months), while spore prints provide long-term 
genetic backup. The spawn bank should be regularly refreshed to maintain 
culture vigor and prevent senescence.

## Record Keeping
Detailed records are essential for effective crop scheduling. A cultivation 
log should track the date and details of each stage: agar transfer dates, 
grain inoculation and colonization dates, substrate preparation and spawning 
dates, casing application dates, environmental conditions throughout the 
grow, pinning dates, harvest dates and weights, and any problems encountered. 

This historical data allows the cultivator to identify patterns, optimize 
timelines, and predict future crop performance.

Over multiple grows, the cultivation log becomes a valuable resource for 
calculating biological efficiency (yield as a percentage of substrate dry 
weight), identifying seasonal trends in contamination or yield, and refining 
the crop schedule for maximum productivity.

## Economic Considerations
Crop scheduling has direct economic implications for commercial cultivators. 

Growing space that sits empty between crops represents lost revenue. Labor 
that is unevenly distributed (intense work during spawning and harvest, idle 
time during spawn running) reduces efficiency. A well-designed crop schedule 
spreads labor evenly across weeks and months, keeps all growing spaces 
productive, and provides predictable harvest dates for market planning.

For home cultivators, the economic consideration is primarily the cost of 
substrate materials and energy relative to the yield obtained. Efficient 
scheduling that prevents contamination losses and maximizes the number of 
successful flushes per substrate batch provides the best return on the time 
and materials invested.
