# Pond Furniture and Structures

Any wetland habitat can be increased in yield and utility by
adding basic facilities that provide specialized habitat.
Together, these transform a simple body of water into a complex,
high-production polyculture system.

## Islands and Hummocks

Even small islands are excellent wildfowl habitat, quickly
occupied by nesting birds. They also serve to isolate invasive
plants like runner bamboos, increase shoreline for fish foraging,
create sheltered bays, and streamline winds for oxygenation.

Peninsulas — islands connected by narrow causeways — serve as
safe building sites in fire-prone areas while elaborating edge
effect.

Hummocks (small earth mounds in shallows) are critical for swan
and other ground-nesting waterfowl, whose populations are often
limited by defended nest sites.

Many territorial waterfowl use hummocks as night roosts, and
several useful tree species are hummock-dependent.

In Florida swamps, alligators serve as the natural hummock
architects.

## Deeps and Kettles

Deep water refuges determine fish species composition in shallow
ponds and marshes.

During extreme cold or heat, several species survive only by
retreating to deeper kettles.

A series of continuous deeps within a marsh is probably the
highest-production water habitat of any natural system.

For carp, galaxiids, catfish, and perch, deeps that flood over
mud and grass spawning beds are essential for breeding.

By managing water levels, designers can arrange artificial
"flood" cycles to induce spawning on cue, and many waterfowl
species also respond to these fluctuations.

## Breeding Substrates

Different fish species require specific substrates: grassy slopes
flooded for carp, thatched shelters for cave breeders, logs and
drums for large perch and Murray River cod, gravels and rock
piles for salmonids, mud caves for catfish, and floating weed or
reed bundles for vegetation-attaching species.

Cannibalistic crayfish and territorial fish defend breeding
refuges, and their population density depends on shelter
availability.

Tyre heaps, broken pipe piles, and hollow containers serve as
effective "condominiums" for such species.

Brush bundles perform dual functions as breeding substrates and
predator refuges for small species.

All such refuges can be hauled out to function as traps —
octopus, eels, and crayfish remain in their holes while shrimp
and crabs cling to brush piles.

## Rafts and Floating Structures

Rafts serve remarkably diverse functions: floating docks for
fish cage access, growing platforms for rope and mesh bag
cultures, observation platforms, self-feeders for fish and
waterfowl, solar energy platforms that economically track the
sun, water level managers for tidal or draw-down conditions, and
insect incubators whose products fall into the water below.

Rudolf Doernach built raft houses on cistern ponds that could
follow the sun throughout the day.

## Screens and Fences

Screens and fences manage polyculture by separating predator
from prey, allowing size-based culling, and regulating species
migration between connected waters.

Configurations include horizontal, sloping, vertical, cylindrical,
and cone designs.

Rotating drum screens are self-cleaning when a small head of
water is available — useful for skimming ponds or collecting
floating plants and algae.

Fences cost-effectively screen shallow areas, separate deeps, or
partition ponds into zones.

Screen fences beside deeps prevent fish escape or permit frogs to
breed without excessive trout predation.

## Outlets and Inlets

Modern flexible pipe makes water level control simple via elbow
or upturned pipe arrangements.

Outlets can double as harvest systems — baskets catching fish
as water flows out, or screened spillways gathering migrating
eels or trout from complex swamp systems.

Inlets require careful screening to prevent entry of silt,
weeds, or unwanted organisms, ranging from simple pipes to
multi-stage filter systems.

## Cage Culture

Cages of wood, natural materials, metal mesh, and synthetic
meshes have been used since antiquity for fish production.

They protect eggs and fry, hold live market fish, and house
species that reduce algal taints.

Circular (salmon ring-net) and square slatted-wood cages are
most common.

Typical floating rearing cages maintain water flow through fish
swimming action, producing some of the highest aquaculture yields.

Mooring options include individual anchors, floating dock sets,
or gangs stretched across bays with old tyres as spacers.

However, predators like octopus, seals, and sharks can attack
caged fish — losses that pond fish avoid through evasion.
Annual inspection and vigilant maintenance are essential.
