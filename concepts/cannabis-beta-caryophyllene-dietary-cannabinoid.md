# Beta-Caryophyllene as a Dietary Cannabinoid

(E)-beta-caryophyllene ((E)-BCP) is a naturally occurring compound found
in many common spices and cannabis that functions as a selective CB2
receptor agonist. Discovered as a dietary cannabinoid by an international
group of researchers from Switzerland, Germany, Italy, and the U.S.
(2008, 2012), it represents a food-based pathway to activating the
[[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] without any
psychoactive effects.

## What Is Beta-Caryophyllene?

Beta-caryophyllene is a sesquiterpene found in the essential oils of
numerous plants. While some chemists classify it as a terpene or
terpenoid, this distinction has little practical value to the many
patients who may benefit from its therapeutic use. As a functional
non-psychoactive CB2 agonist, it is considered by researchers to be a
food-based cannabinoid, sometimes called a "dietary cannabinoid."

Unlike THC, which binds to both CB1 and CB2 receptors and produces
psychoactive effects through CB1 activation, (E)-BCP selectively targets
CB2 receptors, which are primarily involved in immune function and
inflammation regulation. This selectivity means it delivers therapeutic
benefits without any intoxicating effects.

## Mechanism of Action

(E)-beta-caryophyllene activates [[cannabis-cannabinoid-receptor-pharmacology]], which are predominantly found in the immune system and peripheral tissues. CB2 activation modulates immune responses, reduces inflammation, and influences pain signaling without affecting the central nervous system's psychoactive pathways.

Scientists suggest that activation of CB2 receptors via this dietary
plant-based cannabinoid might present a new and additional therapeutic
strategy in the treatment of a multitude of diseases associated with
inflammation and oxidative stress, both underlying factors in a host of
different pathologies.

## Therapeutic Properties

Research has demonstrated that (E)-beta-caryophyllene may:

- Protect against inflammation and inflammatory diseases
- Provide antimicrobial activity
- Offer pain-relieving properties
- Protect against cancer
- Reduce oxidative stress
- Support immune system regulation

These properties overlap significantly with the therapeutic benefits
attributed to cannabis-derived cannabinoids, but through a dietary
pathway that is fully legal and widely accessible.

## Dietary Sources

This food-based cannabinoid is fully accepted by the U.S. government
with the FDA's seal of approval. Key (E)-beta-caryophyllene-containing
organic spices are relatively easy to obtain.

### High (E)-BCP Content Sources

- **Black Ashanti Pepper (Piper guineense)**: (E)-BCP content of 58%
  in test samples
- **White Ashanti Pepper**: (E)-BCP content of 52% in test samples
- **Clove**: Among the richest common culinary sources
- **Cinnamon**: Readily available source with meaningful BCP content
- **Black Pepper**: Common household spice with notable BCP levels

### Moderate (E)-BCP Content Sources

- Basil
- Indian bay-leaf
- Alligator pepper
- Rosemary
- Caraway
- Mexican oregano
- Copal resin (used traditionally as incense)

## Cannabis Connection

Beta-caryophyllene is also found in cannabis, where it contributes to
the plant's [[blesching-cannabis-cannabinoid-terpene-entourage-effect]]. In whole-plant cannabis medicine, (E)-BCP works synergistically
with THC, CBD, and other cannabinoids and terpenes to produce therapeutic
effects greater than the sum of their individual contributions.

The presence of (E)-BCP in cannabis provides another example of how the
cannabis plant contains compounds that mirror and supplement the body's
own endocannabinoid system through multiple pathways.

## Significance for Medical Cannabis Patients

For patients who cannot or choose not to use psychoactive cannabis,
beta-caryophyllene offers an alternative way to engage the
endocannabinoid system's anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating
pathways. This is particularly relevant for:

- Patients concerned about psychoactive effects
- Patients in jurisdictions where cannabis is not legally accessible
- Patients seeking complementary dietary approaches alongside
  conventional treatment
- Patients who want to support their endocannabinoid system through
  everyday nutrition

## The Four Sources of Cannabinoids

Blesching identifies four sources of cannabinoids that can activate the
endocannabinoid system:

1. **Endogenous** (produced by the human body): anandamide, 2-AG,
   noladin ether, virodhamine, NADA
2. **Cannabis plant**: THC, CBD, CBC, CBG, CBN, and others
3. **Other plants**: (E)-beta-caryophyllene and other dietary
   cannabinoids
4. **Synthetic/pharmaceutical**: dronabinol, nabilone, sativex,
   research compounds

Understanding that cannabinoids are not exclusive to cannabis opens up
broader therapeutic possibilities and reinforces the fundamental
importance of the endocannabinoid system to human health.

## Integration with Cannabis Therapy

Patients using medical cannabis can enhance their therapy by
incorporating (E)-BCP-rich spices into their diet. This provides
additional CB2 activation that may complement the broader cannabinoid
profile of cannabis. Cooking with these spices alongside
[[cannabis-tinctures-and-edibles]] creates
a multi-source cannabinoid approach to healing.

The concept of dietary cannabinoids also supports the broader
understanding that the endocannabinoid system evolved to interact with
plant-derived compounds, and that humans have likely been modulating
this system through diet for thousands of years before cannabis was
identified as a specific medicine.
