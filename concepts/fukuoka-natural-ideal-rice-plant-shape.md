# The Ideal Form and Shape of the Rice Plant

Aware of the inherent problems with the process of breaking down and
analyzing a rice plant in the laboratory and reaching conclusions from these
results, Fukuoka chose to abandon existing notions and look instead at the
rice plant from afar. His method of growing rice may appear reckless and
absurd, but all along he sought the true form of rice. He searched for the
form of natural rice and asked what healthy rice is.

## Discovery Through Natural Cultivation

When rice, barley, and clover were grown together, it was found that rice
ripening over a thick cover of clover is short-stalked, robust right down to
the bottom leaf, and bears fine golden heads of grain. After observing this,
experiments with seeding the rice in the fall and winter revealed that even
rice grown under terrible conditions on arid, depleted soil gives
surprisingly high yields.

This experience convinced him of the possibilities of growing high-yielding
rice on continuously untilled fields. By experimenting to learn the type of
field and manner in which rice having an ideal form will grow, eventually he
found what he thought to be the ideal form of high-yielding rice.

## Characteristics of the Ideal Rice Plant

The major characteristics of rice plants with an ideal form include:

1. **Short-stalked dwarf rice of robust appearance**: Leaves are short, wide,
   and erect, with a stalk height of about 21 inches and 15 to 22 tillers
   per plant. At maturity, the stalks are heavy with bright golden grain.

2. **Heavy grain-to-straw ratio**: The weight of unhulled grain is 150 to
   167 percent that of the straw, compared to less than 70 percent in
   ordinary rice. When balanced on a fingertip, equilibrium is near the
   panicle neck rather than the center of the stalk.

3. **Long first internode**: More than fifty percent of stalk length, with
   the panicle extending below the stalk base when bent at the first node.
   The longer this ratio, the better.

4. **Inverted leaf arrangement**: The leaf blade on the second leaf down is
   longest, becoming shorter moving down the stalk. This inverted triangular
   shape gives a rice plant that performs well in the fall.

5. **Long leaf sheaths**: Total leaf length is longest for the first and
   second leaves, decreasing downward. In non-high-yielding rice, the lower
   leaves are longer, with the longest being the fourth leaf.

6. **Minimal nodes**: Only the top four nodes grow, with the fourth at
   ground level or lower, compared to five or six in normal rice. The top
   three leaves alone yield more than 100 full grains per head.

7. **Good grain filling**: Weight per thousand grains of unpolished rice is
   23 grams for small-grained and 24.5 to 25 grams for normal-grained rice.

8. **Dense planting tolerance**: Even at 500 stalks per square yard, hardy
   upright dwarf rice shows no decline in grains per head or ripening.

## The Ideal Shape: Suppressing Vegetative Growth

Both the plant height and length of the leaf blades are much smaller than in
ordinary varieties. This is no accident. Large plants were found unnecessary
in rice production, so effort was made to suppress rather than promote
vegetative growth. No irrigation was applied during the first half of the
growing season, and fresh straw was applied to the field to check plant
response to a basal application of fertilizer.

In ideally shaped rice, the internode lengths each decrease by half from the
top to the bottom of the plant. This indicates steady, orderly growth of the
rice and means that internodal growth occurs only starting at the young
panicle formation stage. The long second leaf and the decreasing leaf length
as one moves down the stalk is the exact reverse of what is generally
thought to be the correct shape of rice, but this inverted triangular shape
gives a rice plant that does well in the fall.

## The Meaning of Concentrated Rice

Ideally, the heads of rice are large and the plant short, having just three
or four nodes above the ground. The leaves get longer in ascending order
toward the top and the internode length between the fourth and fifth nodes at
the bottom is very short. Instead of a feminine form with a high head-to-body
ratio of six or even eight to one, this plant has a more sturdy, masculine,
short-stalked, panicle weight type shape.

Concentrated rice carries a tremendous store of energy that provides high
yields because it maintains an orderly shape receptive to sunlight, matures
well, and is resistant to disease and pest attack, even in a very dense
stand. This connects directly to the [[fukuoka-natural-rice-barley-cultivation]] methods and the [[fukuoka-high-yield-rice-barley]] developed through decades of observation.

## Blueprint for Natural Cultivation

Although raising one high-yielding rice plant with good photosynthetic
efficiency is easy, it was no simple matter to grow full stands of such rice.
Healthy individual rice plants growing in nature have plenty of space to grow.
The sparse seeding of individual seeds allows the rice to assume the natural
form that suits it best and to make full use of its powers. Rice grown in its
natural form puts out leaves in a regular, phyllotaxic order. The leaves open
up and spread in alternation, breaking crosswinds and ensuring the
penetration of sunlight throughout the life of the plant, each leaf
maintaining a good light-receiving form.

Knowing this, healthy rice farming requires sowing individual seeds sparsely.
But because of initial problems of poor germination and weed control when
beginning direct-seeded no-tillage cultivation, dense planting and seeding
was initially necessary to ensure a stable crop. However, dense planting
tended to result in thick growth and poor environment for individual plants.

Fortunately, thanks to gradual improvements in the weed control problem and
soil fertility, conditions fell into place that made it possible to seed
sparsely. Broadcasting, a form of individual seeding, and seeding at uniform
intervals of from 6 to 12 inches were both tried. Sparse seeding gives
healthy, natural rice plants that grow well and provide the high yields
expected. In this way, yields of over one ton per quarter-acre with naturally
grown rice were obtained.

## The Meaning and Limits of High Yields

In natural farming, high yields rely on the absorption and storage of as
much of nature's energy as possible by the crop. For this, the crop must
make the fullest possible use of its inherent powers. The proper role of the
natural farmer is not to utilize the animals and plants of nature so much as
to help invigorate the ecosystem.

Rather than plowing the fields and growing crops, man would be better
occupied in protecting the vitality of all the organisms inhabiting the earth
and in guarding the natural order. The guardian of a watermelon patch does
not watch the watermelons, he looks out for watermelon thieves. Nature
protects itself and sees to the boundless growth of the organisms that
inhabit it. The farmer must hold a vision that is in unity with nature and
never sacrifice something else to human desire.
