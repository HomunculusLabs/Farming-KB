# Endobacteria in Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) harbour obligate intracellular bacteria
within their own cells, creating a remarkable three-tier symbiotic system:
plant, fungus, and bacterium. The discovery that an obligately biotrophic
fungus itself contains obligately intracellular bacteria has fundamentally
changed our understanding of the complexity of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and their
interactions with other microorganisms.

## Candidatus Glomeribacter gigasporarum

The best-characterised endobacterium of AMF is Candidatus Glomeribacter
gigasporarum, a member of the Burkholderiaceae family. This noncultivable
obligate bacterial endosymbiont was first described from spores, mycelium, and
clover intraradical hyphae of Gigaspora margarita. Key characteristics include:

  - Located within fungal vacuoles
  - Present in spores, extraradical mycelium, and intraradical hyphae
  - Cannot be cultured independently outside the fungal host
  - Transmitted vertically through generation of vegetative spores
  - Widespread within the Gigasporaceae family

The bacterium was later phenotypically described in detail and confirmed to be
widespread among Gigasporaceae, indicating an ancient and stable association
between these bacteria and their AMF hosts.

## Vertical Transmission

Endobacteria are transmitted vertically from one fungal generation to the
next through the formation of vegetative spores in Gigaspora margarita. This
mode of transmission ensures that the bacterial symbiont persists alongside
the fungal partner, analogous to the way AMF are transmitted to the next
generation of host plants through spore propagation. This vertical
transmission pathway suggests a long co-evolutionary history between the
endobacterium and its fungal host.

## Ecological and Functional Implications

The presence of endobacteria within AMF has several important implications for
the [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] and the broader
[[fungal-bacterial-partnerships-mycorrhizosphere]]:

### Bacterial-Fungal Interactions

The capacity of rhizobia and pseudomonads to adhere to Gigaspora margarita
spores and hyphae under sterile conditions has been shown to be
strain-dependent. The initial stages of attachment are governed by general
physiochemical parameters such as electrostatic attraction, then later secured
by specific bacterial cell surface components. Bacteria able to form biofilms
through production of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) are more strongly
attached to mycorrhizal structures.

### Biofilm Formation and Attachment

Mutants of Pseudomonas fluorescens affected in EPS production -- essential for
biofilm formation -- were strongly impaired in their capacity to attach to
both mycorrhizal roots and AMF mycelium. Conversely, mucoid mutants with
enhanced alginate biosynthesis adhered more extensively to fungal surfaces.
These findings suggest that AMF may serve as specific substrates that favour
proliferation of certain bacteria and promote biofilm formation.

### Impact on Biocontrol

Bacteria identified within or on the surface of mycorrhizal structures may
contribute to the biocontrol that AMF confer against soilborne pathogens such
as Phytophthora nicotianae. Surface disinfection of Glomus mosseae spores has
yielded bacterial isolates from the genera Paenibacillus, Bacillus, and
Methylobacterium that show in vitro antagonism against multiple soilborne
fungal pathogens. Paenibacillus strain B2, isolated from the mycorrhizosphere
of G. mosseae, was shown to reduce root necroses caused by P. parasitica and
F. oxysporum, producing antibiotic compounds including polymyxine B1.

### Spore-Associated Bacteria

Approximately 80 to 92% of bacteria isolated from decontaminated spores of
Glomus clarum NT4 were Bacillus species. Burkholderia spp. have been found to
colonise both hyphae and spores of G. decipiens. The spore outer layer of
G. geosporum has been observed to be eroded and covered by mucilaginous
products, suggesting that AMF structures may be directly consumed by
associated bacteria.

## The Hyphosphere Concept

The soil volume influenced by AMF extraradical hyphae extends the
rhizosphere concept into the mycorrhizosphere. Bacteria isolated from the
hyphosphere include organisms from the genera Paenibacillus, Bacillus, and
Methylobacterium. AMF inoculation from non-axenically produced inocula is
invariably accompanied by associated bacteria that establish within the
mycorrhizosphere, contributing to modifications in bacterial community
structure detected in the rhizosphere after AMF inoculation.

## Significance for AMF Inoculum Production

The presence of endobacteria and surface-associated bacteria has important
implications for [[mycorrhizal-inoculum-production]]. Inocula produced
non-axenically will contain a suite of associated bacteria that may influence
plant growth, pathogen resistance, and nutrient cycling. Understanding these
bacterial communities is essential for producing consistent and effective
mycorrhizal inoculants.
