## Feedstock Materials

A wide range of organic materials can serve as biogas feedstock. The most
productive systems use a mixture of materials that provides balanced carbon and
nitrogen ratios:

- **Animal manure**: Cattle, pig, and poultry manure are excellent feedstocks.
  The [[mollison-designers-chicken-house-over-swale-integration]] systems produce manure that can be
  piped directly to a digester. Manure also provides the necessary
  methanogenic bacteria to seed a new digester.
- **Human waste**: The [[mollison-designers-cassava-tropical-root-crops]]
  all contribute to gas production.
- **Aquatic plants**: Water hyacinth and duckweed, which are often considered
  invasive, can be harvested and fed to digesters, turning a problem into a
  resource.
- **Legume foliage**: [[mollison-designers-passive-solar-heating-building-design]] principle can maintain digester temperature by placing the unit in
a greenhouse or insulating it with compost.

## Integration with Permaculture Systems

The biogas digester is most effective when integrated with other permaculture
elements. The ideal placement is near the mollison designers animal systems that provide manure,
near the kitchen where gas is used, and near the garden where effluent is
applied. This Zone 1 or inner Zone 2 placement ensures efficient material flows
and easy management.

The effluent from the digester is a valuable resource. It contains most of the
nutrients from the feedstock in a form that is readily available to plants.
Digester effluent can be applied directly to garden beds, used to fertilise
[[mollison-designers-straw-bale-building-techniques]] or earth-bermed structure. The [[mollison-designers-community-garden-design-urban-permaculture]], larger digesters serving multiple
households can be more efficient than individual units. Community digesters
benefit from economies of scale and can process waste from shared livestock,
market facilities, or public toilets. Gas can be piped to multiple households
or used to power a community generator.
