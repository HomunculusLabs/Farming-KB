# Biogas Methane Digester Design

Biogas digestion is a permaculture appropriate technology that converts organic
waste into methane gas for cooking and lighting, while producing nutrient-rich
effluent that serves as fertiliser. Bill Mollison promoted biogas systems as a
key component of integrated permaculture design, particularly in rural and
tropical settings where organic waste is abundant and conventional energy
supplies are unreliable or expensive. The biogas digester closes the loop
between waste management, energy production, and soil fertility.

## How Biogas Digestion Works

Biogas is produced through anaerobic digestion, a process in which
microorganisms break down organic matter in the absence of oxygen. The primary
output is a mixture of methane (approximately 60 percent) and carbon dioxide
(approximately 35 percent), with trace amounts of hydrogen sulphide and water
vapour. This gas burns cleanly with a blue flame and can be used directly for
cooking, lighting, or heating, or converted to electricity with a generator.

The digestion process occurs in three main stages. Hydrolytic bacteria first
break down complex organic compounds into simple sugars, amino acids, and fatty
acids. Acidogenic bacteria then convert these into volatile fatty acids, carbon
dioxide, and hydrogen. Finally, methanogenic archaea produce methane from the
acetic acid and hydrogen. The [[mollison-designers-energy-conversion-efficiency-devices]] efficiency of biogas systems
is typically 40 to 60 percent, with the remaining energy retained in the
effluent as heat and nutrients.

## Feedstock Materials

A wide range of organic materials can serve as biogas feedstock. The most
productive systems use a mixture of materials that provides balanced carbon and
nitrogen ratios:

- **Animal manure**: Cattle, pig, and poultry manure are excellent feedstocks.
  The [[mollison-designers-pig-tractor-systems-and-land-preparation]] and [[mollison-designers-chicken-house-over-swale-integration]] systems produce manure that can be
  piped directly to a digester. Manure also provides the necessary
  methanogenic bacteria to seed a new digester.
- **Human waste**: The [[mollison-designers-compost-toilet-humanure-systems]] can be connected to a biogas system,
  providing a sanitary and productive way to process humanure.
- **Crop residues**: Kitchen scraps, garden waste, and [[mollison-designers-cassava-tropical-root-crops]]
  all contribute to gas production.
- **Aquatic plants**: Water hyacinth and duckweed, which are often considered
  invasive, can be harvested and fed to digesters, turning a problem into a
  resource.
- **Legume foliage**: [[mollison-designers-leucaena-multipurpose-nitrogen-tree]] and other legume tree prunings provide
  nitrogen-rich green material.

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of the feedstock mixture should ideally be between
20:1 and 30:1. Too much carbon slows digestion; too much nitrogen can cause
ammonia toxicity. A mix of manure (nitrogen-rich) and crop residues (carbon-
rich) usually achieves the right balance.

## Digester Designs

### Fixed Dome Digester

The Chinese fixed dome design is the most widely built biogas system worldwide.
The digester is an underground brick or concrete chamber with a gas-tight dome.
As gas is produced, it displaces digestate into the compensating chamber,
creating pressure that forces gas to the point of use. When gas is consumed,
digestate flows back into the main chamber. This design has no moving parts and
is very durable, with a lifespan of 20 years or more.

### Floating Drum Digester

The Indian floating drum design uses a cylindrical digester tank with a
gas-holder drum that floats on the digestate. As gas is produced, the drum
rises, maintaining constant gas pressure. This design is simpler to construct
than the fixed dome and allows easy monitoring of gas production volume. The
floating drum is well-suited to situations where gas demand varies throughout
the day.

### Tube and Bag Digesters
Flexible bag digesters made from PVC or polyethylene are the lowest-cost option.
Feedstock is loaded into a long sealed tube with gas collecting at the top.
They are simple to construct and popular in tropical developing countries, but
plastic degrades in sunlight and typically lasts only three to five years.

## Sizing the Digester
A typical family of five to six people requires a four to eight cubic metre
digester for daily cooking, with roughly 20 to 30 kilograms of wet organic
matter per cubic metre of volume. Retention time varies with temperature: 20 to
30 days in warm tropics, 40 to 60 days in cooler climates. The [[mollison-designers-passive-solar-heating-building-design]] principle can maintain digester temperature by placing the unit in
a greenhouse or insulating it with compost.

## Integration with Permaculture Systems

The biogas digester is most effective when integrated with other permaculture
elements. The ideal placement is near the mollison designers animal systems that provide manure,
near the kitchen where gas is used, and near the garden where effluent is
applied. This Zone 1 or inner Zone 2 placement ensures efficient material flows
and easy management.

The effluent from the digester is a valuable resource. It contains most of the
nutrients from the feedstock in a form that is readily available to plants.
Digester effluent can be applied directly to garden beds, used to fertilise
[[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] as a plankton stimulant, or composted further before use. The
nutrient content of effluent is roughly equivalent to a balanced organic
fertiliser with good levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

## Temperature Management

Methanogenic bacteria are most active at thermophilic temperatures (50 to 60
degrees Celsius) but can operate at mesophilic temperatures (25 to 40 degrees
Celsius) with lower gas production rates. Below 15 degrees Celsius, gas
production essentially stops. In cool climates, the digester must be insulated
or heated to maintain productive temperatures.

Strategies for maintaining digester temperature include: placing the digester
in a greenhouse or polytunnel, wrapping it in insulation or a compost pile,
using solar water heating to warm the feedstock, and housing the digester in a
[[mollison-designers-straw-bale-building-techniques]] or earth-bermed structure. The [[mollison-designers-home-energy-conservation-and-solar-heating]] system can supply
excess heat to the digester during winter months.

## Safety Considerations
Biogas contains hydrogen sulphide, which is toxic and corrosive. Digesters
should be outdoors or well-ventilated. Gas pipes need regular leak checks with
soap solution. The digester must never be entered due to oxygen-deficient,
potentially lethal atmosphere. Effluent from human or animal waste feedstock
may contain pathogens; retaining it for 30 days or composting it before use
kills most pathogens.

## Maintenance and Troubleshooting
A healthy digester produces gas steadily at pH 6.8 to 7.5. Low gas production
usually results from low temperature, insufficient feedstock, or excess carbon.
Sour digesters (low pH) from nitrogen overload can be restored with carbon-rich
material or lime. Scum from fibrous feedstock blocks gas release and can be
prevented by stirring. Fixed dome digesters may develop cracks requiring cement
sealant repair.

## Community-Scale Systems
For [[mollison-designers-community-garden-design-urban-permaculture]], larger digesters serving multiple
households can be more efficient than individual units. Community digesters
benefit from economies of scale and can process waste from shared livestock,
market facilities, or public toilets. Gas can be piped to multiple households
or used to power a community generator.
