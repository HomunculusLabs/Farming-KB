## Core Concept

Fungi play central roles [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]]. Their mycelial networks provide the physical
and biochemical interface between organic and inorganic components of soil.

## Carbon Cycling and Organic Matter Decomposition

Fungi are the dominant decomposers of recalcitrant organic materials —
lignin, cellulose, and chitin — that bacteria cannot efficiently break
down. This decomposition releases carbon dioxide (returning carbon to the
atmosphere) and converts organic carbon into fungal biomass, exoenzymes,
and metabolites that become part of the soil organic matter pool.

### White-Rot and Brown-Rot Strategies
Basidiomycetes employ two distinct wood decay strategies:
- **White-rot fungi**: Produce lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases,
  and laccases that mineralize lignin completely, leaving cellulose exposed.
  This is the only biological process that fully degrades lignin.
- **Brown-rot fungi**: Use Fenton chemistry ([[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] extend hyphae far beyond the root zone,
accessing phosphate from soil volumes that roots cannot reach. Ectomycorrhizal
fungi produce oxalate and other acids that weather phosphorus-containing
minerals at the hyphal tip. The fungal partner absorbs phosphate and
transfers it to the host plant in exchange for photosynthate carbon.

## Metal Transformations

Fungi interact with metals through several mechanisms with significant
biogeochemical implications:

### Metal Mobilization (Solubilization)
Organic acid secretion dissolves metal-bearing minerals, releasing metal
ions into solution. This is important for:
- **Nutrient acquisition**: Solubilizing iron, manganese, zinc, and copper
  from [[mineral-sources-for-soils]] fungal and plant nutrition
- **Toxic metal tolerance**: Some fungi can mobilize and then sequester or
  export toxic metals like aluminum and lead

### Metal Immobilization (Biosorption and Precipitation)
Fungal cell walls, rich in chitin, glucans, and proteins with carboxyl,
phosphate, and amine functional groups, bind metal ions from solution.
Additional mechanisms include:
- **Intracellular sequestration**: Metal-binding proteins (metallothioneins,
  phytochelatins) compartmentalize metals in vacuoles
- **Extracellular precipitation**: [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]], acidify
the immediate microenvironment around hyphae. This acidification enhances
mineral weathering rates, releasing nutrients from silicate minerals. The
"rock-eating fungi" hypothesis suggests that some fungi actively weather
minerals as a nutrient acquisition strategy, particularly in nutrient-poor
soils where mineral dissolution is a primary source of potassium, calcium,
magnesium, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]].

## Ecosystem-Level Significance

The cumulative effect of these mineral transformations is profound:
- Fungi drive the decomposition of 50-70% of net primary production carbon
- Mycorrhizal networks facilitate 70-90% of plant phosphorus uptake
- Fungal weathering rates can exceed abiotic weathering by orders of
  magnitude at the microsite scale
- Soil carbon pools stabilized by fungal residues persist for decades to
  centuries

Understanding fungal biogeochemistry is essential for predicting ecosystem
responses to environmental change, managing soil fertility, and developing
[[bioremediation-strategies]] for contaminated sites.
