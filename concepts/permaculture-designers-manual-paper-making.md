# Paper Making
# Permaculture Designers Manual - Local Paper Production from Plant Fibers

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Paper making in the permaculture context relates to local self-reliance, forest product utilization, and the integration of craft industries into [[permaculture-designers-manual-crafts-and-income]]. The Designers Manual references paper production indirectly through discussions of plant fibers, forest management products, and appropriate technology for self-reliant communities. While the manual does not provide detailed paper-making instructions, it establishes the framework for integrating paper production into permaculture design.

## Fiber Sources for Paper

### Forest Products

The small forest farm model (12-16 hectares) yields a wide range of products including:
- Poles and coppice - potential paper fiber source
- Bark for craft and dyes - inner bark fibers for specialty paper
- Mulches - plant material that could also be pulped

Pulp timber is specifically listed among the products of forest management systems, indicating that paper production is a recognized yield from managed woodlands.

### Bamboo

Bamboo is one of the most important fiber sources for paper production, especially in tropical and subtropical regions. The manual references bamboo extensively as:
- A clump-forming grass suitable for windbreaks, hedges, and garden borders
- A construction material for housing, furniture, and tools
- A source of edible shoots
- A mulch producer
- A craft material

Bamboo pulp produces strong, durable paper and has been used commercially in India, China, and Southeast Asia for paper production.

### Willow

The manual describes willow coppice systems that yield multiple products including:
- Basketry materials
- Medicinals
- Forage
- "Stickwood" for radiant heaters
- Active charcoal filters
- Artist's charcoal

Willow bast fibers (inner bark) can also be used for paper production, producing a high-quality, strong paper.

### Other Fiber Plants

The manual references various plants suitable for fiber production:
- Cotton - referenced in mixed crop systems
- Flax and hemp - traditional paper and textile fibers
- Kenaf and jute - fast-growing annual fibers
- Banana fiber - from pseudostems after fruit harvest
- Sugarcane bagasse - residue after juice extraction
- Rice straw and wheat straw - agricultural residues

## Integration with Permaculture Design

### Fiber Crop Zones

Paper fiber crops can be integrated into the permaculture landscape:
- **Zone 1**: Small-scale hand paper-making from garden weeds and crop residues
- **Zone 2**: Dedicated fiber crops (flax, kenaf) alongside food crops
- **Zone 3**: Bamboo clumps, coppice willow for fiber
- **Zone 4**: Forest management for pulp timber

### Waste-to-Resource

Paper making can utilize materials that would otherwise be waste:
- Crop residues (straw, stalks, bagasse)
- Garden prunings
- Fallen leaves
- Weed harvests
- [[spent-mushroom-substrate]]

## Paper Mulch and Garden Applications

The manual's emphasis on [[permaculture-designers-manual-mulch-systems]] connects to paper in practical ways:

### Paper as Mulch

Waste paper (newspaper, cardboard) is extensively used in permaculture gardens as:
- Sheet mulch (layered under other organic materials)
- Weed suppression barrier
- Moisture retention layer
- Worm food in vermicompost systems

The manual specifically references covering garden areas with wet paper or cardboard, banana leaves, or any mulch material when establishing banana/papaya circles.

### Cardboard in Garden Construction

Cardboard is used as:
- Weed barrier under mulch in new garden beds
- Temporary erosion control on slopes
- Base layer in sheet mulching (lasagna gardening)
- Decomposable weed mat in pathways

## Polynesian Wax Paper

The manual references "Polynesian wax paper" and wrapper for baked foods. This suggests traditional Pacific paper-like materials made from plant fibers treated with natural waxes. Such products demonstrate the integration of paper making with other craft traditions ([[permaculture-designers-manual-charcoal-production]], food preservation).

## Craft Paper Products

Hand-made paper from local fibers can produce:
- Writing and drawing paper
- Greeting cards and stationery
- Seedling pots (biodegradable)
- Gift wrap and decorative papers
- Book covers and binding materials
- Packaging materials

## Environmental Considerations

### Energy Requirements

Industrial paper production is energy-intensive and often polluting. Local, small-scale hand paper making:
- Uses minimal energy (human-powered beating and pressing)
- Avoids chemical bleaching (natural colors)
- Uses local, renewable fiber sources
- Creates zero toxic waste
- Produces a premium, hand-crafted product

### Chemical Use

Commercial paper production uses chlorine compounds for bleaching, creating dioxins and other pollutants. Permaculture paper making can:
- Use unbleached fibers (natural colors)
- Employ hydrogen peroxide for whitening where needed
- Use natural mordants and [[permaculture-designers-manual-natural-dyes]] for colored papers
- Avoid synthetic sizing agents

### Water Use

Hand paper making recycles water within the process:
- Vat water can be reused for multiple batches
- Settling tanks remove fiber for re-pulping
- Greywater from paper making can be used for irrigation
- Closed-loop systems minimize water consumption
