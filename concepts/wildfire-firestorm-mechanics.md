# Wildfire and Firestorm Mechanics

[[wildfire]] is one of the most powerful and destructive natural forces affecting landscapes, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. Understanding the mechanics of wildfire spread and [[firestorm]] development is essential for [[permaculture]] designers working in fire-prone areas.

## The Four Critical Factors for Firestorms

Firestorms develop when four critical factors converge simultaneously:

### 1. Fuel Supply
Fuel supply encompasses the dryness, distribution, and quantity of available combustible material. Loose fuels with a diameter greater than 6 cm are particularly significant. The moisture content of fuels is the key determinant — when dry fuels reach less than 35% moisture content, fire danger escalates dramatically. In pine forests, the threshold is even lower (less than 30% humidity) due to high resin and oil content.

A practical field test involves weighing a chunk of pine wood (75 × 50 mm, unpainted) — when fully saturated, it represents 100% moisture content, allowing assessment of local humidity and wood dryness conditions.

### 2. Oxygen Supply
Winds that fan the flames, especially hot winds (such as desert winds or föhn winds), provide the oxygen supply that accelerates combustion. Wind speed directly affects fire spread rate — at 20 km/h the spread rate follows a quadratic relationship, meaning a small increase in wind speed produces a disproportionately large increase in fire spread.

### 3. Preheating
Preheating occurs through several mechanisms:
- **Upslope effects**: Flames traveling uphill create enhanced preheating of fuel ahead of the fire front
- **Radiant heat**: Heat radiating from the fire front dries and preheats vegetation before flames arrive
- **Advected desert winds**: Hot, dry winds blown into unprotected forests create dangerous preheating conditions

For every 10° increase in upslope angle, fire speed and intensity approximately double. At 0° slope a fire might travel at 16 km/h, but at 30° it can reach 128 km/h — making hill ridge settlements extremely vulnerable.

### 4. Unstable Air Masses
Unstable atmospheric conditions are indicated by:
- Wind shear and shifting winds
- Ground whirlwinds (dust devils)
- Scattered cumulus clouds
- Smoke that ascends to great heights rather than leveling out at low altitude
- Clear air (absence of fog or smog before the fire)
- Volatile oil, terpene, or resin odors detectable in forests
- Light-blue haze developing over resinous forests

## Fire Initiation Sources

While lightning strikes do cause fires in remote forests — creating pyrophilous (fire-dependent) species on ridges — lightning and accidents account for only about 4% of total wildfires. The vast majority are deliberately lit or arise from:

- Previous "controlled" burns left smouldering
- Electrical shorts from power lines
- Backfire flashes from vehicles in grass
- Heat focused by bottles or curved glass
- Welding, campfire, and cigarette accidents
- Arson by pyromaniacs (some of whom even join volunteer firefighting organizations)
- Intentional burning by angry individuals

## Factors Accelerating Fire Spread

Once initiated, wildfire spreads rapidly. Grass fires typically begin spreading after 10-11 a.m. (after dew dries), while forest fires spread most aggressively from midday to 3 p.m. An initial flare-up of about one hour can develop into full firestorm conditions when aided by:

- **Loose fuels** smaller than 6 cm diameter at less than 20% water content
- **Winds** of 10-50 km/h, with spread rate increasing as the square of velocity
- **Backing winds** (shifting direction late in the day), which can blow fire flanks into broad fronts or back on themselves unpredictably

At wind speeds above 80 km/h, ground fires may self-extinguish, but at moderate speeds tongues of fire break up the firefront creating extremely dangerous conditions.

## Fire Tornadoes and Extreme Phenomena

No firebreak — not even 10 km of water — is effective in firestorms. Fire tornadoes can develop on the lee (downwind) ridge side of hills with:
- **Ascent velocities** up to 250 km/h
- **Ground winds** near the base reaching 100 km/h
- The ability to lift large logs, branches, and pieces of houses aloft
- Massive aerial gaseous explosions

These tornadoes carry incandescent material and drop it 1-30 km downwind, starting fresh fires and triggering new firestorm sequences.

## Fire Frequency in Different Vegetation Types

Wildfire occurs on regular schedules depending on vegetation:
- **Wet sclerophyll forest**: approximately every 30 years
- **Dry savannah**: every 8-10 years
- **Unbrowsed grassland**: annually

This creates a specific design challenge when progressing from grassland to forest operations — during the 3-5 year establishment period, fire risk is extremely high, requiring programmed planting mosaics to reduce district-wide risk.

## Nutrient Losses from Fire

Fire causes significant soil nutrient depletion:

| Nutrient | Loss Range | Recovery Time |
|----------|-----------|---------------|
| Nitrogen | 54-75% (109 kg/ha) | ~11 years (via legumes, rain) |
| Phosphorus | 37-50% (3.0 kg/ha) | 20+ years (via rain) |
| Potassium | 43-66% | Very slow |
| Calcium | 31-34% | Very slow |
| Magnesium | 25-43% | Very slow |
| Boron | 35-54% | Very slow |

Fires glow at approximately 650°C and burn fiercely at 1,100-1,400°C under strong winds. At these temperatures, sulphur and nitrogen are volatilized, along with carbon. Phosphorus volatilizes at 774°C and calcium at 1,484°C (at which temperature even cement structures powder). [[humus]] losses of 10-12 cm occur in forest soils, clays lose structure, and mud flows can result. Fires never improve soil status — recovery depends entirely on trace elements brought in by rain or birds, and minerals recycled to topsoil by deep-rooted vegetation.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

The inevitability of wildfire in many biomes means designers must plan for fire survival rather than fire exclusion. Key strategies include:

- Designing fuel-reduction mosaics across the landscape
- Creating fire-resistant settlement zones
- Understanding fire behavior to inform house siting and building design
- Implementing the Ida and Jean Pain system of chipping dry forest fuels for compost and biogas
- Developing fire refuges for people, livestock, and wildlife
- Programming planting sequences that progressively reduce fire risk during the transition from grassland to forest
