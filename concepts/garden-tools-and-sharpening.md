# Garden Tools and Sharpening

[[steve-solomon]] argues that most home vegetable gardens (up to a quarter acre) need only
**three essential hand tools**: a spade, a bow rake, and a hoe — plus a file to keep
them sharp. Tool quality and sharpness matter far more than quantity. A properly
sharpened set of basic tools transforms gardening from exhausting to manageable.

Store-bought tools are **never sharp** when new. Factory edges are coarse ground bevels
that barely qualify as working edges. The difference between a factory edge and a
properly filed edge is the difference between pushing soil and slicing through it.

## The Essential Three Tools

### Spade

The primary ground-breaking tool with a flat, relatively narrow blade. A sharp spade
slices through earth; a dull one bounces off compacted ground and exhausts the
gardener. For new beds, [[double-digging]], or turning under [[cover-crops]], it is
irreplaceable. Its flat blade geometry concentrates force along a clean line, which
is why it penetrates soil more effectively than a curved shovel blade.

### Bow Rake

A rigid head with short, sturdy tines mounted on a curved steel frame — the "bow."
Used for breaking clods, leveling soil, spreading amendments, and creating a fine
seedbed. Unlike a leaf rake, the bow rake is built for heavy contact with earth and
requires sharp tines to work effectively.

### Hoe

The essential weeding and cultivation tool. Its blade should be sharp enough to slice
through weed roots below the soil surface with a single stroke. A dull hoe tears
weeds rather than cutting them, leaving root fragments that regrow.

## Shovel vs. Spade vs. Combination Shovel

Solomon distinguishes three main digging tools:

- **Spade**: Flat, narrow blade. Best for breaking ground, edging, and precise
  digging. Its flat geometry concentrates cutting force for clean vertical cuts in
  undisturbed soil.

- **[[combination-shovel]]**: A hybrid of spade and shovel with a flat, narrow blade.
  Bridges the gap between the two but does neither job as well as a dedicated tool.
  Useful as a general-purpose compromise.

- **Shovel**: Broader, more curved blade designed for scooping and moving loose
  material (soil, [[compost]], gravel). Less effective for breaking undisturbed ground
  because the curved blade spreads force rather than concentrating it.

The key insight: a **spade is the gardener's primary ground-breaking tool**, not a
shovel. Reserve shovels for moving already-loosened material.

## Handle Length Considerations

- **Long-handled tools** are appropriate for adults. They provide the leverage needed
  to work soil efficiently and reduce the need to bend over, minimizing back strain.
- **Short-handled tools** are suited for children. Adults should avoid them — they
  force an uncomfortable bent posture that causes back strain and reduces effective
  force.

Solomon specifically criticizes **short-handled spading forks**, calling them
"instruments of torture." They offer poor leverage, force the user into an awkward
stooped position, and are inferior to long-handled alternatives for serious work.

## Tool Steel Quality

The grade of steel in your tools directly affects edge retention and longevity:

- **Harder steel** stays sharp longer but is more brittle and can chip or crack under
  heavy impact (e.g., striking buried rocks or frozen ground).
- **Softer steel** is more durable under impact but loses its edge quickly, requiring
  frequent re-sharpening.
- **Intermediate-grade steel** is the ideal compromise. It holds an edge well enough
  for practical use while being tough enough to survive contact with stones, roots,
  and other subterranean obstacles without chipping.

Garden tools occupy a middle ground between axes (very hard steel) and pry bars (soft,
tough steel). Intermediate-grade steel strikes the right balance for mixed soil
conditions. Cheap tools use low-grade steel that dulls almost immediately; a quality
tool pays for itself in reduced effort and decades of service.

## Sharpening Technique

Sharpening is essential — not optional. Before any tool sees soil for the first time,
it must be properly sharpened with a file.

### Required Equipment

A **single-cut mill bastard file** (roughly 10-inch) is the only tool needed. No
grinding wheels, no whetstones — just a good file. The single-cut pattern removes
material cleanly without clogging.

### Step-by-Step Sharpening Procedure

1. **Secure the tool** in a vise or clamp so the blade edge is accessible and stable.
   Any wobble will produce an uneven edge.

2. **Set the file angle at approximately 15°** from the flat face of the blade. This
   creates a chisel-like bevel that cuts soil and roots effectively without being so
   acute that it chips easily.

3. **Stroke from the center of the blade outward toward the edge**, applying firm,
   even pressure. Do not sharpen from the edge inward — this damages the cutting edge.
   Lift the file on the return stroke to avoid dulling its teeth.

4. **Maintain a consistent angle throughout every stroke.** Varying the angle produces
   a rounded "rolled edge" that won't cut cleanly. Keep wrists steady and let the
   file do the work.

5. **Create a clean chisel-like bevel** on one face of the blade. You should feel a
   slight "wire" (burr) forming on the back side — this is normal and indicates the
   bevel is properly formed.

6. **Remove the wire (burr) from the back side** by laying the file nearly flat
   against the back face — at roughly **5°** — and taking one or two light strokes.
   This removes the burr without altering the flat back of the tool.

7. **Repeat as needed** until the edge slices cleanly through paper or cardboard.

### Sharpening Frequency

Sharpen **before each major use** and touch up when performance declines. A file takes
only minutes per tool. Regular sharpening extends tool life by reducing the force
required for each cut — less force means less stress on blade and handle.

## Evaluating Tool Quality

When shopping for garden tools, consider:

- **Blade steel**: Look for medium-carbon or alloy steel. Heavier blades with visible
  forge marks generally indicate better steel than smooth, lightweight ones.
- **Handle attachment**: Forged sockets or solid tang construction outlast riveted or
  welded joints. Any play between blade and handle signals poor construction.
- **Handle material**: Solid wood (ash or hickory) or fiberglass. Hickory is the
  traditional standard for its shock resistance.
- **Overall weight**: Quality tools feel substantial but not excessively heavy. Heft
  is a reasonable proxy for material quality.
