## The Numinous Experience and Psychedelic Awe

Simon G. Powell explores the numinous dimension of the psilocybin
experience, arguing that the mushroom reliably elicits states of awe,
wonder, and sacred apprehension that have been the hallmark of religious and
[[shamanic-ecstasy-and-healing]]
"seizing his soul." The mushroom was referred to with awe and reverence by
indigenous peoples, not treated as a common drug but as a sacred mediator
between worlds.

## The Good Friday Experiment

The [[psilocybin-mystical-experiences-personality-change]]. Long-term

## Awe and the Altered Perception of Reality

The numinous quality of psilocybin extends beyond closed-eye visions into
the altered perception of external reality. With eyes open, the most mundane
objects suddenly acquire a "holy aura." The outer world reveals itself as
the living structure of some divine being. This quality of experienced
sacredness is what distinguishes genuine numinous experience from mere
sensory distortion or hallucination. The experience carries epistemological
value, eliciting a special kind of knowledge not ordinarily available but of
immeasurable value in terms of spirit and soul.

## The Sense of Timelessness and Infinity

A recurring feature of the numinous psilocybin experience is the sense of
timelessness and infinity. The Reverend S.J. described feeling "drawn out
into infinity" and "caught up in the vastness of creation," experiencing
"that classic kind of blending" accompanied by an overwhelming sense of
timelessness. These reports echo descriptions of mystical experience found
across religious traditions, from Sufi poetry to Buddhist sutras to
Christian contemplative literature, suggesting that psilocybin taps into
universal dimensions of human consciousness.

## The Birth of Religious Awe

Powell traces the numinous experience induced by psilocybin back to the
very origins of religious consciousness. He suggests that our ancestors'
primeval encounters with entheogenic species were the original source of
religious awe and wonder. The medieval witches who used psychoactive
fungi to induce numinous states of awareness, the Aztec priests who
referred to mushrooms as teonanacatl or "flesh of the gods," and the Greek
initiates at Eleusis all participated in the same fundamental encounter
with sacred mystery mediated by natural [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]] for
interpreting such experiences has shifted dramatically. The experience of
psilocybin "carries epistemological value as it seems to elicit a special
kind of knowledge not ordinarily available." Even the most dogged sceptic
must concede that psilocybin taps deep realms of the unconscious or
imagination that reveal a hitherto unknown creative potential. Powell
emphasises that the numinous cannot be conveniently disposed of with a
diagnostic label.
