# The Numinous Experience and Psychedelic Awe

Simon G. Powell explores the numinous dimension of the psilocybin
experience, arguing that the mushroom reliably elicits states of awe,
wonder, and sacred apprehension that have been the hallmark of religious and
mystical experience throughout human history. The numinous quality of
psilocybin visions represents not a side effect but the core phenomenon that
demands explanation and demands to be taken seriously by any comprehensive
theory of consciousness.

## Defining the Numinous

The numinous, a term derived from the Latin word for "spirit" or "divine
presence," refers to the experience of encountering something wholly other,
something that inspires a mixture of awe, fascination, and trembling. Powell
documents how this quality pervades psilocybin experiences across cultures
and eras. Wasson's descriptions of his first mushroom experience in Mexico
convey a profound sense of awe, with psilocybin-induced shamanic ecstasy
"seizing his soul." The mushroom was referred to with awe and reverence by
indigenous peoples, not treated as a common drug but as a sacred mediator
between worlds.

## The Good Friday Experiment

The Marsh Chapel Good Friday experiment of 1962 provides one of the most
compelling demonstrations of psilocybin's capacity to induce genuine
numinous experience. In this double-blind study, theology students received
either psilocybin or a placebo in a religious setting. The majority of those
who received psilocybin reported profound mystical experiences. Long-term
follow-up by Rick Doblin found that all psilocybin subjects still considered
their experiences to have contained genuinely mystical elements and that
psilocybin had made a uniquely valuable contribution to their spiritual
wellbeing. As Powell notes, "psilocybin doth work, and it doth work well."

## Awe and the Altered Perception of Reality

The numinous quality of psilocybin extends beyond closed-eye visions into
the altered perception of external reality. With eyes open, the most mundane
objects suddenly acquire a "holy aura." The outer world reveals itself as
the living structure of some divine being. This quality of experienced
sacredness is what distinguishes genuine numinous experience from mere
sensory distortion or hallucination. The experience carries epistemological
value, eliciting a special kind of knowledge not ordinarily available but of
immeasurable value in terms of spirit and soul.

## The Sense of Timelessness and Infinity

A recurring feature of the numinous psilocybin experience is the sense of
timelessness and infinity. The Reverend S.J. described feeling "drawn out
into infinity" and "caught up in the vastness of creation," experiencing
"that classic kind of blending" accompanied by an overwhelming sense of
timelessness. These reports echo descriptions of mystical experience found
across religious traditions, from Sufi poetry to Buddhist sutras to
Christian contemplative literature, suggesting that psilocybin taps into
universal dimensions of human consciousness.

## The Birth of Religious Awe

Powell traces the numinous experience induced by psilocybin back to the
very origins of religious consciousness. He suggests that our ancestors'
primeval encounters with entheogenic species were the original source of
religious awe and wonder. The medieval witches who used psychoactive
fungi to induce numinous states of awareness, the Aztec priests who
referred to mushrooms as teonanacatl or "flesh of the gods," and the Greek
initiates at Eleusis all participated in the same fundamental encounter
with sacred mystery mediated by natural psychoactive substances.

## Confronting the Numinous in Modern Context

Powell argues that the numinous power of psilocybin remains fully
accessible to modern individuals, though the cultural context for
interpreting such experiences has shifted dramatically. The experience of
psilocybin "carries epistemological value as it seems to elicit a special
kind of knowledge not ordinarily available." Even the most dogged sceptic
must concede that psilocybin taps deep realms of the unconscious or
imagination that reveal a hitherto unknown creative potential. Powell
emphasises that the numinous cannot be conveniently disposed of with a
diagnostic label.

## The Double Nature of the Numinous

Powell acknowledges that the numinous experience has a double nature: it
can be "frightening or wonderfully illuminating." The potential for a "bad
trip" reflects the overwhelming power of the numinous when encountered
without adequate preparation. Native cultures developed ritual frameworks to
manage this double quality, providing safe containers for the experience.
The modern user, lacking such cultural infrastructure, must exercise
caution and self-knowledge. Nothing remains hidden to the mushroom, which
forces one to confront bad habits and neuroses, making self-knowledge a
prerequisite for beneficial use.

## The Numinous as Evidence

For Powell, the reliable elicitation of numinous experience by psilocybin
constitutes evidence that demands to be incorporated into any serious theory
of consciousness and reality. The numinous is not merely subjective
emotion but appears to involve genuine access to expanded informational
states. The experience suggests that human consciousness has capacities far
beyond those normally accessed, and that something intelligent and intentional communicates through these expanded states. The
numinous quality of the psilocybin experience is precisely what sets it apart
from ordinary pharmacological effects and places it in the realm of the
epistemologically significant.

## The Koestler Challenge: Are Psychedelic Mysticism Authentic?

Powell addresses the philosophical objection raised by Arthur Koestler,
who dismissed psilocybin as producing mere "pressure-cooker mysticism."
Koestler argued that the view obtained after laborious spiritual discipline
— slogging up the mountain on foot — is superior to the view obtained via
an effortless cable-car ride. Powell responds that this objection ignores
psilocybin's status as a natural product of the environment, not an alien
synthetic. Had Koestler picked the mushrooms himself and appreciated their
earthly origin, his experiences might have been more rewarding. Moreover,
the Good Friday experiment's long-term follow-up confirmed that all
psilocybin subjects still considered their experiences genuinely mystical
decades later.

## The Self-Revealing Quality of the Numinous

Powell notes that the long-term follow-up of the Good Friday experiment
also revealed psychological struggles in eight of the ten psilocybin
subjects. These struggles are somewhat inevitable: one sees oneself clearly
without superficial trappings, and the mushroom forces confrontation with
bad habits and neuroses. Nothing remains hidden to the mushroom, which
highlights the timeless truism that one must come to terms with oneself
before inward spiritual development can proceed. This self-revealing
quality is itself part of the numinous power of psilocybin.
