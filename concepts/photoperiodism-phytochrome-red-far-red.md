# Photoperiodism and Phytochrome Red-Far-Red Switch

## Overview

Photoperiodism is the physiological response of plants to the duration of light and dark periods in a 24-hour cycle. First discovered in 1918 through studies of the [[phytochrome-photoreceptor]] system — a biological light-activated switch that uses red and far-red light to measure the length of uninterrupted darkness.

## Discovery of Photoperiodism

The story begins with a tobacco strain called [[chamovitz-maryland-mammoth-photoperiodism-discovery]] that appeared in southern Maryland farms around 1906. Unlike normal tobacco, which grew to a modest height, flowered, and set seed by late summer, this strain grew to 4.5 metres with nearly a hundred leaves and refused to flower until frost forced it to stop. While impressive in stature, its reluctance to flower made it useless for seed production.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture set out to solve the mystery. Their elegant experiment involved two groups of Maryland Mammoth plants:

- **Group 1**: Left outside in natural conditions (long summer days, no flowering)
- **Group 2**: Moved to a dark shed each afternoon, artificially shortening the day

Group 2 flowered normally, proving that the plant was measuring day length to determine when to flower. They named this phenomenon **photoperiodism**.

## Plants Measure Night, Not Day

A crucial follow-up discovery came around World War II when scientists found they could manipulate flowering by briefly interrupting the dark period:

- A short-day plant (like soybean or chrysanthemum) kept in short days could be **prevented from flowering** by a few minutes of light in the middle of the night
- A long-day plant (like iris or barley) kept in short days could be **forced to flower** by the same brief [[chrysanthemum-night-interruption-flowering-commercial-horticulture]]

This proved that plants do not measure the length of the day — they measure the length of the **continuous dark period**. If the dark period is interrupted, the plant's [[chamovitz-circadian-rhythm-internal-clock-plants]] resets and it "forgets" how long it has been in darkness.

## The Red-Far-Red Switch

Scientists then asked: what color of light do plants use to measure the dark period? The answer was surprising — plants responded only to red light flashes. Blue or green light had no effect on [[what-a-plant-knows-photoperiodism-flowering-time-measurement]], but even a few seconds of red light in the middle of the night was enough to disrupt photoperiodic measurement.

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made an even more remarkable discovery: **far-red light could cancel the effect of red light**. Far-red light has wavelengths slightly longer than bright red and is barely visible at dusk. The experimental sequence:

1. Shine red light on irises during the night → they flower (as if it were a short night)
2. Shine far-red light immediately after the red → they don't flower (as if the red never happened)
3. Shine red light after the far-red → they flower again
4. This on/off cycling could be repeated indefinitely

A few seconds of either color was sufficient. The system acts like a **light-activated toggle switch** where the plant "remembers" the last color of light it saw.

## Phytochrome: The Molecular Switch

By 1960, Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single photoreceptor protein called **phytochrome** (Greek: phyton = plant, chroma = color) mediates both the red and far-red responses. Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr** (P660): The inactive form that absorbs red light (~660 nm wavelength)
- **Pfr** (P730): The active form that absorbs far-red light (~730 nm wavelength)

When Pr absorbs red light, it converts to Pfr. When Pfr absorbs far-red light, it converts back to Pr. In nature, sunlight contains both red and far-red in roughly balanced proportions, so during the day phytochrome exists as a mixture. But at sunset, the atmosphere scatters blue light and enriches the remaining light in far-red — this naturally converts most phytochrome to the Pr (inactive) form, signaling to the plant that darkness is approaching.

## Ecological Logic

The phytochrome system makes elegant ecological sense:

- **Sunlight** is rich in both red and far-red, maintaining a mixed phytochrome state during the day
- **Sunset** enriches far-red light, converting phytochrome to the inactive Pr form
- **During the night**, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through a process called **dark reversion**; the rate of this reversion serves as the plant's internal hourglass for measuring night length
- **Dawn** delivers red light, rapidly converting Pr to Pfr and "resetting" the clock

If the night is long enough, Pfr levels drop below a critical threshold, triggering flowering in short-day plants. If Pfr remains above threshold (short nights), long-day plants flower instead.

## Phytochrome in Leaves, Not Stem Tips

Surprisingly, the photoreceptor for photoperiodism is not located in the shoot tip (unlike phototropism). Experiments showed that:

- Illuminating any **single leaf** with red light in the middle of the night is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire plant
- A plant with all leaves removed but the stem and apex intact is completely "blind" to light cues for flowering, even when the entire plant is illuminated

This means phytochrome in leaves receives the light cue and initiates a **mobile signal** — later identified as the protein **Flowering Locus T (FT)** — that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex and triggers the floral transition. The leaves are the "eyes" for photoperiodism.

## Phytochrome Gene Family

Modern molecular biology has revealed that phytochrome is not a single protein but a **family of photoreceptors**. In Arabidopsis, five phytochrome genes (PHYA through PHYE) have distinct roles:

- **phyA**: Mediates responses to continuous far-red light; important for seedling de-etiolation under canopy shade
- **phyB**: The primary mediator of red/far-red reversible responses including photoperiodic flowering; the "classic" phytochrome
- **phyC-phyE**: Play modulatory roles in [[phototropism-and-shade-avoidance]], seed germination, and other [[phototropism-and-plant-light-responses]]
