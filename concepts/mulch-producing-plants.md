# Mulch-Producing Plants

Mulch-producing plants are species deliberately grown to generate abundant biomass that can be cut and used as surface mulch around other plants. They eliminate the need to import mulch materials, build soil fertility on-site, and form a critical component of [[permaculture-plant-guilds]] and [[soil-building-permaculture]] systems.

## The Role of Mulch Plants

Growing mulch under trees and between crops eliminates the need to trudge around with a compost-filled wheelbarrow, since the guild or garden will build its own soil. Mulch makers are soft-leafed plants that can be slashed and left to compost in place, creating a self-fertilising system.

As the nutrient-rich greenery rots, it delivers a large dose of minerals and organic matter to the soil. The resulting thick layer of compost is home to a thriving and diverse population of worms, fungi, bacteria, and other helpful denizens of the soil. This living soil suppresses diseases because the churning soil life competes fiercely for food and habitat below the ground.

With all the resources divided evenly between the soil's many inhabitants, no one microbial species can get out of balance and become a pest. Buildups of harmful fungi are less likely when there is too much competition and too many predators for a single-species population explosion. This biological disease suppression is a key benefit of on-site mulch production, connecting to [[permaculture-dynamic-accumulator-plants]] functions.

## Comfrey (Symphytum officinale)

Comfrey is the champion mulch-producing plant and the winner in the multifunctional sweepstakes. It yields mulch and medicine, attracts insects to its flowers, pulls potassium and other minerals from deep in the subsoil into its leaves (making it a [[permaculture-dynamic-accumulator-plants]]), and can be made into a tea for drinking or for fertilising the garden.

A ring of comfrey around a fruit tree can be hacked down four or five times a summer. Each cutting produces a thick layer of nutrient-rich biomass. Comfrey's deep taproot (extending up to 3 meters) mines minerals from deep soil layers that other plants cannot reach, bringing them to the surface in its leaves.

Comfrey is also used in greywater wetland systems as an additional wetland plant with mulch and insectary functions. See [[comfrey-plant-guide]] for detailed cultivation information.

## Artichokes and Cardoon

Globe artichokes (Cynara scolymus) and cardoon (Cynara cardunculus) are excellent mulch producers in guild systems. They produce large, soft leaves that break down quickly when cut. As perennials, they provide mulch year after year without replanting.

Artichokes also produce edible flower buds as a food crop, making them dual-purpose plants. Cardoon is grown primarily for its leaf stalks (eaten as a vegetable) and for mulch production. Both plants are striking architectural elements in the garden, connecting to [[permaculture-multi-purpose-plants]] design.

## Rhubarb

Rhubarb (Rheum rhabarbarum) produces large, fleshy leaves that make excellent mulch material. The leaves contain oxalic acid and should not be fed to livestock, but they break down readily in garden mulch applications.

Rhubarb is also a food crop (the stems are used in cooking and preserving), a [[permaculture-food-storage-and-preservation]] ingredient, and an attractive ornamental plant. Its perennial nature means it produces mulch year after year from a single planting.

## Clovers

Clovers (Trifolium spp.) serve multiple functions as living mulch. As nitrogen-fixing legumes, they add fertility to the soil while providing a dense ground cover that suppresses weeds. White clover (Trifolium repens) and red clover (Trifolium pratense) are the most commonly used species.

Dutch or New Zealand white clover makes an excellent permanent living mulch beneath fruit trees. It tolerates some foot traffic and mowing, and its flowers attract bees and other pollinators. See [[white-clover-living-mulch]] for detailed information on this species.

## Nasturtiums

Nasturtiums (Tropaeolum spp.) provide both mulch and pest-repellent functions in guild systems. Their large, soft leaves break down readily when slashed, and they may exude substances that repel certain pests. They also produce edible flowers and leaves with a peppery flavour.

Nasturtiums are particularly useful in [[permaculture-plant-guilds]] because they fill multiple roles simultaneously: mulch production, pest deterrence, food production, and ornamental beauty.

## Leguminous Mulch Plants

Many nitrogen-fixing legumes produce abundant biomass suitable for mulch. Alfalfa (Medicago sativa) is one of the best, producing deep-rooted biomass rich in nitrogen and minerals. It can be cut multiple times per season.

Cowpeas (Vigna unguiculata) and fava beans (Vicia faba) provide both mulch and food. In late summer, fava seeds can be poked into the soil beneath fruit trees. The following spring, pods are harvested and stalks mulched in place. Vetch (Vicia spp.) reseeds freely and can be slashed for mulch throughout the growing season.

## Tree Lupin as a Mulch Producer

Tree lupin (Lupinus arboreus) can be cut hard to supply mulch rich in nitrogen. As a nitrogen-fixing pioneer species, it builds soil fertility while producing abundant biomass. Its fast growth makes it useful for quickly establishing mulch supplies on new sites, connecting to [[pioneer-species-permaculture]].

## Dynamic Accumulators as Mulch Plants

Many dynamic accumulator plants double as excellent mulch producers. Chicory, dandelion, yarrow, and plantain all produce biomass that, when cut and used as mulch, returns accumulated minerals to the soil surface where crop plants can access them.

These plants serve a dual function: mining minerals from deep soil layers and then delivering those minerals to the surface through their decomposing leaves. This nutrient cycling is fundamental to [[permaculture-soil-strategies]] and reduces or eliminates the need for imported fertilisers.

## Integration with Chop-and-Drop Systems

Mulch-producing plants are central to [[chop-and-drop-mulching]] systems, where plants are cut in place and left to decompose where they fall. This approach minimises labour (no carrying mulch to where it is needed), maximises nutrient cycling (minerals from decomposing plants stay in the root zone), and builds soil organic matter over time.

The key to successful chop-and-drop mulching is timing: cut plants before they set seed to prevent unwanted spread, and cut during active growth periods when nutrient content in leaves is highest. Multiple cuts per season from the same plants maximise total biomass production.

## Mulch Plants in Different Climates

The choice of mulch-producing plants varies with climate. In temperate regions, comfrey, artichokes, rhubarb, and clovers are the mainstay mulch producers. In tropical and subtropical areas, canna lilies, banana leaves, pigeon pea, and sweet potato vines produce abundant biomass.

In arid regions, mulch production is more challenging but still possible. Native grasses, saltbush, and certain acacia species can produce useful mulch quantities even with limited water. The key is selecting species adapted to local rainfall patterns and soil conditions, connecting to [[permaculture-difficult-sites-plant-guide]].

## Building a Self-Mulching Garden

The ultimate goal of integrating mulch-producing plants is a self-mulching garden where the system generates its own organic matter and fertility. As the system matures, leaf fall from trees, dieback from herbaceous plants, and chop-and-drop from mulch plants combine to maintain a continuous mulch layer.

This self-mulching capacity is one of the hallmarks of a mature [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] system. Once established, the gardener's role shifts from active mulch application to passive observation and occasional management, demonstrating [[permaculture-principle-use-small-and-slow-solutions]].
