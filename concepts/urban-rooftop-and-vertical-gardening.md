## Urban Rooftop and Vertical Gardening

Urban permaculture must make creative use of limited space, and two of the
most productive strategies are rooftop gardening and vertical growing
techniques. Green roofs replace the ecological footprint of buildings with
growing space, while vertical gardens exploit walls, trellises, and other
structures to multiply the productive area of small urban lots. Together these
approaches allow urban dwellers to grow significant quantities of food,
manage stormwater, reduce urban heat island effects, and create habitat for
pollinators within densely built environments.

## Vertical Gardening Techniques

Vertical gardening exploits the microclimate right against a structure's
walls. The permaculture design principle recognizes that the microclimate
against walls may help extend growing seasons or make conditions right for
crops that would not otherwise grow there. Walls absorb heat during the day
and release it at night, creating a warmer microclimate for heat-loving
plants. North-facing walls provide cooler, shadier conditions suitable for
leafy greens and shade-tolerant herbs.

### Trellis and Vine Systems

Vines, trellises, and other vertical gardening techniques can be built on
any structure to take advantage of microclimate effects. Productive vines
include:
- **Grapes**: on south-facing walls for fruit production and summer shade
- **Kiwi**: vigorous vines that need strong support structures
- **Beans and peas**: annual climbers for intensive food production
- **Cucumbers and melons**: space-efficient summer crops on trellises
- **Passionflower**: edible fruit, ornamental flowers, and medicinal leaves
- **Hops**: perennial climbers for brewing and shade

### Living Wall Systems

Living walls are vertical planting systems attached to building facades.
They range from simple trellis-based systems with climbing plants to
elaborate modular systems with planted pockets. In permaculture Zone 0
design, living walls are cited as a method for remediating the ecological
activity of permanent structures. They can include herbs, strawberries, and
even salad greens in specially designed pocket systems.
