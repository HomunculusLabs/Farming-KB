# Light and Basidiocarp Initiation in Psilocybe Cubensis

E.R. Badham's 1980 paper in *Mycologia* (Vol. 72, No. 1, pp. 136-142)
provides the first systematic determination of an action spectrum for
basidiocarp (fruitbody) initiation in Psilocybe cubensis. This research
resolved a significant contradiction in the literature and established
the specific light wavelengths, intensities, and durations required to
trigger mushroom formation in this species.

## Research Background

The requirement of light for basidiocarp initiation in fungi was
well documented across multiple species by 1980. Earlier work by
Heim and Wasson (1958) had suggested that P. cubensis might form
fruitbodies in complete darkness at elevated temperatures (27 degrees
C). However, Jackson and Alexopoulos (1976) stated that the species
requires light for initiation at 22-25 degrees C. Badham's study was
designed to resolve this discrepancy by systematically characterizing
the light requirements.

## Key Findings

**Light is Required:** Basidiocarp initials formed only when cultures
were illuminated. No fruitbody initiation occurred in continuous
darkness under the experimental conditions. The suggestion by Heim
and Wasson that dark fruiting was possible could not be confirmed.

**Extremely Short Durations Sufficient:** Remarkably, extremely brief
light exposures were sufficient to trigger initiation. A single
xenon-arc flash of just 0.0025 seconds (2.5 milliseconds) per day
for five consecutive days was enough to induce fruitbody formation.
This demonstrated an extraordinarily photosensitive response.

**Dose-Response Relationship:** Using monochromatic light at 460nm,
the dose-response relationship was found to be approximately linear
between 0.086 x 10^4 and 0.345 x 10^4 ergs/cm^2, consistent with
the Bunsen-Roscoe law of reciprocity. Response saturation occurred
at doses above 0.345 x 10^4 ergs/cm^2 at this wavelength.

**Action Spectrum:** UV and blue wavelengths of 370, 440, and 460nm
were the most effective for inducing fruitbody formation. Green and
red wavelengths greater than 510nm were completely ineffective. This
pattern is characteristic of blue-light photoreceptor responses in
fungi, as documented by Tan (1978) and Briggs (1976).

## Materials and Methods

The isolate used was obtained in 1975 from a basidiocarp depositing
basidiospores onto agar, deposited with ATCC (#36359). Cultures were
grown on a Brodie (1975) agar medium containing relatively low sugar
levels, asparagine, peptone, yeast extract, and salts. Slant tubes
were grown in the dark for one month before treatment.

Light sources included Cool-white fluorescent lamps with broad-band
filters (UV at 350nm, blue at 450nm, green at 550nm, red at 650nm),
a xenon-arc lamp for flash experiments, and a 300-watt quartz-iodine
projector lamp with a 0.25m Jarrell-Ash monochromator for spectral
sensitivity experiments. All experiments were conducted at 21 degrees
C with 85% relative humidity.

## Cultivation Observations

Several important observations were made beyond the primary
experimental results. Initiation did not occur in non-ventilated
cultures, confirming the importance of gas exchange. Light had no
effect on initiation before vegetative maturity was completed (the
agar surface must be fully colonized by mycelium).

Abnormal formations were noted, including chestnut-colored spheres
up to 5mm diameter containing spores, grown at 10 degrees C. Under
low light levels, a blue zone subtended the pileus, corresponding to
the phototropic zone described by Plunkett (1961). Singer (1958) had
related this blue pigment to the presence of hallucinogenic indoles,
and its location might provide clues to the ecological function of
these compounds.

## Significance for Cultivation

This research directly informs cultivation practices. The finding
that blue light at 440-460nm is the primary trigger for fruiting
explains why standard fluorescent lighting (which has strong blue
output) is effective for indoor cultivation. The extreme
photosensitivity, requiring only milliseconds of exposure, means
that even incidental light during routine maintenance is sufficient
to maintain fruiting cycles. The ineffectiveness of red light
confirms its appropriateness as a "safe light" for manipulation
of cultures without triggering unwanted initiation.

## Broader Significance for Fungal Photobiology

Badham's study placed P. cubensis within the growing body of
literature on fungal photoresponses. The action spectrum with peak
sensitivity in the blue/UV region is consistent with responses
documented across numerous fungal species, suggesting conservation
of the photoreceptor mechanism. This pattern distinguishes fungal
photomorphogenesis from plant photomorphogenesis, which involves
multiple photoreceptor systems including phytochromes responding
to red/far-red light.

The phototropic zone observed at 10 degrees C, where blue
pigment accumulates in a specific band on the pileus, provides
additional evidence for the relationship between light exposure
and indole alkaloid metabolism in this species. The connection
between the phototropic zone and the presence of hallucinogenic
indoles, first noted by Singer (1958), remains an area of
scientific interest and potential ecological investigation.
