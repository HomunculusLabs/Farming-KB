# Soil Temperature and Oxygen for Plant Growth

Two often-overlooked factors profoundly influence plant growth: **[[soil-temperature]]** and **soil oxygen**. [[steve-solomon]] emphasizes that these physical properties of soil can matter as much as or more than [[chelation-and-nutrient-availability]] in determining how well vegetables grow.

## The Nighttime Growth Cycle

A surprising fact about plants: the business of construction — growth — is mainly done **at night**. During the day, plants store energy by converting sunshine, water, and air into sugar through photosynthesis. At night, they burn that sugar as fuel to grow.

The speed of this growth process is directly governed by **temperature**. For every 10°F (5°C) increase in temperature, the speed of growth **doubles**. This means growth increases exponentially:

- At **50°F (10°C)** nighttime low: the plant grows about **1 size unit** overnight
- At **60°F (15°C)**: growth approximately **doubles** to 2 size units
- At **70°F (21°C)**: growth **doubles again** to 4 size units
- At **80°F (26°C)**: growth reaches **8 size units** overnight

This exponential relationship explains why warm-season crops seem to explode in growth during hot weather, and why cool-season crops stall during chilly nights. It also explains why corn can sometimes be heard "growing" on a humid summer night.

## Seed Germination and Soil Temperature

The temperature-growth relationship applies equally to seed germination. Seeds that sit in chilly soil may take **four to six weeks** to emerge, while the same seeds in warm soil could be up and growing in less than one week. Some seeds — particularly warm-season crops like melons, peppers, and eggplant — **require** warm soils to germinate at all and may simply rot in the earth if temperatures are too low.

## Root Growth and Soil Temperature

Root growth speed is also determined by soil temperature. Roots may only grow actively for about five months of the year in cooler climates. Faster root growth means better nutrient and water uptake, leading to healthier, more productive plants.

## The Soil Thermometer

Solomon considers a **soil thermometer** one of the most [[mushroom-charts-educational-tools]] a gardener can own. Watching it closely over a few weeks reveals the connection between daily weather, the rise and fall of temperature a few inches below the surface, and the resulting rate of plant growth. This knowledge helps gardeners time their plantings optimally and diagnose slow growth.

## Soil Oxygen

### Photosynthesis and Root Respiration

Most people understand that plant leaves conduct photosynthesis: they take in carbon dioxide, combine it with water using solar energy, and produce sugar and oxygen. What is less widely appreciated is what happens **without sunlight**. At night, and in any non-green plant tissue, the plant cannot photosynthesize and must **respire** — it breathes in oxygen and breathes out carbon dioxide, just like animals.

### Roots Need to Breathe

Plant roots do not conduct photosynthesis. They are alive, and like all living cells, they must respire. This means roots need a steady supply of **oxygen** from the surrounding soil. If soil becomes waterlogged or compacted, oxygen cannot reach the roots, and they essentially suffocate. Root suffocation leads to reduced nutrient uptake, stunted growth, increased disease susceptibility, and in severe cases, plant death.

### How Oxygen Enters Soil

At a casual glance, soil might seem solid, but it is actually composed of mineral particles with **spaces between them**. In a well-structured soil, these pore spaces are partially filled with air and partially with water. The balance between air-filled and water-filled pores is critical:

- **Well-structured soil** (good tilth): roughly 50% mineral particles, 25% water, 25% air by volume
- **Compacted soil**: pore spaces are crushed, leaving little room for either water or air
- **Waterlogged soil**: all pore spaces are filled with water, displacing air

### Improving Soil Oxygen Through Tillage

The physical working of soil — digging, spading, tilling — serves primarily to **improve soil texture and aeration**. This improved texture is called **tilth**. Good tilth means the soil is loose, crumbly, and well-aerated, allowing roots to penetrate easily and access both water and oxygen.

Tillage is especially important in the early years of a garden. Over time, as organic matter is incorporated and soil biology develops ([[earthworms]], beneficial fungi, etc.), the soil structure naturally improves and the need for intensive tillage decreases.

## Capillarity

After tilling, an important phenomenon called **capillarity** becomes visible. Immediately after tilling, all the soil appears damp. But after the sun dries the surface, visit the plot the next morning and look at your footprints: they will be damp while the surrounding tilled surface is dry. This happens because your weight compressed the soil particles together firmly enough to allow moisture to rise from below through [[capillary-action-in-soils]].

Understanding capillarity is important for two reasons:
1. It explains how moisture moves upward through soil to reach plant roots.
2. It means that **compacted areas** (like footprints, wheelbarrow paths, or plow pans) wick moisture to the surface where it evaporates, effectively **drying out the soil below**.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Plants grow mainly at night** — daytime is for energy collection, nighttime is for construction.
2. **Growth doubles for every 10°F (5°C) increase** — this exponential relationship has enormous practical implications.
3. **Soil temperature controls germination speed** — a soil thermometer is an invaluable tool.
4. **Roots must respire** — they need oxygen from soil air spaces, not from leaves.
5. **Tillage creates tilth** — loose, aerated soil structure that allows root growth and oxygen diffusion.
6. **Capillarity moves moisture upward** in compacted soil, causing surface drying and moisture loss below.
7. **Avoid soil compaction** — it reduces both oxygen availability and water retention.
