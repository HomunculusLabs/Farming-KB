# Fukuoka Fruit Tree Disease and Pest Control with Natural Predators

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s [[cho-natural-farming-philosophy]] extends directly into his approach to
disease and pest control in fruit trees. Rather than relying on [[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides]],
Fukuoka demonstrated that healthy ecosystems populated by natural predators, combined
with proper tree cultivation, can keep pest damage to acceptable levels without
synthetic intervention.

## Natural Resistance

The foundation of Fukuoka's pest-control philosophy begins with a reassessment of
trees' innate defenses: "In nature, trees are constantly attacked and parasitized by
insects and disease, but the widely accepted belief that unless the grower sprays his
trees they will succumb and die just does not hold under natural conditions."

Modern cultivated varieties have been weakened by human intervention. "Crops are more
susceptible to such attack because they have been artificially improved, reducing their
innate resistance, and the environment in which they are grown is unnatural." His
prescription is clear: "If varieties of fruit trees closer to their natural ancestors
are selected and grown properly, pesticides become unnecessary." Different fruit tree
types have different resistance levels; those with "moderate" and "strong resistance"
can be grown without pesticides entirely.

## Arrowhead Scale

Arrowhead scale caused severe infestation of Satsuma, Iyo, and shaddock oranges. For
conventional growers already dependent on chemicals, an immediate stop to spraying
was difficult. Yet Fukuoka found that the damage could be overcome with natural
predators and correcting tree form.

In his [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-development]], [[beneficial-insects-cannabis]] arose spontaneously: "Parasitic wasps and
four or five different types of ladybugs emerged in my natural orchard." These proved
remarkably effective: "In areas where these feast in large number on the scales, I
have not sprayed and yet the trees have escaped serious damage."

Tree architecture plays a decisive role. "No degree of spraying can succeed in
effectively destroying arrowhead scales in trees with excessive branches and foliage."
Dense canopies create sheltered microenvironments that scales thrive in. "The quickest
and most effective solution is to protect the natural enemies that feed on this insect
and to improve the microenvironment."

For those who feel some intervention is necessary, machine oil emulsion in winter or
lime-sulfur in summer during the larval stage is sufficient. Beyond that: "If you are
not concerned about a minor loss in the tree's appearance, then you can certainly do
without any spraying at all."

## Mites

The history of mite infestations illustrates how pesticide use creates the very
problems it aims to solve. Before World War II, a simple lime-sulfur mixture used
twice each summer was all that was needed, and mites were never a major pest.

After the war, organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides destroyed all insects
indiscriminately. The result: "no matter how often they sprayed, they were unable to
prevent large outbreaks of mites from recurring." Explanations ranged from pesticide
resistance to new species emerging, but Fukuoka cuts through the debate: "I prefer
to concentrate on the fact that mite infestation at one time was not a problem."

His approach is grounded in realistic expectations: "Cultivation in the total absence
of mites throughout the year is just not possible. Our goal should be to hold the
damage they cause to a minimum, not total extermination." He places responsibility on
human practice: "The causes for the recent infestations and the extensive damage to
fruit trees lie not in the mites themselves but in human actions."

Mites are more sensitive to microclimatic changes than scales. Strategic use of black
wattle as a windbreak and shade can shift conditions enough that mites and scales "may
drop dramatically or almost entirely vanish." Yet this approach receives no research
attention: "Given that not even the most rudimentary studies have been done on the
effects of sunlight, ventilation, temperature, and humidity on mite infestation, how
totally reckless it is then to try to control these with pesticides."

In his own orchard: "I never had any problem with mites in the citrus trees in my
orchard. Or if I did, the problem solved itself."

## Cottony-Cushion Scale

Once one of the three major citrus pests in Japan, the cottony-cushion scale
disappeared naturally with the release of the vedalia ladybug more than forty years
ago. After the war, widespread organophosphate spraying killed the ladybugs along
with the scales, and serious outbreaks returned. In Fukuoka's natural orchard, where
strong pesticides were not used, the ladybugs continued as predators and the result
was "almost no damage" --- direct proof that conventional [[jadam-combined-pesticide-programs-jwa-js-jhs]] destroy
the very solution nature has already provided.

## Red Wax Scale

Red wax scale, another major pest, follows the same pattern. Originally controlled
with a pine rosin mixture, wartime rosin shortages forced growers to stop. Parasitic
wasps emerged naturally and established themselves as effective predators, making
chemical extermination unnecessary. After the war, a fluorine pesticide was
introduced: "Severe outbreaks of the pest arose at once." When the agent was banned,
"Infestation by the scale declined almost immediately, demonstrating that the most
intelligent way of controlling this particular pest is not to spray."

## Foreign Pests Warning

Fukuoka closes with a sobering warning about foreign pests introduced through
international trade. The Mediterranean fruit fly attacks all major fruit and vegetable
crops; the codling moth ravages rose family fruits. "Extermination of these will be
difficult if not impossible; once they have entered Japan, they may very well cause
incredible damage." Larval worms and maggots bury deep into fruit, making outside
spraying or fumigation ineffective once established.

His warning is philosophical as much as agricultural: "I would simply like to warn
that the free movement of fruit may satisfy the fleeting desires of people, but the
price we will have to pay will be enormous."

## Summary of Principles

- **Select resistant varieties** closer to natural ancestors rather than weakened
  modern cultivars.
- **Rely on natural predators** --- ladybugs, parasitic wasps --- rather than
  broad-spectrum chemicals that destroy them.
- **Improve tree form and microenvironment** through pruning and strategic planting
  to create conditions unfavorable to pests.
- **Accept minor cosmetic damage** rather than pursuing total extermination.
- **Avoid strong pesticides** that kill natural enemies and trigger the very
  outbreaks they are meant to prevent.
- **Recognize that most pest problems originate in human actions**, not in the pests
  themselves; often the most intelligent control is to do nothing at all.
