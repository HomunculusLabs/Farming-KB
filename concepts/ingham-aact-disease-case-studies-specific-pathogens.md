# AACT Disease Case Studies: Specific Pathogen Responses

[[ingham-foliar-compost-tea-application]] spray does have a discernible impact replacing disease organisms on leaf surfaces, the effect should be visible within a day. If no impact is seen, another spray is indicated.

When disease pressure is overwhelming, it may be necessary to use a fungicide or insecticide initially to bring the situation under control, then transition to AACT for ongoing protection. Compost tea is not a pesticide and should not be discussed as being like a chemical control method.

If no diseases are present, [[cordyceps-cultivation]] cannot push them out. The enzymatic ability of many common foliar pathogens is surprisingly weak — they cannot out-compete even modest communities of beneficial organisms for space or resources. The question then becomes: why are these diseases so prevalent? Because conventional management practices continuously destroy the protective biology on leaf surfaces.

Ingham categorizes diseases by difficulty: black spot is "easy," mildew is harder, anthracnose is not easy, and *Fusarium* is "a piece of cake." These assessments reflect the relative enzymatic competitiveness of each pathogen against the beneficial organisms typically present in well-made AACT.

## Black Spot (Diplocarpon rosae)

Black spot is classified as easy to control with AACT. The pathogenic fungus produces black spores — the initial infection appears as vaguely gray hyphae on the leaf surface, darkening as spores mature and the spot enlarges as the fungus grows outward.

The key to prevention is simple coverage. The black spot fungus has such weak enzymatic ability that it cannot out-compete any other organism for space on the leaf surface. If beneficial organisms are already present, the pathogen cannot establish. The solution is straightforward: get the organisms back on leaf surfaces and stop killing them with conventional sprays.

## Fusarium

Fusarium diseases, including snow mold, appear to be preventable with just a bacterial tea. This makes *Fusarium* one of the easiest diseases to address. The relatively simple organism requirements mean that even teas extracted by machines that primarily pull bacteria can be effective. Some *Fusarium* species can be prevented by competition for food resources alone, without requiring the full foodweb.

## Rhizoctonia

Rhizoctonia presents a more nuanced picture. Some *Rhizoctonia* species can be prevented with bacterial tea alone, but not all species. Certain *Rhizoctonia* strains require the full bacterial plus fungal tea to achieve suppression. This variability between species within a single genus illustrates why practitioners need to match their tea composition to the specific disease pressure they face. A bacterial-only tea may work in one field but fail in another with a different *Rhizoctonia* species.

## Anthracnose

Anthracnose location on the plant determines the tea composition needed:

- **Crown, root, or soil anthracnose** requires a strong fungal component in the tea
- **Foliar anthracnose** appears preventable with just a bacterial tea

## Late Blight

Ingham reports extensive experience with late blight but limited data on early blight. AACT work has included prevention of apple scab, leaf curl, anthracnose, and several foliar-feeding insects. Fungi are what would be needed to suppress fireblight, and anecdotal evidence from several estates in New York suggests fireblight was removed through AACT programs (contact Paul Wagner and James Sottilo at the SFI NY lab for details).

## Brown Rot on Cherries

Verticillium prevention is documented on potato, apples, and cherries. The comprehensive coverage requirement reflects the systemic nature of this wilt disease — the pathogen survives on multiple plant parts and in soil, so all potential infection sites must be protected.

## Cedar Apple Rust and Wheat Rust

AACT with excellent fungal biomass will stop cedar apple rust. Rust on wheat has also been addressed with AACT in Nebraska with excellent results. These results confirm the pattern that fungal diseases of above-ground tissues respond best to teas with strong fungal components.

## Peach Leaf Curl (Taphrina)

Leaf curl caused by *Taphrina* is described as "a breeze to shut down" with tea containing good beneficial fungal growth. The recommended program demonstrates the importance of multi-season, multi-application approaches:

1. **Fall**: Apply soil drench to establish healthy organisms on leaves and promote decomposition instead of pathogen overwintering
2. **Spring**: Apply soil drench again before growth begins
3. **Two weeks before budbreak**: Begin foliar AACT applications
4. **During season**: Apply minimum once monthly, more like every 10 to 14 days depending on disease severity
