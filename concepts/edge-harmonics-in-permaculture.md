## Overview

Edge is one of the most important and far-reaching concepts in [[permaculture-design]]. [[bill-mollison]] devoted extensive study to understanding how edges function in natural systems and how designers can deliberately manipulate them to increase yield, diversity, and system stability. The concept draws from Aboriginal Australian ecological knowledge, mathematics, and direct observation of natural patterns.

## Defining Edge

An edge is the interface between two different systems or media. Examples include:

- The steel-strong film between water and air
- The zone around a soil particle where water bonds with force
- The shoreline between land and water
- The interface between forest and grassland (the scrub)
- The area between frost and non-frost levels on a hillside
- The border of the desert

Edge has universal characteristics: everything pauses there. People never walk straight from forest to plain or plain to forest. Edge gives everything pause. In natural systems, edge contains a kind of species richness and productivity that sharply increases, along with potential interactions. Therefore, the energy flux is greater through the edge.

## The Productivity of Edge

Tidal marshes, mangroves, barrier reefs, and light edges all demonstrate that interaction, standing crop, energy flux, and yield are greatest at interfaces. Agricultural scientists traditionally avoid sampling edge — they discard the first two meters and sample within the crop because edge figures are biased toward higher production. They work on a per-acre basis and miss the point.

However, not all edges are productive. When unlike systems are butted together, one or both might show a drop in yield. But generally, the edge is rich because it has species of both media plus species unique to itself.

## The Blueberry Example

Blueberries grow at the edge of clearings in pine forests — they are an edge species. An agricultural college graduate wanting to grow blueberries commercially will clear the area, remove the pines, and set out blueberries as open row crop. If instead the designer loops rows of blueberries through existing pine plantings and runs pastures into the edges, the equivalent of an acre of blueberries can be produced on a quarter acre, probably doubling the yield.

## Edge Harmonics: The Mathematical Discovery

Mollison's key insight came from a conversation with mathematician Dennis McCarthy. While driving through western Australia, Mollison mentioned "edge," and after 20 minutes of silence, McCarthy responded: "Edge harmonics, Mollison." McCarthy had studied edge harmonics mathematically.

The insight is this: when you take an area and move it in an even harmonic about its edge — though you have not altered the total area — you have enormously increased the edge length. Without taking any cows out of the paddock, you can induce enormous yield at the edge. Without reducing the oxygen surface of the pond, you can give it many times more edge than exists in a simple circle.

The harmonics of the edge decide:
- How much genetic material exchanges between sections of media or across the edge
- Matters of shelter
- The productivity of the edge itself

## The Aboriginal Connection

Mollison observed Pitjantjatjara women creating elaborate patterns in pigments on windows and dresses. When he flew over the desert in a light plane, he recognized that these patterns were accurate ecological [[maps]] of the desert — the spacings were precise, and one woman could direct another to a specific stone and salt pan at a distance she had never visited.

These patterns represent the desert's own edge harmonics — the way the land itself organizes its interfaces between different ecological zones. The women's patterns describe aspects of desert ecology in terms of story and myth. In Pitjantjatjara culture, celebration, way, and pattern are indistinguishable — a totality.

## Practical Application: The Box-Thorn Clump

Mollison applied edge harmonics to a 200-year-old box-thorn clump approximately 35 paces in diameter. Using a slash hook, he carved into the clump, creating intricate edge patterns. The result: 286 paces of edge while keeping the clump practically intact. He also designed an enormous number of microclimates — cold winds here, hot salty winds there, shaded areas, dry areas, cold areas, hot areas, and wet areas.

He planted avocado in a hot edge pocket — over a thousand miles south of banana country — and the avocado survived. The thorn bush had created a "banana hole" — a microclimate warm enough for tropical species within a temperate landscape. This demonstrated that elaborating edge creates conditions impossible in simple geometries.

## The Ziggurat Garden

Inspired by the spiral of a sea shell (which efficiently stacks digestion into a small space), Mollison built a ziggurat garden about six feet across at the base — an open spiral ascending a rock cairn. This three-dimensional edge creation produced roughly 51 feet of row in a compact footprint, with no inter-row problems. Different drainages, heats, and shade created multiple microclimates suitable for different culinary herbs — parsley, chives, thyme, rosemary on top, tarragon lower down.

The ziggurat condenses space, reduces intercrop competition, gives every plant plenty of root and climbing space, and relieves the monotony of flattened landscapes.

## The Humid Landscape Profile

Mollison references his "classical humid landscape profile" as a harmonic — often an almost endlessly repeated pattern in nature. This horizontal elaboration of edge is what life forms do when vertical elaboration is not possible. The principle applies equally to biochemical and biophysical harmonics.

## Rectilinear Agriculture vs. Natural Pattern

Mollison challenges the assumption that rectilinear mathematics has partly determined agriculture. When you establish something and measure it on a grid, you impose a simple pattern. This works for per-acre yield calculations but misses the productive potential of edge elaboration.

Natural systems achieve extraordinary complexity through dancing mosaics — grasses, forbs, and legumes in numerous species and varied stages of growth, moving through time as one species matures and another emerges. Both shape and kind unfold complex harmonies as seasons proceed.

## Tools for Creating Edge

Mollison offers several practical approaches:

1. **Carve into existing structure**: Like the box-thorn clump, intrinsically reshape existing vegetation
2. **Superimpose patterns**: On flat land without pre-existing structure, lay down curvilinear patterns
3. **The drunken walk**: Wander through the site, stopping to observe, then continuing. Mike Corbett led bulldozers this way at the Davis village project, and the settlement pattern that emerged was extraordinarily effective
4. **Imitate natural retreat**: A scarp retreats not in a straight line but intricately, throwing out ledges, pinching off islands. Imitating this flux produces easily maintained designs

## Design Implications

The edge concept has several critical implications for [[permaculture]] designers:

- **Superimposed sine waves** create edge harmonics or moiré patterns on a flat plane
- **Spherical or round design elements** (like geodesic domes) minimize energy flows — useful for conservation
- **Curvilinear patterns** amplify energy exchanges — useful when productivity is the goal
- **Pattern recognition** becomes the designer's primary skill — linking disparate phenomena from different disciplines into a single theoretical framework
- **Time stacking** is another dimension of edge — placing lettuce under pole beans so lettuce is harvested before beans shade it

The boundary condition is critically important to many things. Mollison argues we pay too little attention to it and should use it as a powerful design tool wherever it does not cost too much.
