# Mycoremediation Case Studies
Field-verified remediation projects demonstrating fungal cleanup of contaminated environments. Based on [[paul-stamets]]' *Mycelium Running* (2005). See also [[mycoremediation-basics]], [[mycoremediation-oil-spills]], [[mycoremediation-heavy-metals-detailed]], and [[mycorestoration-framework]].
## Battelle Marine Science Laboratory: PCB Bioremediation
### The Experiment
- **Location**: Sequim, Washington (Battelle Marine Science Laboratory)
- **Contaminant**: Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
- **Species**: Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom)
- **Substrate**: Wood chips inoculated with oyster mushroom mycelium
- **Goal**: Test whether white-rot fungi could degrade PCBs in contaminated soil
### Results
- Oyster mushroom mycelium colonized the contaminated substrate and produced fruiting bodies
- **PCB concentration in fruiting bodies was NOT significantly elevated** — mushrooms did not bioaccumulate PCBs
- This was a critical finding: the fungi metabolized the PCBs rather than concentrating them
- Enzyme systems (lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases) were identified as the active degradation mechanism
- After 8 weeks of treatment, measureable reductions in PCB and PAH concentrations were detected
### Significance
- First demonstration that white-rot fungi could degrade PCBs in a field-relevant setting
- Showed that fungal remediation does NOT create contaminated mushrooms that enter the food chain
- Opened the door to using mycoremediation for Superfund sites and industrial contamination
## Washington State Oil Spill Response
### The Scenario
- **Context**: Stamets' proposal for using oyster mushrooms to remediate oil-contaminated shorelines
- **Species**: Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom)
- **Approach**: Inoculate oil-contaminated substrates with oyster mushroom mycelium
### How It Works
1. **Containment**: Oil-soaked materials (straw, hair mats, wood chips) are collected or contained in place
2. **Inoculation**: Oyster mushroom spawn is introduced to the contaminated material
3. **Colonization**: Mycelium grows through the contaminated substrate (1-4 weeks)
4. **Degradation**: Fungal enzymes break down petroleum hydrocarbons into simpler compounds
5. **Fruiting**: Mushrooms may fruit on the contaminated substrate; fruiting bodies tested for contaminant uptake
6. **Ecological recovery**: After fungal treatment, the substrate becomes suitable for plant recolonization
### Petroleum Degradation Pathway
- **Alkanes**: Fungal cytochrome P450 enzymes oxidize alkanes to alcohols, then to fatty acids, then to CO2 and water
- **Aromatic hydrocarbons**: Lignin-degrading enzymes (LiP, MnP, laccase) open aromatic rings
- **BTEX compounds**: Degraded through similar enzymatic pathways
- **Heavy fractions**: Slower degradation; may require multiple fungal species in succession
## E. coli Mycofiltration: Stamets' Property Experiments
### The Discovery
- **Location**: Paul Stamets' property near Olympia, Washington
- **Problem**: Runoff from neighboring livestock operations contaminating downstream water
- **Solution**: Stropharia rugoso-annulata (wine cap) mycelial filters installed in drainage pathways
### Results
- **E. coli reduction**: Water passing through Stropharia mycelial mats showed dramatic reduction in coliform bacteria
- **Mechanism**: Mycelium secretes antibacterial compounds that kill or inhibit E. coli and other coliform bacteria
- **Secondary benefit**: Mycofilters also captured sediment and nutrients from agricultural runoff
- **Durability**: Mycelial mats remained effective for multiple years with minimal maintenance
### Implications
- Mycofiltration can replace or supplement conventional septic systems and stormwater treatment
- Particularly valuable for agricultural areas where livestock runoff threatens water quality
- Stropharia rugoso-annulata identified as the premier mycofiltration species for bacterial removal
## Washington State DOT: Road Construction Mycofiltration
### WSDOT Experiment
- **Location**: Washington State (specific road construction site)
- **Partner**: Washington State Department of Transportation
- **Problem**: Sediment and pollutant runoff from road construction sites
- **Solution**: Mycofiltration barriers using mushroom mycelium
### Design
- Mycelial mats placed in drainage channels and at construction site perimeters
- Multiple fungal species used to address different contaminants
- Compared against conventional silt fencing and sediment traps
### Findings
- Mycofilters effectively captured sediment and reduced turbidity in runoff
- Fungal mycelium also reduced concentrations of petroleum hydrocarbons from construction equipment
- Cost comparison showed mycofilters could be competitive with conventional methods, especially when factoring in ecosystem benefits
- After project completion, mycelial mats decomposed into beneficial organic matter for site restoration
## PAH Biodegradation Studies
### Creosote-Contaminated Sites
- **Contaminant**: Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) from creosote (wood preservative)
- **Species tested**: Pleurotus ostreatus, Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Trametes versicolor
- **Substrate**: Contaminated soil amended with wood chips and fungal inoculum
- **Results**: Significant reduction in PAH concentrations, particularly for lighter molecular weight PAHs (2-3 ring compounds)
- **Limitation**: Higher molecular weight PAHs (4-6 ring) degraded more slowly; required extended treatment times
### Diesel-Contaminated Soil
- Oyster mushroom mycelium successfully colonized diesel-contaminated soil
- Visible degradation of diesel compounds within weeks
- Mushroom fruiting bodies appeared on contaminated substrate
- Subsequent analysis showed reduced total petroleum hydrocarbon (TPH) concentrations
## Heavy Metal Sequestration
### Radioactive Cesium (Cs-137)
- **Context**: Post-Chernobyl contamination in Europe
- **Observation**: Certain fungi bioaccumulate radioactive cesium from contaminated soils
- **Species**: Particularly notable in mycorrhizal fungi (Suillus, Lactarius, Boletus)
- **Application potential**: Fungi could be used to concentrate and remove radioactive materials from contaminated sites, then harvested and disposed of safely
### Lead and Cadmium
- Various fungal species demonstrate ability to sequester heavy metals through biosorption
- Mycelial mats can be deployed as passive filters for metal-contaminated water
- After saturation, metal-loaded fungal biomass can be collected and processed
- See [[mycoremediation-heavy-metals-detailed]] for detailed species data and mechanisms

## Key Lessons from Case Studies
1. **Species selection matters**: Different contaminants require different fungal species
2. **Enzyme systems are key**: White-rot fungi with lignin-degrading enzymes are most versatile
3. **No bioaccumulation in fruiting bodies**: Mushrooms metabolize rather than concentrate most organic pollutants
4. **Fungal-bacterial partnerships**: Combining fungi with bacteria often yields better results than either alone
5. **Site-specific design**: Each contamination scenario requires tailored approach
6. **Cost effectiveness**: Mycoremediation can be significantly cheaper than conventional methods ($50-100K vs $1M+ for excavation)
