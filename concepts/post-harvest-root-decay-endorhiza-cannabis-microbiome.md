# Post-Harvest Root Decay and Its Effects on Cannabis Endorhiza Microbiome

## Overview

A critical finding in the Winston et al. (2014) [[winston-cannabis-microbiome-study-design]] was the dramatic difference in endorhiza [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] between the first experiment (samples collected 8 weeks post-harvest) and the second experiment (samples collected 2 weeks pre-harvest from actively growing plants). The post-harvest samples showed strong evidence of root tissue decay, fundamentally altering the microbial community from what would be present in a living plant. This discovery has important methodological implications for all plant microbiome research.

## The Cellvibrio Signal

The most striking indicator of post-harvest root decay was the abundance of the cellulolytic bacterium **Cellvibrio** in the first experiment's endorhiza samples:

| Experiment | Sampling Timing | Mean Cellvibrio Abundance | Std Dev | N |
|-----------|----------------|--------------------------|---------|---|
| Experiment 1 | 8 weeks post-harvest | 16.9% | 13.0% | 9 |
| Experiment 2 | 2 weeks pre-harvest | 0.095% | 2.7% | 18 |

Cellvibrio is a known aerobic cellulolytic bacterium—meaning it decomposes cellulose, the primary structural component of plant cell walls. Its near-absence in living root tissue (0.095%) versus its dominance in post-harvest roots (16.9%) provides convincing evidence that root cell wall decomposition was actively occurring in the first experiment's samples. This represents an approximately **178-fold enrichment** in post-harvest roots.

## Why Root Decay Matters for Microbiome Studies

The presence of root decay introduces a confounding variable that can mask or distort the signals researchers are trying to detect:

### Community Structure Distortion
Decaying root tissue provides a fundamentally different ecological niche compared to living roots. Cellulolytic and saprotrophic organisms proliferate at the expense of the true endophytic community. The alpha diversity of the first experiment's endosphere was dramatically lower (chao1: m = 916.1, s = 161.7) compared to the second experiment's MB soil endosphere (chao1: m = 1,413, s = 280.1) and OC soil endosphere (chao1: m = 1,374, s = 64.4), consistent with community collapse during senescence.

### Cultivar-Specificity Persistence Despite Decay
Remarkably, cultivar-specificity was still detectable in the post-harvest [[cannabis-rhizosphere-endorhiza-communities]] despite the confounding effects of root decay. This suggests that the cultivar-specific microbial signatures established during the plant's life are robust enough to persist, at least partially, through the initial stages of tissue senescence. However, the magnitude of cultivar-specific effects was almost certainly attenuated by the decay signal.

### Rhizosphere Signal Masking
In the first experiment, no significant rhizosphere differentiation was detected (ADONIS: R² = 0.07, p = 0.07 for unweighted; R² = 0.09, p = 0.10 for weighted), while the second experiment showed clear rhizosphere differentiation (ADONIS: R² = 0.05, p = 0.04 unweighted; R² = 0.13, p = 0.001 weighted). The post-harvest root decay likely diminished the rhizosphere effect by altering the chemical composition of root exudates and leachates from decaying tissue.

## Mechanism of Post-Harvest Microbiome Shift

When a Cannabis plant is harvested, the root system undergoes a cascade of changes that fundamentally restructure the microbial community from a plant-supportive assemblage to a decomposition-driven one:

1. **Cessation of root exudation**: Living roots continuously secrete sugars, amino acids, organic acids, [[plant-defense-mechanisms]] into the rhizosphere. After harvest, this supply is cut off, starving dependent [[lowenfels-rhizosphere-bacteria-plant-interaction]] that rely on these carbon sources. The rhizosphere effect—the enrichment of specific bacteria around living roots—begins to dissipate within days.

2. **Cell wall degradation**: As root cells die, cellulases and pectinases from both endogenous and exogenous microbes begin breaking down cell walls, releasing intracellular contents that feed saprotrophs like Cellvibrio. The transition from endophyte to decomposer community accelerates as more cell wall material becomes available.

3. **Immune system collapse**: Living plant roots maintain chemical defences (phytoalexins, [[reactive-oxygen-species-and-oxidative-stress]], pathogenesis-related proteins) that suppress opportunistic pathogens. Post-harvest, these defences deteriorate rapidly, allowing previously excluded taxa to colonize root tissues that were previously protected.

4. **Oxygen availability changes**: Decaying roots create anaerobic microsites that shift the community from aerobic to facultatively anaerobic taxa. This oxygen gradient drives stratification of [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] within the root tissue itself.

5. **pH shifts**: Organic acid release from decomposing tissue can lower local pH, favouring acidophilic bacteria and inhibiting neutrophilic endophytes that dominated the living root community.

6. **Nutrient pulse from cellular contents**: The release of nitrogen, phosphorus, and micronutrients from lysing root cells creates a brief nutrient pulse that selects for fast-growing copiotrophic bacteria at the expense of slower-growing oligotrophs.

## Implications for Sampling Design

The comparison between the two experiments in this study provides a natural experiment demonstrating the importance of sampling timing in plant microbiome research:

- **Pre-harvest sampling** captures the functional, living microbiome that actually interacts with the plant during its growth cycle. This is the community most relevant for understanding plant-microbe partnerships, disease suppression, and [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]].
- **Post-harvest sampling** captures a transitional community in flux between the living plant microbiome and the soil decomposer community. Results from such samples cannot be reliably extrapolated to understand plant-microbe interactions during growth.
- **Temporal studies** that sample across multiple time points (vegetative, flowering, harvest, and post-harvest) would provide the most complete picture of how microbiome dynamics relate to plant health and [[mycorrhizal-fungi-secondary-metabolite-elicitation]] production.
- **Sample preservation**: The study highlights that even cold storage (4°C) is insufficient to preserve [[weighted-unifrac-strain-clustering-cannabis-endorhiza-community-structure]] over periods of weeks. For accurate microbiome profiling, samples should be processed within hours of collection or immediately flash-frozen.
- **Experimental confounds**: Researchers comparing microbiome studies must account for sampling timing as a potential confound. Studies that sample at different growth stages or post-harvest intervals may report fundamentally different communities even for the same cultivar in the same soil.

## Recommendations for Future Cannabis Microbiome Research

Based on the lessons from the post-harvest decay finding, several best practices emerge:

1. Sample roots from living plants whenever possible, ideally at standardized growth stages.
2. Process samples within 4 hours of collection or flash-freeze immediately in liquid nitrogen.
3. Include metadata on exact sampling timing relative to growth stage and harvest.
4. When studying post-harvest dynamics intentionally, sample at multiple time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8 weeks) to track the decay trajectory.
5. Report storage conditions (temperature, duration, container type) as these directly affect community composition.
6. Consider using root vitality assays (e.g., TTC staining for respiratory activity) alongside microbiome profiling to quantify the extent of tissue degradation.

## Comparison with Post-Harvest Studies in Other Crops

Post-harvest changes in root-associated microbiomes have been documented in several crop systems, though the specific taxa involved vary by plant species and soil conditions. In potato, post-harvest storage leads to rapid proliferation of soft-rot Enterobacteriaceae and pectinolytic Pectobacterium species that degrade starch-rich tuber tissue. In maize, root decomposition after harvest favours cellulolytic Streptomyces and Bacillus species that break down the more lignified maize root cell walls.

The Cannabis pattern—dominance of Cellvibrio—appears somewhat unique among studied crops. This may reflect the specific cell wall composition of Cannabis roots, the sandy loam soils in which the samples were stored, or the aerobic conditions maintained during the 8-week post-harvest period before sampling. Cellvibrio species are particularly efficient at degrading crystalline cellulose, which may give them a competitive advantage in Cannabis root decomposition compared to the broader cellulolytic communities seen in other crop residues.
