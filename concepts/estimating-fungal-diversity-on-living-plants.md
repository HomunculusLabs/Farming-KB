# Estimating Fungal Diversity on Living Plants

Estimating the number of fungi on living plants in a specified area requires two types of information: (1) the numbers and types of plant hosts present and (2) estimates of the number of fungal species associated with those plants. The challenge is considerable given the host specificity of many plant-parasitic fungi and the vastly underexplored tropics.

## Host-Plant Diversity as a Foundation

Good estimates of plant species in tropical and temperate regions are available, as are estimates of the proportions of trees, shrubs, and herbs. Unfortunately, study sites generally cannot be compared directly because of differences in plot size and sampling techniques.

### Neotropical Plant Diversity

Gentry (1992) estimated that less than 25% of species at most neotropical sites are trees. Epiphytes account for 12–16% at moist forest sites and about 25% in wet forests. Up to 50% of plant species at individual sites are herbs and shrubs. Some "tree-rich" areas have nearly 300 species of woody plants greater than 10 cm dbh per hectare. In paleotropical forests, plants other than trees predominate—34% trees, 25% climbers, and 37% herbs or shrubs at a Gabon site.

### Temperate Plant Diversity

Plant-species diversity in temperate forests is generally measured as species richness (number of species per 0.1 ha). The highest tree diversity noted by Whittaker was at an intermediate moisture gradient in the Great Smoky Mountains (18 tree species, 10 shrubs, 16 herbs; 41%, 23%, and 36% of total species, respectively). Low tree diversity can be associated with highly productive climax forest, such as redwood forests with only four tree species.

## Host Growth Form and Parasite Richness

Strong and Levin (1979) determined that species richness of plant-parasitic fungi in temperate zones was highly correlated with host growth form: the more complex the growth form, the greater the number of associated [[fungal-parasites]]. Mean number of fungal parasites per host species was:
- **Herbs**: 5.4 species
- **Shrubs**: 7.1 species
- **Trees**: 15.4 species

It is uncertain whether these figures are representative of tropical regions, and large margins of error exist in estimates of extant fungal species, partly reflecting the degree of host specificity of fungi on tropical plants (Cannon and Hawksworth 1995).

## Economic Bias in Host Recording

Estimates of fungal species richness have been influenced by the economic importance of the host. At least twice as many fungal species have been reported from species of Gramineae as from Cyperaceae. The size of the host seemed to be the major determinant of species richness, with ratios of 35:1 for Zea and 21.2:1 for Phragmites versus 8.1:1 for Triticum. Parasitic fungi associated with epiphytic plants are poorly known and may represent another potentially diverse group in the tropics.

## Key Ratios

- **Powdery mildews**: 4.5% of vascular plant species host them (Hirata 1986)
- **[[smut-fungi]] in British Isles**: 4.8% of plant species
- **Smut fungi in United States**: 3.2% of plant species
- **Smut fungi in Latin America**: Only 0.3% of ~88,000+ vascular plant species are known hosts, probably reflecting under-collection rather than actual low occurrence
- **Rusts in Neotropics**: About one in three plant species parasitized by a unique rust species (Buriticá and Hennen 1980; Nishida 1989)

## Quantitative Estimates

### Costa Rica — 13.4-ha Forest Tract

Using Hubbell's (1979) data (87 trees, 38 shrubs, 10 vines) and Strong and Levin's (1979) ratios, the estimated number of plant-parasitic fungal species present is **2,833**: (87 × 15.41) + (48 × 7.05) + (213 × 5.42).

### Costa Rica — 110,000-ha Plot

With approximately 10,000 plant species (2,500 trees, 1,600 epiphytes, 1,400 shrubs and vines, 4,500 herbs), the estimated total number of plant-parasitic fungal species is **81,457**—more than the number of all currently described fungal species.

### Great Smoky Mountains

Approximately 1,300 flowering plant species (130 trees, 325 shrubs, 845 herbs). Using Strong and Levin's ratios, an estimated **8,873** fungal species should be present—slightly more than one-tenth of the Costa Rican estimate. The hardwood stand composition has been dramatically altered by the introduction of chestnut blight fungus ([[cryphonectria-parasitica]]).

## Sampling Challenges

Standard techniques designed by plant pathologists for monitoring a single pathogen in a genetically uniform crop monoculture are not appropriate for measuring populations of parasitic fungi in natural ecosystems. A host population in the wild has higher genetic variability, different-aged individuals, and is interspersed with nonhost species.

Kranz (1990) noted that when a plant ecosystem is explored for plant pathogens, the majority of plant species encountered are likely to be healthy—only 28% to 52% of plants were hosts for plant pathogens in a 3-year German study. Conversely, the leaf-surface mycota is usually richly developed, often consisting of 12 or more species of mostly saprobic sooty molds.

## Environmental Factors Affecting Diversity

Several factors beyond host-species composition should be considered:
- **Moisture**: Increasing diversity of plant-parasitic fungi with increasing moisture levels
- **Elevation**: Decreasing diversity with increasing elevation
- **Border communities**: Phytoparasitic fungi are often especially diverse in border-plant communities
- **Host density and distribution**: Diversity depends on the density and distribution of host plants

Diversities of plant-parasitic fungi are not uniform across a study plot, and sampling strategies must account for this uneven distribution.

## Endemism Considerations

The highest levels of endemism among plant-parasitic fungi should occur in areas with the highest levels of host-species endemism. Gentry (1992) noted four such areas in the Neotropics, most representing isolated biomes (islands, intermountain regions, mountain ridges). However, endemism is not a measure of biodiversity but of range size, and its exclusive use can reduce the effectiveness of conservation procedures (Williams and Humphries 1994).

## Species Accumulation and Abundance Patterns

In most ecosystems, plants and animals show characteristic patterns of species abundances approximating a log normal distribution. Among fungi associated with plants, recent studies indicate that within an ecosystem a few species are dominant, but most species are rare (Dix 1964; Swift 1976; Bills and Polishook 1991, 1994). Species accumulation curves for plant-parasitic fungi should be related to, but not necessarily parallel, those of their host plants.

## Surveying Methodology

The mycologist investigating host-fungal associations must first determine the identity and heterogeneity of plant populations by working closely with botanists and plant ecologists. This information is critical for determining the number and size of sample plots required. The choice of sampling scheme depends on vegetation type, the objective of sampling, the type of fungus, and its interaction with the host plant. Systemic infections can be detected from presence of sori in host reproductive organs, while localized infections (e.g., Colletotrichum anthracnose) require counting each lesion as a unique infection.
