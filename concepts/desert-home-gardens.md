## Desert Home Gardens

Desert home gardens are a primary strategy for relieving malnutrition and achieving food self-reliance in arid regions. In drylands, gardens — not field crops — are the mainstay against famine. Garden leaf, fruit, and root products require little cooking (saving energy), contain essential minerals and vitamins, and can make every family food self-reliant. This contrasts sharply with cereals and grain legumes, which demand more processing and resources.

## Why Desert Gardens Are Essential

Several factors unique to drylands make home gardens critical:

- **Water solutes** are likely to be factors higher than in humid areas, affecting plant health and nutrient uptake.
- **pH and mineral deficiencies** (phosphorus, zinc, iron, manganese) can have severe effects on both plant and human health.
- **High nitrate levels** in water and food are common dryland pollutants.
- **Non-local food** is likely to lack vitamins and to be expensive, making local production vital.
- **Water use** is necessarily restricted by supply, requiring efficient irrigation methods.
- **Light saturation** of garden plants may create the need for shade to achieve good growth.
- **Nomadic or wild animals** are specific problems requiring protective fencing.

Because of the generally low level of resources in drylands, ordinary people living there are unlikely to afford full nutrition if gardens are not plentiful throughout settlements.

## Planning the Desert Garden

The desert garden must be planned as a very serious affair. Health in deserts is very much a matter of good nutrition, and imported food is often of poor quality and expensive.

### Protective Structures

On flat and dusty plains, protective barriers are essential:

- **Ditch and wall systems** to 3 m height can be constructed with a bulldozer or many hands to discourage wild cattle and deflect winds.
- **Solid pise and mud brick courtyard walls** can be built over time; rock is often plentiful for such protective walls.
- Inside this protection, **beds raised by stones, pise, or logs** can be almost totally mulch-filled as mulch sources are developed or gathered.
- Individual beds can be **shaded to 75%** with shade cloth, palm fronds, or brush supported on frames 1–1.5 m high.
- **Solid pillars of concrete block and mud brick to 2.5 m** can support permanent vine trellis over much of the garden.

### Soil and Water Analysis

A careful soil and water analysis is essential before establishing beds:

- Expect **phosphorus and zinc deficiency**, and probably iron and manganese deficiency.
- Excess **boron from detergents** can cause plant failure, so common soaps are best used where waste water goes to garden beds.
- Water must be checked for excessive **nitrates and fluorine**, both common dryland pollutants.
- All rain run-off and roof water must be carefully **harvested** and used to flush salts if local water exceeds 800 ppm or the garden is not free-drained.

### Plant Selection

A careful plant sequence for all beds should ensure they are always **self-shaded by almost constant crop**. Key plant categories include:

**Staple trees** adapted to dry periods or able to survive on minimal water:
- 5–6 date palms, 4–5 olives, a [[corridor-farming]] is fundamentally different from area cropping. It involves choosing and placing a complex assembly of native and introduced species to give at least a basic food reserve, fuels, and selected structural timbers. Corridors can be rivers, dune series on hostile salted or stony pavement, or the floodplains of exotic rivers. These serve as sites for "semi-wild" plant and animal reserves.

Provision can be made for a good rain year by broadcast sowing into prepared infiltration areas, taking off surplus crops of sunflower, millet, sorghum, panicgrass, or melons for long-term storage or export.

## Economics of the Desert Garden

Planning a garden repays itself quickly. A good gardener expects to amortise establishment costs (fences, beds, seed, mulch, micronutrients) in 6–8 months, thereafter gaining a profit in health, food, and cash.

## Essentials Summary

The essentials of a desert garden are:

1. Small raised flooded beds, thickly mulched
2. Permanent hardy trees on leach fields or in swales
3. Semi-wild very hardy bulbs, tubers, and yams in selected sites
4. Every bit of waste water directed to leach fields, also surplus from roof and run-off
5. Vines — their roots in cool mulch or inside shady walls — as a major feature; every wall, open space, and roof vine-shaded
6. **Mulch, mulch, mulch. Mulch.**

## Real-World Success

On an eroded, alkaline, non-wetting sand dune near [[peter-bennett]] of the Soil Association produced in 6 months an excellent, healthy mixed garden of almost every sort of vegetable and many flowers at 1,100 ppm salt in water. The key ingredients were fine-shredded mulch, [[compost]], coarse mulch on beds, careful watering, and a sound organic approach to soils. Given such a hostile base, this example should encourage any group to achieve the same.
