## Bigwood & Beug Amazon Strain Origin — Pucalpa, Peru

## Overview

The strain of *Psilocybe cubensis* (Earle) Singer cultivated in the landmark 1982 Bigwood and Beug flush-variation study originated from a spore print collected in the Amazon basin near Pucallpa (Pucalpa), Peru. This Amazonian strain served as the primary experimental organism for one of the first controlled studies examining how [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]] where large herbivores deposit dung. Key factors of the Amazonian habitat relevant to the species:

- **Consistent warmth:** Year-round temperatures support continuous [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]]
- **Abundant substrate:** Large herbivore populations provide plentiful dung substrate

## Implications for Strain Research

### Genetic Variability

The Bigwood and Beug study highlighted the enormous variability in psilocybin and psilocin content both within and between strains. Even within their controlled Amazon strain:

- **Psilocybin ranged from 3.2 to 13.3 mg/g** across flushes within a single miniculture
- **Psilocin ranged from 0 to 2.9 mg/g** with a characteristic absence in early flushes
- **Factor of four variation** in psilocybin levels from flush to flush

When comparing to other strains (M.R. strain, Equadorian strain) and street samples, the total variability exceeded a factor of ten.

### Strain as a Variable

The study underscored that "strain" is itself a poorly controlled variable in mycological research:

- **Multispore vs. isolate:** Working from a spore print means genetic heterogeneity — each spore represents a different recombinant genotype
- **No clonal isolation:** Bigwood and Beug did not isolate a single dikaryotic strain, so each miniculture jar contained a unique genetic combination
- **Environmental interaction:** The same genetic material produces different alkaloid profiles depending on flush number, substrate composition, and environmental conditions
