# Victorian Mushroom Spawn Making: Brick and Flake Production Methods

## Overview

William Falconer's 1891 manual *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* provided the most detailed American account of Victorian-era mushroom spawn production. The manufacture of mushroom spawn was, in Falconer's time, almost entirely confined to England for brick spawn and France for flake spawn. The process was considered one of the least understood operations in mushroom cultivation, practiced by specialists who grew mushrooms for sale in winter and spawn in summer. Falconer documented multiple recipes and methods from leading European practitioners, offering American growers their first comprehensive guide to this critical but mysterious trade.

## What Is Mushroom Spawn?

Falconer addressed the fundamental question that puzzled the general public: mushroom spawn (what practical growers called spawn) was identical to what botanists termed mycelium. The spawn was the true mushroom plant, a delicate white mold-like network of whitish threads that permeated the ground or manure in which it grew. Mushrooms themselves were merely the fruit of this plant.

Spawn was obtained through propagation by division rather than from spores. Pieces of spawn were broken up and planted separately in prepared beds, where they developed into vigorous plants bearing fruit within approximately two months. When the spawn had borne its full crop it died, requiring the cultivator to secure fresh spawn before the old material exhausted itself.

## The English Brick Spawn Trade

### Scale of Importation

Falconer documented the substantial scale of the American spawn import trade. Approximately 4,500 pounds of French (flake) spawn and 4,000 bushels (64,000 pounds) of English (brick) spawn were imported annually, with fully half handled by New York City seedsmen. One prominent Boston seedsman reported sourcing through London wholesalers to benefit from lower ocean freight rates. A 20 percent duty applied to all imported spawn.

Individual orders were remarkable in scale: one gardener at Jamaica, Long Island, purchased 1,000 pounds of brick spawn at a single time, while a neighbor bought 400 pounds. Leading importers frequently sold out before November, driven by market gardeners recognizing the profitability of mushroom cultivation. The vicinity of New York was the great mushroom-growing center of the country and the best mushroom market.

### John F. Barter: London Spawn Maker

Falconer corresponded extensively with Mr. John F. Barter of Lancefield Street, London, one of the most successful mushroom growers and spawn makers in Great Britain. Barter confined himself entirely to the mushroom business, growing mushrooms in winter months and making spawn in summer. He employed men for bed-making from August through March, then kept the same hands through summer by producing spawn for sale.

Barter's operation was impressive: he grew and sold approximately 21,000 pounds of mushrooms per year in the London market and produced some 10,000 bushels (160,000 pounds) of brick spawn annually. The output of this single manufacturer was about three times the total annual importation of all mushroom spawn types into the United States, a fact that Falconer cited as evidence that mushroom growing was carried on to vastly greater extent in Europe than in America.

### Barter's Assessment of American Prospects

When asked whether spawn could be made profitably in America, paying $1.50 per day for laborers without certainty of permanent workers, Barter replied that uncertain labor would be of no use. He noted that the wages themselves were not prohibitive—he paid nearly as much for his leading men—but that a man with practical experience was essential. The difficulty of securing trained, reliable labor was identified as the primary barrier to domestic American spawn production.

## Brick Spawn Manufacture

### The Gardeners' Assistant Method

Falconer reproduced the method from Robert Thompson's *The Gardeners' Assistant*, authored by the superintendent of the Royal Horticultural Society's gardens at Chiswick. Thompson was regarded as without a peer in practical horticulture:

1. Mix fresh horse droppings, cow dung, and a little loam with stable drainings to the consistency of mortar.
2. Spread on an open shed floor and cut into cakes six inches square when sufficiently firm.
3. Place the cakes on edge in a dry, airy place, turning frequently and protecting from rain.
4. When half dry, cut a hole in the broadside of each, large enough to admit about one inch square of good old spawn, inserted below the surface.
5. Close the hole with moist material matching the brick composition.
6. When bricks are nearly dry, prepare a nine-inch layer of horse dung prepared as for a hotbed on a dry bottom.
7. Pile the bricks openly on this layer and cover with litter so that steam and heat circulate among them.
8. Keep temperature below 60 degrees Fahrenheit; reduce covering if necessary.
9. When spawn appears throughout as white mold (but before forming threads and tubercles), remove and dry to arrest further vegetation.

### Spawn Brick Recipes

Falconer documented six distinct recipes for spawn brick composition:

- **Recipe 1:** Horse droppings one part, cow dung one-fourth, loam one-twentieth.
- **Recipe 2:** Fresh horse droppings with short litter one part, cow dung one-third, small portion of loam.
- **Recipe 3:** Equal parts horse dung, cow dung, and sheep's dung, with rotten leaves or old hotbed dung.
- **Recipe 4:** Horse dung one part, cow dung two parts, sheep's dung one part.
- **Recipe 5:** Road horse droppings one part, cow dung two parts, with a little loam.
- **Recipe 6:** Horse dung, cow dung, and loam in equal parts.

The consistent principals across all recipes were horse dung and cow dung as the primary ingredients, with loam added to bind the materials together and absorb ammonia that would otherwise escape.

### J. Burton's Method

Burton's method, from *The Kitchen and Market Garden*, began in early spring using cow manure secured before animals received green food. The cow manure was stored in an open, airy shed and turned regularly for one to two weeks. An equal part of fresh horse droppings, a little loam, and chopped straw were then added, worked together, trodden down, and turned two to three times weekly.

When weather permitted, the material was spread six inches thick, trodden and beaten evenly, then lined out and cut with a sharp spade. After drying sufficiently to handle, bricks were set on edge to dry thoroughly, exposed to sun for two to three days before receiving spawn. Two holes per brick admitted pigeon-egg-sized pieces of spawn, beaten in and surfaced with manure. Bricks were then heaped and covered with enough short manure to cause gentle heat, carefully attended until spawn penetrated through.

## French Flake Spawn Manufacture

### Virgin Spawn

Virgin spawn was natural or wild spawn occurring spontaneously in fields, manure piles, or elsewhere without artificial aid. It was presumed to originate directly from spores and was far more vigorous than manufactured spawn. Spawn makers always endeavored to obtain virgin spawn for impregnating their artificial products. When discovered in a manure pile as a netted mass of white strings, it was to be secured, spread to dry, and preserved like French flake spawn.

### Lachaume's First Method

M. Lachaume's method for making virgin French spawn involved taking five or six barrow loads of aged horse droppings mixed with one-fourth their bulk of short stable litter. In April, a trench two feet wide and twenty inches deep was opened at the foot of a north-facing wall, eight inches distant. Layers of chopped straw and prepared manure were alternated, pressed firm by treading, and watered until the trench was filled and rounded above ground level. The mound was covered with stable litter. After three months, the trench was opened; if pieces showed masses of bluish-white filaments with mushroom odor, the spawn was ready. If threads were sparse, the trench was recovered for another month.

### Lachaume's Accelerated Method

The second method, more commonly adopted by commercial growers, accelerated spawn formation by adding pieces of old spawn to the prepared manure in a north-facing trench. Spawn lumps were placed along the wall-facing edge at one-foot intervals, then covered with additional manure to a depth of three to four inches. A second row was inserted and the bed filled level, finished with a layer of fine, dry soil three to four inches thick. After six weeks to two months, new spawn appeared; the earth layer was removed and the spawn cakes were treated as in the first method.

### Lachaume's Guano Method

The third method used a trench filled with alternating layers of one-third pigeon or fowl guano and two-thirds short manure containing spent horse droppings. This produced well-formed cakes of excellent quality after two months or slightly longer.

## Brick vs. Flake Spawn: Relative Merits

Falconer provided practical comparison based on his own experience. Flake spawn cost approximately three times as much as brick spawn but appeared whiter with mycelium, leading many to believe it more potent. In practice, Falconer used two pounds of flake spawn for the same bed space requiring five pounds of brick spawn, obtaining a capital crop with slightly more mushrooms from the flake but considerably greater weight from the brick.

Mr. Barter's assessment was emphatic: brick spawn was far the best. The French spawn typically produced one-third fewer mushrooms than brick spawn from the same bed length, and those from brick spawn were by far the heaviest and fleshiest. However, Barter noted this applied more to outdoor ridge beds than indoor cellar beds. Oddly but truly, flake spawn did not produce as good results in outdoor beds as under cover.

## Spawn Storage and Quality Assessment

### Storage Conditions

Spawn should be kept dry, airy, and somewhat dark, between 35 and 65 degrees Fahrenheit. Dampness coupled with heat caused the mycelium to begin growing, which was ruinous in storage. While natural spawn survived winter frost in the ground, hard frost destroyed the vitality of both brick and flake spawn in storage. Falconer recommended obtaining the full supply in fall and keeping it personally rather than trusting to seed store conditions.

### Assessing Spawn Quality

Distinguishing good from poor spawn was acknowledged as very difficult. Bricks showing little mycelium might be perfectly good, while those well interlaced with bluish-white mold might also be excellent. Prominent yellowish threads or veins indicated mycelium that had started growing and been killed. White mold patches on the surface indicated parasitic fungi. The absence of mushroom smell indicated worthlessness.

Mr. A.D. Cowan of New York, an expert judge, advised that healthy bricks were usually of dusty brown color and light weight, with a moldy surface appearance without showing threads. Too many threads indicated exhausted vitality from bricks being heaped together before sufficiently dried. Black-colored spawn was to be avoided as a sign of incomplete spawn run.

## The American Spawn Gap

Falconer noted that mushroom spawn was not yet made for sale in America, though a few growers saved their own flake spawn. Principal growers including Van Siclen, Gardner, and Henshaw had attempted domestic production with only partial success, ultimately confining themselves to imported article. The demand for fresh mushrooms was so great, the industry so important, and imported spawn prices so high that Falconer predicted domestic production would soon emerge. The critical requirement was producing mycelium of equal fullness and vitality to the best European products.
