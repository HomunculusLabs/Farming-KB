# Bioreactor Design for [[fungal-bioremediation]]

Bioreactors provide controlled environments for fungal degradation of
environmental pollutants, offering advantages over in situ approaches including
process monitoring, parameter optimization, reduced treatment times, and
contained operation. Reactor design for fungal bioremediation must account for
the unique physiological requirements of fungi, particularly their filamentous
growth form, sensitivity to shear forces, and dependence on oxygen transfer.

## Packed Bed Bioreactors

Packed bed reactors (PBRs) contain immobilized fungal biomass on solid support
materials through which contaminated liquid flows. The support matrix may
include natural materials (wood chips, straw, peat), synthetic carriers
(polyurethane foam, alginate beads), or a combination. Key design
considerations include:

- **Bed porosity**: Must balance sufficient void volume for liquid flow against
  adequate surface area for fungal attachment and biofilm development.
  Excessive compaction leads to channeling and dead zones.
- **Flow configuration**: Both upflow and downflow configurations are used.
  Upflow operation provides better oxygen transfer but risks bed fluidization.
  Downflow operation with recirculation is common for continuous treatment.
- **Oxygen supply**: Aeration is critical since fungal degradation of organic
  pollutants (particularly by [[singh-white-rot-fungi-lignin-modifying-enzymes]]) requires oxygen. Packed beds are often aerated by concurrent or
  countercurrent air flow, with dissolved oxygen maintained above critical
  thresholds (typically >2 mg/L).
- **Pressure drop**: Biofilm growth and gas evolution increase bed resistance
  over time, requiring monitoring and backwashing or bed replacement cycles.

PBRs are particularly effective for continuous treatment of contaminated
groundwater and industrial effluents containing phenolic compounds, dyes, and
chlorinated organics as described in [[singh-fungal-degradation-of-chlorinated-compounds]].

## Fluidized Bed Bioreactors

Fluidized bed reactors (FBRs) suspend small carrier particles (0.3-1 mm) in
upward-flowing liquid, creating vigorous mixing and excellent mass transfer.
The fluidization state eliminates channeling and ensures uniform contact between
contaminated liquid and fungal biomass. Advantages over packed beds include:

- Superior mass transfer coefficients for oxygen and substrates
- Reduced clogging and pressure drop issues
- Better temperature control
- Easier biomass replacement and reactor operation

Disadvantages include higher energy requirements for fluidization, potential
for carrier attrition, and shear sensitivity of some fungal species. Light,
porous carriers such as expanded perlite, vermiculite, or plastic beads are
preferred to minimize fluidization velocity requirements.

## Rotating Biological Contactors (RBCs)

Rotating biological contactors consist of partially submerged discs or drums
coated with fungal biofilm that rotate through contaminated liquid. The
alternating exposure to liquid (substrate uptake) and air (oxygen uptake)
cycles provides excellent gas-liquid mass transfer without aeration equipment.

RBCs are well-suited for fungal systems because the attached growth mode
protects mycelium from shear damage while providing alternating wet-dry
conditions that many fungi prefer. Disc material selection (plastic, wood,
metal) affects fungal attachment and biofilm development. RBCs have been
successfully applied for dye decolorization, phenol degradation, and
wastewater treatment using white-rot fungi.

## Airlift Bioreactors

Airlift reactors use air injection through a central draft tube to create
circulating liquid flow, providing mixing and aeration simultaneously. The
low-shear environment is compatible with filamentous fungal growth. Internal
or external loop configurations are available, with internal loop designs being
more compact.

For fungal bioremediation, airlift reactors are advantageous when processing
high-viscosity liquids or when gentle mixing is required to maintain mycelial
integrity. They have been applied for [[singh-white-rot-fungi-lignin-modifying-enzymes]] production and dye degradation. Oxygen transfer efficiency in airlift
reactors is comparable to stirred-tank designs while energy consumption is
lower.

## Solid-State Fermentation (SSF) Reactors

Solid-state fermentation reactors cultivate fungi on moist solid substrates
without free-flowing liquid. This approach mimics the natural growth conditions
of filamentous fungi and is particularly relevant for:

- **Soil bioremediation**: Treatment of excavated contaminated soil in
  biopiles or contained SSF reactors. Fungi are inoculated into moistened,
  amended soil and degradation proceeds under controlled aeration and
  temperature.
- **Spent substrate treatment**: Composting and fungal treatment of organic
  waste materials contaminated with pesticides or other organics.
- **Enzyme production**: SSF often yields higher titers of ligninolytic
  enzymes than submerged fermentation, as the low water activity environment
  more closely induces natural ligninolytic physiology.

SSF reactor designs include tray reactors, rotating drums, packed bed reactors
with forced aeration, and stirred-bed reactors. Key challenges include heat
removal (fungal metabolism generates substantial heat in solid substrates),
uniform moisture distribution, and oxygen penetration into the substrate bed.
Temperature control is the most critical design parameter, as temperature
gradients of 10-20C can develop across a 50 cm substrate bed.

## Process Monitoring and Control

Effective bioreactor operation requires monitoring of:

- **Dissolved oxygen**: Critical for oxidative degradation; monitored with
  membrane electrodes or optical sensors.
- **pH**: Affects enzyme activity and substrate solubility; buffered systems
  or pH-stat control may be required.
- **Redox potential**: Particularly important for reductive dechlorination
  processes where specific redox conditions are needed.
- **Contaminant concentration**: Periodic sampling for residual pollutant
  levels, metabolite profiling, and toxicity assessment.
- **Enzyme activity**: LME assays (LiP, MnP, laccase) to verify active
  fungal metabolism.
- **Biomass**: Estimated through ergosterol content, glucosamine measurement,
  or DNA-based quantification.

## Scale-Up Considerations

Scaling fungal bioremediation from laboratory to field scale requires attention
to mass transfer limitations, heat dissipation, inoculum production (see
[[singh-mushroom-spawn-production-grain-spawn]]), and process economics.
Solid-state systems scale more predictably than liquid systems due to simpler
geometry and reduced mixing requirements. Pilot-scale demonstrations are
essential before full-scale deployment.
