# Water Quality

> From: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 7, 8, 10, 13

Water quality is a central concern in permaculture design. Mollison emphasises that the only long-term insurance of good water supply to a settlement is by rigorous control of a forested catchment, including a total ban on biocides and metallic processing. As there are few such clean areas left in the world, house roof tanks and biological treatment systems must serve for the foreseeable future.

## Sources of Water Pollution

Water pollutants include:

- Heavy metals (lead, zinc, copper, cadmium, mercury).
- Acid-forming compounds; a pH less than 5.5 increases metallic pollution.
- Acid rain dissolves out aluminium and heavy metals from soil and rock.
- Phosphates, sodium, and potassium salts from agricultural runoff.
- Organic compounds, biocides, and sewage.
- The 30-40 additives commonly introduced into town water supplies.

These additives represent the end point of the technological fix: pollution is "fixed" by further pollution. Preservation of the cleanliness and variety of water habitats is as critical to the survival of nations as is the conservation of soils.

## Domestic Water Quality

Given that most dust and leaves are removed from roof catchment, residual organics are usually harmless. These "fix" as an active biological velvety film on tank walls and bases. Taps or outlet pipes are normally fitted 15-20 cm above any tank floor to allow such a film to remain.

A net or bag of limestone, shell, or marble chips is suspended in the tank. This creates hard (alkaline) water, preventing heavy metal uptake from the water and decreasing the incidence of heart attacks in those using the tank. Washing and shower water can be soft (acid) but the water we drink is best made alkaline for the sake of health.

## Lagoon Treatment Systems

The biological treatment of polluted water proceeds through stages of increasing purity:

- Anaerobic lagoon: pH 6.2-7.8, produces methane.
- Facultative lagoon: pH 7.5-8.2, B.O.D. reduces.
- Aerobic lagoon: pH 7.5-8.5, B.O.D. only 3-57 mg/l, suspended solids 32-50 mg/l.

Alternating aerobic and anaerobic (A.A.A.) digestion is most efficient if run at intervals of two hours in six (30% of the time). Nitrogen was reduced from 20 mg/l to less than 5 mg/l, phosphorus from 8.5 mg/l to less than 1 mg/l when ferric chloride was supplied.

## Biological Purification by Plants

Waters polluted with metals, biocides, or sewage can be cleaned by travelling through reed beds of Scirpus, Typha, and Juncus; or by harvesting off floating plants such as water hyacinth. For untreated sewage, a holding time of 10-12 days is necessary, or travel through a series of maze-like gravel-filter canals with floating weeds and sedges.

Species recommended for water purification:

- Phragmites communis and Typha spp.: Flocculate colloids, dry out sludges, eliminate pathogens.
- Schoenoplectus spp.: Takes up copper, cobalt, nickel, manganese; exudes mould antibiotics.
- Scirpus spp.: Breaks down phenols, including toxic pentachlorophenol.
- Juncus spp. (especially J. effusus): Breaks down chlorinated hydrocarbons, cyanide compounds, thiocyanates, and phenols in as little as 7+ hours.

Low to zero populations of E. coli, coliform bacteria, Salmonella, and Enterococci are found after treatment via the above species. Virus and worm eggs are also eliminated.

## Natural Swimming Pools

For swimming pools and less polluted systems, a pumped "cycle" of water through ferns, rushes, and watercress suffices to remove urine and leaves. Such pools need a 23-30 cm coarse river gravel base, with intake pipes below, and a skimming notch for leaves.

## Domestic Greywater Treatment

Domestically, a comfrey bed is one way to absorb the faecal products of animals, where wash-water from yards or pens is available. Comfrey can stand heavy inputs of raw faeces in solution, and the crop may then be used for fodder or trenched for "instant compost" under other species.

## pH in Aquaculture

The pH of water in treatment ponds varies both long-term and in 24-hour cycles. In clogged algal waters at night, pH may climb higher than the normal aerobic range of 7.5-8.5. Monitoring pH is essential for managing pond health and fish production.

## Aquaculture Water Quality Parameters

Mollison emphasises that water quality in aquaculture systems is governed by interconnected chemical and biological factors.
Dissolved oxygen (DO) is the single most critical parameter: warm-water fish generally require DO above 5 mg/l, while cold-water species like trout need levels above 6 mg/l.
DO fluctuates with temperature (warmer water holds less oxygen), time of day (algae produce oxygen during photosynthesis and consume it at night), and organic loading (decomposing waste consumes oxygen).
Temperature affects both metabolic rates and dissolved gas solubility; a 10°C increase roughly doubles fish metabolic rate while reducing oxygen solubility by approximately 2 mg/l.
Ammonia from fish waste and uneaten feed is highly toxic; it is converted to less toxic nitrite and then relatively harmless nitrate by nitrifying bacteria in biofilters.
This nitrogen cycle is the foundation of recirculating aquaculture systems and is closely related to the nitrogen cycling in [[permaculture-designers-manual-pig-systems]].

## Mycoremediation of Water Contaminants

Fungi offer additional pathways for water purification beyond the reed-bed plants Mollison describes.
Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) have demonstrated the ability to break down petroleum hydrocarbons, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and some pesticides in contaminated water.
Mycelial mats can be established in flowing water systems where they act as biological filters, secreting extracellular enzymes that decompose complex organic molecules into simpler, less toxic compounds.
White-rot fungi such as Phanerochaete chrysosporium produce lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase — enzymes capable of degrading a wide range of xenobiotic compounds including DDT, PCBs, and dioxins.
These fungal remediation systems can be integrated into the multi-stage lagoon treatment designs Mollison outlines, positioned after initial anaerobic digestion and before the final aerobic polishing stage.

## Constructed Wetlands Design

Building on Mollison's lagoon treatment systems, constructed wetlands have become a well-established technology for wastewater treatment in both rural and urban settings.
Subsurface flow constructed wetlands (SFCWs) pass water horizontally through a gravel bed planted with reed-bed species, preventing surface water exposure and reducing odour and mosquito breeding.
Free water surface constructed wetlands (FWSCWs) allow water to flow above the substrate through emergent vegetation, providing habitat for wildlife in addition to water treatment.
Hybrid systems combining vertical and horizontal flow stages achieve higher treatment efficiency than single-stage designs.
Typical retention times range from 2-5 days for secondary treatment and 5-10 days for tertiary treatment.
Loading rates depend on climate, wastewater strength, and treatment objectives; in temperate climates, a subsurface flow wetland requires approximately 2-5 square metres per person equivalent for domestic sewage treatment.

## Heavy Metal Speciation and Bioavailability

The toxicity of heavy metals in water depends not just on total concentration but on chemical speciation — the form in which the metal exists.
Free ionic forms (e.g., Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺) are far more bioavailable and toxic than metal complexes bound to organic molecules or precipitated as insoluble compounds.
pH is the primary factor controlling speciation: at lower pH, metals tend to exist as free ions, while at higher pH they precipitate as hydroxides or carbonates.
This is why Mollison's recommendation of limestone in rainwater tanks is effective — the alkaline conditions promote precipitation and adsorption of dissolved metals onto calcium carbonate surfaces.
Redox potential (Eh) also affects speciation; under reducing conditions (low Eh), many metals form insoluble sulfides.
Understanding these chemical relationships is essential for designing effective [[permaculture-designers-manual-wetland-treatment]] systems for metal-contaminated water.

## Water Quality Monitoring

Regular monitoring of water quality parameters is essential for managing treatment systems effectively.
Mollison's emphasis on pH cycling in aquaculture ponds extends to all biological treatment systems.
Key monitoring parameters include pH, dissolved oxygen, temperature, electrical conductivity (a proxy for total dissolved solids), and oxidation-reduction potential.
Biological indicators such as algae colour and density, invertebrate diversity, and plant health provide additional qualitative assessment.
Simple colourimetric test kits are sufficient for most permaculture-scale applications, while digital meters provide greater precision for research or commercial systems.
Monitoring frequency depends on system type: aquaculture ponds may require daily DO checks during summer, while reed-bed treatment systems can be assessed weekly or monthly once established.
