## Mycoremediation of Water Contaminants

Fungi offer additional pathways for water purification beyond the reed-bed plants Mollison describes.
Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) have demonstrated the ability to break down petroleum hydrocarbons, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and some pesticides in contaminated water.
Mycelial mats can be established in flowing water systems where they act as biological filters, secreting extracellular enzymes that decompose complex organic molecules into simpler, less toxic compounds.
White-rot fungi such as Phanerochaete chrysosporium produce lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase — enzymes capable of degrading a wide range of xenobiotic compounds including DDT, PCBs, and dioxins.
These fungal remediation systems can be integrated into the multi-stage lagoon treatment designs Mollison outlines, positioned after initial anaerobic digestion and before the final aerobic polishing stage.

## Constructed Wetlands Design

Building on Mollison's lagoon treatment systems, constructed wetlands have become a well-established technology for wastewater treatment in both rural and urban settings.
Subsurface flow constructed wetlands (SFCWs) pass water horizontally through a gravel bed planted with reed-bed species, preventing surface water exposure and reducing odour and mosquito breeding.
Free water surface constructed wetlands (FWSCWs) allow water to flow above the substrate through emergent vegetation, providing habitat for wildlife in addition to water treatment.
Hybrid systems combining vertical and horizontal flow stages achieve higher treatment efficiency than single-stage designs.
Typical retention times range from 2-5 days for secondary treatment and 5-10 days for tertiary treatment.
Loading rates depend on climate, wastewater strength, and treatment objectives; in temperate climates, a subsurface flow wetland requires approximately 2-5 square metres per person equivalent for domestic sewage treatment.

## Heavy Metal Speciation and Bioavailability

The toxicity of heavy metals in water depends not just on total concentration but on chemical speciation — the form in which the metal exists.
Free ionic forms (e.g., Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺) are far more bioavailable and toxic than metal complexes bound to organic molecules or precipitated as insoluble compounds.
pH is the primary factor controlling speciation: at lower pH, metals tend to exist as free ions, while at higher pH they precipitate as hydroxides or carbonates.
This is why Mollison's recommendation of limestone in rainwater tanks is effective — the alkaline conditions promote precipitation and adsorption of dissolved metals onto calcium carbonate surfaces.
Redox potential (Eh) also affects speciation; under reducing conditions (low Eh), many metals form insoluble sulfides.
Understanding these chemical relationships is essential for designing effective [[permaculture-designers-manual-wetland-treatment]] for biological purification systems.
