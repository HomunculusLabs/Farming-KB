# Cool Climate House Design

In Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison outlines principles
for designing dwellings in cool and temperate climates where heating demand
dominates energy consumption. The approach integrates [[passive-solar-design]]
design, thermal mass management, wind protection, and frost avoidance into
a holistic strategy that minimises energy input while maximising comfort.

## Solar Orientation

The primary facade of a cool climate house faces the equator (north in the
southern hemisphere, south in the northern hemisphere) to capture maximum
solar radiation during winter when the sun tracks low across the sky. Key
orientation principles:

- Long axis oriented east-west to maximise the north/south-facing wall
- Windows concentrated on the solar-facing elevation (60-70% of glazing)
- Minimal glazing on the cold south-facing side (10-15% for cross ventilation)
- Eaves designed to exclude high-angle summer sun while admitting low-angle
  winter sun; the critical angle varies with latitude
- Deciduous trees or vines on the solar side provide summer shade but drop
  leaves to allow winter sun penetration

## Insulation

Insulation is the first line of defence against heat loss. Mollison
emphasises that insulation is far more cost-effective than heating
equipment over the building's lifetime.

Wall and ceiling insulation requirements:

- Roofs/ceilings: R-3.5 to R-5.0 minimum, as rising warm air creates the
  greatest heat loss through the roof plane
- Walls: R-2.0 to R-3.5 depending on climate severity
- Floors: R-1.5 to R-2.5 where floors are suspended over cold air spaces
- Windows: double glazing minimum; triple glazing in severe climates with
  insulating curtains or shutters for night-time use

Insulation materials in the permaculture context favour natural and
recycled options: straw bale, wool, cellulose (recycled newspaper),
cork, and earth-berm construction. These materials have low embodied
energy and can be sourced locally.

## Thermal Mass

Thermal mass absorbs heat during the day and releases it slowly at night,
buffering temperature swings. In cool climates, thermal mass is placed
inside the insulated envelope where it can absorb direct solar gain:

- Concrete or stone floors in sunlit rooms
- Internal masonry walls (rammed earth, stone, compressed earth block)
- Water containers or phase-change materials as supplementary mass
- Earth-sheltered or earth-bermed walls providing both mass and insulation

Critical design rule: thermal mass must be INSIDE insulation, not outside
it. A stone wall on the exterior of insulation provides no thermal benefit
to the interior space.

Thermal mass sizing depends on climate. In mild cool climates with sunny
winters, moderate mass floors and one internal mass wall suffice. In
continental climates with large diurnal swings, extensive mass throughout
the building stabilises indoor temperatures.

## Wind Protection

Cold winds dramatically increase perceived temperature and heat loss from
buildings. Mollison integrates wind protection at multiple scales:

- Site level: windbreaks planted on the windward side of the site,
  typically evergreen conifers or casuarina at 5-10x height distance
  from the house
- Building level: the house shape minimises wind pressure; square or
  rounded plans with hip roofs perform better than long rectangular plans
  with gable ends facing the wind
- Detail level: sealed construction eliminating draughts, air locks at
  entries, and adjustable shutters on windward windows

Wind data from local weather stations or observed vegetation flagging
(known as Griggs-Putnam index) determines the prevailing wind direction
and severity for each site.

## Frost Avoidance

Frost is a critical design consideration in cool climates affecting both
buildings and surrounding plantings:

- Cold air drainage: frost settles in low points as cold air flows downhill.
  Houses and frost-sensitive gardens are placed above the frost pocket on
  mid-slope positions
- Thermal belt: the zone just above valley floors where cold air pools
  provides frost-free conditions; Mollison identifies this as ideal house
  placement in hilly terrain
- Radiant frost protection: dense evergreen planting on the south side of
  gardens and buildings reduces radiative cooling to the night sky
- Frost hollows: low areas where frost regularly forms are used for
  frost-hardy elements like roads, sheds, and firewood storage rather than
  for sensitive plants or living spaces

## House Form and Earth Integration

Mollison advocates earth-sheltered and earth-bermed designs for cool
climates where the ground temperature below frost depth remains relatively
stable year-round. Earth integration strategies include:

- Excavating into a north-facing slope (southern hemisphere) so earth
  covers the cold rear wall while the sunny front remains exposed
- Berming earth up to window sill level on east, west, and south walls
- Green roofs with soil and vegetation providing additional insulation and
  managing stormwater
- Underground or partially buried structures using the earth's thermal
  inertia for temperature regulation

## Heating Systems

While passive design minimises heating demand, Mollison recognises that
supplementary heating is needed in most cool climates. Preferred systems
are:

- Wood-fired masonry heaters with high thermal mass that burn hot and
  clean, releasing heat slowly over 12-24 hours
- Radiant floor heating using hot water from solar thermal collectors or
  wood combustion
- rocket stoves for efficient cooking and space heating with minimal
  fuel consumption
- Passive solar air heaters: glazed panels on south walls that pre-heat
  incoming ventilation air
