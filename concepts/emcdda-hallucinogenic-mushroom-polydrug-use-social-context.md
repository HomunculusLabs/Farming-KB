## EMCDDA Hallucinogenic Mushroom Polydrug Use and Social Context

## The EMCDDA 2006 Thematic Study

In 2006, the European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction published a comprehensive thematic paper on [[emcdda-epod-project-emerging-trend-detection-methodology]] of hallucinogenic mushroom use within the EU. It examined prevalence and patterns of use, markets and availability, dose and effects, consequences for health, and legal and prevention responses. The study was part of the broader E-POD project for developing a European system to detect emerging drug trends.

## The Return to Nature Megatrend

Interest in natural hallucinogens appears related to a "return to nature" trend that has been facilitated by the rapid expansion of internet sales and information. Consumer market analysts identified a megatrend for people to actively seek out more intense experiences and be more prepared to experiment with new products. Increasing interest in natural and organic products enhanced the appeal of hallucinogenic mushrooms over synthetic hallucinogens.

### The Role of Lifestyle Trends

This case study highlighted the importance of lifestyle trends and economic interests in the diffusion of and responses to an emerging drug trend. The marketing of hallucinogenic mushrooms by smartshops, internet shops, and market stalls caused the trend to spread rapidly through the late 1990s and early 2000s. Future work on emerging drug trends must consider the crucial part that contextual forces play.

## Clubbing Survey Prevalence Data

Drug surveys conducted in club settings show that prevalence of illegal drug use is consistently higher than prevalence among general or school populations. Non-probability surveys in dance music settings across Belgium, France, Hungary, Italy, the Netherlands, and the UK recorded diverse estimates for hallucinogenic mushroom use, ranging from 9% of clubbers in Budapest to 55% in France.

### The UK Clubbing Data

In a survey of 760 clubbers from six south-east England nightclubs in 2000, overall lifetime prevalence for hallucinogenic mushroom use was 24%. Among current drug users in these samples, prevalence rose to 44%. In comparison, lifetime prevalence among 16 to 19 year olds in the general population was much lower at 11%. This demonstrates the strong association between mushroom use and broader polydrug involvement.

## EU General Population Prevalence Statistics

Recent general population survey data from 12 EU Member States indicate that among young people aged 15 to 24, lifetime use of hallucinogenic mushrooms ranges from less than 1% to 8%. The Netherlands, Czech Republic, UK, Germany, and Ireland have the highest prevalence estimates, while the lowest are reported in Lithuania, Hungary, and France. Prevalence is generally much lower than for cannabis.

### Current Use Patterns

The proportion of current users among those who have ever used is lower for hallucinogenic mushrooms than for cannabis and ecstasy. The effects of hallucinogenic mushrooms appear to limit the appeal of regular use. A 2003 French survey of 17 to 18 year olds reported 4.3% lifetime prevalence, 2.9% last year prevalence, and 1% last month prevalence.

## ESPAD School Survey Data

The ESPAD school survey conducted in 2003 across 22 EU Member States, Norway, and four candidate countries found that among 15 to 16 year old students, lifetime use of hallucinogenic mushrooms ranges from 0% to 8%. The Czech Republic, Netherlands, France, and Belgium have the highest estimates. Three countries reported zero lifetime prevalence: Cyprus, Finland, and Romania.

### School Students and Other Drug Use

Analysis across 11 EU Member States demonstrates that school students aged 15 to 16 are much more likely to have used hallucinogenic mushrooms if they have used LSD, ecstasy, amphetamines, or cocaine than if they have used only cannabis or legal substances. Lifetime use equals or exceeds ecstasy use in nine countries. Male students generally have higher prevalence rates than females.

## Cultivated Versus Wild Mushrooms

Hallucinogenic mushrooms grow wild in much of Europe, yet it appears that most recreationally used mushrooms are cultivated rather than picked wild. Mushrooms are sold both as fresh and dried products and for home cultivation using mushroom prints, spore bags, and growkits. The most common wild European species is [[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]] and P. tampanensis form sclerotia and are sold as "truffles" or the "philosopher"s stone." These products are widely available through Dutch smartshops and online retailers.

### Brand Names and Marketing

Hallucinogenic mushrooms have been sold under labels like "Herbal highs" following the late-1990s trend toward organic and herbal products. The term "philosopher"s stone" describes sclerotia of P. mexicana sold by many smartshops. Brand names and user terms sometimes reflect changing market strategies, with products marketed alongside herbal ecstasy and Salvia divinorum.

## Barriers to Sustained Use

User accounts suggest that hallucinogenic mushrooms may not be viewed in a sufficiently favourable light to repeat the experience. Unpredictable potency and negative effects such as nausea, panic attacks, and lack of sociable effects may all contribute to limiting recreational use. The Dutch risk assessment found that users do not find mushroom use a particularly pleasant experience, as it lacks the positive entactogenic effects of ecstasy.

### Nausea and Physical Discomfort

Over a quarter of those who had used hallucinogenic mushrooms in the last year experienced nausea or vomiting, according to a UK clubbing magazine survey. Physical effects include dizziness, weakness, muscle aching, shivering, abdominal pain, and pupil dilation. Eating mushrooms mixed with food may cause additional nausea and vomiting, further discouraging sustained use.

### Psychological Barriers
