# Cannabis for Sleep Disorders and Insomnia

## Overview of Insomnia

Insomnia is difficulty or inability to fall or stay asleep. While many
possible causes exist for insomnia, it is interesting to note that its
preceding symptoms mirror the condition's effects. Depending on the severity,
insomnia affects mental and physical performance as well as emotional
expressions (sufferers become moody or irritable).

Insomnia may produce symptoms of:

- Anxiety and depression
- Low energy and affect
- Fatigue
- Hallucinations
- Lowered immunity
- Hormone disruption

Each of these carries its own set of possible complications, including
hypertension or [[blesching-cannabis-heart-disease]].

If insomnia follows acute pain, jet lag, or a new work schedule, it is
usually self-correcting with time. Insomnia due to other causes is more
complex. Insomnia caused by anxiety or use of addictive substances (such as
methamphetamines) requires more time and active intervention to resolve.

## Orthodox Treatment

Orthodox medicine's approach to insomnia includes diagnostics to determine
underlying causes. Common pharmacological treatments include:

- Psychoactive benzodiazepines such as Valium (can be addictive)
- Sedative-hypnotic drugs like Ambien (can be addictive)
- Opiates for the co-treatment of pain (can be addictive)
- Antidepressants (possible serious side effects)

While these agents help manage the symptoms, none of these medications cure
chronic insomnia, which usually returns once the medication is stopped.

## Evidence Base: CHI Value 5 (1 Study Reviewed)

## Cannabis and Insomnia

Like cannabis, the majority of common pharmacological treatments for insomnia
affect both body and mind. Most studies on the effects of cannabis and sleep
took place in the 1970s and 1980s. These [[hofmann-lsd-clinical-research-early-studies]] revealed that cannabis
had a varied impact on sleep. The plant seemed to act like a sedative in some
ways but also reduced deep sleep.

None of the early studies explained this paradoxical influence.

### The Farnborough Study (2004)

The Farnborough study revealed an apparent self-balancing mechanism between
CBD and THC. Dose-dependent THC appears to have sedative effects, while
dose-dependent CBD appears to have some alerting properties.

In this study, 8 healthy human volunteers (4 males, 4 females; 21 to 34
years old) received an oromucosal spray of either placebo or 15 mg THC or 5 mg
THC with 5 mg CBD or 15 mg THC with 15 mg CBD.

The study concluded that THC appears to have some sedative effects while CBD
appears to have some alerting properties.

### Clinical Implications of the THC/CBD Balance

This self-balancing mechanism between THC and CBD is significant. It suggests
that whole-plant cannabis preparations, which contain both cannabinoids in
varying ratios, may provide a more balanced sleep effect than isolated
cannabinoids alone. The sedating properties of THC combined with the modulating
properties of CBD may explain why many patients report improved sleep with
whole-plant cannabis use.

## Strain-Specific Considerations

Practical reports from patients using cannabis to improve their sleep have
shown that the use of indica and indica-dominant strains with their particular
mix of cannabinoid ratios, namely a relatively lower THC:CBD combination,
encourages sedation, relaxation, and grounding effects.
