# Compost Biology and Soil Food Web Inoculation

Updated: 2026-04-18

Compost is a whole universe of diverse [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] organisms. The numbers of organisms per teaspoon in compost are extraordinary: up to a billion bacteria, 400 to 900 feet (150 to 300 meters) of fungal hyphae, 10,000 to 50,000 protozoa, and 30 to 300 nematodes. In addition, compost contains all manner of microarthropods and sometimes worms. It teems with life.

## Compost as a Soil Food Web Tool

Compost has been used to support soil food web organisms long before anyone knew they existed. Properly made compost can inoculate an area with microbes to support a complete soil food web. It is a proven, effective growing medium.

Key rules (from [[teaming-with-microbes-overview]]):

**Rule #4**: Compost can be used to inoculate beneficial microbes and life into soils around your yard, and introduce, maintain, or alter the soil food web in a particular area.

**Rule #5**: Adding compost and its soil food web to the surface of the soil will inoculate the soil with the same soil food web. The organisms will spread life as far as they can -- it is microbial manifest destiny.

## Not All Composts Are the Same

Most gardeners assume all compost is the same, but this is not the case. By using soil food web science, you can make either:

- **Bacteria-dominated compost**: Higher in bacterial populations, suited for vegetables, annuals, and lawns
- **Fungi-dominated compost**: Higher in fungal populations, suited for perennials, trees, and shrubs

What goes into the compost does affect what comes out. The biology and pH of finished compost vary depending on ingredients and management.

### Characteristics of Finished Compost
- Never smells bad (anaerobic microbes indicate problems)
- Should smell earthy and fresh
- Always has a rich, dark, coffee color
- Contains the entire complement of soil food web microorganisms: fungi, bacteria, protozoa, and nematodes
- Full of organic matter providing living space and nutrients for microbes
- Must actually be composted -- partially composted material can create problems including tying up nitrogen as the process completes next to the plant

### Modern Caveat
Know what was used to make the compost. Many chemicals we seek to avoid do not break down quickly enough in compost. Compost should ideally be tested for nutrient content and microbial activity.

## Matching Compost to Plant Needs

You can best satisfy a plant's nutrient needs by adding compost with the right microbial domination:

- **Vegetables and annuals**: Prefer bacterially dominated soils (bacteria release nitrogen in nitrate form, preferred by these plants)
- **Trees, shrubs, and perennials**: Prefer fungally dominated soils (fungi release nitrogen in ammonium form, preferred by woody plants)
- **Lawns**: Bacterial dominance
- **Orchards and forest gardens**: Fungal dominance

## Compost Application Methods

### Surface Application (Recommended)
Applying compost to the soil surface allows the organisms in the compost to spread into the soil naturally. The biology travels downward, establishing populations throughout the root zone.

### Incorporation
While digging compost into soil is traditional, it disrupts existing soil food webs (especially fungal hyphae and mycorrhizal networks). Surface application is gentler and allows natural colonization.

## Compost Nutrient Content

Compost is technically not a fertilizer but rather a [[soil-building-ecological-garden]]. However, it does contain nutrients because organic matter and any clay in the compost will have an impact on its [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] (cation exchange capacity). Compost provides a great environment for the microbial activity needed to cycle natural materials into nutrients.

## Earthworm Castings

Earthworm castings concentrate nutrients: 10 times more potassium, 5 times more nitrogen, 7 times more phosphorus, 3 times more magnesium, 1.5 times more calcium, and 1.4 times more humus than the soil that went into the worm. Castings contain a high concentration of organic matter and N-P-K, as well as calcium, copper, zinc, and other minerals.

## The Role of Protozoa in Compost

Protozoa — single-celled organisms including flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates — play a critical but often overlooked role in compost biology and soil fertility. Protozoa are the primary predators of bacteria in the soil food web, and their feeding activity is the main mechanism by which plant-available nitrogen is released in bacterial soils. When protozoa consume bacteria, they excrete excess nitrogen in the form of ammonium (NH4+), which is immediately available for plant uptake. A single protozoan can consume 10,000 bacteria per day, and the nitrogen mineralization rate from protozoan grazing is 40-80 times faster than the rate at which bacteria release nitrogen through natural turnover. Compost that contains a healthy protozoan population (10,000-50,000 per teaspoon, as noted above) provides not just the organisms themselves but the trophic cascade that makes nitrogen available to plants. This is why compost provides a slow-release fertilizer effect: the bacteria and protozoa in the compost continue their predator-prey cycle in the soil, steadily releasing nitrogen in sync with plant demand. See [[query-what-is-the-soil-food-web-and-why-does-it-matter]] and [[microscope-identification-soil-organisms]] of these key organisms.

## Biochar and Compost Interactions

Biochar — a carbon-rich porous material produced by pyrolyzing biomass in low-oxygen conditions — interacts synergistically with compost. When biochar is pre-charged or "inoculated" by mixing it with compost before soil application, the porous structure of the biochar becomes colonized by compost microorganisms. The biochar then serves as a habitat refuge for these organisms, protecting them from predation and environmental stress while providing a large internal surface area for microbial colonization. Studies have shown that biochar-compost mixtures increase microbial biomass and diversity beyond what either material provides alone. The biochar also helps retain nutrients that would otherwise leach from the compost, particularly in sandy or highly weathered soils. However, applying raw (uninoculated) biochar to soil can temporarily tie up nitrogen as soil microbes colonize the fresh carbon surfaces, so pre-inoculation with compost is recommended. See [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] and [[biochar-in-agriculture]] for detailed guidance.

## Making Bacteria-Dominated vs. Fungi-Dominated Compost

The microbial balance of finished compost can be intentionally steered by controlling the ingredients and management practices during the composting process. To produce bacteria-dominated compost (suited for vegetables, annuals, and lawns), use a higher proportion of green, nitrogen-rich materials such as grass clippings, kitchen scraps, coffee grounds, and young green manure crops. Turn the pile frequently (every 2-3 days) to maintain aerobic conditions that favor bacterial growth. Keep the pile relatively moist but not saturated. The resulting compost will be dominated by bacterial biomass with a bacterial:fungal ratio of roughly 100:1 or higher.

To produce fungi-dominated compost (suited for trees, shrubs, perennials, and orchards), incorporate more woody, carbon-rich materials such as wood chips, sawdust, fallen leaves, and shredded bark. Turn the pile less frequently — every 7-10 days or even less — as fungal hyphae are disrupted by turning and take longer to reestablish. Larger particle sizes and woody materials provide the structural complexity that fungi prefer. Fungi-dominated compost typically has a bacterial:fungal ratio closer to 1:1 or even 1:10. This compost is particularly valuable for establishing trees, restoring forest edges, and supporting [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] in perennial plantings.

## Compost Tea as Liquid Inoculant

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT) extends the reach of compost biology by allowing the microbial populations in finished compost to be multiplied and applied as a liquid spray. To make AACT, high-quality compost is placed in a mesh bag and suspended in aerated water with a food source (such as molasses for bacteria or fish hydrolysate for fungi) for 24-36 hours. The aeration encourages rapid microbial reproduction, producing a liquid teeming with billions of bacteria, fungal hyphae, protozoa, and nematodes per teaspoon. This liquid can be applied as a soil drench to inoculate garden beds, or as a foliar spray to coat leaf surfaces with beneficial microorganisms that compete with pathogens. Compost tea is especially valuable for large-scale operations where applying bulk compost is impractical, or for rescuing soils that have been degraded by chemical inputs. See [[compost-tea-aact]] for detailed brewing instructions and [[thermal-composting-vs-aact]] for a comparison of approaches.

## Thermal Composting vs. Vermicomposting

Thermal (hot) composting and vermicomposting produce different biological products suited to different applications. Thermal composting relies on thermophilic bacteria that generate temperatures of 130-160 degrees F, killing most weed seeds, pathogens, and potential contaminants. The resulting compost is biologically diverse but tends to be more bacterially dominated due to the high temperatures that favor bacteria over fungi. Vermicomposting (composting with earthworms, typically Eisenia fetida) operates at ambient temperatures and produces a product rich in humic acids, plant growth hormones, and a microbial community dominated by mesophilic organisms. Worm castings contain higher concentrations of available nutrients than thermal compost, with the nitrogen largely in nitrate form that is immediately available to plants. The two methods can be combined in a complete system: hot composting processes large volumes of material quickly and sanitizes contaminated inputs, while vermicomposting produces a premium amendment for seed starting, potting mixes, and foliar tea production.
