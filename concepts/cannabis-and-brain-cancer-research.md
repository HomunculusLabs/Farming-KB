## Mechanisms of Action

Cannabinoids attack brain cancer through multiple independent pathways, which is particularly significant because tumors often develop resistance to single-mechanism therapies:

- **Apoptosis induction:** THC and CBD trigger programmed cell death in glioma cells through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and mitochondrial pathway activation. CBD selectively induces ROS-mediated stress in cancer cells while sparing healthy neurons.

- **Angiogenesis inhibition:** Cannabinoids suppress VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) and other pro-angiogenic factors, cutting off the tumor's blood supply. This mechanism was identified by Complutense University researchers in Madrid.

- **Cell cycle arrest:** THC down-regulates E2F1 and Cyclin A, key proteins that drive cell division. This arrests the cancer cell cycle, preventing proliferation.

- **Invasion and metastasis inhibition:** Down-regulation of TIMP-1 and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) reduces the tumor's ability to invade surrounding healthy brain tissue.

- **CB2 receptor-mediated effects:** CB2 receptors are upregulated in glioma cells compared to normal brain tissue, providing a degree of selectivity. JWH-133, a CB2-selective agonist, demonstrated significant anti-tumor effects without psychoactive side effects.

- **TRPV1 receptor activation:** CBD and other cannabinoids activate the TRPV1 (vanilloid) receptor, contributing to calcium influx and apoptosis in cancer cells.

## Clinical Trial Evidence

The 2006 Madrid clinical trial was groundbreaking as the first administration of THC directly into brain tumors of human patients. The intracranial THC administration was well-tolerated and did not produce significant psychoactive effects at the doses used. This study established safety and feasibility for subsequent clinical investigations.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the promising pre-clinical evidence, large-scale randomized controlled trials in humans are still needed. The heterogeneity of glioblastoma subtypes means that individual responses may vary significantly. The optimal ratios of THC:CBD, dosing schedules, and combinations with standard therapies remain under investigation. Patients should view cannabinoid therapy as a potential adjunct to — not a replacement for — conventional cancer treatment.
