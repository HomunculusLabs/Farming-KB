## Models from Nature

All organisms and species obtain a yield from their environment adequate to
sustain them; those that fail quickly disappear. Darwin's emphasis on
competition and predation was influenced by his observation of early industrial
England. Peter Kropotkin provided an important counterpoint with diverse
examples of cooperative and symbiotic relationships in nature and human
history.

Competition in nature helps test the vigour and fitness of individual organisms
for particular conditions. Predation, where it removes weaker individuals,
contributes to survival of the fittest. In human systems, comfort and excessive
protection from challenges can lead to self-satisfied, lazy, and eventually
dysfunctional behaviour.

## The Maximum Power Law

The Maximum Power Law, drawn from thermodynamics and systems ecology, states
that the maximum useful power output from any energy conversion process occurs
at an intermediate level of efficiency — not at maximum efficiency, where the
system stalls, nor at zero efficiency, where energy is wasted.

In a simple heat engine, maximum power is achieved when roughly half the
energy is converted to useful work and half is dissipated as waste heat. This
pattern holds across biological and self-organising systems: there is a
balance between dissipating energy for no purpose and squeezing maximum
efficiency out of every unit.

The consumer economy encourages consumption for its own sake — analogous to an
engine running with no load, consuming energy but doing little useful work. The
conservation ethic focuses on maximum efficiency — analogous to an engine
loaded almost to stalling, efficient but not very productive. [[permaculture-design]]:
design systems and organise lives to obtain a yield in ways that maximise the
power of useful work from whatever is done. The concept of "useful work" is
central and can be difficult to define in complex biological and human systems.

Howard Odum identified ways in which successful self-organised systems maximise
power: they develop storages of high-quality energy, feed back work from
storages to increase inflows, recycle materials, organise control mechanisms,
set up exchanges with other systems, and contribute useful work to surrounding
environmental systems. This list provides scope for including ethical and
cooperative behaviour.

## Staple Foods and Net Energy

In pre-industrial societies, people focused on staple crops — high-yielding
carbohydrate-rich foods that provided clear net energy returns. Because the
yield was relatively high, investing more work in selection, cultivation,
protection, and storage was worthwhile. These crops were often reflected in
cultural and religious practices, such as worship of maize in pre-Columbian
America.

The net energy gain from staples provided sustenance and allowed use of other
resources that could not provide a net energy return. Detailed energy analysis
of traditional New Guinea swidden agriculture showed very high net energy gains
from food gardens but a net loss from raising pigs. However, pigs provided
needed protein and social functions, demonstrating cross-subsidisation of
important functions from net energy supply.

## Hardy and Self-Reliant Species

Hardy and self-reliant species are essential in low-energy sustainable systems.
By selecting locally adapted and self-reproducing plants, the designer minimises
resources required to maintain gardens, farms, and forests. These species are
"competent" at obtaining a yield through minimal demands, relative efficiency,
or associated symbiotic relationships such as nitrogen-fixing microbes.

The obsession with maintaining control limits development of systems less
dependent on non-renewable inputs. In healthy broadacre farm landscapes, there
should be vigorous self-reproducing plants. Farm landscapes with no need to
remove excess tree and shrub growth are generally degrading and dying
landscapes.
