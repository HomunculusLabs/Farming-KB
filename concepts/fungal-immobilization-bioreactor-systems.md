# Fungal Immobilization and Bioreactor Systems

## Overview

Fungal immobilization in bioreactor systems represents a key technology for
applying ligninolytic fungi to the treatment of contaminated effluents and
wastewater. Rather than using free mycelia in suspension, immobilization
techniques entrap or attach fungal biomass to solid supports, dramatically
increasing the effective biomass concentration within reactors and enabling
continuous or semi-continuous treatment processes. This approach is central to
[[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]] produce extracellular
[[staycare-lignin-peroxidase-and-pollutant-degradation]], and
[[singh-fungal-pellet-formation-bioremediation]]**: Naturally grown, self-immobilized pellets of
   T. versicolor have been used directly in packed bed reactors (Bajpai et
   al., 1993), avoiding the need for artificial support materials.

### Reactor Configurations

Bioreactor designs for immobilized fungi vary considerably:

- **Packed bed reactors**: Immobilized fungal biomass is packed into columns
  through which effluent flows. Bajpai et al. (1993) achieved 93%
  decolorization of Kraft effluent in packed beds with mycelial pellets at
  38-hour hydraulic retention time over 30+ days.

- **Fluidized bed reactors**: Biomass carriers are suspended in upward-flowing
  effluent, improving mass transfer and reducing clogging. Pallerla &
  Chambers (1996) reported 69% decolorization with T. versicolor immobilized
  on polyurethane in fluidized bed systems.

- **Trickling filters**: Effluent is distributed over immobilized biomass
  supported on a fixed bed, with air circulation providing oxygen. Messner
  et al. (1990) pioneered this approach for fungal effluent treatment.

- **Hollow fiber membrane bioreactors**: Semipermeable membranes separate
   fungal biomass from the effluent while allowing passage of degraded
  products and enzyme molecules (Chambers & Cheng, 1991).

### Enhanced Performance Through Immobilization

The performance advantage of immobilized systems over suspended cultures is
dramatic. Pal, Lewandowski & Armenante (1995) found that degradation rates
for chlorinated phenols by P. chrysosporium in packed bed reactors were two
orders of magnitude greater than in shake flasks. Similarly, wood chip
