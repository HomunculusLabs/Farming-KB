# Ale vs Lager Fermentation

The fundamental division in the beer world is between ales and lagers. This distinction is not about ingredients or color but about the yeast and fermentation process. Understanding the differences between ale and lager fermentation is essential for any brewer and illuminates how a single category of beverage can produce such an enormous range of flavors and styles.

## The Two Yeast Families

### Ale Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae)

Ale yeast is a top-fermenting yeast, meaning it tends to rise to the surface of the fermenting beer during active fermentation. This is the same species used in [[holzer-stone-fruit-varieties-and-growing]]. Belgian strains can produce intense fruity, spicy, and phenolic character. American strains tend to be cleaner and more neutral. German wheat beer strains produce the distinctive clove and banana character (4-vinyl guaiacol and isoamyl acetate) of Hefeweizen.

### Lager Yeast (Saccharomyces pastorianus)

Lager yeast is a bottom-fermenting yeast that works best at cool temperatures, typically 45-55 degrees F (7-13 degrees C). It is a hybrid species that evolved from a mating event between S. cerevisiae and S. eubayanus, a cold-tolerant [[beer-styles-overview]] do not fit neatly into the ale/lager dichotomy:

### Kölsch

A hybrid style fermented with ale yeast at cool temperatures (60-65 degrees F), then cold-conditioned like a lager. The result is a clean, delicate beer with subtle fruitiness.

### Altbier

German altbier is fermented with ale yeast at cool temperatures (58-65 degrees F), then cold-conditioned for several weeks. The name "alt" means "old," referring to the traditional ale fermentation method.

### California Common (Steam Beer)

Fermented with a lager yeast strain at warmer ale temperatures (58-65 degrees F). This style originated in 19th-century California, where refrigeration was unavailable but lager yeast was used because of the German immigrant influence.

### Belgian Strong Ales

Fermented with specialized Belgian ale yeast at temperatures that can exceed 75 degrees F. These yeasts produce complex spicy, fruity, and sometimes solventy character at high temperatures but remain drinkable due to the high alcohol content and malt backbone.

## Spontaneous and Mixed Fermentation

Beyond the ale/lager divide, several traditions use wild or mixed fermentation:

- Lambic: Spontaneously fermented by [[lactobacillus]] bacteria, producing a sour, tart beer
- Flanders red ales: Long-aged in oak barrels with a complex mix of yeast and bacteria including Brettanomyces, Lactobacillus, and Acetobacter
