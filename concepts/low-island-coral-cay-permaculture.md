# Low Island and Coral Cay Permaculture Strategies

Coral sand cays and low atolls present some of the most challenging environments for human habitation and food production. Lying predominantly within 28° of the equator, these fragile landforms demand exceptionally careful design to address the interrelated challenges of freshwater scarcity, hurricane vulnerability, poor soil nutrition, and limited dietary diversity. Permaculture strategies for these environments focus on working with natural systems—particularly the freshwater lens, coastal vegetation succession, and marine resources—to create sustainable and resilient island communities.

## Environmental Conditions

### Soil Characteristics
- **pH values** typically range from 8.0 to 9.5 (highly alkaline)
- **Texture**: Coralline sand over a hardpan of caliche (calcrete)
- **Nutrient deficiencies**: Widespread deficiency of iron, zinc, molybdenum, and boron in both soils and plants
- **Caliche problems**: Calcrete is worsened by application of superphosphate

### Correcting Soil Alkalinity
Elemental sulphur, iron sulphates, and humus added to garden soils and planting holes lower the pH toward productive ranges. Humus sources on atolls include:
- Palm fronds
- Coconut husks
- Tree trunks and leaf litter from pioneer species such as *Casuarina equisetifolia*, sea grape (*Coccoloba unifera*), coastal shrubs (*Scaevola*, *Tournefortia*, *Pemphis*), mangroves, and *Barringtonia* trees

### Phosphate Sources
Seabird guano typically provides sufficient phosphates if colonies of terns, gulls, boobies, frigate birds, and shearwaters are protected or encouraged. Where natural seabird colonies are absent, domestic poultry (pigeons, quail, pheasants, geese, ducks, and chickens) can serve as phosphate sources, especially since predators such as feral cats and foxes are usually absent on remote islands.

## The Freshwater Lens

The sole natural freshwater source on low islands is the **water lens**—a body of fresh rainwater trapped in the sand below the caliche layer, floating on denser seawater beneath. Understanding and protecting this resource is the single most critical factor in atoll survival.

### How the Water Lens Works

As rain falls on the island surface, it absorbs into soils and percolates through the caliche to accumulate as a freshwater body floating on seawater. The lens itself leaks slowly to sea between or below tide levels. Given that the beach berm is typically 2–4 meters above sea level and the inner atoll about 2–3 meters, the water lens is a remarkably thin and fragile resource.

### Preventing Water Lens Pollution

Pollution prevention is paramount because any contaminant introduced at the center of the island can affect the entire water lens, all plants, and all people. Key design principles:

1. **Peripheral settlement**: All settlement, toilet areas, processing facilities, livestock, and even gardens should be kept at the periphery of the island
2. **Protected interior**: The island interior should be devoted to dense natural stands of food and native trees, kept free of pesticides, industrial pollutants, and fuels
3. **No central pollution sources**: Any polluting activity at the island center potentially contaminates the entire freshwater supply

### Increasing Potable Water Supply

Three primary strategies augment the natural water lens:

**Roof runoff collection**: Rainwater is directed to house tanks, constructed above or below ground from reinforced cement-coral-sand mix (widely used in construction on all but the most remote atolls).

**Deep mulches**: Extensive mulching with coconut fronds, banana leaves, and other organic materials retains soil moisture and reduces evaporation. Growing ever-abundant coconut, banana, arrowroot, papaya, and legumes provides continuous leaf and trunk materials for garden mulching.

**Leaf ferment seals (gleys)**: Gently-sloped coral pits can be lined with approximately 20 cm of mashed or shredded banana, papaya, and other soft green leaf material. This is covered with plastic until fermenting (sometimes only 4–5 days), then pumped full of water from the freshwater lens. Made carefully, such surface ponds also accept roof runoff. Success follows careful small-scale trials before scaling up to significant ponds for ducks, garden irrigation, water leaf crops, and animal drinking water. As such ponds can be sources of cross-infection in children, it is sensible to swim in the lagoon, drink tank water, and use seawater for many toilet purposes.

### Biological Water Storage

Approximately 12 coconut trees provide for one person's drinking water for the year. Baobabs and other large trees also store significant quantities of freshwater in their trunks.

## Planting Trees on Coral Cays

### Tree Planting Technique

Trees on coral cays require special planting techniques to overcome the caliche barrier:

1. **Clear** a patch of topsoil and break open the caliche
2. **Excavate** a pit approximately 40 × 40 cm wide and 60–80 cm deep
3. **Create** a humus pile in the pit from domestic and plant wastes, plus sulphur and mineral trace elements
4. **Plant** a coconut or other seedling tree

### Root Development

Tree roots spread out above the caliche layer, while feeder roots penetrate below the caliche to access the water lens—typically 0.75–1.0 meters below the surface, forming a saturated sand layer 1.0–2.0 meters deep. Trees are thus well anchored and easily obtain water.

## Pit Gardens for Food Production

The primary method for productive atoll gardening is the **pit garden**, which accesses moisture trapped near the water table.

### Construction

A pit garden approximately 8 × 15 meters is excavated to about 2 meters depth, sloped for stability. The sloped sides are stabilized as steps using coconut logs or caliche blocks, with spoil banked around the rim. Thick mulch is applied to the base and behind the stepped terraces.

### Zoned Planting (Base to Crown)

The pit garden is planted in vertical zones according to moisture availability:

1. **Base (damp, mulched)**: Watercress, parsley, chives, brassicas, taro, kangkong, and salad greens
2. **First terrace (18–25 cm above base)**: Tomatoes, peppers, taro, sweet corn, beans, peas, and taller crops
3. **Second terrace (25–60 cm above base)**: Banana, papaya, sweet potato, and cassava (all provide mulch)
4. **Third/highest terrace**: Cassava, sweet potato, banana, dry-tolerant vegetable crops, and mulch trees such as *Leucaena*, *Gliricidia*, *Tipuana tipu*, *Moringa oleifera*, and local legume trees and shrubs. Palms provide frond mulch and high shade. Vines (passionfruit, four-winged bean) climb on the taller plants.

### Surface Gardens

Under light palm-legume canopies, boxes made from palm logs hold thick mulch and household waste for surface garden beds. These can be planted with potato, yam, sweet potato, and standard vegetable crops. The thicker the mulch, the less watering required.

### Essential Elements for Atoll Gardens

The four essentials for productive atoll food production are:
1. **Log boxes** for raised bed gardening
2. **Pit gardens** for moisture access
3. **Thick mulch** for moisture retention and soil building
4. **High shade canopy** from palms and legume trees

### Dietary Staples

Atoll food systems rely on:
- Coconut (multipurpose: food, water, mulch, building material)
- Root crops (taro, cassava, sweet potato, yam)
- Fruits (banana, papaya, breadfruit where available)
- Salad vegetables from pit and surface gardens
- Fish and shellfish from lagoons and coasts

## Humid Tropical Coast Stabilisation and Shelterbelts

### Natural Beach Profile

The natural profile of undisturbed beach vegetation forms a convex shape into the wind. These uncut shores are remarkably stable, demonstrating the effectiveness of natural vegetation succession for coastal protection.

### Tropical Hedgerow Establishment

A classical tropical hedgerow is established by cultivating, manuring, and placing a dripline along the hedgerow site, then setting out concurrently:

- **Tall grasses or clump bamboo** (Pennisetum is typical)
- **Quickset cuttings** of *Erythrina fusca* or *Jatropha*
- **Seedlings** of *Leucaena* or *Acacia*
- **Occasional palms** as seedlings, preferably those with spiny trunks or mid-ribs for additional barrier function

### Orchard Windbreak Systems

When establishing wind-tender orchard crops such as citrus or avocado:
1. Cast up earth ridge systems and plant *Pennisetum* hedges every 30 meters crosswind (and cross-slope if possible)
2. This creates parallel lines (wind and slope aligned), diamond patterns (wind at angle to slope), or squares (wind and slope at right angles)
3. Pay particular attention to ridge tops in wind-prone areas

As the young orchard grows, every second *Pennisetum* row can be combined with *Leucaena*, and every third and every ridge row with *Acacia* and palms. Inner rows can later be removed as mulch as the system matures.

### Complicating the Hedgerow

Productive complexity can be added to windbreaks:
- Tomato trellis on *Leucaena*
- Passionfruit climbing on most trees
- Mango serving as both windbreak and food crop
- *Eugenia* replacing some *Leucaena*
- Clump bamboo providing structural material and effective windbreak

Cross-slope ridges become long-term soil and water traps, accumulating mulch for subsequent evolutions. Over time, these evolve into terraces of remarkable stability. Roads should be provided with concrete or stone fill on the downhill side of mounds.

## Hurricane Damage Prevention

### Airstrip Design

Airstrips on atolls require careful design to prevent catastrophic damage:
- Aligned approximately 20° off prevailing winds
- Both ends and sides should be lined with tall palms and trees
- Coastal borders are especially critical
- Light planes drop in using rudders to straighten below tree crown level
- Airstrips carelessly aligned have destroyed whole islands when hurricane winds cut them in two along the airstrip line

### Reef Entry Design

When creating gaps in reef for boat access:
- Cut gaps on the slant through the reef at east or west quarters
- Winds blow southeast to northwest south of the equator, northeast to southwest north of the equator
- Gaps should be in the most sheltered sector of the reef in any wind conditions
- Width should be just enough (6–10 meters) to admit a vessel or barge

### Coastal Shelterbelt Sequence

Sandy coasts of all atolls and cays need a sequence of perennial shelterbelts to hold shorelines against hurricanes:

1. **Beach level**: Beach convolvulus (*Ipomoea pes-caprae*) and beach pea
2. **Beach berm**: Dense shrubbery of vines, *Tournefortia*, *Scaevola*, and in sheltered bays, mangroves
3. **Behind the frontline**: A 5–6 tree deep layer of coconut palm, *Casuarina*, *Coccoloba*, *Barringtonia*, and other hardy beach trees

This dense frontline shelterbelt protects all inland plantings from hurricane-force winds and salt spray.

## Design Priorities for Low Islands

The comprehensive design priorities for low island systems include:

1. **Hurricane protection**: Erosion prevention and damage mitigation for plantations and coasts
2. **Water security**: Water lens protection and pollution prevention
3. **Nutrition**: Avoiding poor nutrition from limited diets (typically high in carbohydrate and oxalic acids)
4. **Crop extension**: Expanding the range of vegetables and fruits available
5. **Water conservation**: Extending water storage and conserving freshwater supplies
6. **Conservation**: Protecting natural vegetation and unique birds, reptiles, and lagoon or reef fauna
7. **Aquaculture**: Using shallow marine waters for aquaculture and pond fish
8. **Energy**: Developing local energy resources
