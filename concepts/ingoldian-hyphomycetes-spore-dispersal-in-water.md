# Ingoldian Hyphomycetes

Ingoldian hyphomycetes are a diverse group of [[bloomfield-ingoldian-aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal]] that produce remarkably shaped conidia (asexual spores) adapted for dispersal and attachment in flowing water. Named for their discoverer, Cecil Terence Ingold, who first described them in 1938, these fungi play a crucial role in the [[decomposition]] of submerged leaves in freshwater streams and rivers worldwide.

## Discovery

Ingold found the spores in foam that collected at the base of waterfalls and around half-submerged branches in a brook near his home in Leicester, England. The foam is a natural phenomenon caused by fatty chemicals released from decaying leaves; these substances have detergent-like properties and form bubbles when water is churned violently.

Cakes of foam trap and concentrate the same kinds of spores all over the world. The universality of foam as a spore-collecting mechanism means that Ingoldian fungi can be sampled almost anywhere by skimming foam from a waterfall or riffle.

## Spore Morphology

Ingoldian conidia come in an extraordinary variety of shapes, all optimized for life in moving water. Some are star-shaped (tetraradiate) with thin limbs connected to a central hub. Others are crescent-shaped or sigmoid -- an elongated S twisted into an extended helix. A few combine these features and resemble balloon-animal creations.

The largest Ingoldian spore is produced by Actinospora, spanning 0.5 millimeters in diameter. This is the largest spore formed by any fungus, though it remains unlisted in The Guinness Book of Records. Single spores of Actinospora are easily visible with a magnifying glass or hand lens.

## Dispersal Mechanics

The unusual shapes of Ingoldian spores are not for slowing descent through water, as was once hypothesized. Meticulous experiments demonstrated that spores with microscopic arms fall through the water column as fast as spherical spores of similar mass. Water represents a viscous medium for microscopic particles, slowing descent to rates of millimeters per minute.

Instead, spore shape is explained by search theory, originally developed for antisubmarine warfare. Analysis shows that the probability of hitting a target increases in proportion to the length of the "search vehicle." A star-shaped spore has a much higher probability of encountering a submerged leaf than a spore of equal mass with a condensed form.

Because the appendages of tetraradiate spores are no thicker than hyphae, these decorative structures span an enormous area with a minimal investment of cytoplasm. The evolutionary economy of this solution is remarkable: maximum search area with minimum material cost.

## Attachment Process

When the tip of a star-shaped arm strikes a solid surface, water movement forces the spore to pivot around this point of attachment until additional arms complete a three-point landing. Each arm then swells and secretes adhesive to cement the tripod to the leaf.

Sigmoid spores tumble through the water flow, exploring a large volume, and roll or somersault until making firm contact. The variety of shapes within the Ingoldian group reflects different solutions to the same problem: how to maximize the probability of contact with a submerged surface.

## Ecological Role

Ingoldian spores reach concentrations of 20,000 per liter of water in late fall and early winter. They colonize submerged leaves and spawn mycelia that soften and macerate plant tissues by secreting cellulose-degrading enzymes.

Hyphae thoroughly insinuate themselves in decaying leaves, raising the protein content of the debris as dead plant material is transformed into living fungus. This microbial processing is a boon for tiny crustaceans that flourish on the resulting compost. The relationship between Ingoldian fungi and freshwater invertebrates represents a critical link in stream food webs.

## Life Cycle Complexity

Ingoldian fungi are the asexual phases (anamorphs) of fungi that produce sexual spores in other habitats. The mycelia that form tetraradiate and sigmoid spores in streams are connected to the fungal-kingdom-classification. Spore formation is not restricted to submerged locations; Ingoldians are also found on plants that overhang creeks, entering the water during rainstorms.

## Aero-Aquatic Relatives

Related to the Ingoldians are the aero-aquatic fungi, whose conidia are also shaped for aquatic life but float on the water surface rather than sinking. Their inflated, buoyant structures trap air and allow them to remain at the surface until they contact suitable substrates. Like Ingoldian conidia, aero-aquatic spores are clones of the parent organism.

The relationship between Ingoldian and aero-aquatic fungi illustrates the adaptive flexibility of fungi in colonizing aquatic environments through multiple evolutionary pathways.
