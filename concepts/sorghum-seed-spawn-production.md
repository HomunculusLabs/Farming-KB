# Sorghum Seed Spawn Production

Sorghum seed spawn production is a grain spawn method documented in the FAO training manual *Mushroom Cultivation for People with Disabilities* (2001). This technique bridges tissue culture on PDA medium and full-scale substrate bag inoculation, serving as the multiplication step in the mushroom cultivation chain. Sorghum is one of several grains suitable for spawn production, alongside millet and wheat.

## Role in the Cultivation Cycle

Grain spawn production is Step 4 in the FAO training sequence, following tissue culture selection on PDA medium (Step 3) and preceding substrate bag production (Step 5). The purpose is to multiply a small amount of mycelium from a PDA plate into a much larger quantity of inoculum that can colonize substrate bags efficiently.

The sequence is: tissue culture on PDA medium, transfer to sorghum seeds for multiplication, then use the colonized sorghum grains to inoculate substrate bags. Each step increases the volume of viable mycelium available for cultivation.

## Grain Selection Criteria

Multiple grain types can serve as spawn substrate, with sorghum being the primary grain used in the FAO Thailand training program. All grains used for spawn must meet these quality standards:

- **Recent harvest**: Grains should be from the current or most recent harvest season
- **Intact kernels**: Few broken kernels, as cracked grains are more prone to contamination
- **Low contamination**: Minimal foreign matter, dust, or mold spores on the grain surface
- **No pests**: No visible fungal growth or insect damage
- **Moisture content**: No more than 12% humidity to prevent premature spoilage

The choice between sorghum, millet, and wheat depends on local availability and cost. Sorghum is preferred in many tropical programs because of its availability, appropriate kernel size, and reliable performance.

## Materials Required

- Sorghum seeds (sufficient quantity for planned production)
- Flask-type glass bottles
- Cotton (gauze) for plugging
- Paper squares (7 cm by 7 cm) for covering plugs
- Rubber bands or plastic necks for securing covers
- Alcohol lamp for flame sterilization
- Alcohol bottle for surface disinfection
- Autoclave or pressure sterilization chamber
- Laminar flow cabinet or protected clean environment
- UV lamp for pre-operation sterilization
- Special inoculation needle with insulated handle
- PDA culture bottles with verified clean mycelium

## Preparation Process

### Step 1: Soaking

Sorghum seeds are soaked overnight at a ratio of 2 liters of water per 1 kilogram of grain. After soaking, the seeds are washed and strained to remove all excess water. The soaking hydrates the grains and begins the softening process needed for mycelial colonization.

### Step 2: Partial Cooking

The soaked sorghum seeds are steamed for 30 to 45 minutes. The goal is to soften the grains and cook approximately 25% of them. Partial cooking is critical: overcooking makes the grains mushy and prone to anaerobic conditions, while undercooking leaves the endosperm too hard for efficient mycelial digestion.

### Step 3: Drying and Cooling

After cooking, the water is drained and the sorghum seeds are spread out to cool down and decrease surface moisture. Proper drying prevents excess condensation inside the bottles during sterilization, which could create wet spots favorable for bacterial contamination.

### Step 4: Bottling

The prepared sorghum seeds are filled to approximately three-quarters of each bottle volume, leaving headspace for air exchange. Cotton plugs are carefully prepared and inserted tightly into the mouth of each bottle. The cotton allows gas exchange during mycelial growth while filtering out airborne contaminants.

### Step 5: Sterilization

All prepared bottles are transferred to a sterilization chamber. The sterilization protocol requires:

- Complete expulsion of all air from the chamber before the sterilization cycle begins
- Pressure maintained at 15 pounds per square inch (approximately 121 degrees Celsius)
- Duration: 30 minutes for small chambers, 45 minutes for medium chambers
- Gradual cooling after the cycle to prevent thermal shock to the glass

Sterilization eliminates all competing microorganisms, creating a selective environment where only the introduced mushroom mycelium can grow.

### Step 6: Inoculation

The inoculation procedure requires strict aseptic technique in a laminar flow cabinet:

1. Clean the laminar flow chamber thoroughly with alcohol
2. Transfer PDA bottles, sorghum seed bottles, paper, and rubber bands into the chamber
3. Light the UV lamp for 10 to 15 minutes, then turn it off while leaving the laminar flow fan running
4. Clean both hands with alcohol and insert them into the chamber
5. Flame the inoculation needle until it turns red, passing it through the alcohol flame
6. Allow the needle to cool for 15 to 20 seconds
7. Use the needle to cut a small square (5 mm by 5 mm) of PDA containing visible white mycelium
8. Close the PDA bottle immediately
9. Flame around the mouth and shoulder of the sorghum seed bottle
10. Open the spawn bottle near the flame to maintain a sterile air curtain
11. Insert the needle and deposit the PDA mycelium piece into the middle of the sorghum seeds
12. Ensure the PDA mycelium does not touch any surface before entering the bottle
13. Keep the bottle mouth higher than the bottom at all times to prevent contamination falling in
14. Close the bottle immediately
15. Place a paper square over the cotton plug and secure with a rubber band or plastic neck

### Step 7: Labeling

Each inoculated bottle is labeled with:

- Date of inoculation
- Spawn number or strain reference
- Reference to the source PDA culture
- Time of inoculation

## Incubation and Monitoring

Full colonization of sorghum grain mycelium takes approximately 10 to 15 days, depending on the mushroom species and incubation conditions. During this period, bottles should be stored in a clean, warm location appropriate for the species being cultivated.

### Monitoring for Contamination

Regular inspection of bottles during incubation is essential. Contaminated bottles must be identified and removed promptly. Signs of contamination include:

- Dark spots or lines (fungal contamination)
- Slimy or foul-smelling grains (bacterial contamination)
- Unusual colors (green, black, pink mold)

### Expected Contamination Rate

A loss of approximately 3% is considered normal and acceptable. Higher rates indicate problems with sterilization technique, grain preparation, or aseptic procedure during inoculation.

## Storage of Mature Spawn

Once fully colonized, sorghum seed spawn should be kept in a cool place or in the vegetable compartment of a refrigerator. This slows the mycelial metabolism and extends the viable storage period. Spawn should be used as soon as possible after full colonization for maximum vigor, but short-term refrigerated storage is acceptable when scheduling requires it.

## Cleaning and Recycling

Contaminated bottles are transferred to a dedicated cleaning site and cleaned as normal glassware. All organic material must be removed before bottles can be reused. Proper cleaning protocols prevent the buildup of contaminant spores in the laboratory environment.

## Advantages of Sorghum for Spawn Production

Sorghum offers several advantages as a grain spawn substrate:

- **Kernel size**: Individual sorghum grains are small enough to provide many inoculation points per volume, promoting rapid and uniform substrate colonization
- **Hardiness**: Sorghum tolerates the cooking and sterilization process well without disintegrating
- **Availability**: Widely grown in tropical and subtropical regions where mushroom training programs operate
- **Nutrition**: Provides adequate carbohydrates and minerals for robust mycelial growth
- **Cost**: Generally inexpensive compared to specialized spawn substrates

## Data Collection

The FAO manual emphasizes the importance of observing and collecting data throughout the spawn production process. Notes should include dates, contamination rates, colonization speed, and any anomalies observed. This systematic record-keeping enables trainees to draw conclusions about their technique and make improvements over time.
