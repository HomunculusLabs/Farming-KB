Palmer compares this to a farmer who has only ever seen russet apples, calling them "apples" and classifying all other varieties as "crabs" unfit for food. The person who claims they can "tell a mushroom from a toadstool" is really saying they can identify one single species -- the rest remain a mystery.

He notes the absurdity of the term "toadstool" itself: the word is English, but the most common table fungus of England is a mushroom -- and in no other country do the words have different meanings. The idea that the toad "sits on his natural seat" is simply fiction; no one has ever observed a toad on a fungus.

The "mystery" surrounding mushrooms is a modern phenomenon. When Palmer was a boy, all his questions about animals, birds, or fishes were answered, "but when I broke open the snowy white puff-ball the inquiry was met with, 'It's a toadstool, child; throw it away, it's poison.'" Magazine literature offered little more, recycling the same stories year after year: the rapidity of growth story, the Emperor Claudius story (poisoned by a boletus), the Czar Nicholas story (died from a muscarius), and the Russian intoxicant-fungus story.

## Worthless Popular Tests

Palmer systematically debunks every traditional test, drawing on the authority of Dr. F.S. Cordier of Paris and his own twenty years of experimentation:

- **Silver spoon test**: Edible mushrooms often discolor silver just as readily as eggs or onions do, from sulfur gases in decomposition -- not from poison. M. Cordier tested this with Agaricus bulbosus and many other poisonous mushrooms; they never blackened silver.

- **Peeling test**: Nearly all fungi peel off readily. Many poisonous Amanitas peel beautifully. One of Palmer's acquaintances secretly tested every new mushroom handed to him by trying to peel the edge -- despite Palmer having identified them as safe.

- **Solid vs. hollow stem**: Useless as a general rule; both edible and poisonous species occur in both categories. An Italian boy told Palmer "the mushrooms have solid stems and the toadstools are hollow" -- the boy was right for his limited experience, but it would not be wise to eat all fungi with solid stems.

- **Growing location**: "Damp, dark places" produce some of the very best mushrooms. The elm-tree mushroom grows on wood. The edible champignon has a very thin cap and grows in open meadows. Cordier lists fifty varieties contradicting the "dark places" rule.

- **Color changes on bruising**: Many excellent edible species turn red, blue, or green when cut. The maned mushroom turns to ink. The deadly Amanita remains purest white under all conditions. "There is nothing more false" than the claim that color change indicates poison.

- **Thin cap / eccentric stem**: The fairy-ring champignon has a very thin cap yet is excellent. The elm-tree mushroom has a stem set in the side.

- **Milky juice**: The brown milk-mushroom (Lactarius volemus) is unquestionably edible, contradicting the claim that all milky mushrooms are dangerous.

- **Equal gills**: The entire Russula family has equal gills, many of them edible.

- **Snail/slug marks**: Palmer never saw a snail on a mushroom; slugs sometimes spoil portions but do not indicate edibility.

- **Salt test**: No value whatsoever.

- **Onion/pith test**: Not confirmed by experience.

- **Fetid smell**: Some edible species have strong odors; the elm-tree mushroom smells like fish.

## Why Popular Tests Persist

Palmer notes that "each chooses the most ready way to recognize his or her friend" -- people who know one edible mushroom naturally look for the trait that distinguishes it and assume that trait universally separates good from bad. But any test to be of value must know no exception, and "if there is one point on which all students or authorities are agreed, it is that no such touchstone is infallible."

He singles out Professor Palmer of Harvard (not himself) for repeating "the old, worthless traditions and signs to enable one to tell a mushroom from a toadstool" in charts brought from Europe -- even in the face of twenty years of accumulated evidence to the contrary.

## Palmer's Testing Method for New Species

1. First, identify the mushroom to family and species -- never confuse it with its nearest neighbor.
2. Chew a small piece of the flesh (avoiding the spore-bearing surface) on an empty stomach, first thing in the morning.
3. If no ill effects, increase the dose daily until a whole specimen is eaten.
4. Once an edible quality is proven, it never changes. The same species is edible whenever and wherever found, like a chestnut.

He tested "perhaps a hundred varieties" and "never yet been deceived into making a meal of poisonous fungus." His correspondent Paul Casamajor, described as "one of the most distinguished chemists in the world" and a friend of Pasteur, acted on Palmer's suggestions and "gathered with his children a basket full, being careful to exclude all not Russula, and then, tasting each specimen, he cooked them and made an evening meal with perfect safety."

## The Sensory Training Approach

Palmer emphasizes that the eye and hand acquire "a delicacy of analysis impossible to communicate in words." This talent is essential. No one should attempt to gather mushrooms who has not the power to fix in the mind the exact characteristics of any specimen, so as not to be in the least danger of confounding it with its nearest neighbor.

Constant use develops extreme sensitiveness to the properties of fungi -- the system does not become hardened but rather repels noxious elements, and is repelled by them in quantities which a stranger to such diet would not notice. "The intuition of woman, the cleverness with which the feminine mind grasps at an idea over which the stronger sex will reason mentally for hours, is nowhere more valuable than in the pursuit of this study."

His most promising student was a maiden of ten who often put certain kinds into her mouth while picking them. She handled edible Russulas side by side with deadly Amanitas -- both families having pure white gills and stem, with members of corresponding shades -- yet "her instinct was unerring." He never saw her look for the distinguishing marks; "she simply recognized them as we know pears from apples."

## Critical Safety Rules

- Never eat mushrooms in other than a perfectly fresh, hard, young, and sound condition.
- Only eat species you have positively identified by prior study.
- Young specimens with substance too solid to admit of fermentation are safest.
- Beware of specimens that have absorbed excess water from dew or rain, which can cause acidulous fermentation (Palmer was made ill by a normally edible Boletus in this condition).
- Do not eat mushrooms from dust-heaps, brick-yards, or city offal, even if the species is normally edible. The maned mushroom from clean lawns is delicious; from polluted sites, it has no flavor and in quantity would prove deleterious.
- Avoid mixing unknown species in a single dish; an amateur may cook three mushrooms believing them identical and swallow poison with the second.
- Because many mushrooms do not agree with the human system, it does not follow that they are deadly poisons.
- "We must use the same discretion daily employed in selecting other food."

## Historical Context

Palmer began his studies around 1874 and notes that this was only the second American book on the subject. He lamented the state of both popular and scientific ignorance. A prominent physician wrote to him in 1877: "I must plead to a lamentable ignorance, which I regret to find I share with all medical men with whom I have conversed on the subject."

Even among the learned, prejudice persisted. "It seemed to me in 1874, and it appears still, that such general ignorance as exists in regard to a large, well-defined, and important natural kingdom is nothing less than a disgrace."

Palmer's most valuable work was his 1879 article in the Moniteur Scientifique of Paris -- the first attempt "to segregate the Amanite from other non-esculent mushrooms, to define the action of a poison to be found by every wayside, to show how useless had been ordinary remedial measures, and make important suggestions."

Despite decades of advocacy, the general public continued to rely on folklore. "A kind adviser thus dissented from my selection in opposition to such distinction: 'If you are going to feed your friends on those things you'd better invite the undertaker, and tell him to bring along as many coffins as there are seats at the table.'"

Another gentleman was "surprised to see my basket well filled in the afternoon; he thought, like the manna of ancient story, mushrooms only came in the morning." These misconceptions persist even among the educated. A prominent Boston physician told Palmer that "he ate only mushrooms from cans when he tasted them at all."

Palmer concludes: "There is nothing more healthful for both mind and body than the pursuit of any out-door science." His ultimate message is that mushroom foraging is not a mysterious or dangerous pursuit when approached with the same common sense applied to any other food -- but that common sense must include genuine knowledge, not folklore.

## How Many People Get Poisoned?

Palmer estimated that of the roughly 1,000 noticeable fungi in New England, the majority are non-esculent (tough, woody, bitter, slimy, or repellent) but only four varieties of the Amanita have caused authenticated fatal poisonings. The actual danger is small compared to the perceived danger, but the consequences of a mistake are severe.

He notes that "every year the public journals chronicle deaths from toadstool eating, but they never mention what species of toadstool occasions them." Many cases attributed to "toadstool poisoning" are actually caused by eating mushrooms in a partly decomposed state, overloaded with seasoning and rich additions, eaten in large quantities when in an improper condition for food, the leathery nature of some varieties, or want of sufficient cooking.

## The Foraging Mindset

Palmer advises the beginner to "select twenty varieties, easily recognized, advise that all others be let alone, and follow my own counsel" -- rather than trying to learn every species. This is the practical approach: master a manageable set of safe, easily identifiable species and ignore the rest.

He also warns against overconfidence. A gentleman once called at his office in a state of hysteria saying four diners at his table had all fallen ill. "You could feed your family on a plant of which you had never before tasted!" Palmer exclaimed. "Although there are tons of wholesome food wasted in our forests day by day, yet promiscuous use of these could not fail to produce many deaths. Is this not true of any other order of plants?"

The key insight is that foraging skill develops through patient, incremental learning -- exactly like learning to identify any other group of plants or animals. There is no shortcut, no magic test, but also no need for despair. As Palmer demonstrated over twenty years, a dedicated student can learn to gather and eat dozens of species with perfect safety.
