# Community Finance and Alternative Economies

updated: 2026-04-18

Community finance encompasses the informal and formal economic structures that enable local self-reliance, keep capital circulating within a community, and reduce dependence on external financial systems. [[bill-mollison]]'s Permaculture [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]] (Chapter 14) provides a comprehensive framework for building community-based economic systems.

## The Informal Economy

### Barter Systems
Barter is common in rural and neighborhood areas where people know one another. At the household level, people exchange garden products, share labor, and exchange goods and services. Work groups of 3-5 people may form to build houses, create gardens, or do housework in round-robin patterns until all needs are met.

### The Bendigo Home Builder's Club (Victoria, Australia)
A group of 35 people building individual homes. Members pay $5/year (mainly for newsletter costs). Each member can be recipient or donor of labor. Units of exchange are hours of labor; all labor is considered equal. Standard labor exchange forms are legally binding. A Labour Organiser sorts out balance of payments.

### Community Barter Clubs
Work on a system of debits and credits where residents offer goods, services, and skills. A credit equals one hour. Transactions need not be one-to-one -- the organiser keeps records so services can flow as long as desired. Particularly valuable for unemployed or underemployed community members.

### The LET System (Local Employment Trading System)
- Members trade in local "green" dollars
- LET dollars carry no interest; administration charged on cost-of-service basis
- Any member can know the turnover or balance of any other member
- Currency is not issued and cannot be cashed in -- it circulates indefinitely within the community
- Production is never limited by lack of money
- "Foreign" goods become more expensive, local components increase
- An ideal member has many transactions but accumulates modest debits and credits

## The Formal Economy

### Cooperatives
Principles include open membership, democratic organization, strictly limited interest on share capital, surplus belongs to members, education of members, and cooperation between cooperatives.

### The Mondragon Model (Basque Region, Spain)
In less than 30 years, 96 worker-cooperatives emerged employing 17,000 worker-members:
- 10% of profits returned to community; 20% held as capital reserves; 70% distributed to workers
- Cooperatively run bank oversees and finances new cooperatives (up to 90%)
- No redundancies -- workers are retrained and found new jobs
- Pay ratio of lowest to highest never exceeds 1:5
- Each cooperative averages 200-300 worker-owners
- Community has cooperative schools, hospitals, university, housing, and welfare services

### Credit Unions
Any identifiable group of 30+ people can start one. Money borrowed to save energy (gardens, insulation, appropriate vehicles) is soon repaid. Loans amortised over 2-7 years.

### Revolving Loan Funds
People contribute $500-5,000 at 0-10% interest. The fund loans to new community businesses. A skills/research group of volunteers helps select equipment, test markets, train entrepreneurs in bookkeeping, and find resources. Very few publicly needed, publicly funded, and publicly open businesses fail.

### S.H.A.R.E. (Self-Help Association for a Regional Economy)
A local nonprofit corporation in Berkshire, Massachusetts, encouraging small businesses producing necessary goods and services. Members open joint accounts with a local bank at 6% interest; small loans given out at 10% interest. Borrowers must collect community references and demonstrate customer demand.

### C.E.L.T. (Community Enterprise Loans Trust)
New Zealand-wide charitable trust promoting cooperatives and small enterprises. Provides advice, training sessions, and loans. Depositors receive 0-12% interest depending on term and preference.

## Small Business Service Centres
Offer shared facilities: secretarial, bookkeeping, accounting, legal, communication, education, skills registers, research services, and equipment leasing. [[coleman-direct-marketing-and-csa-models]] through group product catalogues cuts out retailers.

## The Segregation-Integration Dynamic

The shift from segregated local and national economies to a highly integrated global economy has been the defining economic trend of the industrial era. The same gravitational pull toward integrated systems that operates in nature is at work in human economic affairs. If we do not creatively design an ethical integration, then larger systemic forces will create one that is less sensitive to human values.

The Yin-Yang symbol of Eastern spirituality, in which polar opposites form an integrated whole and each contains the seeds of the other, is a brilliant graphical representation of the systemic thinking required.

## Local Economic Self-Reliance

As we reduce our dependence on the global economy and replace it with household and local economies, we rebuild economic resilience at the most fundamental level. One permaculture strategy is to encourage household and local economic self-reliance as a foundation for broader [[permaculture-community-resilience]].

At the same time, [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] tends to foster and stimulate new local forms of economic activity. The integration of food production, energy systems, and community enterprises creates economic webs that are more resistant to global economic shocks.

## Global Integration as Inevitable

Whether we are participating as a bioregional community in a globalised world or a corporation seeking market dominance, the dynamics of integration are the same. Rather than constantly struggling to keep the spiritual and the material in separate domains, we need to recognise that the gravitational pull toward integrated systems is always at work.
