# [[geoff-hamilton]] provides detailed guidance
on designing and cultivating a productive herb garden.

## Planning the Herb Garden

The range of herbs you grow will depend on personal priorities: culinary
value, decorative qualities, or a combination. However, you must also
consider practicalities. A young fennel plant seems attractive but will
grow into a bushy plant about 5 ft tall. Sensitive species like bay must
be planted in pots and brought into a frost-free greenhouse during
winter.

Before buying seeds or young plants, ensure you can provide the space and
conditions needed to grow them successfully.

## Design and Layout

### Formal Design
Traditional herb gardens use formal patterns with box or lavender hedges
creating geometric beds. This looks attractive but requires regular
maintenance to keep the hedging trimmed.

### Informal Design
A more natural approach mixes herbs with ornamental plants in borders.
Many herbs are attractive in their own right and work well in mixed
plantings.

### Key Design Principles
- Locate in the sunniest part of the garden (most herbs originate from
  the Mediterranean)
- Ensure good access for harvesting and maintenance
- Plan paths wide enough for comfortable access
- Consider height: place tall herbs (fennel, lovage) at the back
- Group herbs with similar water requirements together

## Soil and Site Requirements

Most herbs prefer well-drained, light soil. Heavy soils are acceptable
if improved by double digging with plenty of organic matter incorporated.
Avoid waterlogging, which will kill most Mediterranean herbs. For very
heavy clay, consider raised beds filled with a lighter soil mix.

## Specific Herbs and Their Cultivation

### Annual Herbs

**Basil**: Grown as an annual in temperate climates. Two types: sweet
(taller, better flavor) and bush. Plant in a sunny, sheltered spot in
the best possible soil. Sow inside in early spring, harden off, and
plant out after frost danger passes. Keep well-watered. Pinch off
flower buds to maintain growth. Leaves can be frozen in ice cubes.

**Borage**: Hardy annual that freely self-seeds. Prefers sunny, open
conditions, tolerant of wide soil range. Sow in mid-spring, thin to
15 inches. Flowers attract bees. Young leaves can be frozen; flowers
can be crystallized.

**Chervil**: Self-seeding hardy annual with spicy, aniseed flavor.
Must have shade and moist soil or it will bolt. Sow in early to mid-
spring, 9 inches apart. Allow some plants to seed, then resow in late
summer. Protect late sowings with cloches.

**Dill**: Hardy annual with feathery leaves and yellow flowers.
Well-drained soil, sunny position. Sow 12 inches apart in mid-spring.
Do not sow near fennel as they may cross-pollinate. Leaves and seeds
can be dried.

**Summer Savory**: Hardy annual, 6 inches apart in mid to late spring.
Sow plenty as yield per plant is low. Both summer and winter savory
attract bees.

### Perennial Herbs

**Chives**: Hardy perennial making attractive border edging with
globular flowers. Prefer moist soil. Divide clumps every three years
in early fall. Cut with scissors leaving half an inch. Can be frozen
in ice cubes but do not dry well.

**Mint**: Several types; apple mint and spearmint best for cooking.
Apple mint has some resistance to mint-rust. Very invasive -- plant in
a bucket sunk into the ground with the rim above soil level. Pick
freely throughout the season. Dig up roots for potting in the
greenhouse for winter supply.

**Balm (Lemon Balm)**: Perennial with pleasant lemon flavor. Trim
regularly to maintain bushiness. Leaves do not dry well but can be
frozen in plastic bags or ice cubes.

**Parsley**: Biennial grown as annual. Several varieties including
curly and French (flat-leaved). Prefers some shade and soil enriched
with well-rotted manure. Seeds take a long time to germinate. Sow
early crop inside in late winter, plant out in mid-spring 6 inches
apart. Also sow direct outside in mid-spring and midsummer.

**Sage**: Perennial evergreen shrub. Prefers sunny, well-drained
position. Propagate by cuttings. Replace plants every 4-5 years as
they become woody.

**Rosemary**: Tender perennial in cold areas. Needs sunny, sheltered
position and well-drained soil. Protect in winter or grow in pots
that can be moved under cover.

**Thyme**: Low-growing perennial. Many varieties including common and
lemon thyme. Attractive flowers attract bees and pollinating insects.
Prefers well-drained soil and full sun.

**French Tarragon**: One of the finest culinary herbs. Prefers rich,
well-drained soil in a sunny position. Does not set viable seed; must
be propagated by division or cuttings. Protect in cold winters.

**Fennel**: Tall perennial reaching 5 ft. Attractive feathery foliage.
Good in borders and as a culinary herb. Self-seeds readily.

## Harvesting and Storing Herbs

For drying, cut leaves before the plant flowers and only take young,
healthy leaves. Tie stems in small bunches and hang in a dry, airy
place. Do not bundle too tightly as this restricts air circulation.
Handle leaves carefully to avoid losing essential oils. Crumble dried
leaves into airtight jars.

For seed saving, harvest stems just as seeds ripen. Hang upside down
with a cloth beneath to catch falling seeds. Remember to save annual
herb seeds for sowing the following year.

## Comfrey

Comfrey deserves special mention as one of the most useful plants in
the organic garden. Its deep roots bring up minerals from subsoil. It
produces large quantities of leaves ideal for mulching, and its high
potash content makes it excellent for liquid manure production.
