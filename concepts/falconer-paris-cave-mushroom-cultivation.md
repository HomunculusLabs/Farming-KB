## Access and Infrastructure

Several of these quarries had horizontal galleries driven into the
calcareous rock from the level of the road, mostly large enough to
accommodate a good-sized cart. However, the majority could only be
entered by vertical shafts 100 to 125 feet deep, down which everything
had to pass. Laborers climbed up and down ladders, while fresh manure
was shoveled down the shaft from above. Waste material and mushrooms
were hauled up in baskets from beneath by means of a windlass.

## Scale of Operations

Some mushroom growers used as much as 2,000 to 3,500 tons of manure
each year for their mushroom beds. To the caves in the immediate
neighborhood of Paris, the manure was hauled out in carts. To more
distant locations such as Merly, it was sent by rail.

## Manure Selection

The mushroom growers made special bargains with contractors for the
manure, as they were very particular about the kind and quality used.
Manure from animals that were worked hard and abundantly fed on dry,
good food was considered the best. The droppings from such animals
were always dry and rich in ammonia, nitrogen, and phosphates.

Manure from entire horses that were worked hard was regarded as the
best, and next in value, that from mules. Manure from pleasure horses
such as carriage and riding horses was considered poor, despite the
high feeding of these animals. Manure from horses fed on grass or
roots, and that of cows, was deemed worthless. Stress was laid on the
importance of having a good deal of urine-soaked straw in the manure,
another reason why manure from draught horses was preferred.

## Manure Preparation

Manure preparation was conducted outdoors near the cave entrances. It
was forked over, shaken up, divested of extraneous matter, and squared
off into a heap forty inches high, trodden down to thirty inches. After
six days it was turned with the outside moved to the inside and dry
parts watered. This was repeated after another six days, and about
three days later the manure was ready for use. It was then shaken up
loosely and dumped down the shaft into the cave.

## Bed Construction

In making the beds, the ground was first marked off. The first bed
was made alongside of the wall and rounded to the front. Other beds
ran parallel and could be straight, crooked, or wavy as the cave
interior suggested. All beds were ridge-shaped: eighteen to twenty
inches wide at the base, eighteen to twenty inches high in the
middle, six inches wide at top, with sloping sides. Pathways twelve
inches wide ran between the beds.

Workmen built the beds by piece-work, receiving one-half cent per
running foot. A good workman could make 240 feet a day. The beds were
built neatly and firmly, but the workmen used no tools. They lifted,
shook up, spread, and built the manure with their naked hands and
packed it firm with their knees.

## Spawn and Spawning

Spawn was obtained from working beds, called "virgin" spawn by the
Paris growers. A bed in capital working order with young mushrooms
appearing was selected, broken up, and the cakes of manure matted
with active mycelium were used for fresh beds. Crops appeared twenty
days sooner than with dry spawn. The spawn was broken into pieces and
inserted in two rows along the sides of the ridges in quincunx
fashion, eight inches apart. Much stress was laid on stratifying dry
spawn on the cave floor about eight days before use to allow moisture
absorption and mycelium growth.

## Earthing Over

The chips and powder of stone from the quarry were sifted, and the
finer part saved and mixed with earth in the proportion of three
parts stone dust to one of earth. This powder was strongly impregnated
with salts advantageous to the mushrooms.

In seven to nine days after spawning, when the beds showed white
filaments radiating in all directions giving a bluish appearance, the
mold was laid on equally and firmly about three-fourths of an inch
deep. It was then thoroughly watered through a fine-rosed watering
pot, allowed to settle overnight, and beaten solid by the back of a
wooden shovel.

## Temperature and Yield

In spacious, high-roofed caves the mean temperature was about 52
degrees Fahrenheit, while in narrow, low-roofed ones it was about
68 degrees. This made a wide difference in bearing time and duration.
On average, the first mushrooms appeared about forty days after
spawning, and the beds continued bearing for forty to sixty days,
with yield diminishing rapidly toward the end.

## Harvesting

Mushrooms were gathered once a day, usually about midnight, so they
could reach the Paris market early in the morning. They ranged from
three-fourths to one and five-eighths inches in diameter and were pure
white. Workmen always gathered by plucking out by the roots, never
by cutting. These mushrooms were likely a close relative of the modern [[mushroom-cultivation-basics]], see the dedicated overview.
