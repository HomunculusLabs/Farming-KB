# Permaculture Principles and Ethics
The ethical foundation and design principles of permaculture provide the
framework within which all specific techniques and strategies operate. Bill
Mollison articulated these ethics and principles throughout the Designer's
Manual, presenting them not as abstract philosophy but as practical guides for
design decision-making. Understanding the ethics and principles is essential for
anyone applying permaculture, as they determine whether a design is truly
permacultural or merely a collection of techniques.
## The Three Permaculture Ethics
### Earth Care
The first ethic, care of the earth, requires that designs maintain and enhance
the health of natural systems. This means building soil, conserving water,
protecting biodiversity, and ensuring that human activities do not degrade the
ecological systems on which all life depends. The [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-5-wilderness-conservation]]
represents the practical expression of earth care in permaculture site design.
Earth care is not passive preservation but active restoration. The permaculture
designer seeks to leave every site healthier and more productive than it was
found. [[mollison-designers-pioneer-species-for-exhausted-tropical-soils]] for land restoration, [[mollison-designers-soil-ph-and-mineral-amendments]], and [[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock]] all serve earth care by
rebuilding ecological function.
### People Care
The second ethic, care of people, requires that designs meet human needs for
food, shelter, energy, community, and meaningful activity. Permaculture is not
about returning to a primitive existence but about creating systems that provide
abundant, high-quality livelihoods while minimising environmental impact. The
[[mollison-designers-settlement-village-design]] chapter explores how people care is expressed through
physical layout, social structure, and economic organisation.
People care also encompasses the social dimensions of design: ensuring equitable
access to resources, creating opportunities for meaningful work, and building
community resilience. The [[mollison-designers-community-garden-design-urban-permaculture]] model demonstrates how food production
can serve both nutritional and social needs.
### Fair Share (Return of Surplus)
The third ethic, sometimes called fair share or setting limits to consumption
and redistributing surplus, requires that designs do not accumulate resources
beyond what is needed and that surplus is shared. This ethic governs the
relationship between the permaculture system and the broader community. Surplus
food, energy, knowledge, and genetic material should be distributed rather than
hoarded.
The [[mollison-designers-legal-trusts-ethical-investment]] framework operationalises fair share by directing capital
toward regenerative enterprises. The [[mollison-designers-seed-saving-variety-preservation]] principle ensures that genetic
resources are preserved and shared for future generations.
## Core Design Principles
### Observe and Interact
Before designing, the practitioner must spend time observing the site and its
patterns through all seasons. The [[mollison-designers-site-analysis-mapping-observation]] process is the formal expression
of this principle. Observation reveals microclimates, water flows, wildlife
patterns, soil conditions, and social dynamics that inform the design.
### Catch and Store Energy
Permaculture designs capture energy flows that would otherwise be wasted and
store them for later use. [[mollison-designers-rainwater-tanks-and-roof-catchment-systems]] store water, [[mollison-designers-dam-construction-and-water-storage]] store both water
and gravitational potential energy, [[mollison-designers-charcoal-production-biochar-soil-amendment]] stores carbon in soil, and [[mollison-designers-solar-water-heating-systems]] store thermal energy. Forests store solar energy as biomass and
release it gradually through decomposition.
### Obtain a Yield
Every element should produce a useful yield. The designer asks of each element:
what does it yield? What does it contribute? Elements without yield should be
replaced. The non-timber forest products concept shows how forests yield food,
medicine, and materials while maintaining ecological function.
### Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback
Designs should include feedback mechanisms that allow the system to self-
regulate. This includes monitoring water quality in [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-water-quality-and-aeration]], observing pest
populations before they reach damaging levels, and adjusting [[mollison-designers-swale-spacing-and-contour-interval]] based
on observed runoff patterns.
### Use and Value Renewable Resources
Permaculture prioritises resources renewed by natural processes. [[mollison-designers-fuelwood-forestry-coppice-pollard]] replaces fossil fuels, [[mollison-designers-legume-tree-inoculation-and-nitrogen-distribution]] replaces
synthetic fertiliser, and [[mollison-designers-appropriate-technology-small-scale-tools]] replaces energy-intensive machinery.
### Produce No Waste
Waste is an output not connected to an input. In permaculture, the output of
every element becomes the input for another. [[mollison-designers-greywater-treatment-reedbeds]] becomes irrigation,
[[mollison-designers-biogas-methane-digester-design]] becomes fertiliser, and [[mollison-designers-pig-tractor-systems-and-land-preparation]] becomes soil amendment.
The [[mollison-designers-food-processing-preservation-storage]] system is designed so byproducts have value.
### Design from Patterns to Details
The [[mollison-designers-pattern-understanding-in-design]] principle requires observing large-scale site
patterns before designing specific elements. Water flow, sun angles, wind, and
vegetation patterns all inform detailed element placement. Zone and sector
planning is a pattern-based approach to site layout.
### Integrate Rather Than Segregate
Permaculture places elements in relationship so they support one another. The
[[mollison-designers-companion-planting-charts-and-principles]] principle, [[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]], and
[[mollison-designers-domestic-animal-integration-design]] all express integration.
### Use Small and Slow Solutions
Large, rapid interventions are energy-intensive and prone to failure. Small,
gradual changes are more manageable and adaptable. The [[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-propagation-bed-design]] propagates
plants locally rather than importing large quantities. [[mollison-designers-swale-spacing-and-contour-interval]] are built
incrementally as resources allow rather than all at once.
### Use and Value Diversity
Diversity provides resilience. A system with many species, many functions, and
many connections can withstand the loss of any single element. The [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]]
resists pests better than a monoculture. The [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]] creates habitat
diversity that supports more species.
### Use Edges and Value the Marginal
The interface between systems is where the most interesting and productive
things happen. Edge design, explored in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]], maximises these
productive transition zones. Marginal land that conventional agriculture ignores
can be productive under permaculture management.
### Creatively Use and Respond to Change
Change is inevitable. The designer anticipates and designs for it. [[mollison-designers-design-for-disaster-resilience]] planning addresses sudden change, while [[hemenway-accelerated-succession]] addresses gradual ecological change. Flexibility and adaptability
should be built into every system.
## Ethics in Practice
The permaculture ethics are not merely aspirational statements but operational
guidelines that shape every design decision. When choosing between alternatives,
the designer asks: does this option care for the earth? Does it care for people?
Does it share surplus fairly? These questions filter out designs that might be
technically effective but ethically deficient.
The [[mollison-designers-design-client-interview-and-site-assessment-process]] should include discussion of the client's values
and ethical commitments, ensuring that the design aligns with both the
client's goals and permaculture ethics.
