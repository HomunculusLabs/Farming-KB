## Seed Germination Factors

Seed germination is governed by a complex interplay of [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] including soil temperature, light exposure, cold stratification, and even passage through digestive systems. Understanding these factors is essential for successful [[permaculture-plant-propagation-guide]] in [[permaculture]] systems, where [[jadam-pesticide-spray-timing-and-technique]] can mean the difference between abundant establishment and total failure.

## Temperature and Germination

Soil temperature has a profound effect on the germination of a wide range of vegetable seeds. Between 0°C and 38°C (32–100°F), the time to germinate (in days) can be reduced to one-tenth or one-fourth of that in cold soils by increasing soil temperatures. However, at the extremes of this temperature range, many plants encounter limiting factors that result in no germination at all.

While almost all vegetable seed will germinate in soils at 15–20°C (59–68°F), notable exceptions exist:

- **Celery** refuses to germinate above 24°C (74°F)
- **Cucurbits, beans, and subtropical species** generally do not germinate below 10°C (50°F)

This means the practical threshold for [[coleman-fall-bed-preparation-early-outdoor-planting]] is when soil temperatures reach at least 10°C. For earlier planting, soil can be warmed using:

- **Greenhouses** for controlled environment propagation
- **Clear or black plastic mulch** in the field to trap solar heat
- **Exposure of bare earth** to direct sun, which naturally raises soil temperature

A simple thermometer inserted 2.5 cm (1 inch) into the soil is sufficient to measure soil temperature, or a dedicated soil thermometer can be purchased. For specific crops, reference tabulations such as those found in Maynard and Lorenz (*Knott"s Handbook for Vegetable Growers*, 1980, Wiley, N.Y.) provide precise germination temperature ranges.

## Light and Germination

Light is a second critical factor affecting germination, and different species have evolved radically different light requirements:

- **Carrots** need a definite quantity of light to germinate and are usually surface-planted to achieve this. If buried, carrots can be pre-treated by soaking seeds overnight and then subjecting them to a day under a low-wattage light bulb or in the open before planting and covering them lightly. Critically, carrots react to this light treatment only if wetted first.
- **Larger seeds** generally accept burial and germinate satisfactorily in the dark.
- **Some weed seeds and desert species** have adapted to germinate when deep-buried, an evolutionary response to being buried by wind-blown sand or animal activity.
- **Wild tobacco and similar species** require only a mere flash of light — such as when a clod of soil is turned over — to trigger germination.

These light-sensitivity mechanisms represent sophisticated evolutionary adaptations that ensure seeds germinate only under appropriate environmental conditions.

## Stratification and Vernalization

Cold-area seeds, and specifically tree and berry seeds from boreal or cold regions, require a period of cold exposure known as **stratification** or **vernalization** to break dormancy. This process reproduces the natural exposure to cold [at about 0–5°C (32–40°F)] that seeds normally experience at the litter level in cold forests or marshes during autumn through spring.

### Practical Stratification Methods

- **Refrigerator storage**: Apple seeds stored in sand, or chestnuts in peat, will sprout when kept refrigerated and can be potted out as they shoot.
- **Aquatic species**: Wild rice and other "soft" aquatic seeds are stored in open ponds, or under water in an ordinary refrigerator.
- **Cold shaded valleys**: In warm climates, stratification can be accomplished by keeping seeds in sand or peat in cold shaded valleys, or under open cool trellis.
- Seeds should be checked periodically in late winter and spring for signs of germination.

## Heat Treatment

The opposite of cold stratification is heat treatment, applicable to many tree legume seeds. Methods include:

- **Oven treatment**: Heating at 95°C (200°F) for a 10–20 minute period
- **Hot water treatment**: Pouring very hot (near-boiling) water over seeds
- **Light fire treatment**: Burning seeds in a light straw fire

These methods scarify hard seed coats and simulate the effects of natural bushfires, which many leguminous species have evolved to exploit for germination.

## Digestive System Treatment

A traditional method used by experienced gardeners involves feeding seeds to themselves (in sandwiches) or to their animals (chickens or cattle), collecting the manure, making a slurry of it, and sowing seeds such as tomatoes, berries, and tree legumes from the result. The voyage through the digestive system is a compounded process involving:

- **Acid/alkali exposure**: Simulating natural seed coat breakdown
- **Hot/cold cycling**: Temperature fluctuations that help break dormancy
- **Mechanical cracking**: Abrasion in teeth or bird crops that scarifies seed coats
- **Packaging in manure**: Many seeds are specifically adapted to germinate from manure, which provides both moisture and nutrients

This method is particularly effective for species that have co-evolved with herbivorous animals as their primary seed dispersal mechanism.

## Photoperiodism and Flowering

Beyond germination, day length (in fact night length, but conventionally discussed as day length) varies over latitudes, and flowering plants are adapted to bloom and set seed in response to specific day lengths and seasonal changes.

### Day-Length Response Categories

- **Day-neutral plants**: Will flower if other factors (temperature, water, nutrients) are satisfactory, regardless of day length.
- **Short-day plants**: Require shorter day lengths to flower; transferring these to long-day climates may prevent flowering entirely, producing only vegetative growth.
- **Long-day plants**: Require longer day lengths; moving these to short-day climates suppresses flowering.

### Practical Applications

Day-length responses can be exploited productively:

- **Tropical short-day corn** transferred to temperate long-day hot-summer climates produces excellent green-leaf fodder without going to seed.
- **Tobacco** moved from temperate to cool areas produces leaf rather than seed.
- Some **decorative foliage plants** maintain better leaf quality when prevented from flowering by day-length manipulation.

This effect underscores the importance of choosing varieties from local growers or selecting for flowering in new introductions, so that a local seed source is available for all crops desired in seed.

### Latitude and Day Length

Different latitude bands have characteristic day-length patterns:

- **Low latitudes (0–30°)**: Usually [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]] with colder mountain climates; relatively equal days and nights year-round.
- **Mid latitudes (30–50°)**: Cool to [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] with boreal mountain regions; pronounced differences between long summer days and short winter days.
- **High latitudes (>50°)**: Very long summer days with good diffuse radiation throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]; no plant growth occurs in winter.

In extreme cases, cabbages from long-day climates may never flower in New Guinea highlands (short days), reaching 1–3 m in height with leaves being plucked off at regular intervals for vegetable fodder, the plant being cut down only when too tall to reach.

## Frost Protection Through Clearings

Frost is caused by radiation loss (rapid cooling) of the earth on clear nights in still air. To reduce frost on any site, it is necessary to create steep-sided clearings or pits so that radiation is restricted to a small area of the sky. Two effects occur in such clearings:

1. **Radiant heat from vertical edges** provides warmth
2. **Obscuring of the horizon** reduces radiant heat loss at night

The proportion of heat loss on a cold night is proportional to the area of the night sky visible to the object losing heat. A mouse in a cardboard tube in the ground loses very little heat, while a mouse on a mound on a flat site is exposed to the whole sky and loses a great deal.

### Design Rules for Frost Protection

- Make the clearing or pit about **one-half as wide as high**
- Keep sides trimmed to **vertical**
- In forests, clearings should not exceed **30 m across**
- Build up a **complete crown cover** to prevent frost site-wide, done in stages using progressively more frost-tender species
