# Stachybotrys chartarum and Indoor Mold Toxicity

## Overview

Stachybotrys chartarum (also known as Stachybotrys atra) is a melanized
conidial fungus that has become infamous as the so-called "black mold"
toxin-producing killer. The organism gained widespread public notoriety in
the mid-1990s after being implicated in a cluster of infant deaths in
Cleveland, Ohio. Its toxic metabolites, called macrocyclic trichothecenes,
were linked to pulmonary hemorrhage in ten infants, one of whom died. The
episode sparked a national panic about indoor mold contamination that
persists to this day.

## The Cleveland Outbreak

The 1993-1994 Cleveland incident remains the foundational case for
public concern about Stachybotrys. The evidence connecting the mold to
the infant illnesses was statistical rather than direct: a comparison
between children who suffered pulmonary hemorrhage and a control group
of similarly aged, unaffected children showed that symptoms were
associated with living in water-damaged homes infested with
Stachybotrys. Subsequently, the county coroner reexamined all infant
deaths in Cleveland from the early 1990s and found that another six
children may have died from the same cause. These fatalities had
originally been classified as sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).

## Black Mold Identification and Prevalence

Stachybotrys is not the only black mold found in homes. Species of
Cladosporium, Penicillium, Aspergillus, and Alternaria, all possessing
melanin-impregnated [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]], are far more prevalent in
water-damaged buildings, although none of these genera produce the same
potent trichothecene toxins. Even when Stachybotrys chartarum is
identified in a home, this does not signify imminent doom, because only
a subset of strains within the species actually produce trichothecenes.

The distinctive appearance of Stachybotrys conidiophores (spore-bearing
stalks) and conidia under the microscope allows definitive
identification. In culture, the colonies are dark green to black and
slimy in texture, quite different from the dry, powdery colonies of
more common indoor molds.

## Moisture and Modern Building Design

Part of the contemporary problem lies in the construction of
well-insulated, energy-efficient homes. Drafty older houses, despite
their thermal inefficiency, can be effective at protecting occupants
from molds because [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]] allows water vapor to escape. Modern
polymer-wrapped houses with hermetically sealed windows create living
spaces with very stale air. The cavities between wall materials can
become extremely humid when there is no opportunity for water vapor to
escape. Air conditioning can exacerbate the moisture problem, which
may explain the prevalence of mold damage in humid, air-conditioned
regions.

The horror begins when a basement is flooded, a burst water pipe goes
undetected, or drywall remains chronically sodden. Isolated patches of
mold can be treated with diluted bleach, but unless the underlying
moisture source is eliminated, the fungus will return.

## Indoor Spore Counts and Allergenic Risk

During summer months, 500 or more spores may drift around in a cubic
meter of indoor air. Current standards suggest that more than 50 spores
per cubic meter presents a significant risk for eye, nose, and lung
irritation, with a proposed limit of 200 spores per cubic meter for
office buildings. In mold-damaged homes, spore counts can number in the
thousands per cubic meter.
