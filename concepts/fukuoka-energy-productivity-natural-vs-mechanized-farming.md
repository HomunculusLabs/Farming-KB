## The Mechanization Paradox

The claim is often made that mechanization has increased the efficiency of
work. Fukuoka challenged this directly: farmers must use the extra hours freed
by machinery to earn outside income to help pay for their equipment. All they
have done is exchange their work in the fields for a job in some company.
They have traded the joy of working outdoors in the open fields for dreary
hours of labor shut up inside a factory.

Mechanization has not saved labor—it has displaced it from the farm to the
factory, from the field to the urban workplace, while multiplying the total
energy cost of food production many times over.

## The Productivity Illusion

People believe that modern agriculture can both improve productivity and
increase yields. Fukuoka called this a misconception. The yields provided by
scientific farming are smaller than the yields attainable under the full
powers of nature. High-yield practices and scientific methods are thought to
have given us yields exceeding natural productivity, but this is not so.

What actually happens is a cycle of degradation and artificial rescue:

1. Man creates adverse conditions through plowing, chemicals, and
   monoculture—hamstringing nature so it cannot exercise its full powers.
2. Productivity declines below what nature would provide unassisted.
3. Scientific methods are applied to artificially restore productivity.
4. The restoration is celebrated as an "increase" over the degraded baseline.
5. The cycle repeats as each intervention creates new problems requiring
   further intervention.

High-yield technologies are no more than glorified attempts to stave off
reductions in productivity that scientific farming itself has caused.
