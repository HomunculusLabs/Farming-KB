# Soil Self-Plowing and Natural Root Action

Fukuoka presents a detailed argument that soil naturally works and
plows itself through the action of plant roots, soil microorganisms,
earthworms, and moles, and that human plowing with hoe and tractor
actually interferes with these natural processes rather than improving
them. This principle forms a cornerstone of his no-tillage philosophy.

## The Natural Tendency of Soil

## How Roots Plow the Soil

Farmers usually plow the soil to a depth of about four to eight
inches, whereas the roots of grasses and [[green-manure-crops]] work the
soil down to twelve inches, fifteen inches, or more. When these roots
reach down deep into the earth, air and water penetrate into the soil
together with the roots. As the roots wither and die, many types of
microorganisms proliferate, feeding on the [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] and creating
humus that softens the soil and improves its structure.

This process operates continuously and at depths far greater than any
plow can reach. The action is gentle and gradual, maintaining the soil's
crumb structure and porosity rather than destroying them through
mechanical compression. Each year, the zone of biologically active soil
deepens as roots push further into the earth, leaving channels for air
and water movement that persist long after the roots themselves have
decomposed.

## The Succession of Soil Organisms

Earthworms eventually appear where there is humus, drawn by the
[[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] the improved soil structure. Their burrowing activity
further aerates the soil and their casts enrich it with readily
available nutrients. As the number of earthworms increases, moles begin
burrowing through the soil, further aerating and mixing it at depth.
This cascade of biological activity achieves what no mechanical tool
can: the creation of a living, breathing soil ecosystem that improves
itself year after year without any external inputs.

## The Mountain Forest as Evidence

The mountain forests provide compelling evidence for the self-plowing
principle. Trees seem to grow almost haphazardly, but the cedar grows
where it can thrive to its great size, mixed woods rise up where mixed
woods must, and pine trees germinate and grow in places suited for pine
trees. One does not see pines growing at the bottom of a valley or cedar
seedlings taking root on mountain tops. One type of fern grows on
infertile land and another in areas of deep soil. Plants that normally
grow along the water's edge are not found on mountain tops, and
terrestrial plants do not thrive in the water.

## The Forest Soil Creation Process

The process by which forests create and maintain fertile soil
operates over geological timescales but can be observed in compressed
form on any piece of abandoned land. The action of wind and snow over
the years weathers rock into smaller particles. A layer of humus forms
and deepens with the fall of leaves each year. Microorganisms multiply
in the soil, turning it a rich black. The soil aggregates and softens,
increasing water retention. Each stage builds on the previous one,
creating increasingly favorable conditions for plant growth.

When [[fukuoka-black-wattle-fertilizer-tree-method]] is planted in depleted soil on a mountainside and
succeeded a number of years later by giant cedars many times their size,
the great productive powers of the soil become evident. If the forest is
left to itself, this process continues indefinitely without any need for
human intervention. The productive capacity of natural soil is not a
gift from human labor but an emergent property of the biological
community that inhabits it.
