# Mushroom Genetics, Mating Systems, and Reproduction

Understanding mushroom genetics is essential for cultivators and breeders who
wish to develop improved strains. This concept covers the fundamentals of
[[fungal-genetics]] including gene structure, chromosome behavior during cell
division, mating systems, and the reproductive strategies employed by fungi.

## Genes, Alleles, and Mutations

Genes contain specific sequences of nucleotides, the nitrogen-based building
blocks of DNA. These sequences specify the order of nucleotides in messenger
RNA, which determines amino acid sequences in proteins. A gene is often called
a genetic locus. Within a population, each copy of a gene is called an allele.

A mutation is the permanent alteration of a gene caused by chemicals,
radiation, or DNA copying errors. Copy errors occur at roughly one per million
genes, providing the background mutations always present in every organism.

## Genotype and Phenotype

Genes specify the structure of RNA and protein molecules that create all
compounds and structures in a living organism. The expression and interaction
of genes is called the phenotype (observable characteristics), while the set
of genes producing it is the genotype. Many genes exist in multiple copies
with different versions, each producing a protein most efficient at different
conditions, broadening the organism's optimal range.

## Chromosomes

Chromosomes are collections of genes on long DNA molecules, each containing
several thousand genes. Fungal chromosomes are extremely small, and the number
of chromosomes has never been determined for most fungi. A complete set of
chromosomes exists in every cell, meaning every cell division requires a
complete copy of every gene in the organism's genome.

## Mitosis

Mitosis is the normal process of chromosome duplication that occurs every time
a cell divides. All chromosomes are duplicated, with copies sticking together
in early stages. The duplicated chromosomes line up in the middle of the cell,
and one copy of each is pulled to either end, resulting in two complete sets.
This process produces genetically identical daughter cells.

## Meiosis

Meiosis is the unique process during sexual reproduction. Chromosomes are
copied as in mitosis, but genes are shuffled through recombination. Sexually
reproducing organisms have two chromosome sets, one from each parent. In
meiosis, these sets line up side by side, and reciprocal exchanges of
chromosome sections take place. After exchanges, two successive cell divisions
produce four cells, each with one copy of each chromosome. None are identical.

In mushrooms these cells are basidiospores; in cup fungi, ascospores; in
animals, sperm or egg cells. Recombination and genome fusion constantly
introduce variety. This contrasts with bacteria reproducing by mitosis, which
produce vast numbers of identical offspring.

## Reproductive Strategies

Fungi employ three primary reproductive strategies:

1. **Asexual (mitotic) reproduction**: Produces large numbers of identical
   offspring rapidly. Used by bacteria, yeasts, many molds, and many plants.
2. **Sexual reproduction**: Slower due to meiosis and gamete fusion. Produces
   varied offspring capable of exploiting wider niches. Used by polypores,
   most plants, and all large animals.
3. **Combined reproduction**: Most effective strategy, used by most fungi.
   Asexual reproduction fills a niche rapidly; sexual reproduction triggers
   when nutrients become scarce, releasing varied, environmentally resistant
   spores adapted to winter or dry conditions.

## Asexual Reproduction in Fungi

Asexual reproduction takes many forms:
- **Budding**: Used by yeasts, new cells grow from the mother cell surface.
- **Conidia**: Mitotic spores produced on conidiogenous cells. Examples
  include Penicillium, Aspergillus, and mildews.
- **Sporangiospores**: Spores formed in saclike sporangia, found in
  Zygomycetes like Rhizopus on bread and strawberries.
- **Vegetative propagules**: Thickened hyphal fragments (arthrospores,
  chlamydospores, gemmae) that break apart and disperse naturally.

## Sexual Reproduction in Mushrooms

Mushrooms reproduce sexually but have no sexes. All gametes are physically
identical basidiospores. Genetic differences between spores arise from meiosis,
specifically through the sorting of mating type alleles. The mating type
prevents a spore or monokaryotic hypha carrying a particular allele from
fusing with any spore or hypha carrying the same allele.

## Unifactorial Heterothallism

If a species has only one locus controlling mating type with varying numbers
of alleles, it has a unifactorial heterothallic mating system. The only
requirement for mating is that two differing alleles of the mating type locus
be present. Since each spore receives only one allele, any random spore is
compatible with half of its siblings. With many alleles in the population at
large, any random spore has a high probability of being compatible with a
spore from another strain, increasing outcrossing.

## Bifactorial Heterothallism

The majority of mushrooms are heterothallic and bifactorial. This system has
two separate and distinct mating type loci, each of which must have differing
alleles present to form a dikaryotic colony. This produces four distinct types
of spores on each basidium, and any random spore is fertile with only one
fourth of its siblings. This strong incest taboo makes it four times as likely
that a naturally formed dikaryon comes from non-related spores.

A complication of bifactorial systems is that two types of spores which are
neither totally identical nor non-identical can form dikaryotic colonies that
look fertile but are incapable of making fruitbodies or basidiospores.

## Homothallism

Some species have mating type systems that have broken down. These homothallic
fungi are capable of mating with themselves. A single spore of a homothallic
fungus is usually capable of making a fertile dikaryotic colony, though some
spores will be incapable of forming fertile colonies without mating with
another strain due to recombination effects.

### Primary Homothallism

The majority of spores initially form monokaryotic colonies that eventually
become dikaryotic and fruit normally. [[volvariella-volvacea]] is a commonly
cultivated mushroom with this mating system.

### Secondary Homothallism

Each spore receives one nucleus of each mating type, generating a dikaryotic
colony from the moment of spore germination. [[agaricus-brunnescens]] is the
best known example of secondary homothallism.
