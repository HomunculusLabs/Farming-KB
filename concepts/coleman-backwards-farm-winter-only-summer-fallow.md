## Overview

One of the most unconventional experiments in Eliot Coleman's development
of the [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]]. The experiment
revealed both the possibilities and the limitations of winter-only farming.

## The Backwards Farm Concept

The idea emerged from the early success of Coleman's winter harvest trials.
If it was possible to produce fresh, high-quality vegetables through the
winter in Maine, perhaps a farm could be built entirely around this
off-season production, reversing the conventional agricultural calendar.
Instead of working furiously through the summer and resting through the
winter, the backwards farmer would rest through the summer and work
through the winter. The joke became a serious question: could a viable
farm be run by producing salads and main-course vegetables from October 1
through May 31, and then taking a long summer vacation?

The appeal was obvious. Winter production faced no competition from the
vast supply of summer produce that flooded markets from June through
September. Winter crops — grown locally, harvested fresh, and sold
directly — commanded premium prices and generated fierce customer loyalty.
The backwards farm would fill a market niche that conventional farms could
not address. And the prospect of a summer vacation was, for farmers
accustomed to twelve months of relentless work, powerfully attractive.

## The Reality of Winter-Only Production

The reality of the backwards farm was good but not quite as dreamy as the
concept. There was indeed a viable eight-month harvest season, and the
quality of the produce was exceptional. However, the promised long summer
vacation proved illusory. Vacation time was limited to the second half
of June — after cleaning out the greenhouses — and the first half of
July. By mid-July, the work of preparing for the coming winter season
had already begun. Greenhouses needed to be moved to their winter
positions, soil needed to be amended, and the first successions of
winter crops needed to be seeded.

The summer months were not entirely idle, even during the brief "vacation"
period. Coleman used the summer to grow green manure crops that boosted
soil fertility to the high levels required for intensive winter production.
Cover crops of oats, peas, buckwheat, and clover were planted, grown, and
incorporated into the soil during the warm months when biological activity
was at its peak. This green manure program was essential for maintaining
soil health and [[comparison-pheromone-based-pest-control-vs-beneficial-insect-biocontrol]], no heat — and the crops were largely self-maintaining once
established. Summer production, by contrast, required intensive
management: irrigation, pest scouting and control, trellising, pruning,
and frequent harvesting. The labor requirements of summer farming were
significantly higher than those of winter farming, which meant that the
profit margins might have been more favorable for winter production even
though gross revenue was lower.

## The Exhaustion of Year-Round Production

The transition from winter-only to year-round production revealed the
physical demands of continuous farming. "It is difficult to work at the
flat-out speed that small farming demands for twelve months a year with
no break," Coleman wrote. The next few years of full summer-and-winter
production were "truly exhausting." This observation has important
implications for the design of small farm enterprises. The conventional
model of year-round production may not be sustainable for farmers working
without hired labor.
