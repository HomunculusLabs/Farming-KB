# AACT Holding Time, Shelf Life, and Microbial Decay

## Summary

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT) is designed to be brewed and applied
immediately, but circumstances sometimes require holding the tea for
extended periods. Ingham establishes a maximum holding window of 5 days
with continuous aeration, though organism diversity begins declining
after 48-72 hours. Adding food to held tea is extremely dangerous due
to the oxygen demand of starving, activated microbes. Once tea goes
anaerobic, the beneficial communities cannot fully recover even if
aeration is restored. (see [[compost-tea-guide]]).

## The Ideal: Brew and Apply Immediately

Ingham's fundamental guidance is clear: "Compost tea is meant to be
made, and applied right away. There is no point in holding it, as you
start to lose diversity after 48 to 72 hours." The standard AACT brew
cycle is approximately 24 hours, during which foods added to the tea
stimulate microbial growth from the compost inoculum. At the end of the
brew, the tea contains the maximum diversity and activity of organisms
extracted and grown from the compost. (see [[ingham-micronized-compost-aact-brewing-technique]]).

The typical 24-hour brew cycle reflects what Ingham calls "Typical
American impatience -- we have to have our stuff right now." While
longer brews without food additions are possible, adding food resources
allows a finished product in 24 hours or less, with active fungi having
"grown out as hyphae" from spores, increasing from "a few micrometers
to hundreds of micrometers in 24 hours."

## The Five-Day Maximum Holding Window

If holding is necessary, Ingham establishes specific parameters: "IF
you make sure that oxygen is maintained in aerobic ranges, you CAN hold
tea for up to 5 days." The critical requirement is that dissolved oxygen
must remain above 5.5 ppm throughout the holding period. This means the
aeration system must continue running continuously. (see [[aact-brewing-methodology]]).

However, Ingham provides an important caveat: "during that time, you
very well may select for an ever-diminishing set of organism diversity. (see [[compost-tea-brewing]]).
The reason for the five day cut-off is that by that time, you are
dealing with a limited set of microbe diversity." Even with adequate
oxygen, the microbial community shifts over time as some species
outcompete others in the contained liquid environment. (see [[compost-tea-brewing-methods]]).

## The Danger of Adding Food to Held Tea

One of the most critical warnings in the Field Guide concerns adding
food to tea that has been held beyond the initial brew cycle. Ingham
explains: "If you add any foods into that 'being-held' tea, oxygen
will PLUMMET to very low levels. You have a bunch of activated,
starving bacteria and fungi in your 2 to 3 day old tea, and they will
be SAVAGE about any added food."

The organisms in held tea have been metabolically active but food-
limited. When new food is introduced, they consume it "very rapidly,
and with a huge oxygen demand." This demand can be so extreme "that it
will result in loss of the beneficial fungi, protozoa and nematodes
from the brew quite rapidly -- within 4 to 6 hours."

If circumstances force you to hold tea, Ingham advises adding "only
minimal amounts of food (no more than a teaspoon or 20 ml, for example)
after 24 hours." This can be done "but with extreme care." The same
constraint applies to tank-mixing nutrients into tea: "you have only a
few hours -- perhaps only 4 to 6 hours -- to get the tea out before
the beneficial fungi have been consumed."

## Signs of Tea Going Anaerobic

When tea goes anaerobic, the consequences are severe and potentially
irreversible. Ingham states: "If tea goes anaerobic, then you lose the
beneficial fungi, the beneficial protozoa and the beneficial nematodes.
If you don't have these organisms, then you have lost most of the
biological benefit of the compost, and it isn't compost tea anymore."

She is blunt about what anaerobic tea should be called: "Putrefying
organic matter tea? Anaerobic bacterial tea?" When liquid goes
anaerobic, "the fungi, protozoa and nematodes are killed, then the
liquid cannot be called compost tea." Anaerobic brews specifically "do
not contain flagellates or amoebae or nematodes. Thus anaerobic brews
cannot cycle nutrients."

Beyond losing beneficials, anaerobic conditions can grow human
pathogens including Clostridium species, E. coli, Salmonella, and
Shigella. Ingham asks rhetorically: "Do we really need to go there?"

## The Sealed Container Test

Ingham provides a practical test for assessing whether aeration during
brewing was adequate: "PUT THE TEA INTO A SEALED CONTAINER AND LEAVE AT
ROOM TEMPERATURE OVERNIGHT. Open the container and smell at 24 hours.
If ok, your tea was likely ok. IF stink can be detected, do another
tea."

This test works because if the tea was truly aerobic when brewed, the
organisms it contains can maintain aerobic conditions in the sealed
container overnight through their own metabolism. If the tea was already
compromised with anaerobic organisms, the sealed environment will
rapidly produce detectable foul odors.

## Factors Affecting Shelf Life

Multiple factors influence how quickly AACT quality degrades:

- **Temperature**: Higher temperatures accelerate microbial metabolism
  and oxygen consumption, shortening the viable window. Cooler
  temperatures extend it, though refrigeration is not specifically
  recommended as a practical solution for large volumes.
- **Food availability**: Tea with residual food resources will sustain
  activity longer but may also drive oxygen depletion faster.
- **Oxygen maintenance**: The single most critical factor. Continuous
  aeration that maintains dissolved oxygen above 5.5 ppm is essential
  for any extended holding.
- **Initial organism diversity**: Tea brewed from high-quality compost
  with full organism diversity retains benefits longer than tea from
  poor compost with limited diversity.
- **Container design**: Open containers allow gas exchange; sealed
  containers accelerate anaerobic conditions.

## Re-aeration After Anaerobic Conditions

Ingham addresses whether tea that has gone anaerobic can be rescued:
"Sure. However, the beneficial communities never return to the full
range of diversity that you want in your soil." While you can restore
aerobic conditions by restarting aeration, "the aerobic fungi do not
survive conditions where anaerobic bacteria start growing." The
beneficial organisms that make compost tea valuable are lost and cannot
be fully recovered simply by re-aerating.

## Best Practices for Use Within the Window

The recommended approach is to brew only what you can apply immediately.
"Holding the brew until tonight isn't a problem as long as you keep it
aerated." For tea held beyond 24 hours, apply it "someplace that you
don't care if the results are a bit funky. I personally let the weed
patch have it." Once tea has been applied as a spray, the thin film on
leaf surfaces "will be nicely aerobic" through diffusion, solving the
aeration concern at the point of application.
