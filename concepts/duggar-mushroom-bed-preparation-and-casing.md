# Duggar Mushroom Bed Preparation and Casing

Duggar provided detailed, scientifically informed guidance on compost
preparation, bed construction, spawning, and casing -- the core
practical operations of mushroom cultivation.

## The Compost: No Substitute for Stable Manure

There was no known substitute for stable manure as a compost for
commercial mushroom production. The best results required fresh manure
hauled directly from stables and subjected to controlled fermentation.
Grain-fed animals bedded with straw (especially rye straw, the French
ideal) yielded the most vigorous spawn. Manure from grass-fed animals
or leguminous fodder stables proved unsatisfactory. Bedding substitutes
like sawdust and shavings were increasingly common but made compost
less retentive of moisture.

## The Fermentation Process

The fermentation served a biological rather than chemical purpose. In
sterilized fresh manure, mycelium grew as rapidly and more profusely
than in fermented manure -- fermentation was not chemically essential.
However, fresh manure contained readily soluble organic products that
supported vigorous bacterial activity hostile to mushroom mycelium.
Fermentation removed or transformed these products and changed the
insoluble material so mycelium could grow and compete with other
microorganisms.

### Practical Fermentation

Fresh manure was thrown into piles not more than 4 feet high, thoroughly
wetted, then turned three or four times at intervals of three to five
days. Adequate moisture was essential throughout. A temperature of
140-150 F between the first three turnings was advantageous. Proper
fermentation took about three weeks. Signs of readiness included loss
of objectionable odor, an oleaginous "feel," pliability, and uniform
brown color of the straw, with temperature declining to 120-130 F.

Experiments with sawdust, decayed leaves, woods mould, and forest
soil all proved inferior to stable manure. Even combining equal
quantities of manure with these substitutes reduced mushroom size and
yield.

## Bed Types

### Flat Beds (English Type)
More generally employed in America, requiring less labor. The surface
area was slightly less than equal space in ridge beds. The American
commercial grower almost invariably used sided flat beds, which
encouraged heavier mushrooms.

### Ridge Beds (French Type)
Alone practiced in the relatively low-roofed Paris caves. More labor
in preparation and subsequent care, but permitted more effective
sanitation and slightly increased floor space. Each ridge was 20-24
inches wide at the base, tapering to not more than 6 inches at top,
and 12-14 inches high. A gentle slope permitted heavy clusters; steep
slopes produced smaller mushrooms that pulled away from the spawn.

## Installation

Compost was pitched into bed areas and immediately compressed with
shovel blows or a compressing board. Flat beds should be 8-10 inches
deep after compression, firm enough to support a person sinking no
more than 1-2 inches. Duggar saw no necessity for depth sufficient to
maintain bottom heat; uniform temperature approximation was preferable.

## Spawning

Beds were monitored daily for temperature. Spawning should not occur
until temperature fell to 70-75 F (and still declining), with 65-70 F
generally preferable for beds maintained at 55 F during growing. Below
50 F, growth would be very slow. Earlier advice to spawn at 85-90 F
was fatal if maintained.

For brick spawn, each brick was broken into 9-12 pieces (hen's egg
size), one piece per square foot. For flake spawn, an amount half as
large as one's fist was not excessive per insertion. The procedure was
to lift the compost, insert the piece deep enough with the right hand,
cover fully 1 inch with manure, and press firmly with the left hand.
An aerating passage was left to one side of each piece. Growth began
in 7-10 days.

## Casing

Casing was not performed until spawn was visibly running well, unless
moisture conditions demanded it. Casing consisted of covering the bed
with 1 to 1.5 inches of fairly rich loam. French growers mixed
calcareous soil with the loam for sanitation. Neither pure sand (lost
moisture too easily) nor heavy clay (subject to baking with artificial
heat) was desirable. Woods mould was objectionable due to competing
fungi and tendency to produce many small fruits. Good garden loam,
potting soil, or soil from beneath a grass sod was excellent.

## Care After Casing

The chief difficulty was moisture management. Many amateurs drenched
beds and destroyed spawn; others feared to water and the spawn would
not grow near the surface. Casing soil should be moistened by repeated
sprinkling, not sudden drenching. Subsequently, the minimum water to
keep beds merely moist was applied, preferably frequently rather than
heavily -- enough to slightly exceed evaporation. A dry room might
need sprinkling twice daily; a favorable cellar, no oftener than once
a week.

## The Growing Period

Under favorable conditions, straw-manure beds bore in 6-8 weeks; if
no sign of fruiting appeared after 10 weeks, trouble existed.
Shaving-manure beds might require 8-12 weeks. Mushrooms appeared first
directly over spawn insertions, producing "flushes" of simultaneous
fruiting. Productive periods ranged from a few weeks to five months.
A satisfactory bed bore continuously for two to four months.

## Harvest and Renewal

Beds yielding heavily required considerable water of approximately bed
temperature. After beds ceased production, removing old soil and
substituting fresh earth could stimulate renewed growth. Between
crops, all old material was removed and the house thoroughly cleaned,
white-washed, or fumigated.
