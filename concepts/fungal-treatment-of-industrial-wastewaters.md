# Fungal Treatment of Industrial Wastewaters

Updated: 2026-04-18

Fungal treatment of wastewaters dates back to the 1960s. Yeasts and fungi are used extensively to reduce the strength of a wide variety of food-processing wastewaters, with the added benefit of producing food or fodder yeast biomass as a valuable byproduct. The most commonly used yeast is Candida utilis, valued for its ability to utilize diverse carbon and nitrogen sources, rapid growth, high yields, and tolerance of low pH. The application of fungi in wastewater treatment represents an important intersection of [[fungal-bioremediation]] and industrial biotechnology.

## Advantages of Fungal Wastewater Treatment

- **Dual benefit** -- pollution reduction combined with production of marketable single-cell protein (SCP)

- **pH tolerance** -- many fungi thrive at acidic pH where bacterial competitors fail

- **Simple recovery** -- fungal biomass forms flocculent masses easily separated by filtration and screening

- **Non-aseptic operation** -- many processes work without sterile conditions, reducing costs

- **Broad substrate range** -- fungi can utilize starches, lactose, phenols, and diverse organic compounds

The ability of fungi to grow at low pH is particularly advantageous for treating acidic industrial effluents that would require pH adjustment before bacterial treatment. Filamentous fungi also produce extensive mycelial networks that provide natural biofilm structures for attached-growth reactor configurations. These advantages are discussed in the context of [[mycoremediation-techniques]] for wastewater treatment.

## Starch-Processing Wastewater

Starch-processing wastewater contains high carbohydrate (2360-3550 mg/L), starch (1520-2580 mg/L), sugar (0.65-1.18%), COD (11,970-18,900 mg/L), and BOD (7980-12,800 mg/L).

Key treatments:

- Aspergillus oryzae in a non-aseptic airlift [[fungal-bioreactor-types-for-pollutant-removal]] removed 95% COD, 93% BOD, and 98% suspended solids; effluent suitable for agricultural irrigation

- Rhizopus oligosporus in a 45-L airlift bioreactor achieved 96% COD reduction with dissolved oxygen maintained above 50% saturation

- Mixed cultures of Candida utilis and Endomycopsis fibuliger reduced 94% COD and 91% BOD from cassava starch effluent

- Fungal biomass from starch wastewater contained 38-46% protein, safe for human and animal consumption

The production of SCP from starch wastewater represents one of the most commercially mature fungal wastewater treatment applications. The economics are favorable because the revenue from protein biomass offsets treatment costs. Rhizopus species are particularly valued because they produce biomass with favorable amino acid profiles and can be harvested as easily separated pellet flocs. This approach connects to broader [[bioremediation-ecosystem-restoration]] for valorizing waste streams.

## Dairy Industry Wastewater (Whey)

The cheese industry produces 121 million tons of whey annually (based on 13.5 million tons cheese). Only 50% is utilized; the rest is discharged to water bodies. Whey has COD 50,000-80,000 mg/L and BOD 40,000-60,000 mg/L, with lactose responsible for 90% of oxygen demand. Lactose cannot be assimilated by many microorganisms, but 98 yeast species across 19 genera can utilize it.

Kluyveromyces marxianus is the most commonly used yeast for whey treatment due to its high lactose assimilation rate, thermotolerance (growth at up to 45 degrees Celsius), and ability to produce ethanol as a co-product. Mixed yeast-fungal systems combining K. marxianus with filamentous fungi such as Trichoderma reesei have been explored for simultaneous lactose utilization and enzyme production. The treatment of whey by fungi is a practical example of [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] applied to food-industry wastes.

## Olive Mill Wastewater

Olive mill wastewater (OMW) is a major pollutant in Mediterranean countries, containing high phenolic content and causing phytotoxicity. Treatment approaches include:

- P. chrysosporium immobilized on polyurethane foam: 70%+ color and COD removal over repeated batches

- Aspergillus niger in polyurethane sponge: up to 5.32 g biomass per gram carrier while reducing phenols

- Lentinula edodes laccase immobilized on chitosan: 67% total phenol and 72% ortho-phenol removal in 24 hours

- Combined anaerobic digestion with aerobic fungal pretreatment increased methane yield coefficients by 30-35%

OMW treatment by white rot fungi is particularly effective because the polyphenolic compounds in OMW are structurally similar to lignin, the natural substrate of ligninolytic enzymes. The high antioxidant content of OMW also means that treated effluent may retain value-added phenolic products after fungal treatment, creating potential for resource recovery alongside pollution control.

## Pharmaceutical and Chemical Industry Wastewaters

Beyond food-processing wastes, fungi have been applied to treatment of more challenging industrial streams:

- **Phenol-containing wastewaters** -- white rot fungi such as P. chrysosporium and T. versicolor degrade phenol and substituted phenols through ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic pathways

- **Pharmaceutical compounds** -- fungal laccases can transform endocrine-disrupting chemicals, antibiotics, and other pharmaceutical micropollutants

- **Dye-containing effluents** -- textile industry wastewaters are treated by white rot fungi using [[ligninolytic-enzymes-bioremediation]] for dye decolorization

These applications demonstrate the versatility of fungal treatment systems for recalcitrant pollutants that resist conventional biological treatment, consistent with the broader goals of [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]].

## Value-Added Products

Fungal wastewater treatment can produce:

- **Single-cell protein (SCP)** -- 4.5-5.2 g dry biomass/L from starch wastewater in 14 hours, with 38-46% protein

- **Enzymes** -- glucoamylase, laccase, and MnP produced during treatment

- **Biogas** -- methane production enhanced by fungal pretreatment

- **Fertilizer** -- rock phosphate solubilization during OMW treatment

- **Mushroom cultivation** -- Pleurotus species grown on OMW substrates

The concept of integrated biorefinery approaches, where wastewater treatment is coupled with production of marketable products, is gaining traction as a way to improve the economics of fungal bioremediation. This aligns with circular economy principles and is relevant to [[fungal-secondary-metabolites-environment]] research.
