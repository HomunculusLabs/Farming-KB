# Mushroom vs Toadstool Terminology and Fungal Growth Patterns

## The False Dichotomy

One of Palmer's most persistent and passionately argued themes was the meaninglessness of the popular distinction between "mushrooms" and "toadstools." The two words, he wrote, "comprise the sum of popular knowledge of a large part of God's creation; that department known as the fungi."

Persons who knew the common [[classification-of-cultivated-mushroom-species]] grouped everything else under the general name of toadstool. This, Palmer argued, was "just as near correct as it would be to call the russets 'apples,' and, knowing no other variety, to style them all 'crabs.'"

In his published articles spanning multiple years, Palmer declared that the words **mushroom**, **toadstool**, and **fungus** were synonymous and interchangeable. Every mushroom is a toadstool and every toadstool is a mushroom. The popular distinction had no scientific basis whatsoever — it merely reflected the fact that cooks and gardeners knew one cultivable species and called everything else by a pejorative name born of ignorance and fear.

Palmer compared this linguistic error to a person who grows up knowing only russet apples and insists that all other apple varieties are not apples at all. Such a person would be "just as proud of their ignorance" as those who dismissed nine hundred edible fungal species because they knew only the common mushroom.

The common law of popular usage, he observed, has higher authority in the public mind than the special statute of scientific truth — tradition is not readily supplanted by reason, and the person who has "always" called one variety a mushroom and all others toadstools will resist correction with the certainty of lifelong habit.

## New England Fungal Diversity

Palmer estimated approximately three thousand specific members of the fungal kingdom in New England. Of these, roughly two-thirds were microscopic — moulds, yeasts, and other organisms invisible to the naked eye — leaving about **one thousand visible kinds** of what the public called "toadstools."

Of this thousand, only one variety — the common horse-mushroom — could be propagated by artificial means in commercial quantities. Hotels, cooks, and gardeners used only this one kind of fungus, which they could confidently distinguish from all others, and called it "a mushroom." The nine hundred and ninety-nine other kinds were grouped under the catch-all name of "toadstools."

To the student of mycology, Palmer insisted, these names meant nothing — it might as well be said that a plant is not a vegetable as that a toadstool is not a mushroom. Each of these species had qualities "just as distinctly its own, properties just as dangerous or the reverse, conditions of growth just as marked, forms just as entirely individual, as the same number of birds, fishes, or flowering plants."

The fungal kingdom was as diverse and as worthy of study as any other branch of natural history, yet it remained almost entirely neglected by the American educational establishment and the general public alike.

## Agaricus Arvensis vs Agaricus Campestris

Palmer drew careful distinctions between the two principal [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]], noting the subtleties that separated them despite their overwhelming similarity:

- **Agaricus (Psalliota) arvensis** — the horse-mushroom, the mushroom of commerce and civilization, propagated artificially in prepared beds of horse manure under [[psilocybe-cubensis-strain-potency-variability-controlled-conditions-bigwood-beug]]
- **Agaricus (Psalliota) campestris** — the meadow or pasture mushroom, probably the same species in a less cultivated state, growing wild in open fields and pastures

The two are so remarkably similar that "cooks and gardeners do not recognize any distinction between the two." The *arvensis* follows the animal whose name it bears — it grows in and by horse manure, and in no other way. This dependency on horse manure is what made it the sole commercially cultivable mushroom, and consequently the only species most people ever encountered in markets or on restaurant menus.

Old manure heaps, Palmer noted, will sometimes generate their own spawn at the base and produce fine mushrooms without deliberate planting — a natural process that hints at the ease with which fungi colonize suitable substrates when conditions are right.

## Spawn and Mycelium: The Hidden Subterranean Vine

Palmer provided one of the most vivid and memorable descriptions of fungal mycelium in 19th-century American natural history writing. He recalled witnessing the banks opposite the Brunswick Hotel in Boston being sodded and leveled to their present grade.

As the workman's pick broke up the soil, a white substance ran through every piece — "starting with large branches, it divided and sub-divided like the veins on the back of the hand." The smell was very strong, quickly noticed on the opposite side of the street.

This "subterranean white vine," which had the appearance of an extensive root system, was nothing but the hidden vegetative part of the **[[coprinus-comatus]]** (shaggy mane) — a mushroom freely eaten in Palmer's time, though merely twenty years earlier it had been thought poisonous. The common name for this white thread-like substance is **spawn**.

Palmer explained the analogy clearly: just as a cutting of grape-vine placed in favorable conditions will shoot up, put forth branches, and bear fruit, a part of this fungal vine transplanted to suitable conditions will continue to ramify and in time produce mushrooms above ground.

The mycelium corresponds to the vine of a higher plant, while the visible mushroom represents the fruit. The most important part of the fungus, Palmer emphasized, is beneath the soil — invisible, persistent, and essential to everything that appears above.

## The Coprinus Comatus Example

The *Coprinus comatus* (shaggy mane) served as Palmer's primary illustration of the hidden life of fungi and the fallibility of popular opinion about edibility. Its extensive [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]] of white threads — the spawn — demonstrated conclusively that mushrooms do not appear spontaneously but grow from established subterranean organisms that may occupy the same ground for years, forming vast networks invisible to the casual observer.

This species had undergone a remarkable rehabilitation in public estimation. Once considered poisonous and shunned by all but the most knowledgeable foragers, it was by Palmer's time recognized as an excellent edible mushroom. Its changing reputation illustrated Palmer's broader point: that public fear of mushrooms was largely based on ignorance rather than on any real understanding of which species were actually dangerous.

## Fungi Return to the Same Locations Yearly

One of Palmer's most important observational contributions was documenting the reliability of **fungal recurrence** — the tendency of mushroom species to fruit in the same locations year after year from the same subterranean mycelial network.

On the very spots where he gathered mushrooms in 1874, he found the identical variety in 1879, five years later. The mushroom lover could therefore have regular harvests "with all the certainty of the farmer who looks for the return of his wheat crop or the results of his cranberry culture."

However, Palmer was careful to qualify this certainty: "just that degree of certainty, no more and no less." Just as certain years were favorable to the production of certain fruits, as the potato crop sometimes fails and the apple orchard is barren, [[mushroom-spawn]] that usually produced its expected variety might pass a year without fruiting, or even become extinct under adverse conditions.

"The blight which may visit all life, animal or vegetable, does not fail to fall at times upon my humble friends."

This observation of yearly recurrence had practical importance for foragers: knowing where edible species had appeared in previous years gave a strong indication of where they might be found again, reducing the randomness of uninformed mushroom hunting.

## Seasonal Succession of Species

Palmer noted that the various varieties of toadstools "succeed each other in rotation just as the bloodroot and anemones of spring are followed by the roses of summer and the cardinal flower or gentian of fall."

This observation — that fungal fruiting follows predictable seasonal patterns with different species appearing at different times — was the result of several years of careful, systematic watching of the same locations through successive growing seasons.

This seasonal succession meant that the fungal landscape changed throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] in an orderly and predictable way. Different species could be expected at different times, just as different flowers bloom in different months. For the knowledgeable forager, this predictability was an asset — knowing which species to expect at which time of year made identification easier and reduced the risk of confusion.

## The Myth of Overnight Growth

Palmer directly challenged the popular notion that toadstools spring up in a single night in most unforeseen places and for unknown causes. This belief had made mushroom growth proverbial for superficiality — a metaphor for things that appear suddenly and without substance.

Palmer argued that this was deeply unfair to the fungi. The common mushroom and many others form for days just below the surface of the soil, developing slowly as the mycelium directs nutrients to the emerging fruiting body. The mushroom exists as a small button beneath the earth, gradually expanding and strengthening.

A heavy dew or an evening shower straightens the stem and expands the cap. The fungus breaks through the earth during the night, and in the morning the gatherer finds white buttons where nothing was visible the day before.

The apparent overnight appearance is an illusion — the organism has been developing underground for days, and the visible mushroom is merely the final stage of a much longer process. Palmer lamented that "popular error has made mushroom growth proverbial for a superficiality which by the fungi, at least, is undeserved."

## The Earth Beneath Is Alive

Palmer painted a vivid picture of the hidden fungal world beneath every footstep on a country walk. "The whole earth beneath your feet is alive with vegetation to a great depth."

This vegetation — the vast network of mycelial threads belonging to the thousand varieties of mushroom spawn — was "just as real, and the various vines" were "just as distinct as the hop-vine and the woodbine, the ivy and the virgin's-bower that twine their tendrils above your head."

The diversity and complexity of the underground fungal ecosystem matched or exceeded that of the visible plant world above. Just where grew a peculiar kind of toadstool one year, there, next year, and for successive harvests, the same plant would be found. There was no more mystery about its appearance than in the growth of the chestnut on the tree that shaded it.

## The Disciplined Perception Required for Identification

Palmer drew a powerful analogy to the diamond merchant to explain the kind of perception required for safe [[mushroom-identification]]. A person who has never seen a diamond cannot distinguish it from cut glass, while the jeweler who has spent years in disciplined observation detects the genuine article at a glance.

No test, no crucible needed. "His sense of discrimination is disciplined, and yours is not." Ask the jeweler to explain his secret, and he cannot — "it has been acquired by years of practice, and may be yours; but only through the same path he trod."

This faculty of cultivated perception, Palmer argued, applies even more strongly to fungi than to higher plants. "Any person can tell at a glance the difference between an oak and a pine, but I have had men of apparent good sense empty a basket containing half a dozen kinds of fungi on my office table and ask, 'There, are those good to eat or poisonous?'"

Those fungi included plants "as diverse in character and appearance as the pine from the red cedar." Once attention is called to these differences, however, few observers are so dull as to remain ignorant.

## The Fundamental Principle of Identification

Palmer's core teaching was that exactly what is done with one variety of fungus must be the rule with all others: one must impress upon the senses of sight, touch, taste, and smell the distinctive traits of each species individually.

"Then that specific member of a great family can be eaten or must be rejected wherever and whenever found." There is no more liability to mistake "than in the attempt to distinguish pears from quinces or hyacinth bulbs from onions."

The key is not general rules about color, habitat, or odor, but specific knowledge of each species — learned through direct experience and disciplined observation, developed over time through the kind of patient practice that transforms confusion into recognition.

Palmer closed one of his articles with a sobering report: since the first number of his mushroom papers was printed, there had been three cases of poisoning reported by the daily press from as many different localities. While wishing that more study might be given to this part of nature's kingdom, he could not too strongly warn persons from simply meddling with fungi. The consequences of casual experimentation, he made clear, could be swift and irreversible.
