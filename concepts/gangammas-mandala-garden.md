# Gangamma's Mandala [[bill-mollison]] using permaculture
"least-path" layout principles to minimize walking distance and maximise productive
access to every planted zone.

## Origins

The concept draws heavily on the East-West Institute (Hawaii) and the Samaka gardens
of the Philippines — traditions of small-scale, high-yield food production for
tropical and subtropical climates. Mollison formalised the layout as a purely
permaculture design, naming it after a permaculture design graduate from Karnataka,
India. The pattern adapts to temperate regions with suitable species substitutions
and the geometry can be altered to fit almost any site form.

## Overview

A typical mandala garden occupies a minimum of 100 m² (1,075 sq ft). The layout
consists of a central circle garden, a surrounding sunken circular path with keyhole
indentations, six radiating keyhole beds, and a multi-layered border system of
grasses, herbaceous plants, and woody species. Every element serves multiple
functions: food, mulch, forage, wind protection, and weed exclusion.

## Construction

### Step 1: Central Circle Garden

At the centre of the garden, mark out a circle 2 m (6 feet) in diameter.

1. Excavate topsoil (or subsoil) into a dish shape with a ridged perimeter,
   0.6–1 m (2–3 feet) deep from hollow floor to rim.
2. Cover with wet newspaper, cardboard, banana leaves, or other sheet mulch
   to suppress weeds and begin [[building-soil]].
3. Fill the hollow — or over-fill as a dome — with rough organic mulch: short logs,
   coarse twigs, hay, rice husks, sawdust, and other humus-creating materials.
4. Scatter manure, ash, lime, dolomite, or fertiliser over the mulch. Bank stones
   to the outside of the rim if available.
5. Plant the rim with 4–5 papaya (tall variety), 4+ dwarf bananas, and 8–10 sweet
   potato plants.
6. Inside the rim, plant yams or taro. As papaya and banana stalks mature, train
   climbing beans onto them for vertical yield.
7. Lay a wooden grid or platform over the mulched area as an outdoor shower or
   wash-up space. Grey water feeds the mulch pit continuously.

The central circle functions as a nutrient sink, water harvest point, and
multi-layered food production zone simultaneously.

### Step 2: Circular Sunken Path

Around the central circle, establish a circular path 0.6–1 m (2–3 feet) wide, set
below the level of surrounding beds. Cover with sawdust or gravel for weed
suppression and comfortable footing.

Cut 5–6 "keyhole" indentations into this path — wide at the outer end and narrowing
to a walkable passage. These allow the gardener to reach deep into each adjacent bed
without stepping on cultivated soil. The system now resembles a mandala pattern: a
radial arrangement of circles and access paths centred on the productive hub.

### Step 3: Keyhole Beds

Around each keyhole, construct a bed 1.5–2 m (5–6 feet) wide, edged with a soil bank
10–20 cm (4–8 inches) high to retain water and prevent run-off. Sheet-mulch each bed
following the same method as the central circle.

Six major keyhole beds form the standard layout, each separated by a thin living
strip of lemongrass (*Cymbopogon citratus*) or vetiver grass (*Vetiveria
zizanioides*) serving as path markers, mulch sources, and erosion controls.

## Planting Zones

### A. Pathside Greens

Planted along the track edge of the central path and the outer rim of each keyhole
bed, within easy stoop-reach. These are frequently plucked, everyday greens of high
nutritional and culinary value. Most of the plant is harvested over much of the year
on a cut-and-come-again basis.

Typical species include:

- Allium family: chives, shallots, garlic chives
- Herbs: parsley, coriander (cilantro), thyme, sage
- Vegetables: celery, broccoli, edible chrysanthemum (*Glebionis coronaria*)

Positioned for maximum access since they are the crops visited most often by the
household cook.

### B. Keyhole Bed Interiors

Each keyhole bed supports a rotating polyculture of seasonal vegetables, root crops,
and companion herbs. The deep mulch base retains moisture and builds soil fertility
over time, reducing external inputs after the first year.
### C. Inner Border Strip

Along the outside periphery ridge, plant strips of lemongrass, comfrey (*Symphytum*
spp.), and arrowroot (*Canna edulis*). These form a dense barrier against invasive
grasses such as kikuyu while providing mulch material, edible tubers, and culinary
herbs.

### D. Outer Hedge and Windbreak

Behind the inner border, establish a taller hedge of cassava, banana, papaya, pigeon
pea (*Cajanus cajan*), leucaena (*Leucaena leucocephala*), and crotalaria
(*Crotalaria* spp.). This multi-species hedge provides:

- **Wind protection** for tender greens and vegetables inside
- **Additional food** — cassava roots, pigeon pea seeds, papaya fruit
- **Forage** — leucaena and crotalaria leaves for livestock or mulch
- **Nitrogen fixation** — pigeon pea and crotalaria are leguminous
- **Privacy and security** — visual screening for the household garden

The entire mandala is fenced or surrounded by a spiny woven hedge to exclude cattle.

## Design Principles

### Least-Path Layout

Every part of the mandala is within a few steps of the central path and its keyhole
access points. The gardener never steps onto cultivated soil, preventing compaction
and maintaining soil structure.

### Multi-Function Elements

No element serves only one purpose. The central circle is garden, shower pad, and
nutrient recycler. Grass barriers are mulch sources, path edges, and weed excluders.
The outer hedge is food, forage, windbreak, and fence simultaneously.

### Vertical Stacking

Tall species (papaya, banana, leucaena) occupy the canopy; medium shrubs and herbs
fill the mid-layers; ground covers and root crops occupy the lowest zone. Climbing
beans use tall stalks as living trellises, adding a fourth vertical layer.

### Sheet Mulching and Soil Building

Every bed is established with sheet mulch rather than tilling. Organic matter
decomposes in place, feeding soil life and building humus. The dish-shaped central
circle captures water and debris, accelerating decomposition.
