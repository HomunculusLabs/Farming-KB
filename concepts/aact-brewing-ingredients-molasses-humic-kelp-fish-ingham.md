# AACT Brewing Ingredients — Molasses, Humic Acids, Kelp, and Fish Products

[[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] Tea (AACT) quality depends heavily on the ingredients added to the brew. These serve two distinct functions: providing **inoculum** (the beneficial organisms extracted from compost) and supplying **food resources** that allow those organisms to multiply during the brewing period. The choice of food resources determines whether the resulting tea will be bacterial-dominant, fungal-dominant, or balanced — which in turn determines what crops and conditions the tea is best suited for.

## The Two Categories of AACT Additions

### 1. Inoculants
Inoculants are the source of [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]]. The primary inoculant is always **high-quality compost** or **vermicompost** that contains the full diversity of soil foodweb organisms — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]]. Additional inoculants can include specific fungal preparations, mycorrhizal products, or commercial microbial blends. The compost must be mature, aerobic, and free of pathogens to produce safe, effective tea.

### 2. Food Resources
Food resources feed the organisms extracted from the compost, allowing them to reproduce during the 24-hour brew cycle. Different foods select for different organism groups:

- **Simple sugars** (molasses, honey) → select strongly for **bacteria**
- **Complex carbohydrates** (kelp, humic acids, fish hydrolysate) → feed both bacteria and fungi
- **Fungal foods** (oatmeal, rock dust, humic acids, wood chips) → specifically promote **fungal growth**

## Blackstrap Molasses

Blackstrap molasses is the most commonly used bacterial food in AACT brewing. It is the final byproduct of sugar refining and contains significant amounts of iron, calcium, magnesium, and potassium in addition to sugars.

### Role in AACT
Molasses provides rapidly available simple sugars that fuel explosive bacterial growth during the first 12–18 hours of brewing. This bacterial bloom is essential for bacterial-dominant teas used on vegetables, annuals, and grasses. The key points:

- **Unsulfured molasses** is preferred — sulfur preservatives can inhibit beneficial organism growth
- Typical application rate is **1 tablespoon per gallon** of tea (approximately 15 mL per 4 L)
- Dried cane sugar and dried molasses products are acceptable alternatives with similar sugar content
- Excessive molasses can drive oxygen levels down too quickly, causing the brew to go anaerobic between hours 14–20

### Molasses as Fungal Food and Sticker-Spreader
Beyond its role as bacterial food, molasses serves two additional purposes:

1. **Sticker-spreader**: The sugar content helps AACT adhere to leaf surfaces when applied as a foliar spray, improving coverage and organism retention
2. **Moderate fungal food**: At lower concentrations, molasses supports actinomycete growth — the filamentous bacteria that bridge the gap between [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] dominance

### Cautions
Too much molasses creates a bacterial bloom so rapid that [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] is consumed faster than the aerator can replace it. The brew goes anaerobic, killing beneficial fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. The smell test is critical: sweet or earthy smells indicate healthy aerobic conditions; sour, putrid, or rotten-egg smells indicate anaerobic conditions that have ruined the tea.

## Cold-Water Kelp

Kelp (seaweed) meal or liquid kelp extract is one of the most versatile AACT ingredients. It provides a broad spectrum of micronutrients, [[gibberellins-plant-growth-hormones]] (cytokinins, auxins, gibberellins), and both bacterial and fungal food sources.

### Benefits in AACT
- Contains **over 60 trace minerals** and elements needed for microbial metabolism
- Provides **complex carbohydrates** (alginates, laminarins) that serve as fungal food
- Supplies **plant hormones** that can stimulate plant growth when the tea is applied as a foliar spray
- Natural chelating agents in kelp help make micronutrients more bioavailable

### Application
- Cold-water kelp extract is preferred over heat-processed products, as heat can destroy hormones and vitamins
- Typical rate: **1 tablespoon per 5 gallons** of tea
- [[ingham-fresh-seaweed-kelp-brewing-ingredients]] can be used in coastal areas but may require rinsing to remove excess salt

### Sea Water Considerations
Diluted seawater (1:100 ratio) can be added to AACT as a mineral supplement, providing a full spectrum of trace elements. However, the sodium content must be monitored — excessive sodium is detrimental to both soil biology and plant health. Kelp products provide the mineral [[query-what-are-the-health-benefits-of-sea-buckthorn-and-how-do-i-grow-it]] water without the sodium risk.

## Humic and Fulvic Acids

Humic substances are the most chemically complex and biologically active components of decomposed organic matter. In AACT brewing, humic acids serve as both fungal food and a chelating agent that improves [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]].

### Humic vs. Fulvic Acids
- **Humic acids**: Large molecular weight compounds, dark brown to black, soluble in alkaline conditions. They are the primary fungal food source among humic substances.
- **Fulvic acids**: Smaller molecular weight, yellow to golden, soluble in all pH conditions. They are more biologically active and can penetrate plant tissues more easily.

### Role in AACT
- **Fungal food**: Humic acids provide complex carbon compounds that selectively feed beneficial fungi
- **Chelation**: Both humic and fulvic acids bind to micronutrients (iron, zinc, manganese, copper) and keep them in plant-available forms
- **pH buffering**: Humic acids help stabilize the pH of the tea during brewing
- **Detoxification**: Humic substances can bind to toxic compounds in soil or water, reducing their bioavailability

### Application Rates
- Liquid humic acid: **1–2 tablespoons per 5 gallons** of tea
- Extracting humic acids from raw humate deposits requires alkaline processing (typically sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide)
- Quality varies significantly between commercial products — dark color and solubility are basic quality indicators

## Fish Products

Fish-based ingredients provide both nitrogen (in the form of amino acids and peptides) and fungal food sources. The key distinction is between [[query-what-is-fish-hydrolysate-and-how-do-i-use-it-as-fertilizer]] fish emulsion.

### Fish Hydrolysate vs. Fish Emulsion
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Cold-processed (enzymatically digested) fish that retains proteins, oils, vitamins, and amino acids. This is the preferred form for AACT because the complex protein molecules serve as excellent fungal food.
- **Fish emulsion**: Heat-processed fish that has been partially rendered. The heat processing breaks down proteins into simpler compounds, making it more of a bacterial food. Some emulsions contain sulfuric acid as a preservative, which can harm beneficial biology.

### Role in AACT
- Provides **amino acids and peptides** that serve as both bacterial and fungal food
