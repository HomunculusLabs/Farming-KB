## Glasshouse and Protected Cropping Systems in Permaculture Design

## Overview

Glasshouses and protected cropping structures extend the growing season and
enable cultivation of warmth-loving species in cool and cold climates.
In [[permaculture-design]], glasshouses serve multiple functions beyond simple
plant production: they provide thermal mass for house heating, create
microclimates for seedling production, enable intensive food production in
small areas, and can integrate with animal housing, composting, and water
purification systems. Mollison treats the glasshouse as a multifunctional
design element rather than a standalone production unit.

## Design Philosophy for Protected Cropping

Mollison advocates for glasshouses that are integrated into the broader
site design rather than isolated structures. A well-designed glasshouse
should connect to the house (for passive solar heat transfer), to animal
systems (for CO2 enrichment and manure handling), and to water systems
(for greywater treatment and irrigation). The glasshouse is best sited
against the north wall of a dwelling in the southern hemisphere (south wall
in the northern hemisphere) to maximize heat gain and transfer.

## Thermal Design Principles

The key to effective glasshouse design in cool climates is thermal mass.
Mollison emphasizes several strategies for heat capture and storage:

- **Rock or water thermal mass walls** on the north side absorb heat
during the day and release it at night, moderating temperature swings.

- **Water-filled containers** (drums, tanks) strategically placed within
the glasshouse provide excellent thermal mass and moderate day-night
temperature differentials.

- **Insulated curtain systems** can be deployed at night to reduce heat
loss through the glazing. These may be automated or manually operated.

- **Earth-sheltered glasshouses** that are partially dug into the ground
on the cold side gain significant insulation from the earth itself,
reducing both heating costs and wind exposure.

## Species Selection for Glasshouse Production

Mollison recommends selecting species based on the thermal zone within the
glasshouse. Warm-loving species such as tomatoes, capsicums, eggplants,
cucumbers, and melons occupy the warmest positions. Cool-tolerant species
including lettuce, brassicas, peas, and herbs can be grown in the cooler
margins or during shoulder seasons.

### Seedling Production

One of the most valuable functions of a glasshouse in permaculture is
seedling production for the broader farm or garden system. Early spring
seedling raises give crops a head start that can mean the difference
between success and failure in short-season climates. The glasshouse
also serves for hardening off seedlings before transplanting.

### Winter Production

In cold climates, glasshouses enable production of winter salads, herbs,
and green vegetables that would otherwise require import or storage. The
addition of simple frost protection (row covers, cloches within the
glasshouse) can extend production through the coldest months.

## Integration with Animal Systems

Mollison describes the integration of poultry or rabbit housing with
glasshouse structures as a productive strategy. Animals provide body heat
and CO2 enrichment for plants, while the glasshouse provides shelter and
warmth for animals. Manure can be directly composted within the glasshouse
or channeled to external composting systems, with the heat from composting
contributing to glasshouse warming.

### Chicken-Greenhouse Integration

A classic permaculture integration places a chicken house adjacent to or
within the glasshouse. Chickens contribute CO2, heat, manure, and pest
control (by consuming insect pests). The deep litter from the chicken house
can be periodically moved to composting bays within the glasshouse, where
decomposition heat contributes to the thermal environment.

## Water Systems Integration

Glasshouses in permaculture design often incorporate [[water-storage-tanks]]
that serve dual functions: storing rainwater for irrigation and providing
thermal mass for temperature moderation. Greywater from the house can be
directed through the glasshouse for subsurface irrigation of fruiting
crops, closing the nutrient loop between household and garden.

## Passive Solar Glasshouse Design

For cold humid to cold climates, Mollison describes passive solar
glasshouses that rely on careful orientation, glazing selection, and
thermal mass rather than active heating systems. Key design elements
include:

- Sloped glazing angled to maximize winter sun capture at the local latitude.
- Double-glazing or insulated panels on the cold (non-sun-facing) wall.
- Foundation insulation to prevent heat loss to the ground.
- Thermal curtain systems for nighttime heat retention.
- North-facing insulated wall (in southern hemisphere) painted black
or fitted with water-filled thermal mass.

## Economic Considerations

The capital cost of glasshouse construction must be balanced against the
value of extended-season production, seedling raising capacity, and the
multi-functional benefits of integration with house heating, animal
systems, and water treatment. Mollison notes that even modest structures
can dramatically extend the productive capacity of a cool-climate property,
particularly when they serve as the central hub of a zone-one intensive
garden system.
