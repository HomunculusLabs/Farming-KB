# Desert Water Storage and Evaporation Control

Water is an expensive and precious resource in arid environments, where evaporation rates can reach 180 cm annually against rainfall as low as 30 cm. Effective water storage in deserts requires strategies that minimize evaporative losses through careful design of storage structures, surface treatments, and placement configurations.

## Evaporation Reduction Strategies

Three fundamental approaches exist for reducing evaporation in desert water storages:

1. **Underground and In-Earth Storage** — Placing water below the ground surface entirely eliminates direct solar exposure. Traditional desert cultures in Peru, Iran, Afghanistan, and the Canary Islands have long used underground galleries (such as the *quanats* of Iran) and clay- or cement-sealed caves to store water without significant loss.

2. **Surface Treatment of Dams** — Applying reflective or insulating materials to water surfaces. Options include silvered or white-painted covers, floating hexagonal blocks of lightweight concrete (specific gravity 0.8), tar-sealed floats, and wax rafts. The South African approach of covering entire catchment surfaces with hexagonal concrete blocks (a slurry of cement, sand, fine gravel, and polystyrene beads) is particularly effective.

3. **Storage Configurations** — Designing the shape and arrangement of storages to minimize the surface-area-to-volume ratio as water levels drop.

## Water Storage Bags

Flexible bags (polyethylene or butyl rubber) can serve as evaporation-free storage when submerged in swales or salt water. These bags need fencing protection from large animals and benefit from being silvered above to reflect light, with trellis or vine shade providing additional cooling. In salt-water environments, bags can be towed to different locations, or pipelines fed across lakes to deliver storage near villages.

## Soil Water Storage

Water absorbed rapidly into soil is safely removed from direct solar radiation. To maximize retention:

- Maintain a 10–15 cm surface layer of loose **gravel or volcanic cinder** as used in the Canary Islands, or use coarse mulch for the same shading and insulating effect.
- Ensure just enough water enters swales or fields to soak the soil without excess escaping to deep groundwater.
- Establish crops that use water effectively; trees can retrieve water stored in soil above impermeable clay or rock at 2 m depth, and desert trees penetrate to 30 m in free soils.

## Dam Structure for Evaporation Control

### Conical Section Dams

The most effective dam shape in desert environments is a **deep conical section**. As water level falls, the surface area decreases rapidly (as the square of the radius), dramatically reducing evaporative losses. "V"-shaped valley storages achieve much the same effect.

### Site Selection

- **Deep, shaded valleys** are far better protected from sun and wind than shallow valleys.
- An **east-west oriented valley** provides optimal shading.
- In desert or dryland foothills, sites with freshwater springs and groundwater (less than 200 ppm salt), shaded valleys for damming, and open free-draining soils should be prioritized.

### Series Dam Systems

Creating a series of 3–5 storages, each drainable by gravity to the next lower dam, is far more efficient than a single storage of equivalent surface area. Active management ensures use proceeds from the top down; as each dam is emptied, the evaporative surface area reduces by one-third, one-fourth, one-fifth, and so on.

Key design principles for series dams:
- It is better to create two series of 3 dams than one series of 6 dams.
- Smaller scale operations are more efficient in desert environments.
- Evaporation loss can be as little as 40% of a single dam of the same capacity.

## Wind Protection

Wind significantly accelerates surface evaporation. Windbreak strategies include:
- **Tree windbreaks** around dams
- **Artificial mesh windbreaks**
- **Trellis or shade structures** over small dams (as Australian Aborigines did with *gnammas* — open rock pools)
- **Covered tanks** and **roofed earth tanks** (still widely used in Australia)

Gnammas up to several metres across were traditionally covered with wooden frames and thick spinifex, providing both shade and wind protection.

## Traditional Underground Water Systems

Several ancient cultures developed sophisticated underground water conveyance and storage:

- **Quanats (Iran)** — Underground galleries transporting water many kilometres to desert farms
- **Clay/cement-sealed caves** — Used across Peru, Iran, Afghanistan, and the Canary Islands
- **Modern equivalents** — Extruded leak-proof pipes, concrete tanks, bores in headwater aquifers, and well-and-windmill systems

## Foothill and Hill Country Water Harvesting

Many ideal desert water sites lie in the inner valleys of spaced fold mountains, where deep sands over clay hold millions of litres of freshwater runoff from hills. A reliable windmill or solar pump can raise this water to a mesa or nearby hill. Since frost rarely exceeds 12–20 m elevation in warm deserts:

- A raised tank can irrigate subtropical crops on upper hill slopes
- Deciduous plants needing a chill factor are planted on lower slopes

Using surplus wet-year runoff to establish forest tree nurseries quickly makes such situations sustainable, self-reliant, and far less subject to serious drought. Success depends on careful site choice, responsible management, and modest scale.
