# Mycoremediation of Petroleum Hydrocarbons

Petroleum contamination is one of the most widespread forms of environmental pollution worldwide. Fungi offer effective bioremediation of crude oil, diesel, gasoline, BTEX compounds, and related hydrocarbons. See [[mycoremediation-basics]] for the general framework.

## Petroleum Contaminants

Petroleum contains thousands of compounds in four main fractions:

| Fraction | Composition | Environmental Concern |
|----------|-------------|----------------------|
| Saturates | Alkanes (straight and branched) | Less toxic but persistent |
| Aromatics | BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene) | Highly toxic, mobile in groundwater |
| PAHs | 2-5 ring polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons | Carcinogenic, persistent (see [[mycoremediation-pahs]]) |
| Asphaltenes | High-MW polar compounds | Very persistent, limit bioavailability |
| Resins | N, S, O-containing compounds | Contribute to soil toxicity |

## Fungal Degradation Mechanisms

### Alkane Degradation
- Initial terminal or subterminal oxidation of the alkane chain
- Alcohol to aldehyde to fatty acid
- Beta-oxidation of fatty acids to acetyl-CoA
- Entry into TCA cycle for complete mineralization
- Key enzymes: cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, alcohol dehydrogenase, aldehyde dehydrogenase

### Aromatic Compound Degradation
- Ligninolytic enzymes (LiP, MnP, laccase) attack aromatic rings
- Cytochrome P450 system for initial ring oxidation
- Ring cleavage via catechol or gentisate pathways
- See [[mycoremediation-pahs]] for detailed PAH degradation pathways

### Biosurfactant Production
- Some fungi produce biosurfactants that increase hydrocarbon bioavailability
- Surfactants reduce surface tension, emulsify oil, and increase contact between microbes and hydrocarbons
- Candida species produce sophorolipids; Pseudomonas (bacterial) produces rhamnolipids

## Key Fungal Species

### Hydrocarbonoclastic Fungi
- **Candida maltosa** — Degrades alkanes and PAHs; well-studied for petroleum bioremediation
- **Candida tropicalis** — Degrades n-alkanes C10-C32; produces biosurfactants
- **Cladosporium resinae** — "Kerosene fungus"; grows in aviation fuel tanks; degrades n-alkanes
- **Aspergillus niger** — Degrades petroleum hydrocarbons; produces organic acids that enhance metal mobility
- **Penicillium simplicissimum** — Degrades polyethylene (plastic) and petroleum hydrocarbons
- **Penicillium chrysogenum** — Degrades diesel fuel components
- **Trichoderma harzianum** — Plant-growth-promoting fungus that also degrades petroleum; synergistic with phytoremediation

### White-Rot Fungi
- **Pleurotus ostreatus** (oyster mushroom) — Used in field-scale oil spill remediation; degrades PAHs and alkanes
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium** — Model organism; mineralizes PAHs under nitrogen limitation
- **Trametes versicolor** — Degrades aromatic petroleum components; high laccase production
- **Bjerkandera adusta** — Effective against creosote and diesel contamination

## Paul Stamets' Oil Spill Work

Stamets' field demonstrations (documented in Mycelium Running):

- **Darrington, WA**: 4 piles of diesel-contaminated soil inoculated with oyster mushroom spawn
  - Pile 1: Oyster spawn only — robust mycelial growth, mushrooms fruited, hydrocarbon breakdown confirmed
  - Pile 2: Oyster spawn + sterile straw — similar results with faster colonization
  - Pile 3: No treatment (control) — foul-smelling, no biological activity
  - Pile 4: Enzyme-treated commercial product — some degradation but less than fungal treatment
- **Ecological succession**: Mycelium attracted insects, which attracted birds, which deposited seeds, creating a self-sustaining ecosystem
- **Timelines**: Significant hydrocarbon reduction within 6-8 weeks; ecological recovery within 4 months

## BTEX Degradation

BTEX compounds (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylenes) are the most mobile and toxic fraction of petroleum:

- **Benzene** — Known carcinogen; volatile; travels quickly in groundwater
- **Toluene** — CNS depressant; more readily degraded than benzene
- **Ethylbenzene** — Moderate toxicity; degraded by white-rot fungi
- **Xylenes** — Three isomers; moderate toxicity; degraded by fungal and bacterial systems

Fungal degradation of BTEX primarily via cytochrome P450 oxidation to corresponding alcohols, aldehydes, and acids. White-rot fungi can also degrade BTEX through their ligninolytic enzyme systems.

## Motor Oil and Used Oil

- Used motor oil contains PAHs, heavy metals (Pb, Zn, Cd from engine wear), and additive chemicals
- Fungal treatment can degrade the organic fraction while biosorbing metals
- Combination of [[mycoremediation-heavy-metals]] biosorption with petroleum degradation

## Bioremediation Strategies

### In-Situ Treatment
- **Mycelial mats**: Inoculated substrate placed on contaminated soil surface
- **Soil mixing**: Mycelial spawn mixed into contaminated soil
- **Bioaugmentation**: Add specific hydrocarbon-degrading fungal strains to contaminated sites
- **Biostimulation**: Add nutrients (N, P) to stimulate native fungal/bacterial communities

### Ex-Situ Treatment
- **Biopiles**: Contaminated soil piled with nutrient amendment and aeration
- **Composting**: Fungal-dominated composting of petroleum-contaminated soil
- **Bioreactors**: Slurry or solid-phase reactors with fungal inoculation
- **Land farming**: Spread contaminated soil, inoculate, and turn periodically

### Combined Approaches
- **Myco-phytoremediation**: Fungi + petroleum-tolerant plants (ryegrass, clover, willow)
- **Fungal-bacterial consortia**: Fungi degrade high-MW compounds; bacteria mineralize intermediates
- **Myco-electroremediation**: Electrokinetic enhancement of fungal degradation in clay soils

## Factors Affecting Petroleum Degradation

- **Hydrocarbon type**: Alkanes degrade faster than PAHs; lighter fractions degrade faster
- **Soil type**: Sandy soils allow better oxygen penetration than clay; organic matter increases sorption (reducing bioavailability)
- **Temperature**: Optimal 20-30C; degradation slows significantly below 10C
- **Moisture**: 40-80% water-holding capacity optimal; too dry limits biological activity
- **Nutrients**: C:N:P ratio of 100:10:1 optimal; petroleum-contaminated soils are often nitrogen-limited
- **Oxygen**: Aerobic conditions required; hydrogen peroxide can be added to support ligninolytic enzymes
- **Bioavailability**: Aged petroleum binds to soil organic matter; surfactants or nutrient addition can enhance availability

## Advantages Over Conventional Methods

- **Cost**: 10-100x cheaper than excavation and landfill disposal
- **In-situ**: No need to excavate or transport contaminated soil
- **Ecological recovery**: Kickstarts natural succession; restores soil biology
- **Non-destructive**: Preserves soil structure and native microbial communities
- **Scalable**: Can be applied from small spills to large contaminated sites

## Limitations

- Not suitable for all sites (high water table, extremely high contamination)
- Monitoring required to confirm complete degradation
- Weather and climate affect performance
- Some petroleum fractions (asphaltenes, resins) are very slow to degrade
- Regulatory acceptance varies by jurisdiction
