# Intensive Vegetable Spacing Systems

Intensive vegetable spacing refers to methods of arranging plants closer together than traditional row-based gardening. Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* critically examines various spacing philosophies, arguing that closer spacing demands proportionally higher soil fertility and water availability — resources that may be scarce during hard times.

## Overview

The core principle behind intensive spacing is maximizing yield per unit area by reducing the distance between plants. While this approach can produce impressive harvests under ideal conditions, Solomon warns that it creates fragile systems dependent on consistent irrigation and high soil [[cannabis-grow-nutrient-deficiency-problem-solving]] symptoms (yellowing leaves, stunting, poor fruit set), the gardener should either increase spacing or invest in building soil organic matter. The most productive approach is usually to do both simultaneously.

## Spacing and Water Availability

Water is the other critical variable. Closely spaced plants share a limited soil volume, and during drought, competition for moisture becomes severe. Solomon distinguishes between four water regimes:

- **Arid (less than 10 inches annual rainfall):** Only traditional or wider spacing is viable without irrigation. Xeriscaping principles apply, and drought-adapted varieties should be selected.
- **Dryland (10–20 inches):** Traditional row spacing with drought-tolerant varieties. Mulching and soil improvement help stretch limited moisture.
- **Supplemental irrigation (20–30 inches):** Moderate spacing is feasible with occasional watering during critical growth stages.
- **Full irrigation (30+ inches or reliable irrigation):** Intensive spacing becomes practical, though it still demands attention to soil fertility.

## Root Zone Competition

Each plant's root system extends roughly as wide as its above-ground canopy. When plants are spaced closely, root zones overlap significantly, creating belowground competition for water and mineral nutrients. In fertile soil, this competition is manageable.
