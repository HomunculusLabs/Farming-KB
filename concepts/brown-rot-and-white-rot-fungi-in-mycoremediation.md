## Overview

Mushrooms that decompose wood can be classified into two subgroups based on the type of rot they cause: brown rotters and white rotters. This distinction is critical for mycoremediation, as the enzymes each group produces determine which toxins they can break down. See also mycoremediation, antiviral-properties-of-mushrooms.

## Brown Rot Fungi

### Characteristics
- Extracellular enzymes break down the white, pulpy cellulose, leaving behind brownish lignin
- Cause checkered cubical cracking and shrinking in wood (commonly seen on downed conifer trees)
- Only about 7% of mushrooms are brown rot fungi; of those, about 70% are polypores (Gilbertson and Ryvarden, 1986-87)

### Key Brown Rot Species
- **Multicolored gilled polypore** (Lenzites betulina)
- **Large lentinus** (Lentinus ponderosus)
- **Sulphur tufts** (Laetiporus sulphureus and L. conifericola)
- **Velvet polypore** (Phaeolus schweinitzii)
- **Split-gill polypore** (Schizophyllum commune)
- **Agarikon** (Fomitopsis officinalis)
- **Dry rot house wreckers** (Serpula lacrymans and S. himantioides)

### Limitations for Mycoremediation
- Primarily break down cellulose, not lignin
- Less effective at breaking down petroleum hydrocarbons and other recalcitrant toxins
- Still useful for general decomposition and soil building

## White Rot Fungi

### Characteristics
- More numerous than brown rotters
- Produce enzymes that break down the recalcitrant brown fiber (lignin), leaving cellulose largely intact (wood appears bleached/white)
- Produce three key enzyme groups: lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases (Schliephake et al., 2003)
- Only white rot mushrooms produce manganese-dependent peroxidase — an enzyme that mineralizes wood and is particularly efficient at breaking hydrogen-carbon bonds
