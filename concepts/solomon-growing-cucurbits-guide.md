# Growing Cucurbits: Squash, Cucumber, Melon

The cucurbit family -- squash, pumpkin, cucumber, and melon -- shares
enough growing characteristics that Solomon treats them together. If you
have grown one successfully, you know them all. The easiest to start with
is squash, because it is the most vigorous, most tolerant of chilling,
and most tolerant of heavy soils.

## The Key to Success: Warmth and Timing

The single most important factor with cucurbits is waiting until the soil
is genuinely warm enough before sowing. You should almost always directly
sow cucurbits; they do not transplant well. Whether while sprouting or
when growing, cucurbits are highly sensitive to cold.

Sow in this order as soil temperature rises:

1. **Squash and pumpkins**: After soil has warmed and during a week of
   sunny weather.
2. **Cucumbers**: After squash has begun to grow well.
3. **Melons**: After cucumbers are established. Melons (especially
   watermelons) need more warmth than cucumbers.

Any time soil turns chilly and damp while cucurbit seeds are trying to
germinate, the seed may rot. Seeds that do sprout may damp-off and die
shortly after. If you lose seedlings in a chilly damp spring, a sowing
made a week or ten days later will outgrow an earlier one that barely
survived. If a spell of chilly or rainy weather hits, resow at another
spot when weather settles.

## Chitting Cucurbits

Because of their sensitivity to cold, damp soil, chitting (pre-sprouting)
cucurbits is especially worthwhile. Solomon's method:

1. Fold a section of paper towelling into roughly a one-foot square.
2. Dip it into tepid water and wring out the excess.
3. Cut it into quarters; place seeds for one station in each quarter.
4. Put the moist towelling with seeds into a container or sealed plastic
   bag at warm room temperature (at least 70F / 21C).
5. Begin checking after two days; plant before roots exceed one inch.

For more on chitting, see [[solomon-disease-identification-and-organic-remedies]].
- **Squash vine borer**: A major pest of squash and pumpkins. Rarely a
  problem with cucumbers and melons. After harvest, promptly burn winter
  squash vines to destroy overwintering larvae.
- **Powdery mildew**: Common on cucurbits, especially late in the season
  when weather gets cool and damp. See
  [[cucurbita-pepo]]* (acorn, delicata) do not store as long.
- *C. maxima* (Hubbard, buttercup) and *C. moschata* (butternut) store
  much longer.

Ideal storage conditions: cool (around 55F/13C) with low humidity and
good air circulation. Curing -- leaving winter squash outside to
experience more than one light frost, then bringing them inside where it
is warm and dry -- improves storage potential.

### Melons

Muskmelons and similar melons are ripe when the vine "slips" (releases
easily from the fruit). They do not ripen after being picked, which is
why supermarket melons, inevitably harvested unripe and hard (to
facilitate packing and shipping), are inferior.

Watermelon ripeness is determined by thumping and listening -- a skill
that must be learned through practice. In maritime climates, ripe melons
can only be produced by growing on wide sheets of black plastic mulch to
warm the soil.

## Varieties

For summer squash, the old OP Yellow Crookneck (vining) is best-tasting
and resists pests better than most hybrids. For winter squash, Solomon
recommends Sweet Meat (from Harris Seeds or Territorial) for long keeping.
