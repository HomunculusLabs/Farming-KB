## Permaculture Ethics of Earth Care and People Care

## Core Ethical Foundation

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] on tree roots exemplify this mutual support — organisms that give and receive, creating networks of benefit that strengthen the whole. This lesson from nature leads to a resolve to cooperate and take support roles in society, fostering interdependence that values individual contributions rather than opposition or competition.

People care begins with family and friends — those whose well-being directly affects our own — but matures into recognizing all humankind as family and all life as allied associations. Species care becomes an extension of people care, grounded in the recognition that all life shares common origins.

### Fair Share (Return of Surplus)

Implicit in the first two ethics is the principle of return — whatever we take, we must give back. This is expressed as the Law of Return: "Every object must responsibly provide for its replacement; society must, as a condition of use, replace an equal or greater resource than that used."

## From Self-Interest to Universal Ethics

The evolution of [[permaculture-ethics]] follows a developmental arc:

1. **Personal survival** — the basic drive to secure food, shelter, and safety
2. **Family and community care** — recognition that helping those close to us serves our own survival
3. **Species-level awareness** — understanding that all humankind is family
4. **Biocentric ethics** — seeing all life as allied associations with common origins
5. **Planetary responsibility** — acting as conscious participants in a self-regulating earth system

Each stage doesn't replace the previous one but encompasses it. Personal responsibility is not abandoned but matured into a broader care ethic.

## The Hypocrisy Test

Permaculture ethics demand consistency between values and actions. Mollison identified several common hypocrisies: pretending to save forests while buying daily newspapers and packaged food; preserving native plants while relying on chemical agriculture; adopting a diet that demands broadscale food production while claiming to care for the land. True conservationists have gardens that support their food needs and are actively working to reduce their energy consumption to a level that can be supplied by local renewable resources.

## Communal Permanence vs. Exploitative Systems

Mollison distinguished three historical approaches to permanent agriculture:

- **Feudal permanence** (ancient China): People hauled nutrients from canals, cesspits, and forests to annual grain cultures. People were bound to the landscape by unremitting toil, leading eventually to famine and revolution.
- **Baronial permanence** (prairie, pampas, modern western farms): Large holdings with few people creating vast grazing leases for single animal species — working at the lowest possible level of land use. Once mechanized, these destroy whole landscapes and soil complexes, creating agricultural deserts.
- **Communal permanence** (forests and stable cultures): Generations of care and knowledge maintained in stable communities — the ability to plant a pecan or citrus when old, knowing it will not be cut by grandchildren.

The characteristic that typifies all permanent agricultures is that the needs of the system for energy are provided by that system itself. Modern crop agriculture, by contrast, is totally dependent on external energies — hence the oil problem and its associated pollution.

## Three Parallel Responses

In a world losing forests, species, and ecosystems, Mollison identified three concurrent responses:

1. **Care for surviving natural assemblies** — leave wilderness to heal itself
2. **Rehabilitate degraded or eroded land** — using complex pioneer species and long-term plant assemblies
3. **Create our own complex living environment** — incorporating as many species as we can save or need, from wherever on earth they come

These responses work in parallel, not in opposition. One can simultaneously protect remaining wild areas, restore damaged landscapes, and create productive human settlements.

## Philosophical Underpinnings

The permaculture ethic draws on multiple philosophical traditions. Philosopher-gardeners and farmer-poets are distinguished by their sense of wonder and real feeling for the environment. When religions generalize love and respect to all living systems as a witness to the potential of creation, they join scientists and poets in admiration of the complexity and self-sustaining properties of natural systems. Gardener, scientist, philosopher, poet, and religious adherent can all conspire in reverence for the earth.

The [[james-lovelock]], supports this ethic: the biosphere actively creates conditions required for its own support and systematically builds up the stock of materials it needs, moving toward increasing complexity, diversity, and stability. Our acts are of great effect on this self-regulating system, and the earth's reaction is to restore equilibrium and balance.

## The Transition from Ethics to Action

Ethics without action are merely sentiments. Permaculture bridges the gap between ethical understanding and practical implementation through its design system. The transition from recognizing an ethical imperative to acting on it requires tools, techniques, and a systematic approach — which is precisely what permaculture provides.

The first step is always personal: examining one's own life for contradictions between stated values and daily actions. The hypocrisy test is not meant to shame but to clarify — to reveal the specific areas where changes in behavior can bring life into alignment with ethics.

## Practical Application

The practical implementation of these ethics begins at home: getting our house and garden in order so that they support us, and working to limit our population on earth. These duties are intimately connected, as stable regions create stable populations. If we do not get our cities, homes, and gardens in order so that they feed and shelter us, we must lay waste to all other natural systems to survive.

### Household-Level Actions

At the household level, earth care and people care translate into concrete practices:

- **Food production**: Growing at least some portion of one's own food reduces dependence on broadscale destructive agriculture
- **Waste cycling**: Composting, mulching, and recycling return nutrients to the soil and reduce landfill burden
- **Energy conservation**: Retrofitting homes for energy efficiency and generating energy from local renewable sources
- **[[water-management]]**: Catching and storing rainwater, recycling greywater, and designing landscapes to absorb and filter water
- **Habitat creation**: Reserving portions of even small gardens for wildlife habitat — insect hotels, frog ponds, native plantings

### Community-Level Actions

Beyond the household, ethical action extends to community participation:

- Supporting local food systems and farmers who practice sustainable methods
- Forming or subscribing to conservation organizations whose legal charter supports habitat protection
- Sharing surplus production with neighbors and community food banks
- Participating in local governance to advocate for ecologically sound policies
- Teaching and demonstrating [[permaculture-principles]] to others

### Bioregional Responsibility

At the largest practical scale, permaculture ethics demand bioregional awareness:

- Understanding the ecological capacities and limits of one's home region
- Matching consumption patterns to what the local landscape can sustainably provide
- Advocating for the protection of remaining natural areas within the bioregion
- Supporting the rehabilitation of degraded lands through reforestation, watershed restoration, and soil building

This bioregional perspective recognizes that ethics are not abstract principles but practical guides for living within ecological limits. The closer one's lifestyle aligns with the carrying capacity of the local landscape, the less pressure is placed on distant ecosystems and the more resilient one's community becomes to global disruptions.

## Permaculture Ethics and Modern Challenges

The permaculture ethics of earth care, people care, and fair share speak directly to the major challenges of the 21st century: climate change, biodiversity loss, soil degradation, water scarcity, social inequality, and food insecurity. These are not separate problems but interconnected symptoms of a civilization that has violated the Law of Return at planetary scale.

Climate change results from extracting fossil carbon without returning it to stable storage. Biodiversity loss results from destroying habitat without restoring equivalent habitat. Soil degradation results from taking nutrients without returning them. Water scarcity results from depleting aquifers without allowing recharge. Each violation of the Law of Return creates a debt that accumulates interest in the form of escalating environmental crisis.

The permaculture response is to apply the ethics systematically at every scale — from the individual garden to the bioregion to the planetary system. Every action that returns more than it takes, that builds rather than degrades, that creates rather than destroys, is an application of permaculture ethics. The cumulative effect of millions of such actions is the transformation of civilization from a destructive force to a regenerative one.

The smallest garden can reserve a few square metres for insect, lizard, frog, or butterfly habitat. Larger gardens and farms can fence off forest and wetland areas of critical value to local species. The practical way to proceed beyond the home garden is to form or subscribe to institutes or organizations whose legal charter carries out conservation activities.
