# Contamination Prevention in The Mushroom Cultivator

Source: The Mushroom Cultivator (Stamets & Chilton, 1983), Chapters II, XII-XIII
Updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Contamination is the central challenge of [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]. The air we breathe is a living sea of microscopic organisms -- fungi, bacteria, viruses and plants use the atmosphere to carry their offspring. In creating a sterile environment, the cultivator seeks to give advantage to the mushroom over myriad competitors. As Stamets states: "skill in [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]] is tantamount to skill in contamination control."

## Five Primary Sources of Contamination

1. **The immediate external environment** -- airborne spores and microorganisms
2. **The culture medium** -- grain, agar, or [[growing-gourmet-substrate-materials-wood-types]]
3. **The culturing equipment** -- tools, containers, filters
4. **The cultivator** -- clothes, skin, breath (humans are major contamination vectors)
5. **The [[query-how-to-store-mushroom-spores-long-term]] or mycelium** -- the culture material itself

## Sterile Laboratory Design

### Construction

An afternoon's work converts a walk-in closet, pantry, or small storage room into a workable inoculation chamber:
1. Remove all rugs, curtains, and cloth-like material that harbor dust and spores
2. Clean floors, walls, ceiling with mild disinfectant
3. Paint with high gloss white enamel for easy future cleaning
4. Cover windows and air leaks with plastic sheeting
5. Construct an antechamber (airlock) on both sides of entrance

### Essential Equipment
- Chair and sturdy table with smooth surface
- Propane torch, alcohol lamp, bunsen burner, or butane lighter
- Spray bottle with 10% bleach solution
- Sterile petri dishes and test tube slants
- Labels, notebook, marking pens
- Agar knife and inoculating loop

### Hygiene Protocol

A strict and unwavering regimen of hygiene is mandatory:
- Clean room with disinfectant, mop floors
- Wash room air with fine mist of 10% bleach solution
- Do not re-enter for minimum 15 minutes after spraying (let suspended particles settle)
- This cleaning MUST precede every set of inoculations
- Contamination is easier to prevent than to eliminate after it occurs

**Contamination follows seasonal patterns:**
- Winter: free spores drastically decrease
- Spring and summer: remarkable increase in spore load
- More cleaning necessary during peak contamination periods
- All contaminated jars and dishes disposed of safely

### Contamination Control Methods

Three escalating levels of control:

1. **Triethylene glycol vaporization** (inexpensive): Creates a cloud of viscous droplets that trap airborne particles. Leaves little noticeable film. Daily hygiene maintenance still recommended.

2. **Glove box** (inexpensive): Airtight chamber providing semi-sterile still air environment. Constructed of wood with sneeze window. Front face covered with removable sterilizable cotton cloth. Main advantage: inexpensive, easily cleaned area with minimal air movement.

3. **HEPA filters** (moderate expense): Screen out all particulates exceeding 0.1-0.3 microns -- smaller than spores of all fungi and practically all bacteria. Built into laminar flow hoods. Some laboratories have entire walls or ceilings of HEPA filters creating positive pressure sterile environments.

## Contamination at Each Cultivation Stage

### Agar Culture

Most contaminants come from airborne spores. Key prevention:
- Work quickly and deliberately with flame-sterilized tools
- Do not leave petri dish lids open longer than necessary
- Discard contaminated dishes immediately
- 10% contamination rate is tolerable; 25% is common for wild specimen isolations

### Grain Culture

Contamination from both airborne spores AND the grain itself (laden with fungi, yeasts, bacteria):
- Sterilize grain for full hour at 15 psi (vs. 25 min for liquids)
- Inoculate immediately upon cooling to room temperature
- The cultivator is a major source -- newly laundered clothes, clean hands
- Good hygiene is of utmost importance when transferring from agar to grain
- In grain culture, contaminants become more elusive (3D vs 2D), often escaping detection

### Bulk Substrate / Spawn Running

Major contributors: materials used, the spawn, the workers, the facilities:
- Spawn under carefully controlled hygienic conditions
- Exclude fungus gnats with tight, well-sealed working area
- Disinfect area and tools one day prior with 10% bleach solution
- COLONIZATION MUST PROCEED AS RAPIDLY AS POSSIBLE
- Once mycelium becomes dominant, natural antibiotics inhibit competitors

### Cropping Stage

Stagnant air pockets breed contaminants like Trichoderma and Pseudomonas ([[growing-gourmet-bacterial-blotch-disease-management]]):
- Maintain air movement across all cropping surfaces
- Avoid over-watering mushroom caps (wet caps are prime disease sites)
- Lower humidity and run casing drier than normal during disease outbreaks
- Agaricus growers use slightly chlorinated water (150 ppm)
- Do not water contaminated regions -- this spreads infection

## Identifying Contamination Vectors

Tracking contamination source uses deduction:
- Mold colony growing beside transferred wedge: contamination from the previous culture
- Mold along outer periphery of petri dish: airborne spores entered during incubation
- Systematic contamination across multiple jars: likely equipment or operator error
- Random contamination: likely airborne or environmental

## Warning Against Over-Kill

Some cultivators wage war on contamination to an unhealthy extreme using toxic fungicides and bacteriocides, exposing themselves to dangerously mutagenic chemical agents. Safer alternatives exist that are just as effective:
- 10% bleach solution
- HEPA filtration
- Glove boxes
- Good laboratory technique
- A positive attitude toward cleanliness is more important than any equipment installation

## Common Contaminant Groups

The Mushroom Cultivator identifies over 30 specific contaminants including:
- **Molds**: Trichoderma (Forest Green), Penicillium (Bluish Green), Aspergillus (Green), Neurospora (Pink), Dactylium (Cobweb), Mucor (Black Pin), Chaetomium (Olive Green)
- **Bacteria**: Pseudomonas (Bacterial Blotch), Bacillus (Wet Spot), Streptomyces (Firefang)
- **Fungal diseases**: Verticillium (Dry Bubble), Mycogone (Wet Bubble)
- **Yeasts**: Cryptococcus (Cream Colored), Torula (Black)
- **Virus**: Die-Back Disease
