# Fungal Glomalin and Soil Carbon

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced abundantly by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi that plays a pivotal role in soil aggregate formation and stabilization, and represents a significant component of soil organic carbon pools. Discovered by Sara Wright and colleagues in the 1990s, glomalin has transformed our understanding of how mycorrhizal fungi contribute to soil structure and long-term carbon sequestration in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Discovery and Characterization

Glomalin was first identified and described by Wright and Upadhyaya (1996) as a copiously produced glycoprotein from arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. The compound was detected using a monoclonal antibody technique developed to specifically recognize glomalin in soil extracts. This immunological detection method allowed researchers to quantify glomalin concentrations in soil aggregates and to correlate glomalin content with measures of soil physical properties.

The name "glomalin" reflects its origin from Glomales, the taxonomic order that was traditionally used to classify arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. Glomalin is operationally defined by its extraction characteristics and immunoreactivity rather than by a precise chemical structure, as the compound has proven difficult to purify to homogeneity. It is extracted from soil using alkaline solutions (sodium citrate at high temperature and pH) and can be quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) methods.

## Chemical Properties

Glomalin is a recalcitrant glycoprotein with unusual stability in soil. Its resistance to microbial decomposition is attributed to several chemical properties including a high degree of cross-linking through iron and other metal ions, the presence of aromatic amino acid residues, and the formation of complexes with soil mineral particles. These properties give glomalin a turnover time in soil estimated at years to decades, far longer than most soil organic matter fractions.

The glycoprotein contains both protein and carbohydrate components, with the protein component including specific epitopes recognized by the monoclonal antibody. Glomalin binds strongly to soil mineral particles, particularly clays and iron and aluminum oxides, through electrostatic interactions and metal-bridging mechanisms. This binding contributes to both its recalcitrance and its role in aggregate stabilization.

## Role in Soil Aggregate Formation

The central ecological function of glomalin is its contribution to the formation and stabilization of soil aggregates. Soil aggregates are combinations of mineral soil particles and organic matter that create the porous structure essential for soil fertility, water infiltration, and gas exchange. Glomalin acts as a biological glue that binds soil particles together into stable aggregates.

Wright and Upadhyaya (1998) examined glomalin content across 16 soils from the Middle Atlantic states of the United States and one from Scotland. They found a strong positive relationship between the logarithm of glomalin content, expressed as milligrams per gram of soil, and the percentage of aggregate stability, with a correlation coefficient of 0.86. This strong correlation was consistent across a range of soil types and cropping histories.

The correlation of immunoreactive, easily extractable glomalin with aggregate stability was particularly strong (0.84), suggesting that the pool of recently produced glomalin is most important for aggregate formation. Total extractable glomalin showed a correlation of 0.70 with aggregate stability, while total soil carbon showed a correlation of 0.65.

## Mechanism of Aggregate Stabilization

Among the hypotheses for the enhanced aggregate-forming abilities of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, the production of glomalin has emerged as the dominant explanation. Other proposed mechanisms include the production of more mucilage than other fungi, the production of stickier mucilage, and the formation of more cation bonds with clay particles and other organic matter. However, the strong correlation between glomalin content and aggregate stability supports the primacy of glomalin in this process.

The mechanism of aggregate stabilization by glomalin involves the coating of soil particles with the glycoprotein, which acts as a binding agent. As arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae grow through soil, they deposit glomalin on the surfaces of mineral particles and organic matter. The sticky glycoprotein binds adjacent particles together, creating microaggregates that can then be further stabilized into macroaggregates by hyphal entanglement.

## Three-Phase Model of Aggregate Formation

Bethlenfalvay and colleagues (1999) proposed a three-phase model of water-stable soil aggregate formation mediated by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and their interactions with bacteria and plant roots.

During phase 1, mean aggregate size initially decreased because of an increase in bacterial numbers, while mycorrhizal fungi were in a lag phase of growth. The metabolic activity of the increased bacterial population was thought to weaken the cohesion of small aggregates.

During phase 2, the rapidly developing network of mycorrhizal hyphae increased the entanglement of small aggregates into larger aggregates through the production of glomalin. This phase represents the dominant period of aggregate stabilization.

During phase 3, compaction of soil by increased root growth was thought to possibly contribute to further aggregate stabilization. The interaction between root growth, mycorrhizal hyphal networks, and glomalin production creates a self-reinforcing system of soil structure maintenance.

## Glomalin and Soil Carbon Sequestration

Glomalin represents a significant pool of soil organic carbon. Because of its recalcitrance and long turnover time, glomalin contributes to long-term carbon sequestration in soil. The concentration of glomalin in soil can range from a few milligrams to over 100 milligrams per gram of soil, depending on soil type, vegetation, and land management history.

The carbon content of glomalin is estimated at approximately 36 to 59 percent by weight, making it a substantial carbon reservoir. In soils with high mycorrhizal activity, glomalin can account for a significant fraction of the total soil organic carbon pool. The contribution of glomalin to soil carbon is particularly important in grassland and forest ecosystems where arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are abundant.

## Agricultural Implications

The role of glomalin in soil aggregation has important implications for agricultural management. Minimal-till or no-till agricultural practices reduce the disruption of hyphal networks in soil, allowing mycorrhizal fungi to continue producing glomalin and maintaining soil aggregate structure. The conversion of pasture to tilled cropland reduces stable macroaggregates due to the physical disruption of hyphal networks and the consequent reduction in glomalin-mediated aggregation.

The presence of ryegrass in an alfisol increased the content of stable macroaggregates from 36 to 78 percent in six months, demonstrating the rapid rate at which mycorrhizal-mediated aggregation can occur when vegetation is established. In contrast, conversion of pasture with 90 percent stable macroaggregates into tilled tomato crop reduced aggregates to 58 percent.

## Synergism with Organic Matter and Bacteria

There is evidence that arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphal growth is stimulated by the presence of organic matter, such as sloughed material from root surfaces. This increased fungal growth and the presence of organic matter support increased bacterial populations. The synergistic effect among organic matter, mycorrhizal hyphae, and bacteria enhances the development of stable soil structure.

The addition of starch to soils provided an easily obtained source of energy for bacterial growth and created hot spots for fungal growth, increasing both the density and mean size of aggregates. The importance of residual islands of plants and their mycorrhizae for rapid soil accretion and stabilization was demonstrated on Mount Saint Helens after the 1980 pyroclastic disturbance.

## Path Analysis of Aggregation Factors

Miller and Jastrow (1990) used path analysis to understand the interactive links between plant communities, rooting characteristics, mycorrhizal associations, and the formation of stable aggregates in soil under prairie restoration. They found that the path from fine root length to root colonization and abundance of extraradical hyphal length gave the greatest correlation to aggregate size.

Interestingly, this pathway was more strongly associated with fine roots common to native plant species than with the abundance of very fine roots associated with non-indigenous plant species. This finding suggests that there may be some degree of coevolution of soil development and plant community type, with native plant-fungal combinations being more effective at building soil structure.
