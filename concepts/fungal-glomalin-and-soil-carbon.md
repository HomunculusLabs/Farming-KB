# Fungal Glomalin and Soil Carbon

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced abundantly by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]
that plays a pivotal role in soil [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]] and stabilization, and
represents a significant component of soil organic carbon pools. Discovered by
Sara Wright and colleagues in the 1990s, glomalin has transformed our
understanding of [[query-how-mycorrhizal-fungi-help-plants]] contribute to soil structure and
long-term carbon sequestration in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Discovery and Characterization

Glomalin was first identified and described by Wright and Upadhyaya (1996) as
a copiously produced glycoprotein from [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] fungi. The
compound was detected using a monoclonal antibody technique developed to
specifically recognize glomalin in soil extracts. This immunological detection
method allowed researchers to quantify glomalin concentrations in soil
aggregates and to correlate glomalin content with measures of soil physical
properties.

The name "glomalin" reflects its origin from Glomales, the taxonomic order
that was traditionally used to classify arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. Glomalin
is operationally defined by its extraction characteristics and immunoreactivity
rather than by a precise chemical structure, as the compound has proven
difficult to purify to homogeneity.

It is extracted from soil using alkaline solutions (sodium citrate at high
temperature and pH) and can be quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent
assay (ELISA) methods.

## Chemical Properties

Glomalin is a recalcitrant glycoprotein with unusual stability in soil. Its
resistance to microbial decomposition is attributed to several chemical
properties including a high degree of cross-linking through iron and other
metal ions, the presence of aromatic amino acid residues, and the formation of
complexes with soil mineral particles.

These properties give glomalin a turnover time in soil estimated at years to
decades, far longer than most soil organic matter fractions. The glycoprotein
contains both protein and carbohydrate components, with the protein component
including specific epitopes recognized by the monoclonal antibody.

Glomalin binds strongly to soil mineral particles, particularly clays and iron
and aluminum oxides, through electrostatic interactions and metal-bridging
mechanisms. This binding contributes to both its recalcitrance and its role
in aggregate stabilization.

## Role in Soil Aggregate Formation

The central ecological function of glomalin is its contribution to the
formation and stabilization of soil aggregates. Soil aggregates are
combinations of mineral soil particles and organic matter that create the
porous structure essential for soil fertility, water infiltration, and gas
exchange.

Glomalin acts as a biological glue that binds soil particles together into
stable aggregates. Wright and Upadhyaya (1998) examined glomalin content
across 16 soils from the Middle Atlantic states of the United States and one
from Scotland. They found a strong positive relationship between the logarithm
of glomalin content, expressed as milligrams per gram of soil, and the
percentage of aggregate stability, with a correlation coefficient of 0.86.
This strong correlation was consistent across a range of soil types and
cropping histories.

The correlation of immunoreactive, easily extractable glomalin with aggregate
stability was particularly strong (0.84), suggesting that the pool of recently
produced glomalin is most important for aggregate formation. Total extractable
glomalin showed a correlation of 0.70 with aggregate stability, while total
soil carbon showed a correlation of 0.65.

## Mechanism of Aggregate Stabilization

Among the hypotheses for the enhanced aggregate-forming abilities of
arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, the production of glomalin has emerged as the
dominant explanation. Other proposed mechanisms include the production of more
mucilage than other fungi, the production of stickier mucilage, and the
formation of more cation bonds with clay particles and other organic matter.
However, the strong correlation between glomalin content and aggregate
stability supports the primacy of glomalin in this process.

The mechanism of aggregate stabilization by glomalin involves the coating of
soil particles with the glycoprotein, which acts as a binding agent. As
arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae grow through soil, they deposit glomalin on the
surfaces of mineral particles and organic matter. The sticky glycoprotein
binds adjacent particles together, creating microaggregates that can then be
further stabilized into macroaggregates by hyphal entanglement.

## Three-Phase Model of Aggregate Formation

Bethlenfalvay and colleagues (1999) proposed a three-phase model of water-
stable soil aggregate formation mediated by arbuscular [[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]]
their interactions with bacteria and plant roots.

During phase 1, mean aggregate size initially decreased because of an increase
in bacterial numbers, while mycorrhizal fungi were in a lag phase of growth.
The [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] of the increased bacterial population was thought to
weaken the cohesion of small aggregates.

During phase 2, the rapidly developing network of mycorrhizal hyphae increased
the entanglement of small aggregates into larger aggregates through the
production of glomalin. This phase represents the dominant period of aggregate
stabilization.

During phase 3, compaction of soil by increased root growth was thought to
possibly contribute to further aggregate stabilization. The interaction
between root growth, mycorrhizal hyphal networks, and glomalin production
creates a self-reinforcing system of soil structure maintenance.

## Glomalin and Climate Change

### Carbon Sequestration Potential

Glomalin represents a significant pool of soil organic carbon that contributes
to long-term carbon sequestration. Because of its recalcitrance and long
turnover time measured in years to decades, glomalin persists in soil far
longer than most other organic matter fractions. The carbon content of
glomalin is estimated at approximately 36 to 59 percent by weight, making it
a substantial carbon reservoir.

In soils with high mycorrhizal activity, glomalin can account for a
significant fraction of the total soil organic carbon pool. The contribution
of glomalin to [[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] is particularly important in
grassland and forest ecosystems where arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are
abundant. Management practices that promote mycorrhizal activity -- such as
[[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], diverse crop rotations, and cover cropping -- can enhance
glomalin production and increase soil carbon stocks.

### Climate Change Impacts on Glomalin Production

Climate change may affect glomalin production through multiple pathways.
Elevated atmospheric CO2 concentrations have been shown to increase belowground
carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi, potentially boosting glomalin
production. However, warming temperatures and altered precipitation patterns
may disrupt plant-fungal associations and reduce mycorrhizal colonization in
some ecosystems.
