# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi in Tropical Forest Restoration
Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) play critical roles in tropical
rain forest restoration by facilitating plant establishment,
improving soil structure, and enabling nutrient cycling.
Deforestation and habitat fragmentation disrupt these fungal
communities, creating barriers to natural regeneration.
## Impact of Deforestation on AMF Communities
Tropical rain forests are losing coverage at approximately 2%
annually. Deforestation causes reduced AMF spore density and
species diversity in disturbed soils, altered [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] that connect plants, reduced pollinator and
seed disperser populations, and changes in soil microbiota including
nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
## Successional Stages and AMF Responses
### Light-Demanding Pioneer Species
- High light requirements, rapid growth rates
- Early reproduction with many small fruits
- Non-mycorrhizal or facultative mycorrhizal
- High invasive growth potential, form seed banks
- Responsive to AMF inoculation in degraded areas
### Shade-Tolerant Late Successional Species
- Low light requirements, slow growth rates
- Late reproduction with fewer larger fruits
- Facultative or obligate mycorrhizal, depend on AMF
  for [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]], organic material retention, improved
soil structure, and reduced water and wind erosion.
## AMF Inoculation Techniques
AMF inoculation for restoration can be accomplished through several
methods, each with distinct advantages. Soil-based inoculum collected
from intact reference forests provides the most complete suite of
native AMF species but carries risks of transmitting soil pathogens.
Spore-based inoculum offers cleaner application and allows targeted
species selection. In vitro propagated AMF, grown on transformed
roots in sterile culture, provides contaminant-free inoculum but is
limited to a few commercially available species. On-farm production
using trap plants in nursery settings balances practicality with
diversity. For large-scale restoration, native topsoil transfer
remains the most effective method for restoring complete AMF
communities.

## Climate Change Impacts on AMF Function
Rising temperatures and altered precipitation patterns pose
significant threats to AMF-mediated restoration outcomes. Experimental
warming studies show that AMF community composition shifts under
heat stress, with potential loss of heat-sensitive taxa that are
important for late-successional tree establishment. Increased drought
frequency may reduce AMF colonization rates and hyphal extension into
soil, limiting nutrient uptake capacity. However, some AMF taxa show
remarkable resilience to climate extremes, suggesting that inoculum
selection for climate-adapted AMF strains could improve restoration
success under future conditions.
