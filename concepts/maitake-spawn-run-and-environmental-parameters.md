## Phase 1: Spawn Run

The spawn run is the period during which mycelium fully colonizes the synthetic substrate. This is the longest phase and sets the foundation for subsequent fruiting.

### Environmental Parameters — Spawn Run

| Parameter | Commercial (Royse & Guardino 1997) | Home Grower (Chalmers 1994) |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | 21–25 °C | 20–24 °C |
| **Relative Humidity** | 70–80% | 70–80% |
| **CO₂ Concentration** | 2,000–5,000 ppm | 2,000–5,000 ppm |
| **Light** | ≤ 50 lux | ≤ 50 lux |
| **Ventilation** | Minimal; filtered air exchange | Minimal; passive exchange |
| **Duration** | 30–42 days | 28–42 days |

### Spawn Run Timeline

The colonization of the substrate follows a predictable morphological progression:

1. **Day 0–9 — Colonization:** Undifferentiated white mycelium spreads outward from the spawn, colonizing the interior substrate. Growth is rapid and hyphae are uniformly white.

2. **Day 9–17 — Surface Penetration:** Mycelium reaches the surface of the substrate block. A dense, white mycelial mat becomes visible on the exterior.

3. **Day 17–30 — Maturation:** Orange-brown discoloration appears on the mycelial surface, forming a protective **mycelial coat**. The surface develops an uneven topography. During this phase, metabolic exudates (droplets of amber liquid) may appear on the block surface — these are normal metabolic byproducts indicating active mycelial metabolism.

4. **Day 30–42 — Primordia Formation:** Grayish primordia begin to emerge from the mature mycelial coat, signaling readiness for the transition to the primordia initiation phase.

> **Note:** The presence of exudates around day 30 is an important visual indicator. Blocks that show uniform browning and exudate formation are typically ready for the environmental shift that triggers primordia initiation.

## Phase 2: Primordia Initiation

Primordia initiation requires a deliberate environmental shock to shift the mycelium from vegetative growth to reproductive mode. The key triggers are a **temperature drop**, **increased fresh air exchange**, and **increased light intensity**.

### Environmental Parameters — Primordia Initiation

| Parameter | Commercial (Royse & Guardino 1997) | Home Grower (Chalmers 1994) |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | 15–18 °C | 15–17 °C |
| **Relative Humidity** | 90–95% | 90–95% |
| **CO₂ Concentration** | 600–1,000 ppm | 600–1,000 ppm |
| **Light** | 200–500 lux | 200 lux |
| **Ventilation** | Increased fresh air exchange | Increased fresh air exchange |
| **Duration** | 7–14 days | 7–14 days |

### Key Transition Triggers

- **Temperature reduction** from spawn-run levels (~22 °C) down to 15–18 °C is the primary cue.
- **CO₂ must be reduced dramatically** — from 2,000–5,000 ppm to below 1,000 ppm — to suppress continued vegetative mycelial growth and encourage primordial knotting.
- **Light intensity increases** from ≤ 50 lux to approximately 200 lux. Light quality (blue spectrum, ~470 nm) is preferred per Stamets (1993).
- **Humidity must remain high** (90–95%) to prevent the young primordia from desiccating.

## Phase 3: Fruiting Body Development

Once primordia have formed, the environmental parameters are adjusted slightly to promote robust fruiting body enlargement while maintaining the quality of the developing mushrooms.

### Environmental Parameters — Fruiting Body Development

| Parameter | Commercial (Royse & Guardino 1997) | Home Grower (Chalmers 1994) |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | 16–18 °C | 16–18 °C |
| **Relative Humidity** | 85–90% | 85–90% |
| **CO₂ Concentration** | 500–1,000 ppm | 500–1,000 ppm |
| **Light** | 200–500 lux | 200–500 lux |
| **Ventilation** | Moderate continuous fresh air | Moderate continuous fresh air |
| **Duration** | 10–21 days | 10–21 days |

### Fruiting Body Characteristics

Maitake forms large, compound, bracket-like clusters with multiple overlapping caps. The fruiting bodies are typically brown-gray with lighter margins. Proper air exchange during this phase is critical — insufficient fresh air leads to elongated, leggy growth with reduced cap development and lower market quality.

## Practical Considerations

### Commercial vs. Home Grower Differences

The parameters defined by Royse and Guardino (1997) reflect controlled-environment growing rooms with mechanical climate control, automated humidification, and CO₂ monitoring. Chalmers (1994) provides ranges achievable in home or hobby settings where environmental control is less precise. The home grower ranges are generally slightly narrower and represent a practical compromise.

### Light Requirements

Light plays a critical role at two distinct thresholds:

- **Spawn run:** Light should be limited to ≤ 50 lux to avoid premature primordia formation before full colonization.
- **Primordia initiation onward:** Light should be increased to ~200 lux. Stamets (1993) emphasizes that maitake responds to blue-spectrum light, and that too little light during fruiting results in pale, poorly formed mushrooms.

### Common Pitfalls

- **Initiating primordia too early** (before the mycelial coat and exudates form) leads to poor pin sets and reduced yields.
- **Insufficient CO₂ reduction** during primordia initiation is the most common cause of failure — mycelium continues vegetative growth instead of forming fruiting structures.
- **Rapid humidity fluctuations** during fruiting can cause primordia abortion or cap cracking.
