# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi in Agriculture - Functions and Management

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapters 1, 3, 20, 21

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), belonging to the phylum Glomeromycota, are ubiquitous in agricultural and natural ecosystems. About 80% of all land plants have a symbiotic relationship with fungi of the phylum Glomeromycota. The fungus penetrates cells in the plant's roots and provides the plant with phosphates and other nutrients from the soil. Fossil evidence and DNA sequence analysis suggest that arbuscular mycorrhiza appeared 400-460 million years ago, when the first plants were colonizing land.

AMF form a symbiotic relationship with more than 80% of plant species living on land. Symbiosis plays an equally important role in natural ecosystems and in agroecosystems. Mycorrhizae have been characterized as an ecosystem service to humanity.

## Nutrient Uptake Enhancement

### Phosphorus Acquisition

The primary benefit of AMF is enhanced phosphorus (P) uptake. Mycorrhizal root systems increase the absorptive area of roots 10-1,000 times. Plant roots alone may be incapable of taking up phosphate ions that are immobilized, for example, in soils with basic pH. The mycelium of the mycorrhizal fungus can, however, access these phosphorus sources and make them available to the plants they colonize.

The mechanisms of increased absorption are both physical and chemical:
- **Physical**: Mycorrhizal mycelia are much smaller in diameter than the smallest root, and can explore a greater volume of soil, providing a larger surface area for absorption.
- **Chemical**: The cell membrane chemistry of fungi is different from that of plants, enabling different nutrient uptake mechanisms.

AMF release powerful chemicals into the soil that dissolve hard-to-capture nutrients such as phosphorus, iron, and other "tightly bound" soil nutrients. This extraction process is particularly important in plant nutrition, and explains why non-mycorrhizal plants require high levels of fertility to maintain their health.

See also: [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-uptake]], [[fungal-role-in-phosphorus-cycling]], [[phosphorus-availability]]

### Nitrogen Uptake

AMF absorb and transfer all 15 major macro- and micronutrients necessary for plant growth, including nitrogen. The interaction between rhizobia and AMF has received considerable attention because of the relatively high phosphorus demand of nitrogen fixation. The two symbioses typically act synergistically, resulting in greater nitrogen and phosphorus content in combination than when each is inoculated onto the legume alone.

Biological nitrogen fixation by Rhizobium in legume hosts can be enhanced through co-infection with AMF. Legumes are typically coarse-rooted and therefore inefficient in extracting phosphorus from the soil. The AM fungi associated with legumes are an essential link for adequate phosphorus nutrition, leading to enhanced nitrogenase activity that in turn promotes root and mycorrhizal growth.

See also: [[rhizobium-legume-symbiosis]], [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]], [[mycorrhizal-nitrogen-transfer-between-plants]]

### Micronutrient Acquisition

AMF enhance uptake of micronutrients including zinc, copper, and iron through their extensive hyphal networks that access micronutrient pools beyond the root depletion zone. This is particularly important in alkaline soils where micronutrient availability is limited.

## Water Relations and Drought Tolerance

AMF improve plant water relations through several mechanisms:
- Extensive hyphal networks access water from soil micropores beyond root reach
- Enhanced root hydraulic conductivity through fungal aquaporins
- Improved osmotic adjustment in host plants
- Altered root architecture (increased branching) improving soil exploration

Mycorrhizae are considered to play a vital role in overcoming drought stress. Under conditions of P-limitation, AMF influence plant community development, nutrient uptake, water relations, and above-ground productivity.

See also: [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]], [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-uptake]]

## Disease Resistance and Biocontrol

AMF contribute to plant protection against root pathogens through multiple mechanisms:

1. **Mechanical barrier**: Especially the mantle of ectomycorrhizae, but AMF also create a physical interface between root and soil
2. **Antibiotic production**: Some mycorrhizal fungi secrete antibiotics that suppress pathogenic fungi and bacteria
3. **Nutrient competition**: AMF compete with pathogens for root carbohydrates and space
4. **Siderophore production**: Iron-chelating siderophores sequester iron from pathogens
5. **Induced systemic resistance**: AMF colonization primes plant defense responses, making plants more resistant to subsequent pathogen attack

In natural ecosystems where phosphorus uptake is low, a major role of mycorrhizal fungi may be protection of the root system from endemic pathogens such as Fusarium spp.

See also: [[mycorrhizal-biocontrol]], [[mycorrhizal-pathogen-resistance]], [[mycorrhizal-plant-defense]]

## Soil Structure Improvement

AMF contribute to soil physical fertility by enhancing soil aggregation. Mycorrhizal hyphae physically bind soil particles together, and glomalin (a glycoprotein produced by AMF) acts as a biological glue that stabilizes aggregates. Improved soil structure enhances water infiltration, erosion resistance, aeration, and root penetration.

## AMF Diversity in Agricultural Systems

### Low Diversity Problem

The diversity of AM fungi is generally low in agriculture compared with indigenous plant communities. This reduced diversity may limit the range of ecosystem services provided. Crop and rotation history also influence the abundance and diversity of AM fungi in agricultural soil. Therefore, the capacity of AM fungi to form mycorrhizas that contribute substantially to plant nutrition, plant health, or soil fertility depends on soil management.

See also: [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]], [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]]

### Mycorrhizal Dependency

Mycorrhizal dependency varies among plant species. Some crops are highly mycorrhizal-dependent and show large growth responses to colonization (e.g., many legumes, alliums, citrus), while others show little or no response (e.g., Brassica species, which do not form AM associations). Understanding mycorrhizal dependency of specific crops is essential for predicting the benefits of AMF management.

See also: [[non-mycorrhizal-plants]], [[mycorrhizal-specificity-and-host-range]]

### Management Impacts on AMF

The soil environment, particularly factors that control soil fertility, strongly influences mycorrhizal function. Key management factors:

- **Tillage**: Soil disturbance with farming practices can reduce the infectivity of AM fungi. The extent of change may depend on the abundance of propagules present.
- **Fertilizer**: High phosphorus fertilizer rates can suppress mycorrhizal colonization as plants reduce their dependency.
- **Crop rotation**: Rotation with non-host crops (Brassicas) can reduce AMF propagule density.
- **Pesticides**: Some fungicides are directly toxic to AMF.
- **Cover crops**: Maintain living roots that support AMF between main crops.

## The Mycorrhizosphere Concept

Since plant roots in most ecosystems are mycorrhizal, the rhizosphere concept has been widened to include fungal structures around plant roots, resulting in the concept of the "mycorrhizosphere." The zone surrounding individual mycorrhizal hyphae is called the "hyphosphere."

The mycorrhizosphere supports distinct microbial communities that differ from the bulk soil and non-mycorrhizal rhizosphere. AMF influence these communities through:
- Alteration of root exudate composition
- Provision of hyphal surfaces for bacterial colonization
- Carbon translocation to soil through the mycelium
- Modification of soil chemistry around hyphae

See also: [[fungal-bacterial-partnerships-mycorrhizosphere]], [[mycorrhizal-helper-bacteria]], [[rhizosphere-fungal-community-analysis-rrna-rdna]]

## The Carbon Cost of Mycorrhizae

Between 10 and 20% of net photosynthates are required for formation, maintenance, and function of mycorrhizal structures. This carbon cost must be weighed against the benefits (improved nutrient uptake, disease resistance, drought tolerance) when evaluating the net effect of mycorrhizal colonization on crop yield.

In nutrient-rich soils where phosphorus is readily available, the carbon cost may exceed the benefit, resulting in a net drain on the plant (parasitism end of the mutualism-parasitism continuum). In nutrient-poor soils, the benefit typically far exceeds the cost.

See also: [[mycorrhizal-carbon-allocation]], [[mycorrhizal-carbon-nitrogen-economics]]

## AMF in Sustainable Agriculture

AMF are central to sustainable agricultural systems because they:
- Reduce dependency on phosphorus fertilizers
- Improve water-use efficiency
- Suppress soil-borne diseases
- Improve soil structure and reduce erosion
- Enhance nutrient cycling efficiency
- Support plant diversity in cropping systems

For biological inoculants to be commercially attractive, they should offer at least a 4-fold return on the inoculum cost. AMF inoculants are increasingly available for agricultural, horticultural, and restoration applications.
