# LSD (Lysergic Acid Diethylamide)

## Overview

LSD (D-lysergic acid diethylamide, LSD-25) is a semi-synthetic ergoline alkaloid and one of the most potent psychoactive substances known. It is classified as a classical psychedelic and acts primarily as a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. Discovered by albert hofmann at LSD Compound Profile Pharmaceuticals in 1938, LSD is active at microgram doses -- making it thousands of times more potent by weight than [[psilocybin-compound-profile]] or [[mescaline-compound-profile]].

## History of Discovery

Albert Hofmann first synthesized LSD-25 on November 16, 1938, as the 25th compound in a systematic series of lysergic acid amide derivatives. The synthesis was part of a program to develop circulatory and respiratory stimulants from ergot alkaloids. Initial pharmacological testing showed no remarkable activity, and the compound was shelved.

On April 16, 1943, Hofmann inadvertently absorbed a trace amount through his fingertips and experienced the first deliberate LSD "trip" -- an event commemorated as Bicycle Day (April 19, 1943), when he rode home from the lab under its influence. He described it as an uninterrupted stream of fantastic images, extraordinary shapes with intense, kaleidoscopic play of colors.

## Chemistry and Synthesis

- **Chemical class:** Ergoline (lysergamide)
- **Molecular formula:** C20H25N3O
- **Full name:** D-lysergic acid diethylamide (from German: Lyserg-saure-diathylamid)
- **Precursor:** Lysergic acid, obtained by chemical cleavage of ergotamine (from the ergot fungus, LSD Compound Profile / Claviceps purpurea)
- **Synthesis route:** Hofmann developed a coupling procedure to combine lysergic acid with diethylamine, producing LSD along with the natural ergot alkaloid ergobasine
- LSD is a semi-synthetic compound: the lysergic acid core is derived from natural ergot, but the diethylamide substitution is synthetic

## Pharmacology

- **Primary mechanism:** Partial agonist at 5-HT2A serotonin receptors (the same receptor family through which [[psilocybin-compound-profile]] and [[5-meo-dmt-compound-profile]] exert their effects)
- **Additional receptor activity:** Binds to a wide range of serotonin receptor subtypes, dopamine receptors, and adrenergic receptors
- **Potency:** Active at 20-100 micrograms -- one of the most potent psychoactive substances known by weight
- The subjective effects of LSD are phenomenologically distinct from [[tryptamine-family-overview]] psychedelics; each compound produces a "phenomenologically defined universe unto itself" (McKenna)

## Duration and Timing

- **Onset:** 30-90 minutes after ingestion
- **Come-up:** 1-2 hours
- **Peak:** 3-5 hours after ingestion
- **Plateau/offset:** 5-8 hours
- **Total duration:** 8-12 hours
- **After-effects/residual:** Up to 24 hours

## Key Effects

- Visual hallucinations with intense color and geometric patterns
- Synesthesia (cross-sensory experience)
- Altered sense of time (time dilation)
- Ego dissolution at higher doses
- Enhanced emotional sensitivity and introspection
- Mystical or religious experiences
- Enhanced connectivity of brain networks (modern fMRI findings)

## Pharmacokinetics

### Absorption
LSD is absorbed rapidly after oral ingestion, with detectable plasma levels appearing within 10-30 minutes. Bioavailability is approximately 70-80% when taken orally. Sublingual and buccal absorption are also effective. The compound is highly lipophilic, facilitating rapid crossing of the blood-brain barrier. Peak plasma concentrations are typically reached 1.5-3 hours post-ingestion, correlating with the onset of peak subjective effects.

### Distribution
LSD distributes widely throughout the body, with the highest concentrations found in the liver, kidneys, lungs, and brain. It crosses the blood-brain barrier efficiently due to its lipophilicity. Plasma protein binding is approximately 80-85%. The volume of distribution is large (estimated at 0.3-0.4 L/kg), indicating extensive tissue distribution. Brain imaging studies using fMRI have shown that LSD increases global brain connectivity and disrupts the default mode network (DMN), correlating with the subjective experience of ego dissolution.

### Metabolism
LSD is primarily metabolised in the liver through oxidative pathways. The major metabolic pathways include N-deethylation (forming nor-LSD, which is inactive) and hydroxylation at various positions on the lysergic ring system. The primary metabolite 2-oxo-3-hydroxy-LSD (O-H-LSD) is pharmacologically inactive but is detectable in urine for longer periods than the parent compound, making it a useful target for forensic drug testing. Other minor metabolites include trioxylated and glucuronidated conjugates.

### Elimination
The elimination half-life of LSD is approximately 2.5-4 hours, though subjective effects persist well beyond this due to pharmacodynamic factors rather than the presence of active drug. The primary route of elimination is renal (excretion in urine), with only small amounts excreted in faeces. Less than 1% of an ingested dose is excreted unchanged in urine. Complete elimination occurs within 24-48 hours.

## Receptor Binding Profile

LSD's pharmacological complexity arises from its affinity for multiple receptor systems:
- **5-HT2A**: Primary receptor mediating psychedelic effects (Ki approximately 2.5-5 nM). Activation of this receptor on cortical pyramidal neurons increases cortical excitability and disrupts normal thalamocortical signalling. This is the same receptor through which [[psilocybin-compound-profile]] exerts its effects, though LSD shows higher intrinsic efficacy and longer receptor residence time.
- **5-HT1A**: LSD acts as a potent agonist at 5-HT1A receptors (Ki approximately 1-2 nM), which may contribute to its anxiolytic and prosocial effects at moderate doses. Activation of 5-HT1A autoreceptors in the raphe nuclei modulates serotonin release.
- **5-HT2C**: Agonist activity may contribute to appetite suppression and mild stimulant effects sometimes reported.
- **5-HT5A, 5-HT6, 5-HT7**: LSD shows significant affinity for these receptor subtypes, though their contribution to the psychedelic experience remains under investigation.
- **Dopamine receptors**: LSD is a partial agonist at D2-like dopamine receptors and an agonist at D1-like receptors, which may contribute to its mild stimulant properties and mood-elevating effects.
- **Adrenergic receptors**: Alpha-adrenergic agonist activity contributes to the sympathetic nervous system activation (pupil dilation, increased heart rate, mild elevation in blood pressure) commonly observed during LSD experiences.
- **TAAR1**: LSD is an agonist at trace amine-associated receptor 1, which may modulate dopaminergic and serotonergic neurotransmission.

## Dose-Response Characteristics

LSD exhibits an unusually steep dose-response curve. Increasing the dose by as little as 50% can dramatically alter the subjective experience. The difference between 75 ug and 150 ug is not merely one of degree but of kind -- qualitative shifts in consciousness occur across relatively narrow dose ranges. This steep curve, combined with LSD's extreme potency (active at 20-100 ug), makes accurate dosing critical. The concept of "set and setting" ([[lsd-set-and-setting-framework]]) becomes increasingly important at higher doses, where the experience is more sensitive to psychological and environmental factors.

## Tolerance and Cross-Tolerance

Tolerance to LSD develops rapidly and is nearly complete within 2-3 days of consecutive daily dosing. A dose that produces full psychedelic effects on day one may produce minimal effects on day three. This tolerance is mediated by downregulation and desensitisation of 5-HT2A receptors. Tolerance dissipates within 3-7 days of abstinence, with full sensitivity returning within one to two weeks. LSD exhibits significant cross-tolerance with other serotonergic psychedelics including [[psilocybin-compound-profile]], [[mescaline-compound-profile]], and [[dmt-dimethyltryptamine-experience-guide]], but not with non-serotonergic substances such as [[mdma-compound-profile]] or ketamine. This cross-tolerance pattern is a defining characteristic of the classical psychedelic class.

## Clinical Research History

The modern clinical renaissance of LSD research began with the founding of the Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies (MAPS) and similar organisations. Key recent studies include:
- **LSD-assisted psychotherapy for anxiety associated with life-threatening illness**: A Swiss study by Gasser et al. (2014) demonstrated significant and sustained reductions in anxiety in patients with terminal illness, with effects persisting at 12-month follow-up.
- **Microdosing research**: While anecdotal reports of LSD microdosing (5-20 ug) are widespread, controlled clinical trials have produced mixed results. Some studies show modest improvements in mood and cognition, while others find no significant difference from placebo, suggesting that expectancy effects may account for many reported benefits.
- **Neuroimaging studies**: Research using fMRI and MEG at institutions including Imperial College London has mapped LSD's effects on brain connectivity, revealing increased global integration and decreased modularity of brain networks, providing a neural correlate for the experience of ego dissolution and synesthesia.

## Legal Status by Country

LSD is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971), making it illegal in most countries worldwide. Specific national schedules include:
- **United States**: Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act (since 1968). No accepted medical use; manufacturing, possession, and distribution carry severe criminal penalties.
- **United Kingdom**: Class A under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971. Possession carries up to 7 years imprisonment; supply carries a maximum of life imprisonment.
- **Germany**: Schedule I (Anlage I) under the BtMG. No medical use permitted.
- **Switzerland**: Exceptionally, Switzerland permits limited medical research with LSD. The study by Peter Gasser was conducted under special licence.
- **Netherlands**: Illegal but decriminalised for personal possession in small quantities.
- **Brazil**: Personal possession and religious use are not criminalised. The use of ayahuasca in religious ceremonies is protected, though LSD itself remains a controlled substance.
- **Canada**: Schedule III under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act. In January 2022, Canada granted an exception allowing palliative care patients to use psilocybin, though LSD was not included in this exemption.
- **Australia**: Schedule 9 (Prohibited Substance). In 2023, Australia rescheduled psilocybin and MDMA for therapeutic use, but LSD was not included in this rescheduling.

## Merged: LSD Chemical Modifications and Derivatives
The chemical modification of LSD (D-lysergic acid diethylamide) was one of the most extensive medicinal chemistry programs undertaken at Sandoz in the mid-20th century. Beginning soon after the discovery of LSD's psychic effects in 1943, Albert Hofmann and his colleague Dr. Franz Troxler produced a large number of LSD derivatives in an effort to understand [[ergot-and-lysergic-acid-history]] within the lysergamide class and to develop compounds with improved or more specific pharmacological properties. Hofmann personally tested all four configurations in self-experiments: - **D-LSD** (D-lysergic acid diethylamide) — the active form, effective at doses as low as 25 micrograms, producing profound alterations of consciousness - **D-iso-LSD** (D-isolysergic acid diethylamide) — inactive at 0.5 mg, which corresponds to 20 times a still distinctly active dose of D-LSD - **L-LSD** (L-lysergic acid diethylamide) — inactive at 0.5 mg, showing no psychic effects whatsoever - **L-iso-LSD** (L-isolysergic acid diethylamide) — inactive at 0.5 mg, the fourth and final stereoisomer This remarkable finding demonstrated the extraordinary stereospecificity of lsd-pharmacology|LSD's pharmacological activity. ## Merged: Tryptamine Hallucinogens And Consciousness
Terence McKenna's 1983 lecture "Tryptamine Hallucinogens and Consciousness," delivered at the Lilly/Goswami Conference on Consciousness and Quantum Physics at Esalen Institute, presents a phenomenological exploration of [[dmt-dimethyltryptamine-experience-guide]], psilocybin, and ayahuasca as tools for probing the nature of consciousness, language, and human evolution. Published in 1992 in *The Archaic Revival*, it remains one of McKenna's most detailed accounts of tryptamine phenomenology. McKenna focuses on three tryptophan-derived hallucinogens: [[5-meo-dmt-compound-profile]], 5-methoxy-compounds|psilocybin, and ayahuasca (a combination of DMT and a monoamine oxidase inhibitor). Each is described as a "phenomenologically defined universe unto itself" — not interchangeable with LSD or mescaline despite superficial classification similarities. These states occur when sensory radiation into the body is restricted, revealing "interior images and interior processes that are psycho-physical." McKenna provides a detailed first-person phenomenology of the [[5-meo-dmt-compound-profile]] based on his experiments beginning in 1965.
