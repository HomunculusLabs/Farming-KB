# Sprinkler Irrigation Methods

For gardens that depend on regular irrigation — whether intensively planted beds or emergency watering during dry spells — a systematic approach to sprinkler irrigation prevents both overwatering and underwatering. Both extremes cause serious reductions in vegetable quality and yield, often without obvious signs until it is too late.

## The Problem with Daily Light Watering

Many gardeners wet down their gardens almost daily with a hose nozzle because they want to make sure the beds seem moist. What actually happens is that the surface inches stay damp but the gardener never discovers that, deeper down, the soil has become increasingly dry. Under these conditions, vegetables will not wilt because they are finding water in the surface layer, but they will become severely stunted due to lack of root development.

## John Jeavons' "Sparkle Test"

John Jeavons, the popularizer of intensive gardening in North America, recommends using a fan nozzle daily, continuing on each bed until the entire surface "sparkles" (becomes shiny wet).

### How It Works

The sparkling results from water that has not yet flowed into the bed beading up on the surface. The shine disappears in a second or so initially, but as the deeper soil becomes saturated, the shine lasts longer and longer.

### The Rule

Jeavons says that when the shine lasts long enough (one to ten seconds), the bed has been given enough water.

### Problems with the Sparkle Test

**One to ten seconds is quite a range!** Jeavons suggests trying different shine times combined with digging test holes to see how deeply the soil is saturated. Without this check, gross overwatering or underwatering is likely.

**Soil-specific issues**:
- Many clayey soils could accept a lot of water before they would show a sparkly surface for several seconds, yet in a few hours be only wet a few inches down
- Sandy soils usually take in water rapidly, making it difficult to get the shine to last more than a second or two, so the gardener overwaters, resulting in leaching or runoff
- Some sands become coated with products of decomposition and, once dry, become water-repellent (hydrophobic) — no matter how much water is applied with a hand nozzle, it rolls off without penetrating

## Sprinkler Types

### Impact Sprinklers (Stop-Sprinklers)

The traditional oscillating or impact sprinkler that throws water in a pattern and can be adjusted for coverage area. Effective for larger gardens.

### Drip Irrigation

Not covered extensively in this source, but worth mentioning as an alternative that delivers water directly to the root zone with minimal evaporation. Excellent for water-limited situations.

### Overhead Sprinklers

Simple oscillating sprinklers. Work reasonably well but lose water to evaporation, especially in hot, dry, or windy conditions.

## How Much to Water

### The Science

Both watering too much and watering too little hugely reduce the garden's performance. Moisture stress does not have to reach the point of wilting to cause a lot of damage. Underwatering may be so subtle that no temporary wilting occurs, yet it still produces serious consequences.

### Calculating Water Needs

1. **Know your soil's water-holding capacity** (see [[soil-moisture-and-water-holding-capacity]])
2. **Know your daily water loss rate** (depends on temperature, humidity, wind, and canopy density)
3. **Decide when to water**: Allow the soil to lose roughly half its available moisture before re-watering
4. **Apply the deficit plus a small surplus**: If 0.75 inches has been lost, apply about 1 inch to recharge the soil

### Example

If your soil can give up about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of water before crops experience moisture stress, and the crops are extracting about 0.07 inches (7 mm) per day during midsummer, you must add about an inch of water every three or four days.

### Accounting for Rainfall

- If it drizzles insignificantly, moisture will be lost from the ground surface, and during the growing season at least during one ground, moisture will be lost from the one ground
- If it rains properly, the garden may gain moisture
- If it rains heavily, any extra moisture will drain away after rising slowly by capillarity
- There is a limit to how much water the soil can hold — any extra simply drains away

## Soil-Specific Irrigation Tips

### Clay Soils

- Accept water slowly
- Water tends to spread broadly at the surface before penetrating deeply
- Easy to overwater (surface looks wet but water hasn't penetrated)
- Allow longer intervals between waterings
- Test by digging to see how deep the moisture has reached

### Sandy Soils

- Accept water rapidly
- Water goes deep and not wide
- Need more frequent, smaller applications
- Susceptible to leaching if overwatered (nutrients wash below root zone)
- Hydrophobic sands need special treatment (wetting agents or very slow application)

### Loam Soils

- The most forgiving
- Good balance of intake rate and water-holding capacity
- Still benefit from systematic approach rather than guesswork

## Timing of Irrigation

### Best Time

Late morning, when the sun is getting strong and can reheat the soil as fast as possible after the cooling effect of watering. The next best time is mid-afternoon, when there is still enough time for the soil to warm before the sun starts setting.

### Worst Time

Evening watering leaves the soil cold all night, and it will not warm up again until well into the next day.

### Why Timing Matters

Watering lowers the soil temperature — the water is almost always colder than the soil, and evaporation from the surface also lowers temperature. Cold soil slows plant growth and promotes fungal diseases.

## The Dust Mulch

An inch or so of loose, dry soil at the surface acts as a mulch, preventing evaporation from the moist soil below. This is critical for non-irrigated gardens:

- After rain, go out with a hoe and loosen the surface of every accessible part of the garden
- This allows the soil to fully accept the next chance shower
- It also helps keep the moisture now in the earth from evaporating
- The surface inch of soil should contain a fair amount of rotted manure to prevent crust formation and help form large stable crumbs that wind won't blow away

## Systematic Irrigation Management

For a garden that depends on regular irrigation:

1. Know your soil type and water-holding capacity
2. Track daily water loss (temperature + humidity + wind)
3. Water when approximately half the available moisture has been used
4. Apply enough to recharge the soil profile
5. Check with a soil probe or dug hole to verify depth of penetration
6. Adjust frequency based on weather, not calendar
