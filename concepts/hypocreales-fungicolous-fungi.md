# Hypocreales: Fungicolous Diversity and Mycoparasitism

The order Hypocreales (Ascomycota) represents one of the most important lineages of fungicolous and mycoparasitic fungi. Comprising the families Hypocreaceae, Nectriaceae, Bionectriaceae, Clavicipitaceae, and Niessliaceae, this order contains an extraordinarily high proportion of species that parasitize, colonize, or exploit other fungi as substrata. Many hypocrealean fungi are mycoparasitic or mycosaprotrophic — a fact that is often unrecognized even by specialists (Rossman et al. 1999).

## Overview of Mycoparasitic Mechanisms

Hypocrealean mycoparasites employ diverse strategies to attack and exploit fungal hosts:

- **Biotrophic contact mycoparasitism**: The parasite contacts the host via specialized structures (haustoria, appressoria) without immediately killing it, withdrawing nutrients from living host tissue. Examples include [[gonatobotrys-simplex]], which grows on Alternaria and Cladosporium species.
- **Necrotrophic mycoparasitism**: The parasite actively kills host tissue through enzymatic degradation and toxic metabolites before colonizing it. [[trichoderma-harzianum]] uses this strategy.
- **Hyphal interference**: Non-contact antagonism through production of diffusible toxic volatile compounds that cause cell vacuolization, collapse, and disintegration of host cytoplasm at a distance.
- **Direct penetration**: Physical breaching of host cell walls, as seen in Trichoderma interactions with Lentinula edodes.

## Key Families and Genera

### Hypocreaceae

**Hypocrea and Trichoderma**: While Hypocrea is reported less frequently as a mycoparasite than its anamorphs in Trichoderma, the genus includes both host-specific and polyphagous fungicolous species found on aphyllophorales and woody substrata. Trichoderma species are among the most polyphagous mycoparasites known, though they are notoriously difficult to distinguish morphologically (Bissett 1991b; Gams and Bissett 1999).

Trichoderma species produce a remarkable diversity of secondary metabolites (Ghisalberti and Sivisithamparam 1991). Beyond enzymatic actions, these metabolites antagonize host fungi over distance. Key compounds include:

- **Volatile alkyl pyrone** from T. harzianum (Claydon et al. 1987)
- **Peptaibols** — potent polypeptide antibiotics found in Trichoderma, Gliocladium, and Clonostachys species, responsible for the often-observed bursting of hyphal tips (Brückner and Przybylski 1984)
- **Gliotoxin** from T. virens (Weindling 1941), with highly specific fungitoxic effects

The interaction between T. harzianum and Lentinula edodes involves three distinct phases: (1) parasitic contact, (2) direct penetration of host cells, and (3) production of diffusible toxic agents prior to physical contact (Hashioka et al. 1961; Hashioka and Komatsu 1964; Komatsu and Hashioka 1964).

A critical practical concern is that strains of T. harzianum used as beneficial biocontrol agents can barely be distinguished microscopically from genetically distinct strains that are aggressive competitors of cultivated mushrooms (Muthumeenakshi et al. 1994). T. virens was distinguished from other Trichoderma species by Webster and Lomas (1964) and later confirmed as T. aggressivum (Rehner and Samuels 1994). Accurate reference to the strain used in experiments is therefore crucial.

**Hypomyces**: This genus comprises the most characteristic mycoparasites on macromycetes (agarics, boletes, and aphyllophorales). The genus is heterogeneous (Rogerson and Samuels 1994; Põldmaa 2000) and can be grouped by host type:

- Species colonizing host sporocarps systemically, lacking known anamorphs, and producing ascospores that do not germinate in vitro
- Species causing decay of sporocarps at various developmental stages, forming copious anamorphs, and growing easily in culture

The largest number of Hypomyces species (23) parasitize aphyllophorales and are usually not host-specific. Anamorphs were originally distributed among 12 genera; most are now combined in the expanded genus Cladobotryum (Rogerson and Samuels 1993). Species that parasitize boletes or agarics tend to be specific to a host family or genus.

**Other Hypocreaceae genera**: Hypocreopsis species (e.g., H. lichenoides on Hymenochaete, H. xylariicola on Xylaria) cannot be cultured and their physiology remains unknown. Sphaerostilbella species grow mainly on Stereum species. Sporophagomyces is characterized by a subiculum that traps host basidiospores from the Ganodermataceae.

### Nectriaceae

Nectria was recently subdivided into several distinct genera (Rossman et al. 1999). Cosmospora (formerly Nectria subgenus Dialonectria) comprises approximately 60 species, most of which are fungicolous. The most conspicuous species occur on carbonized perithecia of pyrenomycetes, particularly members of the Xylariales and Diatrypales. Some species are restricted to specific hosts — for example, Cosmospora leptosphaeriae occurs mainly on Leptosphaeria, and C. coccinea on polypores (Samuels et al. 1991).

### Bionectriaceae

Centered around Bionectria (the former Nectria ochroleuca group), this family includes destructive fungicolous species with anamorphs in Clonostachys (Gliocladium sensu lato), particularly C. rosea and related species. Some Nectria species with Sesquicillium anamorphs are also classified here (Schroers 2001).

### Clavicipitaceae

While numerous Cordyceps species are entomogenous, three species commonly grow on Elaphomyces (truffle) species. Among anamorph genera associated with Cordyceps, Lecanicillium (formerly Verticillium sect. Prostrata) contains species with high chitinase activity and great potential as entomogenous, nematophagous, and fungicolous biocontrol agents (Gams 1971; Zare et al. 2000).

### Niessliaceae

Mostly saprotrophic, but some species are fungicolous, particularly the Monocillium anamorphs of Niesslia. Circinoniesslia and Valetoniella species have been found only on perithecia of Nectria species. Trichosphaeriella decipiens is mostly reported on sporocarps of aphyllophorales (Samuels and Barr 1997). Three Niesslia species are lichenicolous.

## Mycotrophein and Nutritional Requirements

Many ecologically obligate mycoparasites require specific growth factors for axenic cultivation. Mycotrophein — a mixture of tetraethyleneglycol and pentaethyleneglycol mono(nonylphenyl) ethers found in extracts of various fungal cultures — acts as a carrier of biologically active factors required by biotrophic contact mycoparasites (Gain and Barnett 1970; Calderone and Barnett 1972; Hwang et al. 1985).

Species such as Gonatobotrys simplex, [[hansfordia-parasitica]], and Stephanoma phaeosporum can be grown axenically on media supplemented with thiamine, biotin, and fungal extracts containing mycotrophein. However, some parasites like Melanospora zamiae can withdraw all required nutrients from washed host mycelium without mycotrophein (Jordan and Barnett 1978).

The mutualistic relationship between Hansfordia parasitica and Graphium fuscum is particularly noteworthy: each partner produces a vitamin (pyridoxine and biotin, respectively) required by the other, and the parasitic relationship can be reversed when the host overgrows old aerial mycelium of the parasite (Shigo 1960a; Barnett 1968).

## Biocontrol Significance

Hypocrealean fungi, especially Trichoderma species, have enormous significance as biocontrol agents of plant-pathogenic fungi. Their ability to parasitize, antagonize, and outcompete pathogenic species through multiple mechanisms (enzymatic degradation, antibiotic production, mycoparasitism, competition for nutrients and space) makes them among the most commercially important biological control organisms in agriculture.

However, the extreme difficulty of distinguishing beneficial from harmful Trichoderma strains morphologically poses significant challenges for both research and commercial application. Molecular identification methods are essential for accurate strain characterization.
