# Solomon on Permanent Mulching — Why It Fails in Mild Climates

**Source:** Gardening When It Counts (Solomon, 2005)

## Overview

Steve Solomon, who popularized [[geoff-hamilton-the-organic-gardening-companion]] in the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]],
explicitly critiques the permanent mulching movement. While mulching has
enthusiastic proponents (notably Ruth Stout), Solomon's direct experience in
two mild climates convinced him that permanent mulching creates more problems
than it solves for most serious food gardeners. This concept explains his
reasoned critique and specifies when mulching does and does not work.

## When Mulching Works

Mulching can succeed under specific conditions:

1. **Cold-winter climates** where the soil freezes solid, killing pest
   populations that would otherwise build up under the mulch layer
2. **Hot summers** that decompose the mulch rapidly, preventing excessive
   residue buildup
3. **Abundant [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]]** — ideally alfalfa (lucerne) or other
   nutrient-rich material, or spoiled hay/autumn leaves fortified with COF
   seedmeals
4. **Small gardens** where the labor of hauling bulk material is manageable
5. **Physically restricted gardeners** who cannot wield a shovel or hoe

## Solomon's Five Problems with Permanent Mulching

### 1. It Does Not Significantly Reduce Moisture Loss
Mulching enthusiasts claim mulch dramatically reduces watering needs. Solomon
disagrees: "Mulching keeps the surface moist but does not, as enthusiasts
claim, significantly reduce moisture loss." Plants transpire water through
their leaves regardless of soil surface conditions. Most moisture loss occurs
through the plant itself, not through evaporation from bare earth.

### 2. Slow Spring Warmup
A thick mulch layer insulates the soil, keeping it cool. In spring, when soil
temperature determines germination speed and plant growth rate, a mulched bed
warms significantly more slowly than a bare, tilled bed. For gardeners trying
to maximize their [[extending-growing-season]], this delay matters.

### 3. Nutrient Ratio Imbalance
With permanent mulching, large quantities of leaf and straw residue decompose
into the garden. Over time, the soil's nutrient ratios become imbalanced. The
mulch material itself is not a balanced fertilizer — it tends to be high in
carbon and low in the specific mineral nutrients that vegetables need.
Consequently, the nutritional content of the vegetables declines.

### 4. Enormous Bulk Requirements
If your garden is larger than a small plot, maintaining permanent mulch
requires hauling enormous quantities of organic material. The labor of
hauling, stacking, and spreading can become so demanding that owning a pickup
truck becomes practically necessary — adding fuel, licensing, maintenance,
and insurance costs.

### 5. Pest Plagues in Mild Climates
In mild-winter climates where the soil does not freeze solid, permanent mulch
creates an ideal habitat for small animals (slugs, sowbugs, rodents). After
a year under permanent mulch, the garden becomes home to plague levels of
pests that proceed to eat most kinds of vegetables, especially seedlings.
Solomon tried mulching in two different mild climates and explicitly does not
recommend this method where the soil doesn't freeze in winter.

## Solomon's Recommended Alternative

"It works out to be less effort and far more effective to heap-compost enough
organic material to maintain soil humus at healthy levels and then use something
like COF or poultry manure compost as fertilizer, when needed, on medium- and
high-demand vegetables."

This approach:
- Produces higher-quality compost in [[psilocybe-cubensis-strain-potency-variability-controlled-conditions-bigwood-beug]]
- Allows precise [[cannabis-nutrient-management]] through COF supplementation
- Avoids the pest buildup associated with permanent surface residue
- Requires less total bulk material hauling
- Keeps soil warm in spring for faster growth

## The Ruth Stout Context

Solomon acknowledges that permanent mulching has a place. Ruth Stout, author
of *Gardening Without Work*, started the mulching movement. Stout was in her
70s, too frail to wield a shovel, but could manage to carry a flake of hay.
She also had someone willing to haul and stack multiple spoiled hay bales for
her. The method was appropriate for her physical circumstances — but that does
not make it optimal for younger, fitter gardeners serious about food production.
