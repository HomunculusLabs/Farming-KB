# Fungal Woronin Bodies and Septal Pore Organization

Woronin bodies are dense, membrane-bound organelles unique to the Euascomycetes (Pezizomycotina) that function as emergency plugs for septal pores in filamentous fungal hyphae. These remarkable structures were first described by Woronin in 1886 in the cells of Ascobolus pulcherrimus and were named in his honor by Buller in 1933. Woronin bodies represent a key innovation supporting the multicellular lifestyle of filamentous ascomycete fungi.

## Structure and Biogenesis

The Woronin body is generally oval in shape and consists of an electron-dense matrix circumscribed by a single unit membrane. The core is composed primarily of the HEX-1 protein, which self-assembles into a solid crystalline matrix. HEX-1 is specific to the Euascomycetes and the hex-1 gene is not found in basidiomycetes or zygomycetes.

Woronin bodies are peroxisome-derived organelles, as confirmed by their association with microbodies (peroxisomes) in electron microscopy studies. During biogenesis, Woronin bodies form in apical cells of hyphae where they appear irregularly dispersed in the protoplasm, moving periodically before settling against a longitudinal wall or septum.

The diameter of Woronin bodies varies among species. In Neurospora crassa, they are unusually large and hexagonal in cross-section, whereas in most other species they are roughly oval. Their size is closely matched to the diameter of the septal pore they are designed to plug.

## Septal Pore Architecture

Fungal hyphae are divided into cellular compartments by cross-walls called septa. Each septum contains a central pore that allows cytoplasmic streaming and the movement of organelles between adjacent cells, maintaining the coenocytic organization essential for filamentous growth.

Different fungal groups possess distinct septal pore-associated organelles. Hymenomycetes (Basidiomycota) produce a septal pore cap (parenthosome), a barrel-shaped structure associated with the nuclear envelope, sitting over the pore apparatus. Urediniomycetes elaborate perforate septa with associated peroxisome-like organelles that may contain electron-dense polyhedral structures superficially resembling Woronin bodies, but with distinctly striated cores suggesting different composition.

The Woronin body of Euascomycetes is tethered to the septal pore by filamentous structures, allowing it to remain in close proximity and respond rapidly to damage signals.

## Damage Response and Septal Pore Plugging

The primary function of Woronin bodies is to seal septal pores rapidly when adjacent cells are damaged, preventing catastrophic loss of cytoplasm. This was first experimentally demonstrated by Collinge and Markham in 1985 using Penicillium chrysogenum. When hyphae were severed with a razor blade, Woronin bodies rapidly plugged 90 percent of septal pores within the vicinity of induced damage, compared with only 5 percent in undamaged hyphae.

Following pore occlusion, material resembling septal wall is deposited over the Woronin body-septal pore complex. This suggests that membrane re-sealing and local deposition of new cell wall follow septal pore occlusion, providing a complete repair mechanism.

This damage-containment system is essential for maintaining hyphal integrity in the heterogeneous and often physically challenging environments that fungi inhabit. Without functional Woronin bodies, damage to one hyphal compartment could lead to progressive cytoplasmic loss throughout the entire mycelial network.

## Functional Significance in Multicellularity

The evolution of Woronin bodies is closely associated with the development of complex multicellular organization in filamentous fungi. The Hymenomycetes and Euascomycetes, which produce the largest and most complex multicellular fruiting bodies among fungi, both possess sophisticated septal pore-associated organelles (septal pore caps and Woronin bodies, respectively).

This correlation suggests that the ability to maintain cellular compartmentalization while preserving cytoplasmic connectivity was a key evolutionary innovation enabling the development of large, complex fungal structures. The hex-1 gene has been found in diverse Euascomycete genera including Ascobolus, Neurospora, Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Sclerotinia.

## Research Applications

Woronin bodies have become a model system for studying organelle biogenesis, protein self-assembly, and intracellular trafficking in filamentous fungi. The HEX-1 protein crystallizes in vivo to form the dense core, making it one of the few known examples of biological crystal formation.

Mutational studies of hex-1 have demonstrated that loss of Woronin body function leads to increased susceptibility to hyphal damage and reduced fitness under stress conditions. The study of Woronin body tethering proteins has revealed novel aspects of cytoskeletal organization and membrane trafficking in fungi.

The phylogenetic distribution of Woronin bodies and their associated genes has also provided valuable markers for understanding the evolutionary relationships within the Ascomycota, particularly the diversification of the Pezizomycotina.

Woronin bodies are dense, membrane-bound organelles unique to filamentous ascomycete fungi. They function as emergency plugs that rapidly seal septal pores when hyphal compartments are damaged, preventing catastrophic cytoplasmic bleeding.

### Structure and Composition

Woronin bodies are 0.1–1.0 μm in diameter and composed primarily of the HEX1 protein. They are synthesized at the endoplasmic reticulum and tethered near septal pores by a specialized matrix protein (SEA-1/Spa10). The HEX1 protein self-assembles into a crystalline core that provides structural rigidity for effective pore plugging.

### Septal Pore Organization

Ascomycete septa contain central pores that allow cytoplasmic streaming between hyphal compartments. The septal pore apparatus includes:

- **Septal pore**: Central channel (~50–500 nm diameter) in the cross-wall
- **Woronin body tethering complex**: Anchors Woronin bodies adjacent to the pore
- **Septal pore cap (in some species)**: Additional ER-derived structure over the pore

### Plugging Mechanism

When a hyphal tip is damaged, the rapid pressure differential causes Woronin bodies to be swept into the septal pore within milliseconds. The crystalline HEX1 core expands slightly upon contact with the pore rim, creating a tight seal. This response is significantly faster than the alternative strategy of septal plugging used by basidiomycetes (clamp connections and dolipore septa).

### Functional Significance

Woronin body plugging serves several critical functions:

- **Compartmentalization**: Prevents loss of cellular contents after injury
- **Aging and development**: Seals off senescent compartments during colony maturation
- **Heterokaryon maintenance**: Controls nuclear distribution in multinucleate hyphae
- **Pathogenicity**: Some plant pathogens require Woronin bodies for full virulence
