## Fungal Woronin Bodies and Septal Pore Organization

Woronin bodies are dense, membrane-bound organelles unique to the Euascomycetes
(Pezizomycotina) that function as emergency plugs for septal pores in
filamentous fungal hyphae. First described by Woronin in 1886 in Ascobolus
pulcherrimus and named by Buller in 1933, these structures are essential for
maintaining hyphal integrity in [[neurospora-crassa]] they are unusually large and
hexagonal in cross-section, whereas in most other species they are roughly
oval. Their size is closely matched to the septal pore they are designed to
plug.

## Septal Pore Architecture

Fungal hyphae are divided into cellular compartments by cross-walls called
septa. Each septum contains a central pore that allows cytoplasmic streaming
and organelle movement between adjacent cells, maintaining the coenocytic
organization essential for filamentous growth.

Different fungal groups possess distinct septal pore-associated organelles.
Hymenomycetes (Basidiomycota) produce a septal pore cap (parenthosome), a
barrel-shaped structure associated with the nuclear envelope. Urediniomycetes
elaborate perforate septa with associated peroxisome-like organelles. The
Woronin body of Euascomycetes is tethered to the septal pore by filamentous
structures, allowing it to respond rapidly to damage signals.

## Damage Response and Septal Pore Plugging

The primary function of Woronin bodies is to seal septal pores rapidly when
adjacent cells are damaged, preventing catastrophic loss of cytoplasm.
Collinge and Markham (1985) demonstrated this using [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]], and these self-
assembly properties have attracted interest in biomaterials science.
