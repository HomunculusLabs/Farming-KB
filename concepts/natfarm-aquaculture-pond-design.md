# Aquaculture Pond Design and Management

Aquaculture is a closed-loop system of growing fish in a cycle with plants
and other animals. These range from small backyard ponds to large intensive
aboveground tanks. Water systems have the potential to produce much more
protein per square foot than an equivalent area of land. A successful
aquaculture system is patterned after productive land-water edges such as
swamps and coral reefs. In [[permaculture-design]], aquaculture looks like a
natural pond with fish, curvy edges, and plants and animals thriving

## Pond Design Principles

Water holds and reflects heat. As temperature drops at night, heat radiates
into the air and surrounding gardens. A dam or pond will keep plants and
people warmer in the cold, cool them off in the heat, and serve as a place
to grow water-loving plants and fish. Your pond can grow water chestnuts,
rice, bait fish, brine shrimp, snails, aquarium fish, water lilies, reeds,
mushrooms, crayfish, prawns, mussels, clams, and ducks.

### Rules of Thumb for Designing Ponds

- For fish, several small ponds no more than 4 to 6 feet deep work better,
 while storage ponds can be 10 to 20 feet (3 to 6 meters) deep.
- A large pond for irrigation should not have many fruit or nut trees around
 it, or the pond will become polluted with leaves and dropped fruit.
- Maximize the edge by creating a winding shore rather than a smooth oval or
 circle. This edge effect dramatically increases productivity.
- Large livestock cannot have access to ponds for aquaculture, or they will
 destroy the balance of the pond.
- The pond must be near a water source to be filled initially or topped up
 during a dry season.

### Refuge Areas

The pond should have various refuge areas for each species that lives there.
For ducks, a small island can be formed in the center. A series of shallow
shelves around the edges can serve as a home for forage plants. The pond
also needs deep areas at least 6 to 8 feet deep (2 to 2.5 meters), where
fish can retreat in summer heat. Drop in hollow logs or pieces of pipe for
them to hide in.

## Constructing the Pond

### Step 1: Lay Out the Shape
Lay down a rope as a guide for the shape of your pond while you dig.

### Step 2: Divide Into Thirds
Divide the pond into thirds: one-third for the shallow end, one-third for
mid-range depth, and one-third for the deep end.

### Step 3: Waterproof the Bottom
Line the bottom with pond liner or gley. Pond liner is heavy black plastic,
rubber, or geotextile sheet. You may also want pond underlayment beneath
the liner for protection. Gley is a layer of fermented manure.

### Step 4: Stabilize the Edges
Stabilize edges with rocks of varying sizes, bamboo, grass ledges, or logs,
especially at first before other plants prevent erosion. This also helps
hide the liner for a natural appearance.

### Step 5: Build Habitat
Once the pond begins to fill, lay down a couple of inches of straw and
trample it into the bottom. This provides habitat for water insects.

### Step 6: Introduce Plants
Introduce water plants such as lilies, water chestnuts, and duckweed to
build up the bug population. This process takes at least six months. Start
by leaving plants in plastic pots and submerging them, moving them as
needed before transplanting.

### Step 7: Establish the Food Web
The water should become green. If it does not, add a small amount of manure.
Introduce baby fish in the spring after plants are well established.

## Oxygen Management

To raise fish, the oxygen level of your pond is crucial. Besides keeping
the pond clean and clear of too much animal waste and weeds, aeration can
be essential. Solar pond aerators and fountains are available. Plants
should cover around 60% of the surface. The biological controls of the
pond operate on three levels: plants, insects, and fish, each supporting
the others in a balanced food web.

## Group Pond Systems

Several ponds situated in succession down a stream can segregate fish of
different ages. Several smaller ponds and marshes connected to each pond
supply food to the main pond, all protected from predators. Building ponds
parallel to each other rather than downstream prevents disease from
spreading between ponds.

## Polyculture Stocking

Stocking rates depend on oxygen and food supply. The more you aerate, the
more fish you can grow. The more plankton, vegetation, and minnows, the
more food is available. It is a cycle you must keep in balance. Polyculture
scenarios with multiple species produce more total food than monoculture
of a single species. Tilapia is the most versatile and efficient fish to
grow. Common polyculture species include catfish, bluegill, perch, and
various minnow species that form the food chain base.

## The Chinampa Connection

Historically, the South American chinampa structure of canals stretching
between large planting beds was filled with fish and was one of the earliest
examples of highly productive aquaculture. The chinampa canal system is an
even easier aquaculture method to create, with fry stocked at the source
and released into slow-flowing water. Each island is never more than 30
feet (10 meters) wide, and the canal is 100 to 300 feet long on each side.
The canal mud is dredged every year and used to grow seedlings, which are
then transplanted into the garden bed.

## Integration with Other Systems

Aquaculture ponds connect to swales for water supply and
overflow management. They integrate with [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] tanks for
top-up water. Duckweed and other pond plants can be harvested for
[[vermicomposting]] or as animal feed. Pond mud dredged periodically
provides excellent fertilizer for garden beds. The
