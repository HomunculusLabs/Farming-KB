## Cold Shock Selectivity in Fruiting of Psilocybe Species

## Overview

Cold shocking is a cultivation technique in which fully colonised substrate cultures are placed in a refrigerator (typically 2–4 °C) overnight before being transferred to [[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]].

- **Humidity**: Sustained high relative humidity (90–95 % at the substrate surface) prevents desiccation of emerging primordia and developing pins. Evaporation from the substrate surface also plays a role in initiating pin formation, creating a balance between too much and too little moisture.

When cold shock is applied to a responsive species, the combined effect of temperature drop plus these complementary cues produces robust, synchronised fruiting. For species that did not evolve with this trigger, cold shock may be irrelevant or may introduce physiological stress that delays or reduces yields.

## Species That Benefit from Cold Shock

According to the source paper, certain harder-to-fruit strains respond positively to cold shock treatment. These strains, described generally rather than named individually, are more reluctant to initiate fruiting under standard conditions and may require the additional thermal cue to transition from vegetative to reproductive phase. For these difficult species, the overnight cold treatment can be the difference between a successful flush and a stalled culture.

The paper does not identify these strains by specific epithet, but the category likely includes temperate-adapted species, high-altitude isolates, and strains domesticated for fewer generations. Cultivators working with rare or challenging isolates should consider cold shock as a low-risk intervention when standard fruiting parameters alone fail to produce pins within 7–14 days. The benefit typically manifests as a reduction in time to first pin rather than an increase in total yield — cold shock helps synchronise the fruiting response rather than producing more biomass.
