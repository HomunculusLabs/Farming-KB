# Terrace Systems for Slope Management

Terrace systems are engineered earthworks that convert sloping land into a series of level or gently inclined platforms, enabling productive agriculture while preventing soil erosion and water loss. In [[permaculture-design]], terraces represent one of the most fundamental strategies for slope management, allowing steep and marginal land to become productive food-growing areas.

## Overview

Terracing transforms the fundamental challenge of slopes — gravity-driven erosion and water runoff — into a structured advantage. By creating stepped, level surfaces along contours, terraces slow water movement, trap sediment, and provide flat planting areas on otherwise unusable terrain. This practice has been refined over millennia, from ancient rice paddies in Asia to Inca andenes in South America.

## Types of Terraces

### Hand-Made Terraces

Hand-made terraces are constructed manually using simple tools — shovels, mattocks, and baskets. They are suited to small-scale systems, home gardens, and labor-rich contexts. Dimensions are typically modest: benches 1–3 meters wide with risers of 0.5–1.5 meters, depending on slope steepness and soil type. Hand construction allows fine-tuned adaptation to microtopography and is ideal for intimate garden terraces near homes.

### Machine-Made Terraces

Machine-made terraces use earth-moving equipment (bulldozers, graders, excavators) to create broader, more uniform benches. These are appropriate for larger-scale land rehabilitation and broadacre systems. Machine-constructed terraces typically produce wider benches (3–10+ meters) and can handle steeper grades more efficiently, but require careful planning to avoid soil compaction and damage to soil structure.

### Garden Terraces on Steep Humid Slopes

In steep humid tropical and subtropical environments, garden terraces close to the home are essential for intensive food production. These small, carefully maintained terraces support high-value garden crops and benefit from household proximity for management, mulching, and harvest.

## Wet Terrace Culture

### Classical Wet Rice and Taro Terraces

The classical wet terrace system is one of humanity's oldest and most productive agricultural technologies. These terraces maintain a continuous water feed from springs, streams, or redirected catchment, creating flooded bench surfaces ideal for:

- **Wet rice (paddy rice):** The dominant grain crop, thriving in continuously flooded conditions
- **Taro ([[colocasia-esculenta]]):** A staple root crop in Pacific and Asian cultures, grown in saturated soils

The water feed must be reliable and continuous. Excess water cascades from upper terraces to lower ones through spillways, creating a gravity-fed irrigation cascade. The permanent water layer suppresses weeds, moderates soil temperature, and supports aquatic ecosystems.

### Fish Cultivation in Terrace Deeps

A key design element of wet terraces is the integration of aquaculture. Deeper pockets or channels within the terrace system (terrace deeps) are stocked with fish — commonly carp, tilapia, or local species. Fish provide protein, cycle nutrients, control pests and weeds, and their manure fertilizes the rice or taro. This integration creates a synergistic polyculture where crops and livestock support each other.

## Dry Terrace Culture

Dry terraces rely on rainfall and mulch rather than continuous flooding. They are suitable for drier climates, steeper slopes where water retention is difficult, and grain-based farming systems. Dry terraces require more attention to soil organic matter and mulch to maintain fertility and prevent desiccation.

### Dry Terrace Crop Species

Dry terraces support a wide range of grain and pulse crops:

- **Grains:** Millet, dryland rice, barley, wheat, rye, amaranth, quinoa, rape/mustard
- **Pulses and legumes:** Lentils, peas, grams, and other pulses for nitrogen fixation
- **Fodders:** Tagasaste, banna grass, comfrey, Leucaena for livestock feed and green manure

## Growing Situations on Terraced Land

Terraced landscapes create a diverse set of microenvironments, each with distinct growing conditions. Effective permaculture design assigns appropriate species to each zone:

### Banks and Bunds

The raised edges of terraces (bunds) are ideal for tree crops, shrubs, and vine support. Bund tops receive maximum sun exposure and are well-drained, making them suitable for fruit trees, nitrogen-fixing legumes, and perennial herbs.

### Slope Faces

The vertical or near-vertical risers between terrace levels can be stabilized and planted with deep-rooted grasses, trailing vines, and groundcovers that bind soil and reduce erosion.

### Ditches and Water Channels

Terrace drainage channels and irrigation ditches support water-loving species, edge plants, and can serve as micro-habitats for beneficial aquatic organisms.

### Steep Slopes Above Terraces

The upper slopes above the terraced area require permanent vegetation cover to prevent erosion from feeding sediment into the terrace system. These areas are allocated to mulch crops, fodder trees, and soil-stabilizing grasses.

### Flat Terrace Areas

The level bench surfaces are the primary production zone, supporting the main agricultural crops — whether grains, vegetables, or wetland species depending on the terrace type.

## Crop Allocation by Zone

### Garden Terraces Near Homes

Home garden terraces emphasize high-value, frequently harvested crops:

- Banana, papaya, melons, chilies, peppers
- Cucurbits (squash, pumpkin, cucumber)
- Maize, beans, sugar cane

These terraces benefit from kitchen waste composting, household greywater, and daily attention.

### Vine Crops Off Bund Faces

Bund faces provide natural trellising for climbing and vining species:

- Chayote, cucurbits, beans
- Passionfruit, kiwifruit

These crops cascade down the bund faces, utilizing vertical space and producing food from structures that also stabilize the terrace.

### Mulch Crops and Fodders Above Borders

The slopes and borders above the terrace system should be planted with mulch-producing and fodder species. These plants provide:

- Organic matter for terrace fertility
- Livestock feed
- Soil stabilization and erosion prevention
- Microclimate moderation

## Mulch and Tree Crop Allocation

A critical design principle is allocating **one-half to one-third of the total terraced area** to mulch trees and support species. While this may seem like a significant allocation away from food crops, it is essential for long-term sustainability. Mulch trees provide:

- Leaf litter and prunings for soil building
- Nitrogen fixation (if leguminous)
- Windbreak and shade modulation
- Microclimate improvement
- Long-term soil structure maintenance

Without this allocation, terrace soils rapidly degrade and productivity declines.

## Slope Stability

Terrace systems must incorporate slope stability measures to prevent catastrophic failure. Key strategies include:

### Contour Strip Planting

Strips of stabilizing grasses planted on contour above and between terraces:

- **Vetiver grass (Vetiveria zizanioides):** Deep-rooted, dense clump-forming grass that binds soil to depths of 2–3 meters
- **Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus):** Clump-forming grass with economic value as a culinary and medicinal herb
- **Banna grass (Pennisetum purpureum):** Fast-growing fodder grass that stabilizes slopes and provides livestock feed

### Tree Legumes on Contours

Nitrogen-fixing tree legumes planted on contour strips intercrop with stabilizing grasses, adding deep root networks and contributing nitrogen to the system. Species include Leucaena, Gliricidia, and other locally adapted leguminous trees.

## Essentials and Variations

Key design essentials for terrace systems include:

1. **Always follow natural contours** — terraces must be level along the contour to prevent water concentration and breakout erosion
2. **Ensure adequate drainage** — every terrace needs spillways and overflow paths to handle extreme rainfall events
3. **Maintain bund integrity** — bund walls must be regularly inspected and repaired
4. **Integrate multiple functions** — terraces should produce food, conserve soil, manage water, and support biodiversity simultaneously
5. **Design for access** — pathways between terraces must allow movement of people, tools, and harvest without damaging structures
6. **Plan for succession** — terrace systems evolve; initial pioneer species give way to mature polycultures over time

Variations on terrace systems include keyline-based contour swales combined with partial terracing, bench terraces with integrated chinampas, and modern variations using geotextiles and gabions for reinforcement on unstable slopes.
