# Applied Pattern Design in Permaculture

## Overview

Applied pattern design takes the principles of pattern understanding and imposes them on physical landscapes and structures to achieve specific ends—[[water-conservation]], space efficiency, resource capture, and aesthetic integration. These practical applications demonstrate how pattern analysis translates directly into functional design solutions.

## The Herb Spiral

The [[herb-spiral]], evolved by [[bill-mollison]] in 1978 as a kitchen-door design, is one of the most iconic applied patterns in [[permaculture]]. All basic culinary herbs can be planted in an ascending spiral of earth on a 2-metre wide base, ascending to 1–1.3 metres high.

### Design Features

- **Compact footprint**: The spiral ramp and walls exceed 9 metres of plantable space in a modest 2-metre diameter mound
- **Variable microclimates**: Sunny, dry sites at the top for oil-rich herbs (thyme, sage, rosemary); moist, shaded sites at the bottom for green foliage herbs (mint, parsley, chives, coriander)
- **Accessibility**: All herbs planted on the spiral ramp are accessible without stepping on the planting area
- **Water efficiency**: Can be watered with one 2-metre sprinkler
- **Three-dimensional design**: A rare small-scale three-dimensional earth construct that coils a linear herb bed into one mound at the kitchen door

### Adaptations

If kitchens are not at ground level, roof or balcony gardens can carry pot-herbs in stepped walls, on wall shelves, in window boxes, or as stacks of pots in earth mounds.

## Circle-Mulch Grow Pits

Pattern analysis applied to water conservation produces the mulch-pit system: a pit 60 cm wide and deep, surrounded by a planting shelf and spill bank totalling 1.2 metres (4 feet) across.

### Advantages Over Row Cropping

- A 1.2-metre circle has a 3.8-metre (12-foot) perimeter but needs only one low-pressure sprinkler, whereas a 3.8-metre straight row requires three sprinklers
- The central one-drop mulch pit means plants eventually overshade the centre to prevent evaporation
- Circle-mulch pits are scaled up to 1.8 metres (6 feet) across for bananas, and 1.8–3 metres (6–10 feet) across for coconuts
- All out-produce row crops for about one-third of the water use
- A series of such gardens greatly reduces path space and land area needed for home gardens or orchards

## Flood-Plain Embankments and Flow Interceptors

A field application of patterned ground designed to direct flow and capture materials in flow uses flood-plain embankments or tree lines (poplar, willow, tamarack), or both combined. These pattern impositions on the landscape occur naturally as rock dykes or resistant rock strata, but can be deliberately constructed for several beneficial effects:

- Direct floodwaters to productive areas
- Capture silt, mulch, and firewood carried by floodwaters
- Scour out river sand for productive use
- Benefit crop production through nutrient deposition
- Can be constructed as sub-surface structures to keep flow channels open

## Flowform Water Treatment Systems

Flowform models, developed at the Virbella Institute (Emerson College, Kent, UK) by a small group of artist-technicians, represent one of the most sophisticated applications of natural pattern in design. These turbulence basins replicate the shaped basins found in streams flowing over massive sandstones or mudstones—forms even modelled in pozzolanic cement by Roman hydrologists in antiquity.

### Design and Construction

Flowforms are artificial replicates of rock forms carved by turbulent streams, cast in concrete or fibreglass. They can be:

- Elongate or truncate
- Symmetrical or asymmetrical
- Stepped in line
- Stacked like ladders
- Spiralled to conserve space

### Three Distinct Mixing Effects

1. **Plunge or vertical overturn**: Fluid drops from one basin to another
2. **Figure-8 or lateral flow**: Water courses around the basins themselves
3. **Interaction effect**: Water coursing around the basins deflects the vertical drop flow and switches it from side to side in a regular rhythm

Within these major turbulence patterns (portrayed by da Vinci and further analysed by Chappell through computer models and catastrophic theory for coastal uprush and backwash turbulence) are distinct vortices, counterflow, overfolds, and cusps that further mix air and water.

### Practical Applications

- **Sewage treatment**: Primary treatment of sewage and organically polluted waters
- **Aquaculture support**: Oxygenation of ponds for aquatic species production
- **Water purification**: Aeration and oxidation of pollutants
- **Aesthetic value**: Aesthetically pleasing and instructive hydrological pattern-models

## The Arts in Service of Life

These applied pattern designs reconnect art with function. Much tribal art was carefully assembled to assist folk records of the function and history of their society. Modern sculptural forms like Flowform systems serve both aesthetic and practical functions, representing a small step toward applied art as patterning in everyday use.

### The Challenge

It remains a challenge to:
- Study and portray knowledge in compact, memorable, and transmissible form
- Research and recreate surviving art forms that retain their meaning for common use
- Re-integrate art with science, society, and functional needs
- Revive meaningful geometries, songs, and dances that give work and life meaning

## Design Principles

The designer's checklist for applied pattern work includes:

- Study the relationships of orders and forms in nature
- Ensure patterned systems are of appropriate size
- Ensure systems are of the right order (scale matching)
- Apply patterns that serve multiple functions simultaneously
- Design for water efficiency, space efficiency, and accessibility
- Use three-dimensional thinking to maximise yield per unit area
