# Forensic Mycology: Fungi as Evidence and the Corpse Finder

Fungi play an intimate role in the decomposition of animal remains, and this
relationship has begun to attract the attention of forensic scientists. The study
of fungi associated with corpses and crime scenes — forensic mycology — represents
a largely unexplored frontier at the intersection of mycology and criminal
investigation.

## Fungi and Human Decomposition

Every day of our lives, fungal spores pass into our nostrils and lungs. Given
the opportunity, some may germinate and colonize human tissue. The mycological
literature documents ink-cap mushrooms growing in a patient's throat, bracket-
forming basidiomycetes in a gentleman's nose, and yeast infections covering
infants. While these clinical cases are disturbing, they hint at a broader
ecological truth: fungi are among the primary decomposers of animal remains,
and the communities of fungi that colonize bodies follow predictable patterns
that could serve as forensic indicators.

The mycologist Nicholas Money has suggested that a systematic study of human
decomposition from a mycological perspective would be invaluable, and proposed
that a professional colleague with a strong stomach visit the FBI facility in
Tennessee called the "Body Farm" — a research facility where human decomposition
is studied under [[plant-olfaction-chemical-sensing]] through the soil. Buried bodies
release a complex mixture of decomposition products — amino acids, fatty acids,
ammonia, and other organic molecules — that diffuse through surrounding soil.
Fungal hyphae are capable of detecting and growing toward nutrient gradients, a
phenomenon known as chemotropism. The nutrient-rich environment of a decomposing
body would provide a powerful growth stimulus for soil-inhabiting fungi, drawing
[[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]] like iron filings drawn
to a magnet.

The predictable appearance of *Hebeloma* fruiting bodies above burial sites
suggests that other fungal species may show similar associations with particular
stages of decomposition or with specific burial conditions. A comprehensive
survey of fungi associated with human remains could yield a succession model
analogous to the insect succession models already used in forensic entomology
to estimate time since death.

## Ecological Context

The relationship between fungi and decomposing animal tissue is ancient and
deeply rooted. Fungi have been decomposing flesh for hundreds of millions of
years, long before the appearance of mammals or any vertebrate animals. The
oomycete *[[bloomfield-cryptococcus-neoformans-opportunistic-mycoses-aids]]*
and *Pythium insidiosum* — may be descendants of ancient flesh-decomposing
species. The broad host range of *Cryptococcus* supports this hypothesis:
infections have been reported in bats, camels, cats, cheetahs, civets, cows,
dogs, dolphins, ferrets, foxes, goats, guinea pigs, horses, koalas, mice,
pigs, rats, shrews, snakes, and a variety of birds. The yeast may have made
its living in the brains of other hominids millions of years before humans
appeared. The cells possess too many adaptations suited to life inside animal
tissues to be relegated to the status of reluctant intruders.

This deep evolutionary history explains why fungi possess so many adaptations
suited to growth in animal tissue: enzymes for degrading keratin and collagen,
mechanisms for tolerating immune responses, and strategies for extracting
nitrogen from protein-rich substrates. The opportunistic human pathogens that
cause modern mycoses may represent lineages that have been associated with
animal decomposition for geological timescales. As one mycologist put it,
after *Cryptococcus* has entered the brain it is almost certain to drown in
preservatives or roast in crematorium flames — but these niceties of disposal
are very recent inventions. Bodies deteriorate rapidly in nature, and fungal
cells might find their way into new hosts who contact putrefying flesh or
inhale spores shed from its surface.

## Broader Applications

Forensic mycology could contribute to criminal investigations in several ways:

- **Locating concealed remains**: Fruiting bodies of *Hebeloma* and other
  fungi that preferentially colonize buried bodies could direct search efforts.
- **Estimating postmortem interval**: Fungal succession on remains, analogous
  to insect succession, could provide independent estimates of time since death.
- **Determining burial environment**: The species composition of fungal
  communities on remains could indicate whether a body was buried, submerged,
  or left exposed.
- **Linking remains to crime scenes**: Soil fungi adhering to remains or to
  the clothing of a victim could be matched to fungal communities at suspected
  disposal sites.

The field remains largely theoretical, with few case studies in the forensic
literature. Most forensic mycology references are anecdotal — a mushroom
growing where a body was found, a mold species linking a victim to a specific
location. Systematic research programs are needed to establish the reliability
of fungal evidence in court. As awareness grows among both mycologists and
forensic scientists, forensic mycology may develop into a routine component
of death investigation, complementing the well-established fields of forensic
entomology and forensic anthropology.
