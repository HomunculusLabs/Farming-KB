## Mushroom Spawn Storage and Vitality Preservation

## Overview

The vitality of [[history-of-mushroom-cultivation]] is replete with failures attributable to dead or moribund spawn, making the understanding of spawn storage and vitality preservation essential knowledge for every grower.

## The Spawn Viability Problem

### Scale of the Problem

Systematic investigations conducted from 1901 to 1904 revealed the alarming extent of spawn failure. Samples of commercial spawn purchased in various American cities and tested under controlled conditions showed that in an unexpectedly large majority of cases, **the spawn proved to be entirely dead**. This finding identified poor spawn as the primary cause of the failures commonly experienced by amateur mushroom growers.

### Why Imported Spawn Failed

Several factors contributed to the poor viability of imported (European) spawn in the American market:

1. **Extended shipment time**: The additional time involved in ocean transport from England or France to the United States weakened or killed the mycelium
2. **Adverse shipping conditions**: Temperature fluctuations, moisture exposure, and physical handling during transit damaged the living fungal tissue
3. **Poor storage at destination**: Subsequent storage methods at American seed dealers often failed to maintain conditions favorable for spawn survival
4. **Seasonal carryover**: The worst practice was carrying spawn over from one season to the next under the assumption that it was "quite as good" as the fresher product—this was essentially fatal to viability
5. **French [[brick-spawn]]** exhibited the best keeping quality of any product on the market. Several factors contributed to this superiority:

1. **Low moisture content**: Properly dried brick spawn had sufficiently low moisture to slow metabolic activity and prevent premature growth or decay
2. **Compact form**: The compressed structure protected the internal mycelium from physical damage and rapid moisture exchange with the environment
3. **Standardized dimensions**: The regular shape (approximately 5½ × 8¼ × 1½ inches when dry) facilitated uniform drying, storage, and handling
4. **Inoculation depth**: Spawn mycelium distributed throughout the interior of the brick was protected from surface contamination and desiccation

### Flake Spawn Vulnerability

In contrast, French flake spawn—consisting of loose, moist compost colonized by mushroom mycelium—was far more vulnerable to deterioration during storage and transport. Its higher moisture content and loose structure made it susceptible to both desiccation and contamination, explaining why it rapidly lost vitality under importation conditions.

## Principles of Spawn Storage

### Temperature

Spawn should be stored under conditions that minimize metabolic activity without killing the mycelium. Cool temperatures (similar to those in a mushroom growing house, approximately 50–60°F) are generally favorable. Extreme heat is fatal; freezing temperatures may be tolerated for limited periods but are not ideal for long-term storage.

### Moisture

The moisture level must be carefully balanced:

- **Too moist**: Encourages premature growth of the mycelium within the brick, depleting its energy reserves before planting; also promotes growth of contaminant moulds and bacteria
- **Too dry**: Desiccates the mycelium, reducing or eliminating viability

Properly dried brick spawn strikes this balance—the internal moisture content is sufficient to maintain mycelial viability while low enough to prevent premature growth.

### Ventilation

Stored spawn should be kept in a well-drained, ventilated room or cellar. Stagnant air with high humidity promotes mold growth on the brick surfaces. Good air circulation helps maintain the desired moisture equilibrium.

### Protection from Contamination

Spawn should be protected from contact with contaminated materials, soil, or other potential sources of competitive organisms. The cotton plugs of pure culture tubes serve this purpose in the laboratory; commercial brick spawn's dried, compact exterior provides a degree of natural protection.

## The Deterioration Process

### Symptoms of Declining Vitality

Spawn that has lost vitality may show:

- **No visible mycelial growth** when planted, even after extended periods
- **Slow, weak growth** if any growth occurs at all
- **Rapid overgrowth by contaminants** (moulds, bacteria) that outcompete the weakened mushroom mycelium
- **Failure to produce mushrooms** even when the spawn appears to have colonized the bed

### The "Running Out" Problem

Historical experience showed that continuous transfer of spawn from one bed to another—without periodically returning to pure culture material—resulted in gradual deterioration of spawn vigor, known as "running out." After approximately **three years** of successive bed-to-bed transfer, the spawn typically showed marked decline in productivity.

This phenomenon is analogous to the well-documented reduction in pathogenicity that occurs when certain pathogenic fungi are continuously subcultured in the laboratory. The precise biological mechanisms may include:

- **Accumulation of deleterious mutations** during repeated vegetative propagation
- **Depletion of energy reserves** through too many generations of growth without sexual recombination
- **Gradual increase in viral or subcellular pathogen load**

### Prevention Through Pure Culture Renewal

The pure culture tissue method solved the running-out problem by providing a mechanism for periodically renewing the spawn from fresh, vigorously growing mushroom tissue. The author tested one strain for five years by continuous propagation from season to season through tissue cultures, and it consistently showed high yield and quality—demonstrating that running out was attributable to improper care and excessive transfer rather than to any inherent limitation of the method.

## Practical Recommendations for the Grower

1. **Insist on fresh, vigorous spawn**: The improvement in American spawn quality is obvious, but the grower should still demand recent production dates and evidence of viability
2. **Use spawn promptly**: Do not carry spawn over from one season to the next
3. **Store properly**: Cool, ventilated, protected from moisture extremes
4. **Test before full commitment**: If spawn quality is uncertain, test a small quantity before committing to a full planting
5. **Source from reliable producers**: Select spawn makers who use pure culture methods and who have established reputations for quality and reliability
6. **Avoid imported spawn for critical plantings**: Unless the supply chain is very short and reliable

The dramatic improvement in American spawn quality—from the era when most commercial spawn was dead to the point where 80–90 percent of the 1913–1914 crop was grown from reliable American pure culture spawn—demonstrates the transformative power of understanding and controlling spawn viability.
