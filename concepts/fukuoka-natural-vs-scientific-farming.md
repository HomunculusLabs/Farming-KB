# Natural vs Scientific Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka provides a systematic comparison between [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] and [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture]], showing them to be fundamentally opposed in their perceptions, thinking, and the direction of research.

## Three Ways of Farming

Fukuoka divided farming into three distinct categories:

**Mahayana Natural Farming**: When the human spirit and human life blend with the natural order and man devotes himself entirely to the service of nature, he lives freely as an integral part of the natural world, subsisting on its bounty without having to resort to purposeful effort. This type of farming transcends time and space and reaches the zenith of understanding and enlightenment.

**Hinayana Natural Farming**: This type of farming arises when man earnestly seeks entry to the realm of Mahayana farming. Desirous of the true blessings and bounty of nature, he prepares himself to receive it. This is the road leading directly to complete enlightenment, but is short of that perfect state.

**Scientific Farming**: Man exists in a state of contradiction in which he is basically estranged from nature, living in a totally artificial world, yet longs for a return to nature. A product of this condition, scientific farming forever wanders blindly back and forth, now calling upon the blessings of nature, now rejecting it in favor of human knowledge and action.

## Fundamental Differences in Perception

Scientific farming begins with the assumption that nature is imperfect and that human knowledge can improve upon it. The scientist analyzes a crop, identifies what it needs, and develops techniques to supply those needs. Fertilizer is applied because the soil is deemed insufficient. Pesticides are applied because natural pest control is deemed inadequate. Varieties are bred because natural plants are deemed unproductive.

Natural farming begins with the opposite assumption: that nature is already perfect and complete. Crops are fully capable of growing by themselves. The farmer's role is not to supply what nature lacks but to avoid disrupting what nature already provides. The [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] holds that the less the farmer does, the closer the system moves to its natural, optimal state.

## The Problem of Fragmentation

Scientific farming divides the study of agriculture into specialized disciplines: soil science, plant breeding, entomology, plant pathology, agronomy. Each specialist works on their own piece of the puzzle, optimizing one variable at a time. Fukuoka argues that this fragmentation is inherently misguided because in nature everything is connected.

Tillage and seeding specialists believe that improvements in these techniques are critical to increasing yields. A fertilizer specialist will tell you to keep fertilizing your plants. A pest control expert will recommend spraying. Each recommendation makes sense in isolation, but together they create a system that is increasingly dependent on external inputs and increasingly removed from natural processes.

## The Sword-Fighting Analogy

Borrowing an analogy from the art of swordfighting, Hinayana natural farming may be likened to the one-sword school that is directed toward the center, and scientific farming to the two-sword school that is directed outward. The two can be compared. But Mahayana natural farming is the unmoving no-sword school, comparison with which is impossible.

Scientific farming uses all possible means at its disposal, increasing the number of swords, whereas natural farming tries to obtain the best possible results while rendering all means useless, in effect reducing the number of its swords (Hinayana) or doing entirely without (Mahayana).

This view is based on the philosophical conviction that if man makes a genuine effort to approach nature, then even should he abandon all deeds and actions, nature will take each of these over and perform them for him.

## Causation vs. Correlation

A critical difference lies in how each approach understands cause and effect. The scientist observes that applying fertilizer increases yield and concludes that fertilizer causes growth. Fukuoka observes the same phenomenon but draws a different conclusion: the fertilizer causes a particular type of growth that is dependent on continued fertilization, whereas natural growth is self-sustaining.

If one works from the assumption that fruit trees must "be grown," the absorption of fertilizer by the roots becomes the cause, and the full growth of the leaves and branches the effect. This leads quite naturally to the conclusion that applying fertilizer is necessary. However, if we take as our starting point the view that a tree grows of its own accord, the uptake of nutrients by the tree's roots is no longer a cause but, in the eyes of nature, just a small effect.

## The Deformed Harvest

When scientific methods are used to grow a fruit tree, the goal may be to produce large fruit, in which case all efforts will be concentrated to this end. Yet all that will be achieved is the production of what may, in a limited sense, be regarded as large fruit -- but invariably has grave flaws. Essentially, what is being grown is deformed fruit.

The same principle applies to all crops grown by scientific methods. By optimizing for yield, the farmer sacrifices flavor, nutritional content, disease resistance, and ecological integration. The resulting food may look impressive but is fundamentally inferior to food grown in natural conditions.

## The Model of Harvest Yields

Fukuoka used the analogy of a building to explain how crop yields are determined. The hotel is built on a rock foundation that symbolizes nature, and the floors and rooms represent cultivation conditions and factors which play a role in the final yield.

The upper limit of yield is set by the natural environment, represented by the strength of the rock foundation. This maximum yield may be called the natural yield and is, for man, the best and highest yield. The approach usually taken by scientific farming to boost yields is to fill as many of the rooms as possible. But in a larger sense, this is merely a way of minimizing losses in yield. The only true way to raise yields is to enlarge the building itself.

Any attempt to outdo nature, to increase production by purely industrial methods that brazenly disregard the natural order, is analogous to adding an annex onto the building representing nature. If we imagine this annex to be built on sand, then we can begin to understand the precariousness of artificial endeavors to raise yields.

## Energy Economics

Scientific farming is energy-intensive. Traditional farming already required ten times the energy input of natural systems through the use of draft animals. Small-scale mechanization doubled this, and large-scale mechanization doubled it again. Modern agriculture now expends more calories of fossil fuel than it produces in food calories.

Natural farming, by contrast, requires almost no external energy input. The sun provides energy for photosynthesis. Rain provides water. The soil provides nutrients through its own biological activity. The only energy the farmer expends is in harvesting and processing -- tasks that cannot be eliminated.

## The Direction of Research

Scientific farming research moves toward greater complexity, more inputs, and more control. Each advance creates new problems that require further research. The system becomes increasingly brittle and dependent on continued technological intervention.

Natural farming research -- if it can be called research -- moves toward greater simplicity, fewer inputs, and less control. Fukuoka's decades of experimentation were aimed at removing practices, not adding them. Each simplification brought the system closer to natural self-sufficiency.

## A Choice of Worldviews

Ultimately, the choice between natural and scientific farming is not a technical decision but a philosophical one. It reflects a fundamental difference in how one views the relationship between humans and nature. [[fukuoka-critique-laws-agricultural-science]] sees nature as raw material to be shaped by human intelligence. Natural farming sees human intelligence as a tool for understanding and cooperating with nature's inherent wisdom.

Philosophically, scientific farming cannot be superior to Mahayana natural farming because, while scientific farming is the sum of knowledge and forces extracted from nature by the human intellect, this still amounts to finite human knowledge. No matter how one totals it up, human knowledge is but a tiny, closely circumscribed fraction of the infinitude of the natural world.
