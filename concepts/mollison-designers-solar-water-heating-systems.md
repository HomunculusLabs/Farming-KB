# Solar Water Heating Systems

Solar water heating is a key appropriate technology in Bill Mollison's
Permaculture: A Designers Manual, providing domestic hot water using
free solar energy with minimal mechanical complexity. Mollison presents
solar water heating as an essential component of energy-efficient
permaculture homesteads, reducing or eliminating fuel consumption for
one of the largest household energy uses.

## Batch Heaters (Integral Collector Storage)

The simplest solar water heater is the batch heater (also called a
breadbox or ICS collector). A water tank painted black and placed
inside an insulated, glazed box absorbs solar radiation directly. Cold
water enters the tank, heats throughout the day, and is drawn off for
use. No pumps or controls are needed.

Batch heaters are cheap to build from salvaged materials -- an old
water heater tank, a glass pane, and insulation. They work well in
warm, sunny climates but lose heat rapidly at night and during cloudy
periods. Mollison recommends them as entry-level [[appropriate-technology]]
for household hot water. Efficiency ranges from 30-50% overall due to
overnight losses. Insulating covers (manual or automated) improve
performance in cooler climates.

## Thermosyphon Systems

Thermosyphon (passive circulation) systems use the natural buoyancy of
warm water to circulate fluid through the collector without pumps. The
collector is mounted below the storage tank; heated water rises into
the tank while cooler water descends to the collector.

Two configurations exist:

- **Open-loop (direct):** Potable water circulates through the
  collector. Simple and efficient but vulnerable to freezing and
  hard water mineral buildup. Suitable only for frost-free climates.
- **Closed-loop (indirect):** A glycol or water-alcohol antifreeze
  solution circulates through the collector, transferring heat to
  potable water via a heat exchanger in the storage tank. Works in
  freezing climates but slightly less efficient due to heat exchange
  losses.

The storage tank must be mounted above the collector (typically on
the roof or in an elevated location) to ensure proper thermosyphon
flow. Mollison notes this requirement limits retrofit options but
makes thermosyphon systems extremely reliable -- no moving parts means
nothing to break. Systems can provide 50-80% of annual hot water needs
in temperate climates.

## Flat Plate Collectors

Flat plate collectors are the most common type for domestic solar
water heating. They consist of a dark absorber plate (copper, aluminum,
or steel) with attached fluid tubes, insulated on the back and sides,
and covered with tempered glass or polycarbonate glazing. Solar
radiation passes through the glazing, is absorbed by the plate, and
transferred to the fluid circulating through the tubes.

Mollison describes construction of simple flat plate collectors using
salvaged materials: old refrigerators as insulated boxes, copper
tubing from decommissioned systems, and recycled glass. Commercial
collectors use selective surface coatings (black chrome, titanium
nitride) to reduce radiative heat loss and achieve efficiencies of
60-75% under good conditions.

Key design considerations:

- Tilt angle: latitude plus 10-15 degrees for winter optimization;
  latitude minus 10 degrees for summer. Year-round use suggests tilt
  equal to latitude.
- Orientation: True south (northern hemisphere) or true north
  (southern hemisphere) for maximum annual collection.
- Area: 1-2 m2 per person for temperate climates; 0.5-1 m2 per
  person in sunny tropical climates.
- Freeze protection: Drain-back, drain-down, or closed-loop with
  antifreeze for cold climates.

## Active (Pumped) Systems

Active systems use a small electric pump to circulate fluid between
collector and storage tank, allowing the tank to be placed below the
collector (in a utility room or basement). A differential thermostat
activates the pump when the collector is hotter than the tank by a set
temperature differential (typically 8-10 degrees C).

Mollison acknowledges that active systems add complexity and energy
consumption (the pump) but provide greater design flexibility and
are necessary when roof-mounted tanks are impractical. PV-powered
pumps eliminate grid dependency and align with [[holmgren-renewable-resources-detailed]]
principles.

## Evacuated Tube Collectors

Evacuated tube collectors consist of rows of parallel glass tubes,
each containing a vacuum-sealed absorber. The vacuum eliminates
convective heat loss, making these collectors highly efficient
(70-80%) even in cold, cloudy, or windy conditions. They can heat
water to higher temperatures than flat plate collectors.

Mollison views evacuated tubes as appropriate where high temperatures
are needed (radiant floor heating, commercial applications) or where
climate conditions limit flat plate performance. Higher cost is the
main disadvantage.

## Solar Hot Water System Integration

In permaculture design, solar water heating integrates with multiple
systems:

- Pre-heating water for [[aquaculture-in-permaculture]] systems and greenhouses
- Providing warm water for dairy and cheesemaking operations
- Heating water for [[mollison-designers-food-processing-preservation-storage]] and preservation
- Supporting [[permaculture-zone-one-design]] domestic needs as a primary design element
- Connecting to hydronic heating for space heating via radiant
  floors or radiators

Mollison emphasizes that the simplest system that meets the need is
always preferred. A well-designed thermosyphon system with a
appropriately sized tank can provide decades of reliable service with
no external energy input beyond the sun.

## Sizing and Performance

System sizing depends on daily hot water demand (typically 40-60
liters per person), local solar radiation (insolation), and desired
solar fraction (percentage of demand met by solar). In temperate
climates, well-designed systems achieve 50-70% annual solar fraction;
tropical locations can reach 80-100%.
