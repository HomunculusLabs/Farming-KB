## Ericoid Mycorrhizal Carbon Pools

The hair roots of ericaceous plants contain extensive fungal coils of
their mycorrhizal symbionts, typically occupying 10-80% of root volume.
Because uninfected root cortical cells are largely vacuolate, and the
fungal structures turn over faster than the roots, the relative importance
of the fungus as a sink for plant photosynthate is likely much larger than
suggested by volume or biomass measures alone.

Estimates using carbon-14 pulse-labelling of subarctic tundra showed very
marked seasonality with peak values in autumn, the mycorrhizal hair roots
being the dominant sink at that time. Approximately 20-40% of carbon-14
allocation below ground was found in ericoid mycorrhizal hair roots, with
approximately 50% of assimilated carbon reaching hair roots estimated to
pass to the fungi.

## Ectomycorrhizal Carbon Dynamics

Ectomycorrhizal fungi produce robust hydrophobic multicellular hyphal
cords well adapted for nutrient and water transport. The carbon cost of
ectomycorrhizal mycelial networks is substantial, with significant
allocation to the external mycelium extending into the soil. These EM
networks are a primary pathway for carbon flow from host trees into soil
organic matter, particularly in boreal and temperate forests where they
dominate.

### Role in Soil Carbon Sequestration
The most important biomes for soil carbon sequestration are the heathland
and boreal forest regions that together cover approximately 70% of the
vegetated land mass of the Northern Hemisphere and contain the majority of
the world's soil carbon stocks, mainly in peat and raw humus. Variations
in the dominant types of mycorrhizal association are important drivers of
ecosystem carbon cycling and storage.

## Mycoheterotrophic Plants and Carbon Theft

The magnitude of the below-ground carbon energy flow through mycorrhizal
mycelial networks in soil has been so large that it has driven the

their establishment and growth. More than 10% of plant species depend upon
