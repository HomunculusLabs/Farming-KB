## Agar Plate Preparation and Formulation

The complete process of preparing, sterilizing, pouring, storing, and customizing
agar plates for fungal [[query-what-is-tissue-culture-and-why-does-it-matter-for-growers]] [[duggar-mushroom-life-cycle-spore-germination-fruiting]] in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]].

## What Is Agar?

An agar plate is a petri plate (petri dish) filled with agar — a gelatinous extract
of seaweed commonly used as a substrate for tissue cultures. Agar provides a solid,
nutrient-rich surface on which [[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]] can grow, be observed, and be
transferred. It is the foundational medium for all [[mushroom-tissue-culture]] work.

## Step 1: Preparing Agar Gel (Potato Agar)

### Ingredients

- 1 small potato (unpeeled)
- 3–4 cups water
- 1 teaspoon agar powder (~5 grams) per 2 cups of broth

### Procedure

1. Slice or shred the potato into small pieces (no need to peel)
2. Place pieces in a small pot with 3–4 cups of water
3. Boil for one hour, being careful not to let it foam or boil over
4. The water will look like "potato tea" — it will not thicken
5. Add water as needed if the level drops significantly
6. Strain the potatoes from the liquid
7. Measure the hot broth and pour into an Erlenmeyer flask (or wide-bottomed glass
   container)
8. Add agar powder while broth is still hot — this prevents clumping and ensures
   smooth dissolution

### Critical Tip

**Never add agar powder to cold water** — it clumps instantly and becomes very
difficult to dissolve. Always add to hot liquid.

## Step 2: Sterilizing the Agar

### Pressure Cooker Requirements

A pressure cooker rated for **12–15 PSI** is required. The lower the PSI, the longer
the cook time and the lower the maximum sterilization temperature. Features to look
for:

- **Metal-to-metal seals** (preferred): More durable and reliable
- **Rubber gasket models** (acceptable for beginners): Work well for developing
  skills before investing in better equipment
- **Sufficient height**: Must accommodate the flask of agar solution upright

### Sterilization Procedure

1. Fill the pressure cooker with water per manufacturer directions
2. Place the flask upright in the inner pan (stabilize with aluminum foil wads or a
   wire basket if needed)
3. Plug the flask opening with cotton or polyester fill, cover with aluminum foil,
   and compress tightly
4. Heat to 15 PSI and process for **20 minutes**
5. Turn off heat and allow to cool

### Safe Opening Protocol

- At **2–3 PSI**: Safe to move the cooker (use a cart)
- At **slightly above 0 PSI**: Time to open — do this in a clean room or in front
  of the laminar hood only
- **Below 0 PSI (vacuum)**: Will suck in contaminated air — resterilize if this
  happens
- **Well above 0 PSI**: Sudden pressure change causes agar to boil out of the flask

Remove the hot flask using a paper towel saturated with 70% [[isopropyl-alcohol]] as a
mitt, or a sanitized jar lifter. Never touch sterilized vessels with bare hands —
skin oils can be colonized by bacteria.

## Step 3: Pouring the Agar

### Temperature Monitoring

- **Initial temperature**: Up to 200°F (93°C) when removed from sterilizer
- **Pouring temperature**: Approximately **100°F (38°C)** — cool enough to touch
- **Antibiotic addition window**: 105–110°F (41–43°C)
- **Tool**: An infrared temperature gauge ($30 at hardware stores) is one of the
  best investments for agar work

### Preparation

1. Sanitize the entire [[laminar-flow-hood]] work area with 70% isopropyl alcohol
2. Wipe down the outside of the petri plate sleeve with rubbing alcohol
3. Sanitize scissors and trim the bottom off the sleeve
4. Invert the entire stack and set it upright on the work surface, still in the
   sleeve
5. Gently tug the sleeve upward to remove it (save it for storage)
6. Divide into groups of 4–5 plates for faster, more efficient pouring

### Pouring Technique

1. Hold the flask of agar in one hand
2. With the other hand, grasp the lid of the bottom petri plate and lift straight up
   (lifting the entire stack, leaving only the bottom base)
3. Slowly pour agar until the base is approximately half full
4. Lower the stack to replace the lid
5. Slide to the next plate and repeat
6. **Keep the lid off for no more than 3 seconds** per plate to minimize
   [[fungicide-treated-seed-contamination-risk-mushroom-substrate]]

Two cups of agar should fill approximately 20–25 plates. With practice, an entire
sleeve can be poured in one to two minutes.

### Gel Check

After approximately one hour at room temperature, check gelling by tilting a few
plates to the side. Properly gelled agar should be **firm and elastic**. Wet or
improperly gelled agar is prone to contamination and difficult to make clean spore
streaks on.

## Step 4: Labeling, Using, and Storing Agar Plates

### Labeling System

Label plates with date, recipe, and additives. To manage the information density on
small plates, develop a color-coding system:

- **Example**: Green marker = penicillin G antibiotic
  - 1 mark = 1 ml penicillin G
  - 2 marks = 2 ml penicillin G
- Mark entire stacks by dragging a colored marker up the stack once gelled

### Immediate Use

After cooling, plates can be used immediately for streaking spores or transferring
mycelium. Seal cultured plates with tape or Parafilm at the seam between base and
lid.

### Storage

- Seal unused plates in new plastic ziplock bags (quart or sandwich size) in packs
  of 3–4 plates
- Plastic bags are very clean inside (heat-injected during manufacturing)
- Label with date and agar formula code
- Store in the **refrigerator** for 1–2 months (possibly longer)
- Extra plates can be stacked and stored in their original plastic sleeve, taped
  shut
