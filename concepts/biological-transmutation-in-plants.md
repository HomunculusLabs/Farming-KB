# Biological Transmutation in Plants

The medieval alchemist's dream of transmuting one element into another
may be vindicated by living plants, which appear capable of performing
low-energy nuclear transformations unknown to conventional physics. The
work of Louis Kervran, Pierre Baranger, and Albrecht von Herzeele
demonstrates that plants can transmute elements at room temperature
through mechanisms involving biological enzymes, lunar forces, and
forces beyond the scope of classical chemistry and nuclear physics.

## Overview

A young Breton schoolboy, Louis Kervran, noticed chickens pecking at
mica specks in soil entirely lacking limestone, yet producing eggs with
calcareous shells. No trace of mica was found in the birds' gizzards.
It took Kervran many years to establish that the chickens were
transmuting elements. He repeated an experiment by the chemist Vauquelin,
who in 1799 found more lime in eggshells than in the oats fed to hens.
Kervran fed chickens oats alone and found they produced four times as
much calcium as they ingested. When fed potassium, chickens produced
hard-shelled eggs composed of calcium, demonstrating transmutation of
potassium to calcium through addition of a hydrogen proton.

This contradicted Lavoisier's dictum that elements could be rearranged
but not transmuted. Kervran accepted that Lavoisier was right for
chemical reactions, but argued the error lay in assuming all reactions

## Von Herzeele's Pioneering Discoveries

In 1873, Hanoverian baron Albrecht von Herzeele published The Origin
of Inorganic Substances, offering proof that plants continuously create
matter. In hundreds of analyses of seeds sprouting in distilled water,
he found that potash, phosphorus, magnesium, calcium, and sulfur
inexplicably increased. Not only mineral ash but every component
increased, including nitrogen that should have burned off during
incineration. Von Herzeele discovered plants could transmute phosphorus
into sulfur, calcium into phosphorus, magnesium into calcium, and
nitrogen into potassium.
