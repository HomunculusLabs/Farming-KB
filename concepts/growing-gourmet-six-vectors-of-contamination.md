# [[six-vectors-of-contamination]] in Mushroom Cultivation

Cultivating mushroom mycelium in a laboratory is tantamount to not
cultivating contaminants. Diagnosing the source of contamination, and the
vector or pathway through which contaminants travel, is the key to tissue
culture. [[paul-stamets]] identified six distinct and separate vectors of
contamination. If a contaminant arises in the laboratory, the cultivator
should examine each vector category as being the possible cause of the
problem. Through a process of elimination, the distressed cultivator can
determine the vector through which the contaminants are spread. Once
discovered, the vector can be closed and the threat eliminated. If one
vector is open, a multitude of contaminants pass through it, often
confounding the diagnoses of inexperienced cultivators.

## The Six Principal Vectors

The principal vectors of contamination are:

  1. The Cultivator
  2. The Air
  3. The Media
  4. The Tools
  5. The Inoculum
  6. Mobile Contamination Units (MCUs)

The over-riding coefficients affecting each vector are the number of
contaminants and the exposure time. The more of each, the worse the
infestation. All of these vectors are universally affected by one
additional variable: time of exposure. The longer the exposure of any
vector, the more significant its impact. Good laboratory technicians are
characterized not only by their speed and care, but by their rhythm.
Transfers are done in a systematically repetitive fashion.

## Vector 1: The Cultivator

The human body teems with populations of micro-organisms. Diverse species
of fungi (including yeast), bacteria and viruses call your body their
home. When you are healthy, these populations achieve an equilibrium.
When you are ill, one or more groups proliferate out of control. Unhealthy
people should not be in a sterile laboratory, lest their disease organisms
escape and proliferate to dangerous proportions.

Most frequently, contaminants are spread into the sterile laboratory via
touch or breath. The flaking of the skin is a direct cause. Many
cultivators wear gloves to minimize the threat of skin-borne
contaminants. Hands should be washed every 20-30 minutes with
antibacterial soap and disinfected with 80% isopropyl alcohol immediately
before inoculations and every few minutes throughout the procedure.

## Vector 2: The Air

Air can be described as a sea of microorganisms, hosting invisible
contaminants that directly contaminate sterilized media once exposed.
When a person walks into the laboratory, they not only bring in
contaminants that become airborne, but their movement disturbs
contaminant-laden floors, re-releasing particulates into the lab's
atmosphere.

Several steps prevent this vector. One rule-of-thumb is to always have at
least three doors prior to entry into the sterile laboratory from the
outside. Each room should have fewer airborne particulates the nearer
they are to the laboratory. By positive-pressurizing the laboratory with
an influx of air through micron filters, the airstream will naturally be
directed against entering personnel.

For those not installing [[mushroom-laminarar-flow-hood]],
several alternatives exist. "Still-air" laboratories use aerosol sprays
such as commercial disinfectants or dilute isopropyl alcohol. The
cultivator enters the work area and sprays a mist high up in the
laboratory, walking backwards. As the disinfecting mist descends,
airborne particulates are trapped, carrying contaminants to the floor.
Other cultivators use ultraviolet lights which interfere with DNA
replication, though shadowed areas remain unprotected.

## Vector 3: The Media

Often the medium upon which a culture is grown becomes the source of
contamination. Insufficient sterilization is usually the cause. Standard
sterilization time for most liquid media is only 15-20 minutes at 15 psi
or 250 degrees F. However, this is far too brief for many of the
endospore-forming bacteria prevalent in additives currently employed by
cultivators. Stamets recommends at least 40 minutes at 15 psi for malt
extract or [[mushroom-agar-media-recipes]].

Sawdust needs a minimum of 2 hours at 15 psi for small batches. When
loading an autoclave with hundreds of tightly packed bags of supplemented
sawdust, this treatment schedule is insufficient. In the heat treatment
of bulk substrates, absolute sterilization is impractical and more
conceptual than achievable. The best one can hope is that contaminants
are reduced to a level not problematic within the two-week colonization
window.

A good practice is to leave a few samples from each sterilization cycle
uninoculated. These "blanks" serve as quality controls. If uninoculated
containers contaminate within 48-72 hours, this usually indicates
insufficient sterilization of media or containers.

## Vector 4: The Tools

All tools of the trade are included from the scalpel to the pressure
cooker to the media vessels. Insufficient sterilization of tools can be
a direct vector since contact with media is immediate. Flame-sterilizing
scalpels is the preferred method over topical disinfection with alcohol
or bleach.

Although the interior of a pressure cooker has been sterilized, the
outside has not. Contaminants can be easily picked up by the hands of
the person handling the pressure cooker and redistributed to the
immediate workstation. This is all the more reason to disinfect before
beginning transfers.

## Vector 5: The Inoculum

The inoculum is the tissue being transferred, whether this tissue is a
part of a living mushroom, mycelium from another petri dish, or spores.
Bacteria and molds can infect the mushroom tissue and be carried with it
every time a transfer is made. Isolation of the inoculum from the
mushroom mycelium can be frustrating, for many contaminant organisms grow
faster than the newly emerging mushroom mycelium. Cultivators must
constantly "run" or transfer their mycelium away from these rapidly
developing competitors.

## Vector 6: Mobile Contamination Units (MCUs)

Mobile Contamination Units are organisms that carry and spread
contaminants within the laboratory. These living macro-organisms act as
vehicles spreading contaminants from one site to another. They are
especially damaging to the laboratory environment because they are
difficult to isolate. Ants, flies, mites, and even children all qualify
as potential MCUs. Typically, a MCU carries not one contaminant, but
several.

Mites are the most difficult MCU to control. Their minute size, their
preference for fungi (both molds and mushroom mycelium) as food, and
their penchant for travel, make them a spawn manager's worst nightmare.
