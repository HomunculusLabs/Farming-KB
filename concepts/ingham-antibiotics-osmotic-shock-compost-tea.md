# Antibiotics and Osmotic Shock in Compost Tea

## Defining True Antibiotics

Antibiotics have a specific chemical action on the enzymatic or
membrane function of another organism. Even though the word
antibiotic, meaning against life, may suggest that anything that
inhibits another organism is an antibiotic, in fact, from a legal
and scientific point of view, we mean something much more specific.

An antibiotic must demonstrate bio-active compounds that act to
destroy membrane function or complex enzymes so the enzymes do not
function. If a material does not have this specific mechanism of
action, it is not an antibiotic in the pharmaceutical sense. See also [[fungal-antibiotics]] and [[selective-agents-fungal-isolation-antibiotics-fungicides]].

## Honey Is Not an Antibiotic

Honey is not an antibiotic in the sense meant by the pharmaceutical
industry. Honey, like any other material that at high concentration
can draw water away and thus kill things by simple osmotic shock,
is not an antibiotic as we mean antibiotic in the pharmaceutical
sense. If you want to claim antibiotic mechanisms for honey, then
salt would also be an antibiotic, as would most inorganic
fertilizers. See also [[jadam-antibiotic-soil-contamination]].

If honey contains materials that disrupt the enzymes involved in
respiration or other critical functions, proof of such materials
has yet to be demonstrated. Do not confuse osmotic shock effects
with pharmaceutical antibiotic interactions. It would be much
better not to move natural compounds into the world of EPA
registration as pesticides or inhibitory substances. See also [[aact-pathogen-safety-ingham]].

## How Osmotic Shock Works

Osmotic shock occurs when the concentration of dissolved substances
(salts, sugars) in the surrounding environment is so high that water
is pulled out of microbial cells, causing them to collapse and die.
This is a physical mechanism, not a chemical one. Salt, sugar,
molasses, honey, and many other common materials can all cause
osmotic shock at high enough concentrations. See [[ingham-molasses-types-brewing-effects]].

A salt is anything that pulls water away from cells, not just NaCl.
Many seaweeds are high in potassium, magnesium, or other cations
that can cause osmotic problems. The concentration of the material
is what determines whether osmotic shock will occur.

## Honey in Compost Tea

Honey helps bacteria, not fungi. It does not contain fungal foods
in any significant amount. When trying to grow just fungi, honey
is not appropriate. You need materials that are more specifically
geared to fungi, like fish hydrolysate or humic acids. See [[aact-fungal-dominant-tea]] and [[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham]].

I have not seen problems with honey negatively impacting beneficial
organisms in the tea at reasonable concentrations. There is impact
on neutral bacterial species and on pathogenic bacteria, but not
on the beneficial organisms. That is the other thing to recognize:
it is a good thing if the bad bacteria are harmed. You cannot rely
on total bacterial counts or plate count bacterial counts alone.
Some assessment of beneficial organisms is needed.

## Molasses and Osmotic Thresholds

As the concentration of sugar increases, fungi have a greater and
greater competitive edge over bacteria. This is because of osmotic
shock. As sugar concentration rises, fast-growing bacteria have a
harder time dealing with the fact that the high sugar concentration
holds water more and more tightly. This benefits beneficial fungi
and a few beneficial bacteria that have mechanisms for dealing with
high osmotic conditions.

Actinomycetes are not benefited by this kind of water restriction.
They have mechanisms for dealing with hot and dry conditions, which
is different from osmotic shock caused by high solute concentration.

## The Danger Zone

Somewhere above the 3% to 5% range of molasses concentration,
bacteria start to be suppressed by osmotic effects. The worst
concentrations to use are in the 0.5% to 3% range, because that is
where bacterial bloom will drop the oxygen down extremely rapidly. See [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-management]] and [[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea]].
At about 15% to 20% molasses, osmotic shock begins to exceed what
anything can tolerate.

## Gypsum and Salt Impacts

Gypsum can give you salt impacts through osmotic shock. Keep
application down to less than 100 pounds per acre and there should
be no problem. More than that and problems typically develop.
Gypsum helps improve flocculation for a short time, and then the
soil can turn hard because the biology was lost. The same principle
applies to any inorganic amendment: all inorganic amendments are
salts with osmotic shock potential. See [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]].

## Biologically Activated Materials

Anything biologically activated by having organisms turn inorganic
compounds into organic compounds under aerobic conditions will help
keep that nutrient from leaching below the rooting zone. The
osmotic shock capacity of the original salt is decreased because
the organisms have turned it into a wide diversity of organic
materials that do not have osmotic shock capacity. This is why
compost tea can safely deliver nutrients that would otherwise harm
soil biology if applied as raw inorganic salts. See [[compost-tea-aact]] and [[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham]].

## Practical Implications for Tea Brewing

The osmotic shock principle has direct practical implications for
compost tea formulation. When adding food resources, consider the
total dissolved solids concentration in the brew water. If your
source water already has high mineral content, you have less room
to add sugars and other dissolved foods before reaching osmotic
stress levels. Hard water, well water with high total dissolved
solids, or water treated with water softeners all contribute to
the total solute concentration that organisms must tolerate.

## Antibiotics in Manure and Compost

Manure from livestock treated with antibiotics presents a real
concern for composting. Pharmaceutical antibiotics have specific
chemical actions on microbial enzymes or membrane functions. These
compounds can suppress the beneficial organisms needed for proper
composting and can persist through the composting process. The
biggest problems in composting are strongylid de-wormer medicine,
followed by herbicide residues, insecticides, fungicides, and then
copper and sulfur. Antibiotics are generally easier to decompose
than persistent herbicides, as long as the system is not
overwhelmed with high concentrations. See also [[ingham-clopyralid-chlordane-compost-contamination]] and [[ingham-coli-compost-tea-food-safety]].

## Distinguishing Mechanisms of Action

It is important for compost tea practitioners to distinguish between
different mechanisms that can harm microorganisms. Osmotic shock is
a physical effect caused by high solute concentrations. True
antibiotics have specific chemical actions on enzymes or membranes.
Competition for resources is a biological interaction between
organisms. pH extremes create chemical stress. Each mechanism
requires a different management approach, and confusing them can
lead to ineffective solutions.
