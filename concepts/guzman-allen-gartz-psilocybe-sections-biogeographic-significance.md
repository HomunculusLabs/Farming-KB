# Psilocybe Intraspecific Sections and Their Biogeographic Significance

The genus Psilocybe, as treated by Guzmán (1983, 1995), is organized
into several intrageneric sections that carry profound biogeographic
meaning. These sections are not merely taxonomic groupings; they
reflect evolutionary histories, dispersal corridors, and ecological
preferences that map directly onto global [[holmgren-vegetation-patterns-biomass-fertility]]. The
Guzmán, Allen and Gartz (2000) worldwide analysis identified the
bluing guzman [[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-habitat-vegetation-associations]] sections biogeographic significance as the core of the neurotropic fungi, with
116 species distributed across at least nine named sections.

## The Bluing Sections as a Taxonomic Framework

Guzmán followed Singer's (1986) concept of Psilocybe, excluding
Section Chrysocystidiatae, and did not accept the broader concept of
Kühner and Romagnesi (1953) or Kühner (1980), which merged Psilocybe,
Hypholoma and Stropharia into a single genus. The bluing reaction, a
reliable indicator of psilocybin and related indole alkaloids, served
as the primary criterion for assigning species to neurotropic
sections. The bluing species of Psilocybe identified in the study
belong to sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae,
Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae and
Zapotecorum.

## Section Aztecorum: High-Altitude Specialists

Section Aztecorum contains some of the most remarkable altitudinal
specialists in the genus. Psilocybe aztecorum var. aztecorum grows
exclusively in subalpine and alpine habitats of the high mountains of
Mexico, reaching elevations of approximately 4,000 meters. This species
presents strong morphological and phylogenetic relationships with
[[stamets-psi-psilocybe-baeocystis-knobby-tops]] from the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] [[berry-growing]],
specifically Oregon, Washington and British Columbia, and with
[[psilocybe-quebecensis]] from Quebec, Canada.

All three species share a section-level affinity that suggests a
north-south dispersal corridor through mountainous terrain. The
presence of Section Aztecorum in both subalpine Mexico and the
temperate mountains of the Pacific Northwest illustrates how
similar ecological niches can maintain related species across vast
geographic distances. Psilocybe aztecorum var. bonetii, a second
variety, also occurs in Mexico and has been placed within this section
by Guzmán and other taxonomists.

## Section Mexicanae: The Transpacific Connection

Section Mexicanae provides some of the strongest evidence for
transpacific biogeographic relationships among neurotropic fungi.
Psilocybe mexicana, the type species of the section, occurs in Mexico
and Guatemala. Remarkably, [[psilocybe-samuiensis]] from Thailand and
Psilocybe makarorae from New Zealand are both closely related to
P. mexicana and belong to the same section.

This distribution pattern mirrors the observations of Horak (1983),
who documented similar relationships among agarics and boletes in
the South Pacific hemisphere. South American and Mesoamerican species
of these fungal groups are closely related to those of southeastern
Asia, specifically New Guinea, Indonesia and Australia. The Mexicanae
pattern suggests an ancient Gondwanan or transpacific dispersal route
that predates the breakup of continental landmasses or involved
long-distance spore dispersal across the Pacific.

## Section Zapotecorum: A Southern Hemisphere Dominant

Section Zapotecorum contains [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]], one of the most
widely distributed neurotropic species, ranging from Mexico through
Central and South America. The species was originally described from
Mexico but is now known from numerous countries including Colombia,
Brazil, Argentina, Chile, Venezuela, Bolivia, Ecuador, Peru and
Uruguay. Several varieties have been recognized, including P.
zapotecorum var. ramulosum from Brazil.
