# Backyard Livestock Integration

## The Return of Small Animals

Before World War II, the sound of clucking chickens
or the sight of rabbit cages was common in backyards
even in cities. On apartment rooftops, flocks of
pigeons cooed in specially built lofts and dovecotes.
Small livestock provided meat, eggs, feathers, fur,
and manure for gardens.

In the postwar era, small animals disappeared from
urban and suburban yards. It was easier to buy meat
and eggs at the store. Many towns passed laws
against keeping animals, especially roosters. But
the past few years have seen a resurgence of small
animals in backyards, as ecological gardeners
rediscover the multiple benefits these creatures
bring to the landscape.

## The Ecological Role of Animals

In the ecological garden, animals are not mere
pets or production units -- they are integral
components of the ecosystem. They perform critical
functions that connect the producer plants and the
decomposer [[soil-life]]:

- **Consumers**: They eat surplus and unusable
  fruit and foliage, prepping it via their
  digestive tracts for the decomposers
- **Fertilizer factories**: They convert garden
  waste and feed into rich manure
- **Tillers**: Chickens scratch and turn soil,
  rabbits burrow and aerate
- **Pest controllers**: Birds eat insects, snakes
  eat slugs, ducks consume snails
- **Nutrient importers**: They bring in nutrients
  from outside the garden through feed and forage

Hemenway emphasizes that animals will chow down on
surplus and unusable plant material, turning it into
work, more animals, and manure. They are the
consumers that bind producers and decomposers
together.

## Chicken Tractors

The chicken tractor is a bottomless pen on wheels,
perhaps built with 1-inch chicken wire (poultry
netting), roofed with a tarp or boards to protect
from rain and sun. It allows chickens to graze on
fresh ground each day while remaining protected
from predators.

### [[building-soil]] with Chickens

You can use a chicken tractor to build soil in
several ways:

- **[[sheet-mulching]] with chickens**: Leave the
  tractor in one spot, add kitchen scraps and
  weeds daily, along with 1 inch of mulch. The
  chickens work the material into the soil. When
  the mulch is about 4 inches deep, move the
  tractor to a new spot.

- **Cover crop processing**: Plant a cover crop
  of clover, vetch, and grains. Let it grow,
  then wheel the pen and chickens onto it. The
  birds eat the greenery, scratch it up, and
  manure the bed. One gardener rotates chicken
  tractors through each bed every three to four
  weeks.

- **Fallow bed renovation**: For very poor soil,
  leave the chicken tractor in one place for
  several weeks with the birds adding both
  nutrients and organic matter.

### Benefits of Chicken Tractors

- Chickens eat bugs, including many garden pests
- Their scratching aerates compacted soil
- Manure adds nitrogen and organic matter
- They convert kitchen scraps and weeds into eggs
- They clear ground for new plantings
- No need to buy and spread fertilizer

### Design Considerations

- Use on level ground; on slopes, chickens will
  concentrate their scratching at the low end
- Don't put chickens into the garden when crops
  are bearing, as poultry will happily eat
  ripening vegetables and fruit
- Supplement their diet with plants around the
  yard to reduce feed costs
- Provide adequate protection from predators

## Ducks in the Garden

Ducks offer some advantages over chickens for
certain garden situations:

- They enthusiastically eat slugs and snails
- They forage in wet areas where chickens won't go
- Their manure is excellent for garden fertility
- They can be integrated with pond systems

Hemenway describes a duck and koi pond system
where ducks serve multiple functions, processing
greywater and providing fertility for surrounding
plantings. Before a marsh was installed, ducks
wouldn't stay in the pond because oil from their
feathers caused them to sink. The constructed
wetland solved this problem.

## Rabbits

Rabbits are efficient converters of garden waste
and forage into meat and manure. Their droppings
are one of the best fertilizers available and can
be applied directly to plants without composting,
unlike most other animal manures.

Hemenway describes integrating rabbits with wcomposting-systemstems, where rabbit manure feeds
worms that in turn produce castings and serve as
chicken feed. This kind of multi-species integration
exemplifies the [[permaculture]] principle of each
element performing multiple functions.
