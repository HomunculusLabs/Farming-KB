# Greywater Management and Dry Toilets

In [[permaculture-design]], the responsible handling of human waste and domestic wastewater is a critical concern. [[bill-mollison]] emphasises that conventional approaches to sewage and greywater are not only wasteful of water but also introduce persistent pollutants into soils and waterways. A permaculture approach seeks to close nutrient loops, minimise water use, and design systems that treat waste on-site through biological processes rather than relying on centralised, energy-intensive treatment infrastructure.

## Dry Toilets

Dry toilets (composting or dehydrating toilets) are appropriate in a range of situations where conventional flush systems are unsuitable or undesirable:

- **Where no methane digester system is in use** — dry toilets allow direct composting of human waste without the need for water-based transport, eliminating the "flush and forget" paradigm.
- **Where sewage is not intended for use on plants** — if effluent is not being directed to agricultural or forest systems, dry separation at source avoids the creation of contaminated wastewater altogether.
- **Where soils do not suit septic tank systems** — shallow, clay-rich, impermeable, or waterlogged soils prevent effective percolation and treatment through conventional septic leach fields, making on-site aerobic composting the only viable option.
- **In cities experiencing critical water supply problems** — flush toilets are one of the largest single domestic water consumers, and eliminating them can significantly reduce municipal demand, easing pressure on over-stressed catchments and reservoirs.

Dry toilet systems separate urine and faeces at source, allowing each to be managed through appropriate biological pathways (typically aerobic composting or dehydration). This converts potential pollutants into stable, nutrient-rich soil amendments that can safely return organic matter and essential plant nutrients to productive landscapes.

## Handbasin-to-Toilet Water Recycling

A simple but highly effective design strategy is routing handbasin outflow directly into the toilet cistern. This single measure can save approximately **40% of domestic water misuse**, as the relatively clean rinse water from handwashing is perfectly adequate for flushing. It reduces total household water consumption without requiring complex treatment infrastructure and is one of the easiest retrofits available for existing homes.

The principle extends beyond the handbasin: any relatively clean greywater source (bath overflow, shower warm-up water) can potentially be cascaded to a lower-grade use before leaving the site. In permaculture, every drop of water should perform multiple functions before it is released.

## Analysis of Wastewater Pollutants

Domestic greywater — water from baths, showers, sinks, and laundry — carries a complex load of pollutants that must be understood before it can be safely applied to land. Unlike blackwater (toilet waste), greywater contains no faecal matter in theory, but in practice cross-contamination is common and pathogen loads must always be considered.

### Soap and Detergent Components

| Component | Concern |
|---|---|
| **Sodium and potassium salts** | Damage soil clays and plant leaves at concentrations above 1,000 ppm |
| **Polysulphates** | Contribute to soil structural degradation and nutrient imbalances |
| **Silicates** | Generally benign at low levels but accumulate over time in poorly drained soils |
| **Borates** | Harmful to plants at concentrations above 0.5 ppm — even trace amounts are significant |
| **Residual biocides** | DDT, Dieldrin, and Hexachlorophene residues persist from dairy cattle treatments, entering wastewater through milk processing and farm runoff |
| **Additives** | Synthetic resins, artificial scents, dyes, optical brighteners — designed to resist biological breakdown |

### Biological Pathogens

Greywater and blackwater contain faecal bacteria, viral pathogens, and worm eggs (helminths). These biological contaminants require either sufficient retention time in aerobic composting systems or careful land-application design with adequate soil depth and biological activity to ensure pathogen die-off before food crops are potentially exposed. A minimum of 30 days in a hot composting system is generally considered sufficient to destroy most pathogens, though specific time-temperature relationships vary by organism.

## Soil Organisms as Pollutant Processors

The good news is that **healthy soil ecologies can deal with most pollutants**. Diverse communities of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and earthworms break down organic compounds, sequester heavy metals, and outcompete pathogens. Mycorrhizal fungi in particular play a role in immobilising heavy metals within their hyphal networks, preventing plant uptake. However, certain chemical thresholds must not be exceeded, as even robust soil ecosystems have limits:

- **Sodium/potassium salts** above 1,000 ppm damage both soil clay structure (causing deflocculation and loss of permeability) and plant leaf tissues (causing marginal burn and necrosis).
- **Borates** above 0.5 ppm are toxic to many plants, particularly sensitive species and seedlings.
- **Persistent synthetic compounds** (biocides, brighteners, resins) may not degrade readily and should be intercepted before reaching productive soils.

## Design Strategies for Safe Greywater Use

### Route Complex Pollutants to Tree Systems

Rather than applying greywater directly to vegetable gardens, Mollison recommends directing it through **tree-based systems** (orchards, timberbelts, or bamboo). Deep-rooted perennial plants are far more tolerant of transient pollutant loads, have greater biological processing capacity in their extensive root zones, and produce non-edible (or less directly consumed) yields where residual contamination is a concern. A mulched tree system with active fungal communities provides one of the most effective passive greywater treatment systems available.

### Create Special Soaps

By formulating or sourcing biodegradable, low-sodium soaps and detergents free of borates, synthetic brighteners, and biocidal residues, households can dramatically reduce the pollutant load in their greywater. This is a foundational step in any domestic greywater recycling system. Simple soap-making from plant oils and lye, or sourcing genuinely ecological detergent products, eliminates the need for complex downstream treatment of avoidable contaminants.

### Plant Water Purification Species

Strategically planted **water plants** (reed beds, constructed wetlands with species like *Phragmites*, *Typha*, *Iris*) can remove excess nutrients and pollutants before greywater reaches soil systems. These biological filters use plant uptake, microbial activity in the root zone (the rhizosphere), and physical filtration to polish effluent. Reed bed systems are low-maintenance, self-regulating, and can treat substantial volumes of greywater while simultaneously providing habitat and biomass.

## Purification of Polluted Waters

Mollison identifies the **only long-term insurance for clean water as rigorous control of forested catchments**. Intact forest ecosystems act as massive, self-maintaining water treatment systems — filtering, buffering, and biologically processing water as it moves through the soil profile. Removing forest cover eliminates this service and exposes waterways to direct contamination. The message is clear: protect the source, not just the output.

### Problem Contaminants in Water Supplies

Modern water supplies commonly contain **30–40 additives**, and Mollison catalogues several categories of concern:

- **Turbidity** — suspended sediments that reduce light penetration and carry attached pollutants.
- **Bacterial and organic pollution** — including *E. coli* and other pathogens from sewage and agricultural runoff.
- **Metallic pollutants** — chromium, cadmium, lead, and mercury from industrial discharge and mining.
- **Biocides** — Aldrin, Dieldrin, 2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, dioxin, and PCBs from agricultural and industrial sources.
- **Excessive fertiliser** — nitrogen and phosphorus runoff causing eutrophication and algal blooms.
- **Acids** — from industrial emissions and acid rain, lowering pH and mobilising metals from sediments.

### Contaminant Interactions

Pollutants do not act in isolation; their interactions often create more severe problems than any single contaminant:

- **Acid rain dissolves metals** from soil and rock, increasing the mobility of aluminium, cadmium, and lead into waterways.
- **Sewage aids conversion** of inert metals into biologically active, bioavailable forms that enter food chains more readily.
- **DDT co-distils with water**, meaning it can travel long distances through evaporation and condensation cycles far from its point of application — a form of global redistribution.
- **Asbestos pipes** pose an additional threat to water quality in aging infrastructure, releasing fibres into supply systems.

### Conventional Treatment Issues

Municipal water treatment using **ferric and aluminium sulphate** as coagulants introduces its own problems. These chemicals create sludge containing concentrated contaminants, can leave residual metals in treated water, and do not remove many synthetic organic compounds. They address turbidity and some biological contaminants but are largely ineffective against dissolved biocides, heavy metals, and persistent organic pollutants. Biological treatment of contaminants — using wetlands, algae, and microbial communities — offers a more holistic and sustainable alternative.

## Conventional Treatment: The Technological Fix Problem

Municipal water treatment using **ferric and aluminium sulphate** as coagulants introduces its own problems. These chemicals create sludge containing concentrated contaminants, can leave residual metals in treated water, and do not remove many synthetic organic compounds. They address turbidity and some biological contaminants but are largely ineffective against dissolved biocides, heavy metals, and persistent organic pollutants.

As Mollison observes, this represents the endpoint of the **"technological fix"** mentality: pollution is "fixed" by further pollution. Adding chemical coagulants to water already contaminated with industrial and agricultural pollutants merely adds another layer of contamination. Biological treatment — using wetlands, algae, and microbial communities — offers a more holistic and sustainable alternative that works with natural processes rather than against them.

## The Aluminium Threat

A particularly concerning issue highlighted in the source is the role of **aluminium** in water treatment and its downstream health effects:

- **Acid rain leaches aluminium** from soils and rocks into waterways, now thought to be a major cause of tree dieback and lake acidification.
- **Aluminium sulphate** added during water treatment increases dissolved aluminium levels, especially as ambient pH drops due to acid rain.
- **Aluminium binds to proteins** in vegetables and meat during cooking, with cooking potentially increasing water-content metals by a factor of 5.
- **Cooking in aluminium pots** with acidic foods (tomatoes, vinegar, citrus) dramatically worsens aluminium leaching into food.
- **Asbestos cement pipes** in public water supply systems pose additional cancer risks (stomach and bladder), compounding the metallic contamination problem.

These findings underscore the importance of **preventing contamination at source** rather than relying on end-of-pipe treatment technologies that introduce their own hazards.

## Site-Specific Greywater Design

Mollison emphasises that **there is no blanket policy** for greywater management — only specific cases where understanding the composition of local soils, water supplies, and cleaning products leads to effective, site-appropriate design. Key considerations include:

- **Baseline water salinity** — if the water supply is already saline, even low concentrations of sodium from soaps can push soils past tolerance thresholds.
- **Soil type** — sandy soils drain rapidly but offer less biological processing time; clay soils provide longer contact but may accumulate pollutants.
- **Climate** — arid environments concentrate pollutants through evaporation, requiring more careful design than humid climates.
- **Crop sensitivity** — food crops for direct human consumption require the highest water quality; timber, fibre, and fodder crops tolerate lower quality.
- **Chemical inventory** — knowing exactly what chemicals enter the wastewater stream (and at what concentration) is the essential first step before any land application.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Reduce water use at source** — dry toilets, handbasin-to-toilet recycling, and efficient fixtures minimise the volume of wastewater generated.
2. **Control what enters wastewater** — biodegradable soaps and avoiding toxic household chemicals reduce treatment burden.
3. **Use biological treatment** — constructed wetlands, reed beds, and soil organisms rather than chemical coagulants and chlorination.
4. **Apply greywater to non-food systems first** — trees and deep-rooted perennials before vegetables and herbaceous crops.
5. **Protect forested catchments** — the ultimate long-term strategy for maintaining water quality at its source.
6. **Understand contaminant interactions** — design systems that account for synergistic effects between pollutants, not just individual concentrations.
7. **Design for site-specific conditions** — soil type, climate, baseline water quality, and intended end-use all shape the appropriate greywater system.
