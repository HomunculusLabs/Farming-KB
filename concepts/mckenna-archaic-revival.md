# The Archaic Revival

## We Are the Archaic

The world is a corporate, mindless structure of violence and exploitation that is moving with increasing speed toward self-destruction. We have lost contact with the Earth, with each other, and with ourselves. We have lost the sense of the sacred. We have lost the ability to use the ancient shamanic technologies of ecstasy.

This diagnosis reflects what McKenna saw as the central pathology of modern civilization: the severance of the human-nature relationship that characterized all pre-agricultural societies. The Neolithic Revolution, in McKenna's analysis, was not simply a transition from foraging to farming but a fundamental rupture in human consciousness — a closing of the doors of perception that had been opened by regular communal use of psychoactive plants. The [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-thesis]] develops this argument in full detail, tracing the consequences of this rupture through subsequent human history.

We must bring the Archaic forward with us. We must not romanticize the primitive or become New Age primitives; rather, we must integrate the Archaic with the modern, creating a neo-Archaic future. This is the Archaic Revival: the return to and modernization of the values of the partnership society, the recovery of the shamanic relationship to nature, and the restoration of the sacred dimension to everyday life.

## Nature as a Gaian Network

The Archaic Revival is a boomerang. It is the great unmentionable. It is the notion that nature is not mute, that nature is actually a Gaian network of information that can be tapped and used by human beings. The Archaic Revival is a call for the recovery of the shamanic perspective.

What has been happening over the last ten thousand years of human history is the slow process of forgetting, of unlearning, of losing our connection to the living world. The Archaic Revival is the beginning of the process of remembering. McKenna argues that this forgetting was not accidental but was actively promoted by the social structures that emerged with agriculture and urbanization — hierarchies that required the suppression of individual direct experience of the sacred in order to maintain social control through institutionalized religion and centralized authority. The [[mckenna-shamanism-and-the-lost-archaic-world]] narrative frames this as a loss of technologies of consciousness that once allowed humans to communicate directly with the natural world.

## What Must Be Done

McKenna outlines several principles for the Archaic Revival:

1. Re-establish a symbiotic relationship with the natural world and its plant intelligence
2. Recover the techniques of ecstasy — shamanic plant use, drumming, trance, dance
3. Dismantle the dominator model of social organization
4. End the suppression of psychedelic research and the demonization of plant medicines
5. Recognize the primacy of the feminine and the values of partnership
6. Create a new language adequate to the dimensions of the psychedelic experience
7. Face the possibility that the universe is far more complex and alive than materialist science allows

## The Imagination as a Tool

The Archaic Revival is a reminder that imagination is not mere fantasy but a tool for the remodeling of experience. The boundary between "reality" and "imagination" is a construct of the dominator worldview. The shamanic perspective dissolves this boundary, revealing a universe in which mind and matter, self and other, are intimately interconnected.

McKenna drew on the work of psychologist Carl Jung to argue that what Western culture dismisses as "imagination" is actually a mode of perception that gives access to archetypal realities — patterns of meaning that exist independently of any individual consciousness. The shamanic journey, whether induced by psychoactive plants, drumming, fasting, or sensory deprivation, is a technology for accessing this imaginal realm with sufficient clarity and intentionality to bring back useful information. The [[mckenna-techniques-of-ecstasy]] catalog describes these methods as "technologies of the sacred" — reproducible, teachable practices for inducing altered states that serve specific cognitive and healing purposes.

The world is not made of atoms; it is made of stories, of words, of the dreams of the Great Goddess and the songs of her children. We are the stories the Earth tells herself. When we remember this, we become capable of telling a new story — a story of partnership, of sustainability, of reverence for all living things. This narrative reframing is itself a form of Archaic Revival: recovering the understanding, universal in indigenous cultures, that the world is a living, communicative being rather than a collection of inert objects.

## The Next Step

The Archaic Revival is not a rejection of technology or a call to return to the Stone Age. It is a call for the integration of shamanic wisdom with modern knowledge. The goal is a neo-Archaic society that preserves the best of both worlds — the deep connection to nature and community of the partnership model, and the scientific understanding and technological capability of the modern world.

The next step in the adventure of self-understanding can begin only when we take note of our innate and legitimate need for an environment rich in mental states that are induced through an act of will. We can initiate this process by revisioning our origins and by reopening the dialogue with the Other — the vegetable mind, the Gaian intelligence, the hyperspace dimension that has been waiting for us all along.

## A Hypothesis in Need of Testing

McKenna concludes with a modest but radical proposal: that the hallucinogenic indoles, unstudied and legally suppressed, should be recognized as agents of evolutionary change — biochemical agents whose ultimate impact is not on the direct experience of the individual but on the genetic constitution of the species. If the notion that human consciousness emerged out of indole-mediated synergy of neurodevelopment could be proven, then our image of ourselves, our relationship to nature, and the present dilemma over drug use in society would change.

- The stoned ape hypothesis, while controversial, raises important questions about the role of psychoactive substances in human cognitive development
- McKenna's critique of dominator culture draws on both anthropological evidence and contemporary political analysis
- The [[psychedelic-mushroom-history]] provides a broad context for understanding McKenna's specific claims

## Significance in Psychedelic Thought

McKenna's work represents one of the most ambitious attempts to construct a comprehensive theory of the role of psychoactive plants in human history and culture. While academic anthropologists have been skeptical of many specific claims, his ideas have been enormously influential in psychedelic culture and continue to inspire research into the relationship between psychoactive substances and human consciousness. The concept of an Archaic Revival — the recovery of ancient plant-based wisdom — has resonated with the growing interest in ethnobotany, ecological consciousness, and the therapeutic potential of psychedelic substances documented in [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]].

The cultural impact of McKenna's Archaic Revival concept extends beyond academic and scientific circles into art, music, and popular culture. The electronic music festival culture that emerged in the 1990s and 2000s frequently drew on McKenna's ideas, creating temporary intentional communities that embodied, however imperfectly, the neo-Archaic values he advocated. The Burning Man festival, with its emphasis on gift economy, temporary community, and altered states of consciousness, can be read as a large-scale experimental Archaic Revival — an attempt to create, however briefly, a partnership-society enclave within the dominant dominator culture.

In the realm of academia, the growing field of psychedelic studies has begun to engage seriously with questions McKenna raised decades ago. Research into the therapeutic potential of psilocybin, ayahuasca, and peyote — while conducted within a Western medical framework — has partially vindicated McKenna's insistence that these substances have profound implications for human well-being and cultural organization. The [[consciousness-and-psychedelics]] research program, though more cautious in its claims than McKenna, shares his conviction that psychedelic experiences can catalyze fundamental shifts in worldview and behavior.

## Historical Precedents for the Archaic Revival

McKenna was not the first to articulate the idea that modern humanity has lost something vital from its prehistoric relationship with the natural world. Riane Eisler's "The Chalice and the Blade" (1987) independently developed a strikingly similar thesis, distinguishing between "partnership" and "dominator" models of social organization and tracing the shift from egalitarian, goddess-worshipping Neolithic cultures to patriarchal warrior societies. Julian Jaynes' "The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind" (1976) proposed that human consciousness itself underwent a radical transformation in the second millennium BCE, moving from a hallucinatory, auditory mode of cognition to the introspective, self-aware mode we consider normal today. McKenna drew on both of these frameworks, adding his own emphasis on the role of psychoactive plants as catalysts in these transitions.

The Romantic movement of the 18th and 19th centuries represents an earlier Archaic Revival impulse. Wordsworth, Blake, and the German Naturphilosophen all sought to recover a mode of knowing grounded in direct experience of nature rather than abstract rationalism. The Transcendentalists — Emerson, Thoreau, and Whitman — extended this into an American context, advocating for direct experiential knowledge of the natural world. McKenna positioned his own work as a continuation of this tradition, updated with modern understanding of neurochemistry, ecology, and shamanic practice from [[entheogenic-symbiosis-and-gaian-mind]].

## The Gaian Hypothesis and Planetary Intelligence

The Archaic Revival draws heavily on the Gaia hypothesis formulated by James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis in the 1970s — the idea that Earth's biosphere functions as a self-regulating, homeostatic system analogous to a living organism. McKenna extended this beyond its original scientific framing into a more radical proposition: that the biosphere is not merely self-regulating but self-aware, constituting a form of planetary intelligence accessible through altered states of consciousness.

In this framework, the mycelial networks that connect forest trees — described in [[mycelium-network-intelligence]] — become a literal manifestation of the Gaian information network McKenna describes. The underground connections between fungal organisms and plant roots mirror the philosophical connections McKenna posits between individual human consciousness and the broader mind of nature. The [[entheogenic-ceremony-preparation]] practices of indigenous shamans, in McKenna's view, represent technologies for interfacing with this planetary intelligence.

## Psychedelics as Catalytic Agents of Cultural Change

McKenna argued that psychoactive compounds have served as catalysts for major transitions in human cultural evolution throughout history. The Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient Greece, which centered on the consumption of a psychoactive kykeon brew, were for McKenna a prime example of institutionalized Archaic Revival — a formal structure through which Greek civilization periodically renewed its connection to the vegetal mind. The degradation and eventual suppression of these mysteries coincided, in McKenna's narrative, with the increasing dominance of the dominator model in Western civilization.

The rediscovery of psychedelics in the mid-20th century represented, for McKenna, a spontaneous eruption of Archaic Revival energy into modern Western culture. The fact that this rediscovery coincided with other movements — environmentalism, feminism, civil rights, anti-nuclear activism — was not coincidental but evidence that the impulse toward partnership society is deeply rooted and periodically reasserts itself. The suppression of psychedelic research after the late 1960s was, in this analysis, a reaction by dominator institutions against a genuine threat to their authority, as explored in [[mckenna-drug-war-and-human-freedom]].

## Practical Dimensions of the Archaic Revival

While much of McKenna's Archaic Revival framework is philosophical and speculative, it implies concrete practices for individuals and communities. These include the cultivation of direct experiential knowledge of nature through activities such as wild mushroom foraging, organic gardening, and wilderness immersion. The practice of [[mushroom-cultivation-for-gardeners]] as described by Paul Stamets exemplifies the kind of re-engagement with fungal intelligence that the Archaic Revival advocates.

The revival of interest in traditional plant medicines — ayahuasca, psilocybin mushrooms, peyote, iboga — represents perhaps the most direct manifestation of the Archaic Revival in contemporary culture. Clinical research into psychedelic-assisted therapy, while conducted within a Western medical framework, nonetheless represents a partial legitimation of the shamanic plant knowledge that McKenna championed. The growing [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]] field, whatever its differences from McKenna's approach, shares his conviction that these substances have the capacity to fundamentally alter human consciousness and social organization.
