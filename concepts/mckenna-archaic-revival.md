# The Archaic Revival

## We Are the Archaic

The world is a corporate, mindless structure of violence and exploitation that is moving with increasing speed toward self-destruction. We have lost contact with the Earth, with each other, and with ourselves. We have lost the sense of the sacred. We have lost the ability to use the ancient shamanic technologies of ecstasy.

This diagnosis reflects what McKenna saw as the central pathology of modern civilization: the severance of the human-nature relationship that characterized all pre-agricultural societies. The Neolithic Revolution, in McKenna's analysis, was not simply a transition from foraging to farming but a fundamental rupture in human consciousness — a closing of the doors of perception that had been opened by regular communal use of psychoactive plants. The [[mckenna-shamanism-and-the-lost-archaic-world]] narrative frames this as a loss of technologies of consciousness that once allowed humans to communicate directly with the natural world.

## What Must Be Done

McKenna outlines several principles for the Archaic Revival:

1. Re-establish a symbiotic relationship with the natural world and its plant intelligence
2. Recover the techniques of ecstasy — shamanic plant use, drumming, trance, dance
3. Dismantle the dominator model of social organization
4. End the suppression of psychedelic research and the demonization of plant medicines
5. Recognize the primacy of the feminine and the values of partnership
6. Create a new language adequate to the dimensions of the psychedelic experience
7. Face the possibility that the universe is far more complex and alive than materialist science allows

## The Imagination as a Tool

The Archaic Revival is a reminder that imagination is not mere fantasy but a tool for the remodeling of experience. The boundary between "reality" and "imagination" is a construct of the dominator worldview. The shamanic perspective dissolves this boundary, revealing a universe in which mind and matter, self and other, are intimately interconnected.

McKenna drew on the work of psychologist Carl Jung to argue that what Western culture dismisses as "imagination" is actually a mode of perception that gives access to archetypal realities — patterns of meaning that exist independently of any individual consciousness. The shamanic journey, whether induced by psychoactive plants, drumming, fasting, or sensory deprivation, is a technology for accessing this imaginal realm with sufficient clarity and intentionality to bring back useful information. ## Key Points

- McKenna's analysis connects psychoactive plant use to fundamental patterns in human cultural evolution
- The stoned ape hypothesis, while controversial, raises important questions about the role of psychoactive substances in human cognitive development
- McKenna's critique of dominator culture draws on both anthropological evidence and contemporary political analysis

The cultural impact of McKenna's Archaic Revival concept extends beyond academic and scientific circles into art, music, and popular culture. The electronic music festival culture that emerged in the 1990s and 2000s frequently drew on McKenna's ideas, creating temporary intentional communities that embodied, however imperfectly, the neo-Archaic values he advocated. The Burning Man festival, with its emphasis on gift economy, temporary community, and altered states of consciousness, can be read as a large-scale experimental Archaic Revival — an attempt to create, however briefly, a partnership-society enclave within the dominant dominator culture.

In the realm of academia, the growing field of psychedelic studies has begun to engage seriously with questions McKenna raised decades ago. Research into the therapeutic potential of psilocybin, ayahuasca, and peyote — while conducted within a Western medical framework — has partially vindicated McKenna's insistence that these substances have profound implications for human well-being and cultural organization. ## Practical Dimensions of the Archaic Revival

While much of McKenna's Archaic Revival framework is philosophical and speculative, it implies concrete practices for individuals and communities. These include the cultivation of direct experiential knowledge of nature through activities such as wild mushroom foraging, organic gardening, and wilderness immersion. The practice of [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]] field, whatever its differences from McKenna's approach, shares his conviction that these substances have the capacity to fundamentally alter human consciousness and social organization.
