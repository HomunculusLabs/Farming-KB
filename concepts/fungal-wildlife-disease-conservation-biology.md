# Fungal Wildlife Diseases and Conservation Biology

The intersection of fungal diseases and conservation biology represents a
critical but often underappreciated dimension of biodiversity research.
Fungal pathogens of wildlife have been recognized as important drivers of
population declines in several vertebrate groups, most notably amphibians
and bats, and understanding these interactions is essential for effective
conservation planning.

## Fungi at the Conservation Interface

Conservation-oriented ecology has traditionally focused on what is
perceived as natural, with nature viewed as an ideal biological realm
existing beyond the human sphere. However, this boundary is artificial.
Natural and anthropogenically influenced ecologies intergrade in studies
of broad-scale habitat changes, species introductions, and the effects of
agricultural, forestry, and conservation practices on ecosystems.
Similarly, the medical and nonmedical arenas overlap in studies of
environmentally acquired diseases that colonize humans, domestic animals,
and wildlife.

Fungal diseases of wild vertebrates occupy a particularly important
position at this intersection because:

- Many fungal pathogens of wildlife have environmental reservoirs
  (saprobic life stages in soil or water)
- Disease agents participate in regulating wild animal population
  densities, influencing predator-prey dynamics and availability
- Enumeration of such agents is directly salient to conservation biology
- Pathogens often come from well-known fungal groups whose ecological
  roles extend far beyond pathogenicity

## Ecological Sources of Fungal Diseases

The proportion of mycotic diseases of humans and animals that are
regularly communicable is small. Most mycotic diseases are acquired from
non-animal sources in the environment, and most disease agents have other
saprobic ecological roles through which they exert their main ecological
impact.

[[aspergillus-fumigatus]], for example, although capable of causing disease
in heavily exposed or immunocompromised hosts, is primarily a fungus of
compost such as leaf litter or accumulated bird or bat dung. The
environmental distributions of many such facultative pathogens are
primarily elucidated through biomedical isolations rather than conventional
soil and plant material sampling.

Species such as Scedosporium prolificans, Cladophialophora bantiana,
and Acremonium falciforme, along with genera including Blastomyces,
Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Fonsecaea, and Onychocola, are examples of
fungal saprobes whose distributions have been mapped primarily through
clinical and veterinary isolations.

## Amphibian Chytridiomycosis

[[batrachochytrium-dendrobatidis]] (Bd) is a chytridiomycete pathogen of
amphibians that has recently spread to several continents according to
molecular evidence. This pathogen has been implicated as a major driver
of global amphibian declines and represents one of the most significant
conservation challenges posed by a fungal disease agent.

The discovery and characterization of Bd underscored several important
points:

- [[zoosporic-fungi]] in freshwater habitats include not only saprobes and
  algal parasites but also significant vertebrate pathogens
- Molecular evidence (Morehouse et al. 2000) was critical in
  demonstrating the recent intercontinental spread of this pathogen
- The disease highlights the vulnerability of ectothermic vertebrates
  with permeable skin to fungal infection
- Conservation strategies must incorporate disease surveillance as a
  standard component of amphibian monitoring programs

Cryopreservation of Bd and other chytridiomycetes has been developed to
support research into this emerging disease (Boyle et al. 2003).

## Fungal Diseases in Aquatic Animals

The fungi associated with aquatic animals represent a largely
underexplored component of biodiversity. Freshwater habitats have
disappeared at a rapid rate, and many continue to be altered by agrarian
practices, land development, and pollution. Documenting the fungal
species present in existing aquatic habitats is important so that the
effects of habitat alterations on species composition can be detected.

Pathogenic fungi from aquatic animals have been documented in databases
maintained at several mycological herbaria. Environment Canada's
Biotechnology Section has developed a database covering approximately 209
fungal species from aquatic animals and plants worldwide. However, much
of the relevant information remains scattered across the scientific
literature, making it difficult for researchers and conservation
practitioners to access.

## Implications for Conservation Practice

Several key recommendations emerge from considering fungal diseases
within conservation frameworks:

1. **Baseline surveys**: Any well-rounded survey of biodiversity in an
   ecosystem should include data from local medical and veterinary
   authorities on pathogens and their environmental prevalence.

2. **Habitat preservation**: Efforts for the preservation of fungi with
   narrow host ranges should be directed toward conservation of habitats
   that are threatened with extinction, especially those supporting
   threatened animal hosts.

3. **Disease surveillance**: Monitoring programs for wildlife should
   incorporate fungal pathogen screening, particularly in habitats
   experiencing environmental stress.

4. **Database development**: Comprehensive databases linking fungal
   species, host animals, geographic distributions, and relevant
   literature are essential for conservation planning.

5. **Taxonomic training**: Increased capacity for identifying and
   characterizing pathogenic fungi from wildlife is needed to support
   conservation objectives.

## The Convention on Biological Diversity Context

The United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity, signed by 153
nations in 1992, stands to preserve ecological, genetic, social,
economic, scientific, cultural, recreational, and aesthetic values of
biological diversity. The implications of this convention for
fungal-wildlife disease research are immense, particularly as the
interactions between fungi and their animal hosts remain poorly
understood.
