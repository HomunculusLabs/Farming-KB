# Fungal Biodiversity in Built Environments and Indoor Mycobiomes

## Overview

Built environments, including homes, offices, hospitals, schools, and
industrial facilities, harbor distinct fungal communities shaped by
human activity, building materials, ventilation systems, and moisture
dynamics. The indoor mycobiome has emerged as a significant area of
fungal biodiversity research, revealing that human-constructed spaces
support hundreds of fungal species, many of which are poorly
characterized and some of which have direct implications for human
health and building integrity.

## Sources of Indoor Fungi

### Outdoor-to-Indoor Dispersal

The majority of indoor fungal spores originate from outdoor sources.
Airborne spores of Cladosporium, Alternaria, Penicillium, and
Aspergillus species enter buildings through ventilation systems, open
windows, doors, and on the bodies and clothing of occupants. Indoor
communities generally reflect regional outdoor fungal assemblages,
filtered and concentrated by building envelopes.

### Building Materials as Substrates

Construction materials including drywall, wood, insulation, ceiling
tiles, and carpeting serve as growth substrates when moisture is
available. Cellulose-rich materials like drywall paper and wood fiber
insulation are particularly susceptible to colonization by
Chaetomium, Stachybotrys, Trichoderma, and diverse xerophilic
Ascomycota. The fungal community that develops depends on material
composition, water activity, temperature, and nutrient availability.

### Human-Associated Fungi

Human skin, respiratory tracts, and clothing carry Malassezia,
Candida, and other yeasts that contribute to indoor fungal diversity.
Occupant density and activity patterns influence the composition of
human-derived indoor fungi, with higher diversity typically observed
in spaces with more occupants and greater foot traffic.

## Spatial and Temporal Patterns

### Room-Level Variation

Different rooms within the same building support distinct fungal
communities. Bathrooms and kitchens, with elevated humidity, harbor
more hydrophilic species including Aureobasidium pullulans, Exophiala
species, and various black yeasts. Bedrooms and living rooms tend to
be dominated by xerophilic Penicillium and Wallemia species that
tolerate lower water activity. Basements and crawl spaces develop
unique assemblages influenced by soil contact, groundwater, and
limited air exchange.

### Seasonal Dynamics

Indoor fungal communities fluctuate seasonally, tracking outdoor spore
production cycles. Cladosporium peaks in summer and fall, while
Penicillium and Aspergillus are more abundant in winter when heating
systems circulate indoor air. In temperate climates, the outdoor-to-
indoor fungal ratio can vary by an order of magnitude between summer
and winter months.

### Building Age and Design

Older buildings tend to harbor more diverse fungal communities than
new construction, partly due to accumulated moisture damage and
established microbial reservoirs in materials. Modern energy-efficient
buildings with tight envelopes may trap moisture and create localized
microenvironments favorable to fungal growth, sometimes resulting in
higher concentrations of a few opportunistic species rather than
diverse communities.

## Health-Relevant Indoor Fungi

### Allergenic Species

Indoor exposure to fungal spores and fragments is a major trigger of
allergic rhinitis, asthma, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis. Alternaria
alternata, Cladosporium herbarum, and Aspergillus fumigatus are among
the most clinically significant indoor allergens. Exposure risk depends
on spore concentrations, particle size (which determines respiratory
deposition), and individual sensitivity.

### Opportunistic Pathogens

Immunocompromised individuals are susceptible to invasive infections
by Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus flavus, and agents of
mucormycosis (Rhizopus, Mucor, Lichtheimia) that may proliferate in
hospital environments. Nosocomial fungal outbreaks have been traced to
construction activity, contaminated ventilation systems, and water
damage in healthcare facilities.

### Mycotoxin Producers

Stachybotrys chartarum, Aspergillus versicolor, and certain
Penicillium species produce mycotoxins (satratoxins, sterigmatocystin,
ochratoxin A) on damp building materials. The health significance of
inhaled mycotoxins at typical indoor concentrations remains debated,
but dampness and mold exposure are consistently associated with
respiratory symptoms and building-related illness.

## Monitoring and Assessment Methods

### Air Sampling

Viable spore sampling using impactors (Andersen, SAS) and non-viable
particle collection (spore traps, filter cassettes) are standard
methods for indoor fungal assessment. High-volume air sampling captures
temporal variability, while settled dust collection integrates exposure
over longer periods. DNA-based methods including qPCR and metabarcoding
of air and dust samples are increasingly supplementing or replacing
culture-based approaches.

### Surface Sampling

Tape lifts, swabs, and contact plates assess fungal colonization of
building surfaces. These methods are particularly useful for mapping
moisture damage, evaluating remediation effectiveness, and identifying
hidden growth behind walls or under flooring.

## Indoor Mycobiome and the Unknown Fungi Problem

Metabarcoding surveys of indoor environments consistently reveal a
substantial proportion of sequences that cannot be assigned to described
species. Many of these dark taxa are Basidiomycota yeasts and
filamentous Ascomycota that do not grow on standard culture media. The
indoor environment thus represents an underappreciated reservoir of
fungal biodiversity, with novel species potentially occurring in close
proximity to human populations. Understanding this hidden diversity is
critical for accurate exposure assessment and for anticipating emerging
indoor fungal health risks.
