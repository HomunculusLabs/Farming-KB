## Insect and Disease Management in the Organic Garden

## Overview

Steve Solomon's approach to insect and disease management in *Gardening When It Counts* is fundamentally different from conventional pest control. Rather than maintaining an arsenal of pesticides (even organic ones) to be deployed when problems appear, Solomon argues that most insect and disease problems are symptoms of deeper issues: poor nutrition, inappropriate growing conditions, or bad weather stress. The primary defense against pests is growing healthy plants in healthy soil, with intervention used only as a last resort.

## The Nutritional Basis of Pest Resistance

Solomon's central thesis on pest management is that insects and diseases primarily attack unhealthy plants. This concept, supported by the work of soil scientists like William Albrecht and N.S. Krasil'nikov, holds that:

- Plants grown with complete nutrition develop stronger cell walls that resist fungal penetration and insect feeding
- Adequate mineral nutrition supports the plant's own immune responses, including the production of defensive compounds
- Nutrient-deficient plants emit chemical signals that attract herbivorous insects
- Over-fertilized plants (particularly with excess nitrogen) produce soft, succulent growth that is highly attractive to pests

The implication is that the first and most important step in pest management is ensuring complete, balanced nutrition through COF application, proper soil preparation, and appropriate watering. Gardeners who invest in soil health spend far less time and money fighting pests.

## Weather-Related Vulnerability

Bad weather is a major contributor to pest and disease problems. Solomon notes that:

- Prolonged cool, wet weather creates ideal conditions for fungal diseases like blight, mildew, and damping off
- Drought stress weakens plants and makes them more susceptible to both insects and diseases
- Temperature extremes (late frosts, heat waves) cause physiological stress that compromises plant defenses
- Humidity combined with poor air circulation promotes mold and fungal growth

Many pest outbreaks follow weather events rather than cultural mistakes. Understanding this helps gardeners respond appropriately—sometimes the best action is simply to wait for conditions to improve rather than applying unnecessary treatments.

## Specific Insect Pests

### Aphids

Aphids (plant lice) are small sap-sucking insects that cluster on new growth and the undersides of leaves. They reproduce rapidly and can quickly overwhelm stressed plants. Remedies include:

- **Insecticidal soap**: A spray of diluted liquid soap (not detergent) disrupts aphid cell membranes
- **Strong water spray**: A jet of water dislodges aphids from plants; repeat every few days
- **Lady beetles and lacewings**: Natural predators that can be attracted or purchased and released
- **Foliar feeding**: Spraying liquid kelp or compost tea on leaves strengthens plant defenses

### Cabbage Root Maggot

Cabbage root maggots are the larvae of a small fly that lays eggs near the base of brassica plants. The maggots tunnel into roots, stunting or killing plants. Solomon references several remedies:

- **Collars**: Physical barriers (cardboard or tar paper circles) placed around the base of transplants prevent egg-laying
- **Resistant varieties**: Some cabbage family varieties show partial resistance
- **Timing**: Avoid planting during peak egg-laying periods
- **Crop rotation**: Moving brassica crops to different areas each year breaks the pest cycle

Blair Adams is referenced for additional approaches to cabbage maggot control.

### Colorado Potato Beetle

Colorado potato beetles are distinctive yellow-and-black striped insects that defoliate potato plants and can also attack eggplant and other nightshades. Control methods include:

- **Hand picking**: Regular inspection and removal of adults and egg clusters (orange masses on leaf undersides)
- **Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis)**: A biological insecticide specific to beetle larvae
- **Row covers**: Physical barriers that prevent adult beetles from reaching plants
- **Crop rotation**: Moving potato crops prevents overwintering beetles from finding host plants

### Cucumber Beetles

Both striped and spotted cucumber beetles attack cucurbits (cucumbers, squash, melons). They damage plants directly through feeding and indirectly by transmitting bacterial wilt disease. Management approaches include:

- **Row covers**: Effective when applied before beetles emerge, but must be removed during flowering to allow pollination
- **Kaolin clay spray**: A film of refined clay that deters feeding
- **Trap crops**: Planting attractive sacrificial plants to draw beetles away from main crops
- **Companion planting**: Radishes and nasturtiums are sometimes used as deterrents

### Flea Beetles

Flea beetles are tiny jumping insects that create characteristic shot-hole damage on leaves, particularly on brassicas and eggplant. Young transplants are most vulnerable. Controls include:

- **Row covers**: Very effective against these small insects
- **White sticky traps**: Yellow or white traps attract and capture adult beetles
- **Healthy transplants**: Larger, well-established plants tolerate moderate flea beetle damage

### Corn Earworm

Corn earworm larvae bore into corn ears, tomato fruits, and other vegetables. The most practical control is timing—planting corn early enough that silking occurs before peak moth flight. Bt applied to silks can also provide some control.

### Squash Bugs and Vine Borers

Squash bugs are flat, shield-shaped insects that suck sap from cucurbit leaves, causing wilting and plant death. Vine borers are the larvae of a moth that bores into squash stems, disrupting water and nutrient transport. Stinkbugs are related pests that feed on a wide range of vegetables.

Management includes:
- **Physical destruction**: Hand-picking eggs (bronze-colored clusters on leaf undersides for squash bugs) and crushing them
- **Row covers**: Effective until flowering begins
- **Resistant varieties**: Some squash varieties show tolerance
- **Crop cleanup**: Removing plant debris at season's end eliminates overwintering sites

### Ants

Ants themselves rarely damage plants directly but may indicate the presence of aphids (which they farm for honeydew). They can also disturb seedbeds and damage roots by tunneling. Managing aphid populations usually resolves associated ant problems.

### Weevils

Weevils that attack stored seeds (particularly beans) are a post-harvest pest rather than a garden pest. Proper seed storage—cool, dry, and in airtight containers—prevents weevil damage.

### Symphalans

Symphalans are small soil-dwelling arthropods that feed on plant roots, particularly in soils high in organic matter. They are most problematic in recently converted pasture or forest soils. Management is difficult, but reducing excessive organic matter applications and allowing soil to settle before planting can help.

### Worms

Various worm species can be garden pests:
- **Cutworms**: Sever seedlings at ground level; collars around transplants provide protection
- **Cabbage worms/cabbageworms**: Larvae of cabbage white butterflies; Bt and hand-picking are effective controls
- **Earworms**: See corn earworm above

## Fungal Diseases

### Blight

Blight (both early and late blight) affects tomatoes, potatoes, and other members of the nightshade family. Late blight, caused by *Phytophthora infestans*, can destroy entire crops in days during cool, wet weather. Prevention includes:
- Good air circulation through proper spacing
- Avoiding overhead watering that wets foliage
- Removing and destroying infected plant material
- Fungicides (copper-based or biological) as a preventive measure in high-risk conditions

### Mildew

Powdery mildew and downy mildew affect a wide range of vegetables including cucurbits, peas, brassicas, and alliums. Mildew thrives in humid conditions with poor air circulation. Solomon references kelp teas and compost teas as foliar sprays that can suppress mildew by supporting beneficial leaf microorganisms.

### Damping Off

Damping off is a fungal disease that kills young seedlings, causing them to collapse at the soil line. It is most common in:
- Overly wet seedbeds
- Sterile soil mixes lacking beneficial microorganisms
- Crowded plantings with poor air circulation

Prevention through proper watering, using sphagnum moss in seed-starting mixes, and avoiding overwatering is more effective than treatment.

### Molds

Various molds can affect vegetables in storage and in the field. Proper curing (especially for potatoes, onions, and sweet potatoes) and adequate ventilation during storage prevent most mold problems.
