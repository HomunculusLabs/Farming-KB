# Lichen Biology

Lichens represent one of the most successful [[fungal-symbiosis-types]] on Earth, enabling fungi to colonize some of the harshest terrestrial environments. lichens|Lichen-forming fungi are a polyphyletic group of nutritional specialists that derive fixed carbon from populations of living cyanobacteria and/or green algal cells. Every fifth fungus (approximately 14,000 species), or every second ascomycete, is a lichen.

## Lichen Symbiosis Structure

### The Partners

Species names of lichens refer to the fungal partner (mycobiont), while the photoautotrophic symbionts (photobionts) have their own names and phylogenies. Most lichen-forming fungi are physiologically facultatively biotrophic but occur in nature almost exclusively in the symbiotic state. The fungal partner usually amounts to more than 80% of thalline biomass.

### Thallus Architecture

The majority of lichen-forming fungi form crustose, often quite inconspicuous thalli on or within the substratum. About 25% of lichen mycobionts differentiate morphologically and anatomically complex three-dimensional thalli -- either shrubby, leaf- or band-shaped, erect or pendulous -- resulting from remarkable hyphal polymorphism.

Morphologically complex lichen thalli function as sophisticated culturing chambers built by the fungal partner for populations of minute photobiont cells. The main building blocks are:
- **Cortical layers**: Tissue-like conglutinate pseudoparenchyma providing mechanical stability
- **Medullary layer**: Loosely interwoven plectenchyma (gas-filled zones with hydrophobic hyphal surfaces)
- **Algal layer**: Where photobiont cells are positioned by the fungal partner for optimal photosynthesis

### Hydrophobin-Derived Surface Hydrophobicity

Wall surface hydrophobicity in lichen thalli is generated by class 1 hydrophobin proteins produced by the fungal partner. These form semicrystalline rodlet layers on hyphal surfaces. Mycobiont-derived medullary secondary compounds of lichen-forming ascomycetes crystallize on and within this rodlet layer, enhancing hydrophobicity and channelling the flow of solutes during wetting and drying cycles.

## Photobiont Types

Approximately 85% of lichen-forming ascomycetes have green algal photobionts (chlorolichens), 10% have cyanobacterial photobionts (cyanolichens), and about 4% have both simultaneously. In triple symbioses (cephalodiate lichens), a green algal partner provides fixed carbon while a diazotrophic cyanobacterial partner delivers fixed nitrogen. The fungal partner creates microaerobic conditions around the cyanobacterial colony, enhancing nitrogen fixation.

### Chlorolichens vs Cyanolichens

Drought-stressed chlorolichens with Trebouxia photobionts (symbionts of over 50% of lichen-forming ascomycetes) can rehydrate and re-establish partial metabolic activities with water vapour alone. Cyanolichens require liquid water for full recovery. This difference explains their contrasting distributions:
- Chlorolichens with Trebouxia dominate in arid, hot or cold ecosystems
- Cyanolichens with internally stratified thalli (Peltigera, Sticta, Nephroma) are abundant in moist habitats
- Gelatinous cyanolichens (Collema spp.) dominate soil crust communities in arid ecosystems with erratic rainfall

## Water Relations and Stress Tolerance

### Poikilohydry

The key to understanding the impressive environmental stress tolerance of lichen-forming fungi lies in their [[fungal-lichen-stress-tolerance-and-poikilohydry]] and the ability of their desiccated cells to survive temperature extremes in a state of dormancy. Desiccated thalli of Xanthoria parietina grew normally after cryoimmobilization in sub-cooled liquid nitrogen (approximately -200 degrees C), while hydrated thalli did not survive.

Most lichen-forming fungi and their photobionts are fully adapted to continuous wetting and drying cycles but die under continuously moist conditions or when prevented from drying out at elevated temperatures. Both symbionts are fully poikilohydric -- the fungal partner does not protect the photobiont from desiccation, as is often assumed.

### Desiccation Physiology

During desiccation, fungal and algal cells undergo dramatic changes:
- Fungal cells develop excentrically located cavitation bubbles
- Algal cells shrivel and shrink
- The cortex becomes opaque and brittle
- Ascomal shrinkage occurs during desiccation

Concentric bodies appear in cells of lichen-forming and numerous desiccation-tolerant fungi, suggesting a role in desiccation tolerance. The molecular basis of desiccation and stress tolerance is only beginning to be understood through genomic approaches.

## Ecological Roles

### Ecosystem Dominance

[[lichen-ecology-and-soil-formation]] dominate approximately 10% of terrestrial ecosystems, encompassing polar, alpine, and desert regions where higher plants are at their physiological limits. They are commonly pioneer colonizers of fresh rock outcrops and were possibly among the earliest life forms to occupy Earth's land surfaces.

### Soil Formation and Stabilization

Lichens play important roles in:
- [[fungal-weathering-rocks-minerals]] and soil formation
- Retention and distribution of nutrients (C, N) and trace elements
- Biological soil crust formation in arid ecosystems
- Wind erosion reduction in steppe, semi-desert, and desert ecosystems

### Biogeochemical Cycling

Globally, lichens play an important biogeochemical role in nutrient retention, soil formation processes, and rock weathering. They accumulate metals such as lead, copper, and radionuclides, and form metal-organic biominerals during growth on metal-rich substrates.

### Foliicolous Lichens

Foliicolous lichens colonize living leaves in tropical rainforests, forming species-rich communities. Although most species grow on the cuticle without penetrating the leaf, they are considered pests on economic plants such as coffee, tea, cacao, and rubber.

## Farming Relevance

Lichens have several connections to farming and land management:
- [[lichen-ecology-and-soil-formation]] prevent erosion on marginal lands
- Lichen diversity serves as an indicator of air quality and environmental health
- Nitrogen-fixing cyanolichens contribute to nitrogen input in natural and agro-ecosystems
- Understanding lichen ecology informs grazing management in arid rangelands
- Lichen-inhabited soils may harbour unique microbial communities relevant to plant health
- Lichen biomonitoring can identify airborne pollutants that affect crop and livestock health

## Lichen Secondary Metabolites

Lichen-forming fungi produce a remarkable array of secondary metabolites (often called "lichen acids") that accumulate in the medullary layer. These compounds have multiple ecological functions:

- **Antibiotic activity**: Usnic acid and other compounds inhibit microbial growth, protecting the thallus from pathogens
- **Anti-herbivore defence**: Many lichen acids are bitter or toxic to invertebrate and vertebrate herbivores
- **Metal chelation**: Some lichen acids bind metals, contributing to biogeochemical cycling
- **UV protection**: Pigmented compounds may provide additional radiation shielding
- **Water repellency**: Hydrophobic secondary compounds enhance the directional flow of water through the thallus

These secondary metabolites crystallize on the hydrophobin-derived rodlet layer of hyphal surfaces, forming a composite hydrophobic barrier.

## Lichen Reproduction and Dispersal

Lichens reproduce through both sexual and asexual means:

- **Sexual reproduction**: The fungal partner produces ascospores (or basidiospores in rare cases), which must re-associate with appropriate photobionts after dispersal
- **Vegetative propagation**: Specialized structures (soredia, isidia) contain both mycobiont and photobiont, enabling dispersal of the complete symbiosis
- **Thallus fragmentation**: Mechanical breakage disperses lichen pieces that can establish new colonies

The requirement for photobiont re-association in sexually produced spores limits dispersal distances and establishment success, making vegetative propagation particularly important for colonization of new habitats.
