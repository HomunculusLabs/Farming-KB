# Mechanical Soil Conditioning Techniques

[[mechanical-soil-conditioning]] is the use of specialized implements to aerate and restructure compacted, degraded soils without inverting the soil profile. Unlike conventional mouldboard ploughing, which turns soil over and destroys its existing structure, soil conditioning opens the ground by creating air pockets and channels while leaving surface vegetation and root systems largely intact. This distinction is fundamental: conditioning works with existing soil biology, while ploughing works against it.

## The Compacted Soil Problem

On degraded marginal lands, a common pattern emerges: compacted, eroded, lifeless soils that are overgrazed and invaded by flatweeds and non-forage species. These soils are boggy and wet in winter, and dry, cracked, and shallow (bony) in summer, with little depth for root penetration. The compaction prevents water infiltration, root growth, and the activity of beneficial soil organisms, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of degradation.

## The Chisel Plough and Soil Conditioner

### Design and Operation

A chisel plough or soil conditioner consists of a rectangular steel frame (tool bar) towed by tractor or draught animals, to which a number of shanks are attached. These shanks are narrow-edge, axe-edged, forward-curved vertical steel bars. A slip-on steel shoe attaches to the point of each shank. Even a single implement with 5 shanks (requiring a 25–50 brake horsepower tractor) can cover substantial acreage. Multiple manufacturers now produce soil-loosening machines in the USA, Europe, and Australia.

### The Wallace Soil Conditioner

[[geoff-wallace]] developed a particularly effective soil conditioner. A circular coulter first slits the ground — the soil must be neither too dry nor too wet for this to work properly. The slit is then followed by a steel shoe that opens the ground to form an air pocket without turning the soil over. Seed can be dropped in the thin furrows created by this process, and crops such as beans or corn seeded in this way grow through the existing grass cover without any fertiliser or top-dressing. The beneficial effect comes entirely from the entrapped air beneath the earth surface and the follow-up work of soil organisms and plant roots on the reopened soil.

## The Rehabilitation Sequence

### Initial Treatment

At the end of winter or in autumn after rain, when the soil will support a tractor, a chisel plough is pulled 5–10 cm (2–4 inches) deep over the area. The passes are made on contour parallels or on low slopes, starting in the high valley bottoms and driving slightly downhill toward the ridges. Unless there are absolutely no legumes or grasses already growing, no extra seed is applied. The immediate response is increased root penetration, seed germination, and top-growth of pasture.

### The Graze-and-Chisel Cycle

The rehabilitation proceeds through a repeated cycle:

1. **Chisel** at the initial shallow depth
2. **Hard-graze** the new growth, or cut it and leave it to lie on the surface
3. The shocked plants lose most of their root mass and seal their wounds
4. Dead roots add compost to the soil, as does the cut foliage or animal droppings
5. Soil bacteria and earthworms multiply, feeding on the [[green-manure]] and softening the surface
6. **Chisel again** at 23–30 cm (9–12 inches), following the same pattern
7. **Graze or cut again**, then **chisel again** at 23–30 cm

### Results After One Season

During this process, which typically spans one year, the following transformations occur:

- Pasture thickens as weeds are swamped by grasses and legumes
- Myriad roots have died and added humus throughout the profile
- Thousands of subsurface tunnels lead from valley to ridge, directing all water downward into the soil and outward to the ridges
- Earthworms breed prolifically in the green manure
- Bacteria multiply and add both manures and tunnels to the soil
- A 23 cm (9 inch) blanket of aerated, living soil covers the earth

## Measurable Benefits

### Soil Temperature Modification

Geoff Wallace recorded as much as 13°C (25°F) increase on treated versus untreated soils in autumn. This temperature increase is generated by both the biological activity of the soil and the air pockets left by the chisel points at various depths. The result is the ability to grow earlier and more frost-sensitive crops than would otherwise be possible.

### Water Management

Water from even heavy downpours freely passes into the soil rather than running off the surface. Drought effects are greatly reduced by improved soil water storage. Water filtered through soil and living roots runs clear into dams and rivers, reducing sedimentation and improving water quality for downstream users.

### Biological Enhancement

Nodulation of nitrogen-fixing bacteria is greatly increased. The breakdown of subsoil and rock particles is accelerated by carbonic acid and the humic acids of root decay. Methane generated from decomposition aids seed germination. Trees make greatly increased growth due to the combined factors of increased warmth, water availability, root run, and access to deep nutrients.

### Physical Accessibility

After a year or so of treatment, vehicles can be taken on previously boggy country without sinking in — a practical demonstration of how dramatically the soil structure has improved.

## The Herringbone Pattern

There is only one rule in the pattern of this type of ploughing: drive the tractor or team slightly downhill, making herring-bone patterns across the landscape. The spines of the herringbone follow the valleys, and the ribs slope outward and down-slope. This pattern ensures that the soil channels — hundreds of them — become the easiest pathway for water movement. Water moves out from the valleys and below the soil surface, rather than running off as destructive overland flow. Because the surface is minimally disturbed, roots continue to hold against erosion even after fresh chisel ploughing.

## Planting Crops in Conditioned Soil

If tree seed, soybeans, millet, or other crops are to be planted, after a few hard grazings or mowings, a seed box is mounted on the chisel plough frame and seed is placed directly in the chisel furrow. Grazing or mowing follows germination. These new plants — sunflowers, millet, melons — grow faster than the shocked pasture and can be harvested before the grasses recover. There is never any bare cultivation, and grain growers can move to a minimum tillage method with pasture fallows between crops.

## Depth Guidelines

- **First treatment**: No more than 10 cm
- **Subsequent treatments**: 15–23 cm
- **Root penetration after conditioning**: 30–50 cm in pasture, more in forests
- **For sewage waste-water disposal**: Ripping to 90 cm or 1.5 m, using deep-rooted trees or legumes to absorb wastes

## Timing Considerations

The timing of soil conditioning is critical. Early spring or the end of a gentle rainy period is ideal. If the soil is too dry, it will be brought up as dry clods that resist breakdown. If too wet, the soil collapses back on itself when the implement passes. The right moisture level allows the soil to fracture cleanly and maintain the openings created by the shanks.

Soil conditioning can be sequential, allowing a year between treatments, or performed all at once at 20–50 cm depth when preparing for immediate tree crop planting.

## Biological Alternatives on Difficult Terrain

Where land is too steep or stony for mechanical implements, biological "chisel ploughs" serve the same function:

- **Japan**: Fukuoka uses radish and Acacia
- **Africa**: Farmers use [[acacia-albida]] and Glyricidia
- **New Guinea**: Casuarina is the primary species
- **Mediterranean**: Farmers use Tamarix

On difficult terrain including boulder fields, dunes, steep slopes, and laterites, mixed forests of legume and non-legume species (citrus, olive, pine, oak) provide the best permanent solution to soil conservation.

## Maintaining Gains

No matter how soil is aerated — whether with a garden fork, a daikon radish, or mechanical power — the advantage of looseness and penetrability can be quickly lost through overstocking, cropping, heavy traffic, or heavy-hoofed animals on wet ground. All of these compress the soil back into a solid state. The only lasting solution lies in following conditioning with permanent, deep-rooted plant systems (forests or prairies) and maintaining good management practices including minimum tillage.

Intense fire, intense stocking, intense cropping, and intensive production all threaten soil structure. Mechanical soil rehabilitation can be either a one-time beneficial process or another way to waste energy every year — the difference depends entirely on the land management practices that follow.
