# Lysergic Acid Amide Discovery in Ololiuhqui

The chemical investigation of ololiuhqui, the ancient Aztec magic
[[golden-guide-morning-glory-and-ololiuqui]] seeds, led to one of the most surprising discoveries in
ethnopharmacology: that the active principles of a New World sacred
plant were chemically identical to ergot alkaloids previously known only
from a lower fungus. This finding by Albert Hofmann and his assistant
Hans Tscherter in 1960 bridged two widely separated branches of the
plant kingdom in an unprecedented way.

## Ololiuhqui: The Aztec Morning Glory

Ololiuhqui is the Aztec name for the seeds of certain climbing plants
of the Convolvulaceae (morning glory family) that were used in
pre-Columbian times by the Aztecs and neighboring peoples in religious
ceremonies and magical healing practices. Ololiuhqui is still used
today by certain Indian tribes including the Zapotec, Chinantec,
Mazatec, and Mixtec, who until recently still led a genuinely isolated
existence in the remote mountains of southern Mexico.

The earliest records come from Spanish chroniclers of the sixteenth
century. Bernardino de Sahagun wrote that the seeds "stupefy and
deprive one of reason: they are taken as a potion." Francisco
Hernandez, physician sent by Philip II to study Mexican medicaments,
described how Aztec priests ate the plant to commune with gods, seeing
"thousands of fantastic images and demons."

## Botanical Identity

The original ololiuhqui was identified as the seeds of Turbina
corymbosa (syn. Rivea corymbosa). Through the mediation of R. Gordon
Wasson, Hofmann obtained seeds from two sources: round, light brown
seeds from Huautla, correctly identified as [[plants-of-the-gods-turbina-corymbosa-ololiuqui]], and
black, angular seeds from San Bartolo Yautepec, identified as Ipomoea
violacea. While Turbina corymbosa thrives only in tropical or
subtropical climates, Ipomoea violacea is found worldwide as an
ornamental plant, the common morning glory with blue or blue-red
striped calyxes.

The Zapotec utilize both species: the original ololiuhqui (badoh) from
Turbina corymbosa and badoh negro, the seeds of Ipomoea violacea.

## The Astonishing Chemical Discovery

Hofmann and Tscherter advanced the working hypothesis that the active
principles of ololiuhqui could be indole compounds, the same chemical
class as LSD, psilocybin, and psilocin. Extracts of ololiuhqui seeds
gave the blue coloration characteristic of indole compounds with the
appropriate reagent. The identification led to an astonishing result.

The active principles from the ancient Mexican magic drug proved to be
identical with substances already present in Hofmann's laboratory. They
were identical with alkaloids obtained in the course of decades-long
investigations of ergot. Lysergic acid amide, lysergic acid
hydroxyethylamide, and closely related alkaloids were established as
the main active principles. Also present was ergobasine (ergometrine),
whose synthesis had constituted the starting point of Hofmann's
investigations on ergot alkaloids.

## Skeptical Reception

When Hofmann presented these findings at the IUPAC Natural Products
Congress in Sydney, Australia, in 1960, colleagues received the talk
with skepticism. Some suspected that ololiuhqui extracts could have
been contaminated with traces of lysergic acid derivatives from the
laboratory. There was another reason for doubt: the occurrence in higher
plants (morning glory family) of ergot alkaloids known only from lower
fungi contradicted the experience that certain substances are
restricted to respective plant families.

Results were confirmed by independent laboratories in the United States,
Germany, and Holland. The suspicion that seeds could have been infected
with alkaloid-producing fungi was ruled out experimentally.

## Pharmacological Effects

Lysergic acid amide evokes a dreamlike condition, but only with a
tenfold to twenty-fold greater dose than [[turner-lsd]] substitute. The boom
lasted only a short time, as the seeds tasted very bad, were difficult
to digest, and produced less desirable effects than LSD.

## Significance for Phytochemistry

The discovery that ergot alkaloids occur in both a parasitic fungus and
a flowering plant remains one of the rare exceptions to the general
rule that characteristic groups of substances are restricted to specific
plant families. This finding has broader implications for our
understanding of biochemical evolution and the distribution of secondary
metabolites across the tree of life. It raises the question of whether
[[comparison-endophytic-fungi-vs-mycorrhizal-fungi]] living within the morning glory plants might be
responsible for producing these alkaloids, or whether convergent
biosynthetic pathways evolved independently in fungi and plants.

## Cultural Impact

The identification of ololiuhqui's active compounds had a profound
cultural ripple effect. By demonstrating that a sacred Aztec
entheogen contained compounds chemically related to the then-notorious
LSD, the discovery connected indigenous Mesoamerican religious practice
directly to modern psychopharmacology. It validated the Aztec
classification of ololiuhqui alongside teonanacatl ([[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]])
and peyotl ([[peyote-mescaline-cactus-pharmacology]]) as tools for divine communion.

Hofmann himself reflected that nobody at the time of synthesizing lysergic
acid amide could have suspected this compound would be discovered twenty
years later as a naturally occurring active principle of an ancient
Mexican magic drug. The finding underscores the sometimes surprising
interconnections between laboratory chemistry and traditional
ethnobotanical knowledge.
