# Resource Classification and Management in Permaculture

## Overview

In permaculture design, a resource is any useful reserve created when living components and technological units translate incoming energies (sun, wind, rain) into storable forms. Understanding what categories resources fall into determines the strategies appropriate for their use. Mollison's classification system distinguishes five types of resources based on their response to use — a framework that guides ethical and sustainable resource management across all scales of design.

## Defining Resources and Yields

Resources are the useful reserves that systems build from incoming energy. Some must be retained for the system's own purposes — fish stocks must be maintained to produce more fish; soil organic matter must be maintained to support plant growth. An ideal technology should at minimum fuel itself rather than drawing down external energy reserves.

The surplus beyond system needs constitutes the **yield** — any useful resource available for use, export, or trade. The way to obtain yield is to be conservative in resource use, for energy, like money, is much more easily saved than generated. Resource saving involves recycling waste, insulating against heat loss, reducing runoff, and routing resources to their next "use point" rather than allowing them to be lost from the system.

## Two Categories of Resource Ownership

1. **Common heritage resources:** Forests, soils, air, water, sunlight, and seeds — resources that belong to all people and whose presence or absence affects the whole community. These cannot be privately owned without ethical consequence, as their degradation harms everyone.

2. **Developed resources:** Those fabricated, ordered, or otherwise created by human work — tools, buildings, processed materials, bred cultivars. What we create, however, is always made from the common resource, so it is impossible to draw a clean line between private and common. Every developed resource ultimately derives from the shared natural base.

## The Five Resource Categories

Mollison classifies resources by their response to use, which directly informs management strategy. This is perhaps the most practical framework in permaculture for deciding how to treat any resource encountered in design.

### Category 1: Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources benefit from moderate harvesting or engagement and degrade without it. They are the ideal resources for sustainable systems.

- **Green browse:** Deer browsing prevents shrubs from becoming woody and unpalatable. Browsed biennials that cannot flower may tiller out and become perennial — the fireweed *Erechthites* nibbled by wallaby in Tasmania is a documented example of browsing-induced perennialization.
- **Seedling trees:** Grazing keeps them at browse height; ungrazed, they "escape" to unbrowsable height and shade out palatable plants below. Moderate browsing maintains productive pasture-forest edges.
- **Information:** A resource that flourishes when freely used and exchanged, but withers or becomes outdated if hoarded. Too little information impoverishes a system, but when shared widely it increases for all participants. This is why open-source knowledge systems outperform proprietary ones in the long run.

Management implication: Use these resources regularly but moderately. Both overuse (damage, extinction of palatable species, information overload) and underuse (woody decline, knowledge decay) are harmful. The key is finding the appropriate level of interaction.

### Category 2: Resources Unaffected by Use

These can be used without diminishment — the closest thing to free resources in nature.

- **Impalpable examples:** A view, a good climate, scenic beauty, passive solar gain. Using a view does not diminish it for others.
- **Palpable examples:** Water diverted for hydroelectric generation or irrigation (returned to stream after use), stone piles used as mulch or heat stores, a well-managed ecosystem that maintains its productivity despite harvest.

Management implication: These are the ideal resources — use freely without concern for depletion. Design systems to maximize access to category 2 resources (solar gain, gravity flow, views).

### Category 3: Resources That Disappear or Degrade If Not Used

These are time-sensitive surpluses that must be captured when they appear or the opportunity is permanently lost.

- Unharvested annual crops that rot in the field
- Grass that could be stored for winter but instead decomposes or is leached by rain
- Irruptions of oceanic fish that pass by and are gone
- Swarms of bees or grasshoppers that appear and disappear
- Ripe fruit that falls and rots if not picked
- Water runoff during rains that flows to the sea if not captured

Management implication: Timing is critical. Design systems to capture and store these resources when they appear. This means having infrastructure ready — tanks for rain, cold storage for harvest, nets for fish runs. The permaculture designer anticipates periodic abundance and prepares to receive it.

### Category 4: Resources Reduced by Use

These are finite stocks depleted by extraction. They can be used but never replaced on human timescales.

- Fish or game stocks unwisely harvested beyond reproductive capacity
- Clay deposits quarried for ceramics or building
- Mature forests harvested faster than they regrow
- Coal, oil, and natural gas extracted and burned

Management implication: Use sparingly and only to create permanent beneficial changes to the ecosystem. Every unit consumed is gone forever. The ethical use of category 4 resources is to invest their one-time energy into creating category 1, 2, or 3 resources that will yield perpetually.

### Category 5: Resources That Pollute or Destroy Other Resources If Used

These are inherently destructive and should be eliminated from design.

- Residual poisons in ecosystems (persistent pesticides, heavy metals)
- Radioactive materials
- Super-highways and large areas of concrete
- Sewers running pollutants to the sea
- Industrial processes that create toxic byproducts

Management implication: These should be banned entirely. Their use permanently reduces yields of sustainable resources and cannot be justified by any short-term benefit. They represent the antithesis of permaculture design.

## Sustainability Implications

Categories 1–3 are those most commonly produced in natural systems and rural living situations — they constitute the only sustainable basis of society. Categories 4–5 result from urban and industrial development. If category 4 resources are not used to produce permanent beneficial changes, they become pollutants. Category 5 resources are permanent pollutants within human lifetimes.

A sane society manages categories 1–4 wisely, bans the use of category 5, and regulates all uses to produce sustainable yield. This is called resource management, and has been successfully applied to some fish and animal populations, but seldom to the full scope of human activity.

## The Principle of "Enough"

The key principle to wise resource use is "enough." Both over- and undersupply of resources produce pathology. Oversupply causes inflated growth, crowding, and sociopathy in social organisms. Undersupply can be partially managed through reduced growth and wider dispersal of organisms. In people, both gross over-nutrition and under-nutrition are common pathologies of modern societies. Ethical resource management must balance the extremes of famine and obesity — both are signs of system dysfunction.

## Infrastructure Crisis

Mollison cites concrete evidence of unsustainable resource use: superhighways and overpasses in Massachusetts alone need some $400 billion in repairs; collapsing sewer systems of London and New York need $80 billion. Neither jurisdiction can raise this money, demonstrating that unthinking historical development strategies cripple future societies. "Today's luxuries are tomorrow's disasters." Immoral governments tolerate desertification and land salting, concreted highways and city sprawl, which take more good land permanently out of life production than the loss of territory to a conqueror.
