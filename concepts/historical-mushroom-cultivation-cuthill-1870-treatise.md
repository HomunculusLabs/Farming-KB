# Historical Mushroom Cultivation — Cuthill's 1870 Treatise Methods

## Overview

James Cuthill's 1870 "Treatise on the Cultivation of the Mushroom" is a Victorian-era practical guide to growing *Agaricus bisporus* (the common button mushroom) on horse manure beds. Written for the working classes, it represents one of the most accessible historical records of traditional British mushroom cultivation techniques.

## The Working-Class Approach

### Accessibility

Cuthill explicitly addresses "the working classes," advising them to employ their children in collecting horse droppings along the highway. This reflects both the Victorian class structure and the democratic potential of mushroom growing as a food source requiring minimal capital investment.

### Self-Sufficiency

"The easiest way for a cottager to save his own spawn would be to do so when he destroys his old bed; he will find all round the edges or driest parts of the dung one mass of superior spawn."

Cuthill emphasizes self-sufficiency: the farmer should save their own spawn from spent beds rather than purchasing it. This eliminates the recurring cost of spawn purchase.

### The Speed Argument

"I can assure him, mushrooms grow faster than pigs! And the mushrooms do not eat anything; they only want a little attention." Cuthill's humor underscores a serious point: mushrooms require less investment (both capital and labor) than livestock.

## Manure Preparation

### Collection and Storage

Droppings collected along highways should be "deposited in a heap during summer, and trodden firmly." The treading serves to compact the manure, controlling the fermentation heat.

### Temperature Control

"They will heat a little, but the harder they are pressed, the less they will heat. Over-heating must be guarded against: if the watch or trial stick which is inserted into them gets too hot for the hand to bear, the heat is too great, and will destroy the spawn."

The "trial stick" method is a simple but effective temperature monitoring technique. The farmer inserts a stick into the manure pile and checks it by hand; if it's too hot to hold, the pile is too hot for spawn survival.

### The Ammonia Principle

"When fresh dung is trodden very hard it stops over-heating, and retains the heat longer, saving all the ammonia and other salts."

Cuthill understood, empirically if not chemically, that compacting fresh manure preserves nitrogen (as ammonia) that would otherwise be lost through volatilization during aerobic decomposition.

## Spawn Types

### Brick Spawn

"One thing I do protest against, namely, measuring spawn by the bushel. If I buy bricks of spawn for 4d. or 6d. each, I then know what I'm about. A London brick is seven inches square, and two thick, like a small paving tile."

Victorian mushroom spawn was sold in brick form—cakes of manure inoculated with mushroom mycelium. Cuthill advocates for buying by the brick rather than by volume, ensuring consistent quality and quantity.

### Mill Track Spawn

"Those who are fortunate enough to get Mill Track spawn will find it the best of all. This spawn requires no heating beyond summer heat to make it, and no other material than sandy mould and pure droppings; it is mixed and made by the horse's feet."

Mill Track spawn was a naturally occurring spawn that formed in the packed earth of horse-mill tracks—where horses walked in circles grinding grain. The continuous trampling of fresh droppings into sandy soil created ideal conditions for natural spawn formation.

## Bed Construction

### The Two-Layer System

"I care not how the dung is for the under part of the bed, provided there are about six inches of cropping, and unheated dung for the surface."

Cuthill's bed design uses two distinct layers: a lower layer of fermenting manure that provides heat, and a surface layer of cooler, unheated manure where the mushrooms actually fruit.

## The Flagstone Anomaly

"A large mushroom had lifted up one end of the flagstone, at the entrance of a public-house door, to the height of three inches; the spawn in this case having been generated by stable-men scraping their feet at the door."

Cuthill records a remarkable observation: mushroom mycelium generating beneath flagstones can generate enough hydraulic pressure to lift heavy stones. This observation foreshadows modern understanding of the tremendous turgor pressure generated by fungal fruiting bodies.
