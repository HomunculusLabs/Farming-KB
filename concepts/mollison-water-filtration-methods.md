# Water Filtration Methods and Systems

## Overview

Mollison presents a comprehensive toolkit of water filtration
techniques, ranging from classical sand filters to biological systems
using living organisms. The emphasis is on gravity-fed, low-energy
systems that produce clean, safe drinking water without machinery. All
techniques can be used in series for progressively cleaner water.

## Sand Filtration

Sand is the classical and most widely-used filter medium. Britain and
many cities use sand filters followed by chlorination to clean settled
and treated raw sewage water. Filtration by slow drip through 1.2 m
(4 feet) of sand (top half fine, bottom half coarse) is used even in
temporary rural camps for water filtration.

### Construction
- For cities, fixed sand beds with brick bases are used
- The top 1 cm (0.5 inch) or so of sand is periodically swept,
  removed, and dried or roasted to remove organic particles before
  the sand is returned
- Surface sands need washing or roasting for cleaning, usually every
  12-18 months
- Flow is upwards from base to surface

### Sand Filter Types
- Slow sand filters: rely on biological activity in the schmutzdecke
  (biological layer) on the sand surface
- Rapid sand filters: faster flow but require backwashing
- Trickle filters through sand and gravel columns feed resident
  bacteria which remove surplus nutrient

## Activated Charcoal

Activated charcoal, often from bones or plants such as willow or
coconut husks, is used as a fine filter in homes and where purity is
of the essence. As a final process, water can be trickled through a
column (a concrete pipe on end) of active carbon (10%) and silicon
dioxide (90%), otherwise known as burnt rice, oat, or wheat husks.

## Freshwater Mussel Filtration

Mussels are living water filters that individually process up to 100
litres per day, digesting bacteria and depositing wastes in the mud
base. The shells of water mussels can be substituted for pebbles in
filter systems. Mussels and crayfish serve dual functions:

- They monitor acidity, dying at pH 5.5 or thereabouts
- They are very sensitive to biocides such as Dieldrin
- Their living presence is a constant monitor on life-threatening
  pollution

## Nitrogen Removal

Carbon is essential for the removal of nitrogen or its conversion by
bacteria to the gross composition C:H:NO. Carbon is generally added as
carbohydrate, which can be liquids such as methanol, ethanol, or acetic
acids, many derived from plant residues. Unless bacteria are encouraged
and allowed to work, nitrates move easily through subsoils and can
emerge in wells and streams.

## Turbidity Reduction

### Pond Stabilisation
In ponds intended for drinking, light exclusion and surface water
stabilisation reduce both turbidity and algae to a minimal quantity.
Bank stabilisation by grasses and clump plants helps considerably.

### Lime Treatment
Liming further reduces turbidity if pH is 6.0 or less. This is
achieved by placing crushed marble or limestone as a layer in a tank,
or casting burnt lime over a pond before filling. Crushed shells or
whole shells in water tanks and ponds have the same effect. Lime
flocculates particles, causing them to settle out of the water.

### Lime for Phosphorus Removal
Freshly burnt lime is often used to remove phosphorus and sludges in
a primary settling lagoon. Water is then passed to a trickle tower for
ammonia removal by bacteria.

## Multi-Stage Filtration System

Mollison outlines a complete sequence for producing safe drinking water:

1. **Coarse aeration** to release gases and oxidise
2. **Trickle filters** of loose pebbles (2.5-10 cm) forming an
   active bacterial surface layer to absorb nutrients
3. **Sand filter** to absorb bacterial pollution; water rising
   through a sand column emerges fairly clear
4. **Watercress bed** to remove dyes and nitrates; cress can be cut
   and fed to animals or dried and burnt to ash
5. **Active carbon column** of burnt rice, oat, or wheat husks as
   final polish

The results should be clear, sparkling, safe water to drink. No
machinery is involved if the system is laid out downslope to permit
gravity flow.

## Domestic Water Treatment

For household water purification, several approaches are available:

- Fine dripstone (fine-pored stone) used in water cleaners and coolers
  to supply cool water in homes
- Crushed marble or limestone chips in rainwater tanks to buffer pH
- Whole sea shells in tanks for the same purpose
- Activated charcoal cartridges for drinking water
