# Settlement and Village Design

The permaculture approach to settlement and village design integrates the
principles of ecological design with human social organisation. Bill Mollison
dedicated significant attention in the Designer's Manual to the design of human
settlements at all scales, from individual households to villages and regional
communities. The goal is to create settlements that function as integrated
living systems, meeting human needs while enhancing ecological health.

## Scale and Hierarchy in Settlement

Permaculture settlement design operates at multiple scales. The household is the
fundamental unit, and its design follows the zone system from intensive garden
around the dwelling to wilderness at the periphery. The village scale adds
layers of social interaction, shared infrastructure, and collective management
of common resources. The regional scale addresses the relationships between
multiple settlements and the broader landscape they occupy.

Mollison observed that traditional villages worldwide share common design
patterns that reflect ecological wisdom. Settlements are typically positioned
above flood level, near reliable water sources, on slopes that drain well, and
with access to a diversity of land types including forest, arable land, and
pasture. These patterns are not accidental but represent generations of
adaptation to local conditions.

## Village Layout Principles

The permaculture village is designed around several key principles. First, the
settlement should be compact enough to allow pedestrian movement between homes
and shared facilities. This reduces energy consumption for transport and
encourages social interaction. The [[mollison-designers-access-roads-pathways-and-track-design]] should prioritise
pedestrians and bicycles over vehicles.

Second, the village should be positioned to harvest natural energy flows. [[mollison-designers-passive-solar-heating-building-design]] reduces fuel needs, and buildings should be oriented to capture
winter sun while excluding summer heat. The [[mollison-designers-sector-analysis-wind-sun-fire]] identifies wind,
fire, and noise sectors that influence building placement.

Third, water management should be integrated into the settlement layout from the
start. [[mollison-designers-rainwater-tanks-and-roof-catchment-systems]], [[mollison-designers-greywater-treatment-reedbeds]], and stormwater management
should be designed as interconnected systems rather than isolated
infrastructure.

## Clustering and Privacy

Effective village design balances community interaction with individual privacy.
Mollison recommended clustering dwellings in small groups of four to twelve
homes, each cluster sharing common garden space, tool libraries, and social
areas while maintaining private household zones. This cluster model reflects the
[[mollison-designers-climate-zone-1-intensive-garden]] principle applied at the community scale.

Each household cluster should have a shared Zone 1 garden area and communal
food processing facilities. Individual homes then connect to shared Zone 2
orchards and Zone 3 farmland. This arrangement allows economies of scale in
food production while preserving household autonomy.

## Common Land and Shared Resources

The permaculture village designates specific areas as common land managed
collectively by the community. These include [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-4-forage-gathering]], community
forest, shared water catchments, and recreational areas. The commons provide
resources that no single household could manage alone while maintaining the
ecological integrity of the broader landscape.

The legal structures supporting commons management are important. [[mollison-designers-legal-trusts-ethical-investment]] frameworks can protect common land from
enclosure and ensure long-term stewardship.

## Infrastructure Integration

Village infrastructure should be designed for maximum efficiency and minimum
waste. Energy systems, water supply, waste management, and food production
should form interconnected cycles rather than linear flows. The [[mollison-designers-home-energy-conservation-and-solar-heating]] system
connects to community-scale energy generation. [[mollison-designers-food-processing-preservation-storage]] facilities serve
multiple households and add value to local production.

Water infrastructure follows the [[mollison-designers-dam-construction-and-water-storage]] hierarchy: roof catchment for
drinking water, community tanks for irrigation, and landscape-scale dams and
swales for broad-scale water management. The [[mollison-designers-biogas-methane-digester-design]] can process community
organic waste while producing cooking fuel and fertiliser.

## Village Economy

The permaculture village economy is diverse and locally based. Mollison
emphasised that a resilient settlement should produce a wide range of goods
and services internally rather than depending on distant supply chains. This
includes food, building materials, energy, fibre, and medicine.

The [[mollison-designers-appropriate-technology-small-scale-tools]] approach ensures that tools and equipment are
maintainable, affordable, and suited to local conditions. Community workshops
and tool-sharing libraries reduce the capital burden on individual households.

## Settlement and Climate

Settlement design must be adapted to the local climate zone. In [[mollison-designers-cool-humid-climate-design-overview]], villages benefit from compact building forms that reduce heat loss
and shared heating systems. In the [[mollison-designers-monsoon-tropical-design-strategies]], open buildings with cross-ventilation
and raised floors are more appropriate. The [[mollison-designers-house-design-climate-zones]] provides
detailed guidance for building form at different latitudes and elevations.

In dryland areas, settlement design prioritises water harvesting and shade.
Buildings are clustered to create shared shade zones, and streets are oriented
to channel breezes through the settlement. [[mollison-designers-fog-and-dew-harvesting]] systems can
supplement water supplies in arid regions.

## Social Structure and Governance

Mollison recognised that the social structure of a settlement is as important
as its physical layout. A permaculture village needs governance systems that are
participatory, transparent, and adapted to the scale of the community. Decision
making should be distributed to the smallest effective unit, with larger
decisions requiring broader consensus.

The [[mollison-designers-community-garden-design-urban-permaculture]] model provides a starting point for collective
decision-making about shared resources. As the community grows and takes on
more complex management responsibilities, governance structures can evolve to
meet changing needs.

## Wildlife Integration

A well-designed settlement integrates habitat for wildlife within its fabric.
[[mollison-designers-wildlife-corridors-habitat-settlements]] connect the village to surrounding natural areas, allowing
species to move through the settled landscape. [[mollison-designers-shelterbelt-economics-for-livestock-and-croplands]] and hedgerows provide
both wind protection and wildlife habitat.

Domestic animal integration follows the [[mollison-designers-domestic-animal-integration-design]] principles, where
chickens, ducks, geese, and other small livestock contribute to nutrient cycling
and pest management within the settlement zone.

## Evolution and Adaptation

Settlements are not static. Mollison emphasised that permaculture design should
plan for change and evolution over time. The initial village layout should allow
for expansion, infill development, and adaptation as the community grows and
conditions change. The [[mollison-designers-design-for-disaster-resilience]] approach ensures that the settlement
can withstand shocks and recover quickly.
