# Transplanting Techniques

Transplanting seedlings rather than directly sowing seeds is a
cornerstone of the [[jeavons-grow-biointensive-method-overview]]
method. It requires more advance planning and time but offers
several significant advantages for plant health, space
efficiency, and garden productivity.

## Why Transplant Instead of Direct Sow

Transplanting seedlings into a [[jeavons-double-digging-bed-preparation]], composted bed gives the plant a second
"meal" of nutrients, air, and moisture after its first meal in
the flat. Seeds sown directly in beds grow in soil that gradually
recompacts during germination, so the soil will not be as loose
once the seedling stage is reached. Key advantages include:

- **Better space utilization**: Seeds can take 5 days to 12+
  weeks to reach transplanting size. Growing them in flats frees
  bed space for other crops during that period.
- **Reliable germination**: Not all seeds germinate. Transplanting
  ensures each position has a living seedling, eliminating bare
  spots that allow evaporation.
- **Even spacing**: Broadcast seeds fall randomly. Evenly spaced
  seedlings avoid competition while their leaves cover and
  protect soil, creating a beneficial miniclimate.
- **Growth stimulation**: Transplanting stimulates root growth.
  A University of California study found a 2%-4% increase in
  root health can increase yields 2 to 4 times.
- **Water efficiency**: Flats require only 1/2 gallon per day
  versus 10-20+ gallons per 100 sq ft for beds.

## When to Transplant

Seedlings are typically transplanted when they are 2 to 3 inches
high, except for those marked "LG" (large) in the Master Charts,
which are transplanted at 6 to 9 inches. Most vegetables should
be transplanted up to their first two true leaves. Transplanting
up to the first true leaves prevents plants from becoming top-
heavy and developing a tough neck that reduces quality and size.

## Handling Seedlings

Handle seedlings gently and touch them as little as possible.
Hold them only by the tips of their leaves or by the soil around
their roots. For seedlings in flats, use a [[jeavons-tools-equipment-biointensive]] to gently separate a 4-inch
section of soil and plants. If it is dry, hot, or windy, place
sections on a wet towel. Always keep as much soil around the
roots as possible. For pot-grown seedlings, turn the pot upside
down, letting the stem pass between your second and third
fingers, and tap firmly on the bottom.

## Root-Bound Plants

If seedlings are root-bound (roots tightly grown together from
extended time in a flat or pot), gently spread the roots out in
all directions before planting. This is critical because the
plant should not spend growth energy developing a new root system
when a good one already exists. The energy saved goes into
continuous uninterrupted growth.

## Planting Depth and Technique

Place each seedling into a large enough hole so it can be buried
up to its first set of true leaves. The exception is onions and
garlic, which do better when the bulb does not have much soil
weight to push up against. Water immediately after transplanting
to settle soil around roots, eliminate air spaces, and provide
adequate moisture. Press soil firmly but not too tightly—tight
packing damages roots, while loose soil allows air concentration
that causes root burn and decay.

## Timing: When to Transplant

Optimally, transplant in the early evening so seedlings settle
during moderate weather. During hot summer weather, shade newly
transplanted seedlings with 30% shade netting or row cover cloth
for several days to minimize shock and wilt. The [[jeavons-planting-by-moon-phases]]
recommends transplanting at the full moon or up to 7 days
afterward, taking advantage of lunar gravitational forces.

## The Breakfast-Lunch-Dinner Concept

Alan Chadwick's "Breakfast-Lunch-Dinner" concept embodies the
[[jeavons-pricking-out-seedlings]] and transplanting philosophy. Seedlings raised
in good flat soil ("breakfast") are pricked out into a second
flat with fresh flat soil ("lunch"), minimizing shock and
fostering growth. The splendid GROW BIOINTENSIVE "dinner"
greets the plant in the growing bed. With this care, there is
less likelihood of insect and disease damage.

## Spotting: Filling Gaps

Save surplus seedlings left in flats after transplanting. Use
them during the next 10 days to fill in holes where seedlings
died or were eaten. This process, called "spotting," maintains
the protective miniclimate that closely spaced plants create.

## The Biointensive Transplanting Advantage

A Biodynamic gardener once discovered that only two broccoli
plants in a row had aphids. Upon investigation, the infested
plants had experienced root damage during transplanting, while
the healthy plants had enjoyed uninterrupted growth. Nature
eliminated the unhealthy plants. This illustrates a core
principle: transplanting done well improves plant health, but
transplanting done poorly creates vulnerability.

## Spacing and Miniclimate Benefits

Evenly spaced transplanted seedlings create a living mulch that
shades soil, retains moisture, and captures carbon dioxide under
the leaf canopy where plants need it for optimal growth. The
roots of properly spaced plants can find nutrients more easily
and grow without competition. This [[jeavons-biointensive-spacing]] is fundamental
to the system's water efficiency and soil protection.

## Hardening Off Before Transplanting

Before transplanting in cold weather, seedlings should be
hardened off through a gradual process. Move flats to a cold
frame for two days when seedlings are almost transplanting
size, then to the open for two more days to complete
acclimatization. During warm weather, flats can remain in the
open or be moved to shade to slow growth in hot conditions.
This preparation reduces transplanting shock significantly.
