# Neoshamanic Preparation and Integration Practices

In hybrid shamanic-therapeutic circle rituals, the practices surrounding the
[[entheogenic-ceremony]] itself are considered essential to the overall transformative
process. The preparatory activities before and the integration work after the
ceremony distinguish the neoshamanic approach from both purely recreational use
and conventional clinical psychedelic therapy.

These practices draw on contemplative traditions, expressive arts, and
established therapeutic methods to create a comprehensive framework for
psychospiritual development that honors both indigenous and Western wisdom.

## The Centrality of Preparation

Experienced entheogenic explorers understand the importance of set and therefore
devote considerable attention to clarifying their intentions with respect to
[[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]] before entering ceremony. This preparatory intention-
setting serves multiple functions simultaneously:

- It focuses the expanded awareness that the entheogen will facilitate
- It establishes a framework for interpreting experiences that arise
- It creates a conscious orientation toward growth rather than escape
- It builds accountability through shared commitment with the group

Preparation also addresses the practical and psychological dimensions of
setting. Practitioners devote considerable care to arranging a peaceful place
and time, filled with natural beauty and free from outside distractions.

## Common Preparatory Practices

### Intention Sharing

Participants gather before the ceremony to share their intentions and purposes
with the group. This communal practice creates accountability and builds trust
among participants by revealing vulnerability and shared human concerns.

It allows the guide or facilitator to understand each person's specific needs,
fears, and aspirations, enabling more responsive guidance during the ceremony.
The act of articulating an intention verbally transforms a private hope into a
shared commitment.

### Meditation

Silent or guided meditation is commonly used to settle the mind and establish a
centered, receptive state. This practice draws primarily on Buddhist vipassana
meditation traditions, which cultivate present-moment awareness and nonjudgmental
observation of arising phenomena.

Meditation develops the capacity to witness intense experiences without becoming
overwhelmed, a skill that proves essential during the height of the entheogenic
experience when sensations, emotions, and visions may be overwhelming.

### Solo Time in Nature

Some circles include solitary time in natural settings as part of preparation.
This practice connects participants with the larger ecological context of the
entheogenic plants, many of which grow in specific natural environments and carry
the signature of their ecosystem.

Time alone in nature facilitates a sense of humility and openness before the
ceremony. It allows for private reflection and [[blesching-cannabis-emotional-processing-expansive-constricting-emotions]], giving
participants space to settle internal conflicts that might otherwise surface
disruptively during the group ceremony.

### Expressive Arts Modalities

Drawing, painting, and journal work are sometimes used in the preparatory phase.
These creative practices provide nonverbal channels for accessing and expressing
material that may be difficult to articulate in words.

They also serve as baseline documents against which post-ceremony creative
output can be compared, offering a tangible measure of change. The creative
process itself can activate intuitive knowing and emotional processing.

## The Council Practice During Ceremony

During the ceremony itself, some variation of the talking staff or singing staff
is often used. This practice, which originated among the Indians of the Pacific
Northwest and is also referred to as "council," establishes a specific
communication protocol.

Only the person who has the circulating staff sings or speaks, and there is no
discussion, questioning, or interpretation from others. This format creates a
space of deep listening and witnessing, where each person's experience is
honored without analysis or judgment.

This differs significantly from typical group psychotherapy, where interaction,
feedback, and interpretation are central. Some group sessions involve minimal or
no interaction between participants during the expanded state.

## Post-Ceremony Integration Practices

After the circle ritual, sometimes the morning after, there is usually an
integration practice of some kind. This may involve participants sharing
something of the lessons learned and to be applied in their lives.

Integration is recognized as essential because the expanded awareness experienced
during ceremony must be translated into the patterns of daily living to have
lasting therapeutic value. Without integration, even profound visionary
experiences tend to fade.

Integration practices may include:

- Group sharing circles using the council format
- Individual journaling or creative expression to capture ceremonial experiences
- Body-oriented practices to release residual somatic tension
- Meditation or contemplation on specific insights received
- Planning concrete life changes based on ceremonial revelations
- Follow-up sessions with therapists or guides
- Ongoing participation in supportive community
