# Polycultural Terrace Design

Polycultural terrace design is a foundational land-management technique in permaculture that transforms sloped terrain into productive, stable, and biodiverse growing systems. By converting hillsides into a series of level or gently graded platforms, terraces enable controlled cultivation on land that would otherwise be prone to erosion, nutrient runoff, and difficult access. When combined with polycultural planting strategies — integrating trees, annual crops, water systems, and perennial vegetation — terraces become resilient, multi-yield ecosystems that increase overall productivity while conserving soil and water resources.

This concept is central to Bill Mollison's treatment of earthworks and broad-scale landscape design in *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, where terracing is presented as both a practical engineering solution and an ecological strategy for maximizing the productive potential of sloped land.

## Benefits of Terracing

Terraces offer a broad range of agronomic and ecological advantages over unmodified slopes:

- **Ease of access:** Level surfaces simplify planting, weeding, harvesting, and general maintenance, reducing labor intensity on otherwise difficult terrain.
- **Controlled irrigation:** Terraces allow water to be distributed evenly across cultivated surfaces, preventing the concentration of flow that causes erosion on steep ground.
- **Minimal soil loss:** By breaking the slope into manageable steps, terraces dramatically reduce the velocity of runoff and keep topsoil in place.
- **Nutrient gain from irrigation and leaf fall:** Water delivered to upper terraces percolates downward through the system, carrying dissolved nutrients to lower levels. Leaf litter from terrace-edge trees and ground covers contributes organic matter, building soil fertility over time.

These benefits compound over seasons, making terraced systems increasingly productive and self-sustaining as the soil profile matures. Over years of management, the terrace system becomes a self-reinforcing landscape element rather than a static earthwork.

## Slope Angles and Suitability

Not all slopes are equally suited to terracing. The optimal slope range for standard terrace construction is **10° to 18°**. Within this range, the cost of construction is manageable relative to the productive area gained, and structural stability is easily maintained.

- **Maximum practical slope:** Terraces can be constructed on slopes up to approximately **30°**, though engineering requirements increase substantially beyond 18°.
- **Steep-slope production terraces (20°+):** On slopes exceeding 20°, terraces should be narrow production strips rather than broad platforms. These narrow terraces require careful attention to structural integrity and drainage.

Below 10°, the cost of terracing generally outweighs the benefits, as gentle slopes can be managed with contour planting, swales, and other less-intensive techniques. Slope analysis should always precede terrace design to ensure appropriate site selection.

## Construction Methodology

The standard construction sequence for terraces follows a systematic bottom-up approach:

1. **Begin at the lowest terrace:** Establish the first (bottom) terrace platform and wall.
2. **Stockpile topsoil:** As excavation proceeds uphill, set aside the nutrient-rich topsoil layer for later redistribution.
3. **Proceed uphill:** Cut and form each successive terrace, working from the bottom of the slope toward the top.
4. **Return topsoil to final terrace:** Once all terrace platforms are formed, spread the stockpiled topsoil across the surfaces, prioritizing the uppermost terraces where soil may have been most disturbed.

This method ensures that the most fertile soil is preserved and redistributed to maximize growing conditions across the entire terrace system. It also minimizes the amount of subsoil exposed on the surface, which would otherwise require significant amending before becoming productive.

## Structural Specifications

Maintaining structural integrity is critical for terrace longevity and safety:

- **Maximum slope between terraces:** 34°. Exceeding this angle risks wall failure and excessive erosion on the batters (sloped faces between terraces).
- **Maximum terrace wall height:** 2 meters. Walls taller than this require significant engineering reinforcement and are generally impractical for small-scale permaculture systems.

These limits are not arbitrary — they reflect the balance between the retaining forces of the wall material and the lateral pressure of the soil and water behind it. In regions with high rainfall or seismic activity, conservative values should be adopted.

## Terrace Extent Limits

The proportion of a landscape that should be allocated to terracing depends on climate and rainfall patterns:

- **Tropical areas:** Up to **30%** of the landscape may be terraced. Higher rainfall supports more extensive terracing because water is less limiting and vegetative cover recovers quickly.
- **Dryland areas:** Only about **5%** of the landscape should be terraced. In arid and semi-arid regions, over-terracing can expose soil to wind erosion and concentrate insufficient water resources across too many cultivated surfaces.

These guidelines reflect the need to balance cultivated area with the landscape's capacity to support vegetation and retain moisture. Exceeding the recommended proportions risks systemic degradation rather than improvement.

## Wet Terraces and Aquatic Polyculture

A distinctive feature of polycultural terrace design is the integration of **wet terraces** — water-retaining platforms that support both aquatic and riparian plant and animal systems:

- **Allocation:** Wet terraces should comprise approximately **10–20%** of the total terrace area in a given system.
- **Yields:** Wet terraces produce fish, shellfish, and edible or useful water plants in addition to any terrestrial crops grown on their edges.
- **Function:** These terraces serve dual purposes — they provide productive aquaculture yields while acting as water storage and distribution elements within the broader terrace system.

Wet terraces are typically positioned at mid-slope or lower positions in the terrace sequence, where water naturally collects and can be managed with minimal pumping or diversion infrastructure. The nutrient flows between wet and dry terraces create a synergistic loop: waste from aquatic systems fertilizes terrestrial crops, and runoff from dry terraces feeds aquatic systems.

## Narrow Production Terraces on Steep Slopes

On slopes steeper than 20°, broad terraces become impractical. Instead, **narrow production terraces** are employed:

- These are slim, shelf-like platforms just wide enough for a single row of crops or a narrow bed.
- **Staggered path spills:** Access paths between narrow terraces should be staggered rather than aligned vertically. This prevents water from channeling down the paths and eroding the terrace faces.
- **Vegetated slopes:** The batters (uncultivated slopes between terraces) must be vegetated with deep-rooted grasses, legumes, or shrubs to bind the soil and slow runoff.

The staggered path design is a critical detail — vertically aligned paths act as concentrated flow channels during heavy rain, quickly undermining terrace walls. By offsetting each path laterally, runoff is dispersed across the vegetated batters where infiltration can occur.

## Perennial Bund and Terrace Wall Plants

The structural and ecological stability of terraces is enhanced by planting perennials on bunds (the raised edges of terraces) and within or along terrace walls:

- **Stability:** Deep-rooted perennials reinforce terrace walls and bunds, reducing the risk of slumping and collapse.
- **Green manure:** Leguminous and nitrogen-fixing species planted on bunds provide a continuous source of organic matter and nutrients that can be cut and applied to adjacent terrace beds.
- **Microclimate:** Bund plants shade the terrace surface, reduce evaporation, and create favorable microclimates for adjacent crops.

Suitable species include comfrey, pigeon pea, Leucaena, cane grasses, and region-appropriate nitrogen-fixing shrubs. The selection of bund plants should consider root depth, growth habit, and compatibility with adjacent crops.

## Water Catchment and Run-Off Harvesting

In arid and semi-arid areas, terrace design must integrate **water catchment and run-off harvesting** strategies:

- Terrace walls and channels should be designed to capture and direct rainfall and surface runoff into storage or onto productive surfaces.
- Swales, diversion drains, and check dams can supplement terrace systems by slowing water movement across the landscape and maximizing infiltration.
- The goal is to retain every possible drop of precipitation within the system, using terraces as both production surfaces and water management infrastructure.

In dryland contexts, terraces function as much as water-harvesting structures as they do as growing platforms. The integration of catchment, storage, and distribution into the terrace design is essential for system viability.

## Polycultural Integration and Tree Cover

The polycultural dimension of terrace design is expressed through the integration of multiple species and functional groups:

- **Wet and dry crops** are grown together within the same terrace system, with wet terraces providing aquaculture yields and dry terraces supporting staple and vegetable crops.
- **Trees should form 40–60%** of the overall terraced landscape. Tree crops provide canopy cover, leaf litter, wind protection, and long-term yields (fruit, nuts, timber, fodder) while supporting the soil stability of the terrace system.
- The remaining area supports annual crops, perennial herbs, ground covers, and water systems, creating a layered, diverse productive landscape.

This high proportion of tree cover distinguishes polycultural terraces from conventional agricultural terraces, which are typically dedicated to a single annual crop. The tree layer anchors the system ecologically, providing the structural and biological foundation upon which all other yields depend.

## Summary

Polycultural terrace design transforms sloped land into stable, productive, and water-efficient growing systems. By following established slope guidelines, construction sequences, and structural limits, and by integrating wet terraces, perennial plantings, and significant tree cover (40–60%), permaculture practitioners can create resilient agricultural landscapes that yield food, fiber, fuel, and aquatic products while conserving soil and water for long-term sustainability. The key design principles — appropriate slope selection, bottom-up construction, integration of aquatic and terrestrial polycultures, and heavy reliance on perennial vegetation — together form a coherent strategy for maximizing the productive potential of sloped terrain within ecological limits.
