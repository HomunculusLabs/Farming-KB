# Herbal Materia Medica

A materia medica is a comprehensive reference catalog of medicinal substances, documenting their botanical identification, active constituents, therapeutic actions, indications, contraindications, preparations, and dosing guidelines. The herbal materia medica is the core reference tool of the herbal practitioner, providing the detailed knowledge of individual herbs necessary for safe and effective clinical practice.

This page serves as an organizational framework and introduction to key herbs. Individual herbs are organized by their primary therapeutic action categories, consistent with the action classification described in [[herbal-actions-and-energetics]].

## Digestive System Herbs

### Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)

Dandelion is one of the most versatile and widely used herbs in Western herbalism. The root is a primary bitter tonic and liver remedy, stimulating bile production and supporting hepatic function. The leaf is a potent diuretic that is rich in potassium, unlike many pharmaceutical diuretics that deplete potassium. The whole plant is nutrient-dense, containing vitamins A, C, and K, along with minerals including iron, calcium, and manganese.

Dandelion root is traditionally used for sluggish digestion, liver congestion, constipation, and skin conditions associated with liver dysfunction. It is prepared as a decoction, tincture, or roasted as a coffee substitute. The leaves are used as food, tea, or tincture for fluid retention and urinary tract support. Dandelion is generally safe, though it should be used cautiously by individuals with bile duct obstruction or gallstones.

See herbal plant remedies for additional digestive herb profiles.

### Ginger (Zingiber officinale)

Ginger is a warming carminative and antiemetic that is used both as food and medicine across many cultures. Its primary active constituents include gingerols and shogaols, which have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-nausea, and pro-digestive properties.

Ginger is indicated for nausea and vomiting (including motion sickness, morning sickness, and chemotherapy-induced nausea), poor circulation, cold conditions, digestive weakness, and inflammatory conditions. It is prepared as fresh or dried tea, tincture, or incorporated into food. Fresh ginger is more warming and diaphoretic, while dried ginger is hotter and more stimulating to digestion.

High doses of ginger may cause heartburn or interact with anticoagulant medications. It is generally considered safe in food-grade doses during pregnancy for nausea.

### Peppermint (Mentha x piperita)

Peppermint is a cooling carminative and antispasmodic rich in menthol, menthone, and flavonoids. It relaxes smooth muscle in the gastrointestinal tract, reduces gas and bloating, and has mild analgesic properties.

Peppermint is indicated for irritable bowel syndrome, indigestion, gas, headaches (especially tension headaches), and respiratory congestion. It is prepared as tea (infusion), tincture, or essential oil. Peppermint oil capsules (enteric-coated) are a well-researched treatment for IBS.

Peppermint should be used cautiously by individuals with gastroesophageal reflux (GERD), as it may relax the lower esophageal sphincter and worsen symptoms. The essential oil should not be applied to the face of infants or young children due to risk of apnea.

## Nervous System Herbs

### Chamomile (Matricaria recutita)

Chamomile is a gentle nervine, carminative, and anti-inflammatory herb with a long history of use across European, Middle Eastern, and Asian herbal traditions. Its primary active constituents include apigenin (a flavonoid that binds to benzodiazepine receptors), bisabolol (anti-inflammatory), and chamazulene (anti-inflammatory, gives the essential oil its blue color).

Chamomile is indicated for anxiety, insomnia, digestive upset (especially when stress-related), skin inflammation, and mild immune modulation. It is one of the safest herbs for children and is commonly prepared as a gentle tea for colic, teething, and sleep difficulties.

Chamomile belongs to the Asteraceae (daisy) family and may cause allergic reactions in individuals sensitive to ragweed or other Asteraceae plants. It contains natural coumarins and should be used cautiously with anticoagulant medications.

### Valerian (Valeriana officinalis)

Valerian is a sedative nervine and mild anxiolytic that has been used for over 2,000 years as a sleep aid and relaxant. Its active constituents include valerenic acid (which enhances GABAergic neurotransmission), valepotriates, and various volatile oils.

Valerian is indicated for insomnia (particularly difficulty falling asleep), anxiety, nervous tension, and muscle spasms. It is prepared as a tincture, decoction, or capsule. Effects are typically not immediate; valerian works best when used consistently over days to weeks, though some people experience acute sedative effects.

Valerian may interact with benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and other CNS depressants, producing additive sedation. Some individuals experience paradoxical stimulation rather than sedation from valerian. The herb has a distinctive odor that many find unpleasant; this can be masked in capsules or combined with more aromatic herbs in formulations.

### Lemon Balm (Melissa officinalis)

Lemon balm is a gentle relaxing nervine, carminative, and antiviral herb in the mint family. Its rosmarinic acid content contributes to both its calming and antiviral properties. Lemon balm inhibits GABA transaminase, increasing GABA availability in the brain.

Lemon balm is indicated for anxiety, mild depression, insomnia, digestive upset (especially nervous stomach), herpes simplex outbreaks (topical and internal), and cognitive support. It combines well with valerian for sleep, chamomile for digestive anxiety, and passionflower for daytime stress.

Lemon balm may have mild thyroid-inhibiting effects at very high doses and should be used cautiously by individuals with hypothyroidism who are taking thyroid medication. It is generally safe and well-tolerated at standard doses.

## Immune and Antimicrobial Herbs

### Echinacea (Echinacea purpurea, E. angustifolia)

Echinacea is an immunomodulator and mild antimicrobial that is among the most widely used herbs in North America and Europe. Active constituents include alkamides, polysaccharides, and caffeic acid derivatives, which stimulate immune cell activity, increase phagocytosis, and have antiviral and antibacterial properties.

Echinacea is primarily used at the onset of acute infections (colds, flu, upper respiratory infections) to reduce severity and duration. It is less effective as a long-term preventive and is most effective when taken at the first sign of symptoms in frequent, relatively high doses.

Echinacea should be used with caution by individuals with autoimmune conditions, as its immune-stimulating properties could theoretically exacerbate autoimmune activity. It belongs to the Asteraceae family and may cause allergic reactions in sensitive individuals. The root of E. angustifolia is generally considered the most potent preparation.

### Garlic (Allium sativum)

Garlic is a broadly antimicrobial, cardioprotective, and anti-inflammatory herb used both as food and medicine for thousands of years. Its primary active constituent, allicin (formed when alliin is converted by the enzyme alliinase upon crushing), has demonstrated antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, and antiparasitic activity.

Garlic is indicated for cardiovascular support (cholesterol reduction, blood pressure reduction, antiplatelet effects), immune support during infections, and as a broad-spectrum antimicrobial. It is most effective when consumed raw or minimally cooked, as heat inactivates alliinase. Aged garlic extract provides cardiovascular benefits without the pungent odor.

Garlic may increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulant medications and should be discontinued 7 to 14 days before surgical procedures. See [[herb-drug-interactions]] for detailed interaction information.

## Anti-Inflammatory and Pain-Relieving Herbs

### Turmeric (Curcuma longa)

Turmeric is one of the most extensively researched medicinal plants, with its primary active compound, curcumin, demonstrating potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties through multiple mechanisms including COX-2 inhibition, NF-kappaB modulation, and free radical scavenging.

Turmeric is indicated for joint inflammation (arthritis), digestive inflammation, cardiovascular support, and as a general anti-inflammatory. Bioavailability of curcumin is relatively low but can be enhanced by combining with piperine (from black pepper), using liposomal formulations, or consuming with fats.

Turmeric may interact with anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications and should be used cautiously by individuals with gallbladder disease or bile duct obstruction. High doses may cause gastrointestinal discomfort.

### Willow Bark (Salix alba)

Willow bark is a traditional analgesic and anti-inflammatory herb that contains salicin, a precursor to salicylic acid (the active metabolite of aspirin). It has been used for pain relief for over 2,000 years, documented by Hippocrates, Dioscorides, and Galen.

Willow bark is indicated for musculoskeletal pain (back pain, osteoarthritis), headache, and fever reduction. Its effects are slower in onset but longer-lasting than aspirin, with fewer gastrointestinal side effects because the salicin is converted to salicylic acid gradually in the intestines rather than directly in the stomach.

Willow bark should not be used by individuals with aspirin sensitivity or by children under 16 (due to theoretical risk of Reye's syndrome). It may interact with anticoagulant medications and should be avoided by individuals with bleeding disorders.

## Skin and Wound-Healing Herbs

### Calendula (Calendula officinalis)

Calendula is a premier vulnerary (wound-healing herb), anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial herb that has been used topically for centuries. Its triterpene saponins, flavonoids, and carotenoids promote tissue repair, reduce inflammation, and provide antimicrobial protection.

Calendula is indicated for wounds, burns, rashes, eczema, dermatitis, and fungal skin infections. It is primarily used as an infused oil, salve, or cream for topical application. See [[calendula-salve-making]] for preparation guidance. Calendula tea can be used as a wash or compress for wounds and skin inflammation.

Calendula belongs to the Asteraceae family and may cause allergic reactions in sensitive individuals. It should not be applied to deep or heavily bleeding wounds without medical supervision.
