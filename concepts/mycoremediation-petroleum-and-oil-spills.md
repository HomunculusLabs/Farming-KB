# Mycoremediation of Petroleum and Oil Spills

Using fungi to break down petroleum hydrocarbons — from crude oil to diesel, BTEX compounds, and motor oil. Based on [[paul-stamets]]' pioneering work documented in *Mycelium Running* (2005).

## How Fungi Degrade Petroleum

Petroleum is composed primarily of alkanes, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes. White-rot fungi secrete extracellular enzymes — particularly lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), and laccase — that are nonspecific enough to oxidize petroleum hydrocarbons alongside their natural target, lignin. The enzymatic versatility of fungi gives them advantages over bacterial-only approaches, especially for high-molecular-weight (recalcitrant) compounds.

## Key Case Study: Stamets' Oil Spill Demonstration

### The Dharma Oil Spill (2004)

In one of the most dramatic demonstrations of [[mycoremediation-basics]], [[paul-stamets]] organized an experiment on a pile of diesel-contaminated soil (approximately 100+ gallons of diesel fuel mixed with soil) from a construction site:

- **Method**: Inoculated the contaminated pile with oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus) mycelium using bunker spawn
- **Timeline**: Within 8 weeks, mushrooms fruited across the pile. After 12 weeks, analysis showed the petroleum hydrocarbons had been significantly broken down
- **Key observation**: Oyster mycelium first darkened as it absorbed the oil, then turned white again as it metabolized the contaminants. The darkening/whitening cycle was visible as a bioindicator of remediation progress
- **Secondary benefit**: Fly larvae (maggots) appeared, attracting birds, which brought in seeds — initiating ecological succession from a dead zone to a living habitat

### Bunker C Crude Oil Absorption

Cellulose plugs saturated with bunker C crude oil were absorbed by oyster mushroom mycelium. The mycelium darkened with oil absorption, metabolized the hydrocarbons, and became white again after digestion — a visual demonstration of the mycoremediation process.

## Battelle Collaboration: Chemical Warfare Agent Degradation

Stamets provided a library of 26 aggressive mushroom strains to Battelle Marine Science Laboratories for testing against chemical warfare agents and biological warfare surrogates:

- **Strain training**: Strains were adapted by incrementally replacing carbon sources with toxin surrogates (DMMP — dimethyl methyl phosphonate) until toxins became the sole nutrient source
- **Results**: 2 of Stamets' strains neutralized "very close surrogates of chemical weapons such as sarin, soman, and the VX family of compounds" (Jane's Information Group 1999)
- **Mechanism**: Enzymes break phosphorus bonds (dephosphorylation), causing neurotoxin molecules to unravel into nontoxic forms
- **Patent**: U.S. Patent Application 09/259,077 — "Mycoremediation of chemical and biological warfare agents"

## Psilocybe Mushrooms and Phosphorus Metabolism

Stamets hypothesized that Psilocybe mushrooms may be useful for mycoremediation of organophosphate neurotoxins:

- Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) can comprise up to 2% of mushroom mass
- These mushrooms actively absorb and metabolize phosphorus to synthesize psilocybin
- Organophosphate pesticides, chemical weapons, and industrial toxins all contain phosphorylated compounds similar to psilocybin precursors
- Non-fruiting strains could theoretically extract phosphorus from DMMP-like neurotoxins

## Fungal-Bacterial Synergy in Petroleum Remediation

[[fungal-ecology]] reveals that mycoremediation is most effective when fungi and bacteria work together:

- Mycelium secretes extracellular enzymes that break large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller fragments
- These smaller molecules become food for bacteria, which complete the degradation
- Mycelium fosters microbial communities — bacteria ride on the mycelial architecture, held in abeyance by the mycelium's selective antibiotics
- As mycelium declines, bacterial populations bloom and feast on both mycelium and newly available nutrients
- This tandem approach yields better results than fungi or bacteria alone

## Cold Composting for Munitions

A novel approach for decomposing munitions safely:

- Use cold-tolerant mushroom species that metabolize at near- or below-freezing temperatures
- Examples: enoki/winter mushroom (Flammulina velutipes), waxy cap (Hygrophorus camarophyllus)
- The temperatures generated by cold-tolerant decomposers stay below the threshold for spontaneous combustion
- This avoids the dangerous exothermic reactions that can occur in conventional composting of explosive materials

## Practical Deployment

### Spawn Sources for Petroleum Remediation

- **Spent compost** from mushroom farms (myceliated straw after flushing) — far less expensive than pure culture spawn, equally or more effective
- **Bunker spawn**: Burlap sacks filled with wood chips and mycelium, incubated outdoors to naturalize
- **Commercial grain/sawdust spawn**: Higher cost but faster colonization
- **Stem butt spawn**: Using harvested mushroom stems, expanded into bunker spawn (takes ~1 year longer)

### Key Considerations

- Mushroom compost from species that eliminate toxins should be valued as "natural capital"
- Spent compost must NOT be steam-pasteurized ("cooked off") — mycelium must be alive and viable for mycoremediation
- In all known cases except mercury sequestration, live mycelium is required
- Oyster mushroom farms generate tons of spent compost with no current market — ideal for remediation projects
- A cubic foot of spawn contains more than 14,000 miles of mycelia
- Mycelial expansion: a 4x4-foot patch can be expanded to 128,000+ square feet (3+ acres) in 3 years at 1:20 inoculation ratios
