## Psilocybin Mushroom History and Ethnobotany

## Overview

The history of psilocybin-containing mushrooms spans millennia of indigenous use in Mesoamerica, centuries of colonial suppression, mid-twentieth-century rediscovery [[discovery-of-psilocybin-mushrooms-by-western-science]], and a contemporary resurgence of clinical and cultural interest. Terence McKenna's 1983 lecture on tryptamine hallucinogens situates the psilocybin mushroom within a broader framework of plant-human symbiosis, shamanic practice, and the role of ecstatic plants in human cultural evolution. The mushroom's unique [[lsd-discovery-aftermath-pharmacological-properties]] and its capacity to produce experiences of alien intelligence make it one of the most intriguing organisms in ethnobotany.

## Indigenous Mesoamerican Use

### Pre-Columbian mushroom cults

Archaeological evidence suggests that psilocybin mushrooms have been used in ceremonial contexts in Mesoamerica for at least 3,000 years. The primary evidence includes:

- **Mushroom stones**: Carved stone figures dating from approximately 1000 BCE to 500 CE, found throughout highland Guatemala, Mexico, and El Salvador. These depict anthropomorphic figures with mushroom-shaped headdresses or standing on mushroom-shaped bases. Over 200 mushroom stones have been documented.
- **Codex imagery**: The Mixtec codices contain depictions of deities associated with mushrooms and ecstatic states.
- **Nahuatl terminology**: The Aztecs called psilocybin mushrooms *teonanácatl*, meaning "flesh of the gods" or "divine mushroom." The name itself reveals the sacred status accorded to these fungi.

The Franciscan missionaries who accompanied the Spanish conquest of Mexico encountered these mushroom practices and, as McKenna notes, "assumed they had an absolute monopoly on theophagy, the eating of God." The discovery that indigenous peoples were consuming a literal "flesh of the gods" threatened the theological uniqueness of the Catholic Eucharist and motivated vigorous suppression.

### Colonial suppression

The Spanish Inquisition systematically destroyed the mushroom cults:

- Bishop Diego de Landa burned Maya codices in 1562, destroying irreplaceable ethnographic records
- The Inquisition classified *teonanácatl* use as diabolical and persecuted practitioners
- The cults survived only by retreating into remote mountain villages, particularly in Oaxaca state
- Knowledge was maintained through oral tradition within a few Mazatec, Chinantec, and Zapotec communities

McKenna frames this as part of a broader pattern: "Ignorance burned the libraries of the Hellenistic world at an earlier period and dispersed the ancient knowledge, shattering the stellar and astronomical machinery that had been the work of centuries."

### The Wasson rediscovery

The modern chapter of psilocybin mushroom history begins with the 1955 fieldwork of R. Gordon Wasson, a vice president of J.P. Morgan and amateur mycologist, and his wife Valentina Wasson:

- The Wassons traveled to Huautla de Jiménez in Oaxaca, Mexico, and participated in a velada (night ceremony) led by the Mazatec curandera María Sabina
- Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" introduced psilocybin mushrooms to Western audiences
- Albert Hofmann (the discoverer of LSD) subsequently isolated and identified [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] from *Psilocybe mexicana* specimens collected by Wasson
- Roger Heim, a French mycologist, identified and classified numerous psilocybin-containing species

The Wasson rediscovery had complex consequences for the indigenous communities. María Sabina later expressed regret that the publicity had disrupted the traditional ceremonial context: "Before Wasson, I felt that the saint children [mushrooms] spoke to me. After Wasson, the crowds came and they spoke only of money."

## Stropharia cubensis and the McKenna Hypothesis

### Species characteristics

McKenna's work focused particularly on *Stropharia cubensis* (now more commonly classified as *Psilocybe cubensis*), a widely distributed tropical and subtropical psilocybin mushroom:

- One of the most potent commonly available psilocybin-containing species (0.5-1.5% psilocybin by dry weight)
- Coprophilous (dung-growing) — naturally associated with ruminant herbivore feces, particularly cattle
- Extremely widespread in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide
- Easy to cultivate, making it the most commonly grown psilocybin mushroom

### The symbiosis hypothesis

McKenna proposed that *Stropharia cubensis* represents a genuine symbiotic relationship between the mushroom and humanity:

- The mushroom achieved early symbiosis with human societies by associating with domesticated cattle and the nomadic peoples who herded them
- "Like the plants men and women grew and the animals they husbanded, the mushroom was able to inculcate itself into the human family, so that where human genes went these other genes would be carried"
- The mushroom "desires ever deeper symbiosis with the human species"
- McKenna describes it as a "creature of integrity" because one is "dealing with a plant" rather than a synthetic compound

### The extraterrestrial hypothesis

In a more speculative vein, McKenna recounts that within the mushroom trance, he was "informed" that *Stropharia cubensis* may not have evolved on Earth:

- The mushroom claims to be an extraterrestrial organism whose spores can survive interstellar space
- Spores are "deep, deep purple — the color that they would have to be to absorb the deep ultraviolet end of the spectrum"
- The spore casing is "one of the hardest organic substances known" with [[compass-pathways]] received FDA approval for Phase III clinical trials of synthetic psilocybin

### Legal and cultural shifts

- Oregon Measure 109 (2020) legalized therapeutic psilocybin use
- Colorado Proposition 122 (2022) decriminalized and created regulated access to psilocybin-containing mushrooms
- Multiple cities have deprioritized psilocybin enforcement
- The "psychedelic renaissance" has brought psilocybin back into mainstream cultural discourse
