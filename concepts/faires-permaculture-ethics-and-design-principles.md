Permaculture proposes that humans could make do with 40% less energy without sacrificing quality of life by localizing all energy and resource flows. This relates directly to the broader conversation about [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]].

## Three Ethics of Permaculture

These three ethics form the moral foundation of all permaculture design. See also [[permaculture-farm-design-principles]].

1. **Care for the Earth**: All things, living or non-living, have intrinsic worth. Soil, water, air, plants, and animals all deserve protection and stewardship.
2. **Care for people**: Humanity is cared for through self-reliance and community responsibility. Access to resources and knowledge is essential.
3. **Give away the surplus**: The surplus must be shared to fulfill the other two ethics. When each resource is valued rather than exploited, and people care for themselves and their community, an excess of resources is the natural result.

Sustainable systems are circular -- used items go back to their source where they can be recycled naturally.

## Seven Design Principles (Faires' Synthesis)

Faires distills permaculture into seven core principles. For a more detailed treatment see [[permaculture-earthworks-and-water-harvesting]].

1. **Everything is connected** -- Every element is connected to and supported by everything else. This echoes the [[permaculture-principle-use-and-value-diversity]] and [[permaculture-principle-produce-no-waste]].
4. **Maximize useful energy** -- Decrease the waste of energy in any system. See [[permaculture-farm-design-principles]].
6. **Pollution causes chaos** -- Systems that produce pollution break down. See [[competition-cooperation-permaculture]].

## Connecting Principles to Practice

These principles are not abstract philosophy -- they guide every design decision. For example, the principle of multiple functions means a single [[backyard-water-harvesting]] systems that capture and store rainwater on site rather than pumping it in.

## Sustainable Land Goals

When designing a system for land, the following goals guide the process:

1. The system should become self-sustaining and productive long-term -- every creature gets food from that land.
2. About a third of the land for human food, the rest for animal living space and fodder.
3. The system should support humans first, then provide surplus.
4. All energy used must come from the site itself.
5. A single person can manage less than 25 acres effectively.
6. The area should provide full income to workers on the land.
7. Processing of all products should be done on site.
8. Beauty should emerge from functionality rather than ornamentation.
9. Some areas should remain wild for ecosystem health.
10. Use low-energy, simple technology appropriate to the context.
11. Soil fertility and water quality are the #1 concern at all times.
12. Native species should be used whenever possible.
13. Local building materials should be preferred.
14. Low maintenance systems reduce long-term energy inputs.

## The Design Process

At its essence, permaculture is a series of questions: Where does the water come from and where does it go? How to cool a house without electricity? How to grow food abundantly without backbreaking labor? The answers come from working with nature rather than against it.

# Permaculture History and Origins

Permaculture originated in 1974 in Tasmania, Australia, when a young university student named David Holmgren met lecturer Bill Mollison. Both shared deep interests in ecology and human systems. Holmgren wrote a treatise combining ecology, human communities, and agriculture into one cohesive whole. With Mollison's encouragement, this became his graduate thesis and was published in 1978 as the book *Permaculture One*.

## The Word "Permaculture"

The word was inspired by Russell Smith's 1924 book *Tree Crops: A Permanent Agriculture*. Mollison and Holmgren coined "permaculture" (permanent + agriculture), but it came to mean much more than just permanent agriculture.

The concept grew to encompass community systems, cultural ideologies, business, art, and every facet of human life — becoming "permanent culture."

## Key Definitions

- **Bill Mollison:** "The harmonious integration of the landscape, people and appropriate technologies, providing food, shelter, energy and other material and non-material needs in a sustainable way."

- **Bill Mollison:** "A philosophy of working with, rather than against nature; of protracted and thoughtful observation rather than protracted and thoughtless action; of looking at systems in all their functions rather than asking only one yield of them."

- **David Holmgren:** "Consciously designed landscapes which mimic the patterns and relationship found in nature, while yielding the abundance of food, fiber and energy for provision of local needs."

- **Toby Hemenway:** "Turn every liability into an asset."

## The Cultural Context of 1974

Permaculture did not emerge in a vacuum. In 1974, young people around the world were asking fundamental questions about the way society lived and consumed resources. Hippie movements, back-to-the-land communities, and intentional communes cropped up across the globe, experimenting with alternative lifestyles and ecological living.

In Australia, the meeting between Holmgren and Mollison was catalyzed by this broader cultural ferment. Holmgren was in his final year at the University of Tasmania when he met Mollison, a lecturer with a remarkably similar interest in ecology and human systems.

Inspired by their discussions and by experiences in gardens and field sites around Tasmania, Holmgren wrote his groundbreaking treatise on how the world should work. It contained the seeds of a design system that managed to combine ecology, human communities, and agriculture into one cohesive whole.

## Growth of the Movement

After *Permaculture One*, Mollison spoke at educational institutions but became disillusioned by the academic reception. He founded the Permaculture Research Institute and an experimental farm in Australia. He designed the Permaculture Design Certificate (PDC) course, which became wildly popular.

At one point Mollison even tried to trademark the word permaculture without success, though he did succeed in copyrighting it for educational use. Today, anyone who wants to earn a Permaculture Design Certificate must take the PDC course from someone who already holds one.

Mollison and Holmgren eventually disagreed on educational approach. Mollison wanted controlled curriculum; Holmgren wanted free inclusion of spirituality. Both played out — curriculum is controlled but delivery is up to individuals.

This makes it sometimes difficult to find an experienced and knowledgeable permaculture teacher, but as Mollison wrote in his book *Travels in Dreams* (1996): "Finally, with hundreds of itinerant teachers turning up anywhere, the system is beyond restraint. Safe at last, and in geometric growth rate — we have won! Permaculture is permanently ungovernable."

## A Note on Religion and Permaculture

Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, while both equal co-creators of permaculture, disagreed on the spiritual side of things. Mollison, ever the scientist, wanted to distance the official practice of permaculture from any religious features that the community introduced.

Holmgren, on the other hand, grew to believe that the spiritual aspects could not be ignored. If you participate in any permaculture design course (PDC), you are likely to be introduced to a variety of quasi-pagan and earth-centered spiritual practices alongside the technical design curriculum.
