## Planning Earthworks and Post-Earthworks Planting

## Overview

Successful earthworks in permaculture depend on thorough planning before any soil is moved, and immediate planting after disturbance. This two-phase approach ensures that earthworks are properly designed, topsoil is preserved, and disturbed areas are rapidly stabilized with vegetation to [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]] and weed invasion.

## Pre-Excavation Planning

All aspects of the earth-moving process should be planned before machines or laborers arrive on site. The planning sequence follows these steps:

### 1. Initial Placement Decisions

Using contour maps and site plans, determine the placement of roads, dams, house sites, drains, and other earthworks. Consider the overall layout of the property and how each element interacts with others — water flow, access routes, zones of use, and existing vegetation.

### 2. Soil Testing

Test the soil by auger holes, soil samples, and soil pits to determine its suitability for the planned earthworks. For example, a good clay soil is essential for dam construction. Seek professional advice or conduct additional research before finalizing placement decisions. [[soil-testing]] should assess:

- **Soil texture** — proportions of sand, silt, and clay
- **Permeability** — how quickly water moves through the soil
- **Structural stability** — load-bearing capacity and angle of rest
- **Depth to bedrock or impermeable layers** — critical for dams and drainage

### 3. Site Pegging

Peg out the site using a level (simple or complex), a measuring tape, and stakes with red or white cloth attached so that the earth-moving machine operator can clearly follow them. Pegs should be placed at regular intervals along contours, drain lines, and structural boundaries.

### 4. Topsoil Management

Plan a specific location to store all topsoil removed during excavations. **Never allow topsoil to be mixed with subsoil.** Carefully strip and set aside topsoil so it can be later returned to the site as a growing medium and to stabilize subsoil erosion. Topsoil is the most valuable layer — it contains the organic matter, microbial life, and seed bank that enable rapid revegetation.

### 5. Plant Material Preparation

Have seeds and plant materials on hand before earthworks begin. These can be purchased from a nursery or grown in pots on the site several months before the planned earthworks. Having plant materials ready ensures immediate stabilization after disturbance.

## Soil Volume Changes

When soil is moved, it becomes loose and its volume increases. Key relationships:

- **Excavated soil** may increase to approximately 145% of its original volume due to increased air space
- **Machine-compacted fill** may still occupy about 110% of the cut volume
- **Conscientiously compacted clay** in dam walls may settle as little as 1% over time
- **Loose fill** will eventually settle to 75% or less of its uncompacted volume

These volume changes have great relevance to house foundations and wall stability. Designers must account for settlement when calculating cut-and-fill volumes.

### Topsoil Replacement Considerations

- **Over fill areas** — do not over-compact when replacing topsoil, or risk waterlogging
- **Over cut areas (deep subsoil)** — rip or loosen the base subsoil before replacing topsoil to allow root penetration
- **Old roads, quarries, parking areas** — these must be ripped before planting trees and meadows, as the compacted surface prevents root growth and water infiltration

## House Site Preparation Sequence

A systematic approach to preparing a house site with drainage:

1. **Careful survey** — place pegs outside the site boundary
2. **Strip topsoil** — carefully mound it above and at either end of the site
3. **Cut to level** — use subsoil fill for access roads only
4. **Foundation trenches** — call in a ditcher or backhoe to cut foundation and drainage trenches; pour foundations and slab, place paths, drains, and pipes
5. **Replace topsoil** — use a small blade and bucket machine (Bobcat or wheeled tractor) to replace topsoil and neaten the site; mound some soil to the windward side for hedges
6. **Plant or seed** — all topsoil areas must be planted immediately to prevent erosion
7. **Fine-tune** — use barrows and rakes for final grading; verify drainage with water flow tests

## Post-Earthworks Planting

### Why Immediate Planting Is Essential

Two critical reasons demand immediate planting after soil disturbance:

1. **[[dighton-fungal-roles-soil-erosion-prevention]]** — bare slopes as gentle as 2% can suffer severe erosion, especially during rain events. Unprotected soil is quickly carried away by water and wind.
2. **Weed prevention** — disturbed soil is an ideal seedbed for unwanted volunteer plants. If these establish, they become difficult to displace later and compete with desired species.

### Stabilization Strategies

If a full set of ground covers, pioneer species, and long-term plants can be established in new earth, a great deal of time and work is saved. A broadscale scatter of mixed seed, raked in, prepares the way for permanent plantings. Mulching bare soil with hay, hessian (burlap), or woodchips breaks the force of rain and suppresses weeds.

### Recommended Plant Species for Rapid Stabilization

#### Annual Seeds and Root Crops
- Sunflower, mixed parrot seed with sunflower, millets, pulses, chard, parsley, lupin, and clovers
- Parsnip (fresh seed), salsify, daikon radish, radish, turnip — all "spike" the soil, breaking compaction
- Bulbs of lilies, grape hyacinth

#### Perennial Roots and Divisions
- Sunroot ([[comparison-jerusalem-artichoke-vs-potato]]), comfrey, chicory, horseradish, ginger, sweet potato, turmeric
- Bamboo, banana grass, pampas grass, aloes, agave, New Zealand hemp

#### Established Plantings
- Elephant garlic, asparagus, globe artichoke
- Cuttings of small fruits: elderberry, willow, poplar, mulberry, Pride of Madeira (*[[echium-fastuosum]]*)
- Tubed seedlings of Acacia, Prosopis, tagasaste, New Zealand mirror plant (*Coprosma*), pines, eucalypts

These species compete well with self-sown weeds and, with little or no assistance, establish a varied and useful early and perennial crop system. Many species self-sow for subsequent years, and can be further divided or propagated from cuttings.

### Planting Sequence Tips

- **Clover** can be late-sown to allow vegetable seed to establish first
- **Grasses** need not be deliberately sown — they will invade naturally if gaps appear, and can be grazed by geese in subsequent years
- **Failed plants** should be replaced with successful species later in the cycle
- **Smooth-finish banks** so a mower or scythe can be used until selected plants take over
- **Trees on dam walls** are dangerous — if they fall, they take part of the wall with them
- **Bamboos, ginger, sweet potato, pepino, and clump grasses** are excellent for bank stabilization
- **Trees at the base of walls** are advantageous for shading, removing water, and reducing weeds

### Erosion Control on Steep and Degraded Sites

#### Net and Pan System

The "net and pan" planting pattern is effective on overgrazed, eroded, mined, or bulldozed sites. If tires are available, the "pans" can be made from them, filled with mulch, with [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]] led in above the tread level.

#### Log and Branch Barriers

Logs staked cross-slope on a slight downhill grade make water zig-zag across erosion faces, promoting infiltration. Even small logs and branches pegged across erosion channels build up layers of silt and leaves. Fibrous-rooted and hardy species like willow and Acacia can be planted beside these barriers, acting as permanent silt traps. Mulch behind logs and barriers quickly stabilizes the seed bed. Fallen leaves and scattered dung accumulate in these mini-deltas to provide plant nutrients.

#### Wire and Stone Structures

Small wire netting fences with stone-weighted hay on the uphill side trap silt and spread water. Cross-swales of lemongrass or vetiver grass serve the same purpose.

#### Very Steep Slopes

On very steep slopes, the only recourse may be to plant pampas, bamboo, lemongrass, and root-mat pioneers, with upslope plantings of chestnut, Acacia, carob, olive, or other large species that cascade seed downslope over time. Where chisel ploughs can be used, planned chiselling and planting creates permanent and stable changes on hillsides.
