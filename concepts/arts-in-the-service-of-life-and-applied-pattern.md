## Arts in the Service of Life and Applied Pattern

## Overview

Art, in the forms of song, dance, sculptural or painted objects, or designs, is an ancient preoccupation of all peoples. Most tribal art is intended for quite specific ends — much of it serves as a public and ever-renewed mnemonic or memory-aid. Apparently simple spiral or linear designs can combine thousands of bits of information in a single, deceptively simple pattern. The decorative function is incidental to the educational and therefore sacred information function.

## Tribal Art as Information System

Much of modern art is individualistic and decorative. Some "motif" art is plagiarised from ancient origins but no longer has an educational or sacred function. Entertainment and decoration are valid and important functions of the arts, but they are minor or incidental compared to the original purpose.

Social comment is a common art form in theatre and song, and spirited dances and songs are cheering or uplifting. However, modern monoculture offers little in the way of meaningful songs or patterns — it is based instead on the jingles of advertisements and purely decorative, trivial patterns of art, with education divorced from relevant long-term observations of the natural world.

### The Primary Purpose of Sacred Art

The induction of moods and the record of ephemera are not the primary purposes of sacred or tribal art, which is carefully assembled to assist the folk records of the function and history of their society. The challenge to artists is to study and portray knowledge in a compact, memorable, and transmittable form, to research and recreate for common use those surviving art forms which still retain their meaning, and to re-integrate such art with science and with society and its functions and needs.

## Cultural Destruction and Information Loss

Art belongs to and relates to people. It is not a way to waste energy on resources for the few. Sacred calendars melted down to bullion or objets d'art represent a degradation of generations of human effort and knowledge. The sacred art of tribal peoples hidden in museum storerooms constitutes a form of cultural genocide, removing knowledge from its context and trivialising objects to decorations or loot.

Human information, as a tribal art form, is most frequently debased and destroyed not for monetary gain but for the replacement of public information by an exotic, secretive, irrelevant, and basically uninformed centralised belief system. The fanatic cares not what is destroyed if it empowers the repressive hierarchy that is then imposed. Book-burning and image-breaking is the reaction of the alienated or intellectually-deprived to the accumulated wisdom of their revelationary ancestors. We most damage ourselves when we destroy information and aids to understanding.

## Applied Pattern: The Herb Spiral

The [[bill-mollison]] in 1978, represents a sophisticated application of pattern in a kitchen-door design. All basic culinary herbs can be planted in an ascending spiral of earth on a 2-meter wide base, ascending to 1 or 1.3 meters high. All herbs planted on the spiral ramp are accessible.

### Design Features

The construct itself provides variable aspects and drainage:

- **Sunny, dry sites** for oil-rich herbs such as thyme, sage, and rosemary
- **Moist or shaded sites** for green foliage herbs such as mint, parsley, chives, and coriander

This is a rare three-dimensional earth construct on a small scale that compactly coils a linear path or bed of herbs into one mound at the kitchen door, making herbs accessible and convenient. The ramp and walls exceed 9 meters of plant space within a compact footprint. For kitchens not at ground level, roof or balcony gardens can carry pot-herbs in stepped walls, on wall shelves, in window boxes, or as stacks of pots in earth mounds.

## Applied Pattern: Circle-Mulch Grow Pits

Pattern analysis applied to [[flowform]] models, developed at the Virbella Institute (Emerson College, Kent, UK), represent a conscious and portable applied pattern set. These turbulence basins are apparent in nature as shaped basins in streams flowing over massive sandstones or mudstone rocks, and were modelled in pozzolanic cement by Roman hydrologists in antiquity.

### Construction and Function

Flowforms are artificial replicates of rock forms carved by turbulent streams, cast in concrete or fibreglass. Stacked in sets below sewage pipe outfalls or above fish ponds at pipe inlets, they efficiently mix air and water by inducing turbulence in flow through three distinct mixing effects:

1. **Vertical overturn (plunge)**: Fluid drops from one basin to another
2. **Figure-8 lateral flow**: Water courses around the basins themselves
3. **Interaction effect**: Water coursing around basins deflects the vertical drop flow and switches it from side to side in a regular rhythm

### Turbulence Patterns

Within these major turbulence patterns — clearly portrayed by da Vinci and further analysed by Chappell (1978) for coastal uprush and backwash — are distinct vortices and counterflow, overfolds and cusps that further mix air and water at the edges of the basins and in the main flow stream.

### Practical Applications

- **Primary sewage treatment**: Oxidise pollutants in organically polluted waters
- **Aquaculture support**: Oxygenation of ponds for aquatic species production
- **Aesthetic function**: Aesthetically pleasing and instructive hydrological pattern-models of naturally-occurring constructs

### Configuration Variants

Basins can be elongate, truncate, symmetrical, asymmetrical, stepped in line, stacked like ladders, or spiralled to conserve space. Models of this type result from a long evolution beginning with wonder, sketches, analysis, observations, and then proceeding via constructed hydrological basins to practical applications across a wide variety of sites.
