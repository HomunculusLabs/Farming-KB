# Biological Element Transport and Soil pH Management

## Overview

[[toxic-elements-in-soils]] are transported and concentrated not only by physical and chemical processes but also by the ceaseless activity of [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]]. Understanding these biological concentration mechanisms, alongside the fundamentals of [[essential-plant-elements-hydrogen-boron-carbon-nitrogen|Essential]] for effective [[mineral-nutrients-magnesium-aluminium-silicon-phosphorus|Mineral]] deposits.

### Dispersal by Transpiration

Many water plants can dissociate and transpire a great variety of substances to the atmosphere. Reeds do this with mercury, hydrogen, and other elements from phosphates to chlorine. Not all of these are vaporised to drift off in the winds; many are deposited in special leaf repositories, or evaporated to a wax, dust, or efflorescence on the leaves and stems of trees, from where they are washed down again to earth by rain throughfall.

In this way, both major plant nutrients and minor elements are concentrated in the top 4 cm (1–2 inches) of soil below trees. Metals, oxides, halogens, acids, alkalis, and salts are all concentrated in this surface layer through the tree-soil cycle.

### Concentration by Metabolic Processes

All living things build their bodies by ingesting a large range of complex foods. From these ingested bodies, materials are selected to build bones, flesh, blood, brain tissue or organs, nails and hair, fats and milk. Thus, the bodies of animals are complex storages of elements. Even faeces package very different concentrations of potash, nitrates, and pollutants from the different concentrations of foods that are eaten.

By their behaviours, these elements are variously disposed of in a personalised, culturally-determined, or species-specific way in the environment. Plants and animals ceaselessly ingest and defecate, reflect and exude over their whole lifetimes, thus altering the concentration of nutrients in their immediate environment.

## Infiltration and Catenas

### Infiltration Effects

Infiltration effects are most marked in soils where, for very long periods, a succession of light intensity rains have alternately wetted and then dried out the soil. At the wet-dry boundary, concentrations of easily soluble salts and deposits of minerals and nutrients are found (sometimes in over-supply).

### Soil Catenas

Water travels by throughflow, moving by the effects of gravity downslope in the soil profile. This effect produces soil types called catenas, which are sequences of soil types related to slope, drainage, rock type, and landscape position. Understanding catenas is essential for reading landscape potential and placing elements of a [[permaculture-design]] appropriately.

## Soil pH Fundamentals

### The pH Scale

Acidity and alkalinity are measured on a logarithmic scale in which each point is 10 times the concentration of hydrogen ions less than the scale point below it. Thus:
- pH 8 (alkaline) is 10,000 times less acidic than pH 4
- pH 3 is 10 times more acidic than pH 4

### Rock Classification by Silica Content

Parent rocks of soils are classified by silica content:
- **Acid rocks**: 64% or more silica (SiO₂)
- **Intermediate rocks**: 50–64% silica
- **Basic rocks**: 40–50% silica
- **Ultrabasic rocks**: Less than 40% silica

### Acid and Alkaline Waters

We commonly speak of "hard" (alkaline) or "soft" (acid) waters. Soft water used with soap lathers easily and is desirable for washing. Hard water (including seawater or other alkaline waters) is difficult to use for washing, as soaps and detergents are themselves alkaline.

- Soaps (based on sodium or potassium/ash and fats) lather in soft acidic waters
- Detergents (based on phosphates or sulphur) lather in hard waters
- Hard waters contain calcium (Ca²⁺) or magnesium (Mg²⁺) ions
- Soft water contains hydrogen (H⁺) ions

### Sources of Acidity and Alkalinity

Acids and alkalis arise from the solution of oxides, hydroxides, sulphates, or carbonates of metals and non-metals. In water and soil water, the common rock and soil constituents are:

**Metals** (release positive H ions in solution): sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, and minerals (iron, zinc, aluminium, copper).

**Non-metals** (release negative OH ions in solution): [[silicon]], sulphur, traces of phosphorus, boron, fluorine, chlorides. Carbon is also found in organic soils.

## Soil Amendments for pH Management

### Raising pH (Liming Materials)

All of the following are used to raise pH values in soils:
- **Chalk and limestone** — calcium carbonates
- **Gypsum** — calcium sulphate
- **Magnesite** — magnesium carbonate
- **Dolomite** — [[calcium-magnesium-secondary-nutrients]] carbonate

### Lowering pH

Elemental sulphur, sulphuric acid compounds, and ammonium sulphate are used to lower [[soil-ph]], particularly in dryland [[plants-tolerant-of-alkaline-soils]].

## Soil Amelioration Guide

### Sandy Soils (General, pH < 4, Acidic)

- Add magnesium sulphate as spray at 10 g/L, or at 45 g/m²
- Neutralise with lime at 20 g/m²

### Sandy Soils (Humid Climates, pH > 4, Alkaline)

- Add ammonium sulphate or fine dolomite (for Ca)
- Add zinc every 7 years at 1 g/m²
- Add water-retaining polymers
- Add gypsum, sulphur
- Add neutralised superphosphate (for P)

### Sandy Soils (Deserts)

- Add ferrous sulphate (for Fe) at 23 g/L as foliar spray
- Add polymers

### Non-Wetting Sands

- Add 10 g/m² bentonite or montmorillonite clays
- Add polymers

### Coarse Sands (Coastal)

- Add copper sulphate (for Cu) every 7–14 years, if cropped, at 1 g/m²
- Add zinc sulphate (for Zn) every 7–14 years at 1 g/m²

### Loams (Tropics)

- Add silicate or spray silica solutions
- For coarse textures: add cement (calcium silicate) at 100–400 g/m²

### Loams (Deserts)

- Add dolomite, gypsum

### Loams (Coasts)

- Add copper sulphate 1 g/m² every 7–14 years
- Add zinc sulphate 2:1 with lime at 1 g/m² every 7–14 years

### Clays

- Use sulphur to acidify, add manganese sulphate at 2–3 g/L as spray
- Add gypsum to improve drainage, reduce salinity in dry areas
- Molybdenum needs trials at 350 g/ha

### Gravels (Iron Oxides Present)

- Add copper sulphate at 1 g/m²
- Immobilise phosphates
- Molybdenum at 350 g/ha every 10–15 years
