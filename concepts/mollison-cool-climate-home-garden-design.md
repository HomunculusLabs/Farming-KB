# Cool Climate Home Garden Design

One special feature of cool area home gardens is the need to
grow and store crops (in the field or home) over the winter
months, as growth effectively ceases from late autumn to
early spring. The middle of spring is known as the "hungry
gap", when winter crops are finished and new crops are not
yet yielding.

## Bridging the Winter Food Gap

The winter food gap is bridged by growing root crops,
cucurbits, and subsequently processed crops for preservation.
These crops mature in late summer and are stored or preserved.
Staple root crops include potato, parsnip, carrot, and minor
species such as oca, sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke), and bulbs
such as onions.

Field crops left to over-winter include fava bean, kale, chard
(silver beet), parsley, and a variety of cabbage, broccoli,
cauliflower, and sprouts under straw or shelters.

## Planting Periods

There are two main planting periods in cool climates:

1. The first months of spring are for salad vegetables and
   all summer crops.

2. The last month of summer or first month of autumn is for
   all over-winter crops and all root crops of the year.

In milder areas, a green crop (green manure) can be sown
before winter (oats, tares, fava beans) and slashed or
dug-in in the spring.

## Crop Storage Methods

In cool areas, cellar or pit storage is extensively used:

- Potatoes are stored in straw or ash in dark boxes or humid
  cellars.

- Carrots and parsnips (tops removed) are in mounds of sand
  or ash.

- Apples are separately wrapped or spread out on attic floors.
  They are not stored with root crops because of their
  ethylene production, which causes root crops to shoot.

- Cabbage or kale can be stored uprooted by covering with hay
  in the open, as a ferment (sauerkraut) in large jars, or
  as sterilised or frozen storage.

Many crops are stored frozen in deep freezes and retain good
nutritional value.

## Garden Layout and Planning

Planning the garden means small beds for the spring planting,
with main crops of potato, tomato, and sweet corn, then a
reversion to larger mass beds in the autumn planting for
winter storage crops (potatoes, carrots, parsnips, turnips,
cabbage, fava beans, peas, Brassicas).

Chard and parsley are important all-year foods and several
successions can be sown to reseed at irregular intervals, so
that they are always in the garden.

The garden layout is very much the same as that given under
the tropics chapter, but omitting any overshaded areas or
tree crop interplant, as light levels can be low in cloudy
weather and light saturation is rarely a problem. Mulch is
appropriate; the sole exception may be for spring carrot
planting, when mulch is pulled from the seedbed to let it
warm up early. All other crops planted in mulch catch up
with bare-soil crops and mature at the same time.

## Season Extension

For cool areas, a small glasshouse for seedling trays and a
series of cloches for early transplants are invaluable. Even
potatoes can be sprouted on old hessian bags indoors, or in
any open shadehouse, and transplanted complete with leaves
and roots by cutting up the bags into squares.

## Perennialising Annuals

There are several ways to perennialise annuals:

- Carrots: cut a disc off the top, sprout it on soft wet paper
  in a shallow tray, and replant. One gardener reported eating
  the same carrots for 9 years using this method.

- Leeks: let one in four go to seed and dry off, then remove
  the small bulbils from the base and replant. When harvesting,
  cut off the leek 2 cm below ground level. The central shoot
  quickly regrows.

- Lettuce: pick single leaves, allow to go to seed, scatter
  seed. Mignonette variety is a good self-seeder, as are some
  Italian varieties.

- Cabbage: cut the head 8 cm above the ground, then deeply
  crosscut the stem. Each quarter of the stem regrows a small
  cabbage.

- Garden fennel: this plant self-seeds, as does chard,
  parsley, and parsnip if allowed to go to seed. Judicious
  thinning, weeding, and transplanting is in order.

## Seed Saving

When saving seed from any umbelliferous plant, collect the
strong, mature seed from the terminal panicle only; this
germinates best. Side panicles contain immature and small
seeds and can be pruned or rejected.

## Trellis and Small Fruit

Trellis crop in mesothermal climates is important. Many peas,
beans, and cucurbits will trellis, as will varieties of
tomatoes. Vertical zigzag, inverted V, or house wall and
garden fence trellis is best, aligned north/south where
possible for even sun effects.

Small fruit such as currants, gooseberry, raspberry, or
trellis blackberry is a feature of cool-area gardens. Six to
fifteen plants of 7-8 species yields a great deal of fresh or
frozen food of high vitamin C value. Many such rows can act
as windbreak in the garden.
