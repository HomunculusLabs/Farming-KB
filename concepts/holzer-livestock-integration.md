# Holzer Livestock Integration

Livestock play a large role in Sepp Holzer's permaculture system.
They provide high-quality produce and are also industrious and pleasant workers.
Holzer breeds robust, hardy animals suited to the terrain — old domestic breeds as well as wild animals.
The integration of livestock into the farming system is not an afterthought or a separate enterprise but a core design element.
Animals at the Krameterhof perform multiple functions simultaneously: they fertilise soil, control vegetation, break pest cycles, process agricultural by-products into food, and generate income through the sale of high-quality meat, eggs, and dairy.

## Philosophy

Old domestic breeds are significantly hardier, more intelligent and adaptable than over-bred modern varieties.
Their natural instincts are sufficient for them not to fall over their own feet, and their produce is of far higher quality.
These breeds can be kept under near-natural conditions, which is Holzer's main requirement.
Intensive livestock farming amounts to torture and produces food of very low nutritional value.

Holzer's critique of modern livestock breeding parallels his critique of modern plant breeding.
Just as hybrid seeds have been selected for uniformity and maximum yield under optimal conditions at the expense of resilience and flavour, modern commercial livestock breeds have been selected for rapid growth and maximum lean meat production in controlled environments.
The result, he argues, is animals that are physiologically and psychologically unsuited to life outdoors, require constant veterinary intervention, antibiotics, and specialised feed, and produce meat, milk, and eggs of inferior nutritional and gastronomic quality.
His alternative — old breeds on pasture — produces animals that are largely self-sufficient, healthy without pharmaceutical support, and yield food of demonstrably higher quality.

Holzer has also noted that old breeds contribute to the conservation of genetic diversity.
Modern commercial livestock are often based on a very narrow genetic base, with a few highly selected lines dominating global production.
This genetic narrowing creates vulnerability to disease and environmental change.
By maintaining old breeds that retain the genetic diversity developed over centuries of traditional selection, Holzer's approach serves a conservation function as well as a production function.

## Keeping Conditions

On the Krameterhof, livestock are always kept outside in families.
Great care is taken to meet the needs of different animal species.
The proportion of male and female animals is adjusted appropriately.
The size of the area depends on the space naturally required by each species.
This emphasis on family groups and outdoor living reflects Holzer's observation that animals kept in their natural social structures exhibit fewer behavioural problems, better health, and higher reproductive success.
The stress associated with separating young from mothers, mixing unfamiliar animals, or confining animals in close quarters is entirely eliminated in Holzer's system.

Shelter is provided but is open-access rather than compulsory; animals choose whether to use it based on weather conditions.
Winter housing is minimal — the hardy breeds Holzer keeps are adapted to the Austrian mountain climate and can withstand cold temperatures that would threaten modern commercial breeds.
Water is provided from natural sources — streams, springs, and ponds — wherever possible, which Holzer believes contributes to animal health.

## Pigs as Workers

Pigs are indispensable as living ploughs on rough, stony terrain that cannot be ploughed mechanically.
When they search for food they plough and dig through the soil, improving it with their digging activity.
Holzer keeps pigs using a paddock system — moving the paddocks ensures the land is never overgrazed, and the varied diet prevents livestock diseases almost entirely.

The pig's natural behaviour — rooting, digging, and overturning soil — is harnessed as a productive agricultural function rather than suppressed as a problem.
When pigs are rotated through an area of pasture or scrubland, they break up compacted soil, incorporate organic matter, consume weed roots and rhizomes, and deposit manure that accelerates the breakdown of plant material.
After pigs have worked an area for a season, the soil is notably looser, richer, and more friable, ready for planting.
This approach transforms a behaviour that conventional pig farmers consider problematic into the central benefit of keeping pigs.

### Old Pig Breeds

- **Mangalitza**: Hardy old breed, also known as the "woolly pig" for its distinctive curly coat. Originally from Hungary, the Mangalitza produces exceptional quality lard and meat. Its thick coat allows it to withstand the harsh Austrian winters without shelter.
- **Swabian-Hall Swine**: Hardy old breed from the Swabian region of Germany. Known for excellent meat quality and robust constitution.
- **Duroc**: Hardy old breed originally from the United States, valued for its rapid growth on pasture and hard red coat that provides sun protection.
- **Turopolje**: An extremely rare Croatian breed, one of the oldest European pig breeds. Small, hardy, and well-adapted to forest and wetland environments.

Pigs are also used to regulate problem plants: Holzer puts pigs out to graze in areas with unwanted plant growth and supports the process by sowing peas, beans or sweetcorn between the problem plants to make the pigs concentrate there.
The pigs root out the target plants while the supplementary crops provide additional forage incentive.
This technique has been used effectively against persistent weeds like bracken, thistle, and couch grass that are resistant to mechanical or chemical control.

## Wild Cattle and Old Domestic Cattle Breeds

Holzer has successfully bred mouflon, chamois, ibexes and red, roe and fallow deer as well as various species of wild cattle such as yaks, water buffalo and American and European bison.
Old cattle breeds are kept for their hardiness and the superior quality of their produce.
The diversity of large herbivore species on the Krameterhof creates a grazing system where different animals exploit different ecological niches: cattle graze tall grasses, deer browse on shrubs and woody vegetation, and water buffalo thrive in wetland areas.
This multi-species grazing mimics natural savanna and woodland ecosystems and produces a more balanced vegetation management than single-species grazing alone.

Holzer's Highland cattle deserve particular mention.
Originally from the Scottish Highlands, these long-horned, shaggy-coated cattle are extraordinarily well-suited to the Krameterhof's mountain environment.
They calve unassisted, nurse their calves without intervention, maintain condition on rough forage that modern beef breeds would reject, and produce lean, flavourful meat with a high proportion of beneficial omega-3 fatty acids.
Their ability to graze steep, rocky terrain that would be inaccessible to machinery makes them ideal for vegetation management on marginal land.

## Poultry

Birds are kept humanely on the Krameterhof.
Holzer advocates for bird conservation and keeping poultry under natural conditions with access to outdoor space.
Free-range chickens, geese, and ducks perform valuable pest control functions by consuming slugs, snails, insect larvae, and weed seeds.
Their scratching behaviour incorporates manure into the topsoil and disturbs the soil surface in ways that can stimulate weed seed germination (allowing subsequent weeding by other means) or prepare seed beds for planting.
Holzer has also kept rare poultry breeds such as Sulmtaler chickens, an Austrian dual-purpose breed valued for both meat and egg production.
Guinea fowl are kept for their voracious appetite for ticks and other parasites, providing a natural pest control service for both livestock and humans.

## Aquaculture

Holzer integrates fish farming into his livestock system through his network of ponds and lakes.
Carp, trout, and other species are raised in the farm's water bodies, feeding on natural productivity — plankton, aquatic insects, and plant material — supplemented by agricultural by-products.
The ponds are designed to be self-sustaining, with aquatic plants providing oxygen, fish providing fertiliser for edge plants, and water plants providing spawning habitat.
The fish ponds also create habitat for amphibians and aquatic invertebrates that contribute to pest control across the farm.
The integration of aquaculture with terrestrial farming is one of the most distinctive features of the Krameterhof system and demonstrates Holzer's principle that every element should serve multiple functions.
The nutrient-rich water from fish ponds can be used to irrigate adjacent growing areas, creating a fertility loop that benefits both aquatic and terrestrial systems.
Duckweed that grows on the pond surface can be harvested as high-protein feed for poultry and pigs, further closing nutrient cycles within the farm.

## Breeding Philosophy

Holzer's approach to animal breeding is deliberately hands-off.
He allows animals to select their own mates, believing that natural sexual selection produces offspring better adapted to the specific conditions of the farm than any human-directed breeding programme.
This approach produces slower genetic progress for specific production traits but maintains the overall genetic diversity and resilience that characterises old breeds.
Holzer has argued that the artificial insemination programmes used in modern commercial breeding create animals that are genetically uniform and therefore vulnerable to disease outbreaks that could wipe out an entire population.
His natural breeding methods, by contrast, maintain the genetic variability that allows populations to adapt to changing conditions.
This breeding philosophy reflects Holzer's broader ecological principle that natural systems, left to their own devices, produce outcomes that are better adapted and more resilient than human-directed management can achieve.

## Forage Systems

Holzer's approach to animal nutrition is similarly low-intervention.
Rather than planting dedicated forage crops or importing concentrated feed, he designs diverse pastures that provide year-round grazing through a carefully planned succession of plant species.
Deep-rooted plants like chicory and plantain draw minerals from subsoil layers and make them available to grazing animals.
Wild herbs and flowers in the pasture provide medicinal compounds that naturally support animal health — a practice that mirrors the self-medication behaviour observed in wild herbivores, who selectively browse specific plants when they are unwell.
Holzer has observed that animals on diverse pastures rarely require veterinary intervention, and that the nutritional quality of their products — meat, milk, and eggs — is visibly and tastably superior to that of animals fed on monoculture forage supplemented with grain.
