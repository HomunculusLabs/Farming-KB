# Fungal Culture Storage and Preservation

Maintaining viable fungal cultures over time is critical for any [[mycology]] operation. A single contamination event, equipment failure, or natural disaster can wipe out years of selective breeding work. A robust preservation strategy uses multiple methods across multiple locations to minimize risk of total culture loss.

## The Importance of Redundancy

No storage method is foolproof. Power outages can disable freezers and refrigerators for days; fires and floods can destroy entire labs. The single most important principle of fungal culture preservation is **geographic redundancy**:

- Maintain duplicate cultures at a minimum of two physically separate locations.
- Trusted friends or family members can store backup plates in the crisper drawer of a standard refrigerator — no specialized equipment required.
- Label every culture clearly with species, strain designation, isolation date, and generation number.
- Keep a written or digital inventory log so you know what is stored where and when it was last tested.

Distributing cultures widely ensures that a localized catastrophe never results in total loss of a strain.

## Short-Term Storage: Agar Plates

The simplest method for holding working cultures is refrigerated agar plate storage:

- After full colonization, seal the perimeter of the Petri dish with **Parafilm** to slow desiccation and prevent airborne contamination.
- Place the sealed plate inside a **ziplock bag** for an additional barrier against contamination and moisture fluctuation.
- Store refrigerated at approximately 35–38°F (2–3°C).
- Most species remain viable for **several months to one year** under these conditions.
- Expect the [[mycelium]] to grow more slowly upon retrieval; a recovery transfer to fresh agar is usually necessary.

This method is ideal for frequently accessed working stocks but should not be relied upon as the sole long-term backup.

## Submerged Agar Cubes (Water Storage)

Submerging colonized agar in sterile water dramatically extends shelf life by slowing metabolic activity:

- Cut a fully colonized agar plate into small cubes (approximately 5–10 mm per side).
- Place the cubes into **sterile test tubes** filled with sterile distilled water.
- Cap the tubes and store refrigerated.
- Cultures preserved this way routinely remain viable for **over one year**.
- To revive, remove a cube with sterile forceps and place it directly onto fresh agar.

### Mineral Oil Alternative

Mineral oil can be poured over colonized agar cubes or slants instead of sterile water, creating an oxygen-depleted environment that further suppresses metabolism. However, mineral oil–stored cultures **must be rinsed thoroughly with sterile water** before being transferred to fresh media, as residual oil inhibits mycelial growth.

## Agar Slants

Agar slants are a classic mycological preservation technique offering compact, long-term storage:

- Prepare nutrient agar and dispense approximately 5–10 mL into sterile test tubes.
- Autoclave the tubes, then allow them to cool under a **[[laminar-flow-hood]]** propped at an angle so the agar gels as a diagonal slant, maximizing the surface area for inoculation.
- Inoculate the slant surface with mycelium from a clean culture.
- Once fully colonized, seal and store refrigerated.
- Agar slants typically remain viable for **one year or longer**.
- Slants take up very little space, making them practical for maintaining large culture libraries.

## Colonized Grain Storage

Fully colonized [[grain-spawn]] can itself serve as a storage medium:

- Transfer colonized grain into **50 mL centrifuge tubes** and top off with sterile distilled water.
- Refrigerate the tubes; viability generally lasts about **one year**.
- For **cold-sensitive species** that suffer at standard refrigerator temperatures, cover the grain with sterile **mineral oil** instead of water and store at a slightly warmer temperature (50–60°F / 10–16°C).

## Freezing Cultures

Freezing extends preservation to years or even decades, but requires cryoprotection to prevent ice crystal damage to cells:

- Prepare a **5% [[glycerol]] solution** in sterile water as a cryoprotectant.
- Cut small cubes of colonized agar or scoop small amounts of colonized grain.
- Submerge the tissue in the glycerol solution inside cryovials or similar freezer-safe containers.
- **Leave caps loose** during freezing to allow for liquid expansion, then tighten once fully frozen.
- Store at –20°C for medium-term needs or –80°C (ultra-cold freezer) for maximum longevity.

### Professional Slurry Method

For high-volume or commercial operations, a slurry approach offers efficiency:

- Blend fully colonized agar with sterile water in a **sterile blender** to create a uniform mycelial slurry.
- Transfer the slurry to a sterile flask and stir on a magnetic stir plate for **1–3 days** to allow the mycelium to recover from the blending stress.
- Pre-chill the recovered slurry to match the target storage temperature.
- Add glycerol to achieve a final concentration of 5% (e.g., **95 mL slurry + 5 mL glycerol**).
- Aliquot into cryovials, loose-cap during freezing, then seal.

## Testing Viability of Frozen Cultures

Frozen cultures should not be assumed viable indefinitely. Implement a regular testing schedule:

- Perform **monthly thaw-and-transfer tests** on a representative sample vial from each batch.
- Thaw rapidly in a **warm water bath** at 77–86°F (25–30°C) for 2–5 minutes.
- Transfer the thawed tissue onto fresh agar plates.
- After growth appears, verify correct species identification using a microscope — look for characteristic features such as **clamp connections** in basidiomycete cultures.
- Document results in your culture inventory log.

## Cold-Sensitive Species

Species originating from tropical or subtropical environments often lose viability under standard refrigeration. Alternative strategies include:

- **Spore prints**: Dried spores on paper or foil remain viable for **over one year** when kept dry at room temperature in a sealed container with desiccant.
- **Spore syringes**: Spores suspended in sterile water and refrigerated can last approximately **two years**.
- **Grain spawn on large grain**: Store colonized grain at **50–60°F (10–16°C)** using larger grains such as **milo (sorghum)**, which tolerates warmer storage better than smaller grains. Avoid standard refrigerator temperatures for these species.

## Dehydration of Grain Spawn

Removing moisture from colonized grain creates a storable, dormant culture:

- Fill sterilized jars equipped with **micron filter lids** with fully colonized grain spawn.
- Allow the grain to air-dry over **1–2 weeks** in a clean, low-humidity environment.
- Once thoroughly dry, store the jars in a **dark, cool location** — importantly, **do not refrigerate**, as condensation upon removal from cold storage can rehydrate the grain and promote contamination.
- Recovery on fresh agar from dehydrated grain is typically **slow**; patience is essential.
- **"Sandwich" revival technique**: Place dehydrated grain kernels between two pieces of fresh agar in a Petri dish. The moisture from the fresh agar gradually rehydrates the grain, prompting the mycelium to resume growth more reliably than a direct surface transfer.

## Summary of Methods

| Method | Temperature | Expected Viability | Key Consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agar plates (Parafilm + ziplock) | 35–38°F | Months to 1 year | Simplest; for working stocks |
| Submerged agar cubes | Refrigerated | 1+ year | Excellent balance of simplicity and longevity |
| Agar slants | Refrigerated | 1+ year | Compact; good for large libraries |
| Colonized grain in water | Refrigerated | ~1 year | Mineral oil variant for cold-sensitive species |
| Freezing (5% glycerol) | –20°C to –80°C | Years | Cryoprotectant essential; test viability monthly |
| Spore prints | Room temp, dry | 1+ year | Best for cold-sensitive tropical species |
| Spore syringes | Refrigerated | ~2 years | Requires sterile water suspension |
| Dehydrated grain | Dark, cool (not refrigerated) | Variable | Slow recovery; sandwich technique aids revival |

A well-managed culture library combines several of these methods. Store working stocks as refrigerated plates for daily use, maintain medium-term backups as slants and submerged cubes, and archive irreplaceable strains in cryogenic storage — always with geographic redundancy to guard against catastrophic loss.
