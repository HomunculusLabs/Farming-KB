# Dangers of Mistaken Identification of Psilocybin Mushrooms

Mistakes in mushroom identification can be lethal. Since psilocybin
mushrooms are mostly "little brown mushrooms" (LBMs), they share the
general form with many toxic species. The extreme foolhardiness of
randomly collecting little brown mushrooms becomes terrifyingly real for
those whose unreserved enthusiasm overrode admonitions for adequate
preparation.

## The Primary Danger: Galerina Species

Two genera pose the greatest risk to collectors: Galerina and Pholiotina.
Both produce rusty brown spores. The Psilocybes have purplish brown to
black spores, and by this distinction alone, you cannot make a deadly
mistake between a Psilocybe and a Galerina.

### Deadly Galerina Species

The Galerina species most commonly confused with Psilocybes are:

- **Galerina autumnalis**: The most commonly encountered deadly
  look-alike. Often found growing side by side with Psilocybe stuntzii
  in wood chip habitats. Stamets reports finding G. autumnalis and P.
  stuntzii actually touching, which was one of the primary reasons he
  was compelled to write his book.

- **Galerina marginata**: Another deadly species that closely resembles
  small Psilocybes in overall form and stature.

- **Galerina venenata**: A deadly species whose name literally means
  "poisonous," found in grassland habitats where Psilocybe species also
  grow.

### How Galerina Resembles Psilocybe

The general form of Galerinas closely parallels that of the Psilocybes,
differing to the unaided eye only in the overall color of the spores and
to a lesser degree in the color of the caps. Galerina spores are rusty
brown in deposit, while Psilocybe spores are purplish brown. However, when
the caps are dried and/or frozen, the color can be very similar.

Galerinas often grow gregariously or in clusters on decaying logs or in
moss, and have short brittle stems that often darken from the base.
Galerinas may or may not have a partial veil that leaves a membranous
ring or fibrillous annular zone.

### Pholiotina filaris

Another deadly rusty brown-spored mushroom, Pholiotina filaris (formerly
Conocybe filaris), also contains the same amatoxins found in deadly
Amanitas. Some Galerinas can resemble mushrooms belonging to the genus
Gymnopilus as well, since both are orangish brown and produce rusty spores.

## The Amatoxin Syndrome

Galerinas, some Conocybes, and the deadly Amanitas all produce
cyclopeptide toxins (amatoxins). The effects of ingesting these mushrooms
follow a characteristic and deadly pattern:

**Phase 1 (6-24 hours post-ingestion):** First symptoms come late, six
to twenty-four hours (average ten to fourteen hours) after ingestion.
Sharp abdominal pains are followed by violent vomiting and a persistent
choleralike diarrhea (often containing blood and mucus).

**Phase 2 (apparent recovery):** These symptoms tend to subside and the
patient appears to improve. This false recovery period is dangerously
misleading.

**Phase 3 (3-4 days post-ingestion):** The patient's condition begins to
worsen, with symptoms of liver and kidney failure leading to death in
seven to ten days. Autopsy findings include marked gastro-intestinal
edema, hemorrhagic gastro-enteritis, lymph node hyperplasia, fatty
degeneration of the heart and liver, tubular necrosis of the kidneys,
swollen brain with multiple hemorrhages and degenerative nerve cell
damage. Death is primarily from liver and kidney failure.

## Real-World Fatalities

On Whidbey Island, Washington, in 1981, three people picked and ingested
deadly Galerinas, mistaking them for Psilocybes. Fearful of prosecution
for possession of psilocybin mushrooms, they delayed going to hospital
for two days, which is the most critical period for effective treatment.
The girl, sixteen years old, died six days later. Her death is a tragedy
that could have been avoided with proper identification knowledge.

In 1960 and 1961, eight individuals were intoxicated by mushrooms
purported to be Psilocybe baeocystis. One child died after three days of
hospitalization with a 106 degree fever. Autopsy showed cerebral edema.
Upon reexamination, the photographs of the alleged species clearly showed
mushrooms resembling Psilocybe cyanescens, not P. baeocystis. The
identifying mycologist retracted his identification when confronted.

## Other Poisonous Genera

Other brown-spored genera with an abundance of poisonous species include:

- **Inocybe**: Many species contain muscarine. Although some Inocybes
  are psilocybin active (I. aeruginascens, I. coelestium, I. corydalina),
  none have been found to contain both psilocybin and muscarine, and
  identification is extremely difficult even for experts.

- **Cortinarius**: Contains numerous poisonous species.

- **Hebeloma**: Contains poisonous species.

- **Entoloma**: Contains poisonous species.

## Safety Principles

1. **Always take a spore print**: This is the single most important
   identification step. Dark purplish brown to black spore prints
   indicate Psilocybe or Panaeolus. Rusty brown prints indicate the
   possibility of deadly Galerina or other dangerous species.

2. **Galerina as an indicator species**: Stamets notes that he looks
   upon G. autumnalis as an indicator species. When this poisonous
   mushroom is present, Psilocybes are likely to be nearby.

3. **Members of Psilocybe and Panaeolus are generally safe**: Only
   psilocybin, psilocin, and related compounds have been detected thus
   far in these genera. No poisonous species are known from these two
   genera.

4. **Avoid the minor psilocybin genera**: Species in Conocybe, Inocybe,
   and Gymnopilus that produce psilocybin should be avoided by amateurs,
   as their close relatives can be deadly.

5. **If you have any uncertainty, do not eat it**: There is no margin
   for error with amatoxin-containing mushrooms.
