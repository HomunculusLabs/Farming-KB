## Forest, Bamboo, and Plant-Specific Microorganism Culturing

## Overview

Beneficial [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] (LAB) serve as the foundational microorganism in BIM formulations, a diverse community of other beneficial microbes — including photosynthetic bacteria, yeasts, actinomycetes, and fermenting fungi — can be sourced from local environments. This page covers the practical techniques for collecting and culturing these supplementary microorganisms from forests, bamboo groves, specific target plants, and legume root nodules, along with the BIM formulation recipe and best practices for storage and application.

## Lactic Acid Bacteria — The Foundation

Lactic acid bacteria form the backbone (50%) of every BIM formulation. They are collected from air using fermented rice wash, purified through milk saturation, and stored with crude sugar or molasses at a 1:1 ratio. The pure LAB serum is diluted at 1:20 with non-chlorinated water for basic use. LAB produce enzymes and natural antibiotics, control harmful bacteria like salmonella and E. coli, aid decomposition in soil, and improve nutrient assimilation in both plants and animals. For animal applications, pure LAB is used exclusively rather than the full BIM blend.

## Forest Beneficial Microorganisms

### Site Selection

Forest microorganisms are sourced from local aged forests where soil fertility and microbial biodiversity are highest. The key indicators of a good collection site are:

- **Healthy, old, robust trees** — mature trees that have thrived for decades indicate a rich, stable soil ecosystem
- **Deep humus accumulation** — at least 2 feet to 1 yard of decomposed litter, compost, and humus around the base of the tree
- **Active decomposition** — visible signs of ongoing biological breakdown of organic matter

The deep humus layer under old-growth trees harbors an extraordinarily diverse community of aged, adapted beneficial microorganisms that have evolved alongside the local forest ecosystem over many years.

### Collection Technique — Cooked Rice Trapping

The core technique for trapping forest microorganisms uses cooked rice as a carbohydrate bait:

1. **Prepare the container** — Use a flat container with a lid (e.g., a plastic lunch box). Add approximately one inch of cooked rice, leaving ample air space inside. A larger surface area of rice increases the trapping capacity.
2. **Protect from animals** — Cover the container with metal netting or equivalent to prevent rats and other animals from digging it up once buried.
3. **Bury in humus** — Bury the container in the deep litter and humus layer around the selected tree. The rice acts as a food source that attracts local microorganisms.
4. **Wait for colonization** — After 2–10 days (depending on [[root-exudates]] that bamboo roots release into the surrounding soil. These sugary substances act as natural chemo-attractants, drawing in and supporting a robust community of beneficial microbes. In traditional Eastern agricultural practice, bamboo groves have long been recognized as reservoirs of potent microorganisms.

### Collection Technique

The collection method mirrors the forest microorganism technique:

1. Bury a container with cooked rice in the litter and soil around bamboo plants
2. Wait 2–10 days for microbial colonization (visible mold growth on the rice)
3. Add 1/3 crude sugar or molasses and wait one week for the mixture to become sticky and liquid
4. Add equal amount of crude sugar or molasses for storage

Bamboo microorganisms have a specific practical advantage beyond general plant application — they are particularly effective for fermenting animal feeds, making them valuable in livestock production systems.

## Plant-Specific Microorganisms

### Targeted Microbial Collection

This technique allows farmers to trap beneficial microorganisms that are specifically associated with a particular crop they intend to grow. The principle is that healthy, vigorous plants harbor a unique microbial community adapted to support that species.

### Procedure

1. **Select a healthy specimen** — Choose a vigorous, disease-free plant of the target species growing in good conditions
2. **Cut the plant** — Sever the plant at its base
3. **Invert the rice container** — Place a container of cooked rice upside-down (inverted) over the freshly cut plant stump
4. **Allow colonization** — Beneficial microorganisms specific to that plant species will be attracted to and colonize the cooked rice
5. **Culture as usual** — Follow the standard procedure: add 1/3 crude sugar/molasses, wait a week, then add equal sugar/molasses for storage

This method can be applied to virtually any plant species — rice, vegetables, fruit trees, ornamentals — allowing the farmer to build a customized microbial inoculant tailored to each crop.

## Rhizobium Nitrogen-Fixing Bacteria

### The Role of Rhizobium

Rhizobium is one of the most important nitrogen-fixing bacteria in agriculture. When legume seeds are coated with rhizobium inoculant, the bacteria form symbiotic nodules on the legume roots that fix atmospheric nitrogen into the soil, dramatically improving soil fertility and legume growth.

### Simple Culture Method

1. **Identify nodulated legumes** — Locate healthy legume plants, especially those approaching their flowering or fruiting stage, where root nodules are well-developed and nitrogen fixation is at its peak
2. **Harvest the root zone** — Pull out the legume plants, being careful to retain the soil around the root nodules
3. **Mix with crude sugar** — Combine the nodulated soil with an equal ratio of crude sugar
4. **Allow proliferation** — The rhizobium bacteria feed on the sugar and multiply rapidly
5. **Use for seed inoculation** — The resulting culture can be mixed directly with the next batch of legume seeds before planting

## BIM Formulation Recipe

### The 50/50 Rule

The standard Beneficial [[amanita-muscaria-antibiotic-properties-nanobacteria-fungal-medicine]], and rapid nutrient breakdown
- **50% Other Cultured Microorganisms** — A diverse blend of forest, bamboo, and plant-specific microorganisms

### Example Blend

A typical BIM mix might combine:

- 3 parts (50%) lactic acid bacteria
- 1 part forest microorganisms
- 1 part bamboo microorganisms
- 1 part plant-specific microorganisms

The principle is simple: **the more diverse the microbial community, the better**. Farmers are encouraged to experiment with different ratios and source combinations, observe the results, and develop their own optimized formulations based on local conditions and crop needs.

### Application Guidelines by Use

- **For plants** — Use the full BIM formulation (50% LAB + 50% other microbes)
- **For animals** — Use pure lacto bacilli only (isolated and purified as described)
- **For fermenting animal feeds** — Bamboo microorganisms are particularly effective

## Storage and Dilution Ratios

### Storage

- Add **equal amounts of crude sugar or molasses** to the cultured serum to arrest microbial activity and preserve the culture
- Store in a **cool area** — this slows residual microbial metabolism and extends shelf life
- **Never use refined sugar** — chemically bleached refined sugar can harm or kill the beneficial microorganisms
- Crude sugar should be diluted with 1/3 water before mixing with the serum

### Dilution for Use

- **Standard dilution ratio: 1:20** — one part microbial serum to twenty parts non-chlorinated water
- **Critical warning: use non-chlorinated water** — chlorine kills live microorganisms; well water, rainwater, or filtered water is essential
- **Foliar spray for plants** — 2–4 tablespoons of diluted serum per gallon of water
- **Animal feed and water** — 2–4 tablespoons of diluted serum; for larger animals, use without further dilution

## Feeding Microbes with Bionutrients

While carbohydrates and sugars are the primary food sources used during culturing, beneficial microorganisms require a broader nutrient profile for maximum effectiveness in the field. To enhance the efficacy of BIM applications, the following bionutrient supplements are mixed in:

- **[[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]]
- **Brown Rice Vinegar** — provides acetic acid and trace minerals, supporting microbial metabolism and soil pH balance
- **[[microbial-inoculants-and-biological-soil-amendments]]. By combining these diverse microbes with purified lactic acid bacteria at a 50/50 ratio and enriching with bionutrients, farmers can create powerful BIM formulations that improve soil health, plant growth, animal nutrition, and waste decomposition without reliance on commercial imported products. The key principles are sourcing from healthy, biodiverse local environments; using cooked rice as a universal trapping medium; preserving cultures with crude sugar or molasses; and always applying with non-chlorinated water at a 1:20 dilution.
