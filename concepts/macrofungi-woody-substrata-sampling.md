# Macrofungi on Woody Substrata and Sampling Design

## Ascomycetes Identification (Continued)

Identification of tropical agarics presents unique challenges. Artificial keys may or may not work reliably. The recommended approach is:

1. **Try artificial keys first**: Dennis (1970) and Largent and Baroni (1988) are the preferred starting points.
2. **Compare with generic descriptions**: Cross-reference findings against published generic treatments.
3. **Resort to natural keys only if unsatisfactory**: Singer (1986b) provides natural keys but should be used as a secondary resource.

## Macrofungi on Woody Substrata

### General Considerations

Wood-inhabiting macrofungi occupy substrata differing enormously in size, decay state, and moisture content. Community composition includes fungi already present when the tree fell (pathogens, endophytes, heart rots) as well as successional fungi colonizing during deterioration. The full size spectrum must be sampled—from tiny twigs (<1 cm) to large trunks (>20 cm)—to capture the majority of species. Plot size ranges from one to several thousand m², depending on downed wood density. Live woody stems can support conks from decaying internal parts. Gymnosperm hosts support a more restricted mycota than angiosperm hosts. Species richness depends more on the range of decay classes present than on phanerogam species diversity. Most wood-inhabiting fungi lack strong host-genus or host-species specificity, though some ectomycorrhizal species "climb up" on wood to fruit and may obtain nutrients directly from the substrate (Read et al. 1989). Surveys should encompass pathogens, endophytes, and saprobes alike.

### Ecological Roles

Wood-inhabiting fungi play critical roles in forest ecosystems: recycling lignocellulosic and mineral nutrients; softening tissues for animal habitation; facilitating mycorrhizal seedling establishment in decayed wood; serving as moisture sinks for [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] in dry forests; and contributing [[brown-rot]] residues as a major soil component. Standing tree decay occurs as root-rot, butt-rot, or heart-rot (mutually exclusive), predisposing trees to windthrow or breakage. Different fungal species use quite different volumes of substratum for sporocarp production, with important implications for sampling effort.

## Ecological and Evolutionary Questions

Key research questions include: species-area curves for different environments and substrata; effects of habitat variety and woody-plant diversity on mycodiversity; influences of forest productivity and substratum abundance; mainland-island biogeographic patterns; latitudinal gradients (are low latitudes richer?); temporal variation in sporocarp production within and between years; successional changes in species composition through decay stages; and effects of natural (hurricanes, fires) versus anthropogenic (logging, wood removal) disturbances.

## Designing Sampling Protocols

### Core Principles

- **Species richness scales with effort**: species numbers increase with area/substratum sampled; spatial distribution and abundance of woody debris dictate appropriate plot size.
- **Size-class frequency matters**: small debris is far more frequent than large; the area searched must account for diameter-class distribution and fungal frequency within each class.
- **Large-diameter wood is easily undersampled**: logs and standing trees have low density and are often missed in small subplots. Augment small subplot samples with a separate targeted method for large pieces.
- **Asymptote rarely reached**: Scandinavian studies showed species-substrata curves did not reach asymptote; stratification by diameter, decay, and tree species would improve efficiency.
- **Environmental correlates differ by climate**: temperate systems (Heilmann-Clausen 2001) show age, decay stage, soil contact, exposure, moss cover, and soil moisture most strongly correlated with community structure; tropical systems (Puerto Rico) show diameter class and microclimate as most important.

### Inconspicuous and Indistinguishable Species

Resupinate fungi, inconspicuous [[basidiomycetes]], and small ascomycetes are often not detectable in the field. The solution is to remove all woody substrata to the laboratory for examination (precluding resampling unless subplots are divided). Different maturity stages are hard to recognize; do not make assumptions about identity of small ascomycetes—collect all separate patches.

## Plot Size Guidelines

### Small to Medium-Size Woody Debris (1.0–15.0 cm diameter)

Use randomly or regularly spaced subplots. Examine each piece, collect one specimen of every species per piece, record data, and return substratum to its original position. Recommended dimensions: 0.5-m² circular plots for small ascomycetes on branches/twigs; 10-m diameter plots (~78.5 m²) for medium-size wood; practical upper limit of 10–20 m diameter (78.5–314 m²).

### Large Woody Debris, Standing Trees, and Recent Treefalls

Use individual logs or trees as sample units. Sample from base to top, noting specimen locations and decay class. Tag and map each unit for resampling. The point quarter method (Cottam and Curtis 1956) estimates density of large fallen/standing trees along transects; GPS/GIS enables mapping in large areas.

## Data Collection for Woody Substrata

A standardized protocol ensures comparability across studies. Five categories should be recorded for every specimen:

### 1. Substratum/Host Class
- **Type**: trunk, branch, twig, stump, root
- **Condition**: living, dead, dead on living
- **Position**: vertical, prostrate, on ground, suspended

### 2. Substratum Preference (after Mathiassen 1993)

| Code | Description |
|------|-------------|
| W | Wood only |
| Wb | Mainly wood, some bark |
| wb | Equally wood and bark |
| Bw | Mainly bark, some wood |
| B | Bark only |

### 3. Size Class

| Class | Diameter | Class | Diameter |
|-------|----------|-------|----------|
| 1 | <1.0 cm | 4 | 5.1–10.0 cm |
| 2 | 1.0–2.5 cm | 5 | 10.1–20.0 cm |
| 3 | 2.6–5.0 cm | 6 | >20.0 cm |

### 4. Decay Class

| Class | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Hard / not decayed |
| 2 | Hard but decayed (discolored or not) |
| 3 | Softened / punky (typically white rot) |
| 4 | Well decayed / [[humus]]-like (typically brown rot) |

### 5. Bark Condition

- **Tight**: bark firmly attached | **Loose**: present but not firmly attached | **Off**: bark absent

## Summary

Effective sampling of wood-inhabiting macrofungi requires attention to the full range of substratum sizes, decay stages, and environmental conditions. Plot design must accommodate both high-frequency small debris and low-density large woody pieces. Laboratory examination is essential for inconspicuous taxa. Standardized data recording across substratum class, preference, size, decay, and bark condition enables meaningful cross-study comparisons. Sampling frequency should be tailored to target fungal groups.
