One of the most dramatic natural events [[masanobu-fukuoka]] witnessed in his rice fields
was the periodic appearance of enormous spider swarms. These events demonstrated the self-
regulating capacity of a balanced ecosystem and became central to his argument against chemical
pest control. The spider swarm phenomenon illustrates the fundamental principle that nature
maintains its own balance when left undisturbed.

## The Spider Swarm Phenomenon

Fukuoka describes waking one autumn morning to find a neighbour rushing to his door, asking if
he had covered his fields with a silk net overnight. The rice stubble and low-lying grasses had
become completely covered with spider webs, "as though with silk." They waved and sparkled in
the morning mist. Upon close inspection, several spiders occupied every square inch. In a
quarter acre, there must have been thousands, possibly millions.

The spectacle lasted only a day or two. Then strands of web several yards long broke off and
waved in the wind, with five or six spiders clinging to each strand. The young spiders sailed
off in the sky like dandelion fluff or pinecone seeds. Fukuoka calls this "an amazing natural
drama." The phenomenon remains scientifically unexplained: no one knows where the spiders come
from, how they survive winter, or where they go when they disappear. They appear one year and
are absent the next, their population cycles seemingly unconnected to any single variable that
researchers can isolate.

## The Leafhopper Investigation

A professor from the Kochi Prefecture Testing Centre came to investigate why rice leafhoppers
were scarce in Fukuoka's fields despite no insecticide use. The professor examined the habitat,
the balance between insects and natural enemies, and spider propagation rates. He found that
harmful insects were just as scarce in Fukuoka's fields as in the Centre's fields sprayed with
deadly chemicals, while natural predators were far more numerous.

The professor acknowledged that if Fukuoka's method were widely adopted, the leafhopper problem
could be solved. He then got in his car and drove back to Kochi. In the five or six years since,
few changes occurred in Kochi Prefecture. The institutional inertia of agricultural research
centres meant that knowledge which should have been immediately useful was shelved in favour of
further studies that never led to action.

## The Shifting Cast of Predators

Fukuoka told the professor: "Since you are researching spiders, you are interested in only one
among the many natural predators of the leafhopper. This year spiders appeared in great numbers,
but last year it was toads. Before that, it was frogs that predominated. There are countless
variations."

Four or five different spider species inhabit the fields. The dominant predator shifts from year
to year depending on rainfall and other conditions. When heavy rain falls, frogs cause spiders
to disappear. In dry years, neither leafhoppers nor frogs appear. The web of relationships is
too complex for specialized research to capture in its totality. This observation undermines the
entire approach of [[conventional-vs-organic-vs-regenerative|conventional pest management]],
which seeks to identify a single cause and apply a single solution.

## The Ash Incident

Fukuoka once thought there would be nothing wrong with putting fireplace ashes on his fields.
Two or three days later, the field was completely bare of spiders. The ash caused the web
strands to disintegrate. He estimated that thousands of spiders fell victim to a single handful
of apparently harmless ash.

This experience illustrated his broader principle: "Applying an insecticide is not simply a
matter of eliminating the leafhoppers together with their natural predators. Many other
essential dramas of nature are affected." Even organic inputs can disrupt the [[soil- microorganisms-natural-fertility|soil microbiome]] and the insect community in ways that are not
immediately visible.

## The Fusol Disaster in Orchards

The insecticide Fusol was once used in Japanese orchards to control scale insects. The natural
predators were completely exterminated, and the resulting problems still survived in many
prefectures decades later. From this experience, most farmers came to realize that it is
undesirable to eliminate predators because greater insect damage will result in the long run. As
for mites and scales which do appear, a solution of machine oil diluted 200 to 400 times and
sprayed lightly in midsummer is enough. The insect communities should then be left to achieve
their natural balance. This will not work if an organic phosphorous pesticide has already been
used since the predators are also killed by this chemical.

## The Stem Borer Paradox

At the Kochi Testing Centre, Fukuoka carried out experiments on stem borers, insects that enter
and feed on the rice stem, causing the stalk to turn white and wither. One field was sprayed
with insecticide; another was left untreated. When results were calculated, the untreated field
with many withered stalks had the higher yield. The stem borers had attacked the weaker plants,
producing a thinning effect. The withering of some stems left more room for the rest. Sunlight
penetrated to the lower leaves. Remaining rice plants grew more strongly, sent up more grain-
bearing stalks, and produced more grains per head.

Fukuoka noted that only half of research results are typically reported. Results showing lower
yields from chemical treatment are checked off as experimental discrepancies and discarded.
Reports of reduced yields rarely appear in print, especially when published by chemical
companies as advertisements.

## Why Natural Farming Has Not Spread

Despite the evidence, the method did not spread because of the fragmentation of scientific
knowledge. The soil fertility expert did not visit. The crop specialist did not visit. Each
researcher saw only his own specialty and could not grasp the integrated system. When suggested
at conferences, the response was always that more research was needed before approval could be
granted.

Fukuoka also criticized those who tried to mix natural and scientific farming: "The farmer who
moves toward compromise can no longer criticize science at the fundamental level." Self-styled
experts suggested using harvesters or adding fertilizer "in certain cases," which completely
missed the point that the system works as an integrated whole.

## The Morishima Acacia Strategy

In the orchard, Fukuoka implemented a similar predator-support strategy using the Morishima
acacia, introduced from Australia. This tree grows year round, putting out new buds in all
seasons. Aphids multiplied on the buds, which attracted ladybirds. After the ladybirds devoured
all the aphids, they climbed down to the citrus trees and began feeding on mites, arrowhead
scales, and cottony-cushion scales. The tree thus served as a biological nursery for beneficial
insects. The Morishima acacia also fixes nitrogen, acts as a windbreak, provides hard wood, has
flowers that attract bees, and leaves good for fodder. Six to ten trees per quarter acre carry
out soil improvement in the deep soil strata.

## The Green Revolution Contradiction

Fukuoka's observations directly contradict the assumptions of the [[conventional-vs-organic-vs- regenerative|Green Revolution]]. The "Good Rice Movement" depended on weak, improved seed
varieties that required chemicals and insecticides eight to ten times per growing season. In a
short time the soil was burned clean of microorganisms and organic matter. Crops came to be
dependent on nutrients added from the outside in the form of chemical fertilizer.

Fukuoka estimated that abandoning chemicals would cause only about ten percent loss in the first
year, after which nature's recuperative power would cause harvests to increase and surpass
original levels. The most important thing is not to kill the natural predators. Keeping the
field continuously under water or irrigating with stagnant or polluted water also leads to
insect problems. The troublesome summer and fall leafhoppers can be kept under control simply by
keeping water out of the field.

## Broader Ecological Principle

The spider swarm exemplifies [[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s core insight: nature maintains its own
balance when left undisturbed. The problem is not that insects exist but that human disturbance
creates conditions where they become unbalanced. The solution is not to kill specific pests but
to restore the conditions in which natural predators thrive. This principle extends beyond rice
fields to [[fukuoka-textdoc-attaining-natural-form-fruit-trees|orchard management]], [[fukuoka- vegetable-disease-insect-resistance|vegetable growing]], and all forms of agriculture.
