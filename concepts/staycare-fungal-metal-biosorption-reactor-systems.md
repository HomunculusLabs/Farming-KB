# Fungal Metal Biosorption Reactor Systems

Fungal biosorption is the passive uptake of dissolved metal ions by fungal
biomass, primarily through interactions with cell wall functional groups.
Unlike active bioaccumulation, biosorption does not require metabolic energy
and can occur using both living and non-living fungal material. Reactor systems
for fungal metal biosorption have been developed to treat industrial effluents
containing heavy metals at scale, converting laboratory findings into
practical engineering solutions.

## Biosorption Fundamentals

Biosorption relies on physicochemical interactions between metal ions in
solution and binding sites present in the fungal cell wall. Key binding
groups include carboxyl, phosphate, amino, sulfhydryl, and hydroxyl
moieties found in structural polymers such as chitin, glucans, and
glycoproteins. The binding mechanisms involve ion exchange, complexation,
coordination, and in some cases crystallization or precipitation on the
biomass surface.

Maximum uptake levels for cationic metals typically range from 0.1 to 1.0
mmol per gram of dry weight. Members of the order Mucorales, particularly
Rhizopus and Mucor species, consistently show high biosorption capacities
across a broad range of metals. Uranium (as the uranyl ion) and lead are
among the most readily biosorbed metals, with uptake values frequently
exceeding 0.8 mmol per gram.

## Biomass Preparation and Immobilization

Industrial-scale processing requires stable biosorbent particles. Three
principal immobilization approaches are used:

### Biofilm Formation on Inert Matrices
Fungi can be grown as biofilms on inert carriers such as coal, sand, or
foam particles. While conceptually simple, this method often suffers from
poor stability of the resulting biosorbent and limitations in reactor
performance under continuous flow conditions.

### Entrapment in Polymeric Matrices
Natural polymers such as sodium alginate and carrageenan produce porous
gel beads that allow substrate diffusion while protecting the biomass.
Functional groups within these matrices may alter biosorption capacity.
Synthetic polymers including polyacrylamide, polysulfone, and polyvinyl
alcohol offer greater mechanical stability and can retain 70 to 90 percent
of binding efficiency compared to untreated cells.

### Chemical Cross-Linking
Cross-linking agents such as formaldehyde, divinylsulfone, and
formaldehyde-urea mixtures can increase metal uptake capacity by 10 to 30
percent relative to untreated biomass. Cross-linked Penicillium and
Rhizopus biomass has demonstrated enhanced stability for repeated use
in packed column configurations.

## Reactor Configurations

### Batch Systems
Most biosorption studies have been conducted in batch reactors with
varying agitation rates. These systems are straightforward to operate and
control, making them ideal for establishing equilibrium and kinetic data.
However, batch systems are impractical for treating large volumes of
dilute metal-bearing effluents.

### Packed Bed Columns
Packed bed reactors are the most commonly applied continuous flow
configuration for biosorption. Immobilized fungal biomass is packed into
columns through which contaminated water passes. Residence time is a
critical parameter; insufficient contact time markedly decreases metal
removal. Reductions in uptake efficiency of 30 to 70 percent relative
to batch values have been reported for fungal mycelial pellets in packed
columns, often attributed to hydrogen ion competition or mass transfer
limitations within the column.

### Multistage Reactor Systems
Stirred batch and continuous multistage reactors using fungal biomass
have been examined for improved performance. These systems employ
hollow-fiber microfiltration or centrifugation for biomass-liquid
separation and are amenable to mathematical modelling for process
optimization.

## Equilibrium Modelling

Biosorption data are typically expressed as equilibrium isotherms. The
Langmuir model relates metal biosorbed to equilibrium concentration
through a saturation curve, providing estimates of maximum sorption
capacity and binding affinity. The Freundlich model describes
multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Both models generally
show good agreement with experimental data where only passive
biosorption is involved.

Binary and multicomponent isotherms extend these models to systems
containing multiple metals, as is common in industrial effluents.
Three-dimensional sorption isotherm surfaces can predict the effects
of untested co-ion concentrations on individual metal uptake levels.

## Environmental Factors

### pH Effects
Optimal pH for cation biosorption typically falls in the 4 to 7 range.
Below pH 4, hydrogen ions compete for binding sites and diminish uptake.
Above pH 7, metal hydrolysis and precipitation can occur, increasing
apparent removal but not representing true biosorption. For anionic
species such as chromate (Cr(VI)), the optimum pH is much lower, in the
1 to 2 range, where protonated functional groups attract anions
electrostatically.

### Temperature
Temperature has relatively little effect on biosorption levels within
normal ranges of 5 to 30 degrees Celsius, consistent with a passive
physicochemical mechanism rather than a metabolic process.

### Competing Ions
Cation competition typically diminishes the uptake of each individual
metal, though total biosorption levels may remain unchanged. The
simultaneous biosorption of cationic and anionic metal species has
received little research attention despite its industrial relevance.

## Challenges for Industrial Application

Despite extensive laboratory characterization, industrial application of
fungal biosorption remains limited. Key barriers include inconsistent
biomass quality, difficulty scaling up production of reproducible
biosorbent materials, competition from conventional ion exchange
resins, and the challenge of spent biosorbent disposal or metal
recovery. The technology shows greatest promise for treating dilute
effluent streams where conventional methods are cost-prohibitive.
