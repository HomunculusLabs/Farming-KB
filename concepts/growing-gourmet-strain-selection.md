# Strain Selection

When a mushroom is brought into culture from the wild, little is known about its performance until trials are conducted. Each mushroom strain is unique, and even within the same species, strains vary to surprising degrees. [[mycopesticides-stamets]] provides a comprehensive 28-feature evaluation checklist for selecting and developing mushroom strains, along with detailed guidance on the stock culture library, the Stamets P-Value system for tracking strain age, and strategies for preventing genetic decline.

## The Nature of Mushroom Strains

A cultivator develops an intimate, co-dependent relationship with every mushroom strain. The features that emerge express the idiosyncrasies of each culture. Since tons of mushrooms are generated from a few petri dish cultures in a matter of weeks, these factors play essential roles in the success of each production run. Once familiar with a particular culture, variations from the norm alert the cultivator to possible genetic decline or mutation.

## Warning Signs of Genetic Decline

One of the first features in the tell-tale decline of a strain is "mushroom aborts." Aborting mushrooms represent failures in the mushroom colony to sustain total yield of all members to full maturity. The next classic symptom is the decline in the population of primordia. Fewer and fewer primordia appear, and those which do form are often dwarfs with deformed caps.

Other warning signs:
- Delay in fruiting schedules
- Increasing susceptibility to disease
- Inability to colonize media, produce primordia, or develop healthy mushrooms
- Slowing of mycelial growth rate across successive transfers

A good strain is easy to keep, and difficult or impossible to regain once it senesces. Cultivators must continuously scrutinize new strains to find candidates worthy of production.

## The 28 Features for Evaluating a Mushroom Strain

### 1. Recovery Time
The time for a mushroom strain to recover from the concussion of inoculation. Often referred to as "leap off." Oyster and Morel strains are renowned for quick recovery, evident in as short as 24 hours. Some strains show poor recovery and are difficult to grow commercially unless re-invigorated through strain development. Visible recovery of spawn two days after inoculation is considered good; one day is considered excellent.

### 2. Rate of Growth
Strains differ substantially in their rate of growth at all stages. The fastest species is Morels, typically covering a standard 100 x 15 mm petri dish in 3-5 days at 75F (24C). Oyster strains typically take 5-10 days. Strains taking more than 3 weeks to colonize agar, grain, or bulk substrates are susceptible to contamination.

### 3. Quality of the Mycelial Mat
Under ideal conditions, the mycelial mat expands and thickens with numerous hyphal branches. A mycelium properly matched with its substrate forms a mat tenacious in character, requiring considerable strength to rip apart. This is especially true of Oyster, King Stropharia, and Psilocybe mushrooms. Under less than perfect conditions, the mycelial mat is finer and less dense, and the substrate falls apart with ease.

### 4. Adaptability to Substrates
Some strains are well known for their adaptability. Oyster mushrooms can be grown on cereal straws, corn stalks, sugar cane bagasse, coffee leaves, and paper. Although most strains can grow vegetatively on a wide assortment of substrates, many are narrowly specific for mushroom production.

### 5. Speed of Colonization to Fruiting
One group produces mushrooms directly after colonization (Oyster, Lion's Mane, Paddy Straw). Others require sustained resting periods after colonization, sometimes taking weeks or months (Woodlovers like Hypholoma).

### 6. Microflora Dependability/Sensitivity
Some species require a living community of microorganisms. King Stropharia, Zhu Ling, and Button Mushroom require microbially enriched soil or casing layers. Lepista nuda falls into an intermediate category -- not absolutely dependent but quick to fruit when paired with microflora.

### 7. Photosensitivity
Most gourmet and medicinal mushrooms require and favorably react to light. Light affects development in two ways: primordia form when exposed to light, and light sustains normal cap development. Without sustained light post-primordia formation, Oyster mushrooms malform with elongated stems and undeveloped caps. Oyster, Shiitake, and Reishi demonstrate strong photosensitivity.

### 8. Requirement for Cold Shock
For most temperate strains, core substrate temperature must drop below 60-65F (15-18C) before primordia form. Strains isolated from subtropical or tropical climates generally do not require cold shock. Warm weather strains grow more quickly, fruiting in half the time. Experienced cultivators cycle strains through their facility to match prevailing seasons.

### 9. Requirement for High Temperature
[[volvariella-volvacea]] will not produce below 75F (24C) and most strains die below 45F (7.2C). [[pleurotus-citrinopileatus]] fruits when temperatures exceed 65F (18C). With temperature-tolerant strains, higher temperatures cause faster development.

### 10. Number and Distribution of Primordial Sites
A good strain produces a population of numerous, evenly distributed primordia within a short time frame. For cultivators growing Oyster mushrooms in plastic columns, site-specific response to low CO2 at punched holes is a valuable trait that facilitates controlled cropping.

### 11. Site-Specific Response to Low CO2
Strains vary in their sensitivity to CO2 fluctuations. Those super-sensitive to CO2 levels form primordia at sites of oxygen availability (punched holes in bags), giving cultivators a powerful tool for scheduling and controlled cropping.

### 12. Primordia Forming vs. Maturing
Ideally 90% of primordia mature to edible size. Poor strains have 50% or more fail to grow to maturity under ideal conditions. Aborted primordia become sites of contamination by molds, bacteria, and flies.

### 13. Viable Primordia for 2nd and 3rd Flushes
Some cold-weather Oyster and Button strains form the majority of primordia during the first initiation. Many primordia lay dormant yet viable for weeks, developing after the first flush matures and is harvested.

### 14. Duration Between Flushes
The shorter the period between breaks, the better. Strains with long dormancy between breaks are more susceptible to exploitation by insects and molds. By the third flush, a cultivator should have harvested 90% of the potential crop.

### 15. Spore Load Factors
Strains that are not heavy spore producers at harvest time are attractive. Massive spore release by Oyster mushrooms is an environmental hazard that can clog fan motors. The Button mushroom has been genetically selected for small gills, thick flesh, and short stem, extending shelf life.

### 16. Appearance: Form, Size, and Color
The ideal stage for harvest is midway between incurved and decurved cap margins. Young mushrooms are more flavorful, tighter fleshed, often more colorful, and ship and store longer. Crop rotation with much less spore load is accelerated through harvest of adolescent forms.

### 17. Duration from Storage to Spoilage (Preservation)
Shiitake stores and ships far better than Oyster mushrooms. Slow-forming cold weather Oyster strains survive cold storage longer than warm weather varieties. Once spores are released and germinate, bacterial infection quickly sets in. Shiitake harvested by cutting has a stem butt that browns and hardens, forming a protective skin. Most [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]] strains lack this defense.

### 18. Abatement of Growth Subsequent to Harvest
Many mushrooms continue to enlarge, flatten, and produce spores after picking. This places growers and distant distributors into opposing viewpoints about quality. P. pulmonarius "sajor-caju" is described as "biologically out-of-control" in this regard.

### 19. Necrosis Factors
After picking, tissue remnants become attack sites for predator insects and parasitic molds. Shiitake's woody stem browns and hardens, forming a protective coat. P. ostreatus lacks this defense, requiring that no dead tissue remain during harvest.

### 20. Genetic Stability/Instability
Since all strains eventually senesce, genetic stability is of paramount concern. A strain dying shows inability to colonize media, produce primordia, or develop healthy mushrooms.

### 21. Flavor
Strains differ substantially. Americans favor mildly flavored mushrooms; Japanese prefer stronger flavors. Pleurotus citrinopileatus is extremely astringent until thoroughly cooked. Younger mushrooms are generally better-flavored. Shiitake has many flavor dimensions influenced by cap surface moisture and cracking (Donko forms).

### 22. Texture
Judging the best combination of texture and flavor is highly subjective, often influenced by cultural traditions. Most connoisseurs prefer slightly crispy and chewy but not tough. Steamed mushrooms are usually limp and soft; tearing rather than cutting preserves firmness.

### 23. Aroma
Few experiences arouse as much interest in eating gourmet mushrooms as their aroma. The aroma signature of each species is distinctive and undergoes complex transformations during cooking as water is lost and cells are tenderized.

### 24. Sensitivity to Essential Elements
Gray Leatham (1989) found that nanograms of tin and nickel were critical to successful Shiitake fruitbody formation. Without these trace amounts, mycelium is incapable of fruiting. Manganese also plays a determinate role. These compounds are abundant in nature but may be lacking in artificial wood-free media.

### 25. Ability to Surpass Competitors
Strains can be measured by their ability to resist competitor fungi, bacteria, and insects, especially Trichoderma (green mold). Oyster mushrooms are recognized for nematode-trapping abilities. Pleurotus spp., Stropharia rugoso-annulata, and [[ganoderma-lucidum-reishi-comprehensive-profile]] are abundant producers of secondary metabolites (complex acids and metabolites) that forestall competition.

### 26. Nutritional Composition
Mushrooms are a rich source of amino acids (proteins), minerals, and vitamins. Percentages can vary between strains due to different conversion efficiencies of base substrate components into mushroom flesh.

### 27. Production of Primary and Secondary Metabolites
All fungi produce extracellular enzymes that break down food sources. Secondary metabolites usually occur well after colonization. The yellow fluid exudate frequently seen at the bottom of aged spawn containers is an example.

### 28. Production of Medicinal Compounds
Bound within cell walls are heavy molecular weight polysaccharides unique to science, named for the genus in which first found: lentinan (Shiitake), flammulin/FVP (Enokitake), grifolin/grifolan (Maitake). Asian research shows these enhance the human immune system. Cell components are more concentrated in the compact mushroom form than in loose mycelial networks. Precursors in the substrate may play determinant roles when matched with various strains.

## The Stock Culture Library (Genetic Bank)

Once a mushroom strain is taken into culture, whether from spores or tissue, the resultant strains can be preserved for decades under normal refrigeration, perhaps centuries under liquid nitrogen. The Culture Library is the pivotal center of any mushroom cultivation enterprise. Good mushroom strains are hard to come by compared to the number of poor performers isolated from nature.

### Test Tube Slants
Cultures are stored in test tubes filled with media, sterilized and laid at a 15-20 degree angle to cool. One petri dish culture can inoculate 50-100 test tube slants measuring 100 x 20 mm. After incubation for 1-4 weeks, the test tube cultures are placed into cold storage.

**Sealing:** The gap between screw cap and glass tube should be sealed with commercially available elastic, wax-like film (Parafilm). Slants not sealed are prone to contaminate with molds after several months of cold storage.

**Storage temperature:** 35F (1-2C). Culture slants at room temperature have a maximum life of 6-12 months; under refrigeration they survive for 5 years or more. Temperature fluctuation should be limited to 2-3F (1C) to prevent condensation spreading contaminants. When cultures freeze and thaw repeatedly, they die.

**Multiple backups:** Three to four zip-lock bags, each containing 4 slants, stored in at least two locations remote from the main laboratory. This prevents events like fires, electrical failure, or other disasters from destroying the most valuable asset.

**Annual rotation:** Retrieve one or two representative culture slants every year, bring to room temperature for 48 hours, and subculture to newly filled media dishes. Once revived and determined contamination-free, subculture back into test tube slants and return to refrigeration. This circular path ascertains viability and prolongs storage with a minimum number of cell divisions.

**Exception:** Volvariella volvacea should not be chilled. Drastic die-back occurs below 45F (7.2C). Store at no less than 50F (10C) and test frequently for viability.

### Cryogenic Storage
The best way to preserve a strain. Liquid nitrogen vessels held at -302F (-150C). Test tubes filled with specially designed cryoprotectant media (10% glycerol and dextrose) help mycelium survive the shock. Wang and Jong (1990) found that a slow, controlled cooling rate of -1C per minute resulted in higher survival rates than sudden immersion. Strains were better preserved on grain media than agar media for liquid nitrogen storage.

## The Stamets "P" Value System

The Stamets "P" Value (SPV) is an arithmetic scale for measuring the expansion of mycelium through successive transfers from one petri dish to the next. When a culture has been isolated from contaminants, the first pure culture is designated P1. When mycelium fills that dish, the next dish receiving the mycelium is called P2, and so on.

Each culture dish is labelled with:
- Date
- Species
- Collection number
- Strain code
- P-Value
- Medium (if necessary)
