# Mycofiltration

Mycofiltration is the use of fungal mycelium as a biological filter medium to
remove biological and chemical contaminants from water. Developed and
popularized by Paul Stamets, the technique exploits the dense, branching
structure of mycelial mats and the metabolic activity of living fungi to trap
sediment, consume bacteria, sequester heavy metals, and break down dissolved
organic pollutants. Mycofiltration is a component of the broader mycorestoration
framework, which encompasses multiple fungal strategies for environmental
healing.

## Principles

The mycelium of saprophytic fungi forms extensive networks of hyphae that create
a physically dense matrix with enormous surface area. When water passes through
a mycelial mat, several filtration mechanisms operate simultaneously. Physical
straining traps particulate matter, including sediment, protozoan cysts, and
bacterial cells. Biological predation occurs as the mycelium actively captures
and digests bacteria, including pathogens such as Escherichia coli and
Salmonella species. Chemical adsorption binds dissolved pollutants, including
heavy metals and hydrophobic organic compounds, to fungal cell wall components.
Enzymatic degradation breaks down complex organic molecules into simpler,
less harmful metabolites.

## Construction of Mycofilters

A typical mycofilter consists of mycelium grown on a substrate material such as
straw, wood chips, sawdust, or coconut coir. The substrate is inoculated with
fungal spawn, typically of oyster mushroom species (Pleurotus spp.), which are
 vigorous colonizers and produce dense, tenacious mycelial networks. Once fully
colonized, usually within two to four weeks depending on temperature and
substrate, the mycelial mat is ready for deployment. Mycofilters can be
constructed as burlap sack barriers placed in drainage ditches, as layered mats
in treatment troughs, or as living buffers along waterways.

## Applications

### Agricultural Runoff

One of the primary applications of mycofiltration is the treatment of
agricultural runoff, which carries excess nutrients (particularly nitrogen and
phosphorus), sediment, pesticides, and livestock waste into waterways.
Mycofilters placed at the edge of fields or in drainage channels can
significantly reduce the load of these pollutants before they reach streams,
rivers, and groundwater. Stamets has demonstrated that mycofilter barriers
placed around livestock operations can dramatically reduce coliform bacteria
counts in runoff water.

### Stormwater Management

Urban stormwater carries a complex mixture of pollutants including petroleum
hydrocarbons from roads, heavy metals from roofing materials, and nutrients
from landscaping. Mycofilters integrated into stormwater treatment systems, such
as bioswales and retention basins, can provide an additional biological
treatment stage. The mycelium captures fine particulates and degrades dissolved
contaminants that conventional settling and filtration may miss.

### Erosion Control

Mycelial mats stabilize soil and prevent erosion on steep slopes, stream banks,
and construction sites. The hyphal network binds soil particles together,
improving aggregate stability and resistance to water erosion. As the mycelium
decomposes, it contributes organic matter that improves soil structure and water
holding capacity, creating a positive feedback loop for site stabilization.

### Pathogen Reduction

The antibacterial properties of many fungi make mycofiltration effective for
reducing pathogen loads in water. Oyster mushroom mycelium produces natural
antibiotics and secretes enzymes that lyse bacterial cell walls. Field studies
have shown significant reductions in coliform bacteria and other indicators of
fecal contamination in water passing through mycofilter installations.

## Fungal Species for Mycofiltration

Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus and related species) are the most commonly
used fungi for mycofiltration due to their aggressive growth, broad substrate
tolerance, strong antibacterial activity, and effectiveness at degrading
organic pollutants. King stropharia (Stropharia rugosoannulata) is another
excellent candidate, valued for its tolerance of woody substrates and its
effectiveness in garden and landscape settings. Shiitake (Lentinula edodes) and
wine cap mushrooms (Stropharia rugosoannulata) have also been investigated for
filtration applications.

## Limitations

Mycofiltration systems require maintenance and periodic replacement as the
mycelial mat eventually senesces or becomes clogged with trapped material.
Performance is seasonal, with reduced activity during cold weather. The
technique is best suited as a component of integrated water treatment systems
rather than a standalone solution. Long-term performance data from large-scale
installations remains limited, and more research is needed to optimize design
parameters and predict treatment efficiency across different contaminant
profiles and flow conditions.
