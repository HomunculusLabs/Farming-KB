## What Is Tempeh

Tempeh is a traditional Indonesian fermented food made from cooked and inoculated legumes or grains bound together by a dense white mycelium of Rhizopus oligosporus. Originating on the island of Java, tempeh [[comparison-tempeh-vs-miso]] has been a staple protein source in Indonesia for centuries. The fermentation process makes the beans more digestible, reduces anti-nutritional factors, and creates a firm, nutty, cake-like product that can be sliced, crumbled, marinated, fried, grilled, or steamed.

## The Inoculant: Rhizopus Oligosporus

Tempeh fermentation relies on Rhizopus oligosporus, a zygomycete fungus that:

- Produces a strong white mycelium that binds the substrate into a solid cake
- Produces enzymes that break down proteins, oils, and complex carbohydrates
- Inhibits many harmful bacteria through antibiotic compounds
- Grows optimally at 85-90F (30-32C)
- Requires oxygen (unlike anaerobic ferments like miso or kimchi)

The inoculant can be purchased as tempeh starter (spores mixed with rice flour) from specialty suppliers. Store starter in the refrigerator or freezer for long shelf life. (see [[comparison-fermented-vegetables-vs-canning]]).

## Soybean Tempeh (Traditional)

### Ingredients

- 500g dried soybeans
- 1-2 teaspoons tempeh starter (Rhizopus oligosporus spores)
- 1 tablespoon white vinegar (optional — lowers pH to favor Rhizopus over contaminants)

### Step 1: Dehulling

Soybean hulls (seed coats) must be removed for good tempeh. The hulls harbor bacteria and prevent the mycelium from binding the beans tightly. (see [[straw-based-mushroom-substrate-preparation]]).

Methods:
- Split-hull method: soak beans 8-12 hours, split by rubbing or crushing lightly, then float off the hulls in water (hulls float, beans sink). This is the most common home method.
- Peeling: individually peel soaked beans — tedious for large batches
- Pre-dehulled soybeans: purchase split soybeans (sold as "dehulled soybeans" or "soybean splits") to skip this step

### Step 2: Cooking

1. Boil dehulled beans in plenty of water for 30-45 minutes until tender
2. Beans should be fully cooked but not mushy — they should hold their shape
3. Drain thoroughly; spread on towels to surface-dry for 15-30 minutes
4. Beans should be dry to the touch but still internally moist

### Step 3: Inoculation

1. Cool beans to below body temperature (85F / 30C or lower) — heat kills the spores
2. Sprinkle tempeh starter evenly over the beans
3. Toss thoroughly to distribute spores — every bean needs contact with spores
4. If using vinegar, sprinkle it during mixing to lower the pH slightly

### Step 4: Poking Holes in the Bag

1. Place inoculated beans into perforated containers:
   - Ziplock bags with holes poked every 1/2 inch using a clean needle or skewer
   - Specialty tempeh incubation bags with pre-formed perforations
   - Banana leaves (traditional — authentic but impractical for most home setups)
2. Pack beans firmly but not tightly into an even layer about 1 inch thick
3. Seal the bag or container

### Step 5: Incubation

1. Place in a warm environment at 85-90F (30-32C) for 24-48 hours
2. Incubation options:
   - Dedicated tempeh incubator (commercial or DIY)
   - Oven with the light on (monitor temperature carefully)
   - Seedling heat mat in an insulated box
   - Near a radiator or heating vent (use a thermometer)
3. The mycelium will be visible within 12-18 hours as white fuzz
4. By 24-36 hours, beans should be fully bound into a solid white cake
5. Check for even coverage — bare spots indicate poor inoculation or airflow issues

### Step 6: Harvesting

Tempeh is ready when:
- The cake is firm and holds together when handled
- Mycelium is fully white with no bare spots
- The internal temperature has peaked and is beginning to drop
- A pleasant mushroomy/nutty aroma is present

If any black spots appear, these are sporulation — the tempeh is still edible but past its prime. Harvest before significant sporulation.

## Other Substrates

Tempeh can be made from many legumes and grains:

- Black beans — produce a darker tempeh with earthier flavor
- Chickpeas — mild, slightly sweet tempeh
- Lentils — quick-cooking, good for beginners
- Mung beans — lighter color, delicate flavor
- Fava beans — traditional in some regions
- Mixed grain-legume tempeh: combine soybeans with rice, millet, or quinoa for varied texture
- Peanut tempeh — traditional Indonesian variety (botanically a legume)

When using non-soy substrates:
- Cook until fully tender
- Reduce or eliminate the dehulling step (most other beans do not need it)
- Monitor moisture carefully — different beans have different water content
- Fermentation times may vary by 4-8 hours

## Storage

- Fresh tempeh: wrap in breathable material, refrigerate for up to 5 days
- Freezing: vacuum-seal or wrap tightly, freeze for up to 6 months; texture holds well
- Dehydrated tempeh: slice thin and dehydrate at 130F for 8-12 hours for jerky-like strips
- Do not store fresh tempeh at room temperature — it will continue fermenting and eventually spoil

## Cooking with Tempeh

- Steaming (10-15 minutes): softens texture, removes bitterness, prepares for other cooking
- Pan-frying: slice thin, cook in oil until golden and crispy
- Deep-frying: traditional Indonesian preparation, especially for tempeh goreng
- Grilling: marinate and grill slices or slabs
- Crumbling: sautee crumbled tempeh as a ground meat substitute
- Smoking: cold-smoke tempeh slices for a bacon-like flavor
- Marinating: soy sauce, mirin, garlic, ginger, and sesame oil make excellent marinades

## Troubleshooting

- No mycelium growth — temperature too low, starter expired, or beans too wet
- Patchy growth — uneven inoculation, poor air circulation, or beans too dry
- Black spots — sporulation; harvest immediately, still edible
- Yellow, orange, or pink spots — possible contamination; if widespread or foul-smelling, discard
- Ammonia smell — over-fermentation; the tempeh has gone too long
- Slimy texture — bacterial contamination; discard
