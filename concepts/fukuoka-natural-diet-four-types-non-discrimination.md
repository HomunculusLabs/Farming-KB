# Fukuoka Four Types of Natural Diet and Non-Discrimination

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s philosophy of natural diet extends beyond nutrition into a
spiritual understanding of humanity's relationship with nature. He identifies
four types of diet, critiques modern food systems, and proposes a vision of
eating grounded in non-discrimination and divine will.

## The Staff of Life

Fukuoka begins with a fundamental principle: "Taste and nutrition should not be
separated. What is nourishing and good for the human body should stimulate the
human appetite of its own accord and serve as delicious food. Flavor and nutrition
must be one." When food is whole and natural, the body's own discernment guides
eating — no charts or scoring systems are required.

He points to farmers whose simple meals of barley and rice with unrefined soy
sauce and pickled vegetables sustained them in health, strength, and long life.
Their diet emerged directly from the land and seasons, not from external
standards.

Fukuoka delivers a sharp critique of [[fukuoka-critique-western-dietetics]], particularly the
four-group scoring method. "This appears to be eminently sensible and safe, but
what happens when such a system is employed uniformly on a large scale?" The
answer is a cascade of complications: year-round supply, mass production,
long-term storage, and out-of-season vegetables — all severing food from its
natural context. "Out-of-season vegetables and fruit are unnatural and
incomplete."

The scientific pursuit of complete nutrition has a paradoxical result:
"Scientists see themselves as working to ensure that people get all the
nourishment they need anywhere and anytime, but this is having the opposite
effect of making it increasingly difficult to obtain anything but incomplete
nourishment."

He captures the limits of analytical knowledge with a striking metaphor: "Man can
dissect a butterfly and examine it in the greatest detail, but he cannot make it
fly. And even were this possible, he cannot know the heart of the butterfly."

## Four Types of Diet

Fukuoka classifies human eating into four progressively deeper stages, each
reflecting a different relationship between the individual, food, and nature.

### 1. Empty Diet

"A lax, self-indulgent diet, influenced by the external world that submits to
cravings and fancies. This diet, directed by the mind, might be called an empty
diet."

### 2. Scientific Diet

"The physically centered diet of most people, where nutritional food is consumed
to sustain the body. This is a scientific diet that spins centrifugally outward
with increasing desires."

### 3. Diet of Principle

"The diet of natural man based on spiritual laws. Extending beyond Western
science and centered on [[fukuoka-western-eastern-philosophy-agriculture]], it places restrictions on foods,
aiming for centripetal convergence."

### 4. Non-Discriminating Diet

"A diet that lays aside all human knowledge and by which one eats without
discrimination in accordance with divine will. This is the ideal natural diet."

## The Diet of Non-Discrimination

The non-discriminating diet represents Fukuoka's ultimate vision. "This is
founded on the view that man does not live through his own efforts but was
created and is supported by nature." Food is not an object to be selected or
improved upon: "Food is not something that man selects from within nature, it is
a gift bestowed upon him from above."

"A true natural diet becomes possible only when food, the body, and the soul
fuse together completely within nature." When this fusion occurs, the artificial
boundaries between self and environment dissolve, and eating becomes effortless
and joyful.

Fukuoka describes the experience vividly: "To follow the will of the body and
desire freely, to eat when a food is delicious and forbear when it is not, to
partake without restraint, without plan or intention, is to enjoy the most
subtle and exquisite fare — an ideal diet."

## The Diet of Principle

The diet of principle serves as a practical bridge between scientific eating and
the non-discriminating ideal. Its foundational insight: "All things exist in
nature. Nothing is lacking; nothing is present in excess. The foods of nature
are complete and whole in and of themselves."

Because nature's offerings are already complete, elaborate preparation,
combination, and selection are unnecessary. "It is only fitting that nature is
not subject to man's criteria, to his choosing and rejecting, his cooking and
combining."

"It is already enough to eat cooked and salted food, to consume all things in
moderation, to gather foods of the seasons that grow close at hand."

He extends this into a critique of global food systems: "People must realize
that a diet of surfeit which relies on foods from far-off lands leads the world
astray and invites human ills."

## The Diet of the Sick

Fukuoka applies the label "sick" not only to the clinically ill but to modern
civilization as a whole. "Illness begins when man moves away from nature, and
the severity with which he is afflicted is proportional to his estrangement."

He cautions against rigid prescriptions: "To push upon people living in
different environments and of different races and temperaments a rigid,
standardized natural diet is an impossibility." Nature is not uniform, and any
attempt to impose a single dietary template ignores the particularity of place,
season, and individual constitution.

"Nature is a fluid entity that changes from moment to moment. Right and left do
not exist in nature, so there is no happy medium, no good and evil, no yin or
yang." Attempts to systematize nature into categories inevitably miss the living
reality.

His most provocative statement: "Those very people normally thought of as
healthy I would call the seriously ill; saving them is of the greatest
importance."

## Conclusion — The Trinity

Fukuoka's philosophy of diet cannot be separated from his broader vision:
"[[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]], natural diet, and natural healing are all part of one whole."
These three domains arise together from a single orientation toward life.

"Both natural diet [[fukuoka-textdoc-three-stages-of-human-collapse-and-natural-farming-future]] can be achieved only by natural people.
This trinity begins and is realized at once. The goal of all three elements is
the creation of ideal man."

He closes with urgency: "Unless we succeed very soon in clarifying what nature
is and what man should and should not do, there will be no turning back." The
choice between natural and artificial diet is not merely a matter of personal
health but of civilizational direction — whether humanity will find its way back
to nature or continue down the path of estrangement.
