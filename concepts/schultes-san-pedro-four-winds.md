# San Pedro Cactus of the Four Winds

The San Pedro cactus (*Echinopsis pachanoi*, syn. *[[trichocereus-pachanoi]]*)
represents undoubtedly one of the most ancient of the magic plants of South
America. A fast-growing columnar cactus of the Cactus family (Cactaceae),
San Pedro has been employed in Andean shamanism for at least three
millennia and continues to play a central role in the curanderismo healing
traditions of Peru, Ecuador, and Bolivia.

## Archaeological Evidence and Chavin Culture

The oldest archaeological evidence of San Pedro use consists of a stone
carving in a temple at Chavin de Huantar in northern Peru, dating to 1300
BCE. Almost equally old textiles from the Chavin culture depict the cactus
in association with jaguar and hummingbird figures. Peruvian ceramics made
between 1000 and 700 BCE show the plant alongside the deer; others from
several hundred years later feature the cactus with the jaguar and
stylized spirals illustrating the hallucinogenic experiences induced by the
plant. On the southern coast of Peru, large ceramic urns of the Nazca
culture, dated 100 BCE to 500 CE, prominently depict San Pedro.

## Spanish Colonial Accounts

The use of *Trichocereus* was widespread in Peru when the Spanish arrived.
One ecclesiastical report described how shamans "drink a beverage they call
Achuma which is a water they make from the sap of some thick and smooth
cacti," and that "after they drink it they remain without judgment and
deprived of their senses, and they see visions that the devil represents
to them." As with peyote in Mexico, the Roman Catholic Church fought
vigorously against the San Pedro cactus: "This is the plant with which the
devil deceived the Indians in their paganism, using it for their lies and
superstitions... those who drink lose consciousness and remain as if dead."

## Curanderismo and Modern Use

San Pedro is now employed in curanderismo (folk healing) to cure sickness,
including alcoholism and insanity, for divination, to undo love witchcraft,
to counter all kinds of sorcery, and to ensure success in personal
ventures. It is the principal one of many "magical" plants known to and
used by shamans, collected near sacred lagoons high in the Andes. At these
lagoons, shamans go annually for purification and to visit special
individuals, experts in sorcery and "owners" of divine plants capable of
awakening, with San Pedro, supernatural spiritual powers.

The overall context of the moon-oriented ritual surrounding its use
indicates that it is truly an amalgam of pagan and Christian elements, a
syncretism visible in the name itself, possibly originating in the
Christian belief that Saint Peter holds the keys to heaven.

## The Four Winds Symbolism

Shamans specify four "kinds" of the cactus, distinguished by the number of
ribs. Those with four ribs are rare and considered the most potent, with
very special supernatural powers, since the four ribs represent the "four
winds" and the "four roads." As Douglas Sharon observed, "Four-ribbed
cacti are considered to be very rare and very lucky... to have special
properties because they correspond to the 'four winds' and the 'four
roads,' supernatural powers associated with the cardinal points."

## Regional Names

The cactus is known in northern coastal Peru as San Pedro, in the northern
Andean area as Huachuma, and in Bolivia as Achuma. The Bolivian term
chumarse ("to get drunk") is derived from Achuma. Aguacolla and Giganton
are its Ecuadorean names.

## Preparation and Cimora Additives

The stems of the cactus, normally purchased in the market, are sliced like
bread and boiled for up to seven hours in water. After the drinking of San
Pedro, other medicinal herbs begin to "talk" to the shaman, activating his
own "inner power." San Pedro may be taken alone, but often other plants,
separately boiled, are added and the drink is then called Cimora. Among
the numerous plant additives employed are the Andean cactus *Neoraimondia
macrostibas*, a species of *Iresine*, *Pedilanthus tithymaloides*, and
*Isotoma longiflora*. *Brugmansia aurea* and *B. sanguinea*, two potent
hallucinogens in their own right, are frequently added.

## Chemistry

*Trichocereus* contains mescaline as its main alkaloid, responsible for
the visual hallucinogenic effects. From dried specimens of San Pedro, 2
percent mescaline has been isolated. In addition, hordenine has also been
detected. Only in recent years has San Pedro been correctly identified; in
early chemical and psychiatric studies in Peru, the cactus was misidentified
as *Opuntia cylindrica*.

## The Shamanic Flight

The principal effects of San Pedro have been described by a shaman: "the
drug first produces drowsiness or a dreamy state and a feeling of
lethargy... a slight dizziness... then a great 'vision,' a clearing of
all the faculties... It produces a light numbness in the body and afterward
a tranquillity. And then comes detachment, a type of visual force...
inclusive of all the senses... including the sixth sense, the telepathic
sense of transmitting oneself across time and matter... like a kind of
removal of one's thought to a distant dimension."

During the ritual, participants are "set free from matter" and engage in
flight through cosmic regions. The aim of the shamanic curing ritual is to
make the patient "bloom" during the night ceremony, to make his
subconscious "open like a flower," even like the night-blooming
*Trichocereus* itself.
