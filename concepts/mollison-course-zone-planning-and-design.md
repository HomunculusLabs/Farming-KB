# Zone Planning and Design in Permaculture
Zone planning is one of the most fundamental organizational tools in
permaculture design. Zones are a convenient, abstract way to deal with
distances from the core of the design, typically the human dwelling. They
represent places where you are more frequently or less frequently present,
and they are not really concentric circles as often depicted in diagrams.
While you can indicate them as you like, what matters is that they are not
bounded; they are a way of thinking about the relationship between
frequency of visit and placement of elements.

## Zone Zero: The Dwelling

The human dwelling is the core of the design. That design might be for a
settlement, a village, a town, a house, or a modest cave under the rock.
Around that core, we specify zones. The dwelling itself is zone zero, and
all placement radiates outward from this central point based on frequency
of human attention and interaction.

## Zone One: The Intensive Garden Zone

Zone one is the place where you are always present, where you make daily
visits. It is around the entries to your house, and along the pathways
between your house and any other object that you visit frequently.
You place in zone one small plants, small animals, and those high energy,
high production units that comprise the most important elements of
self-reliance. The annuals there are in constant turnover, high demand,
and the perennials there yield constantly.
Keep there small animals that require care, such as calves, chickens and
ducklings. The more attention they must have, the closer you bring them.
Zone one generally does not extend more than 20 or 30 feet from the
foundation of the house, and not from all the foundation. It is a small
area that does not ever reach around to the back of the house. From that
zone, you get most of your food. If you have any sense, you get all your
food except a couple of items from there.
It is nice to have a couple of little ponds in zone one, little four foot
ponds, and one very close to a path. That is the pond from which you can
take pounds and pounds of food, such as watercress. It is also frog city,
providing slug control in the garden. Within zone one, if you are doing
mulch gardening, it is fully mulched. There is no bare soil.

## Zone Two: Orchard and Animal Systems

In zone two, unless you have extraordinary resources, there is no way
that you are going to continue a fully mulched garden. Zone two contains
the orchard, the main crops, spot-mulched crops, thin spread compost,
lightly rather than heavily manured crops.
Zone two contains the foods that comprise the bulk of the storage goods
of the client group, along with products from small animals. The outer
zone is designed to bring the high turnover nutrient to the high turnover
zone.
Whenever possible, zone two should include the range of some high
manurial animals like chickens. House them at the edge of zone one, or
very close to it. We are deliberately exploiting a larger system (zone
two) to enrich a smaller one (zone one) through the medium of an animal
collector. We are creating a biological funnel, a nutrient vortex.
The purposes of the zones is to design distances properly. When you do
this, it just pays hundreds of times over. Every time you break your own
rules, you are sorry. Zone one is for lettuce, spinach, green beans, pole
beans, the things you are picking every day and bringing in.

## Zone Three: Broadacre Cropping

Beyond zone two comes zone three, which is just a name for the main-crop
farming area. This is where you place crops that require only a few visits
per season, such as grains, field crops, and pastured animals. You might
visit zone three once a week or once a month depending on management needs.
The animals within zone three verge on the non-domestic. You might run
poultry in the orchards after harvest, where they glean drops that could
otherwise harbor pests. Cattle can be run through zone three with
carefully designed corridors that allow movement between paddocks without
destroying water systems.

## Zones Four and Five: Forage and Wilderness

Zone four is a forage and collection zone for wild harvest, timber, and
larger animal grazing. It requires infrequent visits. Zone five is an
unmanaged area, left for wildlife habitat and natural ecosystem processes.
You may hardly ever visit zone five, perhaps only once a year or not at
all. These outer zones are critical for maintaining the ecological
integrity of the broader landscape.

## Key Design Principles for Zones

There are two type-one errors, very bad errors, to avoid. One is to site
your client on ridges, or in wind tunnels, or in fire funnels. The other
is to put people in the bush, making a tiny clearing and locating a house
there. From the moment people move to the site, they experience terrible
conflict with wildlife.
When designing zones, think about all elements of the system and their
relationships. Place elements on discs and spin the discs to see how they
fit. Consider access, water systems, and house sites as a single entity.
Everything else will fit into that framework. The more carefully you plan
zone placement, the more productive and self-maintaining the system
becomes.

## Zone Design for Different Climates

Zone planning must be adapted to local climate conditions. The standard
temperate zone model described above does not translate directly to all
bioregions without modification.

### Tropical and Subtropical Zones

In humid tropics, zone one may be smaller because intensive gardens
require less management due to year-round growing conditions. However,
pest pressure is higher, so daily attention to zone one crops is even
more critical. Zone two orchards can include tropical fruits like
mangoes, bananas, and citrus that produce with minimal intervention.
In dry tropics, water becomes the primary organizing principle rather
than distance from the house. Zone one may extend along irrigation lines
or swales rather than radiating uniformly. Shade structures and windbreaks
become essential elements that modify zone placement.

### Temperate Cold Climates

Cold climates require careful zone one design to maximize passive solar
gain. Greenhouses and cold frames may serve as extensions of zone one,
placing high-value crops within easy reach even through winter. Zone two
storage crops become more important as the growing season shortens.
Animal housing in zone one or zone two provides both warmth for the
animals and manure for the gardens. In very cold regions, zone five may
be much closer to the dwelling because wilderness starts just beyond the
cleared area.

### Dryland and Arid Zones

In arid environments, zone one may be the only reliably productive area.
Water harvesting earthworks such as swales and gabions become organizing
features. Zones compress toward water sources, and the distances between
zones shrink. Zone five may be very close at hand, as the natural desert
ecosystem begins almost immediately beyond the cultivated area.
