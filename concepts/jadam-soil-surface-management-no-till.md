# JADAM Soil Surface Management and No-Till Approach

JADAM's approach to soil surface management centers on [[jadam-coexisting-with-wild-grass]] (weeds) and cover cropping rather than conventional
tillage or herbicide-based weed control. The system treats the soil
surface as a living interface between sky and earth that must remain
open and biologically active, rejecting practices that seal, compact, or
sterilize this critical zone.

## The Compacted Soil Layer Problem

JADAM identifies [[jadam-antibiotic-soil-contamination]]
as the single most destructive factor in modern agriculture. Heavy
tractor use compresses soil at approximately 15 cm (6 in) depth,
creating an impermeable plow pan. This hardened layer prevents water
infiltration, causes chemical residues to accumulate in the surface
zone, blocks root penetration, and traps evaporating water that leaves
salt behind. The result is [[jadam-salt-accumulation-in-soil]], nutrient stagnation, and progressively declining crop
health.

Cho recommends minimizing or eliminating heavy tractor use, switching
to lighter vehicles, and keeping wheels off crop-growing areas. The
visual indicator of soil health is root condition: coiled, shallow roots
signal compacted soil, while deep, wide-rooting plants indicate healthy
soil structure.

## Cover Crop as Foundation

[[jadam-cover-crop-method]] is the primary tool
for soil surface management in JADAM. The method addresses multiple
problems simultaneously: temperature regulation, organic matter
addition, mineral extraction from deep soil, nitrogen fixation,
compaction relief, weed suppression, and moisture retention.

### Autumn-Winter-Spring Cycle

The core JADAM cover crop protocol involves sowing grass seeds around
October, before fruit trees drop their leaves. Grass sprouts before
leaves fall, trapping the nutrient-rich leaf litter between grass
blades. This prevents wind-blown nutrient loss and ensures complete
nutrient recycling. The orchard remains fully green through winter.

In spring, the cover crop moderates soil temperature rise, preventing
[[jadam-cover-crop-soil-temperature-management]] of
fruit trees caused by rapid spring warming. Grass roots and microbial
activity keep soil temperature stable and moderate, unlike bare soil
that experiences extreme temperature fluctuations.

### Summer Protection

During summer, cover crop prevents soil overheating. Cho measured
temperatures under black plastic mulch reaching 45 C (113 F) at 1 cm
depth, while roots of most crops stop functioning at 40 C (104 F). Cover
crop maintains soil temperature within the range preferred by crop
roots, enabling continued nutrient absorption throughout the hot season.
The principle is straightforward: big yield comes from big roots, and
big roots come from good soil temperature management.

### Key Cover Crop Species

- **Hairy vetch**: leguminous, fixes approximately 20 kg nitrogen per
  0.1 ha, provides 17 tons biomass, inhibits weeds, low C/N ratio of
  around 10 for rapid decomposition
- **Milk vetch**: leguminous green manure used in rice paddies for
  thousands of years, provides 15 kg nitrogen per 0.1 ha
- **Rye**: gramineous crop, very cold-tolerant (survives below -25 C),
  roots penetrate 2-3 meters deep, provides 15 kg nitrogen per 0.1 ha
  with 20 tons biomass, excellent for breaking compaction and removing
  salt
- **Sudan grass**: summer annual, fast-growing, reduces nematode damage,
  removes salt from protected cultivation soil
- **Rape**: provides oil from pressed seeds, pulp used for fertilizer,
  can be double-cropped with rice

Mixing hairy vetch and rye in a 3:1 ratio combines nitrogen fixation
with deep root penetration for an optimal cover crop blend.

## Symbiosis with Wild Grass

JADAM challenges the conventional view that weeds are enemies to be
eliminated. Cho advocates treating wild grass as a partner that provides
organic matter, moderates soil temperature, pulls minerals from deep
subsoil, increases microbial activity, and reduces disease incidence.

The recommended management approach: leave ample space between crop
rows so inter-row grass does not compete with crops. Cut grass 2-3 times
per year at approximately 5 cm (2 in) height using a sickle for small
fields or mower for larger areas. The 5 cm stubble helps stop the
spread of aphids and mites. Leaving some uncut grass is useful and
reduces labor fatigue.

Cho explicitly advises against hand-pulling weeds as unnecessarily
exhausting labor, and against black plastic mulch for large-scale use
due to its root-choking effects in summer. If plastic mulch must be used,
make ventilation holes on sides and top to prevent extreme temperature
rise.

## Nature's Fertilizer Method

JADAM derives its fertilization approach from observing natural forests,
where three rules govern nutrient cycling: fertilizers are (i) fresh,
(ii) applied on the soil surface, and (iii) applied in autumn. This
contradicts conventional agriculture's prescription for fully fermented
material, deep incorporation, and spring application.

The [[jadam-autumn-fertilization-principle]]
method means that organic materials are scattered on fields in autumn,
break down through winter, and fertilize the soil by spring. No compost
bins, turning, C/N ratio calculations, or aeration management are
required. [[jadam-compost-free-approach]] entirely, instead mixing ingredients, applying JMS, and using
a tiller for initial incorporation.

## Crop Residues as Fertilizer

Unlike conventional advice to destroy crop residues to prevent disease,
JADAM treats all plant parts as nutrient sources. [[jadam-monoculture-disorder-crop-residues]] contain the best nutritional balance for the crop that
produced them. Disease organisms like canker and powdery mildew are so
ubiquitous that burning residues cannot eliminate them. Instead, JADAM
increases microbial diversity to prevent any single pathogen from
gaining dominance, shifting from disinfection to diversity.
