# Reinvigorating Old Mushroom Beds

## The Myth of Bed Revival

There is a widespread impression among horticulturists that worn-out
beds which have ceased to bear may, by means of watering and certain
stimulants and warming up again, be so re-invigorated as to start
into full bearing and yield a second and good crop. William Falconer
gave this question much painstaking and practical consideration, and
concluded that he had absolutely failed to revive a "dead" bed. He had
never been able to do it himself, and no instance of its having been
done had ever come under his observation. This, he acknowledged, may
appear heresy given the multitudinous writings to the contrary.

## Prolonged Bearing vs. Second Crop

A mushroom bed may keep on bearing in a desultory way for many months,
and now and again show spurts of increased fertility. But this is no
second crop; it is merely a prolonged dribbling of the first crop. A
bed, by reason of cold or dryness, may stand still or partially stop
bearing, and soon after it is remoistened, warmed, and otherwise
submitted to congenial conditions, will display renewed energy. But
this is no second crop either; it is merely a spurt of the first crop
caused by extra favorable cultural conditions.

## The Authority of Mr. Barter

To illustrate how vaguely the question of reinvigorating beds was
regarded, Falconer quoted from a letter by Mr. J. Barter, who grew
21,000 pounds of mushrooms per year for the London market. Barter was
asked, "Do you ever get a second crop?" His reply was illuminating:

"My beds last in bearing, on average, each three months, and that I
reckon to be three crops. But whether it be three or six months, the
weight of mushrooms is about the same. As there is say a ton of
manure, only so much mushroom-producing power; if you force it to
produce that weight in two months you are a gainer, as you thereby
save in labor. But when that producing power is exhausted it will
produce no more mushrooms."

## The Exhausted Bed Defined

A spent mushroom bed is one that has been kept in bearing condition
under the most favorable circumstances at the grower's command, has
borne a good crop, lasted some two months in bearing, and now has
stopped bearing (except in a meagerly, desultory way) because the
spawn or mycelium has exhausted itself and is dead.

Some bits of mycelium are still alive and yield the desultory few
mushrooms, but every mushroom that they yield is preying on their
vitality. After a time they too shall die and the bed will be
completely barren, for the mycelium is altogether dead, and without
mycelium mushrooms are an impossibility.

## Perpetuating Mycelium by Transplanting

While mycelium in a bearing condition cannot be made perennial, it
is possible to keep mushroom mycelium in active growth year after
year, providing it is never allowed to bear mushrooms. This is done
by taking the mycelium just before it begins bearing from one manure
bed and planting it in another, and so on from bed to bed.

At every fresh transplanting the mycelium exerts itself into renewed
growth, for it must become a strong plant before it has strength enough
to produce and support a mushroom. The utmost efforts of Victorian
cultivators never rendered mycelium in a mushroom-bearing condition
perennial.

## Practical Implications

The practical implications of Falconer's analysis are significant for
the mushroom grower:

- No amount of watering, stimulants, or warming can revive a bed
  whose mycelium has genuinely died
- What appears to be "revival" is simply the continuation of the
  original crop under more favorable conditions
- The total yield of a bed is roughly proportional to the quantity
  of manure used, regardless of how quickly or slowly it is produced
- Faster production (two months) is more economical than slower
  production because it saves labor costs
- Mycelium can be perpetuated indefinitely by transplanting before
  it bears fruit

## The Yield Ceiling Concept

Barter's observation introduces the concept of a yield ceiling
inherent in the manure itself. There is only so much
"mushroom-producing power" in a given quantity of prepared manure.
Forcing rapid production captures this yield more efficiently by
saving labor, while allowing beds to linger merely stretches out the
same total weight over a longer period without increasing it.

This understanding should guide the cultivator's decision about when
to abandon old beds in favor of new ones. Once bearing has declined
substantially and the bed has been in production for approximately
two months, the most economical course is to remove the spent
material and prepare fresh beds.

## Warnings Against False Hopes

Falconer's candid dismissal of bed-revival techniques stands in stark
contrast to the many publications of his era that promised gardeners
they could coax additional crops from exhausted beds through various
treatments. His practical experience, combined with the commercial
insight of a grower producing 21,000 pounds annually, provides
authoritative guidance that the resources spent on attempting bed
revival would be better invested in preparing new beds.
