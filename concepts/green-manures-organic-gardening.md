# Green Manures in Organic Gardening

Green manure is a crop grown specifically to add organic
matter to beds that are empty for a period of time. It is
sown with the intention of digging it into the soil to
provide organic matter and plant food. While more commonly
associated with farming, green manures have real value in
the home garden, especially for light soils.

## Why Use Green Manures

### Adding Organic Matter
The most obvious value is providing organic matter. Soft
green material rots down quickly, leaving stable organic
matter in the soil. On soils short of organic matter,
any addition is a bonus. The roots also break up the soil
and improve drainage.

### Making Nutrients Available
The greater value lies in making plant nutrients available.
Deep-rooting crops like red clover and lupins can reach
minerals from over 7ft (2m) below the surface. When dug
in, these nutrients become available to the next crop.

### Nitrogen Fixation
Leguminous plants like beans, clovers, and lupins fix
nitrogen from the air through bacteria living in nodules
on their roots. When the plant is dug in, this nitrogen
becomes available to subsequent crops.

### Preventing Nutrient Loss
On light soils, the biggest loss of nutrients occurs in
winter through leaching during wet weather. A winter
green-manure crop prevents this loss and is very valuable
for sandy and chalky soils.

### Weed Suppression
Green-manure crops cover the ground well, providing shade
and competition for water and nutrients that discourage
all but the most tenacious weeds.

## Choosing the Right Crop

Choose a plant that will mature in the time available and
preferably one unlike the previous or following crop. For
example, do not sow mustard (a brassica) after cabbages
because of the risk of perpetuating brassica pests and
diseases such as clubroot.

The soil needs to be in good condition. If it is low in
nutrients, apply a fertilizer before sowing. Prepare the
seedbed as thoroughly as for any other crop and firm it
by walking before sowing.

## Sowing

Small seeds can be sown in rows about 6in (15cm) apart
or scattered by hand and raked into the top inch of soil.
Large seed should be sown in rows about 1ft (30cm) apart.
The biggest problem is that green manure takes up growing
space. In most gardens, there is no room to leave an area
fallow, so manure crops must fit in with cultivated ones.

## Working Green Manure Into Soil

Incorporation must be done correctly for maximum benefit.

### Timing
Do not let the crop become too woody before digging in or
the rotting process will take nitrogen from the soil. Cut
the crop before it flowers.

### Method
Low-growing crops like mustard can be cut down with a
spade and allowed to wilt for a few days before digging
in. Taller plants can be worked into the surface with a
rotary cultivator, then cultivated more deeply after
a few days of wilting.

### Depth
Do not bury the material deeper than about 6in (15cm).
If the crop has become hard and woody, apply liquid
fertilizer such as liquid seaweed or animal manure to
assist decomposition.

## Mustard as a Quick Green Manure

Mustard is a fast-growing, low crop ideal for covering
land empty for a few weeks during the growing season.
Sow seed in rows 0.5in (1cm) deep and 6in (15cm) apart.
When seedlings reach 6 to 9in (15 to 20cm) tall, before
flowering, cut down at the base and allow to wilt before
digging in.
