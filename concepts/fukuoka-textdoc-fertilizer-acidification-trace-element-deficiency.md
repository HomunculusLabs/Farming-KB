# Fertilizer Acidification and Trace Element Deficiency in Crop Nutrition

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] provides a detailed critique of chemical fertilizer use in *The Natural Way of Farming*, focusing on how the three major fertilizers — ammonium sulfate, super-phosphate, and potassium sulfate — systematically acidify soil and create cascading trace element deficiencies that undermine crop health. His analysis draws from decades of orchard observation and challenges the assumption that fertilizer inputs are beneficial to agriculture.

## The Sulfuric Acid Burden

More than seventy percent of the three major chemical fertilizers consists of concentrated sulfuric acid. When these fertilizers are applied to farmland, they progressively acidify the soil. Fukuoka calculates that approximately 1.8 million tons of sulfuric acid are dumped annually onto Japanese farmlands in the form of fertilizer alone. This massive acidic input suppresses and kills soil microorganisms, disrupts the delicate biological balance of the soil ecosystem, and damages soil structure in ways that may ultimately prove catastrophic for agriculture.

The acidification effect is not merely a theoretical concern. Fukuoka observed it directly in citrus orchards where decades of sulfuric acid fertilizer application had transformed the soil environment, killing off beneficial microbial communities and creating conditions favorable to pathogens rather than to healthy plant growth. The deadened soil lost its ability to support the complex web of biological interactions that maintain natural fertility. What remained was a degraded medium capable of supporting plants only through continued chemical intervention — a self-perpetuating cycle of dependence.

## The Nitrogen Denitrification Discovery

A particularly striking revelation concerns nitrogen efficiency. Research showed that approximately thirty percent of the nitrogenous component of ammonium sulfate applied to paddy fields is denitrified by soil microorganisms and simply escapes into the atmosphere. Fukuoka considered it an "unspeakable injury and injustice to countless farmers" that this critical inefficiency was discovered only after decades of widespread ammonium sulfate use. Farmers had been spending money and labor applying fertilizer that was largely wasted, while the denitrification process simultaneously contributed to atmospheric pollution.

He warned that "such nonsense will occur again and again" because the complexity of soil-fertilizer interactions far exceeds the capacity of controlled experiments to predict outcomes in living fields. Each new discovery of fertilizer inefficiency reveals only one dimension of a problem that remains largely invisible to agricultural science.

## The Phosphate Penetration Problem

Fukuoka noted another revelation from soil research: phosphate fertilizers applied to fields penetrate only about two inches into the soil surface. This meant that the enormous quantities of phosphates farmers had religiously spread on their fields year after year were essentially being dumped on the topsoil, never reaching deeper soil layers where they might be accessible to plant roots. Mountains of phosphates applied over decades had accumulated uselessly on the soil surface, a colossal waste of both money and non-renewable phosphate rock resources.

## The Trace Element Crisis

One of the most significant yet overlooked consequences of heavy chemical fertilizer use is the development of trace element deficiencies. By relying on just a few primary nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium — and killing the soil biology that would otherwise make trace minerals available, modern farming creates crops that are deficient in many elements essential to their health. Fukuoka identified this problem reaching alarming proportions in fruit trees, and noted it had also surfaced as one cause of low rice harvests. The problem stems from the inherent incompleteness of supplying nutrients through a small number of chemical inputs while ignoring the complex web of micronutrients that plants require for healthy growth and disease resistance.

## Interference Between Fertilizer Components

The interactions between fertilizer components in orchard soil are what Fukuoka describes as "unspeakably complex." He documented several specific interference patterns:

- **Iodine deficiency** impairs nitrogen and phosphate uptake by plants, creating a hidden bottleneck that limits the effectiveness of applied fertilizers
- **Acidic or alkaline soils** created by heavy lime applications cause zinc, manganese, boron, iodine, and other elements to become less water-soluble and therefore unavailable to plant roots
- **Excess potassium** blocks iodine uptake and simultaneously reduces boron absorption, creating a double deficiency from a single excess
- **Higher nitrogen and phosphate levels** paradoxically reduce manganese deficiency while increasing zinc and boron deficiency, creating a shifting pattern of shortages

Adding too much of one fertilizer renders another ineffective. When there is a shortage of certain trace elements, adding generous amounts of macronutrients provides no benefit whatsoever. The system of nutrient interactions is so complex that applying fertilizers without full knowledge of all interactions amounts to conducting an uncontrolled experiment on living soil. Fukuoka warned that the problems go on multiplying: very limited research was underway on several trace components, but an endless number remained to be discovered, spawning infinite new areas of study such as mutual interactions, leaching in the soil, fixation, and relationships with microbes.

## The Illusion of Controlled Experiments

Fukuoka was particularly critical of how fertilizer research is conducted. At agricultural research stations, scientists place soil in pots and run tests under controlled conditions. In these pots, most of the soil microbes die off. Results obtained from such artificially constrained experimental frameworks cannot be meaningfully applied to situations under natural conditions in living fields. The soil in a pot is dead; the soil in a field is alive. Testing fertilizer on dead soil and applying the results to living soil is a fundamental error.

Yet because a fertilizer slightly accelerates crop growth in such narrow tests, it is praised lavishly and widely reported to be effective. Only the efficacy of the fertilizer is stressed; almost nothing is said about its adverse effects, which Fukuoka described as "innumerable."

## Temporary Growth Versus Lasting Weakening

Fukuoka identified several fundamental problems with the apparent success of fertilizers:

1. Fertilizers speed up crop growth, but this is only a temporary and local effect that does not offset the inevitable weakening of crops — similar to the rapid acceleration of plant growth by artificial hormones
2. Plants weakened by fertilizers have lowered resistance to diseases and pests and are less able to overcome other obstacles to growth
3. The complex interference patterns between fertilizer components and soil biology create problems that compound over time, requiring ever greater inputs to maintain yields

He noted the farmer's common rationalization: "Chemical fertilizers do cause some damage. But I've used fertilizers now for years and haven't had any big problems." Fukuoka's response was stark: "The seeds of calamity have been sown. When we take note of the danger, it will be too late to do anything about it." Fertilizers currently account for thirty to fifty percent of the costs of running an orchard.

## The Missing No-Fertilizer Tests

Fukuoka highlighted a fundamental bias in agricultural science: scientists hardly ever run experiments on no-fertilizer cultivation. In Japan, only a handful of reports had been published on fruit trees without fertilizer in small concrete enclosures and earthen pots. The reason is clear: scientists work from the premise that crops are to be grown with fertilizer, viewing no-fertilizer tests as "idiotic and dangerous."

The standard on which fertilizer experiments should be based is no-fertilizer tests, but three-element tests using nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are the standard actually used. Scientists claim that trees grow only about half as much without fertilizer, but the conditions under which these experiments were conducted have little in common with true [[natural-farming]]. When crops are planted in small earthenware pots or artificial enclosures, the soil in which they grow is dead soil. The growth of trees whose roots are boxed in by concrete is highly unnatural.

## The Living Soil Alternative

One gram of ordinary topsoil contains about one hundred million bacteria, yeasts, molds, diatoms, and other microbes. These organisms exist for a purpose, struggling, cooperating, and carrying on the cycles of nature. Into this living system, chemical fertilizers introduce disruption rather than nourishment. The natural farming alternative is to feed the soil biology — through returning organic matter, maintaining living ground covers, and allowing natural biological processes — rather than trying to feed the plants directly with chemical inputs.

Fukuoka demonstrated through decades of practice that by returning all rice and barley straw and hulls to the land, the soil enriches itself year in and year out without the farmer having to lift a finger. The soil lives of its own accord and plows itself.

## Fertilizer Economics and Farmer Dependency

Fukuoka highlighted the economic trap that fertilizer creates for farmers.
Fertilizers currently account for thirty to fifty percent of the costs of
running an orchard. This creates a dependency cycle: once a farmer begins
using chemical fertilizers, the soil biology degrades and the land becomes
incapable of supporting crops without continued chemical inputs. The farmer
is then locked into purchasing ever-increasing quantities of fertilizer to
maintain yields on progressively degraded soil.

The economic burden is compounded by the fact that the supposed benefits of
fertilizer are largely illusory. The temporary growth acceleration masks a
long-term decline in soil health that eventually manifests as declining
yields, increased pest pressure, and greater susceptibility to drought and
disease — all of which require additional expensive inputs to address.

## Compost as Unnecessary Middle Ground

Fukuoka addressed the organic farming alternative of compost preparation.
When compost such as straw, grasses, trees, or seaweed is applied directly
to a field, it takes a while for this to decompose and trigger a fertilizer
response. This is because microbes help themselves to the available nitrogen
in the soil, creating a temporary nitrogen deficiency that initially starves
the crops.

In organic farming, these materials are fermented and used as prepared
compost, giving a safe, effective fertilizer. But all the trouble taken
during preparation of the compost — frequent turning of the pile, methods
for stimulating aerobic bacteria, the addition of water and nitrogenous
fertilizers, lime, super-phosphate, rice bran, manure, and so forth — all
this trouble is taken just for a slight acceleration in response. Because
the net effect is to speed up [[decomposition]] by at most ten to twenty
percent, this can hardly be called necessary.

Fukuoka stated firmly: "While compost itself is not without value, the
[[composting]] of organic materials is fundamentally useless." The surest way
to solve the problem is to apply a method that adapts to the circumstances
and follows nature.

## The Mountain Forest as Proof

Fukuoka pointed to the mountain forests as living proof that trees grow
without fertilizer. Reforested cedars in a favorable area generally grow
about forty tons per quarter-acre over a period of twenty years, producing
some two tons of new growth each year without fertilizer. This includes
only the lumber portion; counting small branches, leaves, and roots, annual
production is probably closer to four tons.

After a certain period, the trees in a timber stand are felled, and the
entire surface portion — branches, leaves, and trunk — is carried away. So
not only are fertilizers not used, this is slash-and-burn agriculture. How
then are the fertilizer components for this production volume supplied each
year to the growing trees? Fukuoka's answer was simple: "Plants do not need
to be raised; they grow of their own accord. The mountain forests are living
proof that trees are not raised with fertilizer but grow by themselves."

Anyone who sees black wattle planted in depleted soil on a mountainside and
succeeded a number of years later with giant cedars many times their size
will be amazed at the great productive powers of the soil. When black wattle
is planted among cedar or cypress, these latter thrive with the help of the
microbes present on the roots of the black wattle.

## Connection to Natural Farming Principles

This analysis of fertilizer damage is central to Fukuoka's broader argument for natural farming. By taking full advantage of the inherent powers of nature — returning all organic matter to the land, maintaining living soil biology through ground covers, and allowing natural soil processes to operate — one can obtain yields equal to or better than those achieved with heavy fertilization, while simultaneously building rather than degrading soil health.
