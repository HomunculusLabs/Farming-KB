# Ayahuasca Ceremony Practices

Ayahuasca ceremonies are ritualized gatherings centered around the consumption
of the ayahuasca brew, typically led by a shaman (ayahuasquero or curandero).
The ceremony is the primary context through which ayahuasca is traditionally
used in Amazonian cultures, and it has become the main product offered to
tourists in the [[ayahuasca-tourism]] industry. Stuart's 2002 observations from
the Ucayali region of Peru provide valuable ethnographic detail about how
these ceremonies function in practice.

## Traditional vs. Commercial Ceremonies

A fundamental distinction exists between ceremonies conducted for community
purposes and those performed for tourists:

### Community Ceremonies
Traditional ceremonies are conducted primarily for the benefit of the shaman's
relatives and community. These tend to be deeper, more authentic rituals with
stronger spiritual significance. The shaman's reputation and social standing
within the community provide accountability and context. Stuart observed
one practitioner ("E" in San Francisco) widely regarded as an honorable man,
who held private family services where icaros could be heard in the darkness
of his home.

### Commercial Tourist Ceremonies
Unless a tourist spent a long time getting to know a practitioner, the
character of a commercialized ayahuasca ceremony would probably be shallower
than a community ritual. Several factors contribute to this difference:

- **Linguistic barriers**: Communication between shaman and tourist is often
  limited, reducing the ability to provide context, guidance, or respond to
  individual needs.
- **Cultural misunderstandings**: Cultural gaps are likely to confound
  communication even in ordinary consciousness, making the disorienting
  ayahuasca experience harder to navigate cross-culturally.
- **Conservative dosing**: The shaman typically errs on the side of caution,
  preferring to give too little rather than too much, to prevent adverse
  reactions among foreigners unfamiliar with the experience.
- **Observational focus**: Tourists primarily observe ritual procedures and
  listen to icaros rather than receiving the full therapeutic or spiritual
  treatment that a community member might experience.

## The Role of the Ayahuasquero

The ayahuasquero (or curandero) is the central figure in the ceremony. Their
role encompasses several functions:

### Ceremonial Leadership
The ayahuasquero guides the ceremony from beginning to end, determining when
participants drink, leading the icaros (healing songs), and managing the
energetic space of the ceremony. They are responsible for the safety and
well-being of all participants.

### Brew Preparation
Every shaman claims to be the only person in all of Amazonia who knows how
to properly brew the magic potion, according to Stuart. This claim of unique
knowledge serves both as a marketing device and a reflection of the genuinely
varied recipes and approaches that exist among different practitioners and
lineages.

### Healing and Diagnosis
In traditional contexts, the ayahuasquero uses the ayahuasca experience to
diagnose illnesses, identify spiritual causes of disease, and perform
healing work. In tourist ceremonies, this diagnostic role is often
diminished or absent.

## Variation Among Practitioners

Stuart's observations reveal significant variation in the character and
reliability of ayahuasqueros catering to tourists:

- **Shaman B**: An elderly practitioner in Yarina who hosted nightly
  ceremonies attracting up to two dozen visitors. Stuart discovered B was a
  frequent liar and shoplifter who soon asked for money or gifts at every
  opportunity. Despite these character flaws, B maintained a thriving
  practice with tourists.
- **Visiting shamans**: Two shamans visited B's group for a two-week
  training period. One was described as "a sweet and wonderful guy" but his
  partner constantly hustled Stuart for spare change.
- **Shaman E**: In the Shipibo town of San Francisco, E was widely regarded
  as an honorable man who charged about ten dollars per tourist ceremony and
  held genuine private family services.
- **Shaman A**: Another practitioner whose invitations Stuart declined
  after being swindled on the price of a tobacco pipe.

This variation underscores the challenge tourists face in finding a
trustworthy practitioner. Stuart advises that "it is a tricky endeavor to
travel to a third world country and ask a total stranger for a spiritual
experience."

## Icaros

Icaros are the healing songs chanted by the ayahuasquero during the
ceremony. They are a central component of the Shipibo ayahuasca tradition
and serve multiple purposes:

- **Structuring the experience**: The icaros guide the emotional and visual
  trajectory of the ceremony.
- **Protection**: Certain icaros are believed to provide energetic protection
  for participants.
- **Healing**: Specific songs are associated with particular healing
  intentions and spiritual interventions.
- **Cultural transmission**: The icaros encode Shipibo cosmological knowledge
  and are passed down through lineages of practitioners.

## Practical Considerations for Participants

### [[set-and-setting]]
As with all psychedelic experiences, set (mindset) and setting (environment)
profoundly influence the ayahuasca experience. The ceremony setting provides
structure, but the participant's psychological preparation and expectations
play an equally important role.

### Purification Practices
Traditional preparation often involves dietary restrictions (no salt, sugar,
pork, alcohol, or sexual activity for days before the ceremony), though
tourist-oriented ceremonies may relax these requirements.

### Safety
Given the MAOI content of ayahuasca, participants must avoid certain
medications and foods. The ceremony itself involves vomiting (considered a
form of purging), which is accepted as a normal part of the experience.
