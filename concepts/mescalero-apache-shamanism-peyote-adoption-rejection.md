# Mescalero Apache Shamanism and Peyote Use: Adoption and Rejection

## Overview

This chapter by L. Bryce Boyer, Ruth M. Boyer, and Harry W.
Basehart examines peyote use among the Apache of the Mescalero
Indian Reservation in New Mexico. It documents how peyote was
adopted for shamanistic purposes around 1870, used for
approximately forty years, and then deliberately abandoned. The
analysis explores the religious concepts and personality
structures that led to peyote's rejection and why subsequent
shamanistic practices excluded hallucinogen use.

## The Apache Tribes of Mescalero

The reservation was established in 1873 for the Mescaleros.
Present inhabitants include three tribes: Mescaleros,
Chiricahuas (prisoners of war from Geronimo's 1886 capitulation),
and Lipans. All three share similar religio-medical philosophies,
shamanic criteria, and procedures.

## History of Peyote Adoption

Nineteenth-century sources document peyote in religious rites:
Mescaleros in 1867, Chiricahuas in 1875, Lipans in 1885. The
Lipans obtained peyote from the Carrizo Indians; Mescaleros
likely learned rites from Lipans before 1870 or from northern
Mexican groups.

Despite documented use, Mescaleros were excluded from early
peyote distribution maps and listed as non-users by the Bureau
of Indian Affairs. Opler's 1930s fieldwork revealed forty years
of elaborate [[metzner-peyote-ceremonies-native-american-church-and-huichol-traditions]] and Lipan shamanistic peyote use.

## Apache Religious Philosophy

Aboriginal religio-medical philosophies, shamanic criteria, and
procedures were similar among all three tribes. Key tenets:

- The world is permeated by supernatural power with no
  intrinsic attribute of good or evil; its virtue resides in
  potency.
- Power approaches people through plants, animals, or natural
  phenomena by means of dreams or hallucinatory experiences.
- Acceptance of power is frequently accompanied by ordeal.
- Ritual instruction may come directly from the power or from
  other shamans.
- Any person is a potential power recipient. Opler described
  the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially
  active."

### Shaman vs. Witch

An individual owning power used for non-common-good purposes is
a witch. Those using power for the group's benefit (shamans) are
implicitly witches since saving one life requires sacrificing
another. Jealousies, enmities, and suspicion abound. Each shaman
has private instructions and individually owned rites. There has
never been a chief shaman.

## Peyote Ceremonies and Their Disruption

### Fundamental Incongruity

There was a fundamental incongruity between ordinary Mescalero
shamanistic ceremonies and peyote rites. In ordinary practices,
a single shaman is the principal figure; religious ecstasy,
visions, and communication with supernaturals are his
prerogatives validating his power.

Peyote use by others at ceremonies made its effects common,
eliminating the shaman's unique access. Meetings became places
where shamanistic rivalries and witchcraft flourished, producing
disruption rather than cohesiveness.

### Abandonment

The peyote ceremonies were not accompanied by Christian beliefs;
the Mescaleros never joined the Peyote Religion. Peyote use was intended to affirm
traditional practices during reservation confinement. But
antagonisms became so open and bloody that gatherings were
abandoned. Since peyote involved witchcraft, its ingestion was
equated with witchcraft potential.

## Peyote as Intrinsically Evil Power

In native conceptualization, power has no intrinsic good or evil.
Yet peyote power is unique among the Mescaleros in being
uniformly considered bad. Some believe the owl is also
intrinsically evil (its hoot presages death).

During 1959-60 there were thirteen accredited shamans and about
fifteen pseudoshamans. Only two shamans were at times judged
witches. "Ancient One" was the sole living peyote camp
participant. Pseudoshamans who used peyote illicitly suffered:
one lost a leg, another had a relative killed and another lost a
limb, as peyote "turned back" on those not legitimately entitled
to its power.

## Personality Structure and Socialization

### Child-Rearing Practices

Maternal care is grossly inconsistent. Infants receive tender
care but are periodically abandoned to the supervision of
children as young as four or five for hours or days. Husbands
are equally inconstant. Basic trust development is stultified,
producing marked ambivalence and suspiciousness.

When a new baby arrives (previous child 18-24 months old), the
older child is abruptly and brutally displaced. Resultant
sibling rivalry is intense but strongly disapproved, its
repression insecure, becoming blatant under alcohol influence.

## Peyote's Physio-Psychological Role

Boyer et al. propose that peyote's physio-psychological effects
accentuated pre-existing paranoid trends in Apache personality
structure. The shared hallucinatory experience at peyote
ceremonies undermined the shaman's exclusive access to the
supernatural, a cornerstone of Apache religious authority.

The abandonment of peyote thus represented not merely the
rejection of a plant but a cultural solution to the disruptive
potential of shared visionary experience in a society built on
individual shamanic authority and pervasive suspicion.
