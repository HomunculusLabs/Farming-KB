# Ice House and Spring House

Before mechanical refrigeration, ice houses and spring houses were the
primary methods of keeping food cold. Ice houses stored winter-harvested
ice for year-round cooling, while spring houses used the naturally cool
temperature of groundwater springs. Both remain viable options for the
off-grid homestead seeking low-tech food [[homesteading-food-preservation|preservation]].

## Ice Houses

An comparison root cellar is an insulated structure designed to store ice harvested
during winter for use throughout the warmer months. With proper construction,
ice can be retained from January through September with only 50-75% loss.

### History
Ice houses have been used since at least 1000 BCE in Persia. In cuthill [[mushroom-cultivation]]
America, the commercial ice industry harvested millions of tons from frozen
lakes and rivers. The ice house was standard equipment on farms, estates, and
military installations worldwide.

### Ice Harvesting

**Source options:**
- **Natural lakes and ponds** — clean, still water freezes into clear blocks
- **Purpose-built ice ponds** — shallow (8-12 inches deep) ponds designed to
  freeze quickly and cleanly
- **Rivers** — less ideal; moving water freezes more slowly and ice may be
  muddy or uneven

**Harvesting process:**
1. Wait for ice to reach at least 4-6 inches thick (usually January-February)
2. Score the ice surface with an ice plow or saw in a grid pattern (18-24
   inch squares)
3. Use ice saws to cut blocks free
4. Float or drag blocks to shore using ice tongs and hooks
5. Transport blocks to the ice house on sleds or wagons

**Tools needed:**
- Ice saw (large-toothed crosscut style)
- Ice plow or marking tool
- Ice tongs (two-pronged lifting tool)
- Ice spud (chisel for breaking ice free)
- Sled or wagon for transport
- Snow shovel for clearing

### Ice House Design

**Siting:**
- North-facing slope preferred (reduces solar gain)
- Near the ice source but elevated to prevent flooding
- Well-drained site; never in a low-lying wet area
- Shade from deciduous trees (summer shade, winter sun exposure)

**Structure:**
- **Size** — 12-16 feet diameter or 10x14 feet rectangular; holds 10-20
  tons of ice (sufficient for a family of 4-6 through summer)
- **Walls** — double-walled construction with 12-18 inches of insulation
  between (sawdust, straw, cork, or modern rigid foam)
- **Floor** — raised on timbers or gravel; drains must allow meltwater to
  escape without pooling
- **Ceiling** — well-insulated roof; ventilated but not drafty
- **Door** — small, insulated, on the north side; double-door vestibule
  preferred
- **Drainage** — floor slopes to a central drain that exits downslope

**Packing the ice:**
1. Layer ice blocks on the floor, packed as tightly as possible
2. Fill gaps between blocks with crushed ice or snow
3. Add a 6-8 inch layer of sawdust or straw between each layer of blocks
4. Continue layering until the ice stack is 2-3 feet below the ceiling
5. Cover the top with 12-18 inches of sawdust or straw
6. Keep the top insulation layer undisturbed throughout the season

### Ice Retention

With good construction and packing, expect 50-75% ice retention:
- **January-March**: minimal loss (below freezing, ice not needed)
- **April-June**: 10-15% loss; ice house at peak utility
- **July-August**: heaviest losses; 40-50% remaining if well-built
- **September**: remaining ice is mostly gone; transition to other
  preservation methods

### Using the Ice House
- Lower food items into the ice house in insulated containers
- Keep food separated from ice (cold air circulation, not direct contact,
  provides cooling)
- A well-managed ice house maintains 33-40F (1-4C) inside

### Modern Adaptations
- Use rigid foam insulation (R-30+ walls and ceiling) instead of sawdust
- Install a small solar-powered ventilation fan
- Combine with a [[root-cellaring-guide|root cellar]] for a complete
  off-grid food preservation system
- Use food-grade glycol or brine loops to distribute cooling to other areas

## Spring Houses

A spring house is a small stone or concrete structure built over a natural
spring or cold water source. The flowing groundwater maintains a cool
temperature (45-55F / 7-13C) year-round, providing natural refrigeration.

### How It Works
Groundwater emerges at the earth's surface at a constant temperature equal
to the average annual air temperature. In temperate climates, this is
typically 48-55F. A spring house captures this cool water and uses it to
chill food stored in the structure.

### Spring House Design

**Siting requirements:**
- Natural spring with reliable year-round flow (minimum 1-2 GPM)
- Cool, clear water (test for coliform bacteria if using for food)
- Elevated enough to prevent flooding
- Accessible from the kitchen and food preparation areas

**Construction:**
1. **Excavate** around the spring to create a pool or channel
2. **Foundation** — pour concrete footer on solid ground
3. **Walls** — stone or concrete block; 8-10 feet wide by 8-10 feet long
4. **Floor** — poured concrete with drainage channels for water flow
5. **Water channel** — a trough or channel runs through the structure,
   allowing spring water to flow through continuously
6. **Shelves** — stone or wooden shelves positioned above the water channel
   but within the cool air zone
7. **Roof** — stone slab or concrete; gable roof with ventilation
8. **Door** — heavy insulated door; small to minimize warm air entry

### Using the Spring House

**root cellaring energy:**
- Place perishable items (dairy, meat, eggs, vegetables) on shelves or
  directly in the flowing water (in sealed containers)
- Maintain 45-55F — sufficient for dairy, eggs, and short-term meat storage
- Not cold enough for freezing — use in combination with ice from an
  [[root-cellaring-guide|ice house]] or root [[earth-cellar-construction|cellar]] for complete
  preservation

**Other uses:**
- Cool storage for beverages
