# Lichen Ecology and Environmental Sensitivity Lichens are composite organisms arising from symbiosis between a fungus (mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (photobiont), typically a green alga or cyanobacterium. The partnership forms a thallus structurally distinct from either partner alone. Lichens are among the most sensitive organisms to environmental change, making them invaluable bioindicators of air quality, ecosystem health, and climate conditions. ## Lichen Symbiosis Structure In a lichen association, the fungus derives nutrition from the alga through parasitism or controlled parasitism, while the alga is protected by the fungal matrix. Lichens take the external shape of the fungal partner and are named accordingly. The fungal partner is typically a member of the Ascomycota (ascolichens) or rarely the Basidiomycota (basidiolichens). Neither group forms monophyletic lineages but they do form several major lichen-forming groups within each phylum. The structural integration of the [[lichenized-fungi-diversity]] is closely linked to photobiont selection, with different fungal species showing preferences for specific algal or cyanobacterial partners, which in turn influences the ecological niches these lichens can occupy.

## Growth Characteristics

Lichens are extremely slow-growing organisms, exhibiting approximately 1 mm of growth per year. Despite this slow growth, they are highly tolerant to xeric conditions including desiccation and heat, which allows them to colonize tree trunks, rocks, and exposed surfaces. This tolerance derives from the poikilohydric nature of lichens, which can enter a dormant state during dry periods and rapidly resume [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] when rehydrated.

## Air Pollution Sensitivity

Lichens are highly sensitive to atmospheric pollutants, particularly sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx), ozone, and heavy metals. This sensitivity arises because lichens lack protective structures such as cuticles and absorb water and nutrients directly from the atmosphere over their entire surface area. Pollutants disrupt cellular membranes, interfere with photosynthesis, and accumulate to toxic levels in lichen tissues.

As a result, lichens are rarely found near human habitations with significant automobile exhaust or industrial emissions.

## Lichens as Bioindicators

The sensitivity of lichens to air pollution has led to their widespread use as biological indicators (bioindicators) of air quality. Lichen diversity and abundance decrease with increasing pollution levels, and different lichen species have different sensitivities. Crustose lichens are generally more tolerant than foliose or fruticose species.

Lichen monitoring programs use species composition, coverage, and physiological measurements to assess air quality and detect pollution trends over time.

The [[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] are particularly significant in arctic, alpine, and boreal ecosystems, where lichen biomass can account for a substantial fraction of total primary producer standing crop.

## Succession and Community Dynamics

Lichen communities change predictably along environmental gradients and during [[holmgren-ecological-succession-and-pulsing]].

On rock surfaces, crustose lichens typically colonize first, followed by foliose and then fruticose species as the substrate is modified and soil accumulates.

In forest ecosystems, lichen communities shift with canopy closure and light availability, with sun-tolerant species dominating in open areas and shade-tolerant species in closed canopy conditions.

In the [[ozier-pdc-composting-and-soil-building]] benefits from lichen-mediated [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], which can add 1-5 kg N per hectare per year in some boreal forest floor communities.

However, elevated atmospheric [[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]] from human activities can disrupt lichen communities by favoring nitrophilous species over sensitive oligotrophic species.
