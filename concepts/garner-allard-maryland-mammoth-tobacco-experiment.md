## Garner and Allard Maryland Mammoth Tobacco Photoperiodism Discovery

## Overview

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard, two scientists at the US
Department of Agriculture, discovered the phenomenon of photoperiodism by
studying a mutant tobacco strain called Maryland Mammoth. Their experiments
demonstrated that plants measure the length of continuous darkness, not the
length of daylight, to determine when to flower. This discovery fundamentally
changed understanding of plant development and enabled the agricultural
manipulation of flowering times that persists in modern greenhouse
cultivation.

## The Maryland Mammoth Strain

Maryland Mammoth tobacco first appeared around 1906 in the agricultural
valleys of southern Maryland, an area home to major American tobacco farms
since European settlement in the late seventeenth century. The strain was
remarkable for its ability to reach four and a half meters in height and
produce nearly a hundred leaves, continuing [[green-cannabis-vegetative-growth]]
indefinitely. When given artificially shorter days, they flowered normally.
This result proved that the plant was not defective in its flowering
mechanism but was instead responding to an environmental cue, [[extending-growing-season]] never provided sufficiently early for
reproduction.

## Photoperiodism Terminology

Garner and Allard's discovery established the concept of photoperiodism and
led to the classification of plants into categories based on their day-length
requirements for flowering. Short-day plants flower only when days are short
relative to a critical threshold; chrysanthemums and soybeans are examples.
Long-day plants require days longer than a critical threshold; irises and
barley fall into this category. Maryland Mammoth was classified as a
short-day plant that had been growing in a region where natural short days
arrived too late in the season, after frost had already killed the plants.
