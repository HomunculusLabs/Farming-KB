# Fungal Biodiversity in Leaf Litter Leaf litter is one of the most
fungal-diverse habitats in terrestrial ecosystems, serving as a transition zone
between living plant communities and soil. The decomposition of leaf litter is
driven primarily by fungi, making these communities essential for nutrient
cycling and ecosystem functioning. ## Litter as a Fungal Habitat The litter
layer overlies mineral soil horizons and harbors distinct mycotas. It is a
transition area between the living plant community and the soil; thus the
composition of litter mycota is tied closely to that of the plant community and
contains many plant-inhabiting species as well as a distinctive assemblage of
genera and species largely restricted to decaying plant material. In humid
tropical forests, the litter-soil interface is often abrupt, and the mycotas of
the two layers are distinct. The overlying litter is species rich, whereas the
underlying soil is depauperate by comparison (Pfenning 1993; Bills and Polishook
1994). The litter-humus profile is exceptionally species rich because it
integrates fungal species from both aboveground and belowground communities. ##
Leaf Litter Microfungal Diversity Individual leaves in tropical litterfall can
harbor up to 30 different species of hyphomycetes (Sutton 1986), only some of
which grow in colonies well defined by zone lines. In temperate regions it is
common to find similar substrata such as cupules of Fagus and Quercus colonized
by 5-10 species of hyphomycetes and ascomycetes (Sutton 1973b, 1975); other
fungi colonize the fleshier tissues. The complexity of leaf litter fungal
communities presents significant challenges for inventory work. Almost all plant
debris supports more than a single species, and painstaking mounting of
vegetative and spore-forming structures under a compound microscope is the only
way to determine what is present in mixed populations without culturing. ##

## Upper Versus Lower Leaf Surfaces

Fungal communities can differ between upper and lower leaf surfaces. Hudson and
Webster (1958) discovered that [[dighton-fungal-succession-disturbance]] varies
even between upper and lower internodes of grass stems. A. Rambelli (personal
communication, 1996) observed that primary colonizers of newly fallen Pistacia
lentiscus leaves in Sardinia are different on the upper and lower surfaces, even
of individual leaves. This microhabitat specificity must be considered in

## Life Strategies of Litter Fungi

Three primary life strategies adapted from plant ecology (Grime 1977, 1979;
Cooke and Rayner 1984) characterize litter fungi:

- **Ruderal**: short-lived, high reproductive potential species in disturbed,
productive environments (many Mucorales)
- **Competitive**: persistent species that maximize growth and actively defend
captured resources
- **Stress-tolerant**: persistent species adapted to endure conditions of
environmental stress or resource depletion

Many litter fungi can manifest different strategies at different phases of their
life cycles, and nutritional modes frequently shift during decomposition.

## Nutritional Modes

Three nutritional strategies operate in leaf litter:

- **Saprotrophy**: the most frequent mode, using nonliving organic material
- **Necrotrophy**: killing host tissue and then using it for nourishment
- **Biotrophy**: exploiting only living host cells (rare in litter)

Many endophytic fungal species become saprobic on the death of their host, an
advantageous strategy because their intimate association with the plant allows
immediate utilization of the dead tissue. Furthermore, many endophytic species
enter an intermediate necrotrophic stage as the host tissue becomes moribund.
