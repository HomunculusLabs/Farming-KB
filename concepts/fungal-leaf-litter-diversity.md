# Fungal Biodiversity in Leaf Litter Leaf litter is one of the most fungal-diverse habitats in terrestrial ecosystems, serving as a transition zone between living plant communities and soil. The decomposition of leaf litter is driven primarily by fungi, making these communities essential for nutrient cycling and ecosystem functioning. ## Litter as a Fungal Habitat The litter layer overlies mineral soil horizons and harbors distinct mycotas. It is a transition area between the living plant community and the soil; thus the composition of litter mycota is tied closely to that of the plant community and contains many plant-inhabiting species as well as a distinctive assemblage of genera and species largely restricted to decaying plant material. In humid tropical forests, the litter-soil interface is often abrupt, and the mycotas of the two layers are distinct. The overlying litter is species rich, whereas the underlying soil is depauperate by comparison (Pfenning 1993; Bills and Polishook 1994). The litter-humus profile is exceptionally species rich because it integrates fungal species from both aboveground and belowground communities. ## Leaf Litter Microfungal Diversity Individual leaves in tropical litterfall can harbor up to 30 different species of hyphomycetes (Sutton 1986), only some of which grow in colonies well defined by zone lines. In temperate regions it is common to find similar substrata such as cupules of Fagus and Quercus colonized by 5-10 species of hyphomycetes and ascomycetes (Sutton 1973b, 1975); other fungi colonize the fleshier tissues. The complexity of leaf litter fungal communities presents significant challenges for inventory work. Almost all plant debris supports more than a single species, and painstaking mounting of vegetative and spore-forming structures under a compound microscope is the only way to determine what is present in mixed populations without culturing. ## Successional Patterns Fungi exhibit succession downward through the litter and to a lesser extent through soil horizons (Wicklow and Whittingham 1974; Kjøller and Struwe 1982; Aoki et al. 1990, 1992). Succession on decomposing leaves follows predictable patterns: 1. **Early colonizers**: primarily yeasts, some rapidly growing hyphomycetes, and sugar fungi that exploit readily available soluble nutrients 2. **Secondary colonizers**: cellulolytic and pectinolytic fungi that decompose structural polysaccharides 3. **Late-stage decomposers**: lignin-degrading fungi, often basidiomycetes, that attack recalcitrant compounds The duration of litter decomposition varies dramatically between ecosystems: years in temperate and boreal forests but much more rapid (months) in humid tropical ecosystems (Anderson and Swift 1983). ## Life Strategies of Litter Fungi

Three primary life strategies adapted from plant ecology (Grime 1977, 1979; Cooke and Rayner 1984) characterize litter fungi:

- **Ruderal**: short-lived, high reproductive potential species in disturbed, productive environments (many Mucorales)

- **Competitive**: persistent species that maximize growth and actively defend captured resources

- **Stress-tolerant**: persistent species adapted to endure conditions of environmental stress or resource depletion

Many litter fungi can manifest different strategies at different phases of their life cycles, and nutritional modes frequently shift during decomposition.

## Nutritional Modes

Three nutritional strategies operate in leaf litter:

- **Saprotrophy**: the most frequent mode, using nonliving organic material

- **Necrotrophy**: killing host tissue and then using it for nourishment

- **Biotrophy**: exploiting only living host cells (rare in litter)

Many endophytic fungal species become saprobic on the death of their host, an advantageous strategy because their intimate association with the plant allows immediate utilization of the dead tissue. Furthermore, many endophytic species enter an intermediate necrotrophic stage as the host tissue becomes moribund.
