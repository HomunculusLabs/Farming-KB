# Digging a Hole as Permaculture Practice

Digging a hole is presented in this permaculture design course as both
a practical skill and a philosophical entry point into design work.
Nine point five times out of ten the first step in any permaculture
project is to start digging a hole. If you want to study permaculture,
you might as well go out and buy your own shovel now. You will dig
holes and you will move earth.

## The Embodied Knowledge of the First Hole

A single hole seems like a simple thing, but before you can dig that
first simple hole think of all the work you have to do in analyzing
your environment. You have to understand your patterns, analyze your
environment, sample the soil, inventory the wildlife, do a zone and
sector analysis. This is all after the client interview, the site
selection, and the budget discussion. All of this knowledge combines
into the one most crucial piece of information for the success of any
project: where to dig the first hole. There is a lot of embodied
knowledge in that one humble hole. Many patterns have been analyzed to
create this one, first, crucial detail.

## The Philosophical Moment of Digging

Digging a hole is also an amazing philosophical moment. Think about
it. The sound of a shovel going into earth is the sound of imagination
becoming reality. That imagination comes from your understanding of
permaculture. You studied permaculture because of your beliefs. Your
beliefs are a product of your philosophy, and that sound of the shovel
head digging into the earth is the sound of that philosophy
manifesting into and becoming a part of the real world.

This perspective frames all physical permaculture work as the
materialization of ecological design thinking. Every hole, every berm,
every swale, and every planting represents hours of analysis,
observation, and design thinking made tangible through physical labor.

## Stacking Functions in Excavation

One of the beauties of digging a hole is the inherent stacking of
functions. Whenever you are digging you are also mining. Every hole
generates a little pile of earth. Usually the earth excavated from a
hole is totally taken for granted and just thoughtlessly slung aside.
The permaculture student knows better. The permaculture student knows
that every shovel full is a shovel full of usable resource. No
permaculture designer ever just slings dirt. The excavant from the
hole is always placed somewhere. With every shovel full you are working
on two projects at once: one that needs a hole, one that needs some
earth.

If you start out only needing a hole then this shovel full of excavant
becomes an inspiration. Since you got it, what are you going to do
with it? Based on your soil sample, what is it good for? Is it good
topsoil which can go on a garden? Does it have good clay content and
therefore could be used to make earthen bricks or a cob oven? Would it
be good earth to make a berm which diverts rain water?

Before you ever dig a hole you should know what you are going to do
with the material that comes out. The possibilities are limited only by
your own imagination, and it all began with digging a humble little
hole. This approach embodies the permaculture principle of producing
no waste: every material moved in excavation becomes a resource for
another part of the design.

## Safety and Physical Awareness

The humble little hole is also one of the great causes of physical
injury. Digging has torn back muscles, torn ligaments, twisted ankles,
and caused numerous shovel injuries. Shovel edges can slice deeply and
crack skulls. You must be very aware of your body, surroundings, and
movements. You should never take one second for granted while
shoveling, because it only takes one second to get seriously hurt.
Proper technique, appropriate tools, and constant awareness are
essential at all times.

## The Hole as a Multi-Layered Design Element

A hole can be a many-layered, marvelous thing from a permaculture
perspective. A hole might serve as a planting pit, water infiltration
basin, structure foundation, swale start, compost pit, or pond
beginning. The same physical action serves multiple design purposes
depending on placement, depth, and how excavated material is used.
The connection between analysis and physical digging is direct: every
design decision converges in where to place the first hole.

## Health Benefits

Digging a hole is also great exercise. Regular shoveling builds
strength and fitness. As long as proper form and safety precautions
are maintained, the physical labor of permaculture earthworks
contributes to personal health and well-being. The connection between
physical work, ecological design, and personal health is one that
permaculture embodies naturally.

## Key Principles

- The first hole embodies all design knowledge: site analysis, soil
  sampling, zone and sector analysis, and client goals all converge
  in the decision of where and how to dig
- Every shovel full is a resource: excavated material should always be
  planned for and directed to another useful purpose on the site
- Stack functions in excavation: digging a hole and placing the
  excavant means working on two projects simultaneously
- Produce no waste: know what you will do with excavated material
  before you begin digging
- Safety first: shoveling is physically demanding and potentially
  dangerous; awareness of body, surroundings, and technique is
  essential at all times

## Applications

- Planning excavation projects by first determining the destination
  for all excavated soil, whether for gardens, berms, earthen
  construction, or swale building
- Using soil samples from test holes to determine whether excavated
  material is suitable for topsoil, cob, adobe, or thermal mass
  applications
- Creating water infiltration pits where excavated holes serve the
  dual function of managing runoff and providing material for nearby
  berms and mounds
- Building cob ovens or earthen structures using clay-rich material
  excavated from foundation holes or pond excavations on site
- Designing composting pits where the excavation itself creates the
  containment structure and the removed soil becomes part of the
  composting layer
