# Plants and Electromagnetism

Plants are continually affected by wavelengths of the electromagnetic
spectrum, from earth, moon, planets, cosmos, and man-made devices.
From eighteenth-century experiments with static electricity to modern
electroculture, researchers have demonstrated that electrical fields,
atmospheric electricity, and electromagnetic radiation profoundly
influence plant growth, transpiration, and development. The challenge
lies in determining which frequencies and intensities are beneficial
and which are harmful.

## Overview

The study of electricity's effect on plants began in 1729 when French
astronomer Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan noticed his Mimosa pudica
folded its leaves at sunset even when kept in total darkness, suggesting
plants could "sense" the sun without seeing it. In the 1740s, Abbé Nollet
demonstrated that electrifying plants increased their transpiration rates
and that electrified mustard seeds sprouted faster and grew taller than
nonelectrified controls. By 1783, Abbé Bertholon published De l'Electricité
des Végétaux, documenting that electricity altered the viscosity of fluids
in living organisms and changed growth functions.

The electromagnetic spectrum ranges from enormous macropulsations lasting
hundreds of thousands of years to super-rapid energy pulses alternating
10 sextillion times per second. Between these extremes lie gamma rays,
X-rays, visible light, radio, TV, and radar frequencies. Electromagnetic
waves differ from sound waves by traveling through vacuum at 186 million
miles per second, yet no physicist has fully explained how they propagate.

## Key Concepts

- Atmospheric electricity continuously streams between earth and sky;
  on clear days the earth is negative and the atmosphere positive, with
  electrons streaming skyward from soil and plants
- The earth's voltage increases with altitude: 200 volts over a
  six-foot person, 40,000 volts from the Empire State Building, 360,000
  volts between ionosphere and earth's surface
- Electrical stimulation can accelerate seed germination, increase
  transpiration, boost crop yields, and improve fruit quality
- The amount, quality, and duration of electrical stimulation are
  crucially important and vary between species
- Lightning conductors near plants have been observed to dramatically
  increase growth, suggesting plants naturally collect atmospheric
  electricity through their pointed leaves and roots
- Ionized air particles from soil emissions form the basis of
  atmospheric electricity, with an estimated 3,000 to 4,000 electrical
  storms raging globally at any time

## Historical Electroculture Experiments

In 1770, Professor Gardini stretched wires above a monastery garden in
Turin. The plants withered, but revived when wires were removed, suggesting
either deprivation or overdose of natural electricity. Bertholon cited an
Italian report that two jasmine bushes next to a lightning conductor grew
to thirty feet while others reached only four feet. In 1845, Edward Solly
published experiments suspending wires over garden plots and burying them
under soil, finding that of seventy experiments only nineteen were
beneficial and nearly as many harmful, highlighting the importance of
proper dosage.

The Finnish scientist Selim Lemström made four expeditions to subpolar
regions from 1868 to 1884, theorizing that luxuriant Arctic vegetation
was attributable to the aurora borealis rather than extended daylight. He
found that annual growth rings in fir trees correlated with periods of
high aurora and sunspot activity. Connecting plants to a static generator
via overhead wires and a grounded soil pole, he achieved nearly 50 percent
weight gains in eight weeks, more than doubled strawberry yields while
making them sweeter, and increased barley harvests by one-third.

## Lodge and Newman's Advances

Sir Oliver Lodge, the eminent physicist who also joined the Society for
Psychical Research, improved on Lemström's system by suspending wire grids
on insulators attached to high poles, allowing farm equipment to pass
underneath. In one growing season he increased Canadian Red Fife wheat
yields by 40 percent, with bakers reporting superior bread quality. His
collaborator John Newman achieved over 20 percent increase in wheat in
England, larger and sweeter strawberries, and sugar beets with higher
than normal sugar content. Newman notably published his findings in the
Standard Handbook for Electrical Engineers rather than a botanical journal.

## Modern Electrical Applications

Hungarian engineer Joseph Molitorisz applied Abbé Nollet's insights on
electro-osmosis to citrus orchards in California. Running current through
citrus seedlings, he found growth accelerated in one direction and
arrested in the other, suggesting electricity either abetted or blocked
the natural current in plants. Applying fifty-eight volts to six branches
of an orange tree, sap circulated freely in powered branches while
untouched branches showed little flow. He also got a tree to drop ripe
fruit while retaining green oranges through electrical stimulation.

Dr. Larry E. Murr at Pennsylvania State University spent seven years
simulating thunderstorm electrical conditions in a laboratory, achieving
significant growth increases by carefully regulating voltage field
strength over plants in lucite pots on aluminum plates. Dr. George Starr
White discovered that dangling bright pieces of iron and tin from fruit
trees facilitated growth, corroborated by Randall Hay, who found tomato
plants with metallic Christmas tree balls bore fruit earlier than normal.

## Practical Applications

Electroculture systems using overhead wire grids on insulated poles can
increase crop yields 20 to 50 percent. Direct current application to
orchards can stimulate sap flow and enable synchronized fruit dropping
for mechanized harvest. Understanding atmospheric electricity helps
explain why plants in certain locations thrive and suggests methods for
optimizing electrical conditions in greenhouses and open fields.
