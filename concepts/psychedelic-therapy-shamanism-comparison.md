# Psychedelic Therapy and Shamanism Comparison

Western psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy and indigenous shamanic healing with hallucinogenic plants both use psychoactive substances for healing and obtaining knowledge. While superficial similarities exist, there are profound differences in the underlying worldview, the structure of the healing process, and the assumptions about the nature of reality and consciousness.

## Four Paradigms of Entheogenic Use

Ralph Metzner, in his comparative analysis published in the Journal of Psychoactive Drugs (1998), identified four distinct paradigms for the use of hallucinogenic substances in healing and knowledge-seeking contexts.

The first paradigm is psychedelic psychotherapy within the standard Western scientific framework. Here, the drug amplifies and intensifies the processes of internal self-analysis and self-understanding. The goal is therapeutic insight within a materialistic understanding of consciousness.

The second paradigm is shamanic rituals of healing and divination. In these traditions, primarily the shaman or healer takes the medicine to see the causes of illness and determine what remedy to apply. The worldview encompasses multiple realities and spirit beings as real entities.

The third paradigm is syncretic folk religious ceremonies, where the focus is community bonding and celebratory worship rather than individual healing. The Brazilian ayahuasca churches exemplify this approach.

The fourth paradigm is hybrid shamanic-therapeutic rituals, which blend elements of Western psychotherapy with indigenous shamanic practices. These have been developing over the past several decades in North America and Europe.

## The Psycholytic Approach

The psycholytic model emerged from European psychiatric research following the discovery of LSD's psychoactive properties. Albert Hofmann described LSD as bringing about a loosening or opening of the psyche (seelische Auflockerung). The term psycholytic means mind-dissolving.

In psycholytic therapy, neurotic patients suffering from anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorders, or psychosomatic conditions received LSD in a series of sessions at gradually increasing doses, while undergoing standard psychoanalytic interactions from a Freudian perspective. The rationale was that through the loosening of psychological defenses, patients would become more vividly aware of previously unconscious emotional dynamics and reaction patterns.

Stanislav Grof, working within this model, made the significant discovery that beyond resolving Freudian childhood dynamics, patients in high-dose sessions would relive the sensory and emotional features of their birth experience. Grof named these patterns perinatal matrices and developed a comprehensive cartography of the human psyche that extended far beyond conventional psychoanalytic frameworks.

## The Psychedelic Therapy Approach

The psychedelic model was developed more or less simultaneously with the psycholytic approach, primarily in Anglo-American psychiatric circles. Humphrey Osmond, working in Canada with Abram Hoffer on LSD treatment of alcoholism, coined the term psychedelic, meaning mind-manifesting, in correspondence with Aldous Huxley.

Psychedelic therapy typically involved one or a small number of high-dose sessions. The contents of the unconscious mind would manifest as vivid hallucinatory imagery, leading to insight and transformation. This approach was sometimes described as attempting to simulate the life-changing bottoming-out experience that could catalyze recovery from alcoholism.

Timothy Leary and the Harvard research project adapted this approach, shifting focus from clinical treatment to exploring the possibilities of psychedelic experience for healthy individuals. The concept of consciousness expansion was introduced, contrasting the expanded awareness of the psychedelic state with the contracted, fixated patterns characteristic of addictions and compulsions. Leary also formalized the set and setting hypothesis, which holds that internal mindset and external context are the primary determinants of the psychedelic experience.

## Shamanic Healing Rituals

Shamanic rituals involving hallucinogenic plants are the intentional arrangement of set and setting for purposes of healing and divination. The traditional shamanic ceremonial form involves a small group of six to twelve people coming together with respectful, spiritual attitudes to share a profound inner journey facilitated by powerful plant catalysts.

Three features distinguish traditional shamanic ceremonies from typical Western psychedelic therapy sessions. First, traditional rituals involve very little talking among participants except during preparatory or integration phases. Second, singing by the shaman is considered essential to the success of the healing process. The rhythmic chanting provides psychic support for moving through visions and minimizes the likelihood of getting stuck in frightening or seductive experiences. Third, ceremonies are almost always conducted in darkness or low light to facilitate the emergence of visions.

Shamanic healing experiences have three primary variations. The first is extraction of a toxin that may have been implanted through sorcery. The second is retrieval of a split-off psychic fragment or soul, known as soul retrieval. The third is the experience of dismemberment and reconstitution, where the patient is symbolically destroyed and rebuilt with a healthier, stronger spiritual body.

## The Shamanic Worldview

Two elements in the shamanic worldview differ radically from the accepted Western paradigm. The first is the belief and perception that multiple realities or worlds exist and can be explored in expanded states of consciousness. These realms have been referred to as the inner world, spirit world, upper world, lower world, faerie world, dreamtime, or otherworld.

Access to these other-worlds may occur through journeying, perhaps on the back of an animal or carried by a large bird. Alternatively, one may see into the spirit world while remaining aware of the ordinary physical world. During entheogenic experiences, the boundaries between worlds become more permeable.

The second challenging element is the belief that spirits, the beings encountered in dreams and visions, are just as real as physical organisms. Such spirits are associated with particular animals, plants, trees, places, deceased ancestors, and other non-ordinary entities. Healing and divination are experienced as being done by or with the assistance of these spirits, also called allies, power animals, guardians, or helpers.

## Syncretic Religious Ceremonies

Syncretic folk religious ceremonies represent a middle ground between pure shamanic healing and Western approaches. The emphasis shifts from individual healing toward group worship, celebration, singing, and prayer. Instead of shamans or therapists, priests and officiants lead the ceremonies.

The Native American Church (NAC) uses peyote as a sacrament in all-night ceremonies conducted in a tipi around a central fire. A roadman presides with assistance from a drummer, firekeeper, and cedar-man. Traditional peyote songs are sung throughout the night. The NAC has approximately 300,000 members in the United States and Canada.

In Brazil, three major ayahuasca churches exist: Santo Daime, Uniao do Vegetal (UDV), and Barquinia. Each was founded in the 1950s by rubber tappers who encountered ayahuasca through indigenous or mestizo practitioners. All three have several thousand members, official recognition in Brazil, and satellite centers in North America and Europe. Research has confirmed that long-term membership is associated with no adverse health effects and with reduced incidence of alcoholism and drug addiction.

The Bwiti cult among the Fang people of Gabon and Zaire uses iboga for powerful death-rebirth initiatory experiences. Initiates undergo an experience where their head is opened up, after which they can communicate with ancestor spirits for life guidance.

## Hybrid Shamanic Therapeutic Rituals

In modern Western contexts, hybrid neoshamanic medicine circles have emerged that blend psychotherapeutic and traditional shamanic approaches. These circles typically preserve certain basic elements from traditional ceremonies: a circular seating arrangement, an altar or central fire, an experienced guide, low light or eye-shades, music (drumming, rattling, singing, or recorded), dedication of ritual space through invocation of spirits of the four directions, and cultivation of a respectful spiritual attitude.

Participants in these circles are typically experienced in one or more psychospiritual practices including meditation, breathwork, yoga, or various forms of psychotherapy. The insights from these practices are woven into their work with entheogenic medicines. Participants consistently report that entheogenic plant medicines, when combined with meditative or therapeutic insight processes, function to amplify awareness and sensitize perception, particularly amplifying somatic, emotional, and instinctual awareness.

A talking staff or singing staff is often used, originating among Pacific Northwest indigenous peoples. Only the person holding the circulating staff speaks or sings, with no discussion, questioning, or interpretation as might occur in group therapy formats.

## Common Ground and Key Differences

Both Western psychedelic therapy and shamanic healing recognize the central importance of an experienced guide or therapist. Both acknowledge the importance of set (intention) and setting. Both accept that healing and insight can occur through the experience. However, the underlying paradigms of illness and treatment are fundamentally different.

Western therapy operates within a framework of individual psychology, where symptoms arise from personal history, trauma, and maladaptive patterns. The goal is insight and behavioral change within the individual. Shamanic healing operates within a framework that includes spiritual dimensions, where illness may originate from soul loss, spirit intrusion, sorcery, or imbalance with the natural world.

The majority of Westerners who develop ongoing practices with entheogenic plants report expanding their belief systems beyond conventional materialistic paradigms. While accepting the validity of Western psychological insights, they come to accept the reality of nonmaterial spirit beings and multiple worlds of consciousness, positions more aligned with indigenous and esoteric traditions than with mainstream Western science.
