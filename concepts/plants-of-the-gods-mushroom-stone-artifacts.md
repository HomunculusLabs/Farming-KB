# Mushroom Stone Artifacts

## Archaeological Evidence of Sacred Mushroom Use

Mushrooms have apparently been ceremonially employed in
Mesoamerica for many centuries. Archaeological discoveries have
revealed a rich material culture associated with the ritual use
of psychoactive fungi, providing concrete evidence for the great
antiquity of mushroom worship in the region.

## The Mushroom Stones of Guatemala

Miniature mushroom stones, approximately 2,200 years of age, have
been found in archaeological sites near Guatemala City. More than
two hundred mushroom stone effigies have been discovered, the
oldest dating from the first millennium B.C. These carved stone
objects depict stylized mushroom caps mounted on pedestals or
human figures, clearly representing fungi of religious
significance.

Although the majority of mushroom stones are Guatemalan, some
have been unearthed in El Salvador and Honduras, and others as
far north as Veracruz and Guerrero in Mexico. This wide
geographic distribution indicates that the sacred mushroom cult
was widespread throughout Mesoamerica.

It has been postulated that stone mushroom effigies buried with
a Mayan dignitary suggested a connection with the Nine Lords of
the Xibalba, described in the sacred book Popol Vuh. The Popol
Vuh, the Quiche Maya creation epic, describes a underworld realm
populated by powerful lords, and mushrooms may have served as a
medium for contacting these beings.

## Connection to Mayan Underworld

Several early sources have suggested that Mayan languages in
Guatemala had mushrooms named for the underworld. The association
between mushrooms and the realm of the dead is a recurring theme
in Mesoamerican cosmology. The mushroom stones may represent the
iconographic expression of this belief, serving as ritual objects
that mediated between the living and the dead.

The placement of mushroom stones in tombs and burials supports
their funerary and ancestral significance. The stones appear to
have been intentionally buried with the dead, suggesting that
mushrooms played a role in beliefs about death and the afterlife.

## The Xochipilli Statue

A superb statue of Xochipilli, Aztec Prince of Flowers, from the
early sixteenth century, was discovered on the slopes of the
volcano Mt. Popocatepetl. His face is in ecstasy, as though seeing
visions in an intoxication; his head is slightly tilted, as though
hearing voices. His body is engraved with stylized flowers that
have been identified as sacred, most of them inebriating, plants.

The pedestal on which he sits is decorated with designs
representing cross-sections of the caps of Psilocybe aztecorum,
a hallucinogenic mushroom known only from this volcano. The glyphs
depict: a mushroom cap; the tendril of Morning Glory; the flower
of Tobacco; the flower of the sacred Morning Glory; the bud of
Sinicuichi; and stylized caps of Psilocybe aztecorum.

Xochipilli thus undoubtedly represents not simply the Prince of
Flowers but more specifically the Prince of Inebriating Flowers,
including the mushrooms that, in Nahuatl poetry, were called
"flowers" and "flowers that intoxicate."

## The Magliabecchiano Codex

A celebrant depicted in the sixteenth-century Magliabecchiano
Codex is shown ingesting a pair of hallucinogenic mushrooms during
a sacred rite. Behind him is the Lord of the Underworld,
Mictlantecuhtli. The three jade green mushrooms in front of the
celebrant were painted in this color to indicate their great value
as sacred objects. This early colonial manuscript provides direct
ethnohistorical evidence of ceremonial mushroom consumption.

## Prehistoric Mexican Mushroom Cult

Evidence is accumulating to indicate that a mushroom cult
flourished in prehistoric times — from 100 B.C. to about A.D.
300-400 in northwestern Mexico: in Colima, Jalisco, and Nayarit.
Funerary effigies, with two "horns" protruding from the head,
are believed to represent male and female "deities" or priests
associated with mushrooms.

Traditions among contemporary Huichol Indians in Jalisco also
suggest the former religious use of these fungi "in ancient times."
These ceramic figurines, showing figures with mushroom-like
protuberances, provide additional evidence of a widespread
prehistoric mushroom complex in western Mexico.

## South American Evidence

In Colombia, many anthropomorphic gold pectorals with two dome-
like ornaments on the head have been found in the Darien style.
The majority have been unearthed in the Sinu area of northwestern
Colombia and the Calima region on the Pacific coast. For lack of
a better term, they have been called "telephone spirits" but may
actually represent mushroom-headed figures similar to the Mexican
funerary effigies.

The Yurimagua Indians of the Peruvian Amazon were reported in the
late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries to be drinking a
potently inebriating beverage made from a "tree fungus." It has
been suggested that the mushroom might have been Psilocybe
yungensis, which occurs in this region.
