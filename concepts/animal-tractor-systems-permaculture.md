## Animal Tractor Systems in [[permaculture-designers-manual-animal-forage-systems|permaculture-integrated-animal-systems|Permaculture]]

Animal tractor systems use confined livestock to prepare land for cultivation by clearing ground cover, consuming weeds, and fertilizing soil. Rather than using mechanical equipment, these systems harness the natural behaviors of animals—scratching, rooting, grazing, and manuring—to convert weed-infested or degraded land into productive garden and tree crop areas. The two primary systems described here are the chicken tractor and the pig tractor.

## [[dano-gorsich]] developed and executed a highly successful chicken tractor and garden system on a 0.5 hectare farm on Moloka'i, Hawaii, demonstrating the full potential of this approach on stony hillside terrain:

1. **Layout**: The farm is divided into 5–6 fenced plots
2. **Flock**: A 40-chicken flock rotates through these plots over approximately 18 months
3. **Sequence per plot**:
   - Chickens are confined to a plot and scratch it bare of vegetation
   - The bare plot is limed, raked, and immediately sown to vegetable crops (typically brassicas, beans, peas, amaranth, cucurbits, radishes, and root crops)
   - Chickens move to the next plot
   - In 6–8 weeks, vegetables on the first plot reach full production
   - Young papaya are interplanted among the vegetables for succession

### Successional Evolution

The tractor system creates a successional evolution over approximately 18 months to 2 years:
- **Phase 1**: Chickens pioneer and clear the weed layer
- **Phase 2**: Vegetables and papaya succeed the cleared area
- **Phase 3**: A more perennial system succeeds the weed layer entirely

Throughout this evolution, the system produces eggs, chickens, vegetables, and papaya at modest commercial levels. Milk goats and chickens are also let out onto the paths to eat greens when the pens are bare, ensuring no waste.

### Chicken Forage Plants

In more mature cycles, specific plants can be sown into the pens a few weeks before chickens return, providing greens and grains:
- Buckwheat
- Comfrey
- Millet
- Sunflower
- Sorghum

When chickens are the primary crop, chicken forage plants replace vegetables and some fruit crops, making the system nearly self-sufficient for poultry feed. Normal weeds serve as excellent chicken fodder:
- Oxalis, cleavers, dandelion, onion weed, nettle, nut grass
- Solanum family species: huckleberry, black nightshade, pepino, kangaroo apple, tomato, huskberry, Sodom apple

### Infrastructure Considerations

- **Fencing**: Secure pen fencing is the main cost; strong fences also support vine crops
- **Shade**: A few larger legume trees (*Tipuana tipu*, larger *Albizia*) provide high shade for pens and crops
- **Breeding**: Near the house, small top-netted and secure rearing pens allow broody hens to replace culled chickens
- **Versatility**: Rocky or rough country is readily prepared for crop by chickens

### Variations

Numerous chicken tractor variations have been observed, including:
- Systems with passionfruit fence/trellis crop
- Pure vegetable garden systems
- Systems designed for small fruit or herbaceous orchard

All variants share two remarkable characteristics: lack of weeds and high production.

## Pig Tractor Systems

### How It Works

The pig tractor follows the same rotational principle as the chicken tractor but operates at a larger scale (1–40 hectare properties) and handles tougher vegetation. Pigs are particularly effective against:

- **Larger shrub-weeds**: Lantana, gorse, blackberry
- **Deep-rooted weeds**: Bindweed (*Convolvulus*), rhizomatous grasses, comfrey

### Stocking Density and Plot Management

- **Recommended density**: 50 pigs per hectare for full clearance of weeds
- **Plot size**: 0.5–2 hectare plots are fenced using permanent electric fencing, which is significantly cheaper than chicken mesh fencing
- **Clearance time**: Each pen is typically cleared in 6–10 weeks
- **Soil preparation**: Rooting pigs effectively rough-plow the soil, creating a well-worked seedbed

### Post-Clearance Planting

Once a pen is cleared and rough-plowed, it is planted with pig forage crops:
- Lucerne
- Comfrey sets
- Sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke)
- Sweet potato
- Inga trees
- Papaya
- Banana

This rotation continues until the pigs return to the pen in the next cycle.

### Large-Scale Integration (20–40 hectares)

At scale, the pig tractor system can pioneer high-quality milk-cow pasture consisting of:
- Chicory, dandelion, comfrey, dock
- Grasses and clover

Cows follow along 2–3 months behind the pig tractor, creating a continuous rotation. Key synergies include:
- Excess milk products (whey, skim milk) fed to pigs as accessory food
- Piglets ranging over diverse pasture rarely show iron anaemia deficiency
- Parasite cycles are naturally broken through the rotation
- Soil humus content constantly improves

Large animals like pigs and cows require fenced tree strips, tree guards, and border hedgerows to supply tree forage crops within the rotational system.

## Integration with Farm Systems

Animal tractor systems can serve multiple roles within a broader permaculture design:

1. **Pioneering phase**: Used temporarily to clear land before establishing permanent tree crops
2. **Accessory to tree crops**: Ongoing rotational clearing between established orchard rows
3. **Permanent mixed farm feature**: As a continuous element of integrated livestock-crop systems
4. **Seasonal cleanup**: Used periodically to remove crop wastes and fallen fruits

Chickens in particular should be considered a permanent forage system in all mixed orchards, providing continuous weed management, pest control, and soil fertility benefits.

## Grasslands and Range Management Principles

Animal tractor systems fit within a broader framework of tropical grasslands and range management, which includes four key strategies:

### Forage Species Addition
Introducing improved forage species to existing grasslands, including grass legumes and trees that provide foliage, food sugars, seed carbohydrates, or fruits.

### Environmental Modifications
Improving the landscape through water storage, soil structure enhancement, irrigation, windbreaks, and shelter. Key fertiliser or trace elements can be added, and plant species maintained by slashing or light cultivation of pasture.

### Forage Conservation
Ensuring feed availability through:
- Rotational or periodic rests from grazing
- Hay and silage production
- Protein or urea supplements and molasses during drought
- Keeping stocking rates below worst-case carrying capacity

### Stock Management
Strategic buying and selling to keep animal numbers aligned with seasonal conditions:
- Timing calving or buying animals in spring or early summer
- Selling or culling dry cows in autumn to reduce winter feed demand
- At extremes, penning stock and feeding harvested fodders

## Banna Grass (Pennisetum purpureum) in Integrated Systems

Banna (elephant) grass serves as a valuable bridge between animal tractor systems and permanent pasture. It performs best on deep alluvial or coarse flats above 110 cm (alluvium) or 90 cm (coastal) rainfall, reaching 2.5–4.5 meters in height. It can be grazed to 1 meter or cut to 15 cm for forage and mulch. It needs a vigorous legume interplant such as Leucaena, or forage legumes like Calopogon, Centrosema, or Glycine in high-rainfall tropical areas.

Banna grass can carry 7 milk cows per hectare when cut and hand-fed with Leucaena and sugar pods, making it a cornerstone of integrated tropical livestock systems.
