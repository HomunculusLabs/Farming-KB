# Permaculture Water Harvesting

## Overview

Water is the primary limiting factor in most landscapes. Permaculture water harvesting captures, stores, and directs water through the landscape using earthworks, vegetation, and storage systems. The goal is to keep water on the land as long as possible, allowing it to infiltrate and be used by plants and animals before it runs off.

## Principles

- Water travels in a landscape from high to low points
- Trees increase effective rainfall through condensation and interception
- Earthworks on contour slow, spread, and sink water into the landscape
- Multiple small storages are more efficient and resilient than single large ones
- Water stored in the soil (as soil moisture) is more valuable than water in tanks
- Every property should have a sequence of water storage from highest to lowest elevation

## Swales

A swale is a level ditch on contour that catches water running off the land surface and allows it to infiltrate into the soil. Swales are one of the most important tools in permaculture water management.

### Design

- Dug exactly on contour using an A-frame, water level, or laser level
- Bottom of swale is level so water spreads evenly along its length
- Spoil from excavation is placed on the downslope side to form a berm
- Swales do not carry water -- they fill and infiltrate
- Spacing between swales depends on slope: steeper slopes require closer spacing

### Functions

- Capture runoff and allow infiltration into soil
- Recharge groundwater
- Reduce erosion by slowing water flow
- Create a moist planting zone on the berm
- Distribute water across the landscape rather than concentrating it
- Build soil through accumulated [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] on the berm

## Ponds and Dams

Ponds store water at the surface for irrigation, livestock, fire control, and aquaculture. Key considerations:

- Place ponds above the point of use for gravity-fed distribution
- Use natural depressions or excavate in areas with impervious subsoil (clay)
- The catchment area must be sufficient to fill and maintain the pond
- Design spillways to safely handle overflow during heavy rain events
- Multiple small ponds at different elevations provide more resilience than one large dam
- Integrate ponds with swale systems -- swales feed ponds and ponds release water slowly

## Rainwater Collection

[[comparison-drip-irrigation-vs-rainwater-harvesting]] from roofs provides high-quality water for domestic and garden use:

- Every square meter of roof collects approximately 1 liter of water per millimeter of rainfall
- First-flush diverters discard the initial contaminated runoff from roof surfaces
- Storage tanks should be sized for the dry season demand and catchment area
- Tanks placed on stands provide gravity-fed water pressure
- Roof catchment is the cleanest source of water on a property

## Greywater Systems

Greywater (water from showers, sinks, and laundry) can be treated and reused in the landscape:

- Greywater contains nutrients from soaps [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] that benefit plants
- Simple systems use settling tanks, grease traps, and biological filtration
- Reed beds and [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] effectively treat greywater using aquatic plants
- Mulch basins around trees can directly receive filtered greywater
- Never store untreated greywater -- use it immediately or treat it aerobically

## Keyline Design

Keyline is a [[slope-analysis-landscape-planning]] technique developed by P.A. Yeomans that optimizes water distribution on agricultural land:

- The keyline is identified as the point of maximum change in slope (where a convex slope becomes concave)
- Cultivation parallel to the keyline, widening toward valleys, spreads water from valleys to ridges
- Keypoint dams are placed at the keyline where the valley floor steepens
- Water from keypoint dams can be gravity-fed along ridges to irrigate the entire property

## Water Conservation in Design

- Zone planning places high-water-use elements near water sources
- Mulch dramatically reduces evaporation from soil surfaces
- Windbreaks reduce evapotranspiration caused by wind
- Shade trees reduce soil moisture loss
- Deep-rooted perennial plants access water that annuals cannot reach
- Ground covers protect soil from direct sun and rain impact
