# Zone and Sector Planning in Permaculture Design

Zone and sector ground planning is the core spatial organizing methodology of [[permaculture]]. Mollison describes it as "the whole key to efficient energy planning (which is, in fact, efficient economic planning)—the zonation and radial or sectoral placement of plants, animal ranges, and structures."

## Zoning: Frequency-Based Placement

Zones are defined by the frequency of visits required between the inhabitant and the element (plant, animal, or structure). Two factors determine zoning:

1. **(a)** The number of times you need to visit the plant, animal, or structure
2. **(b)** The number of times the plant, animal, or structure needs you to visit it

### Visit Frequency Examples

- **Poultry shed**: 460 visits per year (365 for eggs, 20 for manure, 50 for watering, 5 for culling, 20 other) → Zone I or II
- **Oak tree**: 2 visits per year (to collect acorns) → Zone IV or V
- **Lemon tree**: 66–112 visits per year (60–100 for fruit, 6–12 for tree care) → Zone I
- **Apple tree**: ~15 visits per year → Zone II

### The Five Zones

**Zone I — House Climate / Domestic Sufficiency**
- Complete sheet mulch establishment
- Intensive cup or espalier pruning
- Selected dwarf or multi-graft trees
- Rainwater tanks, well, bore, reticulation
- House/glasshouse/storage integration
- Intensive vegetable gardens, herbs, salad greens

**Zone II — Small Domestic Self-Sufficiency**
- Spot mulch and tree guards
- Pyramid and built trellis pruning
- Grafted varieties
- Earth tank and fire control
- Greenhouse and barns, poultry sheds
- Soft fruits, berry bushes, main vegetable crops

**Zone III — Main Crop, Stock, Orchard, Stored Food**
- Soil conditioning and green mulch
- Unpruned and natural trellis
- Selected seedlings for later grafts
- Water storage in soils and dams
- Feed store, field shelter
- Main staple crops, orchard trees, grazing

**Zone IV — Gathering, Forage, Forestry, Pasture**
- Soil conditioning only
- Seedlings thinned to selected varieties or managed by browse
- Dams, rivers, bores, wind pumps
- Field shelter grown as hedgerow and woodlot
- Timber, firewood, wild foraging

**Zone V — Unmanaged / Wilderness**
- No deliberate management
- Visited rarely for observation and harvesting
- Preserves wildlife habitat and genetic diversity

### The Golden Rule

"The golden rule is to develop the nearest area first, get it under control, and then expand the perimeter." Mollison warns that novices often select a garden away from the house and neither reap efficiently nor care well enough. "Any soil gives a good garden, so stay close to the home."

### The Back Doorstep Principle

"If you do not have the doorstep controlled, there is not much hope for the back paddock, or the back fence." Russian data shows that 4% of land—the small area around peasant houses—produces 60% of the food. Mollison asks: "If 4% produces 60%, what would happen if the peasants were given 8%?"

The ideal home garden is approximately 750–1,000 m² for annual agriculture. Less means too little food; more means too much land to control. But reducing the annual system frees land for perennial planting and free-range animals.

## Sector Planning

Sector planning [[maps]] external energies that affect the site:

- **Fire danger sector**: Direction from which [[wildfire]] threatens
- **Cold winds**: Winter wind patterns
- **Hot, salty, or dusty winds**: Seasonal harsh wind directions
- **Screening of irksome views**: Privacy and aesthetic needs
- **Winter and summer sun angles**: Solar access for passive heating and cooling
- **Reflection from ponds**: Light and heat bouncing from water surfaces

### Placement Example: Pine Tree

A pine tree placement decision demonstrates combined zone/sector thinking:
- Zone IV (infrequent visits needed)
- Away from fire danger sector (pines accumulate fuel and "burn like a tar barrel")
- Toward cold wind sector (pines are hardy windbreaks)
- Should bear edible nuts as forage (multi-function)

### Placement Example: Poultry Shed

- Border Zone I (frequent visits for eggs)
- Away from fire sector
- Border the annual garden (easy manure collection)
- Back onto the forage system
- Possibly attach to a greenhouse (gas exchange, heat)
- Form part of a windbreak system

## Design Criteria

Mollison provides a comprehensive checklist for clients:

- Passive energy systems
- Adequate climate control on site
- Future developments planned
- Provision for food self-sufficiency on site
- Minimal external energy needs
- Wastes safely disposed of on site
- Low-maintenance structures and grounds
- Water supply assured, conserved
- Fire, cold, excess heat, and wind factors controlled and directed

### Implementation Priority

1. **First**: Structures and designs that **generate** energy
2. **Second**: Those which **save** energy
3. **Third**: Those which **consume** energy

### The Simple Check

"The simple way to check on any design is to ask 'Why did you put that structure (or plant) there?'"

## Time as a Resource

"Time, like area, is a resource (as any farmer knows). Just as we can over-extend in area, so can we in time." This is the basis of zonation planning—centralizing on settlement to conserve time for control, which maintains yield and stability.

Mollison warns that if we do not regulate our numbers, appetites, and the area we occupy, "nature will do so for us, by famine, erosion, poverty, and disease." The double jeopardy of "sterile city and delinquent landscape" results when city, forest, and farm are all neglected.

## The Two Energy-Conserving Rules

1. **"No placement without the element (plant, animal, or structure) serving at least two or more functions."**
2. **"Every function (water collection, fire protection) served in two or more ways."**

These two rules, combined with [[zone-and-sector-analysis]], constitute the fundamental methodology of permaculture spatial design.
