## Implicate Order and the Unity of Nature

## Overview

The concept of **implicate order**, as articulated by physicist David Bohm in *Wholeness and the Implicate Order* (1980), describes a fundamental enfolded reality in which all phenomena are interconnected. The word "implicate" derives from the Latin *enfolded*, suggesting that when we isolate individuals, effects, or disciplines from this enfolded order, we are grasping only a fragment of an unknowable totality — not the truth itself. Within this framework, there are no true opposites, only phases of a single phenomenon.

This idea resonates deeply with the permaculture worldview, where the interconnectedness of all living and non-living systems is a foundational principle. Pattern understanding becomes a tool for perceiving this unity.

## Pattern as Revelation of Unity

Contemplating the forms of life and flow enables a recognition of the unity of all things and the enfolded nature of nature. In genealogy, we can become conscious of ourselves within the time and pattern stream — we define and are defined by both our ancestry and our descent. The physical connection to lineage is undisputed, but pattern reveals a deeper truth: "all are present here." Each person is encapsulated within, and a pervading part of, a personal genealogical pattern that is itself the product of innumerable variables.

Patterns reveal that all existence is simultaneously streams, particles, and waves — each defining the other. All is one plan. While we may struggle to perceive pattern throughout the entire plan, it persists. In this view, we are the universe attempting to define its own processes. A Kalahari bushman might express this by saying we are "the dreams of a dreamer."

What can never be fully defined is substance itself — it may be nothing more than process. We can only know a few local patterns, granting us limited predictive capacity. The patterns our local patterns cannot apprehend are what surprise us: the strike of cosmic lightning from an unguessed source or stress.

## Convergence of Science and Indigenous Belief

Pattern understanding contributes to the evolution of new worldviews based on the essential oneness of all phenomena. James Lovelock's *Gaia* hypothesis (1979) best expresses the convergence of scientific insight and ancient tribal belief — both assert the interdependence of animate and inanimate events. The universe and Earth behave as self-regulating, self-generated constructs, akin to a single organism or a thought process.

The conditions that make life possible are balanced about such fine tolerances that it seems certain many processes exist specifically to preserve this equilibrium in its dynamic stability.

### Birch and Cobb: The Liberation of Life

Biologists Birch and Cobb, in *The Liberation of Life* (1984), deny the validity of separate individual organisms or isolated events. All exist within a field of events, or as expressions of one life force. Organisms exist only as inseparable parts of their event environments, in continual exchange with the animate and inanimate entities that surround them. We are simultaneously acted upon and acting, created and creating, shaped and shaping.

Warwick Fox, reviewing this work in *The Ecologist*, asserts: "We must view the cosmos as an infinite complex of interrelated events"; all things "are in actuality enduring societies of events."

### Theoretical Physics and Pattern

Theoretical physicists such as Fritjof Capra (*The Tao of Physics*, 1976) contribute to these unified worldviews. All converge on principles that conflict with the ethics governing current political, educational, and economic systems, yet all contribute to an increasing effort to unify under a common ethic of **earth-care** — without which, Mollison argues, we have no meaning in the universe.

## Implications for Design

This philosophical foundation has direct practical consequences for design:

1. **Holistic thinking**: Design decisions cannot be made in isolation. Every change ripples through the enfolded system.
2. **Humility in prediction**: Our local pattern knowledge is limited. Designs must be resilient to unforeseen disturbances.
3. **Earth-care ethic**: The recognition of oneness demands an ethic of care for the whole system, not exploitation of parts.
4. **Pattern literacy**: Understanding patterns becomes more valuable than accumulating facts, because patterns connect phenomena across scales and disciplines.
