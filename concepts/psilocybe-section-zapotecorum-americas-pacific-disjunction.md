## Comparison with Other Disjunct Fungal Distributions

The Section Zapotecorum pattern is not unique in mycology. Other fungal groups show similar trans-Pacific disjunctions, supporting the idea that long-distance dispersal, while rare, is a significant factor in fungal biogeography. The fungal genera Galerina, Mycena, Cystoagaricus, and Paxillus all show South Pacific-American relationships as noted by Horak (1983).

These parallel patterns across multiple genera suggest that trans-Pacific dispersal may have been more common in fungal evolutionary history than previously appreciated, possibly facilitated by episodic events such as major storms, volcanic ash clouds, or changes in atmospheric circulation patterns during glacial-interglacial cycles. The fact that this pattern recurs across distantly related fungal lineages argues against coincidence and points to shared dispersal mechanisms operating over geological timescales.

The disjunct distribution also has implications for understanding fungal speciation rates. If long-distance dispersal is sufficiently common to establish populations on opposite sides of the Pacific, then allopatric speciation may proceed relatively rapidly once populations are isolated, given the morphological and genetic differentiation observed between American and Pacific taxa.
