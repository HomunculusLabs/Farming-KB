## Psilocybe Section Zapotecorum: Taxonomy and Distribution

Section Zapotecorum is one of the most important taxonomic sections within
the genus Psilocybe, named after the type species [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]].
This section contains species of significant ethnopharmacological importance,
including several used ceremonially by indigenous Mesoamerican peoples. The
section"s biogeographical distribution provides key evidence for understanding
the evolutionary history and dispersal patterns of neurotropic fungi.

## Taxonomic Definition and Characteristics

Section Zapotecorum is characterized by its combination of morphological
features. Members typically produce basidiocarps with a viscid or subviscid
pileus (cap), hygrophanous nature, and distinct bluing reactions when bruised
due to psilocin oxidation. Spores are dark violet-brown to dark purple-brown
in deposit, with distinctive cheilocystidia and pleurocystidia aiding species
identification under microscopy.

The section was established by Rolf Singer in his monographic work on Psilocybe.
His sectional classification remains influential, organizing the genus's ~175+
known species into groups sharing morphological and ecological characteristics.
Section Zapotecorum groups predominantly subtropical and tropical species with
strong representation in Mesoamerica and South America.

The type species, Psilocybe zapotecorum, was described from Oaxaca, Mexico,
in the territory of the Zapotec people. It was identified during R. Gordon
Wasson and Roger Heim's pioneering ethnomycological investigations of the
1950s. The epithet honors the Zapotec, whose [[sacred-mushroom-traditions-in-mexico]] the
Sierra Mazateca represent one of the world's richest indigenous mycological
knowledge systems.

## Species Composition

Section Zapotecorum includes several well-known neurotropic species:

- **Psilocybe zapotecorum** — the type species from Oaxaca, Mexico, growing
  in subtropical forests and disturbed ground. Reported from Mexico, Colombia,
  Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and Brazil. Produces medium to large basidiocarps
  with a distinctive conic to campanulate cap.

- **Psilocybe aucklandii** — reported from New Zealand, representing the
  section's remarkable trans-Pacific distribution. Its morphological
  similarity to P. zapotecorum suggests close evolutionary relationship
  despite vast geographic separation across the Pacific Ocean.

- **[[psilocybe-subyungensis-south-american-mesophytic-distribution]]** — distributed in South America, reaching its
  northern limit in Mexican mesophytic forests through association with
  Alnus trees along stream courses and cloud forest margins.

- **Psilocybe angustipleurocystata** — described from Mexico, named for
  its distinctive narrow pleurocystidia, a microscopic feature distinguishing
  it from closely related species within the section.

- **Psilocybe pintonii** — endemic to Colombia, which is the second-most
  species-rich country for neurotropic Psilocybe after Mexico, with 22
  taxa recorded in the Guzmán, Allen & Gartz worldwide survey.

- **Psilocybe weldeniorum** — Andean cloud forests, demonstrating the section's
  adaptation to high-elevation tropical environments.

The total species count varies across taxonomic treatments, with some authors
recognizing 10-15 species. Molecular phylogenetic studies would help resolve
whether all currently included species share a common ancestor.

## Geographic Distribution Patterns

The distribution follows a distinct biogeographical pattern. The center of
diversity is in Mesoamerica and the northern Andes, with Mexico and Colombia
hosting the most species. From this center, species range northward into
subtropical North America and southward through the Andean corridor to
Bolivia and southern Brazil.

The most striking feature is P. aucklandii in New Zealand, thousands of
kilometers from the nearest Section Zapotecorum population. This disjunct
occurrence supports ancient trans-Pacific connections between the mycobiotas
of the Americas and Australasia. Horak (1983) documented similar agaric
disjunctions, suggesting Gondwanan vicariance or ancient long-distance
dispersal predating [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]] landmass breakup.

The Andean corridor serves as the primary southward dispersal route. Cloud
forests along the eastern Andes provide continuous suitable habitat from
Colombia through Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia, allowing stepwise dispersal
over geological time scales, supported by progressive southward decrease
in species diversity.

## Ecological Preferences

Section Zapotecorum species predominantly inhabit subtropical and tropical
forest environments. In Mexico, they are strongly associated with mesophytic
(cloud) forests — the same habitat supporting the highest overall neurotropic
Psilocybe diversity. Of 42 neurotropic Psilocybe species in Mexico documented
by Guzmán (1983), 34 were found in mesophytic forests.

In South America, members inhabit montane cloud forests along the Andean chain
at 1,000–3,000 meters elevation, where consistent humidity and moderate
temperatures prevail year-round. Their fragmented distribution along mountain
ridges creates isolation mechanisms promoting speciation and endemism.

The section shows notable ecological amplitude. While many species are forest-
dwelling, P. zapotecorum colonizes disturbed habitats including trails,
pastures, and roadside banks. This adaptability may have facilitated dispersal
along the Andean corridor where human activity created openings in continuous
forest cover.

The Alnus (alder) association for P. subyungensis is significant. Alder enriches
soil through nitrogen fixation, and its presence along streams creates favorable
fungal microhabitats. This association in mesophytic forests may represent a
specific niche facilitating northward dispersal of southern species into Mexico.

## Ethnopharmacological Significance

Section Zapotecorum includes species among the first neurotropic fungi to
attract scientific attention. P. zapotecorum was collected during the
Wasson-Heim expeditions, though P. mexicana (Section Mexicanae) received more
attention as the likely Aztec "teonanacatl." The Zapotec people maintain a
rich mushroom-use tradition extending across multiple Psilocybe sections.

South American ceremonial use is less documented, but evidence suggests
indigenous peoples in Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia possess knowledge
of these fungi. P. zapotecorum's distribution coincides with regions where
other psychoactive plants (Ayahuasca, San Pedro cactus, Coca) have long
ceremonial histories, suggesting broader psychoactive plant knowledge in
indigenous Andean cultures.

## Comparison with Other Psilocybe Sections

Section Zapotecorum contrasts with other major Psilocybe sections. Section
Mexicanae (P. mexicana, P. samuiensis, P. makarorae) shows similar trans-Pacific
patterns but favors grasslands. Section Cyanescens (P. cyanescens, P. azurescens)
is predominantly temperate, restricted to coastal dune or woodchip habitats.
Section Semilanceata is circumboreal in temperate grasslands, with P.
semilanceata notably absent from Mexico despite circumglobal distribution.
