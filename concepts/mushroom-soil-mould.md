## Mushroom Soil and Mould

The [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]].

### Chalk and Lime

Chalky and limey soils were equally unsuitable. Cuthill
stated that care must be taken "that no chalk or lime is
mixed with the mould." The alkaline nature of these materials
alters the [[falconer-mushroom-bed-boxing-and-protection]], on
account of their boring and loosening the soil, and perhaps
eating the spawn. Care had to be taken that not one worm was
in the bed at moulding time, requiring careful inspection
before application.

### Unsifted Mould

Cuthill recommended unsifted mould. The coarser texture
provided better structure for the bed surface and required
less preparation. The natural particle distribution helped
maintain proper drainage and aeration.

## Mould Thickness

Thickness depended on soil type:

- Loamy soil: three inches initially, reducing to about two
  inches when trodden and beaten with the spade back
- Light sandy mould: not less than four inches

About two inches when finished was quite enough with the best
rich loam. Too deep moulding did not produce so fine a crop
as shallow moulding. Less of a superior soil was preferable
to more of an inferior one.

## Application Technique

Mould was applied immediately when the bed was spawned. It
was placed unsifted at four inches thick, trodden hard with
feet, and watered all over. The spade back was then used to
make the surface harder and plaster it smooth. For light sandy
mould, scattering a little loam on the surface before
plastering made mushrooms much more solid with finer flavour.

## Road Sand

Cuthill described road sand, swept off by a broom, as a
great improvement. The droppings were finely mixed with sand,
and their strength was preserved from heavy rains. This was
particularly useful for cottagers collecting highway
droppings, as the natural sand mixture improved both manure
quality and [[mushroom-watering]] and trodden
to a firm, solid consistency regardless of initial moisture.

## Mould and Crop Quality

Mould quality directly affected mushroom quality. Rich loam
produced more solid, better-flavoured mushrooms. Light sandy
mould benefited from the loam scatter technique to improve
crop density and taste. Properly prepared rich mould supported
production for three to four months or more on each bed.
