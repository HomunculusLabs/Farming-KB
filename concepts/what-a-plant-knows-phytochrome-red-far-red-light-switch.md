# Phytochrome: The Red-Far-Red Light Switch in Plants

## Overview

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein that enables plants to detect and respond to red and far-red light, functioning as a molecular switch that regulates numerous developmental processes including [[cannabis-seed-germination]], [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]], and [[what-a-plant-knows-photoperiodism-flowering-time-measurement]]. Discovered through the photoperiodism research of the mid-20th century, phytochrome represents one of the most elegant biological sensing systems in the plant [[chamovitz-plant-senses-overview]] kingdom.

## Historical Discovery

The discovery of phytochrome emerged from research into photoperiodism—the phenomenon by which plants measure day length to time their flowering. In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the US Department of Agriculture laboratory (the same facility where the [[garner-allard-maryland-mammoth-tobacco-experiment]] was studied) made a remarkable observation: plants responded to flashes of red light during the night, but only red light influenced flowering. Blue or green flashes had no effect.

The critical breakthrough came when they discovered that far-red light [[phytochrome-signaling-plant-light-perception]]—wavelengths slightly longer than bright red, most visible at dusk—could completely cancel the effect of red light. If irises (which normally don't flower in long nights) received a pulse of red light in the middle of the night, they produced flowers. But if far-red light was applied immediately after the red pulse, it was as if the red light had never been seen. This red/far-red reversibility could be repeated: red turned flowering on, far-red turned it off, and so on.

By the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single photoreceptor protein was responsible for both effects. They named it "phytochrome," meaning "plant [[chamovitz-plant-senses-overview]] colour."

## Molecular Mechanism

### Two Interconvertible Forms

Phytochrome exists in two stable forms that interconvert upon absorbing light:

- **Pr (P660)**: The red-light-absorbing form. This is the inactive or "resting" state of the pigment. It absorbs light maximally at approximately 660 nanometres (red light).

- **Pfr (P730)**: The far-red-light-absorbing form. This is the biologically active form. It absorbs light maximally at approximately 730 nanometres (far-red light).

When Pr absorbs red light, it converts to Pfr. When Pfr absorbs far-red light, it converts back to Pr. This photoconversion is extremely rapid—occurring within seconds—and the switch can be flipped back and forth many times.

### The Light-Activated Switch Model

In its simplest formulation, phytochrome acts as a light-activated toggle:

- **Red light activates** phytochrome (Pr → Pfr), switching it into the biologically active form primed to receive far-red light.
- **Far-red light deactivates** phytochrome (Pfr → Pr), switching it into the inactive form primed to receive red light.

This system functions as a biological flip-flop circuit. Only a few seconds of either colour are sufficient to flip the switch. If the switch is toggled rapidly between red and far-red, the net effect is zero—the plant perceives no net change.

### Ecological Logic

The ecological rationale for this system is elegant:

- In nature, the last light any plant sees at the end of the day is far-red (the setting sun is redder). This far-red signal tells the plant to "turn off" its daytime processes.
- In the morning, the plant receives red light and "wakes up."
- The plant effectively measures how long ago it last saw red light and adjusts its growth accordingly.

This mechanism allows plants to measure the length of continuous darkness—not the length of the day—by tracking the Pfr-to-Pr dark reversion over time. As Pfr slowly converts back to Pr in the dark, the plant can effectively "count" hours of darkness.

## Where Phytochrome Operates in the Plant

### Location of Sensing

Unlike phototropism (where Darwin showed the "eye" is in the shoot tip), phytochrome-mediated photoperiodism is sensed primarily in the leaves, not the shoot apex. If a single leaf is illuminated with red light in the middle of the night, the entire plant responds and flowering is regulated. However, if all leaves are removed, leaving only the stem and apex, the plant becomes blind to light flashes even if the entire plant is illuminated.

### Mobile Signalling

When phytochrome in a leaf absorbs red light, it initiates the production of a mobile signal (now known to include the protein FT, or FLOWERING LOCUS T) that propagates throughout the plant. This signal travels from the leaf to the shoot apex where it induces the transition from [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] to flowering.

This discovery—that a leaf can perceive a light signal and communicate it to the rest of the plant—was one of the first demonstrations of long-distance signalling in plants and has profound implications for understanding plant physiology.

## Physiological Roles of Phytochrome

### Seed Germination

Many seeds, particularly small-seeded species like lettuce and certain weeds, require light for germination. These "light-requiring" seeds sense the presence of red light (indicating they are near the soil surface and not buried too deeply) through phytochrome. Far-red light, which would be filtered through a canopy of green leaves above, inhibits germination—a clever mechanism preventing seeds from germinating in deep shade where they cannot photosynthesise effectively.

### Shade Avoidance Syndrome

When a plant is shaded by neighbouring vegetation, the quality of light changes. Green leaves absorb most red light but transmit or reflect far-red light. Under a canopy, the ratio of red to far-red light decreases dramatically. Phytochrome detects this shift: the low R:FR ratio causes more Pfr to convert to Pr, triggering the shade avoidance response—elongated stems, larger leaves, and accelerated flowering—allowing the plant to grow out of the shade.

### Flowering Time Regulation

The original discovery context: phytochrome measures the length of the unbroken dark period and thus determines whether conditions are appropriate for flowering. Short-day plants (chrysanthemums, soybeans) flower when the dark period exceeds a critical length. Long-day plants (irises, barley) flower when the dark period is shorter than a critical length.

### De-etiolation

When a seedling emerges from soil into light, phytochrome triggers the transition from etiolated (elongated, pale, non-photosynthetic) growth to normal green growth. This includes inhibition of stem elongation, opening of the apical hook, expansion of cotyledons, and synthesis of chlorophyll.

## The Phytochrome Gene Family

In [[arabidopsis-thaliana]] and most higher plants, phytochrome is not a single protein but a small gene family:

- **phyA**: Primarily mediates responses to continuous far-red light; dominates in de-etiolation; is light-labile (degraded in light).
- **phyB**: The major photoreceptor for responses to red light; regulates shade avoidance and flowering; is relatively light-stable.
- **phyC through phyE**: Additional family members with overlapping and specialised functions in various species.

## Agricultural Applications

The phytochrome system has significant practical applications in horticulture and agriculture:

- **Chrysanthemum production**: Growers keep plants from flowering by providing a few minutes of light (red-sufficient) during the night throughout autumn and winter, then stop the night lighting two weeks before the desired harvest date to trigger simultaneous flowering.
- **Greenhouse lighting**: LED grow lights are designed with specific red and far-red ratios to manipulate plant architecture, flowering time, and yield.
- **Seed treatment**: Seeds can be pre-treated with red light to break dormancy before planting, improving germination rates.
