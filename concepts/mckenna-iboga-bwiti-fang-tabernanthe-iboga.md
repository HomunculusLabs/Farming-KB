Unlike psilocybin-containing mushrooms or DMT-rich grasses, iboga is a
forest plant with a relatively recent documented history of human use.
There is absolutely no evidence of its use before the early nineteenth
century. The Portuguese, who had a long history of trade and exploration
in West Africa, never mentioned it. This absence of evidence is
difficult to explain if one believes the plant"s use is very old.

## The Bwiti Religion

The Bwiti religion among the Fang of Gabon and Zaire represents the only
truly African hallucinogenic plant cult that survives into the modern
era. Bwiti is a force not only for group cohesion but also for holding
marriages together. Historically, divorce is a chronic source of group
anxiety among the Fang, easily obtained but requiring complicated and
potentially expensive negotiations concerning the return of dowry.

According to Fang oral tradition, they acquired the Bwiti folkway during
a centuries-long migration, during which they lived in proximity to
Pygmy people who taught them the spiritual power resident in Bwiti. The
Fang believe that many grams of root bark must be eaten in order to
"open one"s head." Lesser amounts are then effective for the remainder
of a person"s life.

## Ibogaine as Aphrodisiac and Pair-Bonding Agent

Ibogaine is widely recognized as a factor holding married couples
together in the face of Fang institutions like easy divorce. It is
perhaps one of the few plants of the many dozens claimed to be
aphrodisiacs that actually performs as advertised. Most other candidates
are merely stimulants that cause generalized arousal and sustained
erection.

Ibogaine seems actually to change, deepen, and enhance the psychological
mechanisms behind sexual drive. One experiences a simultaneous sense of
detachment and involvement that is empowering. Yet in situations where
sexual activity is neither sanctioned nor appropriate, ibogaine does not
cause or even raise the possibility of sexual behavior. In these
situations it functions much as ayahuasca functions among its
traditional users: as a boundary-dissolving visionary hallucinogen.

## McKenna's Assessment

Despite his enthusiasm for ibogaine's social and pharmacological
properties, McKenna did not consider it the catalyst of consciousness in
evolving humans. It has no demonstrated long history of use, is not a
grassland plant, and does not produce the same language-activating
effects as psilocybin. He saw it instead as a fascinating example of how
a hallucinogenic plant can become integrated into the social fabric of a
people, providing both visionary ecstasy and practical social cohesion.
