# Solomon's Gardening Aikido: Pest Philosophy and Ecological Management
**Source:** Gardening When It Counts - Steve Solomon (2005)

## Overview

Steve Solomon introduces the concept of "gardening aikido" as a philosophical framework for approaching pest and [[plant-disease-management-solomon]] in vegetable gardens. Drawing an explicit analogy with the martial art's principle of using an opponent's energy against them rather than meeting force with force, Solomon argues that the gardener's mindset about [[solomon-insects-and-diseases-integrated-approach]] matters far more than any specific control technique. This philosophy stands in direct opposition to what he calls the "American Sanitary System" (ASS) — the widespread cultural expectation that food must be visually perfect and entirely free of insect damage.

## The Core Philosophy

### Redefining Acceptable Damage

Solomon's first and most provocative principle is that gardeners should dramatically raise their tolerance for insect damage and cosmetic imperfections:

- A vigorously growing plant can lose 5% or more of its leaf area to insects without any measurable reduction in yield
- Aphid populations covering less than 5% of total leaf area are not worth treating
- Occasional insect holes in leaves, scars on fruit skins, and minor blemishes do not affect [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] or taste
- The presence of some insects in the garden is actually a sign of ecological health, not a problem to be solved

This attitude shift is fundamental because it changes the gardener's response from "kill everything" to "assess whether intervention is actually needed." Most gardeners, Solomon argues, waste enormous time, money, and ecological damage fighting insects that would have negligible impact on their harvest.

### Plant Twice What You Need

Solomon advocates planting approximately double the number of plants you expect to need at harvest. This "overplanting insurance" strategy has multiple benefits:

- Pest and disease losses are expected and budgeted for, reducing emotional stress
- The strongest plants naturally outcompete weaker ones, allowing a form of natural selection within the garden
- Thinning becomes a productive activity rather than a loss — thinned plants can often be eaten as baby greens
- When losses are lighter than expected, surplus can be shared, preserved, or composted
- If one sowing fails entirely (weather, pests, disease), a second sowing two weeks later provides insurance

The key insight is that seeds cost pennies while the emotional and financial cost of watching a carefully tended but inadequate planting get destroyed is far higher. Overplanting shifts the gardener from a posture of anxiety to one of abundance.

### Pests as Teachers

The aikido analogy extends to viewing pests not as enemies to be eradicated but as indicators of underlying problems:

- Aphid infestations often indicate plants stressed by inadequate nutrition, poor soil conditions, or planting too early in cold weather
- Disease susceptibility frequently reflects species growing in unsuitable soil types (celery and cauliflower in heavy clay, for example)
- Insect pressure often increases on plants already weakened by other stresses

Solomon's key question: "Is the struggling plant simply not growing fast enough to overcome the problem?" If the answer is yes, the solution is usually better growing conditions (warmer soil, better nutrition, later planting) rather than pesticide application. A foliar spray of fish emulsion and liquid kelp may do more good than any insecticide because it directly addresses the underlying vigor deficit.

## Specific Pest Management Approaches

### The Hierarchy of Response

Solomon proposes a clear decision hierarchy when encountering pest or disease problems:

1. **Do nothing and observe** — Is the plant still growing vigorously? Is damage below the 5% threshold?
2. **Improve growing conditions** — Foliar feed with fish emulsion and kelp; check soil moisture and temperature; consider whether the species is suited to the site
3. **Sow again as insurance** — Plant a second succession while the first planting either recovers or fails
4. **Use physical barriers** — Spun-fabric row covers (Reemay, Agribon) exclude flying insects without any sprays
5. **Hand removal** — For larger pests (caterpillars, beetles), handpicking is effective in small gardens
6. **Use the least-toxic option** — Insecticidal soap, Bt ([[cervantes-bacillus-thuringiensis-bt-control]]), neem spray
7. **Accept the loss** — Some years are just difficult years; some species are not suited to your soil or climate

### Spun-Fabric Row Covers

Solomon particularly endorses spun-bonded polyester row covers as the most effective and least harmful pest management tool available to home gardeners:

- Available in brands including Reemay, Agribon, and similar products
- Typically 5-6 feet (1.5-1.8 m) wide, sold in various lengths
- Provide 2-4°F (1-2°C) of [[holzer-frost-protection-techniques]], accelerating spring growth
- Completely exclude flying insect pests when all edges are sealed with soil or staples
- Allow light and water through (though some light reduction occurs — Reemay reduces light by about 25%)
- Almost weightless — plants lift the fabric as they grow, no support structure needed
- Can remain in place until plants begin flowering (at which point pollinators need access)

The primary caveat is that they must be carefully sealed at all edges. Any gap allows pest entry. In summer, they can trap heat and cause problems — some brands are better at heat dissipation than others.

### Bacillus Thuringiensis (Bt)

For brassica crops (cabbage, broccoli, kale, cauliflower), Bt is Solomon's recommended specific tool:

- Marketed as Dipel and other brand names
- Lethal only to caterpillars of the Lepidoptera family (cabbageworms, loopers, cabbage moth larvae)
- Safe for humans, bees, most [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]], and can be sprayed the day of harvest
- Remains active on leaves for about one week before degrading
- Add a small amount of dishwashing liquid (1/4 teaspoon per quart) as a sticker/spreader
- Spray undersides of leaves as thoroughly as tops, since most eggs are laid on undersides

### Neem Spray

Neem, derived from the neem tree (Azadirachta indica) seed, is Solomon's recommended long-lasting natural remedy:

- Acts as both insecticide and fungicide
- Disrupts insect hormone systems, preventing molting and reproduction
- Has systemic activity — absorbed into plant tissue for lasting protection
- Effective against a wide range of pests including aphids, whiteflies, and certain beetles
- Naturally degrades in sunlight, leaving minimal environmental residue
- Relatively expensive but concentrated — a little goes a long way

## The American Sanitary System (ASS)

Solomon's most pointed cultural critique is directed at what he calls the "American Sanitary System" — the expectation that all food must be cosmetically perfect:

- Supermarket standards have conditioned consumers to reject any produce with visible insect damage
- This expectation drives massive pesticide use in commercial agriculture
- Home gardeners import these unrealistic standards into their own gardens
- Finding a single insect in frozen broccoli at the grocery store causes horror, yet the pesticides used to prevent this are far more concerning
- The result is that gardeners fight nature itself, spending enormous effort on an unattainable standard of visual perfection

Solomon's antidote: "Change your attitude about what constitutes acceptable table fare." Remove insects when washing food. If one escapes the cook's notice, slide it to the side of the plate. The nutritional quality of a slightly blemished vegetable is identical to a perfect one.

## Planting Timing as Pest Management

Solomon repeatedly emphasizes that planting too early is the single biggest cause of pest and disease problems:

- Early-planted crops grow slowly in cold soil, remaining small and vulnerable for extended periods
- A sowing on the earliest possible date and a second sowing just two weeks later will show dramatically different pest pressure
- The later sowing typically catches up to or surpasses the earlier one because it grows faster from the start
- When conditions are unfavorable (cold, wet), it is better to wait than to fight the consequences of premature planting
