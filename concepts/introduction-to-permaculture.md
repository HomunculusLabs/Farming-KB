# Introduction to Permaculture

Permaculture, a term coined by [[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]] in the mid-1970s, represents a comprehensive design system for creating sustainable human settlements. Drawing from the 1981 [[permaculture-design]] Course given by Mollison at The Rural Education Center in Wilton, New Hampshire, this page covers the foundational philosophy, historical context, and the urgent ecological motivations that gave rise to the permaculture movement.

## Origins and History

The concept of permaculture emerged in 1975–1976 when Bill Mollison, then an environmental researcher at the University of Tasmania, was invited to speak on a national radio program in Melbourne, Australia. The response was extraordinary — the station's board lit up for the rest of the day with callers wanting to know more. Mollison had been developing ideas in manuscript form, and the public demand spurred him and his student David Holmgren to compile *Permaculture One*, published in 1978. Twenty-five thousand copies were printed and sold out within three years.

From this initial publication, a global movement grew organically. Regional permaculture groups formed — thirty-six arose in Australia alone within the first four years. These groups engaged in practical activities: swapping plants, mapping useful tree species in the bush, operating seed exchanges, and establishing bioregional associations. A quarterly journal (later the *International Permaculture Journal*) eventually reached approximately 3,000 direct subscribers. The movement spread to America when Mollison traveled there in 1980, lecturing from place to place, eventually delivering a pivotal talk at the Futures Conference in Toronto that brought permaculture to a much wider audience.

The educational infrastructure expanded through the 15 Pamphlet Permaculture Design Course Series, published by Yankee Permaculture (Dan Hemenway) and placed in the public domain to encourage free reproduction and dissemination. This open approach to knowledge-sharing became a defining characteristic of the movement.

## The Terrible Time of Day

Mollison opened the 1981 Design Course with a stark assessment of global ecological collapse, framing permaculture not as a lifestyle choice but as an emergency response. His analysis identified four fundamental systems in crisis: **soils, forests, the atmosphere, and nutrient cycles**.

### Forest Collapse

Mollison identified forests as far more important in the oxygen cycle than previously understood. The oceans, long assumed to be the primary oxygen producers, contribute less than 8% of atmospheric oxygen recycling, and many ocean areas are becoming oxygen-consuming. It is primarily forests that preserve atmospheric stability.

Two forest systems are critically important: equatorial multispecies rainforests and cool evergreen forests (the Russian taiga and southern evergreen forests). These systems not only produce oxygen but generate precipitation. When forests are cleared from ridges, measurable rainfall may drop 86%, even on clear nights when no rain is recorded by any gauge. Maritime climates are particularly dependent on forest-generated precipitation, but the effect applies across all climate types.

The logging rate exceeded planting by one million hectares annually, even at the time of the lecture. Of the forests that ever existed, only 2% remained in Europe, 8% in South America, and approximately 15% in other regions. The remaining primeval forests — trees 200 feet to the first branch, described as "gigantic cathedrals" — were being chipped for newsprint and packaging material.

Mollison identified what he called the "phasmid conspiracy" — the tendency to blame insect pests (phasmids, Dutch elm disease, chestnut blight) for forest death when, in his view, the trees were already dying from "multiple insult": changes in light penetration, pollutant loading, and acid rain. Insects were merely decomposers feeding on already-doomed organisms, attracted by the chemical signals of stressed and dying tissue. The trees with the greatest leaf area per unit — chestnuts, elms, beeches, oaks, eucalypts — were the first to succumb.

### Soil Erosion

The soil crisis was equally dire. By the time of the 1981 course, approximately 50% of all soils that existed before 1950 had been lost, with another 30% of the remainder degraded. Soils are created at a rate of approximately four tons per acre per year in humid climates. In Australia, cultivated land was losing 27 tons per acre per year. In America, corn-growing areas could lose up to 400–500 tons per acre annually. For every person who eats grain, Mollison calculated a soil cost of approximately 12 tons per year.

Deforestation was identified as the primary driver of soil loss, operating through a mechanism Mollison explained in detail: forests maintain a net downward transfer of water, carrying salts from rock breakdown into deep subterranean leads. When forests are removed, these saline water tables rise. At three feet below the surface, trees begin dying of "phasmids." At eighteen inches, crops fail. At the surface, the soil visibly turns to salt. This process was already occurring in humid Australian climates, not just arid ones.

Wind erosion compounded the problem. Soils from the American interior were blowing out to Los Angeles as "red rain," and Central Australian topsoil was falling on cities at measurable rates of 12 tons per acre per day.

### Water Crisis

Water tables globally were falling rapidly, affecting ancient aquifer systems some 40,000 years in evolution. Urban sealing (highways, buildings) prevented groundwater recharge. Cities were drawing water at 700 parts per million dissolved salts — approaching the limit of human kidney tolerance. At 1,100 ppm, fainting and tissue water accumulation occur. Industrial pollution, atomic waste seepage, and chemical contamination (DDT, PCBs, dioxins, chlorine) were rendering groundwater permanently unpotable in some areas.

### Climate Instability

Mollison observed that climate was not trending simply toward warming or cooling but had begun fluctuating so wildly as to become unpredictable. In his home region of Tasmania, buffered by ocean and forest, the records showed the windiest, driest, and wettest months in 200 years of recording, occurring in succession. He predicted that climate barriers would crack suddenly and at extremes, preceded by periods of immense variability — a description that has proven remarkably prescient.

## The Philosophy of Permaculture

Mollison articulated several core philosophical principles that distinguish permaculture from conventional agriculture and environmentalism:

**Working with nature, not against it.** Rather than imposing human designs onto landscapes, permaculture seeks to understand natural patterns and work within them. This means protracted and thoughtful observation before action, rather than protracted and thoughtless intervention.

**Looking at systems in all their functions.** Every element in a permaculture system should serve multiple functions. A tree is not merely a source of fruit — it also modifies microclimate, builds soil, provides habitat, intercepts rainfall, and produces mulch. Conversely, every function should be supported by multiple elements (redundancy for resilience).

**Allowing systems to demonstrate their own evolution.** Permaculture design establishes initial conditions and then steps back, allowing natural processes to develop complexity over time. This contrasts with the constant intervention required by conventional agriculture.

**From passive knowledge to active design.** Mollison emphasized that permaculture requires a fundamental shift from passive understanding of natural systems to active, purposeful design intervention. Knowledge of ecology is necessary but insufficient — it must be translated into designed action.

## The Call to Action

Mollison framed permaculture as an ethical imperative, not an academic exercise. He argued that sufficient evidence of ecological crisis existed by 1950, and that continued gathering of evidence was a form of avoidance. The temptation, he said, "is always to gather more evidence. Too many people waste their lives gathering evidence. Moreover, as we get more evidence, we see that things are worse than they had appeared to be."

The response he proposed was systematic and cooperative: converting high-energy industrial systems to low-energy biological systems, building local self-reliance through gardening and water storage, breaking the monopoly of elite control over land and knowledge, and disseminating permaculture design skills as widely and rapidly as possible. The movement was explicitly cooperative rather than competitive, with no interest in patenting techniques or restricting information.

## Practical Applications

Introduction permaculture has several important applications in sustainable agriculture and ecological design. Practitioners integrate these methods into holistic management systems that enhance productivity while maintaining ecological balance.

The development and understanding of introduction permaculture has evolved through decades of observation, experimentation, and knowledge transfer across cultures and scientific disciplines.
