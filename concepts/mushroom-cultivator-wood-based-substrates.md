# Wood-Based Substrates in The Mushroom Cultivator
Source: The Mushroom Cultivator (Stamets & Chilton, 1983), Chapter VI -- Non-Composted Substrates
Updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Chapter VI covers non-composted substrates used for cultivating wood-loving (lignicolous) mushroom species. These include sawdust/bran mixtures, natural log culture, outdoor wood chip beds, and pasteurized straw. The chapter provides detailed recipes, preparation methods, and cultivation guidelines for each approach.

## Lignicolous Mushrooms

Mushrooms that grow on wood or wood wastes are termed lignicolous due to their ability to utilize lignin, a microbially resistant substance that constitutes the heartwood of trees. The main components of wood are cellulose and hemicellulose, which are also nutrients available to mushroom mycelium.

High concentrations of resins, turpentine and tannins make conifers (softwoods) less suitable for mushroom growing. Conifers are used on occasion but mixed one to one with hardwood sawdust.
**Best hardwood species**: Oak, elm, chestnut, beech, maple, and alder.

## Sawdust/Bran Substrate (Sterile Method)

### Standard Formula

Japanese growers of [[flammulina-velutipes]], [[auricularia-auricula]], and [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]] use a standard formula:
- **4 parts hardwood sawdust** (80% by volume)
- **1 part bran** (rice or wheat) (20% by volume)
- This is equivalent to a **10:1 mass ratio** of sawdust to bran

### Preparation Guidelines

1. Soak the sawdust for at least 24 hours, then drain
2. Thoroughly mix in the bran
3. Proper moisture test: firm squeeze produces only a few drops of water between fingers
4. Too much moisture causes loose water to collect at bottom, predisposing to contamination
5. Sawdust can be aged up to one year -- said to improve moisture holding capacity
6. Fill containers firmly (wide mouth jars or bags)
7. Sterilize at 15 psi for 60-90 minutes
8. Cool to ambient temperature, then inoculate with grain spawn or sawdust-bran spawn

### Containers and Shaping

- Mason jars, polypropylene jars, or high-density heat-resistant polyethylene bags
- Closed and sealed with microporous filter ("Space Bag" in Japan)
- During incubation, plastic bags can be molded to desired cropping form:
  - Round mini-logs or rectangular blocks
  - Some Pleurotus growers mold into cylinders 6-8 inches long, 4-5 inches diameter
  - Fully colonized logs stacked on sides with ends exposed as cropping surfaces
  - Alternative: slit bag lengthwise in four places, exposing substrate while retaining plastic as humidity hood
- Flammulina and Pleurotus fruit from exposed surface at jar mouth

## Supplementation Principles

The basic raw material is cellulose (sawdust, straw, cardboard, paper wastes, wood chips, plant fibers), comprising approximately **80%** of the total substrate. Materials should be chopped or shredded but never so finely as to eliminate inherent structural qualities.

### Supplement Categories

**Protein sources** (enhance yield):
- Soya meal or soya flour
- Wheat germ
- Brewer's yeast

**Carbohydrate sources** (starchy materials):
- Rice, potatoes, corn, wheat

**Combined supplements** (both protein and carbohydrate):
- Bran, oatmeal, grains of all types

### Supplement Rates

- Supplements comprise approximately **8-25%** of total dry weight
- Gypsum at **5% of dry weight** improves structure and porosity (optional ingredient)

## Natural Log Culture (Shiitake)

### Traditional Method

1. Cut oak logs 3-6 inches diameter, three feet long
2. Cut between fall and spring when sap content is highest
3. Take special care not to injure the bark layer -- critical for fruiting
4. Scrape logs clean of [[lichens]] and fungi
5. Drill four longitudinal rows of 1-inch deep holes, spaced 8 inches apart
6. Plug holes with spawn and cover with wax
7. Incubate 9-15 months before fruiting begins

Freshly cut logs provide a semi-sterile substrate with no special treatment -- very effective for home cultivators.

### Oak Dowel Spawn

- Pre-soaked wooden dowels or 4:1 sawdust/bran mixture used as spawn medium
- Alternative substrates include alder or oak sawdust and bran mixed 4:1 with moisture content of 60%

## Outdoor Wood Chip Bed Cultivation

### Virgin Spawn Preparation

1. Collect substrate pieces already colonized by mushroom mycelium
2. Use these as "virgin spawn" to inoculate similar materials
3. Freshly cut chips of alder, maple and fir support healthy mycelial growth
4. Alder preferred: high sugar content, no resins, abundant in Pacific Northwest
5. Keep inoculated chips indoors in gallon jars with minimal air exchange
6. Recommended ratio: **1:5 virgin spawn to fresh chips**

### Ridge Bed Construction

- Make beds **4-6 inches deep and 2 feet wide**
- Use irregular chips approximately **1-3 inches** long (better than finely ground sawdust)
- Fresh chips provide greater nutrient and water reservoir plus substantial surface area for primordia formation
- Place beds under rhododendrons, leafy ornamentals, fence rows, or in grass
- Never expose to direct sunlight, but must allow rainfall to reach
- Cover with plastic or cardboard during spring/summer colonization
- Water weekly to maintain moisture
- In fall, uncover and water heavily twice weekly (avoid flooding)

### Perennial Bed Management

- Once established, outdoor beds function like perennial plants
- Indoor spawn preparation becomes unnecessary
- Each year, chips drawn from original bed can inoculate new beds
- **Total bed area can be multiplied by five annually**
- Protect in winter with straw, cardboard, new chips topped with plastic
- Extended freezing weather ends outdoor cropping until following year
- For harsh climates: cold frames or plastic greenhouses
- Use local strains adapted to local conditions

## Growing on Pasteurized Straw

### Straw Preparation

- Wheat straw preferred (coarse fiber, good availability)
- Must be clean, mold-free, unspoiled
- Chop or shred into **1-3 inch pieces** using wood chipper, compost shredder, or power mower
- Shredding increases moisture absorption and substrate density
