Food waste is an excellent nitrogen-rich ingredient for [[jadam-liquid-fertilizer]] (JLF). JADAM considers food waste one of the most readily available and cost-effective sources of organic nutrients for farming, turning a waste problem into a farming resource.

## Why Food Waste Works

Food waste contains a wide range of nutrients because it represents the parts of plants and animals that humans discard. This nutritional diversity mirrors the comprehensive nutrient profile that crops need. Rather than buying expensive commercial organic fertilizers, JADAM farmers use food waste as a core JLF ingredient.

Food waste is classified as a nitrogen-rich ingredient (along with fish waste and livestock manure). When making JLF, it is often best to make nitrogen-rich ingredients separately from carbon-rich ones like wild grass, because their decomposition rates differ.

## Making Food Waste JLF

The process follows standard [[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation]] principles:

1. Fill a container with food waste
2. Fill with water
3. Add a handful of [[jadam-leaf-mold-as-ideal-soil]] (leaf mold)
4. Close the lid securely
5. Wait approximately 7 days in summer, longer in cooler seasons

No sugar, molasses, or special microbial inoculants are needed. The indigenous microorganisms in the leaf mold handle decomposition.

## Important Precautions

- **Do not add sea salt** to food waste JLF (along with human feces and urine). This is an exception to the general rule of adding sea salt up to 0.1% salinity to liquid fertilizers
- Food waste JLF may smell strongly -- JADAM explicitly rejects the idea that fertilizer should not smell. The "bad smell of liquid fertilizer equals the sweet smell of fruits"
- If the smell is unbearable, add [[jadam-mineral-water-jmw]] phyllite powder at 0.2% concentration (1 kg per 500 L)
- Filter well before foliar application to prevent clogging spray equipment
- Mix with [[jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] for foliar spraying to ensure good coverage

## The Salt Problem

JADAM addresses the common misconception that food waste is too salty for use as fertilizer. While it is true that some food waste contains salt, when properly diluted (standard dilution is x100), the salinity level becomes negligible. This is far preferable to discarding food waste and losing all its nutritional value.

## Cost Advantage

Commercial organic liquid fertilizers can cost up to $100 per liter. Food waste JLF costs essentially nothing -- the ingredients are waste products. This aligns with JADAM's core philosophy of ultra-low-cost farming at approximately $100 per acre total input cost.

## Nutritional Perspective

JADAM teaches that "things similar to my crops are good food for my crops." Food waste from vegetable scraps, fruit peels, and plant-based kitchen waste contains the same range of nutrients that crops need. Using food waste closes the nutrient loop between human consumption and agricultural production, mimicking natural cycles where nothing is wasted.

## Integration with Overall System

Food waste JLF works best as part of a complete JADAM input system:
- Use [[jadam-cover-crop-method]] for base fertilizer
- Use food waste JLF as supplemental nitrogen-rich liquid fertilizer
- Apply [[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms]] regularly to maintain soil microbial diversity
- Address [[soil-compaction-solutions-ingham]] first to ensure nutrients can reach roots

## Types of Food Waste and Their Properties

Not all food waste is equal in its fertilizer value. JADAM farmers learn to categorize their kitchen waste by nutrient profile:

- **Fruit waste** (peels, cores, rinds): High in potassium and sugars. Breaks down quickly due to high moisture content. Especially good for fruiting crops like tomatoes and peppers.
- **Vegetable scraps** (ends, leaves, stems): Balanced N-P-K with good mineral content. Leafy green scraps provide nitrogen; root vegetable peels provide potassium.
- **Grain waste** (stale bread, rice, pasta): Carbon-rich but still nitrogen-sufficient for JLF. Adds bulk and feeds diverse microbial populations during fermentation.
- **Eggshells:** Rich in calcium carbonate. Best crushed finely before adding to accelerate decomposition. Calcium is essential for cell wall strength and disease resistance.
- **Tea bags and coffee grounds:** Good nitrogen source with trace minerals. Coffee grounds also contain caffeine which may have mild pest-deterrent properties.

Avoid adding meat scraps, bones, or dairy to JLF — while these decompose, they attract pests and create particularly strong odors. Stick to plant-based food waste for the cleanest fermentation.

## Dilution and Application Rates

The standard JLF dilution ratio is 1:100 (one part fermented liquid to 100 parts water), but food waste JLF can be adjusted based on crop needs and growth stage:

- **Seedlings and young transplants:** Dilute 1:200 to avoid burning tender roots. Young plants have lower nutrient tolerance and can be damaged by concentrated solutions.
- **Vegetative growth stage:** Dilute 1:100 as a standard rate. Apply every 7-10 days as a soil drench or foliar spray for vigorous vegetative growth.
- **Fruiting and flowering:** Reduce to 1:50 or maintain at 1:100 but increase frequency. Potassium-rich fruit waste JLF is particularly beneficial during this stage.
- **Stressed or diseased plants:** Dilute 1:200 and apply as a foliar spray. The microbial diversity in JLF can help suppress foliar pathogens and boost plant immune responses.

Always apply in early morning or late afternoon to avoid leaf burn from midday sun. When using as a foliar spray, add [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] at 1:1000 to ensure even coverage and penetration through leaf surfaces.

## Storage and Shelf Life

Properly fermented food waste JLF can be stored for several months in a sealed container. The anaerobic environment prevents further decomposition once fermentation is complete. Over time, some settling occurs — stir well before use to redistribute suspended nutrients and microorganisms.

In cold climates, JLF may freeze during winter. This does not harm the fertilizer but may kill some microbial populations. Allow frozen JLF to thaw completely and add a small amount of fresh [[ultra-low-cost-jadam-microorganism-solution-and-soil-foundation]] to re-inoculate before spring applications. In warm climates, keep containers in shade to prevent excessive gas buildup from continued fermentation.
