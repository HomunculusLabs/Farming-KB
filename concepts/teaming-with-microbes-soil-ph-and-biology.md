# Soil pH and Biology

In the soil food web framework, pH is not merely a chemical measurement — it is a biological phenomenon. The pH in the rhizosphere is created and maintained by soil organisms and determines which types of microorganisms thrive, what forms of nitrogen are available, and ultimately how well plants grow. Lowenfels and Lewis argue that each plant's optimum soil pH has more to do with the needs of its associated fungi and bacteria than with chemistry alone.

## pH basics

On a scale of 1 to 14, pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+). Low pH means acidic (high H+ concentration); high pH means alkaline (low H+ concentration). Every time a plant root tip exchanges a hydrogen cation for a nutrient cation, H+ concentration increases and pH rises (more alkaline). Root surfaces also take up anions using hydroxyl (OH-) as the medium of exchange, which lowers pH (more acidic). These exchanges usually balance out.

Fungi and bacteria are small enough to have cations and anions on their surfaces, electrically holding or releasing mineral nutrients they take in from [[teaming-with-microbes-decomposition-pathways]]. This too has an impact on soil pH.

## Bacteria raise pH; fungi lower it

The single most important relationship between soil biology and pH is this: [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-the-smallest-workers]] raises soil pH while [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]] lower it.

### Bacterial biofilm (alkaline)

Bacterial slime has a pH above 7 (alkaline). When sufficient bacteria are present in an area, the slime they produce keeps the pH in their vicinity above 7. This creates the right conditions for nitrifying bacteria to convert ammonium into nitrate. The biofilm not only influences pH but also buffers the soil so pH remains relatively constant — a self-regulating system right in the rhizosphere.

### Fungal enzymes (acidic)

Fungal enzymes are decidedly acidic. They produce organic acids to decay organic matter for nutrients. As soils become dominated by fungi, the organic acids they produce lower pH below 7, making the environment increasingly unsuitable for most nitrifying bacteria. More ammonium remains as ammonium instead of being converted to nitrate.

## Nitrification depends on pH

The nitrogen cycle in soil involves conversion of proteins into ammonium (NH4+), typically from [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]]. Specialized bacteria then convert ammonium through two steps:

1. Nitrite bacteria (Nitrosomonas spp.) convert ammonium to nitrites (NO2-)
2. Nitrate bacteria (Nitrobacter spp.) convert nitrites to nitrates (NO3-)

Nitrifying bacteria do not like acidic environments. Their numbers — and thus the conversion of nitrogen into nitrates — diminish when soil pH drops below 7. If pH is 5 or lower, very little if any ammonium is converted.

This has profound implications for gardening:

- In [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] (pH above 7): nitrifying bacteria thrive, nitrogen is converted to nitrate, and nitrate-loving plants (vegetables, annuals, grasses — Rule #2) flourish
- In fungally dominated soils (pH below 7): nitrifying bacteria decline, nitrogen remains as ammonium, and ammonium-preferring plants (trees, shrubs, perennials — Rule #3) do best

## The rhizosphere pH effect

Soil food web organisms influence soil pH where it counts most — right in the rhizosphere. A plant that attracts and receives its preferred form of nitrogen will perform optimally. The biology creates the pH conditions that determine which nitrogen form predominates.

Bacterial biofilm not only influences pH in the rhizosphere but also buffers the soil in the area so pH remains relatively constant. This is a self-regulating system: bacteria create alkaline conditions that support nitrifiers, which produce nitrates for nitrate-preferring plants, whose exudates attract more bacteria.

## Nitrogen-fixing bacteria and pH

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria generally require a pH of 7 or above for good populations. In acidic soils (below pH 7), nitrogen fixation is reduced. This affects legumes in particular, since the symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules need near-neutral pH to function effectively.

[[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-the-smallest-workers]] are adapted to live in a wider pH range than other bacteria, from acidic to alkaline, giving them an advantage in variable pH conditions.

## Fungal dominance and ammonium

As fungal dominance increases along the [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] — from garden to prairie to forest — soil pH drops, nitrification slows, and more nitrogen remains in ammonium form. This matches plant preferences perfectly: early succession annuals prefer nitrates while late succession perennials and trees prefer ammonium.

The transition occurs in prairie plants, which prefer a balance of bacteria and fungi. This is analogous to lawn grass, which also prefers a balanced [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]].

## Chemical fertilizers disrupt pH balance

With the natural buffering action of bacteria and fungi lost (from chemical fertilizer application — Rule #13), soil pH is thrown out of whack. pH gets lower and lower as more nitrate salts accumulate without the biological buffering system.

This creates a downward spiral: chemical fertilizers kill the soil food web microbes that maintain pH balance, which causes pH to drop further, which further limits microbial activity, requiring more lime or other amendments to correct — amendments that would not be needed if the soil food web were intact.

## Practical implications for gardeners

The key insight from the soil food web approach is that rather than chemically adjusting pH, gardeners should support the biology that naturally creates the right pH for their plants:

- Knowing soil pH helps determine what to add to support specific soil food webs
- Bacterially dominated compost, [[teaming-with-microbes-mulch-and-soil-food-web]], and [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-brewing-guide]] support alkaline conditions for nitrification
- Fungal-dominated amendments support acidic conditions where ammonium predominates
- Each plant's optimum pH is really about supporting the right microbes for that plant
- The rhizosphere is where pH matters most — testing bulk soil pH is less relevant than rhizosphere conditions
