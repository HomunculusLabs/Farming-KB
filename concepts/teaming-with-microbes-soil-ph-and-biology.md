# Soil pH and Biology

In the soil food web framework, pH is not merely a chemical measurement — it is a biological phenomenon. The pH in the rhizosphere is created and maintained by soil organisms and determines which types of microorganisms thrive, what forms of nitrogen are available, and ultimately how well plants grow. Lowenfels and Lewis argue that each plant's optimum soil pH has more to do with the needs of its associated fungi and bacteria than with chemistry alone.

## pH basics

On a scale of 1 to 14, pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+). Low pH means acidic (high H+ concentration); high pH means alkaline (low H+ concentration). Every time a plant root tip exchanges a hydrogen cation for a nutrient cation, H+ concentration increases and pH rises (more alkaline). Root surfaces also take up anions using hydroxyl (OH-) as the medium of exchange, which lowers pH (more acidic). These exchanges usually balance out.

Fungi and bacteria are small enough to have cations and anions on their surfaces, electrically holding or releasing mineral nutrients they take in from [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-the-smallest-workers]] raises soil pH while [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]]. Specialized bacteria then convert ammonium through two steps:

1. Nitrite bacteria (Nitrosomonas spp.) convert ammonium to nitrites (NO2-)
2. Nitrate bacteria (Nitrobacter spp.) convert nitrites to nitrates (NO3-)

Nitrifying bacteria do not like acidic environments. Their numbers — and thus the conversion of nitrogen into nitrates — diminish when soil pH drops below 7. If pH is 5 or lower, very little if any ammonium is converted.

## Chemical fertilizers disrupt pH balance

With the natural buffering action of bacteria and fungi lost (from chemical fertilizer application — Rule #13), soil pH is thrown out of whack. pH gets lower and lower as more nitrate salts accumulate without the biological buffering system.

This creates a downward spiral: chemical fertilizers kill the soil food web microbes that maintain pH balance, which causes pH to drop further, which further limits microbial activity, requiring more lime or other amendments to correct — amendments that would not be needed if the soil food web were intact.

## Practical implications for gardeners

The key insight from the soil food web approach is that rather than chemically adjusting pH, gardeners should support the biology that naturally creates the right pH for their plants:

- Knowing soil pH helps determine what to add to support specific soil food webs
- Bacterially dominated compost, [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-brewing-guide]] support alkaline conditions for nitrification
- Fungal-dominated amendments support acidic conditions where ammonium predominates
- Each plant's optimum pH is really about supporting the right microbes for that plant
- The rhizosphere is where pH matters most — testing bulk soil pH is less relevant than rhizosphere conditions
