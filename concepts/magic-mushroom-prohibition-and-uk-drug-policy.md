# Magic Mushroom Prohibition and UK Drug Policy

The story [[european-prevalence-and-patterns-of-magic-mushroom-use]] prohibition in Britain illustrates the
tension between scientific evidence, commercial reality, and political
expediency in drug policy. From a period of legal ambiguity that
enabled a flourishing trade, through hasty legislation driven by
electoral concerns, to the eventual criminalisation of fresh mushrooms
in 2005, the British experience offers a case study in how drug policy
is made.

## Early Prohibition in America

[[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] had been classified as [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]]
under US Federal Law in 1970. The Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention
and Control Act made the knowing possession, sale or use of a
[[emcdda-psilocybin-non-addictive-profile-tolerance-dynamics]] a criminal offence punishable by fine or
imprisonment. But the fact that all mushrooms were illegal did little
to halt their spread. Arrests were few and far between, and were more
often for trespassing than for possession of a prohibited substance.

In Australia, Psilocybe mushrooms were made illegal in Queensland in
1971. Similar legislation followed in other countries as the
"ripples and waves" of [[psilocybin-mushroom-consciousness]] spread around the Pacific
Rim and eventually to Europe. In each case, legislation and alarming
newspaper headlines did little to halt the spread of mushroom use.

## The British Legal Loophole

For many years, British law contained a significant loophole. While
psilocybin and psilocin were controlled substances under the Misuse of
Drugs Act, fresh mushrooms containing these compounds were not
explicitly illegal. This was because the law controlled the chemical
substances themselves, not the raw fungal material in which they
occurred naturally. This loophole allowed the emergence of a
significant trade in fresh [[guzman-magic-mushrooms-african-species]] throughout the late 1990s
and early 2000s.

## The Commercial Boom

As the Dutch pioneered commercial [[grass-seed-cultivation-of-psilocybe-mushrooms]] cubensis,
British entrepreneurs were quick to exploit the legal loophole. Smart
shops and head shops across Britain began stocking fresh mushrooms
alongside their other wares. The trade grew rapidly: at its peak,
the Amsterdam Mushroom Company alone was selling an average of 5,000
portions per week, with an estimated five times that amount being sold
nationally. At roughly 20 grams per average street dose, this
represented approximately 25,000 mushroom trips per week.

The government's response was characterised by confusion. Customs and
Excise were apparently generating revenues in the region of one million
pounds from traders, while simultaneously the Home Office sought to
deter trade by warning sellers that they were breaking the law. The
government foolishly appeared to be demanding tax on a product it
considered illegal, the left hand unaware of quite what the right was
doing.

## The Gloucester Case and Legal Chaos

In 2004, a small number of shops were raided by police and charged
with illegally selling hallucinogenic fungi as a Class A drug. The
first case, concerning Dennis Mardle and Colin Evans who ran the
head-shop Collector's Choice in Gloucester, came to court in December
2004 and was promptly thrown out by the Crown Court. The judge,
Claire Miskin, found the law so ambiguous that it would be an abuse of
process for the two men to stand trial. In a carefully aimed rebuke,
she chided the government for expecting the judiciary to sort out a
legal mess of Parliament's making.
