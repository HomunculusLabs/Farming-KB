# Faunal Grazing Effects on Mycorrhizal Function

Soil fauna exert significant influence on [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and their functional relationships with host plants. Grazing on extraradical hyphae, mycorrhizal roots, and fungal fruiting bodies by collembola, nematodes, mites, and other soil animals can either enhance or impair mycorrhizal function, depending on grazer density, fungal species, and environmental context. Understanding these interactions is critical because mycorrhizae are central to plant nutrient acquisition, and faunal-mediated changes to their function cascade through ecosystem processes.

## Direct Grazing on Extraradical Hyphae

### Negative Effects on Mycorrhizal Effectiveness

The most direct mechanism by which fauna affect mycorrhizal function is through consumption of extraradical hyphae — the fungal network extending from roots into soil that mediates nutrient uptake. Warnock et al. (1982) demonstrated that grazing of extraradical arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae by the collembolan [[folsomia-candida]] decreased mycorrhizal colonization effectiveness in leek (Allium porrum). Severing the mycelial connections between host plant roots and soil reduced the mycorrhiza's ability to increase phosphate inflow above that of non-mycorrhizal plants.

Similarly, McGonigle and Fitter (1987) and Finlay (1985) showed significant declines in mycorrhizal colonization of roots in the presence of collembola. These findings are consistent with the intuitive expectation that consumption of the hyphal network should impair the symbiosis.

### Contrasting Positive Effects

Paradoxically, some studies have found that moderate faunal grazing actually increases mycorrhizal colonization. Lussenhop (1996) added moderate densities of the collembola Folsomia candida and Tullbergia granulata to field-grown soybean and observed a 40% increase in arbuscular mycorrhizal root colonization and a 5% increase in leaf nitrogen, with no effect on phosphorus content or root nodule number.

Lussenhop proposed that the density of animals in his study (6.8 × 10³ animals m⁻²) was considerably lower than in previous experiments showing negative effects, suggesting a density-dependent response. Several mechanisms may explain the stimulatory effect at moderate grazing intensities:

1. **Hyphal fragmentation and branching**: Grazing may sever hyphae in ways that stimulate compensatory branching, similar to pruning effects in plants, potentially increasing the total hyphal network surface area
2. **Reduced intraspecific competition**: Moderate grazing may thin hyphal density enough to reduce self-competition within the mycelial network
3. **Nutrient recycling**: Faunal excretion may return nutrients to the rhizosphere, stimulating both fungal and root growth
4. **Carbon reallocation**: Plants may increase carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi in response to partial hyphal loss, strengthening the symbiosis

## Nematode Interference with Mycorrhizal Formation

Beyond grazing effects on established mycorrhizae, nematodes can actively prevent mycorrhizal formation. Sutherland and Fortin (1968) found that the nematode Aphelenchus avenae could prevent the development of mycorrhizal symbiosis between Suillus granulatus and Pinus resinosa in tripartite culture. The intensity of nematode grazing on S. granulatus was sufficient to destroy the fungal inoculum before it could colonize roots.

However, the authors cautioned that such intense grazing pressure is unlikely in natural ecosystems, where the spatial scale and complexity of soil would dilute the interaction. In mature, established mycorrhizal associations, nematode grazing is more likely to reduce efficiency incrementally rather than cause complete symbiosis failure.

## Differential Fungal Susceptibility to Grazing

Not all mycorrhizal fungi are equally susceptible to faunal grazing, and this differential susceptibility has important implications for plant community dynamics. Ruess and Dighton (1996) demonstrated that among seven ectomycorrhizal fungi, Laccaria laccata was the most favorable food for the nematode Aphelenchoides saprophilus, supporting the highest populations and the greatest proportion of females. In contrast, Paxillus involutus barely sustained a nematode population (Table 4.7).

This differential palatability means that grazing pressure can shift the composition of the mycorrhizal fungal community, favoring less-palatable species regardless of their functional effectiveness for host plants. If, for example, a highly effective mycorrhizal fungus is also highly palatable to soil fauna, grazing could select for less effective but grazing-resistant fungal species, with consequences for plant nutrition and competitive ability.

## Interactions Between Grazing and Mycorrhizal Nutrient Transport

### Phosphorus Dynamics

The effect of faunal grazing on mycorrhizal phosphorus transport is complex. Warnock et al. (1982) showed reduced phosphate inflow to plants when hyphal connections were severed, but Lussenhop (1996) found no effect on plant phosphorus content despite increased colonization. The discrepancy may relate to differences in plant species, fungal species, grazer density, or soil phosphorus availability.

### Nitrogen Dynamics

Lussenhop (1996) found a 5% increase in leaf nitrogen with moderate collembolan grazing, suggesting that faunal activity in the mycorrhizosphere may enhance nitrogen availability. This could occur through several mechanisms: stimulation of fungal nitrogen mineralization, faunal excretion of ammonium, or enhancement of bacterial nitrogen cycling in the rhizosphere.

## Broader Ecosystem Implications

### Spore Dispersal Trade-offs

Faunal grazing on mycorrhizal fungi creates a tension between two ecosystem functions. On one hand, grazing reduces hyphal connectivity and may impair nutrient transport to plants. On the other hand, animals that consume mycorrhizal fungi — particularly those that eat hypogeous fruiting bodies — serve as critical dispersal agents for fungal spores. Small mammals such as voles, chipmunks, and flying squirrels are essential vectors for the dispersal of truffle-like ectomycorrhizal fungi that cannot disperse spores autonomously through the air.

### Canopy Suppression of Mycorrhizae

Indirect faunal effects on mycorrhizae can occur through vegetation-mediated pathways. Walker et al. (1999) showed that Rhododendron maximum thickets in the southern Appalachians suppressed ectomycorrhizae on canopy tree seedlings, potentially through allelopathic effects of rhododendron litter on fungal communities. This demonstrates that faunal-grazing effects on mycorrhizae cannot be considered in isolation from the broader plant community context.

### Agricultural Implications

Roncadori (1997) reviewed interactions between arbuscular mycorrhizas and plant-parasitic nematodes in agro-ecosystems, highlighting the complexity of tripartite interactions among plants, mycorrhizal fungi, and nematode pests. Managing soil faunal communities to optimize mycorrhizal function represents an important but underexplored dimension of sustainable agriculture.

## Research Gaps

Several important questions remain regarding faunal effects on mycorrhizal function:

- The threshold grazer densities at which effects shift from stimulatory to inhibitory are poorly defined for most plant-fungus-fauna combinations
- Long-term effects of chronic grazing pressure on mycorrhizal community composition and function are largely unknown
- The interactive effects of multiple grazer taxa (e.g., collembola plus nematodes plus mites) on mycorrhizal function have received little attention
- Climate change effects on the balance between faunal grazing and mycorrhizal function are entirely unexplored

Understanding these dynamics is essential for predicting how [[soil-food-web]] interactions will respond to land use change, invasive species, and global environmental change.
