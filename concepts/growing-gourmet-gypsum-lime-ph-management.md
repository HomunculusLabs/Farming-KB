# Gypsum, Lime, and pH Management in Substrates

Proper management of substrate chemistry, particularly pH and
mineral content, plays a critical role in successful mushroom
cultivation. Gypsum (calcium sulfate) and lime (calcium hydroxide)
are the two most commonly used amendments. Understanding their
distinct functions helps cultivators optimize substrate conditions
for each mushroom species.

## Gypsum (Calcium Sulfate)

Gypsum serves multiple important functions in [[query-what-are-the-best-mushroom-substrates-for-different-species]]:

1. Structural improvement: A condition of 5% gypsum helps keep
   the substrate loose and aerated. Gypsum prevents the clumping
   of substrate particles, maintaining air spaces essential for
   mycelial respiration.

2. Mineral nutrition: Gypsum provides calcium and sulphur, basic
   elements promoting mushroom metabolism. Research by Stoller
   (1962) and Leatham and Stahlman (1989) demonstrated that gypsum
   stimulated [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] of Shiitake in [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]].

3. Moisture management: In grain spawn formulas, gypsum is added
   to help keep the kernels separated after sterilization. Without
   gypsum, grain kernels tend to clump together, creating sites of
   depressed gas exchange where bacteria proliferate.

4. pH buffering: Gypsum has a mild buffering effect, helping to
   stabilize pH during the colonization period.

## Lime (Calcium Hydroxide)

Hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide) is extremely alkaline and water
soluble. Its primary application is in substrate treatment,
particularly for [[growing-gourmet-straw-pasteurization-methods]]. By immersing straw into
water baths high in hydrated lime, competitor fungi and bacteria
are largely rendered inactive from the drastic change in pH.

Two to four pounds of lime is added for every 50 gallons of water,
equivalent to about 0.5-1.0% by weight. The pH of the water
skyrockets to 9.5 or higher. Once dissolved, chopped straw is
immersed into this highly alkaline bath. Under these caustic
conditions, pH-sensitive microorganisms soon die.

After an overnight soaking, the water is drained and discarded.
It is not unusual for the straw to achieve a pH of 8.5 or higher
after soaking. [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]] mycelia can tolerate this alkaline
environment better than most competitors. After three or four days
of initial growth, pH slowly falls as the mycelium races through
the straw, secreting acids and enzymes.

## Calcium Carbonate

Calcium carbonate is used primarily in casing soils and as a pH
buffer in substrate formulations. In the alternative fruiting
formula for wood-free cultivation, 1 lb of calcium carbonate per
100 lbs of base substrate is recommended. The amount can be altered
to effectively raise pH, offsetting any inherent acidity.

Tan and Chang (1989) found that a Shiitake formula consisting of
71% sawdust, 18% used tea leaves, 7% wheat bran, and 1.4% calcium
carbonate gave the highest yields. Notably, when calcium sulfate was
substituted for calcium carbonate, no yield-enhancing effect was
seen, suggesting that the pH-buffering role of calcium carbonate
was more important than its calcium contribution in that context.

Casing layers are usually composed of peat moss, buffered with
calcium carbonate, and applied directly to the surface of a
myceliated substrate. This buffering is essential because peat moss
is naturally acidic, and most mushroom species prefer a near-neutral
pH for fruiting.

## pH Dynamics During Colonization

The pH of mushroom substrates changes during the colonization
process. Most mushroom mycelia prefer a slightly acidic to neutral
starting pH, typically in the 5.5-7.0 range depending on the
species. As mycelium colonizes the substrate, it secretes organic
acids and enzymes that gradually lower the pH. This natural
acidification is part of the mushroom's competitive strategy,
creating conditions that favor its own growth while inhibiting
many competitor organisms.

For supplemented sawdust, the make-up pH typically hovers around
5.5-6.0. The addition of buffers like calcium carbonate helps
prevent the pH from dropping too low during colonization, which
could stress the mushroom mycelium.

## Lime Bath Warnings

The highly alkaline water from lime bath treatments kills many
plants and should be prevented from entering any sensitive
ecosystem or watershed. Cultivators using the hydrated lime method
must carefully manage the disposal of spent leachate. Similarly,
bleach bath treatments produce toxic leachate that requires proper
handling and disposal.

## Application in Grain Spawn

In grain [[growing-gourmet-sawdust-spawn-production-use]], gypsum is routinely added to formulas.
The addition of 1-4 grams of gypsum per jar helps maintain kernel
separation after sterilization and provides essential minerals.
Grain comes to the consumer with an inherent moisture content of
8-15%, so less water is added than might be expected to achieve the
right moisture content for spawn production.

The optimal moisture for grain spawn falls within 45-55%, with an
ideal around 50%. Gypsum does not significantly affect moisture
content but does improve the physical structure of the spawn,
making it easier to shake and distribute during inoculation.
