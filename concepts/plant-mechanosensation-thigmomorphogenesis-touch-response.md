## Overview

Plants experience mechanical stimulation constantly — from wind and rain to herbivore contact and neighboring
vegetation. Far from being passive recipients, plants actively sense and respond to these forces through specialized
mechanosensory systems. For more on plant senses, see [[plant-thigmomorphogenesis-mechanosensing]]).

## Thigmomorphogenesis: Growth Altered by Touch

Thigmomorphogenesis (from the Greek *thigma* = touch, *morphe* = form, *genesis* = origin) is the process by which
plants alter their growth patterns in response to mechanical stimulation. The term was coined by Mordechai Jaffe in
1973. The most common manifestation is compact, stocky growth: plants exposed to regular wind or touch develop
shorter, thicker stems with smaller leaves compared to sheltered individuals of the same species and genotype. (see [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]] and less in reproductive output, at least in the short term.

## Rapid Mechanical Responses

### Venus Flytrap (*Dionaea muscipula*)
The Venus flytrap demonstrates the most dramatic plant mechanical response. Trigger hairs on the trap surface act as
levers that deform underlying mechanosensitive ion channels. When two hairs are stimulated within approximately
20 seconds, an action potential propagates across the trap, causing cells on the outer surface to rapidly expand
while inner cells contract — snapping the trap shut in roughly 100 milliseconds.

The two-hair requirement prevents false triggering by rain or debris. This represents a sophisticated integration of
mechanical information over time, analogous to a basic memory circuit. If a second hair is not triggered within the
time window, the first stimulus is "forgotten" and the count resets.
