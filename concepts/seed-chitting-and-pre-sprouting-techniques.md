# Seed Chitting and Pre-Sprouting Techniques

## Overview

Chitting is the practice of initiating [[cannabis-seed-germination]] under controlled, near-ideal conditions before placing the seed into the soil. The word "chit" means either to presprout seeds or to green-up seed potatoes by encouraging shoots to emerge from the eyes. By bringing seeds through the most vulnerable early stages of germination in a protected environment, chitting dramatically increases germination success rates, eliminates dead-seed waste, and allows the gardener to identify seed quality before committing to a planting. [[steve-solomon]] presents chitting as one of the most valuable techniques for ensuring reliable crop establishment.

## The Germination Process

### Root-First Biology

Understanding chitting requires understanding how seeds actually germinate. The process follows a consistent sequence:

1. The seed absorbs water and swells
2. The seed first puts down a root (radicle) to insure it has a moisture supply
3. Only after the root is established does the seed produce a shoot (hypocotyl) that heads for the light

Seeds usually need to be kept moist only until they begin putting down their first root. The time required depends on the species, the vigor of the particular seed batch, soil temperature, and weather conditions. In dry soil, the seed may wait indefinitely for rain to initiate the sprouting process.

### Why Chitting Works

When you chit, you initiate sprouting under nearly ideal conditions: consistent moisture, optimal temperature, and protection from soil pathogens. The seed progresses through the water-absorption and root-emergence stages without the risks present in field conditions. After the root emerges but before it gets long (because the radicle is delicate and snaps off easily), you gently place the sprouting seed in its drill or hill and cover it.

Once the seed has an established root system — even a small one — it is essentially immune to drying out. The root will seek moisture from the surrounding soil. This is the fundamental advantage of chitting: the seed's most vulnerable period is managed in a controlled environment rather than in the unpredictable field.

## Benefits of Chitting

### Eliminating Dead Seeds

Perhaps the greatest practical benefit of chitting is that you will not sow dead or nearly dead seeds and then wait in the garden for weeks, hoping they will germinate, while a valuable growing window passes. With chitting, you can observe exactly which seeds are viable. If you chit 50 seeds and only 10 sprout weakly, you immediately know that either the seed lot is poor quality or the germination conditions you provided are inadequate. This direct feedback prevents the common gardening mistake of blaming yourself for poor germination when the real problem is low-vigor seed.

### Faster Field Emergence

A chitted seed already has a root established when it goes into the ground. Instead of spending days to weeks absorbing water and initiating root growth in the soil, it immediately begins pushing a shoot upward. Field emergence is typically 3–7 days faster with chitted seeds compared to direct-sown seeds of the same batch.

### Better Spacing and Thinning

Because you know exactly how many viable seeds you have before planting, you can space them precisely. There is no need to overseed and then thin, which wastes seed and disturbs remaining seedlings. Each planting position receives a known-viable seed.

### Reduced Disease Exposure

Seeds in soil are exposed to damping-off fungi, soil-borne bacteria, and various pathogens that attack germinating seeds. Chitting reduces the time the seed spends in the vulnerable pre-emergence stage in soil, significantly lowering disease risk.

## The Chitting Procedure

### Materials Needed

- Seeds to be chitted
- Moist blotting paper, paper towels, or coffee filters
- Petri dishes, shallow plastic containers, or sealed plastic bags
- A warm location (70–80°F / 21–27°C)

### Step-by-Step Method

1. Moisten the blotting paper thoroughly but not dripping wet — squeeze out all surplus water
2. Place the moist paper in the bottom of a shallow container or plate
3. Spread seeds on the moist surface, spacing them so they are not touching
4. Cover with another moist paper layer or close the container to maintain humidity
5. Place in a warm location — a germination cabinet or the top of a refrigerator works well
6. Check daily after the first 2–3 days for root emergence
7. As soon as roots appear but before they exceed a few millimeters in length, plant immediately

### Critical Timing

The window between root emergence and the point where the root is too long to handle safely is narrow — often just 12–24 hours for small seeds. The root is the most delicate part of the sprouting seed and snaps off easily. Practice and regular checking are essential. For larger seeds (beans, peas), the window is somewhat longer.

## Species-Specific Considerations

### Potatoes

Potato chitting (also called "greensprouting") involves placing seed potatoes in a cool, bright location for several weeks before planting. The eyes develop short, sturdy, green shoots. This process gives potatoes a head start of 2–3 weeks in the field. Unlike other seeds where the root is the focus, potato chitting emphasizes shoot development since the tuber itself provides the initial moisture and nutrient reserves.

### Large Seeds (Beans, Peas, Squash)

Large seeds are easier to chit because they are easy to handle and their roots are more robust. However, they also take longer to germinate. Soaking large seeds in water for 8–12 hours before chitting can speed the process. Do not leave large seeds soaking for more than 24 hours or they may suffocate.

### Small Seeds (Lettuce, Carrots, Herbs)

Small seeds are more challenging to chit because handling tiny [[solomon-sprouting-seeds-and-early-season-nutrition]] requires care. Blotting paper in a sealed plastic bag works well. Some gardeners chit small seeds by mixing them with slightly moist vermiculite in a plastic bag, which cushions the delicate roots and makes planting easier — the seed-vermiculite mix can be sprinkled directly into the drill.

### Very Hard Seeds

Some seeds have hard seed coats that resist water uptake. These may benefit from scarification (lightly nicking the seed coat with a file or sandpaper) or soaking in warm water before chitting. Without breaking the seed coat barrier, germination can be extremely slow and uneven.

## Laboratory Germination Standards

Professional seed testing laboratories follow standardized protocols that provide a useful model for home chitting. Lab procedures specify:

- **Sterile media** to prevent microbial contamination
- **Precise [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]]** for each species
- **Defined day counts** — seeds that emerge after the protocol's maximum day count are considered too weak for field conditions
- **Consistent moisture** without waterlogging

The key finding from laboratory testing is that seeds germinating at the highest percentage are also the fastest to emerge. Speed and completeness of germination are correlated indicators of seed vigor. This principle applies directly to home chitting: if most of your seeds sprout within a few days of each other, you have high-quality seed. If germination is slow and stretched out over many days, the seed lot is weak.

## Relationship to Soil Temperature

Chitting is most valuable in cool spring conditions when soil temperatures are below optimal for germination. In warm summer soil, direct sowing often works well because the soil provides adequate warmth and moisture. But in early spring or in [[greenhouse-cool-climates-mollison]], chitting allows the gardener to bypass the soil temperature limitation entirely by providing warmth artificially during the germination phase.

## Pitfalls and Common Mistakes

1. **Letting roots grow too long**: The radicle becomes fragile and prone to snapping. Check seeds frequently (every 12 hours) once germination begins.
2. **Inconsistent moisture**: If the blotting paper dries out, germination halts and may not resume evenly. Reseal containers and check moisture daily.
3. **Excessive moisture**: Seeds should be moist, not submerged. Standing water promotes rotting and fungal growth rather than germination.
4. **Temperature too high**: Above 80°F (27°C) can actually reduce germination rates for many species. A consistent 70–75°F is better than fluctuating extremes.
5. **Planting too deep after chitting**: Chitted seeds have already used some of their energy reserves. Plant them slightly shallower than you would dry seeds, and cover with fine compost rather than heavy soil to reduce resistance to the emerging shoot.
6. **Handling damage**: Use tweezers for very small seeds. Never touch the root itself — hold the seed body and gently place it in position.
7. **Chitting too many at once**: Only chit what you can plant within the 12–24 hour viable window. Surplus sprouted seeds will need to be discarded or planted in nursery beds.

## Combining Chitting with Other Techniques

Chitting works synergistically with several other germination-enhancing practices:

- **Compost-filled drills**: After placing chitted seeds in the furrow, cover with pure fine compost rather than garden soil. Compost holds moisture far better than mineral soil, almost never forms a crust that blocks the emerging shoot, and provides a welcoming microbial environment for the new root.
- **Pressing down loose soil**: When making a seed drill in recently worked (loose) soil, press the soil down firmly with your hand before placing the seed. This restores capillary contact with deeper moisture layers while still maintaining the loose tilth above. Cover the seed by pushing a bit of loose surface soil back over the drill.
- **Furrow mulching**: In hot weather, chitted seeds can be covered with a thin layer of grass clippings or straw to moderate soil temperature and prevent crusting. The emerging shoot will push through light mulch without difficulty.

## When Not to Chit

Chitting is not universally beneficial. It adds labor and complexity. Direct sowing is preferable when:

- Soil temperatures are already at or above optimal for the species
- The seed lot is known to be fresh and vigorous from prior experience
- Planting large areas where individual seed handling is impractical
- The species has tiny seeds that are difficult to handle individually (carrots, lettuce — though the vermiculite method can help)
- Weather conditions are stable and favorable

For most home gardeners, chitting is most valuable for expensive seed, marginally hardy crops, and any planting made in early spring when conditions are unpredictable.
