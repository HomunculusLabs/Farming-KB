# Canning and Preserving

Canning is a heat-based food preservation method that destroys microorganisms and creates a vacuum seal in jars, making food shelf-stable at room temperature for 1-5 years. There are two primary methods: [[comparison-pressure-canning-vs-water-bath-canning]] for high-acid foods and [[pressure-canning-food-preservation]] for low-acid foods. Understanding which method to use for each food is critical for safety.

## Botulism Safety

Clostridium botulinum is the primary safety concern in home canning. This bacterium produces a lethal toxin in anaerobic (oxygen-free), low-acid environments -- exactly the conditions inside a sealed jar. The toxin is tasteless, odorless, and invisible.

Key safety rules:
- Low-acid foods (pH above 4.6) MUST be pressure canned at 240-250 F to destroy C. botulinum spores
- Water bath canning only reaches 212 F, which is insufficient for low-acid foods
- Acidify tomatoes by adding bottled lemon juice (1 tbsp per pint) or citric acid (1/4 tsp per pint) -- modern tomato varieties are often lower in acid than historical varieties
- Never taste-test food from a jar with a broken seal, bulging lid, or off-smell
- When in doubt, throw it out -- no food is worth the risk of botulism
- Process all canned goods for the full time specified in a tested recipe
- Always use current, tested recipes from reliable sources (USDA, National Center for Home Food Preservation, Ball Blue Book)

## Water Bath Canning

Water bath canning is appropriate for high-acid foods with a pH of 4.6 or below. The boiling water (212 F) is sufficient to destroy yeast, mold, and most bacteria in acidic environments.

### Foods Suitable for Water Bath

- Fruits: apples, peaches, pears, berries, cherries, plums
- Jams, jellies, preserves, marmalades
- Tomatoes (acidified)
- Pickles and relishes (vinegar provides acidity)
- Sauerkraut (fully fermented, see [[food-fermentation-guide]])
- Fruit butters and sauces
- Salsas (formulated for water bath canning)
- Chutneys

### Equipment

- Large canning pot with rack (holds jars off the bottom)
- Mason jars (regular or wide mouth) -- never reuse commercial jars
- New canning lids (never reuse lids -- the sealing compound is single-use)
- Jar bands (can be reused)
- Jar lifter, funnel, headspace tool, bubble remover
- Clean kitchen towels

### Process

1. Inspect jars for chips and cracks. Wash jars, lids, and bands in hot soapy water.
2. Keep jars hot until ready to fill (in simmering water or a warm oven).
3. Prepare food according to tested recipe.
4. Fill jars, leaving the specified headspace (usually 1/4 inch for jams, 1/2 inch for fruits and pickles, 1 inch for pressure canning).
5. Remove air bubbles by running a non-metallic tool around the inside of the jar.
6. Wipe jar rim clean with a damp cloth.
7. Center lid on jar. Screw band to fingertip tightness (do not over-tighten).
8. Place jars on rack in canner. Ensure jars are covered by 1-2 inches of water.
9. Bring to a rolling boil. Start timing when boil is reached.
10. Process for the time specified in the recipe, adjusting for altitude.
11. Turn off heat, wait 5 minutes, then remove jars with jar lifter.
12. Place jars on towel, spaced 1-2 inches apart. Do not disturb for 12-24 hours.
13. Check seals (lid should be concave and not flex when pressed). Remove bands, wipe jars, label, and store.

### Altitude Adjustments for Water Bath

- 0-1000 ft: Add 0 minutes
- 1001-3000 ft: Add 5 minutes
- 3001-6000 ft: Add 10 minutes
- 6001-8000 ft: Add 15 minutes
- 8001-10000 ft: Add 20 minutes

## Pressure Canning

Pressure canning is required for all low-acid foods. A [[pf-tek-pressure-canner-sterilization]] reaches 240-250 F (at 10-15 PSI), which is hot enough to destroy C. botulinum spores.

### Foods Requiring Pressure Canning

- Vegetables: green beans, corn, peas, carrots, beets, squash, pumpkin
- Meats: beef, poultry, pork, fish
- Soups and stews
- Beans and legumes
- Broths and stocks
- Mixed foods with meat and vegetables (spaghetti sauce with meat, chili)

### Equipment

- Pressure canner (not a [[double-layer-tinfoil-jar-covering-pressure-cooker-method]] -- must be large enough for jars and have a dial gauge or weighted gauge)
- Dial gauge canners should be tested annually for accuracy
- Same jar, lid, and tool requirements as water bath

### Process

1. Follow steps 1-8 from water bath canning.
2. Place jars on rack in pressure canner. Add 2-3 inches of hot water.
3. Lock canner lid. Vent steam for 10 minutes (full steam stream, not just sputtering).
4. Close vent (put weight on or close petcock).
5. Bring to target pressure (10 PSI at 0-1000 ft; 15 PSI above 1000 ft for dial gauge).
6. Maintain target pressure for the entire processing time.
7. Turn off heat and let pressure return to zero naturally (do not force-cool).
8. Wait 10 minutes after zero pressure, then open lid away from you.
9. Remove jars. Cool, check seals, label, and store as with water bath.

### Altitude Adjustments for Pressure Canning

Dial gauge canners:
- 0-2000 ft: 11 PSI
- 2001-4000 ft: 12 PSI
- 4001-6000 ft: 13 PSI
- 6001-8000 ft: 14 PSI
- 8001-10000 ft: 15 PSI

Weighted gauge canners: Use 15 PSI for altitudes above 1000 ft.

## Common Mistakes

- Using water bath for low-acid foods: Deadly. Always pressure can vegetables, meats, and mixed foods unless acidified.
- Reusing canning lids: The sealing compound degrades. Always use new lids.
- Not adjusting for altitude: Lower boiling point at altitude means insufficient heat treatment.
- Over-tightening bands: Prevents air from escaping during processing. Fingertip tight only.
- Not removing air bubbles: Air bubbles expand during processing and can break the seal.
- Skipping the venting step in pressure canning: Incomplete venting leaves air pockets that prevent proper pressure.

## Storage

Store canned goods in a cool, dark, dry place (50-70 F). Avoid temperature fluctuations. Shelf life is 1 year for best quality, though properly canned food is safe for much longer. Discard any jars with broken seals, bulging lids, mold, off-odors, or spurting liquid when opened.
