# Woronin Body Biogenesis and Apical Gene Expression

## Overview

Woronin body formation in Euascomycete fungi is not distributed uniformly throughout the mycelium. Instead, it is **programmed to occur specifically in the apical hyphal compartment**—the region between the growing hyphal tip and the first septum. This spatial localisation is achieved through **polarized gene expression**, where *hex-1* mRNA accumulates at the leading edge of the colony. Understanding this biogenesis mechanism reveals how filamentous fungi coordinate organelle production with colony growth and how the resulting distribution of [[woronin-bodies]] ensures efficient septal pore sealing throughout the hypha.

## Historical Observations

### Early Light Microscopy (1900)

Charlotte Ternetz (1900) first observed Woronin bodies in the apical cells of hyphae, noting that they were "irregularly dispersed in the protoplasm" and "move every now and then here and there, then settle down against a longitudinal wall or a septum." Buller (1933) confirmed these observations. At the time, the significance of apical localisation was unclear, and these motile particles were not yet understood to be intermediates in Woronin body biogenesis.

### Electron Microscopy

Later electron microscopy studies across multiple Euascomycetes revealed that apical Woronin bodies occupy a cytoplasmic region underlying the **Spitzenkörper**—the cluster of tip-associated vesicles that directs polarized growth (Brenner & Carrol, 1968; McClure et al., 1968; Collinge & Markham, 1982; Momany et al., 2002). This sub-apical positioning places nascent Woronin bodies near the site of active membrane and cell wall synthesis.

## Apical vs. Sub-Apical Behaviour

### Apical Compartments

In the apical compartment (defined by the hyphal tip and first septum), Woronin bodies are:

- **Motile**: They move freely within the cytoplasm, as observed by live-cell imaging.
- **Numerous**: This is the primary site of new Woronin body formation.
- **Associated with the Spitzenkörper**: Positioned in the cytoplasm beneath the vesicle cluster.

### Sub-Apical Compartments

In sub-apical compartments (defined by two adjacent septa), Woronin bodies are:

- **Immobile**: Once docked, they remain stationary.
- **Septal pore-associated**: Localised near septal pores or docked at the cell periphery.
- **Functionally positioned**: Ready to plug the adjacent septal pore in case of damage.

### Retrograde Transport

In *Aspergillus* germlings that have undergone septation, fewer Woronin bodies are found at the hyphal tip than in pre-septation hyphae. This suggests a process of **retrograde transport** whereby mature Woronin bodies are moved from the apical compartment into sub-apical compartments, where they take up their defensive positions near septal pores (Momany et al., 2002).

## Mechanism of Polarized Gene Expression

### Discovery via Fluorescent Reporters

The genetic basis for apical Woronin body formation was investigated using a fluorescent reporter protein expressed from *hex-1* regulatory sequences (Tey et al., 2005). This revealed:

- A **fluorescent gradient** that was maximal in apical cells, declining towards the colony interior.
- **Endogenous *hex-1* transcripts** were specifically enriched at the leading edge of the fungal colony.
- By contrast, other transcripts (e.g., housekeeping genes) accumulated in the colony interior.

### The mRNA Localisation Model

These findings suggested that **localized *hex-1* mRNA transcripts** determine the site of Woronin body core formation. According to this model:

1. *hex-1* mRNA is transported to and/or stabilised at the hyphal tip.
2. Local translation produces HEX-1 protein in the apical compartment.
3. HEX-1 is imported into peroxisomes and self-assembles into crystalline cores.
4. Mature Woronin bodies are either retained near the tip or undergo retrograde transport to sub-apical septal pores.

### Experimental Confirmation

To test the mRNA localisation model, the *hex-1* structural gene was expressed from the regulatory sequences of a transcript that normally accumulates in the **colony interior**. Under these conditions:

- Woronin body formation was **re-directed to the colony interior**.
- These strains displayed **loss-of-function phenotypes specifically in apical compartments**—the tips were vulnerable to damage.
- Interior-formed Woronin bodies could not efficiently protect the growing tip.

This elegant experiment demonstrated that **local mRNA accumulation is both necessary and sufficient** to determine the site of Woronin body formation. The spatial pattern of *hex-1* expression, rather than any intrinsic property of the apical compartment itself, controls where Woronin bodies are produced.

## Functional Significance of Apical Programming

The apical programming of *hex-1* expression serves a critical functional purpose: it ensures that **mature Woronin bodies are present throughout the hypha**, with particular emphasis on protecting the most vulnerable region—the growing tip.

### Why the Tip Needs Protection

The hyphal tip is the most actively growing and metabolically expensive region of the mycelium. It is also the most exposed to environmental hazards:

- **Physical damage**: The thin, extending hyphal wall at the tip is structurally vulnerable.
- **Predation**: Grazing organisms preferentially attack active growing regions.
- **Environmental stress**: Osmotic shock, temperature changes, and chemical insults most directly affect the exposed tip.

Without Woronin bodies positioned near the septa of the apical compartment, damage to the tip would allow protoplasmic contents to flow backward through the open septal pores into the colony, causing widespread damage.

### The Distribution Strategy

The combination of apical biogenesis and retrograde transport creates an efficient distribution system:

1. **Production**: All new Woronin bodies are made at the tip, where HEX-1 concentration and *hex-1* mRNA are highest.
2. **Deployment**: Mature Woronin bodies move backward to dock at septal pores throughout the hypha.
3. **Coverage**: As the hypha elongates and new septa form, a continuous supply of Woronin bodies from the tip ensures all new septal pores are equipped.

This is analogous to a factory at one end of a production line, continuously manufacturing components that are distributed along the length of the structure as it grows.

## Septal Pore Tethering

### Laser Trap Experiments

Woronin bodies near septal pores are not merely resting nearby—they are actively **tethered**. This was demonstrated using laser-induced optical traps (Berns et al., 1992):

- Woronin bodies could be physically pulled away from the septum using focused laser beams.
- Upon release from the optical trap, they **returned to their original position**.
- This demonstrated the existence of an elastic tethering mechanism.

### Ultrastructural Evidence

Electron microscopy reveals **filamentous material** connecting the Woronin body to the septal pore region (Momany et al., 2002). The composition of this tethering material is currently unknown, but it likely consists of cytoskeletal elements or specialised adaptor proteins that anchor the Woronin body within striking distance of the septal pore opening.

### Functional Importance

Tethering is critical for rapid response to damage:

- The Woronin body must be positioned close enough to the pore to plug it within milliseconds of septal damage.
- Without tethering, diffusion alone would be too slow to prevent significant protoplasmic loss given the high turgor pressures in [[fungal-hyphae]] (estimated at 0.2–0.8 MPa).
- The tether may also provide the mechanical force that drives the Woronin body into the pore when the pressure differential changes upon damage.

## Open Questions

Several aspects of Woronin body biogenesis remain to be resolved:

1. **Membrane re-sealing**: After the Woronin body plugs the septal pore, what mechanism seals the plasma membrane? Electron microscopy shows cell wall material deposited over the Woronin body–pore complex, but the membrane resealing machinery is unknown.
2. **Tether composition**: What proteins or structures form the filamentous tether between the Woronin body and the septal pore?
3. **Frequency regulation**: What determines how many Woronin bodies are produced per unit length of hypha, and how is this adjusted under different environmental conditions?
4. **mRNA transport**: By what mechanism is *hex-1* mRNA transported to and/or anchored at the hyphal tip? Motor proteins on microtubules are a likely candidate but have not been demonstrated.
5. **Retrograde transport mechanism**: How are mature Woronin bodies moved from apical to sub-apical compartments? Active transport along cytoskeletal elements is probable but uncharacterised.
