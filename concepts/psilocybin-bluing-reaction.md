# Psilocybin Bluing Reaction

The characteristic blue-to-green discoloration observed in psilocybin-producing mushrooms when bruised, handled, or aged. Understanding this reaction is essential for [[mushroom-identification]] and distinguishes psychoactive Agaricales from non-psychoactive species. See also [[psilocybin-mushroom-chemistry]] for alkaloid content and [[mushroom-toxicity-identification-warnings]] for lookalike species.

## Biochemical Mechanism

The bluing reaction results from enzymatic oxidation of psilocin (not psilocybin directly):

1. **Psilocybin** (the phosphate-protected form) is the primary alkaloid stored in mushroom tissue
2. When tissue is damaged, **phosphatase enzymes** remove the phosphate group, converting psilocybin to **psilocin**
3. **Psilocin** is then oxidized by air (and accelerated by cytochrome oxidases and laccases in mushroom tissue)
4. Oxidation produces **blue-colored chinone-like compounds** that are unstable

This explains why some species with high psilocybin but low psilocin don't blue strongly — the enzymatic dephosphorylation step may be slow. Conversely, [[psilocybe-bohemica]] displays an exceptionally strong bluing reaction despite low psilocin levels, because the enzyme that removes the phosphate group acts very quickly in that species.

## Species-Specific Bluing Patterns

Different psilocybin-containing species show markedly different bluing behavior:

| Species | Bluing Pattern | Notes |
|---------|---------------|-------|
| *Psilocybe bohemica* | Very rapid, intense cap staining | Strongest reaction observed; enzyme activity is rapid |
| *Psilocybe cubensis* | Strong stem bluing, caps often don't blue | Stems develop deep blue to blackish-blue |
| *Psilocybe cyanescens* | Strong overall bluing | Cap and stem both blue readily |
| *Psilocybe semilanceata* | Slight stem staining, delayed | May take considerable time to appear |
| *Conocybe cyanopus* | Slight stem staining, delayed | Similar to P. semilanceata |
| *Inocybe aeruginascens* | Slight stem staining, delayed | Only trace psilocin |
| *Gymnopilus purpuratus* | Moderate, intermediate timing | Falls between extremes |
| *Panaeolus subbalteatus* | Minimal to none | Despite containing psilocybin at similar levels to strongly bluing species |

## Key Principle: Bluing Is Not a Reliable Potency Indicator

The bluing reaction reveals **nothing** about the type or quantity of specific indole compounds present. Evidence:

- *Psilocybe semilanceata* and *Panaeolus subbalteatus* have similar alkaloid concentrations, but only slight discoloration
- Very old, strongly discolored fruiting bodies of *P. cubensis* have **lower** psilocybin/psilocin levels than younger specimens — the active compounds are consumed by the oxidation reaction
- The blue pigments have high color intensity, so tiny amounts produce visible discoloration regardless of total alkaloid content
- Trace amounts of iron ions (Fe²⁺) accelerate the bluing reaction without reflecting alkaloid content

## Metol Testing: Not Specific to Psilocybes

Metol (p-methylaminophenol), a photographic developer, causes purple coloration in Psilocybe stems — but this is **not specific** to psilocybin-containing species. Metol reacts with the **laccase enzyme** found in many mushroom species, including non-psychoactive ones:

- **Agaricus bisporus** (champignon) — brown and white varieties both change color with metol
- Many other gilled mushrooms react to metol regardless of alkaloid content
- The reaction was propagated as a field identification tool but is "all but useless" in practice

## Melzer's Reagent: Also Non-Specific

Melzer's Reagent (iodine-potassium iodide in chloral hydrate) was proposed for identifying Psilocybe species but proved equally worthless — it does not specifically indicate psilocybin presence.

## Iron Chloride Testing

Iron chloride (FeCl₃) produces a deep blue color with *Inocybe aeruginascens* but not with muscarine-producing Inocybe species. This is more realistic than metol because psilocin itself is a phenol and iron salts react with phenoles to form intensely blue molecules. However, *I. aeruginascens* contains only trace psilocin, so the reaction likely involves other phenolic compounds.

## Practical Guidelines for Foragers

1. **Only spontaneous bluing** (from injury or aging) provides reliable clues about psilocybin presence in gilled mushrooms (Agaricales)
2. **Chemical reagents** (metol, Melzer's) are NOT reliable field identification tools
3. **Bluing absence does NOT mean a mushroom is non-psychoactive** — some species with significant psilocybin barely blue
4. **Bluing presence does NOT indicate potency** — the pigment intensity is independent of alkaloid quantity
5. **All blue-staining boletes are non-psychoactive** — bluing in Boletales is caused by different, physiologically inactive compounds (variegatic acid, xerocomic acid)

## Historical Notes

- **1799**: E. Brande published the first account of psilocybin intoxication (St. James's Green Park, London). J. Sowerby included a rendition of what is now recognized as *Psilocybe semilanceata* in his 1803 "Coloured Figures of English Fungi"
- **Early 1900s**: M.C. Cooke first speculated about the significance of bluing in *P. semilanceata*, wondering if bluing varieties were poisonous or if external factors altered their chemistry
- **1950s**: Singer and Smith linked the bluing reaction to psychoactive ingredients
- **1958**: A. Hofmann confirmed that pure psilocin becomes unstable (turns bluish-green) when exposed to oxidizing agents
- **1960s**: Blaschko, Levine, Bocks, Horita and Weber demonstrated that only psilocin (not psilocybin) oxidizes to blue-green products
