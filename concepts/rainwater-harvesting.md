# Rainwater Harvesting

Rainwater harvesting is the collection and storage of rainwater from roofs and other surfaces for later use in irrigation, livestock watering, household use, and emergency supply. A well-designed rainwater harvesting system can provide 50-100% of a homestead's non-potable water needs and serves as a critical component of [[permaculture-design]] water management. Combined with [[swale-design]], [[graywater-systems]], and [[keyline-design]], rainwater harvesting creates a comprehensive on-site water strategy that reduces dependence on municipal supplies and wells.

## Catchment Calculation

Understanding your water potential is the first step in system design:

### Rainfall to Gallons Formula

Gallons = Roof Area (sq ft) x Annual Rainfall (inches) x 0.623

The factor 0.623 converts inches of rain over one square foot to gallons (1 inch of rain on 1 sq ft = 0.623 gallons).

### Example Calculations

| Roof Area | Annual Rainfall | Annual Collection (90% efficiency) |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 sq ft | 30 inches | ~16,800 gallons |
| 1,500 sq ft | 40 inches | ~33,600 gallons |
| 2,000 sq ft | 20 inches | ~22,400 gallons |
| 2,000 sq ft | 50 inches | ~56,000 gallons |

### Monthly Demand Estimation

Typical household non-potable water use:
- Garden irrigation: 500-2,000 gallons/month (varies enormously by season)
- Livestock: 5-50 gallons/day per animal (species dependent)
- Outdoor cleaning: 100-500 gallons/month
- Total homestead non-potable: 1,000-5,000 gallons/month in peak season

## System Components

### Catchment Surface (Roof)

The roof is the primary catchment surface for most systems:

- **Metal roofing**: Best choice — smooth, non-toxic, long-lasting, efficient collection (90-95% yield)
- **Asphalt shingles**: Most common; collects well but may leach petroleum compounds and granules. Acceptable for irrigation but not recommended for drinking water.
- **Clay tile**: Excellent collection, non-toxic, but brittle. Good for potable systems.
- **Concrete tile**: Good collection, durable, non-toxic. Suitable for potable use.
- **Wood shingle/shake**: Poor choice — absorbs water, leaches tannins and preservatives, supports moss growth.
- **EPDM rubber**: Excellent for flat roofs, non-toxic, high collection efficiency.

**Avoid**: Roofs treated with chromated copper arsenate (CCA), roofs with lead flashing, roofs near industrial pollution sources.

### Gutters and Downspouts

- **Material**: Aluminum, galvanized steel, or PVC. Avoid copper (toxic to plants and aquatic life in concentrations).
- **Sizing**: 5-inch K-style gutters handle most residential roofs. 6-inch for large roofs in high-rainfall areas.
- **Maintenance**: Clean gutters at least twice per year (spring and fall). Gutter guards reduce maintenance but can reduce collection efficiency by 10-15%.
- **Slope**: Gutters should slope 1/16 to 1/8 inch per foot toward downspouts.

### First Flush Diverter

The most critical component for water quality. A first flush diverter captures and discards the first volume of contaminated water from each rain event. This water contains dust, pollen, bird droppings, and debris that accumulated on the roof during dry periods.

**Sizing rule**: Divert the first 0.02 gallons per square foot of roof area.

**Examples**:
- 1,000 sq ft roof: divert first 20 gallons
- 2,000 sq ft roof: divert first 40 gallons

**Types**:
- **Standpipe diverter**: A vertical pipe with a ball float that seals the inlet once full, directing subsequent water to the tank. Simple, reliable, and common.
- **Downspout diverter**: A commercially available unit that installs in the downspout and diverts the first flush to a small bucket or chamber.
- **DIY pipe diverter**: PVC pipe with a drain valve at the bottom. Inexpensive and effective.

### Storage Tanks

The most expensive component and the heart of the system:

| Tank Type | Cost (per gallon) | Lifespan | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Polyethylene (poly) | $0.50-1.50 | 20-30 years | Lightweight, UV-resistant, easy to install | Can degrade in sunlight; not always aesthetically pleasing |
| Galvanized steel | $1.00-3.00 | 30-50 years | Durable, fireproof | Heavy; can rust; condensation issue (sweating) |
| Concrete | $0.75-2.00 | 50+ years | Very durable, can be underground | Heavy; cracks possible; high pH leaching initially |
| Ferrocement | $0.30-1.00 | 30+ years | Low cost, can be any shape, DIY-friendly | Labor-intensive; waterproofing critical |
| Wood (oak, cedar) | $2.00-5.00 | 10-20 years | Beautiful, natural | Expensive; requires maintenance; can leak |

**Sizing**: Size your tank to capture as much winter/spring rainfall as possible for summer use. A common rule: 1 inch of rain on 1,000 sq ft of roof = 623 gallons. In a climate with 30 inches of annual rainfall, a 1,000 sq ft roof generates 18,690 gallons/year. A 5,000-gallon tank captures roughly 2 months of winter rainfall for summer use.

**Location**:
- Elevated (even a few feet) provides gravity-fed distribution — no pump needed
- Near the point of use (garden, livestock)
- On a stable, level foundation (compacted gravel or concrete pad)
- Protected from direct sunlight (UV degrades poly tanks; shade them or paint)
- Accessible for cleaning and maintenance

### Distribution System

- **Gravity-fed**: Tank elevated 10+ feet provides 4.3 psi of pressure at ground level — sufficient for drip irrigation and low-flow hose bibs. Most efficient and reliable.
- **Pump-fed**: For ground-level tanks or higher pressure needs. A small 1/4 HP pump provides adequate pressure for garden hoses and drip systems. See [[irrigation-systems]] for integration details.
- **Drip irrigation**: The most efficient distribution method. Rainwater is ideal for drip systems because it contains no chlorine (which can clog drip emitters over time).

### Filtration

For irrigation purposes, minimal filtration is needed:
- **Screen filter**: 200-500 micron mesh at the tank inlet catches leaves and debris
- **First flush diverter**: Removes the bulk of contamination
- **For potable use**: Additional sediment filters (5-20 micron), carbon filters, and UV sterilization are required. Potable rainwater systems are beyond the scope of this page but are common in off-grid settings.

## System Design Strategies

### Dry System

The conveyance pipe (from gutter to tank) drains completely between rain events. The pipe slopes toward the tank so all water empties out. Simple, clean, and less prone to freezing. Best when the tank is close to the house and downslope.

### Wet System

The conveyance pipe from the gutters runs underground and connects to the bottom of the tank. The pipe remains full of water between rain events. Allows the tank to be placed away from the house (around a corner, behind a building). More complex plumbing; potential for freezing in cold climates. Requires careful installation to ensure proper flow.

### Cascading Tanks

Multiple tanks connected so that the first tank fills and overflows into the second, which overflows into the third, etc. Provides large storage capacity with smaller, more manageable tanks. Each tank can be located at a different elevation for gravity distribution to different zones.

### Underground Cisterns

Large storage tanks buried below ground. Advantages: invisible, protected from freezing and UV, space-saving above ground. Disadvantages: expensive to excavate and install, difficult to inspect and maintain, requires pump for distribution, must be sized for groundwater conditions (can float out of the ground if empty in high water table areas).

## Maintenance

- **After each rain**: Check first flush diverter is functioning; check tank inlet screen
- **Monthly**: Inspect gutters and downspouts for blockages; check tank water level
- **Quarterly**: Clean tank inlet screen; check overflow pipe; inspect connections for leaks
- **Annually**: Clean gutters thoroughly; inspect tank interior (if accessible); check pump (if used); test water quality if used for irrigation of sensitive plants
- **Every 3-5 years**: Clean tank interior; inspect for sediment buildup; check structural integrity

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Low water in tank | Insufficient catchment area; low rainfall; high demand | Add more catchment surface; reduce demand; add another tank |
| Debris in tank | Missing or clogged first flush diverter; no inlet screen | Install or clean first flush diverter; add mesh screen at inlet |
| Water smells bad | Organic matter in tank; stagnant water; no turnover | Clean tank; ensure regular turnover; add aeration |
| Mosquitoes in tank | Openings not screened | Screen all openings (inlet, overflow, vent) with 1/16 inch mesh |
| Algae growth | Sunlight exposure | Shade tank; paint exterior; cover tank |
| Overflow flooding | Overflow pipe undersized or blocked | Size overflow pipe to handle maximum flow; clear blockages |
| Freeze damage | Above-ground tank in cold climate; no winterization | Drain system before first freeze; insulate pipes; heat tape on critical sections; install underground tank |
| Low pressure | Tank too low; pump undersized; clogged filter | Raise tank; upgrade pump; clean or replace filter |
