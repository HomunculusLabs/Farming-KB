# [[fungal-parasites]] and Predators of Rotifers

Rotifers, particularly bdelloid rotifers, serve as hosts for a diverse
assemblage of fungal parasites and predators. Although approximately 60
species of fungi are known to attack bdelloid rotifers, few serious
attempts have been made to recover and characterize members of this
group. Using systematic baiting techniques with a single host species,
that number could potentially extend to 100 or more species, and
developing methods to recover parasites from natural populations of a
variety of rotifer species from soil or aquatic environments could
increase the number to several hundred.

## Diversity and Taxonomic Distribution

The genera of fungal predators and parasites of bdelloid rotifers are
distributed widely across the fungal kingdom:

- **Chytridiomycota**: Catenaria, Endochytrium, Olpidium,
  Rhizophydium
- **Zygomycota**: Rhopalomyces, Brachymyces, Zoophagus
- **Oomycota**: Atkinsiella, Lagenidium, Sommerstorffia, Haptoglossa
- **Anamorph fungi**: Rotiferophthora, Harposporium, Haptospora,
  Pseudomeria, Lecophagus, Cephaliophora, Dwayaangam,
  Medusamyces, Tolyplocladium, Culicinomyces, Triacutus

## Endoparasitic Fungi of Rotifers

Endoparasitic fungi attack rotifers by means of spores that adhere to
the cuticle or are ingested by the animal as food. Once attached or
ingested, the spores germinate, and hyphae proliferate inside the body
of the host. All assimilative stages of the endoparasites are contained
entirely within the body of the host. Only reproductive or fertile
hyphae extend to the exterior of the animal and proliferate.

The parasitic cycle progresses rapidly:
- Within minutes of initial infection, an attacked rotifer may encyst
- Within 24 hours, much of the body is colonized by internal hyphae
- Within 48 hours, the body is filled with hyphae emerging through
  the cuticle
- Within 72 hours, sporulation is initiated on external hyphae
- Following sporulation, the dead host and exhausted reproductive
  apparatus degrade rapidly

At any given time, only a small percentage of individuals in a rotifer
population will be infected by an endoparasitic fungus. The best approach
for collectors is to recover a large number of rotifers from a natural
population and screen them for parasites indigenous to that population.

## Rotiferophthora and Other Key Genera

Species of the genus Rotiferophthora are extremely common parasites of
bdelloid rotifers. Infected rotifers float at or near the surface of the
water film. Verticillate conidiophores grow into the air above the body
and produce abundant spore masses that are hydrophobic. The conidiophores
with conidial masses act like tiny sails, and the infected rotifers
float over the surface of the water for prolonged periods. Such infected
rotifers can be located easily on the surface of a Petri dish under a
dissecting microscope using low-angled light.

## Predatory Fungi

Predatory fungi develop an extensive hyphal system in the substratum;
the hyphae give rise to trapping devices, including adhesive pegs, nets,
or constricting rings, which are used to attract and capture rotifers
and nematodes. Preliminary evidence indicates that several species of
predatory fungi capture both bdelloid and loricate rotifers. If a
predator is present, it will capture rotifers and anchor itself to the
plate by hyphal growth into the agar. Clusters of rotifer bodies on
plates indicate zones where predation has occurred.

## Recovery and Culturing Methods

### Recovery Techniques

1. Living or dead plant material is harvested from ditches, ponds, and
   other aquatic habitats
2. Material is transferred to sterile pond water in stackable, nested
   culture dishes
3. Cultures are incubated and periodically inspected for the presence
   of predators or parasites
4. Plates that have been used to collect endoparasites can be washed
   and inspected for clusters of rotifer bodies indicating predation

### Culturing Parasites

An infected rotifer is transferred to clean agar using a fine needle or
micromanipulator. The rotifer is cleaned by dragging or pushing it over
or under the agar surface to remove debris and contaminants. It is then
transferred to a plate with fresh potato-dextrose agar amended with
streptomycin sulfate (50-100 mg/L) and incubated at room temperature.
Within a few days, hyphae from the rotifer grow into the surrounding
agar.

Sporulation may require transfer to water agar supplemented with rotifer
extract or debris from old rotifer cultures that has been filter-sterilized
through a 0.2-mm Millipore filter. After sporulation begins, the plate
is flooded with a fresh culture of rotifers to produce massive
simultaneous infections.

### Culturing Predators

Predatory fungi can be recovered from debris transferred to stackable
glass dishes. Once a culture is established, the isolate can be
challenged with rotifers by growing it on water agar and adding a few
drops of nematode or rotifer culture.

## Aquatic versus Terrestrial Rotifers

Few serious studies of parasites of aquatic rotifers have been carried
out, and no special techniques for recovery have been described. An
obvious approach is to use a standardized sweep-net technique for
recovery of aquatic invertebrates, then plate out harvested populations
in sterile pond water. Simple, unsophisticated techniques also can be
used. In general, densities of all types of spores are higher in soils
than in water, and the levels of parasitic infections in aquatic
habitats are likely to be low. Fewer hyphomycetous and more zoosporic
parasites are expected in aquatic versus terrestrial environments.

## Protocol for Describing New Species

The minimal protocol for describing new or rare parasitic fungi includes:

1. Ten semipermanent slide mounts of infected rotifers fixed in acetic
   alcohol and mounted in lactophenol
2. A pure culture of the fungus on potato-dextrose agar amended with
   streptomycin
3. New strains deposited in a public culture collection
4. Washed rotifers with developing conidiophores placed into a plate
   with a bait rotifer population to initiate an epidemic
