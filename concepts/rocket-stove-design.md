# Rocket Stove Design

A [[comparison-cob-oven-construction-vs-rocket-stove]] is an efficient, clean-burning cookstove that uses small-diameter wood fuel and an insulated combustion chamber to achieve near-complete combustion. They produce very little smoke, use a fraction of the fuel of open fires, and can be built from inexpensive or salvaged materials. On [[query-how-to-start-beekeeping-on-a-homestead]], rocket stoves serve as primary or backup cooking, water heating, and space [[mollison-designers-solar-water-heating-systems]].

## How Rocket Stoves Work

Rocket stoves operate on the principle of **insulated combustion [[gottlieb-psilocybin-cultivation-fruiting-chamber-design]]**:

1. **Fuel is fed vertically** into a narrow (J-shaped or L-shaped) combustion chamber
2. The chamber is heavily insulated, which keeps the combustion zone extremely hot (1000F+)
3. At high temperatures, wood gasifies and the gases burn completely — this is called "secondary combustion"
4. An insulated chimney creates strong draft, pulling air in through the fuel feed and exhausting through the top
5. The result: a hot, nearly smokeless flame with very high fuel efficiency

Key design ratios:
- The fuel feed (horizontal section) should be approximately the same diameter as the chimney
- The chimney height should be at least 3-4 times the diameter of the fuel feed
- The gap between the pot and the top of the chimney should be narrow (1-2 inches) for efficient heat transfer
- Insulation thickness should be at least 2 inches around the entire combustion chamber and chimney

## J-Tube vs. L-Tube

### J-Tube (Most Common)
The fuel feed is vertical, feeding down into a horizontal burn tunnel, which connects to a vertical chimney. The shape resembles the letter J.

- Cleaner burn due to the vertical fuel feed and preheating of incoming air
- Fuel feeds itself by gravity — you can load sticks and they slide down as they burn
- Slightly more complex to build
- Better for cooking (more focused flame)

### L-Tube (Simplified)
The fuel feed is horizontal, making a simple 90-degree turn into the vertical chimney.

- Simpler to build — just two tubes at a right angle
- Less efficient than J-tube (less preheating of combustion air)
- Can smoke more at startup
- Better for quick, simple builds and emergency situations

## DIY Build: 6-Brick Rocket Stove (Simplest Version)

This takes 10 minutes to assemble and costs nearly nothing. Use standard 8x4x2.25 inch solid bricks (not cored).

1. Lay 4 bricks on the ground in an L-shape: 3 bricks in a row, 1 brick perpendicular at the end forming the base of the chimney
2. Place 1 brick across the top of the horizontal section, leaving the end open for the fuel feed
3. This creates an L-shaped channel. The horizontal section is the fuel feed and burn tunnel; the vertical section is the chimney
4. Place your pot directly on top of the chimney opening

This is functional but not efficient — there is no insulation. It works for demonstration and emergency use but is not a good daily driver.

## DIY Build: Insulated J-Tube Rocket Stove

A proper insulated rocket stove requires more effort but performs dramatically better.

### Materials
- Steel pipe or square tubing for the combustion chamber: 6-inch diameter, 24 inches long
- Steel elbow or custom-cut section for the J-bend
- Steel pipe for the chimney: 6-inch diameter, 24-36 inches tall
- Insulation: perlite, vermiculite, wood ash, pumice, or lightweight concrete (perlite cement mix)
- Outer shell: 55-gallon steel drum, or a box built from sheet metal or brick
- Steel plate or grate for the burn tunnel floor
- Metal pot skirt (optional but recommended — a cylinder that fits around the pot, forcing hot gases against the pot sides)

### Construction

1. **Form the J-tube** — cut the steel pipe to form the J-bend. The horizontal fuel feed section should be 12-16 inches long, the vertical chimney 24-36 inches. Weld or use high-temperature stove cement to join the pieces. Alternatively, use square steel tubing and a pre-made 90-degree elbow.

2. **Build the outer shell** — if using a 55-gallon drum, cut a hole in the top for the chimney and a hole in the side for the fuel feed. If building from sheet metal or brick, form a box that encloses the J-tube with 2-3 inches of clearance on all sides.

3. **Pack insulation** — fill the space between the J-tube and the outer shell with perlite, vermiculite, or a perlite-cement mix (1 part cement to 6-10 parts perlite by volume). The insulation is critical to performance — this is what keeps the combustion chamber hot enough for clean burning.

4. **Add a pot support** — rest the pot on a steel grate or ring at the top of the chimney. The gap between the pot bottom and chimney top should be 1-2 inches for optimal heat transfer.

5. **Add a pot skirt** (optional but highly recommended) — a cylinder of sheet metal that surrounds the pot, extending 2-4 inches below the pot bottom. The gap between the pot and skirt should be 1/4 to 1/2 inch. This forces hot exhaust gases against the sides of the pot, dramatically improving efficiency.

6. **Seal all gaps** — use high-temperature silicone or furnace cement to seal any gaps in the outer shell.

### Fuel

Rocket stoves work best with small-diameter dry wood:
- Sticks 1-2 inches in diameter, 12-18 inches long
- Feed continuously or batch-load depending on your design
- Hardwoods burn longer and hotter; softwoods work fine but burn faster
- Twigs, branches, and scrap wood work perfectly — no splitting required
- Avoid treated wood, plywood, and plastics

## Efficiency and Performance

A well-built rocket stove achieves:
- 60-80% combustion efficiency (open fire is approximately 15-20%)
- 40-50% heat transfer efficiency to the pot (open fire is approximately 10-15%)
- Near-zero visible smoke after initial startup
- Uses 50-75% less fuel than an open fire for the same cooking task

## Applications on the Homestead

- **Daily cooking** — efficient stovetop cooking for soups, stews, boiling, and frying
- **Water heating** — heat water for bathing, cleaning, or [[wood-fired-hot-tub]]
- **Canning** — supports a large pot for [[comparison-pressure-canning-vs-water-bath-canning]] during power outages
- **Space heating** — rocket mass heaters use the same combustion principle with a thermal mass bench for radiant heating (significant project, not covered here)
- **Outdoor cooking** — portable rocket stoves are excellent for outdoor kitchens, camping, and emergency cooking
- **Burning brush** — dispose of small brush and branches efficiently while producing useful heat

## Safety Considerations

- The combustion chamber and chimney reach extreme temperatures (1000F+). Never touch during operation.
- Provide adequate ventilation — rocket stoves are designed for outdoor use or in well-ventilated spaces. They consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide and small amounts of carbon monoxide.
- Never operate a rocket stove in an enclosed space without ventilation.
- Keep flammable materials at least 3 feet from the stove.
- Place on a non-combustible surface (concrete, dirt, stone).
- Monitor children and pets around the stove — the fuel feed opening is at ground level.

## Maintenance

- Clean ash from the combustion chamber and chimney regularly (every few uses)
- Inspect insulation annually and repack if settled
- Check for rust or corrosion on steel components
- Replace the grate/burn tunnel floor when it burns through (this is a wear item)
