## The Limits of Human Knowledge

Fukuoka insists that **man is not in a position to know nature**. This is not a humble acknowledgment of incomplete understanding but a radical epistemological claim: the human mind, by its very structure, is incapable of grasping nature as it truly is. Every attempt to know nature is already an act of separation — the knower stands apart from the known, and in that very act of standing apart, nature is transformed into something other than itself.

He employs the **frog-in-the-well analogy** to illustrate this limitation. The world known to a frog at the bottom of a well is not the entire world, yet the frog has no way of knowing what lies beyond its limited horizon. The frog's knowledge is complete within its frame but fundamentally incomplete as a picture of reality. Similarly, "Man"s nature" — the nature that human beings perceive, measure, and study — is only that nature which they have grasped with their own hands and filtered through their own subjectivity. It is an artifact of perception, not reality itself.

These are not collateral casualties; they are the army that was holding the line. Once they are gone, the pests return with nothing to stop them — and the farmer, seeing pests again, applies more pesticide. The cycle is self-reinforcing and self-destructive.

The result? **Insect damage remains the same.**

The intervention destroys the natural checks and balances while failing to achieve its stated goal, creating a dependency cycle that demands ever more intervention. Each application solves one problem while creating three more. The farmer becomes trapped on what Fukuoka calls a **chemical treadmill** — running faster and faster just to stay in the same place. The pesticide manufacturer profits, the agronomist publishes papers, and the land dies quietly beneath the weight of accumulated toxins.

Worse, the destruction is invisible to the discriminating eye. The farmer sees fewer insects and concludes the pesticide is working. He does not see — because he is not looking, and because his methods do not equip him to see — the spiders that are gone, the soil organisms that have been annihilated, the slow degradation of the ecosystem's capacity for self-regulation. By the time the damage becomes visible, it is irreversible.
