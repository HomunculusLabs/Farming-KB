# Energy-Efficient House Design

## Site Selection and Settlement Design

Settlement site choice, and the surrounding plantings in landscape, are probably so critical as to provide for 70% of the conservation of heat energy in [[mollison-frost-protection-and-thermal-belt-design]]) of a sun-facing slope, preferably with forests and high water storages above the site
- Housing closely placed or conjoined at east and west walls, and preferably of two to four stories, to reduce insulation costs and create a compact site
- Dense windbreak polewards or uphill of the settlement, and to the east and west (a "suntrap" structure)
- Dense attached vines on all sound masonry walls, or out from wooden walls, to assist insulation
- Deciduous trees to sunwards chosen to admit low winter light to all facades to the sunward aspect
- Siting to avoid unstable soils, avalanche tracks, flooding areas, and where possible above local frost lines
- Site to be off radioactive or radio-emitting rocks if possible

Preferably, settlements in cool areas should present a stepped aspect, so that each dwelling presents a full facade to the winter sun.

## Glazing Strategy

Given that settlement site is carefully selected and landscaping is carefully designed, the individual house can self-provide for heat and hot water by good construction technique. Glazing of the facades in any cool-area site should ensure that:

- There is from 30% (Latitude 30-35 degrees) to 100% (Latitude 60 degrees) glazing on the sunward walls
- No glazing on the west walls (a window in the east for a kitchen is controllable)
- Minimal and perhaps double-glazing in the poleward walls (above Latitude 40 degrees, in cold continental interiors or alpine sites)

Houses should be no more than two rooms (10 m) deep on the north/south axis, and may be 1.5 times longer on the east/west axis, so that winter sun can penetrate windows to the walls of the poleward rooms. Sunward roof eave width and sill height of windows should be adjusted to admit winter sun to the interior from early autumn to late spring.

In very cold climates, attach a solarium or glasshouse for day use in winter. Clerestory or attic windows to the sun side heat rear rooms or roof space.

## Floor and Sub-Floor Thermal Mass

Concrete slab or mud, brick, or tiled floors in contact with the ground are ideal to absorb heat into the underfloor earth mass. These can be covered with wooden parquet, carpet, or any preferred surface without long delaying heat transfer efficiency, so that any usual floor covering can be used, including wooden flooring over battens.

The floor area and the earth beneath it are the critical heat stores for winter, radiating solar heat at night, or over cool cloudy periods. As most of this stored earth heat escapes via surrounding soils, and most of that loss occurs within 0.5 m of the earth surface, we must insulate the entire earth mass below the floor by digging a 1 m deep foundation trench, and providing 5-15 cm thickness of insulation around the whole periphery of the floor outline.

## Thermal Mass and Insulation

In house design, the interactions of thermal mass (heat storage) and insulation (buffering for temperature extremes) plus sensible siting permit us to design efficient and safe housing over broad climatic ranges.

Thermal mass materials (concrete, stone, brick, earth, water) absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night. Insulation materials (straw, wool, cellulose, foam) prevent rapid heat loss. The combination of both, properly oriented to the sun, creates a self-regulating temperature environment.

## Non-Toxic Materials

Criteria for permaculture house design include energy-efficient construction and non-toxic materials only. This extends to:

- Natural construction materials (timber, stone, earth, straw)
- Non-toxic paints, glues, and finishes
- Avoidance of high-voltage electromagnetic fields near sleeping areas
- Natural ventilation systems over mechanical air conditioning
- Avoidance of plastics and synthetic materials where natural alternatives exist

## Integration with Landscape

In the permaculture system, good house design includes integration with living components:

- Sod roofs or living roofs
- Vines on trellis attached to walls
- Pot plants and roof gardens
- Companion animals integrated with the household
- Attached glasshouse or shadehouse
- Rainwater catchment from roof areas
- Greywater recycling to garden systems
- Compost systems for kitchen and garden waste
