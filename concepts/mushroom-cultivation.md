# Mushroom Cultivation

Mushroom cultivation refers to the practice of growing fungi for food, medicine,
or research purposes. The cultivation of psilocybin-containing mushrooms has
developed from indigenous practices into sophisticated techniques accessible to
both researchers and home growers, driven by advances in [[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]]. The science of fungal cultivation combines elements of
microbiology, biochemistry, and agricultural engineering.

## Biological Principles

Mushroom cultivation exploits the fungal life cycle, which proceeds through
several distinct phases. Spores germinate to form hyphae, thread-like filaments
that grow and branch to form a network called mycelium. When environmental
conditions are favorable, the mycelium consolidates and forms fruiting bodies
(mushrooms) that produce and disperse spores, completing the cycle.

Cultivation aims to optimize conditions at each stage: colonization requires
warmth, darkness, and sterility, while fruiting requires humidity, fresh air
exchange, and light. Understanding the distinction between vegetative growth
(mycelial colonization) and reproductive growth (fruiting) is fundamental. The
environmental triggers that shift the fungus from one phase to the other include
changes in temperature, humidity, light exposure, and CO2 concentration. See
[[companion-cultivation-of-mushrooms-with-garden-vegetables]] dates back thousands of years in East
Asia, where species like shiitake (Lentinula edodes) were cultivated on logs.
Modern cultivation was advanced significantly by Paul Stamets. For psilocybin
mushrooms, the PF Tek developed by Robert McPherson in the 1990s revolutionized
home cultivation by making the process accessible with minimal equipment.

## Sterile Technique

Contamination by bacteria and competing molds is the primary challenge in
mushroom cultivation. Maintaining sterility throughout the process is essential:

- Working in a clean environment, ideally a laminar flow hood, or at minimum a
  still air box.
- Sterilizing all substrates, containers, and tools using pressure cooking at
  15 PSI for 90 minutes or more.
- Flame-sterilizing inoculation tools (needles, scalpels) before each use.
- Using proper aseptic technique: working quickly, minimizing container open
  time, and avoiding breathing directly on exposed materials.
- Maintaining good personal hygiene: washing hands, wearing gloves, and using a
  face mask to reduce contamination risk.

Common contaminants include Trichoderma (green mold), Aspergillus species, and
bacterial contamination (slimy textures, sour smells). Early detection prevents
spread. See [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]]. Bulk substrates provide additional nutrition and
water-holding capacity for larger fruiting and include coco coir, pasteurized
straw, composted manure, and hardwood sawdust. See
[[mushroom-growing-substrates|substrate selection]] for detailed guidance.

## Cultivation Methods

**PF Tek (Brown Rice Flour Method)**: The simplest approach for beginners.
Brown rice flour and vermiculite are mixed with water, loaded into half-pint
mason jars, and sterilized. After cooling, jars are inoculated with spore
syringes. Colonization occurs in 2 to 4 weeks, after which cakes are placed in
a humidity-controlled [[gottlieb-psilocybin-cultivation-fruiting-chamber-design]]. Yields are modest but the method is
highly reliable for learning.

**Monotub Method**: A popular intermediate technique. Colonized grain spawn is
mixed with a prepared bulk substrate (typically coco coir and vermiculite) in a
large plastic storage tub modified with holes for air exchange. This method
produces significantly higher yields than PF Tek with comparable simplicity.

**Straw Logs and Bags**: Advanced methods suitable for larger scale cultivation.
Pasteurized straw or supplemented sawdust is packed into tubes or bags,
inoculated with spawn, and hung or placed in a fruiting environment. Commonly
used for commercial mushroom farming.
