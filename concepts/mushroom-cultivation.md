# Mushroom Cultivation

Mushroom cultivation refers to the practice of growing fungi for food,
medicine, or research purposes. The cultivation of psilocybin-containing
mushrooms has developed from indigenous practices into sophisticated
techniques accessible to both researchers and home growers, driven by
advances in sterile technique and substrate preparation. The science of fungal
cultivation combines elements of microbiology, biochemistry, and agricultural
engineering.

## Biological Principles

Mushroom cultivation exploits the fungal life cycle, which proceeds through
several distinct phases. Spores germinate to form hyphae, thread-like
filaments that grow and branch to form a network called mycelium. When
environmental conditions are favorable, the mycelium consolidates and forms
fruiting bodies (mushrooms) that produce and disperse spores, completing the
cycle. Cultivation aims to optimize conditions at each stage: colonization
requires warmth, darkness, and sterility, while fruiting requires humidity,
fresh air exchange, and light.

Understanding the distinction between vegetative growth (mycelial
colonization) and reproductive growth (fruiting) is fundamental. The
environmental triggers that shift the fungus from one phase to the other
include changes in temperature, humidity, light exposure, and CO2
concentration. Mastering these transitions is the key to successful
cultivation.

## History

The deliberate cultivation of mushrooms dates back thousands of years in East
Asia, where species like shiitake (Lentinula edodes) were cultivated on logs.
Modern mushroom cultivation was advanced significantly by the work of Paul
Stamets, who developed and popularized many of the techniques used today. For
psilocybin mushrooms, the PF Tek (Psilocybe Fanaticus technique), developed
by Robert McPherson in the 1990s, revolutionized home cultivation by making
the process accessible with minimal equipment.

## Sterile Technique

Contamination by bacteria and competing molds is the primary challenge in
mushroom cultivation. Maintaining sterility throughout the process is
essential. Key principles include:

- Working in a clean environment, ideally a laminar flow hood, or at minimum
  a still air box (a container with arm holes that reduces air currents).
- Sterilizing all substrates, containers, and tools using pressure cooking
  (autoclaving) at 15 PSI for 90 minutes or more.
- Flame-sterilizing inoculation tools (needles, scalpels) before each use.
- Using proper aseptic technique: working quickly, minimizing container open
  time, and avoiding breathing directly on exposed materials.
- Maintaining good personal hygiene: washing hands, wearing gloves, and using
  a face mask to reduce contamination risk.

Common contaminants include Trichoderma (green mold), Aspergillus species,
and bacterial contamination (indicated by slimy textures or sour smells).
Early detection and removal of contaminated cultures prevents spread.

## Substrate Preparation

The substrate provides the nutrition and structural support for mycelial
growth. Different substrates are used at different stages of cultivation:

**Spawn substrates** support initial mycelial colonization. Common options
include brown rice flour and vermiculite (PF Tek), whole grains such as rye,
oats, or millet, and supplemented sawdust. Spawn substrates must be
thoroughly sterilized to eliminate competing organisms.

**Bulk substrates** provide additional nutrition and water-holding capacity
for larger fruiting. Common bulk substrates include coco coir, pasteurized
straw, composted manure, and hardwood sawdust. Bulk substrates are typically
pasteurized rather than sterilized, which kills pathogens while preserving
beneficial microorganisms that can help protect against contamination.

## Cultivation Methods

**PF Tek (Brown Rice Flour Method)**: The simplest approach for beginners.
Brown rice flour and vermiculite are mixed with water, loaded into half-pint
mason jars, and sterilized. After cooling, jars are inoculated with spore
syringes. Colonization occurs in 2 to 4 weeks, after which cakes are removed
from jars and placed in a humidity-controlled fruiting chamber.

**Monotub Method**: A popular intermediate technique. Colonized grain spawn
is mixed with a prepared bulk substrate (typically coco coir and vermiculite)
in a large plastic storage tub modified with holes for air exchange. This
method produces significantly higher yields than PF Tek with comparable
simplicity.

**Straw Logs and Bags**: Advanced methods suitable for larger scale
cultivation. Pasteurized straw or supplemented sawdust is packed into tubes
or bags, inoculated with spawn, and hung or placed in a fruiting
environment. These methods are commonly used for commercial mushroom farming.

## Fruiting Conditions

Triggering fruiting requires specific environmental cues:

- **High humidity** (85 to 95 percent relative humidity) to prevent drying of
  developing fruiting bodies and promote proper cap expansion.
- **Fresh air exchange** to provide oxygen and remove carbon dioxide. High CO2
  levels produce long, stringy stems and small caps, a condition known as
  "leggy" growth.
- **Light** in the blue spectrum (indirect sunlight or 6500K fluorescent)
  signals the mycelium to form mushrooms and helps orient growth upward.
- **Temperature drop** of a few degrees from colonization temperature can
  serve as an additional fruiting trigger.

## Common Challenges

Several common challenges face mushroom cultivators, particularly beginners:

- **Contamination**: The most frequent problem in cultivation. Green mold
  (Trichoderma) is the most common contaminant and can spread rapidly if
  not detected early. Prevention through strict sterile technique is far
  more effective than treatment.
- **Slow colonization**: May indicate suboptimal temperature, old or
  non-viable spores, insufficient moisture, or competing contamination.
- **Failure to fruit**: Usually caused by inadequate humidity, insufficient
  fresh air exchange, or insufficient temperature difference between
  colonization and fruiting phases.
- **Abnormal fruiting**: Long stems and small caps indicate high CO2,
  while cracked or split caps may indicate rapid humidity fluctuations.
