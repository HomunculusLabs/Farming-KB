# Decomposition Cycles

Decomposition cycles are the biogeochemical processes by which organic
matter is broken down into inorganic nutrients, releasing carbon dioxide
and mineral elements back into the environment for reuse by living
organisms. These cycles are driven primarily by fungi and bacteria, with
fungi playing the dominant role in terrestrial decomposition of complex
plant polymers. Decomposition is the engine of nutrient cycling, without
which life would quickly exhaust available resources and cease to function.

## The Decomposition Cascade

Decomposition proceeds through a cascade of increasingly recalcitrant
compounds. Fresh organic matter (litterfall, root exudates, animal
remains) contains labile compounds that are rapidly mineralized: simple
sugars, amino acids, and organic acids. These are consumed within days
to weeks by opportunistic microorganisms. As decomposition progresses,
microbes attack more complex compounds including hemicellulose, cellulose,
lignin, cutin, and suberin, each requiring specific enzyme systems and
progressively longer timeframes.

The rate of decomposition follows predictable patterns governed by
substrate quality, environmental conditions, and decomposer community
composition. The Carbon:Nitrogen (C:N) ratio is a key predictor of
decomposition rate: materials with low C:N ratios (fresh leaves, C:N
20-30) decompose rapidly because nitrogen is not limiting for decomposer
growth, while high C:N materials (conifer needles, C:N 60-80; wood,
C:N 400-1000) decompose slowly as microbes must scavenge nitrogen from
the environment.

## Lignin Degradation

Lignin is the second most abundant organic polymer on Earth (after
cellulose) and among the most resistant to biological degradation.
It is a complex, irregular polymer of phenylpropanoid units linked
by carbon-carbon and ether bonds that resist most enzymatic attack.
Only certain groups of fungi, primarily white rot basidiomycetes, can
efficiently mineralize lignin to carbon dioxide.

[[white-rot-fungi]] employ an oxidative strategy using extracellular
peroxidases and laccases that generate free radicals to non-specifically
attack lignin bonds. This nonspecific mechanism allows fungi to degrade
the irregular, heterogeneous lignin polymer, but at a significant
energetic cost. Lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP),
and versatile peroxidase (VP) are the key enzymes, all requiring
hydrogen peroxide generated by fungal oxidases.

Brown rot fungi use a fundamentally different approach: Fenton chemistry.
They generate hydroxyl radicals (OH*) from hydrogen peroxide and ferric
iron, which non-enzymatically depolymerize cellulose. Brown rot fungi
modify lignin through demethylation but do not fully mineralize it,
leaving a lignin-rich residue.

## Carbon Cycling

Fungal decomposition is a critical mediator of the global carbon cycle.
Terrestrial ecosystems store approximately 2,500 gigatons of carbon in
soil organic matter, roughly three times the amount in the atmosphere.
The balance between carbon inputs (plant production) and outputs
(decomposition, respiration) determines whether ecosystems function as
carbon sinks or sources.

Fungi contribute to both carbon stabilization and release. Through the
production of extracellular enzymes and hyphal turnover, they transform
plant litter into soil organic matter, some of which becomes stabilized
through physical protection within soil aggregates or chemical
association with mineral surfaces. Mycorrhizal fungi produce glomalin,
a glycoprotein that persists in soil for decades and contributes to
stable carbon pools. Simultaneously, fungal respiration returns carbon
dioxide to the atmosphere, with soil respiration (dominated by fungal
and root respiration) estimated at 50-60 gigatons of carbon per year.

## Nitrogen Cycling

Fungi play central roles in nitrogen cycling through several mechanisms.
Saprotrophic fungi mineralize organic nitrogen by producing proteases,
chitinases, and nucleases that release ammonium from proteins, chitin
(from fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons), and nucleic acids.
This ammonium is available for plant uptake or further microbial
processing.

Fungi compete with bacteria for inorganic nitrogen, often winning in
acidic, carbon-rich soils where their hyphal exploration strategy
provides a competitive advantage. Ectomycorrhizal fungi are particularly
important nitrogen mineralizers in boreal forests, where they access
nitrogen from organic matter through protease production and transfer it
to host trees. Some fungi (nitrogen-fixing bacteria aside) are among the
few organisms that can mobilize nitrogen from recalcitrant organic
compounds like polyphenol-protein complexes.

## Humus Formation and Soil Organic Matter

Humus is the relatively stable, dark-colored organic matter fraction of
soil that results from the incomplete decomposition of plant and animal
residues. Fungal decomposition contributes to humus formation through
the selective preservation of recalcitrant compounds (modified lignin,
melanin, cutin) and the synthesis of new compounds through the
condensation of microbial metabolites and degradation products.

Two major theories explain humus formation: the "selective preservation"
model, which holds that humus consists largely of plant compounds
resistant to decomposition, and the "microbial condensation" model,
which holds that humus is primarily synthesized from microbial
metabolites. Current evidence suggests both processes contribute, with
fungal metabolites (particularly melanins and phenolic compounds) playing
a disproportionate role due to their chemical recalcitrance.

## Decomposition in Different Ecosystems

Decomposition rates vary enormously across ecosystems. Tropical
rainforests decompose litter rapidly (weeks to months) due to warm,
moist conditions and diverse decomposer communities. Temperate forests
show intermediate rates (1-5 years for leaf litter). Boreal forests and
tundra decompose slowly (decades for needles, centuries for wood) due to
cold temperatures and short growing seasons. In peatlands, waterlogging
creates anaerobic conditions that slow decomposition dramatically,
leading to massive carbon accumulation over millennia.
