## Overview
The urea cycle is the primary pathway by which mammals convert toxic ammonia into urea
for safe urinary excretion.
It is also called the ornithine cycle because ornithine is regenerated rather than
consumed.
The pathway runs mainly in periportal hepatocytes of the liver, with a smaller
contribution from kidney and intestinal nitrogen handling.
Its importance becomes obvious when the cycle fails: ammonia rises quickly and the brain
is one of the first organs injured.

## Nitrogen Disposal Problem
Amino beta oxidation fatty removes amino groups that cannot be stored safely as free ammonia.
Ammonia is small, diffusible, and chemically reactive enough to disturb cellular pH,
neurotransmission, and mitochondrial metabolism.
Aquatic organisms can often excrete ammonia directly, but terrestrial vertebrates need a
less toxic, water-compatible nitrogen waste.
Urea solves that problem by packaging two nitrogen atoms and one carbonyl carbon into a
neutral, soluble molecule.

## Cellular Location
The pathway is split between mitochondria and cytosol.
Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and ornithine transcarbamylase act in the mitochondrial
matrix.
Citrulline leaves the mitochondrion and the remaining synthetic steps occur in the
cytosol.
The spatial split coordinates nitrogen disposal with [[fungal-respiration-carbon-flux]] through the malate-aspartate shuttle and the
[[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]] activity and neurotransmitter balance.
Clinical signs can include vomiting, lethargy, tachypnea, seizures, confusion,
psychiatric changes, coma, and irreversible neurologic injury.

## Urea Cycle Disorders
Inherited defects include deficiencies of N-acetylglutamate synthase, CPS1, OTC, ASS1,
ASL, ARG1, and mitochondrial ornithine transport.
Neonatal presentations can occur after protein feeding begins and may be mistaken for
sepsis or nonspecific encephalopathy.
Partial defects may remain silent until infection, fasting, high-protein intake,
pregnancy, surgery, valproate exposure, or other stressors raise nitrogen load.
Diagnosis uses plasma ammonia, [[cho-fish-amino-acid-preparation]] profiles, urine orotic acid, genetic testing,
and urgent metabolic consultation.

## Therapeutic Logic
Acute management aims to stop protein catabolism, provide calories, remove ammonia, and
supply missing pathway intermediates when appropriate.
Nitrogen-scavenging drugs such as sodium benzoate or phenylbutyrate create alternative
routes for waste nitrogen excretion.
Arginine or citrulline can bypass specific blocks, but the correct supplement depends on
which enzyme is defective.
Severe hyperammonemia may require dialysis because neurologic injury can progress faster
than medical scavenging can lower ammonia.

## Related Concepts
The cycle is closely connected to [[fish-amino-acid]] metabolism, [[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]].
It also links clinical toxicology with [[glutathione-biochemistry-and-redox-biology]]
because both systems protect cells from chemically reactive nitrogen or sulfur stress.
Understanding the urea cycle clarifies why liver failure, enzyme defects, and catabolic
states can become neurologic emergencies.
