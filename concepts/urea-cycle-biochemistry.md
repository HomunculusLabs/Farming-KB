## Overview
The urea cycle is the primary pathway by which mammals convert toxic ammonia into urea
for safe urinary excretion.
It is also called the ornithine cycle because ornithine is regenerated rather than
consumed.
The pathway runs mainly in periportal hepatocytes of the liver, with a smaller
contribution from kidney and intestinal nitrogen handling.
Its importance becomes obvious when the cycle fails: ammonia rises quickly and the brain
is one of the first organs injured.

## Nitrogen Disposal Problem
Amino beta oxidation fatty removes amino groups that cannot be stored safely as free ammonia.
Ammonia is small, diffusible, and chemically reactive enough to disturb cellular pH,
neurotransmission, and mitochondrial metabolism.
Aquatic organisms can often excrete ammonia directly, but terrestrial vertebrates need a
less toxic, water-compatible nitrogen waste.
Urea solves that problem by packaging two nitrogen atoms and one carbonyl carbon into a
neutral, soluble molecule.

## Cellular Location
The pathway is split between mitochondria and cytosol.
Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and ornithine transcarbamylase act in the mitochondrial
matrix.
Citrulline leaves the mitochondrion and the remaining synthetic steps occur in the
cytosol.
The spatial split coordinates nitrogen disposal with [[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]], amino acid transamination, and mitochondrial energy state.

## Entry Through Carbamoyl Phosphate
The first committed reaction combines free ammonia, bicarbonate, and two ATP molecules
to make carbamoyl phosphate.
Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I catalyzes this step and requires N-acetylglutamate as
an essential allosteric activator.
N-acetylglutamate is synthesized from glutamate and acetyl-CoA, and arginine stimulates
its formation as a feed-forward signal.
This design means abundant amino acid catabolism increases both substrate and activation
for ammonia disposal.

## Ornithine to Citrulline
Ornithine transcarbamylase transfers the carbamoyl group to ornithine, producing
citrulline in the mitochondrion.
Citrulline is exported to the cytosol by a transporter that exchanges it with incoming
ornithine.
Although ornithine resembles an amino acid, it is not incorporated into proteins and
functions here as a recyclable carrier.
Failure of ornithine transcarbamylase is the most common urea cycle disorder and is
inherited in an X-linked pattern.

## Aspartate Provides the Second Nitrogen
Citrulline condenses with aspartate to form argininosuccinate.
Argininosuccinate synthetase uses ATP, but the energetic cost is high because ATP is
cleaved to AMP and pyrophosphate.
Aspartate supplies the second nitrogen atom of urea and is produced by transamination of
oxaloacetate.
This links nitrogen disposal to [[fungal-respiration-carbon-flux]] through the malate-aspartate shuttle and the
[[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]].

## Argininosuccinate Cleavage
Argininosuccinate lyase splits argininosuccinate into arginine and fumarate.
Fumarate can enter cytosolic reactions that regenerate malate, oxaloacetate, and
aspartate.
This connection is sometimes called the aspartate-argininosuccinate shunt.
It lets the urea cycle borrow carbon skeletons from central metabolism without losing
them permanently.

## Arginase and Urea Release
Arginase hydrolyzes arginine to urea and ornithine.
The urea diffuses into blood and is eliminated by the kidney, while ornithine returns to
mitochondria to begin another turn.
The reaction completes the cycle and also explains why arginine status matters in
nitrogen balance.
In some contexts arginine is semi-essential because growth, injury, or enzyme defects
increase demand beyond endogenous production.

## Energy Accounting
One turn consumes three ATP molecules but four high-energy phosphate bonds.
Two ATP are used by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, and one ATP is converted to AMP
during argininosuccinate synthesis.
The energetic expense is justified because ammonia toxicity is severe and because urea
is a safe export form.
Fumarate recovery through central metabolism partially offsets the cost by preserving
carbon and reducing equivalents.

## Regulation
The most important short-term control is N-acetylglutamate activation of carbamoyl
phosphate synthetase I.
Arginine increases N-acetylglutamate synthesis, so a high amino acid load stimulates the
pathway before ammonia accumulates dangerously.
Longer-term regulation changes enzyme expression during high-protein feeding, fasting,
catabolic illness, or glucocorticoid signaling.
Substrate supply also matters: ammonia, bicarbonate, ornithine, citrulline, aspartate,
and mitochondrial transport all constrain flux.

## Hyperammonemia
When the cycle is impaired, ammonia accumulates in blood and crosses into the brain.
Astrocytes detoxify ammonia by making glutamine from glutamate, but excess glutamine
draws water and contributes to cerebral edema.
Ammonia also drains alpha-ketoglutarate and glutamate pools, disturbing [[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]] activity and neurotransmitter balance.
Clinical signs can include vomiting, lethargy, tachypnea, seizures, confusion,
psychiatric changes, coma, and irreversible neurologic injury.

## Urea Cycle Disorders
Inherited defects include deficiencies of N-acetylglutamate synthase, CPS1, OTC, ASS1,
ASL, ARG1, and mitochondrial ornithine transport.
Neonatal presentations can occur after protein feeding begins and may be mistaken for
sepsis or nonspecific encephalopathy.
Partial defects may remain silent until infection, fasting, high-protein intake,
pregnancy, surgery, valproate exposure, or other stressors raise nitrogen load.
Diagnosis uses plasma ammonia, amino acid profiles, urine orotic acid, genetic testing,
and urgent metabolic consultation.

## Therapeutic Logic
Acute management aims to stop protein catabolism, provide calories, remove ammonia, and
supply missing pathway intermediates when appropriate.
Nitrogen-scavenging drugs such as sodium benzoate or phenylbutyrate create alternative
routes for waste nitrogen excretion.
Arginine or citrulline can bypass specific blocks, but the correct supplement depends on
which enzyme is defective.
Severe hyperammonemia may require dialysis because neurologic injury can progress faster
than medical scavenging can lower ammonia.

## Related Concepts
The cycle is closely connected to amino acid metabolism, [[gluconeogenesis-biochemistry-metabolic-pathway]], and [[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]].
It also links clinical toxicology with [[glutathione-biochemistry-and-redox-biology]]
because both systems protect cells from chemically reactive nitrogen or sulfur stress.
Understanding the urea cycle clarifies why liver failure, enzyme defects, and catabolic
states can become neurologic emergencies.
