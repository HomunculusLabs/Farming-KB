# Fungal Culture Preservation

Maintaining and preserving fungal cultures is essential for systematics,
[[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]], and the long-term
availability of genetic resources. Because fungi are enormously diverse, no
single preservation method works for all taxa. Methods range from simple
short-term maintenance to permanent cryopreservation.

## Short-Term Preservation

Short-term methods maintain cultures for up to approximately one year and
suit small collections with cultures in active use:

- **Serial Transfer:** The simplest approach. Inoculum is transferred
  periodically to fresh agar slants or Petri dishes stored at room
  temperature or 4 degrees Celsius. Although inexpensive, serial transfer
  risks contamination, desiccation, and genetic drift. Sporulation ability
  or pathogenicity may be lost. Alternating nutrient-rich and nutrient-poor
  media helps maintain healthy cultures.

- **Standard Freezing:** Most filamentous fungi, zygomycetes, and yeasts
  survive at -20 to -80 degrees Celsius. The failure rate for mitosporic
  ascomycetes after 5 years at -20 degrees Celsius was 5.1%, and only 2.3%
  at -70 degrees Celsius over 6 months to 13 years. Vigorously growing
  cultures survive better. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles must be avoided.

## Intermediate-Term Methods

These low-cost methods extend viability beyond one year but are not
considered permanent. Maximum storage is generally 10 years or less:

- **Oil Overlay:** Sterilized mineral oil covers agar slants to about 10 mm.
  Cultures survive several years, exceptionally up to 32 years at 15 to 20
  degrees Celsius. Suited for nonsporulating cultures not amenable to
  freezing. Oil also reduces mite infestations. Disadvantages include slow
  continued growth and potential mutant selection.

- **Distilled Water Immersion:** Agar slants are covered with sterile
  distilled water. The water suppresses morphological changes. This method
  preserves oomycetes, basidiomycetes, ascomycetes, hyphomycetes, and
  yeasts. Ascomycetes survived up to 10 years at 20 degrees Celsius.

- **Organic Substrata:** [[fukuoka-textdoc-sawdust-wood-chips-orchard-soil-acidification-experiment]], cereal grains, straw, or filter paper
  serve as preservation substrata. Wood chips preserve wood-inhabiting fungi
  for up to 10 years at 4 degrees Celsius. Cereal grains preserve
  *Sclerotinia*, *Magnaporthe*, and *Rhizoctonia* for up to 10 years.

- **Soil or Sand:** Sterile dry soil preserves fungi with resistant
  propagules such as *Rhizoctonia* and *Septoria*. Bottles are stored at
  4 degrees Celsius.

- **[[aminopropyl-silica-gel-chromatography-indole-alkaloid-purification]]:** Preserves sporulating fungi when specialized facilities
  are unavailable. Spores protected by skim milk remain viable for 4 to 5
  years; dictyostelid spores survived up to 11 years. Silica gel prevents
  all [[brassinosteroid-fungal-growth-promotion]] and metabolism. The [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] Stock Center has
  used this technique since 1962.

- **Sclerotization:** Fungi producing sclerotia or microsclerotia can be
  preserved by storing these structures at 3 to 5 degrees Celsius for 2 to
  5 years.

## Long-Term (Permanent) Preservation

Permanent methods arrest metabolism while retaining viability and are
recommended for type specimens and critically important strains:

- **Liquid Nitrogen Cryopreservation:** Storage at -150 to -180 degrees
  Celsius in the vapor phase preserves virtually all fungi, including those
  that cannot be lyophilized. At or below -139 degrees Celsius, ice crystals
  do not grow and biophysical processes are too slow to damage cells.
  Cryoprotectants are essential: penetrating agents (10% glycerol or 5%
  DMSO) protect intracellularly; nonpenetrating agents (sucrose, mannitol,
  dextran, hydroxyethyl starch) protect extracellularly. Cultures are cooled
  programmatically (1 degree per minute from 4 to -40 degrees, then 10
  degrees per minute to -90 degrees) before transfer. Advantages include
  prevention of genetic drift and reduced labor. Disadvantages include high
  equipment costs and constant liquid nitrogen replenishment.

- **Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying):** A lower-cost permanent method for
  species producing numerous small propagules (10 micrometers or less).
  Spores are suspended in a sterile menstruum (5 to 10% skim milk or bovine
  serum), frozen, and dried under vacuum. Dictyostelid spores remained
  viable for up to 30 years. Larger spores collapse during processing.
  Mycelial isolates of basidiomycetes can be lyophilized with trehalose.

## Herbarium Specimens and Voucher Collections

Dried specimens serve as physical vouchers for biodiversity studies. Most
fleshy fungi can be air-dried, desiccated using silica gel, or freeze-dried.
Freeze-dried specimens maintain shape and color better. After 5 to 6 years,
cells of freeze-dried specimens may still be viable. Specimens are stored
in biodiversity-fungi-molecular-methods. DNA banking complements living collections by preserving
genetic material from organisms that cannot be cultured, such as obligate
biotrophs.

## Record Keeping

Each strain should be documented with: strain number, species name, taxonomic
authority, growth conditions, isolation date and locale, substrate or host,
isolation history, preservation method, and viability test results.
Electronic databases are now standard, with backups stored separately.
For small collections, index card files remain an effective low-cost option.
Database fields should also include provenance (chain of custody between
collections), reidentification history, and subjective synonyms.
Viability of preserved cultures should be checked periodically and the
results recorded alongside preservation dates and replicate vial counts.
