Mycelium is the vegetative body of fungi, composed of threadlike
hyphae that form vast, interconnected networks beneath the soil
surface. These networks function as biological communication
systems, processing information about the environment and
coordinating responses across the fungal organism. Paul Stamets
describes mycelium as "the neurological network of nature,"
drawing explicit parallels between fungal networks and the
neural architecture of animal brains.

## Evolutionary History of Fungal Networks

Fungi share a more recent common ancestry with animals than with
plants, diverging approximately 650 million years ago. Both
kingdoms belong to the superkingdom Opisthokonta. Fungi evolved
the strategy of external digestion by secreting acids and
enzymes into their environment, then absorbing nutrients through
netlike cell chains. This approach allowed fungi to colonize land
more than a billion years ago.

Fossil evidence from the Rhynie Chert in Scotland reveals that
early plants formed [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] associations at least
400 million years ago. The organism Prototaxites, dating from
the late Silurian through early Devonian periods (approximately
420 million years ago), was a giant fungus that stood as the
tallest organism on land during its era. Fungi surged after two
major extinction events: the Permian-Triassic boundary (250
million years ago) and the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary (65
million years ago), recycling post-cataclysmic debris and
rebuilding ecosystems.

## Information Processing in Mycelial Networks

Mycelium stays in constant molecular communication with its
environment, devising diverse enzymatic and chemical responses
to complex challenges. These networks can expand to thousands of
acres, achieving the greatest mass of any individual organism on
the planet. The Armillaria ostoyae specimen in Oregon covers
over 2,385 acres, making it one of the largest organisms by
area ever documented.

Stamets proposes that mycelial networks operate at a level of
complexity exceeding the computational power of advanced
supercomputers. The [[mycelium-as-network-organism]] functions
as a "digestive cellular membrane, a fusion between a stomach
and a brain, a nutritional and informational sharing network."
When entering a forest after rainfall, Stamets suggests these
interlacing mycelial membranes "awaken," acting as a collective
fungal consciousness that emits attractants and connects
ecosystems through scent trails.

## Sensing and Environmental Response

Mycelial networks are exquisitely sensitive to environmental
changes. Hikers, deer, and insects walking across these
filamentous nets leave impressions that mycelia can sense and
respond to. The network architecture shares striking
similarities with both neural networks and the Internet, using
branching patterns that optimize information distribution and
resource allocation.

These [[mushroom-spore-dispersal-ecological-networks]] demonstrate several forms
of apparent intelligence. Mycelium steers the course of
ecosystems by favoring successions of species, preparing its
immediate environment to fuel its food chains. The network can
adapt and evolve through changing environmental forces,
demonstrating a form of ecological awareness that operates
without centralized control.

## Chemical Communication Mechanisms

Mycelium communicates through chemical signaling pathways.
Oxalic acid, secreted by many fungal species, combines with
calcium and other minerals to form oxalates, effectively
mineralizing rock and creating dynamic soils that support diverse
populations of organisms. This chemical processing represents a
form of environmental engineering that modifies habitats to suit
fungal needs.

The mycelial network also produces natural antibiotics that
preselect beneficial microbes while defending against pathogens.
A synergistic blend of microbes joining with mushroom mycelium
provides a defensive shield, "much like fortifying an immune
system," that not only prevents competition but streams nutrients
and mycelial stimulants throughout the network.

## Ecological Significance

Mycelial networks play critical roles in ecosystem health and
function. They decompose organic matter, cycle nutrients, form
symbiotic relationships with plants through [[mycorrhizal-networks]], and serve as foundation species in most
terrestrial ecosystems. The Gaia hypothesis, proposed by James
Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, suggests the biosphere intelligently
pilots its course to sustain life. Stamets sees mycelium as the
living network that manifests this natural intelligence, acting
as an "exposed sentient membrane, aware and responsive to changes
in its environment."

## Implications for Understanding Intelligence

The concept of mycelial network intelligence challenges
conventional definitions of cognition. While fungi lack nervous
systems, brains, or centralized processing centers, their
decentralized networks demonstrate information processing,
environmental sensing, resource optimization, and adaptive
behavior. Understanding [[plant-intelligence-and-communication]]
alongside fungal intelligence may require expanding our
definition of cognition beyond animal models. Research into
[[mycorrhizal-network-communication]] continues to reveal the
sophistication of these ancient biological networks.
