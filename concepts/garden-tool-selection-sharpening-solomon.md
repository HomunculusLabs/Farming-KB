# Garden Tool Selection and Sharpening

## Overview

Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* dedicates significant
attention to the proper [[solomon-garden-tools-selection-and-maintenance]] of hand gardening
tools. His core argument: most gardeners struggle unnecessarily with
digging and weeding because they use dull, poor-quality tools. A sharp
tool transforms hard manual labor into manageable work, while a proper
shovel eliminates the need for a power tiller entirely.

## The Three Essential Tools

To handle a garden up to a [[fukuoka-agriculture-tomorrow-quarter-acre-vision]] (1,000 square meters), Solomon
recommends only three essential tools:

1. **A new combination shovel** (also called a combination spade)
2. **A bow rake**
3. **A 10-inch (25 cm) bastard file** for sharpening

With these three items and a fit body, a gardener can prepare beds, weed,
and perform all necessary tasks without power equipment.

## Why Store-Bought Tools Feel Terrible

When you bring tools home from the store, they are never sharp. The
cutting edge is squared off rather than honed into an acute bevel like
a chisel's edge. Most gardeners never properly sharpen their tools after
buying them. Solomon observed this firsthand teaching annual spring
gardening courses — sharpening students' tools is consistently the
second-most-popular part of his class.

A dull shovel makes digging feel like the most exhausting activity a
person ever tried. A dull hoe leaves the gardener dripping sweat after
two minutes. The problem is not the gardener's strength — it is the
tool's edge.

## Combination Shovel vs Spade

Solomon strongly prefers the combination shovel over a dedicated spade:

- **Combination shovel**: Functions as both a shovel and a spade in one
  tool. The blade is narrow enough to push into uncompacted earth
  without excessive effort, yet broad enough to move soil efficiently.
- **Spade**: Has a flat, narrow blade excellent for cutting straight
  down into the earth and loosening compacted soil. However, it is
  less efficient for moving soil from place to place.
- **Round-point shovel**: Designed to scoop loose material (sand, compost).
  Not suitable for cutting into compacted earth.
- **Short-handled spading fork**: Solomon considers these "an instrument
  of torture" — tines bend under the force needed to work heavy
  ground, and they cannot flip soil over effectively.

The combination shovel's rolled-over blade top allows the gardener to
press hard with their foot without pain — a critical feature for
serious digging work.

## Selecting Quality Tools

Solomon offers these criteria for [[hamilton-choosing-garden-tools]] tools:

- **Buy from landscaper suppliers or nursery supply catalogs**, not
  discount stores. Tradespeople who use tools all day cannot afford
  to break one every other day.
- **Handle attachment**: Must be solid and secure
- **Weight and balance**: Should feel light yet strong
- **Steel quality**: The slower a file cuts the metal, the better the
  steel. Soft metal that files quickly will dull rapidly.
- **Blade top**: On shovels/spades, should be rolled over to cushion foot
- **Professional [[emcdda-brand-names-user-terms-smartshop-marketing]]** are the most reliable quality indicator

Tool steel involves inherent compromise: harder steel holds an edge
longer but is more brittle. Garden implements need intermediate-grade
steel — hard enough to hold an edge but tough enough to survive contact
with rocks and roots.

## Sharpening Technique

### The File

A 10-inch bastard file is the essential sharpening tool for both shovels
and hoes. Coarser files remove metal faster for initial shaping; finer
files refine the edge.

### Sharpening a Shovel

1. Place the shovel on the edge of a porch or flat on the ground
2. Put one foot on the handle just behind where it sockets into the
   blade to hold it steady
3. Hold the file at approximately 15° to the blade surface
4. Maintain that angle firmly — do not let it change during strokes
5. For curved edges, start at the center and work outward to one side,
   then alternate to the other
6. After several hundred strokes, a chisel-like bevel will form
7. For maximum sharpness, ensure uniform bevel angle along the edge

It is not necessary to bevel the full width of the blade — only the
central four inches (10 cm) on each side does the actual cutting. During
filing, a thin metal "wire" forms on the back side of the edge. Remove
this wire edge by lightly filing the flat side or rubbing with a
whetstone. A wire edge makes the tool feel sharper than it actually is
and will fold over quickly in use.

## Ongoing Maintenance

- Sharpen tools at the start of each [[winter-gardening-season-extension]]
- Touch up the edge periodically during heavy use
- Clean and store tools dry after use to prevent rust
- A well-maintained quality tool lasts decades; cheap tools will not
  hold an edge and need frequent replacement

## Handle Length

Solomon strongly recommends long-handled tools over short-handled ones:

- **Short-handled spades and forks** are for people under five feet tall,
  children, or emergency use
- **Flipping soil** with a short handle is exhausting and brutal on the
  back
- **Long-handled tools** let you use body weight and leg muscles rather
  than arm and back strength alone
- Short handles are only justified in very confined spaces

## Hoe Sharpening

The same principles apply to hoes as to shovels:

- A sharp hoe slices through weeds at or just below the soil surface
  with minimal effort
- A dull hoe tears at weeds, often leaving roots intact to regrow
- Sharpen only the inside (concave) edge of the hoe blade
- Maintain the same 15° bevel angle used for shovels
- A sharp hoe can replace hours of hand-pulling weeds with minutes of
  efficient chopping

Solomon notes that after sharpening their hoes in class, students are
amazed at how little effort is required to clear a weed-infested bed.
