## Sheet Composting Technique

[[sheet-composting]] is the practice of fertilizing by spreading a layer of nutrient-rich, decomposable organic matter atop the soil and then shallowly digging it in. Materials decompose much faster when mixed into well-oxygenated soil than when merely spread on the surface. This method will grow a good vegetable garden — if done far enough in advance for decomposition to complete before planting time.

## The Microbial Bloom Problem

When undecomposed organic matter is mixed into soil, it creates a microbial bloom. Microorganisms multiply incredibly rapidly on this new food supply, gobbling up almost all the available mineral nutrients in the surrounding soil — nutrients that plants would otherwise use to grow.

### The Nutrient Lock-Up

The nutrients contained in the organic matter are not yet soluble — they are potential, not available. But the organic matter creates such a demand for nutrients from the decomposing microbes that plants growing there are deprived. Strange as it sounds: you sheet-compost to fertilize, but for a time the soil becomes LESS fertile.

### The Recovery

Only when the new food supply has been almost entirely consumed does the decomposer population begin to die for lack of fuel. At this point, plant nutrients held in bacterial bodies are released back into the soil. Some become simple water-soluble chemicals (like commercial fertilizer), while others become complex organic chelates that nourish plants much as vitamins nourish humans.

## Timing

### Autumn Sheet Composting

In temperate climates with freezing winters:
- Decomposition goes slowly in autumn and doesn't happen at all in winter
- By the time the land has warmed up in spring and heat-loving crops go in, the process will almost certainly have stayed incomplete
- Unless the material has an extremely low C/N, avoid autumnal sheet [[composting]] on beds for early spring crops
- Chill-tolerant spring crops may not grow well because decomposition is still ongoing

### Spring and Summer Sheet Composting

- Takes about six weeks for decomposition to proceed to the point of benefiting from all added nutrients
- That's six weeks of prime growing season lost
- Only works if the average C/N was no higher than 30:1 and the layer was no more than an inch (2.5 cm) thick
- Where soil doesn't freeze severely, sheet composting goes on much more rapidly

### The Manure Timing Rule

Fresh manure loses nutritional value rapidly when exposed to air and sun:
- Manure spread on a cool, windless day retains values for several days
- The same manure spread on 98°F ground in sunshine on a windy day loses 25% of nutritional values by sundown
- **Always incorporate manure into soil as quickly as possible after spreading**

## Cautions

### Lime Requirements

Gardeners in high-rainfall regions who depend on sheet composting as a means of soil improvement should spread lime along with the organic material. Spread about 50 pounds (25 kg) of ordinary agricultural lime or dolomitic lime per 1,000 square feet (100 square meters). Don't exceed that rate every year. Add lime every time you sheet-compost, unless the manure is from your own animals (it may already contain lime).

### Calcium and Magnesium

These minerals are just as vital to the formation of insect protein as they are to making plant protein. Gardens lacking calcium and magnesium may suffer pest infestations after sheet composting, because the added organic matter feeds insect populations more than it feeds plants.

### Avoid High C/N Materials

Don't sheet-compost with woody grasses of late summer on a brand-new garden. These materials have a high C/N and will tie up nitrogen for an extended period. This is a potential catastrophe — nothing will grow well until midsummer.

### Poultry and Rabbit Manure

These strong, low C/N materials are best used as fertilizers rather than sheet compost. Plants will grow better immediately after low C/N materials are mixed into soil — if you don't mix in too much. However, if you are sheet-composting leaves and dried vegetation, including a thin layer of poultry or rabbit manure can lower the average C/N and speed the process considerably.

## How Much to Apply

The amount of organic matter you can successfully sheet-compost depends on:

1. **The thickness of the layer** — never more than about 1 inch (2.5 cm) thick
2. **The average C/N of the material** — lower is faster
3. **The preexisting fertility of the soil** — fertile soils can handle more
4. **The amount of time before planting** — more time allows more material

## Risk Assessment

Sheet composting is risky. Before incorporating organic matter into soil, it is safer and the outcome more certain if you decompose it first so its C/N is lowered until it approaches 12:1. This is done with heap composting.

**The rule**: If you can afford to take the land out of production for six weeks and your material has a reasonable C/N (under 30:1), sheet composting can work. If you need the land sooner or your materials are high C/N, heap compost first.
