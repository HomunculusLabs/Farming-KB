## [[mycoremediation-fungal-bioremediation|Mycoremediation]] Fundamentals

Mycoremediation — from *myco* (fungus) and *remediation* (to clean, resolve, or correct) — is the use of fungi, specifically mushrooms, to create simple yet effective biomass capable of breaking down environmental and industrial pollutants. Mycelium acts as a self-healing filter that targets specific [[plant-volatile-communication-chemical-ecology]] pollutants in soil, water, and air.

## How Mycoremediation Works

### The Enzymatic Factory Model

Fungi are decomposers and constructors — agents of habitat renewal. With their enzyme systems secreting into the environment, fungi swim through their own fluids, breaking down materials into smaller molecular units that they absorb through their cell walls as food. This process requires the fungi to interact with their environment for a period called the **factor time** for the chemical reactions of mycoremediation to take place.

### Two Forms of Pollution Addressed

#### Microbial Contaminants

The main mode of pathogen removal is **inactivation** through:
- Disrupting replication or reproduction
- Preventing cell synthesis and division
- Eroding cell membranes
- **Species exclusion** — gobbling up territory in advance to prevent pathogenic entry
- Altering chemical gradients (pH, nutrient availability)

Microbial mycoremediation is especially useful against fecal coliforms in water, manures, livestock waste, and failing septic systems.

#### Chemical Contaminants

Fungi secrete [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] to cleave chemical compound molecules into smaller, more degradable units. This **species sequence approach** allows other organisms to further degrade the products. However, smaller molecules are not necessarily less dangerous — testing is always critical to confirm contamination removal. Targets include herbicides, pesticides, fertilizers, dyes, and many other chemical residues.

### Hyperaccumulation

Many fungi absorb and retain specific elements — particularly **heavy metals** — in their biomass through a process called hyperaccumulation. This renders the mushrooms themselves toxic, but the biomass can filter or treat soil, water, or air and is then removed and disposed of or concentrated for reuse.

## Brown Rot vs. White Rot Fungi

Fungi can be categorized by their primary decomposition mode:

| Type | Primary Target | Secondary Capability |
|------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Brown rot** | Cellulose (more aggressive) | — |
| **White rot** | Lignin and lignin-like polymers | Chlorinated compounds found in pesticides |

### Key Enzyme Systems

All fungi produce different spectra of "chemical keys":

- **Cellulases** — break down cellulose
- **Ligninases** — break down lignin
- **Peroxidases** — oxidative degradation
- **Laccases** — oxidative degradation of phenolic compounds

### The Extracellular Advantage

Fungal enzymes are **cell-free** (extracellular) chemical catalysts. They are secreted into the environment and begin metabolic work **ahead of the mycelium** and even ahead of advancing hyphae. Fungi begin digesting their food before physical contact. Bacteria, by contrast, must come into intimate contact with a compound to begin degradation.

### The Cascading Food Chain

While fungi break down complex compounds into simpler ones, metabolites they don't digest become available to associated microorganisms (bacteria) that degrade them further. The end goal is recycling elements back into a self-perpetuating food chain. Fungi may play a regulatory role in the balance of soil or water microorganism populations.

## Screening Fungi for Remediation Potential

Simple laboratory plating techniques can screen fungi for remediation capability:

1. Position a wedge of mushroom mycelium ~1 inch from a target bacterium or chemical on an agar plate
2. Observe behavior over hours to days
3. Look for **zones of inhibition** — areas where one organism prevents another's growth
4. Note which organism is dominant or submissive
5. Test whether the fungus can adapt its enzyme production to attack previously unencountered compounds

Oyster mushroom (*[[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]*) mycelium, for example, initially stops linear growth when encountering the herbicide atrazine at three times the labeled application rate, but within 8 days shifts its [[oner-cannabis-phenotypes-genetic-expression]] to rapidly disassemble the compound.

## Simple Small-Scale Mycoremediation Projects

### Erosion Control and Water Filtration

Myceliated wood chips or wheat straw bales can:
- Improve soil water-holding capacity
- Reduce erosion from rainfall or water drainage on slopes
- Act as fungal filters that trap and remediate fecal coliforms before they reach local watersheds

Place barriers of myceliated wood chips at the lowest portion of property to capture runoff from pet waste areas.

### Cat Waste Remediation

With 86.4 million domestic cats in the United States, cat litter represents a significant waste stream.

**Best litter types**: Compressed newspaper, wheat gluten, or finely powdered corncob debris (wheat and corn clump best). Pine-based litters are generally incompatible with most cultivated fungi, though *[[neolentinus-lepideus]]* isolates suitable for pine are in development.

**Best fungal species**: King stropharia and oyster mushrooms

**Process**:
1. Scoop soiled litter into a lidded bucket
2. Periodically cover with clean, wet biodegradable litter inoculated with fresh spawn
3. Mycelium threads through the substrate, decomposing and deodorizing in ~12 weeks
4. Run the myceliated litter through a **vermicomposting** cycle
5. Do not mix into garden compost until thoroughly decomposed by worms

**Alternative**: Use [[spent-mushroom-substrate]] from primary decomposers (like oyster mushrooms) as litter. Dry and shred to make fluffy bedding. When changing litter, dump into a bucket, place outdoors to reduce ammonia, rehydrate with warm water, and the mycelium will reawaken to consume the waste.

### Dog Waste Remediation

With 74.3 million dogs in the United States producing significant waste that often pollutes landfills and waterways:

**Process**:
1. Designate a 5-gallon lidded bucket for collection
2. Periodically add spawned substrate (moistened hardwood sawdust, wood chips, spent coffee grounds, or wet newspaper with spawn)
3. Occasionally tamp by lifting and dropping the bucket from a few inches high to increase density and contact points
4. Allow 2–3 weeks for full colonization — the result is a firm block of mycelium smelling sweet rather than putrid
5. Run multiple buckets in rotation
6. Invert finished buckets to release the biomass into a worm composting bin in a shady location
7. The result: rich topsoil and worm castings for garden use

### Chicken Coop Fungal Filters

Urban [[chicken-keeping]] is growing, but odor control and coliform bacteria from poultry droppings present challenges. **King stropharia** (*Stropharia rugoso-annulata*) mycelium provides an effective living filter.

**Benefits**:
- Sweet-smelling mycelium eliminates coop odors
- Consumes excess nitrates and phosphates from poultry waste
- Attracts earthworms and collaborative organisms
- Produces rich, sweet-smelling soil amendment after ~1 year of service
- Easy to install and maintain

#### Materials Needed

- **Hardwood chips**: Super fresh (harvested within 4 weeks), no conifer mixed in. Source from power-line companies, college campuses, or city tree trimming crews
- **Poultry wire**: Separates base mycelial layer from the upper scratching layer
- **King stropharia sawdust spawn**: ~5 pounds covers 1 cubic yard of chips
- **Garden tools**: Wheelbarrow, shovel, rake, hoe

#### Volume Calculation

Measure coop area with 1 foot extra on each side. For a 7×9 foot coop, calculate at 9×11 feet (99 sq ft). Multiply by 8 inches (0.75 ft) = 74.25 cubic feet. Divide by 27 for cubic yards (~2.75, round up to 3).

#### Installation Steps

1. Transfer birds to another area during installation
2. Rake out the coop and lay down 1 inch of wood chips
3. Break up sawdust spawn well and sprinkle over the chip surface; rake in gently
4. Repeat until inoculated chips reach ~2 inches less than total desired depth
5. Install poultry wire over the entire surface — this keeps chickens from digging deep enough to destroy the mycelium while allowing them to scratch and harvest worms in the top 2 inches
6. Cover the wire with the remaining uninoculated wood chips to the full desired depth (8 inches total)
7. The mycelium will colonize upward through the wire from the inoculated base layer, continuously filtering waste from above
