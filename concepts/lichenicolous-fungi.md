# Lichenicolous Fungi

## Overview
Lichenicolous fungi are fungi that grow on lichens, either as parasites,
commensals, or saprotrophs. They represent a diverse and specialized group
found across multiple taxonomic lineages of Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and
their anamorphic states. The number of lichenicolous fungi now probably
exceeds 1,500 species, with many yet to be described, particularly from
tropical regions.

## Taxonomic Distribution

### Ascomycota Orders Rich in Lichenicolous Fungi

**Arthoniales**: Most species of Arthonia, Lecanographa, and Opegrapha are
lichenized, but significant numbers are lichenicolous:
- ~45 species of Arthonia are lichenicolous
- 3 species of Lecanographa are lichenicolous
- 35 species of Opegrapha are lichenicolous
- At least 23 species of Plectocarpon are lichenicolous

**Caliciales**: Species can be lichenized, lichenicolous, fungicolous, or
saprotrophic on bark/wood:
- Several genera (Chaenothecopsis, Microcalicium, Sphinctrina) comprise
  high percentage of lichenicolous species
- Many grow on other lichenized species of Caliciales
- Microcalicium arenarium develops on saxicolous lichens Psilolechia lucida
  and P. clavulifera

**Lecanorales**:
- Toninia: Numerous lichens including several parasitic on other lichens
- Buellia, Carbonea, Catillaria, Rhizocarpon, Tephromela, Thelocarpon:
  Mostly lichenized with some lichenicolous species
- Phacopsis: Exclusively lichenicolous (14 species); most grow on
  Parmeliaceae

**Ostropales**:
- Odontotrema (including Lethariicola): 15 lichenicolous species
- Nanostictis: 3+ undescribed species

**Verrucariales**:
- Several species of Verrucaria are parasitic lichens
- Adelococcus (3 species), Merismatium (9 species), Norrlinia (2 species):
  Exclusively nonlichenized, lichenicolous
- Stigmidium: Approximately 70 species
- Muellerella: 12 species (lichenicolous or hepaticolous)

**Dothideales (Pleosporales)**:
- Tubeufiaceae: Large number of fungicolous taxa on meliolaceous (18
  species), phyllachoraceous fungi, and others

## Ecological Interactions
Lichenicolous fungi engage in a spectrum of interactions with their host
lichens, ranging from destructive parasitism to neutral commensalism. The
nature of these interactions affects both the host lichen community and the
lichenicolous fungi themselves:

### Parasitic Species
Parasitic lichenicolous fungi extract nutrients from living lichen tissue,
often causing visible damage to the host thallus or apothecia. The severity
of parasitism varies widely among species:
- **Gall-forming species**: Some lichenicolous fungi induce galls or
  deformations in host lichen tissue. These galls may represent the
  fungus redirecting host resources toward structures that benefit the
  parasite. Marchandiomyces corallinus is a well-known gall-inducing
  species that attacks Cladonia and other lichen genera.

- **Apothecium-inhabiting species**: Many lichenicolous fungi specialize
  on the reproductive structures of host lichens, colonizing apothecia
  or perithecia and often reducing or eliminating the host's spore
  production. This can significantly impact host population dynamics and
  community structure over time.

- **Killer species**: The most destructive lichenicolous fungi can kill
  entire portions of the host lichen thallus, creating necrotic zones
  that expand progressively. These aggressive parasites can reduce host
  lichen cover substantially in affected areas, particularly in
  lichen communities dominated by susceptible species.

### Commensal and Saprotrophic Species
Not all lichenicolous fungi harm their hosts. Some commensal species grow
on the surface of lichens without apparent negative effects, while others
colonize dead or dying portions of lichen thalli:

- **Non-destructive commensals**: Species such as certain Lichenoconium
  taxa grow on lichen surfaces without penetrating living tissue. These
  commensals may benefit from the microhabitat provided by the lichen
  thallus without imposing a detectable physiological cost on the host.

- **Saprotrophic colonizers**: Many lichenicolous fungi colonize dead
  or moribund portions of lichen thalli, functioning as saprotrophs
  that accelerate decomposition of dead lichen material. These species
  play an important role in lichen community turnover by facilitating
  nutrient release from dead lichen biomass.

- **Hyperparasites**: Some lichenicolous fungi parasitize other
  lichenicolous fungi, creating complex multi-level trophic
  interactions within lichen communities. These hyperparasitic species
  may indirectly benefit the host lichen by controlling populations of
  more damaging primary parasites.

## Host Specificity Patterns
The degree of host specificity varies considerably among lichenicolous
fungi, reflecting differences in ecological strategy and evolutionary
history:

- **Highly host-specific species**: Many lichenicolous fungi are
  restricted to a single host genus or even a single host species.
  Phacopsis species, for example, are largely restricted to
  Parmeliaceae, with individual species often specializing on
  particular host genera. This high specificity may result from
  coevolutionary relationships and adaptations to host-specific
  biochemical defenses.

- **Broad-host-range species**: Other lichenicolous fungi colonize
  multiple unrelated host genera. Stigmidium species, for example,
  have been recorded on hosts across many different lichen families.
  These generalists may possess enzymatic capabilities that allow
  them to overcome diverse host defense compounds.

- **Habitat-mediated specificity**: Some lichenicolous fungi show
  specificity for particular growth forms or habitats rather than
  particular host taxa. Species that colonize crustose lichens on
  rock surfaces may attack any crustose species in that habitat,
  regardless of taxonomic affinity.

## Biogeography and Diversity Patterns
Lichenicolous fungi show distinct geographical patterns that reflect
both historical factors and current environmental conditions:

- **Temperate regions**: Northern temperate regions, particularly Europe,
  have been the most intensively surveyed for lichenicolous fungi. The
  relatively well-documented European lichenicolous mycota includes over
  500 described species, with new species continuing to be discovered
  even in well-studied areas such as the British Isles and Scandinavia.
