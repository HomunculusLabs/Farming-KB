# Aquatic Hyphomycete Conidia and Community Dynamics

[[freshwater-fungi-aquatic-hyphomycetes-ascomycetes]] are a specialized group of fungi that play dominant roles
in plant [[dighton-pine-needle-litter-decomposition-fungal-succession]] in freshwater ecosystems. Their conidia
(asexual spores), often tetraradiate or sigmoid in shape, are morphologically
adapted for dispersal and colonization in flowing water. More than 600 species
of aquatic fungi have been described, many with specific adaptations for life
in [[biodiversity-fungi-freshwater-aquatic-habitats]].

## Conidial Production and Seasonal Dynamics

Thomas et al. (1989) reported the greatest conidial abundance in early autumn
(500-600 conidia per liter) and the lowest during winter (300-500 conidia per
liter). Using stepwise multiple regression analysis, they showed that spore
abundance was significantly related to temperature (R squared = 0.62) and
somewhat related to a combination of both temperature and rainfall. Species
composition, however, was not related to temperature but to rainfall and
conductivity of the water.

The temporal pattern of conidial production reflects both the availability of
substrates for colonization and the environmental conditions favoring
reproductive activity. Gupta and Mehrota (1989) and Thomas et al. (1989) both
identified seasonal changes in fungal species composition of the aquatic fungal
community.

## Adaptations for Aquatic Life

The success of aquatic fungal species over terrestrial fungi entering streams
with plant material is attributed to spore morphology. Tetraradiate and
sigmoid spores of aquatic fungal species colonize submerged plant litter more
efficiently than the rounded spores of terrestrial fungi, which are adapted
for wind dispersal (Wong et al., 1998). This morphological specialization
gives [[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] a competitive advantage in their habitat.

Aquatic hyphomycetes occur on almost all substrates in freshwater systems
(Barlocher, 1992). Fungal biomass is usually greater than bacterial biomass
on decomposing leaf litter in [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]], establishing fungi as the
dominant decomposer group in these habitats.

## Fungal Biomass and Conidial Production During Succession

Gessner et al. (1993) demonstrated temporal successions of aquatic fungi as
leaf litters decompose, analogous to successions observed on terrestrial leaf
litter. On alder leaf litter, the fungal community was dominated by five to
six species during early colonization at 2 weeks (Flagellospora curvula,
Tetrachaetum elegans, Lemonniera centrospharea, L. aquatica, and L.
terrestris). At 4 weeks, the species assemblage became larger (11 species)
and more equitable.

Fungal biomass, measured as ergosterol content, and conidial production both
peak in the middle of this successional sequence (Fig. 2.7, Gessner et al.,
1993). This mid-succession peak in both biomass and reproductive output
reflects the period of maximum resource availability and favorable conditions
for fungal growth.

## Carbon Budgets and Fungal Contribution

Gessner et al. (1997) showed that fungal biomass increases at an early stage
of decomposition and can form a significant proportion of the total detrital
mass, up to 15%. In their carbon budgets, fungal and bacterial action on
decomposing plant residues showed that the rate of decomposition was greatest
in salt marshes and the importance of high mycelial biomass was highest in
freshwater streams.

Concomitant with faster decomposition of Spartina than Plantanus in salt
marshes, a higher percentage of carbon was released as dissolved and fine
particulate organic matter from Spartina. The effects of these end products on
[[mycelial-network-nutrient-dynamics-and-imaging]] the support of primary and secondary productivity within
the ecosystem remain areas for further investigation.

## Resource Allocation Between Growth and Reproduction

Suberkropp (1995) demonstrated the ability of aquatic fungi to allocate
resources between growth and reproduction depending on [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]].
