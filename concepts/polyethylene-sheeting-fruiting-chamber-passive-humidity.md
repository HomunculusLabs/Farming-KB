# Polyethylene Sheeting Fruiting Chamber

## Overview

The polyethylene sheeting fruiting chamber is a minimalist, passive-humidity approach to mushroom fruiting that requires no mechanical humidifiers, cool-mist devices, or automated systems. Developed by early Dutch cultivators, this method relies on evaporative moisture from cased trays to maintain adequate humidity within a simple clear plastic bin covered with polyethylene sheeting.

## Design and Construction

### Container

The system uses clear plastic storage bins that are:

- **Stackable** — Multiple bins can be vertically arranged, making the system extremely space-efficient
- **Clear** — Allows ambient light to reach the developing mushrooms
- **Covered** — A sheet of polyethylene (plastic wrap) is draped over the top of the bin, held loosely in place

### Ventilation

Air exchange is achieved through small holes melted in the sides of the bins:

- Holes are created using a heated tool (soldering iron or heated nail)
- Mesh or filter material can be placed over holes to exclude fungus gnats and other flying insects
- The polyethylene cover is not airtight; some gas exchange occurs naturally at the edges

### Capacity

A standard bin holds approximately **five cased trays** arranged side by side. The evaporation from five trays' worth of casing surface area is sufficient to maintain proper humidity inside the enclosed bin without any supplemental humidification.

## Operating Principle

The system works through passive evaporative humidity cycling:

1. Water evaporates from the moist casing soil surface of the trays
2. The enclosed bin traps this moisture, raising relative humidity
3. Small ventilation holes allow limited gas exchange (CO₂ out, O₂ in)
4. Condensation forms on the bin walls and polyethylene cover, eventually dripping back down

This creates a self-sustaining microclimate. The key insight is that sufficient tray surface area within a properly sized enclosure eliminates the need for external humidification equipment.

## Daily Maintenance Routine

### Misting

The casing surface is misted with water **each day** using a fine spray bottle. The casing must never be allowed to dry out. The cultivator develops a feel for the appropriate moisture level through experience:

- **Underwatered** — Casing surface appears lighter in color, feels dry to the touch, trays feel light
- **Properly watered** — Casing surface glistens slightly after misting, retains dark color
- **Overwatered** — Pooled water on casing surface, waterlogged appearance, slow growth

### Post-Flush Watering

Directly after picking a flush, watering is increased because maturing mushrooms pull substantial moisture from the casing soil. The casing is often significantly drier after a flush than before. Increased watering at this stage supports the development of subsequent flushes.

### Surface Hygiene

Dead pinheads (aborts) must be removed from the casing surface promptly. Aborts are mushroom primordia that stopped developing, turning dark and leathery. If left on the casing surface, they serve as substrate for mold contamination. Regular abort removal is one of the most important hygiene practices in this system.

## Cold Shock Application

Cold shocking is applied selectively based on species difficulty:

| Species | Cold Shock Required? | Notes |
|---------|---------------------|-------|
| *P. cubensis* (Ecuador, etc.) | No | Easy-fruiting strains fruit without cold shock |
| *P. mexicana* | Not beneficial | Does not respond to cold shock treatment |
| *P. tampanensis* | Not beneficial | Does not respond to cold shock treatment |
| *[[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]* | Not beneficial | Does not respond to cold shock treatment |
| Harder-to-fruit strains | Yes (1 night in fridge) | Improves initiation rate |

Cold shock is performed by placing cased trays in a refrigerator (approximately 4°C) for one night before transferring to the fruiting bin. This temperature drop can trigger [[fruiting-initiation]] in recalcitrant strains.

## Flush Expectations

| Parameter | Typical Range |
|-----------|--------------|
| Time to first pinheads | 6-15 days after entering bin |
| Time from pins to mature mushrooms | 5-7 days |
| Typical flush count | 3 flushes (normal management) |
| Maximum flush count | 5-6 flushes (optimal watering) |
| *P. mexicana* flush pattern | One large flush + one small flush |

*P. mexicana* tends to produce a single dominant flush followed by a much smaller second flush, while other species produce more consistent flush-to-flush yields.
