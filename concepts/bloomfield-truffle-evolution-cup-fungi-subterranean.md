## Truffle Evolution: From Cup Fungi to Subterranean Fungi

## Overview

Truffles are among the most prized fungi in the culinary world, yet their
evolutionary origin tells a fascinating story of adaptation from exposed
cup fungi to subterranean [[spore]]-producing organisms. In Mr. Bloomfield's
Orchard, [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] that remain
submerged beneath the soil. Nevertheless, the evolutionary connection

The story begins 300 million years ago in a Carboniferous forest of
gigantic horsetails and pigeon-sized dragonflies. Disc-shaped
apothecia were scattered over the forest floor, looking almost
identical to ascomycete species found in woods today. These open cup
fungi discharged their ascospores through a well-developed mechanism
involving pressurized asci that flipped open a lid at their tips.

Fast-forwarding 70 million years to the Permian period, the next
stage shows a forest of cone-bearing trees. On the ground is a
fruiting body that does not open out flat but remains half-buried in
the soil with a ragged opening at the top. This mutant apothecium
developed following a mutation that crippled its expansion mechanism.
The asci still operated as cannons, but only those spores discharged
directly beneath the opening were jettisoned into the air. Most of the
ascospores accumulated inside the fruiting body.

## Beetle Dispersal and the Advantage of Closure

Attracted by sugary globs of discharged ascus sap, beetles crawled
in through the mouth of the partially closed fruiting body and
emerged covered with sticky ascospores. This serendipitous
relationship provided a critical advantage: the partially closed
fruiting body could survive for much longer in dry air than the
exposed cups, which shriveled after a day or so. Natural selection
favored these more durable, partially enclosed structures.

## The Mesozoic Revolution: Dinosaurs as Dispersers

Moving to the Mesozoic era, the soil surface in a grove of cycads
became unbroken by visible fruiting bodies. A pig-sized dinosaur with
a spade-shaped nose crashed between the fronds and began snuffling
in the soil, unearthing fruiting bodies that looked like hazelnuts.
The truffle had evolved.

No longer formed in a single layer, the truffle's asci became densely
packed around a network of veins running through the interior of the
fruiting body. Each ascus transformed from a cylindrical cannon into a
rounded bag containing beautiful ascospores ornamented with spines or
surface ridges. The entire [[fukuoka-the-one-straw-revolution-final-chapter]] of truffle evolution involves humans. The same
aromatic compounds that evolved to attract rodents have proven
irresistible to human noses and palates. [[bloomfield-truffle-hunting-and-culinary-fungi]] with dogs and
pigs represents a continuation of the ancient mammal-fungus
relationship, with humans substituting for the natural dispersal agents.
The commercial truffle industry, with its carefully managed orchards
and astronomical prices, is the ultimate expression of a coevolutionary
partnership that began in the shadow of Carboniferous horsetails.
