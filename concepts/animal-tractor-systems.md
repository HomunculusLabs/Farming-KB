# [[permaculture-integrated-animal-systems|permaculture|Animal]] Tractor Systems

Animal tractor systems are permaculture techniques that use confined livestock to clear ground cover, eliminate persistent weeds, fertilize soil, and prepare land for subsequent crop planting. Rather than relying on mechanical cultivation or herbicides, these systems harness the natural behaviors of animals — scratching, rooting, grazing, and manuring — to convert weedy or degraded land into productive garden and tree crop areas.

## Overview

Animal tractor systems represent a closed-loop integration of livestock and crop production. Animals are confined to defined areas (pens or paddocks) where they systematically remove vegetation, disturb the soil surface, and deposit manure. Once an area is cleared and fertilized, it is planted to crops, and the animals are moved to the next plot. This creates a rotational cycle that progressively converts degraded land into productive food systems.

Two primary animal tractor systems are documented: the chicken tractor and the pig tractor, each suited to different scales and types of vegetation.

## Chicken Tractor Systems

### Principle

Confined chicken flocks will remove all green ground cover and surface bulbils, depending on flock density relative to area size. They effectively kill or consume persistent weeds including:

- Oxalis (wood sorrel)
- Nut-grass (*[[urtica-dioica|Urtica dioica]]*)
- Nut grass
- Solanum family species: huckleberry, black nightshade, pepino, kangaroo apple, tomato, huskberry, Sodom apple

Near the house, small top-netted secure rearing pens allow broody hens to replace culled chickens, making the system self-replacing.

## Pig Tractor Systems

### Principle

The pig tractor follows the same rotational technique but is better suited to larger properties (1–40 hectares). Pigs are particularly effective against:
- Larger shrub-weeds (Lantana, gorse, blackberry)
- Deep-rooted weeds (Convolvulus, rhizomatous grasses, comfrey)

### Stocking Density

Pigs should be stocked at approximately **50 pigs per hectare** for full clearance of weeds.

### Implementation

1. **Pen size**: 0.5–2 hectare plots are fenced
2. **Fencing**: Permanent electric fencing is most economical (much cheaper than chicken mesh)
3. **Clearance period**: 6–10 weeks until each pen is bare
4. **Soil preparation**: Pigs root and rough-plow the cleared area
5. **Replanting**: Immediately plant forage crops for the next pig rotation:
   - Lucerne (alfalfa)
   - Comfrey sets
   - Sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke)
   - Sweet potato
   - Inga trees
   - Papaya
   - Banana

### Large-Scale Integration (20–40 ha)

On a larger scale, the pig tractor system can pioneer high-quality milk-cow pasture:
- Plant chicory, dandelion, comfrey, dock, grasses, and clover
- Cows follow 2–3 months behind the pig tractor
- A continuous rotation is established
- Excess milk products (whey, skim milk) are fed to pigs as accessory food

**Benefits of this integrated system:**
- Piglets ranging over diverse pasture rarely show iron anaemia deficiency
- Parasite cycles are broken through rotational grazing
- Soil constantly improves in humus content
- Large animals (pigs and cows) need fenced tree strips, tree guards, and border hedgerows for tree forage crop

## System Integration

Intensive animal tractor systems can serve multiple roles within a farm design:

1. **Pioneering phase**: Temporary use to convert degraded land, followed by transition to tree crop
2. **Accessory to tree crop**: Ongoing integration within established orchard systems
3. **Permanent mixed farm feature**: Continuous rotational use
4. **Seasonal cleanup**: Used to remove crop wastes and fallen fruits after harvest

Chickens are recommended as a permanent forage system in all mixed orchards due to their pest control, manuring, and ground-cover management benefits.

## Grasslands and Range Management Context

Animal tractor systems integrate with broader grasslands and range management strategies:

- **Addition of forage species**: Grass legumes or trees providing foliage, food sugars, seed carbohydrates, or fruits
- **Environmental changes**: Water storage, soil structure improvement, irrigation, windbreaks, and shelter
- **Conservation of forage**: Rotational rests from grazing, hay and silage production, protein/urea supplements during drought
- **Stock management**: Planned buying and selling aligned with seasonal fluctuations, timing calving for spring/early summer

The ideal tropical grazing system combines a mixture of legumes with selected grass species plus storable forage. Most grazing systems can extend under palms, between large tree crops, or as complexes with belts of forage tree legumes yielding fodder, fruit, pods, or large seed for food concentrates.

## Key Species for Forage Systems

### Elephant or Banna Grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*)
- Best on deep alluvial or coarse flats above 110 cm (alluvium) or 90 cm (coastal) rainfall
