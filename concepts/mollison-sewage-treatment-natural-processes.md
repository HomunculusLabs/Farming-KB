## Overview of Natural Sewage Treatment

Bill Mollison presents a comprehensive framework for treating sewage through biological and natural processes [[mycorrhizal-heavy-metal-uptake]]s, and carbon compounds that can be transformed into useful products.

Raw sewage contains dangerous levels of bacteria, viruses, and intestinal worm eggs, but these can be effectively managed through staged biological treatment.

## The Three-Stage Pond System

Mollison describes a proven three-stage pond treatment system based on the Werribee sewage treatment works in Victoria, Australia, serving a city of 2.7 million people across 1,500 hectares of lagoons.

The ratio achieved at Werribee is approximately 1 hectare of treatment pond per 1,800 inhabitants, with about 100 primary settling ponds each rarely exceeding 7 hectares in area.

Scaled down, this means 1 hectare of settling pond can serve 3,800 people.

The three stages operate as follows:

### Anaerobic Ponds (Methane-Producing)

The first stage consists of settling ponds where sludge creates anaerobic conditions.

These ponds produce biogas, a mixture of methane, carbon dioxide, and ammonia gas, with traces of nitrous sulphide.

Biogas is a useful fuel gas for engines or cooking, and capturing it rather than releasing it prevents greenhouse gas contribution.

In the anaerobic stage, 90-94% of worm eggs are destroyed along with many harmful bacteria, while useful energy is generated.

The sulphur-loving bacteria of genera Thiosporallum, Chromatium, and Rhodopseudomonas use hydrogen sulphide as a hydrogen source and produce elemental sulphur that binds to heavy metals.

About 1.8-2.0 mg/l of heavy metals are precipitated as sulphides at 1.0 mg/l of elemental sulphur.

### Facultative Ponds

The second stage features both anaerobic conditions at the pond base and aerobic conditions at the surface due to wind and algae.

Algae blooms of Euglena, Chlamydomonas, Chlorella, and Scenedesmus provide oxygen to bacteria.

Rich zooplankton populations develop including rotifers (Brachionus, Trichocerca), cladocerans (Daphnia, Moina), copepods, and ostracods.

These zooplankton concentrate metals at remarkable levels: 1,200 mg/kg iron, 152 zinc, 37 copper, 28 chromium, 12.2 nickel, and 10.3 lead in dry weight.

Harvested zooplankton and algae can be added to poultry feedstocks or pumped to forests and fields as manure.

### Aerobic Ponds

The final stage is totally aerobic, with BOD reduced to only 3-57 mg/l.

Suspended solids drop to 32-50 mg/l, and about 80% of contaminants have been incorporated into living organisms.

Metal levels now meet World Health Organisation standards for safe irrigation water.

## Heavy Metal Removal Efficiency

Mollison cites Hussainey's data showing removal rates through the anaerobic pond stage:

Copper is removed 97% (78% anaerobically).

Cadmium is removed 70% (all anaerobically).

Zinc is removed 97% (83% anaerobically).

Nickel is removed 65% (47% aerobically).

Lead is removed 95% (90% anaerobically).

Chromium is removed 87% (47% anaerobically).

Iron is removed 85% (47% anaerobically).

## Compacted Digester Ponds

For smaller-scale systems, Mollison recommends compacted digester ponds that can process sewage from 1,000 townspeople in as little as 270 square metres, 1 metre deep.

A 3-metre-wide by 90-metre-long narrow pond can be sealed with weighted covers of plastic, metal, butyl rubber, or fibreglass.

The digester operates optimally at 25-30 degrees C over a 20-day period, converting over 90% of solids into methane.

Sealed digesters prevent scum formation, which can slow the breakdown process and cause acid conditions.

Sludge must remain in contact with semi-liquid inputs, so agitation using pumped biogas is critical for maximum breakdown efficiency.

## Seasonal Considerations

Winter sees more hydrogen sulphide released from anaerobic ponds (8-15 mg/l versus summer's 2-5 mg/l) and winds contribute more to oxygenation than algae.

Summer increases oxidative activity; BOD processing nearly doubles from 495 kg/ha/day in winter to 1,034 kg/ha/day in summer.

In winter, cooling water from methane-powered engines can provide essential heat to maintain digester efficiency through closed-loop pipe systems.

## Higher Plant Treatment Systems

Mollison emphasises that even more sophisticated treatment is achieved using higher plants.

Rushes, sedges, and floating plants greatly assist with removal of heavy metals and human pathogens, and some plants can break down halogenated hydrocarbons from herbicides and pesticides.

Species recommended for biological water treatment include Phragmites communis and Typha spp. for flocculating colloids and eliminating pathogens.

Schoenoplectus spp. takes up copper, cobalt, nickel, and manganese while exuding mould antibiotics.

Scirpus spp. breaks down phenols including toxic pentachlorophenol.

Juncus spp. treats cyanide compounds, thiocyanates, and phenols in flow times of 7+ hours.

Water treated through these species shows low to zero populations of E. coli, Salmonella, and Enterococci.

## Final Treatment and Integration

Cleaned water can be routed to field crops, forest systems, or pastures.

At Werribee, treated sewage water has safely supported beef cattle for 35 years.

At Hegerstown, Maryland, sewage waters supplied to selected coppiced poplar plots can produce about 60% of town energy use as wood chips.

Domestically, comfrey beds absorb faecal products from animal wash-water, and the resulting crop can be used for fodder or trenched as instant compost.
