## Rationale for Co-Culture Approaches

Fungi and bacteria exhibit fundamentally different pathways for the catabolism of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and other xenobiotic compounds. Both groups present certain disadvantages when used individually for degradation of pollutants at contaminated sites.

Bacteria are generally unable to hydroxylate high-molecular-weight PAH compounds effectively. They excel at degrading simpler, more polar metabolites but struggle with the initial oxidation of recalcitrant substrates.

Fungi, particularly [[fungal-cytochrome-p450-biodegradation]] pathways. However, fungi often produce polar metabolites that accumulate rather than being further mineralized.

Fungal transformation of PAHs followed by bacterial degradation of the resulting polar metabolites therefore represents an effective combined strategy for complete PAH metabolism and mineralization.

## PAH Degradation by Fungal-Bacterial Co-Cultures

Several studies have demonstrated the potential of fungal-bacterial co-cultures for the detoxification and mineralization of PAHs:

### Benzo[a]pyrene Mineralization

In a landmark study, significant degradation and microbial growth occurred on chrysene, pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, benzo[a]pyrene, and dibenz[a,h]anthracene in basal salts medium in the presence of a combination of fungal-bacterial co-cultures of Stenotrophomonas maltophilia VUN 10010, a bacterial consortium of VUN 10009, and Penicillium janthinellum VUO 10201.

These co-cultures mineralized 25% of benzo[a]pyrene to carbon dioxide after 49 days, along with temporary accumulation and subsequent disappearance of metabolites. Inoculation of the same fungal-bacterial co-cultures resulted in 53% mineralization of radiolabeled benzo[a]pyrene to carbon dioxide in 100 days.

P. janthinellum alone was not able to utilize benzo[a]pyrene on the basal salts medium. However, 16 to 64% of the radiolabeled carbon was identified as polar metabolites in the presence of a cosubstrate (pyrene, glucose, or malt extract yeast extract peptone dextrose medium). Benzo[a]pyrene was mineralized by S. maltophilia and bacterial consortia from these fungal metabolites.

P. janthinellum and S. maltophilia together resulted in 58% benzo[a]pyrene mineralization with 61% water-soluble products after 56 days on media containing pyrene (250 mg/l) and benzo[a]pyrene (50 mg/l).

### Bjerkandera sp. with Indigenous Microflora

Bjerkandera sp. strain BOS55 recovered approximately 8.5% radiolabeled carbon dioxide from benzo[a]pyrene after 15 days, with 73% water-soluble metabolites accumulating. Many unidentified polar metabolites accumulated. Further incubation of up to 215 days accounted for only a small increase in mineralization (13.5%).

However, when inocula of indigenous microflora prepared from activated sludge, acid forest soil, and a culture grown on 2,2-diphenic acid were added to the fungal cultures, benzo[a]pyrene mineralization was enhanced by 20 to 27% by day 56. The amount of polar metabolites decreased from 69% to 37-48%, indicating bacterial consumption of fungal metabolites.

Further incubation for 215 days resulted in the recovery of 34% radiolabeled carbon dioxide and only 16% remaining polar metabolites, demonstrating near-complete degradation over extended time periods.

### Phenanthrene Degradation

Phenanthrene degradation correlates to microbial density and biomass increase. The highest biomass reached 238 mg/l for Rhodotorula glutinis and 50 mg/l for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. R. glutinis was as active as P. aeruginosa during growth on phenanthrene in liquid mineral basal medium, demonstrating that yeast-bacteria co-cultures can also be effective.

## Reduction of Mutagenic Potential

Studies on fungal-bacterial co-cultures have demonstrated significant reduction in the mutagenic potential of cultures or soil extracts as observed in Salmonella typhimurium revertant assays. This is a critical finding because fungal oxidation of PAHs can sometimes produce metabolites that are more mutagenic than the parent compounds.

The combination of fungal oxidation with subsequent bacterial degradation effectively reduces overall mutagenicity, which is an important endpoint for evaluating the success of bioremediation treatments.
