## The Bwiti Cults of the Fang

The Fang people of West Africa maintain the [[aminopropyl-silica-gel-chromatography-indole-alkaloid-purification]] found in the [[plants-of-the-gods-tabernanthe-iboga]]*.
McKenna presents the Bwiti as a living example of a partnership-oriented
society that has successfully resisted both commercial and missionary
incursions into its cultural integrity, a resilience he attributes in
part to the social bonding effects of the ibogaine experience.

The Bwiti perspective on good and evil differs markedly from the
Christian dualism that missionaries attempted to impose. As McKenna
quotes from Fang informants: "We have two hearts, good and bad." The
Christian promise of "one-heartedness" was experienced by many Fang as
a constriction of the self. In Bwiti practice, "one-heartedness" is
understood as a coagulation emerging from a flow of many qualities,
goodness achieved in the presence of badness, an "aboveness achieved
in the presence of belowness." This emergent quality is energized by
the presence of its opposite, a dialectical understanding of spiritual
experience that McKenna finds far more sophisticated than simple moral
binary opposition.

## Ibogaine as Aphrodisiac and Social Bonding Agent

McKenna highlights a paradoxical quality of ibogaine: it is widely
recognized both as a factor holding married couples together in the
face of Fang institutions like easy divorce, and as an aphrodisiac.
He considers ibogaine to be perhaps one of the few plants among the
many dozens claimed to be aphrodisiacs that actually performs as
advertised. Most other candidates, he notes, are in fact merely
stimulants that can cause generalized arousal and sustained erection

notes that dominator societies that arose to replace partnership
societies were far less eager to suppress group sexual activities than
they were to suppress hallucinogenic plant religions. Group sexual
activity without the dissolution of the dominator ego would help the
most ego-obsessed males gain power and rise in the social hierarchy.

Since domination of others ultimately includes sexual domination, this
explains the persistence of orgies and group sexual activities in many
of the mystery religions, at the festivals of Dionysus and the Roman
Saturnalia, and within paganism generally, long after the heart of the
pagan world had ceased to beat. Eventually, the dominator anxiety about
establishing clear lines of male paternity outweighed all other
considerations, and through Christianity's ruthless extermination of
heterodoxy, orgies were recognized and suppressed as the subversive,
boundary-dissolving activities McKenna argues they are.
