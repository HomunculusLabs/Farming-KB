## Overview

Lettuce and leafy greens are among the most accessible and rewarding crops for home gardeners. They are cool-season vegetables that grow quickly, can be harvested multiple times, and fit into small spaces including containers and raised beds. This guide covers lettuce types, planting methods, bolt prevention, and harvest strategies.

## Lettuce Types

### Butterhead (Bibb/Boston)
- Soft, tender leaves forming a loose head
- Matures in 55-75 days from seed
- Varieties: Buttercrunch, Bibb, Tom Thumb, Esmeralda
- Best for fresh salads and wraps
- Moderate bolt resistance

### Crisphead (Iceberg)
- Dense, crunchy heads with tight wrapping
- Matures in 70-85 days
- Varieties: Great Lakes, Salinas, Iceberg
- Requires consistent moisture and cool conditions
- Most demanding type to grow at home

### Looseleaf
- Open, rosette growth habit, no head formation
- Matures fastest: 30-50 days from seed
- Varieties: Red Sails, Black Seeded Simpson, Oak Leaf, Merlot
- Excellent for cut-and-come-again harvesting
- Most heat-tolerant of the lettuce types

### Romaine (Cos)
- Upright, elongated heads with crunchy ribs
- Matures in 60-80 days
- Varieties: Parris Island Cos, Little Gem, Jericho
- Jericho is notably heat-tolerant
- Good for Caesar salads and grilling

## Other Leafy Greens

- **Spinach**: Rich in iron, bolts quickly in heat; varieties like Bloomsdale Long Standing and Malabar climbing spinach for warm seasons
- **Arugula**: Peppery flavor, bolts fast; try slow-bolt varieties like Sylvetta
- **Swiss Chard**: Perennial-like in mild climates, harvest outer leaves continuously
- **Kale**: Cold-hardy, improves flavor after frost; see [[brassica-growing-guide]]
- **Mustard Greens**: Spicy, fast-growing; varieties like Red Giant and Mizuna
- **Mache (Corn Salad)**: Extremely cold-hardy, mild flavor, small leaves

## Direct Sowing

Lettuce and most leafy greens are best direct-sown rather than transplanted, though transplanting works for head lettuce.

### Seed Starting
- Sow seeds 1/8 to 1/4 inch deep
- Space looseleaf types 4-6 inches apart, head types 10-12 inches
- Seeds need light to germinate for some varieties — press into soil surface
- Optimal germination temperature: 60-70 degrees F
- Seeds typically germinate in 7-14 days

### Soil Requirements
- Well-draining soil rich in organic matter
- pH 6.0-7.0 preferred
- Work in 1-2 inches of finished [[composting-systems-design-comparison]] before planting
- Consistent moisture is critical for tender leaves
- Avoid waterlogged soil which promotes damping off

## Succession Planting

Succession planting ensures a continuous harvest throughout the growing season.

### Schedule
- Sow every 2-3 weeks from early spring through late summer
- In most temperate zones, begin sowing 4-6 weeks before last frost
- Stop sowing heat-sensitive varieties 2-3 weeks before summer heat peaks
- Resume sowing in late summer for fall harvest (4-6 weeks before first frost)
- Winter sowing under row covers or in cold frames extends harvest year-round

### Tips
- Use different lettuce types across successions — looseleaf in heat, crisphead in cool
- Keep a garden journal to track sowing dates and results
- Over-seed by 20-30% to account for germination variability

## Bolt Prevention

Bolting (flowering) makes lettuce bitter and tough. Prevention is key for quality harvests.

### Understanding Bolting
- Triggered by day length increasing above 14 hours and temperatures above 75-80F
- Plant sends up a flower stalk, redirecting energy from leaves
- Once bolting begins, it cannot be reversed

### Prevention Strategies
- Choose bolt-resistant varieties (Jericho romaine, Red Sails, Nevada, Anuenue)
- Provide afternoon shade with shade cloth (30-50%) during heat waves
- Mulch heavily to keep soil cool and retain moisture
- Water consistently — drought stress accelerates bolting
- Sow in partial shade positions during summer months
- Use bolt-prone types (spinach, arugula) only in spring and fall
- Row covers with insect mesh can also reduce heat exposure slightly

## Cut-and-Come-Again Harvest

This method maximizes yield from each planting, especially for looseleaf types.

### Method
- Begin harvesting when plants are 3-4 inches tall (about 25-35 days)
- Cut outer leaves 1-2 inches above the crown with clean scissors or a knife
- Leave the center growing point intact
- Plants will produce new leaves from the center for 3-5 additional harvests
- Each successive harvest is slightly smaller

### Best Varieties for Cut-and-Come-Again
- Red Sails, Black Seeded Simpson, Salad Bowl, Oak Leaf
- Mustard greens, arugula, and baby spinach work well too

### Tips
- Harvest in the morning for crispiest leaves
- Wash and dry immediately, store in perforated bags in the refrigerator
- Most cut greens last 5-7 days refrigerated
- After 3-4 harvests, plants may become bitter or exhausted — pull and re-sow

## Common Problems

### Pests
- **Aphids**: Wash off with strong water spray; encourage ladybugs
- **Slugs and snails**: Use beer traps, diatomaceous earth barriers, or copper tape
- **Flea beetles**: Use floating row covers on seedlings; worst on arugula and mustard

### Diseases
- **Damping off**: Improve drainage, avoid overwatering seedlings, use sterile starting mix
- **Downy mildew**: Improve air circulation, avoid overhead watering, choose resistant varieties
- **Bottom rot**: Ensure good drainage, avoid mulching right against stems

## Nutrition and Fertility

- Leafy greens are heavy nitrogen feeders
- Side-dress with compost tea or diluted fish emulsion every 2-3 weeks
- Avoid excessive nitrogen which can cause nitrate accumulation
- Foliar feed with diluted seaweed extract for trace minerals
- See [[seasonal-gardening-calendar]] for timing of fertilizer applications
