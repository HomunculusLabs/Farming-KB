### 2. Care for People

The second ethic extends concern to human communities and individuals. It
recognizes that sustainable systems must provide for human needs — food, shelter,
health, education, social connection, and meaningful work.

Holmgren distinguishes between care based on fear and insecurity (which leads to
controlling behavior and defensive postures) and care based on abundance and trust
(which leads to generosity and open systems). The first, arising from a sense of
scarcity, creates competitive and destructive social dynamics. The second, arising
from experiencing nature's gifts, encourages distribution of surplus beyond one's
immediate circle.

A critical insight is that caring for oneself is not selfish but necessary: "As we
reduce our dependence on the global economy and replace it with household and
local economies, we reduce the demand that drives current inequities." Thus
"look after yourself first" is not an invitation to greed but a challenge to grow
up through self-reliance and personal responsibility.

### 3. Set Limits to Consumption and Redistribute Surplus

The third ethic addresses the relationship between abundance and limits. Holmgren
presents these as complementary rather than contradictory: a sense of abundance
encourages sharing, while a sense of limits encourages restraint.
