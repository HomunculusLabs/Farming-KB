## Allegro Bible Morals and Ten Commandments as Mushroom Word-Play

The prophetic tradition thus preserves, in coded form, the experiential core of the mushroom religion even as some of its exponents argued against its outward expression.

The deity names themselves reinforce this continuity. Yahweh, Baal, Zeus, Jupiter — these are not rival gods from competing pantheons but linguistic variants of the same fertility-deity concept. They all, Allegro argues, "sound a common note of sexual import," pointing to a shared origin in the same fertility-devotion complex that stretched across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean.

The supposed uniqueness of Israelite monotheism dissolves when these names are read as regional expressions of a single, mushroom-centred cultic tradition whose worship was recognisable beneath different linguistic costumes. The names changed; the cult remained.

The mushroom cult's extreme practices ultimately bred their own opposition. The Nehemiah-Ezra reforms of the post-exilic period represent a decisive crackdown, driving the cult underground where it survived in encoded language and secret ritual.

What mainstream scholarship reads as the triumph of monotheistic reform, Allegro reads as the suppression — but crucially not the eradication — of the mushroom religion.

The encoded texts of both testaments are, on this view, the surviving literature of that suppressed tradition, their true meanings hidden beneath layers of pious surface interpretation waiting for the philologist's key to unlock them.

## The New Testament as Deliberate Deception

Allegro extends his argument into the Christian scriptures with a provocative and far-reaching claim: the New Testament was not naive religious literature produced by sincere believers but a deliberately deceptive document crafted by initiates who understood exactly what they were doing.

His central proof-text is the nickname "Boanerges" ("Sons of Thunder") given to James and John in Mark 3:17. If "Boanerges" is itself a mushroom name and "Sons of Thunder" is an equally relevant mushroom reference drawn from the same Sumerian root, then the apparent translation is in fact a pseudo-translation — a surface rendering designed to conceal a secret meaning.

This single pattern, Allegro argues, demonstrates an *intention of deceit* that extends throughout the text. The surface details of the narrative must be read as equally false, pseudo-renderings of secret names that only the initiated would recognise.

Once the pattern is established in one instance, it becomes the default interpretive lens for the entire document.

Yet this was no frivolous or cynical hoax. The New Testament was, Allegro maintains, a "deadly serious" attempt to transmit the old secrets to a scattered faithful community living under hostile Roman authority. The stakes were survival itself — both of the community and of the knowledge it carried.

The text had to be readable at two levels simultaneously: a harmless moral narrative for the censorious outsider, and a precise set of encoded instructions for the insider who knew the key. The entire Gospel project was, in effect, an exercise in cryptographic communication under persecution.

Allegro writes: "If even one only of the mushroom references were correct, then a new element has to be reckoned with" — and in his view, the references are far more than one; they are pervasive and systematic. The cumulative weight of the philological identifications makes coincidence, in his judgment, impossible.

Under this reading, the necessity of a historical founder-figure fades considerably. Christianity becomes not the product of a single charismatic teacher but a latter-day manifestation of a movement thousands of years old, its scriptures a new encoding of the same old mushroom secrets.

Features of the Gospel narratives that have long puzzled historians become comprehensible as deliberate misdirection. Jesus' apparently inexplicable liberal attitude toward "quislings" (collaborators with Rome), his injunction to befriend enemies, his equivocation on the question of paying taxes to Caesar, and the striking detail of Jewish citizens howling for the blood of one of their own before Pilate — all these read as elements of a "deliberate attempt to mislead authorities."

The Gospel was a coded message whose surface meaning was designed to satisfy Roman scrutiny while its real meaning spoke only to the initiated. The improbabilities of the narrative are not flaws but features — deliberate implausibilities signalling to the reader that the text operates on a level beneath the obvious one.

## The Ten Commandments as Mushroom Word-Play

Allegro's most detailed philological work concerns the Decalogue itself. The received understanding of the Ten Commandments as divine moral legislation inscribed on stone tablets is, he argues, a complete misreading of texts whose real subject is the sacred mushroom.

### The Two Tablets

The two "tablets" of stone on which the commandments were inscribed correspond, Allegro argues, to the two halves of the mushroom volva — the egg-like sac from which the *Amanita* fruiting body emerges. When the volva splits at maturity, it forms two cup-shaped halves that bear an unmistakable resemblance to the rounded clay tablets of early Mesopotamian writing.

The very word "tablet" itself preserves the connection: Sumerian TAB-BA-R/LI(-R/LI) passes through Greek and Latin *tabula* into English "tablet," carrying the mushroom reference at every stage of transmission.

The number ten is itself word-play rather than a genuine count of commandments. MASh-TAB-BA-RI can be read as "five words" inscribed on two tablets, yielding ten — a numerical encoding that disguises a description of the physical object (the mushroom) behind an apparently sacred number.

### The Preamble and First Commandment

The preamble — "I am Yahweh your God who brought you out of Egypt" — is read as the mushroom speaking in its own voice. It is "the Egyptian mushroom," which is to say "the stretched or erect one," a phallic description connecting the mushroom to the broader fertility-deity complex.

The first commandment, "You shall have no other gods before me," plays on the repeated invocatory formula *E_LA_UIA, E-LA_UIA, rendered as "there is no god save Elohim." This formulation, Allegro notes, strikingly echoes the Muslim *shahada* ("There is no god but Allah"), suggesting that both confessions encode the same mushroom invocation beneath their monotheistic surface.

### Swearing and Oaths

The prohibition against swearing insincerely draws on E-LA-UIA as a play on the Semitic root '-l-h, "to swear." The concept of "remission of sin" Allegro derives from *MASh_BALAG_ANTA, a Sumerian compound whose mushroom meaning underlies the theological idea of forgiveness.

Jesus' teaching on oaths in the Sermon on the Mount deepens the word-play considerably: "Do not swear at all; let your yes be yes and your no be no" becomes, in Allegro's decoding, a direct mushroom invocation. The Aramaic formula "yes-no; yes-no" approximates the repeated invocatory *E_LA_UIA, *E_LA_UIA.

The follow-up clause, "anything more comes from evil," is read as *MASh_BA(LA)G_ANTA — "what is surplus is evil" — a Sumerian phrase that simultaneously encodes the mushroom name and provides the moral-sounding surface instruction.

The Sermon on the Mount, in this reading, is not ethical teaching but a liturgical recitation of mushroom names disguised as moral exhortation.

### Sabbath

The Sabbath commandment encodes a mushroom name root carrying a cluster of related meanings: "be still," "praise," "working," and "creation" — a set of ideas that the Sabbath observance conveniently assembles into a coherent religious practice. The day of rest thus doubles as a day of mushroom-reference, its theological vocabulary drawn entirely from the fungus lexicon.

### Family Honour and Corban

"Honour your father and mother" Allegro reads not as a family-ethics injunction but as "give praise to those who created you," drawing on sh-b-kh, which means both "honour" and "increase, profit." The dual sense allows the commandment to function as both a moral exhortation and a mushroom invocation.

The sacred offering term Corban connects to *LI_KUR_BA(LA)G_ANTA, read as "for Corban," and is comparable to khurbekhãnā', a known mushroom name in Allegro's philological reconstruction. The Temple offering thus carries a double dedication — to Yahweh on the surface, to the mushroom beneath.

### Killing, Stealing, and False Witness

The commandments against killing, stealing, and bearing false witness round out the Decalogue's social legislation. In Allegro's framework, these too conceal mushroom references beneath their moral surface, though he gives them less detailed philological treatment than the oath and adultery commandments.

Their primary function in his argument is structural: their presence as bare prohibitions without qualification or legal machinery helps demonstrate that the Decalogue was never intended as practical law.

### Adultery and Divorce

The prohibition "Do not commit adultery" Allegro reads as *LIKU_R_ANTA, "using a woman for adultery." The surface moral instruction conceals a Sumerian phrase whose literal subject is sexual misconduct described in terms drawn from the mushroom vocabulary.

Jesus' radical extension of this commandment — that looking lustfully at a woman constitutes adultery in the heart — works by adding TAB-BA-RI to the underlying word-play, yielding the sense "adulterous association in the mind." The inner/outer distinction in Jesus' ethics maps directly onto the surface/hidden distinction in the encoded text.

The divorce vocabulary surrounding this commandment is equally dense with [[gartz-conocybe-cyanopus-rare-psychoactive-mushroom]]. The ethical content becomes secondary to the secret name concealed within it — a message about the sacred fungus wrapped in the guise of divine commandment.

The methodological stakes are enormous. Allegro's approach requires accepting that Sumerian roots survived in Hebrew and Aramaic vocabulary for millennia, that biblical writers deliberately constructed their texts as cryptographic puzzles, and that the entire Western tradition of biblical ethics rests on a misunderstanding of encoded mushroom terminology.

Each of these premises is fiercely contested by mainstream scholarship, and *The [[sacred-mushroom-and-the-cross]]* was met with near-universal rejection upon publication in 1970. Allegro was effectively expelled from the academic establishment, though he never retracted his central claims.

The entire edifice of biblical morality, on this reading, is a sustained act of cryptographic dissimulation, its surface meaning the least interesting thing about it. What generations of believers have received as the foundation of Western ethics may instead be a mushroom manual in moral disguise — the longest-running inside joke in Western civilisation, or its most deeply buried truth.
