# Heavy Metal Biosorption

Biosorption is the passive uptake of metal ions from aqueous solutions by biological materials, including living or dead microbial biomass, plant materials, and biopolymers. It is a physicochemical process driven by interactions between metal ions and functional groups on the surface of biological materials, including carboxyl, hydroxyl, amino, sulfhydryl, phosphate, and imidazole groups.

Biosorption differs from bioaccumulation, which is an active, metabolism-dependent process of metal uptake and intracellular sequestration. Biosorption can occur with both living and non-living biomass and does not require metabolic energy, making it a rapid, reversible, and cost-effective approach for removing heavy metals from contaminated water.

## Mechanisms of Biosorption

Metal biosorption involves several physicochemical mechanisms that may operate simultaneously depending on the type of biomass, the metal species, and the solution chemistry.

### Ion Exchange

Ion exchange is one of the primary biosorption mechanisms. Metal cations in solution exchange with light ions (typically hydrogen, sodium, potassium, or calcium) that are bound to anionic functional groups on the biomass surface. This process is reversible and depends on the relative affinity of the biomass for different ions, the concentration gradient, and pH conditions.

### Complexation

Complexation involves the formation of coordinate covalent bonds between metal ions and electron-donating ligand groups on the biomass surface. Nitrogen-containing groups (amino, imidazole) and sulfur-containing groups (sulfhydryl, thioether) are particularly effective at complexing heavy metals due to their strong metal-binding affinity.

Sulfhydryl groups, present in cysteine residues of proteins and in metal-binding peptides such as phytochelatins and metallothioneins, form especially strong complexes with soft metals including mercury, cadmium, silver, and lead. This is the basis for the high mercury and cadmium biosorption capacity of sulfur-rich biomaterials.

### Electrostatic Attraction

Electrostatic attraction occurs between charged metal ions and oppositely charged sites on the biomass surface. At pH values above the isoelectric point of the biomass, surface groups are deprotonated and carry a net negative charge, attracting metal cations. At low pH, protonation of surface groups reduces the net negative charge and may even create a positive surface charge that repels metal cations.

This pH dependence is a fundamental characteristic of biosorption. Optimal metal uptake generally occurs at pH values between 4 and 6, where surface groups are deprotonated enough to bind metals but not so high that metal hydroxide precipitation competes with biosorption.

### Surface Precipitation

At higher pH values or metal concentrations, metals may precipitate as hydroxides or other insoluble compounds directly on the biomass surface. This process contributes to the overall metal removal capacity but is not strictly biosorption because it does not involve binding to specific functional groups. Surface precipitation is influenced by local pH conditions at the biomass surface, which may differ from bulk solution pH due to ion exchange reactions.

### Van der Waals Forces and Physical Adsorption

Weak intermolecular forces including van der Waals interactions and hydrophobic interactions contribute to the binding of some metal species, particularly larger complexes and colloidal forms. These forces are relatively nonspecific and contribute less to overall biosorption capacity than ion exchange and complexation.

## Biosorbent Materials

A wide range of biological materials have been investigated as biosorbents for heavy metal removal. The selection of biosorbent depends on availability, cost, biosorption capacity, selectivity, mechanical stability, and ease of regeneration.

### Fungal Biomass

Fungi and their byproducts have received considerable attention as biosorbent materials. Fungal cell walls are complex macromolecular structures consisting primarily of chitin, chitosan, and glucans, with additional polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and pigments such as melanin.
