# Growing Sweet Corn and Field Corn

Corn occupies a unique position in the vegetable garden. As both a summer
vegetable (sweet corn) and a potential staple crop (field corn for
cornmeal and flour), it connects the garden to broader questions of
[[solomon-self-sufficiency-garden-production]].
Solomon treats sweet corn as a medium-demand crop and field corn as a
potential calorie staple.

## Sweet Corn as a Garden Vegetable

### Spacing Requirements

The minimum recommended spacing for sweet corn is about 24 square feet
(2,000 sq cm) per plant. Solomon suggests 18 inches (45 cm) on center or
nine inches (23 cm) apart in rows 36 inches (90 cm) apart. Where low soil
moisture threatens, increase spacing to nine inches apart in 48-inch
rows. Where severe drought is certain and watering is not possible, the
maximum space for one sweet corn plant is about 16 square feet (1.5 sq m)
-- imitating traditional Native American gardening with hills spaced four
feet on center, four seeds per hill, thinned progressively to one plant.

### Pollination

Corn ears are wind-pollinated and the pollen is heavy. When corn grows in
a single long row, many ears will be only partly filled. Always grow corn
in a patch at least two rows wide with at least six plants per short row.

### Soil Fertility

It is better to make the entire corn bed moderately fertile rather than
concentrating soil amendments in rows or hills. Extremely high fertility
is not needed for this medium-demand vegetable. Corn sprouts in slightly
cooler soil than beans. Unless you live in a short-season area, it is
safer to sow after the soil has warmed.

### Growing Method

Spread COF (see [[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]])
over the entire patch and dig it in before sowing. Make a hole about two
inches (five cm) deep, drop two or three seeds, and cover. When seedlings
are about six inches (15 cm) tall and securely established, thin to one
plant. Keep the patch well-weeded (and shallowly cultivated) until the
corn has shaded the ground, suppressing newly emerging weeds with its
dense shade.

### Pest Control

After pollen drops, the silks become attractive to the corn earworm moth.
This is the time to dust the silks with Bt if that pest is a problem in
your area. See [[solomon-insects-diseases-pest-management]].

## Sweet Corn Varieties

Hybrids will outproduce the best-maintained open-pollinated classics by at
least half again as much. This is mostly because most classic OP sweet
corn varieties have deteriorated to the point of becoming nonproductive.
Solomon suggests sowing the main patch with hybrid seed while trialing a
few OP classics alongside. The classic sweet varieties are closely related
to old Native American field corns and are usually acceptable for making
cornmeal, flour, or parched corn.

## Field Corn for Self-Sufficiency

If your intention is to grow a large patch of field corn (not primarily as
sweet corn) to use as a family staple, Solomon reverses his advice: use
open-pollinated varieties instead of hybrids. Here is why:

- **Nutritional superiority**: Classic OP varieties produce more nutritious
  grain than hybrids. Hybrid field corn makes more bulk containing less
  protein and fewer minerals per unit weight.
- **Hybrid vigor tradeoff**: Hybrid corn appears more productive because
  hybrid vigor alone increases yield, but the uniform hybrid ears offer
  less nutrition than old-fashioned OP types.
- **Soil requirements**: Hybrid field corn will grow on poorer soil that
  OP types would fail on, but the resulting food is not something to
  maintain family health on long-term.

Solomon advises doing your own trials the first year: test as many sorts
of OP field corn as you can find, determine the best one for your region,
and begin saving your own seeds the next year. See
[[solomon-seed-saving-vegetable-breeding]].

## Saving Corn Seed

Corn is wind-pollinated. The pollen is heavy and will not blow more than
about one mile (1,600 m) except in a gale strong enough to knock corn
plants flat. Isolating varieties by a quarter mile (400 m) will usually be
good enough for home-garden seed, provided the second patch is not upwind
in the direction of prevailing winds.

### Selection Method

It is possible to determine quality by nibbling a portion of the hooking
ear to see how it tastes, thus making selection while the main ear
develops. Allow the seed-making plants to stand until they have fully
dried. If the season is not long enough, harvest the ears shortly after
the first frost. Pull back the wrappers without tearing them off and plait
them, hanging the ears to finish drying.

To produce truly pure seed of a corn variety, out of 100 or more plants
requires a patch of at least 1,600 square feet (150 sq m). Solomon has
also produced acceptable OP sweet corn seed from as few as 50 desirable
plants, though this risks narrowing the gene pool excessively.

## Calorie Production and Staple Crops

Corn is one of the few garden crops that can meaningfully contribute to
caloric self-sufficiency. However, Solomon emphasizes that producing cereal
grains requires five to ten times as much land as growing an equivalent
caloric value of potatoes. The healthful potato is really the thing for
serious calorie production in a home garden. See
[[solomon-garden-planning-calorie-production]].
