# Community Services Financing and Governance

A [[permaculture]] village requires robust financial and governance systems to fund and manage public services, infrastructure, and community assets. Mollison outlines how the trust-based land ownership model generates sustainable revenue streams and how a Community Services Council provides democratic governance of shared resources.

## Financing Through Trust Assets

### The 30% Commons Model

When a village allots **30% of all titles to the village trust** (Trust A), the sale of these titles generates substantial capital for community development. This model ensures:

- **All necessary public services are funded** through interest and capital from the vested titles
- **Residents effectively pay nothing for land** over a period of 20 years
- **Capital or interest becomes available** as soon as sales exceed 60% of total titles

### Capital Deployment Timeline

At about year 5 of development, when residents have a clear idea of future needs, the trust capital itself can be deployed to install **wealth-producing assets** for village employment, including:

- Glasshouses and commercial [[mushroom-growing-facilities]]
- Computer and communications infrastructure
- Machinery and manufacturing equipment

### Credit Union Integration

If a credit union has been established, much of the trust capital can be transferred to a loans account, allowing residents to draw on it at **low interest** for local occupational development. Conscientious use of a credit union by village residents greatly increases capital flow to [[mollison-village-enterprises-occupations]]. Capital may also be available for housing loans, though a **building cooperative** is more effective for this purpose.

### Morale Effects

The result of having capital and interest flowing into the Trust is that **village morale is greatly increased**, with every resident seeing long-term plans fairly rapidly achieved. Well-managed, the capital should actually increase over time, providing a growing annual fund for village use.

## Trust Revenue Sources

The Trust needs ongoing income for maintenance of roads, fences, water supply, fire control, and other site infrastructure. Revenue can be raised through:

- **Lease charges** on utility plots and common properties
- **Hourly rates** for lease, use, or hire of machines and facilities
- **10% levy on net profits** of locally-funded cooperatives

The 10% cooperative levy mirrors the **Mondragon cooperative system** in Spain, which uses the same percentage to fund their banks, schools, and research facilities. This levy applies only to net profits of trust-funded cooperatives—not to gross revenue.

## Community Services Council

A **Community Services Council** should be elected at any new community or village with common lands. Its mandate encompasses:

### Responsibilities
- Administer policy on publicly owned or common assets
- Collect lease monies on utility plots
- Administer funds for schools and medical services
- Ensure that rates are used in maintaining roads, water supplies, and other public services

### Scope of Community Services
Community services may encompass the following areas:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Safety | Fire control services |
| [[integrated-land-management-and-polyculture-design]] | Leasing of common properties for business use |
| Recreation | Public reserves and sports grounds |
| Health | Ambulance and health services; cemetery administration |
| Education | Educational facilities and programs |
| Social | Community centre use and hire |
| Utilities | Power and water supply |
| Forestry | [[mollison-course-coppice-forest-management]] |
| Economic | Tourism and promotion (if desired) |

### Composition and Structure

The Council should be comprised of people **actively involved** in these service areas—not an uninvolved group. The organizational structure works as follows:

- Each area of action has a **basically independent management sub-group**
- Sub-groups report to Council regularly
- Each sub-group appoints one representative to Council
- Council meets monthly, or even less frequently
- Sub-groups handle everyday business within their budget and allotted areas

### Decision-Making Process

- Council can call for and act on submissions or reports relating to specific policies and strategies
- Sub-groups can raise their own funds in addition to accessing public funds
- Sub-groups may have an active business management role in income-producing areas
- Sub-groups supply workers to carry out income-producing businesses

## Income Models for Community Services

Several models can fund community services, each with distinct advantages:

### 1. Levy (Flat Rate)

A yearly levy on each family or property collected by the Community Council. This is probably in addition to normal shire council rates and may create hardship on some households.

**Advantages**: Simple to administer, predictable revenue.
**Disadvantages**: Regressive—creates equal burden regardless of earning power.

### 2. Tithe (Profit-Based)

Individuals, groups, and businesses tithe **10% of net profit** to public services. All [[mondragon-cooperatives]] follow this model, funding schools, libraries, churches, and recreational grounds.

**Advantages**: Sliding scale based on earning power; fairer distribution.
**Disadvantages**: Requires profit reporting; income fluctuates with business conditions.

### 3. Local Currency

The Community Services Council can print its own currency, **backed by a valuable community resource** such as firewood or clean bottled water, to fund projects and new buildings.

A timber forest planted early and harvested at various stages of maturity can produce most of the income needed for subsequent years, as demonstrated by some modern towns that have developed social forestry programs.

**Advantages**: Keeps capital circulating locally; can be tailored to community needs.
**Disadvantages**: Requires community buy-in; management complexity.

### 4. User Charges (Service Fees)

Charges by the school, community centre, and health services can offset costs on a "user-pays" basis for tuition, facility use, medical needs, and similar services.

**Advantages**: Those who use services pay for them.
**Disadvantages**: Covers only non-universal services; should be used in conjunction with another model.

### Recommended Approach

Mollison suggests that user charges should be used **in conjunction with** another model, as they cover only those public facilities which not everybody uses. Universally used services should be proportionally rated across all residents.

## Balancing Income and Expenses

The Services Council needs to balance three types of service areas:

1. **Income-producing** — Commercial leases, retail operations, forestry
2. **State-aided or tax-immune** — Educational facilities, health services
3. **Income-consuming** — Road maintenance, fire control, reserve management

The Council must allot land and assets for income to service groups and ensure that common property and rights are fairly assigned and well managed. This balancing act requires regular review and adjustment as the village matures and its needs evolve.
