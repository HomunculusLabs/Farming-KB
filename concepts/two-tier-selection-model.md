## Two-Tier Selection Model

The two-tier selection model (also called the two-step selection model) describes how plant root-associated bacterial communities are assembled through two sequential filtering stages: first by [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]] are shaped primarily by abiotic soil properties including pH, nitrogen content, salinity, organic carbon, water content, and texture. These edaphic factors determine the available pool of microbial taxa from which the plant can recruit associates. The soil acts as the primary inoculum source for the rhizosphere and endorhiza.

**Tier 2 — Host Genotype Selection (Plant Filtering):** As microbes transition from the rhizosphere into the root interior (endorhiza), a second round of selection occurs driven by host plant genetics. The plant exerts genotype-dependent selection through root exudates, immune responses, and tissue-level compatibility. This second tier acts primarily on community structure (relative abundance) rather than composition (presence/absence).

Under this model, community composition across sample types is determined mainly by soil type, while community structure within endorhiza samples is determined mainly by host cultivar. The model predicts that rhizosphere and endorhiza microbiota should be soil-derived, with the rhizosphere representing an intermediate filtering stage enriched by root exudates (rhizodeposition).

## Historical Development

The conceptual foundations of the two-tier model were developed through several key studies:

1. **Garbeva et al. (2004)** established that microbial diversity in soil is shaped by selection from both plant and soil type, with implications for disease suppressiveness. This work laid the groundwork for understanding how edaphic and biotic factors interact to structure root-associated communities.

2. **Berg and Smalla (2009)** demonstrated that plant species and soil type cooperatively shape the structure and function of microbial communities in the rhizosphere. Their work showed that both factors act simultaneously rather than independently.

3. **Bulgarelli et al. (2012, 2013)** provided the first comprehensive description of the two-step selection process for root-inhabiting bacterial microbiota in [[edaphic-factors-soil-microbial-community-structure]]:

### Taxonomic Shifts

The model predicts broad changes in phylum-level taxon abundance associated with the progressive filtering from soil to root. Specifically:
- **Acidobacteria** should dramatically decrease in abundance within the endosphere relative to bulk soil and rhizosphere
- **Proteobacteria** should increase in abundance within the endorhiza
- **Actinobacteria** should also increase within the endorhiza

These predictions have been consistently validated across multiple plant systems, including Cannabis, Arabidopsis, Populus, and various crop species. The decrease in Acidobacteria is particularly robust and is thought to reflect the oligotrophic nature of many Acidobacteria, which are outcompeted in the relatively nutrient-rich root environment.

### OTU Sharing Patterns

Endorhiza communities should share more OTUs with their own native soil than with a foreign soil in which the same plant strain is grown. This prediction was confirmed in Cannabis: endorhiza samples shared significantly more OTUs with their own soil (mean = 2934) than with a different soil (mean = 2162), demonstrating that the endophytic community is primarily recruited from the local soil pool.

### Beta Diversity Relationships

The model predicts that:
- Rhizosphere communities should be intermediate between bulk soil and endorhiza in terms of community composition
- [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]], such as cannabinoids in Cannabis or alkaloids in other species, which may act as additional selection agents at the root interface
- The fact that the model was developed primarily from studies of herbaceous plants in temperate agricultural systems; its applicability to woody perennials, tropical species, and non-agricultural plants requires further investigation

## Future Research Directions

Several key areas need development to advance the two-tier model from a conceptual framework to a predictive tool:

Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying host genotype-dependent selection is a major priority. Root exudate profiling across cultivars, combined with metatranscriptomic analysis of both plant and microbial responses during colonization, could reveal the specific chemical signals that drive tier-two selection. Additionally, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) linking plant genetic loci to microbiome traits could identify the host genes responsible for microbiome structuring.

The model should also be extended to incorporate temporal dynamics. Root microbiomes are not static — they change with plant development, seasonal cycles, and environmental perturbations. A dynamic version of the two-tier model that accounts for successional patterns would better capture the reality of plant-microbiome interactions across the full growing season.

Finally, integrating fungal communities into the model is essential. The original formulation focuses on bacterial communities, but mycorrhizal fungi and fungal saprotrophs are major players in the root zone. A multi-kingdom version of the two-tier model would provide a more complete picture of root microbiome assembly.
