# Phototropism in [[psilocybe-cubensis]]

The role of light in directing growth and development of Psilocybe cubensis basidiocarps, including basidiocarp initiation, stipe elongation, spore formation, and geotropic sensitivity. Light is necessary in at least two phases of the P. cubensis life cycle, though its mechanism may be indirect, operating through evaporative demand rather than a dedicated photoreceptor pathway. See also [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-gravitropism]], [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-hyphal-orientation]], tropisms psilocybe cubensis phototropism, and [[fungal-resource-translocation-in-mycelial-networks]].

## Light Requirements in Development

P. cubensis requires light at multiple developmental stages:

- Basidiocarp initiation (Badham, 1980): fruiting bodies do not form in complete darkness
- Spore formation and geotropic sensitivity: negative geotropism at Stage 2 requires some light
- The light requirement for geotropism may be indirect -- light may be needed only because it is necessary for spore production
- At least four different light responses have been described for mushrooms generally (Eger-Hummel, 1980)
- P. cubensis is noted as similar to Coprinus congregatus in this respect
- Light exerts some inhibitory effect upon stipe elongation
- This inhibitory effect is consistent with etiolation patterns seen across basidiomycetes
- The balance between light stimulation of development and inhibition of elongation determines final morphology

## Light as Evaporative Stimulus

Badham proposed that light's role in tropisms may be primarily physical rather than hormonal:

- Light heats the mushroom surface, increasing evaporation of water or other gases
- If strong relative to wind and humidity, light could become the dominant factor controlling evaporative demand
- Differential evaporative demand across the stipe could cause cells to grow at different rates
- Unequal cell expansion rates would produce curvature toward or away from the illuminated side
- The flux density used in experiments (~0.002 cal/cm2/min) was approximately 1/10 of field conditions
- At this low intensity, light likely exerted minimal direct phototropic effect
- However, small quantities of light could trigger hormonic responses, potentially changing the picture entirely
- If the phototropic mechanism is simply drying of the illuminated stipe portion, low-intensity light would have minimal effect
- If instead light triggers a hormonal cascade, even low levels could be significant
- The wind tunnel experiments could not distinguish between these two mechanisms

## Inhibitory Effect on Stipe Elongation

Light exerts some inhibitory effect upon stipe elongation in P. cubensis:

- This is consistent with the general pattern in basidiomycetes where etiolation (elongation in darkness) occurs
- Light serves to regulate growth rate, preventing excessive elongation
- Mushrooms grown in continuous light or higher light intensities tend to have shorter, stockier stipes
- The balance between light stimulation of development and light inhibition of elongation determines final morphology
- In Badham's experiments, the 12-hour photoperiod provided sufficient light for development without excessive growth suppression
- Cool white fluorescent at 2685 lux was used for primordia formation, then 60cm distance in the wind tunnel
- The lower intensity in the wind tunnel may have reduced any direct phototropic effect

## Light Directionality in Experiments

Badham tested light from both above and below in wind tunnel experiments:

- When light came from above with horizontal airflow, stipes initially grew into the wind (anemotropic response dominant in Stage 1)
- When light came from below, the same anemotropic response was observed initially
- At Stage 2, negative geotropism became dominant regardless of light direction
- The fact that the same geotropic response occurred with light from above or below suggests light direction per se is not the primary tropic cue at this stage
- When basidiocarps were rotated parallel to the airflow under constant illumination from above, growth and pileus expansion both occurred horizontally
- This horizontal growth under rotation confirms that gravity (not light direction) is the primary orienting stimulus at Stage 2
- Light from above vs below made no difference to the geotropic response, ruling out a directional phototropic mechanism

## Light, Spore Production, and Geotropism

A key finding links light, spore formation, and geotropic behavior:

- Fertile basidiocarps showed negative geotropic recurvation at Stage 2
- Sterile (sporeless) mutants did NOT show this recurvation
- Cultures kept in darkness produced few spores and also lacked the geotropic response
- This strongly suggests the geotropic response depends on a substance produced in the gills during spore formation
- Light is necessary only indirectly -- for spore production, not for the geotropic mechanism itself
- The same pattern was observed regardless of whether light came from above or below
- This decoupling of light direction from geotropic response argues against a direct phototropic mechanism at Stage 2
- The few spores formed in darkness correlated with the absence of geotropic sensitivity

## Mushroom Hormone Hypothesis

Multiple researchers have proposed mushroom hormones involved in tropic responses:

- Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963) postulated hormones regulating [[mushroom-tropisms]]
- Decapitation experiments linked these hormones to the lamellae (gills)
- [[agar]] diffusates from gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus (Hagimoto and Konishi, 1960)
- Some mushrooms contain higher plant hormones (IAA, gibberellin-like substances)
- However, no evidence suggests these regulate mushroom growth (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961; Pegg, 1973)
- The gill-produced substance in P. cubensis remains unidentified
- It may modify the evaporative demand mechanism, causing the mushroom to respond primarily to gravity
- The hormone hypothesis is supported by the fact that removing the pileus alters tropic responses

## Comparison with Other Fungi

Different mushroom species show varied phototropic responses:

- Coprinus congregatus is similar to P. cubensis in having multiple light responses
- Phycomyces blakesleeanus shows well-characterized phototropism mediated by dedicated photoreceptors
- [[agaricus-bisporus]] shows both phototropic and geotropic responses during development (Schwalb and Shanler, 1974)
- Blue light photomorphogenesis is documented across multiple fungal taxa (Eger-Hummel, 1980)
- Polyporus brumalis shows a change in tropisms during stipe development (Plunkett, 1961)
- [[schizophyllum-commune]] shows phototropic and geotropic responses in both normal and mutant fruitbodies
- Carlile (1970) reviewed fungal photobiology broadly, noting diverse light responses across taxa

## Practical Cultivation Implications

Understanding phototropism in P. cubensis has practical relevance:

- Light is essential for fruiting initiation but quantity needed is low
- Uneven lighting can cause stipes to bend, reducing aesthetic quality of harvested mushrooms
- Light may be more important for spore production and subsequent geotropic correction than for direct directional growth
- The evaporative demand model suggests temperature and humidity interact with light in controlling growth direction
- Higher light levels can suppress excessive stipe elongation, producing more compact fruiting bodies
- Complete darkness should be avoided as it prevents both fruiting initiation and spore production
- Light quality may matter: Badham used "cool white" fluorescent; blue light is the active spectrum for many fungal photomorphogenic responses
- For indoor cultivation, uniform overhead lighting produces the most aesthetically pleasing results
- The 12-hour photoperiod used in experiments is a reasonable standard for cultivation
- Multi-directional lighting from multiple sources can reduce directional bending
- Light intensity above the minimum threshold for fruiting does not proportionally increase potency
- The interaction between light and other environmental variables (wind, humidity) in controlling growth direction suggests that optimal cultivation requires balancing all factors rather than maximizing any single one
- Leach (1971) provided a practical guide to effects of visible and ultraviolet light on fungi that remains a useful reference for cultivators seeking to optimize lighting regimes
