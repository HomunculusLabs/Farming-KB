# Arid Landscape Permaculture

Arid landscapes present unique challenges and opportunities for [[permaculture-design]]. Mollison's treatment of desert design in the Permaculture Design Course reveals a sophisticated reading of arid landforms that turns apparent limitations into design assets. The key insight is that water, not food, is the limiting factor -- and that desert landforms naturally concentrate and store water. In total, desert landscapes are angular, and actively eroding. Humid areas, and especially those clothed with grasslands and forests, have softer and more rounded outlines.

## Reading Desert Landforms

Reading the desert mosaic requires noting several key factors: the dominant **PROCESS** (whether wind, water, or infiltration is active locally), **ROCK AND SOIL TYPE**, **ASPECT** (even slight shading by hills promotes growth), **FIRE FREQUENCY**, and the **DATE OF LAST HEAVY RAIN** -- more than 12mm triggers specific plant recruitment.

The major desert landform types include: **MOUNTAINS** with complex long valleys and shear-sided gorges; **ISOLATED GRANITIC DOMES** (inselbergs); **SCARP AND PEDIMENT** landscapes with mesas and wadis (box canyons); **BADLANDS** of complex eroded softer sediments; and **BASIN AND RANGE** topography. True desert pavement is classified as **ERG** (sandy desert, including sandplain near-level sand sheets and dune types), **HAMADA** (rock/boulder pavement), and **REG** (gravel surfaces). See [[permaculture-slope-analysis-and-reading]] for systematic methods.

The classic arid landscape has a characteristic profile: a receding scarp (cliff) traveling geologically backward, with a down-drop, a back slope, and a pediment (the flat area at the base). The scarps are composed of soft rock capped by a thin layer of iron-hard laterite. Buttes and mesas are residual remnants left behind as the scarp retreats.

The most productive location in the desert is at the base of the scarp, where water splashes off the rock and cuts notches into the pediment. These notches collect water and support the richest vegetation. The most satisfactory place to locate a settlement is in box canyons up in the cliffs -- a single fence across the entrance keeps out feral animals, and the cliffs provide shade, water collection, and thermal mass for temperature regulation. See [[mollison-arid-settlement-home-garden-design]].

## Water Strategies

### Natural Water Features
In arid landscapes, water is found at specific locations: just before the drop-off at the top of escarpments, where swirling water creates permanent holes; in the wadi floors after rains, where water soaks into the sand and persists for months or years; and in sand itself, which stores approximately 50% of its bulk as water without evaporation.

Mollison describes building sand-filled tanks that store water in the spaces between sand grains -- it cannot evaporate and other organisms cannot access it. This is an ancient desert technique.

### Silt Traps and Absorption Pans
The primary water strategy is to create silt traps and absorption pans across the wadi floors. Brushwood fences serve as low diversion banks that spread flood waters over the desert surface, allowing absorption. Mollison describes a cascading system of absorption pans connected by siphons: when one pan fills, it overflows to the next, charging successive areas until the entire system is saturated.

### Scarp Water Development
On the upper plateau, erect stone or cement dams across gentle valleys. Cut gutters across the scarp top above useful caves. Clear silt from, deepen, and seal scour-holes as tank storages. Cut gutters away from the main valley head to lessen torrent concentration in any one place. Make concrete or stone chutes into excavated pools at the cliff base. See [[swale-design-construction]] for related contour techniques.

### Wadi Development
Erect strong rock walls across the wadi floor, letting silt fields build up behind. For wide wadis, leave the central channel open and build fields to the sides. When flood spreads to the lower plain, form broad walls of earth and stone to soak water into contained fields, stabilized with unpalatable shrubs. See [[permaculture-earthworks-and-water-harvesting]].

### Nabatean Systems
The ancient [[mollison-arid-lands-design-and-erosion-control]] of the Negev hold 80-90% of run-off behind dykes, spreading to lower dykes across plains. For every irrigated hectare, 20 hectares of run-off surface are reserved as catchment. The goal is to absorb about 0.5-1 m of water into each field. Upper reservoirs are released after torrents cease to top up the system.

### Dams and Lagoons
Permanent lagoons can be developed in large wadis where natural conditions allow. Mollison observed several naturally permanent lagoons supporting mature trees, indicating that with proper design, permanent water bodies are possible even in arid regions. Modest and deep storages are more likely to hold water all year than large shallow storages. The run-off to storage ratio must be 20-30:1 in deserts.

## Mountain and Rock Feature Strategies

### Residuals, Domes and Inselbergs
Insulated granitic domes serve as very solid run-off areas, some covering many tens of hectares. Large trees and humus soils develop at the base of cliffs on the shade side. A ratio of 1 acre of field to 20 hectares of rock run-off is typical, with floods led via fluming to fields. Very small rock slabs (1-10 hectares) are useful for gutter and tank systems supporting a hectare or two of trees.

### Fold Mountains
Fold mountains are the most extensive features in many deserts. **Synclines** erode to canoe shapes. **Anticlines** form whalebacks, with rivers forming on their spines creating valleys. A dam at the exit of such a river creates a freshwater lake. This connects to [[keyline-design]] principles for valley floor water management.

### Palisades
Palisades of harder rock running along slopes like sub-contours create a striking vegetation contrast: growth is taller, greener, and denser on the upslope side because run-off is slowed and forced to infiltrate. These natural sub-contour features can be extended with [[permaculture-earthworks-guide]] to expand productive zones.

## Desert Microclimates
Dwellings should be sited under cliffs: on the shade side in hot deserts, and where winter sun enters but summer sun is excluded. Sixteen feet into rock, temperature variation is negligible. Gutters along cliff tops redirect rain away from dwelling faces. See [[permaculture-climate-strategies]] and [[permaculture-climate-zones]].

### Growth Potential
Given water, desert soils are unexploited and mineral-rich. Growth response is extremely fast: fruiting grapes from cuttings within 15 months. Suitable crops include citrus, grapes, apricots, pistachios, almonds, and melons. Dates grow well in soaked desert sands. See [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]] and [[desert-gardening]] for species selection. Combating [[desertification]] through these strategies is a primary goal.

## Island Permaculture Connections
Mollison draws parallels between desert and island [[permaculture-design]] -- both require extreme water conservation, careful wind protection, and strategies for preventing nutrient loss. On coral atolls, the key technique is breaking through the calcareous platen (hardpan) to access brackish groundwater, then filling the holes with humus to create growing conditions. Ring gardens with central drip irrigation maximize growing area within minimal water use. See [[island-permaculture-design]].

Coastal defense systems on islands parallel desert wind protection: multiple rows of vegetation from salt-tolerant front-line species (Pemphis, Tournefortia, Scaevola) to productive palms behind. Mangroves serve as the ultimate land-sea interface, providing the highest biomass turnover of any system and serving as nurseries for offshore fisheries.
