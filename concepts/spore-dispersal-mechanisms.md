## Animal Dispersal

Many fungi have evolved to exploit animals as dispersal vectors. Truffles and
other hypogeous fungi produce underground fruiting bodies with powerful
aromas that attract mammals. Animals dig up and consume the fungi, and the
spores pass through their digestive tracts intact, deposited elsewhere in
nutrient-rich fecal matter. This strategy is so effective that some truffle
species produce pheromones mimicking mammalian sex hormones.

Cordyceps and related fungi parasitize insects, directing the infected host
to climb to an elevated position before death, where the fruiting body can
optimally release spores into air currents. This zombie ant behavior is one
of the most dramatic examples of behavioral manipulation in the natural
world and has evolved independently multiple times within the fungal lineage.

## Water Dispersal

Aquatic and semi-aquatic fungi often use water currents for spore transport.
Some produce specialized spores with adhesive surfaces or hook-like
appendages that snag on vegetation or substrates in flowing water. Rain
splash dispersal is also important for many terrestrial fungi, where
raindrops striking fruiting bodies eject spores in small droplets that can
travel significant distances from the parent organism.

## Dispersal Distance and Success

The probability of any individual spore landing on a suitable substrate is
astronomically low. Fungi compensate by producing enormous numbers of spores.
A single large puffball (Calvatia gigantea) can contain an estimated seven
trillion spores. A typical agaric mushroom releases millions to billions of
spores over its fruiting period. This reproductive strategy is essentially
a vast statistical bet: produce enough propagules, and some will find their
way to favorable conditions for germination and growth.
