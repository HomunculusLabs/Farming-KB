## Foliar Tea Application and Plant Nutrient Regulation

## Plant-Controlled Nutrient Uptake

One of the most important principles in biological agriculture is that **the plant controls its own nutrient uptake**. This stands in contrast to conventional approaches where nutrients are pushed onto plants regardless of their actual needs.

### The Foliar Nutrient Mechanism

When [[compost]] tea is applied to leaf surfaces, the following sequence occurs:

1. **If the plant needs nutrients**, it pumps sugars out onto the leaf surface
2. These sugars feed the microorganisms living on the leaf surface
3. The growing microorganisms **elevate CO₂** levels at the leaf surface
4. Elevated CO₂ causes **stomata to open**
5. Open stomata allow foliar nutrients to enter the plant

**If the plant does not need nutrients**, it does not pump sugars out, the organisms don't grow, CO₂ doesn't increase, stomata stay closed, and fewer nutrients enter. The plant is effectively in control.

### Why This Matters

This self-regulating mechanism is one of the best features of restoring a normal biological community to growing systems:

- Plants only take up nutrients they actually need
- Over-fertilization risk is greatly reduced
- The system functions the way it naturally evolved to function
- Plant health returns to a [[fukuoka-mutual-cancellation-natural-equilibrium]]

### Compost Tea Application Guidelines

The organisms on leaves enhance nutrient uptake, so it's important not to push too high a concentration of nutrients during foliar applications. The goal is to **feed the bacteria and fungi in the tea**, not overwhelm the plant with nutrients. The tea organisms then facilitate the plant taking up what it needs from its environment.

### A Note on Nitrogen Timing

Pushing nitrate and amino sugars during flowering or fruit set can cause problems:

- High nitrogen at flowering pushes **vegetative growth instead of reproductive growth**
- This can reduce flower formation and fruit set in many crops
- A single compost tea application typically doesn't add enough nitrogen to cause this issue
- The principle is: feed the biology, not the plant directly — let the plant regulate its own nitrogen uptake

## Pleomorphism in Soil Microorganisms

### Definition

Pleomorphism refers to the ability of microorganisms to assume different forms or appearances throughout their life cycles. This has implications for how we identify and count soil organisms.

### Colony Morphology vs. Individual Morphology

An important distinction exists between:

- **Colony morphology** — the appearance of a bacterial colony on a petri plate (size, shape, color, texture, slime quality)
- **Individual morphology** — the shape and size of individual bacterial cells viewed under a microscope

**Pseudomonads** are famous for having many different colony morphologies on plates. However, when bacteria are pulled from colonies with different appearances and examined under a microscope, they often prove to be the same bacterium. The slime quality may differ, but the bacterial cell shape does not.

**Bacillus species** can vary in cell length depending on their food resource, which can lead to counting one species as two based on cell size alone.

### The Real Problem: Type II Errors

The greater challenge with morphological identification is the opposite problem — **different species looking identical**:

- You cannot differentiate Azotobacter species from one another by microscopy — they look the same
- Type II errors (false negatives) are far more prevalent than type I errors (false positives)
- We **underestimate** species diversity using morphology, not overestimate it

This is a known limitation, and as long as it's understood, the morphological direct-count approach remains useful — certainly more useful than plate counts, and far less expensive than [[biodiversity-of-fungi-pcr-molecular-methods-fungal-diversity]].

### Molecular Approaches

As molecular methods become economically feasible, they will supplement morphological approaches:

- Molecular techniques are already used to assess **[[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]]** capability
- Many pathogenic bacteria likely have multiple names because they were named based on the plant they cause disease on, not their actual genetic identity
- The same organism may be pathogenic on one plant species and benign on another
- [[biodiversity-fungal-molecular-identification-dna-barcoding]] is the future but still requires millions of dollars of research before becoming practical for routine [[soil-testing]]

### Equipment and Cost Barriers

As of the research period covered:

- The machine for probe replication and fluorescent excitation identification costs approximately **$190,000**
- Significant problems with repeatability remain
- PCR, isolation, probe development, and specificity testing are still required
- Methods are advancing rapidly for human pathogens, but soil organism testing lags behind

## Brix Testing Protocol for Compost Tea

A structured experimental protocol for testing compost tea effects on brix readings:

### Step 1: Prepare Teas
- Make a **good tea (#1)** with high organism diversity and biomass
- Make a **poor tea (#2)** with low organism counts or poor diversity

### Step 2: Establish Baselines
- Measure brix in four separate plots before any application
- Ensure plots are comparable in soil type, crop, and growing conditions

### Step 3: Apply Treatments
- **Plot 1**: Apply good tea (#1)
- **Plot 2**: Apply poor tea (#2)
- **Plot 3**: Apply water (control)
- **Plot 4**: Apply nutrient mix (conventional comparison)

### Step 4: Verify Application
- Check leaves to confirm tea was or was not applied correctly
- Ensure coverage was adequate on treated plots

### Step 5: Monitor Response
- Check brix through the remainder of the day to document different responses
- Record whether responses differ between treatments
- Repeat over multiple days to identify patterns

This protocol provides data to evaluate the relationship between compost tea quality and plant brix response, regardless of which mechanism (direct nutrient uptake, stomatal opening via CO₂, microbial stimulation, etc.) is ultimately proven correct.

## Cucumber and Fungal Requirements

Cucumbers prefer fairly bacterial soil but still need some fungi for [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]]. If compost lacks adequate fungal content:

1. Obtain **Alaska [[humus]]** (a naturally fungal-dominated material) to supplement the compost
2. Place the compost in a plastic tub
3. Bring moisture to approximately **50%** (it should barely drip a drop when squeezed hard)
4. Cover and keep at room temperature (warm but not hot)
5. Within several days, thick white fungal strands should be visible growing **throughout the compost**

This pre-incubation step ensures the compost tea will have adequate fungal biomass for [[cervantes-damping-off-seedling-disease-prevention]].
