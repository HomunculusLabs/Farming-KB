**The Devonport Model** — The borough of Devonport in Auckland, New Zealand,
operates a total solid waste-recycling system that manages to return a cash
benefit to households, as opposed to the cash payment or rates paid to councils
in non-recycling areas. This system demonstrates that comprehensive recycling is
not merely environmentally responsible but can be economically self-sustaining
and even profitable for the community it serves. The Devonport model provides a
proven template that [[permaculture]] villages and existing communities can
adapt to their own circumstances.

**Colour-Coded Collection Calendar** — The first key feature of the Devonport
system is that the borough issues an [[fukuoka-rice-barley-annual-calendar]]
that is colour-coded by waste category. Collections pick up only one category of
waste on any given day — clean glass one day, metal another, tyres another, then
paper, organic waste, oils, and so on. No other categories and no mixed loads
are collected. This simple scheduling mechanism ensures that recycling begins at
the point of waste generation, with consumers separating their wastes into
designated categories before collection. The colour-coded calendar makes the
system easy to follow and ensures compliance without requiring extensive
enforcement.

**Waste Disposal Site Sorting** — The second key feature is that at the waste
disposal site, loads must also be in a single category. Here, wood is sorted
into useful wood for a community woodwork centre, firewood issued to elderly
people, and chipping or mulch wood. All organics are composted using a small
tractor windrow system, and all oils are collected for resale as lubrication
oils after filtration. [[compost]] is sold, as is mulch, and any surplus
[[using-accumulators-in-compost-and-mulch]] is used to carpet a "ziggurat" — an
ascending spiral ramp constructed from broken pipe, brick, clay, concrete, and
clean fill. As the ziggurat ascends, community [[cotter-natural-pest-control-disease-management|plant disease management]] follow the fill, transforming waste materials into productive
growing space.

**Supervised Waste Sites** — The waste disposal site is actively supervised,
with loads directed and mixed waste sorted on arrival. Saleable or recyclable
items are grouped in clearly marked areas, making it easy for community members
to access recovered materials. This supervision is essential to the system's
success — without it, the separation discipline established by the collection
calendar would break down at the point of deposit.

**The Ziggurat Concept** — The ziggurat is an innovative permaculture element
that transforms construction and demolition waste into productive land. Built as
an ascending spiral ramp from broken pipe, brick, clay, concrete, and clean fill
materials, the ziggurat serves as both a waste disposal solution and a garden
structure. As each level is completed and stabilised, it is covered with compost
and mulch from the recycling operation, and community organic gardens are
established on the resulting growing surfaces. The spiral form maximises the
growing area relative to the footprint, provides good drainage and sun exposure
on multiple aspects, and creates a visually distinctive community landmark.

**Integration with Village Enterprise** — Recycling systems should be integrated
with village economic activity. The Devonport model generates direct income
through the sale of compost, mulch, recovered timber, and filtered oils. The
community woodwork centre uses recovered timber for furniture and building
projects. Firewood distribution to elderly residents serves a social welfare
function while consuming a waste stream that would otherwise require disposal.
The ziggurat gardens produce food for the community while sequestering waste
materials in productive use. Each waste stream is matched with a productive or
economic use, eliminating the concept of "waste" entirely from the community's
material flows.

**[[permaculture-principles]] Applied** — The [[devonport-recycling-system]]
embodies core [[mollisonian-permaculture-principles]]. Every element serves
multiple functions: the collection calendar educates residents while ensuring
separation; the supervised waste site sorts materials while providing community
access to recovered goods; the [[composting]] operation processes organic waste
while producing saleable soil amendments; the ziggurat disposes of inert
construction waste while creating growing space. The system produces no outputs
that cannot be absorbed by other elements in the community, achieving a high
degree of closure in material cycles.

**Application to New Villages** — For new permaculture villages, Mollison
recommends establishing recycling infrastructure from the outset. The
[[permaculture-village-development]] plan should designate a waste sorting and
processing area, include a community woodwork centre, plan for composting and
mulching operations, and identify sites for ziggurat construction. By building
these systems into the initial infrastructure, the village avoids the costly
retrofit required when conventional waste disposal becomes established. The
recycling operation can also serve as an early income source for the village, as
the sale of recovered materials and compost can generate revenue before more
complex enterprises are established.

**Beyond Devonport** — While the Devonport model is presented as the primary
example, Mollison's broader principles suggest that any community recycling
system should share certain features: mandated source separation through simple
scheduling, supervised sorting at disposal points, productive use of every waste
stream, integration with community enterprises, and economic return to
households. The specific categories of waste will vary by community depending on
local industries, consumption patterns, and available markets for recovered
materials, but the structural framework of the system is universally applicable.
