Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a vast network of
branching filamentous cells called hyphae. While mushrooms are the most visible
and familiar fungal structures, they are merely the reproductive organs —
transient fruiting bodies produced by the mycelium for [[cannabis-chemical-burn-vs-deficiency]] of respiration.
The energy captured from this process fuels all chemical reactions in the hypha.
Most of the cytoplasm is water, but it is suffused with a skeleton of protein
strands — the cytoskeleton — resembling a spider web in its multitude of
interconnections and sensitivity to disturbance. Some strands act as rails on
which new cellular materials are transported to the growing hyphal tips.

## The Plasma Membrane and Cell Wall

The cytoplasm is contained by a plasma membrane that controls the entry and
release of chemicals through integral channel and pump proteins. Some transported
molecules carry electrical charges (ions), and their flux electrifies the
membrane, effectively turning the hypha into a microscopic battery. By adjusting
the exchange of chemicals between cell and environment, the membrane regulates
the mixture of dissolved salts, sugars, and other molecules essential for hyphal
function.

The outer surface of the plasma membrane is sheathed with sugar-based
polysaccharides woven into a cell wall. This wall protects the hyphal contents
from abrasion, screens out poisons, and by restricting inflation of the
cytoplasm, allows the cell to become pressurized. This internal pressure —

## Invasive Tip Growth

Fungi grow by extending the tips of their hyphae and by branching to initiate
new hyphae. This process is fundamentally different from the growth of plants
or animals. The hyphal tip is a specialized zone where new cell wall material is
continuously synthesized and deposited, allowing the filament to push forward
into the substrate. Turgor pressure within the hypha provides the mechanical
force, while enzymes secreted at the tip dissolve and digest the surrounding
material.

This invasive growth is a uniquely fungal capability. Fungi flourish by
burrowing into solid substances and transforming them into food. They penetrate
the toughest leaves and woody plant tissues, skin, bone, and every other animal
part. Some species even grow in granite. A fungus that normally infects rice
leaves will, when deliberately starved, punch holes through bulletproof vest
material in its futile search for nourishment.

## Colony and Mycelium Formation

As hyphae extend and branch, they form a maze of filaments whose pattern
resembles a series of river deltas. This collective structure is the colony, or
mycelium — [[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]] of the fungus. Most often, hyphal growth is
invasive, so mycelia develop inside their food source (substrate). In
mushroom-forming basidiomycetes, the mycelium is submerged in soil or rotting
wood beneath the visible fruiting bodies. In pathogenic species, mycelia
penetrate host tissues.

## Growth Patterns and Fairy Rings

When food is plentiful, the mycelium tends to adopt a torus (doughnut) shape,
spreading outward from its point of origin and leaving an ever-widening circle
of dead and dying cells at its core. This distinctive growth pattern is
responsible for the creation of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] as it goes.
