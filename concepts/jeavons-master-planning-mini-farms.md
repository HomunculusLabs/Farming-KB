# Master Planning and Mini-Farm Design

[[jeavons-grow-biointensive-method-overview]] sustainable mini-farming is a whole-system approach to food production. The art of putting theory into a garden plan requires understanding crop timing, space allocation, soil fertility cycles, and dietary goals. No book can make gardening foolproof — the process involves real learning and experimentation, and that is part of what makes it satisfying.

## The Whole System Principle

The GROW BIOINTENSIVE method is a whole system, and its components must be used together for optimum effect. Deep soil preparation, compost, close spacing, [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]], carbon-efficient crops, calorie-efficient crops, open-pollinated seeds, and [[ultra-low-cost-integrated-farming]] system design all work synergistically. If any component is missing — particularly the recycling of all wastes and the growing of sufficient [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] — the high yields can rapidly deplete the soil.

This means that planning a mini-farm is not simply deciding what vegetables to plant where. It is designing a closed-loop system that produces food, carbon, calories, seeds, and soil fertility simultaneously.

## Area Allocation for Sustainability

To mini-farm sustainably, Jeavons recommends dividing the growing area approximately as follows:

- **60%** in carbon-and-calorie crops — dual-purpose seed and grain crops (wheat, rye, corn, oats, barley, amaranth, quinoa) that produce both dietary calories and carbonaceous compost material. Legumes (fava beans, bush beans, cowpeas) should be interplanted with these crops to supply the nitrogen needed for good compost.
- **30%** in special root diet crops — potatoes, burdock, garlic, parsnips, Jerusalem artichokes, and other root crops that produce maximum calories per unit area.
- **10%** in vegetable crops — for additional vitamins and minerals. Up to 1/4 of this area may be planted in income crops if the missing needed vitamins and minerals are provided by the remaining 3/4.

This allocation ensures that the mini-farm produces enough carbonaceous material to maintain soil fertility through compost, while also producing a complete diet.

## Crop Rotation: Companion Planting Over Time

Companion planting "over time" has been known for years as crop rotation. After properly preparing the soil, the rotation follows this pattern:

1. **Heavy feeders** — Most of the vegetables we eat (corn, tomatoes, squash, lettuce, cabbage) take large amounts of nutrients, especially nitrogen, from the soil.
2. **Heavy givers** — Nitrogen-fixing legumes (peas, beans, alfalfa, clover, vetch, fava beans) return nitrogen to the soil. Fava beans also excrete substances that help eradicate tomato wilt-causing organisms.
3. **Light feeders** — Root crops (carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, radishes) give the soil a rest before the next heavy feeder onslaught.

This rotation is a form of agricultural recycling in which humans and plants participate to return as much to the soil as has been taken out. After harvesting heavy feeders, phosphorus and potassium are returned through compost. Heavy givers return nitrogen. Light feeders require minimal nutrients, allowing the soil to rebuild.

## The Garden Year: Seasonal Planning

### Winter
- Plan the garden for the coming year.
- Order open-pollinated, untreated seeds (allow 2 months for mail delivery).
- Make flats, trellises, mini-greenhouses, and shade-netting units.

### Spring
- Plant flats so seedlings can mature while the soil is being prepared.
- Start new compost piles with weeds, grass clippings, and compost crops.
- Harvest compost crops, dig garden beds, and spread cured fall/winter compost.
- Plant cool-weather crops in early spring and warm/hot-weather crops in late spring and early summer.

### Summer
- Plant summer crops.
- Keep the garden watered and weeded.
- Harvest and enjoy the fruits of your work.
- In mild-winter areas, plant fall gardens of cool-weather crops at the end of summer.

### Fall
- Start additional compost piles with plentiful leaves and garden waste.
- Harvest summer crops.
- Plant fall/winter compost crops.

## Progressive Development: The 4-Year Plan

Jeavons recommends a progressive approach to developing a mini-farm:

### Year 1: Foundation (100-140 square feet)
Start with the easiest crops based on yields expected of a good gardener. Focus on learning [[azurescens-outdoor-bed-preparation-woodchips]], composting, and basic transplanting. Leaf lettuce, radishes, carrots, and bush beans are good starting crops.

### Year 2: Expansion (200-280 square feet)
Double the square footage and add more challenging crops. Begin incorporating [[companion-planting]] simple crop rotations. Start learning about calorie crops and compost crops.

### Year 3: Perennials (300-420 square feet)
Add a third bed. Include permanent plantings — fruit trees, herbs, strawberries, and asparagus — placed in soil that has been worked and improved for 2 years.

### Year 4: Consolidation and Diet Crops
With improved gardening skills, the vegetable crop area can often be condensed, freeing improved soil for protein crops (wheat, rye, peanuts, lentils, soybeans, rice), fiber crops (cotton, flax), or special interest crops (grape vines, blueberries, bamboo, herbs, nut trees, or forage for chickens, goats, or bees).

## Bed Layout Considerations

### Sunlight
Optimally, the garden area should have 11 hours of direct sunlight or more. Seven hours may allow plants to grow acceptably. In some instances, 4 hours may work for cool-season crops. Experimentation will be needed for less than 11 hours of sun.

### Bed Dimensions
Standard beds are 3 to 6 feet wide and of varying lengths. The width should allow the gardener to reach the center of the bed from either side without stepping on it. Five-foot width is common and works well for most people.
