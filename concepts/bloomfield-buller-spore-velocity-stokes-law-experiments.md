# A. H. R. Buller: Spore Velocity Experiments and the Discovery of Buller's Drop

**Source:** Nicholas P. Money, *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard* (2002), Chapter 5

## Overview

Arthur Henry Reginald Buller (1870–1944) was the greatest experimental mycologist in
history, a designation earned through decades of painstaking research at the University
of Manitoba in Winnipeg, Canada. His most celebrated contribution was the discovery of
the fluid drop mechanism — "Buller's drop" — that powers basidiospore discharge in
mushrooms. His earlier experiments on spore sedimentation rates were among the first
rigorous quantitative studies of [[bloomfield-fungal-spore-discharge-and-dispersal]] physics.

## Arrival in Winnipeg (1904)

Buller arrived in Winnipeg at age thirty, a doctor of philosophy from the University of
Leipzig, where he had trained under the eminent botanist William Pfeffer. He had also
conducted research in Munich and Naples and taught at the University of Birmingham. He
was appointed the first professor of botany at the University of Manitoba — a position
for which he was ideally qualified, though the choice of a windswept province three
times larger than Britain seemed strange for an ambitious young scientist.

The advantage was autonomy. A young institution lacking the usual herd of disaffected
faculty veterans offered freedom that an inexperienced professor climbing the ranks of
the British academic establishment would never find without an Oxbridge degree.

## Teaching and Public Engagement

Buller was a superb teacher who treated every lecture as a word-perfect performance,
using humor to leaven botanical details and displaying genuine interest in his audience.
Students nicknamed him "Uncle Regie." On Saturday mornings, he would walk to the
farmers' market, stand on a wooden crate, and lecture to the crowd about cereal crops
and the fungal diseases that afflict them. In Winnipeg, this made him a celebrity.

## Early Spore Sedimentation Experiments

Buller's first experiments in Winnipeg involved collecting mushrooms from a woodpile
and investigating spore discharge. He recognized that spore behavior might offer a
critical test of Stokes' law as it applied to microscopic objects. George Stokes had
developed this law in the 1840s while studying pendulums; it relates the speed of a
falling object to the viscosity of its surroundings and helped explain cloud formation.

To substitute [[query-how-to-store-mushroom-spores-long-term]] for water droplets in Stokes' equation, Buller needed
to measure spore size, density, and falling speed. His experimental apparatus was
characteristically ingenious:

1. A thin mushroom slice was enclosed in a glass-sided chamber positioned in a stand
2. The space under the gills was viewed through a horizontally tilted microscope
3. Falling spores appeared jet-black against the bright circular field of view at 25x
magnification
4. Two silk threads strung across the eyepiece calibrated the actual distance spores
traveled
5. An electric tapper connected to an ink pen inscribed marks on a paper drum spinning
at constant speed — the tapper was pressed as a spore passed each thread
6. Spore velocity was calculated by dividing the distance between threads by the time
intervals between paired markings

## The Stokes' Law Discrepancy

Buller recorded the motion of hundreds of spores and found they fell somewhat faster
than Stokes' law predicted. He could not explain the discrepancy. Nevertheless, these
imaginative experiments were published in *Nature*.

The mystery was resolved when Buller observed the formation of the fluid drop at the
base of the basidiospore a few seconds before discharge. The drop was carried with
the spore after separation. Fluid clinging to the spore's surface increased its
effective diameter by a few millionths of a meter, speeding its descent through the
air. Mushroom spores indeed behaved precisely as Stokes suggested — once the drop
was accounted for.

## Discovery of Buller's Drop (1910–1922)

In 1910, Buller observed the formation of the fluid drop — later named "Buller's drop"
— at the base of the mushroom basidiospore a few seconds before discharge. The timing
of drop expansion, coupled with his discovery that the fluid traveled with the spore,
suggested that the drop was a key player in the discharge mechanism.

Buller hypothesized that [[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]] was involved, but the exact mechanism defied
explanation for more than seventy years. His 1922 publication stated: "It may be that
the force of surface tension is used in some way to effect spore-discharge; but exactly
how I cannot satisfactorily explain. Possibly we have an entirely new principle involved
in the mechanism of spore-discharge."

The function of the drop in the catapult mechanism was ultimately solved by later
researchers, but Buller's hypothesis about surface tension and his meticulous
observations were critical to the solution.

## The Seven Volumes of *Researches on Fungi*

Buller's magnum opus, *Researches on Fungi*, eventually comprised seven volumes
published between 1909 and 1950 (the final volume appeared posthumously). To avoid
the slashes of an editor's pen, Buller covered the publication costs for the early
volumes himself. The first three volumes described thirteen years of scientific inquiry
that answered fundamental questions about spore production and dispersal.

Most experiments were performed in Winnipeg, but Buller also worked in laboratories
at Kew and Birmingham during summer pilgrimages to England. His transatlantic voyages
— sixty-five crossings — provided days of enforced contemplation for deciphering data
and transcribing laboratory notes into prose. He had the rare opportunity to follow the
advice: "For God's sake stop doing experiments and think!"

## Additional Research: Fungal Bioluminescence

Beyond spore mechanics, Buller made systematic studies of fungal luminescence. He worked
with *Panellus stypticus*, a bracket fungus that grows on tree stumps, rising at 4:00
A.M. at his hotel and walking to his lab before sunrise so his eyes were adapted to
[[darkness-low-light-facilitation-entheogenic-ceremonies]]. He muffled his head in horse blinders during his commute to avoid street
lamp glare, keeping his eyes in peak condition for darkroom observations.

He studied the dependence of luminescence on oxygen availability and temperature,
photographed using only the light from fungal mycelia and fruiting bodies, and
discovered that [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] strains of *Panellus* were luminous while an English
strain was not — when paired on agar, their hyphae fused to produce a hybrid mycelium
with both luminous and non-luminous sectors.

## Personal Character and Legacy

Buller was a lifelong bachelor who lived for forty years in a two-room suite at the
McLaren Hotel in Winnipeg. His relativity limerick was published in *Punch* in 1923
and appears in *The Oxford Dictionary of Quotations*. He played the piano, memorized
Shakespeare and Milton, wrote poems about fungi, and was a polished billiards player.

His keeness of eyesight was legendary — both for his luminous drawings of fungal
structures and for the hours of painstaking microscopic observation his experiments
demanded. The Buller Library at the University of Manitoba preserves his scrapbooks,
correspondence, and priceless botanical book collection.
