# Magic Mushroom Discovery in the British Underground (1970s)

## The First British Liberty Cap Trip

The first intentional psilocybin mushroom trip in Britain took place in or
around 1970. It was recorded in Oz, the magazine that, along with the
fortnightly newspaper the International Times (IT), was the most vocal
champion of radical political values and psychedelic aesthetics of the day.
The discovery came remarkably late -- Acid had been used illicitly in America
from the early 1960s onwards, and news of mushrooms, Siberian shamans, and
the pilgrimage to Mexico had circulated throughout that decade. All the
pieces of the puzzle were in place, yet the Liberty Cap (Psilocybe
semilanceata) went undiscovered in Britain for an entire decade after the
American psilocybin boom began.

## Why the Delay: The Fly-Agaric Obsession

Several factors explain the delay. Popular belief in Britain still equated the
"magic mushroom" with the fly-agaric (Amanita muscaria), a mushroom almost
nobody actually ate for pleasure. Jeremy Sandford's 1972 book In Search of
the Magic Mushroom swore the fly-agaric was the only British hallucinogen,
and he seems not to have tried it himself -- suggesting that most other
people were equally unaware.

The tenacity with which the improbable fly-agaric clung to the psychedelic
limelight is explicable given its lurid visual appearance, but it is still
surprising when one considers that scientists had known Liberty Caps to be
hallucinogenic as early as 1963. The cultural assumption that magic mushrooms
must be red-and-white effectively blinded an entire generation to what was
growing in every sheep field in the country.

## Scientific Knowledge That Never Reached the Public

Roger Heim and Albert Hofmann (with lab assistant Hans Tscherter) used
chromatographic techniques to test European species for the presence of
psilocybin, and only P. semilanceata tested positive. The finding was
replicated in 1969 at London's Imperial College by biochemist Peter Mantle,
who concluded that ingestion of about 3g of air-dry sporophores would
produce psychoactive effects.

Yet a greater gulf between academia and the underground existed in Britain
than in America. Mantle's interest in psilocybin was purely biochemical -- he
was not a hippy, nor did he ever try the mushrooms himself. He obtained
specimens from the experimental station at Kew, and undertook the assay alone.
His paper appeared in Transactions of the British Mycological Society, an
obscure enough journal to ensure that no one stumbled across it accidentally.
Heim and Hofmann's paper was published in the voluminous French journal
Comptes Rendus Hebdomadaires des Seances de l'Academie des Sciences, which
likewise went undiscovered by the underground.

## Robert Graves: The Missing Link

Robert Graves (1895-1985) played a vital, if largely forgotten, role. A
prolific writer and ultimately Professor of Poetry at Oxford, Graves had been
proselytising about magic mushrooms since the 1950s from his home in Deia,
Mallorca. A friend and correspondent of Wasson, Graves received a letter from
Valentina Wasson in January 1949 concerning the murder of the Roman Emperor
Claudius by mushrooms, which initiated a long and lively correspondence.

Graves relished their "queer exchange of letters" and thought Wasson "one of
the very few people whose mind runs along the same channels as mine." Both
were amateurs operating outside academia, sharing the belief that myths were
attenuated memories of actual historical events. Graves's idiosyncratic 1948
book The White Goddess argued for a universal prehistoric mushroom cult --
ideas that chimed with Wasson's findings but were disseminated only in
highbrow literary circles, not among the counterculture that might have acted
on them.

## The London Underground Scene

Between 1965 and 1972, London was home to an underground movement with its
own cartography, its own "mycelium" of connections and nodes. This
underground had clubs and venues, galleries and theatres, squats and
crash-pads. It had clothes shops, cafes and dope dens, advice centres,
alternative schools and even a university. Within these spaces, politicos
and journalists exchanged opinions in eagerly devoured papers and magazines,
while artists, dancers and musicians chipped away at the boundaries of taste
and form.

For those few short years this young, radical, mostly white and male-
dominated movement was united by the utopian belief that the establishment
was in its death throes. A new psychedelic Albion was about to rise from the
ashes of this political, cultural, sexual and spiritual revolution.

## Cultural Echoes Before the Discovery

The delay did not prevent mushrooms from appearing in British culture. Henry
Livings's play eh? debuted at the Aldwych in 1964, performed by the Royal
Shakespeare Company under Peter Hall. It revolved around a countercultural
dandy growing mushrooms in a factory. Four years later it became the film
Work is a Four-Letter Word (1968), featuring a young Cilla Black clutching a
mushroom and declaring that "God is a circle whose centre is everywhere and
its circumference nowhere." Livings had clearly never taken mushrooms himself
but had an informed idea of the sorts of pronouncements the bemushroomed
were supposed to make.

## The Breakthrough and Its Legacy

When the discovery finally came around 1970, it triggered a wave of
foraging across Britain. The Liberty Cap grows abundantly in "troops" in
pastures across the British Isles, especially in the wet, chilly sheep
fields of Wales, the Pennines, Devon, Cornwall, and Scotland. Once the
connection was made -- that the common little brown mushroom growing in
every sheep field contained the same psychoactive compounds as the
celebrated Mexican species -- the floodgates opened. A generation of young
Britons became autumnal foragers, and the Liberty Cap became "our" magic
mushroom, a cornerstone of British psychedelic culture that persists to
this day.

The British discovery story reveals how knowledge can exist in the
scientific literature for years without reaching the public, and how
cultural assumptions can blind people to what is literally growing under
their feet.
