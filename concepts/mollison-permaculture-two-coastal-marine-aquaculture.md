From Permaculture Two: Practical Design for Town and Country, by Bill Mollison (1979). Sections 7.5: Salicornia Flats, 7.6: Mariculture, including Tidal Stone Traps and Seagrasses.

## The Energy Argument for Shore-Based Fisheries

As in any analysis of calorific versus economic policy, it is clear that offshore and intensive process fisheries, such as are still being developed by Japan, Russia and the west for open sea species and fishmeal, are doomed in energy terms. Sedentary, shore-based, estuarine, inland, and intertidal or tidewater fisheries, now much depleted and neglected, will not only yield higher than deep-sea fisheries, but can reduce or eliminate the main costs of transport, packaging, and storage of products.

There are very few shellfish and inshore species — including oyster, crayfish, eel, octopus, seagrasses, algae, shrimp, sand bivalves, and scalefish — that are not susceptible to rearing or management in pond or barrage cultures, raft culture, and fenced or impounded tidal areas. Yet most research, bureaucratic facilitation, capital input and human effort goes into the offshore multinational, non-sustainable long-range fisheries; fisheries which employ very few men, allow little local industry, and waste enormous tonnages of fish and by-products.

Redirection of resources to inland, estuarine, and inshore systems must become a policy priority if this cheap protein source is to be preserved.

## Salicornia Flats

The intergrade estuary or inlet to land is often over Salicornia flats, weakly inundated by tides twice daily. Swan, where present, graze these systems at high tide, and domestic geese also appreciate the forage of succulents and seagrasses. As few, if any, fish occupy these salt flats, mosquitoes find multitudinous breeding grounds there, and it is more productive to vary the system by pond and bank designs than to try to manage the unmodified system.

### Pond Design for Salt Flats

Data from Colin Sumner (Tasmania Fisheries) suggest that ponds of about 1.8 m deep, with very gradual bank slopes (1:3 or so), allowing a foot of tide as flushing, and provided with browsers such as periwinkles or Salinator shellfish, shrimp, and mullet to keep algae to tolerable levels, would also provide excellent oyster culture conditions, with broodstock at mid-bank and spat caught on broken shell in trays, for transfer to nearby estuaries (40% air time exposure, 60% submerged).

Geese on range, and ti-tree or coastal shrubs on embankments make a more varied ecology, with manurial input to ponds. Mullet species are also viable pond species if some food is available or cultured in ponds; crab, octopus, seaweeds, and shrimp are other probable cultures.

## Essential Structures for Coastal Aquaculture

The essential structures that will reform the many millions of hectares of invariable mud-flats and intertidal sands available are:

- Reef walls of tyres, pipes, and stone — providing substrate and shelter for larger fish (eels, octopus, crayfish)
- Drift fences to catch seagrass and direct fish
- Rafts for rope suspension of mollusc spat and algae, ring-rafts to rear fish in tideways (as in Ireland, where salmon are reared to adulthood in seaways)
- Flow-governed tide pools to permit correct exposure for growing oysters
- Fry traps that provide stock for inshore and Salicornia pools
- Accessory paddle-powered electric or mechanical systems at points of restricted tide-flow
- Evaporative pans for salt, chemical, and brine-shrimp production (the latter as fry food)
- Islands for marine wildfowl refuges and phosphate collection
- Deeps for fish refuges, provided with cover nets or refuges from cormorants
- Manured seagrass fields, wave-protected by low bunds, yielding seagrass and browsing fish
- Sub-surface (permeable) walls to retard tide flow in scoured estuaries
- Trials of substrate materials to catch new fry or algal forms

## Tidal Stone Traps

Where tides fall 1.2 m or more on rocky coasts, as in northwest Tasmania, tide traps are made from well-packed stone, so that rocky clefts or tidal flats are enclosed by a 90 cm wall. At high tide, small school fish (garfish, squid, mullet, perch) enter over the wall, stay to feed on algae in the enclosed area, or are baited by crushed mussels, and are trapped as the tide falls. A refuge pool (or better, a roofed and slotted tank free from bird predators) holds the catch at low water. Late night tides give the best catch. An old gate or door in the wall allows the whole system to remain open when not in use. Such traps provide small fish for stocking manured ponds, and are themselves a substrate for oyster and mussel.

### Stillwater Funnel Traps

When tide-fall is too slight, or in lakes and lagoons, a lead fence plus a series of funnels guide fish (eels, trout, perch) to enclosures. On dams, the end enclosure can be under the house verandah, and fish kept there are always available. The system is widely used in marine shallows both in the U.K. (Scotland) and Malaysia, and in deeper water for tuna schools (Italy). Such traps can be made of brush, stone, wire, or fabric netting. The final catching pens are roofed or completely enclosed to foil predators and jumping fish.

## Seagrasses: Zostera, Heterozostera, and Posidonia

Various seagrasses or swan weeds grow in Australian estuaries. Some, on most open coasts, have short broad leaves, some wiry and strong, some like hay, and some like peat, mixed with a fluffy algae. All are a public resource, excellent insulators, and very resistant to burning. Intelligent use of such resources offers a safe way (safe from lung cancer caused by mineral fibres) to insulate houses.

The tougher material is almost immune to rot, and even under slabs would insulate from the ground if fumed with creosote, soaked in a weak preservative solution, or encased in thin plastic covering. This insulates buildings from ground cold, and prevents heat escape to the earth, making concrete slabs a large heat-storage system.

The spring growth is used by waterfowl as grazing, and there is a heavy summer seed production, usable as forage seeds or for flour.

## The Tide Range Advantage

The greatest advantage of coastal systems is a tide range of 1-9 m, such as is found over most coasts, and therefore enables "free" flushing and governable draining of ponds; the filling of higher-level impoundments for later release to lower ponds; and a flow of open sea species, fry, and algal forms as food.

## Sea-Land Nutrient Cycling

Swan, geese, and some duck species prefer saltwater locations or Salicornia and Zostera fields, while mullet species, eels and bream are all easily managed fish species with different food requirements, giving management potential as mixed-species stocks. Mussels and oysters attach to stone or still-pond Zostera, hold sand banks with byssal "roots" and provide bream and human food.

The guano from seafowl, easily caught as liquid run-off from solid rafts or stony islands, provides the essential local phosphate and nitrogenous fertilizer for adjacent land crops. Even large artificial platforms have proved commercially viable off southwest Africa, where pelican and cormorant use these "islands" for roosting, and deposit tons of guano for fertilizer. Sea-grass mulch and guano close the sea-land cycle of nutrients, and makes the growing of crops near seashores and waterways very profitable.
## Climate Change Implications for Coastal Aquaculture

Rising sea levels and increasing storm intensity pose significant challenges to coastal aquaculture systems, but also create opportunities for permaculture designers. Existing salt marshes and mangrove systems provide critical storm surge protection and carbon sequestration; Mollison's design principles for working with tidal systems align well with climate adaptation strategies that restore these natural buffers. The [[coastal-permaculture-design-mollison]] approach emphasises flexibility and resilience: pond walls that can be raised or lowered as sea levels change, polyculture systems that tolerate a range of salinities, and multiple harvest strategies that spread risk across species with different vulnerabilities. Sea level rise may actually expand the area suitable for salt-tolerant crops like Salicornia and for brackish-water aquaculture species such as milkfish and mullet, though careful design is needed to avoid saltwater intrusion into freshwater systems and agricultural land.
## Species Selection for Coastal Polycultures

Successful coastal aquaculture depends on selecting species adapted to local conditions and complementary in their ecological roles. Mollison's approach, detailed in [[permaculture-designers-manual-marine-resources]], emphasises combining species across multiple trophic levels: filter feeders like oysters and mussels that clean the water; herbivores like mullet and rabbitfish that consume algae and seagrass; omnivores like prawns and crabs that process detritus; and predators like bream that control populations of smaller fish. The [[tidal-estuarine-aquaculture-mollison]] also incorporates species that migrate between fresh and salt water at different life stages, such as eels and salmon, taking advantage of the natural gradients in tidal pond systems. Seaweed cultivation provides both a harvestable crop and water quality regulation, absorbing excess nutrients and providing habitat for juvenile fish and invertebrates.
## Restoration Aquaculture

Mollison's coastal aquaculture designs serve a restoration function alongside food production. Degraded estuaries, over-fished bays, and eroding shorelines can be rehabilitated through the establishment of aquaculture structures that provide habitat complexity, filter water, and rebuild food webs. Reef walls built from tyres, pipes, and stone create substrate for oyster settlement and shelter for fish; these structures simultaneously produce harvestable seafood and restore ecological function to degraded coastal environments. The [[mycoremediation-of-petroleum-hydrocarbons]] of fungi and microorganisms in coastal systems can be enhanced through careful design of tidal flushing patterns and organic matter inputs. Mollison's vision was that coastal aquaculture should leave the environment healthier than it found it, turning food production into an act of ecological restoration.
## Community Management Models

Coastal aquaculture has historically been managed as a commons resource by fishing communities, and Mollison's designs build on this tradition. The [[mollison-permaculture-two-community-self-reliance]] for coastal systems involves shared infrastructure (tidal walls, channels, harvesting equipment) managed by community cooperatives, with individual families or households managing specific ponds or trapping areas. This commons-based approach ensures that the benefits of improved coastal productivity are distributed broadly rather than captured by individual owners. Community management also provides the labour needed for maintenance-intensive activities like cleaning pond walls, repairing storm damage, and managing harvesting schedules. Mollison argued that the legal and social structures for commons management already exist in many coastal communities and simply need to be connected to improved aquaculture design.
## Tidal Energy Integration

Mollison recognised the potential for integrating energy harvesting with coastal aquaculture structures. The same tidal flows that flush ponds and deliver nutrients can be harnessed for mechanical power through paddle wheels, tidal mills, or simple undershot water wheels placed at points of restricted flow in channels and sluices. These devices can pump water, generate electricity, or drive mechanical processing equipment for fish and seaweed products. The [[holzer-alternative-energy]] that Mollison advocated for land-based permaculture apply equally to coastal settings, with the advantage that tidal energy is far more predictable and consistent than wind or solar power. A well-designed tidal aquaculture system can produce food, energy, and clean water simultaneously, with each function supporting the others through the same physical infrastructure.
## Seaweed Cultivation and Processing

Mollison frequently emphasised the underutilised potential of seaweed as both a food crop and a soil amendment. Kelp, dulse, nori, and other edible seaweeds can be cultivated on ropes suspended from rafts in the same tidal ponds used for fish and shellfish culture. The seaweed absorbs dissolved nutrients from the water, improving water quality for the fish while producing a valuable harvest. Beyond direct human consumption, seaweed can be composted, added to [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]], or dried and ground as mineral-rich mulch for gardens and fields. The mineral content of seaweed -- particularly potassium, trace minerals, and growth hormones -- makes it one of the most valuable organic fertilisers available, and coastal communities with access to seaweed resources have a significant agricultural advantage over inland farms that must purchase these minerals in processed form.

## Pollution Sensitivity and Bioindicator Value

Coastal aquaculture species are highly sensitive to water pollution, which makes them valuable both as food producers and as biological indicators of environmental health. Oysters and mussels, as filter feeders that process large volumes of water daily, accumulate heavy metals, hydrocarbons, and microbial contaminants from their environment. While this makes them potentially hazardous to eat from polluted waters, it also makes them excellent sentinels for monitoring water quality in coastal systems. Mollison's approach to [[mollison-water-purification-biological-treatment]] uses the filtration capacity of bivalves and aquatic plants as part of integrated systems that both produce food and clean water. The presence of healthy, growing populations of sensitive species such as oysters, seagrasses, and certain fish species indicates good water quality, while their decline signals environmental degradation that may affect human communities as well. This dual function of coastal aquaculture -- food production and environmental monitoring -- is one of its most valuable but least appreciated attributes.
## Salicornia as a Multi-Purpose Crop

Salicornia, the glasswort or samphire, deserves special attention as one of the most versatile plants in coastal permaculture systems. This salt-tolerant succulent grows naturally on intertidal flats and can be cultivated as a vegetable crop, a livestock forage, a biofuel feedstock, and a soil-building green manure. As a vegetable, Salicornia is prized for its crisp texture and salty flavour, commanding premium prices in specialty markets. As forage, it provides excellent grazing for geese and other waterfowl, which are among the few livestock species that thrive on salt-rich vegetation. The [[mollison-salicornia-flats-and-estuarine-aquaculture]] system described by Mollison uses managed Salicornia plantings to stabilise tidal sediments, provide waterfowl habitat, and create the pond edges and bank slopes needed for successful fish and shellfish cultivation. The salt accumulated in Salicornia tissues can be extracted by burning the dried plants and leaching the ash, providing a locally produced sea salt for agricultural use -- a direct connection to the [[jadam-sea-salt-and-mineral-solutions]] methods used in JADAM farming.
## Mangrove Systems and Coastal Protection

Mollison's coastal aquaculture designs naturally extend to the management and restoration of mangrove systems, which are among the most productive and ecologically important coastal ecosystems. Mangroves provide nursery habitat for commercially important fish and shellfish species, protect coastlines from storm damage and erosion, sequester carbon at rates far exceeding terrestrial forests, and filter pollutants from water flowing from land to sea. The integration of mangrove restoration with coastal aquaculture creates systems where fish and shellfish are harvested from the protected nursery habitat that mangroves provide, while the mangroves themselves are sustainably managed for timber, charcoal, and tannin production. Mollison's [[mollison-tropical-earth-shaping-polyculture-economics]] principles apply directly to mangrove-aquaculture integration, where the earthworks of tidal ponds and channels serve simultaneously as aquaculture infrastructure and mangrove restoration substrate.
## Community Management of Coastal Resources

Mollison stressed that successful coastal aquaculture depends on community-level management of shared coastal resources. Unlike terrestrial agriculture where individual land ownership provides clear boundaries and management authority, coastal systems involve shared water bodies, tidal flows, and marine resources that cross property lines. The [[mollison-permaculture-trusts-and-legal-structures-for-community-land]] principles that Mollison developed address this challenge by establishing community-based management structures that allocate access rights, set harvest limits, and coordinate restoration activities. Traditional fishing communities around the world have developed sophisticated commons management systems over centuries, and Mollison argued that these traditional systems, when combined with improved aquaculture design, provide the most effective governance framework for coastal resource management. The commons-based approach also distributes the benefits of coastal aquaculture more equitably than private ownership models, ensuring that the wealth generated by productive coastal systems flows to the community rather than being captured by individual leaseholders.
## Design Considerations for Storm Resilience

Coastal aquaculture systems face unique challenges from storm events, including wave damage, saltwater intrusion into freshwater systems, physical destruction of pond walls and structures, and loss of cultured organisms. Mollison's approach to storm resilience in coastal systems draws on the [[mollison-designers-design-for-disaster-resilience]] principles that apply to all permaculture installations but with specific adaptations for the marine environment. Key strategies include designing pond walls with adequate freeboard and overflow channels that prevent catastrophic failure during flood tides, using mangrove belts and other natural barriers as the first line of defence against wave energy, siting critical infrastructure above expected storm surge levels, and designing systems with redundancy so that the loss of any single pond or structure does not destroy the entire productive system. Mollison also emphasises that coastal aquaculture systems should be designed to recover quickly after storm events, with multiple small ponds being more resilient than a single large one, and with natural recruitment from wild populations providing a free restocking mechanism after catastrophic losses.
## Integrated Multi-Trophic Aquaculture

Mollison's coastal designs anticipate what modern aquaculture science calls integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA): the practice of culturing species from different trophic levels in the same system so that the waste products of one species become nutrients for another. The [[mollison-aquatic-polyculture-and-pond-culture]] that Mollison describes follow this principle naturally: fed species such as fish produce waste that fertilises extractive species such as seaweeds and filter-feeding bivalves, which in turn improve water quality for all species in the system. In coastal settings, this multi-trophic integration can extend to include sea cucumbers (which consume detritus on the pond bottom), sea urchins (which graze on macroalgae), and various species of crustaceans that occupy different ecological niches within the system. The result is a highly productive system that produces multiple harvestable crops while maintaining water quality within acceptable limits without external filtration or aeration.
