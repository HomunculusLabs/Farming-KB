## Restoration Aquaculture

Mollison's coastal aquaculture designs serve a restoration function alongside food production. Degraded estuaries, over-fished bays, and eroding shorelines can be rehabilitated through the establishment of aquaculture structures that provide habitat complexity, filter water, and rebuild food webs. Reef walls built from tyres, pipes, and stone create substrate for oyster settlement and shelter for fish; these structures simultaneously produce harvestable seafood and restore ecological function to degraded coastal environments. The [[mollison-permaculture-two-community-self-reliance]] for coastal systems involves shared infrastructure (tidal walls, channels, harvesting equipment) managed by community cooperatives, with individual families or households managing specific ponds or trapping areas. This commons-based approach ensures that the benefits of improved coastal productivity are distributed broadly rather than captured by individual owners. Community management also provides the labour needed for maintenance-intensive activities like cleaning pond walls, repairing storm damage, and managing harvesting schedules. Mollison argued that the legal and social structures for commons management already exist in many coastal communities and simply need to be connected to improved aquaculture design.

These devices can pump water, generate electricity, or drive mechanical processing equipment for fish and seaweed products. ## Salicornia as a Multi-Purpose Crop

Salicornia, the glasswort or samphire, deserves special attention as one of the most versatile plants in coastal permaculture systems. This salt-tolerant succulent grows naturally on intertidal flats and can be cultivated as a vegetable crop, a livestock forage, a biofuel feedstock, and a soil-building green manure. As a vegetable, Salicornia is prized for its crisp texture and salty flavour, commanding premium prices in specialty markets. As forage, it provides excellent grazing for geese and other waterfowl, which are among the few livestock species that thrive on salt-rich vegetation. ## Mangrove Systems and Coastal Protection

Mollison's coastal aquaculture designs naturally extend to the management and restoration of mangrove systems, which are among the most productive and ecologically important coastal ecosystems. Mangroves provide nursery habitat for commercially important fish and shellfish species, protect coastlines from storm damage and erosion, sequester carbon at rates far exceeding terrestrial forests, and filter pollutants from water flowing from land to sea. The integration of mangrove restoration with coastal aquaculture creates systems where fish and shellfish are harvested from the protected nursery habitat that mangroves provide, while the mangroves themselves are sustainably managed for timber, charcoal, and tannin production. Mollison's [[mollison-permaculture-trusts-and-legal-structures-for-community-land]] principles that Mollison developed address this challenge by establishing community-based management structures that allocate access rights, set harvest limits, and coordinate restoration activities. Traditional fishing communities around the world have developed sophisticated commons management systems over centuries, and Mollison argued that these traditional systems, when combined with improved aquaculture design, provide the most effective governance framework for coastal resource management. The commons-based approach also distributes the benefits of coastal aquaculture more equitably than private ownership models, ensuring that the wealth generated by productive coastal systems flows to the community rather than being captured by individual leaseholders.
## Design Considerations for Storm Resilience

Coastal aquaculture systems face unique challenges from storm events, including wave damage, saltwater intrusion into freshwater systems, physical destruction of pond walls and structures, and loss of cultured organisms. Mollison's approach to storm resilience in coastal systems draws on the [[mollison-aquatic-polyculture-and-pond-culture]] that Mollison describes follow this principle naturally: fed species such as fish produce waste that fertilises extractive species such as seaweeds and filter-feeding bivalves, which in turn improve water quality for all species in the system. In coastal settings, this multi-trophic integration can extend to include sea cucumbers (which consume detritus on the pond bottom), sea urchins (which graze on macroalgae), and various species of crustaceans that occupy different ecological niches within the system. The result is a highly productive system that produces multiple harvestable crops while maintaining water quality within acceptable limits without external filtration or aeration.
