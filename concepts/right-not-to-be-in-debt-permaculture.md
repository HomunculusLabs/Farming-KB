# Right Not to Be in Debt — Permaculture Perspectives on Shelter and Self-Reliance

In Chapter 14 of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison mounts a provocative argument that the fundamental barriers to adequate shelter and food security are not financial but regulatory. He uses the example of self-built housing without debt to illustrate how existing political and economic systems deny people basic rights to provide for themselves.

## Self-Built Housing Without Debt

Mollison observes that some of the most charming and climatically appropriate houses on earth are built without bank loans, architects, metals, concrete, or contractors. In every documented case, these houses exist in areas where trade unions, building surveyors, health officials, and government regulators do not impede home builders or communities providing their own shelter.

### The Chile Example

Mollison cites a striking contrast: while Chile (guided by economic "experts") accumulated a $12 billion foreign debt in 1985, poor people acting without loans built at least $11 billion worth of housing in slum areas through local cooperation, incurring zero foreign debt. This raises a fundamental question about the relationship between formal financial systems and actual housing provision.

## Traditional Building Materials

Stone, mud, bamboo, round timbers, rope, thatch, baked brick, and tiles are the age-old durable building materials of humanity. All can be locally produced when energy from community forests and human labour is available. Even cement, mortar, pottery, bricks, and roof tiles can be manufactured using wood-fired kilns. None of this requires money if people work cooperatively.

The material basis for adequate shelter exists in virtually every region. The obstacle is not resources but access to them.

## Root Causes of Shelter Deprivation

Mollison identifies the real causes of inadequate shelter:

### Regulatory Bureaucracy

The primary barrier is restrictive practices enforced by regulatory bureaucracies. Building codes, zoning laws, health regulations, and professional licensing requirements collectively prevent people from building their own shelter using local materials and traditional methods.

### Resource Ownership Patterns

State or private ownership of forests, small mines, and lands—devoted to state or corporate profits—denies basic biological and earth resources to the people who work to produce or extract them. These ownership patterns support a largely urban, leisured class of bureaucrats at the expense of rural and self-reliant populations.

### The Inversion of National Service

Mollison draws a pointed comparison: societies have instituted "national service" to fight wars, but no sustainable tribal society requires every person to help shelter and feed themselves. People are drafted to kill strangers but denied the right to preserve life. No armies are created to build houses, grow food, or plant forests. He argues that unemployment is preferred by those who choose power as a method of exploitation.

## Basic Rights Denied

In contemporary societies, the recognized "rights" are to vote, form unions, protest, or go to law—all of which primarily support professional classes. Mollison argues that truly basic rights are:

- The right to grow or protect forests
- The right to build shelter
- The right to grow food
- The right to collect water from roof areas

These fundamental rights to personal responsibility for sustenance are commonly denied by local and state regulations.

## Alternatives to Political Systems

Mollison argues that existing political systems—whether self-described as communist, socialist, capitalist, or democratic—are essentially systems of competing belief elites. They share common characteristics:

- **Short-term orientation** — temporary nature of political terms impedes effective long-term action
- **Power-centred rather than life-centred** — policies serve elite interests, not ecological health
- **Composition by few families** — continuing programs for forest and soil restoration are sacrificed for short-term power maintenance
- **Policy imposition over wide areas** — regardless of local ecological or cultural conditions

### The Power of Personal Action

Mollison quotes Manas (17 Oct 1984): "The argument for simplicity is never a political argument... when people practice it in their lives... they don't even need any politics."

People can act independently of political theory—which rarely addresses ethics, simplicity, local autonomy, or life-oriented action. Such changes come about through education and information. When enough people change their practices, political systems either follow or become irrelevant.

### Starting Points

For this reason, Mollison identifies the correct sequence for change:

1. Start with the individual (oneself)
2. Then work in one's region or neighbourhood

## Natural Law vs. the Laws of Power

Mollison contrasts two systems of exchange:

- **Natural law** demands a fair return for every gift received
- **The laws of power** demand gifts without thought of return—this is called "economic growth" and means unlimited resource exploitation alongside the exploitation of people

## The Wealth of a Region

He concludes with a redefinition of wealth: the wealth of any area lies not in banks or cities, but in the basic resources, skills, and natural systems developed by its peoples.

## Practical Implications

For permaculture designers and community builders, this analysis suggests:

- **Prioritize regulatory reform** as much as technical design — the best design is useless if regulations prevent its implementation
- **Document and publicize successful self-build examples** to build the case for regulatory change
- **Use local, traditional materials** wherever possible to demonstrate independence from industrial supply chains
- **Build community cooperation** as the foundation for housing and food security
- **Challenge the definition of "rights"** in public discourse to include ecological self-reliance
