Two notable Pacific Northwest Psilocybe species, P. baeocystis ("baeos" or
"knobby tops") and P. stuntzii ("Stuntz's blue legs" or "blue ringers"), are
frequently encountered by foragers in the region. Both are wood-associated
species with distinct identification features.

## Psilocybe baeocystis Singer and Smith

### Description

Cap 1.5-5.5 cm broad. Conic to obtusely conic to convex, expanding to plane
only in extreme age. Margin incurved at first and distinctly undulated when
convex; translucent-striate and often tinted greenish. Dark olive brown to buff
brown (occasionally steel blue), becoming copper brown in the center when
drying, hygrophanous, fading to pallid white and easily bruising bluish.
Surface viscid when moist from a gelatinous pellicle, usually separable.

Gills adnate to sinuate, close. Color grayish to dark cinnamon brown with edges
remaining pallid. Stem 50-70 mm long by 2-3 mm thick. Equal to subequal, pallid
to brownish, sometimes covered with fine whitish fibrils while often more
yellowish towards the apex. Brittle, stuffed with loose fibers. Distinct
rhizomorphs present about stem base. Partial veil thinly cortinate, rapidly
becoming inconspicuous.

Spores purplish brown, mango-shaped, 10-12 by 6-7 microns. Basidia 4-spored.
Pleurocystidia absent. Cheilocystidia 20-30 (40) by 4.5-6 (9) microns,
fusoid with a narrow neck.

### Habitat and Distribution

Scattered to subcespitose on decaying conifer mulch, in wood chips, or in
lawns with high lignin content. Occasionally grows from fallen seed cones of
Douglas fir. Found in the fall to early winter and rarely in the spring.
First reported from Oregon, common throughout the Pacific Northwest.

### Potency

Moderately active, losing significant potency in drying or from damage.
Ranges from .15-.85% psilocybin, up to .59% psilocin, and .10% baeocystin
(Repke et al. 1977; Beug and Bigwood 1981, 1982b).

### The Baeocystin Naming Story

MeCawley et al. (1962) first analyzed mushrooms purported to be P. baeocystis
after the unintended intoxication of eight individuals in 1960-1961. One child
died after three days of hospitalization with a 106-degree F fever and cerebral
edema — symptoms consistent with Galerina or Amanita poisoning. The photographs
subsequently showed mushrooms resembling P. cyanescens, not P. baeocystis. The
identifying mycologist, Alexander Smith, retracted his identification upon
confrontation with the discrepancy. The child's death spurred discovery of
baeocystin (Leung and Paul 1968; Benedict et al. 1962), which has NOT been
proven toxic in commonly consumed dosages — the name "baeocystis" is thus
partially a misnomer.

## Psilocybe stuntzii Guzman and Ott

### Description

Cap 1.5-5 cm broad. Obtusely conic at first, soon expanding to convex to
broadly convex-umbonate to nearly plane, with margin uplifting in very mature
specimens. Dark chestnut brown, lighter towards the margin (often olive
greenish); hygrophanous, fading to yellowish brown to pale yellow in drying.
Stem 30-60 mm long by 2-4 mm thick, subequal, slightly enlarged at the apex,
often curved and twisted at the base. Dingy yellow to pale yellowish brown.

Partial veil thinly membranous, typically streaked bluish, leaving a fragile
membranous annulus that soon deteriorates into a fairly persistent annular
zone darkened by spores. Spores dark purplish grayish brown, subellipsoid in
side view, subrhomboid in face view, 9-10.5 by 5.5-7.5 microns.

### Habitat and Distribution

Grows in gregarious to subcespitose clusters on conifer wood chips, in soils
rich with woody debris, in newly placed lawns and fields, along roads, paths,
and gardens. Common in the fall to early winter, and to a minor degree in
spring. Abundant within 90 kilometers of coastal Oregon, Washington, and
British Columbia. Often fruits in prodigious colonies.

### Potency

Weakly to moderately active. Beug and Bigwood (1982b) reported 0-.36%
psilocybin, 0-.12% psilocin. Repke et al. (1977) reported .02% baeocystin.
By weight, P. stuntzii is one of the less potent of the bluing Psilocybes.

### Key Identification Feature

The most characteristic feature is the whitish partial veil that bruises
bluish or is bluish tinged. The field variety, slender and paler, is P.
stuntzii var. tenuis. Named in honor of Dr. Daniel Stuntz, who made the type
collections.
