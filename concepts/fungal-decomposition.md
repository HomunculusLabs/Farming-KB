# Fungal Decomposition

Fungi are the primary decomposers of lignocellulosic material in terrestrial ecosystems. Their ability to break down the structural polymers of wood — cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin — underpins nutrient cycling, soil formation, and carbon sequestration worldwide.

Related: [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]], [[mycoremediation-basics]]

## Wood Composition

Wood is composed of three major polymers (Gadd 2001; Staycare):
- **Cellulose** (40-50%): Linear glucose chains, crystalline structure, most abundant organic polymer on Earth
- **Hemicellulose** (20-35%): Branched chains of various sugars, more accessible than cellulose
- **Lignin** (15-35%): Complex, irregular phenolic polymer. Provides rigidity and resistance to microbial attack. Most chemically recalcitrant component of wood.

## White Rot vs Brown Rot Fungi

### White Rot Fungi

White rot fungi are the only organisms capable of substantial lignin degradation. They attack all wood components simultaneously — cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin — leaving behind a bleached, fibrous residue.

- **Mechanism**: Produce extracellular ligninolytic enzymes:
  - Lignin peroxidase (LiP)
  - Manganese peroxidase (MnP)
  - Laccase (phenol oxidase)
  - versatile peroxidase (VP)
- These enzymes generate highly reactive free radicals that nonspecifically break down the complex lignin polymer. This non-specificity is what makes white rot fungi effective against synthetic pollutants as well (Staycare; Singh).
- **Key species**: Phanerochaete chrysosporium (most studied model organism), Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus, Ganoderma lucidum, Lentinula edodes
- **Ecological role**: Complete decomposers that can mineralize lignin to CO2. Critical in later stages of wood decay.

### Brown Rot Fungi

Brown rot fungi primarily degrade cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving lignin largely intact as a modified, brown, crumbly residue.

- **Mechanism**: Employ a Fenton chemistry system — generate hydroxyl radicals (OH) via iron reduction and hydrogen peroxide. These radicals cleave cellulose chains rapidly without requiring direct enzymatic contact.
- Do not produce ligninolytic peroxidases or laccases in the same manner as white rots.
- **Key species**: Serpula lacrymans, Fomitopsis pinicola, Piptoporus betulinus, Laetiporus sulphureus
- **Ecological role**: Rapid cellulose removal, leaving lignin-enriched residue. Common on conifer wood. Responsible for most structural wood damage in buildings.

## Decomposition Rates

- **Brown rot**: Can cause significant mass loss in weeks to months. Rapid initial cellulose degradation.
- **White rot**: Slower, more complete decomposition. Full lignin mineralization may take months to years depending on species, conditions, and wood type.
- **Factors affecting rate**: Temperature, moisture, oxygen, wood species (hardwood vs softwood), wood size, fungal species, nitrogen availability.
- Hardwoods (high lignin) generally decompose more slowly than softwoods under brown rot but are more susceptible to white rot.
- Substrate C:N ratio inversely affects decomposition rate for nitrogen-limited fungi.

## Wood Decay Stages

1. **Colonization**: Spore germination, hyphal penetration through ray cells and pits. Initial moisture uptake.
2. **Early decay**: Enzyme secretion begins. Brown rot causes rapid strength loss (up to 50% with only 5% mass loss due to cellulose depolymerization). White rot begins lignin modification.
3. **Active decay**: Major polymer breakdown. White rot produces bleached zones. Brown rot produces cubical cracking pattern. Nutrient mobilization peaks.
4. **Late decay**: Most accessible polymers consumed. Material becomes soft, friable. White rot may achieve near-complete mineralization.
5. **Humus incorporation**: Residual material (primarily modified lignin from brown rot; fully mineralized products from white rot) incorporated into soil organic matter.

## Fungal Succession

Wood decomposition involves a predictable succession of fungal species:

- **Primary colonizers** (pioneer species): Fast-growing, low-nutrient-demanding fungi that establish first on fresh wood. Often include Trichoderma, various molds, and some basidiomycetes. Morels (Morchella) and cup fungi (Auricularia) are primary saprophytes that appear first after disturbances like forest fires (Stamets).
- **Secondary decomposers**: Replace pioneers as nutrients become available from initial decomposition. Include many white rot species.
- **Late-stage decomposers**: Specialized species adapted to the chemically modified, nutrient-poor residue. Includes brown rot specialists and certain white rots.
- Succession is driven by changes in substrate chemistry, pH, moisture, and competitive interactions between species.

## Role in Soil Formation

Fungi are fundamental to pedogenesis (soil formation):

- **Rock weathering**: Mycelium penetrates granite and marble, secreting metabolic acids, metal-chelating anions, and enzymes. Oxalic acid from mycelia mineralizes rock by combining with calcium and other minerals to form oxalates (Stamets).
- **Organic matter incorporation**: Fungal decomposition converts woody debris into soil organic matter. As mycelia digest wood, microcavities form and fill with air or water, creating aerobic infrastructures with vast surface areas.
- **Soil structure**: Nearly a mile of mycelium can entangle a gram of pasture soil (Ritz and Young 2004); a cubic inch can contain 8 miles of mycelial cells. These networks physically bind soil particles.
- **Glomalin production**: Mycorrhizal and saprophytic fungi produce glomalin-related soil proteins that create water-stable aggregates, improving soil structure and water retention.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Fungi release nitrogen, phosphorus, and trace minerals from organic matter, making them available to plants.

## Carbon Sequestration

- Fungi store significant carbon in their biomass (mycelium and fruiting bodies) and in soil organic matter they help create.
- White rot fungi mineralize lignin to CO2 — returning carbon to the atmosphere. Brown rot fungi leave modified lignin as long-lasting soil carbon.
- No-till farming preserves mycelial networks and keeps 41% more CO2 in the soil biosphere compared to conventional tilling (Stamets). If all US farmers adopted no-till, an estimated 300 million tons of carbon could be retained in soil.
- Mycorrhizal fungi contribute to long-term carbon storage through formation of recalcitrant organic compounds and physical protection of organic matter within soil aggregates.
