## High Quality Compost and Humanure

Making the highest-quality compost is an aspiration that most home gardeners cannot realistically achieve. The requirements — large quantities of the freshest, most potent manure processed before any nitrate losses occur — demand a scale of livestock operation and waste management that puts it beyond the reach of most homesteaders. However, for those interested in maximum self-sufficiency, understanding what it takes and what alternatives exist is valuable.

## Why Most Gardeners Can't Make High-Quality Compost

### The Freshness Requirement

To make really first-class compost, you must use the freshest manure possible — before any nitrate losses occur. The ammonia smell that develops under rabbit cages or in accumulated poultry manure proves that nitrogen has already degraded. To prevent this, you'd need to process manure about once a week, before any smell develops.

### The Scale Requirement

To have enough material to make a heap of minimum size when processing weekly, you would need:
- A barn full of livestock or a large coop of chickens
- Management systems that effectively capture all the urine
- A great many rabbits (if using rabbit manure)
- Plenty of dry vegetation with a not-too-high C:N to mix in

At this scale, you'd be making a new compost heap each week, year-round. You'd be selling compost as well as livestock. You'd be in the compost business, not just gardening.

### The Honest Assessment

Making high-quality compost is only for the small farmer who possesses a great deal of energy. The home gardener should aspire to medium-quality compost and supplement with COF for high-demand crops.

## The Reference: Sir Albert Howard

The most illuminating information about composting comes from one of the earliest books on the subject: *The Waste Products of Agriculture* by Sir Albert Howard, the founder of the organic farming movement. It describes his composting work in India during the early 1930s and is available as an inexpensive ebook. It's an interesting read that illuminates compost science in its earliest systematic form.

## Biodynamic Composting

The biodynamic agriculturalists also know a great deal about making high-quality compost. Their methods are more demanding than conventional composting but are said to produce superior results. See books by Koepf and Pfeiffer for detailed guidance.

## Humanure

### The Concept

Humanure is the practice of composting human waste (feces and urine) and using the resulting compost to grow food. The Humanure Handbook by Joseph Jenkins fully explores the issues and logistics of recycling human waste, based on one family's actual longstanding practice.

### The Safety Question

If you'll consider the subject without preconception of revulsion, and if you compare the prevailing flush-toilet system objectively, there is no reason not to compost your own family's humanure and use it to grow vegetables. The key safety precautions:

- Compost thoroughly — the thermophilic composting process (130-150°F / 55-65°C for several days) destroys human pathogens
- Allow sufficient composting time before use
- To avoid disease transmission (mainly parasitic), segregate the humanure of visitors and houseguests who may carry parasites you haven't been exposed to

### Historical Context

In low-income rural families in the early 20th century, where meager water supplies came from small rainwater tanks, vegetable gardens were commonly fertilized exclusively with humanure where flush toilets weren't available. This was standard practice, not an exception.

### Practical Considerations

- An outdoor toilet ("dunny") is needed — crudely built but extremely well-ventilated
- Chamber pots for use at night and during stormy weather
- Cover material (sawdust, straw, dry leaves) is added after each use to prevent odor
- The sealed container prevents odor from escaping
- When full, the contents are added to a compost heap and processed like any other manure

### The Comfrey Connection

For someone with extra land who is interested in maximum self-sufficiency, a comfrey patch can provide large quantities of high-quality organic matter. Comfrey:
- Grows deeply rooted and aggressively
- Leaves can be cut repeatedly during spring and summer
- Leaves may be fed to livestock, included in compost heaps, or used to make fertigation tea
- Once established, keep it vigorous by running chickens on it (with grain supplementation)
- May also be the ideal place for sheet-composting humanure

See Lawrence Hills' *Comfrey: A Hundred Tons an Acre of Stock Feed or Compost for Farm, Garden or Smallholding* for detailed guidance on establishing and managing a comfrey patch.

## The Closed-System Reality Check

### Can a Garden Be Self-Sufficient?

A veritable garden cannot operate as a closed system that, all by itself, generates enough organic matter to maintain itself. Not unless you compost every opportunity and every bit of organic material it generates — and that includes humanure.

A farm wisely run on sound biological principles and endowed with reasonably fertile soil can slowly increase its overall level of soil organic matter and can even export a fraction of the organic matter it produces (crops sold to market) without loss of fertility. But a garden? Never!

### What You Need to Import

For most gardeners, importing organic matter at least twice the volume of the garden's own output is necessary. Sources include:
- Ruminant manure (horse, cow, sheep)
- Spoiled hay
- Autumn leaves
- Used coffee grounds from espresso shops
- Spent brewer's malt from local breweries
- Well-rotted feedlot manure

Whatever the imported material, it should have an average C:N of about 25:1 or lower.

## The Comfrey Patch as Organic Matter Factory

Someone with extra land could grow a deeply rooting, aggressively growing plant whose leaves can be cut repeatedly. The leaves may be:
- Fed to livestock (after wilting into "hay")
- Included in compost heaps
- Used to make effective fertigation tea
- Sheet-composted with humanure

A well-established comfrey patch can produce enormous quantities of biomass — Lawrence Hills' book title references "a hundred tons an acre."
