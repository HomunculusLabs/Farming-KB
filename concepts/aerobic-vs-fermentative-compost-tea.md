# Aerobic vs Fermentative Compost Tea

The distinction between aerobic (actively aerated) and fermentative (non-aerated) compost tea preparations is one of the most important and frequently misunderstood topics in biological agriculture. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide provides clear definitions and explains why the brewing method fundamentally determines the biological character and agricultural utility of the finished tea.

## Definitions

### Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)

AACT is produced by extracting organisms from compost into water while maintaining dissolved oxygen levels above 6 mg/L throughout the brewing period. Active aeration — typically from air pumps and diffusers — ensures that aerobic conditions are maintained. The resulting tea contains active, reproducing populations of beneficial aerobic bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and (potentially) nematodes.

Key characteristics of AACT:
- Brewing time: 18–36 hours
- Dominant organisms: aerobic bacteria and fungi
- Dissolved oxygen: maintained above 6 mg/L
- Must be used within 4–6 hours of brewing
- [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] shows diverse, active organisms
- Safe for foliar application and soil drench

### Fermentative Compost Tea (FCT)

FCT is produced by steeping compost in water without active aeration. The initial oxygen in the water is consumed by microbial respiration within hours, after which the brewing environment becomes anaerobic or microaerophilic. The resulting tea is dominated by anaerobic and facultatively [[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]].

Key characteristics of FCT:
- Brewing time: 3–14 days (or longer)
- Dominant organisms: facultative and anaerobic bacteria, yeasts
- Dissolved oxygen: drops to near zero within hours
- Can be stored for extended periods (weeks to months)
- Characteristic fermented odor
- May contain phytotoxic compounds from anaerobic metabolism

## Biological Differences

### Microbial Community Composition

The two [[compost-tea-brewing-methods-aact]] select for fundamentally different microbial communities:

**AACT communities:**
- Aerobic bacteria (*Pseudomonas*, *Bacillus*, *Azotobacter* species)
- Aerobic fungi (diverse saprotrophic species with active hyphal growth)
- Aerobic protozoa (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates)
- Potentially [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]]
- High organism diversity

**FCT communities:**
- Facultative bacteria (*Lactobacillus*, *Clostridium*, *Enterobacter* species)
- Yeasts and some molds tolerant of low-oxygen conditions
- Few or no protozoa
- No nematodes
- Reduced organism diversity

### Metabolic Byproducts

The metabolic byproducts of [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-aerobic-and-anaerobic-composting]] metabolism differ dramatically:

**Aerobic byproducts (AACT):**
- CO₂ and water (harmless end products of complete oxidation)
- Enzymes (cellulases, chitinases, proteases)
- Vitamins and growth factors
- Antibiotics (from beneficial bacterial species)
- Plant growth-promoting compounds

**Anaerobic byproducts (FCT):**
- Alcohol (ethanol, methanol)
- Organic acids (lactic, acetic, butyric, propionic)
- Phenols and phenolic compounds
- Hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg smell)
- Ammonia
- Methane

Many of these anaerobic byproducts are phytotoxic at sufficient concentrations. Alcohol damages cell membranes, organic acids lower pH excessively, phenols are directly toxic to plant tissues, and hydrogen sulfide is toxic to roots. While some of these compounds have [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]] (which is why FCT can sometimes suppress plant diseases), they can also harm beneficial organisms and plant tissue.

## Agricultural Implications

### Foliar Application

Ingham is emphatic that only AACT should be applied to foliage. The aerobic organisms in AACT colonize leaf surfaces and compete with pathogens for space and nutrients without harming plant tissue. The anaerobic byproducts in FCT can burn foliage, especially when applied in concentrated form or during hot weather.

FCT advocates sometimes report successful foliar disease suppression, which may be attributed to the antimicrobial compounds in the tea. However, the risk of phytotoxicity is real and unpredictable. Ingham considers the risk unacceptable for commercial production and recommends AACT exclusively for foliar use.

### Soil Application

Both tea types can be applied to soil, but they serve different purposes:

**AACT soil drench:** Introduces the full spectrum of aerobic soil foodweb organisms, supporting nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil structure improvement. Particularly valuable after soil disturbance (tilling, compaction, flooding) that has reduced aerobic organism populations.

**FCT soil drench:** Provides a different set of organisms and metabolites. The facultative organisms in FCT can survive and multiply in soil, potentially contributing to nutrient cycling. The organic acids can help solubilize minerals. However, if applied in large quantities, the anaerobic byproducts can create temporarily toxic conditions in the soil.

### Compost Pile Activation

FCT has a traditional use as a compost pile activator. The anaerobic organisms and organic acids in FCT can help jump-start decomposition in new compost piles. However, Ingham notes that if the compost pile is properly constructed and aerated, AACT is equally effective and introduces a more diverse and beneficial biology.

## Common Misconceptions

### "All Compost Tea Is the Same"

One of the most persistent misconceptions is that all compost tea preparations are equivalent. Ingham stresses that AACT and FCT are fundamentally different products with different biology, different effects, and different appropriate uses. Confusing the two — brewing FCT and applying it as if it were AACT — is a common cause of problems.

### "Anaerobic Tea Is More Concentrated"

It is sometimes claimed that FCT is more concentrated or potent than AACT because it brews longer. Ingham counters that while FCT may contain higher concentrations of certain metabolites, these are often the phytotoxic ones. The beneficial organism biomass in AACT typically exceeds that in FCT, because aerobic organisms grow faster and more diversely than anaerobic ones when given adequate food and oxygen.

### "FCT Is Traditional and Therefore Better"

The argument that FCT represents a more traditional or natural approach is not persuasive to Ingham. She points out that traditional manure teas were often made from raw manure (not compost) and produced inconsistent results. AACT represents a deliberate effort to optimize the biology for agricultural benefit, not a departure from tradition.

### "You Can Tell Good Tea by Smell"

While Ingham acknowledges that a foul smell indicates anaerobic conditions, she cautions that the absence of a bad smell does not guarantee good tea. Some anaerobic metabolites are not malodorous, and a tea can have lost its aerobic biology without developing a noticeable smell. Microscopic examination is the only reliable quality assessment.

## Brewing Method Comparison

| Parameter | AACT | FCT |
|---|---|---|
| Aeration | Active, continuous | None |
| Brewing time | 18–36 hours | 3–14 days |
| Dissolved oxygen | >6 mg/L maintained | Drops to ~0 mg/L |
| Dominant biology | Aerobic bacteria, fungi | Facultative/anaerobic bacteria |
| Protozoa | Present (diverse) | Absent |
| Fungal growth | Active hyphae | Minimal |
| Shelf life | 4–6 hours | Weeks to months |
| Foliar safety | Yes | Risky |
| Phytotoxicity risk | Low | Moderate to high |
| Quality assessment | Microscopy recommended | Smell test common |

## Practical Recommendations

Ingham's practical recommendations are clear:

1. **Use AACT** for all applications where biological diversity and safety matter — foliar sprays, soil drenches, and compost pile inoculation
2. **Avoid FCT** for foliar application due to phytotoxicity risk
3. **If using FCT for soil application,** dilute it adequately and monitor for negative effects
4. **Always monitor dissolved oxygen** during AACT brewing — if it drops below 5.5 mg/L, the brew is compromised
5. **Learn to use a microscope** to assess tea quality — reliance on appearance or smell alone is insufficient
