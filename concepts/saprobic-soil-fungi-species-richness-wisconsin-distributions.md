# Saprobic Soil Fungi — Species Richness, Wisconsin Surveys, and Distribution Patterns

## Overview

Soil fungi constitute one of the most diverse and ecologically significant components of terrestrial ecosystems. [[saprobic-soil-fungi]] — those that obtain nutrients by decomposing organic matter — vary enormously in species richness across habitats, climates, and vegetation types. Global surveys have produced isolate counts ranging from hundreds to tens of thousands, revealing both the scale of fungal diversity and the methodological challenges inherent in comparing studies with different sampling intensities.

## Global Species Richness and Sampling Efforts

Large-scale isolation studies across diverse ecosystems demonstrate the vast species richness of soil fungal communities. The following surveys, drawn from Table 13.1 of the source, illustrate the range of sampling efforts and corresponding species counts documented worldwide:

- **Alberta (Bissett and Parkinson 1979a):** 3 alpine communities yielded 4,643 isolates representing 128 species.
- **Germany (Gams and Domsch 1960, 1969):** Wheat fields produced 23,500 isolates and 220 species, one of the largest isolation efforts recorded.
- **Sweden (Bååth 1980):** A clear-cut conifer forest generated 3,891 isolates with 96 species.
- **New York — Quercus-Pinus forest (Gochenaur and Woodall 1974):** 15,770 isolates produced 267 species, among the highest species counts in temperate forests.
- **New York — Quercus-Betula forest (Gochenaur 1978):** 10,684 isolates yielded 89 species.
- **South Dakota (Clarke and Christensen 1981):** Grassland sampling produced 250 isolates and 62 species — a notably high species-to-isolate ratio.
- **Arizona/Utah (States 1978):** Desert soils generated 19,000 isolates, 228 species, and 87 genera.
- **Veracruz (Persiani and Maggi 1988):** [[coffea-arabica]] rhizospheres yielded 3,584 + 2,349 isolates and 114 + 116 species across two sampling efforts.
- **Ivory Coast (Rambelli et al. 1983; Maggi and Persiani 1992):** Native, burned, and cultivated forests produced 6,246 isolates, 215 identified species spanning 120 genera, with 164–194 species per study site.
- **Galapagos (Mahoney 1972):** Xerophilic soils and litter contained an estimated 250 species, approximately 50% of which were identifiable.
- **Taiwan (Tzean 1994):** Surveys documented 47 Penicillium species, 42 Aspergillus species, and associated teleomorphs.
- **India (Rai et al. 1974):** More than 60 Aspergillus species were recorded.
- **Australia (Sangalang et al. 1995):** 4,484 Fusarium isolates comprised 22 named and 3 undescribed species.

Several patterns emerge from these data. Temperate forests with large isolate numbers (e.g., New York Quercus-Pinus at 15,770 isolates, 267 species) tend to show high absolute richness but relatively modest species-per-isolate ratios. In contrast, smaller but more intensively curated tropical surveys (e.g., Ivory Coast with 164–194 species per site) reveal exceptional richness per unit sampling effort. Desert surveys demonstrate that harsh environments can support high fungal diversity despite low propagule densities, while specialized single-genus studies (e.g., Fusarium in Australia, Aspergillus in India and Taiwan) reveal that even well-known genera harbor substantial undocumented diversity.

These data collectively underscore the difficulty of direct cross-study comparison: isolate numbers vary by two orders of magnitude, and species-isolate relationships are decidedly nonlinear.

## Species-Abundance Relationships

Two primary graphical tools are used to examine fungal diversity patterns:

1. **Species-isolate curves** plot cumulative species counts against increasing isolate numbers, revealing whether sampling has approached an asymptote.
2. **Rank-abundance plots** display the relative abundance of species in rank order, illustrating both evenness and dominance structure (Fig. 13.1 in the source).

The nonlinear relationship between species richness and isolate number means that simple species-to-isolate ratios are invalid for comparing studies with different sampling efforts. A study with 250 isolates may show a deceptively high species ratio relative to one with 23,500 isolates, not because the community is richer but because the accumulation curve is still in its steep initial phase.

**Rarefaction** addresses this problem by projecting expected species richness at a standardized, smaller isolate number, derived from the ranked abundance distribution of the full sample. This statistical technique permits random resampling and enables meaningful comparison of species richness among studies with unequal sample sizes.

## The Wisconsin Surveys

### Design and Scope

A landmark series of surveys of soil fungi was conducted across different vegetation types in Wisconsin, under the guidance of M. P. Backus and J. T. Curtis at the University of Wisconsin. The state's landscape features reflect three biomes and four major floristic centers (Curtis 1959), with prairies dominating the southwestern half and hardwood forests, conifer stands, and bogs occupying the northeastern half.

The surveys sampled 107 prairies and forests encompassing 18 of the 21 major community types recognized by Curtis, producing more than 25,000 fungal isolates from soil beneath humus and litter layers.

### Species Richness by Vegetation Type (Table 13.2)

| Vegetation Type | Stands | Isolates | Species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prairie | 25 | 5,700 | >111 |
| Southern upland hardwood | 13 | 1,080 | 53 |
| Southern wet-mesic hardwood | 5 | 3,000 | >210 |
| Southern wet forests | 5 | 2,500 | 154 |
| Southern wet shrublands | 5 | 2,500 | 115 |
| Northern upland conifer-hardwood | 36 | 8,061 | >476 |
| Northern conifer swamps/bogs | 15 | 3,000 | >133 |
| Sand barrens | 3 | 1,000–2,000 | >18 |

Northern upland conifer-hardwood forests were by far the most species-rich, with over 476 species — more than double the next-highest community type. This extraordinary richness likely reflects the structural and microhabitat complexity of mixed conifer-hardwood stands, which provide diverse litter types and organic substrates. Southern upland hardwood forests, despite 13 stands, yielded only 53 species, the lowest count among well-sampled types; their relatively uniform litter composition may limit niche differentiation. Sand barrens were the most depauperate, with only 18 species, reflecting the harsh, nutrient-poor conditions of these open, drought-prone habitats. Notably, southern wet-mesic hardwood forests (>210 species from just 5 stands) demonstrated remarkably high richness per stand, suggesting that moisture and soil fertility interact to promote fungal diversity.

### Key Findings

The guiding hypothesis — that soil fungi are responsive to the total environment and that fungal communities would vary systematically with vegetation type — was strongly supported. Fungal communities varied widely both among biomes and within individual community types.

However, the imprint of vegetation on fungal community composition can be overridden by more proximate environmental factors. In conifer-hardwood forests, Christensen (1969) found that calcium content of the litter correlated with the first ordination axis, while dry-mesic sequences aligned with the second and third axes.

**Biome-level indicator assemblages** emerged clearly from the data:

- **Grassland:** Fusarium, Paecilomyces lilacinus, Penicillium restrictum, Mortierella alpina, Pyrenochaeta
- **Conifer forests:** Mortierella vinacea, M. isabellina, M. nana, Paecilomyces carneus, Penicillium daleae, P. janczewskii, Torulomyces lagena, Oidiodendron

Disturbance and treatment effects — including burning, fumigation, and logging — typically produced quantitative rather than qualitative changes in fungal communities, shifting relative abundances without fundamentally altering species composition.

### Extensions and Future Directions

Two promising avenues for extending the Wisconsin surveys were identified:

1. **Expanded quantitative survey with multivariate analysis** — applying modern statistical techniques to the extensive existing dataset to resolve finer-scale environmental gradients.
2. **Expanded species lists using selective isolation techniques** — employing ethanol pasteurization, soil steaming, Botran/dichloran amendments, and high-temperature incubation to recover species missed by standard plating methods.

## Species Distributions and Assemblages

### Environmental Correlates

Fungal community composition correlates strongly with environmental parameters and land use. In temperate regions, a strong positive correlation exists between vascular plant diversity and soil-fungal diversity. Compositions are regulated by climatic and edaphic factors, with ecological similarity superseding geographic proximity as a predictor of community resemblance. An experienced taxonomist can often guess the vegetation type and climate from the 10–20 most abundant fungal species in a soil sample, making soil fungal similarity an outstanding indicator of overall environmental similarity.

### Habitat-Specific Assemblages

**Polar, alpine, and boreal soils** are characterized by Cylindrocarpon, Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, certain Penicillium species, and sterile strains — a cold-adapted assemblage reflecting the physiological constraints of low-temperature environments.

**Cold bogs and conifer swamps** support yeasts, Tolypocladium, Trichoderma, sterile dematiaceous forms, and certain penicillia and eurotialean fungi adapted to saturated, acidic conditions.

**Beech (Fagus) forests** show striking similarities in their principal saprobic species between North America and Europe, suggesting that the genus Fagus and its associated litter chemistry exert a convergent selective pressure on fungal communities across continents.

**Aspergillus** is disproportionately common in desert, subtropical, and tropical soils, where it can constitute up to 20% of total species in some communities. Desert soils more broadly exhibit low propagule densities but high species diversity, with assemblages dominated by melanized conidia, dark-spored ascomycetes, and chlamydospore or sclerotium producers — adaptations for survival under desiccating conditions.

**Grassland and agricultural soils** are typified by Acremonium, Arthrinium, Cladosporium, Drechslera, Fusarium, Idriella, Periconia, Microdochium bolleyi, and Phoma eupyrena, with reduced representation of Mucorales. Clarke and Christensen (1981) identified 12 species of Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium as characteristic of temperate American grasslands, while 9 Fusarium species occur in grasslands worldwide.

**Tropical forests** harbor abundant Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Trichoderma, along with many rare, primarily tropical genera associated with the overlying litter layer.

**Marine and freshwater sediments** contain almost exclusively terrestrial soil fungi, with only a few facultative marine mitosporic fungi present, underscoring the largely allochthonous nature of fungal communities in aquatic sediments.

**Chytridiomycetes** are distributed widely from the Arctic to the tropics, with diversity increasing toward the equator. Their distributions are sensitive to moisture, ion content, pH, and temperature, reflecting the ecological breadth and environmental specialization within this early-diverging fungal lineage.

## Summary

The study of saprobic soil fungi reveals communities of extraordinary diversity whose composition is shaped by a complex interplay of vegetation, climate, soil chemistry, and disturbance history. The Wisconsin surveys remain a benchmark for systematic documentation of fungal diversity across vegetation gradients, while global comparisons highlight both universal patterns — such as the correlation between plant and fungal diversity — and habitat-specific assemblages shaped by local environmental conditions.

Several overarching conclusions emerge from the synthesis of global survey data and the Wisconsin benchmark studies:

- **Scale matters:** Species richness cannot be meaningfully compared across studies without standardization via rarefaction or similar techniques.
- **Environment over geography:** Ecological similarity — in terms of climate, vegetation, and soil conditions — is a stronger predictor of fungal community composition than geographic proximity.
- **Vegetation as a template:** While vegetation type provides a first-order prediction of fungal community structure, proximate factors such as litter chemistry, [[soil-ph]], and moisture can override biome-level patterns.
- **Indicator value:** Soil fungal assemblages serve as sensitive and integrative indicators of environmental conditions, useful for both basic ecology and applied monitoring.
- **Undersampled diversity:** Selective isolation techniques and molecular methods continue to reveal species missed by standard culture-based surveys, suggesting that current species inventories substantially underestimate true fungal diversity.

Methodological advances in rarefaction, selective isolation, and DNA-based community profiling continue to improve the comparability and completeness of species inventories across ecosystems, ensuring that future surveys will build on the foundational work summarized here.
