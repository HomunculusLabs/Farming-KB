# Neurotropic Fungi — Taxonomic Confusion and Identification Challenges

The scientific study of neurotropic (hallucinogenic or psychotropic) fungi
has been plagued by taxonomic confusion, misidentification, and contradictory
[[guzman-allen-gartz-conflicting-chemical-analyses-neurotropic-fungi]] since the first modern investigations began in the 1930s.
Guzman, Allen, and Gartz's comprehensive 1998 review documented 216 species
of neurotropic fungi worldwide, but their paper is itself a catalog of the
difficulties that have made this field one of the most contentious in
mycology. The problems begin with the organisms themselves: many neurotropic
species are morphologically similar to non-active species, the active
compounds degrade over time, and the criteria used to define neurotropic
fungi vary significantly between researchers.

## The Bluing Reaction as a Diagnostic Feature

Among psilocybin-containing mushrooms, the bluing reaction — the rapid
development of blue or blue-green coloration when the flesh of the mushroom
is bruised or cut — has long been used as a field indicator of neurotropic
activity. The blue coloration results from the oxidation of psilocin and
related indole compounds. While the bluing reaction is a useful preliminary
indicator, it is neither perfectly reliable nor universally present. Some
species that contain psilocybin do not bruise blue, and some non-neurotropic
species can develop blue coloration for unrelated reasons. Guzman considers
bluing to be a significant character but not a definitive one, and he
classifies neurotropic Psilocybe species into sections based partly on
whether they exhibit the bluing reaction. The sections with confirmed
bluing include Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies,
Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum.

## Misidentification in Chemical Studies

One of the most persistent problems in the field is the misidentification of
specimens used in chemical analyses. Many chemical studies, particularly
older ones, were conducted without adequate taxonomic expertise. Guzman
discovered that material used by Leslie and Repke to isolate psilocybin was
in fact a mixture of Panaeolus species and [[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]], together with
the non-neurotropic Panaeolus coprophila — all identified in the herbarium
as P. coprophila. Similarly, Psilocybe pseudobullacea, which is not a
bluing species and has no known neurotropic properties, yielded psilocybin
and psilocin in a study of Venezuelan specimens — probably because the
material actually belonged to a different, neurotropic species that had not
yet been formally described. These kinds of errors have propagated through
the literature for decades, creating a web of contradictory reports that
continues to confuse researchers.

## Conflicting Chemical Reports

Even when identification is correct, chemical analyses have frequently
produced contradictory results. The common haymaker's mushroom, Panaeolina
foenisecii, illustrates this problem dramatically. Some studies (Robbers et
al., 1969; Olah, 1969; Pollock, 1976) reported finding psilocybin in this
species, while others (Mantle and Waight, 1969; Ott and Guzman, 1976;
Stijve, 1987) found no psilocybin or psilocin at all. Allen and Merlin
discussed doubts about the psychoactive properties of this fungus, and the
prevailing consensus is that P. foenisecii is more toxic than neurotropic.
Similar contradictions exist for numerous other species. Stijve and Kuyper
failed to find psilocybin in Gymnopilus fulgens, G. spectabilis,
Hygrocybe psittacina, and several other species that had previously been
reported as active. These discrepancies may reflect genuine chemical
variability between populations, differences in analytical methodology, or
ongoing identification errors.

## The Sacred Fungi of Mexico

The ethnographic record adds another layer of complexity. Indigenous peoples
of Mexico have used a variety of fungi in ceremonial contexts for centuries,
and the line between genuinely neurotropic species and fungi that have
acquired sacred status through association or confusion is often unclear.
Guzman reported more than two hundred common names for neurotropic fungi in
Mexico, many in indigenous languages. The most famous, teonanacatl (divine
mushroom), was first recorded by the sixteenth-century Spanish friar
Bernardino de Sahagun and has been applied so indiscriminately that it is
now used to name any Mexican hallucinogenic fungus. Among the most common
Spanish names used by indigenous peoples are san isidros (a reference to
the patron saint of agriculture), pajaritos (little birds), and derrumbes
(landslides). The problem is that some fungi used ceremonially —
particularly certain puffballs and gasteromycetes — may not contain any
neurotropic compounds at all, having been confused with genuinely active
Psilocybe species.

## The Problem of Volatile Active Compounds

The chemical instability of neurotropic compounds creates additional
identification challenges. Psilocybin and psilocin are volatile substances
that degrade over time, particularly in dried specimens. Repke and
colleagues showed that specimens of [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-baeocystis-knobby-tops]] and P. cyanescens
contained no trace of indoles when analyzed 20-60 days after collection.
Ohenoja and colleagues detected decreasing psilocybin concentrations in
herbarium specimens of P. semilanceata according to age: 0.014% dry weight
in specimens from 1869, 0.67% in specimens from 1954, and 0.84% in
specimens from 1976. This degradation explains why Mexican Indians
traditionally discard dried specimens more than a year old. The Mexican
ethnographer [[psilocybe-as-dominant-neurotropic-fungus-genus]]
Guzman himself experienced hallucinations without consuming any fungi when
sleeping in a small room filled with fresh specimens of mixed Psilocybe
species, demonstrating that the [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] can be absorbed through
inhalation.

## Classification Into Four Groups

Guzman, Allen, and Gartz divided the 216 known neurotropic species into
four groups based on their chemical properties and ethnographic status. The
first and largest group contains species with psilocybin and related indole
compounds, spanning 13 genera including Psilocybe (116 species), Gymnopilus
(14), Panaeolus (13), Copelandia (12), and smaller numbers of Hypholoma,
Pluteus, Inocybe, Conocybe, Panaeolina, Gerronema, Agrocybe, Galerina, and
Mycena. The second group contains species with ibotenic acid — Amanita
muscaria, A. pantherina, and A. regalis. The third group consists of ergot
fungi: [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]] and allies (5 species of Claviceps and 2 of
Cordyceps). The fourth group comprises fungi used as sacred mushrooms for
which no reliable chemical studies have been found, including boletes,
Russula species, and gasteromycetes used by indigenous peoples.

## Implications for Research and Safety

The taxonomic and chemical confusion surrounding neurotropic fungi has
serious practical implications. For researchers, it means that much of the
published literature on the chemistry and distribution of these fungi must
be treated with caution. For foragers and recreational users, the
similarities between neurotropic and toxic species create potentially
dangerous identification challenges. Several genera that contain neurotropic
species (particularly Inocybe and Galerina) also contain deadly poisonous
species. The confusion between Copelandia species and the non-active but
edible Panaeolus antillarum, which grow together on cow dung, illustrates
how easily mistakes can occur. Rigorous taxonomic work, combined with modern
chemical analytical methods, remains essential for advancing the scientific
understanding of these culturally and pharmacologically significant organisms.
