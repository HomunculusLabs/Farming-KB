# Sterile Inoculation Technique for Grain Jars

## Overview

Sterile inoculation is the critical procedure [[cervantes-tissue-culture-micropropagation]]

from clean plates provide the most reliable inoculation with known genetics.

## Flame Sterilisation Protocol

Before any inoculation, metal tools that contact the substrate or inoculant
must be flame-sterilised. For syringes, the needle tip is heated until
glowing red using a butane lighter or alcohol lamp. The needle is then
allowed to cool for several seconds before use; a hot needle kills spores on
contact. For agar work, the scalpel blade receives the same treatment.
Flame sterilisation must be performed immediately before each jar is
inoculated, not just once at the start of the session. A critical safety
warning: alcohol spray and Lysol are highly flammable. Flame sterilisation
must never be performed near surfaces that have been freshly sprayed with
these products. Many cultivators spray their work area and then wait several
minutes before beginning flame work.

## Jar Opening and Inoculation

The key principle is minimising the duration that the sterile grain is
exposed to open air. Lids are opened just a crack, not fully removed.
The inoculant is introduced through this narrow opening and the lid is
immediately closed. Working swiftly but deliberately is essential.
Hesitation increases contamination risk, but rushing leads to sloppy
technique that is equally damaging. After the inoculant is introduced,
the jar is shaken vigorously to distribute the inoculant throughout the
substrate. This redistribution is critical for colonisation speed. When
spores or mycelium are deposited in one location, colonisation proceeds
outward from that point. Shaking disperses the inoculant across many grain
kernels, creating dozens of simultaneous colonisation fronts that converge
much faster than a single front expanding outward.

## Lid Management Post-Inoculation

After inoculation and shaking, the lid is loosened again rather than
tightened. The loosened lid allows gas exchange, which the mycelium
requires for respiration during colonisation. Some cultivators use lids
with filtered gas exchange ports (polyfill, tyvek, or synthetic filter
discs) which can remain tight while still allowing breathing. However,
the simple loose-lid technique described in the original grass seed
protocol has been used successfully for decades. The trade-off is that
loose lids provide less protection against airborne contamination during
the colonisation period, so jar placement in a clean draft-free location
becomes more important. Filtered lids are a worthwhile upgrade but are
not strictly necessary.

## The Inoculation Hood

A simple still-air box (also called a glove box or inoculation hood) can
be constructed from a cardboard box with two arm holes cut in the sides.
Even this rudimentary barrier significantly reduces contamination by
eliminating air currents that carry contaminant spores. More elaborate
setups use clear plastic storage containers with tight-fitting lids and
grommeted arm holes. The principle is the same: create a still-air
environment where the only air movement is caused by the cultivator's
arm movements. Professional laboratories use laminar flow hoods that
provide a constant stream of HEPA-filtered air, pushing contaminants
away from the work area. While superior, they represent a significant
investment and are not required for successful hobby cultivation.
