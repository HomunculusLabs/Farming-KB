## From Discovery to Research Program

After the discovery of LSD's extraordinary psychic effects on April 19, 1943,
the substance that five years earlier had been excluded from further
investigation was readmitted into Sandoz's series of experimental preparations.
Most fundamental studies on animals were carried out by Dr. Aurelio Cerletti in
the Sandoz pharmacological department, headed by Professor Ernst Rothlin.

The tests involved healthy research subjects as well as schizophrenic patients.
The dosages, substantially lower than Hofmann's first self-experiment with 0.25
mg LSD tartrate, amounted to only 0.02 to 0.13 mg. The emotional state during
LSD inebriation was predominantly euphoric in these controlled settings, in

## W. A. Stoll's Self-Experiment

W. A. Stoll also performed a self-experiment, the first published by a
psychiatrist, providing a detailed description of characteristic features of
LSD inebriation. At 8 o'clock he took 60 mcg (0.06 mg) of LSD. After about 20
minutes, the first symptoms appeared: heaviness in the limbs and slight ataxia.

A phase of general malaise followed in parallel with a drop in blood pressure,
then euphoria set in. In a darkened room, Stoll experienced "an unprecedented
experience of unimaginable intensity" characterized by "an unbelievable
profusion of optical hallucinations that appeared and vanished with great speed."
He saw circles, vortices, sparks, showers, crosses, and spirals in constant,
racing flux, all colored with bright red, yellow, and green predominating.

The hallucinations progressed from elementary forms (rays, bundles of rays,
rings, vortices, loops, sprays, clouds) to more highly organized visions:
arches, rows of arches, seas of roofs, desert landscapes, terraces, flickering
fire, starry skies of unbelievable splendor. A significant pattern was the
endless repetition of the same elements: many sparks, many circles, many arches,
many windows. He felt himself "one with all romanticists and dreamers," and
stood "at the pinnacle of artistic experience."

## Delysid: LSD as an Experimental Drug

Based on the promising results from Stoll's research and subsequent studies,
Sandoz made LSD available to research institutes and physicians worldwide as an
experimental drug, giving it the trade name Delysid (D-Lysergsaure-diathylamid),
a name Hofmann himself proposed.

The Sandoz prospectus for Delysid described two formulations: sugar-coated
tablets containing 0.025 mg (25 mcg), and ampoules of 1 ml containing 0.1 mg
(100 mcg) for oral administration. The solution could also be injected
subcutaneously or intravenously, producing identical effects but more rapidly.
