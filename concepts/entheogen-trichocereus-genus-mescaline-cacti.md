# Trichocereus Genus Mescaline Cacti

The genus Trichocereus (recently reclassified by many taxonomists into
Echinopsis) comprises approximately 40 species of columnar cacti native to
the subtropical and temperate regions of the South American Andes. Several
species within this genus contain the phenethylamine alkaloid [[peyote-ceremony]] (Lophophora williamsii), Trichocereus
cacti are large, fast-growing, tree-like columns that have been cultivated
for ceremonial use since ancient times. Their ease of propagation and rapid
growth rate have made them a focal point of both traditional indigenous
practice and modern ethnobotanical investigation.

## Botanical Description

Trichocereus species are tall, ribbed columnar cacti that can reach heights
of 3 to 6 meters or more under favorable conditions. The stems are typically
bluish-green to dark green, with well-defined ribs ranging from 4 to 10 in
number depending on the species. Areoles bear spines of varying length and
density, and large nocturnal flowers produce white to pale-colored blooms.
The genus belongs to the family Cactaceae and is distributed along the Andean
cordillera from Ecuador and Peru through Bolivia into northern Argentina and
Chile.

## Species Containing Mescaline

The following Trichocereus species are recognized as containing psychoactive
concentrations of [[peyote-ceremony]] ritual objects have been found dating to 500 BCE, but
the Andean cactus tradition may be even older. An early Peruvian report from
approximately 1571 states that Inca medicine men foretold the future using
these cactus preparations, indicating continuity of use from pre-Columbian
through colonial times.

In the Andean region, San Pedro is known by several indigenous names including
aguacolla, giganton, huachuma, and achuma. The cactus is sometimes planted
along field margins as a living fence to keep sheep and cattle from grazing,
reflecting its integration into everyday agricultural life alongside its
sacred ceremonial role. The modern name "San Pedro" (Saint Peter) likely
stems from the Christian belief that Saint Peter holds the keys to heaven,
reflecting the syncretic blending of indigenous and Catholic traditions.

## Chemical Profile

[[peyote-ceremony]] is also quite distinct from that produced by cacti of the
Trichocereus family, reflecting differences in the full alkaloid profile.

Mescaline belongs to the phenethylamine family, making it chemically distinct
from the indole-based psychedelics such as [[dmt-and-ayahuasca-history]], and
[[mescaline-compound-profile]] was first isolated from
a Trichocereus species in 1896, making it the first psychedelic compound
ever extracted and identified, and it was first synthesized in 1919.

## Cultivation and Propagation

Trichocereus cacti are relatively quick-growing and easy to cultivate from
seed or cutting. Vegetative propagation from cuttings is straightforward:
sections of stem are allowed to callus for several days to weeks and then
placed in well-draining soil. Under favorable conditions, growth rates of
30 cm or more per year are common. Grafting onto faster-growing rootstock
such as Pereskiopsis can further accelerate growth, and this technique is
widely used by horticulturalists seeking to rapidly increase stock of
desirable specimens.

The bitterness of Trichocereus tissue varies considerably between species and
populations, ranging from very bitter to nearly tasteless. This variation in
taste does not reliably correlate with mescaline content, which can differ
significantly depending on growing conditions, age, and specific chemotype.
Trichocereus cacti are considered legal for ornamental cultivation in most
jurisdictions, though extraction of their active alkaloids is generally
prohibited.

## Preparations and Ceremony

The most common preparation method involves slicing short sections of stem
like bread loaves and boiling them in water for several hours to produce a
bitter, nauseating beverage. In Peru and Ecuador, this drink is called cimora.
The ceremony associated with San Pedro is described in greater detail in the
entry on [[peyote-ceremony]] make them a subject of particular
interest for conservation-oriented researchers seeking sustainable
alternatives to wild harvesting of endangered cactus species.
