# Petroleum Degradation by Fungi

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and related petroleum compounds are
widespread, hazardous environmental pollutants released through the burning
of fossil fuels, coal mining, oil drilling, and industrial processes. Several
PAHs are mutagenic and carcinogenic. [[petroleum-hydrocarbon-fungal-biodegradation]]
by fungi, particularly white rot species, represents one of the most promising
biological approaches for remediation of these persistent contaminants.

## The Petroleum Hydrocarbon Problem

Petroleum hydrocarbons encompass a diverse group of compounds including
polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, BTEX compounds (benzene, toluene,
ethylbenzene, and xylenes), and aliphatic hydrocarbons. PAHs are particularly
problematic due to their chemical stability, hydrophobicity, and tendency to
accumulate in sediments and organic matter. Their recalcitrance increases with
molecular weight and degree of aromatic ring condensation.

## White Rot Fungi as Degraders

White Rot Fungi possess lignin-degrading systems (LDS) that catalyze
degradation of both lignin and petroleum hydrocarbons using a non-specific
free radical mechanism. This non-specificity is key to their ability to
degrade a wide variety of structurally diverse pollutants. The three major
families of lignin-modifying enzymes involved are laccases, lignin peroxidases
(LiP), and manganese-dependent peroxidases (MnP).

At least 22 PAHs undergo 70 to 100 percent breakdown in 27 days in
nitrogen-limited cultures of Phanerochaete Chrysosporium. The key step
in degradation by these enzymes involves formation of free radical
intermediates, which are highly reactive and rapidly react with other
chemicals, providing the basis for non-specific degradation.

## PAH Degradation Mechanisms

### Lignin Peroxidase Pathway

PAHs such as benzo[a]pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, anthracene, pyrene, and
perylene are directly oxidized by LiPs of P. chrysosporium to quinone-type
products. Both ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic cultures of P. chrysosporium
degraded radiolabelled phenanthrene to carbon dioxide, suggesting that
ligninolytic enzymes as well as other non-ligninolytic enzymes may be involved
in the degradation pathway.

### Manganese Peroxidase Pathway

The predominant ligninolytic enzyme during anthracene and benzo[a]pyrene
degradation by Phanerochaete laevis was MnP. MnP levels were stimulated by
Mn(II) in the culture medium. In vitro, MnPs produced small amounts of
quinones as intermediates in PAH degradation.

### Laccase Pathway

Laccase from Coriolopsis gallica oxidized benzo[a]pyrene, methylanthracenes,
anthracene, biphenylene, acenaphthene, and phenanthrene. Laccases of
Trametes Versicolor carried out oxidation of acenaphthene,
acenaphthylene, anthracene, and fluorene, mediated by small-molecular-weight
aromatic compounds such as phenol, aniline, and 4-hydroxybenzyl alcohol.

Mediators such as ABTS (2,2-prime-azinobis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonic
acid)) and 1-hydroxybenzotriazole mediate transformation of anthracene by
laccase of T. versicolor. Two- to fivefold augmentation of anthracene,
pyrene, and benzo[a]pyrene degradation occurred in the presence of non-ionic
surfactants such as Tween 80 by Bjerkandera species.

## Degradation of Other Petroleum Compounds

### Polychlorinated Biphenyls (PCBs)

PCB degradation by basidiomycetes depends on the level of chlorination and
the fungal strain employed. P. chrysosporium degraded 82, 31, and 18 percent
of Aroclor 1242, 1254, and 1260 respectively. This was the first conclusive
demonstration of substantial degradation of Aroclor 1260 by a fungus in pure
culture. Bjerkandera adusta, Pleurotus Ostreatus, and T. versicolor were
more efficient than P. chrysosporium at degrading several PCB congeners.

### Chlorophenols

Pentachlorophenol (PCP) is rapidly degraded by P. chrysosporium under
nitrogen-limiting secondary metabolic conditions. PCP degradation is
initiated by a LiP- or MnP-catalyzed oxidative dechlorination reaction.
Laccase-producing cultures of T. versicolor also catalyzed PCP degradation.
The pathway for 2,4,6-trichlorophenol degradation involves cycles of
peroxidase-catalyzed oxidative dechlorination followed by quinone reduction.

### Dioxins

A multistep pathway for degradation of 2,7-dichlorodibenzo-dioxin involving
LiP and MnP in P. chrysosporium has been proposed. Up to 60 percent
degradation of 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) by
Phanerochaete sordida has been reported.

## Field Applications

The ability of P. chrysosporium to degrade PAHs has led to its application
in treating coal and creosote-contaminated soils supplemented with wood
chips, corn cobs, or sawdust. PAH constituents were reduced to 10 to 20
percent of original levels in some trials. However, field-scale experiments
have sometimes shown limited results due to competition from indigenous
organisms and difficulty growing fungi to sufficient biomass.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Practical difficulties in field bioremediation include competition from
indigenous soil organisms, the need for supplemental carbon sources, and
the challenge of establishing sufficient fungal biomass. PCR-based monitoring
methods may prove useful for assessing survival of applied fungi and their
relative contribution to bioremediation in soil.

Enhanced degradation through surfactant addition, mediator compounds, and
nutrient optimization represents promising areas for further research.
Combination of fungal treatment with physical remediation methods may
improve overall effectiveness for heavily contaminated sites.
