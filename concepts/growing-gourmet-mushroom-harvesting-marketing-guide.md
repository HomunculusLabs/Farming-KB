## Harvesting Principles

Mushrooms can be compared to fish in their perishability. Once harvested, they are
quick to spoil unless properly cared for. Simple guidelines prevail in proper
harvesting: young mushrooms last much longer after harvest than aged mushrooms,
and once spores have developed on the gills, perishability is dramatically
accelerated. For mushrooms with partial veils such as the [[mushroom-veil-function-wind-light-protection-prior-to-spore-release]].

The cultivator must constantly counterbalance maximum yield with marketability.
Stamets notes that at some magic moment in the maturity of a mushroom, the form
changes without appreciable increase in mass. The flesh of a mushroom at the
adolescent stage is much thicker than when fully mature, as tissue is re-
proportioned from the cap to the expanding gills and cap margin. No real advantage
in weight is realized by picking a fully mature mushroom versus a mature
adolescent, and the younger specimens store longer and taste better. For species
without veils, the optimal stage is when the cap margins are still incurved.

## Harvesting Clusters and Bouquets

A key insight from Stamets is that clusters yield higher quality mushrooms than
those grown individually, pound for pound. Bouquets of mushrooms have obvious
advantages from both harvesting and marketing perspectives. They can be picked
with ease, needing minimum handling and trimming. Once harvested, the mushrooms
protect one another by being bunched together, limiting the damage caused by
individual loose mushrooms jostling against each other. All the gilled mushrooms
described in the book can be harvested as bouquets, sold much like broccoli.

Most importantly, bouquets at the ideal stage for harvest are composed of younger
mushrooms, extending shelf life far beyond that of individual mushrooms. Each
species passes through an ideal stage as it matures. For most species, the ideal
stage is when the caps are still convex and before flattening out. Cropping
mushrooms in clusters is labor-intensive, and in Holland mechanical harvesters
have been devised to harvest mushrooms from horizontal beds, eliminating the
largest labor contingent on a mushroom farm: the pickers. However, hand harvesting
remains standard for premium markets.

## Post-Harvest Cooling and Packaging

Once mushrooms have been harvested, they must be quickly chilled. Most pickers
place mushrooms directly into open-grate plastic baskets which are frequently
ferried to the cold room. Larger farms utilize blast chillers that precipitously
drop the temperature from room temperature to near freezing. A common mistake is
placing fresh mushrooms directly into cardboard boxes after picking, since
cardboard insulates and prevents rapid cooling. The ideal storage temperature is
34 degrees Fahrenheit (1 to 2 degrees Celsius), which dramatically slows metabolic
activity and extends shelf life.
