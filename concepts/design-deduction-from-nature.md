## Design by Deduction from Nature

Design by [[masanobu-fukuoka]] on his remarkable voyage to [[acacia]] provides a site for birds to roost, initiating soil change so that clumps or coppices of forest form, the same techniques and allied species can be used to pioneer food forests. Many native peoples evolve scattered forest nuclei based on pioneer trees, termite mounds, and compost heaps. Perches can be provided for birds to drop pioneer seeds, setting up plant nuclei in degraded lands.

**Biological pest control**: Nest holes can be provided so that owls move in to control rodents, purple martins reduce mosquitoes, or woodpeckers control codling moth. Many nurse plants allow insect predators to overwinter, feed, or shelter within gardens, as do small ponds for frogs and rock piles for lizards. Woodpeckers alone reduce codling moth by 40-60%.

**Succession for weed management**: To limit a rampant plant or defeat invasive grasses, nature imposes successions and limits on every species. Once the rules are known, succession can be used to limit or exclude problem species. Soft vines will smother prickly shrubs; browsed or cut out, they allow trees to permanently shade out the shrub, or rot its seeds in mulch. Kikuyu grass is blocked from spreading by low hedges of comfrey, lemongrass, arrowroot (Canna spp.), or nasturtiums. Rampant species such as Lantana can be smothered by vines like chayote (Sechium edulis) and succeeded with palm/legume forests.

**Design by analogy**: Hundreds of botanical lessons exist. If thistles grow around a rabbit warren, then disturbing the soil, supplying urine and manure, and sowing seed may produce globe artichokes. Penning goats or sheep on a place, then shutting them out and planting was how the idea of chicken or pig "tractors" evolved to remove stubborn weeds like nut-grass, Convolvulus, onion-weed, and twitch. Fences or pits can trap wind-blown debris — dried leaves, rabbit and sheep manures, seagrasses — for garden use.

### Landscape

Gullies, ridgetops, natural shade, the sides of multi-storey buildings, and exposed sunny sites all demonstrate different opportunities, as do various velocities and grades of streams or rock-falls. Every such special site can be used — as an aid to food storage, food dehydration, as an energy source, or as a site for a special animal or plant. New opportunities are created over time as groves of trees grow, earthbanks are raised, houses are built, or caves are excavated.

It is in the creation of microclimates that natural diversity and richness increase. Every clump of trees invites new species to establish, every shaded area provides a refuge from heat, and every stone pile creates a moist and shaded soil site. These evolutions can be planned, and plantings designed to take advantage of them, using data derived from close observation of natural systems.

### Philosophy

Life is not all survival in a stable ecosystem. By designing well and then observing system evolution, contemplative and celebratory time becomes available. In celebration, myths and skills important to future generations can be incorporated. In contemplation, more refined, profound, or subtle insights into good procedures emerge.

To become a good designer is to be in search of an understanding of nature, and to be content with the search itself. It means designing by natural example: becoming aware, taking notes, sitting a long time in one place, watching the wind behave and the trees respond, thrusting a hand into the soil to feel it for moisture (always more moist on the shade side of tussock grasses), and becoming sensitive to the processes and sights about you. In microcosm and macrocosm, the world offers the very best lessons to adopt.
## Practical Implementation Steps

The process of designing by deduction follows several key steps:

1. **Observe natural patterns** in the local environment over multiple seasons
2. **Identify successful strategies** that nature uses to solve problems
3. **Abstract the underlying principles** from these observations
4. **Adapt and apply** these principles to designed systems
5. **Monitor and refine** based on system response

## Case Studies and Examples

- **Windbreak placement**: Studying natural tree lines on ridge tops informs artificial windbreak positioning
- **Water harvesting**: Observing how water collects in natural depressions guides earthworks design
- **Plant associations**: Natural plant communities reveal companion planting strategies
- **Succession patterns**: Understanding natural succession informs food forest design timelines

## Design Principles Derived

- Work with natural forces rather than opposing them
- Use biological resources to perform functions whenever possible
- Design for multiple functions and elements
- Apply small-scale intensive solutions before large-scale extensive ones
