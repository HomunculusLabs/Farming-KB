## The Codex Evidence

Pre-Columbian codices and stone carvings provide visual evidence of mushroom ceremonial use. The Mixtec codex Vindobonensis Mexicanus I depicts a figure holding mushrooms, and several Aztec stone sculptures show deities with mushroom-like elements. The Nahuatl word teonanacatl literally translates to "flesh of the gods" or "divine mushroom," and Bernardino de Sahagun's Florentine Codex (1569) records detailed descriptions of the mushroom's effects: that it caused visions, that people saw snakes and other visions, and that some saw the devil. The Spanish missionaries who recorded these accounts were explicitly hostile to the practice, so their descriptions likely understate rather than exaggerate its cultural importance.

## Contemporary Mazatec Practice

The Mazatec velada as practiced by curanderos like Maria Sabina follows a structured ceremonial format. The ceremony takes place at night in darkness, with the participants consuming fresh mushrooms in pairs. The curandero (healer) sings icaros — ceremonial songs that guide the experience and call upon protective spirits. Water and tobacco are the only other substances used. The mushrooms are regarded not as drugs but as sentient beings — "los ninitos" (the little ones) — who speak through the participant and reveal the cause of illness or the solution to life problems. The diagnostic and therapeutic dimensions are inseparable: the vision reveals both the problem and its cure.

## Ethnobotanical Species

The primary species used in Mesoamerican ceremonies belong to the genus Psilocybe, with Psilocybe mexicana, Psilocybe caerulescens, and Psilocybe zapotecorum being the most commonly documented. Psilocybe mexicana, the species first identified scientifically by Roger Heim and Albert Hofmann, grows in grasslands and meadows above 1,000 meters elevation. P. caerulescens is found in cloud forests and high-altitude pastures. The Mazatec distinguish between different mushroom varieties by their effects: some are used for diagnosing illness, others for divination, and still others for healing specific conditions. This sophisticated taxonomy of psychoactive effects parallels the entheogenic knowledge systems found in other indigenous cultures throughout the Americas.
