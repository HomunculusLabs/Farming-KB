# Night-Break Photoperiodism Manipulation in Commercial Agriculture

## Overview

The discovery that plants measure the length of continuous darkness
(rather than day length) and that this measurement can be disrupted
by brief light flashes has been transformative for commercial
agriculture. Daniel Chamovitz describes in "What a Plant Knows" how
the **night-break technique** — turning lights on for just a few
minutes in the middle of the night — allows growers to precisely
control when plants flower, enabling year-round production of
seasonal crops.

## The Scientific Discovery

The foundation for night-break technology was laid by Wightman W.
Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture,
who in 1918 discovered photoperiodism through their study of the
Maryland Mammoth tobacco strain. This tobacco grew continuously
without flowering until exposed to artificially shortened days.

Subsequent researchers discovered the critical insight: it is not
day length that plants measure, but **the length of continuous
darkness**. This was demonstrated through night-break experiments:

- Short-day plants (flower in long nights) could be prevented from
  flowering by a brief light interruption during the night
- Long-day plants (flower in short nights) could be induced to
  flower by a brief light flash during an otherwise long night

This worked even when the light interruption lasted only a few
seconds — the duration of light didn't matter, only its presence
during the critical dark period.

## How Night-Break Technology Works

### Short-Day Plant Manipulation

Plants like chrysanthemums and soybeans are short-day plants: they
flower when nights exceed a critical length. In commercial
production, this means they naturally flower in autumn as days
shorten.

Night-break technology reverses this by turning on lights for 2-4
minutes around midnight during the autumn and winter. This brief
interruption signals to the plant that the night is short (as in
summer), suppressing flowering until the grower is ready.

When the grower wants flowering to begin, they simply stop the
night-break treatments. The plant suddenly experiences long,
uninterrupted nights and initiates flowering within approximately
two weeks.

### Long-Day Plant Manipulation

Long-day plants like irises and barley flower when nights are
short. In winter, when nights are naturally long, growers can
induce flowering by providing a brief light flash in the middle
of the night, breaking the long dark period into what the plant
interprets as two short nights.

## The Chrysanthemum Industry

Chamovitz uses chrysanthemum production for Mother's Day as the
canonical example of night-break manipulation:

1. Chrysanthemums naturally flower in autumn as days shorten
2. Mother's Day falls in spring (May in the US), when days are
   naturally long and nights short
3. To delay flowering from autumn to spring, growers subject
   chrysanthemums to night-break lighting throughout autumn
   and winter in greenhouses
4. Approximately two weeks before Mother's Day, night-break
   lighting is discontinued
5. Plants experience their first long nights in months and
   initiate flowering simultaneously
6. All plants flower in synchrony for a coordinated harvest
   and shipping window

This level of production control would be impossible without the
understanding that plants measure continuous darkness duration
rather than day length.

## Wavelength Specificity

The night-break technique is maximally effective with **red light**
(~660nm). Blue and green light flashes during the night do not
influence flowering. This specificity reflects the phytochrome
system's sensitivity: red light converts phytochrome to its active
Pfr form, which the plant interprets as "lights on."

Far-red light (~730nm) has the opposite effect — it cancels the
red light signal. If red light is followed immediately by far-red,
the plant behaves as if neither flash occurred. This red/far-red
reversibility is a unique feature of phytochrome-based photoperiodic
control.

## Maryland Mammoth: The Discovery Strain

The Maryland Mammoth tobacco strain that launched photoperiodism
research was discovered around 1906 in southern Maryland tobacco
fields. It grew continuously to 4.5 meters with nearly 100 leaves,
refusing to flower until frost. While seemingly advantageous, its
failure to flower meant no seed for the next crop.

Garner and Allard's experiments showed that Maryland Mammoth
responded to artificially shortened days by flowering, establishing
that light duration controlled the vegetative-to-reproductive
transition. This was the first strong evidence that plants measure
light intake quantitatively.

## Short-Day vs Long-Day Classification

Plants are classified by their photoperiodic response:

**Short-day plants (SDP)**: Require nights longer than a critical
duration to flower. Examples include chrysanthemums, soybeans,
poinsettias, strawberries, and Cannabis (most photoperiodic
strains flower under 12-hour dark periods).

**Long-day plants (LDP)**: Require nights shorter than a critical
duration to flower. Examples include irises, barley, spinach,
lettuce, and radishes.

**Day-neutral plants**: Flower regardless of photoperiod.
Examples include tomatoes, cucumbers, and rice.

The critical night length varies by species and sometimes by
cultivar, allowing breeders to select for specific flowering
times through photoperiodic response manipulation.
