# European Truffles: Species, Habitat, and Distribution

## Overview

Truffles are subterranean fungi of the family Tuberaceae, remarkable for their structure, unique habitat, and unequaled flavor and aroma. They constitute one of the most valuable fungal products in European commerce, with a history of culinary use extending back to ancient Greece and Rome. The Périgord truffle (*[[tuber-melanosporum]]*) is the species of world-wide fame, though several other species contribute to the truffle trade.

## Historical Background

It appears that **terfas** (of Africa and Asia), not the now-famous truffles of southern Europe, were the subterranean fungi known to be highly prized by the early Greeks and Romans. [[european-truffles]] came into use in the courts of France and Italy prior to the 14th century. The earliest French references concern the **summer truffle**, *[[tuber-aestivum]]*. The Périgord truffle (*T. melanosporum*) made its entrance into Parisian culinary proceedings towards the close of the 18th century, its superior merits first discovered by Bruyerin-Champier, medical adviser to Francis the First and Henry the Second. In modern times, truffles have grown in favor to such an extent that French hotels might be divided into two groups: those with chefs who require and know the value of truffles, and those whose chefs do not.

## Taxonomy and Species

All truffles are members of the genus *Tuber*, the dominant genus in the family Tuberaceae. The genus embraces more than fifty known species. The more important edible species, six in number, divide into two groups:

### Group 1: Dark Brown or Black Truffles (Wart-Covered Surface)

- **Tuber aestivum** Vittad. — The summer truffle; earliest of the ripening period, found from early summer until early autumn
- **T. mesentericum** Vittad. — Found during much the same period as T. aestivum
- **T. uncinatum** Chatin — An essentially autumn species
- **T. brumale** Vittad. — A winter-ripening species
- **T. melanosporum** Vittad. — The Périgord truffle; winter-ripening; the most highly prized and abundant edible species. Varies from the size of a small nut to that of a man's fist; specimens have been found weighing more than one pound. The wart-like surface structures are somewhat pyramidal. A cross section shows a brown ground effect marbled by numerous irregularly coursing veins of lighter color. Asci embedded in quantity in the dark ground tissue, with brown spores giving the tissue most of its color. Spores are not set free until the fruit body disintegrates.

### Group 2: Yellow or Yellowish Brown Truffles (Usually Smooth Surface)

- **[[tuber-magnatum]]** Pico — The only really important species of the lighter-colored group. Fruit body yellow (resembling a spherical yam), smooth but somewhat irregular. Internal tissue lighter in color than the black truffles. Fruit bodies average larger than any other species. The flavor is somewhat onion-like. Highly esteemed in Italy, where it is known as "fiorini" or "tartufo bianco." Particularly abundant in Lombardy and far southward towards Rome.

## Distribution and Geography

### Geographic Range

Truffles are found in the temperate and warm temperate regions of the western part of southern Europe — from Italy westward, and from the 40th to the 48th parallel. France is practically the only country in which truffles constitute an article of commerce, although they are marketed to some extent in Italy and Spain.

### Tree Associations

From early times truffles have been recognized as occurring in close association with the roots of certain trees:

- ***[[quercus-ilex]]*** — Evergreen oak (the most important association)
- ***Q. coccifera*** — Another evergreen oak
- ***Q. sessiliflora*** — Velvety deciduous oak of somewhat wider distribution
- Chestnut, pine (claimed), and a long list of other plants characteristic of temperate regions

The **natural home** of truffles is under the shade of the oak, and no professional truffle hunter would waste time looking elsewhere for these plants in any quantity.

### Soil Requirements

Truffles are **distinctly calcareous plants**, sharply limited in their distribution by the presence of clay, seldom trespassing upon sand. They are even more sensitive to soil type than the plants with whose roots they are ordinarily associated.

**Key indicators:**
- Wherever **Calluna** (heather) and **Erica** are found — plants common in moist silicious soils — truffles may **not** be anticipated
- Truffles are most abundant in the **Jurassic formation**, especially the Lias, the Oolithes, and the Neocomian
- In France, principal truffle regions include the **garigues of the Midi**, the **causses of the southwest**, the **cosses and galluches of Poitou**, and the **jauses of Dauphiny**
- The great truffle field of **Mont Ventoux** is practically one continuous truffière up to a height of 800 meters

### The Calcareous Requirement

The preference for calcareous soils is so strong that it helps explain biogeographic puzzles. European truffles are not found in quantity in portions of Algeria despite similar climate, because the soil chemistry differs. Conversely, African terfas — which occupy a similar ecological niche — occur only in silicious soils. The Sahara sands, however, are claimed to contain a large proportion of lime, suggesting the difference between silicious soils of the northern Sahara and calcareous soils of France may be largely chemical rather than physical.

## The Mycorrhizal Relationship

The parasitism (or more accurately, **mycorrhizal symbiosis**) of the truffle has been much discussed. The prevalent belief is that truffles are to a considerable extent dependent upon the living roots of associated plants for a portion of their nourishment. Professor Mattirolo of Turin, who devoted more study to these plants than any other botanist, strongly supported this view. However, the organic matter of the soil may also play some part in nourishment, and mycelium is found at considerable distances from living roots, in close connection with decaying leaves. Truffles are not found in dense, moist woods, and the habitats of mosses would never foster them.
