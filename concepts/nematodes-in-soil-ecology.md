# Nematodes in Soil Ecology

## Overview

Nematodes are nonsegmented, blind roundworms that are the second most dominant form of animal life after arthropods. Over 20,000 species have been identified, with scientists suggesting as many as 1 million may exist. Most gardeners only know the parasitic root-feeders, but the majority of nematodes are beneficial mineralizers crucial to soil [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]].

## The Five Feeding Groups

### Bacterial-Feeding Nematodes (Bacterivores)

Equipped with hollow tube mouthparts, these nematodes consume multitudes of bacteria per hour. They mineralize the nutrients held in bacterial bodies, releasing them (especially nitrogen as ammonium) into the rhizosphere. They release more nitrogen than protozoa do because nematodes need less nitrogen for their own metabolism. They are crucial [[nitrogen-cycle-in-soil|nitrogen cyclers]].

### Fungal-Feeding Nematodes (Fungivores)

Have stylet mouthparts for puncturing the chitin cell walls of fungal hyphae. Like bacterivores, they mineralize nutrients from their prey. Their grazing on fungi actually frees up food resources to the point that fungal populations can increase, resulting in more decomposition of organic litter. Nematodes are thus indirectly responsible for some soil decomposition.

### Predatory Nematodes

Feed on protozoa, algae, other small soil food web members, and even other nematodes. They prevent overgrazing of bacteria and fungi and keep destructive nematode populations (especially root-feeders) in check. The first [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]] sold commercially for gardening were used to control slugs.

### Root-Feeding Nematodes (Herbivores/Plant Parasites)

Have needle-like stylets that puncture plant cell walls. They can be ectoparasitic (feeding on root surfaces) or endoparasitic (entering roots to feed). They create lesions, cysts, and root knots that damage plants. In a healthy food web, these are kept in check by predatory nematodes and competition.

### Omnivorous Nematodes

Eat any and all of the above, down to the merest [[bloomfield-sphaerobolus-artillery-fungus-spore-launch]]. Some also ingest [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] directly, contributing to decomposition.

## Numbers in Soil

A teaspoon of good garden soil averages:
- ~20 bacterial-feeding nematodes
- ~20 fungal-feeding nematodes
- A few predatory and plant-eating nematodes
- Total: 40-50 nematodes

The ratio of fungal to bacterial feeders directly reflects the availability of their food sources, making it a useful indicator of soil food web balance.

## Mineralization Role

Nematodes that eat fungi and bacteria release previously immobilized nitrogen into the rhizosphere in ammonium form. Because nematodes need less nitrogen than protozoa, they release proportionally more. If nitrogen-fixing bacteria are present (pH above 7), the ammonium is converted to nitrate. If not, nitrogen remains as ammonium.

## Movement and Soil Requirements

Because nematodes are larger than bacteria, fungi, and protozoa, they require more porous soils to travel through. Their numbers are reduced in compacted soils, heavy clays, or soils with poor structure. They need adequate moisture and aeration to move and feed.

## Significance in Soil Food Web

Nematodes serve multiple critical functions:

- **Nutrient cycling**: [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] feeders mineralize nutrients
- **Population control**: Predators keep root-feeding and other nematode species in check
- **Food source**: They are prey for larger organisms (microarthropods, some fungi)
- **Decomposition**: Their movement and feeding indirectly promote organic matter breakdown
- **Soil structure**: Their movement through soil creates small channels

## Managing Nematodes

**To encourage beneficial nematodes:**
- Maintain good soil structure (avoid compaction)
- Add organic matter to feed bacteria and fungi (which feed beneficial nematodes)
- Use [[compost-tea-recipe-guide|compost tea]] to inoculate with diverse biology
- Minimize tillage which disrupts soil structure and fungal networks

**To suppress root-feeding nematodes:**
- Build a diverse food web with adequate predators
- Use [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]] (some, like marigolds, have nematicidal properties)
- Ensure good fungal dominance where appropriate ([[bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]])
- Avoid over-fertilization with nitrogen which can favor root-feeders

## Key Points

- Most nematodes are beneficial, not harmful
- Bacterial and fungal feeders are crucial nutrient mineralizers
- Predatory nematodes keep root-feeding species in check
- The ratio of fungal to bacterial feeders reflects soil biology balance
- Nematodes need porous, well-structured soil to thrive
- Healthy soil food webs naturally suppress plant-parasitic nematodes
