# Insect and Arthropod-Associated Fungi — Biodiversity and Sampling

Vast numbers of fungi are associated with insects and other arthropods, forming symbioses of various types including parasitism, mutualism, commensalism, and gardening. The fungi involved include necrotrophic and biotrophic parasites dispersed by their hosts, as well as fungi used directly by insects as food.

## Types of Associations

### Necrotrophic Parasites
Kill host cells and use them as nutrient sources. Include many entomopathogenic fungi used in biological control.

### Biotrophic Parasites
Require living host cells. Include the Trichomycetes (gut inhabitants) and Laboulbeniales (external parasites).

### Gardening Symbioses
Insects cultivate fungi for food:
- **Leaf-cutter ants (Attini):** Cultivate Leucoagaricus gongylophorus on leaf substrata
- **Termites (Macrotermitinae):** Cultivate Termitomyces species in fungus combs within nests
- **Ambrosia beetles:** Culture fungi in tunnels within wood
- **Wood wasps (Siricidae):** Carry Amylosterium and Cerrena fungi for larval nutrition

### Spore-Dispersal Interactions
Many free-living fungi and lichens are dispersed by arthropods — on body surfaces, through gut passage, or via specialized structures. Some fungi are entirely dependent on arthropod dispersers.

## Major Groups

### Trichomycetes (Gut Fungi)
Found attached to the hindgut lining of aquatic insect larvae (midges, black flies, mosquitoes, stoneflies, mayflies) and other arthropods (crustaceans, millipedes). Include:
- **Harpellales:** >190 isolates in axenic culture; genera include Smittium (13+ species in culture), Genistelloides, Capniomyces
- **Asellariales:** In isopods and amphipods
- **Eccrinales:** In various arthropod guts

### Laboulbeniales
Obligate biotrophic parasitic ascomycetes lacking mycelium, living on diverse arthropods. Known from Coleoptera, Dermaptera, Diptera, Heteroptera, Hymenoptera, Isoptera, Orthoptera, Thysanoptera, mites, and millipedes. None has completed its life cycle in axenic culture.

### Bark Beetle-Associated Fungi
Ophiostomatoid fungi (Ophiostoma, Ceratocystis) associated with Scolytinae and Platypodinae beetles. Beetles carry fungi in specialized structures called mycangia. Some cause blue-stain of wood; certain species can kill trees.

### Termite Fungus Gardens
Termitomyces species cultivated by Macrotermitinae termites in tropical Africa and Asia. Xylaria regularly co-occurs in combs but quickly overgrows Termitomyces when combs are removed from nests.

## Sampling Methods

### Trapping Insects
- **Flight-interception traps:** 3m wide black netting; collect Coleoptera and Diptera
- **Light traps:** UV and mercury vapor lamps; attract beetles including aquatic species
- **Pitfall traps:** Plastic containers sunk flush with soil; catch ground-dwelling invertebrates
- **Canopy fogging:** Pyrethroid fogging of individual trees; yields enormous beetle samples
- **Litter samples:** Known volumes processed through Berlese funnels
- **Malaise traps:** For flying insects; comparison of catches can be difficult

### Isolation from Insects
- Surface sterilization of beetles before crushing on agar
- Homogenization in sterile water, plating aliquots
- Direct isolation from mycangia (dissected beetle pronota)
- Isolation from phloem/xylem around insect galleries

### Culture Media
- **Trichomycetes:** Dilute brain-heart infusion (1/10 BHI) with thiamine and biotin; water overlay on agar essential
- **Bark beetle fungi:** Malt extract agar with or without benomyl for semiselective isolation
- **Termite fungi:** Oatmeal flakes, Sabouraud glucose, malt extract, yeast extract agars

## Storage and Handling

- Material should be kept as unchanged as possible until study
- Many fungi tolerate drying and can be cultured from herbarium specimens years old
- Others die within hours of drying
- Refrigeration useful but high humidity can cause contamination
- Trichomycetes: liquid nitrogen storage preferred; do not withstand lyophilization
