# Insect and Arthropod-Associated Fungi — Biodiversity and Sampling

Vast numbers of fungi are associated with insects and other arthropods,
forming symbioses of various types including parasitism, mutualism,
commensalism, and [[gaias-garden-beneficial-insects-pest-management|garden]] ing. The fungi involved include necrotrophic
and biotrophic parasites dispersed by their hosts, as well as fungi
used directly by insects as food.

## Types of Associations

### Necrotrophic Parasites
Kill host cells and use them as nutrient sources. Include many
entomopathogenic fungi used in biological control programs against
agricultural and forest pests. These fungi actively invade host
tissues, producing toxins and enzymes that overcome insect immune
defences. Death of the host typically occurs within days of infection.

### Biotrophic Parasites
Require living host cells for sustained growth and reproduction.
Include the Trichomycetes (gut inhabitants) and Laboulbeniales
(external parasites). These fungi have evolved intimate relationships
with their hosts and often cause minimal harm, instead exploiting a
steady supply of nutrients from living tissue.

### Gardening Symbioses
Insects cultivate fungi for food, representing some of the most
complex mutualisms in nature:
- **Leaf-cutter ants (Attini):** Cultivate Leucoagaricus gongylophorus
  on leaf substrata in underground chambers. The [[fungal-interactions-living-plant-surfaces|fungal]] garden is
  the sole food source for ant larvae.
- **Termites (Macrotermitinae):** Cultivate Termitomyces species in
  fungus combs within nests. The comb is constructed from plant
  material mixed with termite faeces and inoculated with fungal spores.
- **Ambrosia beetles:** Culture fungi in tunnels within wood. The
  fungal mycelium serves as the primary food for both adults and
  larvae. Beetles carry fungal inoculum in specialised pockets
  called mycangia.
- **Wood wasps (Siricidae):** Carry Amylosterium and Cerrena fungi
  for larval nutrition. The fungi are deposited into wood during
  oviposition and pre-digest the wood for developing larvae.

### Spore-Dispersal Interactions
Many free-living fungi and lichens are dispersed by arthropods on body
surfaces, through gut passage, or via specialised structures. Some
fungi produce sticky spores or spore-bearing structures adapted for
arthropod attachment. Certain truffle-like fungi rely entirely on
mammalian and insect vectors for [[spore-dispersal]] al]].

## Major Groups

### Trichomycetes (Gut Fungi)
Found attached to the hindgut lining of aquatic insect larvae (midges,
black flies, mosquitoes, stoneflies, mayflies) and other arthropods
(crustaceans, millipedes). These obligate commensals are among the
most poorly studied fungal groups. Major orders include:
- **Harpellales:** Over 190 isolates in axenic culture; genera include
  Smittium (13+ species in culture), Genistelloides, Capniomyces
- **Asellariales:** In isopods and amphipods; form branching thalli
  attached to the gut cuticle
- **Eccrinales:** In various arthropod guts; produce unbranched or
  sparingly branched coenocytic thalli

### Laboulbeniales
Obligate biotrophic parasitic ascomycetes lacking mycelium, living on
diverse arthropods. Known from Coleoptera, Dermaptera, Diptera,
Heteroptera, Hymenoptera, Isoptera, Orthoptera, Thysanoptera, mites,
and millipedes. None has completed its life cycle in axenic culture,
making study extremely difficult. Individual thalli are microscopic,
typically less than 1 mm in length, and are often host-specific to
a single genus or species of arthropod.

### Bark Beetle-Associated Fungi
Ophiostomatoid fungi (Ophiostoma, Ceratocystis) associated with
Scolytinae and Platypodinae beetles. Beetles carry fungi in
specialised structures called mycangia located in the pronotum,
elytra, or other body regions. Some cause blue-stain of wood, reducing
timber value. Certain species are tree pathogens capable of killing
standing trees by blocking water-conducting tissues.

### Termite Fungus Gardens
Termitomyces species cultivated by Macrotermitinae termites in
tropical Africa and Asia. The fungus comb is a complex structure of
partially digested plant material colonised by the fungus. Xylaria
regularly co-occurs in combs but quickly overgrows Termitomyces when
combs are removed from nests, suggesting a competitive relationship
modulated by termite behaviour.

## Sampling Methods

### Trapping Insects
Effective insect trapping is essential for studying fungal associates:
- **Flight-interception traps:** 3m wide black netting; collect
  Coleoptera and Diptera in flight
- **Light traps:** UV and mercury vapor lamps; attract beetles
  including aquatic species
- **Pitfall traps:** Plastic containers sunk flush with soil surface;
  catch ground-dwelling invertebrates
- **Canopy fogging:** Pyrethroid fogging of individual trees; yields
  enormous beetle samples from forest canopies
- **Litter samples:** Known volumes processed through Berlese funnels
  to extract arthropods from leaf litter
- **Malaise traps:** For flying insects; tent-like intercept traps

### Isolation from Insects
- Surface sterilisation of beetles before crushing on agar media
- Homogenisation in sterile water, plating aliquots on selective media
- Direct isolation from mycangia by dissecting beetle pronota
- Isolation from phloem and xylem tissue around insect galleries
- Whole-mount microscopy for Laboulbeniales (non-culturable)

### Culture Media
- **Trichomycetes:** Dilute brain-heart infusion (1/10 BHI) with
  thiamine and biotin; water overlay on agar is essential
- **Bark beetle f [[malt-extract-agar]] act agar with or without benomyl
  for semi-selective isolation of Ophiostoma
- **Termite fungi:** Oatmeal flakes, Sabouraud glucose, malt extract,
  and yeast extract agars support good growth

## Entomopathogenic Fungi in Biological Control

Several genera of fungi have been developed as biological control
agents against insect pests:
- **[[metarhizium-anisopliae]]:** Used against soil-dwelling insects,
  termites, and grasshoppers. Commercial formulations are available
  as mycoinsecticides.
- **[[beauveria-bassiana]]:** Broad host range; used against whiteflies,
  thrips, and coleopteran pests in agriculture.
- **Lecanicillium spp:** Effective against aphids and whiteflies;
  commercially produced for greenhouse pest management.

These fungi infect through the insect cuticle, do not require
ingestion, and are safe for non-target organisms including mammals,
birds, and most beneficial insects.
