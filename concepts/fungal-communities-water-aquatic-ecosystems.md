# Fungal Communities in Water and Aquatic Ecosystems

Fungi play essential roles in aquatic and marine ecosystems as decomposers of organic matter, nutrient cyclers, and food sources for invertebrates. Although less intensively studied than terrestrial [[fungal-community-ecology]], aquatic fungi are recognized as the dominant decomposers in many freshwater and marine systems.

## Role as Dominant Decomposers

Wong et al. (1998) emphasize that although fungi are the dominant decomposers in aquatic systems, mechanisms of decomposition and interactions between fungi and other organisms remain poorly understood. Resource succession by saprotrophic fungi has been identified (Gessner et al., 1993), but the relationship between fungal biodiversity and ecological function in aquatic systems is still largely unexplored.

Fungi are ubiquitous in aquatic environments, occurring as unicellular yeasts, polymorphic and filamentous fungi, and as both free-living and [[singh-endophytic-fungi-plant-protection-mechanisms]]. Their roles in aquatic [[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] parallel their terrestrial importance but remain comparatively understudied.

## Fungal Succession on Aquatic Leaf Litter

Fungal communities on alder leaf litter in streams show clear successional patterns analogous to terrestrial [[fungal-leaf-litter-succession]] (Gessner et al., 1993):

- **Early colonization (2 weeks)**: Communities dominated by 5-6 species including Flagellospora curvula, Tetrachaetum elegans, Lemonniera centrospharea, L. aquatica, and L. terrestris.

- **Mid-succession (4 weeks)**: Species composition becomes more equitable with larger assemblages of approximately 11 species, which persist with some changes in dominance at 8 weeks.

- **Peak biomass and conidial production**: Occurs in the middle of succession, measured as ergosterol content and conidial output.

The successional patterns observed in aquatic leaf litter decomposition parallel those seen in [[fungal-succession-wood-substrates]], where early colonizers are typically fast-growing, opportunistic species that are gradually replaced by more competitive, slower-growing taxa as resource quality declines.

## Resource Quality and Community Structure

Resource quality of plant material dictates community structure of aquatic saprotrophic fungi. Gulis (2001) performed multivariate analysis of 146 samples from 92 aquatic systems showing that fungal species assemblages on wood and grass leaves have different composition from those on tree leaf litter.

However, correlations between fungal communities, resource quality, decomposition rates, and physiological function of individual species remain largely unexplored. This parallels gaps in understanding of [[fungal-fungal-community-assembly]] processes in terrestrial habitats. The [[fungal-functional-diversity-decomposition]] of aquatic fungi — encompassing their enzymatic capabilities, growth rates, and stress tolerances — is likely a better predictor of ecosystem function than species richness alone, but quantitative links between functional traits and decomposition processes in aquatic systems remain to be established.

## Environmental Controls on Decomposition

Griffith et al. (1995) demonstrated that leaf litters decompose more readily in streams of higher pH and higher temperature. Differences between sites were less obvious when data were temperature-corrected, suggesting temperature as a primary driving variable. The temporal pattern of pectinase enzyme production differed among leaf litter types and related to chemical composition.

Both white oak and red maple exhibited skeletonization during decomposition, whereas yellow poplar leaves simply became softer. These patterns mirror those seen in [[fungal-decomposition-processes]] on land, where lignin content and resource quality strongly influence decay rates.

## Fungal Biomass in Aquatic Systems

Fungal biomass increases at an early stage of plant material decomposition and can form a significant proportion of total detrital mass (up to 15%) (Gessner et al., 1997). In salt marsh systems, the importance of high mycelial biomass is highest, and decomposition rates are greatest. Concomitant with faster decomposition of Spartina than Plantanus, a higher percentage of carbon is released as dissolved and fine particulate organic matter.

As in terrestrial ecosystems, high lignin content reduces decomposition rate, which can be ameliorated by exogenous supply of nitrate nitrogen. This [[fungal-feeding-invertebrates-soil]] contributes substantially to the total organic matter budget of aquatic systems.

## Effects on Carbon and Nutrient Budgets

The effects of fungal and bacterial action on carbon flux in decomposing plant residues show that fungal biomass and activity significantly influence carbon partitioning between dissolved organic matter, fine particulate organic matter, and incorporation into microbial biomass. The effects of these end products on nutrient dynamics and support of primary and secondary productivity within aquatic ecosystems remain unknown.

Regulation of fungal degradation is positively enhanced by increased fungal biomass, reflected in conidial production, and by activity measured as ATP. Understanding these [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]] in aquatic systems is critical for ecosystem management.

## Fungi as Food in Aquatic Ecosystems

Aquatic fungi form an important food source for invertebrates and alter the physical structure of wood substrates to allow faunal penetration (Suberkropp, 1992). Some faunal shredders prefer to feed on leaves already colonized by fungi, while others consume fungal mycelia selectively.

The palatability of resources is enhanced during fungal decomposition by the increase in nitrogen content during initial stages of fungal attack (Gessner et al., 1997). This dynamic mirrors [[fungal-nematode-feeding-interactions]] documented in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Marine and Salt Marsh Fungi

Marine fungi, including species associated with [[fungal-biodiversity-marine-ecosystems-coastal-deep-sea]] and salt marsh ecosystems, contribute to decomposition of standing-dead plant material. Approximately 50% of aquatic and salt marsh plants are mycorrhizal (Khan and Belik, 1995; Cooke and Lefor, 1998), but the extent of hyphal development into sediments and their roles in nutrient acquisition and sediment stabilization remain poorly characterized.

Molecular approaches have begun to reveal previously unrecognized diversity in marine/estuarine fungal assemblages, particularly in [[fungal-biodiversity-marine-ecosystems-coastal-deep-sea]] habitats where fungi are major agents of organic matter processing. Marine-derived fungi have also attracted attention as sources of novel bioactive compounds, with several species producing antimicrobial, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory metabolites not found in their terrestrial relatives. The [[marine-oomycete-zoospore-biology-encystment]], while technically straddling the fungal and protist kingdoms, provides additional insight into the adaptation of fungus-like organisms to marine environments.

## Knowledge Gaps

The role of fungi in aquatic ecosystems has not been studied as intensively as terrestrial systems. Key gaps include:
mechanisms of decomposition,
fungal-bacterial interactions,
the relationship between fungal biodiversity and ecological function,
correlation between community composition and physiological function of species,
and the impacts of environmental constraints on fungal activity.

The application of fungal-metagenomics|metagenomic tools and [[fungal-species-recognition-genetics]] to aquatic systems offers promise for addressing these gaps, but methodological challenges in DNA extraction from water and sediment samples remain significant.

Addressing these knowledge gaps is increasingly urgent as aquatic ecosystems face mounting pressures from climate change, nutrient pollution, and habitat alteration. The [[freshwater-fungi-diversity-and-sampling]] may be declining as streams and lakes are affected by agricultural runoff, urban development, and water extraction, but without comprehensive baseline data, these trends remain difficult to quantify.

## Freshwater Hyphomycetes and Stream Ecology

Freshwater hyphomycetes (also called Ingoldian fungi or aquatic hyphomycetes) represent one of the most well-studied groups of aquatic fungi. These asexual fungi are adapted to running water and produce specialized conidia (spores) that are branched or tetraradiate — a morphology that enhances their attachment to submerged substrates and increases the probability of being ingested by invertebrate grazers. The [[freshwater-fungi-diversity-and-sampling]] of these fungi has revealed hundreds of species distributed globally, with peak diversity in temperate forest streams where leaf litter inputs are abundant.

Ingoldian fungi play a central role in the decomposition of leaf litter in streams, a process that has been extensively studied in the context of the "leaf litter breakdown" paradigm of stream ecology. Their enzymatic capabilities include production of cellulases, pectinases, lignin-degrading enzymes, and cutinases, enabling them to break down the complex polymers in autumn-shed leaves. The rate of leaf decomposition mediated by these fungi is a standard metric in biomonitoring programs, as decomposition rates respond predictably to water quality, nutrient enrichment, and other anthropogenic stressors.

## Adaptations to Aquatic Environments

Fungi inhabiting aquatic environments exhibit a range of morphological and physiological adaptations distinct from their terrestrial relatives. These include tolerance of low oxygen conditions in sediments, ability to grow at low temperatures typical of many freshwater systems, production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that facilitate attachment to submerged surfaces, and the capacity for both sexual and asexual reproduction under aquatic conditions. Some aquatic fungi produce specialized hyphal structures called rhizoids or holdfasts that anchor them to substrates in flowing water, preventing dislodgement during flood events.

The [[fungal-adaptations-environmental-gradients]] in aquatic systems differs from terrestrial patterns in several important respects. While terrestrial fungi experience relatively stable moisture conditions within soil aggregates, aquatic fungi must cope with fluctuating water levels, flow velocities, dissolved oxygen concentrations, and nutrient availability. Seasonal flooding events can dramatically reshape aquatic fungal communities, importing terrestrial fungi into aquatic habitats and exporting aquatic propagules to riparian soils.

## Fungal-Bacterial Interactions in Aquatic Biofilms

In aquatic ecosystems, fungi and bacteria coexist within complex biofilm communities on submerged surfaces including leaf litter, wood, rocks, and artificial substrates. These fungal-bacterial interactions include both competitive and mutualistic relationships. Fungi often colonize leaf litter first, conditioning the substrate through enzymatic softening and nitrogen enrichment, which subsequently facilitates bacterial colonization. Bacteria, in turn, may stimulate fungal enzyme production through chemical signaling or compete with fungi for released soluble carbon compounds.

The balance between fungal and bacterial dominance in aquatic biofilms shifts along environmental gradients. In oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) streams, fungi tend to dominate leaf litter decomposition due to their ability to produce extracellular enzymes that access recalcitrant carbon sources. In eutrophic (nutrient-enriched) streams, bacterial activity increases and may outpace fungal decomposition. This shift has implications for the quality of organic matter entering downstream food webs, as fungal-dominated decomposition tends to produce larger particulate organic matter that supports detritivorous invertebrates, while bacterial-dominated decomposition releases more dissolved organic matter.

## Implications for Water Quality Monitoring

The sensitivity of aquatic fungal communities to environmental conditions makes them promising indicators of water quality and ecosystem health. Changes in fungal species composition, biomass, and enzymatic activity respond to pollution, habitat degradation, and climate change. The [[soil-chytridiomycetes-diversity-distribution]], an early-diverging fungal lineage found predominantly in aquatic habitats, are particularly sensitive to heavy metal contamination and have been proposed as bioindicators for monitoring metal pollution in freshwater systems.

## Fungi in Extreme Aquatic Environments

Fungi have been isolated from a remarkable range of extreme aquatic habitats, including hot springs with temperatures exceeding 45 degrees Celsius, Antarctic lakes with near-freezing water, highly acidic mine drainage (pH below 3), and hypersaline lagoons. Thermophilic aquatic fungi contribute to decomposition in geothermal streams, while cold-adapted (psychrotolerant) species remain metabolically active in ice-covered lakes and glacial meltwater streams. Acidophilic fungi in acid mine drainage play important roles in iron and sulfur cycling, and their [[fungal-diversity-arid-environments]] has potential applications in biomining and bioremediation of acidic, metal-rich effluents. These extremophilic aquatic fungi expand our understanding of the physiological limits of fungal metabolism and suggest that fungal diversity in aquatic systems may be far greater than current estimates indicate.
