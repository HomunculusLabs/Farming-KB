# Salt Tolerant Fodder Plants

## Overview

Salt tolerant fodder plants are essential components of [[permaculture]]
systems in coastal, arid, and saline environments where conventional
pasture species fail. These halophytic plants provide critical forage
for livestock in [[silvopasture]] systems while simultaneously performing
soil restoration functions including salt removal, erosion control, and
windbreak protection. Many salt-tolerant species are also [[nitrogen-fixing-trees]],
adding fertility to marginal lands as part of broader [[agroforestry]] strategies. The selection draws from Australian native species and
international halophytes that have proven value in difficult-site
agricultural systems.

## Key Concepts

### Saltbush Species (Atriplex)

Saltbushes are the most important group of salt-tolerant fodder shrubs.
They concentrate salt onto the surface of their leaves, effectively
removing salt from the soil. See [[salt-tolerant-plant-guide]] for a
comprehensive catalog of halophytic species, and [[atriplex-halimus]] for a detailed
species profile. Yields can exceed 10 tonnes per hectare per year
year for some species, though better growth rates are experienced in
non-saline soils. Atriplex nummularia (old man saltbush) is widely
recognized for fodder production. Atriplex canescens (four-winged
saltbush) is endemic to North America with 18 to 20 percent protein
content and 63.5 percent digestibility. Atriplex undulata provides 900
sheep grazing days per hectare in 350 mm rainfall areas.

### Bluebush Species (Maireana)

Maireana species are native Australian shrubs that are generally good
companions to saltbushes in mixed fodder systems. Maireana brevifolia
reaches up to 0.9 meters with 15 to 26 percent crude protein. Maireana
sedifolia (pearl bluebush) grows one meter tall and wide on calcareous
soils with 17.2 percent crude protein. Maireana pyramidata reaches
nearly two meters with 15.1 to 22.0 percent protein and 58 percent
digestibility. These species complement saltbush by providing year-round
browse in mixed plantings.

### Salt-Tolerant [[acacia]] Species

Several Acacia species provide valuable fodder in saline conditions.
Acacia ampliceps (salt wattle) tolerates alkaline, highly saline, and
waterlogged conditions and is heavily grazed by cattle. Acacia cyclops
(coastal wattle) withstands wind and salt spray on shallow calcareous
soils. Acacia salicina grows in saline, alkaline, heavy clay soils and
is palatable to sheep as reserve fodder. These wattles are nitrogen
fixers, improving soil fertility while providing browse. See
[[salt-tolerant-fodder-species]] for additional species listings.

### Casuarina Species in Fodder Systems

Casuarinas tolerate very saline conditions and serve as nitrogen fixers
through their symbiosis with Frankia bacteria. While generally poor
fodder plants due to high tannin content, they function as emergency
fodder and medicinal browse in mixed systems. [[casuarina-equisetifolia]]
(coast sheoak) is valuable in coastal erosion control and provides
salt-tolerant windbreaks. In small numbers within a fodder system, they
cause no problems to stock and contribute nitrogen fixation.

### Halophytic Grasses and Herbs

Grasses adapted to saline conditions provide ground cover and additional
fodder. Tall wheatgrass (Agropyron elongatum) grows on poorly drained,
saline soils and provides summer grazing. Palmer saltgrass grows in
coastal saline environments. Samphires (Halosarcia spp) are native to
waterlogged saltlands of Western Australia. Puccinellia ciliata is a
creeping perennial adapted to heavy, poorly drained saline and alkaline
soils, capable of withstanding long periods of total drought.

### Lock-Up Fodder Systems

In saline environments, lock-up fodder systems allow salt-tolerant trees
and shrubs time to recover from grazing. Rather than continuous browsing,
animals are rotated through paddocks, giving plants adequate recovery
periods. This management approach is essential for maintaining productive
salt-tolerant fodder systems over the long term. The combination of
shrubs, grasses, and trees in a lock-up system provides diverse nutrition
throughout the year.

### Salt Tolerant Trees for Shelter

Tamarix articulata (athel tamarisk) is a vigorous tree tolerating most
soils but with a warning that most tamarisks cause salinisation of upper
soil through salt excretion on leaves. Melaleuca species provide honey,
essential oils, and tolerate saline conditions. Many Eucalyptus species
are adapted to saline soils and serve dual roles as windbreaks and
emergency fodder. See [[salt-tolerant-plant-guide]] for comprehensive
species selection across plant types.

## Applications

### Coastal Farming Systems

Coastal agricultural areas face salt spray, saline groundwater, and
sandy soils. Salt-tolerant fodder species enable livestock production
in these challenging environments while providing windbreak protection
and soil stabilization. Mixed plantings of saltbush, bluebush, and
coastal wattles create productive coastal grazing systems. See also
[[windbreak-shelterbelt-species-selection]] for complementary shelter plantings.

### Saline Land Rehabilitation

Salt-tolerant fodder plants are primary tools for rehabilitating saline
degraded land. Their ability to extract and concentrate salt while
providing agricultural output makes them economically viable
rehabilitation agents. For [[acid-and-alkaline-soil-management|soil amendment]] approaches that complement
fodder systems, see [[ultra-low-green-manure-cover-crop-species]]. Over time, saltbush and companion species can
reduce soil salinity levels, eventually allowing less salt-tolerant
species to be introduced.

### Arid Zone Livestock Production

In arid zones with rainfall below 300 mm, salt-tolerant shrubs provide
reliable fodder when grasses fail. Species like Atriplex and Maireana
maintain nutritional value during drought and provide essential protein
supplements to livestock that would otherwise face starvation.
