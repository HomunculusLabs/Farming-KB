# Macrofungi Sampling

Approaches to sampling macrofungi have developed considerably over the past several decades, driven by the need to document fungal diversity in forests and other ecosystems. Macrofungi — fungi that produce visible, fleshy, or woody sporocarps — present unique challenges for [[fungal-zoospore-biology-and-flagellate-motility]] because sporocarp production is episodic, influenced by weather and season, and often poorly correlated with the actual presence and distribution of fungal mycelia in the environment.

This page summarizes protocols and considerations for sampling macrofungi, drawing on the work of O'Dell, Lodge, Mueller, and colleagues as part of broader efforts in [[fungal-biodiversity-inventory-design]] and [[fungal-ecology-forest-floor]].

## Inventorying Versus Monitoring

A fundamental distinction must be made between inventorying and monitoring macrofungi, as these goals require different sampling approaches.

Inventorying is conducted to increase knowledge of fungal diversity and to learn about habitat preferences and geographic distributions of different taxa. Inventories may target all macrofungi at a site or focus on specific taxonomic or ecological groups. Recent inventory work includes surveys of target taxa for conservation purposes legally mandated as part of federally approved management plans for old-growth forests in the Pacific Northwest, where 225 species of old-growth-associated macrofungi are legally required to be surveyed and managed.

Monitoring is undertaken to assess effects of commercial harvest on edible fungal sporocarp production, determine long-term trends in populations of target species, and determine effects of pollutants on different fungi. Monitoring programs are designed for determining population trends rather than overall richness, extend for longer periods, generally focus on fewer taxa (often indicator species), and include many more sites. Baseline data on diversity and distributions from inventories are normally necessary before monitoring can begin.

The use of fungal indicator species to monitor environmental change should be considered carefully. Macrofungi are poorly known taxonomically at the species level, difficult to survey in natural ecosystems, and their natural histories are poorly known. However, they are sensitive to human disturbances such as air pollution and logging, have globally distributed genera as well as locally endemic species, include economically valuable taxa, and influence distributions of other organisms such as fungivorous insects and rodents.

Drawbacks to monitoring macrofungi include limited taxonomic expertise, annual fluctuations in sporocarp occurrence and abundance, and lack of well-tested protocols for sampling. Annual variation in species occurrence remains particularly troublesome — a species may not fruit at a site for consecutive years but then be abundant in other years.

## Types of Sampling Units

### Fixed Plots

Studies of terrestrial macrofungi usually use arbitrary sampling units, or plots. Plots range in size from 1 square meter to 1000 square meters and can be square, rectangular, or circular. The same plots often are scrutinized for several years. Permanent plots provide a good estimate of diversity for a defined area, information on annual variation in fruiting phenology, and are easy to relocate and measure.

When studies involve removal of most sporocarps, some investigators move plots on each sampling occasion to avoid effects of disturbance. An important drawback of changing plot location is that annual variation in the occurrence of fruiting species becomes confounded with variation in plot location.

### Convenience (Opportunistic) Sampling

Convenience sampling involves experienced individuals visiting a location and documenting the taxa they find. It can be an efficient way to obtain a snapshot of the diversity present and is useful for rapid assessment of potential sites for reserves. However, it precludes comparisons of diversity between sites or at a single site over time because it does not provide for the necessary standardization of sampling effort. Collector bias may cause some habitats or taxa to be overlooked.

### Dispersed Subsamples

Because of the clumped distribution of sporocarps of many fruiting macrofungi, subplots adjacent to one another are more likely to contain the same species than those at a distance. That spatial autocorrelation can be addressed by dispersing plots across the study area in various ways, rather than using contiguous subplots, which may underestimate species richness.

### Adaptive Sampling

Adaptive sampling refers to any case in which sampling effort is modified in response to observations made in the course of sampling. Usually some threshold value for the number of samples of target organisms is set; when the number in a plot exceeds that value, another area is sampled in some standard predetermined way. Adaptive sampling is useful for increasing the number of interesting observations, particularly for organisms that are rare and have patchy distributions.

## Sampling Frequency

How often a plot should be sampled depends on the amount of species turnover between sampling dates. Different fungal groups require different sampling intervals:

Xylariaceae and small ascomycetes in the tropics: 3-month intervals provide an optimal balance between discovery of previously unrecorded species and sampling effort.

Fruiting agaric species: weekly or biweekly intervals, because turnover is high in both temperate and tropical forests.

Corticioid and polypore fungi: approximately 2-month intervals.

Ideally, subplots should be sampled every 2 weeks during the fruiting season. When that intensity is not possible, sampling logs four times per year (in the dry season and at the beginning, middle, and end of the rainy season) has worked well for tropical inventories such as the Costa Rican National Fungal Inventory.

## Data Collection Protocols

In addition to standard collection data (date, macrohabitat, forest type, plant association, climatic zone, elevation, host, plot number), the following information should be collected when sampling from woody substrata:

Substratum/host class: type (trunk, branch, twig, stump, or root), condition (living, dead, or dead on living plant), and position (vertical greater than 60 to 90 degrees, prostrate, on ground, or suspended less than 1 m or greater than 1 m above ground).

Substratum preference: whether the fungus is fruiting on wood only, mainly on wood but some on bark, equally on wood and bark, mainly on bark but some on wood, or on bark only.

Substratum size class: diameter of the substratum, using size classes of less than 1.0 cm, 1.0 to 2.5 cm, 2.6 to 5.0 cm, 5.1 to 10 cm, 10.1 to 20 cm, or more than 20 cm.

## Determining Adequate Sampling

Deciding how much area to sample, the size, number, and distribution of plots, and the frequency of sampling is not a trivial undertaking. Sampling design should be considered an iterative process — data from early efforts can be used to make decisions about modifying later sampling.

Arnolds (1992) recommended plots of 1000 square meters in forest or 500 square meters in grasslands, with a minimum of five plots per community type. Sampling an area larger than that required for plants is generally necessary for fungi. Estimates of richness based on modest amounts of sampling may be improved using extrapolation techniques such as the jackknife and Chao's second estimator, although none of the currently used extrapolation techniques has proven fully robust when applied to macrofungal data.

Complementarity is an empirical measure of the degree of species uniqueness between different samples, expressed as the proportion of total species encountered that is unique to one sample or the other. Sampling for richness is most efficient when complementarity among samples is about 0.5. High complementarity indicates that many taxa probably are being overlooked.

## Recommended Protocols

An integrated set of sampling protocols is recommended for macrofungi, including opportunistic sampling, fixed-size plot sampling, and sampling of a fixed number of downed logs.

### Opportunistic Sampling

By opportunistic sampling, investigators carefully walk through a study site and collect conspicuous sporocarps, sampling as many habitats as possible. This does not yield quantitative data but is an important adjunct to plot-based methods because additional species will be seen off plot due to the patchy distribution of sporocarps. A combination of opportunistic and plot-based sampling is necessary to maximize the macrofungal diversity documented at a site.

### Fixed-Size Plot Method for Conspicuous Macrofungi

The recommended protocol for conspicuous macrofungi using fixed-size plots follows these steps:

1. The investigator selects a representative area within the site for the permanent plot, choosing to optimize the diversity of habitat types sampled by the entire study. The plot should be in a homogeneous area, easily accessible, and not susceptible to edge effects.

2. Each plot consists of 10 transects that are 100 m long, typically laid out parallel at 10-m intervals. Transects are marked every 5 m with flags, with each transect assigned a unique letter and each flag numbered sequentially (A1 through A20, B1 through B20, etc.).

3. Each sampler uses a rope 1.262 m long to circumscribe 5-square-meter circular subplots around each flag, giving 20 subplots per transect and 200 subplots total (a sampling area of 1000 square meters, or 0.1 hectares, per plot).

4. All macrofungi occurring in a subplot are collected, labeled with the transect letter and subplot number, and placed in appropriate bags. At the end of the collecting day, specimens are transported back to the field station for sorting, describing, photographing, and drying. The substratum (soil, leaf litter, wood) is noted for each specimen.

### Microplot Method for Small Ascomycetes

The goal when sampling small ascomycetes is not to cover a large area but to obtain a quantitative sample of microfungi growing on small substrata, especially those species that are infrequently or rarely collected. The protocol involves the following steps:

1. Investigators establish a microplot for sampling microfungi adjacent to a macrofungal subplot along the transects. Because substrata in the plots are collected, different plots are laid out each time a sample is taken.

2. Each person sampling carries a 0.56-m-long or 1.128-m-long plastic pipe or wood pole to circumscribe 1-square-meter circular subplots. The number of subplots per site depends on resources; most individuals sample only two or three 1-square-meter subplots in a week because of the time involved in scanning substrata.

3. All microfungi on twigs, branches, and leaves occurring within a plot are collected, labeled, and transported for sorting, examination under a stereomicroscope, description, division, and drying.

4. Microfungal plots should be sampled at minimum twice a year — in the middle and at the end of the fruiting season.

## Voucher Specimens and Documentation

Voucher specimens are essential for addressing taxonomic problems and authenticating the taxa being monitored or inventoried. Careful documentation of sites and methods is a priority, with consideration given to facilitating the repetition of a study at a site 20 years in the future. Each specimen should be labeled with complete collection data, described, photographed, and dried for deposition in a herbarium.

## Challenges

Several persistent challenges complicate macrofungal sampling. For background on the field, see [[mycological-terms-glossary]].

Annual fluctuations in sporocarp occurrence and abundance make it difficult to distinguish true absence from temporary non-fruiting. A species may be present as dormant propagules or active but non-fruiting mycelium.

Limited taxonomic expertise, especially for difficult groups such as corticioid fungi and small ascomycetes, constrains the accuracy of species determinations.

Sporocarps represent an unknown fraction of total fungal biomass, which also includes vegetative mycelium, sclerotia, mycorrhizae, and rhizomorphs. Molecular analyses have shown that substantial numbers of species present on host roots at some sites rarely fruit.

Most studies incorporate only 1 to 3 years of sampling, although 5 to 10 years would be more appropriate for assessing true diversity.

Protocols continue to converge, but differences in plot sizes, subplot configurations, and sampling frequency still interfere with direct comparisons between studies.
