# Mollison: Extended Families and Community Structure

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.6)

## The Family as Society in Embryo

From The I Ching: "The family is society in embryo: it is the native soil on which the performance of moral duty is made easy through natural affection, so that within the family circle a basis of moral practice is updated; this is later widened to include human relationships and society in general."

The concepts of village and bioregion refer to a base or home area, but many people travel about today. Many societies extend as close affinity groups across many nations, forming a non-national network. Such groups develop a familial rather than a competitive or conflicting inter-relationship. With a common interest and ethical base, cooperative interdependence supplants competition.

## Structure of Extended Families

A "family" of shared ethical values, with 1,000 or fewer members, can ally with like groups to create a tribe, and 20 to 40 such tribes form a nation. Families, unlike many societies, have child care and the welfare of their members at heart.

Such families already exist in Europe, with small groups living in scattered households across many existing national boundaries; some have existed for 18 or more years. In practice, any person has 3 to 5 close friends (who change slowly over time), a support group of 30 or more acquaintances, and resource access to the whole system.

## Functions of Extended Families

A familial system of shared ethics can:
- Keep in touch via a registrar and news service.
- Co-invest in family property development and joint enterprises.
- Rationalise resources for most efficient sharing of space and equipment.
- Develop a series of social contacts via visits.
- Care for children as one group, and set up a fund for their needs.
- Provide the cultural and support base for households.

Membership does not conflict with any other membership or duties, and is mainly a matter of organisation of a family registrar and some common funds.

## Individual Rights Within the Family

Such families need to define each adult as an individual, with a right to the essentials of their own space (bed and work space), garden, and occupation. As nuclear family households are a minor part of modern societies (13 to 18 percent of all households), households based on friendship, work affinity, or designed for students, single, or elderly people are needed.

Like land ownership, the ownership of people is an illusory aim. Some couples can tolerate years of close work, but many might prefer a slightly more independent existence.

## Children and the Common Fund

Children need a wider alliance and support group than just one or two parents. People can find "aunts and uncles" to take part of the responsibility for children in any such extended family. If the children have a common fund (like their own credit union) for basic needs, then their care at a basic level is assured. They also have more than one household to relate to, or to visit or dwell in when educational needs change.

## Green Party and Political Affiliations

There are two ways to ensure political changes which will bring ecological changes. The first is to mobilise ground support in every electorate where a candidate of any party takes a stand on good ecology, or against nuclear and polluting industry. The second is to form a local Ecological or Green Party, or a bioregional group.

The broad aims of such a party are: ecological, socially egalitarian, grassroots democratic, and non-growth. Common policy always leaves room for local issues but gives strong principles for guidance.

## Evolving a New Policy Base

The structure for gathering common policy:
1. Define what is seen to be the problem or concern; give weight to priorities on a scale from 1 to 10.
2. State intent of policy for your region; what it is intended to do.
