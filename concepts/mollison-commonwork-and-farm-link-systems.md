# Mollison: Commonwork and Farm Link Systems

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.12)

## Commonwork Systems

Any trust, tribe, or even an individual farmer can allow multiple land use by setting up a commonwork system, akin to the African mahisa or livestock loan system of Botswana.

The land area is closely assessed for:
- Earth resources, wildlife, forest, and aquaculture potential.
- Small and large livestock (bees to bullocks).
- Arable land and mixed orchard.
- Socio-educational potential.
- Local needs for primary processing, building, and consultancy or implementation services.

The trust or owners advertise locally for people to run any one of these enterprises with levels ranging from a hobby (developing a butterfly forage system) to a full-time occupation (a trout farm). Proper legal safeguards (lease documents) set out rights of use, length of lease, responsibilities of trust and lessee, and terms of payment (adjustable, but usually 10 percent of gross income). This 10 percent forms a capital fund together with any capital raised by the trust or lessees.

All lessees are self-employed; land costs are minimal (their share of services) and land access is secure. As enterprises develop, the capital fund enables further research and development. It is ideal to plan so that any one enterprise (energy supply, bees, tree nursery) helps to supply others, as well as regional needs. More than one farm or trust can join in a commonwork system.

## Commonwork Priorities

A developmental design program for a commonwork land trust:

- Priority 1: Maximise water storage by constructions of Keyline, swale, soil conditioning, ridge forestry, and broad canals or dams for diversion and irrigation.
- Priority 2: Specify forest sites and forest types, allowing specific forage forests for locally acceptable or endemic animal species.
- Priority 3: Specify crop areas for orchard and perennial crop, based on best soils, low slopes, windbreaks, and water access.
- Priority 4: Specify complex access and trackways, critical edges for flowers, bee fodders, trellis systems, wayside crop.
- Other: Select caretaker house sites, designate and stockpile earth materials, check history and archaeology, keep a journal and open plan of all development.

## Probable Livelihoods for 100 ha, 40+ Families

Bees, poultry, ducks, fish species, forage systems, fuel forestry, structural forestry, cut flowers, nursery, methane and wind energy systems, accommodation and tours, workshops and field days, training programmes, cooperative store, credit union, insurance, leasing, processing of raw materials (pottery, bricks, dried/pickled/smoked food products).

## Commonwork Requirements

Commonworks need to be close to towns or on a public transport route, so that townspeople can participate. Remote areas do not suit unless paired with a village development.

Commonwork members are free to leave, sell out to new lessees, and eventually be refunded their 10 percent, less base maintenance and service costs, but plus any interest paid.

All occupations can be "open," available to all, but a proportion can be assigned to specifically disadvantaged groups. In Turkey, for instance, the totally blind have the sole right to sell pigeon food in public places, giving them an independent income.

## The Concept of "A Living"

In the concept of commonwork, Mollison arrives at a new synthesis, a future model not only for farms but for complex small communities. In his meaning of "a living," it is the beekeeper, the bees, their water, flowers, pollen, propolis, and the means to make the beehives. It is a clear legal access to forage, and a registered and secure bee site; and it is the right to sell or market product to pay for other life needs. It is a human ecological totality, provided with abstract and real self-reliance, and sufficient to pay for any tuition, travel, health service, and insurance needed by the family.

Income differentials in the Mondragon system are limited in the ratio 1:4 or 5; a sweeper cannot receive less than 25 percent of the total wage of a doctor. This is a good basis for adjusting any ethical sharing in any system.

## Farm Link System

From 20 to 50 families link to one or more farms in the nearby countryside. Usually an arrangement is made with an already established market gardener. Quarterly meetings work out what products can be trucked direct from the farm to the families, who use the product and can retail any surplus.

Benefits:
- Producers have an assured market at a mutually agreed price set a year ahead.
- Consumers can reduce costs to below wholesale by assisting with harvest.
- Townspeople can access farm facilities; valuable education for children.
- The system builds friendships.
- Consumers control quality and quantity by pre-order and stated preferences.

The system is best developed in Japan, although scattered examples operate elsewhere for products as diverse as fish and game, wheat and firewood. This strategy enables compact urban areas while retaining farmlands uncluttered by settlement.

# Mollison: Extended Families and Community Structure

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.6)

## The Family as Society in Embryo

From The I Ching: "The family is society in embryo: it is the native soil on which the performance of moral duty is made easy through natural affection, so that within the family circle a basis of moral practice is updated; this is later widened to include human relationships and society in general."

The concepts of village and bioregion refer to a base or home area, but many people travel about today. Many societies extend as close affinity groups across many nations, forming a non-national network. Such groups develop a familial rather than a competitive or conflicting inter-relationship. With a common interest and ethical base, cooperative interdependence supplants competition.

## Structure of Extended Families

A "family" of shared ethical values, with 1,000 or fewer members, can ally with like groups to create a tribe, and 20 to 40 such tribes form a nation. Families, unlike many societies, have child care and the welfare of their members at heart.

Such families already exist in Europe, with small groups living in scattered households across many existing national boundaries; some have existed for 18 or more years. In practice, any person has 3 to 5 close friends (who change slowly over time), a support group of 30 or more acquaintances, and resource access to the whole system.

## Functions of Extended Families

A familial system of shared ethics can:
- Keep in touch via a registrar and news service.
- Co-invest in family property development and joint enterprises.
- Rationalise resources for most efficient sharing of space and equipment.
- Develop a series of social contacts via visits.
- Care for children as one group, and set up a fund for their needs.
- Provide the cultural and support base for households.

Membership does not conflict with any other membership or duties, and is mainly a matter of organisation of a family registrar and some common funds.

## Individual Rights Within the Family

Such families need to define each adult as an individual, with a right to the essentials of their own space (bed and work space), garden, and occupation. As nuclear family households are a minor part of modern societies (13 to 18 percent of all households), households based on friendship, work affinity, or designed for students, single, or elderly people are needed.

Like land ownership, the ownership of people is an illusory aim. Some couples can tolerate years of close work, but many might prefer a slightly more independent existence.

## Children and the Common Fund

Children need a wider alliance and support group than just one or two parents. People can find "aunts and uncles" to take part of the responsibility for children in any such extended family. If the children have a common fund (like their own credit union) for basic needs, then their care at a basic level is assured. They also have more than one household to relate to, or to visit or dwell in when educational needs change.

## Green Party and Political Affiliations

There are two ways to ensure political changes which will bring ecological changes. The first is to mobilise ground support in every electorate where a candidate of any party takes a stand on good ecology, or against nuclear and polluting industry. The second is to form a local Ecological or Green Party, or a bioregional group.

The broad aims of such a party are: ecological, socially egalitarian, grassroots democratic, and non-growth. Common policy always leaves room for local issues but gives strong principles for guidance.

## Evolving a New Policy Base

The structure for gathering common policy:
1. Define what is seen to be the problem or concern; give weight to priorities on a scale from 1 to 10.
2. State intent of policy for your region; what it is intended to do.
