## Laying Turf

Prepare soil as above and rake flat. Always work from boards—never walk on prepared soil or freshly laid turf.

1. Place a board along the longest straight edge and lay the first row of turf along it. Tap strips down with the back of a rake.

2. Lay subsequent rows parallel to the first, pulling each into the previous row. No bonding between pieces is needed.

3. At the end of a row, butt the last turf on top and cut off the excess with a penknife.

4. When finished, keep the area well watered. Once grass establishes roots, water less frequently.

5. Shape edges with an edging knife after establishment.

Turf must be bought from a reliable source. It looks good immediately and is ready for use about six weeks after laying in spring.

## Mowing

Mow only during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Begin by raking earthworm casts around the lawn, otherwise the mower flattens them, creating perfect seedbeds for weeds.

1. First cut: roll with the mower (blades off the ground) to push in loose stones and bruise grass stems, causing buds at their base to grow out, greatly thickening the lawn. Raise blades as high as possible and trim tips.

2. Lower blades progressively with each cut to about 1/2in (1cm) above soil surface.

3. Cut in opposite directions on alternate cuts. First cut once around the entire edge, then work diagonally across from a corner.

4. Never mow too close—this encourages bare patches colonized by moss and weeds.

5. Always leave grass 1/2in (1cm) long and remove cuttings. Grass cuttings are the best possible [[hamilton-drought-gardening]], raise the cutting height to reduce stress on the grass. Leaving clippings on the lawn temporarily can help conserve moisture, though they should not be left so thickly that they smother the turf beneath.

## Maintaining Established Lawns

### Drainage

Proper drainage is essential. If the soil is waterlogged, air cannot reach roots. Improve by adding sand or [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]. For heavy soils, use a hollow-tined fork to remove cores and fill holes with sand. For light soils, fill cores with garden compost.

## Alternative Lawns

For areas where conventional grass struggles—deep shade, very dry conditions, or steep banks—consider alternatives such as creeping thyme, chamomile, or clover lawns. These low-growing plants require less mowing, tolerate poor conditions better, and support pollinators.
