# Television as the Dominator Drug Par Excellence

## Overview

In the final section of *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna advances the
provocative thesis that television is an "electronic drug," the most powerful
and pervasive instrument of social control ever devised. He argues that
television functions pharmacologically as an addictive substance that delivers
users into an alternative reality by acting directly on the sensorium, without
chemicals being introduced into the nervous system. Unlike any previous drug,
television's content is entirely controlled by its distributors, making it the
ideal tool for fostering fascism and totalitarianism.

## The Concept of Electronic Drugs

McKenna defines electronic drugs as high-technology substances that deliver the
user into an alternative reality through direct sensory manipulation rather than
chemical intervention. Television is the prototype and, so far, the only fully
realized example. He compares its introduction to Philip K. Dick's fictional
scenario in *The Man in the High Castle*, in which occupying Japanese
authorities introduced and legalized marijuana as one of their first moves at
pacifying the population of California. In "this world" too, the victors
introduced an all-pervasive, ultra-powerful, society-shaping drug: television.

No epidemic, addictive craze, or religious hysteria in history has ever moved
faster or made as many converts in as short a time as television. It operates as
a drug despite being chemically non-invasive: it allows the participant to blot
out the real world and enter a pleasurable and passive mental state. The worries
and anxieties of reality are as effectively deferred by becoming absorbed in a
television program as by going on a "trip" induced by drugs or alcohol. The
television habit is every bit as addicting and physiologically damaging as any
chemical drug.

## Parallels with Heroin Addiction

McKenna draws a direct and sustained analogy between television and heroin
addiction. Heroin flattens the image; with heroin, things are neither hot nor
cold; the junkie looks out at the world certain that whatever it is, it does not
matter. The illusion of knowing and of control that heroin engenders is
analogous to the unconscious assumption of the television consumer that what is
seen is "real" somewhere in the world. In fact, what is seen are the
cosmetically enhanced surfaces of products.

Both substances produce characteristic patterns of denial and distorted self-
assessment. Just as alcoholics only vaguely acknowledge their addiction while
overestimating their control over drinking, television viewers similarly
overestimate their control over their viewing habits. People feel that they
control their television watching more than they really do. The television habit
distorts the sense of time, renders other experiences vague and curiously unreal
while taking on a greater reality for itself, and weakens relationships by
reducing and sometimes eliminating normal opportunities for talking and
communicating.

## The Hidden Persuader: Controlled Content

What distinguishes television from all previous drugs is that its content is a
manufactured data stream that can be sanitized to "protect" or impose cultural
values. The user is confronted with an addictive, all-pervasive drug that
delivers an experience whose message is whatever those who deal the drug wish it
to be. This makes television, in McKenna's analysis, the ideal medium for
fostering totalitarianism.

Control of content, uniformity of content, and repeatability of content make
television inevitably a tool of coercion, brainwashing, and manipulation.
Television induces a trance state in the viewer that is the necessary
precondition for brainwashing. As with all other drugs and technologies,
television's basic character cannot be changed; it is no more reformable than
the technology that produces automatic assault rifles. The medium is the
message, and the message is dominion.

## Statistical Dimensions of the Addiction

McKenna cites alarming statistics to underscore the scale of television
addiction in American society. In the United States, there are more televisions
than households. The average television set is on six hours a day. The average
person watches more than five hours per day, nearly one-third of their waking
time. Aware as we all are of these simple facts, McKenna observes, we seem
unable to react to their implications.

These figures describe a society in the grip of a mass addiction more total than
any drug epidemic in history. No drug in history has so quickly or completely
isolated the entire culture of its users from contact with reality, and no drug
has so completely succeeded in remaking the values of the culture that it has
infected. Serious study of the effects of television on health and culture has
only begun recently, yet the scale of the phenomenon is already staggering.

## Television and the Suppression of the Sixties Counterculture

McKenna integrates television into his broader narrative about the suppression
of psychedelic consciousness and the co-optation of dissent. Television arrived
at precisely the right time from the dominator elite's perspective. The nearly
150 years of synthetic drug epidemics that began with morphine in 1806 had led
to public disgust at institutional drug marketing. When hard drugs were made
illegal, underground markets flourished, but drugs as stated instruments of
national policy had been discredited.

The postwar American culture created by television was, in McKenna's phrase, of
the "Ken-and-Barbie variety." The children of this manufactured culture briefly
broke out of television intoxication in the mid-1960s through the use of
hallucinogens. "Oops," responded the dominators, and they quickly made
psychedelics illegal and halted all research. A "double dose of TV therapy plus
cocaine" was ordered up for the errant hippies, and they were quickly cured and
turned into consumption-oriented yuppies. Nearly everyone learned to love Big
Brother. The few who did not are still clucked over by the dominator culture
each time it compulsively scratches in the barnyard dust of its puzzlement over
"what happened in the Sixties."
