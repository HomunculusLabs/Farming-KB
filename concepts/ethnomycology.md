# Ethnomycology

Ethnomycology is the study of the cultural and religious significance of mushrooms, particularly those with consciousness-expanding properties. The term was coined by the late [[ethnobotanical-use-of-sacred-mushrooms-in-indigenous-cultures]] worldwide relied on individuals known by many names — Shaman, Healer, Priest, Mage, Sage, Yogi, or Magician — who served as spiritual leaders. These "plant-knowers" derived their insight, authority, and social power primarily from their understanding of **pharmacopoeia** (the use of plants). The English words "pharmacy" and "pharmacist" derive from this root, which has also been translated as "witchcraft."

Different tribes and cultures used different entheogens, largely determined by local availability. The social power, respect, reverence, and authority held by these native shamans was a direct threat to centralized government and organized religion, which inspired campaigns to demonize and discredit shamanism and pharmacopoeia.

## Amanita muscaria: The Primary Focus

The **[[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]]) mushroom is the single most written about and mystically symbolized mushroom throughout history. This distinctive red-and-white mushroom is found at the roots of most of the religious writings on the planet and played a major role in the formulation of nearly every world religion and mythology.

Amanita muscaria can be found growing worldwide under **pine and other coniferous trees**, birch, and sometimes oak. It grows in a **mycorrhizal (symbiotic, not parasitic) relationship** with these trees — the actual "plant" is the underground mycelium attached to tree roots, while the mushroom itself is only the fruit-body. This botanical reality gave rise to the widespread symbolic motif of the "Fruit of the Tree."

## Political Suppression

The sacramental use of entheogenic plants has only recently come into disrepute. This shift is largely attributed to:

- **Lumping all substances together** — psychoactive plants used for spiritual purposes were classified alongside addictive drugs like heroin and cocaine, then universally condemned as "evil" or "dirty"
- **The Spanish Inquisition and witch hunts** — resulting in the murder of over eight million people tried as witches, and the confiscation of their land and property, done in the name of God by the Church-government through the 19th century
- **Colonial extermination** — an estimated twenty million indigenous Central Americans were killed, nearly exterminating the Aztec, Inca, and Mayan peoples, with similar fates for indigenous North Americans
- **Governmental prohibition** — continuing to deny individuals their human right to directly access and understand spirituality through the traditional means provided by nature

The underlying agenda across all these campaigns is identified as the **repression of entheogenic plant knowledge**, which would otherwise ensure people's ability to experience God for themselves rather than depending on established religious institutions.

## The Scholarly Field

Thousands of PhD professors worldwide — in fields including botany, ethnobotany, archaeology, anthropology, philology, philosophy, psychology, and many inter-related disciplines — have written thousands of books and papers investigating psychedelic plants. These writings document the use of entheogenic substances by spiritual practitioners in nearly every religion formed on the planet.

The study of ethnomycology cannot simply examine religion's association with mushrooms, because by its nature it must also examine the **philosophical principles** of the religions that show evidence of ethnomycological connection. This interdisciplinary scope encompasses psychology, ethnology, philology, anthropology, archaeology, sociology, art, history, religion, myth, culture, symbology, and philosophy.
