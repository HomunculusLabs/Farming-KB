# Fungal Bioaccumulation and Biosorption of Metals

## Overview

Fungi can accumulate extraordinary concentrations of heavy [[singh-bioaccumulation-heavy-metals-fungi]] metals from
their environment through both passive and active mechanisms. This
capacity makes them important agents in metal cycling, soil remediation,
and biomonitoring. Metal bioaccumulation by fungi occurs through three
primary mechanisms: biosorption to [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]], intracellular accumulation
via transport, and extracellular precipitation.

## Biosorption: Passive Metal Binding to Cell Walls

Biosorption is the passive binding of metal ions to [[bioremediation-fungal-biomass-biosorbent-material]]
components including cell walls, pigments, and extracellular
polysaccharides. This process occurs in both living and dead fungal
biomass (Gadd, 1990, 1993), making it particularly valuable for
bioremediation since non-living fungal material can be used as a
biosorbent without the complications of maintaining [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]].

### Cell Wall Binding Sites

Fungal cell walls provide abundant binding sites for metal ions:

- [[chitin-and-chitosan-in-agriculture]] contain amino groups that bind cationic metals
- Glucans provide hydroxyl groups for metal coordination
- [[fungal-melanin-environmental-protection-stress-resistance-gadd]] contain phenolic groups with high metal affinity
- Carboxyl groups from uronic acids in cell wall polysaccharides
- Extracellular polymeric substances extend the biosorptive surface
  beyond the cell itself

### Metals Accumulated

Fungi can biosorb a wide range of metals including nickel (Ni), zinc
(Zn), silver (Ag), copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb). They are
especially efficient at accumulating metals from dilute external
concentrations, making them effective at cleaning up low-level
contamination that is difficult to address by other means (Gadd, 1993,
2000, 2001a,b; Baldrian, 2003).

## Intracellular Accumulation: Active Metal Transport

Beyond passive biosorption, living fungi can actively transport metal
ions across their plasma membranes and accumulate them intracellularly.
This energy-dependent process allows fungi to concentrate metals far
above external environmental levels. Intracellular accumulation may
serve various biological purposes including:

- Detoxification through sequestration in vacuoles
- Use of essential metals as enzyme cofactors
- Production of metal-binding peptides (metallothioneins, phytochelatins)
- Deliberate concentration for unknown metabolic reasons

## Extracellular Precipitation

Fungi can immobilize metals through extracellular precipitation,
forming [[gadd-mycogenic-mineral-precipitation-secondary-minerals]] that incorporate the metal into insoluble
compounds. This occurs through both metabolism-dependent and
-independent processes:

### Oxalate-Mediated Precipitation

Fungi produce metal oxalates with a variety of metals including
calcium (Ca), cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), manganese
(Mn), strontium (Sr), zinc (Zn), nickel (Ni), and lead (Pb)
(Arnott, 1995; Gadd, 1999, 2000; Sayer et al., 1999). Oxalate
secretion is one of the primary mechanisms by which fungi transform
and immobilize toxic metals in their environment.

### Carbonate Precipitation

Microbial carbonate precipitation coupled with silicate weathering
could provide an important sink for CO2 in terrestrial environments.
[[fungal-hyphae]] serve as nucleation sites for calcite (CaCO3)
precipitation, and chitin — the major component of fungal cell walls —
is a substrate on which calcite readily nucleates (Manoli et al., 1997).
