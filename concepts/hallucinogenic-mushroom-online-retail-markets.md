# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Online Retail Markets

The retail market for hallucinogenic mushrooms has undergone dramatic
transformation since the late 1990s, evolving from a niche foraging
activity into a commercial industry served by smartshops, market
stalls, and online retailers across Europe and beyond. As documented
in the EMCDDA's 2006 thematic paper on hallucinogenic mushrooms as
an emerging trend, the internet has played a pivotal role in
democratizing access to both mushroom products and cultivation
supplies, creating a borderless market that challenges national
regulatory frameworks.

## The Rise of Smartshop Markets

The commercial market for hallucinogenic mushrooms emerged most
prominently in the Netherlands, where smartshops specialized in
psychoactive herbal substances began marketing mushrooms alongside
other 'natural' products in the late 1990s. Smartshops are retail
outlets found primarily in the Netherlands that specialize in
psychoactive herbal substances legal under Dutch law, in addition to
vitamins, mineral supplements, and health products.

In the Netherlands, an estimated 120-150 smartshops operate, with the
majority concentrated in Amsterdam. These shops sell predominantly
natural products including magic mushrooms, and some also sell them
through coffee shops. Specialized 'paddo cabs' even make home
deliveries of hallucinogenic mushrooms. According to the Dutch Risk
Assessment Report, magic mushrooms make up approximately 50% of
smartshop turnover.

In the UK, approximately 300 shops and market stalls sold
hallucinogenic mushrooms until the legal change in July 2005
prohibited sales. The UK Treasury collected sales tax estimated at
up to 175,000 pounds annually on a turnover of approximately 1 million
pounds per annum. According to a Mixmag readership survey, 54.6% of
users purchased their last batch from a club, shop, or stall, while
16% obtained them from a friend and 4.2% via the internet.

## Most Commonly Sold Species

The types of mushrooms most commonly sold through retail channels are
[[psilocybe-cubensis]], most notably the Psilocybe mexicana.
None of the primary retail species are reported to grow wild in
Europe. Most recreationally used mushrooms are cultivated rather than
picked wild. The cubensis varieties are cultivated specifically,
mostly in the Netherlands, because they are easy to grow in terrariums
and possess a low water content that allows them to stay fresh for
7-10 days.

## Online Retail Landscape

A 2006 survey of online shops selling hallucinogenic mushroom
products identified 39 distinct online retailers through searches on
Google and AltaVista using keywords including 'magic mushrooms shop',
'buy magic mushrooms', '[[psilocybe-cubensis]] shop', and 'buy psilocybe
cubensis'. Analysis revealed that the vast majority (82%) of these
online shops were based in the Netherlands. The remaining 18%
included four Austrian online shops selling growkits and sporeprints,
two German sites, and a UK site linked to a Dutch online shop.

Website statistics from one Dutch online shop showed that between May
2004 and February 2006, the site was accessed via servers based in
the Netherlands (73%), followed by Belgium (14%), Germany (2%), and
France (2%). This demonstrates the Netherlands' dominant role in the
European market.

## Products Available Online

Online shops sell a wide variety of hallucinogenic mushroom products:

- Fresh mushrooms: Psilocybe cubensis (Mexican) sold at 8.5-45.28
  euros per 30 grams
- Fresh mushrooms: Copelandia cyanescens at 13-17.5 euros per 10g
- Truffles/sclerotia: Psilocybe mexicana (Philosopher's stones) at
  10-17.5 euros per 15g
- Spawn bags: [[psilocybe-azurescens]] at 24-25 euros each
- Growkits: Various species at 25-100 euros per kit
- Spore prints: Various species at 9-28 euros per print or syringe

The majority of online shops offer international shipping, although
many sites do not ship to countries where sales are prohibited and
advise customers to verify local legal status before ordering. Many
sites provide different language versions, mainly English, French,
and German, to attract foreign customers.

## Cultivation Supplies Market

Beyond finished mushroom products, a significant market exists for
cultivation supplies. Spore prints are created by pressing the gills
of a mature mushroom onto paper, or spore syringes are created by
transferring spores from a print into a syringe under sterile
conditions. Spores are used to grow mushrooms on a substrate of
nutrients free of bacteria and molds. Users can also obtain various
strains for approximately 1 euro per print from the Free Spore Ring
Europe, a non-profit organization. Instructions for growing mushrooms
using spore prints and substrate are easily found online.

## Legal Responses and Market Adaptation

The legal landscape has significantly impacted the online market. Six
EU countries tightened legislation on hallucinogenic mushrooms between
2001 and 2006: Denmark (2001), the Netherlands (2002), Germany,
Estonia, the UK (2005), and Ireland (2006). Following the UK ban in
July 2005, internet sites stopped selling hallucinogenic mushrooms.
The Irish online headshop THC placed a notice that products were no
longer available since becoming illegal on January 31, 2006.

Notably, legal prohibitions have prompted retailers to pivot toward
legal alternatives. Following the UK reclassification, retailers
began marketing Amanita muscaria (fly agaric) and Amanita pantherina
(panther cap) as legal alternatives. Purple Ohms capsules containing
Argyreia nervosa (Hawaiian Baby Woodrose) with lysergic acid amide
(LSA) have also been increasingly marketed as legal alternatives to
hallucinogenic mushrooms, demonstrating how market forces adapt to
regulatory changes.

## Key Findings

The hallucinogenic mushroom market demonstrates how lifestyle trends
and economic interests drive the diffusion of and responses to
emerging drug trends. The internet has been instrumental in spreading
both products and cultivation knowledge across national borders,
creating challenges for regulators while simultaneously providing
harm reduction information to users. Future monitoring of emerging
drug trends must consider the crucial role that contextual forces
play in reinforcing or legitimating forms of regulation.
