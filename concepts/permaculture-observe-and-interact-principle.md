# Permaculture Observe and Interact Principle

## Overview

"Observe and Interact" is the first of David Holmgren's twelve permaculture design
principles, articulated in *Permaculture: Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability*
(2002). Good design depends on a free and harmonious relationship to nature and people,
in which careful observation and thoughtful interaction provide the design inspiration,
repertoire and patterns. It is not generated in isolation, but through continuous and
reciprocal interaction with the subject.

The icon for this principle is a person as a tree, emphasising ourselves in nature and
transformed by it. It can also be envisaged as the keyhole in nature through which one
sees the solution. The accompanying proverb — "Beauty is in the eye of the beholder"
— reminds us that the process of observing influences reality and that we must always be
circumspect about absolute truths and values.

## Energy Intensity Framework

Holmgren frames observation within the arc of human energy use. In hunter-gatherer and
low-density agricultural societies, the natural environment provided all material needs,
with human effort mainly required for harvesting. In pre-industrial societies with high
population densities, agricultural productivity depended on large and continuous input of
human labour. Industrial society depends on large and continuous inputs of fossil fuel
energy to provide its food and other goods and services.

Permaculture designers use careful observation and thoughtful interaction to reduce the
need for both repetitive manual labour and for non-renewable energy and high technology.
Thus: traditional agriculture was labour intensive, industrial agriculture is energy
intensive, and permaculture-designed systems are information and design intensive.

## The Thinking and Design Revolution

Holmgren places this principle within what he calls the "thinking revolution" — the
emergence of design as a universal skill alongside literacy and numeracy. Drawing on
Victor Papanek's definition of design as "the conscious and intuitive effort to impose
meaningful order," he emphasises that design depends on both rational-analytical and
intuitive-integrative capabilities. The capacity to imagine other possibilities is
another important aspect of design thinking.

The most creative design involves the promiscuous hybridisation of possibilities from
apparently disconnected, or even discordant sources, to create a new harmony. From a
systems ecology perspective, "design by nature" is not simply a metaphor but a result
of the forces of self-organisation which can be observed everywhere in the living and
wider universe. This imposition of meaningful order is a counter-flow to the prevailing
entropic forces of disorder within nature. Self-organisation occurs wherever energy
flows are sufficient to generate storages.

## Design Thinking Guidelines

Holmgren provides nine specific maxims that operationalise the principle:

### All Observations Are Relative
Observation can be a reflection of an internal state rather than objective fact. Even
the concept of objective fact in science is now acknowledged as flawed. Ethics and
ideology act as filters that determine what and how we see. These filters are
unavoidable — in fact, essential — but the rush to judgment of right and wrong
frequently clouds observation and prevents understanding. This commonly occurs in our
attitude to pest plants and animals.

### Top-Down Thinking, Bottom-Up Action
Stepping back to look for connections and contexts reveals a subject as part of
large-scale systems. This assists in identifying important inputs outside system control
and feedback effects, as well as outputs and losses that larger-scale systems absorb.
"Top-down" systems thinking is a useful balance to "bottom-up" reductionist
perspectives. Bottom-up action focuses on leverage points where small-scale elements
can influence large-scale systems — essential when managing rangelands, forests, and
fostering appropriate community change.

### The Landscape Is the Textbook
The natural world provides such vast diversity of subject material that we can
characterise the landscape as the textbook to follow. All the knowledge needed to
create and manage low-energy human support systems can come from working with nature.
All of our senses have great potential to provide valuable information. Smelling or
tasting soil can reveal otherwise invisible aspects of its biological, physical and
chemical balance. The development of good observation skills takes time and a quiet,
centred condition, requiring a lifestyle shift from indoor, media-dominated existence
to one that is outdoor, mainly daytime, and nature-focused.

### Failure Is Useful So Long as We Learn
Holmgren presents an action learning loop that allows starting wherever we are, no
matter how little knowledge exists, and proceeding from a narrow perspective to a
broader, more holistic one by incremental adjustment. When working with complex natural
systems, cause and effect are often a loop or a web rather than a linear chain. When
trying some action, don't assume you were the reason for any success. Conduct small
trials and think about other possible causes for success or failure.

### Elegant Solutions Are Simple, Even Invisible
In science the simplest answer that explains all the facts has more validity than a
complex answer. Similarly in design, enormous complexity often indicates poor design.
A really effective solution may be remarkably simple — either inherent, or arising
because self-organising living complexity is doing most of the job without being
understood or controlled. Really effective systems may work so well that we don't
even notice them, as with free environmental services like air and water purification.

### Make the Smallest Intervention Necessary
Large-scale interventions are likely to do more harm than good, and require large
amounts of energy and resources to implement. Masanobu Fukuoka wrote eloquently about
the value of doing nothing and the damage from large-scale intervention in nature.
The Bradley method of bush regeneration, based on careful observation and minimal
interventions, has been highly effective in conserving bushland from environmental
weeds through minimal disturbance.

### Avoid Too Much of a Good Thing
Good ideas can be over-applied. The concept of "inoculation against good ideas" —
where too early and strong a promotion before ideas are well tested leaves communities
resistant — applies directly. Over-promotion of permaculture solutions has sometimes
led to naive, misguided, or counterproductive implementations that were later discarded.

### The Problem Is the Solution
Every problem carries within it the seed of its own solution. In permaculture thinking,
the perspective shifts from seeing a "pest" or "problem" to recognising the opportunity
embedded in the challenge. This reframing is central to the creative problem-solving
process that distinguishes permaculture design from conventional approaches.

### Recognise and Break Out of Design Cul-de-Sacs
Design processes can reach dead ends where no further progress seems possible.
Recognising these cul-de-sacs and having the flexibility to abandon a design direction
in favour of a new approach is essential. This connects to the action learning loop —
the willingness to iterate and start fresh based on observed feedback.

## The Two-Way Process

Observation and interaction involve a two-way process between subject and object: the
designer and the system. The maxim "everything works both ways" serves as a general
reminder. In consultancy work, Holmgren found that skills in "reading landscape" are
the most important a permaculture designer can develop to be useful in advising on
potentials, limitations, land use history and successional processes. At Melliodora,
the family balances indoor deskwork with observation and physical work in the garden.

## Practical Applications

1. Spend extended time on a site before making any design decisions
2. Map sun patterns, wind, water flow, and existing vegetation across seasons
3. Use all senses — touch soil, smell compost, listen to wildlife calls
4. Conduct small trials rather than large-scale interventions
5. Document observations systematically for pattern recognition
6. Engage with local knowledge holders who have decades of site-specific observation
