# Methylophilus as Cultivar-Specific Endorhiza Marker in Cannabis

## Overview

Methylophilus emerged as the most statistically significant bacterial genus
driving cultivar-specific differences in [[alpha-diversity-gradient-bulk-soil-cannabis-endorhiza]] microbial
communities in the Winston et al. (2014) study. This methylotrophic bacterium
showed dramatically different abundance patterns across three Cannabis
cultivars, comprising 13% of the [[Proteobacteria]] in Bookoo Kush while
being nearly absent in Burmese (0.13%) and completely absent in Sour Diesel.
This extreme cultivar-specific enrichment makes Methylophilus a notable
case study in host plant genotype filtering of [[alpha-beta-diversity-cannabis-root-microbiomes]].

## Taxonomic Classification and Ecology

Methylophilus is a genus of methylotrophic bacteria within the class
Betaproteobacteria. Methylotrophs are organisms capable of utilizing
single-carbon compounds such as methanol as their sole carbon and energy
source. In soil and plant-associated environments, Methylophilus species
typically metabolize methanol released through plant cell wall degradation
and pectin demethylation, processes that occur at the root-soil interface.
This metabolic niche positions Methylophilus as a specialist adapted to
the unique chemical environment of the plant root zone.

## Cultivar-Specific Abundance Patterns

The first experiment in the [[cannabis-microbiome-cultivar-specificity]] study compared endorhiza
communities of Burmese, Bookoo Kush, and Sour Diesel cultivars grown in the
same soil type. Division of [[cannabis-rhizosphere-endorhiza-communities]] by strain was significant
for both weighted (ADONIS: R2 = 0.59, p = 0.004) and unweighted (ADONIS:
R2 = 0.39, p = 0.003) analyses. Methylophilus abundance explained a
significant portion of this difference (FDR: p = 0.012). The dramatic
disparity, from dominant community member to complete absence across
cultivars grown in identical soil, provides compelling evidence for
genotype-dependent selection.

## Implications for Genotype-Dependent Selection

The Methylophilus pattern strongly supports the second tier of the two-tier
[[cultivar-cannabis-microbiome-two-tier-selection-model]], where host plant genotype fine-tunes the endorhiza community.
Since all three cultivars were grown in the same soil with minimal edaphic
variation, the source pool of available bacteria was essentially identical.
The fact that Methylophilus was abundant in one cultivar and absent in
another cannot be attributed to soil differences. Instead, Bookoo Kush must
either produce [[mycorrhiza-root-exudates]] or maintain root surface conditions that
selectively favor Methylophilus colonization, while Burmese and Sour Diesel
must lack these selective factors or actively exclude the bacterium.

## Root Exudate Hypothesis

The most plausible explanation for cultivar-specific Methylophilus
enrichment involves differential root exudate profiles. Cannabis cultivars
are known to produce different arrays of [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]], including
cannabinoids and terpenes. These compounds, along with primary metabolites
such as sugars, amino acids, and organic acids, are released into the
rhizosphere as root exudates. Bookoo Kush may produce exudates containing
methylated compounds or methanol-releasing substrates that specifically
support Methylophilus metabolism. Alternatively, the cultivar may lack
antimicrobial compounds present in Burmese and Sour Diesel that suppress
Methylophilus colonization.

## Contrast with Core Community Members

The cultivar-specific behavior of Methylophilus contrasts with the core
endorhiza community shared across all cultivars. All endorhiza samples
maintained a core community of Pseudomonas, Cellvibrio, Oxalobacteraceae,
Xanthomonadaceae, Actinomycetales, and Sphingobacteriales. These core
members were primarily well-known [[endorhiza-endophytic-bacteria]] within the orders
Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria. Methylophilus, as a
Betaproteobacterium, falls outside this core community, suggesting that
cultivar-specific enrichment may disproportionately affect non-core taxa
while leaving the foundational endophyte community relatively stable.
