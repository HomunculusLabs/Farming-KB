# AACT Nematode and Protozoa Management (Ingham)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: The Field Guide I for Actively [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] Tea, Dr. Elaine Ingham, Soil Foodweb Inc.

Ingham provides detailed information about managing [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]] and protozoa in compost, compost tea, and soil — critical components of the soil foodweb that are often overlooked.

## Nematode Types

### Beneficial Nematodes
Three functional groups of beneficial nematodes:

1. **Bacterial-feeding nematodes**: The easiest to culture. Relatively simple to get going. Need old-growth forest conditions for optimal habitat.

2. **Fungal-feeding nematodes**: Require fungal foods in the compost/worm bin. Most [[ingham-thermal-compost-versus-worm-compost]] lacks sufficient [[bioremediation-fungal-biomass-biosorbent-material]] for these nematodes — "too active or total fungal biomass" is the problem.

3. **Predatory nematodes**: Feed on other nematodes including root-feeders. Critical for root-knot and lesion [[nematode-management]].

### Root-Feeding (Parasitic) Nematodes
- Root-knot nematodes: Cause galling on roots. Managed through VAM fungi and trapping fungi in compost
- Lesion nematodes: Attack root surfaces. Managed through non-host [[cover-crops-and-green-manures]] biological controls
- Cyst nematodes: Specific to certain crops. Managed through rotation and biological controls

## Managing Nematode Populations

### From Near-100 to Undetectable
Ingham's documented approach has reduced root-feeder nematodes from nearly 100 per gram to less than detectable within 6 months, using:

1. VAM (mycorrhizal) inoculation of root systems
2. [[cervantes-trichoderma-beneficial-fungi]] in compost (including nematode-trapping and parasitic species)
3. Bacterial-feeding, fungal-feeding, and predatory nematodes that interfere with root-feeders
4. Non-host cover crops
5. Soil testing to identify the specific root-feeder present

### Trapping Nematodes
To trap [[comparison-free-living-vs-parasitic-nematodes]], the compost must contain fungi that form:
- Tiny lassos
- Fishing-line-like structures
- Adhesive networks

These are produced by certain beneficial fungal species. The best source is forest soil from undisturbed areas. Testing with a Baermann funnel extraction and DIC microscope is needed to confirm presence.

### Root-Knot Nematode Protocol
1. Get soil tested to identify the specific root-feeder
2. Ensure root systems are colonized with VAM fungi (inocula available from EPM, Mycorrhizal Applications)
3. Ensure compost contains trapping fungi (check SFI website for certified composts)
4. Apply beneficial nematodes if present in compost, or collect and add them
5. Use non-host cover crops (consult nematologist for crop-specific recommendations)
6. Document with soil testing to track reduction

## Worm Compost: Best Nematode Habitat

Worm compost (vermicompost) provides ideal conditions for most beneficial nematodes:

- Consistent moisture maintenance (critical — nematodes die if too dry)
- High numbers of bacteria and fungi (food for bacterial- and fungal-feeding nematodes)
- Worm activity modifies bacterial, fungal, and protozoan communities
- Paper and cardboard added to worm bins promotes beneficial fungi

[[aact-vermicompost-vs-thermal-compost-ingham]] CAN have good nematode numbers, but not consistently — most thermal composters don't manage for biology. They compost to reduce waste, with the wrong attitude about the purpose of compost.

### Getting Nematodes into Compost
- Forest soil is the best source for beneficial nematodes
- Worm bins placed in old-growth forest habitat acquire beneficial nematodes naturally
- Commercial nematode inocula are under development — stay tuned to SFI e-zine
- For existing compost, collect from local undisturbed areas and add to bins

## Protozoa

### Role in Nutrient Cycling
Protozoa are essential for nutrient cycling:
- They consume bacteria, releasing plant-available nitrogen (and other nutrients)
- Without protozoa, nutrients locked in bacterial biomass remain unavailable to plants
- Worm compost is the best source of protozoa

### Wake-Up Time
Protozoa take approximately 2 weeks at 72°F to wake up and begin cycling nutrients after being added to a new environment. If immediate nutrient cycling is needed:
- Add a protozoan inoculant
