# Cannabis and Melanoma

**Source:** The Cannabis Health Index, Uwe Blesching, PhD (2017)
**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Evidence-Based Therapeutic Potential

- **Total Studies Reviewed:** 1
- **CHI Value:** 1

## Overview

Melanoma is a malignant form of skin cancer. While several studies have confirmed the anti-emetic benefits of cannabinoids on melanoma patients undergoing radiation treatment and chemotherapy, scientific studies examining the direct efficacy of cannabis on melanoma itself are limited.

Melanoma arises from melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells in the skin. It is the most dangerous form of skin cancer due to its high metastatic potential — melanoma can spread rapidly to lymph nodes, lungs, liver, brain, and bones if not detected early. Risk factors include UV radiation exposure (particularly intense intermittent exposure and sunburns), fair skin, multiple atypical moles, family history, and immunosuppression. The incidence of melanoma has been rising globally, with approximately 324,000 new cases diagnosed worldwide annually.

## Cannabis and Melanoma

Case study reports of successful applications of extracted cannabis oil against melanoma exist, notably from Rick Simpson's citizen experiments and reports by Cannabis Science, Inc.

The National Institute of Oncology in Budapest (2008) proved that CB1 modulation induces apoptosis of human melanoma cells. The synthetic cannabinoid Met-F-AEA (similar to naturally occurring anandamide) was used, and the CB1 antagonist AM251 confirmed the CB1-specific mechanism.

## Mechanisms of Action

The [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] interacts with melanoma biology through several pathways:

- **CB1 receptor-mediated apoptosis:** The Budapest study demonstrated that CB1 activation induces programmed cell death in human melanoma cells. The use of AM251 (a CB1 antagonist) to block this effect confirmed that the mechanism is specifically CB1-mediated. This is notable because CB1 receptors are more commonly associated with psychoactive effects than with anti-cancer activity, suggesting a previously underappreciated anti-cancer role for CB1 signaling.

- **Met-F-AEA mechanism:** Met-F-AEA (methanandamide) is a stable analog of anandamide that resists degradation by FAAH. By activating CB1 receptors more persistently than natural anandamide, Met-F-AEA triggers sustained pro-apoptotic signaling in melanoma cells. The mechanism involves mitochondrial pathway activation, reactive oxygen species generation, and caspase cascade initiation.

- **CB2 receptor involvement:** While the Budapest study specifically identified a CB1 mechanism, subsequent research has suggested that CB2 receptors may also play a role in melanoma treatment. CB2 activation reduces inflammation in the tumor microenvironment and may enhance immune-mediated tumor surveillance.

- **TRPV1 receptor activation:** Melanoma cells express TRPV1 (vanilloid) receptors, and CBD and other cannabinoids can activate these receptors. TRPV1 activation increases intracellular calcium, which can trigger apoptosis in cancer cells. This represents an additional non-CB1/CB2 pathway through which cannabinoids may affect melanoma.

- **Anti-metastatic effects:** Some pre-clinical studies have suggested that cannabinoids may reduce melanoma cell migration and invasion, potentially limiting metastatic spread. This involves modulation of adhesion molecules and matrix metalloproteinases.

## Additional Research

Research on cannabinoids and melanoma has expanded since the original review:

- A 2015 study from the University of Teramo, Italy, found that CBD enhances the effectiveness of the melanoma drug dacarbazine, suggesting potential for combination therapy.

- Research has shown that the endocannabinoid system is dysregulated in melanoma, with altered levels of endocannabinoids and changes in receptor expression in melanoma tissue compared to normal skin.

- Topical cannabinoid preparations have been investigated for skin cancer, with some pre-clinical studies showing that topical application of cannabinoids can reduce tumor growth in animal models of skin cancer.

- Beta-caryophyllene, a dietary cannabinoid that activates CB2 receptors, has demonstrated anti-melanoma activity in laboratory studies.

## Topical Application Considerations

Melanoma's location on the skin makes it potentially amenable to [[cannabis-topical-applications]]:

- Topical cannabinoid preparations could deliver cannabinoids directly to the tumor site with minimal systemic absorption

- This approach would avoid psychoactive side effects while providing localized anti-cancer effects

- Topical preparations may be particularly suitable for early-stage melanoma or for adjuvant therapy following surgical excision

- However, topical application cannot treat metastatic melanoma that has spread beyond the skin

## Dosage and Administration

For melanoma patients considering cannabinoid therapy, several factors are relevant:

- [[cannabis-topical-applications]] (creams, oils, balms) may provide localized effects for accessible melanoma lesions

- Oral preparations for systemic effects in cases of metastatic or advanced melanoma

- [[cannabis-concentrates-and-extracts]] may provide higher cannabinoid concentrations

- Patients must be monitored for potential [[cannabis-drug-interactions-and-contraindications]] with immunotherapy drugs (checkpoint inhibitors like ipilimumab, nivolumab) and targeted therapy (BRAF inhibitors)

- [[cannabis-dosing-guidelines]] should be followed with medical supervision

## Strain-Specific Considerations

The Hungarian experiment highlights a CB1 receptor activation pathway for destroying human melanoma cells. Met-F-AEA is similar to naturally occurring anandamide, which binds relatively equally to CB1 and CB2. Sativas and sativa-heavy strains with higher THC:CBD ratio may increase CB1 activation compared to indicas.

Strains rich in [[cannabis-minor-cannabinoids]] like CBG may provide additional anti-cancer effects. The [[cannabis-terpene-profile]] of selected strains can enhance therapeutic outcomes, with limonene (studied for anti-melanoma properties) and beta-caryophyllene (anti-inflammatory, CB2 agonist) being particularly relevant.

## Complementary Approaches

- **Bush Tea (Rooibos):** Contains DNA-protective and antimutagenic properties; topical application inhibits skin tumor formation

- **Clove:** Aqueous solutions may have protective properties against skin papillomas (skin tumors)

- **Myrrh:** Furanosesquiterpene in myrrh essential oil has antiseptic, anesthetic, and anti-tumor properties; guggulsterone from related Commiphora mukul produces apoptosis including against melanoma, breast carcinoma, and ovarian carcinoma

- **Sun protection:** UV radiation avoidance remains the primary prevention strategy for melanoma

- **Regular skin examinations:** Early detection dramatically improves melanoma outcomes

## Limitations of Current Research

The evidence for cannabis and melanoma is limited to one laboratory study and anecdotal reports. The synthetic cannabinoid used (Met-F-AEA) is not commercially available. The effective concentrations in vitro may not be achievable through typical cannabis consumption. No clinical trials have evaluated cannabis for melanoma treatment. Cannabis should never replace surgical excision or conventional melanoma therapy, which has well-established survival benefits when initiated early.
