# Open-Air Mushroom Culture and Compost Preparation

## Overview

While the majority of commercial [[mushroom-cultivation]] takes place in controlled environments—caves, cellars, and purpose-built houses—there has long been interest in the possibility of growing mushrooms in open-air conditions. At the same time, the foundation of all successful mushroom growing, whether indoors or out, rests upon the preparation of a suitable **compost** (growing medium), for which there is no known substitute for stable manure. This page examines both the possibilities and limitations of outdoor mushroom culture and the critical requirements for compost preparation.

## Open-Air Culture in England

### Modified Hot Beds and Cold Frames

In Southern England, where winter temperature extremes are less severe than in many other regions, open-air mushroom cultivation has been practiced with some success using **modified hot beds or cold frames**. The system employed simple culture frames of the usual type, with one critical modification: **no glass was used**. Instead, the frames were covered with sheet iron or boards, upon which a layer of straw was placed for additional insulation when necessary.

To provide bottom heat and stimulate early growth of the spawn, **fresh manure could be placed under the compost**. This technique leveraged the heat generated by microbial [[decomposition]] of fresh manure to maintain favorable temperatures in the growing bed even during cold weather. This form of modified outdoor culture was often very successful in the English climate.

## Limitations of Open-Air Culture

Despite the English success, open-air mushroom culture cannot be regarded as having great possibilities of success in most regions. Several fundamental difficulties arise:

1. **Temperature variability**: Commercial mushroom growing demands a more or less uniform temperature. The variation in daily temperature outdoors is usually considerable, creating stress for the developing [[mycelium]] and fruiting bodies
2. **Drying winds**: Desiccating winds are not infrequent in open-air settings and can rapidly reduce the moisture content of the beds below the level required for healthy fungal growth
3. **Moisture regulation difficulty**: It is difficult to regulate the moisture factor in open beds. Rain can waterlog the compost, while sun and wind can desiccate it—maintaining the optimal moisture balance requires constant vigilance that is impractical at commercial scale
4. **Protection requirements**: Under the most favorable conditions, deeper-than-usual beds are necessary, fresh manure at the bottom to maintain bottom heat, mulching with clean straw, and probably cloth covers—all of which significantly increase labor and material costs

### Successful Open-Air Requirements

For any prospect of success, open-air beds require:

- **Deeper beds** than ordinarily made in indoor cultivation
- **Fresh manure at the bottom** to generate and maintain some degree of bottom heat
- **Mulching with clean straw** to moderate temperature and moisture fluctuations
- **Cloth covers** appropriate to the daily range of temperature
- **Protection from drenching rains**, which can waterlog the compost and create anaerobic conditions hostile to mushroom mycelium

## Potential for Open-Air Culture in California

It was believed that certain sections of **California** might be suitable for profitable open-air mushroom cultivation, based on several favorable indicators:

- Both *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. arvensis* are found in parts of California during the winter months, indicating naturally favorable conditions
- The mean monthly temperature of San Francisco and an important section of the state is normally **50° to 60°F**, closely matching the optimal range for mushroom cultivation
- The daily temperature range in these areas is not pronounced, reducing the thermal stress that limits open-air culture elsewhere
- If English experience serves as a guide, a mean temperature **slightly below** rather than above requirements is desirable—approximately **48° to 56°F**

Despite these favorable indicators, no experimental evidence on a commercially significant scale was available at the time of writing.

## Spawning Pastures and Lawns

A common inquiry was whether it was possible to **spawn pastures and lawns** to establish natural cultures that would increase the "spontaneous" autumn mushroom supply. The reported results of such experiments were consistently disappointing:

- No permanent success had been achieved under a variety of conditions
- Results from persons reported as successful were usually found to depend entirely upon the season and were **wholly inconstant**
- English correspondents gave a similar account of this "natural or field culture" idea
- The practice was therefore not to be depended upon as a reliable production method

Despite these discouraging results, the low labor investment involved in spreading spawn about lawns or pastures meant that occasional favorable seasons could repay the minimal effort. Similarly, spawn of other edible varieties expected to grow under local conditions might be spread about with some prospect of occasional success.

## The Compost: Foundation of Mushroom Growing

### No Substitute for Stable Manure

There is **no substitute known for stable manure** as a compost for commercial mushroom production. Wherever *Agaricus campestris* and related species are cultivated, stable manure is the nutrient substratum employed. The best results are obtained only when manure is hauled fresh from the stables and permitted to undergo a definite type of fermentation before use.

### Selecting Quality Manure

Only manure recognized as of the **highest quality** should be selected for mushroom work. Several factors determine manure quality:

#### Bedding Material

Considerable bedding straw should be present with the manure. If the straw is of good quality, it is unlikely to contain too much. Even a small quantity of "weeds" (inferior bedding plant material) is considered a nuisance. The straw of various grains possesses distinct advantages over that of other grasses, likely on account of a certain **resistance to complete fermentation or decay**, yielding a highly porous substratum that maintains excellent physical condition in the beds.

**Sawdust and shavings** had become increasingly common as bedding substitutes (being cheaper than straw), and while these did not render manure unfit for mushroom work—compost containing these substances was extensively used—the resulting compost might differ in its physical and chemical properties from that made with straw.

#### Bedding to Avoid

- **Refuse hay and weeds**: Produced "short, trashy" compost after fermentation that was probably less retentive of moisture and did not make the best substratum for mushrooms
- **Manure from veterinary hospitals** or stables using disinfectants freely: Chemical residues could inhibit fungal growth

#### Animal Diet Considerations

The diet of the animals producing the manure affected its quality for mushroom cultivation:

- **Grass-fed animals**: Manure had not proved satisfactory in tests
- **Leguminous fodders**: French experience ruled out manure from stables employing chiefly leguminous fodders
- **Grain-fed animals**: The French ideal—manure from grain-fed animals bedded with **rye straw** was considered the gold standard

### The Increasing Scarcity of Quality Manure

Even at the time of writing, it was becoming increasingly difficult in some cities to obtain straw manure, as animals were bedded with straw less and less, sawdust and shavings being the cheaper substitutes now commonly used. This trend posed a long-term challenge to the industry, as the physical properties of straw-based compost were considered superior for mushroom production.

### The Fermentation Process

Fresh manure hauled directly from the stables required a **definite type of fermentation** before it was suitable for mushroom cultivation. This fermentation process—variously called "sweating," "heating," or "composting"—was critical to transforming raw manure into a suitable growing medium. The process involved:

1. **Microbial decomposition**: Bacteria and other microorganisms break down the organic compounds in the manure and bedding
2. **Heat generation**: The decomposition process generated considerable heat, reaching temperatures that would kill weed seeds, pathogens, and competing organisms
3. **Chemical transformation**: Complex organic compounds were broken down into simpler forms more readily assimilated by the mushroom mycelium
4. **Physical modification**: The structure and texture of the compost changed, developing the porosity and moisture-retention characteristics essential for mushroom growth

The specific management of this fermentation process—including turning schedules, moisture management, and duration—was one of the most critical skills in mushroom cultivation, and variations in compost preparation could profoundly affect the quality and quantity of the resulting mushroom crop.
