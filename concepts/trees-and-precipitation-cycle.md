# Trees and the Precipitation Cycle

## Overview

Trees are fundamental drivers of the precipitation cycle. Far from being passive recipients of rainfall, forests actively generate, multiply, and distribute precipitation across landscapes. Understanding the six primary mechanisms by which trees affect total precipitation reveals why deforestation leads to desertification and why reforestation is essential for restoring reliable water cycles.

## The Six Mechanisms of Tree Influence on Precipitation

The following summary applies where winds blow inland from an ocean or large lake:

### 1. Condensation from Clear Air and Fog (80–85% of Forest Water)

The water in air masses arriving from the sea contains few salt particles and is relatively "clean." Only 15–20% of this water falls as rain. The remaining 80–85% is **condensed** out of clear night air or fogs by the cool surfaces of leaves. Of this condensate, 15% re-evaporates by day and 50% is transpired by trees. The remainder enters groundwater. As a result, trees are responsible for **more water in streams than rainfall alone provides**.

### 2. Transpiration-Driven Rain Multiplication

Of the rain that falls, 25% re-evaporates from crown leaves, and 50% is transpired. This moisture is added to clouds, which are now at least 50% "tree water." These enriched clouds travel further inland and rain again. Through this process, **trees can double or multiply rainfall itself**, repeated many times over extensive forested plains or foothills.

### 3. Orographic Enhancement in Mountains

As air rises inland, precipitation and condensation increase. Moss forests and standing clouds may form in mountain zones, adding considerably to total precipitation and infiltration to the lower slopes and streams. Mountains covered in forest amplify this effect far beyond bare rocky peaks.

### 4. Ekman Spiral Enhancement at Forest Edges

Whenever winds pass over tree lines or forest edges of 12 m (40 feet) or more in height, **Ekman spirals develop** — rotational wind patterns caused by the friction between the moving air mass and the forest canopy. These spirals add approximately 40% to rainfall in bands that roughly parallel the tree lines. This means that strategically planted forest edges can significantly boost downwind precipitation.

### 5. Air Mass Penetration and Rehumidification

Within the forest, up to 40% of the incident air mass enters the canopy and either loses water (through condensation on leaf surfaces) or is rehumidified by transpiration. This means the forest is constantly processing and enriching the air that passes through it.

### 6. Organic Nuclei for Condensation

Rain is more likely to fall as a result of **organic particles** (bacteria, spores, pollen) forming nuclei for condensation. Industrial aerosols, by contrast, are too small to cause rain and instead produce dry, cloudy conditions without precipitation. Forests release vast quantities of biological particles that serve as effective rain nuclei.

## The Consequence of Deforestation

The summary above leads to a stark conclusion: "if we clear the forest, what is left but dust?" Without trees:

- Condensation from fog and clear air ceases
- Transpiration-driven rain multiplication stops
- Ekman spiral effects disappear
- Air masses pass through unmodified
- Biological rain nuclei are no longer released
- Streams lose their sustained baseflow as soil water reserves deplete

## Water as the Primary Selection Factor for Land

Water availability must be the primary factor when selecting cropland property for development. The criteria include:

- **Adequate rainfall**: minimum of approximately 80 cm (31 inches) and upwards, preferably well-distributed and reliable
- **Water storage capacity**: the ability of the area to hold water as dams in clay or clay-loam storages
- **Stream flow**: any permanent or seasonal stream flow within the boundaries is a bonus

All other factors — soil type, present uses, number of titles, market potential, access, and forested areas — are **secondary** to water availability.

## The Global Water Crisis

Very little of the world's total water reserves are available for present human needs. Many areas face absolute water shortages:

- **Dryland areas** with insufficient rainfall
- **Over-developed cities** outstripping local water supplies
- **Cities surrounded by polluting industry and agriculture**

By the mid-1980s, millions of city-dwellers were purchasing water at prices equivalent to or greater than refined petroleum. Water as a commodity was already being transported by sea on a global scale. Property owners with a constant source of pure water possess an economically-valuable product from their land.

## The Neglected Potential for Water Conservation

While there are no economically-feasible technologies for creating freshwater from the sea or from polluted sources, several currently neglected strategies exist:

- **Recycling** of greywater and industrial process water
- **Purification** through biological systems and wetlands
- **Conservation** through appropriate appliances and reduced waste
- **Increased storages** of rainwater via tanks, dams, and soil treatment

Waste ranges from over-irrigation and non-recycling in industry to inappropriate domestic appliances and unnecessary uses on lawns and car washes. These have not been adequately costed by legislators or householders.

## Freshwater Distribution on Earth

Freshwater constitutes only 3% of all water on Earth, and very little is in active circulation:

| Storage | % of Freshwater |
|---------|----------------|
| Ice and glaciers | 77.2% |
| Groundwater > 800 m deep | 13.5% |
| Groundwater < 800 m deep | 10.0% |
| Lakes | 0.3% |
| Soils | 0.06% |
| Atmosphere | 0.035% |
| Rivers | 0.03% |

The renewal times for these storages vary enormously — atmospheric water turns over every 9 days, biological water every 3.4 days, rivers in about 12 days, lakes in 13 years, while deep groundwater takes 4,600 years and glaciers 16,000 years to renew.

## Key Principles

- Trees generate far more precipitation than they receive through rainfall alone
- Transpiration cycles can multiply rainfall across continental interiors
- Forest edges create Ekman spirals that boost downwind rainfall by ~40%
- Organic particles from forests serve as effective rain nuclei; industrial aerosols do not
