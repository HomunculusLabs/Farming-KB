# Fungal Biodiversity Estimates

Fungal biodiversity remains one of the most poorly quantified frontiers in
biology. Unlike plants and vertebrates, fungi are microscopic for much of
their life cycle, making traditional survey methods unreliable. Current
estimates of global fungal species richness range from 2.2 million to 3.8
million, though some models suggest numbers could exceed 5 million. These
figures stand in stark contrast to the roughly 150,000 species that have been
formally described by taxonomists.

## Historical Estimates

Early estimates by Hawksworth in 1991 proposed a ratio of approximately 6:1
for fungal-to-plant species, yielding an estimate of 1.5 million fungal
species globally. This landmark calculation was based on the observed ratio
of fungi to plants in well-studied temperate regions, extrapolated to the
estimated 270,000–300,000 plant species worldwide. The 6:1 ratio became a
benchmark for decades of discussion.

Subsequent revisions pushed estimates higher. Hawksworth and Lücking (2017)
re-evaluated the ratio using data from intensive surveys in the tropics,
finding that fungal-to-plant ratios in species-rich forests could be 10:1 or
even 17:1. Applying these ratios to updated plant species counts produced
estimates of 2.2–3.8 million fungal species.

## Modern Computational Approaches

Recent work has leveraged high-throughput DNA sequencing, particularly
metabarcoding of environmental samples, to generate data-driven estimates.
These molecular surveys consistently reveal vast numbers of operational
taxonomic units (OTUs) or amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) that do not
correspond to any described species. Studies of individual forest plots have
recovered thousands of fungal taxa from a handful of soil cores.

 extrapolation from metabarcoding data is complicated by several factors:
the relationship between sequence variants and biological species is not
one-to-one; sampling effort varies enormously between studies; and many
sequences represent dormant or dead propagules rather than active community
members. Despite these caveats, molecular data consistently support higher
diversity estimates than morphology-based surveys alone.

## Global Distribution Patterns

Fungal diversity is not uniformly distributed. Tropical regions, particularly
Southeast Asia and tropical South America, harbor the highest estimated
species richness. This pattern mirrors plant diversity and likely reflects
the greater structural complexity and age of tropical ecosystems.

However, undersampling in temperate and boreal regions means that diversity
there may also be substantially underestimated. Many temperate fungi fruit
only briefly and unpredictably, and their below-ground mycelia are rarely
sampled comprehensively. Arctic and alpine fungi are especially poorly known.

## The Description Gap

At current rates of species description — roughly 1,500–2,000 new fungal
species per year — describing even the lower-bound estimate of 2.2 million
species would take centuries. The taxonomic workforce is small and shrinking
in many countries. DNA-based approaches can accelerate discovery but cannot
replace formal taxonomic description, which remains the gold standard.

## Emerging Approaches
Citizen science platforms such as iNaturalist and fungal recording
networks are increasingly contributing to biodiversity knowledge by generating
georeferenced occurrence data at scales impossible for professional
taxonomists alone. Meanwhile, machine learning models trained on environmental
variables are being used to predict where undescribed species are most likely
to occur, helping to prioritize survey effort. These complementary approaches
are gradually narrowing the vast gap between known and estimated fungal
diversity.
