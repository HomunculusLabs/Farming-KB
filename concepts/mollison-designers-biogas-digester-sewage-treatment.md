# Biogas Digesters and Sewage Treatment in Permaculture Design

## Overview

Biogas digestion represents a cornerstone of permaculture [[biogas-digesters-and-anaerobic-digestion-systems]] with
aerobic polishing ponds and biological filtration creates closed-loop systems
that produce fuel, fertilizer, and clean water simultaneously.

## The Problem with Conventional Sewage

Modern sewage systems are fundamentally wasteful, flushing nutrient-rich
[[carbon-dioxide-retention-jar-lid-mushroom-cultivation]], and ammonia gas, with
traces of nitrous sulphide or marsh gas. It serves as a useful fuel gas for
engines or cooking gas for homes. Critically, Mollison notes that biogas is
a greenhouse gas contributor and should be captured and used rather than
released to the atmosphere.

### Sealed Digester Design

A long, narrow pond can be converted to a sealed anaerobic digester by
fitting water seals and a weighted cover of plastic, metal, butyl rubber,
or fibreglass. Deeper digesters (2-3 metres) condense the required pond
area but require artificial agitation using pumped biogas to stir the sludge,
as settled sludge becomes inactive.

### Scale Calculations

For a community of 1,000 people, approximately 270 square metres of
settling pond at 1 metre depth is needed. This can be configured as a
3-metre-wide by 90-metre-long pond, or as three parallel ponds of 3 by 30
metres. Doubling the depth to 2 metres halves the required length, and
tripling to 3 metres reduces it to a 3 by 30 metre digester.

### Digestion Performance

In a sealed and agitated digester operating at 25-30 degrees Celsius over
a 20-day retention period, a very high percentage of dissolved solids are
converted to methane. Each cubic metre of methane generated removes
approximately 2.89 kilograms of solids and reduces biological oxygen demand
accordingly. The process also destroys 90-94% of worm eggs and many harmful
bacteria.

## Reed Bed and Biological Filtration

Final water polishing is achieved through biological filtration using
reed beds and aquatic plants. Mollison references systems developed in
Holland and recommended by the Max Planck Institute in Switzerland.

### Key Purifying Species

- **Phragmites communis** and **Typha** species: Flocculate colloids,
dry out sludges, and eliminate pathogens. These are the primary
workhorses of [[juncus-effusus]]**: Features large pith cells (aerenchyma) effective
against chlorinated hydrocarbons, cyanide compounds, thiocyanates,
and phenols in flow times as short as seven hours.

### System Design Parameters

For untreated sewage, a holding time of 10-12 days is necessary, achieved
by passing water through maze-like gravel-filter canals with floating weeds
and sedges. For swimming pools and less polluted systems, a pumped cycle
through ferns, rushes, and watercress suffices. Such pools need a 23-30 cm
coarse river gravel base with intake pipes below and a skimming notch for
leaves.

## Domestic Applications

At the domestic scale, Mollison recommends comfrey beds to absorb animal
faecal products from wash-water. Comfrey tolerates heavy inputs of raw
faeces in solution, and the resulting crop can be used for fodder or
trenched as instant compost under other crops such as potatoes. Flow-through
systems for methane production take little plant nutrient from faecal
matter, and comfrey or algae ponds deal with the residues.

## Integration with Agricultural Systems

Treated sewage water has been safely used to rear beef cattle at Werribee
for 35 years, demonstrating the viability of integrated waste-to-food
systems. The effluent from aerobic ponds can be directed to firewood or fuel
forest systems, irrigated grasslands, or via trickle irrigation to crops in
arid areas. The integration of waste water with crop and manure residues
is described by Mollison as a far more efficient and non-polluting energy
system than the combustion of solid wastes.
