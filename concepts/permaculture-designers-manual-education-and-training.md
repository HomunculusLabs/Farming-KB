# Permaculture Designers Manual - Education and Training

updated: 2026-04-18

Education and training pervade the Designers Manual as both a theme and a practical strategy. The manual itself was written for teachers, students, and designers. Mollison emphasises that permaculture is an applied design system, not merely theoretical knowledge, and that effective education requires modelling what is taught.

## The Permaculture Design Course

### Origins

By 1981, permaculture had "matured sufficiently to be taught as an applied design system," when the first 26 students graduated from an intensive 140-hour lecture series. By the time of the manual's publication, "thousands of people had attended permaculture design courses, workshops, lectures, and seminars."

### Teaching Restrictions

"The only restriction on use is that of teaching; only graduates of a Permaculture Institute can teach a permaculture design course." This restriction exists to ensure consistent quality and adherence to the core curriculum, while otherwise placing no restrictions on the application of permaculture principles.

### The Diploma System

At the first international permaculture conference in 1984, approximately 50 applied diplomas were awarded "to those who had served two years of applied work since their design course." The diploma is not awarded for course completion alone but for demonstrated practical achievement.

### The Global Network

"Graduates now form a loose global network, and are effectively acting in many countries. The permaculture movement has no central structure, but rather a strong sense of shared work." This decentralised network is a deliberate design feature, not an organisational weakness.

## Teaching Philosophy

### Applied, Not Theoretical

"This volume was written for teachers, students, and designers; it follows on and greatly enlarges on the first book, Permaculture One." The manual emphasises field-based learning, case histories, and practical design exercises.

### Modelling What You Teach

In Section 14.15 (Aid and Assistance), Mollison insists that "advisers or educators should adopt their own advice!" Teaching must be based on "successful trials, not theoretical models."

### Teach Models from Successful Trials

Effective education means:
- Teaching models based on proven results, not untested theory
- Excellent research combined with excellent local teaching
- Local teachers encouraged for the long term
- Feedback assessed and flexibility of approach maintained
- Effort to reach all sections of society
- Respect for traditional diets, methods, cultures, and languages

### Leave Local Teachers Behind

Permaculture aid work aims to "leave a local group able to further extend or educate others in such strategies." The goal of permaculture teachers is to reach groups in need and "leave a core of teachers locally to continue appropriate educational work."

## Permaculture Institutes

### Formation and Purpose

Institutes serve as centres for teaching, research, and demonstration. The manual references "several small independent but cooperative Permaculture Institutes" with associated non-profit trusts that operate businesses to fund their activities. Many institutes are "independent of gifts or grants, and become self-reliant for funds."

### Institute Functions

- Conducting design courses and workshops
- Awarding diplomas for applied work
- Maintaining demonstration sites
- Publishing newsletters and educational materials
- Running seed banks and plant nurseries
- Providing consultancy and design services
- Hosting field days and educational events
- Operating ethical investment and funding programmes

### Income Streams

Institutes can generate income from:
- Course fees and workshop charges
- Consultancy and design services
- Field days and open days
- Plant, seed, and publication sales
- Trust-funded activities and ethical investments

## Community Education

### Village-Scale Learning

Within village development (Section 14.9), education is integrated into daily life:
- School, seminar, and workshop rooms reserved in village planning
- Computers and library facilities as shared community resources
- Crafts areas for skill transfer between generations
- Food processing centres as learning spaces

### The Non-Formal Education Tradition

The manual references the Mondragon cooperatives and the Trusteeship Institute's work on "non-formal education processes." This tradition emphasises:
- Learning by doing (practical work as the primary teaching method)
- Mentoring and apprenticeship
- Community-based skill sharing
- Cross-generational knowledge transfer

### Children's Education

Mollison emphasises that children need "a wider alliance and support group than just one or two parents." The extended family model provides:
- Multiple household environments for learning
- Access to diverse adult skills and occupations
- A children's permanent fund for educational needs
- More than one household to relate to as educational needs change

Student dormitories near educational institutions can be maintained by the extended family network.

## Ethical Investment Education

Section 14.13 describes the "earthbank" educational approach:
- Local groups set up research and seminar-convening organisations
- Annual seminars (like "The Other Economic Summit" -- TOES) bring together ethical investors
- Banks, insurance companies, cooperatives, and credit unions send representatives
- Education directed at redirecting public money to ethical ends

Permaculture began teaching community funding and ethical investment from 1983, following seminars with the E.F. Schumacher group.

## Self-Education Through Design Practice

The manual's ultimate educational message is that the landscape itself is the teacher. Mollison writes: "We must all try to increase our skills, to model trials, and to pass on the results." Every permaculture installation is simultaneously a productive system and a learning site.
