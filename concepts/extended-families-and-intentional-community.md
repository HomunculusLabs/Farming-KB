# Extended Families and Intentional Community

## Overview

The concept of the extended family in [[permaculture]] goes far beyond the traditional blood-relative model. It describes intentional affinity groups — networks of people united by shared ethical values, common interests, and mutual support — who may be scattered across households, regions, or even nations. This framework addresses the fundamental inadequacy of the nuclear family as the primary social unit, proposing instead a flexible, scalable model of community that provides economic resilience, child care, cultural enrichment, and personal freedom simultaneously.

## The Philosophy of Intentional Family

The I Ching describes the family as "society in embryo" — the native soil on which the performance of moral duty is made easy through natural affection, providing a basis of moral practice that later widens to include human relationships and society in general. In permaculture, this concept is extended beyond blood relations to encompass any group of people who share an ethical philosophy and choose to organize their lives cooperatively.

Unlike many societies, intentional families have child care and the welfare of their members at heart. With a common interest and ethical base, cooperative interdependence supplants competition. A "family" of this type, with 1,000 or fewer members, can ally with like groups to create a tribe, and 20 to 40 such tribes form a nation. This layered structure — from individual to family to tribe to nation — creates a resilient social fabric without the rigid hierarchies of traditional nation-states.

## Social Structure of the Extended Family

In practice, any person in an extended family network has:

- **3 to 5 close friends** who change slowly over time, forming the intimate core of their social world
- **A support group of 30 or more acquaintances** who can be called upon for practical assistance, advice, and collaboration
- **Resource access to the whole system**, including skills, tools, property, and capital across the entire network

Such families already exist in Europe, with small groups living in scattered households and locations across many existing national boundaries. Some have existed for 18 or more years, and members report individual satisfaction with a larger support group. The system is not theoretical — it is a proven model already in operation.

## Core Functions of Extended Families

A familial system of shared ethics can:

- **Keep in touch** via a registrar and news service, maintaining communication across distributed households
- **Co-invest** in family property development and joint enterprises, pooling capital for larger projects than any individual could undertake
- **Rationalize resources** for the most efficient sharing of space and equipment, reducing redundancy and waste
- **Develop social contacts** through regular visits, strengthening interpersonal bonds
- **Care for children as one group**, setting up a common fund for their needs and providing multiple adult role models
- **Provide the cultural and support base** for individual households, ensuring no one is isolated or without assistance

## Financial Structure

Extended families operate through several interlocking financial mechanisms:

### Common Enterprise Fund
The family fund, held in two or three locations and convertible to a variety of currencies, is managed by a few individuals as a full-time job. All savings and contributions are accounted to individuals and available as loans across all currencies and regions, functioning like a credit union or revolving fund for the extended family network.

### Annual Membership Fund
Invested with the interest used to service the registry, publish the newsletter, and pay part-time or full-time wages to a collator — the person who maintains the network's communication and coordination.

### Children's Permanent Fund
All adults (age 17 or over) contribute an initial amount (e.g., $50) to this non-returnable fund, with additional gifts encouraged. The fund is managed alongside the Common Enterprise Fund for essential child-oriented ventures or education. Each mother or mother-to-be encourages five or six support people to contribute donations and agree to help in other ways. Until age 12, parents can apply for loans, and thereafter (until age 17) the children can themselves apply, after which they are recognized as adults.

### Special Venture Funds
Raised by proposal, open to groups or individuals, and handled by the Common Enterprise Fund administrators for specific group ventures such as a shared house, boat, overseas program, or [[mushroom-business-startup-guide]].

## Population and Demographic Balance

The extended family model offers a natural approach to population stability. Taking group responsibility for a very few children sometimes cures the urge to breed, and those who want more than two children are far outnumbered by those who do not. Where children are not seen as "future insurance" — as in very poor rural families — the population can soon achieve a steady state.

A steady-state demographic profile shows a roughly equal sex ratio (about 102 males per 100 females at birth), a relatively even age distribution without extreme spikes, and sustainable resource-to-population ratios. An out-of-control demographic profile shows rapid population explosion with unsustainable pressures on resources. A declining demographic profile shows aging population problems with shrinking workforce and increasing dependency ratios.

A registry can inform people of the balances in sex ratio and age structure, and recruitment in the late stages of enrollment can be adjusted to give a fair balance of sex-age distribution. Resources remain plentiful only if people remain relatively few.

## Spatial Organization

The spatial relationship of an individual to their extended family network follows a natural hierarchy:

- **Immediate household**: The home base, providing privacy and personal space
- **Bioregion**: The local area containing 15 to 190 people (typically one extended family)
- **Tribe**: A cluster of related families containing 5 to 150 family groups
- **Nation**: An alliance of tribes, potentially spanning continents but bound by shared ethics rather than geography

This nested structure allows for local autonomy within a global network. Each person has the right to their own space (bed and work space), garden, and occupation, while maintaining access to the resources and social support of the wider network.

## Advantages Over Nuclear Family

Membership in an extended family of shared ethical values does not conflict with any other membership or duties. It is mainly a matter of organizing a family registrar and some common funds. The advantages over nuclear family isolation include:

- **Freedom from entrapment**: People are not locked into unsatisfactory work or personal relationships, or isolated in the context of nuclear family "ideals" which in real societies apply to the few (only 13-18% of all households are nuclear families)
- **Child welfare**: Children have a wider alliance and support group than just one or two parents, with "aunts and uncles" sharing responsibility, a common fund for basic needs, and multiple households to relate to
- **Problem resolution**: Issues such as lack of shelter, land access, capital and services, deserted children, infectious disease transmission, and population control can largely be dealt with by internal group behavior
- **Skill acquisition**: Through selective recruitment, skills and resources can be acquired or developed by education and group capital investment
- **Cultural diversity**: The framework supports unique bioregions, intact language and culture, and cross-cultural enrichment, preventing the world from becoming one vast monoculture

## Global Network

Given an extended family, a bioregional network locally, and some form of common work opportunity, any individual is assured of access to resources, capital, cultural exchanges, and good work. Meta-networking (tribe to tribe) enables higher-level organizations such as travel and accommodation networks to be set up on a global basis, cash to be transferred to areas of need, and larger joint enterprises to be developed. The framework for such global nations already exists — it remains to give those frameworks the mechanisms that create true interdependence via a new type of extended family.
