# Arbuscular Mycorrhizae and Plant Pathogen Protection

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi provide a significant but complex defense mechanism for host plants against root-infecting pathogens. This protective interaction occurs at the soil–root interface, where mycorrhizal fungal communities encounter elevated populations of fungi and bacteria encouraged by [[root-exudates]] and dead root cells. The relationship between AM fungi and plant pathogens represents a delicate ecological balance that influences plant community dynamics and ecosystem productivity.

## Mechanisms of Protection

AM fungi protect host plants through several mechanisms rather than through direct competition with pathogens:

### Enhanced Plant Nutrition and Physiology
The primary mechanism by which AM fungi reduce pathogen damage is through improved host plant nutrition. By enhancing phosphorus uptake and overall nutrient status, AM colonization makes the host plant more vigorous and better able to defend itself against pathogen attack (Dehne, 1982; Smith, 1988; Volpin et al., 1994). This is particularly important in low-phosphate soils where the mycorrhizal benefit is greatest.

### Altered Root Physiology
AM colonization changes root morphology and physiology, including:
- Modified root exudate patterns that may be less attractive to pathogens
- Increased production of defensive compounds
- Enhanced cell wall lignification and callose deposition
- Changes in root surface chemistry

### Reduced Pathogen Fecundity
Beyond protecting the host, AM fungi can directly reduce the reproductive capacity of pathogens. Mycorrhizal plants show lower propagule numbers of pathogenic fungi isolated from various plant tissues compared to non-mycorrhizal plants.

## Evidence from Agricultural Studies

### Protection Against Fungal Pathogens
Abdalla and Abdel-Fattah (2000) demonstrated significant protective effects of the AM fungus *[[glomus-mosseae]]* against two pod rot pathogens of peanut:

- **[[fusarium-solani]]** and **Rhizoctonia solani** both significantly reduced shoot weight, root weight, pod number, and seed weight
- Mycorrhizal plants showed significantly higher growth and biomass than non-mycorrhizal plants, even in the presence of pathogens
- The pathogens reduced root colonization by the mycorrhizal fungus, but propagule numbers of each pathogen were significantly lower in mycorrhizal compared to non-mycorrhizal plants

### Protection Against Nematodes
AM fungi also impede nematode invasion of roots. The effects include:
- Reduction in fertility and egg production of the nematode
- Reduced penetrability of the root
- Enhanced phosphorus content of the plant, affording improved growth (Roncadori, 1997)

Elsen et al. (2001) showed that *Glomus intraradices* significantly reduced the reproductive capacity of the burrowing nematode *Radopholus similis* on carrot root tissue, suppressing the nematode population by almost 50%.

## Meta-Analysis Findings

Borowicz (2001) conducted a meta-analysis of studies on AM fungi and plant disease prevention published between 1970 and early 1998, revealing:

- Most studies were performed on economically important plant species (agricultural crops) in low phosphate soil conditions
- Experiments were predominantly conducted in greenhouse or microplot settings
- Approximately **50% of studies** showed that AM fungi afforded some degree of protection against pathogenic fungi and nematodes
- Pathogenic fungi usually reduced the growth of the AM fungus, but this effect was seen less frequently for nematodes
- In only **16% of reported cases** did the interaction between AM and pathogenic fungi result in reduced growth of both competing fungi

## Interactions with Hemiparasitic Plants

The protective role of AM fungi does not always benefit the host in straightforward ways. Salonen et al. (2001) found:

- AM colonization of annual grass (*Poa annua*) had little effect on plant growth, while hemiparasite infection (*Odontites vulgaris*) caused significant biomass reduction
- AM status of *P. annua* did not affect the biomass of the parasitic plant
- In contrast, AM colonization of clover (*Trifolium pratense*) greatly increased host biomass, but the hemiparasite (*Rhinanthus serotinus*) infection had no negative effect
- AM fungi actually **increased** the parasitic plant biomass and induced more flower production when attached to mycorrhizal clover hosts

This demonstrates that improved performance of parasitic plants depends on the degree of growth promotion afforded to the host by the mycorrhizae.

## The Fungal Community Balance

Newsham et al. (1994) demonstrated the fine balance between beneficial and detrimental fungi using fungicide experiments on the annual grass *Vulpia ciliata*:

- The fungicide **benomyl** significantly reduced mycorrhizal colonization but did not significantly lower plant growth or phosphorus inflow
- However, benomyl did increase plant fecundity (seed number)
- The explanation was that benomyl simultaneously reduced both mycorrhizal fungi and root pathogenic fungi (such as *Fusarium oxysporum* and *Embellisia chlamydospora*)
- The loss of mycorrhizal benefit was offset by relief from pathogen stress

This raises critical ecological questions:
- How is the balance between mycorrhizae and pathogens maintained?
- Has this been an evolutionary pathway to maintain a balance between two different functional groups of fungi?
- How much do these interactions determine plant fitness and plant community composition?
- Could the balance shift with plant phenology or changes in edaphic and environmental conditions?

## Phylloplane Interactions

Beyond root pathogens, interactions among phylloplane (leaf surface) pathogenic fungi and the leaf surface bacterial and saprotrophic fungal communities are important in determining the ability of the pathogen to develop pathological symptoms. The effect of leaf surface biocontrol agents depends on:

- The phenology of the plant
- The infectiveness of the pathogen relative to that phenology
- Environmental conditions (atmospheric SO₂ and elevated CO₂ can alter community structure of both saprotrophic and pathogenic fungi)

## Emerging Pathogens and Climate Change

Simulation models predict significantly enhanced risks of fungal pathogen outbreaks on crop plants with predicted increases in temperature and reduced rainfall. Key emerging pathogens include:

- **Fungal**: Late blight (potato/tomato), downy mildew (corn/sorghum), rust (soybean), Karnal bunt (wheat), rice blast
- **Viral**: African mosaic (cassava), streak disease (maize), bunchy top (bananas), tungro (rice)
- **Bacterial**: Leaf blight (rice), wilt (banana)

The race to promote disease resistance involves both selecting naturally evolving resistance and artificially manipulating genes.

## Limitations of Current Knowledge

Most documented evidence of AM protective effects comes from agricultural ecosystems with low species diversity (usually monospecific crops) or from the forest nursery industry. The role of AM fungi in protecting plants in natural ecosystems remains poorly understood, and extrapolation from agricultural to natural systems requires caution.
