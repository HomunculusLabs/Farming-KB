# Soil Food Web — Advanced Topics

Deep dive into the nutrient cycling engine and the fungal dimension of the soil ecosystem. See [[soil-food-web]] for the foundational overview, trophic levels, bacterial vs. fungal dominance, and building soil biology.

## The Nutrient Cycling Engine

How organic matter becomes plant-available nutrients:

1. **Plant debris** (leaves, roots, stems) falls to soil
2. **Fungi** begin breaking down complex compounds (lignin, cellulose)
3. **Bacteria** multiply on simple compounds (sugars, amino acids)
4. **Protozoa and nematodes** graze on bacteria/fungi, excreting ammonium (NH4+)
5. **Nitrifying bacteria** convert ammonium to nitrate (NO3-)
6. **Plant roots** absorb ammonium and nitrate
7. **Mycorrhizal fungi** deliver phosphorus, zinc, copper, water directly to roots in exchange for carbon (sugars)
8. **Plants** allocate 20-40% of photosynthate to root exudates, feeding their microbial partners

This cycle is self-sustaining in healthy soil. External inputs (fertilizers, compost) supplement but don't replace the biological engine.

## Fungi in the Soil Ecosystem (Stamets, Symbiotic Fungi)

### The Fungal Decomposition Role
Fungi are the **primary decomposers of recalcitrant organic matter** — materials that bacteria cannot break down:
- **Lignin**: The tough structural polymer in wood — only white-rot fungi can fully degrade it
- **Cellulose**: The main component of plant cell walls — decomposed by both fungi and cellulolytic bacteria
- **Chitin**: Found in insect exoskeletons and fungal cell walls — decomposed by chitinolytic fungi and actinomycetes

### Saprophytic Mushrooms in Nutrient Cycling
Mushroom-forming fungi (Basidiomycota) are keystone decomposers in forest ecosystems:
- They break down woody debris, leaf litter, and other complex organic matter
- Their mycelial networks can cover enormous areas (some individuals span hundreds of acres)
- As they decompose, they release nutrients in plant-available forms and build soil humus
- [[mushroom-cultivation-basics]] leverages this decomposition ability in controlled settings

### The Rhizosphere as Microbial Hub
- 90% of all vascular plant families enjoy symbiotic relationships with microorganisms (Varma & Kharkwal)
- The rhizosphere (root zone) has **enhanced microbial populations** due to root exudates
- Root exudates include sugars, amino acids, and organic acids that selectively feed beneficial microbes
- Legumes release flavonoids that attract specific Rhizobium species for nitrogen fixation
- Mycorrhizal mycelia extend the effective root zone by orders of magnitude, accessing nutrients beyond the depletion zone

### Symbiosis Beyond Mycorrhizae
- **Rhizobium-legume symbiosis**: Nitrogen-fixing bacteria form root nodules, converting atmospheric N2 to plant-available ammonium. This process can provide 50-300 kg N/ha/year in well-nodulated legume crops.
- **Frankia-actinorhizal symbiosis**: Actinomycete bacteria (Frankia) fix nitrogen in alder, Casuarina, and 200+ other woody plant species — critical for [[food-forest-design]] with nitrogen-fixing trees
- **Endophytic fungi**: Live inside plant tissues without causing disease; some produce plant growth hormones, others confer drought/pest resistance
- **PGPR (Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria)**: Free-living bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Azotobacter) that solubilize phosphate, produce growth hormones (IAA), and suppress pathogens through antibiotic production
