# Soils in [[permaculture-design]]

Soil is the cradle of the nutrient cycle which feeds the entire
ecosystem. From soil all things come, and to soil all things return.
Soil is the living skin on the surface of the Earth, literally the
interface between geology and sunlight that converts minerals into
living things, bringing our planet to life. All civilizations depend
on healthy soil for food, clean water, shelter materials, and the
plants that produce the oxygen we breathe.

## The Living Ecosystem of Soil

Soil is not dirt and should never be referred to as such. Soil is
itself an active living ecosystem. Within the soil are
micro-organisms and a host of other living things all acting together
to keep the nutrient cycle alive. One of the hallmarks of healthy
soil is the presence of insects and mycelia which look like white
threads. Good soil also has a dark, rich black color, feels soft in
the hands, and is moist. Healthy soil is soft and almost sponge-like,
able to absorb and hold water.

The ecological activity within the soil keeps producing the
micro-nutrients that plants need to uptake into their roots to live.
As things die the micro-organisms in the soil decompose them into
their micro-nutrient building blocks which then feed the plants.
However, soil can only support so much plant life. If the rate of
nutrient uptake exceeds the rate of nutrient replenishment then soil
can die. This is why soil sometimes needs amendments, that is
nutrients added to it, to maintain its health.

In nature, a bear does not wait until the local soil is depleted of
nutrients before it adds amendments by pooping in the woods. The bear
just always poops in the woods, always adding amendments. And animals constantly add amendments to their local soils: animals poop,
pee, shed, molt and die, returning their micro-nutrients to the soil.
Plants return micro-nutrients by shedding leaves, dropping twigs, and
eventually falling over when they die. The permaculture designer
endeavors to return micro-nutrients to the soil through techniques
such as composting old food, mulching prunings and clippings, and
even harvesting their own human waste in sanitary fashion.

## Topsoil: Formation and Fragility

Soil has many layers, each with unique characteristics and ecological
importance. For agricultural purposes the most important is the
topsoil, the uppermost layer. It can be less than an inch thick and
still support life, however you generally need at least two inches of
topsoil to support an ecology. In nature topsoil ranges from one to
about eight inches thick, and it can take anywhere from three hundred
to one thousand years for natural processes to build one inch of
stable topsoil.

For all of its importance topsoil is very fragile because it is a
very thin layer and because it takes so long to replenish by natural
means. Because topsoil is so thin the greatest natural danger to it
is erosion. The loss of topsoil in a biome can lead to rapid
[[desertification]].

## Erosion Control Strategies

Erosion is caused mainly by two culprits: water and wind, with water
being the greater threat. To protect topsoil you need to slow the flow
of water across it. Ground covers such as grasses, shrubs, and ground
vines physically hold soil in place with their root systems. Their
leaves protect the ground from wind and provide shade which stops the
ground from drying out and blowing away. The tangle of ground covers
also slows water down, preventing out of control water flows.

Another method of combating erosion is creating berms and swales. The
technique of using berms and swales for erosion control is called
keylining. Keylines are berms and swales that run across the slope of
a hill, catching and slowing water as it flows down. By filling berms
and swales with ground covers and soft loamy soil it serves to soak
up the water and control the flow so you do not get erosion.

Windbreaks help reduce erosion by reducing wind load. Wind can dry
soil out, and when soil becomes dry it begins to literally blow away
in thin slices. Evaporation, caused by heat and wind, also
contributes to erosion. Put all of these factors together and you can
develop a complex yet harmonious strategy for erosion control by
choosing various plants to provide ground cover, wind breaks, and
shade, combined with systems of berms and swales. The beauty of this
approach is that the more plants in the soil the healthier the
ecosystem becomes.

## Earthen Construction with Soil

For eons humans have been building structures from earthen materials.
Soils used for construction need a relatively high clay content,
anywhere from twenty to thirty percent, with the rest being sand and
aggregate.

Cob is made by mixing clay-rich soil with sand and straw in water,
forming hand-made loaves that are stacked. Higher straw content means
better insulation; lower straw content means better thermal mass.
Adobe is a similar mix made into bricks dried in the Sun, with higher
load bearing capacity. [[rammed-earth]] puts the mixture into formwork
and tamps it down, sometimes with stabilizers such as cement, lime, or
dung. Earth bags are similar to sandbags, filled with earthen
materials and stacked like bricks.

## Soil Sampling Techniques

To determine soil types on your site, use a clear glass jar like a
mason jar. Fill it about half way with soil, then fill the rest with
water, close the lid tight, and shake vigorously. After a day or so
the soil will settle into layers with heavier types at the bottom.
Measure each layer with a ruler to determine the percentages of each
soil type.

Another simple test: take a handful of soil and ball up your fist.
When you open your hand if the sample still holds the shape of the
inside of your fist then it has a high clay content. When taking soil
samples remember to clearly mark where each sample is from.

## Soils and Microclimate

Soils affect the microclimate of your site. Good agricultural soil
supports plant life which provides shading and cooling by
evapotranspiration. Soils that will not support life tend to be bare
and compacted, functioning as thermal mass. Soft, healthy soils
absorb water and slow its movement, while dry compacted soils cause
water runoff. Understanding these effects allows the designer to
harness them for ecological health.

## Key Principles

- Soil is a living ecosystem, not dirt: it contains micro-organisms,
  insects, mycelia, and nutrients that cycle continuously
- Topsoil takes centuries to form but can be lost in moments:
  protecting this thin layer is one of the highest priorities in
  permaculture design
