    11|# Soils in [[permaculture-design]]
    12|
    13|Soil is the cradle of the nutrient cycle which feeds the entire
    14|ecosystem. From soil all things come, and to soil all things return.
    15|Soil is the living skin on the surface of the Earth, literally the
    16|interface between geology and sunlight that converts minerals into
    17|living things, bringing our planet to life. All civilizations depend
    18|on healthy soil for food, clean water, shelter materials, and the
    19|plants that produce the oxygen we breathe.
    20|
    21|## The Living Ecosystem of Soil
    22|
    23|Soil is not dirt and should never be referred to as such. Soil is
    24|itself an active living ecosystem. Within the soil are
    25|micro-organisms and a host of other living things all acting together
    26|to keep the nutrient cycle alive. One of the hallmarks of healthy
    27|soil is the presence of insects and mycelia which look like white
    28|threads. Good soil also has a dark, rich black color, feels soft in
    29|the hands, and is moist. Healthy soil is soft and almost sponge-like,
    30|able to absorb and hold water.
    31|
    32|The ecological activity within the soil keeps producing the
    33|micro-nutrients that plants need to uptake into their roots to live.
    34|As things die the micro-organisms in the soil decompose them into
    35|their micro-nutrient building blocks which then feed the plants.
    36|However, soil can only support so much plant life. If the rate of
    37|nutrient uptake exceeds the rate of nutrient replenishment then soil
    38|can die. This is why soil sometimes needs amendments, that is
    39|nutrients added to it, to maintain its health.
    40|
    41|In nature, a bear does not wait until the local soil is depleted of
    42|nutrients before it adds amendments by pooping in the woods. The bear
    43|just always poops in the woods, always adding amendments. Plants and
    44|animals constantly add amendments to their local soils: animals poop,
    45|pee, shed, molt and die, returning their micro-nutrients to the soil.
    46|Plants return micro-nutrients by shedding leaves, dropping twigs, and
    47|eventually falling over when they die. The permaculture designer
    48|endeavors to return micro-nutrients to the soil through techniques
    49|such as composting old food, mulching prunings and clippings, and
    50|even harvesting their own human waste in sanitary fashion.
    51|
    52|## Topsoil: Formation and Fragility
    53|
    54|Soil has many layers, each with unique characteristics and ecological
    55|importance. For agricultural purposes the most important is the
    56|topsoil, the uppermost layer. It can be less than an inch thick and
    57|still support life, however you generally need at least two inches of
    58|topsoil to support an ecology. In nature topsoil ranges from one to
    59|about eight inches thick, and it can take anywhere from three hundred
    60|to one thousand years for natural processes to build one inch of
    61|stable topsoil.
    62|
    63|For all of its importance topsoil is very fragile because it is a
    64|very thin layer and because it takes so long to replenish by natural
    65|means. Because topsoil is so thin the greatest natural danger to it
    66|is erosion. The loss of topsoil in a biome can lead to rapid
    67|[[desertification]].
    68|
    69|## Erosion Control Strategies
    70|
    71|Erosion is caused mainly by two culprits: water and wind, with water
    72|being the greater threat. To protect topsoil you need to slow the flow
    73|of water across it. Ground covers such as grasses, shrubs, and ground
    74|vines physically hold soil in place with their root systems. Their
    75|leaves protect the ground from wind and provide shade which stops the
    76|ground from drying out and blowing away. The tangle of ground covers
    77|also slows water down, preventing out of control water flows.
    78|
    79|Another method of combating erosion is creating berms and swales. The
    80|technique of using berms and swales for erosion control is called
    81|keylining. Keylines are berms and swales that run across the slope of
    82|a hill, catching and slowing water as it flows down. By filling berms
    83|and swales with ground covers and soft loamy soil it serves to soak
    84|up the water and control the flow so you do not get erosion.
    85|
    86|Windbreaks help reduce erosion by reducing wind load. Wind can dry
    87|soil out, and when soil becomes dry it begins to literally blow away
    88|in thin slices. Evaporation, caused by heat and wind, also
    89|contributes to erosion. Put all of these factors together and you can
    90|develop a complex yet harmonious strategy for erosion control by
    91|choosing various plants to provide ground cover, wind breaks, and
    92|shade, combined with systems of berms and swales. The beauty of this
    93|approach is that the more plants in the soil the healthier the
    94|ecosystem becomes.

    96|## Earthen Construction with Soil
    97|
    98|For eons humans have been building structures from earthen materials.
    99|Soils used for construction need a relatively high clay content,
   100|anywhere from twenty to thirty percent, with the rest being sand and
   101|aggregate.
   102|
   103|Cob is made by mixing clay-rich soil with sand and straw in water,
   104|forming hand-made loaves that are stacked. Higher straw content means
   105|better insulation; lower straw content means better thermal mass.
   106|Adobe is a similar mix made into bricks dried in the Sun, with higher
   107|load bearing capacity. [[rammed-earth]] puts the mixture into formwork
   108|and tamps it down, sometimes with stabilizers such as cement, lime, or
   109|dung. Earth bags are similar to sandbags, filled with earthen
   110|materials and stacked like bricks.

   112|## Soil Sampling Techniques
   113|
   114|To determine soil types on your site, use a clear glass jar like a
   115|mason jar. Fill it about half way with soil, then fill the rest with
   116|water, close the lid tight, and shake vigorously. After a day or so
   117|the soil will settle into layers with heavier types at the bottom.
   118|Measure each layer with a ruler to determine the percentages of each
   119|soil type.
   120|
   121|Another simple test: take a handful of soil and ball up your fist.
   122|When you open your hand if the sample still holds the shape of the
   123|inside of your fist then it has a high clay content. When taking soil
   124|samples remember to clearly mark where each sample is from.
   125|
   126|## Soils and Microclimate
   127|
   128|Soils affect the microclimate of your site. Good agricultural soil
   129|supports plant life which provides shading and cooling by
   130|evapotranspiration. Soils that will not support life tend to be bare
   131|and compacted, functioning as thermal mass. Soft, healthy soils
   132|absorb water and slow its movement, while dry compacted soils cause
   133|water runoff. Understanding these effects allows the designer to
   134|harness them for ecological health.
   135|
   136|## Key Principles
   137|
   138|- Soil is a living ecosystem, not dirt: it contains micro-organisms,
   139|  insects, mycelia, and nutrients that cycle continuously
   140|- Topsoil takes centuries to form but can be lost in moments:
   141|  protecting this thin layer is one of the highest priorities in
   142|  permaculture design
   143|- Erosion control requires a multi-strategy approach combining ground
   144|  covers, berms, swales, windbreaks, and shade to address water,
   145|  wind, and evaporation
