# Medicinal Mushrooms

Source: Mycelium Running by Paul Stamets (2005), Chapter 4

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mushrooms have been ingested for their medicinal properties for hundreds and, in some cases, thousands of years. They are a hot topic with medical researchers, the subject of clinical studies examining their usefulness in adjunct therapies. Mushrooms contain polysaccharides, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, ergosterols, triterpenoids, enzymes, acids, and antibiotics that can stop infection. Each species has a signature architecture and defense against microbes.

## Antimicrobial Properties

Mushrooms share a deeper evolutionary history with animals than with any other kingdom, so humans and mushrooms share risks of infection from some of the same microbes. Mushrooms naturally produce a surprising array of antibiotics. The cell surface of mycelium "sweats" out antibiotics (exudates or secondary metabolites). A Merck survey assessing the antibacterial activities of 204 mushroom species showed that many target specific species of bacteria.

Useful antibiotics isolated from mushrooms include:
- Calvacin from giant puffballs (Calvatia gigantea)
- Armillaric acid from honey mushrooms (Armillaria mellea)
- Campestrin from meadow mushrooms (Agaricus campestris)
- Coprinol from inky caps (Coprinus species)
- Corolin from turkey tail mushrooms (Trametes versicolor)
- Cortinellin from shiitake (Lentinula edodes)
- Ganomvein from reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)
- Agaricin from agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)
- Sparassol from cauliflower mushrooms (Sparassis crispa)

## Antiviral Properties

Suzuki and others (1990) discovered an antiviral water-soluble lignin in extracts of shiitake mycelium. Collins and Ng (1997) identified a polysaccharopeptide from turkey tail mushrooms inhibiting HIV type 1. Sarkar and others (1993) identified antiviral substances from shiitake. Derivatives of reishi mushrooms selectively inhibited herpes simplex 1 and 2. Wang and Ng (2000) isolated a novel ubiquitin-like glycoprotein from oyster mushrooms that inhibited HIV.

The gypsy mushroom (Cortinarius caperatus) contains compounds that inhibit replication and spread of varicella zoster (shingles), influenza A, and respiratory syncytial virus. Fomes fomentarius inhibited the tobacco mosaic virus.

## Immune System Support

Mushrooms offer protection from infectious diseases in three ways:
1. Directly as antimicrobial agents (antibiotics)
2. By increasing the immune system's natural defenses -- the host-mediated response
3. Through mycofiltration mats that reduce risk of infection from environmental sources

Mushroom derivatives activate natural immune response in mammalian cells, boosting an organism's resistance to microbial infection. Mushrooms having both antiviral and antibacterial properties may prevent opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients.

## Anti-Cancer Properties

Several mushroom species contain documented anticancer compounds:
- **Maitake (Grifola frondosa):** Grifolan, anticancer and antidiabetic
- **Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum):** Triterpenoids, beta-glucans, anticancer, anti-inflammatory
- **Turkey tail (Trametes versicolor):** Polysaccharopeptides with antitumor activity
- **Chaga (Inonotus obliquus):** Betulinic acid, anticancer, antiviral
- **Agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis):** Agaricin, anticancer properties
- **Birch polypore (Piptoporus betulinus):** Anticancer compounds
- **Irofulven:** An anticancer compound derived from the jack o'lantern mushroom (Omphalotus illudens)

Some mushrooms inhibit the conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone, increases of which are associated with prostate cancer growth.

## Mushrooms and AIDS

Several mushroom species -- oyster, shiitake, maitake, turkey tails, and other polypores -- have shown anti-HIV activity. The NIH funded a clinical study using oyster mushrooms with protease inhibitors, studying anti-hyperlipiclemic effects in HIV/AIDS treatment. Oyster mushrooms contain a natural isomer of lovastatin that reduces LDL cholesterol by up to 30 percent, addressing a common side effect of protease inhibitors.

Mushroom cultivation enterprises in developing countries are well positioned to fight HIV by addressing urgent needs: fighting famine, recycling waste, building soil, protecting crops, creating jobs, and combating the disease simultaneously.

## Forest Health and Medicinal Fungi

Old-growth forests are reservoirs of novel medicinal compounds. More than 50 percent of biologically active secondary metabolites isolated from fungi come from those associated with old-growth forests. Fungi from old-growth forests may be sources of new medicines active against a range of germs, including HIV/AIDS and potential bioterrorist threats.

Nonblighting fungi with medicinal uses may be the best defense against parasitic fungal blights. The introduction of select saprophytic or endophytic species can forestall the spread of parasitic species. In Western Australia, inoculating karri stumps with Hypholoma proved competitive against Armillaria root rot, reducing disease in seedlings by up to 67 percent.

## Nutritional Properties

Mushrooms are medicinal foods rich in nutrition. They contain numerous bioactive compounds, including beta-glucans, ergosterol (a precursor to vitamin D), selenium, and B vitamins. Some species concentrate selenium at levels useful for prostate cancer prevention.
