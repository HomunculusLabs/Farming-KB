# Marcel Vogel and Plant Communication

Marcel Vogel, an IBM research chemist in Los Gatos, California, demonstrated
that plants can enter into recordable communication with human beings through
the bioelectric field. Building on Cleve Backster's work, Vogel developed
refined electrode techniques and showed that the quality of human-plant
interaction depends on emotional bonding, positive mental attitude, and the
cultivation of a sensitivity analogous to deep meditative states.

## Overview

Vogel's path to plant research began when he was challenged to give a course
in "creativity" for IBM engineers. A student brought him an Argosy magazine
article on Backster's work entitled "Do Plants Have Emotions?" Vogel's first
reaction was to throw it away, convinced Backster was a charlatan. Something
gnawed at his mind, and days later he retrieved it, completely reversing
his opinion. He divided his class into three teams to replicate Backster's
results. None succeeded, but Vogel himself duplicated them, demonstrating
that plants anticipate having leaves torn and react with even greater alarm
to threats than to actual harm.

Vogel had spent fifteen years building Vogel Luminescence into a leader in
the field, developing the red color for television screens, fluorescent
crayons, and black light inspection kits. At IBM he researched magnetics,
optic-electrical devices, and liquid crystal systems, winning awards for
crucial patents in computer information storage. His early fascination with
the human mind led him through books on magic, spiritualism, hypnotic
technique, and stage demonstrations as a teenage hypnotist.

## Key Concepts

- Plants appear to become an extension of the human experimenter when a
  bond of mutual sensitivity is established
- The quality of plant response depends on the emotional state and
  sincerity of the person interacting with the plant
- Negative attitudes and skepticism can shut down plant communication
  entirely, while positive attitudes enhance it
- Individual plants and even individual leaves have unique "personalities"
  with different response characteristics
- Plants go through phases of activity and inactivity corresponding to
  times of day or month
- Psychic energy appears to be storable in living matter and transmissible
  between humans and plants

## Psychic Energy and Leaf Experiments

Vogel reasoned that if "psychic energy" existed, as theorized by Mesmer,
Coue, and Carl Jung, it must be storable like other forms of energy. His
spiritually gifted friend Vivian Wiley picked two saxifrage leaves, placing
one by her bedside and ignoring the other. Each morning she willed the
bedside leaf to continue living. A month later, the ignored leaf was brown
and decayed; the attended leaf was radiantly vital and green, with its
severed stem appearing to have healed.

Vogel replicated this with elm leaves on a glass plate. Each morning he
stared concentratedly at two outer leaves for one minute, exhorting them
lovingly to live, while ignoring the center leaf. In a week, the center
leaf was brown and shriveled; the attended leaves were still green with
healed stems. Wiley continued her experiment, keeping a saxifrage leaf
green and alive for two full months while the control was completely
dehydrated. Vogel concluded he was witnessing "psychic energy" in action.

## Electrode Technique and Communication

Vogel realized that Backster's experiments, conducted between midnight
and dawn, suffered from random electromagnetic interference. He developed
a mucilaginous paste of [[agar]], karri gum, and salt, applied to leaves
before carefully polished stainless-steel electrodes. This sealed the
electrode faces in a moist interior, eliminating signal variability and
producing a perfectly straight base line without oscillations.

With a philodendron attached to the galvanometer, Vogel stood relaxed,
breathing deeply, almost touching the plant with outspread fingers. He
showered it with affectionate emotion. Each time, ascending oscillations
appeared on the chart, and Vogel tangibly felt an outpouring of energy
from the plant on his palms. After three to five minutes, further emotion
evoked no response; the plant had "discharged all its energy," analogous
to the dynamic between lovers or close friends meeting.

## Distance and Selective Attention

In a botanical nursery, Vogel could identify sensitive plants by running
his hands over groups until feeling a cooling sensation followed by
electrical pulses. Increasing distance, he got reactions from outside the
house, down the block, and even from eight miles away at his IBM
laboratory. When he wired two plants to the same recorder and snipped a
leaf from the first, the second responded only when Vogel was paying
attention to it. If he cut a leaf while ignoring the second plant, there
was no response.

This led Vogel to conclude that the plant's response was not that of an
intelligence in plant form, but that the plant became an extension of the
experimenter. One could interact with the bioelectric field of the plant,
or the plant could serve as a relay for the experimenter's own energy.
This mutual sensitivity allows plant and man not only to intercommunicate
but to record these communications via the plant on a polygraph.

## The Importance of Attitude

Vogel found that the greatest barrier to effective communication was
negative attitude. Skepticism, hostility, or aggressive testing caused
the equipment to "go dead" with no response until a positive attitude
was restored. A botanist from Canada who terminated plants in his work
caused all of Backster's plants to faint. Only after he left did the
plants recover, forty-five minutes later.

Vogel stated: "It is extremely important that one understand that the
plant's response is, in my opinion, not that of an intelligence in plant
form, but that the plant becomes an extension of oneself." Masters of
Yoga and Zen meditation, unaware of disturbing influences in meditative
states, produce different brain waves when alert to the everyday world.
Similarly, Vogel found he had to give up his normally aggressive
scientific approach to establish harmonious intercommunication.

## Practical Applications

Vogel's refined techniques for measuring plant bioelectric responses have
applications in botanical research and the study of human-plant
interaction. His work suggests that emotional bonding with plants can
enhance their vitality and that human consciousness can directly influence
living systems. His electrode paste technique improved the reliability of
plant response measurements.
