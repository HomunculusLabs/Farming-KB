# Hemenway Soil Food Web for Gardeners

The health of your garden depends on the life in your soil. Understanding the soil food web -- the community of organisms living in soil -- is the foundation of ecological gardening. Healthy soil contains billions of organisms per teaspoon, from bacteria and fungi to earthworms and beetles.

## The Soil Ecosystem

An intact subterranean ecology develops when soil is not disturbed by tilling. This ecology is:
- Woven by silken fibers of mycelium
- Riddled with channels of traveling microfauna
- Bound into perfect tilth by gummy exudates and carbon-rich liquors of metabolism
- Inhabited by oxygen-gulping microbes in upper layers
- Worked by shy anaerobes further below
- Churned by exploding worm populations loosening earth deep down
- A thousand-specied hive of interlinked decomposition and resurrection

The organisms include sowbugs, worms, mites, amoebae, and fungi in fertility-building concert.

## How Soil Organisms Help Plants

### Bacteria
- Decompose organic matter into plant-available nutrients
- Some fix nitrogen from the air (rhizobia, azotobacter)
- Produce growth-stimulating hormones
- Protect against disease by competing with pathogens
- Convert organic matter into humus

### Fungi
- Mycorrhizal fungi extend root systems dramatically (can increase absorptive area 100-1000x)
- Transport water and nutrients (especially phosphorus) to plant roots
- Receive sugars from plants in exchange
- Decompose tough organic matter (lignin, cellulose)
- Create soil structure through hyphal networks that bind soil particles
- Some fungi form vast underground networks connecting multiple plants

### Earthworms
- Loosen and aerate soil as they burrow
- Create channels for water infiltration and root growth
- Produce nutrient-rich worm casts (vermicast)
- Pull organic matter from the surface deep into the soil
- Their populations explode when mulch is applied (seen when birds scratch through mulch hunting worms)

### Other Soil Organisms
- Protozoa eat bacteria, releasing nitrogen in plant-available form
- Nematodes (beneficial types) eat fungi, bacteria, and other nematodes
- Microarthropods (mites, springtails) break down organic matter
- Beetles and other insects process larger organic debris

## What Destroys Soil Life

### Tilling
- Ruptures mycelial networks
- Kills earthworms and exposes them to predators
- Disrupts soil structure built over years
- Releases carbon dioxide from disturbed organic matter
- Creates a boom-bust cycle: bacterial bloom after tilling, then crash

### Chemical Fertilizers
- Feed plants directly, bypassing soil organisms
- Plants stop feeding soil life via root exudates
- Soil organisms starve and die without root exudates
- Creates dependency on continued fertilizer use
- Salt-based fertilizers can harm soil life directly

### Pesticides and Herbicides
- Kill beneficial organisms along with targets
- Disrupt the soil food web balance
- Some persist in soil for long periods
- Mycorrhizal fungi particularly sensitive to fungicides

## How to Build Soil Life

### Sheet Mulching
- Builds soil from the top down, mimicking forest floor
- Creates undisturbed habitat for all soil organisms
- See [[hemenway-sheet-mulching-guide]] for detailed method

### Cover Cropping
- Living roots feed soil organisms via root exudates
- Roots add organic matter deep in the soil through growth/death cycles
- Protects soil from erosion and compaction
- See [[hemenway-cover-crops-guide]] for variety table

### Composting
- Creates concentrated inoculant of beneficial organisms
- When applied as mulch, spreads soil life through the garden

### Minimizing Disturbance
- No-till methods preserve mycelial networks
- Permanent beds with defined paths prevent compaction
- Use broadfork or spading fork instead of tiller to aerate (don't turn soil)

### Adding Organic Matter
- Mulch (straw, leaves, wood chips) feeds soil organisms from above
- Worms and other organisms pull it down
- Creates steady decomposition from the surface

## The Root Exudate Cycle

Plants actively cultivate soil life through root exudates -- sugars, amino acids, and other compounds released by roots. Different plants release different exudates, feeding different communities of soil organisms. This is why polycultures support more diverse soil life than monocultures. The plant essentially "trades" with soil organisms: exudates for nutrients. When we add chemical fertilizer, plants stop producing exudates (they don't need to trade), and soil organisms lose their food source.

## Practical Observations

- Within a day or two of laying down mulch, worms migrate into it from the moist soil below
- Birds (robins, towhees) are attracted to mulched areas to hunt worms
- Birds deposit nitrates and phosphates into the mulch via droppings
- Mulched soil darkens to chocolate brown, seethes with worms, and develops crumbly tilth
- Deep mulch should never be tilled; few weeds appear and are easily pulled
- To stop future weeds, pile on more mulch (weed seeds rot without light/disturbance)
