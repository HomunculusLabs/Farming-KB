# Manure Quality and Soil Amendment Variability

## Overview

One of the most overlooked realities [[hamilton-kelp-and-seaweed-in-organic-gardening]] is that not all manure and compost is created equal. Steve Solomon draws on decades of practical experience to demonstrate that the nutritional value of animal manures varies enormously — and that relying on manure alone without understanding its quality is a recipe for disappointing gardens and nutritionally poor food.

## The Problem with Manure Reliance

Many organic gardeners assume that adding manure to their garden is sufficient for good plant growth and nutritious food. Solomon challenges this assumption directly: manure quality is so variable that you cannot be confident it will produce a great garden or highly nutritious food without additional mineral amendments.

### Why Manure Quality Varies So Much

The nutrient content of any manure depends on several factors:

1. **The animal's diet**: This is the single most important variable. An animal fed high-quality, mineral-rich feed produces nutrient-rich manure. An animal fed poor-quality forage produces poor manure.

2. **Bedding material**: Straw, sawdust, wood chips, or other bedding mixed into the manure dilutes its nutrient concentration. The type and amount of bedding varies enormously between sources.

3. **Storage and handling**: How manure is stored (piled, spread, covered, uncovered) affects nutrient loss through leaching and volatilization.

4. **Age and decomposition**: Fresh manure has different characteristics than aged or composted manure. Nitrogen content in particular can change significantly.

## The Horse Manure Example

Solomon provides a compelling case study comparing two sources of horse manure from neighboring properties:

### Source A: The "Racehorse" Manure
A neighbor obtains manure from a stable that keeps bred racehorses fed like endurance athletes:
- Hay is purchased from knowledgeable farmers who manage for proper protein content and careful drying
- Horses receive a broad range of protein-rich supplements including oilseed meals and abundant vitamins and minerals
- The resulting manure is nutrient-rich and produces excellent garden growth
- This is the manure most people envision when they think of "good horse manure"

### Source B: The "Pasture" Manure
Another neighbor keeps horses in a back pasture:
- The vegetation in their pasture is visibly poor despite heavy urine deposition
- They must buy most of the horses' feed because the pasture cannot sustain them
- The hay they can afford is of such low quality that the racehorse stable wouldn't use it even for bedding
- The manure from these animals will barely grow anything — perhaps helping low-demand roses grow a little better, but useless for vegetables

### The Lesson
Both are "horse manure." Both would be described the same way if offered for free or sold. But their value as garden amendments is poles apart. Without knowing the animal's diet and management, you cannot assess the manure's quality.

## Typical Nutrient Ranges

Published nutrient analyses of various manures show the enormous variability:

| Dairy manure | 0.5–0.7 | 0.2–0.3 | 0.4–0.6 |
| Feedlot manure | 0.7–1.1 | 0.3–0.7 | 0.4–0.8 |
| Sheep manure | 0.8–1.4 | 0.2–0.7 | 0.5–1.2 |
| Hog manure | 0.4–0.9 | 0.2–0.6 | 0.3–0.5 |
| Chicken manure | 1.0–2.5 | 0.5–1.8 | 0.8–1.8 |
| Pigeon manure | 4.0–5.5 | 1.5–2.8 | 1.0–2.0 |
| Rabbit manure | 2.0–2.4 | 0.8–1.4 | 0.8–1.2 |
| Duck manure | 0.8–1.2 | 0.5–1.0 | 0.5–0.9 |

### Processed Animal Products

| Product | Nitrogen (%) | Phosphorus (%) | Potassium (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood meal | 12.0–14.5 | 1.0–2.0 | 0.5–1.0 |
| Feather meal | 12.0–15.0 | 0.5–1.5 | 0.3–0.5 |

### Key Observations

- **Horse manure nitrogen varies by a factor of 2.6×** (0.5% to 1.3%)
- **Chicken manure nitrogen varies by a factor of 2.5×** (1.0% to 2.5%)
- **Potassium and phosphorus show similarly wide ranges**
- Poultry manures (chicken, pigeon) are generally much richer than livestock manures
- The ranges overlap so much that a "poor" sample of a rich manure type can be worse than a "good" sample of a poor manure type

## Solomon's Recommendation

Because of this extreme variability, Solomon recommends a clear division of labor between amendments:

### Manure and Compost: Feed the Soil Biology
- Use **only the minimum amount of manure** necessary to supply the soil ecology's requirement for organic matter
- Use compost made from vegetation, kitchen scraps, and available wastes
- The purpose is to maintain soil structure, water retention, and biological activity
- Do not rely on manure/compost as the primary source of plant nutrients

### COF: Feed the Plants
- Use the [[complete-organic-fertilizer]] (COF) to supply the plants' mineral nutrient requirements
- COF provides known, consistent quantities of all essential nutrients
- This removes the guesswork inherent in manure-based fertilization
- Together, modest manure/compost + COF produce incredibly nutritious food

## Compost Variability

Compost suffers from the same problem as manure — its quality depends entirely on its ingredients:

- **Kitchen scraps and yard waste** from mineral-deficient soils produce mineral-deficient compost
- **The decomposition process does not create minerals** — it only releases what was already present in the ingredients
- Compost is valuable for organic matter and soil biology, but should not be expected to correct mineral imbalances

## The Danger of Over-Application

Adding too much manure or compost can cause problems:

- **Nutrient imbalance**: Excessive potassium from manure can suppress uptake of [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]]
- **Nitrate accumulation**: Over-fertilized soil can accumulate nitrates, some of which may leach into groundwater
- **Salt buildup**: Some manures contain significant salt, which can damage soil structure over time
- **pH shifts**: Heavy manure application can make soil too alkaline
- **The "more is better" fallacy**: More manure does not mean more nutritious food — it often means less

## Practical Guidance

1. **Source manure carefully**: If possible, know what the animals were fed and how the manure was handled
2. **Use modest amounts**: Manure is for soil biology, not as primary fertilizer
3. **Supplement with COF**: Use a balanced mineral amendment for consistent plant nutrition
4. **Don't overdo it**: Building up soil excessively wastes money and effort while potentially lowering food quality
5. **Be skeptical of "free manure"**: The hidden cost may be poor garden performance and poor nutrition
6. **Test if possible**: A soil test can reveal what your manure is actually providing (or not providing)

## Key Takeaways

1. Manure nutrient content varies enormously — the same type from different sources can differ by 2–3× in nitrogen alone
2. The animal's diet is the primary determinant of manure quality — racehorse manure ≠ pasture horse manure
3. Manure should be used for organic matter and soil biology, not as a primary nutrient source
4. COF provides consistent, complete mineral nutrition that manure alone cannot guarantee
5. Compost quality is only as good as its ingredients — it cannot create minerals that weren't present
6. Over-application of manure or compost can cause nutrient imbalances and reduce food quality
7. The combination of modest manure/compost plus COF produces the most nutritious vegetables
