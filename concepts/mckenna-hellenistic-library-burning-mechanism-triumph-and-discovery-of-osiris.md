## The Triumph of Mechanism

The inheritors of the Hellenistic-Christian-Judaic tradition built what McKenna calls "a triumph of
mechanism." Having destroyed the organic, nature-based knowledge systems of antiquity, this tradition
developed its own approach to understanding reality — one based on mechanism, atomism, and the separation
of observer from observed. This mechanistic worldview eventually enabled the realization of alchemical
dreams: the transformation of elements and the discovery of gene transplants, achievements that McKenna
acknowledges as genuine accomplishments of the Western scientific tradition.

However, McKenna argues that the mechanistic paradigm, while powerful, is radically incomplete. It
produces a universe that is "dualistic" and "unsophisticated when applied to the nature of consciousness
and language." The description given by physicists — that the universe has lasted billions of years and
will last billions more — is, in his view, an inductive projection that fails to account for the
centrality of consciousness and the possibility that consciousness can "collapse the state vector" and
cause what Alfred North Whitehead called "the formality of actually occurring."

## The Conquest of the New World

Having developed its mechanistic civilization, the Western tradition expanded outward through conquest.
McKenna describes how, after conquering the New World and driving its indigenous peoples into cultural
fragmentation and diaspora, the conquerors came unexpectedly upon what he calls "the body of Osiris —
the condensed body of Eros" — in the mountains of Mexico. This body of Osiris is identified with  mushroom, which McKenna understands as Eros having retreated to the mountains of Mexico "at
the coming of the Christus."

## Eros, Christus, and the Rivalry of Sacred Substances

McKenna"s identification of the mushroom with both Osiris and Eros draws on the ancient Egyptian
mythological complex in which Osiris is dismembered and scattered, with his phallus consumed by a fish
(and in some versions replaced by a carved substitute). The "condensed body of Eros" found in Mexico
suggests that the same archetypal force that the Egyptians knew as Osiris/Eros and the Christians knew
through the Eucharist was present in a different form in the Americas — one that was more direct,
more embodied, and more threatening to the institutional monopoly on the sacred.

The rivalry between Christian theophagy (the Eucharist) and mushroom theophagy (*teonanácatl*) is not
merely historical but structural. Both involve the consumption of a substance understood to be divine,
but the mushroom version operates through direct psychoactive effect rather than through symbolic
[[transubstantiation]]. The Franciscans" horror at *teonanácatl* was thus not mere cultural chauvinism but
reflects a genuine recognition that this rival practice threatened to undermine the theological
framework within which their own sacramental system operated.

## Unleashing the Mushroom

McKenna's key observation is that the Western tradition, having destroyed the ancient knowledge
systems and built a mechanistic civilization in their place, then "unleashed" the mushroom by
discovering it in the New World. The Wasson expeditions of the 1950s represent the moment when the
suppressed knowledge began to re-enter Western consciousness through the medium of scientific
documentation and personal experimentation. This "unleashing" is both accidental — the result of
ethnographic curiosity — and, in McKenna"s eschatological framework, inevitable: the mushroom
consciousness could not be permanently suppressed because it represents a fundamental dimension of
human cognitive potential.
