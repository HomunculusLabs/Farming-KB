# No-Tillage Rice-Barley Succession

No-tillage rice-barley succession is a [[natural-farming]] method developed by
[[masanobu-fukuoka]] in which winter grain (barley or wheat) and rice are grown in
continuous rotation without plowing, tilling, [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], or pesticides.
The system relies on a living cover of white clover to suppress weeds, fix nitrogen,
and maintain soil structure. Fukuoka practiced this method for over thirty years on
his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan, achieving yields comparable to or exceeding
conventional methods while reducing labor to essentially two tasks: sowing seed and
spreading straw.

Fukuoka summarized the entire method as "the symbiosis of rice and barley or wheat
in clover." He challenged critics by noting that this simple method produced
better-than-average yields, suggesting something was fundamentally wrong with
conventional practices that required such unnecessary labor.

## The Annual Cycle

### Autumn: Broadcasting Before Rice Harvest

The cycle begins in autumn while the rice crop is still standing in the paddy.
Fukuoka broadcast a mixture of clover seed and winter grain directly onto the field.
The seeds fell among the standing rice plants and settled onto the soil surface. The
ripening rice provided partial shade that helped the clover and grain seeds
establish without drying out. No prior soil preparation was required — the field had
not been plowed in over thirty years.

### Late Autumn: Rice Harvest and Straw Mulch

The rice was harvested by hand while young winter grain shoots were already emerging
beneath the standing stalks. Fukuoka deliberately trod on the young grain shoots
during harvest, which did not harm them at this resilient early stage. After
harvesting, the rice was dried for three days then threshed. All rice straw was
scattered uncut over the entire field, creating a thick protective mulch layer.

### New Year: Clay Pellet Seeding

[[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal|Clay pellets]] containing seed rice were scattered
over the straw-covered field before the New Year. The clay coating protected seeds
from birds and mice and prevented premature germination. The winter grain was now
well established while the rice seeds lay dormant in their clay shells beneath the
mulch.

### Winter: The Dormant Period

With the winter grain growing and the rice seed sown, there was nothing left to do
until the winter grain harvest. The entire winter passed with no human intervention
whatsoever. The clover continued growing slowly through the cold months, protecting
the soil surface from erosion and maintaining biological activity.

### Late May: Winter Grain Harvest

In late May, while harvesting the winter grain, the clover was growing luxuriantly
and small rice shoots had emerged from the clay pellets. After harvesting, drying,
and threshing the winter grain, all straw was again scattered uncut over the field.
This dual mulch of clover plus grain straw created ideal conditions for rice.

### Early June: Temporary Flooding

The field was flooded for four to five days. This brief flooding weakened the dense
clover cover without killing it, giving young rice shoots a competitive advantage
as they broke through the clover canopy. Unlike conventional paddy farming where
fields remain flooded for months, this single short flooding was the only water
management of the entire rice-growing cycle.

### Summer: Minimal Irrigation

From June through July, the field was left unirrigated. The rice grew in moist,
clover-covered soil without standing water. In August, water was run through
drainage ditches once every seven to ten days. The clover mulch and decomposing
straw retained soil moisture effectively. No pesticides, herbicides, or fertilizers
were applied at any stage.

## Yields and Labor

Fukuoka consistently harvested approximately 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) each of
winter grain and rice per quarter-acre — comparable to or better than regional
conventional averages. The dual-crop system effectively doubled productive capacity
compared to single-crop rice paddies. After thirty years of refinement, the labor
of one or two people per quarter-acre was sufficient. Fukuoka described this as
the realization that "human effort is unnecessary because nature, not man, grows
the rice and wheat."

## The Role of White Clover

White clover ([[trifolium-repens]]) serves multiple simultaneous functions:

- **Living mulch**: Forms dense ground cover that blocks light from weed seeds
- **[[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]**: Rhizobia bacteria in root nodules convert atmospheric N₂
  into plant-available ammonia
