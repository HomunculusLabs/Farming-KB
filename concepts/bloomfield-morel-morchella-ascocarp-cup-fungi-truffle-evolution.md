# Morels, Cup Fungi, and the Evolutionary Continuum to Truffles

## Overview

The morel, Morchella, occupies an intermediate position in one of
the most compelling evolutionary narratives in mycology: the
transformation of cup-shaped ascomycete fruiting bodies (apothecia)
into the subterranean, aromatic truffles of the genus Tuber.
Stalked like mushrooms, the head of the morel is an enormous cup
covered with asci lining the gouges in its pitted surface. Truffles,
though dramatically different in appearance, are relatives of these
apothecial fungi. By making sensible guesses about the alterations
necessary to convert a cup into an aromatic nugget, we can piece
together a logical story of truffle evolution spanning 300 million
years.

## The Ascocarp Spectrum

Mycologists recognize three categories of ascomycete fruiting body:

- **Cleistothecia:** Closed fruiting bodies (e.g., Eurotium). Asci
  develop within an enclosed structure; eventually the wall breaks
  open to free spores, but without explosive discharge
- **Perithecia:** Flask-shaped fruiting bodies (e.g., Cordyceps).
  Asci develop within a narrow-necked flask open at the top
- **Apothecia:** Open cup-shaped fruiting bodies with asci exposed
  at the surface for direct spore discharge into the air. This
  is the category from which morels and truffles are derived

## Spore Discharge in Cup Fungi and Morels

The apothecia of Ascobolus immersus are unassuming, gooey yellow
blobs growing on herbivore dung, but the asci are astonishing: a
succession of crystal-clear cannons blasts their octets of purple
spores into the sky. The ascospores are sheathed in mucilage,
holding them together during flight, and the large projectile lands
up to 30 centimeters from the launch pad. By expelling spores as a
single mass, Ascobolus maximizes range, since individual spores
would be severely limited by air resistance. A pressure of about 2.5
atmospheres builds inside the ascus, relieved when a lid at the tip
flaps open at discharge. Each ascus orients itself toward sunlight,
automatically bypassing shadowed flight paths to maximize spore
range.

Stalked like mushrooms, the morel's head is an enormous cup with
asci lining the gouges in its surface. Asci shoot spores roughly
horizontally, creating a spore cloud dissipated by wind gusts. The
morel is essentially a greatly enlarged, contorted cup raised on a
stalk, with fertile surface folded into ridges and pits that
dramatically increase spore production area.

## Morels as Wild Edibles

Wild mushrooms, including morels, reproduce after weight gain
followed by starvation. Although morels are ascomycetes, they are
large enough to be called mushrooms. Morels are prized edibles,
found in late April or early May in the eastern United States.
People who would never venture into the woods for any other reason
will collect morels in pouring rain.

These fruiting bodies are not abundant every year, but appear
according to long-term cycles often misrepresented in local
folklore. Mycelia are exhausted by a year of exuberance and then
demand time to restore biomass before another flush.

## A Movie of Truffle Evolution

The evolutionary history of truffles can be reconstructed as a
series of hypothetical episodes spanning 300 million years.

### Episode One: Carboniferous Forests (300 mya)

We begin 300 million years ago in a Carboniferous forest of gigantic
horsetails and pigeon-sized dragonflies. Disc-shaped apothecia
scattered over the forest floor look almost identical to ascomycete
species found today. These simple cup fungi with exposed fertile
surfaces and explosive spore discharge represent the ancestral
condition.

### Episode Two: Permian Mutants (230 mya)

Fast-forwarding 70 million years to a Permian forest of cone-bearing
trees, a fruiting body on the ground does not open out flat but
remains half buried with a ragged opening at the top. This odd
apothecium developed from a mutation in a cup fungus that crippled
its expansion mechanism. The mutant's asci still operate as cannons,
but only spores discharged directly beneath the opening reach the
air. Most ascospores accumulate inside. Attracted by sugary ascus
sap, beetles crawl in through the mouth and emerge covered with
sticky spores. This rumpled design survives longer in dry air than
exposed cups that shrivel after a day or so.

### Episode Three: Mesozoic Truffles (150 mya)

On the soil surface among Mesozoic cycads, a pig-sized dinosaur with
a spade-shaped nose unearths fruiting bodies resembling hazelnuts
and swallows them whole. The truffle has evolved. The truffle's asci
are densely packed around a network of interior veins. Each ascus is
a rounded bag rather than a cylinder, containing spores ornamented
with spines or ridges. Explosive discharge is wasted energy for a
subterranean organism, and individuals suppressing these stages
flourished.

### Episode Four: Cenozoic Coevolution (65 mya to present)

Truffle ancestors survived the Cretaceous asteroid strike but lost
their spore-dispersing dinosaurs. Since then, modifications have
been biochemical: scent-producing pathways whose products mimic
mammalian sexual attractants. Fungus and mammal have coevolved,
truffles becoming more attractive to rodents and rodents becoming
better at finding truffles.

## Mycorrhizal Relationships

Truffle ancestors maintained connections with plant roots throughout
their evolutionary history. Through mycorrhizal relationships with
oak, poplar, and other trees, modern truffles obtain sugars in
exchange for soil nutrients. In France and northern Italy, truffle
development is encouraged by planting tree seedlings inoculated with
truffle spores or mycelium. Seven to fifteen years pass before any
tartufo appear. After 300 million years of evolution, the final
scene unfolds in Cortona, Tuscany: a waiter shaves a white truffle
over linguini.
