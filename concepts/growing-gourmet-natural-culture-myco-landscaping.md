# Natural Culture: Creating Mycological Landscapes

Natural culture is the cultivation of mushrooms outdoors. After
mycological landscapes are constructed and inoculated, the forces of
Nature take over. This is also called "laissez-faire" cultivation -- the
mushroom patch is left alone, subject to the whims of Nature, except for
some timely watering. The mushroom habitat is specifically designed,
paying particular attention to site location and the use of native woods
and/or garden by-products.

## Advantages of Outdoor Cultivation

Every day, gardeners, landscapers, arborists, and nurseries utilize the
very components needed for growing mushrooms. Every pile of debris,
whether tree trimmings, sawdust or wood chips, will support mushrooms.
Unless selectively inoculated, debris piles become habitats of
miscellaneous "weed" mushrooms, making the likelihood of growing a
desirable mushroom remote.

When inoculating an outdoor environment with mushroom spawn, the
cultivator relinquishes much control to natural forces. There are obvious
advantages and disadvantages. The mushroom patch is controlled by volatile
weather patterns, but outdoor beds have the advantage of needing minimum
maintenance. The ratio of hours spent per pound of mushrooms grown
becomes quite efficient.

A major advantage of growing outdoors compared to growing indoors is
that competitors are not concentrated in a tight space. When cultivating
mushrooms outdoors you have entropy as an ally.

## Spawn Rates and Inoculation

While the cultivator is competing with molds indoors, wild mushrooms are
the major competitors outdoors. Starting with fresh materials is the
simplest way to avoid unwanted competition. Piles of aged wood chips
commonly support four or five species within just a few square feet.
Unless the cultivator uses a high rate of inoculation (25%
spawn/substrate) and uniformly clean wood chips, the concurrence of
diverse mushroom species should be expected.

In the Pacific Northwest of North America, a 5-10% inoculation rate
usually results in some mushrooms showing late in the first year, the
most substantial crops occurring in the second and third years, and a
dramatic drop-off in the fourth year. As the patch ages, it is normal to
see more diverse mushroom varieties co-occurring with the planted
mushroom species.

## Polyculture Approach

In one outdoor wood chip bed, Stamets created a "polyculture" mushroom
patch about 50 x 100 feet in size. In the spring, mixed wood chips from
the county utility company (mostly alder and Douglas fir) were acquired
and three species inoculated into it. One year after inoculation, in
late April through May, Morels showed. From June to early September,
King Stropharia erupted with force, providing several hundred pounds. In
late September through November, an assortment of Clustered Woodlovers
popped up. With non-coincident fruiting cycles, this Zen-like polyculture
approach is limited only by imagination.

## Species Succession

Species succession can be accomplished indoors. After [[growing-gourmet-shiitake-cultivation]] stops producing on logs or sawdust, the substrate can be broken apart, re-moistened, re-sterilized, and re-inoculated with [[growing-gourmet-phoenix-oyster-pulmonarius-cultivation]]. Once the Oyster mushroom life cycle is completed, the substrate can be sterilized again and inoculated with the next species. Shiitake, Oyster, King Stropharia and Shaggy Manes can all be grown on the same substrate, increasingly reducing the substrate mass without adding new materials.

## Wild Mushrooms in Wood Chip Beds

The following decomposers are wild mushrooms most frequently occurring
in wood chips in northern temperate regions. Outdoor cultivators must
hone their identification skills to avert accidental ingestion of
undesired mushrooms:

Ground lovers:
  - Agrocybe spp. and Pholiota spp.
  - Clitocybe spp. (mildly poisonous)
  - Coprinus atramentarius, C. comatus, C. disseminatus, C. lagopus
  - Fuligo cristata (slime mold)
  - Galerina autumnalis and allies (deadly poisonous)
  - Lepiota spp. (deadly poisonous)
  - Hypholoma capnoides (the Clustered Woodlover)
  - Hypholoma fasciculare (mildly poisonous)
  - Hypholoma sublateritium (the Chestnut Mushroom)
  - Pholiotina filaris and allies (deadly poisonous)
  - Pluteus cervinus (the Deer Mushroom)
  - Psathyrella spp.
  - Psilocybe cyanescens and allies

Wood lovers:
  - Gymnopilus spp. and Kuehneromyces spp.
  - [[trametes-versicolor]]

Note: Mushrooms marked as deadly poisonous (Galerina, Lepiota,
Pholiotina) can inhabit the same plot where a cultivator lays down wood
chips. The mushrooms in the Galerina autumnalis and Pholiotina filaris
groups are deadly poisonous.

## Spore Mass Inoculation

By far the simplest way to grow mushrooms outdoors is to broadcast
[[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]] onto prepared substrates. For gilled mushrooms, the caps can be severed from the stems and laid, gills down, on clean typing paper, glass, or similar surface. After 12 hours, most mushrooms will have released thousands of spores, falling according to the radiating symmetry of the gills in an attractive outline called a spore print.

By collecting spores of many mushrooms, one creates a Species Library. A
mushroom hunter may find a species only once in a lifetime. Under these
circumstances, the existence of a spore print may be the only resource a
cultivator has for future propagation. Spores collected in this fashion
remain viable for years, although viability decreases over time. They
should be stored in a dark, cool location, low in humidity.

## Site Selection

The rate of growth, time to fruiting, and quality of the crop depends
upon the spawn, substrate materials, and weather conditions. Generally,
when mushrooms are fruiting in the wild, the inoculated patches also
produce. Mushrooms that fruit primarily in the summer, such as the
[[growing-gourmet-king-stropharia-cultivation]], require
frequent watering. Shaggy Manes prefer the cool fall rains, requiring
little attention.

In comparison to indoor cultivation, outdoor crops are not as frequent.
However, outdoor crops can be just as intense, sometimes more so,
especially if one is paying modest attention to the needs of the mushroom
mycelium at critical junctures throughout its life cycle.
