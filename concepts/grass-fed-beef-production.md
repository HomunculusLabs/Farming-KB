# Grass-Fed Beef Production

Grass-fed and grass-finished beef comes from cattle raised entirely on pasture and forage -- never grain-finished in a feedlot. This model fits homestead and small-scale operations, producing premium meat with strong market demand and lower input costs than grain-based systems.

## Terminology

- Grass-fed: animal has received grass/forage for a portion of its life (loose term)
- Grass-finished: animal spent its entire life on grass/forage -- no grain, ever
- 100% grass-fed: same as grass-finished; the meaningful label for consumers
- Pasture-raised: access to pasture (does not preclude grain supplementation)
- Certified Organic: no synthetic inputs in feed or pasture; may still be grain-finished
- AGA Certified: American Grassfed Association standard -- grass-only, no confinement, no antibiotics/hormones

## Breed Selection

Best breeds for grass-fed systems:
- Angus: marbling on grass, calm temperament, widely recognized
- Hereford: efficient foragers, hardy, good mothers
- Devon: heritage breed, excellent grass converter, docile
- Dexters: small-framed (600-800 lbs), ideal for homesteads with limited acreage
- Belted Galloway: cold-hardy, efficient foragers, distinctive appearance (marketing advantage)
- Murray Grey: Australian breed, calm, good marbling on grass

Crossbreeding (e.g., Angus x Hereford) often produces the best grass-fed carcass: hybrid vigor, good growth rates, acceptable marbling.

## Pasture Management

### Stocking Density
- General rule: 1-2 acres per cow-calf pair per year (varies by region and rainfall)
- High stocking density with rotational grazing: can support 2-4 animal units per acre during growing season
- Rest periods: minimum 30-60 days between grazing; 90+ days ideal

### Rotational Grazing System
- Divide pasture into 8-16+ paddocks
- Move cattle every 1-3 days during peak growth
- Graze to 4-6 inch residual height, then rest
- Goal: eat the top third, leave the rest for regrowth
- Prevents selective grazing (cows eat only the tasty parts) and overgrazing

### Forage Species
- Cool-season perennials: orchardgrass, tall fescue (endophyte-free), Kentucky bluegrass, white clover, red clover
- Warm-season perennials: bermudagrass, bahiagrass, switchgrass, eastern gamagrass
- Annuals for fill: oats, rye, annual ryegrass, turnips, radishes
- Legumes fix nitrogen: clover, alfalfa, birdsfoot trefoil (15-30% of pasture mix)

### Seasonal Considerations
- Stockpile tall fescue for winter grazing (strip-graze with temporary fencing)
- Stockpile brassicas (turnips, radishes) for late fall/winter
- Hay feeding: plan 2-4 tons of hay per cow for a 120-day winter (climate dependent)
- Frost-seed clover in late winter to maintain legume content

## Finishing Timeline

Grass-fed finishing takes longer than grain-finishing:

| Stage | Age | Weight Range | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Birth to weaning | 0-7 months | 60-500 lbs | 7 months |
| Backgrounding | 7-14 months | 500-750 lbs | 7 months |
| Finishing | 14-24 months | 750-1,100 lbs | 6-12 months |
| Slaughter | 20-28 months | 1,000-1,200 lbs | -- |

Key factors:
- Grass-finished steers reach slaughter weight 6-12 months later than grain-finished
- Daily gain on grass: 1.5-2.5 lbs/day (vs. 3-4 lbs on grain)
- Optimal slaughter weight: 1,000-1,200 lbs live (500-650 lbs hanging/carcass)
- Finish on the best pasture available -- lush spring growth or stockpiled fall forage

## Carcass Quality

Grass-fed beef differs from grain-fed:
- Leaner: less intramuscular fat (marbling)
- Higher in omega-3 fatty acids, CLA, vitamin E
- Yellow fat (beta-carotene from grass -- desirable for grass-fed consumers)
- Firmer texture, more pronounced beef flavor
- Target: USDA Choice or Select grade; Prime is difficult on pure grass

Improving finish quality:
- Consistent energy intake (no "slides" in nutrition)
- Finish on high-quality forage (not mature, stemmy pasture)
- Low-stress handling reduces dark-cutting beef
- Proper aging: 14-21 days dry-aged for tenderness

## Economics

### Revenue Per Animal
- Live weight sale: $1.50-2.50/lb (varies widely by region)
- Custom processing: $600-900 per animal (processing + cut/wrap)
- Retail cuts: $5-12/lb average (ground $6-8, steaks $12-20)
- Revenue per finished animal (retail): $2,500-4,500

### Costs Per Animal
- Calf purchase or breeding cost: $600-1,200
- Winter hay: $200-400
- Mineral supplement: $50-100/year
- Veterinary: $50-150/year
- Processing: $600-900 (if custom)
- Pasture maintenance: $100-300/year
- Miscellaneous: $100-200

### Profitability
- Selling live: $200-800 profit per animal (thin margins)
- Selling retail cuts: $800-2,000 profit per animal
- Key: direct marketing to capture retail price, not commodity price
- Break-even: 3-5 head sold retail covers most small-farm overhead

## Marketing

- Farmers markets: sell frozen cuts, take pre-orders
- Buying clubs: sell quarter/half beef shares (best margin, least labor)
- Restaurant sales: chefs pay premium for local grass-fed
- Online: online homestead sales platforms
- On-farm store: if location permits
- Word of mouth and repeat customers are the strongest channels

## Handling and Low-Stress Management

- Quiet, patient handling reduces stress and improves meat quality
- Bud Williams low-stress methods: move cattle using their natural instincts
- Never yell, hit, or use electric prods excessively
- Work cattle through well-designed facilities with solid sides
- Dark-cutting beef (from stress) loses $100-200 per carcass in value
