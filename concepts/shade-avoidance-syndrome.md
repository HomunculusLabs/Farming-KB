# Shade Avoidance Syndrome

## Overview

Shade Avoidance Syndrome (SAS) is a suite of coordinated developmental
responses that plants deploy when they detect the presence of neighbouring
vegetation competing for light. Rather than passively tolerating reduced light
availability, plants actively remodel their architecture to escape shade by
elongating stems, reorienting leaves, suppressing branching, and accelerating
flowering. These responses are triggered by the [[phytochrome-photoreceptor]]
system detecting changes in the ratio of red to far-red light that occur when
sunlight is filtered through or reflected from neighbouring plant canopies.

Shade avoidance represents one of the most dramatic examples of adaptive
phenotypic plasticity in the [[chamovitz-red-far-red-phytochrome-switch-and-plant-memory]]0 nm) being more abundant than far-red light
(approximately 730 nm). This yields a red to far-red (R:FR) ratio of
approximately 1.1 to 1.2 in full sunlight.

Chlorophyll, the primary photosynthetic pigment, absorbs red light very
efficiently but reflects and transmits far-red light. When a plant is shaded by
neighbouring vegetation, the light reaching its leaves becomes depleted in red
wavelengths and enriched in far-red wavelengths because the overlying canopy
has selectively absorbed the red component.

This shift in the R:FR ratio is the primary cue that plants use to detect
competition. Under dense canopy shade, the R:FR ratio can drop below 0.1,
representing a tenfold reduction from full sunlight. The phytochrome
photoreceptor system is exquisitely sensitive to these changes and can detect
even subtle reductions in R:FR ratio that precede actual shading.

Critically, far-red light is not only transmitted downward through canopy
layers but also reflected laterally from neighbouring stems and leaves. This
means a plant can sense a competitor growing nearby even before it is directly
overtopped or shaded. The detection system provides an early warning network,
alerting plants to competitive pressure before it becomes critical for light
interception.

## Phytochrome-Mediated Detection Mechanism

The shade avoidance response is mediated through the [[phytochrome-photoreceptor-plant-vision]]
system operating as a molecular switch. In its active Pfr form, phytochrome
signals open growing conditions with abundant red light. Under canopy shade,
the enriched far-red light drives phytochrome toward the inactive Pr form,
triggering the shade avoidance response through derepression of downstream
growth-promoting pathways.

The detection system responds on multiple timescales. Within minutes of a
reduction in R:FR ratio, changes in gene expression can be detected, including
upregulation of auxin biosynthesis genes. Over hours to days, these molecular
changes translate into visible alterations in growth patterns. The speed of
initial detection allows plants to begin adjusting their growth trajectory
before they experience actual reductions in photosynthetic photon flux.

## Stem and Petiole Elongation

The most conspicuous shade avoidance response is rapid elongation of stems and
petioles. This growth is achieved primarily through increased [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]]
in the internodes rather than increased cell division. The plant essentially
invests resources in vertical growth to overtop competitors, often at the
expense of leaf expansion and root development.

In extreme cases, stem elongation rates can double or triple compared to plants
grown in full sunlight. The elongation response is mediated by increased auxin
biosynthesis in the stems and upregulation of gibberellin signalling that
promotes degradation of DELLA growth repressor proteins.

## Leaf Reorientation and Hyponasty

Leaves and petioles reorient upward under shade, adopting a more vertical
angle to intercept unfiltered light above the canopy. This response, called
hyponasty, reduces self-shading and maximises exposure to direct sunlight.

Hyponasty is mediated by differential cell expansion on the adaxial (upper)
versus abaxial (lower) surfaces of the petiole. The cells on the lower surface
expand more rapidly, causing the petiole to bend upward. This response is
regulated by ethylene and auxin signalling in the petiole.
