# Phototropism — Darwin's Experiments on Plant Light Sensitivity

In 1880, Charles Darwin and his son Francis published a landmark finding
in plant biology: they demonstrated that plants possess a form of sight.
Their experiments on [[darwin-power-of-movement-in-plants]] in Plants*, remain among the most
elegant and influential in the history of botany.

## The Background: Phototropism and Blue Light

Almost all plants bend toward lateral light. This everyday observation
— houseplants bowing toward a window, seedlings arching toward the sun
— is called phototropism. But in the [[chamovitz-julius-von-sachs-blue-light-phototropism]] Sachs, a contemporary of Darwin's, discovered that blue
light is the primary colour that induces phototropism. Plants, Sachs
found, are generally blind to other colours when it comes to bending
toward light. This was the first hint that plants differentiate between
wavelengths of light — that they possess colour-specific sensitivity.
However, the question of *where* in the plant the light was detected
remained unanswered.

## The Canary Grass Experiments

Darwin and his son Francis designed a remarkably simple experiment to
identify which part of the plant "sees" the light. They grew canary
grass (*Phalaris canariensis*) seedlings in a totally dark room for
several days, then placed a very small gas lamp twelve feet (about 3.5
metres) away. The lamp was so dim that the Darwins "could not see the
seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper." Yet after only
three hours, the plants had curved toward the faint light. The bending
always occurred in the same region — about an inch (two centimetres)
below the tip.

This initial observation was critical because it established that the
bending response was extremely sensitive and that the site of detection
(the tip) was physically separated from the site of response (the
midsection). This spatial separation implied a signal must travel from
the tip downward, coordinating the bending below.

## The Five Experimental Conditions

The Darwins then devised their classic experiment with five conditions
to pinpoint exactly which part of the seedling was responsible for
detecting light:

**Condition A — Untreated control.** The seedling was left completely
intact. It bent toward the light as expected, confirming that the
experimental conditions were conducive to phototropism.

**Condition B — Tip removed.** The tip of the seedling was pruned off
before exposure to light. The seedling did not bend. Without its tip,
the plant went "blind" and could not respond to the light at all.

**Condition C — Tip covered with a [[lightproof-cap-experimental-design-phototropism]].** The tip was
covered with a small cap that blocked all light. The seedling did not
bend. Even though the midsection was fully exposed to light, blocking
the tip alone was sufficient to eliminate the phototropic response.

**Condition D — Tip covered with a clear glass cap.** The tip was
covered with a transparent glass cap. The seedling bent toward the
light normally. Since the glass allowed light to reach the tip, the
plant could still detect the light's direction and respond accordingly.

**Condition E — Middle covered with a lightproof tube.** The midsection
of the seedling was wrapped in a lightproof tube while the tip was left
exposed. The seedling bent toward the light normally. Blocking light
from the bending region had no effect, confirming that the midsection
does not need to "see" the light to respond.

## The Conclusion: The Plant's "Eye" Is at the Tip

The results were unambiguous. Conditions B and C — where the tip was
either removed or shielded from light — both eliminated phototropism.
Conditions D and E — where the tip could still receive light — both
showed normal bending. The Darwins concluded that the "eye" of the
plant resides at the shoot tip. The tip detects the direction of light
and sends a signal downward to the midsection, which carries out the
mechanical work of bending.
