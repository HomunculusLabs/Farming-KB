Soil is not a uniform substance but a layered system of distinct zones called
horizons. In *Teaming With Microbes*, Lowenfels and Lewis explain that
understanding soil horizons helps gardeners appreciate where the biological
activity that supports plant life actually occurs and why the top layers are so
critical. A soil profile is essentially a map of these layers, and for
gardeners, the top horizons are the ones that matter most.

## How Soil Horizons Form

Soil horizons develop as weather breaks rock down into mineral components and
[[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] accumulates from the activity of plants and animals on the
surface. Water moving down through the soil concentrates certain materials in
specific zones. Particle size causes particular materials to be filtered or
accumulated at different depths. Over time, distinct layers form, visible when
you dig a trench or examine a road cut, similar to the rock strata visible in
the walls of the Grand Canyon. Each horizon has unique physical, chemical, and
biological characteristics determined by its position and history.

## The O Horizon: Organic Layers

The O horizon sits on top of the mineral soil and consists of organic material
in various stages of decomposition. Soil scientists subdivide it further. The Oi
horizon contains organic material that can still be specifically identified as
particular plant parts, known as fibric soil.  The Oe horizon has experienced
more decay; while materials are identifiable as plant matter, you cannot tell
which specific plants they came from, even with training. This is called humic
soil. The Oa horizon is where organic material has decomposed so completely that
you cannot tell its origin at all, whether plant or animal. This is sapric soil,

## The C Horizon and Parent Material

Below the A and B horizons, the C horizon consists of partially weathered parent
rock that has not yet been significantly altered by biological activity. This
layer contains the raw mineral material from which the overlying horizons
developed. While roots rarely penetrate this deeply, weathering of the C horizon
releases [[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]] around plants introduces
both organic material and living microorganisms. Within six months, new soil
life becomes evident in the top 6 to 15 inches, and after a year it extends to
approximately 18 inches deep. The goal is to develop a deep, dark, biologically
active A horizon that supports robust plant growth. Protecting existing topsoil
from erosion, compaction, and inversion is always preferable to rebuilding it.

## Summary

Soil horizons are distinct layers formed by the interaction of weathering,
organic matter accumulation, and biological activity over time. The O and A
horizons at the surface contain the vast majority of organic matter and soil
food web organisms and are where plant roots grow. Soil color provides visual
clues about horizon conditions. Human activities like tilling, grading, and
compaction frequently damage these critical surface layers. Building and
protecting topsoil through [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] is the foundation of soil food web
gardening.
