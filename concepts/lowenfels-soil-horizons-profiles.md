Soil is not a uniform substance but a layered system of distinct zones called
horizons. In *Teaming With Microbes*, Lowenfels and Lewis explain that
understanding soil horizons helps gardeners appreciate where the biological
activity that supports plant life actually occurs and why the top layers are so
critical. A soil profile is essentially a map of these layers, and for
gardeners, the top horizons are the ones that matter most.

## How Soil Horizons Form

Soil horizons develop as weather breaks rock down into mineral components and
organic matter accumulates from the activity of plants and animals on the
surface. Water moving down through the soil concentrates certain materials in
specific zones. Particle size causes particular materials to be filtered or
accumulated at different depths. Over time, distinct layers form, visible when
you dig a trench or examine a road cut, similar to the rock strata visible in
the walls of the Grand Canyon. Each horizon has unique physical, chemical, and
biological characteristics determined by its position and history.

## The O Horizon: Organic Layers

The O horizon sits on top of the mineral soil and consists of organic material
in various stages of decomposition. Soil scientists subdivide it further. The Oi
horizon contains organic material that can still be specifically identified as
particular plant parts, known as fibric soil.  The Oe horizon has experienced
more decay; while materials are identifiable as plant matter, you cannot tell
which specific plants they came from, even with training. This is called humic
soil. The Oa horizon is where organic material has decomposed so completely that
you cannot tell its origin at all, whether plant or animal. This is sapric soil,
essentially [[lowenfels-humus-formation-benefits]].

## The A Horizon: Topsoil

The A horizon lies directly beneath the O horizon and is what gardeners commonly
call topsoil. Here, [[lowenfels-humus-formation-benefits]] particles
accumulate as water runs through the O horizon above and pulls organic particles
downward. This horizon has the highest organic matter content of any soil
horizon and the highest biological activity. Plant roots grow primarily in the A
horizon, along with the majority of [[lowenfels-bacteria-role-soil]],
[[lowenfels-fungi-role-soil]], protozoa, nematodes, and other members of
the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]]. The A horizon is where
nutrient cycling, decomposition, and the interactions between plant roots and
soil organisms are most intense.

## Subsurface Horizons

Below the A horizon, several other layers extend downward toward bedrock.  The E
horizon, where present, is a zone of leaching where minerals and organic matter
have been washed downward. The B horizon accumulates materials leached from
above, including clays, iron, and dissolved minerals. Biological activity
decreases significantly in these deeper layers, though some roots and organisms
may extend into them. The C horizon consists of partially weathered parent rock,
and below it lies the R horizon of unweathered bedrock. For gardeners, these
deeper layers are less relevant since most plant activity occurs in the top 12
to 18 inches of soil.

## Soil Color and What It Reveals

Soil color provides clues about the horizons and their conditions. Dark soils
indicate high organic matter content from humus accumulation in the O and A
horizons. Red and yellowish tints suggest the presence of iron that has
oxidized, or rusted, typically indicating good drainage and aeration.
Purple-black hues point to manganese oxide, also associated with well-drained
conditions. Gray soils can indicate a lack of organic material or anaerobic
conditions where microbes use iron and manganese, rendering them colorless. For
gardeners, dark coffee-colored soil is the ideal, reflecting adequate organic
matter and biological activity.

## Disruption of Natural Horizons

Human activity frequently disrupts natural soil horizons. [[lowenfels-tilling-no-till-soil-health]] and plowing invert the soil, burying
the organic-rich O and A horizons and bringing less fertile subsoil to the
surface. Grading and excavation during construction can remove topsoil entirely,
leaving only subsoil for landscaping. Compaction from heavy equipment destroys
the pore structure of the A horizon, reducing its ability to support biological
activity. When natural horizons are disrupted, rebuilding organic matter through
[[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]] and
[[lowenfels-mulch-cover-cropping]] applications becomes essential to
restore the biological function of the surface layers.

## The C Horizon and Parent Material

Below the A and B horizons, the C horizon consists of partially weathered parent
rock that has not yet been significantly altered by biological activity. This
layer contains the raw mineral material from which the overlying horizons
developed. While roots rarely penetrate this deeply, weathering of the C horizon
releases [[lowenfels-soil-minerals-nutrients]] that are
gradually transported upward into the zones of biological activity. Below the C
horizon lies the R horizon of unweathered bedrock, the solid rock from which all
soil ultimately derives. The mineral composition of the parent material
influences the nutrient content of the soil above it.

## Building Healthy Topsoil

Gardeners working with degraded or shallow topsoil can rebuild the A horizon by
consistently adding organic matter. Applying 0.5 to 2.5 inches of
[[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]] around plants introduces
both organic material and living microorganisms. Within six months, new soil
life becomes evident in the top 6 to 15 inches, and after a year it extends to
approximately 18 inches deep. The goal is to develop a deep, dark, biologically
active A horizon that supports robust plant growth. Protecting existing topsoil
from erosion, compaction, and inversion is always preferable to rebuilding it.

## Summary

Soil horizons are distinct layers formed by the interaction of weathering,
organic matter accumulation, and biological activity over time. The O and A
horizons at the surface contain the vast majority of organic matter and soil
food web organisms and are where plant roots grow. Soil color provides visual
clues about horizon conditions. Human activities like tilling, grading, and
compaction frequently damage these critical surface layers. Building and
protecting topsoil through organic amendments is the foundation of soil food web
gardening.
