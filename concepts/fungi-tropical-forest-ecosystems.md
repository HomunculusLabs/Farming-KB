# Fungi in Tropical Forest Ecosystems

Tropical forests harbor some of the most diverse fungal communities
on Earth, yet they remain among the least studied ecosystems for
mycological diversity. The warm, humid conditions, complex
vegetation structure, and enormous plant species richness of
tropical forests create a vast array of niches for fungi. Despite
their importance, tropical fungal diversity is poorly documented
compared to temperate regions.

## Diversity Patterns

Fungi on living plants in tropical forests include leaf parasites,
sooty molds, endophytes, and epiphyllous fungi. The phylloplane
fungi are especially conspicuous in the tropics; under humid
conditions, leaves of many plants are covered with a thick mat
of dark fungal mycelium composed largely of sooty molds. In humid
tropical forests, the litter-soil interface is often abrupt, with
species-rich litter overlying depauperate mineral soil.

Estimates suggest that a 110,000-hectare plot in Costa Rica, with
approximately 10,000 plant species, could harbor more than 81,000
species of plant-parasitic fungi, exceeding the total number of
all currently described fungal species. Similar calculations for
temperate forests yield approximately one-tenth that number.

## Canopy Fungi

The forest canopy is a critical but understudied habitat for
tropical fungi. Accessing canopy samples requires specialized
techniques including rope climbing, platform systems, and even
shooting lines through the canopy with arrows. Wolf (1993) used
a technique of shooting an arrow with a nylon rope attached
through tree crowns and then climbing with jumars to collect
epiphyte samples from multiple strata within trees.

Light intensities and fluctuations of ambient temperatures and
moisture levels are more extreme in the upper canopy than in
the sheltered lower canopy. Representative areas of each tree
must be sampled to create comprehensive lists of host-fungus
associations. Canopy soils resulting from humus accumulation on
large limbs may harbor fungal communities quite different from
those in mineral soils.

## Myxomycetes in Tropical Forests

Myxomycetes have been reported to be less abundant and diverse in
tropical forests than in temperate forests. They are uncommon in
tropical forests at the wet end of the moisture gradient. The
delicate fruiting bodies may disappear more rapidly in the tropics
because of high rainfall, high incidence of myxomyceticolous
fungi, and myxomycetophagous invertebrates. Alternatively,
tropical myxomycetes may spend more time in the trophic state
so that fruiting bodies occur with low frequency. In tropical
rainforests of Puerto Rico, the distinction between litter and
aerial microhabitats is less pronounced than in temperate
forests.

## Endophytic Fungi

Endophytic fungi are exceptionally diverse in tropical forests.
Herbaceous plants, shrubs, and trees in tropical ecosystems are
colonized by diverse communities of endophytic fungi, many of
which may be unique to tropical hosts. The high plant species
richness of tropical forests, combined with evidence of host
specificity among some endophytes, suggests that tropical
endophyte diversity may be substantially greater than that of
temperate regions.

## Soil Fungi

Less is known about characteristic assemblages of soil fungi in
humid tropical forests than in temperate regions. Aspergillus,
Penicillium, and Trichoderma species are usually abundant.
Tropical soils contain many minor or rare genera that appear
primarily tropical in distribution and commonly are associated
with overlying litter. Their rarity in mineral soils could
indicate incidental introduction. Species richness declines
rapidly with increasing depth in tropical mineral soil.

## Saprobic Fungi on Wood

Tropical forests support diverse communities of saprobic fungi
on woody substrata. For Xylariaceae and small ascomycetes in
the tropics, 3-month sampling intervals provide optimal balance
between discovery of previously unrecorded species and sampling
effort. Fungi fruiting on large-diameter wood and in the upper
canopy represent particularly understudied components of
tropical fungal diversity.

## Challenges of Tropical Mycology

Tropical mycology faces several unique challenges: difficult
access to canopy habitats, rapid decomposition of fungal
specimens in humid conditions, high diversity of hosts making
comprehensive surveys extremely labor-intensive, limited
infrastructure and expertise in many tropical countries, and
the rarity of fruiting bodies in wet tropical environments.
Molecular methods are increasingly important for detecting
fungi that do not produce visible fruiting structures.
