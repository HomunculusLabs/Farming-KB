# Social Permaculture: People Care and Community Design

updated: 2026-04-18

Social permaculture is the application of permaculture principles to human social systems. While earth care and fair share are well understood in terms of landscapes and resources, people care extends permaculture thinking to how we organize ourselves, make decisions, build communities, and create the social infrastructure that supports ecological living. After all, the most ecologically brilliant design is meaningless if it fails to meet human needs and build social resilience.

## The Third Ethic

Permaculture's three ethics are:
1. **Care of the Earth**: Provision for all life systems to continue and multiply
2. **Care of People**: Provision for people to access those resources necessary for their existence
3. **Fair Share** (or "set limits to population and consumption"): By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles

Social permaculture focuses on the second ethic while recognizing that all three ethics are interconnected. A design that cares for people must also care for the earth and distribute resources fairly.

## Invisible Structures

The world is made up of many things, some visible, some not. Invisible structures are the forces that govern the behavior of both the natural and human-made world. They are called invisible because you cannot point at them and see them as a discrete thing -- yet they manifest through physical structures and shape our lives.

Examples of invisible structures:
- **Seasons**: A natural invisible structure that manifests through snow, wind, and falling leaves
- **The 8-hour work day**: A human-made invisible structure whose concurrent physical structure is the freeway system that must absorb commuter traffic
- **Economics**: Money systems, trade patterns, and resource flows
- **Governance**: Decision-making systems, laws, and organizational structures
- **Cultural traditions**: Practices, beliefs, and social norms
- **Communication patterns**: How information flows through a community

The designer must recognize that placing a physical structure in a design will create an invisible structure around it. Every human creates an invisible structure around themselves. The permaculture designer must be able to identify invisible structures on the site, assess which are beneficial, design new ones, and ensure they and their necessary physical structures are feasible within the new design.

## Community Design

### Designing for Connection

Permaculture designs should foster human connection:
- **Central gathering spaces**: A fire circle, outdoor kitchen, or community garden as the heart of the design
- **Shared infrastructure**: Common tools, composting areas, water systems, and processing facilities
- **Edges as meeting places**: Design productive edges (between garden and forest, water and land) as places where people naturally gather
- **Zoning for social interaction**: Place shared resources and social spaces in Zone 1 where people naturally pass daily

### Decision Making

Effective community governance requires clear, fair decision-making processes:
- **Consensus**: Everyone agrees or at least does not block; slower but more inclusive
- **Sociocracy**: Consent-based decision making with defined roles and circles
- **Modified consensus**: Consensus with a fallback to supermajority vote when needed
- **Clear processes**: Whatever method is chosen, it must be clearly defined and consistently applied
- **Facilitation skills**: Good facilitation is essential for productive group processes

### Community Structures

Different structures suit different purposes:
- **Ecovillages**: Intentional communities with shared land, resources, and values
- **Housing cooperatives**: Shared ownership and management of housing
- **Community land trusts**: Community-owned land for affordable housing and agriculture
- **Transition towns**: Community-led responses to climate change and resource depletion
- **Bioregional organizations**: Communities organized around natural boundaries (watersheds) rather than political ones
- **Time banks and gift economies**: Alternative exchange systems that build social capital

## Disaster Preparedness and Community Resilience

Educated in permaculture design, local areas can organize themselves to recoup from disasters. Community resilience requires:

- **Local food production**: Multiple local food sources reduce dependence on fragile supply chains
- **Water security**: Diverse water sources (rainwater, wells, springs, dams) and purification capability
- **Energy independence**: Solar, wind, and other renewable energy systems; energy-efficient buildings
- **Communication systems**: Reliable communication that does not depend on centralized infrastructure
- **First aid and health**: Community members trained in first aid; herbal medicine and natural health knowledge
- **Shelter**: Buildings designed for passive comfort; knowledge of emergency shelter construction
- **Skills base**: Community members with diverse practical skills (building, growing, repairing, preserving)
- **Social networks**: Strong interpersonal connections that enable mutual aid and rapid coordinated response

## Bioregional Organization

Organizing communities around natural boundaries (watersheds) rather than political ones makes ecological sense. Watersheds can be as small as a city park or large enough to encompass several states. According to the US Geological Survey there are approximately 2,110 individual watersheds in the continental US, each supporting particular and specific ecological systems, or biomes.

Bioregional organization means:
- Managing resources at the watershed scale
- Making decisions that account for upstream-downstream impacts
- Building community identity around shared ecological features
- Coordinating land use across property boundaries

## Learning Exercises

1. Identify three invisible structures in your life, their parallel physical structures, and the environmental impact they have.

2. Go for a hike through a forest and try to see it through new eyes. Identify layers, succession, and ecological patterns. Then take a walk through your city and try to look at it as a forest.
