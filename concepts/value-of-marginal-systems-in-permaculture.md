# Value of Marginal Systems in Permaculture

The permaculture principle "Use Edges and Value the Marginal" extends beyond physical landscape edges to encompass social, economic, and intellectual margins. Marginal systems — those elements that only peripherally contribute to a dominant function or that exist at the boundary between domains — often serve as wellsprings of innovation, resilience, and renewal. Discarding the negative connotations of "marginal" allows us to recognise the hidden value in these overlooked systems.

## Urban Edge Examples

### The Urban-Rural Fringe

The urban sprawl, lamented by generations of urban planners, is driven by the constant search for the edge between town and country, between the human and natural worlds. The edge of suburbia, with its open paddocks and abandoned spaces, has been a great place to grow up for generations of Australians, but it is inevitably destroyed as the suburban monoculture takes over. Suburban landscapes like Sydney's North Shore have always been attractive real estate because topographic constraints retained a network of escarpment bush and harbour inlets through the suburban fabric.

### The Shopfront as Edge

In city retail centres, shopfront is a limited resource in great demand — the interface between the public domain of the street and the private stores of goods for sale. As city retailing intensified, the development of arcades and redevelopment of service lanes increased the length of edge where exchange could take place. Today's shopping mall has captured and enclosed the edge so that the whole character of the previously public street domain is managed and manipulated to maximise the exchange of money for goods.

## Classic Permaculture Edge Designs

### Keyhole Garden Beds

[[keyhole-garden-beds]] increase the accessible edge for a given area of path and bed, maximising the growing area reachable from a single access point.

### Pond and Dam Design

Ponds and dams built with a sinuous edge of peninsulas, marshy flats and islands have greater total biological productivity than those with a simple shoreline. Greater edge increases the yield of farmed fish by providing more habitat and feeding surface for aquatic organisms on which fish feed.

### Shelterbelts and Hedgerows

Shelterbelts and hedgerows are traditional farm landscape examples of edge. In high-fertility arable soils with middling rainfall, and lower-rainfall pastoral farming areas, continuous forest may be unsuccessful or a poor use of resources. The most effective revegetation is often linear (not necessarily straight) belts of trees. If these belts are deep enough (five or more rows) to provide for forest edge functions and species, they optimise passive functions and yields from trees without the disadvantages of fence-to-fence reforestation.

### Agroforestry and Alley Farming

Wide-spaced tree systems (agroforestry) where tree-growing is completely integrated with cropping or grazing face several problems: most trees prefer to establish in self-sheltering dense young stands; establishing good timber form in wide-spaced trees is difficult; and inter-row spacing dictates equipment types. These problems have naturally led to alley farming and shelterbelt forestry systems where trees are concentrated in belts but strong beneficial interaction between trees and cropland remains.

Planting shrubs along the edges of, rather than throughout, plantations offers multiple advantages: reduced branching problems on the first row of timber trees, sheltered forest floor against wind and fire, and prolonged healthy shrub growth with better access to sunlight and moisture. This edge design actually mimics the development of natural forest edges once tree canopy reduces light available for understorey shrubs.

### Designed Edge as Appropriate Segregation

The balance between integration and segregation of system elements is a fine one. Often a design that allows the separate nature of system elements to be fully expressed will be more successful than complete integration. The carefully designed edge created by retained timber belts — proposed by P.A. Yeomans and demonstrated by progressive farmers in the 1950s — represented appropriate segregation of grazing animals and trees, and has stood the test of time.

## Kakadu National Park: A Lesson in Edge Loss

Fire management in [[kakadu-national-park]] provides an example of the consequences of failing to consider edge effects. During the 1990s, park management burnt about half the park every year. Falls in small mammal numbers were noted in 1993 surveys and accelerated dramatically by 1999. Burning vast areas in one year creates a monoculture reflecting concepts applied at too large a scale to maintain edges between burnt and unburnt savannah.

A more appropriate approach would burn only in the early dry season, so only the driest parts of the landscape would burn. In successive seasons, the same areas would tend to burn, and over time edges between burnt and unburnt areas would become self-reinforcing as ecological drift increased differences between adjacent areas. This use of edge on a grand scale would mimic traditional Aboriginal management.

## Wild Foods and Marginal Productivity

In pre-industrial Europe, woods and hedgerows provided not only fuel and structural materials but also animal fodder, human food (berries, hazelnuts, rabbits, mushrooms), and medicinal herbs. This diversity was critical for the poorest people and during times of famine, whole communities depended on it for survival.

The cultural bias that ignores or undervalues the wild is not restricted to modern affluent society. In many peasant societies, wild diversity was denigrated because its value was greatest for the poor. Taste memory allows negative attitudes to persist for a lifetime — many Australians who consumed rabbit during the Great Depression never saw it as flavoursome meat. At the other end of the spectrum, wild foods have gained special status in affluent urban society due to rarity, reconnection to nature, concerns about industrial agriculture, and high cost.

## Rundown Neighbourhoods as Innovation Incubators

[[jane-jacobs]] identified that rundown urban neighbourhoods provided cheap rents in old warehouses, shops and houses where small start-up businesses could establish themselves. Elimination of these areas by urban renewal programs in the 1950s and 1960s killed the economic, artistic, and cultural life of American cities. There is a tension between the "tidy up" mentality that wants to recycle everything and the mindset that values leaving things be to see what emerges.

## The City-Hinterland Relationship

For thousands of years, the city and its institutions distinguished civilisation from simpler cultures. Over most of that time, the relationship between city and hinterland was that of nucleus to cell — exploitation existed but had limits because dependence on the hinterland was never completely overcome. Today the city has become conceptually the whole; rural life, farming and nature are a surrounding membrane or fringe that continues to wither as every aspect of nature becomes controlled from the city.

Small regional economies and all small towns are deemed non-viable by economic orthodoxy. On a global scale, the relationship between rich and poor countries has led to hinterlands being seen as dispensable. However, the inspiration, examples and wisdom for dealing with the environmental crisis come not from the centre but from the margins, where people live at the edge between culture and nature, between modernity and the past.

## Permaculture's Origins at the Margin

Permaculture emerged from Tasmania — a place where modernity and nature are in close proximity, far enough from dominant paradigms to allow independent thinking, yet with the benefits of modern education and relative affluence. From the property on Mount Wellington's footslopes where the Permaculture concept was born in the mid-1970s, one could travel 5 kilometres to the city centre or university in one direction, or 5 kilometres on walking tracks to the alpine southwest face with nothing manmade between you and the great wilderness.

This pattern repeats: Tasmania had the world's first green political party, and was where organic agriculture first grew from isolated farmers to a vibrant network. New Zealand, with its even smaller population, has an even stronger record of innovation. In places like Tasmania, New Zealand and Denmark, the small scale of political and social institutions gives those with radical ideas the feeling that they can make a difference.

## Ideas and Innovation at the Fringe

The university as an institution is losing its grip on intellectual culture more than 2,500 years after Pythagoras founded the first university. Factors include devaluation of undergraduate education, failure to develop holistic integration across disciplines, replacement of scholarly community with corporate culture, and new communications technology that gives those at the fringes more equal access to information and ideas.

[[james-lovelock]], the independent atmospheric physicist who co-originated the Gaia hypothesis from his cottage laboratory in Cornwall, provides one model of the new scientist no longer tied to the city, university, discipline, or funding institutions. Wendell Berry described modern agriculture as controlled by orthodoxy characterised by progressive contraction around increasingly limited notions of what is productive and economic. Around this orthodoxy, a "widening margin of divergent possibilities" exists to be explored. Permaculture, organic agriculture, and the broader countercultural movement all represent those divergent possibilities emerging from the margins.
