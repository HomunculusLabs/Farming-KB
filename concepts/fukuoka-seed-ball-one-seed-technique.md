# Fukuoka Seed Ball One Seed Technique

Updated: 2026-04-19

Masanobu Fukuoka developed seed ball techniques that place one seed per clay pellet for precision in no-tillage natural farming. This is a refinement of his basic [[fukuoka-seed-ball-technique]] method.

The seed ball technique is central to Fukuoka's system of [[fukuoka-natural-rice-barley-cultivation]] and [[fukuoka-no-tillage-direct-seeding-history]], enabling autumn sowing of rice that overwinters in the field without pesticide protection.

## The One-Seed Method

1. Place seeds moistened with water in a bamboo basket or concrete mixer

2. Sprinkle with clay powder while spraying a fine water mist with an atomizer

3. Move the basket in a swirling motion so seeds become coated with clay and grow larger

4. The result is small pellets about a quarter- to half-inch in size

This produces individually coated seeds, compared to the simpler method of mixing seed with five- to ten-fold clay and forming larger pellets containing four to five seeds each.

Fukuoka describes the process: "To prepare one-seed pellets, place the seed moistened with water in a bamboo basket or a mixer. Sprinkle the seed with clay powder while spraying water mist onto the mixture with an atomizer and moving the basket in a swirling motion. The seeds will become coated with clay and grow larger in size."

## Advantages of One-Seed Pellets

- More precise seed placement and spacing

- Reduced seed waste

- Better protection of each individual seed

- More predictable germination rates

- Easier to calculate seeding rates

## Larger-Scale Production

For field-scale production, a concrete mixer can substitute for the bamboo basket. The swirling action of the mixer replicates the hand-motion coating process at larger scale. "When a large quantity of pellets is to be prepared, one alternative is to do this with a concrete mixer."

## Quality Control

The key to good one-seed pellets is even coating thickness. Too thin and the pellet crumbles prematurely, exposing the seed to predation by birds and rodents. Too thick and moisture may not penetrate in time for spring germination. Pure clay or red earth provides better coating quality than soil-clay mixes that contain topsoil.

Fukuoka warns: "Topsoil-containing clay may also be used to form the pellets, but if the pellets crumble too early in spring, the seed will be devoured by rodents and other pests." This is critical for overwintering rice seed in the field.

## Materials

- Seeds of desired crop varieties

- Fine clay powder or well-crushed red earth

- Water with atomizer for fine misting

- Bamboo basket or concrete mixer for tumbling

## The Multi-Seed Method

The simpler alternative method is described as: "Mix the seeds in at least a five- to ten-fold quantity of well-crushed clay or red earth, add water, and knead until hard by treading. Pass the kneaded mixture through a half-inch screen and dry for a half-day, then shape the clay mixture into half-inch pellets by rolling with the hands or in a mixer."

There may be several (4-5) seeds in each pellet, but with experience this can be brought closer to the ideal of one seed per pellet. Fukuoka developed the one-seed technique only after years of working with the multi-seed method.

## Role in Eliminating Pesticides

The seed ball technique plays a crucial role in Fukuoka's system because it eliminates the need for chemical pesticide coatings. Conventionally, overwintering rice seed required a coating of pesticide and synthetic resin for protection against winter damage by rodents and other pests. Fukuoka's next step was to eliminate the need for this protectant, which he accomplished by enclosing seed in clay pellets.

See [[fukuoka-pest-balance-without-chemicals]] and [[fukuoka-five-principles-natural-farming]].

## Seasonal Considerations

Seeds enclosed in clay pellets can be scattered in autumn and will overwinter in the field. The pellets protect against frost, moisture loss, and animal predation until spring conditions trigger germination. The weight of the clay ensures pellets contact the soil surface rather than being blown away.

The timing connects to the [[fukuoka-natural-rice-barley-cultivation]] and [[fukuoka-winter-grain-rice-rotation]]. Rice seed enclosed in clay pellets is scattered over the field in mid-November or later, completing the sowing of rice and barley for the coming year.

## Connection to Desert Greening

The seed ball technique connects to the broader practice used in Fukuoka's desert greening projects. He proposed that "the seeds of certain plants be sown over the deserts in clay pellets to help green these barren lands." The pellets would contain seeds of green manure trees such as black wattle, clover, alfalfa, bur clover, grain, and vegetable seeds.

Once scattered, the seeds within the hard clay pellets will not sprout until rain has fallen and conditions are just right for germination. Nor will they be eaten by mice and birds. See [[natural-farming-desert-reclamation]].

## Integration with the Rice-Barley System

In the [[fukuoka-rice-barley-step-by-step-method]], seed balls are scattered directly over the straw mulch after rice harvest. The combination of straw moisture retention and clay pellet protection creates ideal conditions for winter survival and spring germination.

Fukuoka describes his practice: "I form clay pellets containing seed rice and scatter the pellets over the straw before the New Year. With the winter grain growing and the rice seed sown, there is now nothing left to do until the harvesting of the winter grain."

## Merged: Seed Saving and Natural Seed
Masanobu Fukuoka placed great importance on seed saving as a practice of [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]]. For him, seeds were not merely agricultural inputs but living expressions of the ecosystem in which they were grown. This technique was central to his [[fukuoka-rice-barley-no-till-method]]. The clay pellet technique embodies the [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] -- nothingness. The [[fukuoka-barley-clover-cultivation]] that Fukuoka practiced depended entirely on locally adapted seeds. ## Merged: Fukuoka: Seed Balls (Clay Pellets) for Natural Revegetation
[[natural-farming-seed-balls]] (also called clay pellets or earth balls) are one of Fukuoka's most distinctive and widely-adapted innovations. They are small balls of clay (roughly the size of a marble or a cherry tomato) containing seeds of various plants mixed into the clay matrix. The vegetables grow up through the [[fukuoka-white-clover-ground-cover]] and weed cover, creating a diverse, self-seeding vegetable garden that requires no planting or cultivation. Fukuoka proposed using seed balls for large-scale [[fukuoka-green-gulch-zen-center-desert-reclamation]]. The [[fukuoka-sowing-seed-ball-desert-revegetation]] proposal to the United Nations was based on this concept.
