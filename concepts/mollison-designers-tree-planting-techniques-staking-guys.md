# Tree Planting Techniques: Staking, Guy Wires, and Aftercare

Bill Mollison devotes considerable attention in Permaculture: A Designer's Manual to the
correct methods for establishing trees, emphasizing that most tree failures in
permaculture systems result from poor planting technique rather than species
selection. The goal is to establish a deep, well-anchored root system that
minimizes long-term dependency on human intervention.

## Site Preparation and Hole Digging

Before planting, assess the site's drainage, soil depth, and microclimate. On
slopes, plant on the contour to intercept water flow and reduce erosion. For
windbreak plantings, stagger rows to improve turbulence reduction.

The planting hole should be wide rather than deep. Mollison recommends digging
the hole two to three times the width of the root ball and only as deep as the
root ball itself or slightly shallower to prevent settling. A wide hole
encourages lateral root exploration into the surrounding soil rather than
circling within a narrow pit. On compacted or degraded sites, break up the
sides and bottom of the hole to prevent glazing (a smooth, impermeable surface
created by digging tools in heavy clay).

For bareroot trees, create a mound of soil at the bottom center of the hole to
spread roots over. For container-grown stock, gently tease out circling roots
before placement. Never bend or cram roots to fit the hole.

## Planting Depth

Planting depth is critical. The root collar (the junction between trunk and
roots) must remain at or slightly above the soil surface. Planting too deep
is the single most common cause of tree mortality -- it deprives roots of
oxygen, encourages bark rot, and suppresses the development of trunk flare
roots that provide structural stability. Mollison stresses that on heavy,
poorly drained soils, planting slightly high (2-5 cm above grade) is preferable
to risking root suffocation.

Backfill with the same soil removed from the hole. Avoid filling with organic
matter or compost in the planting hole itself, as this creates a "pot effect"
where roots refuse to leave the enriched zone. Reserve compost and organic
amendments for the surface mulch layer.

## Staking and Guy Wires

Mollison advises against unnecessary staking. Trees allowed to sway freely
develop stronger, more tapered trunks and better root anchorage. However,
staking is warranted on exposed, windy sites, for top-heavy specimens, or
where vandalism or animal damage is likely.

When staking is necessary, use two or three stakes placed outside the root
ball. Attach the tree with flexible ties (rubber, canvas, or tree tape) at the
lowest point that provides stability. The tie should allow the trunk to move
several centimeters in any direction. Remove stakes after one growing season
(12-18 months). Prolonged staking weakens trees and can cause girdling injury.

Guy wires are appropriate for larger specimen trees or trees planted on steep
slopes. Anchor guy wires to deadmen (buried logs or concrete blocks) or ground
anchors driven into undisturbed soil beyond the root zone. Protect the trunk
from wire contact using rubber hose or commercial tree guying cushions. Guy
wires should be slackened after the first year and removed within two to three
years.

## Mulch Rings

Apply a mulch ring extending from near the trunk to the drip line or beyond.
Use coarse organic materials -- wood chips, straw, leaf mold, or shredded bark.
Mollison recommends a layer 8-15 cm deep. Keep mulch 5-10 cm away from the
trunk to prevent bark rot and rodent damage. Mulch rings suppress competing
weeds, conserve soil moisture, moderate soil temperature, and feed soil
organisms that in turn support the tree's mycorrhizal network.

On degraded or eroded sites, consider installing a [[mollison-designers-terracing-slope-stabilization]]
micro-terrace or swale above the planting to direct water toward the root zone.

## Watering Schedule

Newly planted trees require consistent moisture during establishment, which
typically spans one to three years depending on species, climate, and site
conditions. Mollison recommends deep, infrequent watering over shallow, frequent
irrigation. Deep watering encourages roots to grow downward into the subsoil.

During the first month after planting, water every 2-3 days in the absence of
rain. Gradually extend intervals to once per week, then once every two weeks as
roots establish. In arid climates, use deep pipe irrigation (a vertical perforated
pipe placed alongside the root ball) to deliver water directly to the root zone
with minimal surface evaporation.

Drought-hardy species and those planted on [[swale-design-construction]] may
need supplemental water only during extreme dry periods after the first season.

## Aftercare and Protection

After planting, form a shallow soil berm around the edge of the mulch ring to
retain water. Inspect ties regularly for girdling. Protect young trunks from
rabbits, hares, and livestock using tree guards or wire mesh cylinders. In
frost-prone areas, consider trunk wrapping or planting frost-hardy nurse trees
to moderate microclimate extremes.

[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] beneath and around young trees (a [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]]
approach) provides nitrogen fixation, pest confusion, and dynamic nutrient
accumulation. Leguminous groundcovers, comfrey, and aromatic herbs are
particularly valuable nurse associates.

Mollison emphasizes that the first three years after planting are the
establishment phase. After this, well-sited trees in a designed permaculture
system should be largely self-maintaining, requiring only periodic pruning and
harvest management.

## Common Mistakes

- Planting too deep (most frequent error)
- Over-watering or watering too shallowly
- Leaving stakes or guy wires in place too long
- Filling the planting hole with rich compost instead of native soil
- Failing to break up compacted soil on hole sides and bottom
- Placing mulch directly against the trunk
- Ignoring wind exposure and failing to provide temporary shelter
