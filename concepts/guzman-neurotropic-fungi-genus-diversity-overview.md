
# Neurotropic Fungi — Genus Diversity and Taxonomic Overview

## Overview

Neurotropic fungi are fungi capable of altering the central nervous system, producing changes in perception, mood, and cognition. The landmark 1998/2000 paper by Gastón Guzmán, John W. Allen, and Jochen Gartz provided the most comprehensive global survey of these organisms, cataloging **216 known species** across more than a dozen genera. The genus is notable for its robust basidiocarps and the rusty-orange spore print that distinguishes it from *Psilocybe*.

### Panaeolus (Fr.) Quél. — 13 species

Coprophilous mushrooms found worldwide on herbivore dung. The genus is cosmopolitan, with species appearing wherever livestock or wild herbivores are present. *[[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]* and *Panaeolus cyanescens* are among the best-known neurotropic species in this genus. They are generally small, fragile mushrooms that fruit prolifically after rains. The genus is distinguished by its mottled gills, a feature caused by the simultaneous maturation of basidiospores in patches rather than evenly along the gill surface.

### Copelandia Bres. — 12 species

Often treated as a section or subgenus of *Panaeolus*, *Copelandia* is recognized by Guzmán and others at the genus level. These are tropical and subtropical dung-inhabiting fungi distinguished by their strongly bluing reaction when bruised and their relatively high psilocybin content. *Copelandia cyanescens* is one of the most potent psilocybin-containing mushrooms known. The genus is particularly diverse in Southeast Asia and has been widely documented in Thailand, Cambodia, and Indonesia, where it fruits abundantly in pastures and buffalo fields during the rainy season.

### Hypholoma (Fr.) P. Kumm. — 6 species

Wood-inhabiting fungi, some of which have been confirmed to contain psilocybin. These species are typically found on decaying logs and stumps. Neurotropic members of this genus are less well known than those of *Psilocybe* or *Panaeolus*, and their distribution is more restricted.

### Pluteus Fr. — 6 species

A genus of wood-inhabiting saprotrophs that includes several psilocybin-containing species, primarily in temperate forests. *[[gartz-pluteus-salicinus-wood-inhabiting-psilocybin]]* is the most widely recognized neurotropic member of this genus. The free gills and pink spore print distinguish *Pluteus* from many other wood-inhabiting genera. Neurotropic species tend to be found on decaying hardwood logs and stumps in moist, shaded environments.

### Inocybe (Fr.) Fr. — 6 species

A large genus of ectomycorrhizal fungi, most of which are NOT neurotropic and some of which are dangerously toxic (containing muscarine). However, at least six species have been confirmed to contain psilocybin or related tryptamines. The presence of psilocybin in a mycorrhizal genus is noteworthy, as most other neurotropic fungi are saprotrophic. This ecological distinction raises interesting evolutionary questions about the selective pressures that might favor psilocybin production in different nutritional modes.

### Conocybe (Fr.) P. Kumm. — 4 species

Small, fragile, conical-capped mushrooms found in grasslands, dung, and disturbed soil. Neurotropic *Conocybe* species are easily overlooked due to their diminutive stature. They are found in both temperate and tropical regions. The genus includes some species containing deadly amatoxins alongside those with psilocybin, making identification especially critical.

### Panaeolina (Maire) Maire — 4 species

Closely related to *Panaeolus*, this genus contains several species confirmed to produce psilocybin. *[[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]]* (the haymaker's mushroom) is common in lawns worldwide, though its psilocybin content is generally low and variable.

## Additional Genera with Reported Psychoactive Properties

Beyond the nine major genera listed above, Guzmán, Allen & Gartz noted additional genera with sporadic reports of neurotropic activity or inconclusive chemical analyses. These include *Mycena* (several species reported to contain psilocybin or related indoles, though results have been inconsistent), *Stropharia* (closely related to *Psilocybe*, with *S. cubensis* now reclassified as *Psilocybe cubensis*), and *Agrocybe* (with rare reports of psilocybin-like compounds).

The total number of genera implicated in neurotropic activity continues to grow as analytical methods improve, but many reports require independent verification before species can be confidently placed in Group 1. The phylogenetic distribution of psilocybin across such diverse lineages within the Agaricales strongly suggests multiple independent evolutionary origins of this biosynthetic pathway, a finding that has been supported by subsequent molecular phylogenetic studies.

## Problems of Identification and Toxic Confusion

One of the most serious practical concerns in mycology is the potential for confusion between neurotropic fungi and toxic look-alikes. Species in genera such as *Galerina* (which contains deadly amatoxins) can closely resemble *Psilocybe* species in the field. *Galerina marginata*, for example, grows in similar wood-inhabiting habitats as *Psilocybe cyanescens* and has been responsible for fatal poisonings when misidentified. Similarly, *Inocybe* species that contain muscarine rather than psilocybin can cause serious poisoning characterized by sweating, salivation, and gastrointestinal distress. Guzmán, Allen & Gartz emphasized that accurate identification requires [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]], including spore morphology, pileipellis structure, and the presence or absence of chrysocystidia.
