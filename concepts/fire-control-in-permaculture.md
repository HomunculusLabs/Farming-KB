## Fire Periodicity

In areas with 30-40 inches of rainfall, catastrophic fires occur approximately every 25-30 years. The critical insight is that fire modifies its own periodicity: "The less a site burns, the less it is likely to burn, because there will be more humus and more moisture incorporated into the site. On the other hand, the more it burns, the more likely it is to burn again soon." Fire removes moisture-retaining humus and kills more than it consumes, creating a fire-prone litter build-up.

The fire regime can shift dramatically. Areas that naturally burn every thirtieth year may burn every eighth or tenth year once shorter fire intervals are established. Greece, once covered in wet rainforests with enormous oak and columnar beeches, has been reduced to a "skeleton of its former self" through repeated burning until "the dirt is burned, the plants are burned, the hills are burned, the rocks slip down hill and you can't burn rocks."

## Radiation: The Real Killer

The primary danger of fire is not flame contact but radiation. Mollison provides specific distances:
- **400 feet** before a fire: hair catches alight
- **200 feet**: body starts to split and fat catches alight
- **100 feet**: a person becomes a torch

Radiation kills birds hundreds of feet from the fire front. Goats survive well by leaning into it. The design imperative is to create "fire shadows" -- areas shielded from radiation by earth banks, dams, and high-water-content vegetation.

## Defensive Sequences

Fire defense in permaculture involves multiple layered strategies:

1. **Reduce fuel**: The primary strategy. Create non-fuel surfaces (roads, ponds, swales), use pit mulching, and maintain grazing animals to reduce standing litter.

2. **Plant fire-resistant species**: Select plants with high ash content, high water content, low total bulk, and dense growth. Ice plants and thick-leaf evergreens with rapidly decomposing litter are preferred. Avoid eucalypts and pines -- both are "fire weeds" that carry cones opening only after fire.

3. **Create fire shadows**: Earth banks, dams with earth walls, and high-water-content trees (willows, poplars) that throw up steam clouds block radiation. "You can stand behind a Coprosma and you don't even feel the fire, just a hot steam bath."

4. **Gravity-flow water systems**: "A characteristic of fire on site is that you have no water unless you have gravity flow." Electrical pumps fail first in fires. Place [[permaculture-earthworks-design]].

## Fire Shelters

For high-risk sites, Mollison recommends digging T-shaped or L-shaped pits and earthing them over, or using road conduit buried in an earth bank. These provide radiation shelter where residents can wait out the fire front. The shelter should be at the back of the house, on the downhill side, about six feet deep, with water available. "Jumping in the pond is a no-no" -- oxygen is depleted in heated water.

## Landscape-Scale Fire Design

Fire defense works from valleys upward. Valleys naturally accumulate moisture and support less flammable vegetation. By re-establishing valley species and planting fire-resistant vegetation on slopes, the landscape progressively becomes more fire-resistant. Swales covered with old carpet for rapid decomposition, combined with dense low-fuel plantings, create firebreaks that integrate with the water management system.
