# Wildlife Habitat Creation

Creating wildlife habitat on the homestead supports biodiversity, natural pest control, pollination, soil health, and the ecological resilience of the land. A homestead designed with wildlife in mind functions as an integrated ecosystem rather than a monoculture, with each species playing a role in the food web. Habitat creation involves providing food, water, shelter, and space -- the four fundamental needs of wildlife.

## Food Plots

Food plots are designated areas planted specifically to provide food for wildlife, particularly game species, songbirds, and pollinators.

### Designing Food Plots

- Size: Even small plots (1/4 acre) provide significant benefit. Larger plots (1-3 acres) are better for deer and turkey.
- Location: Edge habitats (where forest meets field) are most productive. Place plots where they connect existing cover.
- Shape: Long, irregular shapes provide more edge habitat than square plots.
- Diversity: Plant multiple species for year-round food availability.

### Food Plot Species

Warm season annuals:
- Soybeans: High protein for deer. Plant after last frost.
- Cowpeas: Heat-tolerant, attractive to deer and quail.
- Sunflowers: Seed production for songbirds. Plant in late spring.
- Buckwheat: Quick-growing soil builder, attracts pollinators and game birds.

Cool season annuals:
- Winter wheat and rye: Provide winter forage and green browse.
- Crimson clover: Nitrogen-fixing, excellent for deer and pollinators.
- Brassicas (radish, turnip): Late-season food source. Leaves browsed in fall, roots dug in winter.

Perennials:
- White and [[red-clover]]: Long-lived, provides years of forage.
- Chicory: Deep-rooted, drought-tolerant, high protein.
- Alfalfa: Excellent forage but requires well-drained soil.

## Native Plantings

Native plants are the foundation of wildlife habitat because they co-evolved with local insects, birds, and mammals. Native plantings support 10-50 times more pollinator species than non-native ornamentals.

### Selecting Native Plants

Choose species native to your specific ecoregion. Key categories:

- Flowering perennials: Milkweed (monarch butterflies), coneflower, black-eyed Susan, asters, goldenrod, Joe-Pye weed, bee balm. These provide nectar and pollen for pollinators.
- Native grasses: Little bluestem, big bluestem, switchgrass, Indian grass. Provide nesting cover, winter cover, and seed.
- Native shrubs: Elderberry, serviceberry, viburnum, chokecherry, dogwood, hazelnut. Provide berries, cover, and nesting sites.
- Native trees: Oak (supports 500+ caterpillar species), cherry, willow, birch, maple, pine. Provide food, cover, and nesting.
- Fruit-bearing plants: American persimmon, [[pawpaw]], mulberry, blackberry, raspberry, wild plum.

### Establishing Native Plantings

- Start with locally sourced seed or plants from native plant nurseries
- Prepare site by removing existing vegetation (smother with cardboard, solarize, or lightly till)
- Seed in fall (dormant seeding) or early spring
- Use a nurse crop of annual rye or oats to prevent erosion while perennials establish
- Mow annually for the first 2-3 years at 12 inches to control weeds without killing seedlings
- Do not fertilize native plantings -- they are adapted to poor soils
- Be patient: native perennials spend their first year establishing roots, with minimal above-ground growth

## Shelter [[permaculture-wildlife-corridors|Corridor]]s

Wildlife corridors are strips of vegetation that connect larger habitat areas, allowing animals to move safely between feeding, nesting, and resting areas. On the homestead, corridors connect woodlots, wetlands, food plots, and the [[food-forest-design]].

### Hedgerows

A hedgerow is a linear planting of trees and shrubs that functions as a living fence, windbreak, and wildlife corridor.

Design principles:
- Width: Minimum 15-20 feet for adequate cover
- Composition: 5-10 species in mixed rows (deciduous and evergreen)
- Layers: Ground cover, shrub layer, understory trees, canopy trees
- Plant densely (4-6 foot spacing)
- Include thorny species (hawthorn, wild rose, blackberry) for nesting protection

### Windbreaks

Windbreaks protect livestock, crops, and buildings from wind while providing wildlife habitat.

- Plant 2-5 rows of trees and shrubs on the windward side
- Row 1 (windward): Dense, low shrubs (dogwood, serviceberry)
- Row 2: Medium trees (cedar, spruce)
- Row 3: Tall deciduous trees (oak, maple)
- Spacing: 6-8 feet within rows, 10-15 feet between rows
- Protect from livestock until established (fencing or tree tubes)

## Ponds and Water Features

Water is the most limiting resource for wildlife. Even a small water feature dramatically increases habitat value.

### Farm Pond

- Size: 1/4 acre to 1 acre provides excellent habitat
- Depth: Minimum 8-10 feet in the deepest part to prevent winter kill and summer overheating
- Shelves: Sloped edges with shallow shelves (0-2 feet) for emergent vegetation and amphibian breeding
- Edges: Leave vegetated buffer (minimum 30 feet) around the pond to filter runoff and provide cover
- Stocking: Native fish species ([[bluegill]], bass) provide food for herons, otters, and raptors
- Attract: Waterfowl, amphibians, dragonflies, mammals (deer, raccoons)

### Smaller Water Features

- Vernal pools: Seasonal shallow pools that fill in spring and dry by summer. Critical for amphibian breeding. Dig a shallow depression (6-18 inches deep) in a low area.
- Wildlife guzzlers: Simple water troughs with ramps for small animals to escape.
- Birdbaths: Shallow (1-2 inches deep) with a rough surface for grip. Change water every 2-3 days.

## Nesting Boxes

Artificial nesting structures supplement natural cavities, which are often limited in young forests and managed landscapes.

### Bluebird Boxes

- Entrance hole: 1.5 inches diameter
- Box dimensions: 5x5 inch floor, 8-12 inches deep
- Mount 4-6 feet high on a pole (not a tree -- easier to predator-proof)
- Face east or southeast
- Install predator guard (cone or stovepipe baffle)
- Monitor weekly during nesting season

### Bat Houses

- Bats consume 500-1000 mosquitoes per night per bat
- Multi-chamber design, 2 feet tall or larger
- Mount 12-20 feet high on a pole or building
- Face south or southeast for warmth
- Paint dark color in cool climates, light color in hot climates
- Install near water if possible (attracts more insects)

### Other Nesting Structures

- Wood duck boxes: Mounted over water, 4-6 feet above surface
- Owl boxes: Large cavity boxes, mounted 15-30 feet high
- Kestrel/screech owl boxes: 8-inch entrance hole, mounted 10-30 feet high
- Mason bee houses: Bundles of hollow stems or drilled wood blocks for native pollinators
- Brush piles: Stacked branches and brush provide cover for rabbits, songbirds, and reptiles

## Invasive Species Management

Invasive plants degrade habitat by outcompeting natives and providing poor food value for wildlife. Common invasives to control:

- Japanese honeysuckle: Smothers native vegetation. Cut and treat stumps.
- Autumn olive: Aggressive shrub that displaces natives. Cut and treat stumps.
- Multiflora rose: Forms impenetrable thickets. Mow or treat.
- Garlic mustard: Outcompetes spring wildflowers. Pull by hand before seed set.
- Oriental bittersweet: Strangles trees. Cut vines and treat stumps.
