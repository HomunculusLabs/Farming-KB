# Fire-Resistant Housing Design

In fire-prone regions, the design and siting of buildings is the single most important factor determining survival during [[wildfire]]. Fire-resistant design integrates building architecture, landscaping, water systems, and [[emergency-preparedness]] into a comprehensive survival strategy.

## House Siting Principles

The location of a house relative to topography dramatically affects its fire risk. Houses are at greatest danger from upslope fire, and certain positions are exceptionally hazardous:

### Dangerous Sites (Avoid)
- **Sharp ridge tops**: Fully exposed to fire from multiple directions
- **Hill saddles with diverging ridgelines**: Create a wind funnel effect that intensifies fire
- **Driveways lined with inflammable trees** (eucalypts) or grasses (pampas grass): Act as blowtorch-shaped funnels directing fire toward the house
- **Houses raised on stilts or stumps on downslopes**: Expose the underfloor area to fire

### Safe Sites (Preferred, in Order of Safety)
1. **Damp valley mouths**: Natural moisture reduces fire intensity
2. **Well-tended built-up areas**: Collective fuel management provides protection
3. **Farms with flood-flow or Keyline irrigation**: Water availability for fire suppression
4. **Irrigated areas**: Constant moisture reduces fire risk
5. **Peninsulas in dams and lakes**: Surrounded by natural firebreaks
6. **Plateau sites with rigorous design and maintenance**: Protected by earthworks and fire-damping vegetation

### Optimal Hillside Placement
For houses on slopes, the ideal approach is to:
- Site the house **off the ridge** and on **downslope plateaus**
- **Excavate the site** into the hillside rather than building on stilts
- Create a **nested shelf** on the hill protected from radiation
- Eliminate open underfloor areas
- Install a **rimwall, pond, or earthbank** on the plateau edge

## Building Design for Fire Resistance

### Roof and Wall Design
- **Simple outlines only**: No internal roof valleys or re-entrant wall corners that can trap incandescent ash
- **No tarpaper roof linings** projecting into gutters
- **Roof gutters** should have leaf-free profiles or be pluggable and water-fillable during fire events (plugs chained to gutters near downpipes)
- **Thatch and shingle roofs** must be replaced by tile or metal cladding in fire-prone areas (mandatory by law in some districts)
- **White paint** on exterior walls and roof areas significantly reduces radiation absorption

### Ventilation and Openings
- All windows must be **screened with fine metal mesh** to reduce ember entry
- **Underfloor vent spaces** need fine-mesh metal screens
- **Wall cavity vents** must be screened
- Unsreened openings allow large embers to enter and ignite interior materials, including bedding and stored firewood

### Surroundings and Storage
- **No inflammable door mats** near entrances
- **No wood piles or shrubs** against house walls
- **Fuel and explosive materials** (petrol cans, etc.) stored in a separate shed, tightly lidded, away from the house
- **Firewood and fuel liquids** must not be stored in cellars or underfloor spaces

### Insulation
Fire-proof or slow-to-burn insulation materials help keep interiors cool during fire passage:
- Mineral wool
- Seagrass
- Sawdust
- Feathers
- Wool

Wooden panelling transmits relatively little heat, while stone, brick, and mud may convey heat indoors unless of sufficient thickness to absorb and dissipate it.

## Firebreak and Fuel Reduction Zones

A critical requirement around all buildings is the creation of a **30-meter (100-foot) fuel-reduced zone**. This does not mean bare ground or tree removal — rather, it involves strategic planting of fire-dampening species:

### Recommended Trees and Shrubs (within 30m zone)
- **Coprosma** species
- **Deciduous fruit trees** (apples, pears, peaches, etc.)
- **Figs**
- **Willows**
- **Poplars**
- **Fern-leaved [[acacia]]** (sacrificial hedge — fire-killed but rejuvenates from roots)

### Avoid Near Buildings
- Olives (high oil content)
- Pines (high resin content)
- Eucalypts (extremely inflammable oils)

### Ground Cover Plants (fire-dampening)
- **Lilies**: [[agapanthus]], spring bulbs, arum, Canna
- **Summer-green ground covers**: Comfrey, iceplants, Tradescantia, Impatiens
- **Short grass sward**
- **Succulent groundcovers and lily clumps**
- **Succulent vines** interspersed with crops

### Forest Management for Fire Safety
In forested settings near dwellings, annual maintenance is essential:
- Remove all **lower limbs** from trees to prevent fire "crowning"
- Strip **loose bark** that can carry fire into the canopy
- Clear **twigs, fallen leaves, dry brush, dry grass, and dead stumps**
- Chop cleared material and stoneweight it into **hollows and swales**, or use as cooking fuel
- The tall tree stems that remain will save the house from fire wind and can regenerate after fire

## Radiation Shields

Radiant heat from the fire front — not direct flame contact — is what kills most plants and animals and destroys structures. Most human fire casualties are killed not by burning but by toxic smoke from furniture and plastics, or by radiant heat.

### Effective Radiation Shields
- Houses and buildings
- Stone walls (white-painted brick is ideal)
- Thick tree trunks
- Hollows or caves
- Hedgerows
- Car bodies
- Earth banks

### Critical Design Principle
A fire shadow tapers to approximately **4-5 times the height and width** of the solid radiation shield. This means shields of trees or walls must extend well past the house to provide complete protection.

## Fire Refuges

Every house in a fire-prone area should incorporate a dedicated fire refuge:
- Located **behind the shelter of the house itself**
- **Fully earthed over** with a wet blanket door
- **Dogleg or curved entrance** to escape direct radiation
- **270-liter permanent water tank** incorporated into the refuge
- **Old blankets and buckets of water** stored inside
- Serves as absolute "fire insurance" for those caught at home (often women and young children)

## The Sacrificial Hedge Strategy

A proven technique involves planting a **downslope line of willows and fern-leaved Acacia** as a sacrificial fire barrier. This hedge:
- Is fire-killed above ground but rejuvenates from the roots when cut down
- Dampens out ground fire through the mass of moist vegetation
- Even the leaves do not combust but shrivel and release steamy ash
- Creates a protective zone between the fire front and the dwelling

## Emergency Preparedness Checklist

Before fire season, householders should:
- Prepare fish ponds and fill metal buckets with water
- Prepare blankets and important papers for quick evacuation via the rear door
- Fill roof gutters (or plug and flood them)
- Remove doormats
- Hose down the garden
- Tie up or pen stock animals
- Prepare wool or cotton clothing to wear
- Fill baths and sinks with water

## Community Infrastructure

For scattered suburbs in fire-prone areas, fire-resistant design should be compulsory:
- Houses built to fire specifications
- Large roof tanks and ponds on every property
