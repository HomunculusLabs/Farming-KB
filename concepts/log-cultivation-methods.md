# Log Cultivation Methods

From Chapter 11 of *Mycelium Running* by [[paul-stamets]]. Growing mushrooms on logs and stumps is the most traditional and long-lasting cultivation method, producing for years or even decades.

## Choosing the Right Wood

The ideal trees are those the mushroom species grows upon naturally. Consult a field guide for habitat preferences. Many saprophytes are opportunistic and adapt to non-native woods.

### Wood Density and Timeline

| Wood Type | First Fruiting | Production Lifespan | Notes |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|-------|
| Rapidly decomposing (alder, birch, aspen) | 6-12 months | Until bark peels | Fastest returns |
| Medium density (oak, maple, elm) | 1-2 years | 4-7 years | Best balance |
| Dense hardwoods (ironwood, hickory) | 2-3 years | 5-10 years | Longest production |
| Cedar, redwood | 20+ years (aged) | Unknown | Only Hypholoma capnoides known to work |

### Key Principles
- Oak is the natural host for shiitake, but alder, eucalyptus, and other fast-decomposing hardwoods are readily colonized
- Many mushrooms stop fruiting once bark peels off — but Hypholoma capnoides and H. sublateritium continue fruiting on barkless, decomposed logs
- Denser woods = longer lifespan but slower start
- The bark layer is critical for moisture retention and fruiting longevity

### Tree Selection
- Cut logs in late winter/early spring when sap runs (highest sugar content)
- Avoid wood with decay, wounds, blight signs, or preexisting mushroom growth
- Check cut ends: healthy logs show clean growth rings; infected logs show marbling
- If infection is near the cut zone, cut 1 full meter away from last visible discoloration and inoculate at double rate
- Storm debris = free substrate; some arborists now offer mycological services

### Unsuitable Trees
- Cedars and redwoods: Too resinous/tannin-rich for most species (exception: Hypholoma capnoides on 20+ year aged logs)

## Log Inoculation

### Plug (Dowel) Spawn Method

The standard method for homeowners and small-scale growers:

1. **Drill holes**: Use 5/16-inch (8mm) drill bit; holes 1.5x deeper than plug length
2. **Spacing**: 4-6 inches apart in staggered rows (checkerboard pattern)
3. **Insert plugs**: Tap dowel spawn into each hole with a hammer
4. **Seal with wax**: Melted cheese wax, bee's wax, or paraffin dabbed over each hole to prevent beetle/bug damage
5. **Quantity**: 30-50 plugs per 4-8 inch diameter log
6. **Large logs** (>10 inch diameter): Much slower to fruit; require significantly more spawn

### Sawdust Spawn Method
- Faster for commercial operations using automated inoculators
- Less expensive by weight but less dense — prone to falling out of holes unless packed tightly
- Some growers cap holes with Styrofoam plugs (less sustainable)
- Korean equipment can auto-insert and cap in a single pass

### Wafer Spawn
- Thin cellulose wafers grown through with mycelium
- Inserted into cuts in the log or stump
- Innovative Japanese method; equipment sized for quick insertion

### Combination Method
Using multiple inoculation strategies simultaneously:
- Spores in chain-saw bar oil + plug/sawdust spawn of matching strain
- Overcomes resistance barriers through synergistic complementary inoculations
- Recommended when single methods fail

## Log Management After Inoculation

### Placement
- Stack logs on pallets or in shady areas
- Cover with shade cloth or tarp in drier locations to buffer humidity fluctuations
- Avoid ground contact during colonization (soil organisms jump onto logs)
- Some species prefer elevated placement (shiitake); others work well on the ground
- Once fully colonized (mycelium visible at cut ends as discolored bands), logs are ready to fruit

### Timing
- Most 4-8 inch diameter logs fruit within 1 year of inoculation
- Some produce in 6 months; others take up to 2 years
- Maximum production in the first third of the log's life span
- Fruitings occur 2-3 times per year depending on strain, wood, and conditions
- Yield: ~1/2 to 2 lbs mushrooms per log per year

### "Pony Logs" for Reishi
- 2-foot-long logs inoculated with plug spawn, holes waxed
- Placed in black plastic pots filled with gravel or sand
- Store under greenhouse benches for moisture and ground contact benefits

### Forcing Fruitings
- Immerse logs in water overnight to shock them into fruiting (especially effective for shiitake)
- Timing: 2 weeks before desired harvest

### End-of-Life
- By year 5, alder logs slow production; oak logs expire by year 10
- Logs disintegrate into long white pulped fibers
- Spaulded/tiger wood (from fungal staining) can be milled 2-4 years after inoculation for unique decorative lumber — reishi on maple produces shimmering patterns; beefsteak fungus on oak produces red staining

## Stump Cultivation

### Advantages
- Stumps and root systems can weigh hundreds of pounds
- Colonization occurs for years before fruiting, but fruiting can persist for decades
- Stumps interspersed among overshadowing tree stands have the best success

### Method
1. Select fresh stumps with bright, homogeneous wood color (no fungal activity or preexisting mushrooms)
2. Use plug spawn, sawdust spawn, rope spawn, or spored oils — separately or in combination
3. For plug/sawdust: drill into the stump face and around the sides near the ground line
4. For rope spawn: place myceliated rope into chainsaw cuts
5. Cover inoculation sites with fresh wood chips to retain moisture
6. Patience: may take several years before first fruiting

### Stump Inoculation Tips
- Wound the stump face with a chain saw before applying spawn (creates more surface area)
- Place sawdust spawn over the wounds and across gaps between stacked logs
- Cover with fresh wood chips to protect from drying
- Stumps of recently felled trees have the highest success rate
- Long-lived species like reishi and turkey tail are excellent stump candidates
