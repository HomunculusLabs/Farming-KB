# Negative Geotropism in Mushroom Fruiting

Negative geotropism is the growth response by which an organism or organ
grows away from the direction of gravity (upward). In mushrooms, this
response becomes dominant during the later stages of fruiting body
development and is responsible for the pileus (cap) turning upward to
present the gills in a horizontal orientation for spore release.

## The Transition from Anemotropism to Geotropism

In *[[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]]*, Badham (1982) identified a clear transition
between two tropic phases during fruiting body development:

- **Phase 1 (Stages 1–2)**: Anemotropism dominates — the stipe grows into
  the wind
- **Phase 2 (Stage 2 onward)**: Negative geotropism becomes dominant — the
  pileus turns upward away from gravity

This transition occurs at approximately Stage 2, when the pileus becomes
nearly spherical. At this point, a negative geotropic curvature of the
stipe causes the pileus to turn upward from whatever direction the
anemotropic bending had established.

## Relationship to Spore Production

A critical finding of Badham's study is the connection between negative
geotropism and spore production:

- **Fertile basidiocarps**: Exhibit clear negative geotropic recurvation —
  the pileus turns upward and opens with lamellae facing downward
- **Sterile ([[sporeless-mutant-geotropism-link-psilocybe-cubensis]]) basidiocarps**: Do NOT exhibit negative
  geotropic recurvation
- **Darkness**: When fruiting occurs in the absence of light, few spores
  form and the geotropic response is reduced or absent

This strongly suggests that negative geotropism is linked to the process
of spore formation itself. The hormone or signaling molecule responsible
for the geotropic response may be produced in or by the developing gill
tissue.

## Role of Gill-Derived Hormones

The hypothesis that gill-derived substances regulate tropic responses has
a long history in mycology:

- **Gruen (1963)** and **Hagimoto (1963)** independently postulated the
  existence of a mushroom growth hormone involved in tropisms
- **Decapitation experiments** linked these hormones to the lamellae
  (gills) — removing the pileus altered tropic behavior
- **Hagimoto and Konishi (1960)** showed that agar diffusates from gills
  could partially replace the influence of the pileus on stipe growth

Badham's observation that sporeless mutants lack the geotropic response
supports the hypothesis that a substance produced in or by the gills
(possibly in association with spore development) is necessary for the
switch from anemotropism to negative geotropism.

## Rotation Experiments

When basidiocarps are rotated horizontally (parallel to the airflow at 3
rpm) under constant illumination, growth and pileus expansion both occur
horizontally rather than upward. This demonstrates that:

- The negative geotropic response can be counteracted by continuous
  rotation that equalizes the gravitational stimulus
- The mushroom integrates both gravitational and evaporative demand cues
- When gravity is effectively neutralized by rotation, the remaining
  directional cue (wind/evaporative demand) determines growth direction

## Light Requirement

Some light is necessary for the negative geotropic response, but this may
be indirect:

- Light is required for spore production in *P. cubensis*
- Since geotropism is linked to spore production, light may only appear
  necessary for geotropism because it is necessary for sporulation
- The light intensity used in the experiments (0.002 cal/cm²/min) was
  approximately one-tenth of natural field conditions, suggesting the
  response is not highly light-intensity-dependent

Light plays at least three roles in the life cycle of *P. cubensis*:
1. Initiation of basidiocarps ([[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]])
2. Spore formation and geotropic sensitivity
3. Inhibitory effect upon stipe elongation (excess light can suppress
   vertical growth)

## The Veil as a Tropic Shield

Badham proposed that the partial veil (the tissue connecting the pileus
margin to the stipe in young basidiocarps) may serve a tropic function:

- The veil protects the uppermost, unexpanded portion of the stipe from
  wind and light during early development
- This shielding may prevent premature activation of the geotropic
  response before the mushroom is ready for spore release
- When the veil ruptures (at maturity), the previously protected stipe
  tissue is exposed to full environmental stimuli, allowing the final
  readjustment of the cap before spore release

## Adaptive Significance of Negative Geotropism

The negative geotropic response ensures that the pileus achieves a
horizontal orientation with gills facing downward, which is optimal for
spore dispersal. **Plunkett (1961)** suggested this is particularly
important for lignicolous (wood-dwelling) and coprophagous (dung-dwelling)
species because their habitat is unstable:

- On dung, the substrate may be on uneven, sloped, or irregular surfaces
- On wood, the growth orientation may be vertical or inverted
- Negative geotropism ensures that regardless of the initial growth
  orientation, the gills end up facing downward for gravity-assisted spore
  release

## Comparison with Other Fungi

The negative geotropic response in *P. cubensis* parallels observations
in other basidiomycetes:

- ***[[agaricus-bisporus]]***: Shows geotropic responses linked to pileus
  development (Gruen, 1963; Hagimoto, 1963)
- ***[[schizophyllum-commune]]***: Phototropic and geotropic responses vary
  between normal and mutant fruiting bodies (Schwalb and Shanler, 1974)
- ***Polyporus brumalis***: Changes in tropism during stipe development
  described by Plunkett (1961)
- ***Phycomyces blakesleeanus***: Sporangiophore responds to gravity and
  centrifugal force (Dennison, 1961)
