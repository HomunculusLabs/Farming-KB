## Why Dry Herbs

Drying herbs preserves their flavor, aroma, and medicinal properties for use long after the growing season. Properly dried and stored herbs retain 50-80% of their volatile oils (compared to fresh) and can last for 1-3 years. Drying concentrates flavors, making dried herbs roughly 3 times more potent by volume than fresh — the general conversion is 1 teaspoon dried herb equals 1 tablespoon fresh. (see [[herb-growing-guide]]).

Home-dried herbs are significantly cheaper and more flavorful than store-bought dried herbs, which may have been sitting on shelves for months or years before purchase. (see [[echinacea-growing-guide]]).

## When to Harvest

- Harvest herbs in the morning after dew has dried but before the heat of the day — volatile oil content is highest at this time
- For leafy herbs: harvest before flowering for peak flavor (basil, mint, oregano, thyme)
- For flowers: harvest at full bloom (chamomile, lavender, calendula)
- For seeds: harvest when seeds are dry and brown but before they shatter (dill, fennel, coriander)
- For roots: harvest in fall after the plant has died back (valerian, echinacea, marshmallow)
- Cut stems 4-6 inches from the base for leafy herbs, leaving enough foliage for the plant to recover
- Never harvest more than one-third of a plant at one time

## Drying Methods

### Air Drying (Best for Most Herbs)

The simplest and most traditional method. Works well for low-moisture herbs like rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage, dill, marjoram, and lavender.

**Bundle method:**
1. Gather 5-10 stems into small bundles (large bundles mold in the center)
2. Tie stems tightly with string or rubber bands (stems shrink as they dry)
3. Hang bundles upside down in a warm, dry, dark, well-ventilated area
4. Upside-down drying preserves the essential oils in the leaves (they flow downward from the stem)
5. A paper bag with holes punched in it can be placed over bundles to protect from dust and light
6. Herbs are dry when leaves crumble easily between your fingers (typically 1-3 weeks)

**Screen method:**
1. Lay herbs in a single layer on a screen, mesh, or window screen
2. Place the screen in a warm, dry, well-ventilated area
3. Turn herbs daily for even drying
4. Works well for individual leaves, flowers, and short-stemmed herbs

Ideal conditions:
- Temperature: 70-90F (21-32C)
- Humidity: below 50%
- Airflow: good circulation — a fan on low can help
- Light: dark or indirect light — direct sunlight degrades volatile oils and color

### Dehydrator

Fast and consistent. Best for high-moisture herbs like basil, cilantro, chives, mint, and parsley.

1. Remove leaves from stems (or dry whole stems and strip later)
2. Arrange in a single layer on dehydrator trays — do not overlap
3. Set temperature to 95-115F (35-46C) — higher temperatures destroy volatile oils
4. Check every 2-4 hours
5. Most herbs dry in 4-12 hours depending on moisture content
6. Herbs are done when brittle and crumbly — they should snap, not bend

### Oven Drying

Less ideal (risk of overheating) but works in a pinch for sturdy herbs.

1. Spread herbs in a single layer on a baking sheet
2. Set oven to the lowest possible setting (usually 150-170F)
3. Prop the oven door open slightly with a wooden spoon to allow moisture to escape
4. Check frequently — herbs can go from dry to scorched quickly
5. Total time: 1-4 hours
6. Best for robust herbs (rosemary, sage, oregano); avoid delicate herbs (basil, cilantro)

### Microwave Drying

Fastest method but most uneven. Only suitable for small quantities.

1. Place herbs in a single layer between two paper towels
2. Microwave on high for 30-second intervals
3. Check between intervals — herbs can burn quickly
4. Typically done in 1-3 minutes total
5. Best for herbs that dry quickly: oregano, thyme, parsley

### Freezing

Not technically drying, but freezing preserves herbs well for culinary use.

- **Ice cube method:** chop herbs, pack into ice cube trays, cover with water or oil, freeze; drop cubes directly into soups and stews
- **Flat freeze:** spread herbs on a baking sheet, freeze until solid, transfer to freezer bags
- Best for: basil, cilantro, chives, dill, parsley — herbs that lose flavor when dried
- Frozen herbs keep for 6-12 months

## Storing Dried Herbs

### Containers

- Glass jars with tight-fitting lids — best overall option
- Airtight plastic containers — acceptable but can absorb odors over time
- Paper bags — breathable but not airtight; for short-term storage only
- Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers — best for very long-term storage
- Avoid storing in paper or cardboard (absorbs oils and moisture from the air)

### Conditions

- Cool (60-70F / 15-21C)
- Dark (light degrades both color and volatile oils)
- Dry (humidity below 50%)
- Away from heat sources (stove, dishwasher, direct sunlight)
- A pantry, cupboard, or dark cabinet is ideal

### Shelf Life

| Herb Type | Expected Shelf Life |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Leaves (basil, oregano, thyme) | 1-3 years |
| Seeds (dill, fennel, coriander) | 2-4 years |
| Roots (valerian, ginger) | 2-3 years |
| Flowers (chamomile, lavender) | 1-2 years |
| Ground spices | 6-12 months |

## Potency and Quality

### Signs of Good Quality

- Vibrant color (green herbs should still be green, not brown)
- Strong aroma when crushed
- Full, intact leaves or pieces (not powdered or dusty)

### Signs of Decline

- Faded or brown color — volatile oils have degraded
- Weak or absent aroma — the herb has lost its potency
- Musty or stale smell — moisture contamination or age
- Powdery texture — the herb has deteriorated beyond culinary use

### Testing Potency

- Rub a small amount between your fingers — fresh, potent herbs release strong aroma
- Taste a tiny amount — flavor should be clearly present
- If an herb has no scent or flavor, it is past its useful life and should be composted

## Labeling

Every container should be labeled with:
- Herb name (common and botanical if desired)
- Date dried or packaged
- Source (garden location, purchased, wild-harvested)
- Any notes on variety or use

A simple label system prevents the common problem of unidentifiable dried herbs in the back of the pantry.
