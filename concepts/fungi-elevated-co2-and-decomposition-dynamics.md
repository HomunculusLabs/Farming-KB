## Fungi, Elevated CO₂, and Decomposition Dynamics

## Overview

Rising atmospheric CO₂ concentrations fundamentally alter the relationship between plants, their associated fungi, and soil decomposition processes. Changes in plant litter chemistry under elevated CO₂, combined with altered carbon allocation patterns, create cascading effects on saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungal communities, nutrient cycling, and soil carbon storage. Understanding these interactions is critical for predicting ecosystem responses to global climate change.

## Effects on Plant Litter Chemistry

### Carbon:Nitrogen Ratios

Elevated CO₂ generally increases the C:N ratio of plant tissues, though responses vary considerably:

- Gifford et al. (2000): Review of leaf chemistry data showed mixed results with a general trend toward ~15% increase in C:N ratio under doubled CO₂
- C:P ratios show no consistent trend across studies
- De Angelis et al. (2000): Oak leaf litter in Mediterranean forests showed increased C:N and lignin:N ratios under elevated CO₂, retarding decomposition

### Species-Specific Responses

Cotrufo et al. (1998) demonstrated that nitrogen content reduction in leaves grown under elevated CO₂ varied dramatically:

| Species | Nitrogen Reduction |
|---------|-------------------|
| Sweet chestnut (*[[castanea-sativa]]*) | 50% |
| Sycamore (*[[acer-platanoides]]*) | 19% |

These changes in leaf quality stimulated grazing by isopods (*Oniscus asellus* and *Porcellio scaber*), suggesting that herbivore-mediated decomposition pathways may partially compensate for reduced saprotrophic activity.

### Woody Tissue

Beech twigs grown in elevated CO₂ showed significant increases in C:N ratio (45.6 to 72.7) and lignin:N ratio (16.3 to 22.4), yet Cotrufo and Ineson (2000) found no change in decomposition rate or nitrogen and lignin dynamics during decomposition. This disconnect between litter chemistry and decomposition rate highlights the complexity of predicting decomposition responses from chemical proxies alone.

## Impact on Saprotrophic Fungi

Reduced litter quality under elevated CO₂ directly affects saprotrophic fungal communities:

1. **Reduced substrate quality**: Higher C:N and lignin:N ratios make litter less favorable for fungal decomposers
2. **Decreased decomposition rates**: De Angelis et al. (2000) measured 5–8% drops in decomposition constant (k) for field experiments and 12.5% drops in microcosm experiments under elevated CO₂
3. **Altered community composition**: Reduced litter quality may shift competitive dynamics among fungal species

However, Gorisen and Cotrufo (2000) cautioned that changes in leaf chemistry may not directly correlate with decomposition rates. Their carbon tracing experiments showed that carbon accumulated under elevated CO₂ was primarily translocated to roots (~26% contribution to remaining carbon during decomposition) rather than remaining in leaves (~6%), limiting the effect on above-ground decomposition.

## Soil Microbial Responses

The response of soil microbial communities to elevated CO₂ is inconsistent across studies:

- Kandeler et al. (1998) and Bardgett et al. (1999): Increased CO₂ and temperature increased microbial carbon but decreased metabolic quotient under temperature increase alone
- Zak et al. (2000): Review of 47 publications showed coefficients of variation between 80–800% for C and N cycling responses, spanning graminoid, herbaceous, and woody plant ecosystems

### General Patterns from 47 Studies

1. Greater plant growth under elevated CO₂, with more carbon entering below-ground components
2. Greater metabolic activity of soil microbial communities under elevated CO₂
3. Significant but highly variable changes in C and N cycling between life forms

Hunt et al. (1991) observed increased soil organic matter accumulation in temperate grassland ecosystems, though not enough to keep pace with atmospheric CO₂ buildup rates.

## The Dighton-Jansen Model for Ectomycorrhizal Responses

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed a model for ectomycorrhizal plant responses to climate change with three scenarios:

### Scenario A: Elevated CO₂ Only
- Enhanced photosynthesis → larger carbohydrate pool
- Supports greater mycorrhizal development, fruiting, community diversity
- Increased investment in extraradical hyphal exploitation of soil
- Higher C:N ratio of plant litter increases importance of mycorrhizal saprotrophy
- Favors species capable of producing enzymes for organic nutrient acquisition

### Scenario B: Elevated Temperature
- Increased saprotrophic microbial activity
- Accelerated litter decomposition and nutrient mineralization
- Larger available nutrient pools (fertilizer effect)
- Reduced plant dependence on mycorrhizae
- Decline in mycorrhizal diversity and soil exploitation

### Scenario C: Combined CO₂ and Temperature with Changed Litter Chemistry
- Enhanced nitrogen content in litter changes resource quality
- Mycorrhizal fungi must compete with saprotrophs for mineralized nutrients
- Drives increased diversity and extraradical hyphal exploitation

## Below-Ground Carbon Allocation

### Root Responses to Elevated CO₂

BassiriRad et al. (2001) found no consistent pattern in root system adjustments to elevated CO₂, including root:shoot ratios, root architecture, nutrient absorption capacity, and nutrient use efficiency. However, specific studies show significant responses:

Dhillion et al. (1996) observed in a Mediterranean old field of *Bromus madritensis* (CO₂ increased from 350 to 700 μmol mol⁻¹):
- Root biomass increased 31%
- Root length increased 88%
- Soil microbial biomass increased 42%
- Soil fungal hyphal length increased 20%
- Total arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization increased 57%

### Photosynthate Drain to Mycorrhizae

The carbon cost of mycorrhizal symbiosis is substantial:
- Douds et al. (1988): 5–6× greater carbon translocation to mycorrhizal vs. nonmycorrhizal Carrizo citrange roots
- Wang et al. (1989): Mycorrhizal *Panicum* plants showed 20% increased carbon exchange rate, 45% increased leaf photosynthate storage, and more than doubled sink activity to roots

### Root Architecture and Energy Costs

Wullschleger et al. (1994) showed that enhanced root growth under elevated CO₂ improves N and P exploitation but that more branched root systems have higher energy demands due to greater meristematic tissue. Since ectomycorrhizal colonization increases root branching, part of the increased carbohydrate drain is attributable to morphological changes rather than direct fungal consumption.

## Ectomycorrhizal Species-Specific Responses

### Carbon Allocation Strategies

Gorisen and Kuyper (2000) compared two ectomycorrhizal species with different ecological strategies:

| Species | Habitat | Carbon Fate | Plant N Return |
|---------|---------|-------------|----------------|
| *Suillus bovinus* | N-poor | Incorporated into root and fungal biomass | Twice as high as *Laccaria* |
| *Laccaria laccata* | N-rich | Respired away | Lower |

This supports the concept that carbon use for nitrogen acquisition is more important in mycorrhizal symbioses characteristic of N-limiting ecosystems.

### Community Shifts

Rygiewicz et al. (2000) observed changes in Douglas fir ectomycorrhizal communities under elevated CO₂ and temperature:
- Total mycorrhizae increased under elevated CO₂
- Increased temperature drove increased diversity
- *Rhizopogon* and *Cenococcum* morphotypes dominated

O'Neill et al. (1987) showed 66% (pine) and 56% (oak) biomass increases in seedlings due to increased mycorrhizal colonization under doubled CO₂.

## Summary

The effects of elevated CO₂ on fungal-mediated decomposition are complex and context-dependent. While reduced litter quality generally decreases saprotrophic efficiency, increased below-ground carbon allocation stimulates mycorrhizal and soil microbial communities. The net effect on ecosystem carbon balance depends on the balance between enhanced primary production and decomposition, species-specific mycorrhizal responses, and the timescale of soil carbon stabilization. The Dighton-Jansen model provides a useful framework for predicting outcomes, but high variability across ecosystems limits broad generalizations.
