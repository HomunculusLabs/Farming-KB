# Fungal Enzymatic Capabilities
Extracellular enzyme systems of fungi: lignin degradation, cellulose decomposition, chitinases, phosphatases, proteases, and applications in bioremediation and biotechnology. Based on Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006). See also [[fungal-enzyme-systems-bioremediation]], [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]], [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]], and [[mycoremediation-basics]].
## Overview
Fungi produce an extraordinary diversity of extracellular enzymes that allow them to decompose virtually every class of biological polymer. Their enzymatic capabilities far exceed those of bacteria in the degradation of complex, recalcitrant materials, making fungi the dominant decomposers of wood, leaf litter, and other tough organic matter in most terrestrial ecosystems. These same enzyme systems have significant biotechnological applications in bioremediation, industrial processing, and green chemistry.
## Lignin-Degrading Enzymes
Lignin is a complex, irregular phenylpropanoid polymer that provides structural rigidity to plant cell walls. It is the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth (after cellulose) and one of the most difficult organic compounds to degrade. Only white-rot basidiomycetes can accomplish complete mineralization of lignin.
### Lignin Peroxidase (LiP; EC 1.11.1.14)
- **Produced by**: White-rot fungi, especially *Phanerochaete chrysosporium*, *Trametes versicolor*, *Bjerkandera adusta*
- **Mechanism**: A heme-containing peroxidase that uses hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to oxidize non-phenolic aromatic substrates via a one-electron oxidation mechanism. LiP creates aryl cation radicals that undergo various non-enzymatic reactions leading to lignin depolymerization.
- **Substrate range**: Non-phenolic lignin units (which constitute ~90% of lignin), polymeric dyes, PAHs, and various organic pollutants
- **Requirements**: Requires H2O2 (generated by fungal oxidases) and veratryl alcohol as a redox mediator
- **pH optimum**: Typically pH 2.5-3.5
### Manganese Peroxidase (MnP; EC 1.11.1.13)
- **Produced by**: Most white-rot fungi; the most commonly produced ligninolytic peroxidase
- **Mechanism**: Oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III), which chelated with organic acids (oxalate, malonate) acts as a diffusible oxidant that can attack phenolic lignin units and other substrates
- **Advantages over LiP**: Mn(III)-organic acid complexes are small and diffusible, allowing MnP to oxidize substrates beyond the reach of the fungal hypha. MnP is also more stable than LiP and produced by a wider range of fungi.
- **Substrate range**: Phenolic lignin units, manganese, various organic pollutants
- **pH optimum**: Typically pH 4-5
### Laccase (EC 1.10.3.2)
- **Produced by**: White-rot fungi, some soft-rot fungi, many ascomycetes, some bacteria and insects
- **Mechanism**: A multi-copper oxidase that uses molecular oxygen (O2) as the electron acceptor (producing water as a byproduct). Laccases oxidize phenolic compounds and, in the presence of appropriate mediators, non-phenolic compounds.
- **Substrate range**: Phenolic lignin units, aromatic amines, polymeric dyes, various xenobiotics
- **Mediator systems**: Natural mediators (ABTS, 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid) extend the substrate range to include non-phenolic compounds
- **pH optimum**: Typically pH 3-6
- **Industrial importance**: Laccases have numerous applications in textile dye decolorization, pulp bleaching, food processing, and bioremediation. They are the most commercially exploited ligninolytic enzymes.
### Versatile Peroxidase (VP)
- A hybrid enzyme with characteristics of both LiP and MnP
- Found in *Pleurotus eryngii* and related species
- Can oxidize both Mn(II) and non-phenolic aromatic compounds
- Represents an evolutionary intermediate between LiP and MnP
### Regulation of Ligninolytic Enzyme Production
Ligninolytic enzyme production is tightly regulated by nutrient availability:
- **Nitrogen limitation**: Triggers production of LiP and MnP in *P. chrysosporium*. Under nitrogen-limited conditions, secondary metabolism (including lignin degradation) is favored over growth.
- **Carbon limitation**: Also stimulates ligninolytic enzyme production in some species
- **Manganese**: High Mn(II) levels induce MnP production
- **Oxygen**: Lignin degradation is an oxidative process; oxygen availability regulates enzyme expression and activity
## Cellulolytic Enzymes
Fungal cellulase systems are among the most efficient biological systems for converting cellulose to glucose:
### Endoglucanases (EG; EC 3.2.1.4)
- Randomly hydrolyze internal beta-1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose chains
- Create new chain ends for exoglucanases to act on
- Reduce the degree of polymerization of cellulose
- Produced by cellulolytic fungi including *Trichoderma reesei*, *Humicola insolens*, and many white-rot fungi
### Exoglucanases / Cellobiohydrolases (CBH; EC 3.2.1.91)
- Processively remove cellobiose (glucose disaccharide) units from the reducing and non-reducing ends of cellulose chains
- CBH I acts on the reducing end; CBH II acts on the non-reducing end
- *T. reesei* CBH I is one of the most studied individual enzymes; it can account for up to 60% of the total cellulase protein produced
- Critical for the degradation of crystalline cellulose, the most recalcitrant form
### Beta-Glucosidases (BGL; EC 3.2.1.21)
- Hydrolyze cellobiose and short cellodextrins to glucose
- Relieve product inhibition of EG and CBH by removing cellobiose
- Often the rate-limiting step in cellulose saccharification
- *T. reesei* produces relatively low levels of BGL, which can be a bottleneck in industrial applications
### Synergistic Action
The three enzyme classes work synergistically: EG creates chain ends, CBH processively releases cellobiose, and BGL converts cellobiose to glucose. The combined activity is much greater than the sum of individual activities. This synergy is essential because crystalline cellulose is extremely resistant to degradation.
## Hemicellulose-Degrading Enzymes
Hemicelluloses (xylan, mannan, glucomannan, xyloglucan, arabinan) are the second most abundant polysaccharides in plant cell walls:
- **Xylanases**: Hydrolyze xylan (the main hemicellulose in hardwoods and grasses). Endoxylanases cleave internal bonds; beta-xylosidases remove xylose from chain ends.
- **Mannanases**: Degrade mannan and glucomannan (abundant in softwoods and some seeds)
- **Accessory enzymes**: Alpha-L-arabinofuranosidases, alpha-glucuronidases, acetyl xylan esterases remove side-chain substituents from hemicellulose backbones, exposing them to backbone-cleaving enzymes
## Chitinases
Chitin is the second most abundant biopolymer in nature (after cellulose) and a major component of fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons:
- **Fungal chitinases**: Produced by many fungi, serving roles in cell wall remodeling during growth, autolysis, and parasitism of other fungi
- **Mycoparasitism**: *Trichoderma* species use chitinases to parasitize pathogenic fungi (used as biocontrol agents)
- **Ecological role**: Fungal chitinases recycle nitrogen and carbon from dead insects and fungal necromass
- **Classification**: Multiple families (GH18, GH19) with different mechanisms and substrate specificities
## Phosphatases
Fungal phosphatases are essential for phosphorus cycling in soil:
- **Acid phosphatases**: Most soil fungi produce extracellular acid phosphatases with pH optima of 4-6. These enzymes hydrolyze organic phosphorus compounds (phytates, nucleic acids, phospholipids, sugar phosphates) to release plant-available phosphate.
- **Phytases**: Specialized phosphatases that hydrolyze phytate (myo-inositol hexakisphosphate), the main form of stored phosphorus in seeds and a major pool of organic phosphorus in soil. Fungal phytases generally have higher activity at acidic pH than bacterial phytases.
- **Mycorrhizal phosphatases**: Both AM and ECM fungi produce phosphatases that enhance phosphorus availability to their host plants. ECM fungi produce particularly high levels of acid phosphatases.
## Proteases
Fungal proteases break down proteins, the primary nitrogen source in many soils:
- **Serine proteases**: Most common type in saprotrophic fungi; include subtilisin-like proteases
- **Aspartic proteases**: Important in pathogenic fungi for penetration and tissue degradation
- **Metalloproteases**: Require metal ions (Zn2+, Co2+) for activity
- **Ectomycorrhizal proteases**: ECM fungi produce extracellular proteases that enable them to access organic nitrogen from soil organic matter, giving ECM forests a distinct nitrogen economy
## Pectin-Degrading Enzymes
Pectin is a structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls, especially in middle lamellae:
- **Polygalacturonases**: Hydrolyze the alpha-1,4-galacturonan backbone of pectin
- **Pectate lyases**: Cleave pectin via a beta-elimination mechanism (important in plant pathogenesis)
- **Pectin methylesterases**: Remove methyl groups from pectin, making it susceptible to other pectinases
- **Role in pathogenesis**: Pectin degradation is a key step in plant tissue maceration by soft-rot pathogens (*Botrytis*, *Rhizoctonia*, *Sclerotinia*)
## Laccases Beyond Lignin
Laccases have remarkable substrate versatility:
- **Dye decolorization**: Effective against azo, anthraquinone, and triphenylmethane dyes
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)**: Oxidize PAHs, facilitating their further degradation
- **Pesticide degradation**: Transform various organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides
- **Phenol removal**: Used in wastewater treatment to remove phenolic compounds
- **Biosensors**: Laccase-based biosensors detect phenolic compounds in environmental samples
- **Food industry**: Used in juice clarification, wine stabilization, and baking

## Industrial and Biotechnological Applications
- **Biofuel production**: Fungal cellulases are used to convert lignocellulosic biomass to fermentable sugars for bioethanol production. *Trichoderma reesei* is the main industrial source.
- **Textile industry**: Laccases for denim bleaching, cellulases for "biostoning" of denim
- **Pulp and paper**: Xylanases and laccases for pulp bleaching, reducing chlorine use
- **Food industry**: Pectinases in fruit juice clarification, cellulases in wine production
- **Animal feed**: Phytases added to animal feed improve phosphorus digestibility, reducing phosphorus pollution from manure
- **Detergent industry**: Proteases, lipases, and cellulases in laundry detergents
- **Pharmaceuticals**: Fungal enzymes used in synthesis of drug intermediates
