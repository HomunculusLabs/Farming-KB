## The Mycorrhizal Divide

### Weeds That Form Mycorrhizae

Some common weed species form arbuscular mycorrhizae:
- Common dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)
- Green foxtail (Setaria viridis)
- Wild oat (Avena fatua)
- Canada thistle (Cirsium arvense)
- Chickweed (Stellaria media)

### Weeds That Do NOT Form Mycorrhizae

Many prolific weeds do NOT form mycorrhizal associations. These belong primarily to the families:
- **Amaranthaceae** — redroot pigweed (Amaranthus retroflexus), kochia (Bassia scoparia)
- **Chenopodiaceae** — lamb's quarters (Chenopodium album)
- **Brassicaceae** — stinkweed (Thlaspi arvense), wild mustard
- **Polygonaceae** — wild buckwheat (Fallopia convolvulus)

This distinction is the foundation of mycorrhizal weed control strategies.

***

## Competition Mechanisms

### Phosphorus Starvation

Arbuscular mycorrhizal plants compete effectively and can starve non-mycorrhizal weeds of phosphorus. The mechanism works as follows:

1. Mycorrhizal crops are inoculated and establish extensive fungal networks
2. These networks access phosphorus from soil volumes unavailable to non-mycorrhizal weed roots
3. The mycorrhizal crops absorb the available phosphorus efficiently
4. Non-mycorrhizal weeds, unable to access mycorrhizal phosphorus pathways, suffer phosphorus deficiency
5. Weakened non-mycorrhizal weeds are outcompeted

### Direct Negative Effects

Some mycorrhizal fungi may directly negatively impact certain weed species. Research is exploring whether specific fungal strains can be paired with crops to selectively suppress target weeds while benefiting the crop.

## Future Applications

### Targeted Fungal Strains

Scientists are exploring the possibility of developing mycorrhizal fungal strains that:
- Kill dandelions while benefiting turf grass
- Pair with specific crop plants whose fungal partner not only provides nutrients but also suppresses nearby chickweed
- Selectively target non-mycorrhizal weeds while supporting mycorrhizal crops

### Strigolactone Manipulation

Plants produce strigolactone hormones that stimulate branching and growth of mycorrhizal fungi. Potential applications include:
- Adding synthetic strigolactones to soil to accelerate mycorrhizal formation
- Manipulating crop plants to restrict strigolactone production to prevent arbuscular mycorrhizal formation in weeds, resulting in weak weeds that die
- Engineering strigolactone responses to favor beneficial plant-fungal partnerships over weed-fungal partnerships

## Allelopathic Effects

### Garlic Mustard Example

A case in Minnesota illustrates how invasive species can disrupt mycorrhizal networks:
1. Fishermen introduce non-native Asian earthworms to lakeside forests
2. These worms reduce the duff layer too quickly, creating bare soil patches
3. Garlic mustard (Alliaria petiolata), an invasive non-mycorrhizal plant, colonizes the bare soil
4. Garlic mustard releases allelopathic chemicals (allelochemicals) that kill the mycorrhizal symbionts of maple trees
5. Without their mycorrhizal partners, maple trees decline and eventually die

This example demonstrates both the vulnerability of mycorrhizal networks to disruption and the potential of allelopathic chemicals to influence mycorrhizal communities.

### Crops That Release Allelochemicals

Some crop plants release allelopathic chemicals when they decompose:
- Mustards (Brassica spp.)
- Rapeseed
- Radishes

These chemicals can prevent the formation or propagation of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal spores and may remain in the soil even after the plants are removed. This has important implications for crop rotation planning.

***

## Practical Implications for Gardeners and Farmers

1. **Plant mycorrhizal crops** to outcompete non-mycorrhizal weeds
2. **Avoid brassicas in rotation** immediately before mycorrhizal-dependent crops
3. **Maintain mycorrhizal networks** through reduced tillage and appropriate cover crops
4. **Consider selective inoculation** with specific fungal strains targeted at problematic weed species
5. **Monitor non-mycorrhizal weed pressure** as an indicator of mycorrhizal network health
