# Pond Design and Aquaculture

A pond holds and reflects heat, moderating temperature for surrounding gardens. It grows water-loving plants, fish, crustaceans, and provides habitat for frogs that eat bugs. A pond can produce water chestnuts, rice, bait fish, brine shrimp, snails, aquarium fish, water lilies, basketry materials, crayfish, prawns, mussels, clams, and ducks.

## Pond Design Rules

- Several small ponds 4-6 feet deep work better for fish; storage ponds can be 10-20 feet deep
- Large irrigation ponds should NOT have many fruit/nut trees (pollution from leaves and dropped fruit)
- Maximize edge with winding shore rather than smooth oval
- Zone 1 tiny ponds: any waterproof container; disguise edges with stones (even a bathtub sunk into the ground)
- Large livestock must NOT have access to aquaculture ponds
- Must be near a water source for initial filling or seasonal topping

## Pond Construction

1. Lay rope as a shape guide while digging
2. Divide into thirds: 1/3 shallow end, 1/3 mid-range, 1/3 deep end
3. Line bottom with pond liner or gley (fermented manure layer)
4. Stabilize edges with rocks, bamboo, grass ledges, or logs
5. Do NOT introduce fish immediately — no food chain yet
6. Lay 2 inches of straw on the bottom, trample it in (habitat for water insects)
7. Introduce water plants (lilies, water chestnuts, duckweed) — wait 6+ months
8. Plant grass at pond inlet to filter incoming water
9. Water should turn green; if not, add small amount of manure
10. For muddy water, add 1 tsp gypsum per sq ft (486 lbs/acre)
11. Aerate if needed — plants don't oxygenate without sun; emergency aeration device for hot/cloudy periods
12. Plants should cover ~60% of surface; submerged plants at 1 bunch per 2 sq ft
13. Introduce baby fish in spring; add buckets of pond water from nearby ponds for aquatic insects
14. Bottom will acidify over time; add lime to balance; drain every few years

## Aquaculture Species

A closed-loop system growing fish with plants and other animals. Historically, the chinampa was one of the earliest examples.

### Per Quarter Acre (0.10 ha) of Pond Surface:
- 40 lbs bass (predator)
- 80 lbs catfish
- 120 lbs bluegill (insect eater)
- 350 lbs tilapia (plankton/plant eater)

### Species Details

**Tilapia:** Most versatile and efficient. 4 months from fingerling to edible. Prefers warm water; feed with duckweed. Stocking: 3,000/acre. Winter a few indoors in aquarium; release in spring.

**Bluegill:** 3 years to edible size. Prefers warm temps. Eats insects, fish eggs, crayfish. Stocking: 500/acre.

**Bass:** Best population control — eats smaller fish. Warm water, lots of plants. Stocking: 100/acre.

**Minnows:** Eat algae and insects; food for bass. Introduce a year before bass. Stocking: up to 2,000/acre.

**Trout:** Need cold water (34-60°F / 1-15°C). Deep ponds or winter-only. Don't do well with other species besides minnows. Stocking: 400/acre.

**Shrimp/Prawns:** Fewer shrimp = larger size. Need 65°F+; don't survive cold winter. Stocking: 16,000-24,000/acre.

**Mussels:** 50-90°F; eat tiny plankton at bottom; natural filter (1 gallon/hour). Use local species. Stocking: ~200/acre.

**Catfish:** Eat minnows and fry (need 1,000 minnows per 500 catfish). Winter hardy. Clean pond preferred. Stocking: 1,500/acre.

**Crayfish:** Bottom feeder; 65-85°F. Burrow dormant below 45°F or above 88°F. Need vegetation and hiding places. Stocking: 200/acre.

### Stocking Rules
- Reduce rates at least 1/3 for enough oxygen
- Avoid species competing for same food supply unless experienced
- Balance: more plants/fish = less oxygen; must keep in equilibrium

## Pond Groups and Canals

- Several ponds in succession down a stream segregate fish by age
- Parallel ponds prevent disease transmission (vs. downstream)
- Canals suit plant-eating fish (tilapia); easier net catching
- Chinampa design: islands max 30 feet wide, canals 100-300 feet per side, water 3 feet below garden surface, canals 5-8 feet deep
