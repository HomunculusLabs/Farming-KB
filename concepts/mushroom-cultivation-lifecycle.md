# Mushroom Cultivation Lifecycle

## Overview

Mushroom cultivation follows a predictable sequence of stages, each with
distinct environmental requirements. Understanding this lifecycle is essential
for managing the grow effectively and troubleshooting problems. The complete
cycle from inoculation to harvest typically ranges from 4 to 12 weeks depending
on the species, strain, substrate, and environmental conditions. Each stage
presents its own challenges, and failure at any point can result in
contamination, poor yields, or total crop loss.

## Stage 1: Inoculation

The cycle begins when fungal genetic material — spores, liquid culture, or [[agar]]
wedge — is introduced into a prepared, sterile substrate. For grain spawn, this
means injecting spore solution or LC into hydrated, sterilized grain jars. For
bulk grows, colonized grain spawn is mixed into pasteurized or sterilized bulk
substrate at a typical ratio of 10–30% spawn to substrate by volume.

Proper aseptic technique during this stage is critical, as the substrate is
most vulnerable to contamination before mycelium has established itself. The
inoculation environment should be as clean as possible, and all tools should be
flame-sterilized. Even brief exposure to open air can introduce mold spores or
bacteria that will outcompete the mushroom mycelium.

## Stage 2: Incubation (Colonization)

After inoculation, the substrate is kept in warm, dark conditions optimized for
mycelial growth. Temperature requirements vary by species but generally fall in
the 22–27°C (72–80°F) range. The incubation area should be dark or dimly lit,
as light is not needed at this stage and can sometimes trigger premature
pinning. Humidity should be moderate — jars should be loosely covered or fitted
with filter tape to allow gas exchange while preventing contamination.

During this stage, mycelium spreads through the substrate by extending hyphae,
secreting enzymes to break down nutrients, and absorbing the resulting simple
molecules. The mycelium functions as a distributed digestive system, converting
complex organic polymers into accessible food.

Key indicators of healthy colonization:

- White, rhizomorphic mycelium spreading from inoculation points within 3–7
  days.
- Even colonization throughout the substrate (not just at the surface).
- A clean, mushroomy or earthy smell; any sour, sweet, or putrid odor
  indicates bacterial contamination.
- No visible colored patches (green, black, pink, orange) which indicate mold
  contamination.

Colonization time varies from 1–2 weeks for fast species like oyster mushrooms
on supplemented substrates to 4–8 weeks for slow colonizers like shiitake on
hardwood sawdust. Grain jars can be gently shaken at 20–30% colonization to
redistribute the growing mycelium and accelerate full colonization.

## Stage 3: Consolidation

Many cultivators allow the fully colonized substrate to rest for an additional
1–2 weeks before inducing fruiting. This consolidation period allows the
mycelium to digest remaining nutrients and strengthen its network throughout the
substrate. The result is often more even and productive fruiting with larger,
more numerous mushrooms.

Consolidation is particularly important for grain-based substrates, where the
mycelium may not have fully penetrated the interior of individual grains during
the initial colonization window. During consolidation, the mycelium completes
its digestion of the grain interiors, producing a more resilient block ready
for the stresses of fruiting.

## Stage 4: Fruiting Initiation (Pinning)

The transition from vegetative growth to reproductive fruiting is triggered by
environmental changes that signal the mycelium that conditions are favorable for
reproduction. The key triggers are:

- **Fresh air exchange (FAE)** — elevated CO₂ levels during incubation suppress
  fruiting; introducing fresh air with lower CO₂ triggers pin formation. Most
  species initiate pinning when CO₂ drops below 800–1000 ppm.
- **High humidity** — 85–95% relative humidity prevents pins from drying out
  before they can develop. Misting or a humidifier is typically required.
- **Light** — while mycelium grows in darkness, most species require some light
  (indirect ambient or 12/12 fluorescent/LED cycle) to initiate and guide
  fruiting. Light quality matters: blue spectrum (6500K) is most effective.
- **Temperature drop** — a 5–10°C reduction from incubation temperature signals
  the seasonal shift that triggers fruiting in nature. Cold shocking (brief
  exposure to near-freezing temperatures) is used for some species.

When these conditions are met, the mycelium forms primordia (pins) — tiny
knots of dense hyphal tissue that will develop into mushroom fruiting bodies.
This pinning stage is the most delicate phase; pins are vulnerable to drying,
contamination, and physical damage. Even slight fluctuations in humidity or
temperature can cause pins to abort.

## Stage 5: Fruiting (Maturation)

Pins rapidly expand into mature mushrooms over 3–14 days, depending on species.
During this stage, the cultivator must maintain the same environmental conditions
used for pinning but with increased attention to fresh air exchange, as growing
mushrooms consume oxygen and produce CO₂ rapidly. Stagnant air leads to tall,
leggy stems and small caps — a condition known as "fuzzy foot."

Mushrooms grow by cell expansion (not cell division), absorbing water from the
substrate and expanding dramatically in a short period. Oyster mushrooms can
double in size every 24 hours during peak growth. The cap expands and the veil
(if present) stretches and eventually tears as the mushroom matures.

## Stage 6: Harvest

Mushrooms are harvested just before or as the veil beneath the cap begins to
tear (for species with partial veils). Harvesting at the right moment maximizes
flavor, texture, and potency. Waiting too long results in spore release, which
reduces culinary quality, creates a mess, and can inhibit subsequent flushes.

Harvesting involves gently twisting or cutting the stem at the base. Cutting is
preferred for substrates that will produce additional flushes, as twisting can
damage the mycelial surface. After the first flush, the substrate often produces
additional flushes (typically 2–4) with diminishing yields, before it is
exhausted and should be discarded or composted.
