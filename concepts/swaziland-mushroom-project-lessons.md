# Swaziland Mushroom Project Lessons

The mushroom project in Swaziland (now Eswatini) provides important lessons about
government-initiated vs. farmer-initiated mushroom development models. Initiated
by the king after visiting Thai mushroom farms in 2000, the project established
a spawn-impregnated bag distribution center at Malkerns Research Station. The
project illustrates both the potential and vulnerabilities of centralized mushroom
development approaches in [[mushroom-industry-african-countries]].

## Project Structure

The distribution center performed all key production steps: substrate mixing,
bagging, sterilization, inoculation, and incubation. Well-incubated bags were
distributed free to farmers who only needed to manage fruiting, watering, and
harvesting in their own [[mushroom-growing-houses]]. Equipment included imported Thai mixers
and bagging machines, and locally fabricated sterilizers. Three [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]]
strains were used.

Two Export Production Villages (EPVs) were selected as pilot sites: Siphofaneni
in the dry east and Mbangweni in the more humid south. Each received 300 bags
for about 50 farmers. By end of 2002, approximately 20,000 bags had been
distributed, including 2,000 imported from Thailand. Average farmer income was
SZL300/month (USD45.57) from 300 free bags.

## Substrate Research

The center identified sugarcane bagasse as the primary substrate (available free
from factories). Five promising bagasse-based mixes were selected. When the
sugarcane mills discovered their waste had value, they stopped giving it away
and began using it as fuel instead. The center then researched alternatives
including Bermuda grass hay, mixed Lowveld grasses, and corncobs.

Spent oyster [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-substrate-preparation]] was also researched for agricultural use.
Cucumbers, spinach, and eggplant were successfully grown on SMS-fertilized
fields. Experimental [[cultivation-of-shiitake]] and [[stamets-reishi-ganoderma-lucidum-cultivation]] was also
conducted.

## Village Outcomes

### Mbangweni (South) - Success
Farmers established markets at Nhlangano, supplying mushrooms at SZL20/kg
(USD3.04). They constructed a mushroom center with office, storage, packing,
and processing rooms. The government provided additional business management
training. The higher humidity was favorable for [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]].

### Siphofaneni (East) - Challenges
Farmers faced difficulties from dry climate and lack of clean water access. Eight
farmers installed piped water systems. Growing houses made of logs and thatched
grass deteriorated quickly and could not regulate temperature or humidity. Cattle
and goats ate the grass walls during dry season. Some farmers borrowed plastic
sheeting to wrap their houses.

## Project Failure and Root Causes

In April 2003, the distribution center stopped operating due to:
- Suspension of free sugarcane bagasse supply when mills discovered its value
- Critical lack of funding as UNDP Swaziland blocked funds from the ZERI Regional
  Project, citing endless research without considerable results
- High contamination rate (20%) of bags requiring better facilities
- Need for [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]] in the inoculation room

Farmers were left helpless, maintaining empty growing houses, unable to produce
mushrooms without the distribution center because they had never been trained in
the full cultivation process.

## Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up Models

The Swaziland case crystallizes the debate between two approaches:

### Top-Down (Government-Controlled)
**Advantages**: More effective when farmers lack mushroom knowledge (common in
Africa where [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]] is limited). Ensures quality control through
centralized production. Swaziland has adequate extension workers for support.
The farmer's job ([[pf-tek-fruiting-and-harvesting]]) requires minimal skill.
