## Fractals, Branching, and Conduit Design

## Overview

Fractals — from the Latin *fractus*, meaning shattered — are among the most ubiquitous [[permaculture-design]], understanding fractals and branching systems is essential for creating efficient pathways, distribution networks, and exchange surfaces that mirror natural patterns.

## Fractal Forms in Nature

Fractals are as common in nature as in abstractions. Examples span an extraordinary range of scales and media:

- **Geological**: Impact shatter zones, fulgurites (sand fused by lightning) in sand dunes, the complex lengths of coastlines, the intricacies of turbulence
- **Biological**: Neurone nets and their signals, tree branches and roots, [[permaculture]] tree model, fractal patterns (as branches and roots) are contained within a form that would be comprehensible to Euclid — having straight axes, a plane, and regular curved lines drawn as arcs of perfect circles. Thus the apparent chaos of fractals can be seen to underlie quite regular (but never perfect) shapes in nature, just as branches underlie the crown canopy of a tree. As Mandelbrot demonstrated, fractals have their own regular generators and evolutions.

### Roots as Underground Shatter

Tree roots are, in fact, a slow shatter or explosion underground. One practical application of this insight: a way to plant an apple tree in very hard ground is to detonate a small plug of gelignite a foot or two below the surface. The roots will follow the shatter pattern and further elaborate it, demonstrating the fractal connection between explosion and root growth.

## The Physics of Branching Conduits

### Large vs. Small Conduits

Fractal theory provides a way to measure, compute, and design for branched or scattered phenomena, but understanding the physical advantages of developing ever-smaller conduits is equally important:

**Large conduits** (trunks, main stems, main roads):
- Useful in mass transport
- Laminar flow patterns within them
- Small surface area relative to volume
- Inefficient for diffusion of materials or conduction of heat across walls

**Small conduits** (branches, minor roots, footpaths):
- Flow is slow, almost viscous in very small tubes
- Direction changes possible without turbulence or energy losses
- Walls can be permeable
- Efficient collection, exchange, and transfer of materials, heat, and light
- Many small conduits efficiently interpenetrate the exchange media

### The Surface Area Principle

The high-pressure/low-flow nature of minor branches demands a very large total cross-sectional area relative to the main supply arteries. Small conduits may develop areas which in sum are 300–1,600 times that of the supply artery. Our main roads are therefore much less in area than the foot tracks that lead off them — a principle with direct design applications.

## Design Applications

### Garden Path Design

Wherever there is a need to collect or distribute materials, or to trade both ways with media, branching is an effective response. In home garden design:
- Use "many paths" — the garden's main activity is always trading nutrients
- Little advantage in forming paths as straight lines (speed is not of the essence)
- Better to develop cul-de-sacs or **keyhole-shaped beds** — this is also the shape of sacs in lungs
- Convoluted paths bring the gardener into better contact with the garden
- They enable collection and servicing to occur
- They create better mutual exchange between species in the garden

### Multiple Paths for Access

As an applied strategy, multiple small paths enhance access to food systems, or any system where we both take and give materials. The lung analogy is instructive: any form specialized for diffusion or infusion (lungs, kidneys, mushrooms, palm trees) is usually subject to branching and develops a "half-model" in one medium. The alveoli of the lungs further resemble the "keyhole beds" of a well-designed garden.

### Wall Elaboration

Another way to effect interchange is to elaborate on the walls of larger conduits:
- Involutions
- Attached fins
- Irregular surfaces
- Spirals in fluids or gases (by bending or spiralling the conduits themselves)
- In general, inducing a larger surface of contact between the material transported and the media with which exchange occurs

### Redundancy and Resilience

In organisms, multiple branches give the being:
- A chance to recover from injury
- Preservation of information
- Permitted regrowth in the event of minor damage

It is a foolproof system of interchange. This redundancy principle is directly applicable to designing resilient human systems — multiple pathways, multiple connections, and distributed functions create systems that can survive partial failure.

## Branching as Interaction with Environment

Branching in trees is as often a result of external forces (wind and salt pruning, secateurs, or insect attack) as it is a result of internal cell patterns. It is as much forced upon things as it is the "best thing to do." Designers must therefore see the branched form as an interaction between:
1. An organism or process
2. The purpose it serves
3. The external forces of the media in which the organism is immersed

## The Tree Model: Gathering and Dispersal

Along the streamlines of the permaculture tree model, fluids and gases pass in conduits or along "transmission cords." Food and signals are relayed to cells, and gases exchanged. Organs served by or serving these systems are either half-models of the tree (kidneys, lungs) or branching fractals (mesenteries).

No matter how long or complex conduits are, their contents ultimately diverge, escape, and disperse. At the intake, materials are gathered from dispersed sources. This gathering and dispersal from both ends or margins of events is a basic function of the tree-like forms that pervade living natural systems and phenomena such as rivers or lava flows.

## Orders of Magnitude in Branches

### Stream Orders

Streams usually have from one to seven orders, depending on their age, size, or gradient. As branches join to make ever-larger orders of channels, about 3 times as many smaller branches join to make each larger group. However, individual branch lengths increase by approximately 2 times as the order increases from 1 to 6. This is a very general rule of stream branching, even in non-dendritic patterns.

Similarly, meanders or bends occur in a predictable way depending on volume and gradient (flow). The ratio for meanders or trails is about 1:3.6 (Vogel, 1981) — the same ratio that governs Von Karman vortex trails.

### Life Changes with Order

Such regularity in branching connects to pulsers (wave fronts). As each size order changes, so does:
- The behavior of water flow (turbulent, spiraling, laminar)
- The associated flora and fauna and their shapes
- Gaseous exchange efficiency
- Oxygen levels

**High-gradient rills and runnels**: Streamlined flow, turbulent, high oxygenation — insects and fish with suctorial parts, flattened fins, streamlined profiles.

**Middle-order streams**: Less turbulent, more spiraling, less oxygenation — free-swimming but very active fish of high oxygen demand.

**Lower streams and estuaries**: Weak swimmers, less streamlined shapes, flat fish, bulky molluscs, jellyfish in quiet areas with lower oxygen levels.

### Broader Applications of Order

This order-of-branches principle extends well beyond streams:
- **Trees**: Birds and insects are confined to or modified to suit the orders of branching — on bark of stems or leaf laminae
- **Human settlements**: Villages are conservative at about 1,000 people; unstable much below or above that number
- **Trophic levels (food pyramids)**: Size factor changes with dimensions (times 10 for trophic pyramids vs. times 3 for river branches)
- **Physical entities**: From protons to universes, size change brings changes in ratio, dimensions, and behaviors (giant and dwarf stars behave very differently)
- **Social hierarchies**: Fewer than 15 people rarely clap to applaud as an audience

## Function Over Status

In human systems, the order of hierarchical function has been confused with status and power — as though a tree stem were less important than the leaves in total, or the fingers less desirable than the palm of the hand. What should be recognized is that each part needs the other, and none functions without the others.

When a dominant animal is removed from a behavioural hierarchy, another is created from lower orders. When subordinates are removed, others are created from within the dominants. Streams behave the same way. The principle is clear: **it is not what you are; it is what you do in relation to the society you choose to live in.** Water is of the whole, not to be thought of in terms of its parts.
