## How Trees Interact With Rain — Canopy and Throughfall

## The Problem of Bare Soil Under Rain

Rain falling on bare soils and thinly spaced cultivated crops creates destructive impact. The force of droplets carries away soil, typically removing 80 tonnes per hectare, or up to 1,000 tonnes in extreme downpours. When we bare the soil, we lose the earth itself.

Water runoff and pan evaporation — estimated at 80-90% of all rain falling on Australia — carries off nutrients and silt to the sea or to inland basins. As land is cleared, runoff initially increases, which pleases people who see their dams fill quickly. But the dams will silt up, the river eventually ceases to flow, and the clearing of forests results in alternating flood and drought rather than a long-regulated and steady supply of clean water.

## The Forest Canopy: First Line of Defence

When rain falls on a forest, a complex process begins. The tree canopy shelters and nullifies the impact effect of raindrops, reducing the rain to a thin mist below the canopy, even in the most torrential showers. There is slight measurable silt loss from mature forests — exceeded by the creation of soils by forests. The canopy transforms violent rainfall into gentle, distributed moisture.

## Interception: The Primary Effect

### What Is Interception?

If rain is light, little of it penetrates beyond the canopy. A film of water spreads across the leaves and stems and is trapped there by surface tension. The cells of the tree absorb what is needed, and the remainder evaporates to air. Where no rain penetrates through the canopy, this effect is termed **"total interception."**

Interception is defined as the amount of rainfall caught in the crown. It is the most important primary effect of trees or forests on rain.

### Factors Affecting Interception

The degree of interception is influenced by five key factors:

1. **Crown thickness** — deeper crowns intercept more rain
2. **Crown density** — denser foliage captures more water
3. **Season** — evergreen trees intercept year-round; deciduous trees vary seasonally
4. **Intensity of rain** — light rain is more easily intercepted than heavy downpours
5. **Evaporation after rain** — sunny conditions after rain increase total interception as canopy water evaporates

### Interception Rates

Broadly speaking, interception commonly falls between 10-15% of total rainfall:

- **Least interception (approximately 10%)**: Thinned and deciduous forests, winter rain, heavy showers, and cloudy weather conditions
- **Most interception (up to 100%)**: Dense evergreen trees, light summer rain, and sunny conditions

The extreme range demonstrates how much the forest canopy can modify rainfall reaching the ground.

## Throughfall: Enriched Water

### The Nature of Throughfall

When more rain falls, or heavy rains impact on the trees, water commences to drift as mists or droplets to earth. This water is called **throughfall**. Throughfall depends on the intensity of rain — there is little interception effect in heavy downpours. As an average figure, throughfall represents approximately 85% of humid rainfall.

### Throughfall Is Not Rainwater

Critically, throughfall is no longer just rainwater — any more than bathwater is rainwater. Throughfall contains many plant cells and nutrients and is a much richer solution than rainwater. The dissolved salts, organic content, dust, and plant exudates included in throughfall make it a significant nutrient delivery system to the forest floor.

Research on red gum forests (Murray and Mitchell, Soil Conservation Authority, Victoria, Australia) found that: "Rain washes large amounts of potassium and smaller amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium from the canopies to the surface soil. Litter adds organic matter, and is a rich source of calcium and nitrogen and a moderately rich source of magnesium and potassium."

### Directed Water Flow in Forests

Throughfall cannot be accurately measured in rain gauges because trees provide special receptors, conduits, and storages for water. The random fall of rain is converted into well-directed patterns of flow that serve the needs and growth of the forest:

- **Aerial ponds**: In the stem bases of palms, plantains, and many epiphytes, or the flanged roots of Terminalia trees and figs, water is held as aerial ponds, often rich in algae and mosquitoes
- **Moss and epiphyte storage**: Stem mosses and epiphytes absorb many times their bulk of water
- **Bark channels**: The tree directs water via insloping branches and fissured bark to its tap roots
- **Spider webs**: Spiders catch their share of throughfall on webs
- **Fungal absorption**: Fungi soak up what they need from the dripping water
- **Weeping branches**: Some trees trail branches to direct throughfall to their fibrous peripheral roots

## Organic Particles as Rain Nuclei

### The Upward Spiral of Life

The upward spirals of humid air rising from the forest carry insects, pollen, and bacteria aloft. This is best observed as flights of gulls, swifts, and ibis spiralling up with the warm air and actively catching insects lifted from the forest — their gastric pellets consist of insect remains. It is these organic aerial particles (pollen, leaf dust, and bacteria) that create the nuclei for raindrop formation.

### The Kenya Tea Hailstorm Case

A striking example of vegetation providing rain nuclei comes from Kenya: the violent hailstorms that plague tea plantings may well be caused by tea dust stirred up by local winds and the feet of pickers. Once above the ground, particles are easily drawn up into thunderheads to help form hailstorms.

Research found that Kenyan organic tea leaf litter caused water to freeze in a test chamber at only -5°C, compared with:
- **-11°C** for eucalyptus grove leaf litter
- **-8°C** for litter from local indigenous forests of Colorado
- **-8°C to -10°C** for silver iodide (the artificial cloud-seeding agent)

Tea litter proved to be a much better seeding agent than silver iodide. This finding demonstrates that the materials given up by vegetation may be a critical factor in rainfall patterns inland from forests.

## Indigenous Wisdom About Trees and Water

The connection between forests and the water cycle was well understood by traditional peoples. To doubt this connection, Mollison writes, "is to doubt that milk flows from the breast of the mother, which is just the analogy given to water by tribal peoples." Trees were called "the hair of the earth" which caught the mists and made the rivers flow.

The Hawaiians — who had themselves brought on earlier environmental catastrophes — placed forest cutting under *tabu* (prohibition), even forbidding the making of tracks on high slopes. Mountain trees were placed in a sacred or protected category. Now that the scientific reasons for these beliefs are understood, Mollison suggests we could look on trees as our essential companions, giving us all the needs of life and deserving of our care and respect.

## Key Principles for Design

The evidence presented leads to clear design principles:

1. **Protect existing forests** — especially on sea-facing hills and high slopes
2. **Never bare the soil** — maintain canopy cover to prevent erosion
3. **Design for interception** — use dense evergreen plantings where maximum rain capture is needed
4. **Understand that local precipitation can be modified** — trees provide cooling, condensation surfaces, nuclei, and streamline compression in an integrated system
5. **Recognise the forest as a water factory** — not merely a consumer of water but a primary producer through interception, condensation, and rehumidification
6. **Plan for throughfall distribution** — the forest naturally directs water to where it is needed most; mimic this pattern in designed systems

The fundamental insight is that on-site strategies make water either a scarce or plentiful resource. Given that there is always some free water in the air and earth around us, we can usually increase local precipitation through thoughtful design that works with, rather than against, the natural processes trees have refined over millions of years.
