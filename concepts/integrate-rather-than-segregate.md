# Integrate Rather Than Segregate — Permaculture Principle 8

Integrate Rather Than Segregate is the eighth of [[david-holmgren]]'s twelve
permaculture-design-principles. It emphasises that the relationships
between elements in a system are often more important than the elements
themselves. By placing elements in mutually beneficial relationships,
designers can create systems where each element supports and is supported by
others, reducing the need for external inputs and producing more total output
than the same elements in isolation.

## The Power of Relationship

In nature, productivity and resilience arise from the complex web of
relationships between species. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] connect tree root systems,
allowing nutrient exchange between different plants. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria
in legume roots provide fertiliser for neighbouring species. Pollinators
depend on diverse flowering plants, which in turn depend on pollinators for
reproduction. These integrated relationships create self-maintaining systems
that require no external management.

[[permaculture-design]] seeks to replicate these integrated relationships in
human-designed systems. The goal is to place every element so that it
performs multiple functions and is supported by multiple other elements.
This creates a web of beneficial connections that makes the system more
productive, resilient, and self-reliant.

## Kropotkin's Mutual Aid

Peter Kropotkin's work "Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution" provides an
important intellectual foundation for this principle. Writing in response to
the social Darwinists who used Darwin's ideas to justify competitive
individualism, Kropotkin documented numerous examples of cooperation and
mutual aid in both nature and human societies. He showed that cooperation
rather than competition has been a primary driver of evolutionary success.

Kropotkin's work supports the permaculture understanding that
integration and cooperation produce more robust outcomes than segregation
and competition. This does not deny the role of competition (explored in
[[obtain-a-yield-permaculture-principle]]) but places it in a broader
context where cooperation is equally fundamental.

## Classic Permaculture Examples

### Chicken-Garden Integration

The classic permaculture example of integration is the chicken tractor
or chicken forage system. Chickens in a garden or orchard provide pest
control, weed management, manure for fertiliser, and soil cultivation
through scratching. The garden or orchard provides food, shelter, and
foraging opportunities for the chickens. Each element supports the other,
creating a system more productive than either element alone.

### Food Forests

Food forests integrate multiple layers of plants — canopy trees,
understorey trees, shrubs, herbs, ground covers, and root crops — into a
single productive system. Each layer occupies a different niche, captures
different resources (light, water, nutrients), and supports the others
through shade, mulch, nitrogen fixation, or pest control. The integrated
system produces more total yield than the same plants grown separately.

### Guilds

Plant guilds are communities of species that have beneficial relationships
with each other and with a central element, often a fruit tree. A classic
guild might include a fruit tree with nitrogen-fixing shrubs, insectary
plants, nutrient accumulators, and ground covers. Each member contributes
something to the whole, reducing or eliminating the need for external
inputs.

## Integration in Social Systems

The principle extends beyond biological design to social and economic
systems. Segregated human systems — where production, consumption, work,
leisure, and living are separated — create enormous inefficiencies in
transport, energy use, and social cohesion. Integrated communities where
these functions overlap reduce resource consumption and improve quality of
life.

Intentional communities need diversity in age, interests, and livelihoods
for effective economic exchange and natural authority. The tension between
commonality and difference is productive: too much similarity leads to
stagnation, while too much difference prevents cooperation.

## Industrial Segregation

Industrial culture exemplifies the opposite of this principle. Agriculture
is segregated from cities. Work is segregated from home. Production is
segregated from consumption. Age groups are segregated in schools,
workplaces, and retirement facilities. This segregation creates enormous
resource costs in transport and infrastructure and weakens the social
fabric that supports mutual aid.

Holmgren's contrast between industrial and sustainable culture identifies
centralised organisation as characteristic of industrial culture and
distributed networks as characteristic of sustainable culture. Integration
tends to create distributed networks; segregation tends to create
centralised hierarchies.

## Functional Connection

The key design skill in applying this principle is identifying functional
connections between elements. For each element in a design, the designer
should ask: What does this element need? What does this element provide?
What other elements could supply its needs? What other elements could use
its products? By systematically mapping these connections, designers can
identify opportunities for integration.

## Relative Location

Relative location is a core [[permaculture-design]] concept that implements
this principle. Elements that interact frequently should be placed close
together. Elements that need regular attention should be near the centre of
activity. Elements that produce outputs needed by others should be located
where those outputs can be easily delivered. The zone system described in
[[design-from-patterns-to-details]] is a tool for organising relative
location.

## Synergy and Emergence

When elements are well integrated, the system exhibits synergy: the total
output is greater than the sum of individual outputs. This emergent property
of integrated systems is one of the most powerful arguments for integration.
It suggests that the most productive and efficient systems are not those with
the most sophisticated individual components but those with the best
connections between components.
