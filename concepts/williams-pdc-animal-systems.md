# Animal Systems in Permaculture

Animals are integral components of ecological systems,
participating in atmospheric, hydrological, and nutrient
cycles, and in permaculture design they serve as active
participants performing multiple functions beyond just
providing food.

## Animals in the Ecological Cycles

Animals are integral in the atmospheric cycle, they are
active participants in the hydro-cycle. Through the cycles
of birth, eating, excreting, and decomposing upon death
animals are an integral part of the nutrient cycle. Animals
improve the health of soil and help plants to propagate.
Understanding these ecological roles helps designers
integrate animals as functioning components of the system.

## Permaculture Principles and Animals

Permaculture Principle 2 calls for us to catch and store
energy, and animals generate a lot of energy which can be
harnessed. Chickens spend all day generating energy as they
run around scratching and pecking, and their poop represents
energy in the form of fertilizer.

Principle 5 calls for us to integrate rather than segregate,
so animals should be integrated right into the design, not
kept separate from it. They should be active participants in
the ecology alongside all other ecological functions.

Principle 7 informs us to obtain a yield. Animals produce
all sorts of yields beyond meat, eggs and milk, including
work and ecological activity for the design. Principle 9
calls for diversity of animal types for a variety of
purposes. Principle 10 reminds us that well-fed animals are
a renewable resource providing many renewable services.

## Animals as Workers

Actually using animals for meat is probably the least
sustainable way to use them. An animal can only serve as
dinner once, while alive it can function in an ecology for
months or years. Animals can work the soil preparing garden
spaces. A common permaculture strategy is to fence flocks
of chickens into an area where you wish to start a food
system. While there they eat weeds, aerate the soil through
scratching, fertilize with their droppings, and eat bugs
that could damage plants. By moving the flock from place to
place you harness chicken energy to prepare varying tracts
of land. Goats and other animals can be used similarly.

## Diverse Animal Types

Your design can harness a variety of animal types beyond
traditional livestock. Aquaculture systems house fish and
use the dirty water on plants. Bees pollinate plants and
make honey. Bat boxes attract bats whose guano serves as
fertilizer while keeping flying insects down. Bird houses
bring birds that introduce fertilizer, control insect
populations, pollinate plants, and drop volunteer seeds.
Worms in vermiculture systems break down food scraps. You
can also design to attract wild animals that volunteer their
services to benefit your system.

## Design Considerations

Think of animals as components in an ecological system,
performing the same roles as they do in nature, but in a
domesticated setting. Be careful when designing large
animals into a system. Not everyone can properly care for
goats or chickens, and feed costs may be burdensome. Check
local codes for urban animal keeping; many people are
surprised by how many chickens are allowed in urban areas.
Ducks lay excellent eggs and a raised duck pond can be
integrated so that the nutrient-rich water flushes into
gardens. Animals can also serve as indicator species that
tell you something about the local environment.

## Indicator Species

An indicator species tells you something about the local
environment. They can be birds, insects, ground animals,
crustaceans, and more. For example, the Brown-headed
Nuthatch lives in pine forests, so seeing one indicates a
healthy pine forest is near. The presence or absence of
particular animals can indicate environmental conditions,
prey populations, or the health of the broader ecology.
Learning to read animal indicators is a valuable skill
for any permaculture designer assessing a site.

## Animals and Nutrient Cycling

Animals play a crucial role in closing nutrient loops within
a permaculture system. They consume plant material and
convert it into manure, which becomes fertilizer for future
plant growth. In this way animals help maintain the local
nutrient cycle that modern infrastructure systems so often
break. By integrating animals into the design the designer
ensures that nutrients circulate within the system rather
than being imported and exported as in conventional farming.

## Scale and Context

The appropriate scale of animal integration depends
entirely on the site and the client. A rural homestead
might support goats, chickens, bees, and fish, while an
urban backyard might be limited to a worm bin and a few
chickens. Even without domesticated animals, a design can
attract beneficial wildlife through habitat creation, bird
houses, bat boxes, pollinator gardens, and water features.
The key is to integrate animal energy at whatever scale is
appropriate and feasible for the specific design context.
