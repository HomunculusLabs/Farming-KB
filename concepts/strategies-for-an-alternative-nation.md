# Strategies for an Alternative Nation

## Overview

Chapter 14 of *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* represents a philosophical and strategic pivot from the technical land-management content of the preceding chapters. Mollison argues that all practical permaculture techniques come to nothing if society continues to invest in arms and destruction, permit land abuse, and fail to tackle the social and political impediments to reclaiming damaged lands. The strategies presented address the social and economic dimensions necessary for meaningful, lasting change.

## The Problem of Power and Control

Mollison identifies a fundamental tension between sustainable self-reliance and centralized economic and political control. He observes that the techniques and biological materials needed to solve every food, clean energy, and sensible shelter problem in every climate already exist and have been tested. The tragic reality is that very few sustainable systems are designed or applied by those who hold power.

The reason is straightforward: to let people arrange their own food, energy, and shelter is to lose economic and political control over them. This creates a structural resistance to adoption of permaculture and similar systems within existing power hierarchies. The implication is clear—those seeking sustainable systems should not look primarily to governments, corporations, or centralized institutions for solutions.

## Self-Reliance as First Strategy

The very first strategies needed are those that put our own house in order, while simultaneously refusing to give credibility to distant power-centered or unethical systems. In the present fiscal or money-run world, the primary responsibility that people need to take charge of is their wealth—which Mollison defines as the product of their sweat and their region, not representable by valueless currency.

This reframing of wealth from abstract financial instruments to tangible regional productivity is central to the permaculture vision of alternative economics. Real wealth is soil fertility, clean water, food production, healthy ecosystems, and skilled communities.

## The Role of Traditional Water Cultures

The chapter references traditional systems as evidence that sustainable self-governance is not only possible but has been achieved for millennia:

- **[[ifugao-terraces]]** (Philippines): In use for at least 3,000 years, these wet terrace systems demonstrate that community organization and self-control are inherent to the construction and maintenance of common canal systems and shared irrigation
- **Nile flood cultures**: Probably 17,000 years old, now sadly but perhaps temporarily ended
- **Chinampa systems** (Mexico): Also antique, combining canal and bank systems for intensive food production
- **Flood-plain, lakeside, and river cultures** of Asia: Demonstrating long-term sustainability of water-based food systems

[[harold-conklin]], in "Ethnographic Atlas of Ifugao" (*Scientific American*, February 1982), calculates that 1,000 years of toil created the Ifugao terrace cultures. He makes a commentary that Fox (1977) makes on Indonesian palm cultures—that these peoples recognize no external authority. Not only is external authority inappropriate and interfering in self-sustained systems, but the creators of such systems have developed a self-governance never achieved by any central authority in history.

### Hydraulic Despotism

The construction of common canal systems and shared irrigation necessitates community organization and self-control. By contrast, hydraulic despotism—as found in colonized areas—predisposes societies to exploitation and centralized government. Water is, in all sane or democratic societies, regarded as a public resource. This distinction between community-managed and state-managed water systems has profound implications for political organization.

### Ifugao Productivity Profile

Growing 0.5 hectares of padi rice and 0.25 ha of sweet potato as staples, with 1 ha of woodlot and grove crop, domestic pigs and chickens fed from gleanings and crop, the Ifugao family of 5 spends 400 person-days of productive labour per year, or 80 days per able individual. Rainfall is an incredible 3 to 6 meters per year, necessitating constant terrace maintenance. The terraces have only 2 cm of soil, and half the mass of the bunds is in stone. Canal and ditch maintenance necessitate annual earth and stone repairs; the equivalent value or objective cost is one day of rice for one hour of work.

## Building a Global Network

Mollison envisions building a global, interdependent, and cooperative body of people involved in ethical land and resource use. This network's teaching would be founded on research but also locally available everywhere, and locally demonstrable in many thousands of small enterprises. The scope would cover the whole range of human endeavours, from primary production to quaternary system management, from domestic nutrition and economy to a global network of small financial systems.

Such work is described as urgent, important, and necessary—and something that cannot be left to the whims of government (always short-term) or industry as it is currently structured.

## The Need for Ethical Professionals

The chapter argues that society has far more competent farmers and engineers than it has ethical bankers or lawyers whose work relates to curing or preventing (not just treating) social and environmental problems. The implication is that legal and financial reform are at least as important as agricultural innovation in creating sustainable societies. Skills of land management, while necessary, are insufficient without parallel reform in the economic and legal systems that shape how land is used, owned, and governed.

## Wealth as Regional Productivity

In the present fiscal or money-run world, the primary responsibility that people need to take charge of is their wealth—which Mollison defines as the product of their sweat and their region, not representable by valueless currency. This reframing has practical implications:

- **Regional food systems** that keep value circulating within communities rather than extracting it to distant markets
- **[[local-currencies]]** or barter systems that are not subject to inflationary monetary policy
- **Land stewardship** that builds real capital (soil, water, biodiversity) rather than financial speculation
- **Skill-based economics** where practical knowledge and productive capacity are the primary forms of wealth

## From Technical Skills to Social Strategies

Mollison's progression through the book mirrors the journey from individual technique to collective action. The preceding chapters cover well-tried and common-sense techniques of earth restoration. But all of this comes to naught if society continues to invest in arms and destruction, to permit land abuse, and to fail to tackle the social and political impediments to reclaiming desertified and abused lands, or even to prevent the poisoning of land.

The strategies presented in Chapter 14 may, in fact, be of more assistance to real change than the skills of land management, for society has far more competent farmers and engineers than it has ethical professionals working to cure or prevent social and environmental problems.

## The Biocide Imperative

Running throughout the aquaculture and broader design chapters is the imperative for minimal to zero biocide use near or in waterways. This principle extends to all land management: the susceptibility of living systems to poisons means that sustainable design must exclude chemical interventions almost entirely. This has political implications—it requires resisting agricultural chemical companies, industrial pollution, and regulatory frameworks that permit the poisoning of water and soil.

## Spiritual and Mystical Dimensions

Mollison acknowledges but deliberately sets aside spiritual and mystical dimensions of human experience—not because these are not normal parts of human experience, but because they are arrived at as a result of long contemplation or intense involvement with the mysteries that eternally surround us. These are things that nobody else can help us with, and they cannot be demanded, defined, or taught. In a book written to help people make real-life decisions, to build new landscapes, and to regenerate damaged forests, the present need is for clear and practical approaches.

## Design Implications

For permaculture designers, these strategies suggest:

- **Start locally**: Put your own household and community systems in order first
- **Build demonstration sites**: Locally demonstrable examples are more persuasive than abstract arguments
- **Develop community organization**: Shared resource systems (water, irrigation, common lands) require and build self-governance
- **Resist centralization**: Design systems that reduce dependence on distant power structures
- **Redefine wealth**: Measure prosperity in land health, food security, and community resilience rather than monetary terms
- **Teach others**: The goal is not personal self-sufficiency in isolation but building a cooperative network
- **Address economics and law**: Technical land management skills must be complemented by work on financial and legal structures
- **Urgency and necessity**: This work is urgent, important, and necessary—it cannot be left to the whims of government (always short-term) or industry as currently structured
- **Global cooperation**: Build an interdependent, cooperative body of people involved in ethical land and resource use
- **Comprehensive scope**: Cover the whole range of human endeavours, from primary production to quaternary system management, from domestic nutrition to global financial networks

## Self-Governance Through Shared Resources

The construction of common canal systems and shared irrigation necessitates community organization and self-control. Hydraulic despotism, as found in colonized areas, predisposes societies to exploitation and centralized government. Water is, in all sane or democratic societies, regarded as a public resource. This distinction between community-managed and state-managed water systems has profound implications for political organization.

The creators of long-lasting sustainable systems like the Ifugao terraces have developed a self-governance never achieved by any central authority in history. This is not merely an incidental observation—it is a fundamental principle: sustainable resource management requires distributed decision-making power. Centralized control, by contrast, creates the conditions for hydraulic despotism, where control of water becomes a mechanism for political domination.

## The Teaching Imperative

First we must learn to grow, build, and manage natural systems for human and earth needs, and then teach others to do so. In this way, we can build a global, interdependent, and cooperative body of people involved in ethical land and resource use. The teaching must be:

- **Founded on research** but also locally available everywhere
- **Locally demonstrable** in many thousands of small enterprises
- **Covering the whole range** of human endeavours from primary production to quaternary system management
- **From domestic nutrition and economy** to a global network of small financial systems

The scope is deliberately comprehensive: from domestic nutrition and economy to a global network of small financial systems. Such work is urgent, important, and necessary, and we cannot leave it to the whims of government (always short-term) or industry as we know it today.

## Inverting the Dependency Relationship

Mollison's core insight is that we already have all the knowledge and biological materials needed to solve every food, clean energy, and sensible shelter problem in every climate. The barrier is not technical but political and economic. We should cease to look to power structures, hierarchical systems, or governments to help us, and devise ways to help ourselves.

This means building systems that are:
- **Locally self-reliant** in food, water, energy, and shelter
- **Economically independent** from extractive global markets
- **Politically autonomous** through distributed governance structures
- **Ecologically regenerative** rather than merely sustainable

## Connection to Aquaculture Design

The [[aquaculture-design-checklist]] from the preceding chapter gains additional meaning when viewed through the lens of Chapter 14's strategies. The emphasis on minimal biocide use, integrated polycultures, downstream utilization, and community organization are not merely technical recommendations—they are political acts that resist the industrial agriculture model and its dependence on chemical inputs, monoculture, and centralized supply chains.

The traditional water cultures described—the Ifugao, the chinampa builders, the Nile flood farmers—demonstrate that the most productive and enduring systems are also the most politically autonomous. Their productivity is inseparable from their self-governance.
