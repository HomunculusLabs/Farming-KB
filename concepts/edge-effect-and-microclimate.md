# Edge Effect and Microclimate

Edges are the interfaces between two systems or media, and they are where the most diversity, productivity, and exchange occur. Holmgren's Principle 11 ("Use Edges and Value the Marginal") and Mollison's pattern understanding both emphasise edge as a primary design tool. See [[permaculture-design-methods]] for how edge analysis fits the design process.

## The Theory of Edge

The edge between any two systems is where energy and materials are exchanged. This is true at every scale:

- **Global**: Coastlines -- shallow coastal waters fed by land nutrients support enormous diversity vs. barren deep ocean
- **Landscape**: Ecotones -- where two bioregions overlap, species from both intermingle, creating greater biodiversity than either region alone
- **Farm**: Hedgerows, pond edges, forest margins -- the most productive and diverse zones
- **Micro**: Root surfaces, soil particle interfaces, lung tissue -- maximum surface area for exchange

"Edges are dynamic and productive parts of all natural systems where exchange of materials and energy take place." (Holmgren)

Key insight: "Increasing edge is one important way to increase system intensity and productivity. It is possible to see increasing complexity of edge as an alternative mode of growth and development to gross system expansion."

## Types of Edge in Design

### Forest/Field Edge
The transition between forest and open land supports more bird species than either system alone because resources of both are available. Design hedgerows as managed forest edges:

- Plant in multiple layers (tall trees, understory, shrubs, ground cover)
- Include fruiting species for wildlife and harvest ([[permaculture-plant-catalog]])
- Use low-fire-potential species where needed
- Hedgerows provide windbreak, wildlife corridor, and forage simultaneously

### Pond/Water Edge
The littoral zone of ponds and waterways is the most biologically productive:

- Shallow shelves (0-30 cm deep) support emergent and marginal plants
- Gradual slopes support more edge species than steep drop-offs
- Riparian vegetation along streams is denser and more diverse than upslope vegetation
- Design pond edges with varying depths and shelf widths for maximum productivity ([[pond-and-aquaculture]])

### Ecotones
Where bioregions meet (altitude, latitude, coastal transitions), species overlap creates unique conditions:

- Three-way ecotones are especially diverse (e.g., warm temperate / cool temperate / dry plateau)
- Sites near ecotones support unusual species combinations and weather patterns
- Observe ecotone locations in your region for plant selection insights

## Creating Microclimates

Microclimates are local climate variations caused by topography, vegetation, water, and structures.

### Thermal Mass
Stone walls, water bodies, and earth banks absorb heat by day and release it at night:

- South-facing stone walls extend the growing season
- Ponds moderate adjacent temperature by 2-5 degrees C
- Earth-sheltered structures use ground temperature stability

### Wind Protection
Windbreaks create sheltered zones extending 10-15x their height downwind:

- Multi-layered hedgerows (evergreen + deciduous) provide year-round and seasonal wind protection
- Gap-free windbreaks are more effective than porous ones for close protection
- Permeable windbreaks reduce turbulence better than solid walls at distance

### Frost Management
Cold air drains downhill and collects in low points:

- Avoid placing frost-sensitive plants in valley bottoms
- Stone walls and dense vegetation slow cold air drainage
- Water bodies moderate frost by releasing heat
- Slope position matters: mid-slope positions are often frost-free when valley floors freeze

### Shade and Sun Traps
Deciduous trees create seasonal microclimates:

- Summer shade under canopy, winter sun through bare branches
- Evergreen windbreaks on north (southern hemisphere: south) side, deciduous on east/west for seasonal light
- Sun traps formed by U-shaped plantings or walls facing the equator

## Designing for Maximum Edge

1. **Winding paths** instead of straight lines increase path/garden edge
2. **Crescent-shaped garden beds** or keyhole beds maximise perimeter-to-area ratio
3. **Pond design**: Irregular shorelines with bays, peninsulas, islands, and varying depth shelves
4. **Hedgerow networks**: Connect Zone 5 wilderness to Zone 2-3 production areas
5. **Intercropping**: Row mixes and polycultures create more species interfaces than monocultures

## The Traditional English Landscape

Holmgren cites old English farm landscapes as the archetypal edge-rich design: "irregular fields bordered by hedgerows, with woods and small copses, with tightly clustered villages and a network of roads and lanes, streams, ponds and drains -- in other words, it was full of carefully maintained edges." The shift to large monocultures eliminated this complexity, reducing both environmental value and agricultural resilience.
