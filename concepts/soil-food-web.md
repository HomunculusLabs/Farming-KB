# Soil Food Web

The soil food web is the interconnected community of organisms living in soil — from bacteria and fungi to nematodes, earthworms, and beetles. These organisms decompose organic matter, cycle nutrients, build soil structure, and suppress disease. Healthy soil has a diverse, balanced food web; degraded soil has a simplified, dysfunctional one.

## Why Soil Biology Matters

Plants don't eat fertilizer — they absorb mineral nutrients that soil organisms make available through biological processes. The soil food web is the engine that drives nutrient cycling:

1. **Bacteria and fungi** decompose organic matter and rock minerals
2. **Protozoa and nematodes** eat bacteria and fungi, releasing plant-available nutrients (the "microbial loop")
3. **Microarthropods** shred organic matter, increasing surface area for decomposition
4. **Earthworms** burrow, aerate, and create nutrient-rich casts
5. **Plants** exude sugars from roots to feed specific microbes (the "rhizosphere effect")

This is the foundation of Korean Natural Farming, JADAM, living soil cannabis cultivation, and all biological agriculture. As Master Cho states: "In non-chemical agriculture we do not feed the plant. We nurture the soil and the soil nurtures the plant."

## The Two-Tier Selection Model (Plant Microbiome)

Research on cannabis (Winston et al. 2014) has revealed a general model that likely applies to most plants:

### Tier 1: Soil-Driven Selection
Bulk soil and rhizosphere communities are shaped primarily by **edaphic (soil) factors**:
- Nitrogen content (strongest predictor)
- Salinity
- Organic carbon content
- Water content
- ph

This means: **change the soil, change the microbiome**. Amendments, compost, organic matter, and mineral balance directly determine which microbes thrive.

### Tier 2: Plant-Driven Selection
Root interior (endorhiza) communities are shaped primarily by **plant genotype**. The plant's immune system selectively allows certain microbes inside root tissue. Different cultivars of the same species recruit different internal microbial partners.

**Research evidence** (Winston et al. 2014):
- 71 significant OTU abundance differences between cannabis strains, but zero presence/absence differences — cultivar affects *abundance*, not *which* microbes are present
- Endorhiza shared significantly more OTUs with their own soil than with foreign soil (mean 2934 vs 2162 shared OTUs, p = 1.2e-15)
- Dramatic phylum shifts from bulk soil to root interior: Acidobacteria decrease sharply (especially order iii1-15, p = 1.12e-7), while Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria increase
- Alpha diversity drops ~33% from bulk soil to endorhiza, but endorhiza diversity does NOT differ between soil types — the plant imposes a diversity ceiling

**Implication**: You cannot force a specific endorhiza community — but you CAN build a diverse Tier 1 soil from which the plant selects its preferred partners. The richer the soil microbial diversity, the better the plant can find its optimal symbionts. See [[cannabis-root-microbiome]] for full details.

## The Soil Food Web Trophic Levels

### Level 1: Decomposers (Primary)
**Bacteria** (most numerous soil organisms):
- Decompose simple organic compounds (sugars, proteins, amino acids)
- Dominant in disturbed, annual crop, and bacterial-dominated soils
- Preferred food of protozoa — the bacterial loop is fast nutrient cycling
- Key groups: Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Rhizobium, Nitrosomonas, Cellulomonas
- Pseudomonas species are core cannabis root associates (PGPR, phosphate solubilizers)

**Fungi** (less numerous but greater biomass):
- Decompose complex organic compounds (lignin, cellulose, chitin)
- Dominant in undisturbed, perennial, and forest soils
- Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with 90%+ of plant species
- Key groups: Saprophytic fungi (decomposers), Mycorrhizae (symbionts), Endophytes (root colonizers)
- Mortierellaceae — early successional fungi found in cannabis roots, promote plant growth

### Level 2: Grazers (Secondary)
**Protozoa** (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates):
- Eat bacteria — each protozoan eats 10,000+ bacteria per day
- Release plant-available nitrogen (ammonium) through excretion
- Flagellates dominate in waterlogged soils, amoebae in moist soils, ciliates in anaerobic conditions
- Protozoa populations indicate soil health — high numbers = active bacterial loop

**Bacterial-feeding nematodes**:
- Graze on bacteria, release plant-available nutrients
- Important in bacterial-dominated soils (vegetable gardens, annual crops)
- Some species are plant parasites — diversity keeps them in check

### Level 3: Predators (Tertiary)
**Fungal-feeding nematodes**:
- Graze on fungi, release plant-available nutrients
- Important in fungal-dominated soils (forests, perennials, no-till)

**Predatory nematodes**:
- Eat other nematodes (including root-feeding ones)
- Natural biological control of plant-parasitic nematodes

**Microarthropods** (springtails, mites):
- Shred organic matter — increase surface area for bacterial/fungal decomposition
- Disperse fungal spores and bacterial colonies
- Springtails (Collembola) indicate healthy fungal activity

### Level 4: Higher Predators
**Earthworms**:
- "Nature's tillers" (Cho) — burrow through soil, creating channels for air and water
- Consume organic matter and soil, produce nutrient-rich casts (5-11x more N, P, K than surrounding soil)
- Stimulate microbial activity — worm gut is a microbial incubator
- Surface castings contain high populations of beneficial bacteria (Azotobacter, Pseudomonas) and actinomycetes
- Indicator of healthy, well-aerated soil with adequate organic matter

**Ground beetles, centipedes, ants, spiders**:
- Top predators of the soil food web
- Control pest populations
- Ants aerate soil and bring organic matter underground

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Dominance (Ingham's Ranges)

Per Dr. Elaine Ingham's Soil Foodweb research, different plant types and successional stages require specific soil food web structures. The bacteria:fungi ratio determines which plants thrive:

| Soil Type | Bacteria:Fungi | Best For |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Disturbed/conventional | 100:1+ | Weed succession, bare soil |
| Vegetable annuals | 10:1 - 100:1 | Brassicas, lettuce, annual herbs |
| Row crops (corn, wheat) | 5:1 - 20:1 | Grasses, grains |
| Pasture/grassland | 1:1 - 10:1 | Grasses, grazing |
| Perennial shrubs | 1:1 - 5:1 | Berries, small fruit, grapes |
| Trees/forest | 1:10 - 1:100 | Fruit trees, timber, forest |
| Old-growth forest | 1:100 - 1:1000 | Mature ecosystems |

**Cannabis** prefers a slightly fungal-dominated soil (similar to perennial crops), especially during flowering. Rhizophagus intraradices and Funneliformis mosseae are documented cannabis mycorrhizal partners. The core cannabis endorhiza includes both bacterial groups (Pseudomonas, Cellvibrio) and fungal/actinomycete groups (Actinomycetales, Mortierellaceae).

### Ingham's Desired Biomass Ranges (ug/g soil)

| Organism Group | Vegetables/Annuals | Pasture/Grass | Shrubs/Berries | Trees/Forest |
|---------------|-------------------|---------------|----------------|--------------|
| Total bacteria | 100-1000 | 100-500 | 100-500 | 50-300 |
| Active bacteria | 10-150 | 10-100 | 10-75 | 5-50 |
| Total fungi | 10-100 | 50-300 | 100-500 | 500-3000 |
| Active fungi | 5-50 | 25-150 | 50-250 | 100-1000 |
| Protozoa (flagellates) | 1000-10000/g | 1000-10000/g | 5000-50000/g | 5000-50000/g |
| Beneficial nematodes | 10-100/g | 10-50/g | 20-100/g | 30-200/g |

## Soil Biology Indicators

| Healthy Sign | Unhealthy Sign |
|-------------|----------------|
| Earthworms visible, dark crumbly structure | No earthworms, compacted soil |
| Sweet earthy smell, fungal threads visible | Sour/putrid smell, no fungal growth |
| Mulch decomposes within months | Mulch persists for years |
| Roots branching and dense, water infiltrates | Roots stunted, water pools or runs off |
