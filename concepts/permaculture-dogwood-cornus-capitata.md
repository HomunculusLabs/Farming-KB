# Dogwood (Cornus capitata)

The Himalayan strawberry tree or evergreen dogwood (Cornus capitata) is an attractive evergreen shrub or small tree native to the Himalayas and southwestern China. It is valued in permaculture systems for its ornamental qualities, wildlife habitat, and edible fruit.

## Botany and Growth Habit

An evergreen shrub or small tree reaching 6-12 meters, with leathery dark green leaves that provide year-round cover. The large, showy flower heads are surrounded by conspicuous creamy-white bracts in summer, giving a display similar to the better-known flowering dogwoods. The strawberry-like red fruits follow in autumn and persist into winter, providing extended visual interest and wildlife food during the lean season.

The bark is smooth and gray-brown when young, becoming rougher with age. The branching pattern is naturally spreading and architectural, making it an excellent specimen tree. Growth rate is moderate — expect 30-60 cm per year in favorable conditions.

The species is sometimes referred to as thammal or thanboi in its native range. It should not be confused with Arbutus unedo, which also bears the common name "strawberry tree."

## Fruit and Uses

The round, red, strawberry-like fruits are about 2-3 cm in diameter, produced in late autumn. While not as flavorful as true strawberries, they are edible raw, with a mildly sweet, mealy texture. They can also be cooked into preserves or used in baking. More significantly, the fruits are eagerly consumed by birds and small mammals, making this tree a valuable wildlife food source.

In [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] systems, the primary value of the fruit is as bird forage. By feeding birds through autumn and winter, the tree supports insectivorous bird populations that provide critical pest control services during the growing season. This ecological function is arguably more valuable than the direct food yield.

The shrub can also be included in a chicken forage system, as the fallen berries are popular with poultry.

## Climate and Soil Requirements

The evergreen dogwood prefers a sheltered position in full sun to partial shade. It requires well-drained, humus-rich soil and regular moisture during establishment, though it becomes moderately drought tolerant once established. Frost tolerance extends to approximately -10°C when mature, though young plants need protection for their first two to three winters. It is best suited to warm temperate and subtropical climates (USDA zones 8-9).

Flower buds may be damaged by excessive winter cold, which can reduce the following season's fruit set.

## Permaculture Functions

- **Ornamental value:** Spectacular bracts and persistent red fruits provide extended visual interest from summer through winter
- **Wildlife habitat:** Dense evergreen foliage provides year-round shelter for birds; fruits sustain birds through autumn and winter when other food is scarce
- **Pest control support:** By feeding insectivorous birds in winter, maintains the bird population that controls garden pests during the growing season
- **Bee forage:** Summer flowers attract pollinators, supporting overall orchard productivity
- **Understory tree:** Tolerates partial shade, suited to the mid-layer of food forest systems beneath taller canopy trees
- **Erosion control:** The spreading root system helps stabilize soil on slopes
- **Zone 2-3 plant:** Ornamental and productive value justifies placement in zones closer to living areas
- **Shelter belt:** Makes a good quick-growing shelter belt and responds well to clipping
- **Fire retardant:** The shrub has fire retardant properties, making it useful in fire-sensitive landscape designs

## Companion Plants

Dogwood combines well with other evergreen understory shrubs and benefits from the nitrogen-fixing canopy of nurse trees during establishment. It pairs well with other Cornus species for extended seasonal interest. Good companions include permaculture-tagasaste|tagasaste as a nitrogen-fixing overstory, and shade-tolerant ground covers like comfrey or sweet woodruff beneath.

## Propagation

Propagated from seed, which requires cold stratification for 2-3 months. Semi-hardwood cuttings taken in late summer can also be used, though success rates are moderate. The plant begins flowering within 3-5 years from seed. Layering of low branches is another reliable propagation method.

## Maintenance

Minimal once established. Prune only to remove dead wood or maintain shape — the natural branching habit is one of its ornamental strengths. Mulch well to retain moisture and suppress weeds during establishment. Watch for dogwood anthracnose in humid climates, though Cornus capitata is more resistant than deciduous dogwood species.

## Pruning and Training

Minimal pruning is needed for Cornus capitata. The natural habit is spreading and architectural. Remove dead, damaged, or crossing branches in late winter or early spring before new growth begins. Avoid heavy pruning, which can spoil the natural form. If shaping is desired, do it gradually over several years rather than making dramatic cuts.

## Pests and Diseases

Cornus capitata is relatively pest-free. The main concern in humid climates is dogwood anthracnose (Discula destructiva), though this species shows greater resistance than deciduous dogwoods. Good air circulation and avoidance of overhead watering help prevent fungal issues. Leaf spot can occur in damp conditions but is rarely serious enough to warrant treatment.

## Landscape Design Applications

In permaculture design, Cornus capitata serves multiple functions beyond food production. Its evergreen foliage makes it an excellent screening plant, providing year-round privacy. The showy bracts and red fruits create strong visual accents in the landscape. It works well as a specimen tree in lawns, as part of mixed hedgerows, or as an understory tree in food forests. Its moderate size makes it suitable for smaller properties where larger trees would be overwhelming.

## Varieties and Related Species

Several named cultivars exist, selected for variations in flower size, fruit production, and cold hardiness. Related species worth considering include Cornus kousa (Kousa dogwood, deciduous but with excellent edible fruit), Cornus mas (Cornelian cherry, with tart fruits used for preserves), and Cornus florida (flowering dogwood, primarily ornamental). Each has different characteristics that may suit particular site conditions and design goals.

## Pollination and Flower Biology

Cornus capitata produces its distinctive inflorescences in early to mid-summer, with the true flowers forming a dense central cluster surrounded by four to six large, creamy-white bracts that serve as visual attractants for pollinators. The individual flowers are small and tubular, containing both male and female reproductive organs. Pollination is primarily entomophilous (insect-pollinated), with bees, butterflies, and other nectar-seeking insects serving as the main pollinators. The extended flowering period, which can last several weeks, provides sustained forage for pollinators during a time when many other tree crops have finished blooming. Flower bud formation occurs in the previous growing season, meaning that severe winter cold or drought stress can reduce the following year's display. Cross-pollination between different Cornus capitata individuals generally improves fruit set, so planting multiple specimens is recommended where reliable fruit production for wildlife is desired.

## Soil Ecology and Mycorrhizal Associations

Cornus capitata supports a diverse soil ecology beneath its canopy. The evergreen leaf litter it produces is moderately decomposable, contributing a steady supply of organic matter to the soil surface throughout the year. Like many Cornus species, C. capitata forms arbuscular mycorrhizal associations that enhance its nutrient uptake, particularly for phosphorus and micronutrients. The root system is relatively shallow and spreading, which makes it compatible with understory plantings that access deeper soil layers. The combination of leaf litter, mycorrhizal networks, and moderate shade creates a favorable microhabitat for soil organisms including earthworms, beneficial fungi, and microarthropods. In permaculture food forests, this soil-building function complements the more visible roles of wildlife habitat and ornamental display. The tree's root exudates may also support beneficial microbial communities that suppress soil-borne pathogens, contributing to the overall health of the plant guild.

## Nutritional Composition of Fruit

While Cornus capitata fruit is not considered a primary food crop, it has measurable nutritional value that contributes to its role as wildlife forage. The fruit contains moderate levels of sugars (primarily glucose and fructose), vitamin C, and several phenolic compounds with antioxidant properties. The flesh is relatively low in fat and protein compared to dedicated fruit crops. The seeds within the fruit are not typically consumed by humans but are dispersed by birds and mammals that eat the fruit whole. Research on related Cornus species has identified anthocyanins, flavonoids, and ellagic acid derivatives in the fruit, compounds associated with anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects. For human consumption, the fruit is best used in cooked preparations such as preserves, jams, or baked goods, where the mildly sweet flavor can be enhanced with added sweeteners and complementary spices.

## Carbon Sequestration Potential

As an evergreen tree, Cornus capitata provides year-round carbon sequestration, which is a significant consideration in permaculture and regenerative agriculture design. The evergreen habit means continuous photosynthesis during mild winter periods when deciduous trees are dormant. A mature specimen at the upper end of its size range (12 meters height, spreading canopy) can sequester an estimated 10-25 kg of carbon dioxide per year through net primary productivity. The woody biomass accumulated in trunk, branches, and root system represents long-term carbon storage. When incorporated into hedgerows or shelterbelts with multiple Cornus capitata specimens, the cumulative carbon sequestration becomes substantial. Additionally, the tree's contribution to soil organic carbon through leaf litter decomposition and root turnover provides a belowground carbon sink that is often overlooked in aboveground biomass calculations. In fire-prone landscapes, the species' fire-retardant properties help protect existing carbon stocks from combustion.

## Design Integration Examples

Cornus capitata can be integrated into permaculture designs in several specific configurations. As a mid-layer food forest tree, it occupies the space between tall canopy trees (chestnut, oak, apple) and low shrubs (currants, berries), filling a structural niche that many food forests lack. In windbreak design, its evergreen foliage provides year-round wind protection, making it more effective than deciduous species for winter wind mitigation. As a screen or hedge, multiple specimens planted 2-3 meters apart create a dense evergreen barrier that provides privacy, wind reduction, and wildlife corridor connectivity simultaneously. In public or demonstration permaculture gardens, the showy bracts and persistent fruit make it an excellent educational specimen that draws attention to the multi-functional design principles on display. The species also works well in riparian buffer plantings where its tolerance of regular moisture and soil-stabilizing root system provide erosion control along waterways.

## Cultural History and Ethnobotany

Cornus capitata has a long history of use and appreciation in its native range across the eastern Himalayas, southwestern China, and northern India. In traditional Himalayan communities, the fruit has been gathered as a supplementary food source during autumn months when other fresh fruits are scarce. The wood, though not commercially harvested at scale, is dense and fine-grained, suitable for small woodworking projects, tool handles, and fuel.

In traditional Chinese medicine, several Cornus species have documented medicinal uses: Cornus officinalis (shan zhu yu) is a well-established remedy for kidney and liver conditions, and while C. capitata is less studied in this context, its fruit shares some of the same phenolic and iridoid glycoside compounds found in medicinally used Cornus species. Research on related Cornus fruits has identified anthocyanins, flavonoids, and iridoid glycosides with documented anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and antioxidant properties.

The plant was introduced to Western horticulture in the early 19th century by plant collectors working in the Himalayas, and it quickly gained popularity in temperate gardens for its distinctive evergreen habit and showy bracts. The Royal Horticultural Society has awarded C. capitata the Award of Garden Merit, recognizing its reliability and ornamental value in UK gardens.

In Bhutan and parts of northeastern India, the tree holds cultural significance and is sometimes planted near temples and homesteads as a multi-purpose ornamental and ecological tree. The common name "Himalayan strawberry tree" reflects both its geographic origin and the superficial resemblance of its fruit to strawberries, though the two are unrelated botanically. In Nepal, the species is occasionally used as a boundary marker between agricultural plots, taking advantage of its dense, evergreen growth habit to create living fences that also provide seasonal fruit for wildlife and livestock.

The species has also become naturalized in parts of southeastern Australia and New Zealand, where it was introduced as an ornamental plant and has spread into adjacent forests and scrublands. In these regions, C. capitata is sometimes considered a minor weed due to its ability to establish in disturbed areas, though its ecological impact is generally regarded as low compared to more aggressive invasive species. Its value as a wildlife food source is recognized even in areas where it is naturalized, with native bird species readily consuming the fruit and facilitating seed dispersal.

In cultivation, C. capitata is relatively easy from seed, though the cold stratification requirement means planning ahead is necessary. The species is worth considering for permaculture designs not only for its ecological functions but also as a conversation piece — its unusual combination of evergreen foliage, showy bracts, and strawberry-like fruit makes it a distinctive and memorable element in any planting scheme.

For growers in suitable climates, C. capitata rewards patience with minimal maintenance demands once established. Its multi-functional role as ornamental, wildlife support, soil builder, and edible fruit producer exemplifies the permaculture principle of stacking functions in a single element, making it a strong candidate for food forest mid-layers and mixed hedgerows alike.
