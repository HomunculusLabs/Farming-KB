# Brassinosteroid Effects on Psilocybe cubensis Mycelial Growth and Fruiting

## Overview

In 1990, Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt reported the first documented growth-promoting effect of a brassinosteroid on a fungus. Application of 10⁻² ppm (0.01 ppm) synthetic 22S,23S-homobrassinolide to cultures of *Psilocybe cubensis* dramatically accelerated mycelial colonisation, advanced the onset of fruiting, increased flush size and total dry mass, and nearly eliminated the formation of incomplete (aborted) fruit bodies. This finding extended the known biological activity of brassinosteroids — previously considered strictly plant phytohormones — into the fungal kingdom.

## Brassinosteroids: A New Class of Growth Regulators

Brassinosteroids are steroidal plant-growth regulators regarded by several laboratories as a further group of phytohormones. At the time of this study, approximately 30 members had been detected across a wide variety of higher plants including Angiospermae and Gymnospermae, as well as in a green alga. Their strong growth-promoting activity involves complex physiological effects, including increases in both cell elongation and cell division. Promising results in the application of such compounds to produce higher crop yields and anti-stress effects had been reported, but no studies on the occurrence or activity of brassinosteroids on fungi had been published prior to this work.

The specific brassinosteroid used was 22S,23S-homobrassinolide, a C₂₈ brassinosteroid synthesised from stigmasterol. Stigmasterol is a readily available phytosterol isolated from soybean oil and other plant sources, making the starting material inexpensive and abundant. The synthesis from stigmasterol to 22S,23S-homobrassinolide involves multiple steps including selective oxidation, introduction of the 22,23-diol, and construction of the side chain. This compound contains the characteristic brassinosteroid structural features: a 2α,3α-diol in ring A, a 22α,23α-diol on the side chain, a 6-ketone in ring B, and a lactone spanning C-16 and C-17 (the B-ring lactone is replaced by a ketone in homobrassinolide). The stereochemistry at C-22 and C-23 is S,S configuration, which is the biologically active form found in plants.

## Experimental Design

### Fungal Strain

The study used a strain of *Psilocybe cubensis* (Earle) Singer previously described by Gartz, characterised by high stability in growth and fruiting behaviour. This strain consistency was important for reproducibility across the ten replicate runs performed for each condition. The strain was maintained on 6% malt agar and was subcultured prior to each experimental run to ensure active, vigorous mycelium. The choice of *P. cubensis* as the test organism reflects both its relevance as a model psychoactive fungus and its relative ease of cultivation compared to other *Psilocybe* species, which often have more exacting substrate and environmental requirements.

### Cultivation Systems

Two parallel cultivation systems were employed to test the brassinosteroid effect:

1. **Malt agar system:** 6% malt agar plates, assessing radial mycelial growth rate.
2. **Dung/grain substrate system:** Horse dung mixed with rice grain in water, used to produce fruiting bodies. This substrate mimics the natural substrate of *Psilocybe cubensis*, which in the wild fruits on bovine and equine dung in tropical and subtropical grasslands. The horse dung provides a nitrogen-rich, complex organic substrate while the rice grain serves as a supplemental carbohydrate source. Both systems contained 5 g KH₂PO₄ per litre of substrate as a buffer/mineral supplement, which helps maintain pH stability during mycelial growth and provides phosphorus and potassium for metabolic processes.

### Dose

The brassinosteroid was applied at a concentration of 10⁻² ppm (0.01 mg/L or 10 ppb). This extremely low dose is consistent with the known high biological potency of brassinosteroids in plants, where effects are typically observed at nanomolar concentrations. The authors do not report testing other concentrations, so the dose-response relationship remains unknown. It is possible that even lower concentrations could produce measurable effects, or that higher concentrations might produce stronger growth promotion with more pronounced morphological changes. Ten replicate runs were conducted for each system to establish statistical reliability of the observed effects.

## Results: Mycelial Growth on Malt Agar

On malt agar, the brassinosteroid-treated cultures showed mycelial growth that was **two to three times faster** than the untreated control series. This dramatic acceleration was consistent across all ten replicates and was one of the most striking findings of the study. The mechanism by which the brassinosteroid promotes such rapid mycelial extension is not fully understood but likely involves stimulation of both cell elongation and cell division, as has been documented in plant systems. In plants, brassinosteroids promote cell elongation by activating plasma membrane H⁺-ATPases and by upregulating genes involved in cell wall loosening (expansins and xyloglucan endotransglycosylases). Whether analogous mechanisms operate in fungal hyphae — where cell wall composition differs significantly from plant cell walls — remains an open question.

The rapid colonisation observed on agar has direct practical implications: faster colonisation means shorter incubation periods, reduced risk of contamination, and faster throughput in cultivation operations. A culture that might take three weeks to fully colonise a substrate without brassinosteroid could potentially be ready in one to two weeks with treatment. Faster colonisation also means that the window of vulnerability to contamination is significantly shortened, since uncolonised substrate is highly susceptible to invasion by competing moulds and bacteria.

## Results: Fruiting on Dung/Grain Substrate

### Accelerated Onset of Fruiting

The most commercially significant finding was the advancement of the first fruiting flush. In the control cultures, the first sporocarps (fruit bodies) appeared within **4 to 5 weeks** after inoculation. With the addition of brassinosteroid, fruiting began in just **3 to 3.5 weeks** — a reduction of approximately one week, representing a 20–30% acceleration of the total cultivation cycle. For commercial mushroom cultivation, where time-to-harvest directly impacts profitability and facility throughput, such an improvement is substantial. The figure accompanying the original paper visually demonstrates this difference: at five weeks post-inoculation, the control tray shows relatively sparse fruiting while the brassinosteroid-treated tray at only three weeks already displays a dense cluster of well-developed mushrooms.

### Increased Flush Size

The first flush showed a remarkable increase in the number of fruit bodies produced. Control cultures produced **1 to 3 mushrooms** in the first flush, while brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced **4 to 7 mushrooms** — a two- to three-fold increase in first-flush yield. This larger initial flush suggests that the brassinosteroid promotes more uniform and extensive [[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]] across the substrate surface. In standard cultivation, primordia density is often limited by the availability of nutrients and moisture at the casing-substrate interface, and it is possible that the faster mycelial growth and improved [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] under brassinosteroid treatment allows the mycelium to support a larger number of simultaneously developing fruit bodies.

### Higher Total Dry Mass

The steroid-treated cultures afforded a remarkably higher dry mass than the control cultivations: **3.4–3.9 g versus 2.3–2.9 g per 10 g of substrate**. This represents an approximately 30–50% increase in [[stamets-biological-efficiency-yield]] (dry mass of mushrooms relative to dry mass of substrate). The increase is consistent with both the larger flush size and the likelihood of more flushes within a given time frame. The authors report observing results across five flushes during the fruiting period, with the brassinosteroid-treated cultures producing more total fruit bodies across all flushes compared to the control. This cumulative effect means that the yield advantage compounds over the entire cropping cycle, not just in the first flush.

### Suppression of Aborted Fruit Bodies

Incomplete fruit bodies (aborts) are a common problem in mushroom cultivation, where primordia initiate but fail to develop into mature mushrooms. The formation of such incomplete fruit bodies was **almost completely suppressed** by the brassinosteroid treatment. This is a particularly valuable effect because aborts represent wasted metabolic resources that the mycelium invested in initiating primordia, and they can serve as entry points for contamination by moulds and bacteria. The near-elimination of aborts suggests that the brassinosteroid promotes more uniform and vigorous development of initiated primordia, possibly by improving nutrient transport to developing fruit bodies or by enhancing the coordination between the [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] and individual fruit body primordia.

## Morphological Changes: Suppressed Annulus

An interesting and unexpected observation was that the brassinosteroid influenced the morphology of the fruit bodies. Specifically, the treated mushrooms **lacked the typical membranous annulus** (ring) on the stems. The annulus is the remnant of the partial veil that connects the cap margin to the stem during fruit body development and is a characteristic taxonomic feature of *Psilocybe cubensis* and many other agarics. In untreated control cultures, the annulus develops normally as the cap expands and the partial veil tears, leaving a skirt-like ring on the upper stem.

This morphological alteration suggests that brassinosteroids can interfere with normal developmental patterning in fungal fruit bodies. The partial veil may develop differently — either failing to form properly, or forming but then integrating into the cap or stem tissue rather than remaining as a distinct ring structure. This effect has implications for both taxonomy (since annulus presence is a diagnostic character) and for understanding how steroidal signals influence fungal morphogenesis. The authors do not provide histological analysis of the veil region, so the precise developmental origin of this suppression remains speculative.

## Practical Implications for Mushroom Cultivation

The results suggest several potential applications of brassinosteroids in mushroom cultivation:

1. **Reduced incubation time:** 2–3× faster colonisation and 1-week earlier fruiting could significantly reduce production timelines.
2. **Increased yield:** 30–50% higher dry mass per unit substrate improves biological efficiency.
3. **Improved quality:** Near-complete suppression of aborts means more uniform harvests and less waste.
4. **Very low dose required:** At 0.01 ppm, the cost of brassinosteroid supplementation would be minimal.
5. **Anti-stress potential:** Brassinosteroids are known to confer stress tolerance in plants; similar effects in fungi could improve culture resilience against temperature fluctuations, suboptimal moisture, or competitive microorganisms.
6. **Simplicity:** The brassinosteroid can be mixed directly into the substrate during preparation, requiring no special equipment or modified protocols.

However, the morphological changes (suppressed annulus) may be undesirable for certain applications where normal fruit body appearance is important.

## Limitations and Open Questions

The study was a short communication in *Naturwissenschaften* and as such presents limited experimental detail. It was conducted with only one brassinosteroid (22S,23S-homobrassinolide) at one concentration (10⁻² ppm) on one fungal strain of one species. No statistical analysis (standard deviations, p-values) was reported for the quantitative data. Key unanswered questions include:

- Whether the effect is generalisable to other *Psilocybe* species, to other psilocybin-containing fungi, or to commercially cultivated edible mushrooms (e.g., *[[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]*, *Pleurotus ostreatus*).
- The optimal dose range and whether higher concentrations produce diminishing returns, toxicity, or more pronounced morphological changes.
- Whether brassinosteroids are naturally produced by fungi as endogenous signalling molecules.
- The molecular mechanism of action — whether brassinosteroid receptors exist in fungi (analogous to BRI1 in plants) or whether the effect is mediated through non-specific membrane effects.
- Whether the morphological changes (annulus suppression) are dose-dependent and can be avoided at lower concentrations while retaining the growth-promoting effects.
- Whether the effect persists across multiple generations of subculturing or whether the fungus acclimates to the brassinosteroid stimulus.

## Biological Significance: Do Fungi Produce Brassinosteroids?

The finding that a plant brassinosteroid strongly stimulates fungal growth raises the question of whether fungi themselves produce brassinosteroid-like compounds as endogenous signalling molecules. If fungal brassinosteroids exist, they could play roles in regulating hyphal extension, branching patterns, and the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting — transitions that are analogous to the vegetative-to-reproductive transitions regulated by brassinosteroids in plants. The authors note that no studies on the occurrence of brassinosteroids in fungi had been published at the time, and suggest that brassinosteroids could play a physiological role in higher fungi. If fungi possess brassinosteroid receptors or biosynthetic pathways, this would represent a significant expansion of our understanding of fungal hormone biology and could explain some aspects of fungal development that are currently poorly understood. Further studies on commercially important species were noted as being in progress at the time of publication. The Gartz group was affiliated with the Institute of Biotechnology and the Institute of Plant Biochemistry of the Academy of Sciences of the GDR (East Germany), reflecting the broader Eastern European interest in brassinosteroid biology during the 1980s and early 1990s.

## Comparison with Other Growth Promoters

The growth-promoting effects of brassinosteroids on *P. cubensis* can be compared with other known fungal growth promoters. In mushroom cultivation, various supplements have been investigated to improve yield and colonisation speed, including vegetable oils (which increase biological efficiency by providing additional lipids), calcium salts, and various plant extracts. However, brassinosteroids are unique in that they operate at extremely low concentrations (parts per billion) and appear to act as true hormonal signals rather than simple nutritional supplements. The combination of accelerated colonisation, earlier fruiting, higher yield, and reduced aborts across multiple parameters is unusual for a single additive. Most growth promoters tend to improve one or two parameters while having neutral or negative effects on others. The brassinosteroid effect on *P. cubensis* appears to be a systemic enhancement of fungal metabolic activity rather than a targeted intervention on a specific developmental pathway.

## Historical Context

This 1990 study was published during a period of active brassinosteroid research. The first brassinosteroid (brassinolide) was isolated from rapeseed pollen by Grove et al. in 1979, and throughout the 1980s researchers worldwide were discovering new brassinosteroids and exploring their agricultural applications. The Gartz study is notable as one of the earliest reports of brassinosteroid activity outside the plant kingdom. It predates the cloning of the first brassinosteroid receptor (BRI1, identified in *Arabidopsis* in 1997) and the elucidation of brassinosteroid signalling pathways. Whether fungi possess receptors homologous to BRI1 remains an open and intriguing question that has not been systematically investigated.
