# Concrete and Cordwood Building

While concrete isn't the most ecological option, it is one of the simplest tools for the amateur homebuilder and is also relatively inexpensive. Using concrete in conjunction with green building materials like rocks or cordwood is still much greener than traditional building methods and more energy-efficient.

## Stone Construction (Slip Form Method)

Stone is time-consuming but worthwhile, especially on properties with lots of rocks. Collect stones during routine gardening — as you go through your garden, weeding or planting, you will inevitably come across many stones, which you can set aside in a rock pile for a building project.

### Two Methods

**Stack and Mortar:** Lay rocks along a straight line, carefully stacking and shaping with a hammer. Wall should be 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) thick with 1/2 inch (1 cm) mortar between rocks.

**Slip Form Method:** Build a wood framework that holds rocks in place while concrete hardens. Push the flat side of rocks against the outside wall for aesthetics; pour concrete behind. When the form is removed, you get beautiful flat stonework. Same dimensions as stack and mortar.

Both methods require a trench footer filled with concrete or a poured concrete slab first.

### Advantages

- Extremely long-lasting

- Impressive appearance (especially slip form)

- Minimal interior finishing needed

- Can add exterior insulation covered in stucco or wood siding

- You don't have to do much finishing work on the inside; covering the beautiful rockwork with drywall would be a shame

### Foundation Requirements

Every home should have a solid foundation regardless of building method. For stone construction, the footer trench must be filled with concrete or a poured slab must be laid first. In wet and cold climates, trenches need to be dug below the frost line. The foundation should be steel reinforced concrete (or rebar), especially in earthquake zones where flexibility in the structure above is also important.

## Cordwood Construction

Round wood discs ("face cords") at least 6 inches (15 cm) wide are stacked and sealed with concrete. The wood must be **debarked and dried** — if it shrinks after placement, holes form and bugs enter. But if dried too long, the wood splits.

### Process

1. Debark and partially dry logs

2. Cut into discs (face cords) at least 6 inches wide

3. Stack on their sides (outside looks "polka-dotted")

4. Seal with mortar/concrete between each piece

5. Wall forms similar to stone construction

### Key Points

- Wood lasts longer if NOT sealed

- Simple, cheap, foolproof method

- Requires little engineering knowledge

- More time-consuming than other methods

- Better insulation than many modern building methods

- The wood is stacked to form a wall the same way that a stone house is built

### Wood Selection and Drying

Choosing the right wood and timing the drying process is critical for cordwood construction. Softwoods like cedar and pine are commonly used because they dry faster and are easier to debark. The wood must be debarked when fresh — bark that remains will create gaps as it loosens over time.

After debarking, the logs should air-dry for at least several months but must be monitored closely. The ideal window is when the wood has dried enough to prevent significant shrinkage but has not dried so long that splitting occurs. Storing logs off the ground in a well-ventilated, covered area helps achieve this balance.

## Comparison: Slip Form vs. Cordwood

If you have a supply of rock, choose slip form — it looks more impressive and lasts even longer. Both methods are accessible to amateur builders and create durable, beautiful homes with minimal special skills. However, they are much more time-consuming than other methods.

Some insulation can be added to the outside of either type of wall and covered in stucco or wood siding, allowing you to retain the beauty of the natural wall on the interior.

## Concrete in Permaculture Building

While purists may avoid concrete entirely, it serves essential structural roles in many natural building methods. Steel reinforced concrete is recommended for foundations, footers, and earthquake-resistant construction. Concrete is also used for slip form stone walls, earthbag footers, and as the base for humanure composting systems.

The key permaculture principle is to use concrete sparingly and strategically — in the structural elements where strength and durability are non-negotiable — while using greener materials for the bulk of the walls and living spaces.

## Complementary Building Methods

Cordwood and slip form walls pair well with other natural building techniques. A stone foundation can support cordwood walls above. A slip form wall can serve as a windbreak or fire-resistant barrier on one side of a home, with lighter strawbale or cob walls on the sheltered side. The versatility of these methods allows hybrid designs that optimize for local materials, climate, and the builder's skill level.
