# Psilocybin and Psilocin Biosynthesis Variation Across Flushes

The levels of psychoactive alkaloids in *Psilocybe cubensis* vary significantly across [[psilocin-biosynthetic-delay-hypothesis-successive-flushes]] (harvests) from the same culture. This finding, documented by Bigwood and Beug (1982) in the *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, has important implications for both the biosynthetic pathway understanding of these compounds and for the predictable dosing of cultivated mushrooms. The study revealed that psilocybin levels remain relatively stable while psilocin levels increase dramatically from flush to flush, suggesting distinct biosynthetic regulation for the two closely related compounds.

## Key Findings

### Psilocybin: Variable but No Trend

Psilocybin (4-PO-DMT) levels varied unpredictably from one flush to the next but showed no consistent upward or downward trend across five to six flushes. Across three minicultures of the same Amazonian strain:

| Flush | Miniculture 1 (mg/g) | Miniculture 2 (mg/g) | Miniculture 3 (mg/g) |
|-------|----------------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | 8.3 | 5.1 | 7.6 |
| 2 | 6.5 | 7.0 | 6.2 |
| 3 | 13.3 | 4.7 | 5.3 |
| 4 | 4.8 | 3.7 | 3.2 |
| 5 | — | 5.2 | 6.7 |
| 6 | 6.8 | — | — |

The variation spans approximately a four-fold range (3.2 to 13.3 mg/g dry weight) among cultures grown under rigidly controlled conditions. This inherent variability means that even with identical genetics, substrate, and environmental conditions, the psilocybin content of harvested mushrooms cannot be predicted with precision.

### Psilocin: Absent Initially, Peaks at Flush 4

Psilocin (4-HO-DMT), the dephosphorylated and more labile counterpart of psilocybin, showed a strikingly different pattern:

| Flush | Miniculture 1 (mg/g) | Miniculture 2 (mg/g) | Miniculture 3 (mg/g) |
|-------|----------------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | 0.5 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 1.5 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 | 1.0 | 1.7 | 0.9 |
| 4 | 2.6 | 2.9 | 1.8 |
| 5 | — | 2.2 | 1.7 |

Psilocin was generally absent in the first one or two flushes, reached maximum concentration by the fourth flush, and appeared to begin declining thereafter. This flush-dependent accumulation pattern suggests that psilocin biosynthesis is activated or enhanced as the mycelial culture ages or as the available nutrient pool becomes depleted.

### Psilocybin:Psilocin Ratio

Psilocybin levels were nearly always at least twice the level of psilocin. This ratio is consistent with the biosynthetic relationship between the two compounds, where psilocybin (the phosphorylated form) is considered the primary biosynthetic product and psilocin (the dephosphorylated form) may arise through enzymatic dephosphorylation or degradation of psilocybin.

## Biosynthetic Implications

The divergent patterns of psilocybin and psilocin accumulation across flushes suggest that the two compounds are regulated differently within the biosynthetic pathway:

1. **Psilocybin as the primary product**: Psilocybin appears to be synthesized constitutively throughout the fruiting cycle, with levels fluctuating but not showing a systematic trend. This is consistent with psilocybin being the end product of the tryptamine biosynthetic pathway in *P. cubensis*.

2. **Psilocin as a secondary product**: The delayed appearance and gradual increase of psilocin suggest that it may be produced through a separate enzymatic step that becomes more active as the culture matures. Alternatively, psilocin may accumulate because the phosphatase enzymes that convert psilocybin to psilocin increase in activity over time, or because the phosphatase that converts psilocin back to other metabolites decreases.

3. **Nutrient depletion hypothesis**: As the substrate is progressively depleted of nutrients over successive flushes, the metabolic state of the mycelium changes. This metabolic shift may favor the production or accumulation of psilocin over psilocybin, possibly as a stress response or as a consequence of altered precursor availability.

## Tissue Distribution: Cap vs. Stem

Analysis of cap and stem tissues separately in three different strains revealed important distribution patterns:

### M.R. Strain (1st flush)
- **Caps**: 6.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0 mg/g psilocin
- **Stems**: 4.2 mg/g psilocybin, 0.35 mg/g psilocin

### Equadorian Strain (1st flush)
- **Caps**: 7.6 mg/g psilocybin, 0 mg/g psilocin
- **Stems**: 4.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0.4 mg/g psilocin

### Amazon Strain (1st flush)
- **Caps**: 5.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0.1 mg/g psilocin
- **Stems**: 5.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0 mg/g psilocin

Key observations:

- Caps generally contained approximately twice as much psilocybin as stems (in M.R. and Equadorian strains)
- The small amount of psilocin present was entirely (or almost entirely) in the stems in M.R. and Equadorian strains
- The Amazon strain showed a different pattern with equal psilocybin distribution and a trace of psilocin in the cap only
- These inter-strain differences in tissue distribution suggest genetic variation in the localization of biosynthetic enzymes

## Street Sample Variability

Five street samples of dried *P. cubensis* analyzed by Bigwood and Beug showed highly variable psilocybin levels (0.7 to 6.2 mg/g) and consistently low psilocin levels (0 to 0.3 mg/g). The tenfold variation in psilocybin among street samples — compared to the fourfold variation in controlled cultures — reflects the additional variability introduced by different strains, growing conditions, harvest timing, and post-harvest handling.

The consistently low psilocin in street samples may reflect the fact that these samples were predominantly from early flushes (first or second), where psilocin levels are typically negligible. It may also reflect degradation of psilocin during drying and storage, as psilocin is less stable than psilocybin.

## Analytical Methods

Bigwood and Beug used reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for quantification, calibrated against standards from the National Institute on Drug Abuse. Key methodological details:

- Linear relationship between concentration and peak area from 0.2 to 3 μg total psilocybin or psilocin
- 10% repeatability
- Detection limit of approximately 0.01 μg psilocybin or psilocin
- Qualitative confirmation by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using butanol-acetic acid-water (12:3:5)
- Mushrooms were freeze-dried immediately after harvest, sealed in plastic, and stored at -5°C until analysis

## Implications for Users and Cultivators

The variation documented by Bigwood and Beug has several practical implications:

1. **Unpredictable dosing**: Users have no way to predict the amount of psilocybin and psilocin they are ingesting with a given dry weight of mushroom. Variation in [[dosage-unpredictability-psilocybin-subjective-experience]] will stem not only from set and setting but also from large dosage differences.

2. **Flush selection**: Later flushes (3rd and 4th) contain significantly more psilocin, which may contribute to the overall psychoactive effect. However, total biomass per flush typically decreases, so the total yield of active compounds may peak at intermediate flushes.

3. **Strain selection**: Different strains show different alkaloid profiles and tissue distribution patterns, adding another layer of variability.

4. **Harvest timing**: The maturity of individual mushrooms at harvest may affect alkaloid levels, though this was not specifically studied. Harvesting before the veil tears (immature stage) versus at full maturity (post-veil tear) could affect both psilocybin and psilocin concentrations.

5. **Drying and storage**: The method of drying (air-drying vs. freeze-drying) and the temperature and duration of storage all affect the stability of psilocin. Freeze-drying and storage at -5°C (as practiced by Bigwood and Beug) preserves psilocin better than air-drying at room temperature, which is the more common practice among casual cultivators.

6. **Bioassay vs. chemical analysis**: The psychoactive potency of a mushroom cannot be determined from psilocybin content alone, because psilocin is pharmacologically active at lower doses and may contribute disproportionately to the subjective experience. The flush-dependent variation in psilocin levels means that mushrooms from later flushes may produce qualitatively different experiences even at the same psilocybin-equivalent dose.
