# Mollison Edge Effect Design

The edge effect is one of the most fundamental concepts in Bill Mollison's
permaculture design framework. Drawing from ecology's observation that the
boundary between two ecosystems supports greater species diversity and productivity
than either ecosystem alone, Mollison elevated edge design from natural phenomenon
to deliberate design tool applicable at every scale from garden bed to landscape.

## Ecological Foundation

In natural systems, the interface between forest and field, water and land, or any
two distinct ecological communities creates a zone of heightened biological
activity. This edge zone has access to resources from both adjoining communities
while providing unique niches neither offers independently.

Species preferring conditions of one community, the other, or the specific mix
found at the interface can all coexist in the narrow edge zone. Mollison cites
examples where edge zones support 2-3 times the species diversity of either
adjacent community and produce significantly more total biomass per unit area.

## Maximizing Edge Through Shape

Mollison's signature contribution is deliberate edge increase through design
choices. Straight lines and simple geometric shapes minimize boundary-to-area
ratio. Complex shapes with curves, lobes, and indentations maximize it, creating
more interface between different conditions within the same total area.

A pond with deeply indented shoreline has significantly more productive habitat
than a circular pond of the same surface area. The herb spiral is the most widely
recognized application, arranging growing space rising from moist base to dry sunny
top, creating maximum microclimate edge in a compact footprint. A single spiral
incorporates shady moist north face, warm sunny south face, well-drained top, and
water-retentive base, each supporting different species.

## Pond and Water Body Design

Water bodies are prime candidates for edge maximization. Mollison advocates for
irregular pond shapes with peninsulas, bays, and islands. Lobate shorelines create
more shallow water zones for emergent plantings and more habitat diversity for both
aquatic and terrestrial species.

The chinampas system of the Aztecs is edge design applied to water-based
agriculture. Long narrow islands separated by canals create enormous edge between
land and water, supporting intensive food production where each island has water
access on both sides simultaneously.

## Zone Interface Design

Mollison applies edge thinking to boundaries between permaculture zones. The
interface between Zone 1 and Zone 2 should be a designed transition serving as pest
buffer and gradual microclimate change. The cultivated-wild edge provides pest
control habitat, wind protection, wildlife corridors, and pollinator foraging zones.

## Functional and Temporal Edge

Beyond physical boundaries, Mollison extends the concept to functional connections
between elements. A chicken coop next to a greenhouse creates productive edge where
chickens provide heat and CO2 while the greenhouse provides warmth. Maximizing
beneficial connections produces more yield with less input than isolated elements.

Temporal edge, the boundary between seasons or successional stages, is also
productive. Designing for temporal edge ensures one element's output becomes
another's input at every transition, maintaining continuous productivity rather
than boom-and-bust cycles of conventional agriculture.

# PDC Permaculture Design for Fire Control

Pamphlet VII of the Permaculture Design Course provides detailed guidance on designing landscapes for fire protection. Fire is treated as a serious design consideration with specific periodicities, defense sequences, and architectural strategies.

## Fire Periodicity

"Fire has a periodicity specific to the site." Two factors determine it: rate of fuel accumulation, and amount of moisture on site. Ridge tops are far more fire-prone than valleys. Sun-facing sites are more fire-prone than shaded slopes.

At 30-40 inches of rainfall, catastrophic fires occur about every 25-30 years. The periodicity shortens with each burn: "The more it burns, the more likely it is to burn again soon" because fire removes moisture-retaining humus and creates fire-prone litter.

"The less a site burns, the less it is likely to burn, because there will be more humus and more moisture incorporated into the site."

## The Real Danger: Radiation

"The real danger of fire is radiation. Four hundred feet before a fire, your hair catches alight. Two hundred feet, your body starts to split and your fat catches alight. At 100 feet, you are a torch. Radiation kills birds hundreds of feet from the fire."

Goats survive fires by leaning into them. Human beings survive by dodging about and hiding behind shadows.

## Defense Sequences

Primary strategy: reduce fuel. Methods include creating non-fuel surfaces (roads, ponds), constructing [[pdc-water-systems-design-and-management]] and pit mulching, and reducing fuel by browsing or grazing.

Valley-upward planting of low-fuel vegetation. "Re-establish the rain forest that would be on the site if it did not burn."

House protection: "You only need a hundred feet of non-fuel systems between the house and the forest." Select plants with high ash content, high water content, low total bulk, and dense growth.

Avoid: eucalypts ("a positive no-no"), pine trees (fire weeds with cones that don't open until fires), plants with high volatile oil content on the downhill/fire-danger side.

Mulches are safe: "You don't need to worry about mulches." Sawdust, too, is "good safe mulch."

## Water Defense

"Primary protection in fire is to have good sprinklers down hill." Sprinklers must be turned on BEFORE the fire arrives -- once fire is present, water doesn't travel far from nozzles.

Roof sprinklers: "Put a monsoon sprinkler on the ridge of the roof... the tap to it should be outside. Turn it on, and the whole house is being washed down." Use a gravity system, "because if it is part of a public system... the system will be inadequate."

## Architecture for Fire

Wooden houses have 13-15% higher survival than stone or brick. "Stone transmits heat rapidly to the inner surfaces." White painted houses reflect radiation.

Roof design: rolled-under section on bottom edge, gutters placed below it. "Leaves will fall off. They can't get in the gutter. Snow will slide off... Fire ash will slide down and fall off."

Fire hardware mesh screens prevent large particles from entering houses.

Fire shelters: T-shaped or L-shaped pits, earthed over. "Go maybe six feet deep. Open your back door, and hop down into your little root cellar, which is also a fire shelter."
