# Terrace Design and Construction

Terraces are among the most powerful tools in permaculture for converting slopes into productive, stable, and accessible growing systems. By creating level or gently graded platforms on hillsides, terraces eliminate the problems of erosion, water runoff, and difficult access that make steep land challenging for agriculture. Mollison considered terracing an essential component of landscape design in any region with significant slope, particularly in tropical and high-rainfall environments.

## Benefits of Terracing

The advantages of well-designed terrace systems are substantial:

- **Easy crop access** on slopes that would otherwise be difficult or dangerous to work
- **Controlled and effective irrigation** through controllable water inlets and outlets
- **Minimal soil loss** from overland water flow or slope cultivation
- **Nutrient capture** through silts, leaf fall, and irrigation water retention
- **Polycultural potential** enabling both wet and dry crops in the same slope system

## Optimal Slopes for Terracing

Terracing becomes most efficient on slopes between 10° and 18°, though it can be worthwhile up to about 30°. As slopes steepen, the ratio of earth moved to area of cropland developed becomes increasingly impractical. The sweet spot balances the cost of earthmoving against the productive area gained. At slopes below 10°, the cost of terracing may not be justified; above 30°, the volume of earth and instability of the system make it rarely worthwhile except with narrow, hand-cut terraces.

## Construction Sequence

Terrace construction follows a specific, critical sequence:

1. **Begin at the lowest level** — the first terrace is carved into the base of the slope
2. **Strip and stockpile topsoil** — all topsoil from the lower terrace area is carefully removed and set aside
3. **Proceed uphill** — each successive terrace is built above the previous one
4. **Transfer topsoil downward** — topsoil cleared from upper terraces is placed on the terrace below
5. **Finish at the top** — the stockpiled topsoil from the very lowest terrace is finally carted or lifted to the last terrace at the top of the series

This sequence ensures that the most fertile material (topsoil) ends up on every terrace surface, not buried under subsoil or spoil.

## Bund and Wall Plantings

Every terrace system should incorporate permanent plants on bunds and terrace walls. These serve multiple functions:

- **Structural stability** — roots bind the soil and prevent wall collapse
- **Green manure** — nitrogen-fixing and leaf-shedding species maintain fertility
- **Microclimate creation** — shade and wind protection for crops below
- **Additional yield** — fruit, nut, fodder, or timber species provide productive returns from the terrace infrastructure itself

Trees on and between terraces should constitute 40–60% of the total landscape plan, ensuring long-term soil fertility through leaf litter and root systems.

## Extent and Scale Limitations

Terrace area must be limited relative to the catchment:

- **Tropical areas** — terraces should occupy no more than 30% of the landscape, limited by heavy rainfall and expected runoff volumes
- **Arid/dryland areas** — terraces should occupy no more than 5% of the landscape, and total terrace area should not exceed one-twentieth of the harvested catchment. Water-demanding crops should be avoided; only perennial or drought-adapted species are appropriate

Water catchment areas in drylands should be developed to maintain terrace fertility, so that runoff carries leaf mulch and organic material down to the terraced slopes.

## Wet Terraces for Aquaculture

In humid cool or tropical areas, 10–20% of terrace areas can be designed as wet terraces devoted to fish-plant polyculture. These integrated systems yield fish, shellfish, and water plant products simultaneously. Protein yields from well-managed water cultures can exceed all land-based production systems at equivalent levels of husbandry and care.

## Steep Slope Terracing (Above 20°)

On very steep sites, especially in high-rainfall areas, broad terracing should be abandoned in favor of a series of 4–6 narrow production terraces. Each series must be carefully drained to spill excess rain down permanently vegetated slopes. Key techniques include:

- **Ridging the terrace tips** to contain soil and direct spillage
- **Mulching paths** to absorb water and prevent erosion between terraces
- **Staggering path spills** in short series to distribute runoff energy

This approach provides the benefits of terracing on steep slopes without the erosion risk. Machines are inappropriate for such construction — steep slope terracing is always hand-cut.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Making very large series of terraces in high-rainfall areas, which concentrates dangerous volumes of runoff
- Using machines on steep slopes where hand-cut narrow terraces are more appropriate
- Failing to stockpile and redistribute topsoil during construction
- Exceeding the recommended proportion of terrace area relative to catchment in drylands
- Neglecting to plant permanent species on bunds and terrace walls

## Irrigation Design for Terraces

Soils and installed water inlets/outlets on terraces must permit safe and controllable irrigation. Stable bunds — either rock-walled or maintained at a 1:3 slope — form the structural backbone of the system. Long-term production from terraces depends on integrating polycultural design (wet and dry crops), stable bund structures, and adequate tree cover into a unified system.
