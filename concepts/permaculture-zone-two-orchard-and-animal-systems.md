## Permaculture Zone Two: Orchard and Animal Systems

Zone Two in [[permaculture-design]] represents the second concentric ring outward from the dwelling (Zone One). It is the area of less frequent but still significant management, typically visited once every few days rather than daily. Zone Two is where the primary productive systems of a [[permaculture]] site are concentrated — orchards, main crop gardens, and domestic animals that form the backbone of food production [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]].

## Overview of Zone Two

Zone Two serves as the transition between the intensively managed domestic zone (Zone One) and the broader farm-scale production areas (Zones Three through Five). While it requires regular attention, its systems are designed to be productive without daily oversight. The key characteristic of Zone Two is its role as the site's primary food-producing layer, housing elements that yield substantial harvests with moderate maintenance inputs.

Mollison describes Zone Two as the area where "you still go fairly often" but not with the daily regularity demanded by herbs, salad greens, and other Zone One staples. The placement of Zone Two systems adjacent to Zone One allows for efficient movement of materials, nutrients, and attention between the two zones.

## Main Crop Gardens

Main crop gardens in Zone Two differ from the intensive daily-harvest beds of Zone One. These are crops that are harvested in bulk two or three times per season and often require storage rather than immediate consumption. Typical main crops include:

- **Tomatoes** — high-yielding, harvested in multiple passes throughout the growing season
- **Winter squash and pumpkins** — single or dual harvest crops stored for months
- **Potatoes and root vegetables** — bulk harvest and cellar storage crops
- **Sweet corn** — harvested in concentrated seasonal windows
- **Beans and peas** — succession-planted with multiple harvest periods

The management approach for these crops emphasizes soil building, efficient water use, and mulching to reduce weed pressure and retain moisture between visits.

## Perennial Food Plants

Zone Two is also the appropriate placement for semi-intensive perennial food systems that benefit from proximity to the dwelling but do not require daily attention:

- **Strawberries** — productive for several years with periodic renovation; benefit from mulching and bird protection
- **Asparagus** — long-lived perennial requiring seasonal cutting and bed maintenance
- **Raspberries and other brambles** — productive hedgerow crops that need pruning and trellis management
- **Grape vines and kiwi** — perennial fruit requiring annual pruning but producing reliably

These perennial systems represent significant capital investments in soil and infrastructure that repay themselves over many growing seasons.

## Orchard Placement and Management

Orchards are a defining feature of Zone Two. Fruit and nut trees are placed here because they require regular but not constant attention — seasonal pruning, pest monitoring, harvest, and occasional feeding. The orchard layout in Zone Two typically incorporates:

- **Mixed species plantings** rather than monoculture blocks, following the permaculture principle of diversity
- **Understory plantings** beneath fruit trees, including [[comfrey]], nasturtiums, and herbs that serve as ground covers, nutrient accumulators, and pest deterrents
- **Nurse trees** that provide initial shelter for slower-growing productive species
- **[[pollinator-habitat]]** integrated throughout the orchard to support fruit set

The orchard in Zone Two is designed for multi-strata production, with canopy trees, understory trees, shrubs, and ground covers all contributing to total yield.

## Domestic Animal Systems

### Poultry

Poultry — chickens and ducks — are typically housed at the edge of Zone One and Zone Two. This placement is deliberate: poultry need daily attention (feeding, water, egg collection) which places them operationally in Zone One, but their foraging and manure contributions benefit Zone Two plantings. The chicken house serves as a nutrient factory, converting food scraps, insects, and green material into eggs and high-quality manure.

### Animal Collector Strategy

Mollison introduces the concept of the **animal collector strategy**, a fundamental Zone Two design principle. Animals are used as biological nutrient transfer agents, moving fertility from outer zones inward. Chickens foraging in Zone Two orchards collect insects, fallen fruit, and weed seeds, converting them into manure that fertilizes the trees. When poultry housing is placed at the Zone One/Two boundary, the concentrated manure output directly benefits the most intensively managed areas.

This strategy extends to all animals in the system. Animals are placed not just for their direct products (milk, eggs, meat) but for their role as nutrient cyclers that create a biological funnel — what Mollison calls the **nutrient vortex** — drawing high-turnover organic matter from outer zones toward the center of the system.

### Goats, Milk Cows, and Pigs

Larger animals — goats, milk cows, and pigs — are managed in Zone Two with specific design considerations:

- **Corridor designs** allow animals to move between paddocks without human herding, using lanes that connect grazing areas, water sources, and shelter
- **Goats** serve as browsers and brush clearers, converting woody vegetation into milk and manure
- **Milk cows** in Zone Two require daily milking (a Zone One frequency activity) but graze on Zone Two pastures; their placement reflects this dual-zone demand
- **Pigs** function as rototillers and compost accelerators, rooting through crop residues and converting them into manure while aerating the soil

## Mulching Strategies

Mulching in Zone Two follows two primary approaches, each with trade-offs between labor input and effectiveness:

- **Spot-mulching** applies organic material directly around individual plants or trees. This is less labor-intensive but provides less complete weed suppression and moisture conservation
- **Full mulching** covers the entire soil surface with organic material. While more labor-intensive to establish, full mulching provides superior weed control, moisture retention, and soil-building results

Mollison recommends that Zone Two mulching strategies be chosen based on available labor and material. [[sheet-mulching]] — layering cardboard, newspaper, and organic matter — is particularly effective for establishing new Zone Two garden areas over existing lawn or pasture.

## The Nutrient Vortex

The nutrient vortex is a central organizing concept for Zone Two design. It describes the biological funnel that brings high-turnover nutrients from outer zones toward the inner, more intensively managed zones of the permaculture system. The mechanism operates through:

1. **Animal movement** — livestock foraging in outer zones deposit manure closer to the center during nightly housing or milking routines
2. **Plant harvesting** — crops grown in outer zones are consumed near the dwelling; food scraps and human waste return to inner-zone compost systems
3. **Gravity and water flow** — designed water systems carry dissolved nutrients from higher outer zones downhill toward settlement areas
4. **Insect and bird activity** — pollinators and insectivores move between zones, transferring biomass and nutrients

The nutrient vortex ensures that the system becomes increasingly fertile at its center over time, mirroring the natural concentration of organic matter in forest systems around nurse logs and root zones.

## Cultural Commentary: Lawns and Productive Landscapes

Mollison offers a pointed critique of the British landscape tradition and its global influence. The manicured lawn, he argues, is fundamentally a "salute to power" — a cultural status symbol signaling that the landowner possesses sufficient wealth to maintain land in a non-productive state. This aesthetic, exported through British colonialism, established lawns as markers of respectability and social standing worldwide.

This cultural norm creates a significant barrier to productive landscaping. Homeowners face social pressure — and sometimes legal enforcement — to maintain non-productive turf rather than food gardens. Mollison documents instances of resistance: people planting pumpkins on nature strips (the grassy verges between sidewalks and roads), converting front lawns to vegetable gardens, and defying aesthetic conventions in favor of productivity.

## The Davis Village Project

The Davis village project in California serves as a documented example of replacing the non-productive landscape paradigm with productive permaculture design. The project demonstrated that residential landscapes could be designed for food production while maintaining aesthetic appeal and community function. It showed that productive landscapes are not inherently messy or unattractive but can be designed with the same intentionality as ornamental landscapes.

## Economic Self-Sufficiency on Small Acreages

Zone Two design is central to the economic argument for small-scale productive land use. Mollison cites examples ranging from an eighth of an acre to 500 acres where individuals and families achieve significant economic self-sufficiency through intensive Zone Two management:

- **The parsley man near Melbourne** who made a living on a small intensive plot, demonstrating that even very small acreages can be economically viable when managed with skill and intensity
- Urban and suburban plots that produce the majority of a household's food needs through Zone One and Two systems alone
- Small farms (1-10 acres) that achieve full [[economic-viability-water-retention-landscapes]] through diversified Zone Two production including vegetables, fruits, eggs, and dairy

## Fencing Specifications

Zone Two animal systems require appropriate fencing. Mollison specifies **1-inch mesh hardware cloth** (wire mesh) for chipmunk and rabbit exclusion, buried at least 6 inches below ground to prevent burrowing. This specification reflects the practical reality that small mammals can cause significant damage to young orchard trees and garden crops, and that fencing must be designed to exclude them from the outset.

## Design Principles Summary

The key design principles for Zone Two, as articulated by Mollison, are:

1. Place productive elements that need regular but not daily attention
2. Integrate animals as nutrient collectors and cyclers
3. Design for nutrient flow inward through the nutrient vortex
4. Use mulching appropriate to labor availability
5. Mix species and functions rather than segregating by type
6. Resist non-productive cultural aesthetics in favor of productive design
7. Scale systems for economic self-sufficiency within available land area
