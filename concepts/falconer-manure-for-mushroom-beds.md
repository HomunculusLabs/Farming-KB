# Falconer Manure for Mushroom Beds

William Falconer's 1891 treatise *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* dedicates
an entire chapter to the selection and evaluation of manure for mushroom
beds, reflecting the paramount importance of this material in Victorian-era
commercial cultivation. The questions of manure and [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]] are described as "the most important that we
have to deal with," and Falconer insists that "success with it depends
absolutely upon ourselves."

## The Ideal Manure

The very best manure comes from strong, healthy, hard-worked, well-kept
animals liberally fed with hard food such as timothy hay and grain, and
bedded with straw. Manure from farm and teamsters' stables is preferred
over that from stylish establishments where everything is kept scrupulously
dry and clean, because the urine-soaked and trampled bedding enriches the
fermenting qualities of the material.

The fresher the manure the better, though stable manure may accumulate in
a cellar for a couple of months and still be first rate. Falconer
describes stacking stable manure high in the yard from June through
August, breaking it down in late summer to shake out the best manure for
mushrooms while discarding long straw and rotted parts.

## Droppings vs. Strawy Manure

Some growers used only the droppings, rejecting all strawy parts. This
produces excellent manure for small-scale beds, boxes, narrow troughs, and
half barrels. However, for large beds the droppings alone would require too
much material and cost too much without being any better. Falconer
recommends preserving the wet, strawy part along with the droppings,
mixing and fermenting them together to add bulk and retain the benefits of
the urine.

## Horse vs. Mule Manure

While it is generally believed that the manure of entire (stallion) horses
is superior, Mr. John G. Gardner, who had abundant opportunity to test the
matter at the Rancocas Farm, found no difference in quality or quantity
of mushrooms between beds made from entire horse manure and those from
other equally well-fed animals. Parisian growers, however, insisted on
entire horses, especially hard-worked carting animals.

Mule manure is regarded as next in value to that of entire horses, and
some French growers considered it quite as good. The heaviest crop
Falconer ever saw was at Mr. Wilbur's place in South Bethlehem,
Pennsylvania, from beds of clean mule droppings from the coal mines.

## Cellar Manure

Manure dropped into cellars under stables, accumulating for weeks or
months, can be splendid for mushrooms provided it has not become too dry
and "fire-fanged." Falconer advises rejecting very dry and white-burned
parts. Mixed pig manure is rejected entirely, as mycelium evades hog
manure and it propagates noxious insect vermin.

## City Stable Manure

Around New York, city stable manure was always available in quantity at
reasonable rates and was the material used by nearly all large
[[falconer-mushroom-marketing-economics]]. Market gardeners
hauled in vegetables to market and returned with loads of manure.
Agents handled the business of supplying manure from 500-pound bales to
unlimited loads, delivered near or far at moderate prices.

## Baled Manure

A relatively recent innovation (circa 1890) was baled manure, each bale
containing 350 to 500 pounds, pressed and tied like baled hay. The
advantages included: only the cleanest horse manure was used; cow manure,
offal, and malodorous materials were excluded; railroads permitted bales
on platforms closer to stations; and small-scale growers could buy one or
more bales as needed rather than a full load.

## Carrot-Fed Horse Manure

Most writers emphatically condemned manure from carrot-fed horses, but
Falconer presents evidence from the Dosoris establishment where beds of
horse manure three parts and chopped sod loam one part, saved from horses
fed about a third of a bushel of carrots daily, produced very thick crops
of good mushrooms. He concludes that while not the best, it is still good
manure, and the "complete denunciation" found in horticultural literature
is "not always founded on fact."

## Other Materials

Falconer briefly discusses German peat moss stable manure, sawdust stable
manure, tree leaves, and spent hops as bedding materials. He notes that
sawdust and salt hay are far less desirable than straw, field hay, peat
moss, or corn stalks. Spent hops may be used but should never be employed
alone, nor placed so near the surface that the [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]] will have to traverse them.
