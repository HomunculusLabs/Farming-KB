## Evolutionary Significance

Animals are more closely related to fungi than to any other kingdom. The two
lineages diverged approximately 650 million years ago, sharing a common
ancestor within the superkingdom Opisthokonta. Fungi were among the first
organisms to colonize land, and their early partnership with plants, which
largely lacked digestive enzymes, was instrumental in enabling plants to
inhabit terrestrial environments around 700 million years ago. While one
branch of fungi eventually gave rise to animals that evolved internal
digestion, mycelium retained its netlike external form and went underground,
forming the food web upon which terrestrial life flourishes.

## Information Sharing

Mycelial networks exhibit behaviors analogous to neural networks. They sense
and respond to environmental changes, redirecting resources toward areas of
need. When a tree is damaged, mycorrhizal mycelium connecting it to
neighboring trees can channel carbon and nutrients from healthy trees to the
stressed one. This "wood wide web" enables forest-wide communication and
resource distribution. The mycelium collectively has the long-term health of
the host environment in mind, devising diverse enzymatic and chemical
responses to complex challenges.

## Soil Structure and Hydrology

Mycelial networks are master architects of soil structure. Hyphae secrete
glomalin-related soil proteins and other sticky compounds that bind mineral
particles together, forming stable soil aggregates. These aggregates improve
soil porosity, allowing better water infiltration and root penetration. In
forests, the sheer density of mycelial biomass means that a significant
fraction of the soil carbon pool consists of living and dead fungal tissue.
When mycelium dies back, it leaves behind a porous network of channels that
functions like a microscopic sponge, increasing the water-holding capacity
of soil and reducing surface runoff. This hydraulic role is critical for
watershed health and is one reason why deforestation so often leads to
flooding and erosion downstream.
