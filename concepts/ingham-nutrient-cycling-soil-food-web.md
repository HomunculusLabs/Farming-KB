# Ingham — Nutrient Cycling Through the Soil Food Web

Elaine Ingham's model of nutrient cycling centers on the soil food web as the engine that makes nutrients available to plants. Nutrients in organic matter, compost, and mineral sources are not directly plant-available — they must be processed through biological cycling. The form of nitrogen, phosphorus, and other elements matters critically.

## The Bacteria-First, Fungi-Second Cycle

Nutrient cycling follows a sequential process:

1. **Bacteria start decomposition** by consuming easy-to-use sugars and simple, rapid-growth food resources
2. **Fungi follow**, producing complex "waste products" — what we call humus or humic acid
3. **Protozoa eat bacteria**, releasing plant-available nitrogen (ammonium)

## Key Organisms in the Nutrient Cycling Process

The soil food web involves thousands of species interacting in complex food chains, but several key groups play outsized roles in nutrient cycling. Bacteria are the most abundant soil microorganisms and are the primary decomposers of simple organic compounds (sugars, amino acids, proteins). Fungi dominate the decomposition of more recalcitrant materials (lignin, cellulose, cutin) and serve as the primary mycorrhizal partners for most crop plants. Protozoa (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates) consume bacteria and release plant-available nitrogen in the process. Nematodes occupy multiple trophic levels — bacterial feeders, fungal feeders, and predators — and their grazing activities regulate microbial populations. Microarthropods (springtails, mites) fragment organic matter, increasing its surface area for microbial colonization. Earthworms are ecosystem engineers that create burrow networks, mix organic matter into the soil profile, and stimulate microbial activity through their gut processes.

## The Bacterial vs. Fungal Dominance Spectrum

Ingham's work emphasizes that different soils and different plant communities are characterized by different ratios of bacterial to fungal biomass. Annual agricultural crops and disturbed soils typically have bacterial-dominated food webs, with bacterial biomass exceeding fungal by ratios of 10:1 or more. Perennial grasslands tend toward a more balanced ratio (approximately 1:1), while mature forests are fungal-dominated, with fungal biomass exceeding bacterial by 10:1 or more. This ratio has profound implications for nutrient cycling: bacterial-dominated systems cycle nutrients rapidly but may lose nitrogen through leaching, while fungal-dominated systems retain nutrients more effectively but cycle them more slowly. Understanding this spectrum allows growers to manage their soil food web toward the composition that best supports their desired crops — most vegetables prefer bacterial-dominated soils, while most fruit trees and perennials perform better in fungal-dominated soils.
