# Aquaculture Designers Checklist

A comprehensive reference checklist for designing and managing aquacultural systems, drawn from [[bill-mollison]]'s [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual. This checklist covers the essential decisions and considerations that determine the success of integrated fish and water systems.

## Fundamental Principle

Due to the special susceptibility of water life, minimal to zero biocide use is essential near or in waterways. Any chemical contamination propagates rapidly through aquatic food webs and can collapse entire pond systems.

## Fish Management

### Stocking

- Stock at rates below, at, and above suspected optimum (plus or minus 2,000/ha) to empirically determine the best rate for your specific conditions
- Select disease-free stock from reputable suppliers
- Select fast-growing brood stock suited to the particular site, from fry to adults
- Select fish for good local value, but always assess food costs for each species before committing
- Select fish for no pond aeration requirements, or to suit the aeration method provided

### Water Quality

- Fertilise pond and bring pH to 7+ before stocking
- Vary [[pond-depth-and-series-arrangements]], pond size, and pH to suit a set of food species and productive fish for the district
- Analyse landscape for natural or cultivated food resources that can supplement feeding

### Predator Management

- Exclude or guard against predators (birds, mammals, reptiles)
- Provide shelter from predators and excess light and heat
- Introduce predator fish at about 1:5 ratio to control overpopulation of forage species

### Polyculture

- Devise a fish/plant polyculture for the ponds
- Carefully analyse pond configurations for specific polycultures, easy management, site, and weather effects
- Devise accessory food systems for fish or invertebrates as vegetation, [[root-crop-harvest-and-storage]], and invertebrates

## Landscape Integration

### Edge and Margin Management

- Maximise edge and [[fukuoka-natural-foods-movement-america]] — pond margins are the most productive zones
- Pay attention to aquatic and marginal crops as integral system components
- Include downstream crops as part of the total system design
- Consider total landscape balance when planning water features

### Downstream Benefits

Water from densely-stocked fish ponds serves as nutrient-rich irrigation water for land plants. Yields from sewage or fish-pond water are 2-5 times that from intake water. These downstream land crops — as fuels, food, forage, or structural products — must be integral to pond development to realize the full value of the system.

## Integrated Yield Systems

### Flow-Down Design

The most productive systems route nutrients through multiple yield steps:

1. **Land crops** fertilized and fed to animals
2. **Animal manures** directed to fish ponds
3. **Pond water** used to irrigate downstream crops

Beyond this basic integration, permanent low-fertiliser crops for pigs (banana, papaya, acorns) can be grown with sparing fertiliser inputs for even greater total yields.

### Advanced Integration

Alcohol recovery and subsequent biogas digestion of green feed, tubers, starchy food, manures, and wastes produces a flow-on slurry not one whit less fertile than the original substances. This creates an integrated flow-down system:

**Tree forage > Animal protein > Manures > Alcohol > Biogas > Water crop (plants) > Forage-fish**

Even within this flow, side cycles to worms, notonectids, or Daphnia give better utilisation and a yield at every step.

## Harvesting Strategies

- For non-carnivorous species, catch-and-restock systems can greatly increase yields over batch or total removal
- Always consider returning or keeping the largest fish, and eating the slightly smaller grades
- Processing of fish on farm (drying, smoking, freezing, salting, paste and sauce production) has many advantages
- Up to 40% of a catch can be lost to spoilage and damage — processing infrastructure is critical
- Fermentation of fish wastes produces protein-rich, salt-stabilised liquid sauce of great value

## Wetland Inclusion

In any design, include some appropriate wetlands — small or large — even if only from waste water. Wetlands serve critical functions for water purification, [[amphibian-habitat-creation]], and biodiversity support.

## Social Considerations

The complexity of maximum-yield integrated systems requires a higher order of social organisation than a single family or person can manage. Community organisation around shared canal systems and irrigation becomes necessary, and historically has been a driver of cooperative governance structures.
