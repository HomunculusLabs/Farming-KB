## Mycelial Network Architecture and Grazing Tolerance

The interconnected, network-like architecture of mycelial colonies provides
inherent resilience against grazing. Unlike a collection of independent
single-celled organisms, a mycelial network can redistribute resources from
undamaged regions to compensate for locally grazed areas. When a collembolan
grazes a patch of hyphae, the surrounding network can mobilize stored
nutrients and cytoplasmic reserves to support regrowth at the damaged site.
This resource redistribution is facilitated by the vacuolar system and
cytoplasmic streaming, which can move materials over distances of centimeters
within the mycelium. The network architecture thus converts local damage into
a network-level problem that the fungus can manage through internal resource
reallocation.
## Chemical Defense Mechanisms

Many filamentous fungi produce [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] that repel collembola and nematodes from
colonized substrates. Others produce non-volatile toxins that are incorporated
into the hyphal cell wall or released into the surrounding substrate. The
diversity of [[comparison-trichoderma-vs-entomopathogenic-fungi]], have been co-opted for
agricultural use as biopesticides.
## Physical Defense and Hyphal Morphology

Fungi can modify their hyphal morphology and colony structure in response to
grazing pressure. Some species produce thicker, more melanized hyphal walls
that are physically harder for grazers to consume. Others form dense, cord-
like aggregates of hyphae (rhizomorphs or cords) that are more resistant to
grazing than individual hyphae. The formation of sclerotia, hard, dense

and may in some contexts be a beneficial interaction.
