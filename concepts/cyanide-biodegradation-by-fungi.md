# Cyanide Biodegradation by Fungi

Fungal biodegradation of cyanide and metal-cyanide complexes is an important bioremediation strategy for industrial effluents from gold mining, electroplating, steel manufacturing, and coal gasification. Key fungal genera include Fusarium, Gloeocercospora, and Stemphylium.

## Sources of Cyanide Contamination

- Gold mining: cyanide used in heap leaching and tank leaching operations

- Electroplating and metal finishing industries

- Steel manufacturing

- Coal gasification

- Pharmaceutical industry

- Illicit drug manufacture

- Road salt (ferrocyanide as anti-caking agent)

## Cyanide Hydratase: Key Fungal Enzyme

### Reaction

HCN + H2O --> HCONH2 (formamide)

Formamide is subsequently hydrolyzed by formamidase to formate and ammonia:

HCONH2 + H2O --> HCOOH + NH3

### Properties of Cyanide Hydratase

Native molecular mass of approximately 300 kDa, with subunits of 43-45 kDa. Key properties across fungal species:

| Species | pH Optimum | K(m) (mmol/L) | Molecular Mass |
|---------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| Fusarium solani | 7.5 | 4.7 | 300/45 kDa |
| Fusarium lateritium | 8.5 | 43 | 300/43 kDa |
| Gloeocercospora sorghi | 7-8 | 12 | 300/45 kDa |
| Stemphylium loti | 7-9 | -- | 960 kDa |

The true substrate is HCN (not free CN-), as indicated by pH optima below the pKa of cyanide (9.36). Cyanide hydratase genes are approximately 65-82% homologous between fungal species.

## Metal-Cyanide Complex Degradation

The mechanism for metal-cyanide degradation is not fully resolved. Four possible routes:

1. Complex dissociates outside cell; free HCN diffuses in and induces enzyme; metal binds to cell wall

2. Complex biosorbed onto cell wall; cyanide released through interaction with wall

3. Complex taken into cell; enzyme acts upon whole complex

4. Abiotic/biotic interactions cause dissociation; HCN induces enzyme activity

Evidence supports free cyanide as the true substrate: growth rate correlates with complex stability, and the same cyanide hydratase gene is induced by nickel cyanide, iron cyanide, and KCN.

### Mixed and Pure Culture Studies

Pure and mixed cultures of fungi have been found to biodegrade metal cyanides. A mixed culture of Fusarium solani and Trichoderma polysporum was obtained on tetracyanonickelate [K2Ni(CN)4] at pH 4.0. A second mixed culture of three fungi (Fusarium oxysporum, Scytalidium thermophilum, and Penicillium miczynski) occurred on hexacyanoferrate [K4Fe(CN)6] at pH 4.0.

The culture supernatant displayed 50% removal of total cyanide associated with the termination of growth. Cyanide was removed in 5 days by cultivation of Fusarium isolated on K2Ni(CN)4 at pH 7.0 and occurred simultaneously with removal of iron on K4Fe(CN)6 at pH 4.0 by the fungi.

Cyanide metabolism by fungi and molecular biology of cyanide degradation has been reviewed by Barclay and Knowles (2001). A pathway for the utilization of cyanide by Fusarium solani has been depicted in the literature.

### Fe(III)-Cyanide Complex Anions

Initial pH, initial Fe(III)-cyanide complex anions, and biosorbent concentration play significant roles in the binding capacity to Rhizopus arrhizus. pH 13.0 was responsible for the decrease in Fe(III)-cyanide complex anions. The Langmuir, Freundlich, and Redlich-Peterson models correlated well with equilibrium data at pH 3.0, 7.0, and 13.0, respectively. There is good scope for the uptake of Fe(III)-cyanide complex anions in high concentrations of wastewaters, up to 2000 mg/L.

## Cyanide-Insensitive Respiration

Since cyanide inhibits cytochrome oxidase, cyanide-degrading fungi possess alternative terminal oxidases. These are induced by cytochrome inhibitors (cyanide, antimycin A) and operate when the ubiquinone pool is reduced to 50-60%. Alternative oxidase genes are highly conserved across organisms.

## Notable Degrading Fungi

- Fusarium solani IHEM8026: Efficient cyanide degradation, high tolerance

- Fusarium oxysporum: Possesses cyanide-resistant alternative oxidase

- Gloeocercospora sorghi: Moderate K(m) for cyanide

- Fusarium lateritium: Well-characterized enzyme with high K(m)

- Trichoderma polysporum: Effective in mixed cultures on metal-cyanide complexes

- Scytalidium thermophilum: Thermotolerant fungus for cyanide degradation

- Penicillium miczynski: Contributes to mixed-culture metal-cyanide degradation

## Biosensor Applications

Fungal biosensors have been developed for the detection of cyanide in environmental samples. A biosensor device has been described for the detection of cyanide alongside chlorophenols, atrazine, and carbamate pesticides, demonstrating the versatility of enzyme-based detection systems for monitoring cyanide-contaminated effluents.

## Cyanogenesis in Fungi

Several fungi produce cyanide as a secondary metabolite. The phenomenon was first reported in the basidiomycete *Marasmius oreades* in 1871 and is widespread among Basidiomycetes, Ascomycetes, and Zygomycetes. Cyanide is produced from glycine via HCN synthase, which is membrane-bound and linked to respiratory activity. Some phytopathogenic fungi cause plant diseases through cyanide liberation, while others (such as *Stemphylium loti*) detoxify cyanide using cyanide hydratase as a defence mechanism.

## Molecular Biology

The cyanide hydratase gene (*chy*) has been sequenced for *F. lateritium*, *G. sorghi*, and *L. maculans*, showing 65-82% homology at the amino acid level. Site-directed mutagenesis revealed an essential cysteine residue at position 163 (part of the active site), placing cyanide hydratase in the nitrilase enzyme family. In *F. solani*, *chy* transcription begins 30-60 minutes after cyanide exposure and ceases after 12-24 hours. Both free and metal-complexed cyanides induce the same gene, supporting free cyanide as the true enzymatic substrate.

## Cyanide Chemistry and Environmental Sources

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN, pKa 9.36) is a colourless gas that inhibits electron transfer to oxygen in the respiratory chain. Free cyanide and simple cyanides (KCN, NaCN) are the most toxic forms. Twenty-eight elements form 72 different metallocyanides of varying stability; iron cyanides are among the most stable, while weak complexes with cadmium, lead, nickel, and zinc dissociate under neutral to acidic conditions to release free cyanide.

Industrial sources include former gaswork sites (containing spent oxide with up to 50 g/kg cyanide), gold and silver mining operations that use cyanide leaching, electroplating wastewaters (up to 100 g/L total cyanide), and chemical manufacturing (acrylonitrile, adiponitrile production). Prussian Blue (ferriferrocyanide) forms in low-pH spent oxide environments and represents the most stable and least toxic cyanide compound.

## Bacterial Cyanide Degradation

*Pseudomonas fluorescens* NCIMB 11764 uses three concentration-dependent pathways: cyanide oxygenase (dominant at 0.5-10 mmol/L), cyanide dihydratase (converting HCN + 2H2O to NH3 + HCOOH), and cyanide hydratase. Cyanide oxygenase has higher affinity (Km 1.2 mmol/L) and incorporates oxygen from both O2 and H2O into CO2. Commercial applications include the Homestake gold mine treatment process using *P. paucimobilis*.

## Advantages of Fungal Cyanide Biodegradation

Fungal approaches to cyanide degradation offer several advantages over bacterial systems. Fungi tolerate a wider pH range, enabling treatment of acidic industrial effluents. Their filamentous growth allows better penetration of contaminated matrices. The cyanide hydratase pathway converts cyanide directly to formamide and subsequently to formate and ammonia, which are far less toxic end products compared to incomplete bacterial degradation pathways.
