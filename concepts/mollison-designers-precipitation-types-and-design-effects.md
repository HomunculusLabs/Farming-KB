# Precipitation Types and Their Effects on Permaculture Design
Understanding the types and processes of precipitation is
fundamental to permaculture design. Rainfall is not a simple
or uniform phenomenon; it arrives through different
mechanisms that have profound implications for
[[mollison-designers-climate-zone-5-wilderness-conservation]]
classification, [[mollison-water-harvesting-management-permaculture]], and [[mollison-windbreak-design]].
## Three Primary Rainfall Mechanisms
Rainfall is conveniently distinguished by the processes
causing rain:
### Orographic Rainfall
Orographic rain results from the cooling of air as it rises
over mountains or hills. This is the dominant rainfall type
in mountainous regions and is responsible for the rain
shadow effect on leeward slopes. [[mollison-orographic-and-forest-rainfall-effects]] are particularly important in permaculture because
they determine where forests can grow, where water collects,
and where drought conditions prevail.
Designers must identify orographic zones when siting
[[mollison-settlement-siting-on-slopes-and-landscape-profile]]
and planning [[mollison-farm-forestry-and-woodlot-design]]
systems. Windward slopes receive dramatically more
precipitation than leeward slopes, and this difference can
determine the viability of entire agricultural systems.
### Cyclonic or Frontal Rainfall
This results from the over-riding of cool and warm air
masses of the polar circulation. Cyclonic rainfall tends to
be widespread but variable, producing the major rain events
of temperate and sub-polar regions. It is less predictable
than orographic rainfall and more associated with seasonal
storm patterns.
Frontal rainfall design considerations include the need for
[[mollison-earthworks-planning-and-post-earthworks-planting]] systems and the recognition that large
individual rain events may supply much of the annual water
budget.
### Convective Rainfall
Convective rain results from columns of hot air rising from
deserts or oceans into cooler air. This process creates the
intense thunderstorms of tropical and subtropical regions.
Convective rainfall is often highly localised and intense,
producing large volumes of water in short periods.
The design implications of convective rainfall include the
need for rapid [[mollison-pdc-swale-design-water-harvesting]] that can absorb intense downpours, and the
recognition that between convective events, long dry periods
may prevail.
## Precipitation as a Design Limiting Factor
As precipitation rises, available light decreases. In
extremely cloudy industrial or fog-bound humid climates,
light becomes the limiting factor for some plants to ripen
or even flower. At the dry end of the rainfall spectrum (as
we reach 50 cm or 20 inches mean rainfall), sun is plentiful
and evaporation in excess of precipitation becomes the
limiting factor.
That factor determines our [[mollison-drought-proofing-strategies]], just as the depth of seasonally frozen
soils and ice cover determines water reticulation strategies
in cold climates. The designer must identify which factor
is limiting in their specific location and design
accordingly.
## Dew and Fog as Precipitation Supplements
Beyond rain, dew and fog represent important precipitation
sources in many climates:
- Dew is a radiation heat loss effect from earth with clear
  night skies, most significant in deserts
- Fog can provide extraordinary quantities of moisture: up
  to 329 cm (128 inches) for Table Mountain, South Africa,
  from fog drip alone
- [[mollison-designers-fog-and-dew-harvesting]] can supplement or replace rainfall in
  appropriate locations
## Precipitation and the Water Cycle
The sustained flow of rivers is remarkable considering that
precipitation is an unusual event in most areas. At Paris,
it rains for only 577 hours a year, or about 7% of the time,
yet rivers flow continuously. The sustaining source of flow
is effluent groundwater. The amount of soil water is about
fifteen times the amount in channel storage in rivers.
This means that the designer's primary task is often not to
capture rain directly but to ensure that precipitation enters
the soil where it can be stored and released slowly. The
[[mollison-designers-soil-water-storage-in-forest-systems]] system, maintained by forests and organic
matter, is more important than any tank or dam.
## Precipitation and Design Strategy
Design strategies must be matched to precipitation type:
- OROGRAPHIC zones: maximise forest cover on windward
  slopes, use rain shadow areas for drought-adapted
  systems
- FRONTAL zones: design for seasonal water storage,
  flood-safe [[mollison-zone-planning-frequency-distance-and-placement]]
- CONVECTIVE zones: emphasise rapid infiltration via
  swales and contour earthworks, plan for extended dry
  periods between intense events
- FOG zones: deploy [[mollison-designers-forest-condensation-and-micro-climate-rainfall]] systems, select fog-adapted species
## Design Implications for Climate Zones
The broad climatic zones each present characteristic
precipitation patterns that drive design:
- EQUATORIAL (over 50 degrees latitude, over 250 cm rain):
  rainforests, monsoon forests, frequent rainfall
- SUBTROPICAL (30-40 degrees): palms, coasts, seasonal
  rainfall patterns
- TEMPERATE (40-55 degrees): moderate, evenly distributed
  rainfall, four seasons
- COLD (over 55 degrees): snow-dominated precipitation,
  frozen ground storage
Each zone demands specific approaches to water management,
building design, and [[mollison-tropical-mulch-systems-and-materials]] suited to the local precipitation
regime.
