## Capillary Action in Soils

### Temporary Wilting Point
Most vegetable species experience temporary wilting during hot afternoon hours when transpiration demand exceeds the roots' ability to extract water. This is a normal, non-damaging phenomenon if the soil still contains moisture above the permanent wilting point. Plants recover overnight as transpiration demand drops. However, Solomon warns that many vegetables experience subtle moisture stress well before any visible wilting occurs, and this invisible stress significantly reduces growth and quality.

## Capillarity and Seed Germination

Seeds require consistent moisture to germinate. The seed must absorb water to trigger the biochemical processes of growth. If the soil surface dries out, even briefly, germination can fail. This is why capillary connections to the soil surface are so critical during the germination period.

Solomon's seeding technique takes direct advantage of capillarity. After preparing a seedbed, he advises letting it settle for a few days so that capillary connections are restored throughout the soil profile. Then, seeds are placed at the proper depth and covered with fine, loose soil. The capillary network below the seed draws moisture upward continuously, keeping the seed environment consistently moist without the need for surface watering (which can create crusting and wash seeds away).

For tiny seeds that must be sown very shallowly, Solomon recommends covering them with a thin layer of fine compost or aged manure rather than mineral soil. These organic materials hold more moisture at the surface and maintain better capillary contact with the seed, improving germination rates.

## Capillarity and Drought Gardening

Solomon's entire approach to low-irrigation and dry gardening is built on understanding and managing capillary action. The key principles are:

### Wide Spacing
Plants grown far apart develop much larger root systems. Each plant has access to a larger volume of soil moisture. More importantly, the wide spacing reduces the total leaf area per unit of ground, reducing overall transpiration demand. This means the rate at which plants remove moisture from the soil is slower than the rate at which capillary action can replenish it from below.

### Surface Maintenance
The capillary network at the soil surface must be preserved. This means avoiding deep cultivation that breaks capillary connections. Solomon advocates shallow weeding with a sharpened hoe that cuts weeds just below the surface without disturbing deeper soil layers. Any cultivation that fluffs the soil surface actually reduces its ability to conduct water upward.

### Mulching Considerations
A thin mulch can reduce surface evaporation, but thick mulches create their own problems. They keep the surface moist but do not significantly reduce overall moisture loss, because most water is lost through plant transpiration, not through soil surface evaporation. In mild-winter climates, permanent mulching can create habitat for pest animals and slow spring soil warming.

### Soil Structure
Soil with good structure — a mix of sand, silt, clay, and organic matter in roughly equal proportions — has the most effective capillary network. Pure sand has large pores where gravity dominates over capillary forces. Pure clay has such tiny pores that water moves through them extremely slowly. A well-structured loam provides the optimal balance of water retention and capillary conductivity.

## Capillarity and Irrigation

For gardeners who do irrigate, understanding capillarity helps avoid both under-watering and over-watering:

### Under-watering
Many gardeners wet the surface with a hose nozzle daily, creating the appearance of adequate moisture while deeper soil remains dry. The surface inches stay damp, the gardener assumes all is well, but plant roots in the deeper dry zone are stunted. Plants may not wilt (because their shallow roots find surface moisture) but they suffer severe growth restriction. The capillary network from the watered surface zone is insufficient to rewet deeper dry soil.

### Over-watering
Applying too much water pushes moisture below the root zone through gravity, carrying water-soluble nutrients with it (leaching). The surface soil may appear well-watered while the fertility has been flushed beyond the plants' reach. Solomon advises watering deeply enough to bring the entire root zone to field capacity, then waiting until the upper portion of the root zone has dried sufficiently before watering again.

### The Ideal Pattern
Water thoroughly but infrequently. Each irrigation should wet the soil to the full depth of the root zone. Then allow time for the plants to draw down the moisture through transpiration before irrigating again. This pattern encourages deep root growth and minimizes nutrient leaching.
