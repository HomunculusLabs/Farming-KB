# Swale Design

Swales are shallow, level-bottomed channels dug on contour across a slope to capture, slow, and infiltrate surface water runoff. They are one of the most important tools in the permaculture water management toolkit, transforming erosive runoff into deep soil hydration. Swales work in conjunction with [[keyline-design]] principles and are fundamental to [[permaculture-design-methods]] for sloped landscapes.

## What Swales Do

Swales perform several critical functions simultaneously:

- **Capture runoff**: Intercepts water flowing downhill across the land surface during rain events
- **Slow water**: The level-bottomed channel eliminates the velocity that causes erosion
- **Infiltrate water**: Gives captured water time to percolate deep into the soil profile, recharging groundwater
- **Prevent erosion**: By stopping concentrated water flow, swales eliminate gully formation and topsoil loss
- **Build soil**: Organic matter and sediment settle in the swale, building fertile soil on the downhill berm
- **Create growing zones**: The berm below the swale becomes a moisture-rich planting zone ideal for trees and perennials
- **Reduce irrigation needs**: By recharging soil moisture deep in the profile, swales can reduce or eliminate supplemental irrigation for downslope plantings

## Contour Mapping

Finding and marking the contour (line of equal elevation) across your landscape is the essential first step.

### Tools for Finding Contour

- **A-frame level**: The classic permaculture tool. Two legs connected by a crossbar, with a plumb bob hanging from the apex. When the string aligns with a center mark, the two feet are at the same elevation. Simple to build from scrap lumber and accurate enough for homestead-scale earthworks.
- **Laser level**: Modern, fast, and highly accurate. Place the laser on a tripod at one point and use a detector on a staff to mark contour points. Best for large properties or precision work.
- **Water level**: A clear hose filled with water. The water level at both ends will be at the same elevation. Simple but less precise than other methods.
- **Transit or builder's level**: Surveyor's instrument. Most precise option but requires some expertise and equipment investment.

### Marking Contour

1. Start at the highest point where you want to place a swale
2. Use your leveling tool to find the next point at the same elevation
3. Move along the slope, marking stakes every 10-20 feet
4. Connect the stakes with flagging tape to visualize the swale line
5. Walk the line and look for anomalies: does it dip or rise unexpectedly? Adjust as needed
6. Repeat for additional swales at intervals down the slope

## Spacing Swales

The vertical distance between swales depends on slope, soil type, rainfall, and intended land use:

| Slope (%) | Vertical Interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3% (gentle) | 50-100 feet | Wide spacing; swales can be shallow |
| 3-8% (moderate) | 25-50 feet | Standard homestead spacing |
| 8-15% (steep) | 15-25 feet | Close spacing; deeper swales needed |
| >15% (very steep) | 10-15 feet | Consider terracing instead; erosion risk high |

**Rule of thumb**: On a 5% slope with average rainfall, swales spaced every 30-40 vertical feet capture most runoff while maintaining adequate planting area between them.

## Sizing

### Width

- **Standard swale**: 2-4 feet wide at the base
- **Small property swale**: 1-2 feet wide
- **Large-scale swale**: 4-8 feet wide (tractor-dug)
- **Width determines capacity**: A 3-foot wide swale on a 5% slope can capture the runoff from approximately 15-30 feet of slope above it in a moderate rain event

### Depth

- **Standard swale**: 1-2 feet deep
- **Key consideration**: The swale should be deep enough to hold the expected water volume but shallow enough that it drains within 24-48 hours (to prevent mosquito breeding and root rot)
- **Deeper is not always better**: Excessively deep swales on steep slopes can create instability

### Volume Calculation

For a typical swale on a homestead:
- Cross-section: approximately triangular, width x depth / 2
- A 3-foot wide by 1.5-foot deep swale holds about 2.25 cubic feet per linear foot
- 100 linear feet holds 225 cubic feet or approximately 1,685 gallons

For heavy rainfall areas, size swales to handle at least a 10-year, 24-hour storm event without overflowing.

## Construction

### Manual Construction

1. **Mark the contour line** with stakes and flagging tape
2. **Clear the line** of large rocks, roots, and debris
3. **Dig the swale** along the contour, piling excavated soil on the downhill side to form the berm
4. **Level the bottom**: Check with your level tool frequently. The bottom must be perfectly level. Even a 1% slope will cause water to flow to one end and overflow.
5. **Shape the berm**: Compact the downhill berm slightly. Round the top. The berm should be 2-3 feet wide at the base and 1-2 feet tall.
6. **Stabilize immediately**: Plant the berm and swale bottom with cover crop seed or mulch heavily to prevent erosion during the first rains

### Machine Construction

1. Hire an excavator or use a tractor with a box blade
2. Mark the contour with stakes set high enough for the operator to see
3. Make a single pass along the contour, adjusting depth until level
4. Check level frequently with a laser level or by filling the swale with a small amount of water
5. Shape the berm with the machine's bucket
6. Stabilize immediately with mulch or cover crop

### Integration with Keyline Design

Swales and keyline design share the goal of hydrating landscapes but work differently:

- **Swales** intercept surface water at specific contour intervals
- **Keyline cultivation** uses shallow plowing parallel to keylines to spread water from valleys to ridges without ponding

On a well-designed homestead, both can be combined: swales capture and infiltrate the bulk of surface water, while keyline pattern cultivation manages the remaining subsurface flow. See [[keyline-design]] for detailed keyline principles.

## Planting the Swale System

### Berm (Downhill Side)

The berm is the most productive planting zone. It receives moisture from the swale above and nutrients from settling sediment.

**Tree planting on the berm**:
- Plant fruit and nut trees on the downhill side of the berm, not on top (to avoid waterlogging)
- Space according to mature tree canopy size
- Mulch heavily; the berm is the foundation of your [[food-forest-design]]
- Good tree choices: apple, pear, plum, cherry, chestnut, hazelnut, pawpaw, persimmon

**Understory planting**:
- Nitrogen-fixing shrubs (eleagnus, seaberry, Siberian pea shrub)
- Berry bushes (currants, gooseberries, aronia)
- Comfrey and other [[dynamic-accumulators]]
- Perennial herbs (chives, oregano, thyme)
- [[cover-cropping-guide]] species for ground cover

### Swale Bottom

The swale bottom can be planted with moisture-loving species or left as a infiltration channel:

- **Grass cover**: Easy to maintain, filters water effectively
- **Mint family**: Thrives in moist conditions; be aware of spreading tendencies
- **Rushes and sedges**: Native species that stabilize the swale bottom
- **Berry bushes**: Elderberry, gooseberry, and currant tolerate periodic moisture

### Swale Above (Upslope Side)

The area above the swale should be managed to reduce sediment loading:
- Maintain ground cover with [[cover-cropping-guide]] or permanent pasture
- Avoid bare soil that erodes into the swale
- Consider [[no-till-farming]] practices in the upslope catchment

## Spillways

Every swale needs an emergency spillway to handle water volumes that exceed the swale's capacity:

- **Location**: At the natural high point of the swale (usually the widest point where overflow would be most gentle)
- **Design**: A reinforced, level section of the berm that allows water to flow over in a controlled sheet rather than cutting a channel
- **Armoring**: Line the spillway with stone, grass, or geotextile to prevent erosion
- **Never pipe the spillway**: Allow water to flow over as a broad, shallow sheet to dissipate energy

## Maintenance

- **Year 1**: Check after every significant rain event. Fill any erosion channels. Reseed bare spots. The swale and berm will settle; add soil as needed.
- **Ongoing**: Clear sediment buildup from the swale bottom annually. This sediment is rich topsoil — spread it on the berm or garden beds. Check the level bottom annually and re-level if settling has occurred.
- **After 3-5 years**: The swale system should be largely self-maintaining as vegetation stabilizes all surfaces. Minimal intervention needed.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Water flows to one end | Swale bottom is not level | Re-check with level; re-dig the low or high end |
| Swale overflows at the wrong spot | No spillway; blockage in swale | Create a spillway at the natural high point; clear debris |
| Berm erodes | Insufficient vegetation; concentrated flow | Reseed and mulch immediately; check for channelized flow |
| Water stands for more than 3 days | Impermeable subsoil; compacted bottom | Break up compacted layer; add organic matter; consider french drain |
| Trees on berm are waterlogged | Trees planted on top of berm instead of downhill side | Replant on downhill side; improve drainage |
| Mosquitoes breeding | Standing water for too long | Ensure drainage within 48 hours; add Bacillus thuringiensis (Bti) dunks; encourage dragonflies |
