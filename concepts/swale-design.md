# Swale Design

Swale design is the planning and construction of level or near-level channels built on contour to intercept, slow, and infiltrate surface water runoff into the landscape. Swales are one of the most important earthworking tools in permaculture, serving as the primary means of rehydrating degraded landscapes, preventing erosion, and establishing tree crops on previously arid or marginal land. Bill Mollison devotes significant attention to swales in both his dryland strategies and general water harvesting chapters.

## Definition and Function

A swale is a dead-level hollow built on contour, although the base need not be perfectly level, which creates temporary pools during and after rainfall. Swales are ripped, gravelled, sanded, or planted at the base to assist rapid water infiltration. They differ from drains in that their purpose is to hold and infiltrate water, not to carry it away. A drain removes water; a swale keeps it on the land.

The primary functions of swales include intercepting surface runoff before it gains erosive velocity, spreading water across the landscape rather than concentrating it, allowing time for water to infiltrate into the soil profile, recharging groundwater aquifers, creating moist planting zones for tree establishment, and reducing the need for irrigation in [[permaculture-plants-food-forest-ground-covers-bamboos]].

Swale dimensions vary with site conditions and purpose. For broadacre applications, swales are typically 1-2 metres deep and 2-4 metres wide at the base. For home gardens, smaller swales of 30-60 cm depth and 60-100 cm width are appropriate. The berm height equals the excavation depth, creating a total height difference of 1.5-3 metres from swale bottom to berm top for broadacre swales.

## Contour Identification

Accurate contour identification is essential for swale construction. Tools for finding contour include an A-frame level, a bunyip water level, a laser level, or a dumpy level. The A-frame is the simplest and most accessible tool, made from three poles forming an A-shape with a plumb bob or spirit level at the apex. When both legs touch the ground at the same level, the A-frame is on contour.

For large-scale earthworks, professional survey with a laser level or total station provides the most accurate results. However, small errors in level are acceptable for most swale applications, as the swale will function effectively as long as water is broadly retained and distributed. In practice, swales are built approximately on contour with slight adjustments for site-specific conditions.

## Design Considerations

Swale spacing depends on slope, soil type, and rainfall intensity. On gentle slopes of 1-3 degrees, swales may be spaced 20-50 metres apart. On steeper slopes of 5-15 degrees, spacing may be 5-15 metres apart. The spacing should ensure that water from any point on the slope above reaches a swale before gaining enough velocity to cause erosion.

Soil type affects swale design. On sandy, well-drained soils, water infiltrates quickly and swales can be smaller and more widely spaced. On clay soils with slow infiltration, swales must be larger to handle the volume of water without overflowing during heavy rain. In rocky terrain, swales may be shallow with rock-filled bases to increase infiltration.

The swale bottom can be planted to water-loving species such as willow, bamboo, or useful grasses. In dry climates, the swale bottom may be left bare or planted to sparse grasses to maximise water infiltration. In humid climates, the swale bottom can support a dense planting of useful plants.

## Swales in Arid Climates

Mollison emphasises the critical importance of swales in arid landscapes. Water is the dominant theme for designers in arid areas, and swales are the primary tool for harvesting and infiltrating water into the soil. In arid regions, swales may need to handle very large volumes of water from infrequent but intense rainfall events.

The cheapest water storages for trees are permeable earth swales alongside roads, which are essentially large compacted collection areas. Swales must be large enough to take all tank or storage overflow, pavement runoff, and additional harvested and diverted overland water flow from the environs outside the settlement. They may need a capacity of 200 percent of village runoff, which then infiltrates locally as soil water storage to 3-5 metres down in the earth.

## Swales in Humid Climates

In humid climates, swales serve to prevent erosion on slopes and distribute excess water. Swales in high-rainfall areas must be designed with adequate spillway capacity to handle large volumes of water during storms. The planting zone is more productive in humid climates, with a wider range of species thriving in the moist conditions.

Swales in humid climates can be designed as part of integrated aquaculture systems, with water from swales feeding ponds and wetland areas. The transition from swale to pond creates edge habitat that is among the most productive in the landscape.

## Swales and Keyline Design

Swale design relates to keyline [[pdc-urban-permaculture-design-principles]] for managing stormwater runoff. Road runoff can be directed to swales where trees are growing, as practised at Village Homes in Davis, California. Urban swales reduce the need for stormwater drains, gutters, and kerbing, while growing the trees needed to shade pavements, courtyards, and parking areas.

Small domestic swales of stone-filled construction provide shaded soil-water sources for vine trellis and garden beds. Rooftop rainwater can be directed to swales in the garden for infiltration and tree irrigation. This approach reduces municipal water demand and prevents stormwater pollution of waterways.

## Maintenance

Established swales require minimal maintenance. The primary maintenance tasks include ensuring spillways remain clear and functional, replanting any bare areas on the berm, removing sediment that accumulates in the swale bottom, and monitoring for erosion or structural failure. Well-planted swales with established tree cover become largely self-maintaining as the root systems stabilise the soil.

Sediment accumulation in swale bottoms gradually reduces infiltration capacity. Periodic cleaning, every 5-10 years depending on erosion rates upstream, may be necessary to maintain performance. Accumulated sediment can be spread on the berm as topsoil.
