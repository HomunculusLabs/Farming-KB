# Mushrooms as Medicine — Mycelium Running

In "Mycelium Running," Paul Stamets presents medicinal mushrooms as a largely untapped pharmacological frontier. He documents antibiotic, antiviral, anticancer, and immunomodulatory properties across numerous species, arguing that old-growth forests harbor undiscovered medicines critical to human health and national defense. He frames mushrooms as organisms that can simultaneously address the causes of disease while bolstering the food web.

## Antibiotics from Mushrooms

Mushrooms naturally produce a surprising array of antibiotics. The cell surface of mycelium "sweats" out exudates -- [[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]]), cortinellin from shiitake (Lentinula edodes), ganomvein from reishi ([[maitake-grifola-frondosa-comprehensive-profile]], [[growing-gourmet-polyporus-umbellatus-zhu-ling-cultivation]], and Trametes versicolor.

Stamets hypothesizes that studying mushroom-bacteria-virus-bacteriophage interrelationships will reveal medically significant antibiotics. Mushrooms having both antiviral and antibacterial properties may prevent opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients, such as those whose respiratory viral infections make them susceptible to Streptococcus pneumonia. Virologists are concerned about the threat of viral infection from animals, as demonstrated by the 2003 SARS epidemic.

## Agarikon and Biodefense

Stamets describes his discovery that agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis) mycelial extract protects human blood cells from orthopox viruses, the family that includes smallpox. Strains varied in potency. Through the Biodefense BioShield program, jointly administered by NIH and USAMRIID, agarikon showed potent activity against vaccinia pox and cowpox viruses. The genome of this species may yield novel antivirals and should be protected.

He draws a parallel to the 1941 discovery of Penicillium chrysogenum from a moldy cantaloupe sent to an army research lab, noting that while Penicillium enjoys cosmopolitan distribution, agarikon is restricted to endangered old-growth forest habitat in the Pacific Northwest. Less than 5% of these old-growth forests survive after 150 years of logging. Stamets argues that old-growth forests become more valuable as a source of antiviral medicines than as timber, and that protecting fungal genetic diversity is a matter of national defense against both natural and weaponized diseases.

## Cancer Research

Stamets presents research tables showing antitumor activity for multiple species including Agaricus brasiliensis, Clitocybe illudens (poisonous but active), Cordyceps sinensis, Flammulina velutipes, Ganoderma lucidum (reishi), Grifola frondosa (maitake), Hericium erinaceus (lion's mane), Inonotus obliquus (chaga), Lentinula edodes (shiitake), Phellinus linteus, Piptoporus betulinus, Pleurotus ostreatus, Polyporus umbellatus, Schizophyllum commune, and Trametes versicolor (turkey tail). The research spans 30 years of study, and many of these species show activity against specific cancers including breast, cervical, colon, gastric, lung, liver, prostate, and sarcoma cancers.

## Therapeutic Cross-Index

The book provides a cross-index of mushrooms and targeted therapeutic effects, listing properties such as antibacterial, anti-Candida, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumor, antiviral, blood sugar moderation, cholesterol reduction, immune enhancement, cardiovascular support, kidney tonic, liver tonic, lung/respiratory support, nerve tonic, sexual potentiator, and stress reduction. Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) and Cordyceps sinensis show activity across the widest range of categories. Chaga (Inonotus obliquus) and shiitake (Lentinula edodes) are also notably broad-spectrum.

## Nutritional Medicine and Complementary Therapy

Mushrooms contain polysaccharides, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, ergosterols, triterpenoids, enzymes, acids, and antibiotics useful as complements to conventional medicine. Shiitake consumption for 30 days reduced LDL cholesterol by up to 30% in one study (Cunde-Cimerman and Plemenitas 2002). Oyster mushrooms contain a natural isomer of lovastatin, an FDA-approved cholesterol-lowering drug. Researchers have found that mushrooms contain compounds that when used individually and in concert can stop infection, and each species has a signature architecture and defense against microbes.

## Mobilizing Mushrooms against AIDS

Several mushroom species -- oyster, shiitake, maitake, turkey tails, and other polypores -- have shown anti-HIV activity. The NIH funded a clinical study using oyster mushrooms with protease inhibitors, supervised by UCSF School of Medicine -- possibly the first NIH-funded clinical trial studying medicinal effects of ingesting mushrooms. Protease inhibitors cause LDL cholesterol accumulation; oyster mushrooms' cholesterol-reducing properties combined with anti-HIV glycoproteins suggest they could dually mitigate protease inhibitor side effects while fighting AIDS.

Stamets argues that mushroom cultivation enterprises in developing countries are well positioned to help fight HIV while also addressing famine, waste recycling, soil building, crop protection, and job creation. Mushroom farms could reinvent themselves as healing arts centers.

## Three Modes of Protection

Stamets proposes that mushrooms offer protection from infectious diseases in three ways: first, directly as antimicrobial agents (antibiotics); second, by increasing the immune system's natural host-mediated defenses; and third, through custom construction of mycelial mats for mycofiltration to reduce infection risk from environmental sources such as sewage from feedlots and slaughterhouses. The key, he emphasizes, is to match the mushroom with the pathogen.

With the threat of bioterrorism from weaponized viruses, a readily available, inexpensive, broad-spectrum antiviral antidote would serve the public's health. Mushrooms, especially combinations of mushrooms, offer this potential.

## Old-Growth Forests as Medicine Cabinets

Stamets argues that preserving old-growth forests is not merely an environmental issue but a medical imperative. Two-thirds of our pharmaceuticals still originate from nature. He cites taxol from Pacific yew bark as an example, and notes the endophytic fungus Taxomyces andreanae, found inhabiting yew trees, also synthesizes taxol. He has little doubt that many other mushrooms will provide antiviral or anticancer drugs -- provided our forests survive the effects of short-sighted political and corporate agendas.
