## Endorhiza-Soil OTU Sharing Patterns in Cannabis Cultivar Specificity

## Overview

A critical test of the two-tier selection model for plant-microbiome assembly is whether [[cannabis-rhizosphere-endorhiza-communities]] share more operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with the soil in which they are grown than with a different soil in which the same cultivar is grown. Winston et al. (2014) tested this prediction using Cannabis, providing direct evidence that soil serves as the primary source pool for root-colonizing microbes, even when host genotype exerts selective pressure on community structure.

## The Two-Step Colonization Hypothesis

The two-tier selection model posits a sequential process for root microbiome assembly:

**Step 1: Soil-driven rhizosphere selection.** [[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]] (pH, nitrogen, salinity, carbon, water content) determine the composition of the local soil microbiota. When plant roots exude compounds into the rhizosphere, the available microbial pool from which recruitment occurs is defined by the soil type. This first step is primarily abiotic.

**Step 2: Genotype-driven endorhiza selection.** Following rhizosphere enrichment, migration of bacteria from the rhizosphere into root tissue (endorhiza) is governed by host genotype-dependent factors. Plant-derived compounds, root architecture, and immune responses selectively filter which rhizosphere organisms can colonize the internal root environment.

If this model is correct, endorhiza communities should be soil-derived (sharing more OTUs with their own soil than with a different soil), even when the same cultivar is grown in different soil types.

## Experimental Test: White Widow in Two Soils

The key test was enabled by growing the White Widow cultivar in two distinct soil types within Experiment 2:

- **MB soil:** Higher salinity (5.12), lower organic carbon (3.02%), lower water content (0.113)
- **OC (Orange County) soil:** Lower salinity (1.73), much higher organic carbon (20.0%), higher water content (0.371)

By comparing the OTU composition of White Widow endorhiza communities from each soil, the researchers could directly test whether the same plant genotype produces different root microbiomes depending on which soil microbial pool is available for colonization.

## Results: OTU Sharing Analysis

The analysis compared the number of shared OTUs between endorhiza samples and their "own" soil (the soil they were grown in) versus the "other" soil (the different soil in which other White Widow plants were grown):

- **Shared OTUs with own soil:** Mean = 2,934 OTUs (n = 45 paired comparisons)
- **Shared OTUs with other soil:** Mean = 2,162 OTUs (n = 45 paired comparisons)
- **Statistical significance:** t = -10.05, p = 1.209 × 10⁻¹⁵

The difference of approximately 772 OTUs (26% more shared with own soil) is highly statistically significant. This demonstrates that the soil microbial pool fundamentally constrains which organisms are available to colonize the endorhiza, even when the host plant is genetically identical.

## Interpretation Within the Two-Tier Framework

These results support both tiers of the selection model:

**Tier 1 confirmation (soil as source pool):** Endorhiza communities share significantly more OTUs with their own soil than with a different soil. This confirms that soil type determines the available microbial diversity from which root colonization draws. The soil acts as a "menu" from which the plant selects its endorhiza community.

**Tier 2 confirmation (cultivar as filter):** Despite the soil-driven source pool, the same cultivar grown in different soils still maintains recognizable genotype-specific community features. This indicates that the plant exercises selective pressure on the available soil-derived candidates, recruiting specific taxa into the root interior based on genotype-dependent compatibility factors.

## Comparison with Beta Diversity Findings

The OTU sharing results are consistent with the broader beta diversity patterns observed in the study:

- Rhizosphere and bulk soil communities are more similar to each other than to endorhiza communities
- Beta distances between rhizosphere and bulk soil are significantly lower than between rhizosphere and endorhiza
- [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] across all samples is determined predominantly by soil properties
- Within-endorhiza community structure is significantly shaped by cultivar identity

The OTU sharing analysis provides complementary evidence to the beta diversity metrics by showing not just that communities differ, but that they share more taxonomic units when they derive from the same soil source.

## Cultivar-Specific OTU Differences

While soil determines the available pool, specific OTUs showed significant abundance differences between Cannabis cultivars. These cultivar-differentiating OTUs were predominantly Proteobacteria, including:

- **Pseudomonadales:** Differentially abundant between strains
- **Burkholderiales:** Strain-specific abundance patterns
- **Sphingomonadales:** Including the notable *[[sphingomonas-wittichii-cannabis-endorhiza-strain-specificity]]* prevalent in [[maui-wowie]]
- **Rhizobiales:** Strain-specific root-colonizing bacteria
- **Sphingobacteriales and Flavobacteriales:** Bacteroidetes orders also contributing to strain differences

The fact that these strain-differentiating OTUs belong to lineages present in the soil (not introduced de novo) reinforces the two-tier model: soil provides the organisms, cultivar selects among them.

## Soil-to-Root Migration Distance

The OTU sharing analysis also sheds light on the spatial gradient of microbial community change:

- **Bulk soil to rhizosphere:** High OTU sharing (Pearson's rho = 0.92 for mean abundance correlation), reflecting the close proximity and direct influence of [[root-exudates]] on adjacent soil
- **Rhizosphere to endorhiza:** Moderate OTU sharing (Pearson's rho = 0.63), reflecting the selective barrier at the root surface
- **Bulk soil to endorhiza:** Lower OTU sharing (Pearson's rho = 0.42), representing the cumulative effect of two filtering steps

This gradient confirms that each compartment transition involves progressive selection, with the sharpest filtering occurring at the rhizosphere-to-endorhiza boundary where plant immune responses and tissue compatibility factors act.

## Implications for Cannabis Cultivation

The OTU sharing patterns have practical implications for Cannabis agriculture:

- **Soil selection matters.** The choice of growing medium fundamentally determines which beneficial or pathogenic microbes are available for root colonization. Different soils provide different "menus" of potential root associates.
- **Cultivar-specific probiotics may need soil matching.** If a beneficial endorhiza organism is to be introduced, it must be compatible with both the target cultivar and the soil environment. An organism that thrives in one soil may not colonize roots in another.
- **[[soil-health-management]] is foundational.** Because the soil microbial pool constrains the endorhiza community, maintaining diverse and healthy soil biology is a prerequisite for developing beneficial root microbiomes, regardless of cultivar selection.
