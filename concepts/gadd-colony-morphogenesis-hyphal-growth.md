# Colony Morphogenesis and Hyphal Growth in Filamentous Fungi

The defining cellular element of filamentous fungi is the hypha, a
tip-growing cellular element that undergoes regular branching, is
typically multinucleate, and possesses incomplete cross-walls (septa)
which allow movement of cytoplasm and organelles between compartments.
Hyphae exhibit extraordinary developmental versatility and phenotypic
plasticity, responsible for the enormous diversity in patterns of
cellular and multicellular development within filamentous fungi.

## The supracellular nature of fungal colonies

Vegetative hyphae possess a supracellular nature because they form part
of a network of interconnected hyphal compartments and hyphae within
the colony. The colony combines features of a coenocyte (individual
mitoses not associated with cell divisions) and a syncytium (hyphae
fused together). This confers mechanisms for long-distance intracellular
communication, translocation of water, and transport of nutrients. It
also allows hyphae to act cooperatively and span heterogeneous
environments.

## Hyphal tip growth and the Spitzenkorper

The vegetative hypha grows by apical extension, with the Spitzenkorper
concentrating secretory vesicles at the hyphal tip. The extension zone
occupies a few micrometres at the hyphal tip. Hyphal compartments are
separated by septa possessing pores. In young colony regions, pores are
open, allowing organelle and cytoplasm passage. In older parts, septal
pores frequently become occluded. The apical hyphal compartment is
typically much longer than sub-apical compartments.

## Cell types derived from hyphae

Filamentous fungi produce several cell types: uninucleate spores, yeast
cells, and other determinate cell types including [[neurospora-crassa]] has 28 morphologically distinct
cell types. The mechanism of fungal multicellular development, involving
specialisation of hyphal compartments from aggregated hyphae, is
fundamentally different from animal or plant multicellularity.
