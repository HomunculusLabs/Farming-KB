# Brassinosteroid Promotion of Psilocybe Cubensis Mycelial Growth

Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt published a brief but significant
communication in *Naturwissenschaften* (Vol. 77, pp. 388-389, 1990)
documenting the first known growth-promoting effect of a
brassinosteroid on a fungal species. The synthetic compound
22S,23S-homobrassinolide, applied at 10^-2 ppm, dramatically
accelerated mycelial growth and enhanced fruiting in Psilocybe
cubensis cultures, suggesting a previously unsuspected
physiological role for these plant hormones in higher fungi.

## Background on Brassinosteroids

Brassinosteroids are a class of steroidal plant growth regulators
first identified in the 1970s. By 1990, approximately 30 members
had been detected across a wide variety of higher plants including
angiosperms, gymnosperms, and a green alga. They were recognized
as a distinct group of phytohormones involved in complex
physiological effects including cell elongation, cell division,
crop yield improvement, and anti-stress responses.

Despite extensive study in plants, no research had been published
on the occurrence or activity of brassinosteroids in fungi prior
to this study. The investigation was conducted by Jochen Gartz
(Institute of Biotechnology, Leipzig) and G. Adam and H.-M.
Vorbrodt (Institute of Plant Biochemistry, Halle/Saale), all of
the Academy of Sciences of the GDR.

## Experimental Design

The study used an earlier-described strain of P. cubensis
characterized by high stability in growth and fruiting. Two
cultivation systems were employed:

**Agar Cultivation:** 6% malt agar with 5g KH2PO4 per liter,
with and without 10^-2 ppm brassinosteroid. Ten experimental
runs were conducted for each condition.

**Dung/Grain Substrate:** A horse dung/rice grain mixture in
water with 5g KH2PO4 per liter, again with and without
brassinosteroid supplementation. The brassinosteroid used was
synthetic 22S,23S-homobrassinolide prepared from stigmasterol.

## Results

**Mycelial Growth on Agar:** Brassinosteroid-treated cultures
showed mycelial growth two to three times faster than untreated
controls. This dramatic acceleration of vegetative growth was
the primary finding on agar media.

**Fruiting on Dung/Grain Substrate:** The effects on fruiting
were even more pronounced. In control cultures, the first flush
of fruitbodies appeared within 4-5 weeks. With brassinosteroid
supplementation, fruiting began in just 3-3.5 weeks, a reduction
of approximately 25-30% in time to first harvest.

**Yield Enhancement:** Brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced
4-7 mushrooms in the first flush compared to only 1-3 in controls.
Total dry mass was also significantly higher: 3.4-3.9g per 10g
substrate versus 2.3-2.9g for controls, representing an increase
of approximately 35-48%.

**Quality Improvements:** The formation of incomplete fruitbodies,
normally present in mushroom cultivation, was "almost completely
suppressed" by the brassinosteroid treatment. Interestingly, the
treated mushrooms also lacked the typical membranous annulus (veil
ring) on the stems, indicating the steroid influenced morphological
development as well as growth rate.

## Significance and Implications

This study was the first demonstration that brassinosteroids
affect fungal growth, suggesting these compounds may play a
biological role in higher fungi that had been entirely overlooked.
The authors concluded that "brassinosteroids could also play a
physiological role in higher fungi" and called for further studies
including commercially important fungal species.

The morphological changes (absence of the annulus) were particularly
intriguing, as they suggested brassinosteroids influence
developmental gene expression in fungi, not simply growth rate.
The suppression of abortive fruitbodies and increased yield could
have practical applications in commercial mushroom cultivation.

The concentration used (10^-2 ppm) is extremely low, consistent
with the known potent activity of brassinosteroids in plant systems
where effects are observed at nanomolar concentrations. This
suggests that if brassinosteroids do occur naturally in fungi,
they may function at trace levels as endogenous growth regulators.

## Follow-Up Research Directions

The study opened several avenues for further investigation:
determination of whether brassinosteroids occur endogenously in
fungal tissues; testing of brassinosteroid effects on commercially
important mushroom species such as Agaricus bisporus and
Pleurotus ostreatus; characterization of the molecular mechanism
of brassinosteroid action in fungi; and exploration of optimal
concentrations and application timing for maximum yield enhancement.
Subsequent research in other fungal systems has confirmed that
brassinosteroids can affect a range of growth parameters, including
mycelial extension rate, biomass accumulation, and enzyme activity.
The commercial mushroom industry has shown increasing interest in
biological growth stimulants as alternatives to synthetic additives.
