# Brassinosteroid Promotion of Psilocybe Cubensis Mycelial Growth

Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt published a brief but significant communication in *Naturwissenschaften* (Vol. 77, pp. 388-389, 1990) documenting the first known growth-promoting effect of a brassinosteroid on a fungal species. The synthetic compound 22S,23S-homobrassinolide, applied at 10^-2 ppm, dramatically accelerated mycelial growth and enhanced fruiting in Psilocybe cubensis cultures, suggesting a previously unsuspected physiological role for these plant hormones in higher fungi.

## Background on Brassinosteroids

Brassinosteroids are a class of steroidal plant growth regulators first identified in the 1970s. By 1990, approximately 30 members had been detected across a wide variety of higher plants including angiosperms, gymnosperms, and a green alga. They were recognized as a distinct group of phytohormones involved in complex physiological effects including cell elongation, cell division, crop yield improvement, and anti-stress responses.

Despite extensive study in plants, no research had been published on the occurrence or activity of brassinosteroids in fungi prior to this study. The investigation was conducted by Jochen Gartz (Institute of Biotechnology, Leipzig) and G. Adam and H.-M. Vorbrodt (Institute of Plant Biochemistry, Halle/Saale), all of the Academy of Sciences of the GDR.

### Brassinosteroid Chemistry

The brassinosteroid used in this study — 22S,23S-homobrassinolide — is a synthetic analogue derived from [[stigmasterol-as-brassinosteroid-biosynthetic-precursor]]. Brassinosteroids share structural features with animal steroid hormones, featuring a cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene skeleton with specific hydroxyl and ketone substitutions. Their biosynthesis in plants proceeds from campesterol through a series of oxidation and hydroxylation steps catalyzed by cytochrome P450 enzymes. The existence of sterol biosynthesis pathways in fungi (producing ergosterol rather than cholesterol) raises the question of whether fungal endogenous brassinosteroid-like compounds might also be produced.

## Experimental Design

The study used an earlier-described strain of P. cubensis characterized by high stability in growth and fruiting. Two cultivation systems were employed:

**Agar Cultivation:** 6% malt agar with 5g KH2PO4 per liter, with and without 10^-2 ppm brassinosteroid. Ten experimental runs were conducted for each condition.

**Dung/Grain Substrate:** A horse dung/rice grain mixture in water with 5g KH2PO4 per liter, again with and without brassinosteroid supplementation.

The brassinosteroid used was synthetic 22S,23S-homobrassinolide prepared from stigmasterol. The [[brassinosteroid-experimental-methodology-gartz-1990]] followed a straightforward paired comparison design with ten replicates per condition, providing reasonable statistical confidence despite the brief communication format.

## Results

### Mycelial Growth on Agar

Brassinosteroid-treated cultures showed mycelial growth two to three times faster than untreated controls. This dramatic acceleration of vegetative growth was the primary finding on agar media. The [[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]] effect was consistent across all ten experimental runs, suggesting a robust physiological response rather than experimental artifact.

### Fruiting on Dung/Grain Substrate

The effects on fruiting were even more pronounced. In control cultures, the first flush of fruitbodies appeared within 4-5 weeks. With brassinosteroid supplementation, fruiting began in just 3-3.5 weeks, a reduction of approximately 25-30% in time to first harvest.

### Yield Enhancement

Brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced 4-7 mushrooms in the first flush compared to only 1-3 in controls. Total dry mass was also significantly higher: 3.4-3.9g per 10g substrate versus 2.3-2.9g for controls, representing an increase of approximately 35-48%.

### Quality Improvements

The formation of incomplete fruitbodies, normally present in mushroom cultivation, was "almost completely suppressed" by the brassinosteroid treatment. This [[abort-suppression-brassinosteroid-incomplete-fruit-body-reduction]] effect has practical significance for commercial growers, where abortive mushrooms represent wasted biological energy and reduced harvest quality.

Interestingly, the treated mushrooms also lacked the typical membranous annulus (veil ring) on the stems, indicating the steroid influenced morphological development as well as growth rate. The [[membranous-annulus-loss-brassinosteroid-induced-morphological-change]] suggests that brassinosteroids interact with developmental gene expression pathways in fungi, not simply with metabolic rate.

## Significance and Implications

This study was the first demonstration that brassinosteroids affect fungal growth, suggesting these compounds may play a biological role in higher fungi that had been entirely overlooked. The authors concluded that "brassinosteroids could also play a physiological role in higher fungi" and called for further studies including commercially important fungal species.

The morphological changes (absence of the annulus) were particularly intriguing, as they suggested brassinosteroids influence developmental gene expression in fungi, not simply growth rate. The suppression of abortive fruitbodies and increased yield could have practical applications in commercial mushroom cultivation.

The concentration used (10^-2 ppm, equivalent to approximately 18.5 nM) is extremely low, consistent with the known potent activity of brassinosteroids in plant systems where effects are observed at nanomolar concentrations. This suggests that if brassinosteroids do occur naturally in fungi, they may function at trace levels as endogenous growth regulators.

## Mechanism of Action: What We Know

The precise molecular mechanism by which brassinosteroids promote fungal growth remains unresolved, but several hypotheses have been proposed:

1. **Membrane interaction**: As steroidal compounds, brassinosteroids may interact with fungal cell membranes, altering membrane fluidity and permeability in ways that enhance nutrient uptake. This is consistent with the known role of sterols (particularly ergosterol) in maintaining fungal membrane integrity.

2. **Signal transduction**: In plants, brassinosteroids bind to BRI1 receptor kinases, triggering a phosphorylation cascade that regulates gene expression. Fungi possess receptor kinase systems, though no brassinosteroid-specific fungal receptor has been identified.

3. **Metabolic activation**: Brassinosteroids may upregulate metabolic pathways involved in cell wall synthesis, protein production, or energy metabolism, effectively increasing the rate at which mycelium can extend and accumulate biomass.

4. **Hormesis**: The [[ultra-low-dose-brassinosteroid-response-psilocybe-cubensis]] may represent a hormetic response — a beneficial effect from a low-dose compound that could be inhibitory or neutral at higher concentrations. This biphasic dose-response pattern is well-documented for many bioactive compounds.

## Comparison with Standard Cultivation Parameters

For context, the [[oss-oeric-growing-parameters-cubensis]] specify standard growth rates for P. cubensis on malt agar without supplementation. Typical mycelial extension rates range from 3-6 mm per day depending on strain and temperature. The two to three-fold acceleration reported by Gartz et al. would translate to an effective rate of 6-18 mm per day, which is remarkable for a compound applied at parts-per-billion concentrations.

## Follow-Up Research Directions

The study opened several avenues for further investigation:

- **Endogenous detection**: Determination of whether brassinosteroids occur endogenously in fungal tissues using mass spectrometry and radiolabeling techniques
- **Commercial species testing**: Effects on Agaricus bisporus, Pleurotus ostreatus, Lentinula edodes, and other commercially important mushroom species
- **Dose-response curves**: Systematic exploration of optimal concentrations and whether higher doses produce inhibitory effects
- **Molecular mechanisms**: Characterization of brassinosteroid binding sites and downstream signaling pathways in fungal cells
- **Combination treatments**: Testing brassinosteroids alongside other growth promoters, such as [[mycelial-growth-enhancement-with-phytohormones]] including auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins

## Broader Context: Phytohormones in Fungi

The Gartz et al. study fits into a growing body of evidence that phytohormones — compounds traditionally considered plant-specific — have activity in fungal systems. Auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, ethylene, and abscisic acid have all been reported to affect fungal growth, development, or secondary metabolism. Some fungi even produce these compounds endogenously. The cross-kingdom activity of plant hormones suggests deep evolutionary conservation of steroid and isoprenoid signaling pathways that predates the divergence of plants and fungi.

The [[brassinosteroid-cross-kingdom-growth-regulation-plants-fungi]] concept has implications beyond cultivation: understanding how steroidal growth regulators function across kingdoms may reveal fundamental principles of eukaryotic cell biology and identify new tools for biotechnology and agriculture.
