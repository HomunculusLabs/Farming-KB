# Fukuoka Reflections on Japanese Character and Culture

After traveling through Europe and America, [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] turned his critical eye toward his own people, producing one of the most searching self-examinations of Japanese national character in modern agricultural literature. His observations cut through both flattery and condemnation to reveal the psychological vulnerabilities beneath Japan's postwar economic success.

## The Arms Race Paradox

Fukuoka opens with a meditation on the absurdity of the arms race:

- Weapons have taken on a life of their own, "moving people" rather than the reverse
- Robots are building an endless supply of weapons and may one day use them
- "The more powerful the arms we fight with, the more intense our wars"
- Building monstrous weapons creates the cause of monstrous tragedy
- He links this to the militarization of children through war toys, films, and video games
- Japanese youth are being "thoroughly inculcated" with military culture through entertainment

The Japanese characters for halberd and shield together mean "contradiction," which Fukuoka takes as emblematic: "The more we talk of offense and defense, the more these escalate, until everything gets out of hand."

## The Japanese Mentality

Fukuoka identifies a core psychological trait of the Japanese people:

- Brought up in homes full of affection, Japanese children become "obedient children who comply meekly with the desires of their parents"
- This fosters "an irresponsible personality that is only too willing to leave things up to others"
- On the surface, Japanese appear attentive to others' needs, obedient to society, cooperative
- But this obedience conceals pent-up anger that, when it explodes, transforms people into barbarians
- "If everyone does it, it's not frightening" -- the dangerous psychology of the group
- "The nail that sticks out shall be hammered in" and "One cannot win over one's betters" are sayings that reveal a culture of conformity

His devastating conclusion: "The Japanese are a good people who love chrysanthemums. But, depending on the leadership, they can be transformed with great ease into barbaric warriors."

## The Absence of Religious Core

Fukuoka argues that the Japanese have never possessed genuine religious conviction:

- When the integral blend of Shinto and Buddhism was rejected, "this was radically transformed and split apart without the slightest objection"
- Christianity, Islam, or any other religion "would have suited the Japanese just as well as Buddhism"
- The Japanese have become "caught in a quagmire of heresy from which they can no longer extricate themselves"
- Their ethics and morality "float about unmoored like waterweed on a pond, spinning and tumbling without end"
- Religious ceremonies have become tourist attractions while men of religion "busily amass money and power"

He distinguishes between the philosophers who "noncommittally lay out plausible-sounding arguments" and the true task of philosophy: to unify discordant value systems and show the one absolute value.

## Americanization and Cultural Decline

Fukuoka saw Japan rapidly being Americanized and plunging "into a culture of decadence":

- Japan is looked down upon as "an economic animal, a materialistic nation of worker bees"
- Japanese fail to understand what this reputation means
- Scientists "dance about wildly, drunk on the sound of the words 'high technology'"
- Politicians "rush ahead madly along the road to the military-industrial merchants of death while hoisting high the flag of peace"

He saw this as a suicidal trajectory: following "in the footsteps of an aberrant America where news of a 50-percent divorce rate and 50,000 child abductions a year is normal."

## East-West Philosophical Divergence

Fukuoka contrasts Eastern and Western approaches to resolving contradiction:

- Western philosophy seeks balance through dialectic, walking a "middle road" between opposing forces
- This is "just a half-way street that leads nowhere"
- Oriental philosophy holds that ours is "an absolute, selfless world that transcends space and time"
- The standpoint of "absolute universality" -- "which may be called the standpoint of God" -- is the only position from which true governance is possible
- "Any attempt for dialogue and cooperation from a relativistic perspective will only make the road almost impossibly long"
- But if one stands where God stands and opens his heart, "consensus and dialogue will no longer be necessary"

He invokes Tolstoy's Ivan the Fool and Gandhi as representatives of the alternative path: "The swords of demons cannot stand up to the hands of babes."

## Fukuoka on Japanese Agriculture

Fukuoka viewed modern Japanese agriculture as a betrayal of the relationship between people and land. The postwar push for high yields through chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and mechanization had, in his view, destroyed the biological foundation of Japanese soil within a single generation. Rice paddies that had been productive for centuries without external inputs became dependent on ever-increasing chemical applications. He saw the green revolution in Japan not as progress but as a form of spiritual and ecological bankruptcy, where farmers abandoned their role as stewards of the land and became instead chemical applicators. The beauty of the traditional Japanese agricultural landscape, with its terraced rice paddies, irrigation canals, and integrated fish farming, was being replaced by monoculture fields sustained by petroleum-based inputs. For Fukuoka, the decline of Japanese agriculture mirrored the decline of the Japanese spirit: both had traded their inherent wisdom for imported technology and the illusion of control.

## Mountain Village Life and Spiritual Connection

Fukuoka's deepest spiritual experiences came from his years living in a mountain village on Shikoku Island, where he practiced [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] on a small farm surrounded by citrus orchards and mixed forest. He saw the mountain village as the last repository of authentic Japanese culture, a place where people still lived in direct relationship with the cycles of nature. The seasonal rhythms of planting, tending, and harvesting were not merely economic activities but spiritual practices that connected human beings to the larger order of the universe. In the mountain village, he observed that the boundary between human and nature was permeable: farmers who listened to the land knew when to plant by observing bird migration, flowering times, and soil moisture rather than consulting calendars or weather forecasts. This intuitive knowledge, accumulated over generations and transmitted without formal instruction, represented what Fukuoka considered the highest form of human intelligence, one that modern education systematically suppressed.

## Spiritual Traditions: Beyond Buddhism and Shinto

While Fukuoka is often associated with Buddhist or Daoist philosophy, his spiritual outlook was in fact a rejection of all organized religious systems. He regarded the traditional Japanese blend of Shinto and Buddhism not as a living spiritual tradition but as an empty ritual framework that had lost its connection to genuine spiritual experience. True spirituality, for Fukuoka, resided in the direct perception of nature's unity, a state he called the "world of no-mind" or "mu." This state could not be achieved through study, meditation, or religious practice but only through surrendering the discriminating intellect. His years of observation in the mandarin orange orchard led him to the realization that nature is inherently self-organizing and self-sufficient, and that human attempts to improve upon nature through knowledge and technology were the root cause of both environmental destruction and spiritual alienation. He drew inspiration from the Dao De Jing of Laozi and the teachings of the Zen sage Matsuoka Roshi, but he insisted that these texts were merely pointers toward a truth that could not be contained in words.

## Critique of Western Scientific Agriculture

Fukuoka's critique extended to the Western scientific worldview that underpins modern agriculture. He argued that the scientific method, which fragments nature into discrete variables for analysis, cannot comprehend the wholeness of natural systems. The reductionist approach of agronomy, which studies soil chemistry, plant pathology, and entomology as separate disciplines, misses the fundamental reality that these are aspects of an indivisible whole. In his [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]], he wrote that "the more scientists study nature, the further they move away from it." He saw the Western scientific project as driven by a desire for control and domination that was incompatible with the humility required to work with natural processes. The Japanese adoption of Western agricultural science was, in his view, doubly destructive because it replaced an indigenous agricultural wisdom that was already well-adapted to local conditions with a universalizing system that treated all land as identical and all farming problems as soluble through chemical or technological intervention.

## Philosophical Influences and the Way of Mu

Fukuoka's philosophy was shaped by a convergence of influences: the Daoist concept of wu wei (non-action or effortless action), the Buddhist teaching of emptiness (sunyata), and the Shinto sense of the sacred in nature. But he synthesized these traditions into something uniquely his own, which he called the "philosophy of mu" or the way of nothingness. This philosophy held that the highest human achievement was not to do something but to undo the artificial constructs of human knowledge that separate people from nature. In agricultural practice, this meant not adding fertilizers, not applying pesticides, not pruning fruit trees, and not plowing the soil. Each of these "don'ts" represented a surrender of human arrogance and a return to nature's own way of doing things. Fukuoka's rice and barley cultivation, which produced yields comparable to or exceeding conventional methods without any external inputs, was presented as proof that nature's way was not only ecologically sound but practically superior. His philosophy challenged the fundamental assumption of both Western and modern Japanese culture: that human intelligence can improve upon nature.

## The Peace March and Global Activism

In his later years, Fukuoka extended his critique of Japanese and Western culture into direct action through international peace activism. He organized a series of peace marches that carried the message of natural farming and ecological wisdom across continents, including journeys through Europe, Africa, India, and the United States. These marches were not merely symbolic gestures but practical demonstrations of his philosophy: participants ate natural foods, planted seeds along their routes, and engaged with local communities about sustainable agriculture. Fukuoka saw the peace march as an extension of his farming philosophy, arguing that the same mindset that separates humans from nature also drives nations to war. He believed that if people could learn to see themselves as part of the natural order rather than as its masters, both ecological destruction and warfare would cease. His global travels convinced him that the principles of [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] were universal and applicable in every climate and culture, from the rice paddies of Southeast Asia to the grain fields of the American Midwest.

## Fukuoka on Food Culture and the Japanese Diet

Fukuoka was deeply critical of Japan's postwar dietary transformation. The traditional Japanese diet, based on rice, barley, vegetables, miso, pickles, and small amounts of fish, represented for him a perfect integration of food production, human nutrition, and ecological wisdom. The postwar shift toward Western-style eating habits, including increased consumption of meat, dairy, refined sugar, and processed foods, was in his view both a symptom and a cause of Japan's cultural decline. He argued that the Japanese had abandoned their food self-sufficiency without understanding the consequences: dependence on imported food made Japan vulnerable to global market fluctuations and tied the nation to an agricultural system based on chemical inputs and environmental destruction. His [[fukuoka-critique-western-dietetics-nutritional-science]] extended to the scientific reductionism of modern nutrition, which isolates individual nutrients while ignoring the holistic quality of whole foods grown in healthy soil. For Fukuoka, the act of eating was itself a spiritual practice: consuming food grown naturally on healthy land connected the eater to the land, the farmer, and the larger cycles of nature.
