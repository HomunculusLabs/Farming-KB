## Tribal Uses of Patterning

## Overview

Indigenous and tribal peoples worldwide employ an enormous variety of traditional patterns — decorating weapons, houses, skin, woven textiles, and baskets. Far from being mere decoration, these patterns carry sophisticated meanings, and almost all are associated with songs or chants. Tribal art, including Celtic and ancient engraving, exhibits pattern complexity that conveyed important information to their peoples. To call such people "illiterate" is to ignore their patterns, songs, and dances as valid literature and accurate recording systems.

The abandonment of pattern learning in modern education, in favor of number and alphabetical symbols, represents what Mollison calls a "gross error." Simple patterns link so many phenomena that learning even one significant pattern is akin to learning an underlying principle — always applicable to specific data and situations.

## Polynesian Navigation Charts

The Maori of New Zealand use tattoo and carved patterns to record and recall genealogical and saga information. Polynesians developed **pattern [[maps]]** that lacked scale, cartographic detail, and trigonometric measures, yet sufficed to locate 200–2,000 island specks across the vast Pacific Ocean. These maps encoded:

- **Star sets** and their positions
- **Ocean currents** and their interactions
- **Wave interference patterns**
- Physical construction from sticks, flexed strips, cowries, and song cycles

A pattern map often had little meaning without its associated **song keys** to unlock that meaning. The map and the song were inseparable knowledge systems.

## Aboriginal Song Maps and Sand Patterns

The Pitjantjatjara people of Australia sing over **sand patterns**, and can "sing" strangers to a single stone in an apparently featureless desert. Their designs accurately reflect:

- **Lobular shapes** and elaborate micro-elevations of the desert terrain
- **Vegetation changes** across the landscape
- **Fire patterns**, pollen distributions, and flowering stages of specific plants
- **Rain tracks** and cloud streets
- **Hunting, saga, and climatic data**

What appears to Westerners as abstract art is in fact a sophisticated information encoding system — a geographic and ecological database rendered in pattern form.

## Song as Encoded Knowledge

Children in many tribes are taught hundreds of simple chants whose words hide deeper, secondary meanings about medicinal, sacred, or navigational knowledge. This encoded information becomes meaningful only when the initiate receives the decoding system, or discovers it through personal revelation (intuition).

### Nootka Navigation Songs

Anne Cameron (*Daughters of Copper Woman*, 1981) documents the **song navigation** of the Nootka Indians of British Columbia: "There was a song for goin' to China, and a song for goin' to Japan, a song for the big island and a song for the smaller one. All she [the navigator] had to know was the song and she knew where she was."

The navigation songs recorded "the streams and creeks of the sea" — ocean currents, headlands, bays, star constellations, and ceremonies of ecstatic revelation. Steerswomen used the accurate rhythm of song duration to time both current speed and boat speed. Song stanzas functioned as highly accurate, reproducible timers over long periods, while the song content served as a record of observations from prior voyages.

## Dance as Knowledge Transmission

Dances involve muscular learning and memory, coupled with chants, to carry accurate long-term messages, saga details, and planting knowledge. Many dances and chants evolved from work and travel movements. Dance-imitations of animal species interpret for people the postural meanings of those species in a non-verbal, universally transmittable way.

Mollison notes that many formal attitudes of prayer and submission are basic imitations of primate postures, taken from other species. Even the chair enables the seated posture of baboons — and baboons were revered as gods and embalmed by Egyptian chair-makers. Humans can remember hundreds of songs, postures, and chants, but little prose and even less tabulated data.

## Interspecies Communication

People who can call the deer (Paiute wise men), the dolphin (Gilbert Islanders), the kangaroo (tribal Tasmanians), and other species to present themselves possessed profound behavioral, interspecific "pulser" pattern understanding. The Eskimo navigated in fog by listening to quail dialects specific to certain headlands. Song and postural signals from other species form a rich encyclopedia of a world unnoticed by those lacking pattern knowledge.

Some tribal practitioners could even induce fibrillation in heart nerves — a practical insight into pulser stress induction. Many tribespeople could induce such behavior in other animal species or in people (sometimes referred to as voodoo or "singing").

## The Anasazi Spiral Calendar

Perhaps the most striking example of pattern encoding is the **[[anasazi-spiral]]** petroglyph, inscribed on a rock surface near the top of a mesa in the desert country of the southwestern USA. The apparently simple spiral form has 19 intercepts on its "horizon" line. Three carefully balanced and shaped rock slabs serve as gnomons that cast moon-shadows or direct vertical daggers of sunlight to the points of the spiral.

The 19 intersection points mark where the shadow of the moon is cast by a gnomon on the spiral, indicating the **lunar elevation or 19-year (actually 18.6-year) Metonic cycle** caused by the sway of the Earth's axis. Thus, one simple spiral simultaneously records:

- **Lunar cycles** for ceremony timing
- **Solar cycles** (equinoxes and solstices) for planting regulation
- The **18.6-year cycle of drought and flood**

The Anasazi culture is now extinct, and only persistent investigation by [[anna-soaer]] (an artist with intuitive observational skills) revealed the significance of this arrangement. Scientists had often doubted the capacity of tribal peoples to pattern such long-term, complex events.

## Wisdom and Revelation in Tribal Society

In tribal society, one is not considered wise by years lived, but by **degree of revelation**. Those who understand and embody advanced knowledge are the most intuitive and entitled to special veneration. This knowledge is almost invariably based on pattern understanding, and is independent of sex or even age. One is "aged" by degree of revelation, not time spent living.

## Phenomenological Time

Among the complex time-concepts of Australian Aborigines, only one — the least important — is the linear concept used by modern societies. Of far greater everyday use was **phenomenological (phenological) time**: time as given not by clocks, but by the life-phenomena of flowers, birds, and weather.

A Pitjantjatjara woman, upon seeing a small desert flower come into bloom, knew that dingoes in the ranges of hills far to the north were rearing pups, and that it was time for her group to leave for the hills to collect them. Thousands of such cross-species, cross-landscape temporal relationships are known to tribal peoples. Some signals may not occur in 100 or 500 years (like the flowering of a bamboo), but when they do, the appropriate actions, ceremonies, and linked phenomena are known.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
Field trials provide essential data for validating theoretical approaches and refining methodologies.

Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.
Peer-reviewed publications and practitioner reports contribute complementary perspectives.
