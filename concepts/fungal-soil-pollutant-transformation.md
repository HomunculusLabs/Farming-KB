# Fungal Soil Pollutant Transformation

## Overview

Soil is the primary receptor for persistent organic pollutants (POPs)
and represents the most common target for fungal bioremediation.
Fungi offer unique advantages for soil remediation through their
filamentous growth habit, which enables efficient colonization and
exploration of solid substrates, and their extracellular enzyme
systems that can attack pollutants without requiring cellular uptake.
However, the soil environment presents significant challenges including
variable physicochemical conditions, competition from indigenous
microorganisms, and limited pollutant bioavailability. Understanding
how these factors interact is essential for successful application of
fungal bioremediation in soil systems.

## Fungal Growth in Soil

Soil is not the natural habitat for many fungi used in bioremediation,
most of which are wood-decay organisms. White rot fungi such as
Phanerochaete chrysosporium, which naturally colonize dead wood, may
require soil conditions to be altered to encourage their growth. Soil
physicochemical factors including pH, moisture content, temperature,
and nutrient availability dramatically affect pollutant transformation
rates. Different fungi have different soil preferences: Lentinula
edodes was more effective at transforming pentachlorophenol at lower
soil moisture contents, while P. chrysosporium performed better at
higher moisture levels. Optimal transformation by both species occurred
at pH 4, reflecting the generally acidic preference of white rot fungi.

## Competition with Indigenous Microorganisms

Indigenous soil microorganisms can antagonize introduced fungi and
significantly reduce remediation effectiveness. Microbes antagonistic
to P. chrysosporium have been isolated from soil, and different fungi
show varying abilities to compete with native soil microflora.
Pleurotus species are generally better competitors than several other
white rot fungi, making them more suitable as soil inoculants. However,
in non-sterile soil, Dichomitus squalens achieved greater pyrene
degradation than Pleurotus, suggesting D. squalens better stimulated
overall soil microbial degradation capability despite being a poorer
direct competitor. Complex interactions between inoculant, soil type,
and microflora remain poorly understood.

## Soil Amendment Strategies

Various soil amendments improve fungal colonization and pollutant
transformation. Straw amendments increase hyphal length and generally
give the greatest initial pollutant breakdown rates. Corn cobs, wood
chips, sawdust, and composted lignocellulosic waste serve as
inexpensive growth substrates. Fungal inoculation combined with
amendment gives greater pollutant degradation than amendment alone.
Rosenbrock and colleagues demonstrated that fungal inoculation
produced greater dibenzo-p-dioxin breakdown than straw and compost
amendment without inoculation. Amendments also sorb pollutants,
decreasing toxic pollutant concentrations available to inhibit
fungal growth. Significant quantities are typically required,
such as straw-to-soil ratios of 1:4 or corn cob-to-soil ratios of 4:1.

## Inoculation and Formulation

Correct preparation of fungal inoculants is crucial. Fungi have been
grown on substrates including corn cobs and specially prepared pellets
that enable good soil colonization. Solid-state fermentation grows
mycelia on grain or composted lignocellulose, with the colonized
substrate becoming a matrix encapsulating the hyphae. Pelletized
inocula can be dried, stored, and applied directly. Initial inoculum
density is important, with higher densities producing faster pollutant
transformation. Encapsulation in calcium alginate protects inocula
during storage and from adverse conditions after application.

## Pollutant Bioavailability

Bioavailability is a major factor controlling pollutant
transformation in soil. POPs in aged contaminated soils may be
strongly sorbed to soil organic matter and therefore less accessible
to fungal enzymes than freshly added compounds. Surfactants have
shown positive effects on PAH transformation by fungi in both liquid
culture and soil. Non-ionic surfactants such as Tween 80 produced
two- to fivefold increases in degradation of anthracene, pyrene, and
benzo[a]pyrene by Bjerkandera species. Wetting and drying cycles can
also affect bioavailability of organic compounds sequestered in soil.
Aged contamination generally shows lower biodegradation rates than
freshly spiked soils.

## Manganese Addition

Manganese stimulates fungal biotransformation of atrazine by increasing
membrane permeability and stimulating manganese peroxidase activity.
Normal soil manganese ranges from 0.1 micromolar per litre in aerated
alkaline soils to 400 micromolar per litre in submerged soils, while
phenanthrene transformation by Pleurotus pulmonarius was stimulated by
up to 300 micromolar per litre. Since fungi grow best in aerated soils
where manganese is reduced, manganese addition may be beneficial.
Reducing soil pH also increases manganese availability and fungal
growth.

## Pollutant Transformation versus Mineralization

Complete mineralization of POPs to carbon dioxide is rarely achieved
in non-sterile soil by fungal inoculants. Typical degradation levels
are approximately 14% for PAHs and 9 to 29% for pentachlorophenol.
Instead, a variety of transformation products form, subject to
further transformation by soil microbes, extracellular enzymes,
chemical reactions, and sorption to soil surfaces. For PCP, usually
only one identifiable product (pentachloroanisole) is detected, but
large amounts of unidentified compounds also form. Bound residues
represent a significant fate for transformed pollutants.

## Soil Binding and Complexation

Covalent binding of POPs to humic material is catalyzed by fungal
oxidative enzymes including peroxidases and laccases. This
complexation reduces pollutant bioavailability and presumably
reduces toxicity. In studies of PCP transformation, Pleurotus
ostreatus bound 65% of PCP to soil organic matter, compared with 34
to 46% binding by Irpex lacteus, Bjerkandera adusta, and Trametes
versicolor. Similar bonding of PAHs to soil organic matter has been
observed with P. ostreatus, though to a lesser extent. This
complexation represents an important detoxification mechanism that
reduces environmental risk even without complete mineralization.

## Monitoring Fungal Activity in Soil

Several techniques monitor fungal growth and activity in soil. Image
analysis quantifies hyphal length and distribution. Extracellular
enzyme activity assays measure LiP, MnP, and laccase levels.
In situ fungal gene expression via mRNA extraction and quantitative
reverse transcriptase PCR allows assessment of active fungal
metabolism. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) procedures can detect
and quantify fungal biomass in soil, providing data on survival
of inoculated strains. These monitoring approaches are essential
for linking observed pollutant disappearance to specific fungal
activity.
