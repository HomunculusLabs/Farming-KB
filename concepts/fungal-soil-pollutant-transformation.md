## Fungal Soil Pollutant Transformation

## Overview

Soil is the primary receptor for persistent organic pollutants (POPs)
and represents the most common target for [[dighton-fungal-extracellular-enzyme-systems-decomposition]]
systems that can attack pollutants without requiring cellular uptake.
However, the soil environment presents significant challenges including
variable physicochemical conditions, competition from indigenous
microorganisms, and limited pollutant bioavailability. Understanding
how these factors interact is essential for successful application of
fungal bioremediation in soil systems.

## Fungal Growth in Soil

Soil is not the natural habitat for many fungi used in bioremediation,
most of which are wood-decay organisms. [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] dramatically affect pollutant transformation
rates. Different fungi have different soil preferences: Lentinula
edodes was more effective at transforming pentachlorophenol at lower
soil moisture contents, while P. chrysosporium performed better at
higher moisture levels. Optimal transformation by both species occurred
at pH 4, reflecting the generally acidic preference of white rot fungi.

## Competition with Indigenous Microorganisms

Indigenous soil microorganisms can antagonize introduced fungi and
significantly reduce remediation effectiveness. Microbes antagonistic
to P. chrysosporium have been isolated from soil, and different fungi
show varying abilities to compete with native soil microflora.
Pleurotus species are generally better competitors than several other
white rot fungi, making them more suitable as soil inoculants. However,
in non-sterile soil, Dichomitus squalens achieved greater pyrene
degradation than Pleurotus, suggesting D. squalens better stimulated
overall soil microbial degradation capability despite being a poorer
direct competitor. Complex interactions between inoculant, soil type,
and microflora remain poorly understood.

## Soil Amendment Strategies

Various soil amendments improve fungal colonization and pollutant
transformation. Straw amendments increase hyphal length and generally
give the greatest initial pollutant breakdown rates. Corn cobs, wood
chips, sawdust, and composted lignocellulosic waste serve as
inexpensive growth substrates. Fungal inoculation combined with
amendment gives greater pollutant degradation than amendment alone.
Rosenbrock and colleagues demonstrated that fungal inoculation
produced greater dibenzo-p-dioxin breakdown than straw and compost
amendment without inoculation. Amendments also sorb pollutants,
decreasing toxic pollutant concentrations available to inhibit
fungal growth. Significant quantities are typically required,
such as straw-to-soil ratios of 1:4 or corn cob-to-soil ratios of 4:1.

## Inoculation and Formulation

Correct preparation of fungal inoculants is crucial. Fungi have been
grown on substrates including corn cobs and specially prepared pellets
that enable good soil colonization. Solid-state fermentation grows
mycelia on grain or composted lignocellulose, with the colonized
substrate becoming a matrix encapsulating the hyphae. Pelletized
inocula can be dried, stored, and applied directly. Initial inoculum
density is important, with higher densities producing faster pollutant
transformation. Encapsulation in calcium alginate protects inocula
during storage and from adverse conditions after application.
