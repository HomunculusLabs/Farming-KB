# Permaculture Food Storage and Preservation

Permaculture food storage and preservation aims to extend harvest abundance
through the lean seasons using methods that minimize energy inputs and
maximize nutritional retention. The emphasis is on passive, low-tech, and
resilient approaches that work during power outages and in off-grid
situations.

## Root Cellaring

Root cellaring uses the natural insulating properties of the earth to
maintain cool, humid, stable conditions for food storage:

### Optimal Conditions

Temperature: 32-40 degrees F (0-4 degrees C) Humidity: 85-95% for root vegetables, 60-70% for squash and alliums Darkness: complete or near-complete Ventilation: gentle [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]] to [[query-how-do-i-prevent-mold-and-mildew-in-my-cannabis-grow]] and ethylene gas
  buildup

### Suitable Crops

Root vegetables: potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, rutabaga Alliums: onions, garlic, shallots (drier conditions) Winter squash: butternut, acorn, spaghetti squash Cabbage: stores well in cool humid conditions Apples and pears (store separately from root vegetables -- ethylene
  sensitive)

### Design Considerations

Earth-bermed or excavated structures with insulated doors Shelving allows air circulation around stored produce Separate chambers for different humidity requirements Monitor temperature with max-min thermometers Natural ventilation via intake and exhaust pipes

## Canning

### Water Bath Canning

Suitable for high-acid foods: pH below 4.6 Fruits, tomatoes, pickles, jams, jellies, relishes, chutneys Boiling water bath at 212 degrees F (100 degrees C) for specified time Acidification of tomatoes with lemon juice or citric acid is recommended

### Pressure Canning

- Required for low-acid foods: vegetables, meats, soups, beans
- Reaches 240 degrees F (116 degrees C) at 10-15 PSI to destroy
  Clostridium botulinum spores
- Dial gauge canners require annual calibration
- Weighted gauge canners are simpler to use

### Permaculture Considerations

Canning requires energy input (stove fuel) Best for preserving peak harvest abundance Glass jars are reusable for decades Store in cool, dark, dry conditions Inventory rotation: first in, first out

## Fermentation

Fermentation is perhaps the most permaculture-aligned preservation method
-- it requires no energy input beyond human labor and transforms food while
increasing nutritional value:

### Lacto-Fermentation

Uses lactic acid bacteria (LAB) naturally present on vegetables Salt concentration: 1.5-3% by weight of vegetables Examples: sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, fermented hot sauce Anaerobic environment (airlock or weighted fermentation crock) Fermentation time: 1-4 weeks depending on temperature and preference Store fermented products at cool temperatures to slow fermentation

### Other Fermentation Methods

**Kombucha** -- sweet tea fermented with SCOBY **Vinegar** -- fruit scraps fermented to vinegar via acetobacter **Miso, tempeh, soy sauce** -- soybean and grain fermentations **Kefir and yogurt** -- dairy fermentations (if livestock are part of
  the system)

### Advantages for Permaculture

No cooking energy required Preserves without refrigeration Increases bioavailability of nutrients Introduces probiotics to the diet Uses excess and imperfect produce

## Drying and Dehydrating

### Solar Drying

[[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] dehydrators use convection and sun exposure Screen trays with airflow on all sides Protect from insects with mesh screens Works well in hot, dry climates Design: angled collector with chimney effect for airflow

### Electric Dehydrating

Consistent results regardless of weather [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]] preserves nutrients better Most efficient method: 125-135 degrees F for vegetables,
  135-145 degrees F for fruits
- Energy cost is moderate but provides reliable preservation

### Suitable Foods for Drying

Herbs (retain best flavor dried at low temperatures) Fruits: apples, pears, berries, stone fruits, bananas Vegetables: tomatoes, peppers, kale, zucchini, green beans Jerky (if livestock are part of the system)

## Cold Storage and Freezing

### Cold Frames and Cool Storage

Unheated structures that maintain above-freezing temperatures Extended season harvest rather than long-term storage Hardy greens, carrots, leeks can be harvested in-place with row cover

### Freezing

Fastest method for preserving nutrients Requires ongoing energy input (vulnerable to power outages) Blanch vegetables before freezing to preserve quality Freeze fruits on trays before bagging to prevent clumping Vacuum sealing extends freezer storage life significantly

## Energy-Efficient Preservation Hierarchy

Prioritize methods by energy input and resilience:

1. **Root cellaring** -- zero ongoing energy, longest storage for
   appropriate crops
2. **Fermentation** -- zero ongoing energy, nutritional enhancement
3. **Drying** -- minimal energy (solar) or one-time energy input
4. **Canning** -- significant energy at time of processing, zero ongoing
5. **Freezing** -- continuous energy requirement, least resilient

## Seed Saving as Preservation

Seed saving is a form of food preservation that extends beyond a single
season. By saving seeds from the best-performing plants in a
[[permaculture]] system, growers preserve [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] adapted to
local conditions and reduce dependence on external seed sources.

### Principles of Seed Saving

Select seeds from the healthiest, most productive plants each season Allow some plants to go to full seed maturity before harvesting Store seeds in cool, dark, dry conditions with moisture below 8% Label varieties with date, source, and any notable characteristics Maintain minimum population sizes to preserve genetic diversity

### Seed Storage Methods

Paper envelopes in airtight glass jars with desiccant packets Freezing seeds for long-term storage (must be thoroughly dry first) Silica gel or powdered milk as moisture absorbers in storage containers Fermentation of wet-seeded crops (tomatoes, cucumbers) to remove the
  gelatinous coating before drying seeds

### Integration with the Preservation Cycle

Seed saving connects directly to other [[egg-preservation-methods]]. Fermentation
of tomato seeds produces both viable seeds and tomato water that can be
used in cooking. Drying peppers provides both culinary spice and seed for
next year's crop. The [[water-wise-vegetable-gardening]] harvest that
feeds the household also feeds the seed bank.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Food preservation systems should be designed as integral components of
the overall [[permaculture]] site plan, not as afterthoughts. Their
placement and design follow the same zone principles as other elements.

### Zone Placement of Preservation Infrastructure

Root cellars and cool storage rooms belong in or adjacent to zone one,
close to the kitchen where preserved foods are accessed daily. Canning
and fermentation stations should be in zone one or the zone one/zone two
