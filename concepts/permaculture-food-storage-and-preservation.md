# Permaculture Food Storage and Preservation

Permaculture food storage and preservation aims to extend harvest abundance through the lean seasons using methods that minimize energy inputs and maximize nutritional retention. The emphasis is on passive, low-tech, and resilient approaches that work during power outages and in off-grid situations.

## Root Cellaring

Root cellaring uses the natural insulating properties of the earth to maintain cool, humid, stable conditions for food storage:

### Optimal Conditions
- Temperature: 32-40°F (0-4°C)
- Humidity: 85-95% for root vegetables, 60-70% for squash and alliums
- Darkness: complete or near-complete
- Ventilation: gentle air circulation to prevent mold and ethylene gas buildup

### Suitable Crops
- Root vegetables: potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, rutabaga
- Alliums: onions, garlic, shallots (drier conditions)
- Winter squash: butternut, acorn, spaghetti squash
- Cabbage: stores well in cool humid conditions
- Apples and pears (store separately from root vegetables — ethylene sensitive)

### Design Considerations
- Earth-bermed or excavated structures with insulated doors
- Shelving allows air circulation around stored produce
- Separate chambers for different humidity requirements
- Monitor temperature with max-min thermometers
- Natural ventilation via intake and exhaust pipes

## Canning

### Water Bath Canning
- Suitable for high-acid foods: pH below 4.6
- Fruits, tomatoes, pickles, jams, jellies, relishes, chutneys
- Boiling water bath at 212°F (100°C) for specified time
- Acidification of tomatoes with lemon juice or citric acid is recommended

### Pressure Canning
- Required for low-acid foods: vegetables, meats, soups, beans
- Reaches 240°F (116°C) at 10-15 PSI to destroy Clostridium botulinum spores
- Dial gauge canners require annual calibration
- Weighted gauge canners are simpler to use

### Permaculture Considerations
- Canning requires energy input (stove fuel)
- Best for preserving peak harvest abundance
- Glass jars are reusable for decades
- Store in cool, dark, dry conditions
- Inventory rotation: first in, first out

## Fermentation

Fermentation is perhaps the most permaculture-aligned preservation method — it requires no energy input beyond human labor and transforms food while increasing nutritional value:

### Lacto-Fermentation
- Uses lactic acid bacteria (LAB) naturally present on vegetables
- Salt concentration: 1.5-3% by weight of vegetables
- Examples: sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, fermented hot sauce
- Anaerobic environment (airlock or weighted fermentation crock)
- Fermentation time: 1-4 weeks depending on temperature and preference
- Store fermented products at cool temperatures to slow fermentation

### Other Fermentation Methods
- **Kombucha** — sweet tea fermented with SCOBY
- **Vinegar** — fruit scraps fermented to vinegar via acetobacter
- **Miso, tempeh, soy sauce** — soybean and grain fermentations
- **Kefir and yogurt** — dairy fermentations (if livestock are part of the system)

### Advantages for Permaculture
- No cooking energy required
- Preserves without refrigeration
- Increases bioavailability of nutrients
- Introduces probiotics to the diet
- Uses excess and imperfect produce

## Drying and Dehydrating

### Solar Drying
- Passive solar dehydrators use convection and sun exposure
- Screen trays with airflow on all sides
- Protect from insects with mesh screens
- Works well in hot, dry climates
- Design: angled collector with chimney effect for airflow

### Electric Dehydrating
- Consistent results regardless of weather
- Temperature control preserves nutrients better
- Most efficient method: 125-135°F for vegetables, 135-145°F for fruits
- Energy cost is moderate but provides reliable preservation

### Suitable Foods for Drying
- Herbs (retain best flavor dried at low temperatures)
- Fruits: apples, pears, berries, stone fruits, bananas
- Vegetables: tomatoes, peppers, kale, zucchini, green beans
- Jerky (if livestock are part of the system)

## Cold Storage and Freezing

### Cold Frames and Cool Storage
- Unheated structures that maintain above-freezing temperatures
- Extended season harvest rather than long-term storage
- Hardy greens, carrots, leeks can be harvested in-place with row cover

### Freezing
- Fastest method for preserving nutrients
- Requires ongoing energy input (vulnerable to power outages)
- Blanch vegetables before freezing to preserve quality
- Freeze fruits on trays before bagging to prevent clumping
- Vacuum sealing extends freezer storage life significantly

## Energy-Efficient Preservation Hierarchy

Prioritize methods by energy input and resilience:

1. **Root cellaring** — zero ongoing energy, longest storage for appropriate crops
2. **Fermentation** — zero ongoing energy, nutritional enhancement
3. **Drying** — minimal energy (solar) or one-time energy input
4. **Canning** — significant energy at time of processing, zero ongoing
5. **Freezing** — continuous energy requirement, least resilient
