The taxonomy of psilocybin-containing mushrooms has undergone
significant revision since the discovery of psilocybin and
psilocin as the primary active compounds in the 1950s. The
classification of these fungi continues to evolve as molecular
techniques and phylogenetic analysis reshape our understanding of
their evolutionary relationships. Approximately 95 species are
currently recognized as psilocybin active, with more being
discovered each year as field research expands globally.

## The Genus Psilocybe

The genus Psilocybe is the most prominent group of
psilocybin-containing mushrooms. These fungi are closely related
to Hypholoma and Stropharia, and the three genera are difficult
to separate when relying solely on macroscopic features. Together
they constitute the family Strophariaceae. Psilocybe mushrooms
are saprophytes found in a wide range of habitats including
dungs, mosses, soils, grasslands, and decaying wood debris.

Guzman's monographs on Psilocybe (1983, 1995) remain the most
thorough taxonomic treatments to date, recognizing 173 species.
The number of valid taxa now approaches 180 species. Taxonomic
concepts remain in flux as mycologists debate the boundaries
between genera. Many mycologists advocate for an expanded
Psilocybe sensu lato that would encompass species currently
placed in Stropharia and Hypholoma, reflecting a more natural
phylogenetic classification.
