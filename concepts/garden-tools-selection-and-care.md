# Garden Tools Selection and Care

Beyond the four essential tools ([[combination-shovel]], bow rake, hoe, and file), several additional items significantly improve garden efficiency. Selecting quality tools and maintaining them properly extends their useful life for decades. In an era when most manufactured goods are designed to be disposable, proper tool care is a vanishing skill that every serious gardener should practice.

## The Wheelbarrow

### Why a Wheelbarrow Beats a Garden Cart

Garden magazine advertisements often promote expensive two-wheeled garden carts over "old-fashioned" wheelbarrows. However, for most vegetable gardeners:

- Unless practicing no-dig gardening, there is no need to haul dozens of pickup-truck loads of organic matter
- A large wheelbarrow holds enough compost or aged manure to restore a 100 sq ft (10 sq m) bed — adequate capacity
- The wheelbarrow maneuvers neatly down narrow paths between beds that a two-wheeler cannot manage
- A wheelbarrow costs considerably less

### Selecting a Quality Wheelbarrow

- **Handles**: Strong wooden handles (no plastic)
- **Tire**: Broad pneumatic tire with inner tube, on a steel rim, turning on ball bearings
- **Size**: The largest barrow of the best quality you can barely afford
- **Source**: Farm supplier or commercial hardware — not a national discount chain
- **Fit is essential**: When you lift the handles and hold the barrel so it slightly tips forward, your arms should be only slightly bent at the elbows and your back nearly straight. There should be room to stand between the handles and walk forward without your knees hitting the barrel.

**Avoid**: Builders' concrete wheelbarrows — they have deep, rounded channels, thick-walled tubular metal handles, and are super-heavy. The style you want is capacious but lightweight, since you will be carrying bulky, light materials like compost, not dense materials like concrete.

### Longevity

A properly fitted, quality wheelbarrow should last 20 years or more if handles are maintained.

## The Sprayer

### When You Need One

There are times when foliar spraying is necessary:

- Liquid fertilizer on leaves (fertigation)
- [[compost-tea-for-specific-crops]] [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]]
- [[bacillus-thuringiensis]] (Bt) for corn earworms or cabbageworms
- Home remedies like soapy water or rhubarb leaf juice [[insectary-plants-for-pest-control]]

### Selecting a Quality Sprayer

- **Capacity**: 5-6 quarts (5-6 liters)
- **Type**: Pump-up sprayer carried by the pump handle
- **Nozzle quality**: The best sprayers use interchangeable, inexpensive nozzle inserts — small, usually brass, allowing different volumes and spray patterns
- **Avoid**: Built-in adjustable cone nozzles made of plastic (usually indicates cheap construction)
- **Parts availability**: Ask if the seller stocks spare parts — pump pads and O-rings
- **Pump construction**: Metal is better than plastic, which becomes brittle after a few years

### Sprayer Longevity

Protected from the sun, a plastic sprayer might last a decade. Left in the sun, it survives two or three years at most. Store sprayers indoors or under cover.

### Sprayer Maintenance

- **Strain everything** that goes into the sprayer — no grit near the pump seals
- **Clean immediately** after each use — wipe off the pump before unscrewing to fill the tank
- **Use a surfactant**: A quarter teaspoon (1.5 ml) of mild liquid dishwashing soap per quart (liter) of spray acts as a spreader-sticker, helping the spray coat leaves evenly. This quantity has never been observed to burn or damage leaves. Do not use strongly antiseptic detergents.

## The Garden Knife and Sharpening Stone

### The Setup

Keep two identical thin-bladed, sharp, pointed kitchen paring knives stuck into the top of a short wooden stake in the garden. Next to the stake, place an inexpensive large, double-sided (medium/coarse) sharpening stone.

The knives should have bright red (or other highly visible) handles — because you will always be trying to remember where you last put one down. The stone stays in the garden permanently.

### Why a Garden Knife

- Knife steel is not made for running through earth — it dulls quickly when thinning seedlings or weeding close to emerging rows
- A thin, pointed knife slips between crowded seedlings, allowing precise cutting of excess plants
- Close weeding work that would take three times as long with fingers can be done quickly with a knife
- The knife snicks off weeds at ground level, below the crown

### Maintenance

- Hone the knife on the stone after every five or ten minutes of use
- In an emergency, the stone can also hone a dull hoe

## Buckets

Buckets do more than carry things — they are essential for **fertigation** (fertilizer applied through irrigation water). A few cheap plastic 10-quart (10-liter) laundry buckets, or better yet, some recycled 5-gallon (20-liter) institutional kitchen buckets, are indispensable around the garden. It seems one can never have too many.

## Tool Handles: Wood vs. Composite

Composite (fiberglass) handles are lighter in weight and stronger than wood, and they resist rot. However, they are sensitive to ultraviolet light degradation. Keeping a composite handle out of the sun when not in use helps it outlast the blade.

Wooden handles remain the traditional choice and, with proper maintenance, provide decades of service.

## Tool Care: Preventing Rust and Rot

Two things go wrong with every shovel, rake, and hoe:

1. **The metal bits rust**
2. **The wooden bits dry out, crack, and split**

### The Traditional Method: Oiled Sand

In the old days, garden sheds typically had a wooden box on the floor filled with coarse sand saturated with used motor oil. When garden tools were put away for the day, their working ends were wiped clean and then thrust into the sand so they came away lightly coated with oil. This prevented rust on the metal and the oil soaked into the wooden parts, preventing cracking.

### The Modern Method: Coconut Oil

A simpler modern approach:

1. Keep a half-gallon (2-liter) jar half full of coconut oil
2. When everything is being put away for the day, take the jar into the house to warm the oil so it becomes liquid
3. Rub the oil onto wooden tool handles, making sure to coat the junction where blades attach to handles
4. Hang the tools up

Coconut oil is food-safe, pleasant to handle, and provides excellent protection against both rust and wood degradation.

### Key Principles

- **Clean before storing**: Remove soil from metal parts to prevent moisture-trapping dirt from accelerating rust
- **Oil the metal**: Even a light oil film prevents rust formation
- **Oil the wood**: Prevents drying, cracking, and splitting at the handle-socket junction
- **Store properly**: Hang tools or stand them blade-up — never leave them lying on the ground where moisture collects
