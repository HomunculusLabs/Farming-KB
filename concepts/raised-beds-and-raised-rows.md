# Raised Beds and Raised Rows

Raised beds and raised rows are fundamental garden layout techniques that reduce labor, improve plant growth, and solve several common gardening problems simultaneously. By making a single choice about bed layout, gardeners can avoid much unnecessary work — from weeding to seed starting to watering.

## The Core Advantages of Raising Growing Areas

Raising the growing areas a few inches provides several interconnected benefits:

1. **Easier seedbed creation**: Small seeds need a fine seedbed to germinate. On flat ground, raking produces a narrow valley of fine soil edged by ridges of clods that smother seedlings. On raised beds, clods can be raked down the sides into the paths, creating a large, even, fine surface with little effort.

2. **Better weed control**: Weeds along the edges of raised beds can be hoed efficiently. With flat beds bordered by retaining walls or frames, weeds grow in the crevices where the hoe cannot reach, requiring painful hand-pulling.

3. **Improved soil structure**: Raised beds stay looser because people naturally keep their feet off them. Walking on loosened soil compacts it, making root development difficult for vegetables.

4. **Reminders for foot traffic**: The physical elevation of beds reminds gardeners to stay on the paths and off the growing areas.

## Bed Width: The Ergonomic Rule

Raised beds should not be wider than a person can comfortably reach to the center while bending over or squatting from the adjacent path:

- **Standard width**: 4 feet (120 cm) for most adults
- **Minimum width**: 3 feet (90 cm) for less flexible people or those with shorter arms
- **Maximum width**: 4.5 feet (140 cm) for very flexible people with long arms

Narrower beds are less space-efficient because more area is consumed by paths. Below 4 feet, it becomes impossible to have two parallel rows of large plants running lengthwise.

Path width between beds is about 1 foot (30 cm).

## Raised Wide Rows for Dry Gardening

Raised wide rows are superior to wide beds when gardening without irrigation or with minimal watering. These are essentially narrow raised beds:

- **Width**: About two shovel-blades wide, or 20-24 inches (50-60 cm)
- **Spacing**: 1-foot paths between rows, making row centers about 36 inches (90 cm) apart
- **Dry climate spacing**: Increase to 4-5 feet (120-150 cm) between row centers where summer rainfall is minimal

## Bed Height: Less Is More

Unless the gardener is too inflexible to squat or bend over, there is no point in raising beds or rows more than 2-3 inches (5-8 cm). This modest elevation is easily accomplished and provides all the benefits of raised growing areas without the expense and labor of constructing tall frames.

## How to Make Raised Beds

### New Garden Construction

Immediately after digging a new garden area with a combination shovel:

1. Walk along what will become the paths
2. Scoop up about 2 inches (5 cm) of soil from the paths
3. Toss it onto what will become the growing bed
4. Level with a bow rake
5. String lines or peg corners if precise layout is desired

### Restoring an Existing Bed for Replanting

After a raised bed has grown a crop, the restoration procedure is straightforward:

1. **Remove vegetation**: Clear all crop remnants to the compost area
2. **Fertilize**: For medium- or high-demand crops, spread 4-6 quarts of Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) per 100 sq ft (10 sq m), or cover with about 1/8 inch (3 mm) of strong chicken manure. For low-demand crops, apply manure or compost at 1/8 inch depth.
3. **Scrape the paths**: Use a shovel to scoop about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of soil from paths onto the bed. If paths are weedy, the shovel cuts weeds as it lifts soil. Otherwise, hoe first, then shovel.
4. **Turn the bed**: Roughly turn the bed over to the shovel blade's depth. This mixing need not be thorough. It is acceptable to stand on the undug portion while turning.
5. **Return spilled soil**: Toss any soil that ended up back in the paths onto the bed again.
6. **Level**: Roughly level the bed with a rake.
7. **Prepare seedbed**: If sowing small seeds, cover with 1/4 inch (6 mm) of fine, well-decomposed manure or fine compost. Sieve compost if necessary for direct-seeded crops. Rake level and pull clods to the edges.

The entire restoration process — digging, scraping paths, sieving compost, spreading, and leveling — takes about one pleasant hour for a 100 sq ft bed.

## Which Crops Go Where

### Raised Beds
Best for small-sized vegetables: lettuce, bush beans, bush peas, beets, carrots, radishes, salad turnips. Also suitable for larger plants like Swiss chard, most cucumber varieties, cabbage, kale, and Brussels sprouts.

### Raised Wide Rows
Better for dry gardening. Excellent for most vegetables when irrigation is limited.

### Hills
Best for large, vigorously sprouting plants: squash, melons, some kinds of tomatoes, sweet corn. Sweet corn needs to grow in a large cluster for proper pollination and also does better "on the flat" in hills.

### On the Flat
Some crops genuinely do better without raising: bush peas, bush beans, and sweet corn all perform well planted on the flat or in hills rather than in raised beds.

## The Double-Digging Debate

Many intensive gardening advocates recommend double-dug raised beds 24 inches (60 cm) deep. This is generally unnecessary:

- Digging 12 inches (30 cm) deep is sufficient for most purposes
- Loosening the second foot produces little additional benefit for the substantial effort required
- Over time, the second foot becomes looser naturally as worms transport organic matter downward and plant nutrients leach into the subsoil (a process called "flocculation" in clay soils)
- The gardener's limited time and energy are better spent on other tasks

## Measuring Amendments for Beds

A standard raised bed of 100 sq ft (10 sq m) is typically 4 x 25 feet (120 x 760 cm). A raised wide row (two shovel blades wide) is about 100 sq ft per 50 lineal feet (15 meters).

Application rates:
- 1/8 inch (3 mm) deep: 1 cubic foot per 100 sq ft (20 liters per 10 sq m)
- 1/4 inch (6 mm) deep: 2 cubic feet per 100 sq ft (44 liters per 10 sq m)
- 1/2 inch (12 mm) deep: 4 cubic feet per 100 sq ft (88 liters per 10 sq m)
- 1 inch (25 mm) deep: 8 cubic feet per 100 sq ft (176 liters per 10 sq m)
