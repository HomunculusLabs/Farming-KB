# Humid Tropics — Introduction and Climatic Types

## Overview

The humid tropics encompass a range of climatic zones characterised by high temperatures, abundant rainfall, and rapid biological productivity. These regions present unique challenges and opportunities for sustainable land management. Permaculture design in the humid tropics must account for rapid nutrient cycling, soil leaching, intense biological competition, and the need for complex multi-storied plant systems that mirror natural forest structure.

## Climatic Zones of the Humid Tropics

### Wet Tropics

The wet tropics occupy river basins and wet coasts between latitudes 0–25°, covering approximately 10% of the Earth's surface and 5% of the human population. Major localities include the Amazon and Congo basins, Central America, Sri Lanka, Malaya, Borneo coasts, and New Guinea.

**Climate characteristics:**
- Temperature fluctuates little, ranging 21–32°C (70–90°F)
- Humidity is constantly high; frost is unknown
- Rainfall ranges from 152–328 cm (60–129 inches), with rain on most days
- Frequent thunderstorms (75–150 per year), typically towards evening or late afternoon

**Landscape features:**
- Perennial streams and deeply weathered rock (regolith) over bedrock
- Rounded hills with rapid water runoff and evaporation
- Swamps confined to coasts and lowlands
- Extensive alluvial plains and deltaic deposits along rivers
- Inland access often via rivers and tributaries

**Vegetation:**
- Luxurious broadleaf rainforest with lianas and epiphytes
- Up to 800 tree species per square kilometre
- Shaded forest floor with subdued light and little ground-level growth
- Extensive mangroves on coastal and estuarine sites
- Growth is rapid, uninterrupted, and continuous
- Insects, birds, and aquatic species are plentiful and varied
- Most fauna is nocturnal and arboreal; ground grazers are rare

**Critical soil characteristic:** About 85% of nutrients are held in plants and animals, not in the soil itself. When forest is cleared, soils become infertile, erode rapidly, and leach to insoluble oxides of iron and aluminium. Only terraces, floodplains, and areas near recent volcanic activity maintain soil fertility under cultivation.

**Staple foods:** Plantain, banana, cassava, yams, coconut, corn, taro, paddy rice, ducks, pigs, poultry, and fish.

**Trade crops:** Spices, copra, palm oil, cacao, rubber, banana, manilla hemp, rare hardwoods, balsa, tropical nuts, chicle, and drug plants.

### Wet-Dry Tropics

The wet-dry tropics adjoin the wet tropics but lie poleward, occupying about 15% of Earth's surface between latitudes 0–25°, biased toward the southern hemisphere. Key areas include the Campos, Llanos, Gran Chaco of South America, parts of Central America, areas encircling the Congo basin, and many central Pacific islands including Hawaii.

**Climate characteristics:**
- Dry season (winter, low-sun): Clear skies, intense sunlight, temperatures to 38°C (100°F) or more, low humidity, strong desiccating winds
- Wet season (summer, high-sun): Similar to wet tropics but with more episodic flooding
- No frosts; temperatures range 21–27°C wet season, 32–38°C dry season
- Rainfall: 25–152 cm (10–60 inches), decreasing towards desert margins
- Windward slopes may receive up to 1016 cm (400 inches); rain shadows on leeslopes

**Landscape features:**
- Intermittent streams, wadis, flood plains
- Karst (limestone) areas with sinkholes and cenotes
- Rounded hills susceptible to rapid gully erosion on slopes
- Extensive inland swamps and rift lakes

**Vegetation:**
- Vast savannah grasslands with thorn-bush and flat-topped Acacia trees
- Steppe grassland on plateaus with baobabs and dry-deciduous trees
- Grasses reach 1–6 m in wet season, often burnt off
- Gallery forests in valleys; tree islands in savannah
- Enormous herds of zebra, gnu, antelope and their predators
- Termites, ostrich, rhea, locust, and abundant reptiles

**Soils:** Generally more fertile and alkaline than wet tropics, especially towards desert margins. Still at risk from erratic rain, erosion, leaching, and wind. Overgrazing and fire can extend desert into these zones.

### Monsoon Tropics

A sub-type of the wet-dry tropics influenced by continental land masses and oceanic onshore winds, confined to the Indo-Thailand region, northern Australia, East Java, Timor, southern New Guinea, and extending to latitude 35°N in India.

**Climate characteristics:**
- Late summer: Continental heating causes onshore sea winds and heavy rains
- Winter (dry): Reversed winds from cool interiors to coasts, cooler period (13–21°C)
- Spring: Rising temperatures with hot dry winds exceeding 38°C
- About 60% of rain falls in summer; rainfall varies 102–1016 cm (40–400 inches)
- Floods and droughts equally unpredictable but common

**Vegetation:** Once clothed in tropical forests on hillslopes and river plains, with grasslands extending towards deserts. Population pressure, deforestation, and marginal agriculture have devastated this ecology in India. Dry-deciduous broadleaves are common; teak and bamboo were once extensive.

## Design Principles for Humid Tropical Systems

### Key Design Essentials for Wet Tropics
- Hygienic faeces disposal
- Clean water sources
- Integrated and benign insect control
- Gradual replacement of ground crops by trees
- Preservation of natural tree stands
- River-based transport development
- No-dig (mulch) techniques on root crops
- Domestic foragers for snail and insect pests
- Appropriate medicinal plants

### Key Design Essentials for Wet-Dry Tropics
- Small domestic water storage and reticulation
- Hedgerows against winds
- In-crop tree legumes such as *Acacia albida*
- Improved stock varieties and management
- Natural herding systems using local herd species
- Mulch use of grasses
- Tree crops of high forage value
- Decreased fire frequency
- Keyline techniques for flood control
- Soakage pits and swales across slopes
- Domestic fuelwood systems near villages

### Emulating Natural Forest Structure

The most effective strategy in humid tropics is to emulate the tropical forest itself: establish a dominant series of legumes, palms, and useful trees with a complex understory and ground layer of herbaceous and leguminous food and fodder plants. Vines and epiphytes further complex the system as it evolves.

In wet-dry tropics, more open palm polycultures are appropriate, where the excesses of heat, light, and rain are moderated by an open canopy of palm fronds and fern-like leaves of tree legumes.

## Challenges of Tropical Living

Humid tropical environments pose significant health challenges including skin cancers, dengue fever, filaria, malaria, chronic bowel and skin diseases, and constant battles with rampant growth. Water-borne and mosquito-transmitted diseases are nearly impossible to fully control given aerial water reservoirs in palms and bromeliads. Housing design must prioritise cross-ventilation and mosquito control.

## Lessons from Traditional Systems

James Fox's analysis of Indonesian palm polycultures (1977) demonstrates that ancient tropical civilisations achieved remarkable stability through sustainable land use patterns. The FAO has acknowledged failure in transferring mechanised monocultural systems — barely sustainable even in temperate moist areas — to fragile tropical soils. Complex perennial fodder and food systems are known to be stable but remain outside mainstream agricultural funding. What is needed is continuous local education of experienced people and lateral transfer of their evolved skills.
