# Island Coastal Defense and Shoreline Management

## Overview

The interface between ocean and land is the most dynamic and vulnerable zone on any island. Waves never cease their assault, and the loss of even a small strip of coastal vegetation can trigger rapid and irreversible erosion. [[bill-mollison]]'s approach to island coastal defense is built on understanding wind-wave interactions, selecting appropriate plant species for successive defense lines, and managing human activities (particularly airstrips and development) to avoid undermining natural protection systems.

## The Physics of Coastal Erosion

### Wind and Wave Interaction
The process of island erosion follows a clear physical pattern:

- Winds sweep on shore with waves, driving the erosion process
- When vegetation deflects wind upward, a **low pressure zone** forms on the lee side
- This causes waves to break further out and run less aggressively up the beach
- As the number of trees increases, the wind is increasingly deflected and the trees begin to **win out against the sea**
- A whole succession of plants can establish along the shoreline, progressively stopping wave erosion

### The Danger of Removal
- Removing even a strip of deflecting vegetation creates a **much broader wave** running inland
- The broader wave carries **much greater force** because it travels in a streamlined wind corridor
- Mollison has observed from the air that waves are actively wiping out islands in bays
- The process proceeds very rapidly once vegetation is lost

### Vegetation Building Capacity
- The highest that vegetation alone can build an island is approximately **6 feet**
- This makes shoreline vegetation critically important — don't tamper with it without having alternative defenses ready
- Re-establishing a coastline after erosion has started is very difficult

## Wind Patterns on Oceanic Islands

### Two-Set Wind System
All oceanic islands, whether low or high, share a characteristic wind pattern:

- **Spring-autumn**: Periods of calm
- **Winter-summer**: Active wind periods
- Wind directions are typically either northeast-southwest or northwest-southeast
- These are more consistent than winds in many land situations

### Design Implications
- Windbreak problems and erosion problems go hand-in-hand on islands
- Windbreak design must account for both seasonal wind directions
- The two calm periods provide windows for construction and planting

## Coastal Defense Plant Succession

The coastal defense system is built in **successive lines**, each with different species adapted to increasing distance from the ocean's edge:

### First Line: Ocean-Front Species
These plants stand directly where waves break, growing in sea spray:

- **Pemphis** — hardy coastal shrub
- **Tournefortia** — salt-tolerant coastal plant
- **Scaevola** — beach-front pioneer
- These may grow to approximately **15 feet high**
- They are principally useful as **windbreak**, not for yield
- They take the full force of oceanic assault

### Second Line: Tall Canopy Species
Behind the front-line species, taller plants provide the main windbreak structure:

- **Palms** — including coconut and surprisingly, date palms
- Date palms are typically thought of as oasis palms, but the basic conditions on islands are very similar to desert oases
- The accidental establishment of date palms on the Hawaiian coast occurred when a Catholic priest discarded date pits that sprouted and thrived

### Third Line: Productive Species
Within approximately **50 feet** of the front line, normal yields begin:

- Nut trees, fruit trees, and vegetable crops become viable
- The windbreak effect of the first two lines creates a protected microclimate

### Needle-Leaf Front Line
A separate group of needle-leaf plants serves as additional front-line defense:

- **[[casuarina]]** — excellent coastal windbreak
- **[[tamarix]]** — salt-tolerant and drought-resistant
- **Monterey pine** — wide climatic range
- **[[norfolk-island-pine]]** — iconic island tree
- **Monterey cypress** — excellent coastal specimen

These trees are valued more for **mulch potential** than yield, as they stand front on the ocean and receive sea winds first.

### Broad-Leaf Coastal Species
A smaller group of thick-leafed plants:

- **Sea grape** — tolerates salt spray
- **Coprosma repens** (New Zealand mirrorbush) — good in cold climates
- **Bayberries** — stand well towards the forefront
- **Beach plum (Prunus maritima)** — grows right on the beach
- **Prunus catalonica** — coastal stone fruit
- **Rosa rugosa** — salt-tolerant rose

### Species Sensitivity to Salt
Some species cannot tolerate any salt on their leaves and must be positioned carefully:

- **Chestnut** — very salt-sensitive
- **Walnut** — not fond of salt
- **Almond, peach, apricot, apple** — require some shelter from direct salt spray
- **Pistachio** — wants no salt at all
- **Most vegetables** — surprisingly, none suffer badly from salt; many grow naturally near the sea

### Important Caveat
Many plants grow on the coastline of **sheltered water** but will not survive the front-line oceanic assault. Trees brought in for coastal defense must be proven on **oceanic beaches**, not just sheltered shorelines.

## Airstrip Design on Islands

### The Critical Mistake
Removing trees to create an airstrip allows the sea to march inland across the cleared area, cutting the airstrip to pieces. Mollison calls this **disaster** — it will be very hard to put the island back again.

### Correct Approach
- **Contain the airstrip within vegetation** — do not clear broad corridors to the sea
- **Orient at an angle to prevailing winds** — reduces direct wave run-up
- **Maintain trees alongside** the airstrip for wind deflection
- **Preserve coastline vegetation** at both ends of the airstrip
- When aircraft descend below tree level, they can land — trees at the ends don't obstruct approaches

## Harbor Creation (Controlled)
It is possible to **deliberately** remove coastal vegetation to create a harbor effect, but this requires:

- **Previously-built defenses** around the area you intend to flood
- Understanding that you are trading land for water access
- This should only be done with proper sea walls and breakwaters in place
- The original vegetation removal must be accompanied by hard engineering defenses

## Mangroves as Coastal Builders

### Mangrove Genera
- **Sonnerata, Rhizopodia, Aegiceras** — subtropical and tropical
- **Nipa** — palm mangrove
- **Avicenna** — extends to latitude 40

### How Mangroves Build Land
- All mangrove species have twisty stems and leathery leaves
- They stand in quiet tidal waters (atolls) or estuarine zones
- They lay down **very thick leaf mulch** among their roots
- This mulch creates loose mud that builds land outward
- The process extends the island into the lagoon or estuary

### Island Extension Strategy
Two methods for using mangroves to extend islands:

1. **Raised barriers**: Place mangroves along barriers made from logs and sand, forming fish-trap enclosures that increase fish populations while building land
2. **Natural colonization**: Allow mangroves to establish in quiet lagoon waters where they will gradually extend the island perimeter

### The Mangrove Food Web
- Leaf mulch passes through **shrimp, anthropods, and diatoms**
- The whole food chain originates within mangrove systems
- **Mangrove jacks** and specific fish species are associated with mangrove stands
- The system supports **most offshore shrimp fisheries**
- **Aegiceras** is a superb honey plant
- Some mangroves provide **edible fruits, durable woods, and stem products**

### Mangrove Destruction
Mangroves are being degraded, filled in, drained, and chopped worldwide. When mangroves are removed:

- People get washed away by waves and storm surge
- Fish populations decline as nursery habitat is lost
- Shrimp fisheries collapse
- Shoreline erodes rapidly without the root stabilization

### Crocodile Habitat
Mangroves are habitat for crocodiles and alligators. Mollison vividly describes canoeing through mangroves at two miles per hour with a 40-foot crocodile capable of 30 miles per hour behind — illustrating the richness and wildness of these ecosystems.

## Cross-Habitat Species Exchange

Mollison suggests that designers should experiment more boldly with moving species between similar habitat types:

- **Coastal species in deserts** — many coastal plants tolerate salt and drought simultaneously
- **Desert species on coasts** — the basic conditions of desert oases and coral atolls are similar
- **Mangroves inland in deserts** — an unexplored possibility
- Date palms as a case study — originally an oasis/desert plant that accidentally established on Hawaiian coasts and thrived

## Nutrient Management on Islands

### Nitrogen Turnover
Islands often start from a sterile environment:

- **Introduce nitrogen-fixing species** — many island legumes exist as vines, ground covers, small trees, and tall trees
- **Bring soil with each plant** — plants have essential soil associates (mycorrhizae, bacteria)
- **Import compost starters** — introduce bacterial, fungal, and algal forms
- **Bring worms** — they may be entirely absent from coral islands

### The Moringa Tree
The horse-radish tree (Moringa) is recommended for island gardens:

- Edible **leaves, flowers, and pods**
- Functions as a **vegetable hedge**
- Grows to 20-30 feet tall
- Provides continuous harvestable green vegetable material

## Water Balance Management

### The Freshwater Threat
- Excessive water use draws down the freshwater lens, allowing **saltwater intrusion**
- This is first signified by the **death of trees** from salt effects
- Pollutants from septic systems create a **reverse flow** toward the island center
- Beaches become unusable when too many people occupy an island — pollutants appear in the sparkling green waters

### Design Responses
- **Minimal water use** — recommend conservative consumption
- **Maximum surface storage** — maintain potential surface storage at all times
- **Peripheral settlement** — houses on the periphery, water drawn from the center
- **Dry toilets** — prevent contamination of the freshwater lens
- **Careful septic placement** — only at the island perimeter if absolutely necessary

## Key Design Principles

1. **Never clear coastline vegetation** without engineered alternatives in place
2. **Build defense in successive lines** — ocean-front species, then tall canopy, then productive species
3. **Match species to salt tolerance** — position sensitive species well behind the front line
4. **Orient airstrips at angles to wind** and contain them within vegetation
5. **Encourage mangroves** — they build land, support fisheries, and protect shorelines
6. **Import soil biology** — islands may be sterile; bring worms, compost, and soil associates
7. **Manage water conservatively** — the freshwater lens is finite and easily contaminated
8. **Settle on the periphery** — protect central freshwater from waste
9. **Use mulch-producing species** as front-line defense — they protect while [[building-soil]]
10. **Think in terms of habitat similarity** — desert and coastal island environments share more in common than is generally recognized
