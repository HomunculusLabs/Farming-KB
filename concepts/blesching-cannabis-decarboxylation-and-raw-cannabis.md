# Cannabis Decarboxylation and Raw Cannabis Use

Decarboxylation is the chemical process by which the acidic precursors of
cannabinoids in fresh cannabis are converted into their active forms through
heat, time, or light exposure. Understanding this process is essential for
anyone using cannabis therapeutically, as it determines whether the medicine
will produce psychoactive effects and influences the [[cannabidiol-cbd-therapeutic-profile-blesching]] of
the plant material consumed.

## Overview

In fresh and raw [[cannabis-leaf-morphology-and-phyllotaxy]], CBD and THC cannabinoids exist as CBD-acid
and THC-acid, both of which are non-psychoactive in their raw state. Once the
plant cannabinoids become heated, dried, or stored, decarboxylation takes
place, changing the molecular structure and its resultant properties. While
CBD remains non-psychoactive after decarboxylation, THC-acid converts to THC,
the main psychoactive molecule in cannabis. This transformation fundamentally
limits the maximum potential dose of psychoactive cannabis that can be safely
consumed, since excessive THC produces [[blesching-cannabis-safety-adverse-effects-evidence]] including increased
anxiety and pain.

Fresh cannabis contains THC in the form of THC-carboxylic-acid (THC-A), which
is considered only minutely psychoactive. Once dried, the chemical composition
of THC-acid changes through the process of decarboxylation, becoming active
THC. This process occurs naturally as the plant dries or when it is burned,
vaporized, or cooked. Once devoid of its carboxyl group, THC becomes fully
psychoactive and binds with both CB1 and CB2 receptors to produce its
characteristic physical and mental effects.

William L. Courtney, MD, a physician who has worked extensively with dietary
raw cannabis, considers fresh and raw cannabis leaf an especially rich source
of CBD, further increasing the therapeutic influence potential. Courtney
suggests that unheated large cannabis leaf or leaf juice can be tolerated at
doses 60 times higher than when cannabis is heated. The juice tastes bitter
and can be diluted with another vegetable juice at a ratio of 1:10. Ten to
twenty large fan leaves juiced daily is a commonly recommended dose for
patients seeking non-psychoactive therapeutic benefits.

## Key Concepts

- Raw cannabis contains cannabinoids in their acidic, non-psychoactive forms
  (THC-A and CBD-A)
- Decarboxylation converts THC-A to active THC and CBD-A to active CBD through
  the application of heat, light, or time
- CBD remains non-psychoactive even after decarboxylation, while THC-A becomes
  the psychoactive THC molecule
- Raw cannabis can be consumed at doses up to 60 times higher than heated
  cannabis because of its non-psychoactive nature
- Raw THC-A has unique therapeutic properties independent of its decarboxylated
  form, including inhibition of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-alpha)
- Fresh raw cannabis leaf medicine allows patients to benefit from therapeutic
  properties without altering consciousness
- THCa content in fresh plant material provides anti-inflammatory and
  immunomodulatory effects without intoxication

## Decarboxylation Chemistry

The decarboxylation process involves the removal of a carboxyl group from the
cannabinoid acid molecule. In chemical terms, THC-A (tetrahydrocannabinolic
acid) loses a [[carbon-dioxide-retention-jar-lid-mushroom-cultivation]] molecule (CO2) when exposed to sufficient heat,
converting it into delta-9-THC (tetrahydrocannabinol). This reaction typically
occurs at temperatures between 200 and 310 degrees Fahrenheit (93 to 154
degrees Celsius), depending on the method and duration of heating.

Vaporization provides a controlled form of decarboxylation, heating plant
material to a precise temperature that evaporates cannabinoids just below the
burning point of the plant matter. This method allows patients to inhale
active cannabinoids while minimizing exposure to combustion byproducts. Smoking
provides rapid and complete decarboxylation, while cooking with cannabis-infused
oils or butter at moderate temperatures achieves partial decarboxylation over
longer periods.

The rate of decarboxylation is affected by several factors including
temperature, duration of heat exposure, moisture content, and the specific
cannabinoid being converted. THC-A decarboxylates more readily than CBD-A,
meaning that under identical conditions, more THC will be produced relative to
CBD. This has practical implications for patients who wish to maximize either
the psychoactive or non-psychoactive components of their medicine.
