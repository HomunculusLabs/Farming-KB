## Cortes and the Conquest (1519)

When Hernan Cortes arrived in the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlan in 1519, he and his chroniclers encountered a civilization with sophisticated mushroom ceremonies. Bernal Diaz del Castillo, a soldier in Cortes's expedition, described being served mushrooms by Aztec hosts and reported the effects: intoxication, visions, and spiritual experiences. The Spanish initially observed these ceremonies with a mixture of fascination and horror, understanding them as diabolical practices that needed to be suppressed.

The encounter between Spanish conquistadors and Aztec mushroom culture represents one of history's starkest collisions between mycophilic and mycophobic worldviews. The Spanish, arriving from a culture with no tradition of intentional mushroom use, immediately classified the practice as demonic — a judgment that would shape European attitudes toward psychoactive fungi for centuries.

## Fray Bernardino de Sahagun's Documentation

Fray Bernardino de Sahagun, a Franciscan friar who arrived in New Spain in 1529, produced the most detailed colonial-era documentation of Aztec mushroom use. His monumental work, the Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva Espana (General History of the Things of New Spain), recorded Nahuatl-speaking informants describing mushroom ceremonies in detail:

- Mushrooms were consumed during specific religious festivals
- Priests and healers administered them in controlled ceremonial settings
- Effects included visions, prophetic insight, and emotional catharsis
- The mushrooms were treated with deep reverence as sacred substances

Sahagun's work preserved invaluable ethnographic information that might otherwise have been lost to the Spanish suppression of indigenous religious practices. His informants described how those who consumed teonanacatl saw visions, sometimes weeping, sometimes laughing, experiencing what they believed to be direct communication with the divine.

## Fray Diego Duran's Account

Fray Diego Duran, another Franciscan chronicler, provided complementary documentation of Aztec religious practices involving mushrooms. His Historia de las Indias de Nueva Espana described mushroom use within the broader context of Aztec religious cosmology, connecting it to deity worship, calendar ceremonies, and rites of passage. Duran's accounts helped establish that mushroom use was not marginal but central to Aztec spiritual practice.

## The Tizoc Funeral (1481)

The funeral of the Aztec ruler Tizoc in 1481, documented in indigenous accounts, featured elaborate mushroom ceremonies as part of the royal funerary rites. This pre-Columbian documentation confirms that psilocybin mushroom use was established well before European contact and was associated with the highest levels of Aztec society. The use of psychoactive mushrooms at royal funerals suggests they served a role in facilitating communication with ancestors and guiding the deceased ruler's spirit.

## Mushroom Stones and Pre-Classic Origins

Archaeological evidence extends the history of ceremonial mushroom use in Mesoamerica far beyond the Aztec period. Mushroom stone figures (piedras hongos) dating to the pre-classic period (c. 1000 BCE – 200 CE) have been found throughout the Guatemalan highlands and surrounding regions. These carved stone objects, depicting various mushroom forms, suggest that mushroom ceremonies may have been practiced for over three millennia.

The mushroom stones range from simple representations to elaborate carved figures with anthropomorphic faces emerging from mushroom caps. Their distribution across a wide geographic area indicates that ceremonial mushroom use was not limited to a single culture but was part of a broader Mesoamerican tradition shared by the Maya, Olmec, and their predecessors.

## The Nahuatl Vocabulary of Mushroom Use

The richness of Nahuatl mushroom terminology reveals the depth of indigenous mycological knowledge:
- **Teonanacatl** — "Flesh of the gods" (the most sacred term)
- **Nanacatl** — Generic term for mushroom
- **Xochinanacatl** — "Flower mushroom," referring to psilocybin species
- Various specific names for different species, indicating detailed taxonomic knowledge

## Spanish Suppression

The Spanish colonial authorities, particularly the Inquisition, actively suppressed indigenous mushroom ceremonies. The Church classified teonanacatl as a tool of the Devil, and possession or use of psychoactive mushrooms became grounds for persecution. Despite centuries of suppression, mushroom ceremonies survived in remote areas of Oaxaca, Guerrero, and other Mexican states, eventually coming to the attention of the outside world through R. Gordon Wasson's famous 1955 visit to Maria Sabina.

## Primary Sources

The most comprehensive primary accounts come from Fray Bernardino de Sahagun's *Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España* (c. 1565-1570), which records Nahuatl informants describing teonanacatl ("flesh of the gods") in detail. Sahagun's manuscript includes descriptions of mushroom preparation, ceremonial consumption, divinatory practices, and the specific visions reported. The Florentine Codex preserves the original Nahuatl alongside Sahagun's Spanish translation. Additional accounts from Juan de Torquemada's *Monarquia Indiana* (1615) and Jacinto de la Serna's *Manual de Ministros de Indios* (1656) provide corroborating evidence of ongoing indigenous mushroom ceremonies decades after the Conquest.

## Modern Scholarship

R. Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" brought scholarly attention to the Aztec mushroom cult, though his identification of teonanacatl with Psilocybe mexicana has been debated. Subsequent ethnomycological work by Gastón Guzmán, Peter T. Furst, and others has expanded the understanding of pre-Columbian psychedelic mushroom use, confirming a rich and well-documented tradition spanning millennia. Archaeological evidence including mushroom stones from Guatemala (dating to 1000-500 BCE) suggests the practice predates the Aztecs by over two thousand years.

Despite this systematic suppression, mushroom use was never fully eradicated. Indigenous communities continued ceremonies in remote areas of Oaxaca and the Sierra Madre, maintaining oral traditions that preserved the knowledge across generations. The Mazatec curandera Maria Sabina, whose ceremonies were documented by R. Gordon Wasson in the 1950s, represented a direct living link to these pre-Columbian practices, demonstrating the remarkable continuity of teonanacatl traditions over more than four centuries of colonial rule.
