# Neurotropic Fungi of the African Continent

Africa presents one of the most significant knowledge gaps in the
worldwide [[global-distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] fungi. Despite the continent's
extraordinarily high biodiversity, only 6 to 8 neurotropic species
of Psilocybe are known from Africa, a number that almost certainly
reflects undercollection rather than true absence. Guzmán, Allen and
Gartz (2000) highlighted Africa as a continent where mycological
exploration for neurotropic fungi remains severely inadequate.

## The Scale of the Exploration Gap

The contrast between Africa's documented and probable neurotropic
fungal diversity is stark. Mexico, a country with approximately 2
million square kilometers, has 76 neurotropic fungal species. Africa,
with over 30 million square kilometers and vastly greater habitat
diversity, has only a handful of confirmed records. The authors
specifically noted the absence of records from many regions of
Africa on their [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]] map (Figure 19).

Several factors contribute to this gap. Mycological research in
Africa has historically focused on agricultural pathology and food
security rather than biodiversity surveys. Many regions lack trained
mycologists, and political instability has limited field research in
certain areas. Additionally, the predominantly [[query-how-to-design-a-food-forest-for-a-tropical-climate]] of
much of Africa means that fungi fruit during rainy seasons when
field access is often most difficult.

## North Africa: The Psilocybe mairei Connection

North Africa has produced some of the most interesting African
neurotropic fungal records. Psilocybe mairei is known from Morocco
and Algeria in North Africa, as documented by Malençon and Bertault
(1970) and Singer and Smith (1958). Remarkably, the species is also
known from Europe, specifically Czechoslovakia, where Semerdzieva
and Nerud (1973), Auert et al. (1980), Kubicka (1985) and others
have documented it.

This North African-European disjunction in P. mairei mirrors
patterns seen in other fungal groups and may reflect ancient
Mediterranean vegetation corridors that connected North Africa to
southern Europe. Morocco also records [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]], A.
pantherina, Copelandia bispora, [[inocybe-calamistrata]], I.
corydalina, [[pluteus-cyanopus]], P. atricapillus, P. salicinus,
P. villosus and [[panaeolus-fimicola]] and P. papilionaceus,
making it the most thoroughly documented North African country
for neurotropic fungi.

## Psilocybe cubensis and the Columbian Exchange Hypothesis

A particularly intriguing biogeographic question concerns
Psilocybe cubensis, which is very common in Mexico, Central
America and South America, growing on cow dung. The cattle in the
Americas were introduced by Spanish colonists during the sixteenth
and seventeenth centuries, and P. cubensis does not grow natively
in Europe. Guzmán (1983) proposed that this fungus was introduced
to the Americas through the [[guzman-allen-gartz-cubensis-introduction-african-slave-commerce-hypothesis]] during Spanish colonial
times, potentially from Africa.

If this hypothesis is correct, it raises the question of where
the ancestral populations of P. cubensis existed. Reports from
Kenya of P. cubensis are confused and uncertain, with Cullinan
et al. (1945), Charters (1957, 1958) and Vendcourt and Trump
(1969) providing ambiguous identifications. Pegler (1977) reported
only P. aquamarina from Kenya, a species closely related to
P. cubensis. Resolving the origin and native range of P. cubensis
requires additional field work in tropical Africa.
