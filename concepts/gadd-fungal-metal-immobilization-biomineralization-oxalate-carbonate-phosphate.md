# Fungal Metal Immobilization and Biomineralization

Fungi possess remarkable abilities to transform soluble toxic metal ions into
insoluble mineral forms through biomineralization. This process converts
mobile, bioavailable metals into stable crystalline or amorphous precipitates,
effectively removing them from solution and reducing their environmental and
biological toxicity. Gadd's research documents multiple biomineralization
pathways including oxalate precipitation, carbonate formation, and metal
phosphate biogenesis.

## Why Metal Immobilization Matters

[[bioremediation-monitoring-and-assessment]] aim to
immobilize metals, converting them from soluble ions to insoluble minerals
that remain fixed in place and cannot enter biological systems.

Fungi are particularly effective metal immobilizers because they combine
biosorption (passive binding to cell walls) with active biomineralization
(conversion to crystalline mineral phases). This dual mechanism provides
both rapid initial sequestration and long-term stability.

## Oxalate Biomineralization

Oxalic acid is the most common organic acid produced by fungi and plays a
central role in metal immobilization. When oxalic acid reacts with divalent
and trivalent metal cations, it forms insoluble metal [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-and-carbonate-precipitation]]** (CaC₂O₄): The most common fungal biomineral, forming
  weddellite (CaC₂O₄·2H₂O) and whewellite (CaC₂O₄·H₂O) crystal forms.
- **Copper oxalate** (CuC₂O₄): Moolooite — a blue-green crystalline precipitate.
- **Zinc oxalate** (ZnC₂O₄): Forms when fungi encounter elevated zinc.
- **Lead oxalate**: Highly insoluble, effectively immobilizing lead.
- **Cadmium oxalate**: Though less studied, cadmium forms stable oxalate
  precipitates that reduce its bioavailability.

The oxalate-to-metal ratio determines the mineral form produced. At low
oxalate concentrations, metals may form soluble complexes rather than
precipitates. At high oxalate concentrations, the mineral oxalate phase
predominates and immobilization is most effective.

### Intracellular vs Extracellular Oxalate

Fungi can biomineralize metals both extracellularly and intracellularly.
Extracellular precipitation occurs when fungal hyphae excrete oxalic acid
into the surrounding medium, where it reacts with dissolved metals.
Intracellular precipitation occurs when metals are absorbed into fungal cells
and precipitated within vacuoles or cytoplasmic compartments.

Intracellular immobilization is particularly important for essential metals
like zinc and copper, which fungi absorb for metabolic use and then store
as inert mineral deposits to prevent toxicity. These intracellular
biominerals remain sequestered as long as the fungal biomass persists.

## Carbonate Biomineralization

Fungi can precipitate metal carbonates through two pathways:

### Urease-Mediated Carbonate Precipitation
Some fungi produce the enzyme urease, which hydrolyzes urea to ammonia and
carbon dioxide. The carbon dioxide dissolves to form carbonate ions, which
react with metal cations to form insoluble metal carbonates:

    M²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → MCO₃ (s)

This pathway is particularly effective for calcium, strontium, and radium.
Calcium carbonate precipitation by ureolytic fungi is being explored for
biocementation applications and for immobilizing radioactive strontium-90.

### Respiratory Carbonate Precipitation
Fungal respiration produces CO₂ that equilibrates with water to form
bicarbonate and carbonate ions. In alkaline environments, this respiratory
CO₂ can drive carbonate precipitation of calcium, magnesium, and other
divalent cations. The process is slower than urease-mediated precipitation
but operates continuously wherever fungi are [[fungal-hyphae]]
