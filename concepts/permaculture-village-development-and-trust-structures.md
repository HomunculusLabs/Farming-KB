## Permaculture Village Development and Trust Structures

## Overview

[[permaculture-village-development]] follows a structured, phased approach that integrates legal, financial, and social frameworks from the outset. Bill Mollison outlines a comprehensive process for establishing self-reliant communities that balance individual autonomy with collective [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]] through carefully designed trust structures.

## The Development Process

### Step-by-Step Procedure

The development of a permaculture village follows a methodical sequence:

1. **Locate suitable land** — assess climate, water, soil, and access.
2. **Establish a development group** — a small core of 2–4 people with complementary skills (real estate, design, planning, law). Surveyors, road-builders, and landscapers are typically sourced locally.
3. **Define stages of development** — coordinate with the shire (local government) if staged building completion is required.
4. **Prepare financial estimates** — establish prices based on roading, water supply, and sewerage costs.
5. **Create a detailed site plan and proposal** for the village.
6. **Advertise and convene prospective customers** — obtain firm commitments and issue the proposal and site plan.
7. **Obtain sealed subdivision permission** from the local authority.
8. **Sell to prospective buyers** — use a trust fund for road, water, and site preparation.
9. **Reinvest profits** — when costs are cleared, decide on future projects from profits and skills gained.

### Financing Methods

Several approaches can finance village development:

- **Developer-funded**: The developer holds all funds for survey and plans.
- **Investor-raised**: The developer raises funds from investors for the proposal.
- **Buyer trust account**: Potential buyers form a trust account to purchase and develop the land collectively.
- **Hybrid approach**: A mix of the first two, with trust funds established by developers and released when the shire council grants full approval.

## Trust Structures

### Trust A — The Land Bank (Public Good Trust)

Trust A is the foundational legal structure. It holds commons (village land) for the common good and for later development.

**Key characteristics:**
- Holds assets for the public good
- Does not take financial risks
- Leases or rents to Trust B (the trading trust)
- Administers common lands, recreation reserves, and sites for future structures (schools, restaurants, machine shops, primary production areas)

**Governance:**
- The developer acts as the initial foundation director (settlor)
- Should retire as soon as the site has 10–12 residents
- Residents then assume directorship of trust lands and cash assets
- 3–4 directors are sufficient
- Small sub-committees can be allotted specific project work

### Trust B — The Trading Trust (Discretionary Trust)

Residents should set up a separate unit discretionary trust for trading operations as early as possible.

**Key characteristics:**
- Currently immune from company tax
- Reduces family tax
- Enables a wide variety of enterprises under one organisation
- Does trade and takes risks
- Has Trust A as one of its beneficiaries
- Can duplicate or triplicate to accommodate new enterprises
- Insulates successful operations from risk
- Handles financial systems such as leasing and lending units

### The Relationship Between Trusts

The two-trust system creates a clear separation between public-good asset holding and commercial activity:

- **Trust A (Land Bank)**: Holds commons and reserves, non-risk-taking, leases to Trust B, receives income from leases
- **Trust B (Trading Trust)**: Conducts all commercial operations, takes risks, pays beneficiaries (including Trust A), can replicate for new enterprises

### Land Tenure Options

Although cluster and strata titles give privacy, negotiability, and autonomy to individuals and families (titles and houses can be resold, traded, or given to Trust A), a land trust is necessary to administer:
- Common lands
- Recreation reserves
- Sites for future structures (schools, restaurants, machine shops, primary production)

## Village Size and Social Dynamics

### Optimal Population

Human settlements vary in their ability to provide resources, develop self-reliance, and foster neighbourly behaviour according to population size:

| Population | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| ~30 adults | Good starting point for a pioneer village |
| ~100 income-producing people | A significant financial institution can be village-based |
| ~200–300 | All people can potentially know each other |
| ~500 | Upper limit before social cohesion declines; "calving" into new villages is recommended |
| ~2,000 | Theft and competitiveness increase; sects form in opposition; "ecumenical alliances" are lost |

### The "Calving" Strategy

Mollison recommends starting small (about 30 adults), building to 200–300 people, and proceeding slowly and by choice to 500, then "calving" into new neighbourhoods or new villages. This mirrors the tribal practice of confederations of 200 or so grouping into alliances of 5,000–7,000.

### The Mondragon Model

The [[mondragon-cooperatives]] of Spain initially grew large (3,000–5,000 members) but later reduced to cooperatives of 300–500 to preserve individual identity. However, a group of such small cooperatives can manufacture any vehicle or machine if each produces a component part — this is organised by the smaller cooperatives in the Mondragon system.

### The Neighbourhood Factor

Knowledge of each other's names is identified as a primary survival factor. This was assessed in the 1967 Hobart fires, where casualties in "anonymous" commuter suburbs were many times higher than in neighbourhoods where people knew and cared for others.

### Confederation of Villages

Alliances of 200–500 household-size hamlets can form viable manufacturing or trading alliances while maintaining a safe genetic base. Pioneer villages can seek alliances with others for the common good, sharing special products by trade or arranging out-marriages.

## The Development Group

The developmental group works best as a small core of 2–4 people, each with special skills such as real estate, design, planning, or law. Surveyors, road-builders, builders, and landscapers are usually locally available. Some contractors may work for equity in the project, either as village occupants or for resale at a later date.

## Design Handover

The development group should hand over a site design and user's manual to the directors of Trust A, who can:
- Display and circulate the initial design
- Record changes and modifications
- Keep clear the essential land areas for productive use

All initial designs are made to be changed. The challenge is to change the design for the better — a design gives a starting point, not an end point.

## Alliances and Confederation

Many tribes of 200 or so confederate to alliances of 5,000–7,000, sharing special products by trade or arranging out-marriages. Pioneer villages can seek alliances with others for the common good, extending their economic resilience and genetic diversity beyond what a single village can sustain.
