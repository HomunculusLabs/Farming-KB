# Mycorrhizal Diversity and Ecosystem Function

## Overview

The relationship between [[fungal-ecology]], with
significant implications for understanding how
fungal-biodiversity|biodiversity supports [[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]].
Research has demonstrated that the diversity of mycorrhizal fungal
partners on plant roots can significantly influence plant growth,
nutrient acquisition, and community dynamics, though the nature and
direction of these effects depend on environmental context and the
specific functional attributes of the fungal species involved.

## Evidence from Experimental Studies

### Multi-Species Benefits

Multiple experimental studies have shown that plants perform better
with multiple mycorrhizal partners than with single species. Chu-Chou
and Grace (1985) showed that radiata pine seedlings performed better
in the presence of three [[biodiversity-fungi-ectomycorrhizal-diversity]]
rather than the level of root colonization. This suggests that the
interaction among ectomycorrhizal fungi on the same root system has an
influence on plant performance independent of either total colonization
level or species composition.

### Nutrient Uptake Efficiency

Dighton et al. (1990) used radiotracer phosphorus to show that the
uptake of phosphorus into the canopy of field-grown birch trees was
significantly higher in root zones colonized mainly by Hebeloma
mycorrhizae compared to Lactarius or Laccaria. This demonstrates that
different mycorrhizal species differ in their nutrient acquisition
efficiency, providing a mechanistic basis for the benefits of diverse
mycorrhizal communities.

## Context Dependence

The effects of mycorrhizal fungal diversity may depend on the context
in which they exist. The community structure may be beneficial,
detrimental, or neutral depending on nutritional conditions of the
soil, plant age, or other factors. Fitter (1985, 1991) observed that
it is not always obvious what the function of mycorrhizae is in a
natural ecosystem, though their role is better understood in
agricultural systems.

### Enzyme Diversity

Different mycorrhizal species exhibit different enzyme capabilities.
Conn and Dighton (2000) showed that the mycorrhizal communities on
pine roots exploiting oak and oak-pine mixed leaf litters had a higher
proportion of phosphatase-producing mycorrhizae than on pine alone.
This reflects adaptation to local nutrient conditions, as oak litter
immobilizes phosphorus during initial decomposition stages.
