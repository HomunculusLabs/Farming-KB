# Gangamma's Mandala Garden Design

Gangamma's Mandala is a compact, intensive permaculture garden design capable of feeding a family of five year-round from as little as 100 square metres (1,075 square feet). Developed by [[bill-mollison]], it integrates traditional intensive garden techniques from Taiwan and the Philippines (notably the Samaka gardens and East-West Institute methods) with permaculture's "least-path" layout principles to create a sophisticated yet simple-to-build sustenance garden.

## Design Philosophy

The mandala pattern is a circular layout that maximizes growing area while minimizing path space and movement. Every element is placed for ease of access, efficient water use, and integrated waste recycling. The overall pattern can be adapted to almost every site form, though it is presented as a flat-site model. Although building such a garden is fast and simple, its design is sophisticated — every zone serves multiple functions.

## Construction Steps

### Step 1: The Central Banana Circle

At the centre of the garden area, describe a circle 2 metres (6 feet) across and excavate the topsoil to a dish shape, ridged on the perimeter, approximately 0.6–1 metre (2–3 feet) deep from hollow to rim. This becomes the **banana/sweet potato/papaya circle garden**.

The entire circle is covered with wet paper, cardboard, banana leaves, or any mulch material. The hollow is filled (or over-filled as a dome) with rough mulch of short logs, coarse twigs, hay, rice husks, sawdust, or any humus-creating materials. A scatter of manure, ash, lime, dolomite, or fertilizer can be added. Stones are banked to the outside of the rim if available.

The rim is planted with 4–5 papaya (tall variety), 4–6 bananas (dwarf types), and 8–10 sweet potato. Yams or taro can be placed inside the rim. Later, beans climb the papaya and banana stalks. A wooden grid or platform over the mulch can serve as an outside shower or wash-up area, with greywater directly nourishing the circle.

### Step 2: The Circular Sunken Path

A circular sunken path 0.6–1 metre (2–3 feet) wide is covered with sawdust or gravel around the central circle. Off this main path, 5–6 "keyholes" or indentations are made — these are access points into the surrounding beds that allow the gardener to reach the centre of each bed from the path.

### Step 3: Keyhole Beds

Around each keyhole, a bed 1.5–2 metres (5–6 feet) wide is constructed. The beds are first edge-banked with soil 10–20 cm (4–8 inches) high to prevent water run-off, then papered and mulched identically to the banana circle. Six major keyhole beds result, each separated from the next by a thin strip of lemongrass (*Cymbopogon citratus*) or Vetiver grass (*Vetiveria zizanioides*).

## Perimeter Border System

The mandala is enclosed by a multi-layered border system working outward from the keyhole beds:

1. **Inner grass barrier:** Strips of lemongrass, comfrey, and arrowroot (*Canna edulis*) form a dense barrier against invasive grasses like kikuyu
2. **Outer hedge/windbreak:** Cassava, banana, papaya, pigeon pea, Leucaena, and Crotolaria form a productive barrier providing mulch, forage, wind protection, and food
3. **Fence or spiny woven hedge boundary:** For cattle exclusion if necessary



## Planting Zones



### Zone A: Pathside Greens

On the track edge border of the central path and keyholes, within stoop-reach, plant frequently-plucked everyday greens of high value. The selection criterion is that all or most of the plant is picked for much of the year. Species include all chive and shallot varieties, parsley, coriander, thyme, sage, celery, broccoli, and edible chrysanthemum.



### Zone B: Inner Keyhole Bed Crops

The inner portions of keyhole beds contain seasonal vegetables and herbs carefully planned for year-round production. These beds are mulched from the hedge and the greywater treatment area. Food scraps, ashes, mulch, shredded paper, and plant waste are incorporated, along with crop wastes and cuttings from windbreak plants.



### Zone C: Banana Circles

Additional banana circles (or papaya circles, or mixed papaya/banana/pigeon pea/tomato/yam circles) receive water from the hand basin and kitchen sink and are mulched from the hedge. They serve as primary greywater treatment and nutrient recycling points.



### Zone D: Avenue Crops

Main crops of value are planted in avenue layouts — spiral or zig-zag patterns of self-mulched, stable intercrop systems. Examples include maize/beans, yam/beans, cassava/beans, or melon/beans with productive legume hedges of Leucaena, Gliricidia, or Sesbania.



## Water and Waste Integration

The mandala design integrates all household water and organic waste flows:

- **Clean water** (tank overflow, roof catchment) goes to wet food patches growing watercress, kangkong, and Chinese water chestnut
- **Greywater** from hand basins and kitchen sinks feeds banana circles
- **Blackwater** from toilets goes to dedicated mulch-forage treatment areas growing comfrey, banna grass, lemongrass, and legumes — these are regularly cut for animal feed or garden mulch
- **Shower and bath water** can be run along low-pressure leaky pipes to garden beds



## Climate Adaptation

The mandala design originates in tropical and subtropical regions but can be adapted to temperate zones by substituting appropriate species. The fundamental principles — circular layout, central greywater-recycling pit, keyhole bed access, multi-layered borders, and intensive polyculture — apply across climates. Species selection and timing of plantings are the primary variables to adjust.
