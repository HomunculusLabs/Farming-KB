## Symbiosis in Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, and Gymnosperms

## Bryophyte Symbiosis (Liverworts)

Liverworts such as Anthoceros, Notothylas, and Blasia possess blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) as symbiotic partners. The endophyte is the cyanobacterium Nostoc, which colonizes mucilage cavities on the ventral side of the thallus.

In these cavities, Anthoceros produces branched hypha-like filaments interspersed with the algal colony. The contact between the alga and the bryophyte tissues is increased by warty outgrowths, facilitating transport and exchange of materials. Photosynthate is transported to Nostoc by the bryophyte, while fixed nitrogen is transferred in reverse from Nostoc to the bryophyte (Rashid 1998).

This nitrogen-fixing partnership is especially significant in nutrient-poor habitats such as tropical montane forests, where bryophyte-cyanobacteria associations can contribute substantially to ecosystem nitrogen inputs. In some cloud forests, bryophyte mats with cyanobacterial partners fix measurable quantities of atmospheric nitrogen that become available to the broader plant community through leaching and decomposition.

## Pteridophyte Symbiosis (Azolla)

Azolla is a floating fern that forms a remarkable symbiosis with the filamentous cyanobacterium Anabaena. Anabaena filaments live within ovoid cavities inside the leaves of Azolla, forming a nitrogen-fixing partnership.

### Heterocyst Production

When free-living, Anabaena develops only 5-10% of its cells into heterocysts. In symbiotic association with Azolla, this increases to 25-30%. The epidermal cells inside the leaf cavity project inward, forming numerous multicellular hairs with warty outgrowths that serve as transfer cells for [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] under aerobic conditions. Mature heterocysts lack functional photosystem II and cannot produce oxygen. They contain only photosystem I for cyclic photophosphorylation and ATP regeneration, providing appropriate conditions for the oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase enzyme.

## Gymnosperm Symbiosis (Cycas Coralloid Roots)

Cycas produces two types of roots: normal roots growing downward, and some lateral root branches that become aerial apogeotropic roots, repeatedly branching dichotomously. These are known as **coralloid roots**.

### Formation

Bacteria of the soil enter and multiply rapidly in the cells of the apical portion. The cells disorganize and intercellular spaces enlarge. Algae such as Anabaena and Nostoc gain entry into the disorganized zone through apertures formed by bacteroid infection. These algae form a conspicuous ring in the midcortical zone.

The root becomes negatively geotropic, coming just above or slightly below the soil surface. They are dichotomously branched in profuse manner, forming "root tubercles" with coralloid masses.

### Significance

Coralloid roots are known to occur only in cycads, and cycads are the only known gymnosperms to form symbiotic relationships with nitrogen-fixing organisms.
