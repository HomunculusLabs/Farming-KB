# Psychedelic Safety and Harm Reduction

Throughout most of history, natural psychedelics have been considered gifts of the Gods, associated with healing of body, mind, and soul. Problems from psychedelic use are rare when proper precautions are observed. DM Turner's Essential Psychedelics Guide provides a comprehensive safety framework.

## Historical Context

Contrary to popular belief, many psychedelic scare stories from the 1960s-70s were politically or financially motivated. Claims like LSD causing chromosome damage have been proven false. Millions of doses are consumed monthly with very few incidents. Indigenous shamans have consumed psychedelics hundreds of times per year for their entire lives with no ill effects. Maria Sabina, the shamaness who performed the ceremony for R. Gordon Wasson's first mushroom voyage, died of old age at 97 after some 67 years of regular mushroom use. Turner notes he knows of but one case of physical harm from psychedelic use aside from ecstasy and Belladonna — the ultimately potent combination of Harmaline and 5-MeO-DMT.

## Physical Safety

### Drug Properties
- Most psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, DMT) have a wide margin between active and toxic doses
- Exceptions: MDMA/ecstasy can exhibit toxic effects at just twice the average dose
- Belladonna alkaloids are genuinely dangerous
- Legal research on maximum safe dosages is practically non-existent

### Dosage Principles
- Always start with a small dose when trying a new substance
- Work up gradually while monitoring body and mind response
- Some individuals may have powerful reactions to amounts that are minimal for others
- Individual sensitivity varies enormously

### Common Physical Effects
- **Nausea**: Common on many psychedelics; do not resist the urge to vomit — users usually feel better afterwards. Indigenous people consider this a cleansing purge.
- **Body awareness**: Increased awareness of bodily functions can mimic illness. Learn to distinguish substance effects from anxiety-generated symptoms.
- **Post-trip fatigue**: Feeling exhausted or "out of touch" the next day is normal. Plan an extra recovery day.

### Diet and Preparation
- Fasting before a trip can reduce nausea
- Some users experiment with different diets prior to tripping
- Hydration is essential, especially with MDMA (avoid dancing for hours without water)

### Driving and Activity Warnings
- While high, it is dangerous to drive, fly a plane, or operate machinery
- At large doses, users may have difficulty recalling their name; the world undergoes massive changes each moment
- The psychedelic experience is not compatible with split-second decisions required while driving
- Smaller doses produce similar but milder changes in consciousness
- Have a designated driver, take a cab, or stay put

### Medical Precautions
- Anyone with health problems or taking prescription/OTC medicines should be cautious
- Most psychedelics increase pulse rate and blood pressure
- Effects of using psychedelics with medical problems or prescription drugs is largely unknown
- Consult a knowledgeable doctor

## Mental Safety

Mental safety is individual and must be evaluated by each user. Each person's mind and capacity for handling psychedelics is different; psychedelics are not for everybody. Key principles:

### Set and Setting
- **Set**: Your mindset, mood, expectations, and mental state
- **Setting**: Physical environment, social context, and available support
- Both must be carefully prepared for a positive experience

### Optimal Setting
If choosing one ideal setting, Turner recommends the time-honoured methods: natural psychedelic (mushrooms or Peyote), outdoors in nature, away from city noise, with a group of respected friends or family. For novices, the session should be guided by trusted and experienced "elders."

### Recreational vs. Dedicated Use
The "recreational" use of smaller amounts in social settings (raves, concerts, parties) seldom provides a full spectrum of the psychedelic experience. To get the most from a psychedelic, one must put aside time completely devoted to the experience and take a dose that significantly alters perception.

### Difficult Experiences
- Anxiety about physical illness is a common mind-generated response during psychedelic transformation
- The Psychedelic Experience (Leary, Metzner, Alpert) describes techniques for navigating difficult periods
- Becoming aware of the body/mind relationship can help ease discomfort by releasing mental/emotional blocks
- "Bad trips" are often transformed into the most valuable experiences with proper navigation
- The main reason for negative experiences is lack of preparation, not anything inherent in the person or substance

### Reimprinting the Mind
Psychedelics can "reimprint the mind" — they dissolve identity and perceptual framework ("ego death"), then the user reassembles a new (hopefully improved) personality. This powerful process underscores why careful [[psychedelic-experience-preparation]] and [[psychedelic-integration-guide]] are essential.

### Long-Term Mental Effects
- Turner has never heard of anyone experiencing long-term adverse effects through natural psychedelics (mushrooms, mescaline cacti) — users seem to "take it in stride and come back to center"
- People with schizophrenia or mental disorders beyond common neurosis should exercise extreme caution

### Integration
- Avoid returning to familiar routines immediately after a trip
- Extra recovery day helps retain insights from the journey
- Journaling and reflection amplify long-term benefits

## Contraindications

- Personal or family history of psychosis or schizophrenia
- Current use of certain medications (especially [[mao-inhibitors-and-psychedelics]])
- Pregnancy
- Heart conditions (especially with compounds that affect blood pressure)
- Inexperienced users should not combine substances

# Psilocybin Public Awareness and Media in Australia

The public discovery of psychoactive mushrooms in Australia did not occur through scientific channels or indigenous knowledge transmission, but rather through a combination of surfer subculture, popular media, and word-of-mouth communication. [[insect-allies]] documented how awareness spread from a small group of surfers in the late 1960s to nationwide recognition by the 1980s, driven largely by newspaper reports that inadvertently served as foraging guides.

## The Surfer Subculture Connection

An Australian physician suggested that the general public and the drug-using subculture first became aware of the visionary properties of psychoactive mushrooms through visiting surfers, likely from New Zealand or the United States (Hawaii), who provided ethnomycological information to local surfers. This was documented by McCarthy in 1971.

A survey conducted in Southern Queensland during 1969 relied on interviews with 51 people belonging to the "surfer subculture at local beach resorts." The physician reported that use of psychoactive mushrooms, along with 21 other drugs, "was well demonstrated" during this survey. While the survey involved surfers and their friends, the physician noted there was no suggestion that use was confined to this group, which constituted only a proportion of Australia's young drug-taking community.

Word-of-mouth communication thus made a significant contribution to the increasing use of "magic mushrooms" in both Australia and New Zealand. Information about mushroom locations, identification, and effects spread through informal networks that paralleled traditional ethnomycological knowledge transmission.

## The Role of the Popular Press

Many early users of psychoactive mushrooms in Australia became aware of their mind-altering effects by reading published literature and news items in the popular Australian press during the late 1960s and early 1970s. These news items often described both accidental and deliberate intoxications resulting from the ingestion of "magic mushrooms," inadvertently providing practical information about where to find them.

One 1972 newspaper report from Brisbane provided an account that functioned as a de facto foraging guide: "Children at a suburban school are getting high on mushrooms called 'Gold Tops.' The mushrooms are common along the Brisbane River near Toowing High School, and children in search of 'kicks' have been experimenting with them."

As Allen observed, it would be obvious to anyone reading such a report that those searching for hallucinogenic mushrooms would be able to find them if they so desired. The media thus played an unintentional but significant role in promoting mushroom use by publishing specific location information.

## The Wasson Effect

Another significant factor in promoting interest in psychoactive mushrooms was the publication of R. Gordon Wasson's account of his rediscovery of a hallucinogenic mushroom cult among the Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico. Wasson's report appeared in an international edition of Life Magazine in the late 1950s, providing many drug users and others with the incentive to seek out and experiment with these mushrooms.

This journalistic account reached a vast audience and transformed public understanding of psychoactive mushrooms from obscure botanical curiosities into culturally significant entheogenic substances. See [[wasson-hofmann-psilocybin-connection]] for details on Wasson's research and its global impact.
