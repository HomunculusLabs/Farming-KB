# Grain Spawn Production and Expansion

## Overview

Grain spawn bridges the gap between agar cultures and fruiting substrates. Agar
cultures dry quickly and offer no springboard for outcompeting molds and
bacteria. Once transferred to grain, mycelium quickly colonizes each grain
kernel, creating a powerful fuel tank loaded with nutrients. This
first-generation "master spawn" can inoculate additional grain rounds, with the
ability to expand into thousands of pounds of mushrooms in just a few weeks.

## Choosing Grain Types

Common seed grains include corn, millet, milo, wheat, and rye. Cotter's
laboratory uses wheat and rye for first-generation master spawn, then expands
to soaked millet (birdseed component). Requirements for suitable grains:

- Maintain shape when soaked in hot water and sterilized
- Do not become sticky, gummy, or explode when heated
- Have a waxy or durable coating for easy separation during mixing

## Containers for Grain Spawn

Narrow-mouthed canning jars are most common, but any thick glass jars with
metal lids work. Aeration is essential -- options include:
- Commercial filter disks that fit [[mason-jar-preserving-guide]] lids
- Drilled holes stuffed with cotton or polyester fill
- Tyvek (woven house wrap) secured over drilled holes

Autoclavable bags with aeration filters are used for large quantities. Oven
bags or microwavable bags work in a pinch. The preferred expansion path is
[[brown-rice-petri-plate-inoculation-vermiculite-transfer-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]] to jar to bags.

## Precooking Grain

Cotter prefers precooking grains before sterilization rather than mixing dry
grain with water in jars. This provides two advantages:

1. **Better moisture control**: Mixed results from dry grain/water methods can
   range from too dry (mycelium cannot spread) to too wet (bacteria thrive
   and grain turns to mush)
2. **Endospore germination trick**: Allowing cooked grain to rest before
   sterilization causes hardy bacterial endospores to drop their defenses and
   germinate, making them more vulnerable to sterilization temperatures

## Making Master Spawn: Step-by-Step

### Step 1: Measure and Boil

Since grain doubles in size when hydrated, measure half the needed volume into
a large pot at least three times the grain volume. Cover with just over twice
the water amount. Bring to a boil, stirring occasionally to prevent scorching.

### Step 2: Soak

Remove from heat at about 160 degrees F (71 degrees C), before boiling. Cover
and let stand 30 minutes to 1 hour. Check grains periodically by cutting them
in half -- ideally mostly moist throughout with a small dried center.

### Step 3: Drain and Cool

Strain grains and let cool. If overcooked or slimy, rinse repeatedly with cold
water. Stir occasionally to prevent sticking and evaporate lingering surface
moisture. Grains should be swollen and moist but never wet on the outside.

### Step 4: Fill Containers

Fill jars or bags half full with cooled grain. Seal with aeration filters.

### Step 5: Sterilize

Place in a [[double-layer-tinfoil-jar-covering-pressure-cooker-method]] or autoclave. Process at 12-15 PSI for two hours.

### Step 6: Prepare Workstation

While sterilizing, set up the [[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]] hood. Sanitize the interior, run the
HEPA-filtered ventilation unit for an hour, and sanitize and lay out all tools
and cultures for inoculation.
