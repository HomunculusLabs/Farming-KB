# Spawn Production

Spawn production is the critical bridge between pure culture mycelium on agar and the bulk substrates on which mushrooms fruit. Paul Stamets provides exhaustive detail on grain spawn preparation, the grain master system (G1, G2, G3), inoculation techniques, and the expansion ratios that define successful spawn manufacturing.

## Purpose of Grain Spawn

Grain spawn serves two functions: it is a vehicle for evenly distributing mycelium, and a nutritional supplement. Whole grain is used because each kernel becomes a mycelial capsule, a platform from which mycelium can leap into the surrounding expanse. Smaller kernels of grain provide more points of inoculation per pound of spawn. Millet, a small kernel grain, is used by many large spawn producers because end-users like its convenience. Most small-scale gourmet mushroom growers utilize organically grown rye or wheat grain.

## Grain Selection and Quality

The grain used for spawn production must be free of fungicides and ideally should be organically grown. Grain obtained in the spring was probably harvested 6 or more months earlier. The resident contamination population gradually increases over time. With the proliferation of more contaminants per pound of grain, cultivators will have to adjust their sterilization schedules to compensate. Experienced cultivators are constantly searching for sources of fresh, high quality grain with endemically low counts of bacteria and mold spores.

## Two Methods for Preparing Grain

### Method 1: Boiling/Steeping
Submerge grain in a cauldron of boiling water. After an hour of boiling (or steeping), the saturated grain is drained of water (discarded) and scooped into awaiting spawn containers. Fitted with a lid having a 1/3 to 1/2 inch hole and lined with a microporous filter disc, the grain-filled jars are sterilized in a pressure cooker. This method is widely used and recommended because even moisture absorption and consistency is assured.

### Method 2: Dry Grain in Jars
Place dry grain into glass spawn jars, add the recommended amount of water (preferably hot), and allow the jars to sit overnight. The jars are capped with lids complete with a 3/8 to 1/2 inch hole fitted underneath with a microporous filter disc. By allowing the grain to soak for 12-24 hours, heat-resistant endospores of bacteria germinate and become sensitive to heat sterilization. This is a one-step process but causes uneven moisture content.

## Moisture Content

Moisture content plays a critical role in successful colonization. The optimum moisture for grain spawn falls within 45-55%, with an ideal around 50%. If the grain is too dry, growth is retarded with fine threads and slow growth. Should too much water be added, the grain clumps, dense slow growth occurs, and higher moisture contents encourage bacterial blooms. With excess water, grain kernels explode, exposing nutrients and making them more susceptible to contamination. Exploded kernels also cause clumping and depressed gas exchange.

Gypsum is added to help keep kernels separated after sterilization and to provide calcium and sulphur, basic elements promoting mushroom metabolism.

|-----------|---------|------------|------------|-----------|
| 16 oz bottle | 100 | 150 | 0.5 | 60% |
| Quart/liter jar | 200 | 200 | 1 | 50% |
| 1/2 gallon/2L jar | 480 | 400 | 2 | 45% |

*Moisture contents are not meant to be taken literally. Natural moisture content inherent within grain can affect absolute moisture by 15% or more. Grain comes with inherent moisture of 8-15%, so less water is added than might be expected.

## Sterilization Times

Sterilization duration varies by container size:
- 16 oz or quart jars: 1 hour at 15 psi (250F / 121C)
- 1/2 gallon jars: 1.5 hours at 15 psi
- Gallon jars: 2 hours at 15 psi
- Standard spawn bags: 4 hours at 15 psi

Adjustments in pressure must be made for elevations more than 3000 feet above sea level.

## Suitable Containers

- 16 oz mineral spring water bottles
- Quart mason jars, liter bottles
- 1/2 gallon jars
- 1 gallon jars
- 2.5 gallon jars
- Polypropylene plastic bags (no more than 3500 grams of dry grain in a standard autoclavable spawn bag with 1-inch filter patch)

## First Generation: Grain Master (G1)

The first time mushroom mycelium is transferred onto grain, that container of spawn is called a Grain Master, or G1. The preferred containers for incubating Grain Masters are traditionally small glass jars or bottles with narrow mouths to limit contaminant exposure.

Since the Grain Master is used to generate 100 to 1000 times its mass, special attention is given to its purity. Otherwise, the slightest amount of contamination is exponentially expanded with each step, not by a factor of 10, but by a factor of thousands.

### Steps for Creating G1

1. Visually ascertain the purity of a mushroom culture, selecting a petri dish culture showing greatest vigor. Ideally, the culture should be no more than two weeks old, with a margin of uncolonized media (~1/2 inch) along the inside peripheral edge to detect recently landed contaminants.

2. Open the pressure cooker in the laboratory clean room. Ideally, the pressure cooker has formed a vacuum in cooling. If not, outside air will be sucked in, potentially contaminating the recently sterilized jars. The pressure cooker should be placed in the clean room directly after the sterilization cycle.

3. Cut the mushroom mycelium into 8 or more wedges using a sterilized scalpel. The blade cools instantly on contact with the agar.

4. Spear wedges simultaneously and thrust into the sterilized grain. Replace and screw the lid tight. Shake each jar so wedges move throughout the interior mass.

5. Set inoculated jars on a shelf to incubate undisturbed for several days.

6. Three days from inoculation, inspect each jar for: (a) recovery of mycelium "leaping off" onto contacted grain kernels, and (b) absence of competitor molds, yeasts, mites or bacteria.

7. Seven days after inoculation, shake each jar again. Ten to fourteen days after inoculation, incubated at 75F (24C), each jar should be fully colonized. If colonization is not complete after 3-4 weeks, something has gone awry (unbalanced moisture, contaminants, weak strain, residual fungicides, poor quality grain).

Spawn at peak cell development is best, correlating to about two weeks after inoculation. Grain masters can be kept at room temperature for a maximum of four to eight weeks. Best used within a week of full colonization. Stamets strongly discourages refrigerating grain masters. The rule: use it or lose it.

## Second and Third Generation Spawn (G2, G3)

The next generation of spawn jars is denoted as G2. Each Grain Master can inoculate 5 to 20 times its mass. Many start with narrow-mouth quart mason jars for Grain Masters and use 1/2 gallon or 2 liter jars for Second Generation spawn. Third Generation spawn (G3) is typically in 2.5 gallon jars or bags.

### Standard Inoculation Rates

- **Standard rate**: 1 quart Grain Master to five 1/2 gallon jars (1:10 expansion)
- **Diluted rate**: 1 quart to twenty 1/2 gallons (1:40 expansion)
- Exceeding 1:40 expansion is associated with a greater than 20% failure rate, unacceptable to any spawn laboratory
- Each failed spawn jar is likely to release thousands of contaminants back into the laboratory

### Steps for G2/G3 Production

1. Select a Grain Master showing even, luxuriant growth. Avoid jars having zones of heavy growth, discoloration, or excess liquid.

2. Using a cleaned rubber tire, carefully slam the jar against it to loosen the grain. If overgrown, more forcible shaking is required. Never strike the jar against your palm.

3. Move the grain master upstream to the first jar, hovering inches above it. Remove the lid, tilt the Grain Master downward and rotate so kernels of spawn fall into the awaiting jar. Continue to the next jar.

4. Tighten lids securely. Shake each jar to evenly disperse the Grain Master spawn kernels throughout the sterilized grain. Thorough shaking encourages fast grow-out.

5. Set jars on a shelf at 75F (24C), spaced at least 1/2 inch apart. Closely packed jars self-heat and encourage contamination. Prefer jars incubate at an incline for more transpiration.

6. After 3-4 days, shake each jar again using the spiral technique: grasp firmly, accelerate each jar downwards in a spiral, pulling back at the end to send top grain kernels into the bottom recesses.

7. In 7-10 days, re-inspect for even dispersal. If any unusual pungent odors are noticed or grain appears greasy, contamination may be present although not yet visible.

8. By day 14, all jars should be thoroughly colonized. Each G2 can inoculate another set of 5 gallon jars at a 1:10 expansion. G3 can inoculate 10-20 times its mass in sawdust or straw.

## Spawn Bags

Spawn generated in bags is far easier to use than from jars. Flat, non-gusseted bags are popular for incubating grain spawn. The more grain filled into a bag, the greater the danger of poor gas exchange. Three-dimensional gusseted bags are used primarily for holding supplemented sawdust. Bags contacting hot surfaces become elastic, deform, and fail. The industry uses polypropylene or polymethylpentene bags with and without microporous filters.

## Terminal vs. Propagation Spawn

Most spawn producers only select a portion of the spawn inventory for further propagation. The remainder are designated as "terminal" and are not used for further expansion onto sterilized grain. Although less contaminated, terminal spawn jars are usually of sufficient quality for inoculating bulk substrates. A single petri dish, in three generations of transfers, can create 250 gallons of spawn. A stack of twenty petri dishes can give rise to 5000 gallons.

## Liquid Culture Alternative

An alternative method for generating spawn is via Liquid Culture. This method saves time, money, and is less susceptible to contamination. Liquid inoculation techniques allow a much greater exponent of expansion than traditional grain-to-grain methods.

## Inoculation Rates for Substrate

- Commercial spawn purchase: 3-7% of substrate mass (wet spawn to dry substrate)
- Self-generated spawn: 8-15% rate of moist spawn to dry substrate
- Higher spawning rates accelerate colonization, narrow the window of opportunity for competitor invasion, and boost yields
- One major drawback of high spawning rates is increased thermogenesis
