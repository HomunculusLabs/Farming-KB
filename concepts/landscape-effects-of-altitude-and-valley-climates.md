## Landscape Effects of Altitude and Valley Climates

Climate is shaped not only by latitude and proximity to oceans but also dramatically by altitude, slope, and valley geometry. Understanding these landscape-scale effects is essential for [[continental-climate]] is the third major complication on simple temperature-rainfall classifications (after temperature and [[desert-precipitation-patterns-and-opportunistic-ecology]]). After this, only one special factor remains: the effect of hills or mountain ranges on local climate — effects very like latitudinal effects on a global scale.

## Latitude-Altitude Equivalence

An average measure of temperature fall with altitude:

- **9.8°C/km** (5.4°F/1000 feet) in rainless or dry air
- **4–9°C/km** (2.2–5°F/1000 feet) in humid and saturated conditions

### The 100-Meter Rule

As a rough approximation, every **100 m (330 feet) of altitude is equivalent to 1° of latitude**. This has profound implications for site design:

- At **1000 m** on the equator, temperatures approximate a climate 10° off the equator with the same humidity
- At **10° latitude** off the equator, a plateau at **1850 m (6000 feet)** has a climate more like that at 30° latitude, with a probability of wind chill to below freezing

For high islands or mountain ranges, this altitudinal factor is crucial to [[mollison-farm-design-strategies-for-yield-increase]] homes and gardens. Altitude effect alone enables growing a wide range of plant species on a high island, using the area from ocean to mountaintop.

## High Altitude Effects

Mountains are not strictly "latitude equivalents" because:

- Air is more rarefied
- Air pressure is less
- Radiation is therefore higher

### Challenges Above 4000 m

On very high mountains of 4000 m (13,000 feet) and more, people may experience:

- **Oxygen deficiency** (mountain sickness) — not felt by locals but causing extreme fatigue, insomnia, and laboured breathing in visitors
- **Snow or radiation blindness**
- Extremes of day-night temperature fluctuations

### The Peruvian Andes Example

In the Peruvian Andes, day temperatures remain at 16–19°C (61–66°F) all year, but night temperatures fall rapidly to **-10°C (14°F)**. Shade temperatures are lower than at sea level due to less effective heat transfer and insulation effects of rarefied air. Water boils at lower temperatures due to low atmospheric pressure, and snow may sublime directly to water vapour rather than melting. High mountains reduce the range of foods available.

### Snow Cover Dynamics

Snow cover serves as an insulating blanket that:

- Prevents early spring thawing
- May prevent autumn freezing if it covers unfrozen ground
- Causes intense reflection, raising air temperatures just above snow by day
- At night, radiation from snow causes an **extremely cold ground air layer**, so plants protruding from snow suffer extreme diurnal temperature swings

## Slope and Aspect Effects

As great as the effect of altitude is, the effect of **slope** is even more pronounced. [[daubenmire]] (1974) records that slopes of just **5° towards the poles** reduce soil temperatures as much as **168 km distance** towards the poles. Even a gentle slope away from the sun creates much cooler conditions locally.

The effect of cold ravines in near-permanent shadow is extreme — one may stand in hot sunlight in the Himalayas and gaze into icy depths where only the hardiest life forms exist, and where ice may permanently cover rocks and spray zones caused by waterfalls or rapids.

## Valley Climates

### Hot Valleys

An opposite effect from shaded gorges occurs in **sun-facing wider valleys sheltered from winds**. Here:

- Hot air builds up rapidly
- Soils are drier
- Strong winds are generated: upslope and up-valley by day, downslope and down-valley at night

### Cloud Generation

In large valleys, especially in cool moist climates, the upslope wind may generate a chain of cumulus clouds at the valley head, trailing off as a succession of clouds from mid-morning to evening. In more tropical humid climates, cloud may be continuously held on mountain tops, forming part of the **standing cloud of high islands**.

Such cloud and rainfall effects are accentuated by **forest on valley sides and ridges**, as trees actively humidify air streams by [[transpiration]] in hot weather.

### Valleys as Refugia

Valleys in tundra and desert support tree populations absent from the surrounding plains or peneplains, but for different reasons:

**Tundra valleys**:
- Protected by driven deep snow cover
- Snow preserves warm or sub-lethal soil temperatures in winter
- Provides excess summer melt moisture

**Desert valleys (wadis)**:
- Remain moist due to deep detritus filling their floors
- Shaded soils lose less moisture to evaporation
- Lethal soil temperatures are avoided by partial shading
- Ice-blast and sand-blast are modified or absent
- Unprotected seedlings can survive high winds in valley shelter

Valleys and wadis are therefore **preferred growing sites** in deserts and provide tree products in otherwise treeless tundras, although tundra sites are rarely occupied by human settlement.

### Pre-Dawn Cold Pool

In the field, a sudden coldness is often noticed just before dawn in valley areas. This is the time of greatest depth of cold air and greatest intensity of cold. Air flowing down from mountains has pooled all night, and just before sunrise, people and animals are at their greatest exposure and lowest ebb. At this time, winds off glaciers flowing down cold valleys reach their maximum speed.

### Frost in Clearings and Hollows

Without wind or air flow, **radiation frost** can form in sheltered hollows and tree clearings. Opening up clearings or draining them of cold air may help reduce frost.

### The Clearing Trade-off

When crops such as tomatoes and cucurbits are grown inside open tree canopies, frost can be prevented, but crop may be lost due to:

- Low light levels
- Lack of wind or insect pollination

**Solutions**:
- A shade-side screen of reflector plants facing the sun helps maintain light levels
- Plants to attract bees need to be placed around the clearing
- Arboreal or ground browsers within forests are also a concern in gardens (possum and porcupine, for example)
- Green-leaf vegetables are not usually eaten by browsers and can be successfully grown in small forest clearings or in open forest in frosty areas

## Design Implications

The interaction of altitude, slope aspect, and valley geometry creates an enormous range of microclimates within relatively small geographic areas. Key design principles include:

1. **Use altitude equivalence** to predict what species will thrive at different elevations
2. **Exploit valley microclimates** for frost-sensitive crops in otherwise cold regions
3. **Design cold air drainage** to prevent frost pockets in hollows and clearings
4. **Plant forest on ridges and valley sides** to enhance cloud formation and rainfall
5. **Position structures and gardens** on slopes with appropriate aspect for the climate — poleward slopes for cooling in tropics, equatorward slopes for warmth in cold climates
6. **Account for the diurnal extremes** of mountain climates in all structure and plant selections
