# Mineral Sources for Soils

## Overview

As soils are leached over time, minerals are carried away and become concentrated in the sea, marine organisms, or inland saltpans. Understanding these mineral sources and how to cycle them back into agricultural soils is fundamental to sustainable soil management. Seaweeds, seagrasses, and fish residues have always been part of agricultural fertilisers and have maintained their place even in modern times.

## Marine Sources

### Evaporite Sequences

As seawater evaporates, minerals crystallise out in a predictable sequence based on their solubility:

1. **Calcite and dolomite**: First to separate out. Used for soil conditioning and pH adjustment.
2. **Gypsum and anhydrite**: Next to crystallise. Used to restore soil [[crumb-structure]] and improve infiltration in clay soils.
3. **Rock salts**: Common salt crystallises next. Only wet tropical uplands may actually lack this common nutrient. Even there, specific plants (often aquatic) concentrate salt that can be gathered or leached from their ashes.
4. **Potash, magnesium salts, and trace elements**: These remain in the final liquid after common salt deposition. This residue is sold as **bitterns** — bitter oily fluids rich in bromine and many early periodic table elements, plus rare minerals.

### Bitterns

Bitterns are a concentrated source of minor minerals and trace elements, used in two ways:

- **Soil incorporation**: Diluted and mixed into crop soils.
- **Foliar sprays**: Applied at 1-20 litres per hectare at dilutions of 100-500:1 with water.

Bitterns are very corrosive and must be handled only via non-corrosive vessels and pipes (polyethylene pipes and drums are standard). They are cheap and easily transported to leached areas, but their effects must be established by local trials on specific crops. Because these evaporites are so easily dissolved, they are also the minerals most likely to be carried to sea in rains.

### Guano and Marine Phosphates

Both phosphate (concentrated by seed-eating birds) and potash (from burnt and rotted plants or compost) can be locally produced where birds are plentiful and their manures are used. However, phosphates mined from marine guano may contain concentrated levels of cadmium and uranium, which can be taken up by oceanic fish and shellfish consumed by marine bird colonies. Continual heavy use of such resources is likely to become polluting to soils. The ethical strategy is to use just enough of these resources and to conserve them locally.

## Rock and Rock Dust Sources

### Granite

Granites contain felspars that yield potash or sodium salts. As one of the most common rocks in the earth's crust, granite dust is widely available from quarrying operations.

### Limestone and Dolomite

These rocks yield calcium and magnesium. Dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate) is especially valuable except where magnesium is already in high ratio to calcium in the soil.

### Mineral Deposits and Ores

Mineral deposits and their ores provide traces of basic minor elements. The priority of need is generally:

1. **Calcium**: Most needed in all but highly calcareous areas.
2. **Dolomite**: Next in importance, except where magnesium is already high.
3. **Phosphates and felspars**: Follow in importance.
4. **Trace elements**: Required in small quantities — as low as 5-7 kg per 100 hectares for zinc, copper, cobalt, and molybdenum.

### Field Trial Evidence

Field trials have established that cheap ores, finely ground, are as effective as more refined sulphates or oxides (Leeper, 1982). This is an important finding for sustainable agriculture, as it means local rock sources can replace expensive, energy-intensive refined fertiliser products.

### Animal Mineral Supplementation

Some mineral elements reach plants indirectly through animals. Minerals can be provided to animals as:
- **Salt licks**: Free-choice mineral blocks.
- **Molasses supplements**: Minerals dissolved or suspended in molasses.
- **Water additives**: Dissolved minerals in drinking water.
- **Injections or "bullets"**: Slow-release elements (such as cobalt) in pellets that lodge in the rumen.

Foliar sprays are more rapid-acting and effective than soil application for many mineral deficiencies.

### Rock Dusts in Practice

Fine rock dusts of specific rock types suited to local needs are often cheaply available from quarries or gravel pits:

- **Basalt dusts**: Helpful on leached tropical soils.
- **Rock phosphate**: Contains 8-15% phosphorus but releases nutrients very slowly. May be completely absorbed onto leached clays and clay-loams, requiring super-acid phosphate added to compost for adequate release.

### Cautions

Rock dust as an unselective category can do as much harm as good on soils. It may add excessive or poisonous nutrients in some cases, or excessive micronutrients. Local soil testing and careful matching of rock dust type to soil deficiency is essential.

## Element Distribution in the Soil Profile

Two important determinants control the concentration of elements or nutrients through the soil profile (the vertical column from surface to approximately 2 metres deep):

### Water Penetration

Water acts as a universal solvent, enabling compounds to dissociate into ions and transporting them to various deposits at microsites in the soil. This effect works in three dimensions:

1. **Infiltration**: Water travels by infiltration to varying depths, soaking in from the surface downward.
2. **Capillary rise**: Water rises from the soil water table upward by capillary action, especially during flooding or from shallow water tables.
3. **Lateral movement**: Water moves laterally through the soil profile following slope and permeability gradients.

### Soil Catena

The catena concept describes predictable soil sequences that develop along slopes. As water moves downhill, it creates a characteristic pattern of soil types:

- **Upper slopes**: Thin, non-calcareous, well-drained soils subject to erosion and leaching.
- **Mid-slopes**: Soils deepen and change in acidity/alkalinity as materials are transported.
- **Lower slopes and valleys**: Deep, often poorly drained soils with accumulated fines and soluble materials.

This predictable sequence — erosion and leaching on upper slopes, deposition and concentration on lower slopes — creates a characteristic soil catena that experienced farmers and traditional peoples have long recognised and used for crop placement decisions.
