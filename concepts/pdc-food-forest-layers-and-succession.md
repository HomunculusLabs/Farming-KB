# Food Forest Layers and Succession

updated: 2026-04-18

Food forests are among the most productive and ecologically integrated systems in [[pdc-food-forest-design-and-forest-ecology]]. They apply the vertical dimension and multi-layered structure of natural forests to edible landscape design, combining many [[pdc-permaculture-principles-explained]] into one harmonious food system.

## The Seven Forest Layers

Every forest follows the same basic seven-layer structure. A food forest modeled after nature can have up to seven layers of production:

1. **Canopy (Overstory)** -- The topmost level, composed of large dominant trees that grow best in full sun. In a food forest, these are tall fruit and nut trees such as oaks, hickories, walnuts, or standard-sized fruit trees. They form the protective roof of the system.

2. **Understory** -- Shaded by the overstory, this layer is made up of smaller versions of overstory trees and shade-tolerant species. In food forests, shorter fruit or nut trees like dwarf apples, plums, or persimmons fill this layer.

3. **Shrub Layer** -- Contains woody shrubs such as gooseberry, currants, elderberry, blueberry, coralberry, and highbush cranberry. Berry-producing shrubs are essential food producers in this layer.

4. **Herbaceous Layer** -- The most diverse layer, composed mostly of non-woody species: grasses, sedges, wildflowers, culinary herbs, and perennial vegetables. Plants like comfrey, yarrow, and perennial kale thrive here.

5. **Ground Layer (Forest Floor)** -- Holds the soil builders and stabilizers: fungi, mosses, liverworts, lichens, creeping thyme, and strawberry. This layer protects soil, retains moisture, and drives decomposition.

6. **Vertical/Climbing Layer** -- Vines such as grapes, hops, kiwi, passionflower, and climbing beans grow through all the other layers, exploiting the three-dimensional growing space on trellises, fences, or tree trunks.

7. **Root Layer (Subsurface)** -- Below the soil surface, root crops like carrots, potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, and daikon radish take advantage of growing space underground.

## Forest Succession

All forests follow the same process pattern of evolution and growth, called succession. There are two types:

- **Primary Succession** -- When a forest develops on a blank slate, such as bare rock or volcanic soil.
- **Secondary Succession** -- When a pre-existing forest or ecosystem collapses and the process begins anew.

Succession follows five basic stages:

### 1. Pioneering Phase
Pioneer species such as grasses, ground covers, and sub-surface fungi colonize barren areas. They "work the soil," increasing health enough for larger plants. This stage supports populations of small ground animals, many burrowers, and grazing animals. Pioneer ecological activity builds topsoil, setting the stage for future evolution.

### 2. Shrubs
Ground covers provide nutrient-rich soil, water retention, and reduced soil erosion, enabling shrubs to establish. Shrubs provide habitat for small birds, increase insect activity, and attract diverse ground animals. Their ecological activity -- dropping leaves, contributing urine and feces -- further enriches soil. Vulnerable saplings of dominant tree species take root under the protective shrub umbrella.

### 3. Young Forest
Saplings of dominant tree species break through the shrub layer alongside shorter-lived, shorter-stature trees. These trees cause significant shifts in [[pdc-climate-and-microclimate]], shading out shrubs and ground covers that once protected them. This creates a temporal ecotone where one biome gives way to another.

### 4. Mature Forest
The first generation of large, long-lived trees reaches mature state, forming a canopy that covers the forest. The canopy creates its own ecology supporting birds, arboreal animals, insects, and secondary plant life including vines, mosses, and lichens on bark and branches.

### 5. Climax Forest
Multiple generations of dominant tree types grow side by side. The ground is covered in forest mulch building true topsoil. Many plants now grow in the mulch layer itself. Systems and cycles have stabilized into a multi-layered biome with the dominant trees as keystone species.

## Food Forest Design Principles

A food forest system modeled after a forest would:
- Compose many three-dimensional layers containing various plants, animals, insects, fungi, and microbes in dynamic harmony
- Provide shelter for varied animals
- Be powered by renewable energy (sunlight)
- Maximize space usage through [[pdc-pattern-understanding-and-design-application]] and [[ozier-pdc-plant-guilds-and-polyculture-design]]
- Recycle 100% of its resources
- Produce as much food as possible
- Maintain hydrological and nutrient loops

The concept of food forests connects directly to density and complexity principles. In modern thinking, each food-bearing plant is typically planted in its own area -- trees here, lettuce over there, carrots elsewhere. This takes enormous surface area. By using three-dimensional stacking of food-producing plants, the same food yield can be achieved on far less surface area, leaving the rest wild.

## Related Forest Concepts

- **Agro-Forestry** -- The practice of integrating human-planted crops into existing forests, preserving the forest while harnessing its ecological power.
- **Eco-Forestry** -- Sustainable management of forest systems that allows humans to benefit while helping forests reach and maintain climax state.
- **Urban Forests** -- Planting indigenous trees in urban areas to restore some habitat, species diversity, and ecological health. Urban forests provide cooling, improve air quality, block wind, clean runoff water, and reduce storm water runoff.
- **Wildcrafting** -- Harvesting plants from natural habitats rather than cultivated gardens, always leaving plants able to continue growing and propagating.

## Integration with Other Systems

Food forests integrate closely with [[pdc-water-harvesting-design]] (swales on contour help reduce erosion in food forests), [[pdc-earthworks-and-earth-resources]] (terracing creates garden space on slopes), and [[pdc-zone-analysis-guide]] (food forests are typically zone 2-3 elements). The [[pdc-animal-forage-systems]] within food forests provide pest control, pollination, and fertilizer cycling.

## Guild Design Within Layers

Plant guilds are functional groupings of species that support each other within the food forest. A classic apple tree guild, for example, might include comfrey as a nutrient accumulator beneath the tree, daffodils or alliums as pest deterrents at the base, nitrogen-fixing clover as a ground cover, and climbing beans on a nearby trellis that fix nitrogen for the canopy tree. Guild design considers the specific functions each plant provides: nitrogen fixation, nutrient accumulation, pest deterrence, pollinator attraction, mulch production, and root exudation. By designing guilds rather than individual plantings, food forest managers create self-maintaining polycultures that reduce or eliminate the need for external inputs.

## Accelerating Succession

Permaculture designers can accelerate natural succession to reach productive food forest states faster than would occur through natural processes alone. Techniques include planting fast-growing nurse trees (such as alder, black locust, or tagasaste) that fix nitrogen, provide shade, and create the microclimate conditions needed by slower-growing climax species. The nurse trees are eventually removed or coppiced as the desired canopy species mature. Sheet mulching and composting accelerate the soil-building processes that normally take decades during early succession phases. Intensive initial planting of multiple layers simultaneously, rather than waiting for each layer to establish sequentially, can produce a functional food forest within 5-10 years rather than the 50-100 years natural succession might require.

## Climate Considerations

Food forest design must account for local climate conditions. In tropical regions, food forests can achieve year-round productivity with multiple harvests from each layer, and species diversity is typically higher. Temperate food forests have pronounced seasonal rhythms, with spring ephemerals filling the herbaceous layer before canopy leaf-out, and winter requiring reliance on stored root crops, preserved fruits, and nut harvests. Arid-region food forests depend heavily on deep-rooted trees that access groundwater and create shade microclimates for understory species. Understanding the local frost dates, rainfall patterns, and growing season length is essential for selecting appropriate species for each layer.

## Root Zone Architecture

The root layer concept extends beyond edible root crops to include understanding the three-dimensional root architecture of the entire food forest. Canopy trees typically have deep taproots and extensive lateral root systems. Shrub roots occupy intermediate soil depths. Herbaceous plants concentrate roots in the top 30-60 cm of soil. This vertical root stratification means that well-designed food forests can access nutrients and water from the full soil profile without excessive competition between layers. Mycorrhizal networks connect root systems across species and layers, facilitating nutrient exchange between overstory and understory plants. These fungal networks are a critical but often overlooked component of the root layer, functioning as a biological internet that redistributes resources according to plant needs.

## Economic Yields from Food Forests

A mature food forest can produce yields from every layer simultaneously. The canopy provides nuts, timber, and animal fodder. The understory yields fruit and seeds. The shrub layer produces berries and medicinal plants. The herbaceous layer provides culinary herbs, perennial vegetables, and cutting flowers. The ground layer yields mushrooms and strawberries. The climbing layer produces grapes, kiwifruit, and other vine crops. The root layer provides staple carbohydrates. This multi-layered harvest strategy means that food forests can approach the caloric and nutritional production of conventional agriculture on a per-area basis while requiring far less energy input, no annual planting, and providing ecosystem services including carbon sequestration, water filtration, and wildlife habitat.

## Maintenance and Management

Food forests require less maintenance than conventional gardens but are not zero-maintenance systems. Regular tasks include selective pruning to maintain light access for understory layers, chopping and dropping biomass to accelerate nutrient cycling, managing vigorous species that may outcompete desired plants, and harvesting at appropriate times. The intensity of management decreases as the system matures and ecological self-regulation strengthens. Young food forests require the most attention during the first 3-5 years as the multi-layered structure establishes. Mature food forests may need only occasional intervention to maintain diversity and productivity, such as replacing individual plants that die or removing species that become overly dominant.

## Food Forest Case Studies

Several established food forest projects demonstrate the viability of this approach. Robert Hart's forest garden in Shropshire, England, is often cited as the inspiration for modern temperate food forest design, demonstrating that a multi-layered edible landscape could be productive in a cool climate. Martin Crawford's forest garden at the Agroforestry Research Trust in Devon has documented yields and species performance over decades, providing invaluable data for temperate food forest design. In the tropics, traditional agroforestry systems such as homegardens in Kerala, India, and pekarangan in Java, Indonesia, have maintained multi-layered food production systems for centuries, demonstrating the long-term sustainability of the food forest approach when adapted to local conditions and cultural contexts.

## Climate Considerations

Food forest design must account for local climate conditions including minimum winter temperatures, growing season length, rainfall patterns, and humidity. Tropical and subtropical food forests have the widest species palette and fastest growth rates, with many species producing year-round. Temperate food forests face the challenge of winter dormancy but can still be highly productive by selecting species adapted to cold conditions. Cold-hardy fruits, nuts, and berries form the productive backbone of temperate food forests, supported by nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs that provide fertility. Mediterranean climates present their own challenges with summer drought, requiring selection for drought-tolerant species and design strategies that maximize water capture and retention.

## Species Selection Principles

Selecting species for a food forest involves balancing multiple criteria including climate adaptability, soil tolerance, shade tolerance, pest and disease resistance, nutritional value, yield reliability, and culinary versatility. A useful principle is to select a diversity of species within each functional category rather than relying on one or two varieties. For fruit trees, this might mean planting several apple varieties with different ripening times and disease profiles, plus pears, plums, cherries, and persimmons for resilience. For the shrub layer, combining berry-producing shrubs with nitrogen-fixing species and insectary plants creates a functional guild that supports the entire system. Understory species should be selected for shade tolerance, as light levels decrease significantly under a mature canopy.

## Root Zone Management

Understanding root zone interactions between food forest layers is essential for designing productive systems. Canopy trees typically have deep taproots that access water and nutrients from deep soil layers, while understory shrubs and herbaceous plants have shallower roots concentrated in the top 30-60 cm of soil. This natural root stratification reduces competition between layers, but can be disrupted if inappropriate species are placed together. Nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs are particularly valuable because their roots release nitrogen into the rhizosphere through root exudation and root turnover, making this nitrogen available to neighboring plants. Designing root zone interactions is one of the more advanced aspects of food forest design, requiring knowledge of individual species' root architectures and resource requirements.
