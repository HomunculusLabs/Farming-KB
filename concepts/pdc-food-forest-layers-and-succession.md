A food forest modeled after nature can have up to seven layers of production: 1. **Canopy (Overstory)** -- The topmost level, composed of large dominant trees that grow best in full sun. In a food forest, these are tall fruit and nut trees such as oaks, hickories, walnuts, or standard-sized fruit trees. They form the protective roof of the system. 2. **Understory** -- Shaded by the overstory, this layer is made up of smaller versions of overstory trees and shade-tolerant species. In food forests, shorter fruit or nut trees like dwarf apples, plums, or persimmons fill this layer. 3. **Shrub Layer** -- Contains woody shrubs such as gooseberry, currants, elderberry, blueberry, coralberry, and highbush cranberry. Berry-producing shrubs are essential food producers in this layer. 4. **Herbaceous Layer** -- The most diverse layer, composed mostly of non-woody species: grasses, sedges, wildflowers, culinary herbs, and perennial vegetables. Plants like comfrey, yarrow, and perennial kale thrive here. 5. **Ground Layer (Forest Floor)** -- Holds the soil builders and stabilizers: fungi, mosses, liverworts, lichens, creeping thyme, and strawberry. This layer protects soil, retains moisture, and drives decomposition. 6. **Vertical/Climbing Layer** -- Vines such as grapes, hops, kiwi, passionflower, and climbing beans grow through all the other layers, exploiting the three-dimensional growing space on trellises, fences, or tree trunks. 7. **Root Layer (Subsurface)** -- Below the soil surface, root crops like carrots, potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, and daikon radish take advantage of growing space underground. ## Forest Succession All forests follow the same process pattern of evolution an...
