# Food Forest Layers and Succession

updated: 2026-04-18

Food forests are among the most productive and ecologically integrated systems in [[pdc-permaculture-principles-explained]] into one harmonious food system.

## The Seven Forest Layers

Every forest follows the same basic seven-layer structure. A food forest modeled after nature can have up to seven layers of production:

1. **Canopy (Overstory)** -- The topmost level, composed of large dominant trees that grow best in full sun. In a food forest, these are tall fruit and nut trees such as oaks, hickories, walnuts, or standard-sized fruit trees. They form the protective roof of the system.

2. **Understory** -- Shaded by the overstory, this layer is made up of smaller versions of overstory trees and shade-tolerant species. In food forests, shorter fruit or nut trees like dwarf apples, plums, or persimmons fill this layer.

3. **Shrub Layer** -- Contains woody shrubs such as gooseberry, currants, elderberry, blueberry, coralberry, and highbush cranberry. Berry-producing shrubs are essential food producers in this layer.

4. **Herbaceous Layer** -- The most diverse layer, composed mostly of non-woody species: grasses, sedges, wildflowers, culinary herbs, and perennial vegetables. Plants like comfrey, yarrow, and perennial kale thrive here.

5. **Ground Layer (Forest Floor)** -- Holds the soil builders and stabilizers: fungi, mosses, liverworts, lichens, creeping thyme, and strawberry. This layer protects soil, retains moisture, and drives decomposition.

6. **Vertical/Climbing Layer** -- Vines such as grapes, hops, kiwi, passionflower, and climbing beans grow through all the other layers, exploiting the three-dimensional growing space on trellises, fences, or tree trunks.

7. **Root Layer (Subsurface)** -- Below the soil surface, root crops like carrots, potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, and daikon radish take advantage of growing space underground.

## Forest Succession

All forests follow the same process pattern of evolution and growth, called succession. There are two types:

- **Primary Succession** -- When a forest develops on a blank slate, such as bare rock or volcanic soil.
- **Secondary Succession** -- When a pre-existing forest or ecosystem collapses and the process begins anew.

Succession follows five basic stages:

### 1. Pioneering Phase
Pioneer species such as grasses, ground covers, and sub-surface fungi colonize barren areas. They "work the soil," increasing health enough for larger plants. This stage supports populations of small ground animals, many burrowers, and grazing animals. Pioneer ecological activity builds topsoil, setting the stage for future evolution.

### 2. Shrubs
Ground covers provide nutrient-rich soil, water retention, and reduced soil erosion, enabling shrubs to establish. Shrubs provide habitat for small birds, increase insect activity, and attract diverse ground animals. Their ecological activity -- dropping leaves, contributing urine and feces -- further enriches soil. Vulnerable saplings of dominant tree species take root under the protective shrub umbrella.

### 3. Young Forest
Saplings of dominant tree species break through the shrub layer alongside shorter-lived, shorter-stature trees. ## Practical Considerations

When working with Food Forest Layers and Succession, several practical factors should be
carefully considered to achieve optimal results. These include
the specific conditions of the implementation context, available
resources, timing requirements, and the interactions between this
topic and other elements of the broader system. A holistic view
that considers these interconnections produces better outcomes.

Environmental conditions such as temperature, moisture, and
seasonal patterns significantly influence results. Monitoring these
variables and adapting practices accordingly is essential for success.
The most effective practitioners develop keen observation skills and
respond flexibly to changing conditions rather than following rigid
protocols regardless of circumstances or local variation.

Resource management encompasses not only material inputs but also
knowledge, time, and ongoing attention. Realistic assessment of what
can be sustainably maintained helps prevent overextension and ensures
that implementations remain viable and productive over the long term.

## Common Challenges and Solutions

Several recurring challenges tend to arise in work related to this
topic. These include variability in environmental conditions, the
complexity of multi-variable interactions, and the difficulty of
predicting outcomes with certainty in dynamic systems. Anticipating
these challenges enables more proactive and effective management.

Building resilience into implementations through diversity, redundancy,
and adaptive capacity helps buffer against unpredictable events and
conditions. This approach recognizes that some degree of uncertainty is
inherent in working with natural systems and plans accordingly rather
than assuming perfect predictability or control over outcomes.

Documentation and record-keeping support continuous improvement by
creating a reference base of observations, interventions, and results.
This accumulated knowledge enables progressively better decision-making
and helps identify patterns that might otherwise be overlooked in the
complexity of day-to-day management and observation activities.

## Future Directions

Ongoing developments in research and practice continue to expand our
understanding and improve available approaches. New techniques, tools,
and analytical methods offer opportunities for refinement and innovation
that can enhance both the effectiveness and efficiency of implementation.

Integration with other disciplines and approaches creates synergies that
advance the field as a whole. Cross-pollination of ideas from biology,
ecology, data science, and traditional knowledge systems generates novel
perspectives and solutions that may not emerge within any single domain.

For continued learning, recommended resources include current research
publications, established practitioner networks, hands-on experimentation,
and systematic observation of outcomes across different conditions and
approaches. The combination of study and practice provides the strongest
foundation for developing deep expertise and contributing to the field.
