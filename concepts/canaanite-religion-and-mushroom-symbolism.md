# Canaanite Religion and Mushroom Symbolism

John Allegro's [The Sacred Mushroom and the Cross proposed extensive connections between Canaanite
fertility religion and the [fly-agaric mushroom. His interpretation of Canaanite religious
practices, temple architecture, and divine names formed a significant part of his
argument that Christianity originated in a mushroom-centred fertility cult.

## Allegro's Canaanite Framework

Allegro placed the mushroom cult within the broader framework of Canaanite
religion, arguing that the religious practices of the ancient Canaanites were
fundamentally fertility cults in which the mushroom played the central sacramental
role.

He proposed that the pattern of religious thought traceable through the ancient
Near East, including Canaanite religion, all reached back to "the mushroom cult of
Sumer and her successors." The god names of the Canaanites, when decoded through
his [philological method, were mushroom
epithets.

## The Name of Baal

One of Allegro's most significant claims concerned Baal, the best known of the
Canaanite fertility gods. He derived "Baal" from a Sumerian verb AL, "bore,"
which, combined with a preformative element BA, gave words for "drill" and "penis"
and ultimately the Latin "phallus."

In Semitic, ba'al was not only the divine name but also carried the general
meaning of "lord, husband." Allegro pointed to the prophet Hosea, where Yahweh
says to Israel: "in that day you will call me 'my man' and you will no more call
me 'my baal'" (Hosea 2:16). He interpreted this as evidence that Yahweh and Baal
were originally the same fertility deity.

This identification of Yahweh with Baal was crucial to Allegro's broader argument
that all Near Eastern gods were variants of a single fertility deity, whose cult
centred on the mushroom.

## Temple Architecture as Womb

Allegro interpreted Canaanite temple architecture as a microcosm of the female
reproductive system. He described the temple as divided into three parts:

- **The Porch**: Representing the lower end of the vagina up to the hymen,
  or "Veil."

- **The Hall**: The vagina itself.

- **The Inner Sanctum** (Holy of Holies): The uterus.

The priest, "dressed as a penis" and "anointed with various saps and resins as
representing the divine semen," entered through the doors of the Porch, the
"labia" of the womb, past the Veil or "hymen" and into the Hall. On special
occasions, the priestly phallus penetrated into the uterus where the god himself
dwelt.

Allegro argued that even Christian ritual and architecture owed much to this
ancient tradition, "as the priest heads the processional through the body of the
'womb', to reach its climax before the altar."

## The Semen Theology

Central to Allegro's interpretation of Canaanite religion was what might be called
a "semen theology." The seed of God was supremely holy. Whether it appeared as
rain, as sap or resin of plants, or as human sperm, it was sacred and to waste it
was a grievous sin.

The word "sin" had, in Allegro's reading, the basic meaning of "seed running to
waste." This explained the sin of Onan (coitus interruptus) and of Sodom. The
Catholic strictures on birth control were, Allegro argued, ultimately rooted in
this ancient Canaanite regard for the sanctity of semen.

Similarly, the doctrine of the balance of nature rested upon the cycle of seed,
death, and new life. "Socially, as agriculturally, all life depends upon keeping
the balance between giving and taking, and avoiding extremes."

## The Name "Canaan" Itself

Allegro even interpreted the name "Canaan" through his mushroom lens. Hebrew
Kena'an was, he claimed, to be understood in its fertility context: the "glans"
poised for entry into the Canaanite womb. The geographic designation was thus
itself a mushroom reference.

## The Mount Hermon Connection

Mount Hermon, a significant location in Canaanite and biblical geography, was
identified by Allegro as the "organ of support" in Canaanite religion. The name,
derived from Sumerian roots meaning "burden" and "support," marked it as a sacred
mountain associated with the fertility cult.

This mountain was significant in biblical tradition as the supposed gathering
place of the "sons of God" who descended to mate with the "daughters of men"
(Genesis 6), a passage that Allegro naturally interpreted as a mushroom
reference.

## Scholarly Assessment

Allegro's interpretation of Canaanite religion has been almost universally rejected
by scholars of ancient Near Eastern religion. The standard scholarly understanding
of Canaanite religion is based on the extensive Ugaritic texts discovered at Ras
Shamra in 1929, which provide a detailed picture of Canaanite mythology and
religious practice without any trace of mushroom worship.

The temple architecture Allegro describes is a schematic interpretation that
over-simplifies the considerable variation in ancient Near Eastern sacred
architecture. His etymologies of divine names, particularly Baal, do not accord
with established Semitic philology.

Nevertheless, Allegro's emphasis on the fertility dimensions of Canaanite religion
aligns with mainstream scholarship, even if his specific mushroom interpretation
does not. The sexual and fertility imagery of Canaanite religious texts is well
attested and uncontroversial; it is Allegro's identification of the mushroom as
the central referent of this imagery that scholars reject.
