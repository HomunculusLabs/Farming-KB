# Wickiup and Quick Shelter Construction

Quick shelters built from locally available natural materials are an essential
component of [[permaculture-design]] for settlement and land restoration
projects. In the [[permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo]], Bill Mollison emphasises
that shelter should be constructed from on-site materials wherever possible,
minimising imported resources and energy costs. The wickiup, tipi, debris hut,
and bender are among the simplest and most effective shelter forms requiring
no manufactured inputs.

## Design Philosophy

Permaculture shelter design follows the principle of using biological
resources and locally available materials before resorting to imported or
manufactured goods. Quick shelters serve multiple purposes: emergency
protection during initial site establishment, temporary worker housing during
[[permaculture-earthworks]] and construction phases, demonstration of low-cost building
techniques, and long-term culturally appropriate housing in many climates.

The key criteria for quick shelter are: minimal tool requirements, rapid
construction (hours to days), effective thermal performance, durability
sufficient for the intended use period, and use of renewable or waste
materials found on or near the site.

## Wickiup Construction

The wickiup is a domed or conical shelter traditional to many Indigenous
peoples of North America, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. It is
one of the simplest permanent shelters to construct:

1. **Frame:** Three or more long saplings are set into the ground at an
   angle, their tops tied together to form a tripod. Additional flexible
   poles are leaned against the tripod in a circular pattern, creating a
   dome or cone shape 2 to 4 metres in diameter at the base.

2. **Covering:** The frame is thatched with grass bundles, reeds, bark
   slabs, brush, or woven mats. Thatching material is laid from bottom to
   top so that each layer sheds water onto the layer below. A smoke hole
   is left at the apex if an interior fire is planned.

3. **Insulation:** In cold climates, the exterior is further covered with
   earth, leaves, or pine needles for insulation. The interior may be
   lined with woven mats or animal hides.

Wickiups are well-suited to dry climates where thatch materials are readily
available and rainfall is moderate. They provide excellent wind resistance
due to their aerodynamic shape.

## Tipi (Teepee)

The tipi, associated with Plains Indigenous peoples of North America, is a
conical shelter with remarkable engineering properties. Its steeply angled
conical form sheds wind from any direction, its smoke hole at the apex
creates a draft for interior fires, and its tilted cone geometry allows it
to withstand high winds that would flatten a vertical-sided structure.

Mollison notes the tipi's efficiency as a transportable shelter. The cover
is made from stitched canvas, hides, or woven fabric. The pole framework
uses 12 to 18 long straight poles lashed together at the peak. The tipi
can be erected by one person and provides comfortable shelter in
temperatures ranging from extreme cold to moderate heat.

## Debris Hut

The debris hut is an emergency survival shelter that requires no tools
beyond what can be improvised. It consists of:

1. A ridgepole (a long straight branch) resting on a stump, rock, or
   low branch at about hip height, sloping down to ground level.

2. Ribs (shorter branches) leaned against both sides of the ridgepole
   at steep angles to create an A-frame structure.

3. Massive quantities of dry leaves, pine needles, forest duff, and
   debris piled over the entire frame to a depth of at least 60 cm.

4. An interior bedding of dry leaves or debris for insulation from the
   ground, which is the primary source of heat loss.

The debris hut relies on the insulating properties of dead air space within
deep leaf litter. It can maintain survivable internal temperatures even in
sub-zero conditions, and requires no cordage, tools, or fire. It is
discussed in Mollison's treatment of emergency and survival skills
within the broader permaculture design framework.

## Bender Construction

A bender is a simple shelter made by bending living or freshly cut saplings
into an arch and covering them with tarpaulins, plastic sheeting, or
thatch. Willow, hazel, and other flexible saplings are ideal. The bent
poles are either inserted into the ground at both ends or rooted in place
if live willow is used. Bender shelters can be extended indefinitely by
adding more arches. They are common in protest camps, temporary settlements,
and low-cost rural housing in Britain and Europe.

## Thatching Techniques

Thatching is the primary weatherproofing method for all quick shelters.
Materials include long-stemmed grasses (reed, thatching grass), palm fronds,
sedge, rushes, heather, and bracken. Proper thatching technique involves:

- Starting from the eaves and working upward, each course overlapping the
  one below by at least two-thirds.
- Securing thatch bundles with horizontal sways (rods or cordage) tied to
  the frame.
- Maintaining a minimum pitch of 45 degrees (steeper in wetter climates)
  for adequate water shedding.
- Ensuring even coverage with no gaps at ridges, valleys, or junctions.

A well-thatched roof can last 10 to 60 years depending on materials and
climate. Thatch also provides excellent thermal insulation, keeping
interiors cool in summer and warm in winter.

## Material Selection

Mollison emphasises using materials available on the site. This reduces
transport costs and ensures that the shelter is appropriate to local
conditions. Common natural materials include:

- **Structural:** Bamboo, coppiced poles, roundwood timber, stone, earth
- **Roofing:** Thatching grasses, palm leaves, bark slabs, clay tiles,
  wooden shingles
- **Insulation:** Straw, leaves, wool, moss, earth, animal hides
- **Waterproofing:** Clay slip, lime wash, beeswax, pine pitch
