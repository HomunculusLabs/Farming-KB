# Wickiup and Quick Shelter Construction

Quick shelters built from locally available [[building-with-natural-materials]] are an essential
component of permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo, [[permaculture-earthworks]] and construction phases, demonstration of low-cost building
techniques, and long-term culturally appropriate housing in many climates.

The key criteria for quick shelter are: minimal tool requirements, rapid
construction (hours to days), effective thermal performance, durability
sufficient for the intended use period, and use of renewable or waste
materials found on or near the site.

## Wickiup Construction

The wickiup is a domed or conical shelter traditional to many Indigenous
peoples of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. It is
one of the simplest permanent shelters to construct:

1. **Frame:** Three or more long saplings are set into the ground at an
   angle, their tops tied together to form a tripod. Additional flexible
   poles are leaned against the tripod in a circular pattern, creating a
   dome or cone shape 2 to 4 metres in diameter at the base.

2. **Covering:** The frame is thatched with grass bundles, reeds, bark
   slabs, brush, or woven mats. Thatching material is laid from bottom to
   top so that each layer sheds water onto the layer below. A smoke hole
   is left at the apex if an interior fire is planned.

3. **Insulation:** [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]], the exterior is further covered with
   earth, leaves, or pine needles for insulation. The interior may be
   lined with woven mats or animal hides.

Wickiups are well-suited to dry climates where thatch materials are readily
available and rainfall is moderate. They provide excellent wind resistance
due to their aerodynamic shape.

## Tipi (Teepee)

The tipi, associated with Plains Indigenous peoples [[berry-growing]], is a
conical shelter with remarkable engineering properties. Its steeply angled
conical form sheds wind from any direction, its smoke hole at the apex
creates a draft for interior fires, and its tilted cone geometry allows it
to withstand high winds that would flatten a vertical-sided structure.

Mollison notes the tipi's efficiency as a transportable shelter. The cover
is made from stitched canvas, hides, or woven fabric. The pole framework
uses 12 to 18 long straight poles lashed together at the peak. The tipi
can be erected by one person and provides comfortable shelter in
temperatures ranging from extreme cold to moderate heat.

## Debris Hut

The debris hut is an emergency survival shelter that requires no tools
beyond what can be improvised. It consists of:

1. A ridgepole (a long straight branch) resting on a stump, rock, or
   low branch at about hip height, sloping down to ground level.

2. Ribs (shorter branches) leaned against both sides of the ridgepole
   at steep angles to create an A-frame structure.

3. Massive quantities of dry leaves, pine needles, forest duff, and
   debris piled over the entire frame to a depth of at least 60 cm.

4. An interior bedding of dry leaves or debris for insulation from the
   ground, which is the primary source of heat loss.

The debris hut relies on the insulating properties of dead air space within
deep [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]]. It can maintain survivable internal temperatures even in
sub-zero conditions, and requires no cordage, tools, or fire. It is
discussed in Mollison's treatment of emergency and survival skills
within the broader [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] framework.
