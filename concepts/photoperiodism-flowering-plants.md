# Photoperiodism in Flowering Plants

## Overview

Photoperiodism is the physiological response of plants to the relative lengths
of light and dark periods. As [[daniel-chamovitz]] explains in *What a Plant Knows*,
this capability allows plants to time critical life events — particularly
flowering — to the most favorable seasons. The [[maryland-mammoth-tobacco-and-the-discovery-of-photoperiodism]]
fundamentally changed our understanding of plant physiology, revealing that
plants possess sophisticated biological clocks and light-measuring systems
that rival those of animals. Photoperiodic responses are not limited to
flowering; they also affect tuber formation, bud dormancy, leaf fall, and
other seasonal processes. See also [[phytochrome-photoreceptor-plant-vision]]
for the molecular mechanism that underlies photoperiodic detection.

## The Maryland Mammoth Tobacco Discovery

The story of photoperiodism begins with a serendipitous discovery. In 1906,
a farmer in Maryland noticed an unusual tobacco plant growing near his
greenhouse that was dramatically taller than normal tobacco and refused to
flower as autumn approached. This giant plant, later named "Maryland Mammoth,"
was brought to the attention of scientists at the U.S. Department of
Agriculture. Researchers eventually determined that Maryland Mammoth was
a tropical variety adapted to short days — in Maryland's long summer days,
it simply continued vegetative growth. The breakthrough came in 1918 when
Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, working at the USDA's Arlington
Experimental Farm, published their systematic experiments showing that
Maryland Mammoth flowered only when exposed to day lengths shorter than
approximately 12 hours. This was the first definitive demonstration that
day length controls flowering, and they coined the term "photoperiodism"
to describe the phenomenon.

## Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Garner and Allard's work led to the classification of plants into three
broad categories based on their [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] to day length. Short-day
plants (SDPs) flower when the day length is shorter than a critical threshold.
These include chrysanthemums, soybeans, poinsettias, and cannabis. In their
[[mushroom-natural-habitats]], these plants typically flower in late summer or autumn as
days shorten. Long-day plants (LDPs) flower when the day length exceeds a
critical minimum. Examples include irises, barley, spinach, lettuce, and
many spring-blooming flowers. These plants naturally flower in spring or
early summer as days lengthen. Day-neutral plants, such as tomatoes and
rice, flower regardless of day length. Chamovitz notes that the terminology
can be misleading — as later research would reveal, the critical factor is
not day length but rather night length.

## The Critical Discovery: Plants Measure Night Length

One of the most important insights in photoperiodism research was the
realization that plants measure the duration of darkness, not the duration
of light. This was demonstrated through night interruption experiments.
If a long night (which would normally induce flowering in a short-day plant)
is interrupted by a brief flash of light in the middle, the plant behaves
as if the night were short and does not flower. Conversely, interrupting a
short night with light can induce flowering in a long-day plant. This
demonstrates that the plant is continuously monitoring the length of
uninterrupted darkness, using it as a reliable indicator of seasonal
progression. The duration of the dark period is a more reliable signal
than day length because dawn and dusk times vary with weather and cloud
cover, while the length of darkness is consistent and predictable.

## Red and Far-Red Light Experiments

The photoreceptor responsible for photoperiodic detection is phytochrome,
and the night interruption experiments provided elegant evidence for this.
When researchers used different wavelengths of light for the night
interruption flash, they discovered a striking pattern. A brief flash of
red light (approximately 660 nm) in the middle of a long night was fully
effective at preventing flowering in short-day plants (or promoting
flowering in long-day plants). However, if a far-red light flash
(approximately 730 nm) was given immediately after the red flash, the
effect of the red flash was completely cancelled — the plant flowered as
if no interruption had occurred. This perfectly mirrors the Pr/Pfr
switching mechanism of phytochrome described in [[phytochrome-photoreceptor-plant-vision]].
