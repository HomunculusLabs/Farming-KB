# cannabis disease pest resistance breeding clarke

Disease and pest resistance are among the most important traits for
Cannabis breeders to consider, yet they are often overlooked in favor of
more visible characteristics like potency and yield. [[robert-clarke]] treats
resistance breeding as both a practical necessity and a conservation
imperative in Marijuana Botany, warning that genetic uniformity in
commercial Cannabis crops creates vulnerability to epidemic crop failure.

## The Threat of Genetic Uniformity

Cannabis strains are genetically unique, each containing at least a few
genes not found in other strains. If a strain becomes extinct, those unique
genes are lost forever. Modern commercial agriculture pressures native
strains by replacing them with improved hybrid varieties. Native strains
then hybridize with introduced strains through wind-borne pollen dispersal,
and some genes are squeezed from the gene pool.

When excessive inbreeding of commercial strains produces genetic
weaknesses, new varieties may lack resistance to an unrecognized
environmental threat. A disease could spread rapidly and wipe out entire
fields simultaneously. Many pathogens are known to infect Cannabis, and any
one has the potential to reach epidemic proportions in a genetically
uniform crop.

### The Southern Corn Leaf-Blight Lesson

Clarke draws a parallel to the 1970 Southern corn leaf-blight epidemic
caused by Helminthosporium maydis. A virulent mutant strain appeared in
1969 and by the following season its wind-borne spores had destroyed
approximately 15% of America's corn crop. The badly infected fields all
contained strains descended from a single parent line ("the Texas strain")
that carried hidden vulnerability to the mutant fungus.

If patented hybrid Cannabis strains gain popularity, Clarke warns, the same
situation could arise. Only native gene pools can provide the genetic
material needed to combat future problems.

## Breeding for Disease Resistance

Disease resistance in Cannabis breeding follows the same principles as
breeding for [[cannabis-breeding-traits-selection-criteria-clarke]]. A strain may be bred for resistance to a specific disease through
natural selection under controlled conditions.

### Damping-Off Fungus Resistance

Clarke provides damping-off fungus as a detailed example of disease
resistance breeding. If flats of seedlings are infected by damping-off
disease and nearly all of them die, the surviving few will possess some
degree of resistance. The breeding procedure follows these steps:

1. Infect flats of seedlings with damping-off fungus
2. Most seedlings die; the surviving few carry inheritable resistance
3. Cross the surviving plants to pass resistance to offspring
4. Inoculate flats of seedling offspring with damping-off fungus
5. Select the most resistant individuals for further crossing
6. Repeat over several generations until a pure-breeding resistant strain
   is established

Each generation is tested by inoculation to verify that resistance is
being strengthened. This approach relies on the natural genetic variation
present within the population to provide the raw material for selection.

### Greenhouse Mold Resistance

With the popularity of greenhouse Cannabis cultivation, Clarke identifies
a need for strains with increased resistance to mold. Molds usually
reproduce by wind-borne spores, so negligence can rapidly lead to epidemic
disaster in the confined greenhouse environment. Selection and breeding of
the least infected plants from affected populations should result in
strains with increased resistance over successive generations.

## Breeding for Pest Resistance

Resistance to pest attack works through many of the same mechanisms as
disease resistance. It is common to find stands of Cannabis where one or a
few plants are infested with insects while adjacent plants remain untouched.

### The Role of Cannabinoid and Terpenoid Resins

Cannabinoid and terpenoid resins are most probably responsible for repelling
insect attack in Cannabis. Levels of these defensive compounds vary from
plant to plant, creating natural variation that breeders can exploit.
Cannabis has evolved defenses against insect attack in the form of
resin-secreting [[cannabis-glandular-trichome-types-resin-production-clarke]], which cover the reproductive and associated
vegetative structures of mature plants.

Insects, finding the resin disagreeable, rarely attack mature Cannabis
flowers. However, they may strip the outer leaves of the same plant because
these develop fewer glandular trichomes and protective resins than the
flowers.

### Nonglandular Defenses

Nonglandular cannabinoids and other compounds produced within leaf and stem
tissues may inhibit insect attack. These internal compounds likely account
for the varying resistance of seedlings and vegetative juvenile plants to
pest infestation. Since seedlings lack the fully developed capitate-stalked
trichomes of mature plants, their defense relies more on these tissue-level
compounds.

### Specific Pest Targets for Breeding

Clarke identifies several pest problems that warrant breeding attention,
particularly for greenhouse cultivation:

- Mold resistance (fungal pathogens spread by wind-borne spores)
- Spider mite resistance
- Aphid resistance
- Whitefly resistance

## Integration with Overall Breeding Strategy

Disease and pest resistance should be integrated with selection for other
[[cannabis-breeding-traits-selection-criteria-clarke]] such
as potency, maturation rate, and yield. A currently unrecognized gene may
control resistance to a pest or disease, accessible only by maintaining
broad genetic diversity in breeding populations.

The conservation of diverse
[[cannabis-gene-pool-preservation-acclimatization-clarke]]
is a practical breeding necessity. Breeders working only with narrow
commercial gene pools risk losing access to resistance genes critical when
new pathogens emerge.

## Early Selection for Resistance

Some resistance traits can be selected at the seedling stage. Whorled
phyllotaxy and damping-off resistance may be identified just after
emergence. Early selection for vigor, hardiness, and resistance provides
a head start on breeding and allows more efficient use of growing space.
