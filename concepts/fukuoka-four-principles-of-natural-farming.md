## The Four Principles of Natural Farming

The four principles of natural farming are the foundational precepts established by Japanese farmer and philosopher [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]], which he argued creates dependencies by first upsetting the natural balance and then requiring increasingly complex interventions to correct the resulting problems.

The four principles are described by Fukuoka as complying "with the natural order and lead[ing] to the replenishment of nature"s richness." He considered them "the heart of my method of [[fukuoka-straw-scattering-testing-station-resistance]]. His initial attempt at "natural farming" involved simply abandoning his father"s mandarin orange orchard without any intervention. The result was disastrous: branches became tangled, insects attacked the trees, and nearly two acres of trees withered and died. Fukuoka later acknowledged that this was "abandonment, not natural farming."

Over the following decades, through continuous experimentation — wiping out another 400 citrus trees in the process — he gradually refined his understanding. He ultimately reached the conclusion that "there was no need to plough, no need to apply fertilizer, no need to make compost, no need to use insecticide. When you get right down to it, there are few agricultural practices that are really necessary."

The reason that improved techniques seem necessary, Fukuoka explained, is that the natural balance has been so badly upset beforehand by those same techniques that the land has become dependent on them. He extended this logic beyond agriculture: "Doctors and medicine become necessary when people create a sickly environment. Formal schooling has no intrinsic value, but becomes necessary when humanity creates a condition in which one must become educated just to get along."

He drew a telling analogy: human beings with their tampering do something wrong, leave the damage unrepaired, and when adverse results accumulate, work with all their might to correct them. When the corrective actions appear successful, they view these measures as splendid accomplishments. "It is as if a fool were to stomp on and break the tiles of his roof. Then when it starts to rain and the ceiling begins to rot away, he hastily climbs up to mend the damage, rejoicing that he has accomplished a miraculous solution."

## First Principle: No Cultivation

Fukuoka argued that ploughing and tilling the soil are unnecessary and ultimately harmful. Cultivation breaks down soil structure, destroys earthworms and other soil organisms, and leaves the earth hard and lifeless. Once soil has been cultivated, it must be turned every year, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of dependency on mechanical intervention.

Fukuoka"s fields were among the only ones in Japan that had not been ploughed for over twenty years at the time of writing, and the quality of the soil improved with each season. He estimated that the surface layer, rich in humus, had become enriched to a depth of more than four inches during those years. This improvement resulted largely from returning all organic residues (except the grain itself) to the field surface as mulch rather than tilling them in.

Historical context supports this principle. Fukuoka noted that a thousand years ago, agriculture was practiced in Japan without ploughing. Shallow cultivation was not introduced until the Tokugawa Era (300–400 years ago), and deep ploughing came with Western agriculture. In discussions with Kyoto University Professor Iinuma, Fukuoka predicted that the next generation would return to the non-cultivation method. Although this way of farming disavows modern science, he noted that it had been shown in university laboratories and agricultural testing centres across Japan to be "the most simple, efficient, and up-to-date method of all."
