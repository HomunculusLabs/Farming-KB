## Water Spreading Techniques in Drylands

## Overview

Water spreading is the practice of deliberately intercepting, diverting, and distributing concentrated water flows across broad areas to maximise infiltration into soils. In arid and semi-arid regions, where as little as 12% of rainfall may be absorbed by bare hill country, water spreading transforms flash floods from destructive events into opportunities for soil recharge and plant growth.

## The Problem of Run-off in Drylands

### Fate of Rainwater in Degraded Desert Areas

In thinly-vegetated or overgrazed desert areas, most rainwater runs off over the land rather than soaking in. The consequences are severe:

- Even modest rains create sudden and turbulent flood flow in previously dry streams
- Large boulders are swept down steep-sided wadis
- Enormous quantities of silt, sand, dry leaf and stick material, dry manures, seed, desert snails, and other debris are washed off hill surfaces and carried onto the plains
- Very little soil water is retained

### Salinisation During Run-off

As floodwaters spread over the plains, they increase in salinity, becoming rapidly more saline the further they travel. Headwater rains may reach salt-pans within days or weeks, pooling there for months or years until they evaporate to salt-encrusted flats. In total, little but ephemeral plant growth results.

### The 88% Loss Problem

In Australia, 88% of rainfall runs off or is evaporated from land and water surfaces. Other hot deserts show similar losses, with only 12% of rainfall available to plants. The imperative for water spreading is clear: capture and infiltrate water before it is lost.

## The Fresh Water Principle

If water is to be used productively, flows must be halted and diverted, creating places for water to soak into soils while it is still fresh enough for plant use. Once fresh water is infiltrated, it forms a soil reserve or aquifer that travels very slowly underground toward river beds and salt pans, effectively desalting the shallow water reserves—even though below this fresh aquifer, very salty deep leads continue to flow.

## Water Spreading Structures

### Tree Lines

Trees are the most effective long-term water spreading strategy. They:

- Help recycle water through growth and transpiration
- Keep deeper salty water below root level through continuous extraction
- Provide organic matter that improves soil infiltration capacity
- Create microclimates that reduce evaporation

When forests are cut, burned, and overgrazed, infiltration is effectively reduced and deep saline leads rise to the surface due to flooding and bare-soil evaporation.

### Swales

Swales are dead-level hollows built on contour that create temporary pools and allow water to infiltrate. They are among the most versatile water spreading structures, applicable from domestic garden scale to broadacre landscape rehydration.

### Deep-Ripped Contours

Ripping along contour lines breaks compacted soil layers, allowing water to penetrate to greater depths. This is particularly effective on claypans and hard-setting soils where surface crusting prevents infiltration.

### Silt Dams

Silt dams trap sediment carried by floodwater, gradually building fertile terraces. Over time, these trapped sediments create productive growing media that support vegetation establishment.

### Dams and Retention Basins

Dams, retention banks, and interception banks all reduce or delay flood peaks. Local soil absorption is much more efficient in small streams of high density, while large catchments have greater dampening capacity when retardation areas are preserved.

### Stone Lines and Palisades

Lines of stones or palisades of natural rock placed across slope slow sheet flow and encourage infiltration. They function similarly to windrows on gibber desert, creating micro-catchments where water accumulates and organic matter collects.

## Flash Flood Considerations

### The Flash Flood Factor

Rain in the desert typically falls at approximately 2.5 cm per day for 2–3 days, producing severe local flooding. This flash-flood factor becomes critical when settlements are built in:

- Wadis
- On the upstream side of restricted gaps in hills
- In hollows near dry watercourses

### Design Requirements

To store or use intense, sudden, and infrequent desert rainfall, designers need:

- Oversize channels and drains
- Infiltration systems of adequate capacity
- Diversion structures to route floodwater away from vulnerable areas

## Factors Affecting Run-off

Understanding the determinants of run-off is essential for designing effective water spreading systems.

### Catchment Size

Small catchments discharge greater peak flood flow than large catchments. Foothill areas peak to floods more rapidly than plains.

### Stream Gradient

Steep streams discharge more rapidly than downstream areas of gentle gradient. Headwater streams are usually steeper than larger streams in the plains, which are often sand-clogged with wide flood plains.

### Storm Size

Large storms may totally wet a small catchment, causing local floods, whereas the same storm may have little downstream effect. In deserts, floods commonly disappear a few kilometres from headwaters except during very widespread rains.

### Storage and Retardation

Dams, retention or interception banks, swales, natural swamps, and created swamps all reduce or delay flood peaks. Many natural retardation areas are drained, filled, or built upon, increasing flood risk downstream.

### Catchment Form

Normal dendritic (tree-like) drainage patterns, where branches join at small angles, are subject to faster flooding due to greater streamlining. Fold-mountain or fault-area grid catchments with abrupt turns, constricted valleys, and long runs of middle-order streams experience slower flood propagation.

### Drainage Density

The actual length of stream per square kilometre determines how efficiently water is removed. Very dense streamlines create higher and faster flood peaks. Sheet flow across flat interfluves retards floodwaters, as do braided and broad streams of flat desert areas.
