# Rhizobia-Legume Symbiosis: Establishment and Nodule Formation

## Overview

The symbiotic relationship between bacteria of the genus Rhizobium and members of the family Leguminosae (peas, beans, clovers, soybean) forms an important nitrogen-fixing cooperative. An essential feature is the formation of [[actinorhizal-symbiosis-frankia]] on the roots of the plants.

## Rhizobial Strain Types

Rhizobia exist in two broad categories:
- **Fast growers**: R. leguminosarum, Bacillus radicicola
- **Slow growers**: R. japonicum, R. lupinii

The collective name "rhizobia" encompasses representatives of gram-negative genera Azorhizobium, Bradyrhizobium, Mesorhizobium, Allorhizobium, Rhizobium, and Sinorhizobium. They are grouped in families Rhizobiaceae, Phyllobacteriaceae, and Bradyrhizobiaceae.

## Presymbiosis Stage

A diverse group of rhizobia may inhabit the rhizosphere of a specific legume, but only one or a limited number of species will successfully interact. Each legume species excretes a spectrum of flavonoids, stachydrines, and aldehydic acids into the rhizosphere, creating a unique biochemical environment. This concentration gradient and spectrum of flavonoids attracts appropriate rhizobial species through chemotaxis and/or electrotaxis.

Root exudates also serve as nutrients -- homoserine released by pea plants is the preferred carbon and nitrogen source for R. leguminosarum biovar viciae.

## Rhizobial Attachment and Root Hair Deformation

Recognition factors between host root and Rhizobium involve proteins and sugars:
- **Lectins** (glycoproteins) are present on the host root surface
- **Polysaccharides** are present on the bacterial surface
- Lectins have multiple attachment sites specific to particular polysaccharide types

Four types of polysaccharides participate:
1. Extracellular polysaccharides (EPS)
2. Capsular polysaccharides (CPS)
3. Lipopolysaccharides (LPS)
4. beta-2 glucans

Initial plant responses to rhizobial attachment include:
1. Calcium influx (triggering signal transduction and nodulin gene induction)
2. Potassium/chloride efflux

Lipo-chitin nodulation signals produced by the bacterium initiate root hair curling ("Shepherd's crook").

## Infection Thread Formation

The attachment point on the plant cell wall grows inward (invaginates), forming an infection thread made of plant cell wall material and membrane. Inside the thread, rhizobia divide by gliding motility or connection to reoriented microtubule cytoskeleton. The thread extends from the root hair cell toward underlying cortical cells.

Bacterial cell surface polysaccharides play a crucial role -- mutants defective in exopolysaccharide or lipopolysaccharide synthesis form blocked or incomplete infection threads.

## Nodule Formation and Organogenesis

Root cortical cells begin dividing into a nodule primordium when root hair deformation starts. The infection thread eventually reaches the developing nodule and delivers rhizobia via endocytosis. Each rhizobial cell becomes enclosed in a host plant cell membrane forming a **symbiosome** -- a quasi-organelle specialized for nitrogen fixation. The rhizobial cell differentiates into a bacteroid capable of nitrogen fixation.

Vascular tissue extends into the nodule for nutrient exchange between partners.

## Nitrogen Fixation Mechanism

Once inside the symbiosome, the bacteroid expresses the nitrogenase enzyme complex, which catalyzes the reduction of atmospheric N2 to ammonia (NH3). This reaction requires:
- A supply of ATP generated by bacteroid respiration
- Carbon substrates (primarily malate and succinate) provided by the plant
- An anaerobic environment maintained by leghemoglobin, which binds oxygen with extremely high affinity

Leghemoglobin is produced by the plant and gives active nodules their characteristic pink color. By regulating free oxygen concentration, leghemoglobin protects the oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase while still allowing aerobic respiration. The ammonia produced by nitrogenase is assimilated by the plant into amino acids via the GS-GOGAT pathway.

## Agricultural Importance

The rhizobia-legume symbiosis is the single most important biological nitrogen fixation system in agriculture. Legume crops including soybean, clover, alfalfa, peas, and beans fix an estimated 40-80 million metric tons of nitrogen annually worldwide. This reduces dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, which are energy-intensive to produce and contribute to water pollution through runoff. In sustainable farming systems, legumes are often rotated with grain crops to maintain soil nitrogen levels.
