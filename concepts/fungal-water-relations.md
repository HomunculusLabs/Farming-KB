# Fungal Water Relations and Moisture Physiology

Water relations are fundamental to fungal physiology and ecology, governing growth rates, enzyme activity, spore germination, survival, and distribution across all ecosystems. Fungi occupy a unique position among organisms in their relationship with water, as their filamentous growth form and ability to regulate internal water potential allow them to function across a wider range of moisture conditions than many other microorganisms. Understanding fungal water relations is essential for predicting fungal responses to drought, flooding, and climate change.

## Water Potential and Fungal Growth

Fungal growth is governed by water potential, which includes both matric potential (binding of water to surfaces) and osmotic potential (solute concentration). Most fungi grow optimally at water potentials between -0.1 and -5 MPa, though some species can grow at much lower potentials. Fungi inhabiting canopies of tropical forests have adapted to desiccating environments by producing copious mucilage and growing at low moisture potentials of -4 to -8 MPa (Hedger et al., 1993). These canopy-dwelling fungi, particularly species of Marasmius and Marasmiellus, use rhizomorphs to trap and decompose leaf litter held in the canopy, functioning despite the rapidly fluctuating moisture conditions of the aerial environment.

The ability of fungi to regulate their internal osmotic potential allows them to survive in environments with extreme water stress. Marine fungi, for example, have adapted to the osmotic stress of saline environments through physiological mechanisms that regulate their osmotic potential (Jennings, 1983; Clipson and Jennings, 1992). Fungi even exist in the hypersaline conditions of the Dead Sea at total salt concentrations of 340 g/L, where 55 species have been reported (Kis-Papo et al., 2001).

## Lichen Water Relations

Lichens represent one of the most striking examples of fungal adaptation to extreme water conditions. Lichens are poikilohydric, meaning their water content equilibrates with the surrounding environment rather than being internally regulated. They can survive periods of complete desiccation and rapidly resume metabolic activity upon rehydration. During desiccation, lichens enter a state of suspended animation that affords them protection, and are able to respond rapidly to short-term pulses of moisture.

The water relations of lichens in arctic and alpine environments are particularly noteworthy. Photosynthesis and water relations in lichens at high latitudes are constrained by the timing and duration of moisture availability. Lichens are often the primary producers in these extreme environments, where their ability to tolerate desiccation and function during brief periods of moisture availability gives them a competitive advantage over vascular plants.

## Mycorrhizae and Plant Water Relations

Mycorrhizal fungi significantly influence plant water relations, particularly under drought conditions. Arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization has been shown to increase water relations of plants by enhancing root hydraulic conductivity, exploring larger soil volumes for water, and producing the soil glomalin-related protein that improves soil water-holding capacity. The decrease in leaf water potential was less in arbuscular mycorrhizal plants during drought compared to non-mycorrhizal plants, indicating improved drought tolerance conferred by the mycorrhizal symbiosis.

The effects of mycorrhizae on plant water relations are influenced by the mycorrhizal dependency of the host plant and the severity of water stress. Under mild drought conditions, mycorrhizal plants typically show improved water status relative to non-mycorrhizal plants. Under severe drought, however, the carbon cost of maintaining the mycorrhizal symbiosis may exceed the benefits, and mycorrhizal effects on water relations may become neutral or even negative.

## Moisture and Decomposition

Soil moisture is a primary regulator of fungal decomposition activity. Fungal biomass and decomposition rates are positively correlated with soil moisture up to an optimum, beyond which waterlogging restricts gas exchange and reduces fungal activity. In salt marsh sediments, aerobic conditions quickly give way to anaerobic conditions with increasing depth, and fungi cede to the abilities of bacteria to derive energy from chemoautotrophic processes in the anaerobic zones. Mansfield and Barlocher (1993) found that fungal biomass, measured as ergosterol content, was negatively related to redox potential in Spartina salt marshes, showing rapid decline of fungal activity with increasing sediment depth.

Moisture pulses following dry periods can stimulate bursts of fungal activity, a phenomenon particularly important in arid and semi-arid ecosystems. Fungi capable of tolerating low moisture levels, particularly in the form of lichens, are able to more readily respond to rapid and short-term pulses of moisture than other organisms. This rapid response capability makes fungi critical for nutrient cycling during brief periods of favorable moisture conditions in dry environments.

## Fungal Adaptations to Moisture Stress

Fungi have evolved multiple strategies to cope with moisture stress. Melanin production in hyphal cell walls reduces water loss and provides protection against UV radiation. The formation of resistant structures such as sclerotia and chlamydospores allows fungi to survive extended dry periods. Some fungi produce extracellular polysaccharides that enhance water retention around hyphae. Mycelial cords and rhizomorphs, which have specialized water-conducting tissues, can transport water from moist to dry areas of the mycelial network, extending the effective range of fungal activity into drier microsites.

## Moisture and Fungal Community Dynamics

Moisture availability structures fungal communities at multiple scales. At the landscape scale, precipitation gradients determine the distribution of fungal functional groups and the balance between fungal and bacterial decomposition pathways. At the microhabitat scale, moisture heterogeneity creates niche differentiation among fungal species with different moisture requirements and tolerances. Seasonal patterns of moisture availability drive seasonal dynamics in fungal community composition and activity, with many fungal species showing pronounced seasonal fruiting patterns linked to moisture conditions.

## Osmotic Regulation and Compatible Solutes

Fungi regulate their internal water potential through the accumulation of compatible solutes—organic compounds that do not interfere with normal metabolic function even at high concentrations. Common fungal compatible solutes include glycerol, erythritol, arabitol, mannitol, and trehalose. Glycerol is particularly important as the primary compatible solute in many fungi, with intracellular concentrations increasing dramatically under osmotic stress. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the HOG (High Osmolarity Glycerol) MAP kinase pathway orchestrates the osmotic stress response, activating glycerol biosynthesis and regulating membrane transporters to maintain turgor pressure.

Xerotolerant and xerophilic fungi, such as species of [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] and penicillium entity, can grow at water activities below 0.80, a capability that makes them important spoilers of dried foods and stored grains. These fungi have evolved enhanced glycerol production capacity, more efficient membrane transporters, and cell wall modifications that reduce water loss. The ability to grow at low water potential is correlated with the production of secondary metabolites, including mycotoxins like ochratoxin A by Aspergillus ochraceus, which is produced optimally under water-stressed conditions.

## Moisture Dynamics in Decomposition

The rate of fungal decomposition is strongly influenced by moisture availability, with optimal decomposition occurring near field capacity (approximately -0.01 to -0.03 MPa). Below this threshold, decomposition rates decline as enzyme diffusion becomes limited and fungal metabolic activity decreases. In terrestrial ecosystems, the relationship between moisture and decomposition follows a bell-shaped curve, with decomposition also declining under waterlogged conditions due to oxygen limitation. This pattern has important implications for understanding the effects of changing precipitation patterns on carbon cycling in forests and grasslands.

Fungal foraging strategies are intimately linked to water relations. Species producing rhizomorphs and cords, such as [[gourmet-mushroom-species-guide]] species and many woodland basidiomycetes, can translocate water over considerable distances, effectively connecting moist microsites within otherwise dry substrates. This hydraulic redistribution capacity allows cord-forming fungi to maintain decomposition activity in heterogeneous moisture environments and may play a significant role in maintaining soil moisture heterogeneity.

## Drought and Fungal Community Responses

Prolonged drought selectively pressures fungal communities, favoring species with drought-tolerant traits such as melanized cell walls, efficient osmotic regulation, and the ability to enter dormant states. Melanin, found in the cell walls of many dematiaceous (dark-pigmented) fungi, provides protection against UV radiation and desiccation while also influencing water repellency of hyphae. Drought-tolerant [[fungal-interactions-with-microbes]] in soil include species of [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]], penicillium entity, and various dematiaceous hyphomycetes that can survive extended dry periods as spores or melanized sclerotia.

Climate change projections suggest increased frequency and intensity of drought events in many regions, which will likely shift fungal community composition toward more drought-tolerant assemblages. This shift may have cascading effects on decomposition rates, nutrient cycling, and plant-fungal interactions, as drought-tolerant species often differ in their enzymatic capabilities and substrate preferences from moisture-sensitive species.

## Ecological Significance

Fungal water relations govern the distribution and activity of fungi across all terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. The ability of fungi to function across wide ranges of water potential, from fully saturated soils to desiccating aerial surfaces, underpins their ecological dominance as decomposers. Water availability determines whether decomposition proceeds through fungal or bacterial pathways, with cascading effects on carbon storage, nutrient cycling, and food web structure.

The hydraulic redistribution capacity of cord-forming fungi, which can transport water from moist to dry areas of their mycelial networks, represents an underappreciated mechanism for maintaining soil moisture heterogeneity. This water translocation may be particularly important in arid and semi-arid ecosystems, where fungal hydraulic connections between moist and dry soil microsites could extend the period of biological activity following rain events and influence the spatial distribution of decomposition and nutrient cycling.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Plant Water Relations

Mycorrhizal fungi profoundly influence plant water status beyond their direct effects on nutrient uptake. Extraradical hyphae of both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal fungi extend into soil microsites that roots cannot access, effectively increasing the absorptive surface area for water uptake. In drought conditions, mycorrhizal plants consistently show higher leaf water potential, stomatal conductance, and photosynthetic rates compared to non-mycorrhizal plants. This drought mitigation effect operates through several mechanisms: the extensive hyphal network accesses water from smaller soil pores, hydraulic lift moves water from deep moist soil to surface layers, and improved phosphorus nutrition enhances root hydraulic conductivity. The [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] association can reduce plant water stress by 20-40 percent under moderate drought conditions.

## Fungal Hydraulic Redistribution

Some fungi, particularly ectomycorrhizal species associated with forest trees, participate in hydraulic redistribution — the passive movement of water through roots or fungal hyphae from moist to dry soil zones. During the day, water uptake by plant roots creates tension that can pull water through mycorrhizal hyphae connected to roots in drier soil patches. At night, when transpiration ceases, water moves from deep moist soil layers to drier surface soil through the same pathway, benefiting both the fungus and neighboring plants. This hydraulic redistribution can supply 10-30 percent of the daily transpirational water demand of forest trees during dry periods.

## Research Methods

Fungal water relations are studied through gravimetric water potential measurements, psychrometric techniques for assessing osmotic potential, and isothermal calorimetry for measuring metabolic heat production under controlled moisture conditions. Ergosterol-based fungal biomass measurements combined with soil moisture monitoring reveal moisture-biomass relationships in situ. Stable isotope techniques using deuterated water (2H2O) allow tracing of water movement through mycelial networks, providing direct evidence of hydraulic redistribution.
