# Fossil Fuel Pulse and Energy Descent Cycle Updated: 2026-04-18 Source: David Holmgren, Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability (Principle 12: Creatively Use and Respond to Change) ## The Large-Scale Pulse of Fossil Fuels The burning of fossil fuels is analogous to the rapid combustion of a forest after a long period of accumulation. In geological terms, the fossil fuels represent hundreds of millions of years of accumulated solar energy captured by plants and stored in the earth. Over the last two centuries, humanity has been burning through this stored energy at an accelerating rate. In 1996, 425 quadrillion Btu (451 exajoules) of energy were consumed globally. In that same year, humanity used up a stock of 4000 quadrillion Btu (4220 exajoules) of ancient sunlight. For every unit of solar energy captured and used, humanity consumed nine units of ancient sunlight. This is the ratio that is used to explain why global civilisation is so powerful, and why it is so vulnerable. The concept of "energy slaves" -- the number of human labour equivalents represented by our energy consumption -- provides a useful way of understanding the scale of our dependence. Each Australian has the equivalent of about 100 energy slaves, each American about 200. This means that the average American has the energy equivalent of 200 full-time labourers working for them continuously. ## The Nature of the Pulse This large-scale pulse of fossil fuel energy has several characteristics that distinguish it from natural pulses: - The pulse is being driven by a single species (humanity) rather than by natural ecological processes - The pulse is consuming stored capital (fossil fuels) rather than cycling through current productivity - The pulse is global in scope, affecting every ecosystem on the planet - The pulse is accelerating rather than decelerating, as each new source of energy enables the exploitation of additional sources - The pulse is creating waste products (greenhouse gases, toxic pollutants) that are accumulating in the global commons ## Energy Descent Energy descent is Holmgren's term for the period following the peak of fossil fuel production, during which available energy per capita will decline. This decline is not optional; it is a consequence of the finite nature of fossil fuel resources and the thermodynamic reality that energy sources of declining quality require more energy to extract. Energy descent is not the same as "running out of oil." It is a process of gradual contraction in the energy available to power human civilisation. The rate of descent will depend on many factors, including the rate of discovery of new energy sources, the efficiency with which energy is used, and the ability of societies to adapt to declining energy availability. Holmgren identified several possible pathways through [energy descent: - The "techno-fantasy" pathway assumes that new energy technologies (solar, wind, nuclear fusion) will allow energy consumption to continue growing indefinitely - The "green technology" pathway assumes that energy efficiency and renewable energy can maintain current levels of energy services while reducing environmental impact - The "energy descent" pathway assumes that energy consumption per capita will decline, requiring fundamental changes in the way society is organised ## The Pulsing Patterns of Life on Earth The history of life on earth includes vast periods of tens of millions of years when all the earth's land masses were worn down, shallow seas covered most of the earth's surface, and the climate was almost uniformly mild and moderately wet. Biodiversity was low and ecosystems changed little over vast areas and millions of years. This stability has been punctuated by shorter periods of great change. The last 1.6 million years, known as the Quaternary, has been characterised by tectonic uplift and volcanism, global and montane ice caps, more extensive continents with vastly varied climates, ice ages and warmer interglacials, massive extinctions and prolific speciation. Much of the wondrous biological and geographic diversity of the earth exists only in these periods of great change. Within these ages of great geologic, climatic and biotic change, there are small-scale patterns of stability, followed by brief pulses of activity. Ice ages lasting hundreds of thousands of years represent the stable norm of the Quaternary, with interglacials lasting roughly ten thousand years representing the explosive pulse of life. Within this current benign and remarkably stable interglacial climate, humanity has developed agriculture, civilisation, and finally global industrial culture. All of them have accelerated the pace and power of human cultural and environmental change, but all are dependent on the fragile stability of the interglacial paradise. ## Hindu Cultural Cycle The ancient Hindu cosmology gives us a breathtaking perspective on the cyclical nature of time and proportional pulsing balance between change and stability. A 60,000-year cultural cycle is dominated by a long matriarchy phase composed of three great enduring ages; it collapses in a vortex of chaos before the birth of the patriarchy phase, the Kali Yuga. The Kali Yuga (or Age of Conflict) lasts only 6000 years before another vortex of change leads to the next cycle. By the precise reckoning of Hindu numerologists, there were over 440 years of chaotic change remaining within the Kali Yuga at the time of Holmgren's writing (early 2000s). Without granting any particular credence to this or any other predictions, it is reasonable to conclude that life for our children, grandchildren and their descendants is hardly likely to be characterised either by a steady state of illusory continuous growth or by a quiet, predictable, sustainable culture. ## Preparing for Uncertainty We must prepare our descendants for uncertainty and rapid change, while instilling in them a living appreciation of the enduring rhythms of nature along with the value of long-term thinking, persistent ethical behaviour, and the importance of the simple, ordinary, even mundane, aspects of life. Permaculture is a dynamic interplay between two phases: on the one hand, sustaining life within the cycle of the seasons, and on the other, conceptual abstraction and emotional intensity of creativity and design. The relationship between these two is like the pulsing relationship between stability and change. It is the steady, cyclical and humble engagement with nature that provides the sustenance for the spark of insight and integration, which in turn informs and transforms the practice. ## The Four Phase Model Applied to Energy Descent Applying the four-phase model of ecosystem change (Conservation, Release, Reorganisation, Exploitation) to the global energy system: - Conservation: the long period of fossil fuel accumulation (hundreds of millions of years) - Release: the rapid burning of fossil fuels over the last two centuries - Reorganisation: the period we are now entering, where new energy sources and social arrangements must emerge - Exploitation: the establishment of new systems based on current solar income rather than fossil capital The reorganisation phase is the most uncertain and dangerous. It is characterised by: - Open niches in the economy as old industries decline - Soluble energy and resources available from the declining fossil fuel system - Potential for both positive and negative pathways - High risk of ecological and social collapse - Opportunity for creative innovation and cultural renewal ## Related
