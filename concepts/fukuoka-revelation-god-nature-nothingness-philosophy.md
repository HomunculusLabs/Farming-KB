## The Moment of Revelation

The [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophical-foundation]] of [[fukuoka-natural-farming]] rests on a single
transformative experience that occurred when he was twenty-five years old. Working
as a plant pathologist at a research institute, he collapsed in the orchard one
night and experienced what he described as a revelation. In that moment, he suddenly
understood that human knowledge was essentially worthless, that all the scientific
research, agricultural techniques, and intellectual understanding he had accumulated
amounted to nothing when compared to the wisdom inherent in nature itself.

This revelation was not an intellectual insight but an experiential one, a direct
perception of reality that bypassed the conceptual mind entirely. Fukuoka described
it as seeing the world as it actually is, without the filter of human judgment,
analysis, or desire. In that state of pure perception, he saw that nature was
already perfect, that plants grew, insects lived, and ecosystems functioned in
complete harmony without any need for human management. All human problems, he
realized, arose from the mistaken belief that nature needed to be improved.

From this experience, Fukuoka derived a philosophy that was simultaneously radical
and ancient. It was radical in its rejection of virtually every assumption
underlying modern civilization: the assumption that knowledge is power, that
technology improves life, that human beings can and should control their
environment. It was ancient in its resonance with Taoist, Buddhist, and indigenous
traditions that recognized the fundamental wisdom of allowing nature to unfold
without interference.

## The Concept of Mu (Nothingness)

Central to [[fukuoka-philosophy-eating]] is the concept of Mu, the Japanese term for
nothingness or emptiness that also carries connotations of non-differentiation, non-
interference, and the absence of artificial boundaries. Mu does not mean nihilism or
the absence of existence; rather, it describes a state of consciousness in which the
distinctions between self and other, subject and object, human and nature dissolve
into a unified field of being. In this state, there is no separation between the
observer and the observed, no gap between human intention and natural process.

In practical terms, Mu manifests as non-action or Wu Wei in the Taoist tradition. It
does not mean doing nothing in the sense of lethargy or apathy, but rather acting
without ego, without the desire to control outcomes, without imposing human will
upon natural processes. The farmer who practices Mu does not decide what should
grow, how it should grow, or what should be eliminated. Instead, the farmer observes
what nature is already doing and cooperates with it, making only the minimal
interventions necessary to harvest food.

Fukuoka insisted that Mu was not a philosophical abstraction but a practical reality
that could be directly experienced. His rice paddies and orchards were living
demonstrations of Mu in action: complex, productive ecosystems that arose not from
human design but from the farmer willingness to step back and allow natural
processes to operate. The more the farmer abandoned the desire to control, the more
productive and harmonious the farm became. This paradox, that doing less produces
more, was the core teaching of Fukuoka natural [[cho-natural-farming-philosophy]].

## God, Nature, and Human Separation

Fukuoka understanding of God was deeply influenced by his native Shinto tradition,
in which divinity is not a remote being but an immanent presence pervading all of
nature. God, in Fukuoka view, was not separate from the natural world but was the
natural world itself, expressing through every tree, every insect, every gust of
wind. The human error was to conceptualize God as something apart from nature,
something to be worshipped in temples or sought through prayer, when in fact God was
directly present in every moment of natural experience.

The separation of God from nature, in Fukuoka analysis, was the original sin of
human civilization, the fundamental mistake from which all other mistakes flowed.
Once people began to see God as separate from nature, they began to see themselves
as separate from nature as well. This dualism, the split between spirit and matter,
human and natural, sacred and profane, created the conditions for exploitation,
domination, and ecological destruction. Science, technology, and agriculture as
practiced in the modern world were all expressions of this fundamental separation.

Fukuoka did not advocate a return to animism or nature worship in the conventional
sense. His point was more subtle: that the perceived separation between human beings
and nature was itself an illusion, a product of the conceptual mind that divided the
indivisible reality into categories. The path back to nature was not a physical
journey but a perceptual one, a shift in consciousness that revealed the unity that
had always been present. Natural farming was both the method and the expression of
this shift.

## Nature as Teacher and Healer

For Fukuoka, nature was the ultimate teacher, and the failure of modern education
was its substitution of human knowledge for natural wisdom. Children, he believed,
were born with an innate connection to nature that was systematically destroyed by
an education system that taught them to think conceptually rather than perceive
directly. The result was generations of people who could analyze ecosystems but
could not feel them, who could describe the life cycle of a plant but could not
sense its vitality.

Healing, in the Fukuoka framework, meant restoring this lost connection. Eating
natural food was one form of healing, because it reconnected the body to the energy
of living soil and unmodified plants. Working in a natural farm was another, because
it required the practitioner to observe nature closely and respond to its cues
rather than imposing preconceived plans. Simply spending time in wild nature was
perhaps the most direct form of healing, as it allowed the conceptual mind to quiet
down and the natural perception to reawaken.

Fukuoka was critical of alternative medicine and spiritual practices that, while
well-intentioned, still operated within the framework of human knowledge and
control. Herbal medicine, acupuncture, meditation, and other [[shamanic-healing-modalities-extraction-soul-retrieval-dismemberment]]
could be beneficial, but only to the extent that they facilitated a return to
natural wholeness rather than substituting one form of human intervention for
another. The truest healing was the healing that happened spontaneously when the
conditions of natural life were restored, when people ate natural food, breathed
clean air, drank pure water, and lived in harmony with the rhythms of nature.

## The Road Back to Nature

The title of Fukuoka seminal work, "The Road Back to Nature," encapsulates his
entire philosophical project. The road was not a path to be followed in the
conventional sense but a process of unlearning, of shedding the accumulated layers
of human knowledge and desire that obscured the natural reality. It was a road that
led not forward to some technological utopia but backward to a state of being that
human beings had once inhabited naturally and effortlessly.

Fukuoka was under no illusions about the difficulty of this journey. Modern
civilization was built entirely on the premise of separation from nature, and to
question that premise was to question the foundations of everything that modern
people considered progress, prosperity, and success. Yet he maintained that the road
back to nature was the only road that led to a viable future, that the path of
increasing technological control and environmental destruction was a dead end that
would ultimately destroy both human civilization and the natural world that
sustained it.

In his later years, Fukuoka became increasingly focused on spreading his message
beyond agriculture to encompass the broader human relationship with nature. His
desert [[fukuoka-desert-reclamation-projects]] in Africa, his visits to America and Europe, and his
countless writings and lectures were all expressions of a single conviction: that
human beings could either return to a harmonious relationship with nature or face
the consequences of their continued separation. The choice, he believed, was
ultimately simple, even if the path to making that choice was the most difficult
journey a person could undertake.
