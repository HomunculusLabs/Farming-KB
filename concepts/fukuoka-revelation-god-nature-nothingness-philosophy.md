## The Moment of Revelation

The [[fukuoka-natural-farming]] rests on a single
transformative experience that occurred when he was twenty-five years old. Working
as a plant pathologist at a research institute, he collapsed in the orchard one
night and experienced what he described as a revelation. In that moment, he suddenly
understood that human knowledge was essentially worthless, that all the scientific
research, agricultural techniques, and intellectual understanding he had accumulated
amounted to nothing when compared to the wisdom inherent in nature itself.

This revelation was not an intellectual insight but an experiential one, a direct
perception of reality that bypassed the conceptual mind entirely. Fukuoka described
it as seeing the world as it actually is, without the filter of human judgment,
analysis, or desire. In that state of pure perception, he saw that nature was
already perfect, that plants grew, insects lived, and ecosystems functioned in
complete harmony without any need for human management. All human problems, he
realized, arose from the mistaken belief that nature needed to be improved.

From this experience, Fukuoka derived a philosophy that was simultaneously radical
and ancient. It was radical in its rejection of virtually every assumption
underlying modern civilization: the assumption that knowledge is power, that
technology improves life, that human beings can and should control their
environment. It was ancient in its resonance with Taoist, Buddhist, and indigenous
traditions that recognized the fundamental wisdom of allowing nature to unfold
without interference.

## The Concept of Mu (Nothingness)

Central to [[fukuoka-natural-alfalfa-black-wattle-ground-cover]] [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]], was not separate from the natural world but was the
natural world itself, expressing through every tree, every insect, every gust of
wind. The human error was to conceptualize God as something apart from nature,
something to be worshipped in temples or sought through prayer, when in fact God was
directly present in every moment of natural experience.

The separation of God from nature, in Fukuoka analysis, was the original sin of
human civilization, the fundamental mistake from which all other mistakes flowed.
Once people began to see God as separate from nature, they began to see themselves
as separate from nature as well. This dualism, the split between spirit and matter,
human and natural, sacred and profane, created the conditions for exploitation,
domination, and ecological destruction. Science, technology, and agriculture as
practiced in the modern world were all expressions of this fundamental separation.

Fukuoka did not advocate a return to animism or nature worship in the conventional
sense. His point was more subtle: that the perceived separation between human beings
and nature was itself an illusion, a product of the conceptual mind that divided the
indivisible reality into categories. The path back to nature was not a physical
journey but a perceptual one, a shift in consciousness that revealed the unity that
had always been present. [[dom]]
