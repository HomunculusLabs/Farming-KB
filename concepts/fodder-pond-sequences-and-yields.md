# Fodder Pond Sequences and Yields

[[fodder-pond-sequences]] represent one of the most elegant and productive designs in [[permaculture]] aquaculture. By arranging a series of interconnected ponds so that water flows from one to the next—each progressively hosting species higher on the trophic ladder—a self-regulating polyculture system is created where waste from one pond becomes food for the next. This cascading design dramatically reduces or eliminates the need for external feed inputs while producing multiple harvestable products at each level.

## The Sequential Pond Concept

The fundamental principle is that as water flows downstream through a series of ponds, upstream migration is prevented by pipes or screens while downstream migration is possible or even aided. Each pond in the sequence hosts species appropriate to its position in the food chain, creating a natural trophic cascade.

### Origin Pond (Pond A)

The first pond in the sequence receives the initial water input and hosts the lowest trophic level organisms:

**Animals:**
- Manurial species such as ducks (adding nitrogen-rich manure to stimulate plankton)
- Freshwater mussels
- Amphipods and phreatocids (mud shrimp)
- Small freshwater crabs (*Halicarcinus*)
- Snails
- Shrimp (*Atya*, *Macrobrachium*)
- Frog larvae (*Hyla*, *Rana*, *Crinea*)

**Plants:**
- Non-flowering or non-invasive species such as taro
- Manurial species such as [[azolla]] (nitrogen-fixing aquatic fern)
- Insects and their larvae (inevitably present)

**Structures:**
- Rotted logs, reed beds, and brush for habitat complexity
- Small cover elements for refuge

**Edges:**
- Comfrey, vining legumes, and fruit trees

### Second Pond (Pond B)

The next pond downstream receives organisms that migrate or flow from Pond A, plus additional more predatory species:

**Animals:**
- Any organisms from Pond A
- More predatory invertebrates
- Very small fish working at planktonic level (e.g., minnows, *Paragalaxids*)
- Mud-dwelling species (*Saxilaga*)

**Plants:**
- Useful edible species such as kangkong (water spinach) planted on mounds

**Structures:**
- Reed beds, small pipes, and logs

**Edges:**
- Mulberry, berry fruits, legumes, and comfrey

### Subsequent Ponds (C and D)

Ponds C and D follow similar sequences to B, with progressively larger and more predatory species. Products or yields can be harvested at any level: shrimp, snail, ducks, frogs, taro, Tilapia, perch, or trout. Even pH can be altered between ponds to accommodate different species, and niches arranged for specialized plant or animal groups so that both high-oxygen and low-oxygen-demand species are accommodated.

## Flow Design Options

Ponds arranged along **spoon or V-drains** enable multiple origins and destinations, with forage fish or invertebrates always migrating downstream. A **perched pond** above the trout or predator system can maintain a trout-free forage-fish polyculture while still providing a downstream food source.

These systems can accept water polluted by phosphates and nitrates as part of their intake, provided plants and organisms can be found to cope with that pollutant level—effectively using the pond sequence as a biological water treatment system.

## Strategies for Increasing Water Diversity

Three strategies increase species diversity in small still-ponds, marshes, or perched ponds:

1. **Headwater and ridge location**: Creating small ecological islands isolated from main waterways, which develop unique species assemblages
2. **Salting small ponds**: Developing semi-estuarine species assemblages that add brackish-water organisms to the system
3. **Manuring small ponds**: Applying trace elements, animal manures, and phosphatic or nitrogenous fertilizers to produce large quantities of forage fish, algae, or crustaceans for feeding downstream predators

## Shallow Water Proportions

Swingle (1966) proposes that up to **50% of a catfish pond** can be in shallows. These shallows provide food for the main fish (primarily as shrimp habitat), not at a cost of reducing fish numbers, but at a gain in the energy needed to feed them. The shallows produce the forage base that sustains the deeper-water fish populations.

## Integrated Pond-Agriculture Systems

### Garden-Pond Symbiosis

Fish such as Tilapia and carp are commonly fed on starchy foods from adjacent crops—pumpkin, banana, sweet potato, yam, taro, beans and bean flours, grains, and doughs. They also eagerly eat fallen insects, seed, and fruit from fringing vegetation, along with selected water weeds. This relationship means **market gardens and fish ponds belong together**.

Tilapia in particular eat many fruits and edible leaves from garden weeds and vines. Fish-pond water is also of good nutrient value for gardens, so the relationship is mutually beneficial: **crop production from well-fertilized ponds may be as much as twice that from reservoir irrigation** alone.

### Grass Carp Integration

Grass carp neatly trim fringing vegetation such as *Dolichos* species and prevent encroachment of weeds into taro. They mix well with *Macrobrachium* prawns and **actually increase prawn yields** without artificial feeding. Prawn yields on grass carp fecal detritus match those on chicken pellets in nearby monoculture ponds.

Barry Costa-Pierce's polyculture trials in Hawaii demonstrated that ponds with grass carp and prawns had neatly-trimmed edges (bitten by fish, not mowers) while maintaining excellent prawn growth. Even if prawn growth is somewhat reduced, the overall system efficiency is superior when pond margins are planted with comfrey, clover, *Dolichos*, *Tradescantia*, and lucerne for fodder—**creating conditions for yield at the pond rather than importing energy from elsewhere at great cost**.

## Orders of Yield

The productivity of aquaculture systems spans an enormous range depending on the level of management intensity:

| Yield (kg/ha/year protein) | Conditions |
|---|---|
| 10–50 | Deep, cold waters with few shallows; rocks around lakes |
| 30–60 | Deep artificial reservoirs |
| 60–90 | Artificial reservoirs modified for fish and forage culture |
| 80–150 | Natural shallow lakes, unfertilized; coastal lagoons of low pH |
| 200–500 | Basic standing crop in fertile but unfed fish ponds (bass, trout, bluegill); unfertilized brackish lagoon cultures of milkfish; modified rice paddy carp |
| 500–1,000 | Fertilized extensive carp, milkfish, and mullet cultures; intensive prawn culture; upper range of totally natural systems |
| 1,000–2,000 | Well-chosen, unfed polycultures achieving these yields |
| 2,000–5,000 | Intensively fertilized and fed pond polycultures of selected species; water quality monitored; food is 60+% of total costs |
| 5,000–20,000 | Intensive cage and corf cultures or small aerated ponds; fertilizer and food now 96% of costs; waste products may limit production; disease control critical |

### Key Observations

- **Natural systems** can yield 500–1,000 kg/ha/year at their upper range
- **Well-designed polycultures** without feeding can match or exceed fertilized monocultures
- **Intensive systems** multiply yields 10–40× but at the cost of massive external inputs (96% of total costs are feed and fertilizer)
- **Disease control** becomes the critical limiting factor in intensive systems
- **Waste product accumulation** (nitrates, phosphates) can limit production and requires land-based cycling

The implication for [[permaculture-design]] is clear: well-designed extensive polycultures with integrated food production at the pond edge can achieve yields in the 1,000–5,000 kg/ha/year range with minimal external inputs, representing the optimal balance of productivity and sustainability.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Design pond sequences** with trophic cascades—lowest organisms upstream, predators downstream
2. **Integrate ponds with agriculture** for mutual benefit—pond water fertilizes crops, crop waste feeds fish
3. **Maximize shallow areas** (up to 50%) for forage production rather than prioritizing deep water
4. **Harvest at multiple levels**—ducks, snails, shrimp, taro, and various fish species from a single system
5. **Use polyculture over monoculture**—grass carp increase prawn yields while managing vegetation
6. **Target the 1,000–5,000 kg/ha/year range** through design rather than expensive intensive inputs
7. **Accept nutrient-polluted water** where appropriate species can be found to treat it biologically
