# Thinning and Spacing

Thinning is the gradual removal of excess seedlings to give the remaining plants adequate room to develop. It is one of the most important — and most resisted — practices in vegetable gardening. Many gardeners sow too densely and then fail to thin adequately, resulting in stunted, unproductive plants. Understanding the biology behind competition and the practical techniques for thinning transforms garden productivity.

## Why Thinning Is Necessary

### Nature's Way vs. the Gardener's Way

In nature, a single plant may disperse ten thousand seeds for one to survive and breed. Seeds often germinate in clusters where a single pod or fruit fell. Each seedling in the cluster competes for water, nutrients, and light. The most vigorous one dominates the space as the rest slowly die. Nature's method is wasteful, slow, and cruel. Your method — gradual thinning — is quick and merciful by comparison.

### The Contract

When you sowed those seeds, you undertook to maintain the terms of a contractual agreement we humans made with that species long ago: it agreed to become our vegetable and we agreed to free it from having to compete. If you don't hold down your end of the bargain, the vegetables won't be able to do their best.

### What Happens Without Thinning

- **Crowded radishes** will not bulb at all
- **Crowded carrots** mostly develop tops
- **Densely packed bush beans** set small, and frequently irregular pods that take a long time to pick
- **Crowded tomatoes** and **cucumbers** stop setting much fruit
- **All crowded plants** are more susceptible to disease, produce lower yields, and may bolt prematurely

## Overseeding as Insurance

Some seedlings are always going to die. If you sow one seed to get one plant, prepare to be disappointed — you will soon be going to the garden center and purchasing seedlings. Better to sow three or five (or sometimes even more) seeds for every plant you want. Then when nature kills off a few, you can consider it helping with your task of thinning.

### General Rule

The bigger the seed, the higher the germination percentage and the smaller the number of extra seeds you need. The smaller the seed and the lower the germination rate, the more extra seeds you must sow.

- **Large seeds** (beans, peas, squash, corn, cucumbers): sow two to three per station
- **Small seeds** (lettuce, carrots, basil, celery): sow five to ten per station
- **Very tiny or expensive seed** (celery, kohlrabi): use the thumbprint method

## Progressive Thinning

### Three Steps Over Three to Five Weeks

Gradual thinning helps you avoid ending up with gaps in the row if bad weather slows early growth, if germination is low, or if seedlings are lost to predation.

**Step 1 — After emergence (first week):**
A few obviously dead seedlings may need removal. Weaker seedlings may thin themselves through damping-off diseases and insects. Let this natural selection proceed.

**Step 2 — First true leaf stage (second week):**
Help nature along so seedlings stand about one inch (2.5 cm) apart. For big seeds, thin to at least 1.5 inches (4 cm) apart.

**Step 3 — Open-pollinated variety evaluation (two to three weeks after emergence):**
With open-pollinated varieties, the most vigorous individuals will stand out. Remove the weakest seedlings to give the stronger ones more room. **Watch for super-vigorous rogues** — a cabbage-kale cross or a zucchini-pumpkin cross may outgrow everything but will produce something certainly disappointing. Pull these out.

**Final thinning — Three true leaves:**
When seedlings are established with three true leaves and growing well, they are largely immune to sudden loss from insect or disease. Thin to the desired final spacing.

## The Thumbprint Method

For small seeds that will grow into large plants (cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, Chinese cabbage, celery, celeriac, and kale):

1. Spread complete organic fertilizer or chicken manure compost and dig the bed
2. Let a few days pass for the soil to resettle and capillary connections to restore
3. Place manure/compost on the surface and rake level
4. With your thumb, press a small indentation slightly over half an inch (1.25 cm) deep
5. Carefully count four or five seeds into the depression and cover with loose soil
6. Use a fingertip to trace a 6-inch-diameter (15 cm) circle in the soil, with the seeds in the center
7. Place stations on whatever final spacing you want (e.g., 24 × 24 inches / 60 × 60 cm)
8. For tiny seeds like celery (low germination), make the depression shallower and put in up to ten seeds per spot, covering with compost or aged manure instead of soil

### Celery Exception

For celery and other tiny, expensive seeds, cover with a thin mulch of fine compost or aged manure that holds more moisture than soil.

## Saving on Seed Purchases

### The Economics of Bulk Seed

When you allow for seeds that don't sprout and for thinning, you will want to end up with more plants than you initially thought. Buying bulk seed rather than small packets can save enormous amounts of money.

Example: Johnny's Selected Seeds sells a mini-packet of kohlrabi (about 45 seeds) for $2.85. A thousand seeds of the same variety costs $7.60. If you purchased 1,000 seeds in mini-packets, you'd need eight or nine of them, costing $23.75 instead of $7.60.

### When Bulk Buying Makes Sense

You need fair certainty about two things:
1. You are going to grow that variety for more than one year
2. The seed isn't going to die before you use most of it up

Even if you only use half of 1,000 seeds, it is still cheaper than purchasing three mini-packets containing only 360 seeds.

### Seed Storage Is Key

The whole trick to making bulk purchases economical is understanding how to preserve germination — see [[seed-storage-and-preservation]].

## Seedlings Should Never Compete

Seedlings should never be allowed to strongly compete with each other for light, water, and nutrients. Competition from the very first days of growth permanently stunts plants. The stress of early crowding cannot be recovered from later, even if you eventually thin to proper spacing.
