## Food as a Strategic Weapon — Fukuoka's Critique

Masanobu Fukuoka identified a fundamental contradiction at the heart of modern
industrial agriculture: food produced with petroleum inputs becomes a tool of
geopolitical power rather than a source of nourishment. His observations during
travels in the United States led him to conclude that America's agricultural
surplus was less a sign of abundance than evidence of systemic dysfunction.

## Food as Geopolitical Leverage

Fukuoka observed that the United States used its agricultural surplus as a
strategic weapon to influence other nations. The Carter administration pressured
Japan to buy American oranges and wheat, creating dependency.

When Japan proposed shipping its surplus rice to Vietnam, the U.S. State
Department protested vigorously. American grain sales to [[fukuoka-american-agricultural-decline]] export power.

Fukuoka found this use of food morally repugnant. Food should nourish people,
not serve as an instrument of coercion between governments.

## The Poverty of the American Farmer

Despite America's vast agricultural output, Fukuoka concluded that the American
farmer was poor. Farmers growing fruit for export in California sold melons for
one dollar each at roadside stands while complaining about making ends meet.

In Japan, the same melons sold for 1,500 yen apiece. The price differential
did not enrich the farmer. It enriched the distribution chain and the export
infrastructure.

Fukuoka visited the United Nations in New York and told an American official
that America was not an affluent nation at all. The food tasted bad, the soil
was impoverished, and the country bought foreign oil to produce food it then
shipped abroad as a weapon.

The real reason American farmers exported crops was not surplus abundance but
domestic poverty. They could not survive selling only to their local
communities at fair prices.
