## The Missionary Campaign

The Spanish response was swift and merciless. Following the standard colonial
playbook, indigenous practices deemed idolatrous were targeted for eradication.
The clergy recognised that mushroom ceremonies were deeply embedded in the
spiritual and social fabric of indigenous communities, and that eliminating them
was essential to the project of conversion.

The most vivid account of the Inquisition's activities comes from the bullish
cleric Jacinto de la Serna, who contrived to eradicate persistent "idolatrous"
practices by writing a guidebook for ministers, his Manual de Ministros de
Indios para el Conocimiento de sus Idolatrias y Extirpacion de Ellas. In a
section devoted to local witchcraft, he described how an Indian named Juan
Chichiten ("little dog"), a "great maestro of superstitions," performed a
mushroom ceremony in Serna's own village.

On investigating, Serna discovered that the mushrooms were customarily picked
after a night of prayer and superstitious entreaties, gathered once the dawn
breezes had subsided, and that whether eaten or drunk, they intoxicated the
locals, deprived them of their senses, and produced visions. Serna was
horrified, recognising that the practice represented a competing spiritual
system that threatened the hegemony of the Catholic Church.

## The Disappearance of the Historical Record

Moving forward in time to the period of Spanish rule, the historical and
material evidence for mushroom use all but disappears after the seventeenth
century. The Spanish chronicles cluster together in the sixteenth century, with
only one further account from the seventeenth. This silence does not necessarily
mean that mushroom use ceased entirely — merely that it was driven so deeply
underground that it escaped the attention of colonial record-keepers.

The suppression was effective enough that by the time Western ethnographers and
mycologists began seriously investigating Mexican mushroom practices in the
twentieth century, the surviving traditions were confined to a few remote

## Material Evidence

Throughout Central America, approximately three hundred stone and pottery
mushroom effigies have been uncovered, originating mainly in the highlands of
Guatemala with a few found in the southernmost Mexican states. These sculpted
figures, about a foot high and free-standing, depict mushroom shapes with a stem
and domed cap, often with carved human or animal figures at the base. The
earliest date from the pre-classic period, roughly the first millennium BCE,
suggesting the practice extended back thousands of years before the Spanish
conquest.

The Codex Vindobonensis (Vienna Codex), a Mixtec work depicting mythological
origins, shows several gods including the feathered serpent Quetzalcoatl
clutching mushrooms. The Lienzo de Zacatepec shows a man with mushrooms in his
hair. These codices, combined with the mushroom stones, provide compelling
archaeological evidence of a mushroom-using culture that predates the colonial
encounter by millennia.

## Theological Arguments Against Mushroom Use

The Catholic Church's condemnation of mushroom use was rooted in a sophisticated
theological framework that went beyond simple cultural chauvinism. Central to
the Church's position was the doctrine of transubstantiation — the belief that
during the Eucharist, bread and wine were literally transformed into the body
and blood of Christ. The indigenous claim that teonanacatl was "God's flesh"
constituted a direct theological challenge to this sacramental monopoly. Where
the Church held that divine communion required priestly mediation and occurred
through specifically sanctioned elements, the mushroom ceremonies offered
direct, unmediated access to the sacred without any need for ordained clergy.
This was not merely a difference of practice but a fundamental threat to the
ecclesiastical power structure.

Spanish theologians classified mushroom ceremonies under the broad category of
idolatry (idolatria), which encompassed any practice that attributed divine
power to created things rather than to the Christian God. The concept of
superstition (supersticio) was similarly deployed — the Church drew a careful
distinction between legitimate religious practice and superstition, defining the
latter as attributing power to natural objects or forces independently of God.
Mushroom use was further condemned as a form of witchcraft (brujeria) and
necromancy, particularly because of the visions participants reported. The
Church held that any supernatural vision not explicitly sanctioned by Christian
theology must be diabolical in origin — a position codified in the Malleus
Maleficarum (1487) and reinforced throughout the colonial period.

The mendicant orders — particularly the Franciscans, Dominicans, and
Augustinians — who led the evangelisation campaign in New Spain developed an
elaborate demonological framework for understanding indigenous practices. They
argued that the visions experienced during mushroom ceremonies were not genuine
spiritual experiences but rather illusions created by the Devil to deceive the
indigenous peoples and maintain his hold over them. The physical effects of the
mushrooms — the laughter, the tears, the altered perceptions — were interpreted
as evidence of demonic possession rather than as pharmacological phenomena. This
framework allowed the Inquisition to treat mushroom use not as a harmless
cultural practice but as a spiritual crime requiring the most severe remedial
measures, including public confession, whipping, and forced participation in
Catholic rites.

The concept of "extirpation of idolatry" (extirpacion de idolatrias) became an
institutionalised practice in colonial Mexico, with dedicated ecclesiastical
courts established to investigate and punish indigenous spiritual practices.

Holy Office of the Inquisition. The extirpation campaigns were systematic:
clergy compiled detailed manuals describing indigenous ceremonies so that local
priests could recognise and destroy them. Indigenous informants were recruited
