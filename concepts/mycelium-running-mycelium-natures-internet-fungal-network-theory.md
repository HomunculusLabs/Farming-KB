## Evolutionary Antiquity of Fungal Networks

Fungi share a common ancestor with animals from approximately 600 million years ago,
placing them closer to the animal kingdom than to plants on the tree of life.
Molecular phylogenetics confirms fungi and animals are sister kingdoms within the
supergroup Opisthokonta, sharing features such as chitinous cell structures and
heterotrophic nutrition.

However, fungi colonized land far earlier than animals — evidence suggests fungal
organisms existed on terrestrial surfaces for over a billion years, making them among
the oldest colonizers of dry land. This deep history means mycelial networks have been
organizing terrestrial ecosystems far longer than any plant root system, animal
nervous system, or human technology. Stamets contends that the fundamental logic of
networked biological organization was pioneered by fungi and later inherited by animal
nervous systems. The architecture of the human brain and the internet both
recapitulate [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]], which accumulate in the mycelial mat
and contribute to the mineral content of developing soils.

Through this mechanism, mycelium is not merely a decomposer but a primary agent of
soil creation — literally eating rock and excreting the building blocks of future
ecosystems. The process is continuous and self-reinforcing: as mycelium breaks down
rock and creates soil, it enables plant colonization, which produces organic matter
that feeds further fungal growth, accelerating more rock decomposition. This positive
feedback loop has been running for over a billion years.
