# Garden Sizing and Land Requirements

Determining the right amount of land for a productive food garden is one of the most important decisions a gardener can make. Steve Solomon, in *Gardening When It Counts*, provides detailed guidance based on historical precedents, personal experience, and practical analysis of what it takes to meaningfully contribute to a household's food supply.

## Historical Precedents

### British Wartime Allotments

During World War II, the United Kingdom required every local council to make allotment garden plots available to any resident who requested one. By law, each plot had to be at least 300 square yards (2,700 square feet, approximately 250 square meters), provided for a token rental. People would spend time on their allotments after work, and Sunday afternoons became gratifying social events. Many plots sprouted tiny lockable shacks made of recycled materials where gardeners kept tools and an old chair for resting while chatting with neighbors.

During hard times, having 2,700 square feet of vegetable garden made all the difference between health and sickness, between having enough to eat and low-grade hunger. Britain's cool, frequently cloudy summers mean vegetables grow more slowly than in the United States or Canada, but the mild English winters allow gardening of frost-hardy crops year-round. During wartime, the British were allowed to keep chickens and rabbits, and vegetables including potatoes made up about one-third of the family's total caloric intake.

### Cuban Urban Gardens

After the fall of the Soviet Union in 1991, Cuba faced especially hard times. Previously they had barely survived by raising sugar for export and importing food and fuel. The Cubans responded by breaking up huge cooperative sugar farms and converting them to individual holdings. City people were freely granted garden blocks at the edges of towns and cities, each block being a third of an acre. By the year 2000, half of the produce consumed in Havana was grown in urban gardens, and urban gardens produced 60 percent of the vegetables consumed in all of Cuba.

Each garden area produces two crops each year. All of this produce is organically grown, making Cubans leaders in developing holistic non-petroleum-based horticulture. Neighborhood gardens and community horticultural groups not only produce food for their members but also donate produce to schools, clinics, and senior centers, still having enough excess to sell at neighborhood markets at 30 to 50 percent below farmers' market prices.

## Practical Garden Size Estimates

Solomon's own garden uses about 2,000 square feet (185 square meters) of actual growing area, which feeds a family of two. Vegetables make up about half the daily calories, with paths and surrounds adding to the total. Adding the 2,700 square feet of garden area plus additional space currently at rest brings the total to about 4,500 square feet (420 square meters). A typical small-town building block is about a half acre (21,000 square feet or 1,950 square meters), which has plenty of room for a significant garden in the backyard.

### Factors Affecting Garden Output

How much food can be produced depends on many variables:
- How deep and what type the soil is
- How skilled the gardener is
- How much water is available, either from rainfall or irrigation
- How long the growing season is
- What sorts of vegetables will be attempted
- Whether varieties are well-suited to the climate and soil
- Quality of seeds
- Latitude (which determines duration and strength of the growing season)
- Number of frost-free days

### For Full Family Food Supply

If the goal is to produce not half but nearly all calories and nutrition, the standard potato is essential. For nearly complete family diet based on nutritious potatoes or sweet potatoes, approximately 2,700 square feet (250 square meters) of growing area is needed per adult in the family. If living in a rainier climate, another 500 square feet (45 square meters) is needed. Where there is no irrigation, roughly 4,000 square feet (370 square meters) per person is required.

Growing cereal grains would require five to ten times as much land per person to produce the same amount of nutrition as root crops, coles, and salad greens. The healthful potato is the most efficient calorie crop for home gardeners.

## The Ley System and Soil Rotation

### The Problem with Continuous Cropping

If the soil does not freeze solid in winter to a depth of at least 18 inches for at least a few continuous months, it is probably not possible to grow a vegetable garden on the same land for more than three to five years before serious troubles arise with diseases and/or soil-dwelling insects. Many people in mild climates have grown the family garden in the same place for more than a generation and think everything is fine, but they have forgotten that some kinds of vegetables that were once easy to produce have become impossible, and output has declined considerably.

### How a Ley Works

A ley is a period of rest where garden beds are put down to grass and clover. The grass is mowed a few times each summer and all the clippings are left in place to decompose. Over three to five years, a ley rebuilds the soil's content of organic matter and restores the biological process to a stable, healthy balance.

Every four or five years in Solomon's garden, most vegetable beds are put to rest in grass, and the grass beds are turned over and begin growing vegetables. For the first two years after breaking the sod, vegetables on the new ground grow noticeably better than the ones on the old beds. By about the fourth year, the appearance of disease and slower overall growth signals that it is time to rotate again.

### Total Land Requirement with Ley Rotation

Because of ley rotation, the actual garden footprint doubles. Solomon's case went from 2,000 square feet of growing beds to over 4,000 square feet (370 square meters). Adding paths, currant bushes, four small fruit trees, asparagus, and perennial herbs around the fringes, the total enclosed area is about 733 square yards (6,500 square feet or 600 square meters) inside a wildlife-proof fence.

## Community Gardens in North America

Community gardens have not yet caught on in North America to the extent they did in Britain. The ones supported in Eugene, Oregon during the 1980s and early 1990s provided plots that were small for a serious gardener. Many plots went unclaimed — a serious gardener could have rented three or four or ten and had a decent garden, but no one did. This reflects how people think in fat years, when the urgency to grow food is not yet felt.

## Calculating Garden Area Requirements

Food self-sufficiency requires approximately 4000 square feet (370
square meters) per person for a mixed vegetable diet, though this
varies significantly by climate, soil quality, and growing methods.

## Intensive Gardening Methods

Biointensive methods can reduce land requirements by 60-80%
compared to conventional row gardening. Raised beds, square foot
gardening, and vertical growing maximize yield per unit area.

## Climate and Regional Considerations

Growing season length, frost dates, and regional precipitation
patterns directly affect how much land is needed. Warm climates
with long seasons can produce more food per square foot than
short-season northern locations.
