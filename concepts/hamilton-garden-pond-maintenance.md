# Garden Pond Maintenance Throughout the Year

updated: 2026-04-18

The organic garden will be more attractive to wildlife if it contains a pond, because it provides drinking water for birds and small mammals, and a home for many [[query-how-to-get-rid-of-slugs-and-snails-organically]]. A pond will also enable you to grow a much wider range of plants in your garden.

## Siting the Pond

Ideally, the pond should form a feature in the garden, rather than being tucked away out of view. Choose a site that receives plenty of sunlight for at least part of the day. Most aquatic plants prefer sunny conditions and fish prefer warmer water, although they do not like very sudden changes in temperature.

Site the pond as far from deciduous trees as possible. If leaves fall into the pond they will turn the water sour as they rot anaerobically. Trees will also shade the pond. If you have small children, make sure you site the pond where it can easily be seen from the house, and consider safety fencing.

## Pond Construction

### Liner Options

- **Fiberglass shells**: Simple but limit shape and size
- **Plastic liners**: Inexpensive but eventually disintegrate
- **Rubber liners**: Most durable and flexible; allows custom shapes

### Calculating Liner Size

Measure the maximum length and width of the pond. Add to these measurements twice the maximum depth. This gives the minimum size of liner needed.

### Protecting the Liner

Line the hole with a thick layer of soft material (old carpet, sand, or newspaper) to protect the liner from damage by sharp rocks in the soil.

### Pond Zones

- **Marsh garden**: Damp soil at the edge for moisture-loving plants
- **Shallow area**: 2 inches (5cm) deep for plants with roots barely covered
- **Marginal shelf**: 3-5 inches (7-12cm) deep, wide enough for containers of marginal plants; make very shallow in places as a refuge for fish
- **Deep water area**: At least 18 inches (45cm) deep to accommodate most water lilies

### Planting Containers

Use heavy garden soil or rotted turf for aquatic plants. Do not use soil rich in [[sweet-flag-acorus-calamus-ethnobotany]]), flowering rush (Butomus umbellatus), bog arum (Calla palustris), marsh marigold (Caltha palustris), iris, arrowhead (Saggitaria japonica), water forget-me-not (Myosotis palustris), and primula (Primula sp.).

## Maintenance Through the Year

### Algae Control

Soon after filling, the water may turn pea-green from rapid algae growth. Pond water contains minerals and receives plenty of sunlight, so algae flourish. Do NOT empty and replace the water -- this gives algae a fresh supply of mineral salts and causes them to flourish again. Leave the water alone; the mineral supply is soon depleted, and as water plants grow larger they exclude light. Deprived of food, the algae die, and the water clears naturally.

### Blanket Weed

Remove slimy green blanket weed as soon as you see it. This ugly weed floats on top and chokes out other vegetation. Regular removal prevents further infestation.

### Leaf Management

Keep leaves out of the pond, or they will turn the water sour as they rot. Try to avoid building near deciduous trees; if this is impossible, cover the surface with a piece of netting in the fall.

### Thinning Plants

Thin out oxygenating plants occasionally by pulling some out by hand. Return any animal life on the plants to the pond and throw the plants on the compost heap.

### Fish Care

If you keep ornamental fish, include a very shallow area (no more than 2 inches/5cm deep) as a refuge. Fish eat their own eggs and fry, plus any frog spawn. In the shallow water, the smallest fish gather (it is warmer there) and you can guide frog spawn into this safe area. Include rocks in the marsh garden as hibernation sites for frogs.
