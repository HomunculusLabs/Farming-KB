# Spreader Banks and Interceptor Drains

Spreader banks and interceptor drains are complementary earthworks used in permaculture and broadacre land management to control water movement across landscapes. While spreader banks distribute water evenly for irrigation and erosion prevention, interceptor drains capture and redirect overland flow to prevent waterlogging and salinisation. Together they form critical components of integrated water harvesting and land rehabilitation systems.

## Spill Gates and Flags

Spill gates control water release from drains and dams. Two main types exist:

- **Fixed gates**: Concrete slide holders and aprons, permanently installed
- **Moveable gates ("flags")**: Plastic sheets weighted with a chain sewn into the foot, supported across a drain by a light pipe sleeved into the top

Flags can be placed and removed at any point along a drain, enabling one or two people to water 200–240 hectares (400–600 acres) in a morning. They also serve as effective **wildfire control systems** in forested areas. Sophisticated installations can be remotely operated by radio signal with storage batteries or buried electrical conduits powering small motors or hydraulic slides. Complete wildfire control is achievable through dams and sheet irrigation using infrared sensors and automatic spill-gates.

## Diversion Drains

Unlike swales, diversion drains run from streams to dams or collect overland flow and convey it to storages. They form part of any rainwater harvesting system. A simple plastic sheet with one end supported by channel banks and the other weighted by a chain forms a temporary dam, causing the channel to flood out and irrigate downhill land.

## Interceptor Drains

Interceptor drains (sealed interceptors) were developed by **Harry Whittington** of West Australia. They prevent overland waterflow and waterlogging that collapses dryland valley and downslope soils in desert soil catenas.

### Design Specifications

- **Depth**: Always 1.5–2.5 m (5–8 feet)
- **Slope**: 1:600 to 1:1500 cross-slope
- **Block width**: 100 m to maximum 300 m (985 feet)
- **Spacing**: Carefully calculated to isolate each block from flooding and salty cascade flow

### Construction Method

Construction involves ramming subsoil layers hard against the downhill bank using a bulldozer blade. This effectively prevents water seepage through the downhill wall. Where deeper sand seams carry salty water, trenches can be cut and stopped with a **vertical plastic barrier** backed by compacted clay on the uphill side.

### Functions

- Totally intercept overland flow, directing it to streams or valley run-offs
- Stop salt water seepages in shallow sand seams
- Isolate large blocks of soil from waterlogging and salt seepage
- Cut off seepage from salt lakes
- Divert early (salted) overland flow around saltpans, allowing later fresh floodwater to fill pans or shallow lakes

After interceptor preparation, trees can be planted in previously desertified soils.

## Spreader Banks

Spreader banks are designed to spill a thin sheet of surplus overflow water down a broad grassy slope for irrigation or to prevent channel scour and gullying in deserts. They take overflow from swales and dams and may be tens or hundreds of metres long.

### Design Features

- **Lower side**: Dead level, compacted or concreted
- **Spoil**: Piled uphill, preferably in mounds, with frequent openings to allow downhill overland flow to enter without carrying silt loads
- **Water flow**: Enters from a dam, swale, or minor stream; leaves as thin sheet flow downslope

### Irrigation Bay Systems

For flatland irrigation, the bank is pierced by dead-level pipe outlets, each feeding an irrigation bay confined by low side walls called **steering banks**. Key components include:

- **Head race**: The primary uphill feeder drain with cross-slides that block flow, causing levelled pipes to flood into bays
- **Tail drain**: At the lower end of each bay, carries excess water to a stream or secondary storage dam
- **Bay dimensions**: Should not exceed 100 m long or water is wasted to evaporation

In established systems, the entire lower lip may be concreted for a permanent level spill.

## Integrated Earthworks Philosophy

A well-planned combination of dams, drains, spreader banks, swales, pipes, gate-valves, spill gates, flags, and culverts can harvest, store, and use surplus flood or overland flow waters for multiple purposes:

- Flushing salts from soils
- Spreading water evenly over crops
- Wildfire suppression
- Modest irrigation

### Rehabilitative vs. Damaging Use

Earthworks are considered **rehabilitative** when they replace salted and eroded lands with perennial browse or forests. They are **damaging** and exploitative when used to:

- Irrigate high water-demand crops (like lucerne/alfalfa) in drylands
- Cut off flow to dry areas
- Run water for unessential uses to large urban centres devoted to lawns and car washes

## Sewage System Water Reduction

Approximately 40% of domestic water misuse comes from toilet flushing. Hand-basin moulded into toilet flush tanks can redirect handwash water to the toilet, effectively using no extra clean water for flushing. Low-flush toilet bowls are essential with such systems to prevent incomplete flushing and pathogenic bacteria buildup.

### When Dry Toilets Are Appropriate

Dry toilets are specifically useful where:

- No methane system is used
- Sewage is not used in plant production
- Soils do not suit septic tanks
- Cities have critical water supply problems

In Sweden, dry toilets are compulsory in remote, unsewered, or unsuitable areas. On farms or in well-drained soils, or wherever sewage feeds methane digesters, conventional systems may be preferable.

## Greywater Chemical Considerations

Wastewater from kitchens, bathrooms, and laundries carries chemicals that must be understood before release to gardens and soils. Typical contaminants include:

- Sodium or potassium polysulphates, silicates, sulphates, bicarbonates
- Borates (harmful at >0.5 ppm; >1.0 ppm damages soil life)
- Residual biocides (DDT, Dieldrin, Hexachlor from dairy cattle)
- Additives: resins, scents, dyes, brighteners
- Faecal bacteria and viral/worm pathogens

Sodium and potassium salts can deflocculate soil clays or damage leaves at concentrations exceeding 1,000 ppm. Woody perennials cope better with complex pollutants than garden vegetables, allowing more decomposition time. In critical areas, special soaps (unpolluted oils, potash or sodium) and water plants that remove excesses may be necessary before greywater reaches soils and streams.
