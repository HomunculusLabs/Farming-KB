# Electric Fencing in Permaculture Systems

Electric fencing is a key enabling technology in permaculture, allowing
flexible and economical livestock management across multiple zones.
Mollison references electric fencing in contexts ranging from intensive
rotational grazing to pig tractor systems and poultry protection. It
represents an [[mollison-permaculture-appropriate-technology]] that reduces the cost and increases the flexibility of
animal integration in designed systems.

## Rotational Grazing with Electric Fencing

Mollison cites the case of a farmer (Norm Sims) who achieved remarkable
results using electric fencing for rotational grazing. On a property
using 26 paddocks grazed in a 21-day rotation (average field of 1.6
hectares each, with a trough water-point for cattle), the farmer
reported:

- Expectation to double production using half the irrigation waters
  (four times benefit)
- Salinity reduced
- In severe drought: "Pasture production has never looked better and
  water is available"
- Irrigation time reduced from six days to two

This case demonstrates the synergy between water management, fencing,
and rotational grazing. The expense is in fencing (now much reduced by
the use of electric fencing), and the results show that primary
increases in crop yield come from attention to site design and
development, followed by wise enterprise selection.

## Pig Tractor Fencing

For pig tractor systems, electric fencing is the most economical
containment method. Permanent electric fencing is much cheaper than
chicken mesh fencing for enclosing 0.5 to 2 hectare plots. Once each
pen is bare and rough-ploughed by rooting pigs, the fence can be moved
to the next area.

Mollison notes that 20 pigs per 4,000 square metres will plough an
area for planting comfrey, sunroot, lucerne, chicory, and clover. The
fencing investment is modest compared to the land preparation value
achieved. Later, pens can be planted out in blocks using electric
fencing to keep pigs away from young trees as they establish.

## Poultry and Electric Fencing

The effects of electric fences in tropical areas, if not within forest
edges, are often nullified by the rank growth of coarse grasses, and
become inoperative for repelling python, pig, bandicoots, and foxes.
For this reason, fence lines must be planted with a dense perennial
ground carpet of low herbaceous plants to exclude grasses, or
overshaded by tree canopies.

Where chickens are the main crop, chicken forage plants replace
vegetables and some fruit crop, and the system then provides all food.
Even rocky or rough country is prepared for crop by chickens, the main
cost being secure pen fencing. Strong fences also support vine crop, and
a few larger legume trees provide high shade for pens and crops.

## Strip Grazing

Strip grazing uses electric fences, either permanent or temporary, to
allocate fresh forage progressively. This technique maximises the
efficiency of pasture utilisation by preventing animals from selectively
grazing only the most palatable species while trampling or wasting less
preferred plants.

A temporary electric fence will clean off rocky knolls of rank growth
that cannot otherwise be economically cleared. This makes otherwise
unproductive areas available for planting or improved pasture.

## Advantages of Electric Fencing

The key advantages of electric fencing in permaculture systems include:

- Low cost compared to conventional fencing materials
- Flexibility: paddocks can be easily reconfigured as system needs
  change
- Portability: temporary fences can be moved seasonally
- Low environmental impact: no need for extensive post-hole digging or
  permanent wire structures
- Compatibility with [[mollison-zone-planning-frequency-distance-and-placement]]: different paddock sizes can be created to match management intensity

## Limitations in Tropical Environments

Mollison notes an important limitation: in tropical areas where coarse
grasses grow rapidly, electric fence lines can become short-circuited
by vegetation pressing against the wires. This is a particular problem
for repelling larger animals such as pythons, pigs, bandicoots, and
foxes. The solution is to maintain a dense, low-growing ground cover
along fence lines, or to overshadefence lines with tree canopies.

## Integration with Design Systems

Electric fencing enables the integration of livestock with
[[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]],
allowing animals to be precisely placed where their foraging,
manuring, or land-clearing functions are needed. It supports
[[mollison-mixed-livestock-rotation-systems]]
and [[mollison-animal-range-management]] by making
paddock subdivision economically viable.

Mollison believes that with modern machines, fencing, and water
reticulation, the primary and certain increases in crop yield come not
from varietal selections but from attention to site design and
development. Electric fencing is a key enabling technology that makes
such design-intensive approaches accessible to farmers.
