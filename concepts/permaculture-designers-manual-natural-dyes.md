# Natural Dyes
# Permaculture Designers Manual - Plant Dyes for Craft and Textile

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Natural dyes derived from plants are a traditional craft product that fits well within permaculture's emphasis on local self-reliance and multiple yields from integrated systems. The Designers Manual references dyes in several contexts: as products of forest farming, as contaminants in wastewater, and as part of the batik textile tradition. The small forest farm model specifically lists bark for craft and dyes as one of the sustainable yields from managed woodland.

## Forest Farm Dye Production

A small (12-16 hectare) forest site managed by a family or families can yield multiple products including bark for craft and dyes on a sustainable basis. This demonstrates that dye production can be integrated into a broader forest management strategy that also produces:
- Fungi and seed
- Small fruit
- Furs and meat
- Wild fruits and nuts
- Honey
- Poles and coppice
- Medicinals and mulches

## Dye Sources in the Manual

### Bark for Dyes

Bark from various tree species is a primary source of natural dyes. The Designers Manual references tannin as one of the useful products obtained from tree bark, alongside craft applications. Trees managed for coppice provide regular bark harvests for dye extraction.

### Tannin-Rich Species

The manual references several species and contexts where tannins (which serve as both mordants and dye sources) are produced:
- Acacia species (wattle bark) - traditional source of tannin for leather tanning
- Oak bark - rich in tannins, used for both tanning and dyeing
- Various species noted for smog tolerance that also produce medicinals, food dyes, and stabilisers

### Food Dyes

Some species are noted specifically for food dye production. Plants that produce food-safe colorants are valuable additions to the permaculture system, providing natural alternatives to synthetic food colorings.

## Dyes as Wastewater Contaminants

The Designers Manual provides important information about dyes from the perspective of water quality. A typical analysis of powdered detergent or soap includes dyes among the additives, along with resins (hardeners), scents, brighteners, and other chemicals. These synthetic dyes are pollutants that need to be removed from wastewater.

Biological systems using watercress can remove dyes and nitrates from wastewater. The cress is harvested and composted, removing the contaminants from the water cycle.

## Batik and Traditional Textile Arts

The Designers Manual references the batik (wax-dye) process as an example of pattern application in traditional arts. Older patterns were created using batik techniques, and the manual notes that fractal patterns can be seen in many phenomena, including the use of batik to print fabrics. This connects the art of natural dyeing to broader principles of pattern and design.

## Integration with Permaculture Systems

### Dye Garden Design

A dedicated dye garden can be incorporated into Zone 1 or Zone 2 of the permaculture design, close to the home for easy harvesting. Suitable locations include:
- Edges of vegetable gardens
- Under fruit trees (shade-tolerant [[natural-dye-plants]])
- Along fence lines
- In dedicated craft production areas

### Species for Natural Dye Production

While the Designers Manual does not provide an exhaustive list of dye plants, it references the following categories:
- Tree bark (various species, harvested sustainably from coppice)
- Tannin-producing species for mordants and brown/yellow dyes
- Food dye species for culinary applications
- Species with smog tolerance that also produce dyes

### Sustainable Harvest

The permaculture approach to dye production emphasizes sustainable harvesting:
- Use coppice systems for bark harvest (tree regrows after cutting)
- Harvest leaves and flowers without killing the plant
- Collect fallen plant material for some dye sources
- Integrate dye plant production with other forest products

## Tannins and Their Uses

Tannins are water-soluble phenolic compounds found in many plants, especially in bark, leaves, and galls. They serve multiple functions:

### In Dyeing
- Act as natural mordants, helping dye bind to fiber
- Produce yellow, brown, and black colors directly
- Improve colorfastness of other dyes

### In Leather Tanning
- Convert animal hides to leather by cross-linking proteins
- Traditional use of oak bark, wattle bark, and other tannin-rich barks

### In Water Quality
- Humic acids, tannins, and saponins may issue from forested catchments, affecting water chemistry
- In acid conditions (pH 4.0 or less), tannins and other acids affect water quality
- Understanding tannin release helps in managing water quality from forested catchments

### Acorn Processing
The manual references acorn meals and the need to fix bitter tannins as insoluble ferric compounds to make acorns edible. This demonstrates the broader significance of tannins in food processing.

## Dye Plants in the Small Farm System

The forest farm layout (Figure 12.10) zones for frequency of harvest, with dye-producing species integrated into the overall management plan. Bark for craft and dyes comes from:
- Coppiced woodland (regular harvesting cycles)
- Managed nut and fine timber stands (thinning operations)
