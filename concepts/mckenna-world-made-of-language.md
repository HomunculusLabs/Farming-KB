# The World Made of Language

Terence McKenna's proposition that "the world is made of language" stands as one of the most radical and far-reaching claims in *Food of the Gods*.

It draws on millennia of shamanic experience, modern linguistic theory, and the phenomenology of psychedelic intoxication to argue that language does not merely describe reality but actively constructs it.

This idea inverts the Western assumption that language is a transparent medium for communicating about an independently existing material world.

The evidence gathered from millennia of shamanic experience argues that the world is actually made of language in some fashion, a radical proposition that is nevertheless in agreement with much of current linguistic thinking.

## The Linguistic Revolution

McKenna anchors his argument in the twentieth-century linguistic revolution, citing Boston University anthropologist Misia Landau, who observed that "language is not merely a device for communicating ideas about the world, but rather a tool for bringing the world into existence in the first place."

Reality, on this view, "is not simply experienced or reflected in language, but instead is actually produced by language."

This position aligns with strong versions of linguistic relativity associated with Whorf and Sapir, but McKenna pushes it further: language does not shape perception of a pre-existing world; it calls that world into being.

The shamanic perspective, accumulated over thousands of years of hallucinogen-mediated exploration, arrives at the same conclusion through a different route.

From the point of view of the psychedelic shaman, "the world appears to be more in the nature of an utterance or a tale than in any way related to the leptons and baryons or charge and spin that our high priests, the physicists, speak of."

For the shaman, the cosmos is a tale that becomes true as it is told, and as it tells itself.

This perspective implies that human imagination can seize the tiller of being in the world. Freedom, personal responsibility, and a humbling awareness of the true size and intelligence of the world combine in this point of view to make it a fitting basis for living an authentic neo-Archaic life.

A reverence for and an immersion in the powers of language and communication are the basis of the shamanic path.

## Language as Magic

McKenna explicitly connects this linguistic philosophy to the concept of magic. "Perhaps language is more properly understood when thought of as magic," he writes, "for it is the implicit position of magic that the world is made of language."

This equation of language and magic is not mere metaphor. In magical traditions worldwide, the utterance of words, names, or incantations is understood to have causal power over reality.

The shaman who speaks the true name of a thing gains power over it. The creation myths of many traditions begin with a divine word or act of naming that brings the cosmos into existence.

If language is accepted as the primary datum of knowing, then the Western rationalist tradition has been fundamentally misled.

Only shamanic approaches, McKenna argues, can answer the deepest human questions: who are we, where did we come from, and toward what fate do we move?

These questions have never been more urgent than in the present, when "evidence of the failure of science to nurture the soul of humanity is everywhere around us."

Ours is not merely temporary ennui of the spirit; if we are not careful, ours is a terminal condition of the collective body and spirit.

## Grammars as World-Builders

McKenna supports his argument with cross-linguistic evidence. The grammars of languages, their internal rules, have been carefully studied, yet too little attention has been devoted to examining how language creates and defines the limits of reality.

Common sense assumes that, though languages are always evolving, the raw stuff of what language expresses is relatively constant and common to all humans.

Yet the Hopi language has no past or future tenses or concepts. How, then, can the Hopi world be like ours?

And the Inuit have no first-person pronoun. How, then, can their world be like ours?

These are not merely curiosities of grammar. They point to the fundamental insight that each language constructs a different world.

The categories available in a language determine what can be thought, what can be experienced, and what can be imagined.

Different languages do not simply provide different labels for the same objects; they constitute different objects of experience.

Each language is a different world, and the boundaries of that world are set by the grammar and vocabulary available within that language.

Perhaps language is more properly understood when thought of as magic, for it is the implicit position of magic that the world is made of language.

## Higher Dimensional Perspective

By entering the domain of plant intelligence through hallucinogens, the shaman becomes "privileged to a higher dimensional perspective on experience."

This higher dimensional perspective does not invalidate ordinary language or everyday reality. Rather, it reveals the constructed nature of what we take for granted.

It shows that the seemingly solid world of objects and events is sustained by an act of linguistic creation that is normally invisible to us.

The psychedelic experience temporarily disrupts this act, allowing the underlying processes to become visible.

Only by gaining access to the Transcendent Other can those patterns of time and space and our role in them be glimpsed.

Because our maps of reality are determined by our present circumstances, we tend to lose awareness of the larger patterns of time and space.

Shamanism strives for this higher point of view, which is achieved through a feat of linguistic prowess.

## The Shaman as Linguistic Pioneer

The connection between mushrooms and language was powerfully articulated by Henry Munn in his essay "The Mushrooms of Language."

McKenna quotes Munn extensively: "Language is an ecstatic activity of signification. Intoxicated by the mushrooms, the fluency, the ease, the aptness of expression one becomes capable of are such that one is astounded by the words that issue forth from the contact of the intention of articulation with the matter of experience."

"The spontaneity the mushrooms liberate is not only perceptual, but linguistic. For the shaman, it is as if existence were uttering itself through him."

This is why the shaman is the remote ancestor of the poet and artist. Our need to feel part of the world seems to demand that we express ourselves through creative activity.

The ultimate well-springs of this creativity are hidden in the mystery of language.

Shamanic ecstasy is an act of surrender that authenticates both the individual self and that which is surrendered to, the mystery of being.

A shaman is one who has attained a vision of the beginnings and the endings of all things and who can communicate that vision. To the rational thinker, this is inconceivable, yet the techniques of shamanism are directed toward this end and this is the source of their power.

Preeminent among the shaman's techniques is the use of the plant hallucinogens, repositories of living vegetable gnosis that lie, now nearly forgotten, in our ancient past.

## The Shamanic Approach to Knowledge

The shamanic approach to knowledge is fundamentally different from the scientific method.

Where science seeks to stand apart from its object of study, the shaman seeks to merge with it. Where science demands reproducibility, the shaman values uniqueness.

Where science aims for prediction and control, the shaman aims for understanding and participation.

These two approaches are not merely different techniques; they are different relationships to reality itself, encoded in different forms of language.

Of course, when McKenna arrived in the Amazon twenty years before writing, he knew nothing of the above. Like most Westerners, he believed that magic was a phenomenon of the naive and the primitive, that science could provide an explanation for the workings of the world.

In that position of intellectual naivete, he encountered psilocybin mushrooms for the first time, at San Augustine in the Alto Magdalena of southern Colombia.

The experiences he had during those travels were personally transforming and introduced him to a class of experiences vital to the restoration of balance in our social and environmental worlds.

He shared the group mind generated in the vision sessions of the ayahuasqueros and saw the magical darts of red light that one shaman can send against another.

## Cultural Blindness and the Archaic Revival

The rational, mechanistic, antispiritual bias of Western culture has made it impossible to appreciate the mind-set of the shaman.

We are culturally and linguistically blind to the world of forces and interconnections clearly visible to those who have retained the Archaic relationship to nature.

This blindness is not merely intellectual; it is constitutive. The language of Western science and rationality literally prevents the shamanic worldview from being articulated within it.

This creates a paradox: the very tool that might allow us to understand the shamanic perspective, language itself, is the medium through which the dominator culture maintains its conceptual monopoly.

To break out of this bind requires not just new ideas but new forms of language, new modes of expression that can bridge the gap between the rational and the shamanic.

McKenna offers his account as a kind of witness, an Everyman: if these experiences happened to him, then they can be part of the general experience of men and women everywhere.

The recovery of this understanding through the Archaic Revival is not a retreat into the past but an advance into a more comprehensive and authentic mode of being.

The world made of language is a world that can be remade through language, and the psychedelic experience provides access to the linguistic resources necessary for that remaking.

New developments in mind-machine interfacing, pharmacology, and data retrieval techniques are coalescing into the potential for either a demonic or an angelic self-imaging of our culture.

The shamanic response, the Archaic response, the human response, should be to locate the art pedal and push it to the floor.
