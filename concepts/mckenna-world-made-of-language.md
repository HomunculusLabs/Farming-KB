# McKenna's World Made of Language and the Linguistic Foundations of Shamanism

## Overview

Terence McKenna's "World Made of Language" is a central thesis from *Food of the Gods*
(1992) that draws on millennia of shamanic experience to argue that language does not
merely describe reality — it produces it. This concept places McKenna at the intersection
of [[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]].

## The World Made of Language

McKenna contends that the accumulated evidence from shamanic traditions across cultures
and millennia points toward a radical conclusion: the world is actually made of language.
This proposition stands at odds with the dominant scientific worldview but finds strong
support within the linguistic revolution of the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]].

### The Linguistic Revolution

McKenna invokes Boston University anthropologist Misia Landau to anchor his argument in
academic discourse. Landau wrote:

> "The twentieth-century linguistic revolution is the recognition that language is not
> merely a device for communicating ideas about the world, but rather a tool for
> bringing the world into existence in the first place. Reality is not simply
> 'experienced' or 'reflected' in language, but instead is actually produced by
> language."

This reframing moves language from the periphery of human experience to its very center.
If language produces reality, then the structures of grammar, syntax, and metaphor are not
descriptive ornaments but generative forces — the hidden architecture of the world itself.

### The Cosmos as a Tale

For the psychedelic shaman, the cosmos is a tale that becomes true as it is told. This
carries a profound implication: human imagination can seize the tiller of being in the
world. McKenna positions the shaman as the remote ancestor of the poet and the artist,
suggesting that [[dmt-black-hole-effect-and-shamanic-practice]] guage" thesis within the broader framework of
shamanic practice, which he characterizes through Mircea Eliade's definition as "archaic
techniques of ecstasy."

### The Primacy of Ecstasy

Not all shamans employ intoxicating plants, but all shamanic practice aims to give rise
to ecstasy — a state of dissolution and transcendence of ordinary consciousness. Various
methods exist: drumming, breath manipulation, ordeals, fasting, theatrical illusions,
and sexual abstinence. However, McKenna argues that plant hallucinogens remain the most
effective, ancient, and overwhelming of these techniques.

A crucial distinction in [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]] is that the shaman, not the patient, takes the
psychoactive substance. The plants serve as vehicles allowing the healer to journey into
an invisible realm — a domain where language, ideas, and meaning hold greater power than
[[fukuoka-textdoc-no-cause-and-effect-in-nature]]. In this nonordinary reality, sympathies, resonances, intentions, and
personal will are linguistically magnified through poetic rhetoric. The imagination is
invoked and, at times, beheld visibly.

## The Shamanic Meme

McKenna frames the resurgence of psychedelic shamanism as a cultural "meme" — a
self-replicating idea spreading through modern society. Thousands of people, many of
them scientifically sophisticated, have concluded that psychedelic plants and shamanic
institutions constitute profound tools for inner psyche exploration.

This growing worldwide subculture of hyperdimensional explorers draws on what McKenna
calls "the clear running spring of the oldest human religion." Plant hallucinogens, he
maintains, remain the most direct and ancient connection to the shamanic worldview.
