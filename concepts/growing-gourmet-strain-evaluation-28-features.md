# Strain Evaluation: The 28-Feature Checklist

Evaluating a mushroom strain is one of the most important skills a
cultivator can develop. The characteristics of a strain determine
everything from yield and quality to marketability and ease of
cultivation. [[paul-stamets]] identified 28 features that should be
systematically evaluated when selecting or comparing mushroom strains.
This comprehensive checklist forms the backbone of intelligent strain
selection.

## The 28 Features for Strain Evaluation

### Speed of Colonization (Features 1-2)

  1. Rate of [[growing-gourmet-mushroom-life-cycle]] growth on
     agar media: The speed at which mycelium expands across a petri dish
     is a primary indicator of vigor. Faster-growing strains typically
     colonize substrates more quickly, narrowing the window of
     opportunity for competitors.

  2. Rate of mycelial growth on grain: The speed of colonization on
     sterilized grain is often different from growth on agar. Some
     strains that excel on agar may be slower on grain.

### Fruiting Characteristics (Features 3-12)

  3. Speed of primordia formation: How quickly the strain initiates
     mushroom formation after being exposed to fruiting conditions.

  4. Number of primordia per unit substrate: The density of pin
     formation directly affects yield potential.

  5. Percentage of primordia that develop into mature mushrooms: Not
     all pins mature. A high maturation rate is desirable.

  6. Size and shape uniformity of the fruitbody: Market demands
     consistency. Strains producing uniform mushrooms command premium
     prices.

  7. Total yield per flush: The weight of harvestable mushrooms per
     flush.

  8. Number of flushes: More flushes mean greater total
     [[growing-gourmet-biological-efficiency-yield]] per block or bed.

  9. Post-harvest mushroom quality: How well mushrooms maintain
     quality after being picked.

  10. Spore load characteristics: Some strains produce enormous spore
      loads that create environmental hazards. Oyster spores clog fans
      and trigger allergic reactions. Reishi is notorious for weeks-long
      spore production that stains surfaces and affects air quality.

  11. Shape of the cap and stem: Contributes to marketability and ease
      of packaging.

  12. Color: Attractive coloration is important for market appeal.

### Storage and Preservation (Features 13-18)

  13. [[growing-gourmet-stock-culture-library]] storage
      longevity: How long the strain remains viable in storage.

  14. Rate of senescence: All strains eventually senesce. Slower
      senescence is preferred as it extends the useful life of the
      strain.

  15. Clustering tendency: Mushrooms that grow in tight clusters can be
      more difficult to harvest but may have higher total yields.

  16. Appearance: Form, size, and color of the mushroom as a whole.

  17. Duration from storage to spoilage: An important aspect of
      evaluating any strain is its ability to store well. Shiitake
      mushrooms store and ship far better, on average, than Oyster
      mushrooms.

  18. Abatement of growth subsequent to harvest: Many mushrooms
      continue to enlarge, flatten out, and produce spores long after
      being harvested. Strains of [[pleurotus-pulmonarius]], especially the
      so-called "sajor-caju," are described as "biologically out of
      control" in this regard.

### Biological Resilience (Features 19-20)

  19. Necrosis factors and protection of dead tissue from competitors:
      After a mushroom is picked, tissue remnants become sites for
      attack. Shiitake forms a protective woody skin on the stem butt.
      Most [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] strains lack this defense, requiring
      extreme caution during harvest.

  20. Genetic stability/instability: Signs of a strain dying include
      inability to colonize substrate, failure to produce primordia,
      production of deformed mushrooms, delays in fruiting schedules,
      and increasing susceptibility to disease. These symptoms suggest
      strain senescence.

### Sensory and Nutritional Quality (Features 21-28)

  21. Flavor: Strains of the same species differ substantially in
      flavor. Younger mushrooms are generally better-flavored than older
      ones. The King Stropharia is exquisitely edible when young but
      quickly loses flavor with maturity.

  22. Texture: The stage at harvest, cooking duration and temperature,
      and cooking method all markedly affect textural qualities. Most
      connoisseurs prefer mushrooms that are slightly crispy and chewy
      but not tough. By tearing rather than cutting, firmness is
      preserved.

  23. Aroma: Few experiences arouse as much interest in eating gourmet
      mushrooms as their aroma. The aroma of Shiitake and Shimeji is
      particularly prized.

  24. Sensitivity to essential elements: Gray Leatham (1989) noted that
      nanograms of tin and nickel were critical to successful fruitbody
      formation in Shiitake. Without these minute amounts, Shiitake
      mycelium is incapable of fruiting. Manganese also plays a
      determinate role.

  25. Ability to surpass competitors: An essential measure of a
      strain's performance is its ability to resist competitor fungi,
      bacteria and insects. Oyster mushrooms are recognized for their
      nematode-trapping abilities and can even trap Sciarid flies on
      aerial mycelium.

  26. Nutritional composition: Mushrooms are a rich source of amino
      acids (proteins), minerals and vitamins. The percentages can vary
      between strains due to different conversion efficiencies of base
      substrate components into mushroom flesh.

  27. Production of primary and secondary metabolites: Extracellular
      enzymes like ligninase break down structural components in wood.
      Secondary metabolites usually occur well after colonization, such
      as the yellow exudate frequently seen at the bottom of aged spawn
      containers.
