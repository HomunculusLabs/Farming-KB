# Earthworks for Water Management

Earthworks are deliberate modifications to the land surface to control, direct, store, and infiltrate water. They are foundational infrastructure in [[regenerative-agriculture]], permaculture design, and homesteading. Well-designed earthworks transform how water moves through a landscape, turning erosion problems into hydration opportunities and creating the foundation for productive land.

## Design Principles

### Slow, Spread, Sink

The three imperatives of regenerative [[water-management]]:

1. **Slow** the movement of water across the landscape. Fast water erodes, carries away topsoil, and delivers nothing to the soil profile.
2. **Spread** water out across as much surface area as possible. Concentrated flow causes erosion; distributed flow infiltrates.
3. **Sink** water into the soil. Every drop that enters the soil is a drop that feeds plants, recharges groundwater, and does not become runoff.

### Work with Gravity, Not Against It

Water flows downhill. All earthworks should use gravity to move water where you want it, never requiring pumping unless absolutely necessary. See [[keyline-design]] for the most sophisticated gravity-fed water distribution system.

### Start at the Top (High Point)

Always begin earthworks at the highest point of the watershed or property and work downward. This prevents water from undermining higher structures and ensures a logical flow from capture to storage to use.

### Plan for Extreme Events

Design for a 1-in-100-year storm as the maximum event. All spillways, overflows, and emergency discharge points must handle extreme rainfall without catastrophic failure. A failed earthwork during a storm causes more damage than no earthwork at all.

## Types of Earthworks

### Ponds

Ponds store water on the surface for irrigation, livestock, wildlife habitat, fire protection, and aquaculture.

**Site Selection Criteria:**
- A suitable depression or valley where a dam can be economically constructed
- Adequate catchment area to fill the pond (minimum 5:1 catchment-to-pond area ratio in moderate rainfall)
- Impermeable soil (clay content >20%) or a suitable liner strategy
- Location below a spring, seep, or reliable runoff area
- A stable spillway route for overflow
- Not in a streambed without proper permitting

**Pond Types:**
- **Excavated pond**: Dug below grade. No dam needed. Suitable for flat land with high water table.
- **Embankment pond**: Dam across a valley to impound water behind it. Most common on sloping land.
- **Spring-fed pond**: Impounds water from a natural spring. Most reliable water source.

**Construction Steps:**
1. Mark the pond perimeter and dam location
2. Strip topsoil from the dam foundation area and set aside
3. Excavate the pond basin, using material for the dam
4. Build the dam in layers (lifts), compacting each layer
5. Install a pipe through the dam for drain and overflow control
6. Cut a vegetated spillway for emergency overflow
7. Replace topsoil on dam and pond edges, plant immediately with grass

**Liner Options:**
- **Compacted clay**: Cheapest if suitable clay is available on site. Compact to 95% density.
- **Bentonite clay**: Sodium bentonite spread and compacted. Swells to form impermeable layer.
- **HDPE liner**: Durable plastic liner. Most reliable but expensive.
- **Gley**: Organic method — fill pond with manure/organic matter, flood, let anaerobic bacteria seal the soil.

### Berms

Berms are raised earthen mounds used to direct water, create level planting areas, block wind, or define spaces.

**Types:**
- **Diversion berm**: Directs water away from an area. Common along roads, buildings, or across slopes above gardens.
- **Retention berm**: Holds water on the surface to allow infiltration. Often combined with swales.
- **Windbreak berm**: Low berm (1-3 feet) planted with trees for wind protection.
- **Terrace berm**: Creates level shelves on slopes for planting.

**Construction:**
- Berms should have 3:1 or flatter side slopes for stability
- Compact the core, leave the surface loose for planting
- Always provide an overflow/spillway point
- Plant immediately with deep-rooted grasses and ground covers

### Diversion Swales

Swales with a slight grade that move water from one area to another. Unlike contour swales, diversion swales are not level.

**Uses:**
- Divert water away from buildings or roads
- Direct runoff to a pond or storage area
- Move water from a wet area to a dry area
- Connect multiple water harvesting features

**Design:**
- Grade: 0.5-2% slope (gentle enough to prevent erosion, steep enough to keep water moving)
- Size for peak expected flow with 25% safety margin
- Line with grass, gravel, or riprap to prevent erosion
- End at a safe discharge point (pond, another swale, stable vegetated area)

See [[swale-design-construction]] for detailed swale building techniques.

### Retention Basins

Shallow, broad depressions designed to hold water temporarily and allow infiltration. Unlike ponds, they drain within hours to days.

**Applications:**
- Capture stormwater runoff from impervious surfaces (roofs, driveways)
- Reduce peak flow in drainage systems
- Recharge groundwater
- Provide temporary wildlife habitat

**Design:**
- Side slopes: 4:1 or flatter for safety and ease of mowing
- Depth: 0.3-1.5 meters
- Include an overflow outlet at the designed maximum water level
- Plant the bottom and sides with water-tolerant native species
- Can be integrated with [[rainwater-harvesting-systems]] for comprehensive water management

### Terraces

Level or nearly level platforms cut into slopes, creating flat planting areas and preventing erosion. Terraces are one of the oldest earthworks, used worldwide for thousands of years.

**Types:**
- **Broad-base terrace**: Gentle, graded terrace for pasture or row crops. Less expensive, less disruptive.
- **Bench terrace**: A level shelf with a steep riser. Used for intensive cropping on steep land.
- **Paddy terrace**: Bench terrace with sealed bottom for flooded rice or other aquatic crops.

**Design Considerations:**
- Spacing depends on slope: steeper slopes require more closely spaced terraces
- Provide drainage for each terrace — water must be able to exit without overflowing the riser
- The riser (vertical face) should be planted with grass or reinforced with stone
- Terraces are permanent infrastructure — build them right the first time

### Gabions and Check Dams

Small structures placed in drainage channels to slow water flow and trap sediment.

**Gabions:** Wire cages filled with rocks, placed across ephemeral streams or drainage channels. They slow water, trap sediment, and eventually create level areas behind them.

**Check Dams:** Small rock, log, or brush dams placed in drainage channels. Similar function to gabions but simpler and smaller. Often used in series (several dams along a channel, each creating a small pool behind it).

## Earthworks Machinery and Tools

### Hand Tools (for areas under 0.5 acres)

- Shovel, mattock, pickaxe for small-scale shaping
- Wheelbarrow for moving soil
- Manual tamper for compacting berms and dam cores
