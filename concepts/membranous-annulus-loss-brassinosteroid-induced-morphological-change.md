## Possible Mechanisms

### Hormonal Interference with Developmental Timing

Brassinosteroids are known in plants to influence the timing of developmental transitions. In P. cubensis, the brassinosteroid may alter the relative growth rates of the pileus versus the partial veil, causing the veil to either not form properly or to be obliterated during the more rapid cap expansion.

### Cell Division and Elongation Effects

The primary known mechanism of brassinosteroid action is promotion of cell elongation and cell division. If the cells of the partial veil respond differently to brassinosteroid stimulation than the surrounding cap and stem tissues, the differential growth could prevent normal veil formation. The veil tissue might be stretched to the point of non-existence during the accelerated development.

Fungi produce their own steroid-like compounds (ergosterols, various fungal steroids). The introduction of a plant brassinosteroid might interact with fungal steroid [[fruiting-body-development]].

### Altered Gene Expression in Veil Tissue

The partial veil is a specialized tissue with distinct gene expression patterns compared to the cap and stem. Brassinosteroids, through their effects on cell division rates, may alter the relative proliferation of different cell populations within the developing primordium. If the cells destined to form the partial veil divide more slowly relative to surrounding tissues, the veil structure may be eliminated during the rapid expansion phase.

### Tension-Based Mechanical Failure

Another possibility is that the accelerated growth rate creates mechanical tensions that exceed the structural integrity of the developing partial veil. As the cap expands more rapidly under brassinosteroid influence, the veil — a thin membrane — may be torn and destroyed before it can fully differentiate into a persistent annulus.

## Implications for Taxonomy and Identification

The loss of the annulus could theoretically cause identification problems if brassinosteroid-treated specimens were encountered in the wild or in unmarked cultivation contexts. However, the concentration used (0.01 ppm) is unlikely to occur naturally at effective levels, making this primarily a laboratory observation rather than a practical concern for field mycologists. Natural brassinosteroid levels in soil and dung environments are typically orders of magnitude lower than the concentrations applied experimentally.

Together, these effects suggest that brassinosteroids do not merely accelerate growth but specifically enhance the efficiency of the fruiting developmental program while eliminating certain morphological features that may represent "waste" in terms of reproductive output.

The brassinosteroid effect is unique in that it specifically targets the partial veil/annulus structure while simultaneously improving other aspects of fruiting body quality (yield, completeness, dry mass). This selectivity suggests a specific interaction with the annulus developmental pathway rather than general stress or toxicity.
