# Ectomycorrhizal Ecosystem Impact

Dighton (2003) details the profound influence of ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi
on [[dighton-aquatic-hyphomycete-conidia-community-dynamics]], carbon cycling, and ecosystem
resilience. ECM associations occur between a limited number of plant species
(mainly trees) and a huge diversity of fungal taxa (basidiomycetes,
ascomycetes, some zygomycetes), forming one of the most functionally
significant symbioses in forest ecosystems.

## Structural and Functional Basis

ECM fungi form a Hartig net between root cortical cells and a surface sheath
of fungal material of varying complexity. The extraradical mycelium accounts
for only 5% of the nutrient-absorbing system dry weight (fungi and roots
combined) but provides 75% of the absorbing area and over 99% of the
absorbing length (Rousseau et al., 1994). This represents a small investment
in structural carbohydrate for a large return in nutrient absorption capacity.

Many ECM fungi develop rhizomorphs or mycelial strands with conductive elements
analogous to plant vascular tissue, enabling long-distance transport of
nutrients and water (Duddridge et al., 1980). The distal parts of extraradical
hyphal structures produce enzymes usually associated with saprotrophic
decomposer fungi, blurring the functional boundary between mycorrhizal and
saprotrophic roles.

## Nutrient Acquisition Efficiency

Different ECM species vary greatly in their nutrient acquisition capacity.
Dighton et al. (1990) used radiotracer phosphorus to show that birch trees
colonized by Hebeloma mycorrhizae had significantly higher P uptake (134 ng
P/g leaf weight) compared to Lactarius (55) or Laccaria (52). ECM fungi
produce enzymes enabling access to organic N and P sources, including
proteases, phosphatases, and even lignin-degrading enzymes. Some ECM fungi
can derive ammonium-N from organic sources and contribute to rock dissolution
through organic acid secretion.

Landeweert et al. (2001) discuss "rock-eating" ectomycorrhizal fungi that
mobilize nutrients from minerals, linking plants directly to the mineral soil
matrix. Seven ECM fungal species investigated by Connolly et al. (1998)
showed variable abilities to dissolve minerals and access nutrients from
inorganic sources.
