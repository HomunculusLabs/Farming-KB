## Mazatec Shamanic Discourse as Medicine

## Overview

Among the Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico, the shaman"s spoken discourse during mushroom ceremonies is not merely an accompaniment to healing — it *is* the healing. Drawing on the analysis of Henry Munn and the structural anthropology of Claude Lévi-Strauss, the concept of "words as medicine" reveals a sophisticated understanding of the therapeutic power of language that parallels and in some ways anticipates modern psychotherapeutic theory.

## The Shaman Speaks For the Patient

Claude Lévi-Strauss, in his essay "The Effectiveness of Symbols," identified a fundamental structural distinction between psychoanalysis and shamanic healing:

> "The psychoanalyst listens, whereas the shaman speaks."

In psychoanalysis, the patient puts words into the mouth of the analyst through transference — attributing feelings and intentions to the therapist. In shamanic incantation, the relationship is reversed: the shaman speaks for the patient, questioning them and putting answers into their mouth that correspond to an interpretation of their condition.

This establishes:
- A **direct relationship** with the patient"s conscious mind
- An **indirect relationship** with the patient's unconscious

The shaman provides the sick person "a language by means of which unexpressed and otherwise inexpressible psychic states can be immediately expressed." It is this transition to verbal expression — making it possible to undergo "in an ordered and intelligible form a real experience that would otherwise be chaotic and inexpressible" — that induces the release of physiological processes and the reorganization of the patient"s condition "in a favorable direction."

## Poesis as Healing Action

The Mazatec shaman embodies the original Greek concept of *poesis* — not poetry as literary art but poetry as *action*, as a making or transmutation of reality. The shaman"s speech is understood as literally transformative:

- **Words are materializations of consciousness**: Language is "a privileged vehicle of our relation to reality"
- **To name is to heal**: By articulating the nature and cause of illness, the shaman transforms the patient"s relationship to their condition
- **Discourse creates therapeutic reality**: The structured, rhythmic enunciation of meaning provides a framework within which the patient can reintegrate fragmented experience

As Munn notes, the Greek word *poesis* "was employed by the writer of an alchemical papyrus to designate the operation of transmutation itself." For the Mazatec shaman, the act of speaking during the mushroom state is precisely this kind of transmutation — an alchemical operation on consciousness and reality.

## The Metaphor of Tracking: "Searching for the Tracks"

A recurring metaphor in Mazatec shamanic discourse is that of following footprints or tracks. The shaman says:

> "Let us go looking for the tracks of the spirit... Let us go to the cornfield looking for the tracks of the spirits" feet in the warm ground."

This metaphor operates on multiple levels:

### Diagnostic Level
The shaman "tracks" the illness through the patient"s body: "From the head to the feet, what the matter is." The illness is understood as something that has arrived from somewhere, leaving traces — "tracks of her feet, the tracks of her nails" — that must be found and followed to their origin.

### Epistemological Level
Footprints represent "images of meaning, traces of a going to and from, sedimented clues of significance." The hunt for meaning is temporal — carried into the past ("what happened?") and projected into the future ("what will happen?"). Between departure and arrival lie the footprints: "manifestations of human, existential ecstasis."

### Methodological Level
The shaman"s method of looking — "from the right side to the left side" — represents the articulation of different intuitions, facts, feelings, and wishes, bringing to light meanings whose associations are like "the discovery of a path where the contents to be uttered are tracks to be followed into the unexplored, the unknown and unsaid."

### Existential Level
Meaning is intentional. Possibilities are paths to be chosen between. For the shaman, healing requires finding "the way in which to go" — not merely treating symptoms but restoring the patient"s capacity to navigate their existence with purpose and direction.

## The Medicine Woman's Discourse: Irene Pineda de Figueroa

Munn provides extensive transcribed passages from the discourse of a Mazatec medicine woman, Irene Pineda de Figueroa, during a mushroom ceremony. Her discourse illustrates the principles described above:

### Invocation and Supplication
She begins by invoking beneficent forces: "My God, you who are the master of the whole world, what we want is to search for and encounter from where comes sickness, from where comes pain and affliction."

### Diagnostic Inquiry
She systematically investigates the cause of illness: "Was it a whirlwind or bad air that fell in the door or in the doorway?" This reflects the Mazatec etiology of illness, where external forces (winds, bad air, malevolent spirits) can penetrate the body and cause harm.

### Communal Healing
She speaks not for a single patient but for everyone present — "all who are afflicted, troubled, unhappy, puzzled by the predicaments of their condition." Her discourse addresses the collective suffering of her community, including the poverty and deprivation that affects all Mazatec people.

### Fertility and Birth
When the patient is a pregnant woman, the shamaness"s discourse focuses on the emergence of life: "With the baby that is going to come there is no suffering, says. It"s a matter of a moment, there isn't going to be any suffering." She addresses the unborn child as an "innocent creature, her elf" — the Mazatec term for a being still in the womb.

### Agricultural Metaphor
As agriculturalists, the Mazatecs understand growth through cultivation. The shamaness uses agricultural language: "That come a good harvest. That come richness, that come well-being for all of our children. All my shoots, my children, my seeds." The parallel between agricultural fertility and human fertility is fundamental to Mazatec cosmology.

## The Mushrooms and Linguistic Centers of the Brain

Munn proposes a provocative hypothesis about the neuropharmacological basis of the shamanic linguistic phenomenon. He suggests that the chemical constituents of psilocybin mushrooms "have some connection with the linguistic centers of the brain," based on the consistent observation that the mushroom experience liberates not merely perceptual spontaneity but specifically linguistic spontaneity — "the spontaneity of speech, of fervent, lucid discourse, of the logos in activity."

This hypothesis, while speculative, aligns with contemporary neuroscientific interest in the effects of psychedelics on language processing and the default mode network. The mushrooms may reduce the inhibitory filtering that normally constrains verbal expression, allowing a more fluent, spontaneous, and associative form of speech that the Mazatecs recognize as inspired.

## Contrast with Western Notions of Psychedelics

Munn highlights the paradox that Western culture"s rediscovery of psychedelic mushrooms led to their being associated with madness and pejoratively labeled as "drugs," while the shamans who used them spoke of them as medicines and described their effects as putting one "into communication with the spirit." The Mazatec medicine woman directly addresses this misunderstanding:

> "They are doing nothing but talk, those who say that these matters are matters of the past... the people who call them crazy mushrooms."

She insists that the mushrooms" power "to awaken consciousness and cure existential ills is not any the less relevant now than it was in the past," and that to think their effect is insanity represents "ignorance of our dimension of mystery, of the wellsprings of meaning."
