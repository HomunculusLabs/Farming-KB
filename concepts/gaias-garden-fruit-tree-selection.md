Selecting the right fruit trees involves matching species and rootstock to your climate, space, and the role they will play in the overall garden design. Trees reach deep into the earth for nutrients and water, and far and wide into the sky for solar power -- they are life's largest, most effective natural collectors of energy and matter.

## Choosing Trees for the Ecological Garden

The ecological garden treats trees as full, integrated partners rather than scattered specimens. When selecting fruit trees, consider:

### Rootstock and Size

Fruit tree rootstocks determine the mature size of the tree and are critical for fitting trees into the garden design:

- **Standard rootstocks** produce full-sized trees (15-30+ feet). Best for the tall-tree layer of a large forest garden, or for Zone 3 areas visited infrequently. Examples include standard apple, pear, and Myrobalan plum rootstocks.
- **Semi-standard rootstocks** produce medium-sized trees (12-20 feet). A versatile middle ground for Zone 2 plantings.
- **Semi-dwarf rootstocks** produce smaller trees (8-14 feet). Excellent for the low-tree layer in a food forest, allowing more light to reach understory plants.
- **Dwarf rootstocks** produce small trees (6-10 feet). Ideal for small yards, dense plantings, espalier against fences, or as the canopy in a compact forest garden.

For a typical 1/4-acre suburban lot, Hemenway recommends dwarf and semi-dwarf fruit trees in Zone 1 and 2, with semi-standard trees in Zone 2 or 3.

### Spacing and Light

The canopy trees define the major patterns of the forest garden, so they must be chosen carefully with regard to their mature size. Enough light must fall between them to support other plants. In northern gardens where the sun is weaker, more space is needed between trees. In southern climates, denser spacing provides needed shade.

### Recommended Fruit Tree Species

**For the Tall-Tree Layer (standard/semi-standard rootstocks):**
- Standard and semi-standard apples (Malus domestica)
- European pears on standard rootstocks
- Full-sized sweet cherries
- Chestnut trees (especially Chinese chestnuts, Castanea mollissima)
- Walnut trees, especially heartnut and buartnut (Juglans species)
- Pinyon pines for nut production (low-frost climates)

**For the Low-Tree Layer (dwarf/semi-dwarf rootstocks):**
- Apples on dwarf rootstocks (M9, M26, etc.)
- Pears on semi-dwarf rootstocks (OHxF series)
- Naturally small trees: apricot, peach, nectarine, almond, mulberry
- Shade-tolerant species: persimmon (Diospyros virginiana), pawpaw (Asimina triloba)

**Nitrogen-Fixing Trees for Soil Building:**
- Black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia)
- Mesquite (Prosopis species)
- Alder (Alnus species)
- Golden-chain tree (Laburnum)
- Silk tree / mimosa (Albizia julibrissin)
- Mountain mahogany (Cercocarpus species)
- In low-frost climates: algoroba (Prosopis glandulosa), tagasaste (Chamaecytisus proliferus), carob (Ceratonia siliqua)

## Multigrafting and Diversity

Hemenway highlights examples of maximizing fruit diversity in limited space:
- One apple tree with five different varieties grafted onto it provides extended harvest season and cross-pollination in a single footprint
- Multiple stone fruit varieties on one tree extend the season of availability

Grafting multiple varieties onto a single tree is especially valuable in small yards where space is limited.

## Integrating Trees with the Garden Design

### The Pear Tree Connection Example

Hemenway uses a pear tree as a detailed case study in ecological design, listing its products, needs, activities, and qualities, then finding connections between them:

**Products:** Fruit, pollen for pollination, shade, leaves for mulch, carbon storage, beauty, habitat
**Needs:** Pollinators, pruning, soil nutrients, water, protection from pests
**Connections found:** Pollinator-attracting plants placed near the pear tree; the tree's shade used to cool a patio; fallen leaves used as [[hamilton-fruit-tree-selection-pollination]]
