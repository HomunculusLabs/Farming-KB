## Inner Layer Row Cover Management

Inner layer row covers are the second line of defense in a [[double-layer-protection-system]] system for winter vegetable production. Used inside [[coleman-unheated-greenhouse-design]] or [[high-tunnel]], they provide additional frost protection and thermal modulation for cold-hardy crops during the deepest months of winter. Eliot Coleman's research at Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine, demonstrated that row covers deployed inside protected structures behave fundamentally differently from those used in open fields, with significant implications for material selection, installation technique, and thermal management strategy.

## Advantages of Interior Row Cover Deployment

Row covers used inside a greenhouse or high tunnel are dramatically easier to manage than those used in open-field situations. The primary challenge of outdoor row cover application — wind — is eliminated entirely within a protected structure. In the open field, row covers must be secured along every edge with sandbags, soil, or staples to prevent wind from lifting and tearing the fabric. Inside a greenhouse, the still air environment means there is no need to weigh down the edges of the cover. This reduces both the labor required for daily handling and the physical wear on the fabric, extending its useful life.

The sheltered environment also means that lighter-weight fabrics can be used successfully indoors, since there is no wind stress to tear or abrade the material. This is a critical advantage because, as Coleman's trials demonstrated, lighter fabrics transmit significantly more sunlight — and in winter growing, sunlight transmission is more important than insulation value (see [[coleman-row-cover-trial-lightweight-heavyweight-sun-run]]).

The absence of wind inside the greenhouse also eliminates the evaporative cooling effect that plagues outdoor row covers. In exposed field conditions, wind passing over and through row cover fabric increases evaporative water loss from both the soil surface and the crop canopy, which cools the growing environment. Inside a protected structure, this evaporative cooling is minimal, further improving the thermal performance of the row cover system.

## Wire Wicket Support Construction

Row covers are supported over the crop beds using wire wickets — simple hoops bent from heavy-gauge wire. Coleman specifies the following construction details:

- **Wire gauge**: Number 9 wire, which provides sufficient stiffness to support row cover fabric and resist snow loads without bending
- **Overall length**: 76 inches (193 cm)
- **Dimensions**: A 30-inch (76 cm) flat top section with 23-inch (58 cm) legs on each side
- **Spacing**: Wickets are placed every 4 feet (1.2 m) along the length of the bed

The flat-top design (as opposed to a fully rounded hoop) maximizes the interior air volume directly above the crop, providing a thicker insulating air pocket. This shape also makes it easier to handle the row cover fabric during daily opening and closing, since the flat top provides a natural resting surface and the straight legs create a stable base when pushed into the soil.

Wickets are pushed directly into the prepared bed soil, where they hold firmly due to the firm seedbed preparation standard in intensive vegetable production. No additional anchoring hardware is needed, and wickets can be removed and reused across seasons with minimal maintenance.

## Clipping and Handling

Row covers are clipped to the wire wickets using ordinary spring-type clothespins. This simple, low-tech fastening system has several advantages:

- **Speed**: Clothespins can be applied and removed rapidly, which is essential because covers must be opened and closed daily during sunny winter weather
- **Cost**: Clothespins are inexpensive and widely available
- **Gentleness**: They grip the fabric without tearing it, preserving the integrity of spun-bonded materials
- **Adjustability**: The cover can be easily repositioned or temporarily opened for ventilation and harvesting

In the still-air greenhouse environment, clips need only hold the fabric against the wickets — there is no need to seal edges against wind. This means that full edge-to-edge clipping is not required; clips at each wicket along the top and sides are sufficient to keep the cover in place.

## Frost Damage from Fabric-Leaf Contact

A critical operational warning: row cover fabric must not be allowed to touch the leaves of the crop. When spun-bonded or woven fabric rests directly on plant foliage, it acts as a thermal conductor rather than an insulator. During freezing nights, the fabric itself drops to ambient temperature, and where it contacts leaf tissue, it draws heat directly out of the leaf through conduction. This causes localized frost burn on the leaf surfaces in contact with the fabric, even when the overall temperature under the cover may be above freezing.

This is one of the primary reasons that properly sized wire wickets are essential — they maintain a gap between the fabric and the crop canopy. The wicket height should be set to keep the fabric several inches above the expected crop height throughout the growing season. As crops grow taller through the winter, wickets may need to be replaced with taller ones to maintain this critical clearance gap.

## Lightweight vs. Heavyweight Spun-Bonded Fabric

Coleman conducted comparison trials between lightweight and heavyweight spun-bonded row cover fabrics, with results that challenge common assumptions about winter protection:

| Property | Lightweight Fabric | Heavyweight Fabric |
|---|---|---|
| Light transmission | ~85% | ~50% |
| Insulation value | Lower | Higher |
| Nighttime warmth | Slightly less | Slightly more |
| Daytime warmth | Significantly more | Significantly less |

The counterintuitive finding is that the heavyweight fabric, despite its superior insulating properties, results in **colder overall conditions** for the crop. This is because it blocks 50% of incoming sunlight, preventing the soil and crop from absorbing solar energy during the day. The trapped daytime heat is the primary source of warmth that carries the crop through the night. When a heavyweight fabric blocks too much sunlight, the soil temperature drops progressively over successive cloudy or short-day periods, and even the better nighttime insulation cannot compensate for the energy deficit.

This finding is central to the [[coleman-row-cover-trial-lightweight-heavyweight-sun-run]] philosophy: in winter growing, the ability to capture and store daytime solar energy is more important than nighttime insulation. Coleman recommends lightweight spun-bonded fabrics as the standard choice for interior row covers in winter production.

## PVA (Polyvinyl Alcohol) Row Covers

Coleman also evaluated PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) row covers imported from Japan. These represent the premium tier of row cover technology with distinct performance characteristics:

- **Light transmission**: 90–92%, which is higher than even lightweight spun-bonded polypropylene
- **Durability**: PVA fabric can last up to 8 years with proper handling, compared to 1–3 seasons for standard spun-bonded materials
- **Cost**: Approximately 5 times the price of conventional spun-bonded row covers

The extremely high light transmission of PVA makes it theoretically ideal for the sun-run approach, since it minimizes daytime energy loss. The long service life partially offsets the higher initial cost on a per-season basis, though the upfront investment remains substantial. For a commercial operation calculating cost per season of use, a PVA cover lasting 8 seasons at 5× the price of a spun-bonded cover lasting 2 seasons yields a per-season cost ratio of 5/8 versus 1/2 — or roughly 1.56× the cost per season. Whether this premium is justified depends on the value of the improved light transmission and the labor savings from not having to frequently replace torn or degraded fabric.

PVA row covers are water-soluble to some degree and must be stored carefully to prevent premature degradation from moisture exposure during the off-season. They should be dried thoroughly before storage and kept in a dry, protected location. Despite this handling requirement, their superior tear resistance during daily use makes them an attractive option for operations that can absorb the initial investment.

## The Sun-Run System

The "sun-run system" is the foundational concept underlying Coleman's approach to winter crop protection. The principle states that **the daily cycle of sunlight-driven rewarming of the soil is more important than the quality of nighttime insulation**.

The logic operates as follows:

1. During the day, even weak winter sunlight penetrates the greenhouse glazing and the row cover fabric, warming the soil beneath
2. The soil acts as a thermal mass, storing this heat energy
3. At night, the stored heat radiates upward, keeping the air space under the row cover warmer than the ambient outside temperature
4. If the row cover blocks too much sunlight during the day, the soil does not recharge adequately
5. Over successive days, the soil temperature declines, and eventually no amount of nighttime insulation can prevent freezing

This principle explains why lightweight row covers (85% light transmission) outperform heavyweight ones (50% light transmission) in overall crop protection. It also informs the daily management routine: row covers should be opened on sunny days to maximize solar gain, even if this temporarily exposes the crop to cooler air, because the long-term soil warming benefit outweighs the short-term temperature fluctuation.

The sun-run system reframes winter growing as an energy capture and storage problem rather than an insulation problem. The grower's primary job is to maximize daytime solar energy input; nighttime protection is secondary and relatively easier to achieve.

## Aluminized Cloth Trials

Coleman experimented with aluminized (reflective) row cover cloth, testing the hypothesis that a reflective upper surface would bounce long-wave radiation back toward the crop at night, providing additional warmth beyond what standard row covers could achieve. The trial results were as follows:

- **Temperature benefit**: The aluminized cloth provided approximately 3°F (1.7°C) of additional warmth compared to standard row cover material
- **Crop quality**: No measurable improvement in crop quality or growth rate was observed
- **Labor cost**: The aluminized material was significantly more difficult to handle — it was stiffer, harder to clip to wickets, and more cumbersome to fold and store
- **Overall verdict**: Not worth the additional labor and complexity for the modest temperature gain

The 3°F improvement, while measurable with instruments, did not translate into meaningful differences in harvest timing, yield, or crop quality. Combined with the increased handling difficulty, the aluminized cloth was judged impractical for daily-use row cover applications. The effort required to deploy and manage the material each day outweighed the small thermal benefit. This result reinforced the broader lesson that thermal performance alone does not determine the value of a row cover material — ease of daily handling is equally important in a commercial operation.

## Moisture Film and Radiative Heat Reflection

One of the more subtle thermal mechanisms of row covers involves the moisture film that forms on the underside of the fabric during the night. As the air under the row cover cools, moisture condenses on the inner surface of the fabric, forming a thin film of water. This moisture film has an important radiative property: it reflects long-wave (infrared) radiation back toward the soil and crop.

At night, the warm soil and crop surfaces emit long-wave infrared radiation upward. Without a row cover, this radiation escapes to the cold sky, and the plants cool rapidly. When a row cover is in place, the condensed moisture film on its underside acts as a partial mirror for this outgoing infrared radiation, bouncing a portion of it back toward the crop. This passive radiative reflection contributes to the overall thermal benefit of row covers beyond simple convective insulation.

This mechanism operates most effectively when the row cover is properly sealed at the sides and the interior air is relatively still — conditions that are naturally met inside a greenhouse. The moisture film effect is an additional argument for lightweight, high-light-transmission fabrics: they still develop the condensation film at night (since condensation depends on temperature differential, not fabric weight), while allowing maximum solar energy input during the day.

The moisture film mechanism also has implications for timing of row cover deployment. Covers should be closed in the late afternoon, before the temperature under the cover drops below the dew point, so that the condensation film can form on the fabric surface before the coldest part of the night. If covers are closed too late, the radiative cooling of the soil may have already progressed too far for the moisture film to provide meaningful benefit.
