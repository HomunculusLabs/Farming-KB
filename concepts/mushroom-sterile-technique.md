# Mushroom Sterile Technique

Contamination is the primary cause of failure in mushroom cultivation. A disciplined approach to sterile technique separates successful cultivators from frustrated ones.

Related: [[mushroom-cultivation-basics]], [[mushroom-substrates]]

## Sterile Technique Hierarchy

### 1. Still Air Box (SAB)

The most accessible option for home cultivators. A transparent container (typically a large plastic tote) with two arm holes.

- Cost: $5-20
- Effectiveness: Moderate — reduces airflow but does not filter air
- Best for: Agar transfers, grain-to-grain, spore syringes
- Technique: Wipe interior with 70% isopropyl alcohol. Work slowly. Minimize time with container open. Flame-sterilize tools before each transfer. Spray interior with Lysol or alcohol and wait 5 minutes before working.

### 2. Glove Box

An airtight chamber with rubber gloves or a removable cotton cloth front (Stamets). Provides a semi-sterile still air environment.

- Cost: $50-200 (commercial) or DIY for $20-50
- Effectiveness: Good — no air movement, easy to clean
- Best for: Tissue culture, agar work, sensitive transfers
- Limitation: Awkward hand movement, limited workspace

### 3. Laminar Flow Hood

The gold standard. HEPA filters screen out all particulates exceeding 0.1-0.3 microns — smaller than spores of all fungi and practically all bacteria (Stamets, Appendix IV).

- Cost: $150-400 DIY; $500-2000+ commercial
- Effectiveness: Excellent — provides continuous stream of sterile air
- Best for: All lab work, commercial production, bulk transfers
- Key spec: HEPA filter rated 99.97% efficient at 0.3 microns
- Airflow: 100+ CFM per square foot of filter face; ~800 CFM for a 2x4 ft hood
- Sizing: Standard is 2x4 ft working face; smaller 12x24 hoods for budget builds
- Blower: Squirrel cage fan matched to filter static pressure (~1.0-1.2 in w.g.)
- Testing: Smoke test with incense — smoke should flow in smooth parallel lines; no turbulence or eddies
- See [[laminar-flow-hood-guide]] for complete DIY build instructions

## Surface Sterilization

- **70% isopropyl alcohol** — primary tool. Wipe all surfaces, tools, and containers before work. Flame scalpel blades until red hot. 70% is more effective than 91% because water content aids penetration.
- **Bleach (10% solution)** — for surface cleaning of work areas and decontamination. Not for direct use on cultures.
- **UV sterilization** — Germicidal UV-C (254 nm) can reduce airborne spore load. Aero-disinfectors using triethylene glycol vaporization are another option (Stamets). UV is supplementary, not a replacement for HEPA filtration.
- **Flame sterilization** — inoculation loops, scalpels, and tool tips. Hold in flame until red hot, cool briefly before contacting tissue.
- **Microwave irradiation** — NASA research (MSC-22484, Atwater et al.) demonstrated that 2.45 GHz microwaves effectively sterilize surfaces contaminated with bacteria, yeasts, and molds. The mechanism: microwaves couple with rotational transitions of dipolar water molecules, killing vegetative cells (which contain intrinsic water). Spores are more resistant due to less free water, but can be destroyed by introducing ~9 uL of water per cm2 of surface, which flashes to steam on irradiation. Parameters: 13.1 W-hr total exposure at 3.6 W/cm2 reduced initial populations of 2x10^5 CFU to zero. The technique can even penetrate elastomeric materials to sterilize enclosed systems. This approach has advantages for thermally labile materials since the thermal impact is localized and minimal compared to autoclaving.

## Grain Jar Preparation and Sterilization

1. Measure grain and water: 1 cup rye + 170-200 ml water per quart jar
2. Soak grain for 12-24 hours (optional but improves hydration)
3. Load jars loosely — grain should not be packed. Leave headspace.
4. Cover with filter disc + lid with 3/8 inch hole, or 2 layers of tyvek under ring
5. Pressure cook at 15-18 psi:
   - Quart jars: 60-90 minutes
   - 0.5 gallon jars: 90-120 minutes
   - Bags of grain/sawdust: 2-2.5 hours
6. Allow to cool naturally (do not fast-release pressure — causes grain to burst)
7. Shake jars when cooled to distribute moisture and break up clumps

## Agar Work

Agar is essential for isolating clean genetics, rescuing contaminated cultures, and long-term storage. See [[agar-work-guide]] for comprehensive instructions.

### Media Preparation

Standard formulas (Stamets):
- **PDA (Potato Dextrose Agar)**: 300g sliced potatoes boiled in 1L water, strained, 10g dextrose, 15-20g agar
- **MEA (Malt Extract Agar)**: 20g malt extract, 15-20g agar per liter
- **Light MEA**: 10g malt extract, 15-20g agar per liter — cleaner growth, easier to spot contamination
- **Dog Food Agar**: 1 can plain dog food blended with 1L water, strained, 20g agar — excellent for old/weak spores
- **Grain Water Agar**: Water from boiling grain spawn, 15-20g agar — zero-waste option
- **Yeast supplementation**: 1g yeast extract added to either formula improves nutrition
- **Antibiotics**: gentamicin sulfate survives autoclaving; streptomycin must be filter-sterilized and added after. Use sparingly — some mycelia are adversely affected.

### Pouring Plates

1. Sterilize media in pressure cooker at 15 psi for 30-45 minutes
2. Cool to ~120F (pourable but not hot enough to condense heavily)
3. Pour in still air box or in front of flow hood
4. Fill plates 1/3 full (~20ml per 90mm plate)
5. Allow to solidify with lids slightly cracked to prevent condensation
6. Store inverted at room temperature; use within 2-4 weeks

### Inoculation Techniques

- **Spore syringe**: Single drop at center of plate or streak across surface
- **Agar wedge transfer**: Cut 3-5mm wedge from leading edge of clean growth, place face-down on fresh plate
- **Tissue culture**: Tear fruiting body open (don't cut through skin), take tiny inner tissue piece with flame-sterilized scalpel

### Sectoring and Isolation

Spore-grown mycelium shows sectors (wedge-shaped zones of different genetics). Transfer the fastest, most rhizomorphic (ropy) sector repeatedly (3-5 times) to isolate a monoculture. Rhizomorphic growth generally indicates stronger fruiting potential than cottony (tomentose) growth.

### Long-Term Storage

- **Refrigerator**: Parafilm-sealed plates at 35-38F; transfer every 6-12 months
- **Slants**: Agar in test tubes cooled at angle; store 6-12 months at room temp, 1-2 years refrigerated
- **Mineral oil**: Sterile mineral oil over colonized slant; storage 5-20 years

### Transferring Culture

1. Sterilize work area and tools with 70% alcohol
2. Flame scalpel until red hot, cool in sterile agar or air
3. Cut small wedge (3-5mm) from donor plate, cutting ahead of the growth front
4. Place wedge face-down on fresh plate
5. Seal plate with parafilm if available
6. Incubate at species-appropriate temperature in darkness

## Contamination Identification

| Contaminant | Appearance | Response |
|------------|-----------|----------|
| Trichoderma (green mold) | Fast-growing forest green, cottony then granular | Most common. Discard immediately. Do not open indoors. |
| Aspergillus | Green, yellow, or black powdery colonies | Discard. Spores spread easily. |
| Penicillium | Blue-green, powdery | Discard. Common on agar plates. |
| Botrytis (brown mold) | Gray-brown, fuzzy growth on casing layers | Reduce humidity, improve air exchange. |
| Bacteria (wet spot) | Slimy, sour-smelling, cloudy on agar | Often from grain too wet or poor sterilization. |
| Cobweb mold | Fine, spiderweb-like gray mycelium | Increase FAE. Sometimes confused with mushroom mycelium. |

## Contamination Prevention

- Work clean: shower, wear clean clothes, tie back hair
- Reduce airborne spores: wet-mop floors, avoid working during cleaning
- Do not open contaminated cultures in your workspace
- Maintain proper moisture content — too wet favors bacteria
- Use appropriate sterilization times; under-sterilization is the most common error
- Inoculate quickly but deliberately; minimize open time
- Keep cultures at appropriate temperature during incubation
- Use filter-equipped lids on all spawn containers
