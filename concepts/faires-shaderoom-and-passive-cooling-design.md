## The Shaderoom

The shaderoom is essentially a fancy patio located on the shady side of the house. Key design elements:

- Walls must be made of sturdy reinforced trellising
- Trellises should be grown with vines such as grapes
- A water system can be installed to spray a vine mist
- A regular roof is optional; vine-covered trellis walls are essential
- A doorway connects the shaderoom to the house

### How It Works

During summer, open the door between the shaderoom and the house and turn on the mist system. Water evaporating from the mist cools the air flowing into the house. In a super hot or tropical climate, the shaderoom can extend to porches around the entire house, creating a continuous cooling zone.

A water tank can be kept in the shaderoom for passive thermal storage. The cool water moderates temperature swings.

## Climate-Specific Design Strategies

### Hot Climates

- Palm trees can shade the roof with very little watering
- Keep paved areas small immediately around the house
- Cover larger areas with grass or other green groundcover
- Pavement and gravel reflect heat back onto the house -- avoid large expanses
- A separate kitchen placed near the shaderoom keeps cooking heat out of the house
- A covered barbecue area with outdoor sink and tables attached to the cool shaderoom provides pleasant summer cooking
- Leave windows open at night with screens and bed netting for bug protection
- Paint the roof white to reflect heat
- Use a steep roof angle to allow rain to run off
- In hurricane/monsoon zones, use deep underground anchors
- In desert climates, consider a flat roof with shaderoom and clothesline

### Cold Climates

- A small house footprint with steep roof sheds snow effectively
- Several stories or attachment to a warm building (like a barn) reduces exposed surface area
- The option to close off the greenhouse from the rest of the house is essential
- A wall partition between greenhouse and bathroom allows temperature control

### Very Cold Climates

- Snow can damage roofs -- steep pitch is critical
- Condensation on windows causes mold -- ventilation is essential
- Dark curtains or wood shutters at night minimize heat loss
- A vent with an electric fan prevents overheating and excessive humidity, even in winter
- Multiple stories or attached buildings reduce heat loss through exterior walls

## Passive Heating and Cooling Principles

### Thermal Mass (Heat Banks)

- Tile floors and brick walls act as heat banks in winter when sun hits them
- They radiate stored heat back into the house at night
- In summer, let cool night air hit the thermal mass -- it radiates coolness during the day
- This 12-hour thermal cycle matches the solar day-night cycle perfectly

### Attached Greenhouse Heating

An attached greenhouse provides passive solar heating:
- Build on the sun-facing side of the house (south in Northern Hemisphere)
- Use concrete, brick, or stone for passive heat storage
- Double-paned windows maintain heat longer than single sheets
- Wooden window frames are better than metal (less thermal bridging)
- Water containers (20-gallon) on shelves or under growing benches provide thermal mass
- Rabbits or chickens kept in the greenhouse during winter add heat
- Composting biomass generates additional heat
- The greenhouse side attached to the house for warmth makes winter growing possible

### Cooling Through Ventilation

- Cross-ventilation through shaderoom and opposite windows
- Stack effect: hot air rises and exits high vents, pulling cool air through low openings
- Evaporative cooling through vine mist systems
- Shading from deciduous vines (shade in summer, drop leaves for sun in winter)
- Earth tubes or buried pipes cool incoming air

## Rainwater in Dry Climates

Rainwater is even more critical in dry climates. Desert landforms (steep mesas and canyons) can be used:
- Dams and storage tanks at the top of mesas provide gravity-fed water
- The house can be set directly into the rock mid-slope
- Water flows by gravity from the dam and storage tank down to the dwelling
- Irrigation channels follow natural contours

## Retrofitting an Existing House

Most affordable homes are energy-inefficient. Key improvements:
1. Caulk and seal all windows and doors
2. Add insulation to the attic or roof
3. Add a greenhouse along one wall, ideally opening into a stone or brick bathroom
4. Build a brick or rock wall inside facing windows for passive heat storage
5. Attach a shaderoom on the shady side
6. Replace appliances with energy-efficient options (European brands exceed North American standards)
7. Add solar hot water heater and on-demand water heater

## Grape Species and Selection

Faires recommends two main species for the home grower, both of which are far hardier than typical cultivated wine grapes:

**Fox Grape (Vitis labrusca):** Self-pollinating and adaptable to any climate. Fox grapes are naturally resistant to many pests and diseases that plague cultivated varieties, making them ideal for low-input permaculture systems. They are the parent of many North American hybrid grapes and have the characteristic "foxy" flavor.

**Muscadine Grape (Vitis rotundifolia):** Native to the southeastern United States and requiring the warm, humid climate found in the South. Muscadines are NOT self-pollinating — you must plant several vines to ensure both male and female varieties are present for fruit set. They are among the most disease-resistant grapes available and produce thick-skinned, highly nutritious fruit.

## Growing Requirements

- **Sun and heat:** Grapes require full sun and a long, hot growing season to produce fruit. They enjoy the reflected heat of the south side of a house, making them excellent candidates for shaderoom trellises and pergolas.
- **Watering:** Regular watering is needed in well-drained soil. Grapes will not tolerate waterlogged conditions.
- **Trellising:** Grapes grow heavy and tree-like within a few years. The trellis must be extremely strong — this is not a plant for flimsy structures. In a few years the vine can become massive. See [[faires-growing-and-season-extension]] for trellising strategies.
- **Pruning:** More effort in pruning is required than for many other fruit crops, but the yield makes it worthwhile.

## Cold Climate Challenges

Grapes grow almost anywhere as plants, but they do not produce fruit unless they have a long, hot growing season. This is the primary challenge in northern climates. The entire vine must be laid flat on the ground and mulched for winter, and the buds must be protected from frost every year. In marginal climates, it may not be worth the effort compared to other fruit crops.
