# Nitrogen-Fixing Trees in Permaculture

## Overview

Nitrogen-fixing trees are among the most valuable elements in any permaculture
design. Through symbiotic relationships with soil bacteria, these trees convert
atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms, fertilizing the soil for
themselves and neighboring plants.

They serve as nurse crops, windbreaks, fodder sources, timber producers, and
bee forage, making them truly multipurpose design elements.

## The Nitrogen Fixation Process

Nitrogen makes up approximately 78 percent of the Earth's atmosphere, but this
atmospheric nitrogen is unavailable to most plants in its gaseous form (N2).
Nitrogen-fixing trees solve this problem through symbiotic relationships with
bacteria in their root systems.

Leguminous trees (Fabaceae family) form nodules on their roots that host
Rhizobium bacteria. These bacteria possess the enzyme nitrogenase, which can
break the triple bond of atmospheric N2 and convert it into ammonium (NH4+), a
form that plants can absorb.

The tree provides the bacteria with sugars produced through photosynthesis,
while the bacteria provide the tree with nitrogen. This mutualistic exchange
is one of the most important biological processes on Earth.

Non-leguminous nitrogen fixers, such as alders (Alnus spp), form similar
relationships with actinomycete bacteria of the genus Frankia. Casuarina and
Allocasuarina species also fix nitrogen through Frankia associations.

## Why Nitrogen-Fixing Trees Matter

Nitrogen is typically the most limiting nutrient in agricultural and
ecological systems. Without adequate nitrogen, plant growth is stunted and
ecosystem productivity is reduced.

Conventional agriculture addresses this through synthetic nitrogen fertilizers,
which require enormous energy inputs to manufacture and cause water pollution
when excess nitrogen runs off into waterways.

Nitrogen-fixing trees provide a biological alternative running on free solar
energy. A healthy stand can supply enough nitrogen to support productive
orchards, pastures, or food forests without external fertilizer inputs.

## Leguminous Pioneer Trees

### Tagasaste ([[chamaecytisus-palmensis]])

Tagasaste is a nitrogen fixing legume from the Canary Islands, growing to 7
meters in low-rainfall areas (300mm). It provides stock feed with crude
protein of 17-31 percent of dry matter.

As a nurse crop, tagasaste improves growth of fruit and nut trees planted
alongside it. It flowers in winter providing bee forage, and its prolific seed
production provides chicken forage.

### Black Locust ([[robinia-pseudoacacia]])

Black locust is a fast growing, nitrogen fixing legume reaching 25 meters and
living up to 200 years. Native to the Appalachian and Ozark mountains, its
foliage is readily eaten by livestock.

It produces honey regarded as one of the finest in the world. The timber is
hard, heavy, and extremely rot resistant, widely used for fence posts, mine
timbers, and railway sleepers. It coppices well and burns like coal.

Commonly planted for erosion control and shelter belts, its main drawback is
a suckering habit, though non-suckering cultivars exist.

### Honey Locust ([[gleditsia-triacanthos]])

Honey locust is a moderately fast growing, long-lived legume reaching 24-30
meters. Its deep root system provides exceptional drought tolerance, and it
tolerates alkaline and acid soils as well as salt.

The sweet, nutritious pods are relished by all classes of stock: pigs, sheep,
cattle, and poultry. Being deciduous, it allows winter sunlight through to
pasture or understory plants.

Branches form thorns that protect against ring barking by stock, though
thornless cultivars are available. The wood is hard, strong, and durable.

### Leucaena ([[leucaena-leucocephala]])

Often called the fastest growing tree in the tropics, leucaena reaches over 20
meters. It withstands rainfalls as low as 250mm but prefers 600-3000mm. Young
leaves, pods, and flower buds are edible by humans.

As stock feed, leucaena produces some of the highest weight gains ever
recorded, recovering from browsing in just two weeks. However, it should form
only about 30 percent of the long-term diet due to mimosine toxicity.

The timber has similar density and strength to oak. Giant forms provide shade
as a classic nurse tree to crops like cacao.

### Rosewood ([[tipuana-tipu]])

Pride of Bolivia is a fast growing nitrogen fixing tree reaching 40 meters. In
warm climates evergreen, deciduous in cold. Grown as a nurse crop for coffee
and shade-loving crops. Its timber is highly prized for furniture in Argentina.

## Non-Leguminous Nitrogen Fixers

### Alders (Alnus spp)

Alders are remarkable because, despite not being legumes, they fix up to 300
kilograms of nitrogen per hectare per year through root nodules formed by
Schinzia alni bacteria. They are mostly deciduous and many are notable
softwood timber trees.

Wide-spaced alder planting has been known to increase fodder production
several-fold through nitrogen fixation and shelter.

[[alnus-glutinosa]] (common alder) grows along watercourses and in wet swampy
lands, even tolerating salt or brackish water. Its wood grows harder in water,
making it ideal for waterside structures, pumps, and sluices.

[[alnus-rubra]] (Oregon alder) is one of the most productive trees in North
America at 10-11 cubic meters per hectare per year on a 20-30 year rotation.
Its vigorous growth makes it an ideal nurse crop.

### Casuarinas

Casuarinas fix nitrogen through Frankia bacteria associations. Casuarina
cunninghamiana (river sheoak) is a fast growing timber tree to 35 meters.
Casuarina glauca (swamp sheoak) tolerates saline and waterlogged conditions.

Casuarina equisetifolia (coast sheoak) is widely used for sand dune
stabilization in the tropics.

## Integration into Permaculture Designs

Nitrogen-fixing trees should be integrated throughout the landscape. In Zone
1, smaller nitrogen fixers like clover and pea shrubs support vegetable
gardens. In Zone 2, medium fixers support fruit trees and berry bushes.

In Zones 3 and 4, large nitrogen-fixing trees support orchards, pastures, and
timber systems. A key design principle is to place nitrogen fixers upslope or
upwind of nutrient-demanding plants.

This positioning ensures leaf litter and root-zone nitrogen flow toward the
beneficiaries. In a food forest, nitrogen fixers are interspersed among fruit
and nut trees at a ratio of roughly one nitrogen fixer for every two to four
productive trees.

Nitrogen fixers also serve as windbreaks, erosion controllers, fodder banks,
and bee forage. Their ability to improve soil while producing multiple yields
makes them indispensable in any sustainable land-use system.
