# Forests and the Atmosphere

Forests exert profound and complex influences on atmospheric conditions, including wind patterns, rainfall, humidity, air quality, and energy exchange. The relationships between forests and the atmosphere are far more intricate than commonly understood, and removing forests triggers cascading effects on climate, soil, water, and ultimately the viability of human civilizations.

## Wind Interaction

### Wind Absorption and Force Dissipation

Wind completely disappears within an effective forest at approximately 1,000 meters. The forest absorbs the total force of even gale-force winds within this distance, except at the crown where some effect persists. The energy of the wind is converted within the forest — likely into wood production, as anchored trees maintain constant stem diameter while wind-exposed trees rapidly increase theirs, suggesting wind aids transpiration, cell production, or some other growth process.

### Rainfall Enhancement

The forest forces approximately 60% of incoming wind upward, creating a high-pressure zone on the windward side with decreased evaporation. The forest face toward the wind catches significantly more rain than the leeward side. This upward deflection has been measured to increase rainfall by 15-20% in studies conducted in Holland and Sweden. When forests are cut, regional rainfall decreases by 10-30%.

A secondary effect occurs when forced-up wind creates sidewise spiraling, producing belts of rain extending several tree lengths past the forest edge transverse to wind direction. This creates a pattern of wet and dry zones past tree belts at intervals of approximately five tree lengths. The descending winds on the leeward side are warmer, less humid, more turbulent, and cause drying.

### Effective Windbreak Design

For a tree belt to function as an effective windbreak, it needs to be approximately five trees wide. A single belt at 40% penetrability still has measurable effect. When only about 40% of wind passes through a tree belt, wind diminishes rapidly within 100-200 meters and becomes negligible. The hedge row principle — where vegetation comes to ground level or starts above a stone wall — is preferred over trimmed hedges, which can create undesirable wind tunnel effects below the canopy.

### Humidity Interception

On foggy nights, air moving into the forest reduces humidity in the passing air mass by about 50% within 100 meters — a phenomenon called **positive interception**. This is considered a major factor for all coastal forests and ridges within fifty miles of the coast. When warm, humid night air blows into forested areas, the relatively cool leaf surfaces cause moisture to precipitate rapidly across the enormous combined leaf surface area.

In Tasmania, up to 60% of total precipitation is attributed to this interception effect. Only 14% falls as conventional rain — trees catch 86%. Screens erected to imitate trees create measurably higher precipitation. The effect is so significant that removing trees reduces measurable rainfall at gauges by over 15%, yet only 14% of moisture originally came from conventional rainfall — meaning the forest effectively generates most of its own precipitation.

### Particle Filtration

Wind-carried particles — dust, industrial pollutants, and other aerosols — are reduced to approximately one-quarter of their previous concentration after passing through about 100 meters of forest. This represents tons of material, particularly from winds crossing soils and industrial areas. Forests are net absorbers of inorganic materials and net releasers of organic materials (pollens, bacteria, organic droplets).

Historical accounts illustrate the scale of organic particle release: early voyagers approaching the North American coast found pollen coating ship decks up to 80 miles offshore. The sea turned yellow with pollen. Crews mistook it for sulfur from volcanic eruptions and advanced with trepidation through "sulfurous" yellow skies.

Organic particles are far more effective precipitation nuclei than inorganic particles, and are suspected to be the primary factors in atmospheric precipitation. While forests absorb inorganic particles from the air, they release organic particles that travel downwind and enable rain condensation further inland. Approximately 60% of inland rain falls from forest-derived clouds rather than maritime clouds.

## Rainfall and the Forest Canopy

### Energy Absorption

The forest canopy absorbs almost all the energy of rainfall. A violent thunderstorm enters a forest in leaf as nothing more than a fine mist. The mechanical energy of tons of water falling at high velocity is completely dissipated in the crown through frictional slowing, impact absorption, and wind entanglement. This has profound consequences:

- **Erosion prevention**: The pelting force of rain, an enormous erosive agent, simply does not reach the forest floor
- **Evaporative cooling**: Light rain may be completely absorbed by the canopy and evaporated without ever reaching the ground — roads under trees remain dry during light rain
- **Energy transactions**: Multiple energy exchanges occur simultaneously at the crown — friction, absorption, reflection, evaporation

### Water Distribution Through the Canopy

Different tree species channel water in different ways:

- **Bark channeling**: Some trees funnel 10-20 times the actual rainfall volume down their bark
- **Crown drip**: Other trees distribute water around the crown as circular rainfall
- **Mixed forest effect**: In diverse forests, rain falls in every direction — dripping outward, running under branches, funneling down trunk crevices
- **Snow interception**: Each tree species intercepts snow in unique ways based on branch structure and crystalline interactions

## The Forest as a Water Storage System

### Biomass Water Content

The forest is an enormous water mass. Approximately 86% of forest mass is water, and 96% of leaves and twigs consist of water. This represents a tremendous weight on the Earth's surface — forests function as vertical water tanks of staggering capacity.

### Soil Water Storage

Below the canopy, the forest floor provides additional water storage:

1. **Litter and [[humus]] layer**: Absorbs water until fully charged, representing a significant mass of stored water (potentially 6 inches to a foot)
2. **Mineral soil**: Every foot of soil holds approximately one inch of rain; with 30 inches of soil, a 3-inch rainfall produces zero surface movement
3. **Root channels**: Old rotted root traces create conduits allowing rapid percolation to deeper levels
4. **Deep storage**: Up to 60 inches of rain can be held in deep forest soils with good structure

Water entering the forest always travels downward initially, and only moves upward as pure water released to the atmosphere through transpiration. In a forest, water never travels upward to the soil surface for evaporation. This means:

- No salting occurs in forest soils
- No upward migration of salts to the surface
- Essential nutrients drawn up as salts are fixed within the forest biomass

### Leaf Absorption

Much forest water enters through leaves, not just roots. Leaves directly absorb moisture and dissolved substances from the air, and manufacture nutrients as materials pass inward into the tree. Branches can break under the weight of accumulated water on still, humid nights when transpiration ceases — the leaf water mass exceeds the structural capacity of branches.

### Runoff Characteristics

Forest runoff is extremely slow. The forest floor contains twigs, leaves, and debris in immense quantities that repeatedly halt and divert water flow. Water follows convoluted paths through the forest, persisting in the landscape far longer than in open areas where water "just goes whistling off."

Engineers who cut forests to increase reservoir runoff are making a fundamental error: while short-term runoff increases, total rainfall drops to roughly 70% of pre-cut levels, ultimately reducing the total water available.

## Mineral Cycling and Soil Protection

### Salt and Mineral Retention

Forests collect, hold, and cycle essential minerals — particularly calcium — through their biomass. When forests are cut, these minerals enter runoff and flow to the sea. Measurements in Tasmania show that cut-over forest land may yield more tonnage of dissolved salts (particularly calcium) than actual silts. Forests accumulated these minerals slowly over centuries; their loss through clearing is essentially irreversible on human timescales.

### Snow Melt Regulation

Forests delay snowmelt by at least a month compared to open ground. Without forest cover, winter snow melts extremely quickly, causing sudden flooding. The forest holds winter precipitation and releases it gradually over extended periods, providing sustained streamflow rather than destructive floods.

## Energy Transactions and Light

### Solar Energy Management

Forests cannot be treated as a uniform mass — they are collections of individuals that manage light individually:

- **Sumac**: On a sunny day with light wind, sumac shifts from being a light absorber to a reflector, changing its entire energy balance dynamically
- **Aspen**: Uses both wind-based and orientation-based energy management systems
- **Ivy**: Continuously adjusts orientation surfaces relative to sunlight
- **Species with shiny underleaves**: Trade off wind and light energy management

In some Tasmanian forests, light is completely intercepted — descending 200 feet into valleys reveals no measurable light. Forests filter ultraviolet light and transmit more red light, creating a different light quality within.

### Thermal Regulation

Dark trees become radiators; birches serve as reflectors. Some species function as heat storage systems, with 86% water-based heat storage. On cold nights, cold air entering the upper forest creates a slow downdraft through thousands of enormous water-filled thermal masses that have absorbed heat all day, producing warm air at ground level.

### Air Quality

The quality of air moving through forests changes markedly:

- **Negative ions** increase sharply in forest air streams
- **Carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide** are absorbed efficiently by forest vegetation
- A relatively small plant in an office can handle much of the CO₂ and CO problems
- Negative ions are excellent precipitators, accounting for the disappearance of dust particles in forests

## Root Systems: The Hidden Forest

At least 40% of a forest's mass exists below the soil surface. Roots are enormous storage organs that:

- Throw masses upward toward the surface and pull them back seasonally
- Live and die within the soil, creating channels and pathways that greatly affect water movement
- Break down primary rock material
- Represent a biomass component often omitted from forestry calculations

## The Consequences of Deforestation

The removal of forests triggers cascading collapse:

- **Desertification**: Removal of ridge forests alone can produce deserts in any climate
- **Soil loss**: Forest removal removes soils
- **Civilizational collapse**: The removal of forests has been the main cause of the collapse of nations — without forests, there is insufficient water, soil, or climate quality to sustain human life

The continents themselves are affected: forests represent an enormous water mass, and loading and unloading this mass through deforestation can cause Earth movements. Modest dams are known to cause local earthquakes; the far greater water mass of continental forests, when removed, may contribute to geological instability.
