# Mushroom Iconography in Medieval European Art

## The Paradise Tree and German Medieval Tradition

During Germany's Middle Ages, a popular play symbolic of the paradise story was
staged on December 24th, the religious feast day of Adam and Eve. Central to
this tradition was "The Paradise tree," a fir tree decorated with apples
representing the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge in the Garden of Eden. As James
Arthur demonstrates, this "fruit" is the Amanita muscaria mushroom. Two more
symbolic connections with the mushroom were the candles and cookies also hung on
the tree, where candles represent Christ and cookies represent the wafers of
the Christian sacrament.

This ornament tradition indicates that there have always been people "in the
know" from time to time. According to Apocryphal texts and other older sources,
the "Fruit of the Tree of Life" and the more recently added "Fruit of the Tree
of Knowledge" were originally one and the same. The tradition of bringing
coniferous trees into homes and placing gifts underneath them preserves, in
ritual form, the actual location where the Amanita muscaria grows in nature --
in a mycorrhizal relationship with pine tree roots.

## The 13th Century Fresco of France

A 13th Century fresco from France explicitly depicts the Amanita muscaria as the
"fruit" of the Tree of the "knowledge of Good and Evil." In this medieval
artwork, Adam and Eve had their eyes opened after consuming the mushroom. The
Gods looked down and said "Behold, they have become as one of us." Contemporary
scholars and researchers have since uncovered many such representations. The
mushroom as the fruit of the tree is actually quite common among esoteric
circles, and medieval European art contains numerous such depictions that have
survived through the centuries.

## Christian Adoption of Pagan Symbolism

Christmas icons, traditions and stories from the European Middle Ages have
hidden meanings that, upon closer investigation, reveal far more symbolic
content than originally suspected. At the roots of this symbolism is information
about the secrets of the mushroom -- its habitats, forms, uses, preparations,
and effects. The Christmas traditions are well documented as Pagan in origin,
yet it is easy to obscure the Egyptian, Mithraic, Germanic, Norse, Celtic,
Greek, Hindu and Buddhist roots by lumping all non-Christian religions together
and labeling them Pagan. These are certainly not simple country-folk religions.

Christianity was the product of a governmental council acquiring and examining
as many of the world's religious doctrines as they could find, in order to
create a "One World Religion." They took ideas and doctrine from Egyptology,
Mithraism, Hinduism, Buddhism, the Torah of the Jews, and many others, picking
the parts they wanted, altering them as they wished, and discarding the parts
they did not want. Since this was a plagiarized and altered conglomeration of
many older works, many symbolic tenets managed to retain their original
meanings.

## The Fruit of the Tree in Medieval Christian Art

The knowledge of good and evil seems a strange thing to condemn someone for,
unless the gods wanted to dictate what was right and wrong rather than have them
know the difference for themselves. In the original Sumerian cuneiform
tablets, the eating of the fruit was portrayed in a completely different light
-- what was originally a glorious story about the hope and potential of
humanity became, in later adaptations, a primary story of a horrible fall from
grace and condemnation of the entire race.

Medieval European artists, working within a Christian framework, nonetheless
preserved the mushroom imagery in their depictions of the Garden of Eden, the
Tree of Life, and the fruit of knowledge. These artists were part of a
tradition stretching back through the Egyptian mystery schools and the
Rosicrucians, Freemasons, and Templars -- secret initiatory orders that
elaborated upon and used as a model the "tiers of learning" which the Egyptian
mystery schools incorporated.

## The Virgin Birth and Seedless Growth

The Virgin Birth, central to Christian iconography throughout the medieval
period, is symbolic for the "seedless" growth/germination pattern of the
mushroom. To the ancient and medieval mind, with no microscope to see the
spores, the mushroom's appearance was thought to be miraculous -- it seemed to
spring from the ground without any visible seed. This botanical reality
provided the basis for one of the most enduring symbols in all of Christian
art, reproduced endlessly in medieval cathedrals, illuminated manuscripts, and
fresco cycles.

## The Doctrine of Transubstantiation

The Catholic church, in the early 1100s, decided to have the final word on the
nature of the sacrament by establishing, under Emperor/Pope Innocent III, the
"Doctrine of Trans-Substantiation." This is whereby the Priests, by their
assumed holy power, claim to be able to say some magical words and turn
ordinary bread into the literal "Body of Christ." In the original tradition,
this substance (Body/Soma) was the Amanita muscaria mushroom itself. The
sacramental wafers and cookies of medieval Christian practice, hung on the
Paradise tree during German Christmas plays, preserve this connection.

## Medieval Mushroom Motifs in Religious Contexts

Mushroom-associated symbolism is found throughout medieval religious artwork.
Amanita muscaria is a "good luck charm," associated with the four-leafed
clover in artwork and traditional folklore. It is not restricted to pictorial
art in obvious places. The mushroom appears in contexts ranging from cathedral
architecture (where pillars are shaped like giant mushrooms) to illuminated
manuscripts, stained glass windows, and stone carvings.

The symbols that survived from Winter Solstice celebrations of old have a
commonality that deserves study and reverence. Understanding that these
traditions are borrowed ones is central to getting at the heart of their true
meaning. The medieval European artists who encoded mushroom symbolism into
Christian artwork were part of a long chain of initiates who preserved the
knowledge while fearing persecution for speaking openly.

## The Transformation of Symbols Through the Ages

The Santa Claus and Christmas traditions of today have metamorphosed out of
many older mythologies. The icons, symbols, and relics that have managed to
survive from the Winter Solstice celebrations of old, through the medieval
period, have a commonality that reveals their shared origin. Medieval European
art stands as a crucial bridge between the ancient mushroom-worshipping
cultures and the modern holiday traditions that preserve, in distorted form,
the original entheogenic meaning.
