# Mushroom Nutrition

The nutritional biology of cultivated mushrooms reveals a fungus that is
far more adaptable than traditional practice suggests. While commercial
growing has always depended on fermented stable manure, laboratory
experiments demonstrate that [[agaricus-campestris]]
can grow on chemically defined synthetic media, raising important
questions about what the mushroom truly requires.

## The Role of Fermentation

The central paradox of mushroom nutrition is that fermentation, which is
absolutely necessary for commercial success, is probably more chemically
harmful than beneficial to the manure. When fresh manure is sterilized
and inoculated with pure mushroom mycelium in the laboratory, growth is
faster and more profuse than in fermented manure treated identically.

Fermentation is therefore a biological requirement, not a chemical one.
Fresh manure contains readily soluble, unstable organic compounds that
support intense bacterial activity. This bacterial bloom creates an
environment so hostile that mushroom mycelium cannot establish itself,
even when the manure quantity is too small to generate significant heat.
Fermentation removes or transforms these fermentable products and
modifies the straw structure so that the mushroom mycelium can grow
slowly enough to compete with whatever microorganisms remain.

## Synthetic Media

Agaricus campestris mycelium grows well on completely synthetic nutrient
media prepared from known chemical substances, including standard
fertilizer salts supplemented with casein or peptone. Laboratory
experiments further indicate that all nitrogen requirements can be met
with inorganic salts alone, provided a suitable organic carbon source is
available. This demonstrates that the mushroom is not dependent on any
mysterious qualities unique to manure.

## Organic and Inorganic Nutrients

The once-prevalent belief that the "ammonia" content of manure
constitutes the sole important fertilizer for mushrooms is erroneous.
The mushroom utilizes both gradually soluble organic products and
essential inorganic salts from its substrate. However, manure treated so
as to lose much of its free ammonia is genuinely diminished as a growing
medium.

## Fertilizer Additives

Experiments with mineral nutrient supplements to manure have produced
contradictory results, largely because of the inherent variability of
both manure quality and spawn vigor. The addition of phosphorus (as
phosphate) and lime has often proven advantageous. Cotton seed meal
fermented together with manure has shown promise for increasing yields,
but practical large-scale cooperative trials with commercial growers had
not yet been completed at the time of Duggar's research.

The difficulty of conducting controlled nutritional experiments in
commercial settings, combined with the variable quality of both spawn and
compost, has historically prevented adequate study of mushroom nutrition
on a practical basis, despite extensive preliminary laboratory work.

## Food Value

Mushrooms are approximately 90 percent water. Their dry matter consists
primarily of protein and carbohydrate, with small amounts of fat and ash.
They contain several B vitamins and are valued as a flavorful,
low-calorie food. The protein, while present in modest quantities, is
of reasonable biological quality. Their principal culinary value lies in
their distinctive flavor and texture rather than their caloric or
protein contribution.


## Nutritional Strategies by Species

Different mushroom species have evolved distinct nutritional strategies.
Primary decomposers like oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.) can colonize
fresh lignocellulose directly, while secondary decomposers like Agaricus
bisporus require substrate that has been partially broken down by
composting microorganisms. This distinction determines cultivation
approaches: primary decomposers grow on pasteurized straw or sawdust,
while secondary decomposers need fully composted substrate with specific
microbial communities established during the composting process.

Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N) is a critical parameter in mushroom
substrate formulation. Most cultivated species prefer a C:N ratio between
25:1 and 40:1. Too much nitrogen promotes bacterial contamination and
ammonia accumulation, which is toxic to mycelium. Too little nitrogen
limits mycelial growth and reduces yields. Supplementing substrates with
nitrogen-rich materials (bran, legume hay, or commercial supplements) can
increase yields by 30-100%, but requires careful calculation to avoid
exceeding the species' tolerance for free ammonia.
