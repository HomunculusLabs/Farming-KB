# Food Civilization Critique

Masanobu Fukuoka's critique of modern civilization centered on food -- how it is grown, how it is processed, and how it shapes both the body and the mind. His analysis went beyond nutrition to encompass the entire relationship between human culture and the natural world. This critique is deeply connected to [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] and [[fukuoka-critique-agricultural-development]].

## The Reversal of Diets

Fukuoka observed a remarkable historical reversal in dietary patterns between East and West. After World War II, the Japanese abandoned their traditional grain-and-vegetable diet in favor of Western-style meat and processed foods. Meanwhile, many Westerners were beginning to adopt vegetarian and natural food diets.

"People in Japan used to look in envy at the proud Westerners and the thick steaks they were eating in the restaurants, and think, 'Gee, I'd like to try eating steak like that just once.' Well today they are eating steak, while Westerners have taken to vegetarianism. This reversal in diet seems to suggest something."

This reversal was not merely a culinary trend but a symptom of a deeper civilizational confusion -- a failure to understand the relationship between food, health, and cultural identity. See [[fukuoka-natural-diet-theory]].

## Diet Shapes Mind and Body

Fukuoka believed that food directly shapes not only physical health but mental disposition and cultural character. "Confusion over diet confuses the body and the mind. It affects everything. The health of the body comes from the diet. And thought arises from the body."

He observed that a diet with lots of meat results in acidic blood while a vegetarian diet makes the blood alkaline. "Alkaline blood gives one a gentle, peaceful disposition. Grain-eating peoples become quiet and peace-loving. When hunting and fishing races eat meat, their blood becomes acidic. One is masculine, active, and aggressive."

This analysis led Fukuoka to a provocative conclusion: the aggressive expansionism of Western civilization was, at least in part, a product of its meat-heavy diet. "This assertiveness helped the West conquer the entire world at one time. Largely vegetarian races -- such as the Oriental and Negroid races -- were suppressed and defeated."

## The Loss of Traditional Japanese Food

Fukuoka mourned the disappearance of traditional Japanese food culture. The "five grains" -- rice, barley, foxtail millet, proso millet, and barnyard millet -- had been the staples of Japanese farming communities for centuries. Today, only rice and barley are widely grown.

"The primitive, small-seed crops closest to nature, such as the millets, the azuki bean, and buckwheat, are best for human health," he wrote. "But in our modern world, everyone yearns after what is large and tasty. 'Bigger is better' seems to apply today to fruits as well as grains."

The preference for large, visually appealing produce over nutritionally dense, modest crops reflects a civilizational value system that prizes appearance over substance, quantity over quality, and human desire over natural order. This connects to [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]].

## Petroleum-Based Food

Perhaps Fukuoka's most alarming observation was that modern Japanese food had become essentially petroleum-based. "The foods eaten here have become Western-style petroleum-based products," he wrote. "That includes everything from vegetables such as tomatoes, eggplants, and cucumbers, to rice and wheat, and even fish."

Chemical fertilizers are derived from petroleum. Pesticides are derived from petroleum. Food processing relies on petroleum-based additives and packaging. Transportation of food over long distances consumes petroleum. In this sense, modern industrial agriculture is a system for converting petroleum into food -- and when the petroleum runs out, so does the food.

This petroleum dependency makes modern civilization existentially fragile. An agricultural system that cannot produce food without fossil fuels is not truly agriculture at all but a form of industrial manufacturing that happens to produce calories.

## The Japanese Farmer's Kitchen

Despite his critiques, Fukuoka held deep respect for the traditional Japanese farmer's kitchen. "No other people originally had such a sharp sense of taste and were as good at cooking as the Japanese," he wrote. "I would even venture to suggest that the skill of the farmer's wife in flavoring food would allow her to pass for a master chef anywhere in the world."

But even this culinary tradition was being undermined by the decline in food quality. "No matter how skilled the cook, if the materials he works with are no good, there is nothing he can do."

The degradation of ingredients -- through chemical farming, long-distance transport, and industrial processing -- has destroyed the foundation upon which traditional cuisine was built. A civilization can preserve its recipes but not its food culture when the food itself has lost its vitality. See [[fukuoka-food-and-human-health]].

## Natural Food vs Processed Food

The fundamental distinction in Fukuoka's food philosophy is between food that is grown naturally and food that is produced industrially. Natural food carries the vitality of the soil and the season. Processed food is stripped of its vitality and filled with artificial substitutes.

"The Japanese farmer did not ask what food to grow and eat. He simply harvested and ate what grew naturally in his fields in each passing season. And he cooked this food in accordance with the principles of nature."

Modern food, by contrast, is designed to satisfy cravings rather than nourish the body. "Man thinks he has made time and space his, but people today no longer have any time." This is why they are delighted with instant foods. "As a result, food has lost its essence as real food and become only a concoction."

## The Four Types of Diet

Fukuoka identified four major types of diet, representing stages of awareness:

1. A lax, self-indulgent diet influenced by the external world, that submits to cravings and fancies -- an "empty diet" that is "the root of a thousand diseases."

2. The physically centered diet of most people, where nutritional food is consumed to sustain the body -- a "scientific diet that spins centrifugally outward with increasing desires."

3. The diet of natural man based on spiritual laws, centered on Eastern philosophy, restricting foods and aiming for "centripetal convergence" -- a "diet of principle" that includes what is normally called a natural diet.

4. A "non-discriminating diet" that lays aside all human knowledge, eating without discrimination in accordance with divine will -- the ideal natural diet.

Fukuoka urged people to move progressively through these stages: "People should begin by discarding empty, self-indulgent diets that are the root of a thousand diseases and, failing to find satisfaction in a scientific diet that does no more than sustain the life of the organism, move on to a diet of principle. But they must then go beyond theory and strive toward the ultimate goal of becoming true people who partake of an ideal natural diet." This framework is central to [[fukuoka-philosophy-eating]].

## Farming for All: A Civilizational Alternative

Fukuoka proposed "Farming for All" as an alternative to the destructive path of modern civilization. He observed that while Japan's cities had achieved material affluence, "the inner life of people has become stunted, natural joy has been lost."

People have become alienated from nature: "They no longer tread over the bare earth. Their hands have drawn away from the grasses and flowers, they do not gaze up into the heavens, their ears are deaf to the songs of the birds, their noses are rendered insensitive by exhaust fumes, and their tongues have forgotten the simple tastes of nature. All five senses have grown isolated from nature."

"The objective of my 'do-nothing' philosophy is the revival of villages of true man where people can return to the original form of nature and enjoy genuine happiness." Fukuoka envisioned a society where "the return of all people to the country to farm and create villages of true men is the road to the creation of ideal towns, ideal societies, and ideal states."

## The Illusion of Loving Nature

Fukuoka was scathingly critical of superficial environmentalism. "The lack of coherence in this world and the flood of disjointed campaigns attest to one thing: what everyone really loves is not nature but himself." The painter who sketches mountains loves sketching, not nature. The farmer who works the earth "merely loves the thought of himself laboring in the fields." The agricultural scientist "only really loves the study of nature."

"No method is needed for loving nature. The only road to nature is non-action." This insight, at the heart of [[fukuoka-return-to-nature-philosophy]], challenges the entire framework of modern environmentalism, which seeks to manage and improve nature rather than simply live within it.

## Merged: Critique of Synthetic Food and Vegetable Factories

Fukuoka warned that modern agriculture's trajectory toward synthetic food production represents not progress but a path to suicidal self-destruction. His [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] stands in direct opposition to the factory farming paradigm.

## Modern Agriculture as Processing Industry

Modern agriculture is a processing industry that uses oil energy in the form of fertilizers, pesticides, and machinery to manufacture synthetic food products which are poor imitations of natural food. The farmer has become a hired hand of industrialized society. All that man learns, performs, and creates with science is a mere imitation of nature.

## Hydroponics and Vegetable Factories

The development of hydroponic cultivation represents the drive toward total artificiality:
- Plants grown in plastic or polymer netting and containers
- Tightly sealed, completely sterile chambers
- Nutrient solutions sprayed over entire plants regularly
- Temperature increased and light raised with artificial lighting
- Carbon dioxide sprayed, oxygen pumped in
- Growth several times faster than field cultivation

Products grown in such environments — beautifully networked melons, large red tomatoes, supple green cucumbers — appear perfect. But they are a far cry from natural products. Grown unnaturally, they are inferior in quality, although perhaps in ways unknown to man.

## Nature Strikes Back

Nature has struck back fiercely against the affront of artificial cultivation in the form of increased insect damage. The response by man has been agriculture increasingly dependent on pesticides and fertilizers — a spiral of intervention and counter-intervention.

## The Road to Total Food Synthesis

Artificial cultivation leads ultimately to total food synthesis:
- Synthesis of urea enables production of any organic material
- Protein synthesis enables man-made meat from various materials
- Butter and cheese can be made from petroleum
- Photosynthesis research will teach man to synthesize starch
- Saccharification of wood and oil may provide additional pathways
- Man has learned to synthesize nucleic acid and cellular proteins
- He has begun synthesizing and recombining genes and chromosomes
- Man has begun fancying himself as the Creator

## Production Costs Never Come Down

It is a mistake to believe that progress in agricultural technology will lower production costs and make food less expensive. A vegetable factory cannot be built and run without considerable outlays for capital and materials. No matter how efficient, such a plant cannot possibly grow produce more cheaply than crops grown naturally with sunlight and soil.

Nature produces without calling for supplies or remuneration, but human effort always demands payment in return. The more sophisticated the equipment and facilities, the higher the costs. Man never knows when to stop — when a highly efficient robot is developed, people applaud, then soon demand even more advanced technology. Everyone seems intent on lowering costs, yet costs have skyrocketed.

## People Become Anti-Natural

People brought up eating unnatural food develop into artificial, anti-natural human beings with a weakened ability to make sound judgments. The more advanced food processing technology becomes, the further it drives man from nature.

## The Quarter-Acre Alternative

Against this trajectory, the [[fukuoka-quarter-acre-farmer-do-nothing-farming]] represents the opposite path: growing food with sunlight, soil, and nature's free services. The labor of one or two people is enough to grow crops on a quarter-acre using methods that cost almost nothing in inputs. No factory can compete with the economics of natural productivity.

## The Fundamental Error

The assumption that man can improve on nature through synthesis and processing is the fundamental error. Agriculture arose from human cravings over food, and the pursuit of ever more processed, artificial food has driven farming further from its natural foundation. The failure of modern agriculture has its roots in this departure from natural principles.
