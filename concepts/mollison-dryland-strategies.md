# Mollison Dryland Strategies

Bill Mollison's dryland strategies address the challenge of designing productive
systems in arid and semi-arid climates where water is the primary limiting factor.
These strategies form some of permaculture's most innovative contributions to land
management, drawing on traditional desert agriculture worldwide and integrating it
with modern earthworks design and water harvesting techniques.

## Water as the Central Design Element

In drylands, every design decision flows from water availability. Mollison insists
designers first map water resources including rainfall patterns, runoff corridors,
groundwater depth, dew potential, and existing water features. The entire system
is organized around capturing, storing, and distributing water efficiently.

Mollison's key insight is that even extremely arid regions receive significant
total rainfall across their area, but most is lost as surface runoff carrying away
topsoil and nutrients. The designer's task is converting this runoff into soil
moisture by slowing, spreading, and sinking water across the landscape.

## Swales and Contour Earthworks

Swales, contour banks, and check dams are the backbone of dryland permaculture. A
swale is a level ditch on contour with excavated soil placed downhill as a berm.
During rainfall, water collects in the swale and infiltrates rather than running
off downhill, creating a moisture lens supporting tree crops and gardens.

Mollison describes systems where multiple swales capture virtually all rainfall,
converting barren hillsides into productive terraced landscapes. These earthworks
simultaneously reduce erosion, build soil through sediment capture, and create
microclimates supporting a wider range of species than the unmodified landscape.

## Keyline Scale of Permanence

In drylands, Mollison applies P.A. Yeomans' keyline principles to prioritize water
storage. The keyline is a contour where the valley floor changes from concave to
convex. Roads, trees, and buildings are placed relative to the keyline to optimize
water distribution across the property systematically.

## Zonation Adapted for Aridity

Zone 1 must be where water is most available and protected from hot winds, typically
near the house where greywater supplements irrigation. Zone 2-3 tree crops follow
swale lines where soil moisture is highest. In drylands, production follows the
water rather than water following production, a fundamental reorientation of the
standard zonation principle.

## Species Selection

Mollison advocates for drought-adapted species including mesquite, acacia, carob,
date palm, prickly pear, jojoba, pomegranate, fig, and olive. Deep-rooted pioneers
establish canopy first, accessing groundwater and improving soil for understory
species. He warns against introducing temperate species, noting European techniques
imposed on arid landscapes have caused massive soil degradation worldwide.

## Soil Surface Protection

Bare soil is the enemy in drylands. Exposed surface temperatures can exceed 70
degrees Celsius, killing microbial life and oxidizing organic matter. Mulching
with organic materials, rock mulches, ground covers, and living mulches reduce
temperatures by 30-40 degrees, allowing organisms to thrive and plants to access
moisture otherwise lost to evaporation.

## Greywater and Waste Water Recycling

In drylands, waste water becomes a valuable resource. Mollison describes greywater
treatment using reed beds and gravel filters producing water for fruit tree
irrigation. Composting toilets conserve water while producing fertilizer, critical
where every liter is precious.
