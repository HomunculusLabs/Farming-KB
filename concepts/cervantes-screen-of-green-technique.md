## Screen of Green (SCROG) Technique

## Overview

[[screen-of-green]] (SCROG) is an advanced [[cannabis-training-techniques]] technique that
uses a horizontal screen or net to create an even, horizontal canopy
of bud sites. Unlike [[sea-of-green]] (SOG), which relies on many small plants
grown close together, SCROG typically uses fewer plants trained through a
screen to maximise light penetration and yield per square foot. The technique
is especially effective for indoor growers with limited plant counts and
medium to high-powered lighting systems.

## SCROG Concept and Principles

The fundamental principle of SCROG is to create a perfectly even canopy
height where every bud site receives approximately equal light intensity.
A screen of wire mesh, chicken wire, or nylon netting is suspended above
the plants at a fixed height (typically 20-40 cm above the [[cervantes-green-algae-growing-medium]]).
As plants grow through the screen, branches are woven back under the mesh
to spread horizontally, filling the screen opening by opening until the
entire screen surface is covered with growth tips.

This horizontal training transforms the natural vertical growth pattern of
cannabis into a horizontal plane, dramatically increasing the number of
cola sites exposed to direct light. The result is a uniform sea of buds
rather than one large central cola and smaller lower branches.

## Screen Setup and Materials

The screen material should have openings of 5-10 cm (2-4 inches) to allow
branches to be easily woven through. Chicken wire (poultry netting) is
the most common and economical choice. Nylon trellis netting offers easier
handling and no sharp edges. The screen is mounted on a frame that can be
raised or lowered as needed, often attached to the walls or suspended from
the ceiling with adjustable hooks or chains.

The screen height depends on the available vertical space and the strain's
expected stretching during flowering. A general rule is to position the
screen so that the expected flowering stretch will fill the space between
the screen and the light source without burning the canopy.

## Vegetative Training Phase

SCROG training begins during the [[cervantes-vegetative-phase]] when plants have
developed 4-6 nodes. The main growing tip is gently bent horizontally and
secured under the screen. As lateral branches develop, they are similarly
woven through the mesh openings and directed to fill empty areas of the
screen. The training process requires daily attention during the active
vegetative phase to ensure even canopy distribution.

Growers should remove lower branches and foliage below the screen that will
not receive adequate light. This energy redirection focuses the plant's
resources on the developing bud sites at the canopy level. Pruning should
be gradual to avoid stressing the plant.

## Flowering Phase Management

When flowering is initiated (by switching to a 12/12 light cycle), the
trained branches will continue to stretch upward through the screen. Most
of the vertical growth occurs during the first 2-3 weeks of flowering.
Growers should continue tucking any growth that extends too far above the
screen back under the mesh to maintain an even canopy.

After the third week of flowering, training should cease and the plant
should be allowed to develop buds naturally. Removing the screen at this
point is optional; some growers leave it in place for [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]],
especially with heavy-yielding strains that may require staking.

## Strain Selection for SCROG

SCROG works best with strains that exhibit vigorous lateral branching and
a moderate flowering stretch. Sativa-dominant strains often perform
exceptionally well due to their [[fukuoka-phyllotaxy-natural-branching-law]] tendency and stretching
behaviour. Indica-dominant strains with tight internodes can also produce
excellent results but may require more aggressive training to fill the
screen. Pure indicas with minimal lateral branching are the least suitable
for SCROG.

## Advantages of SCROG

- Maximises yield per plant, making it ideal for growers with legal plant
count limits.
- Creates an exceptionally even canopy for uniform [[cervantes-reflective-hoods-light-distribution]].
- Increases the number of main cola sites, replacing one large cola with
dozens of medium-sized colas.
- Excellent light utilisation with minimal wasted light penetrating below
the canopy.
- Works well with a wide range of lighting systems, from fluorescents to
high-intensity discharge lamps.
- Lower plant count reduces costs of growing media, containers, and
genetic stock.

## Disadvantages of SCROG

- Labour-intensive during the vegetative phase, requiring daily training
attention.
- Not suitable for growers who cannot tend their garden regularly.
- Difficult to implement with automated commercial-scale operations.
- Screen removal and harvest can be cumbersome with large installations.
- Plant health issues (pests, disease) can spread easily in the dense
canopy.
- Requires more vegetative time than SOG to fill the screen.

## SCROG vs Sea of Green (SOG)

While both techniques aim to maximise yield per square foot, they achieve
this through opposite approaches. SOG uses many small plants with minimal
vegetative time, harvested quickly in a perpetual cycle. SCROG uses fewer
plants with longer vegetative training to create a horizontal canopy. SOG
is faster to set up and harvest but requires more genetic stock. SCROG
extracts more yield per individual plant but demands more training effort
and longer vegetative periods.

## Common Problems

The most common SCROG problems stem from uneven canopy filling. If one
area of the screen fills faster than others, light distribution becomes
uneven and yield suffers. This is addressed by redirecting vigorous
branches toward empty areas and tucking tall growth back under the screen.
Airflow can also be restricted in a dense SCROG canopy, increasing the
risk of mould and pest issues. Oscillating fans and adequate spacing
between the screen and light source help maintain airflow.
