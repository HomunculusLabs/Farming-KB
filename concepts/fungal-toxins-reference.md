# Fungal Toxins Reference

## Overview

Fungi produce a diverse array of toxic secondary metabolites known as mycotoxins. These compounds range from deadly poisons found in poisonous mushrooms to contaminants of agricultural crops that affect human and animal health on a global scale. This reference covers the most significant fungal toxins, their mechanisms of action, clinical effects, and management.

## Amatoxins (Amanitin)

### Source

Amatoxins are produced primarily by species in the genus Amanita, most notably:

- **[[amanita-phalloides]]** (Death Cap) — responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide
- **[[amanita-virosa]]** (Destroying Angel)
- **[[amanita-verna]]** (Fool's Mushroom)
- **[[amanita-bisporigera]]** (Eastern Destroying Angel)
- Also found in some Galerina and Lepiota species

### Mechanism of Action

Amatoxins are bicyclic octapeptides containing a tryptophan-derived indole bridge. The principal toxin is alpha-amanitin:

- **Inhibits RNA polymerase II**: Alpha-amanitin binds to and irreversibly inhibits eukaryotic RNA polymerase II, the enzyme responsible for messenger RNA (mRNA) synthesis
- **Cellular protein synthesis arrest**: Without mRNA, cells cannot produce new proteins. This is particularly devastating in cells with high protein turnover rates
- **Primary target organs**: Hepatocytes (liver cells) and proximal tubule cells (kidney), which have the highest rates of protein synthesis and the highest concentration of amatoxin uptake transporters (OATP1B3)

### Clinical Presentation

Amatoxin poisoning follows a characteristic biphasic course:

**Phase 1 — Gastrointestinal (6-24 hours post-ingestion)**:
- Severe nausea, vomiting, profuse watery diarrhea, abdominal cramping
- Profound fluid and electrolyte losses
- Patients may feel better after 24-48 hours (dangerous "false recovery")

**Phase 2 — Latent (24-72 hours)**:
- Apparent improvement; symptoms subside
- Behind the scenes: progressive hepatocellular necrosis
- Liver enzymes (AST, ALT) begin rising

**Phase 3 — Hepatorenal (72-96 hours)**:
- Fulminant hepatic failure: jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, coagulopathy, hypoglycemia
- Acute kidney injury: oliguria, rising creatinine
- Mortality without treatment: 50-80%

### Treatment

- **Gastrointestinal decontamination**: Activated charcoal if within 1-2 hours of ingestion
- **Silibinin (milk thistle extract)**: Competitive inhibitor of amatoxin uptake into hepatocytes; IV silibinin (Legalon SIL) is the treatment of choice in Europe
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**: Antioxidant, supports glutathione levels
- **Penicillin G**: Competitively inhibits hepatocellular uptake of amatoxin
- **Aggressive fluid resuscitation**: For GI losses
- **Liver transplantation**: May be necessary for fulminant hepatic failure

### Lethal Dose

The LD50 of alpha-amanitin in humans is estimated at 0.1 mg/kg — approximately 50-100g of fresh Amanita phalloides (a single cap can be lethal).

## Orellanine

### Source

Orellanine is produced by Cortinarius species, particularly C. orellanus (Fool's Webcap), C. rubellus (Deadly Webcap), and C. speciosissimus.

### Mechanism of Action

Orellanine is a bipyridyl compound that:

- Causes DNA strand breaks and inhibits DNA synthesis
- Generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) leading to oxidative damage
- Primary target: renal proximal tubule epithelial cells
- The mechanism is fundamentally different from amatoxins — orellanine primarily affects the kidneys with relative sparing of the liver

### Clinical Presentation

Orellanine poisoning has a uniquely long latency period:

- **Latent period**: 3-14 days (sometimes up to 3 weeks) after ingestion, during which patients are asymptomatic
- **Onset**: Nausea, vomiting, burning thirst, headache, followed by oliguria (decreased urine output)
- **Progressive renal failure**: Rising creatinine, electrolyte imbalances, uremia
- **Chronic kidney disease**: Even with supportive care, permanent kidney damage is common. Dialysis or transplantation may be required
- **Mortality**: 10-15% with modern supportive care; higher without treatment

### Treatment

- No specific antidote exists
- **Hemodialysis**: May remove orellanine if initiated early, but the long latency usually means significant damage has occurred
- **Corticosteroids**: Sometimes used to reduce inflammation, but evidence is limited
- **Supportive renal care**: Dialysis as needed; renal transplantation in severe cases

## Gyromitrin

### Source

Gyromitrin is found in several species of false morels:

- **[[gyromitra-esculenta]]** (False Morel) — the most significant source
- **Gyromitra gigas**, **Gyromitra infula**, and related species

### Mechanism of Action

Gyromitrin is a volatile, water-soluble hydrazone compound metabolized to **monomethylhydrazine (MMH)**:

- **MMH inhibits glutathione synthesis**: Depletion leads to oxidative stress and cellular damage
- **MMH disrupts GABA synthesis**: Causes central nervous system excitation and seizures
- **MMH causes methemoglobinemia**: Oxidizes hemoglobin, reducing oxygen-carrying capacity

### Clinical Presentation

- **Gastrointestinal**: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea (2-6 hours after ingestion)
- **Neurological**: Headache, dizziness, ataxia, tremor, seizures (in severe cases)
- **Hepatic**: Hepatomegaly, elevated liver enzymes, potential hepatic failure (2-3 days)
- **Hemolytic**: Hemolysis and methemoglobinemia (rare but serious)

### Treatment

- **Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6)**: Specific antidote for MMH-induced seizures; restores GABA synthesis. Dose: 25 mg/kg IV
- **Benzodiazepines**: For seizure control
- **Methylene blue**: For symptomatic methemoglobinemia
- **N-acetylcysteine**: For hepatoprotection
- **Supportive care**: IV fluids, monitoring of liver and kidney function

### Toxicity Variables

Gyromitrin is volatile and water-soluble, making it partially removable through thorough boiling with copious water changes (multiple changes of boiling water). However, this is not considered reliable and consumption of Gyromitra species is never recommended.

## Aflatoxin

### Source

Aflatoxins are produced by [[aspergillus-flavus]], A. parasiticus, and A. nomius, contaminating peanuts, corn, rice, wheat, soybeans, tree nuts, spices, and dried figs. Contamination is most prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions with hot, humid climates.

### Mechanism of Action

Aflatoxins are difuranocoumarin compounds, with B1 being the most potent and prevalent:

- **Metabolic activation**: Aflatoxin B1 is metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP1A2, CYP3A4) to the reactive intermediate **aflatoxin B1-8,9-epoxide**
- **DNA adduct formation**: The epoxide forms covalent bonds with DNA, primarily at the N7 position of guanine
- **TP53 mutation**: Aflatoxin-DNA adducts cause a characteristic G-to-T transversion mutation in the TP53 tumor suppressor gene (codon 249), which is strongly associated with hepatocellular carcinoma
- **Immunosuppression**: Aflatoxins suppress cell-mediated immunity and reduce antibody production

### Clinical Effects

**Acute aflatoxicosis**:
- High-dose exposure: severe liver damage, hemorrhagic necrosis, jaundice, ascites, edema
- Fatal dose: approximately 1-2 mg/kg body weight in adults
- Outbreaks have occurred in developing countries, with mortality rates of 25-40%

**Chronic aflatoxin exposure**:
- **Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**: Aflatoxin B1 is classified as a Group 1 human carcinogen by IARC. Combined with hepatitis B infection, it multiplies HCC risk by approximately 30-fold
- **Growth impairment**: In children, chronic exposure causes stunted growth, underweight, and developmental delay
- **Immunosuppression**: Increased susceptibility to infections
- **Reproductive effects**: Reduced fertility and birth weight

### Regulatory Limits

Most countries regulate aflatoxin levels in food:

- **FDA**: 20 ppb total aflatoxins in human food; 0.5 ppb in milk (aflatoxin M1)
- **EU**: Stricter limits — 2-12 ppb depending on food type
- **Codex Alimentarius**: International standards range from 5-20 ppb

### Mitigation

- **Pre-harvest**: Resistant crop varieties, biological control with non-toxigenic A. flavus strains, proper irrigation
- **Post-harvest**: Rapid drying, proper storage (low moisture, low temperature), sorting contaminated kernels
- **Processing**: Nixtamalization (alkaline cooking of corn) significantly reduces aflatoxin levels
- **Monitoring**: Regular testing using ELISA, HPLC, or lateral flow assays

## Other Notable Mycotoxins

- **Ochratoxin A**: From Aspergillus ochraceus and Penicillium verrucosum. Nephrotoxic, carcinogenic (Group 2B), immunosuppressive. Found in cereals, coffee, wine, spices
- **Fumonisins**: From [[fusarium-verticillioides]]. Inhibit ceramide synthase, causing equine leukoencephalomalacia, neural tube defects. Found in corn
- **Trichothecenes** (T-2 toxin, DON/vomitoxin): From Fusarium species. Inhibit protein synthesis, immunosuppressive, hemorrhagic. Found in wheat, barley, corn
- **Ergot alkaloids**: From Claviceps purpurea on cereal grains. Cause ergotism (St. Anthony's Fire) — gangrene, hallucinations. Partial agonists at adrenergic and serotonergic receptors
