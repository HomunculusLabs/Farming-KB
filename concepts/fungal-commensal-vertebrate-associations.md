# Fungal Commensal Associations with Vertebrates

Commensal organisms primarily colonize keratinized or mucous-covered
animal surfaces and often use host secretions from the skin (such as
sebum) or vagina, or gut materials such as oral or buccal cavity food
residues and fecal material. The boundary between commensalism and pathogenicity in fungi associated with vertebrates is fluid; a
commensal or ordinarily harmless environmental fungus may become
pathogenic with changes in the immunological or ecological status of
the animal host.

## Commensal Yeasts

### Malassezia Species

Yeasts in the genus Malassezia are the most specialized fungal
commensals of vertebrates. Malassezia species are obligate commensals,
occurring only on skin or within outer ear canals of mammals, including
humans. Six of the seven described species are so specialized that they
will not grow on laboratory media without special lipid supplements
substituting for skin lipids. These lipophilic yeasts are adapted to
exploit the lipid-rich microenvironment of the skin surface and hair
follicles.

Malassezia species are among the most ubiquitous components of the
human skin mycoflora, colonizing the scalp, face, trunk, and other
sebum-rich areas. Under certain conditions, Malassezia species can
shift from commensal to pathogenic roles, associated with conditions
including pityriasis versicolor, Malassezia folliculitis, and
seborrheic dermatitis. They have also been implicated as a trigger
factor in atopic eczema and dandruff.

### Candida Species

Certain Candida species, particularly C. albicans, C. tropicalis, C.
glabrata, C. parapsilosis, and C. krusei, are associated strongly
with mammals and birds, especially their gastrointestinal tracts
and/or normal skin, and seldom are isolated from other sources except
as transient inoculum or as active contaminants of fermented food and
beverages. These species are essentially saprobic but are resistant
to clearance by the immune system in certain body sites.

[[fusarium-solani]] and Pseudallescheria boydii, for
example, may proliferate on wounds and in deep cracks in the skin,
where serous exudate (pus) and dead skin are available nutrient
sources.

## Categories of Association

The categories of fungal-vertebrate association overlap to some
extent because a commensal or ordinarily harmless environmental fungus
may become pathogenic with changes in the immunological or ecological
status of the animal host. The principal categories include:

### True Commensals

Organisms that persist on body surfaces as part of the normal
microbiota without typically causing disease. Malassezia species and
certain Candida species are the primary examples.

### Opportunistic Pathogens

Ordinarily harmless fungi that can invade animal tissues or
extensively colonize body surfaces when a host becomes
immunocompromised or is exposed to a heavy load of potentially
immunodepressing [[ingham-fungal-inoculum-sourcing]]. This is by far the largest category
of fungi associated with vertebrate disease.

### Specialized Pathogens

Fungi with specific adaptations for vertebrate pathogenesis, including
thermal dimorphism, resistance to phagocytic killing, and specific
[[cannabis-soil-and-nutrient-requirements]] met by the host environment. The Onygenalean
systemic pathogens (Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Paracoccidioides,
Coccidioides) exemplify this category.
