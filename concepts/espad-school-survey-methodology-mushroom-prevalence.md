## Overview

The European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) is the
primary data source for measuring psychoactive substance use among European
adolescents. The EMCDDA draws heavily on ESPAD data for its assessments of
mushroom use prevalence across European countries, making ESPAD's methodology
critically important to understanding the reliability of European drug
prevalence statistics.

The survey's approach to measuring mushroom use involves specific questions,
sampling strategies, and [[blesching-cannabis-contaminants-testing-quality-control]] measures that determine the accuracy
and comparability of cross-national prevalence data.

## ESPAD Survey Design

ESPAD is a cross-sectional survey conducted every four years among students
aged 15-16 in European countries. The survey uses a standardized questionnaire
translated into multiple languages, allowing for direct comparison of substance
use patterns across participating nations.

The target population is students born in a specific year, typically surveyed
during the spring of the data collection year. This birth-cohort approach
ensures consistent age ranges across countries and survey waves.

The sampling methodology employs a two-stage cluster design. In the first stage,
schools are selected with probability proportional to enrollment size. In the
second stage, classes within selected schools are randomly chosen for survey
administration. This approach provides nationally representative estimates while
keeping the logistics of school-based survey administration manageable.

## Mushroom-Specific Questions

The ESPAD questionnaire includes specific items related to mushroom use that
feed into the EMCDDA's monitoring of psychoactive fungi. These questions
typically cover:

**Lifetime prevalence** — whether the respondent has ever used "magic mushrooms"
or similar hallucinogenic fungi in their lifetime. This is the broadest
measure and captures both experimental and regular use.

**Last 12 months prevalence** — whether the respondent has used mushrooms
within the past year. This measure captures more recent use and is less
affected by recall decay for events that occurred long ago.

**Last 30 days prevalence** — whether the respondent has used mushrooms in
the past month. This is the most restrictive measure and captures current or
very recent use patterns.

The question wording is critical for data quality. ESPAD uses the term "magic
mushrooms" in most language versions, as this is the most widely recognized
colloquial term among European adolescents. Some survey versions also include
"[[emcdda-eu-legislative-timeline-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-2001-2006]]" or local slang terms to improve recognition.

## Methodological Challenges

Measuring mushroom use through [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-school-surveys-adolescent-use]] presents several unique
methodological challenges that affect data quality:

**Recognition and recall.** Adolescents may not recognize the term "magic
mushrooms" or may confuse mushrooms with other substances. The informal
nature of mushroom use means that respondents may not know the specific
identity of what they consumed, particularly if they obtained the substance
from a friend or at a party.

**Social desirability bias.** Like all self-report drug surveys, ESPAD is
subject to social desirability effects. Students may underreport illegal
substance use, particularly in school settings where they may fear that
responses could be linked to them personally despite assurances of anonymity.

**Sclerotia confusion is significant in countries where sclerotia ("truffles")
are commonly sold. Respondents who have used sclerotia may not recognize them
as the same category as "magic mushrooms," leading to underreporting in
countries with significant sclerotia markets like the Netherlands.

**Polydrug use context.** Mushroom use often occurs in the context of
polydrug use, particularly at parties and festivals. Respondents may have
difficulty isolating their mushroom use from other substance use experiences
when recalling past events.

## Data Quality Assurance

ESPAD employs several quality control measures to ensure data reliability
across participating countries:

Standardized translation procedures require that the questionnaire be
translated from English and then back-translated by an independent translator.
Discrepancies are resolved through consultation, ensuring meaning is preserved.

Survey administration follows a standardized protocol. Questionnaires are
administered during a regular class period by teachers or trained administrators
who are not the students' regular classroom teachers, reducing pressure.

Response rates are monitored, and countries with low participation may be
excluded from cross-national comparisons to prevent bias from differential
non-response.

Built-in validity checks include consistency checks between related items and
"fake drugs" (fictional substances) to identify respondents exaggerating use.

## Cross-National Comparability

One of ESPAD's primary strengths is its ability to compare substance use
prevalence across European countries. However, several factors limit the
comparability of mushroom use estimates specifically:

Legal status differences affect both mushroom availability and respondents'
willingness to report use. In countries where mushrooms are legal or tolerated,
reported prevalence may be higher due to both actual higher use and greater
willingness to acknowledge use.

Cultural differences in substance naming mean that the term "magic mushrooms"
may have different connotations or levels of recognition across countries.
In some cultures, mushrooms may be known primarily by local names that do not
map directly to the survey question.

Market differences, particularly the availability of sclerotia versus dried
mushrooms, mean the same question may capture different products in different
countries, complicating cross-country prevalence comparisons.

## Historical Trends in ESPAD Data

ESPAD mushroom use data shows notable [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] survey waves and
between countries. Generally, lifetime prevalence of mushroom use among
15-16 year olds in European countries ranges from approximately 1% to 8%,
with some outlier countries showing higher or lower rates.

The Netherlands consistently shows higher prevalence rates than most other
European countries, reflecting both the legal availability of mushrooms and
sclerotia through smart shops and the cultural acceptance of psychoactive
substance use.

Trends over time show mushroom use prevalence among adolescents has generally
declined or remained stable in most European countries since the late 2000s,
following overall illicit drug use trends in this age group.
