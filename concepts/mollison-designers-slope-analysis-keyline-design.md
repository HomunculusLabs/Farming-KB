**8-15% (moderate slope)**: Suitable for orchards, agroforestry, and

**15-30% (steep slope)**: Limited to tree crops, forestry, and controlled

require extensive foundations. Access is difficult and expensive. Best used
for timber production, watershed protection, and permaculture-zone-planning.

## Landform Elements

Mollison identifies the basic landform elements that compose any landscape:

**Ridges**: Elevated areas dividing watersheds. Ridges receive maximum
exposure to wind and sun. They are dry, well-drained, and often nutrient-poor
due to erosion. Windbreaks and drought-tolerant species are appropriate.
Buildings on ridges are exposed but have views and ventilation.

**Valleys**: Low points collecting water from surrounding slopes. Valleys
are moist, fertile, and often contain watercourses. They are prime locations
for dams, water storage, and high-value crops. Roads and structures in
valleys must account for flood risk.

**Convex slopes**: Slopes that curve outward like the surface of a sphere.
Water spreads and accelerates on convex slopes, making them inherently
erosive. They tend to be drier than concave slopes at the same elevation.
Cultivation on convex slopes requires careful contour management.

**Concave slopes**: Slopes that curve inward like the inside of a bowl.
Water collects and slows on concave slopes, depositing nutrients and
[[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]. They are naturally more fertile and moisture-retentive.
Concave slopes are preferred for cultivation and water harvesting.

**Saddles**: Low points on ridgelines connecting two higher areas. Saddles
are natural crossing points for roads, trails, and wildlife. They may
contain springs where water tables intersect the surface.

**Spurs**: Ridge extensions projecting into valleys. Spurs provide views
and ventilation but are exposed and dry. They define drainage boundaries.

## The Keyline Concept

P.A. Yeomans developed the keyline system in Australia during the 1950s.
Mollison adapted and promoted it extensively in permaculture. The keyline
is a specific [[comparison-swale-vs-contour-line-water-management]] identified on each landform that defines the
pattern for all subsequent cultivation, tree planting, and water management.

The keyline is found at the point where a convex slope changes to concave
-- the "keystone" of the valley. On a primary valley (the main drainage
channel), the keyline passes through the highest point where the valley
floor begins to widen. This point marks the transition from water
concentration (in the narrow upper valley) to water dispersal (in the
widening lower valley).

## Keyline Pattern Cultivation

Once the keyline is identified, Mollison describes a specific cultivation
pattern:
1. Plow or cultivate parallel to the keyline, maintaining the same contour
   spacing across the entire landform
2. On ridges above the keyline, these parallel lines are slightly off-
   contour, draining water away from the ridge toward the valley
3. In valleys below the keyline, the same parallel lines are slightly off-
   contour in the opposite direction, spreading water from the valley onto
   the adjacent ridges

This pattern achieves two critical outcomes simultaneously: it prevents
erosion on convex slopes by dispersing concentrated water flow, and it
distributes water from wet valleys to dry ridges, improving the moisture
distribution across the entire landform.
