# Stephen Hales and the Foundation of Plant Physiology

Stephen Hales (1677–1761) is widely considered one of the pioneers of
plant physiology for his groundbreaking studies of water transport,
transpiration, and [[nehemiah-grew-plant-anatomy]] and Marcello
Malpighi had established what plants looked like on the inside, but the
question of how they functioned, how water moved through them, and how
they exchanged gases with the atmosphere remained largely unanswered.
Hales addressed these questions with an experimental rigor that was
exceptional for his era.

## Vegetable Staticks (1727)

*Vegetable Staticks*, published in London in 1727, is Hales's most
important botanical work. The full title, *Vegetable Staticks: or, An
Account of some Statical Experiments on the Sap in Vegetables*, reflects
the approach Hales took: applying the principles of statics, the branch
of mechanics concerned with forces in equilibrium, to the study of plant
function. This quantitative, experimental approach was novel and
influential.

### Water Transport and Root Pressure

One of Hales's most significant discoveries was the existence of root
pressure, the force that pushes water upward through the plant from the
roots. He demonstrated this by attaching manometers, U-shaped glass tubes
containing water, to cut stems and observing the height to which sap rose
in the tubes. These experiments provided the first quantitative
measurements of the forces involved in water in plants.

Hales showed that roots actively absorb water from the soil and generate
a positive pressure that drives the sap upward. While the full mechanism
of water transport, including the role of transpiration pull and the
cohesion-tension theory, would not be understood until the late
[[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]].

### Transpiration

Hales was among the first scientists to measure transpiration, the loss
of water vapor from plant leaves. By weighing potted plants over time
and measuring the amount of water they absorbed through their roots, he
was able to quantify the rate at which plants lose water to the
atmosphere. His experiments revealed that plants transpire enormous
quantities of water relative to their mass, a finding that surprised many
of his contemporaries and laid the foundation for the modern study of
plant water relations.
