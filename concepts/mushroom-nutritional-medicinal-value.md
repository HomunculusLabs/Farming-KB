## Nutritional Profile

### Macronutrients

Mushrooms are low in calories, fat, and sodium while providing meaningful amounts of protein, dietary fiber, and complex carbohydrates. Their nutritional profile varies by species but general characteristics include:

- **Protein**: 2-5% fresh weight (20-35% dry weight) -- contains all essential amino acids, though limiting in methionine and cysteine. The protein quality is enhanced by the presence of [[mushroom-training-shiitake-cultivation]], *Lentinula edodes*): A beta-1,6 branched beta-1,3 glucan evaluated as an anti-cancer drug. Lentinan is approved as an adjuvant cancer therapy in Japan and is administered intravenously alongside chemotherapy to improve immune response and survival rates. See [[turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor]], *Trametes versicolor*): Another protein-bound polysaccharide with significant anti-cancer activity. PSK is approved as a cancer adjuvant in Japan, prescribed for gastric and colorectal cancers. It has been shown to stimulate natural killer cell activity and enhance immune surveillance. See [[turkey-tail-research-psk]].
- **D-fraction** (from [[maitake-d-fraction-research]].

## Mushrooms in Global Food Security

### Advantages for Developing Regions

The Fungi Magazine sustainability review (Spinosa, 2008) highlighted several properties that make mushrooms particularly valuable for addressing food insecurity:

- **Rapid growth cycle**: Oyster mushrooms fruit in as little as one month from inoculation
- **Low technology requirements**: Simple growing houses, no expensive equipment needed
- **Small land footprint**: Can be cultivated on small plots, vertically stacked
- **Family-scale management**: Entire operations manageable by a single family or small community
- **High market value**: Gourmet mushrooms command premium prices compared to other produce
- **Growing global demand**: Worldwide demand for specialty mushrooms is increasing
- **Dual function**: Both a food source and a marketable cash crop

### Case Study: Hong Kong Poultry Crisis

The 2002 avian influenza outbreak led to mass slaughter of millions of poultry in Hong Kong, devastating small farmers who had raised poultry for generations. The Hong Kong Department of Agriculture responded by offering mushroom cultivation seminars as an alternative livelihood. This case demonstrates how mushroom cultivation can serve as an economic safety net when traditional agriculture faces disruption.

### Case Study: Coffee Waste Valorization (Chinchina, Colombia)

Low-income coffee growers in Chinchina, Colombia face volatile coffee prices. The ZERI Foundation project demonstrated that coffee waste (which represents the vast majority of the coffee plant's biomass) could be used as substrate for shiitake cultivation at 75% biological efficiency. This transformed a waste disposal problem into a secondary income stream while providing nutritional supplementation. See [[spent-mushroom-substrate-recycling]] retains significant value:

- Contains all the nutrients, protein, and medicinal compounds of the harvested mushrooms
- Mycelium-loaded substrate has increased protein content compared to the original material
- Research by Adamovic et al. (1998) demonstrated that spent *Pleurotus* substrate is palatable and nutritious for cattle
