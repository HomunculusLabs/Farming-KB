# Pulsing Agricultural Ecosystems

Pulsing agricultural ecosystems are farming and land management systems that deliberately alternate between phases of biological accumulation (production) and phases of consumption or harvest, mirroring the natural pulsing patterns observed in wild ecosystems. These systems recognize that separating the production aspect (plant growth) from the consumption aspect (grazing, harvesting, cultivation) increases total energy processing and system resilience.

## Cell Grazing

### Origins and Theory

Allan Savory and others proposed that roaming herds of grazing animals maintained rangeland health and vigor by grazing hard and then moving on. This insight has been successfully applied to pastoral farming as **cell grazing**.

### How It Works

Cell grazing is based on the principle that pastures grow better without constant animal nibbling, but a short burst of grazing maintains vegetative growth and recycles nutrients. An occasional hard graze exposes and cuts the soil surface, providing favorable conditions for regeneration of short-lived legumes and other useful species.

### Key Management Questions

The consumption phase is always brief and relatively intense if the system is to remain healthy. Two critical management questions for pastoral farmers are:
- **How brief?** — The duration of the grazing pulse
- **How intense?** — The stocking density and grazing pressure during the pulse

In a systems ecology sense, separating production from consumption increases the total energy processing and resilience of the system.

## Pasture Ley Cropping

### The Traditional System

Another important agricultural example of pulsing systems is the rotation of fertility-demanding annual crops with longer rotations (**leys**) of soil-improving perennial pastures. In moderate rainfall temperate climates, mixtures of clovers, perennial grasses, and other herbaceous plants improve:
- Soil organic matter
- Soil structure
- Soil fertility (especially under pulsing grazing regimes)

Ploughing under the pasture releases accumulated fertility, leading to high yields in annual crops. However, maintaining annual crops for too many years leads to fertility decline.

### Modern Decline

Variations of this system prevailed in Australian cropping zones until the pasture phase supporting sheep became uneconomic. Today's typical regime of direct drill seeding, herbicide weed control, and bagged fertilizer compensates for the lost pasture phase. Whether this intensive land use rebuilds organic matter and mineral fertility or leads to another downward spiral of degradation remains contentious, but evidence is not encouraging.

### Organic Alternatives

Organic sources of bagged nitrogen fertilizer are too expensive for broadacre organic farms. Green manure and legume pasture leys remain a more sustainable method of soil improvement that reflects the pulsing ecosystem model.

## Bush Regrowth as Soil-Conserving Ley

In high-rainfall, low-fertility hill country in southern Australia, regrowth bush (especially soil-improving wattles) has acted by default as a fertility-rebuilding phase between more opportunistic clearing and sowing to pastures. These pasture phases typically last a decade or two before bush reclaims the land.

### The Downward Spiral Problem

Inexperienced landholders, spurred by observations of good pasture growth following clearing, have repeated this cycle over the last 100 years. In most cases, the clearing and pasture-growing phase caused more erosion and loss of organic matter and nutrients than 20 or more years of bush regrowth could accumulate. Each pulse contributes to a downward spiral of land degradation rather than a sustainable cycle.

## Tropical Slash and Burn (Swidden Agriculture)

### The Most Evolved Pulsing System

Of the pulsing agricultural systems that have been well documented, the most evolved and sustainable example is the **swidden or slash-and-burn agriculture** of New Guinea and other tropical regions. The process works as follows:

1. Small areas of rainforest are cleared and burnt
2. A great mixture of perennial crops is planted, forming food forests thriving on released fertility
3. The garden structure mimics rainforest with multiple levels of tree, shrub, vine, and root crops minimizing nutrient leaching
4. Over time the garden becomes harder to harvest with more woody non-food plants
5. The garden is abandoned and returns to forest

### Mother of the Gardens

Sometimes naturally regenerating forest trees are retained during cultivation, known as "mother of the gardens" in recognition of the need for rainforest regeneration to rebuild soil fertility.

### Sustainability Parameters

In the New Guinea highlands before Europeans arrived in the 1930s, this system sustainably supported dense populations. It is the most energy-efficient form of agriculture documented by scientists. The rotation time under regrowth forest is critical — 20 years is generally necessary to fully rebuild fertility. Consequently the ratio of garden to forest is always small.

### Modern Failure

In tropical regions, swidden agriculture is now widely regarded as unsustainable because the exploitation phase has become more frequent due to population pressure on declining forest areas. The fundamental lesson remains: the benefits from the pulse depend on the slow accumulation of the building phase.

## Sheet Mulching as Pulsing System

### Strengths and Limitations

Sheet mulching is an easy way to convert lawn and pasture into productive food gardens without digging and weeding, making use of locally abundant and wasted organic materials. However, it has limitations:
- Creates dependence on constant mulch supply
- If mulch is high-quality animal feed (like lucerne hay), sustainability is questionable
- Excessive continuous mulching may smother soil life (biodynamic perspective)
- Over time, typically results in excessively high potassium levels (Albrechtian perspective)

### Successional Alternatives to Continuous Sheet Mulch

Three successional alternatives include:

1. **Fallow reversion**: Reverting to grass and weed fallow, and moving the garden to a fresh location
2. **Food forest succession**: Gradual dominance by fruit trees and shade-tolerant perennials, with natural litter fall and prunings providing adequate mulch
3. **Intensive soil management**: Application of high-quality compost, carefully selected rock minerals, modest mulching, combined with light surface cultivation and deep aeration forking to maintain balanced productive soil

## Food Forest Succession

Many intensive sheet-mulch Permaculture gardens of mixed annuals and perennials apply the initial floristic composition model of succession. The succession to fruit trees, climbers, shade-tolerant herbs, and perennial vegetables has resulted in many varied food forests providing yields for very little work.

However, high-yielding production of valued annual vegetables requires open sunny space. After a decade of nurturing an expanding food forest, permaculturalists sometimes take chainsaws to overgrown leguminous trees to release fertility and sunlight for vegetable gardening — a reinvention of [[slash-and-burn-agriculture]] in miniature.

## Generalized Model of Land Degradation

The pulsing model reveals a generalized pattern of land degradation: addiction to a particular management pattern provides immediate benefits but produces long-term decline. The key insight is that the benefits from any productive pulse depend entirely on the slow accumulation phase that precedes it. Pulsing too frequently leads to a downward spiral, whether in cell grazing, pasture ley cropping, bush clearing, or slash-and-burn agriculture.
