# Arbutoid Mycorrhiza

Arbutoid mycorrhiza is a distinctive type of [[mycorrhiza]] formed between members of the Arbutoideae subfamily (within Ericaceae) and certain fungi that are primarily ectomycorrhizal on other host plants. Arbutoid mycorrhizae combine structural features of both [[ectomycorrhizal-ecology]] and [[ericoid-mycorrhiza]], with a well-developed Hartig net AND intracellular penetration of root cortical cells.

## Host Plants

Arbutoid mycorrhizae are formed by plants in the Arbutoideae subfamily of Ericaceae:

- **Arbutus** (madrone, strawberry tree): A. menziesii (Pacific madrone), A. unedo (strawberry tree)
- **Arctostaphylos** (manzanita, bearberry): A. uva-ursi (kinnikinnick), A. manzanita
- **Arctous** (bearberry): A. alpinus, A. ruber
- **Comarostaphylis**: C. diversifolia and related species
- **Kalmiopsis**: K. leachiana, a rare Oregon endemic

These plants are typically found in Mediterranean climates, montane regions, and acidic soils. They often occupy ecological intermediates between ericaceous heathlands and coniferous forests.

## Fungal Partners

The fungi forming arbutoid mycorrhizae are predominantly basidiomycetes that also form typical ectomycorrhizae on other trees:

- **Basidiomycetes**: Laccaria, Rhizopogon, Piloderma, Cortinarius, Thelephora, Pisolithus
- **Ascomycetes**: Some ascomycete species may also form arbutoid associations
- **Shared fungal partners**: Many of these fungi simultaneously form ectomycorrhizae on nearby conifers (Pinus, Pseudotsuga, Tsuga)

This shared fungal partner relationship creates a potential [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]] linking ericaceous plants with forest trees, with ecological implications for nutrient cycling and plant community dynamics.

## Structural Features

Arbutoid mycorrhizae exhibit a unique combination of structural characteristics:

### Hartig Net

A well-developed Hartig net is formed, similar to ectomycorrhizae:

- Fungal hyphae penetrate between epidermal and outer cortical cells
- The Hartig net may extend through several layers of cortical cells
- Hyphae form a labyrinthine intercellular network for nutrient exchange
- This intercellular phase is the primary site of nutrient exchange

### Intracellular Penetration

Unlike typical ectomycorrhizae, arbutoid mycorrhizae also feature intracellular colonization:

- Fungal hyphae penetrate individual cortical cells
- Within cells, hyphae may form coils or branching structures
- These intracellular structures are functionally analogous to the arbuscules of AMF, providing an increased surface area for nutrient exchange
- The intracellular phase develops after the Hartig net is established

### Mantle

A fungal mantle may or may not be present:

- Some arbutoid mycorrhizae develop a substantial fungal sheath around the root
- In others, the mantle is thin or absent
- When present, the mantle may influence root exudation patterns and protect against soil-borne pathogens

### Root Morphology

Arbutoid mycorrhizal roots are typically:

- Short, swollen, and coral-like in appearance
- Lacking root hairs (as the fungal mantle or hyphal network replaces their function)
- White to cream-colored when young, darkening with age
- Often profusely branched in a monopodial pattern

## Physiological Function

### Nutrient Exchange

The dual intercellular/intracellular architecture provides efficient nutrient exchange:

- **Hartig net**: Primary pathway for carbon flow from plant to fungus and mineral nutrients from fungus to plant
- **Intracellular coils**: May provide supplementary exchange surfaces, particularly for nitrogen and phosphorus
- **Carbon allocation**: Host plant supplies photosynthate to the fungus; the magnitude of carbon drain is intermediate between AM and EM associations

### Ecological Context

Arbutoid plants often grow in nitrogen-poor, acidic soils where mycorrhizal nutrient acquisition is critical. The shared fungal partners with forest trees create potential for interplant nutrient transfer through common mycorrhizal networks.

## Ecological Significance

Arbutoid mycorrhizae are ecologically important because they:

- Enable Arbutoideae species to colonize nutrient-poor, acidic habitats
- Create mycorrhizal linkages between ericaceous understory and overstory conifers through shared fungal partners
- Contribute to nitrogen cycling in forest-ecotone environments
- May facilitate succession from heathland to forest communities

## Evolutionary Considerations

The arbutoid condition is thought to represent an evolutionary intermediate or hybrid between ectomycorrhizal and ericoid mycorrhizal strategies. The combination of Hartig net (ectomycorrhizal feature) and intracellular colonization (ericoid/arbuscular feature) in a single root system is unique among mycorrhizal types and may reflect the evolutionary plasticity of the ericaceous mycorrhizal habit.
