**Santo Daime**, founded by a Brazilian of African descent, involves
the entire congregation singing hymns led by a small group of women
singers. Some ceremonies also involve dancing in simple rhythmic,
swaying steps, reminiscent of African-American gospel services.

**Barquinia**, less well known outside Brazil, has incorporated
elements of Umbanda, an Afro-Brazilian religious movement involving
contact with deceased ancestors and deities known as orixas. These
ceremonies involve both singing and dancing.

## Social Functions of Entheogenic Churches

Metzner identified several important social functions of these
organized churches. Participation tends to reduce the incidence of
alcoholism and drug addiction. The churches provide a protective
shield of traditional lore against the encroachments of Christian
missionaries and the seductions of Western consumer culture. They
strengthen community bonds and give members a sense of participation
and belonging.

Research by Charles Grob, Dennis McKenna, and others confirmed that
membership in these churches and long-term use of ayahuasca tea is
associated with no adverse health effects and indeed with a reduction
of addictions and other psychopathologies.

## The Native American Church

The Native American Church (NAC) legally incorporated as an organized
religion using peyote as a sacrament. For most of the twentieth
century, the NAC enjoyed legal access to peyote in most Western
states. Ceremonies follow a consistent format with a "roadman"
presiding, assisted by a drummer, firekeeper, and cedar-man.
Participants sing peyote songs with rapid, rhythmic beats throughout
the night. There is virtually no discussion of healing processes or
visions during or after the ceremony.

NAC ceremonies are legally limited to persons with 25% or more Indian
ancestry, although some ceremonial leaders have opened their
ceremonies to non-Indians. The social function of reducing alcoholism
among Native American populations has been well documented.

## The Bwiti Cult

The Bwiti cult among the Fang people in Gabon and Zaire uses iboga in
both shamanic and syncretic religious forms. Originally learned from
the Pygmies, deep forest dwellers, Bwiti ceremonies involve temples
with altars and officiating priests. Initiates are taken through a
powerful death-rebirth experience in which their "head is opened up,"
enabling conversation with ancestor spirits. The ceremonies involve
chanting, singing, and sometimes dancing, with participants daubed
with white mud.

Pharmacological research indicated ibogaine may have specific action
on cocaine receptor sites, raising the possibility of a chemical
antidote to addiction. Metzner suggested the more plausible mechanism
is that all "consciousness-expanding" hallucinogens can counteract the
consciousness-contracting effect of addictive substances.
