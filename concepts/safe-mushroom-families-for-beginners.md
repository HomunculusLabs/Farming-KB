## Safe Mushroom Families for Beginners

Julius A. Palmer identified several families of fungi that he considered
particularly safe for amateur experimentation, providing detailed descriptions
of each along with their distinguishing characteristics. His practical guidance
was designed to help novices build confidence in [[mushroom-identification]] without
the risk of poisoning, while simultaneously teaching the observational skills
necessary for more advanced study.

## Puffballs (Lycoperdon)

Palmer declared puffballs "the safest mushrooms for the beginner" — a judgment
that modern mycology largely confirms. The giant puffball (*Lycoperdon
giganteum*) in its infant state has a smooth skin and perfectly white interior.
The color of the skin varies from pure white to almost black, but the critical
test is the interior: if a knife leaves a yellow stain, the specimen is too old
and should not be eaten. Otherwise, Palmer advised eating it without fear.

He noted several smaller varieties that he ate "indiscriminately" and stated his
belief that all white puffballs could be safely used if cooked immediately after
gathering. The critical caveat was freshness: puffballs change very rapidly
after collection and should only be eaten in their freshest state. Palmer's
assurance that "there is no poisonous fungus resembling them" holds true with
one important modern caveat — the young button stage of the deadly *Amanita*
species can superficially resemble a small puffball. However, slicing any
suspect specimen open horizontally will reveal the outline of gills within an
Amanita, while a true puffball shows pure, featureless white flesh throughout.

## Teeth-Bearing Fungi (Hydnei)

The teeth-bearing toadstools were also recommended as safe for amateur
experimentation. Palmer had found only one variety personally but noted that
Worthington G. Smith confirmed all species of any size "enjoy a good character."
The spreading hydnum (*[[hydnum-repandum]]*) was the primary example: usually
yellow, sometimes reddish, always firm-fleshed, with a deformed, indistinct, or
eccentric stem. One side of the cap is frequently much higher than the point
diametrically opposite, giving it a characteristically asymmetrical appearance.
The teeth or spines, which in young specimens easily rub off, are sufficient to
distinguish the genus from all other mushrooms.

## Boleti

The Boleti presented a more nuanced case for the beginner. Palmer noted that
they could only be confused with their "twin brothers," the Polyporei, but the
distinction was straightforward: Polyporei grow mostly on wood with abortive
stems, while the edible Boletus grows from the ground with a distinct stem and
tubes that are easily separable from each other.

For determining edibility within the Boleti, Palmer provided clear rules. Pore
color was the primary indicator: brown, yellowish, or greenish pores with a
russet or brown cap indicated an edible or harmless variety. Flesh color was the
secondary indicator: if the cut flesh remains white, the specimen is likely
safe. Danger signs included brilliant coloring (red or yellow caps), blue
bruising when cut, or red tubes at the pore openings. Modern mycology confirms
these general guidelines, though with additional nuances. The *[[boletus-edulis-and-pore-fungi-polyporaceae]]*
group (porcini) remains among the most prized [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]] worldwide, while
the red-pored species of the *Suillellus* and *Rubroboletus* genera are indeed
toxic.

## The Maned Mushroom (Coprinus comatus)

The maned or shaggy ink cap (*[[coprinus-comatus]]*) was Palmer's personal favorite
for beginners because it had "no poisonous counterpart." When fit for food, it
is shaped like a turtle's egg — ovate and alike at each end. The cap is brown
and smooth, but toward the base the outer skin ruffles up into a white mane or
fringe of concentric layers. The stem is hollow and, when cut, appears like
macaroni as it cooks. A ring around the stem is movable in well-developed
plants.

Palmer's critical instruction was about timing: very soon after acquiring its
full growth, the gill tips turn black, then the cap opens umbrella-like and
melts into an inky fluid. It should never be eaten when stale or when the gills
have turned black. This was the first fungus Palmer dared to eat on his own
recognition, and modern mycology confirms it as an excellent edible — though it
causes unpleasant reactions when consumed with alcohol due to coprine, a fact
unknown in Palmer's time.

## The Elm-Tree Mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius)

Palmer described the elm-tree mushroom as growing only in the fall on elm trees,
with a thick solid stem (sometimes set to one side), broad white gills, firm
white flesh, and a light-yellow top that is initially smooth but becomes spotted
and cracked with age. Older specimens develop yellow gills and a very leathery
cap surface. It is most commonly found in a cleft or where a limb has been sawn
off, and often reappears yearly in the same spot. Palmer knew of no other large
fungus with gills and white flesh growing from elm trees, making identification
relatively straightforward once the [[ectomycorrhizal-host-tree-partnerships]] was noted.

## The Russulae

The Russula family was considered "quite a safe one for beginners,"
distinguished by its extreme brittleness. Unlike the common mushroom, whose
gills are pliable and flesh can be bent without breaking, the Russulae snap
suddenly when bent and their gills break into small pieces rather than matting
down. Palmer's rule was: if the specimen exudes milk or juice when cut, reject
it. If perfectly dry, taste a small piece — non-edible Russulae will bite and
sting the tongue, while all edible varieties are tasteless, mild, or have the
flavor of chestnuts.

Palmer described the common *[[russula-alutacea]]* with its bright red cap, buff-
colored gills, and stout short stem, but noted that *R. emetica* and *R.
rosacea* resembled it so closely that he invariably tasted a small piece of each
stem while washing them to avoid any possibility of mistake. This taste-test
method, while effective for the Russulae, was one Palmer explicitly warned
against applying to the deadly Amanite family.

## The Meadow and Horse Mushrooms

The meadow mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) and the horse-mushroom (*Agaricus
arvensis*) were the most celebrated edible fungi, honored by English-speaking
people with the title of "a mushroom" (as if they were the only kind). At first
growth they resemble a puffball, but soon disclose under the cap a veil or web
that ruptures to expose gills free from the stem with a faint shade of pink.
This color deepens through purple into black as the mushroom matures. Though
difficult to describe precisely, "the taste and smell are so peculiar to the
species, that there is not much danger of deception when once you have made
their acquaintance."

## Palmer's Testing Protocol

Palmer advocated a cautious protocol for all unfamiliar mushrooms: trace the
specimen to its family, then broil a small piece with salt and butter on an
empty stomach. Increase the quantity in successive experiments. Non-edible fungi
typically revealed themselves during cooking — nauseous slime, grassy odor, or a
taste that destroyed all desire for another mouthful. This methodical approach
allowed Palmer to test perhaps forty varieties, of which he ate regularly more
than half.
