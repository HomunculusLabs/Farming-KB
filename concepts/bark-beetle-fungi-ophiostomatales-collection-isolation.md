## Overview

Fungi are often associated with beetles inhabiting bark and wood of living or recently dead trees, falling into two categories: (1) species commonly occurring with phloem-feeding beetles (bark beetles) usually in living trees, and (2) species associated with beetles requiring fungi as a primary nutrient resource in all life history stages (ambrosia beetles). The distinction is artificial, and the fungi are often closely related. The primary fungal associates include members of Ophiostomatales (Ophiostoma, Leptographium, Sporothrix, Raffaelea), Microascales (Ceratocystis, Chalara, Ambrosiella), yeasts (Saccharomycetales), corticioid basidiomycetes (Entomocorticium), and Xylariaceae (Xylaria).

True hosts are found in Curculionidae, especially subfamilies Scolytinae and Platypodinae. These fungi are economically important as dispersers of serious plant pathogens, causes of blue-stain wood discoloration, and in some cases as tree-killing agents.

## Mycangia

Certain beetles have evolved specialized structures called mycangia for storage, culture, and transport of fungi. Mycangia vary in complexity: some are elaborate with secretory cells (e.g., southern pine beetle), while most are simple pits in the exoskeleton of the head, pronotum, or elytra. Mycangia can be oral, pronotal, mesonotal, prothoracic pleural, promesonotal, or elytral (scolytines) or pitlike (platypodines). Mycangial fungi are typically mutualists, often displaying yeastlike morphology while in the mycangium and hyphal form outside. The sex of the beetle is important because mycangia usually develop only in the sex initiating the brood gallery.

## Bark Beetles: Collection Methods

### Trap Types

- **Pitfall traps:** For root-infesting weevils and beetles; constructed from capped plastic pipe with small entrance holes, baited with ethanol and turpentine, with pine stem section in bottom
- **Stem sections (billets):** Placed on soil surface to collect root-feeding beetles
- **Lower-stem flight traps:** Inverted plastic jug with collection jar, baited with turpentine and ethanol
- **Bounce traps:** Black pipe baited with ethanol and turpentine over water-filled pool
- **Lindgren multiple-funnel traps:** Hung near ground or in mid-upper canopy, baited with species-specific pheromones — must be sampled same day deployed to prevent predators destroying specimens
- **Flight traps baited with host compounds and/or aggregation pheromones**

### Important Considerations

- Collection jars filled with soapy water or insecticidal strips kill beetles but may alter fungal flora
- Dead beetles become overwhelmed by saprobic contaminants interfering with isolation
- Empty collection jars or daily collection from electric cooler preserve viable fungal associates
- Pheromones may serve as kairomonal attractants for predators, destroying specimens
- Tree-tissue samples from around feeding sites, entrance holes, and galleries can be collected aseptically for direct fungal isolation
- Phoretic tarsonemid mites can also be sources of fungi for culture

### Storage

Isolate fungi as soon as possible after collection. When not feasible, store insects, mites, and tree tissue in sterile vials in ice-filled coolers and refrigerate. Small pine twigs and moist paper in collection vials increase insect survival. Keep insects in separate containers to minimize cross-contamination. Tree tissue can be refrigerated or stored at room temperature in moist chambers to promote fungal sporulation.

## Isolation Techniques

### From Beetles (Bark Beetle Mycangia)

For southern pine beetle: remove pronotum from head and abdomen of adult female, remove legs with sterile forceps, place pronotum in sterile distilled water (1 min), modified White's solution (4 min), then two successive sterile distilled water rinses. Quarter the pronotum aseptically and place sections on malt extract agar (MEA) or MEA amended with 2 mg/ml benomyl for semi-selective isolation of mycangial fungi (Ceraotocystiopsis ranaculosus, Entomocorticium species A).

### From Tree Tissue

Phloem and xylem from around insect feeding sites, entrance holes, and galleries placed on water agar (WA). Fungal fruiting structures lining galleries may have spore masses transferred directly with a fine needle.

### Selective Media for Ophiostomatales

Ophiostomatales tolerate high levels of cycloheximide. Use MEA, WA, or PDA amended with:
- 200 ppm cycloheximide (inhibits non-ophiostomatalean fungi)
- 100 ppm streptomycin sulfate (inhibits bacteria)

### Crush and Plate Method

Beetles can be crushed on agar medium or ground in sterile distilled water in a glass tissue homogenizer. Aliquots plated directly or diluted and plated. This allows quantitative estimates of fungal propagules per insect.

### Incubation

Plates normally incubated at 20-25 degrees C; some associates grow better at cooler temperatures. Cultures stored on weak agar in vials at -20 degrees C or lyophilized. Serial transfer and storage on rich media not recommended.

## Ambrosia Beetles: Collection and Isolation

### Biology

Ambrosia beetle larvae are either mycetophagous (consume only fungi) or xylomycetophagous (consume both wood and fungal material). Adults transmit species-specific obligate fungal symbionts via ectodermal mycangia. Primary symbionts include Ambrosiella and Raffaelea (related to Ophiostoma and Ceratocystis). In warmer regions, Fusarium may be an ambrosial associate. Auxiliary or secondary ambrosial fungi have mucilaginous spores transmitted by phoretic mites and beetles.

### Seasonal Flight

In northern temperate regions, adults fly only during a short period (generally one month) in spring when temperatures reach 18 degrees C. Most species have a single annual flight; some (e.g., Monarthrum) have two generations and two flights. In semitropical/tropical regions, many species have multiple generations but seldom fly during dry seasons. Beetles tend to fly in late afternoon and early evening.

### Collection

- Live beetles caught in fine-mesh nets, mechanical rotary traps, or hand nets
- Unprocessed timber at sawmills, wind-thrown trees, and diseased trees attract ambrosia beetles
- Ethanol or beer used as additional attractant (beetles attracted to fermentation products of host timber)
- Beetles placed individually into sterile vials with damp sterile filter paper, cooled during transport
- Infested timber cut into ~1-meter lengths, surface cleaned and disinfected with ethanol, cut ends painted with paraffin wax to slow dehydration

### Isolation

Primary fungi are abundant in galleries only when larval stages are present (1-2 months after initial infestation). Galleries exposed by sawing thin sections from infested bole using alcohol-flamed saws and wood chisels. Visible fungal growth isolated with sterile fine forceps. Gallery slices preserved, dried, and mounted or mounted directly on slides with lactophenol-aniline blue.

Primary ambrosia fungi form thick, whitish palisade layers on gallery walls when eggs/larvae are present (Corthylus and most Xyleborus species). For xylomycetophagous insects, small slices and chips of wood should be placed aseptically in a sterile moist chamber to encourage fungal growth before contamination by saprobes.

### Mycangium Dissection

Beetles dissected under dissecting microscope on sterile alcohol-flamed glass slides in three drops of sterile saline or bovine serum. Body part containing mycangium separated in first drop, mycangium separated in second drop, broken apart and plated in third drop. Fungal cells verified under compound microscope (sink to bottom; insect fat droplets stay in suspension). Spot plated/streaked on isolation media, incubated at 22-25 degrees C.

### Surface Disinfection

Beetles surface disinfected by washing in sterile 0.1% HgCl2 solution or dilute sterile bleach (NaHCl2) for 2-4 minutes, followed by several sterile water rinses. Alternatively, alternately placed on sterile wet filter paper (18 hours) and dry sterile filter paper (6 hours) for several transfers.

### Culture Media

PDA, malt extract-yeast extract agar (MEYE), and dilute malt extract-yeast extract-glucose agar used for culturing primary mycangial symbionts. Prokaryotic microbes seldom encountered; antibiotics generally not used. Axenic cultures stored on slants of dilute malt extract-yeast extract-glucose agar.

### Sporulation Induction

Many filamentous ambrosia fungi fail to sporulate in culture. Approaches to induce sporulation:
- Increasing nitrogen content (L-proline) of the medium
- Buffering agar to near-neutral pH
- Elevating CO2 levels in culture container

Molecular studies show Ambrosiella is polyphyletic (some species related to Ceratocystis, others to Ophiostoma); Raffaelea species are related to Ophiostoma.
