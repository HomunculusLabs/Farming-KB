# Psilocybin and Addiction Treatment

While *The Psilocybin Solution* is primarily a philosophical work about consciousness and reality,
Powell discusses the therapeutic potential of psychedelics for addiction — both directly through
psilocybin's capacity for psychological restructuring and through related entheogenic compounds
like ibogaine that were receiving scientific attention at the time of writing.

## The Harvard Psilocybin Project and Non-Addictiveness

Powell details the systematic study of 175 subjects conducted under the Harvard Psilocybin
Project, where the experimental emphasis was upon providing a relaxed and supportive setting.
A key finding relevant to addiction was that there was no evidence for psychological or physical
addiction to psilocybin, although 90% of subjects wished to repeat the experience. No hangovers
were reported. In a six-month follow-up study, none of the subjects developed enduring psychotic
or neurotic symptoms.

This non-addictive profile contrasts sharply with substances like alcohol, which Powell notes
were used to "drown one's sorrows or deaden oneself to reality." Robert Graves predicted that
"jaded sensation seekers" would be dissatisfied with psilocybin as it was not a "drug" as such
since it failed to stupefy like alcohol.

## The Aztec Model: Sacred, Not Recreational

The Aztec and indigenous Mexican approach to psilocybin was fundamentally different from
recreational drug use. The mushroom was not taken "frivolously for excitement" but was spoken
of as "muy delicado" — perilous. It was referred to with awe and reverence. The Indians claimed
that the mushrooms "carry you there where God is." Indian shamans used the fungus for oracular
reasons in order to cure and prophesy. Wasson was at pains to portray this cultural phenomenon
in the respectful light it deserved. No Indian ate the mushroom frivolously for excitement.

This sacred, purposeful approach contrasts with the recreational use patterns that lead to
addiction with other substances. The mushroom was understood as a tool for healing and
knowledge, not as an escape or indulgence — a distinction Powell believes is essential for
understanding psychedelics' therapeutic potential.

## Ibogaine and the Breaking of Addiction Patterns

Powell dedicates significant attention to ibogaine, an alkaloid derived from the West African
plant *Tabernanthe iboga*, as a compelling case study of psychedelics treating addiction. The
Bwiti cult of Gabon and the Congo use ibogaine to free the soul and connect with God and
ancestors. A typical report from a native African user reads: "I wanted to know God — to know
things of the dead and the land beyond....I walked or flew over a long, multicoloured road or
over many rivers which led me to my ancestors, who then took me to the great gods."

Scientists established that ibogaine's psychological effects can break hard drug addiction.
Howard Lotsof, an ex-heroin addict cured through ibogaine, formed a company to promote its
medical use and patented ibogaine treatments in the mid-1980s. So assured was he of ibogaine's
capacity to break drug addiction that he invested heavily in proving its efficacy.

## The Visionary Mechanism

The mechanism Powell describes is one of visionary self-confrontation. Ibogaine induces
unusually intense and personally significant visionary effects — described by patients as
"movie-clips" or "a slide-show, each slide containing a picture of a specific event or
circumstance in the viewer's life." The presentation of visual material is rapid; some patients
describe it as a movie run at high speed.

Lotsof refers to these visions as having Freudian and Jungian connotations, conveying deep and
significant meaning that lies at the heart of ibogaine's efficacy in breaking patterns of
addiction. He believes ibogaine enables patients to re-evaluate their lives and see mistakes
that may have led them into uncontrollable bouts of drug-taking. After treatment with a single
dose, the majority of patients remain free from chemical dependence for three to six months,
indicating that ibogaine therapy needs to be on-going and accompanied by other treatments.

## Self-Knowledge as the Key Mechanism

This mechanism aligns with Powell's broader argument about psychedelics and self-knowledge. He
argues that psilocybin "forces one to confront bad habits and neuroses" and that "nothing
remains hidden to the mushroom." The tenet "know thyself" is bound up with all spiritual
disciplines, suggesting that one must come fully to terms with oneself before inwardly
developing consciousness.

Powell notes that "psychological struggles" are "somewhat inevitable if one has engaged with
the psychedelic experience." One sees oneself clearly without the superficial trappings of a
contrived image and personality. This will often lead to a psychological "shake-up" in persons
hitherto blind to self-knowledge. After all, the tenet 'know thyself' is bound up with all
spiritual disciplines, suggesting that one must come fully to terms with oneself before one can
begin to inwardly develop one's state of consciousness.

## Gaian Psychotherapy and Homeostasis

Powell frames shamanic use of psychoactive plants as a form of "Gaian psychotherapy." In Gaian
terms, the shamanic ingestion of plants and fungi is an entirely natural process which, when
the ecological system of shaman, tribe, and plant is taken into account, can be seen as
essentially homeostatic — one part of the environment acts upon another to restore harmony. In
this case, certain plants and fungi yield aid through their psychological effects.

He argues that shamanism is perhaps the oldest form of religious psychotherapy, and that the
knowledge gained by visionary shamans was used precisely to help heal the tribe. There is no
reason to assume such psychedelic shamanism is now impotent, especially within the Gaian
paradigm. In native societies without a health service or subjugation to pharmaceutical
conglomerates, the curative role of the shaman becomes an essential feature of daily life, with
natural plant allies being very much a tool of the healing trade.

## The Contrast with Conventional Intoxication

Powell repeatedly contrasts entheogenic use with conventional drug use. The plant substances
are "not inherently evil" but "become destructive if used in excess or for the wrong reasons,
much as any benign substance can become harmful if used beyond sensibility." Had Koestler been
in the possession of the right frame of mind, his experience might have revealed the mushroom's
"cultural healing potential" rather than confirming his pre-existing biases.

## Leary's Radical Perspective Shift

Powell notes that Leary reported learning more about the mind and the brain in four hours under
psilocybin than in fifteen years as a professional psychologist. Under the right conditions the
mushroom is able to restructure one's culturally determined concepts about reality, and proffer
an entirely different set of beliefs with which to navigate oneself through life. This capacity
for radical perspective shift is what Powell believes underlies the therapeutic potential of
psychedelics for conditions rooted in rigid, self-destructive patterns of thought and behaviour.
