## Biochar, Charcoal, and Seawater as Compost Tea Ingredients

[[elaine-ingham]]'s field guide explores unconventional [[compost]] tea ingredients including [[biochar]] (charcoal) and seawater, discussing their roles in supporting microbial communities and providing mineral nutrition.

## Charcoal and Biochar in Compost Tea

Biochar is a carbon-rich material produced by pyrolysis — the thermal decomposition of biomass in the absence of oxygen.

Ingham addresses charcoal as a compost tea ingredient, noting its capacity to provide surface area for microbial colonization and its ability to adsorb and slowly release nutrients.

The porous structure of biochar provides an enormous internal surface area (300-1500 m²/g) that serves as habitat for beneficial bacteria, fungi, and protozoa.

When added to compost tea, biochar particles become inoculated with the diverse microbial communities from the tea, creating a biological delivery system that can be applied to soil.

The biochar-microbe combination is particularly valuable for restoring degraded soils, because the biochar provides stable physical habitat while the inoculated microbes establish biological function.

## Biochar Maturation and Pre-Inoculation

Raw biochar straight from the kiln is not immediately beneficial and can actually harm soil biology if applied directly.

Fresh biochar has a highly adsorptive surface that can temporarily bind nutrients and water, making them unavailable to plants and soil organisms. This "nutrient lockup" effect is most pronounced in sandy or low-organic-matter soils.

Ingham emphasizes that biochar should be matured or pre-inoculated before use. Composting biochar with organic materials, or soaking it in compost tea, charges the biochar's surfaces with nutrients and microbial communities.

Adding biochar to the [[compost-tea-brewing]] process serves this pre-inoculation function, as the microbial community colonizes the biochar surfaces during the brewing period.

The residence time of biochar in compost tea matters — at least 12-24 hours of contact during brewing allows meaningful microbial colonization of the internal pore surfaces.

## Charcoal versus Biochar

Ingham distinguishes between simple charcoal (low-temperature production, often from brush piles or campfires) and purpose-made biochar (controlled pyrolysis at 400-700°C).

Simple charcoal can be used similarly to biochar in compost tea, but it has lower surface area, less pore structure, and may contain incomplete combustion products.

Purpose-made biochar from clean feedstocks at controlled temperatures produces more consistent results, with greater surface area and fewer contaminants.

For compost tea applications, either form provides microbial habitat, but biochar is preferred when consistent, predictable results are needed.

## Mineral Contributions from Biochar

Biochar contains residual minerals from the original feedstock — ash components including potassium, calcium, magnesium, and trace elements.

These minerals are gradually released as the biochar weathers in soil, providing a slow-release mineral supplement alongside the microbial habitat function.

The mineral content varies dramatically with feedstock: wood biochar is lower in ash minerals, while biochar from rice husks, nut shells, or bone contains higher mineral concentrations.

When biochar is included in compost tea, some of these minerals become immediately available through microbial solubilization during the brewing process.

## Seawater in Compost Tea

Ingham discusses seawater as a potential compost tea ingredient, noting its complete spectrum of dissolved minerals in roughly the proportions found in plant tissue.

Seawater contains all essential plant nutrients and trace elements in soluble, ionic forms — approximately 3.5% dissolved solids dominated by sodium chloride but also including magnesium, calcium, potassium, and all trace elements.

The concept is that diluted seawater provides a balanced mineral supplement that complements the biological activity of compost tea.

However, the sodium chloride content of seawater requires careful dilution. Full-strength seawater would create toxic salt levels in compost tea and soil.

Typical dilution rates are 1:100 to 1:1000 (seawater to fresh water), depending on application sensitivity. At these dilutions, the sodium concentration becomes manageable while trace minerals remain available.

## Fresh Seaweed versus Seawater

Ingham distinguishes between seawater as a mineral source and fresh seaweed as a biological inoculant and nutrient source.

Fresh seaweed provides both minerals (similar to diluted seawater) and organic compounds including alginates, mannitol, cytokinins, and auxins that stimulate plant growth.

Fresh seaweed also carries diverse marine microbial communities that can contribute unique organisms to the compost tea biology.

Seawater provides minerals without the organic growth stimulants or microbial diversity of fresh seaweed.

For compost tea, fresh seaweed (rinsed to remove excess salt) is generally preferred over seawater, because it contributes both biological and mineral benefits.

## Practical Considerations for Seawater Use

When using seawater in compost tea, several practical concerns must be addressed.

Dilution must be precise and consistent. Too much seawater introduces excessive sodium that can kill beneficial microbes during brewing and harm soil biology after application.

Source water quality matters — coastal seawater near pollution sources may contain heavy metals, hydrocarbons, or pathogens that could contaminate the tea.

Salt-sensitive crops and soils (especially those already prone to salinity) should not receive compost tea made with seawater.

The minerals in diluted seawater are primarily in simple ionic forms that are immediately available but also quickly leached. They complement the slower-release minerals from biochar and organic ingredients.

## E. Coli and Pathogen Safety

Ingham addresses pathogen concerns when discussing unconventional compost tea ingredients, particularly seawater and fresh materials.

Coastal seawater can contain elevated levels of fecal coliform bacteria, especially near estuaries, harbors, or areas with agricultural runoff.

If seawater is used in compost tea, it should be drawn from clean offshore sources and the dilution rate must be sufficient to reduce any pathogen load to safe levels.

The competitive aerobic environment of properly brewed AACT (maintaining dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L) naturally suppresses pathogenic bacteria including E. coli, providing an additional safety margin.

## Honey as a Microbial Tool

Ingham notes honey's antimicrobial properties and discusses how it affects "bad guys" — pathogenic organisms — while the beneficial organisms in compost tea can tolerate or even thrive with honey as a food source.

Honey's antimicrobial activity comes primarily from its high sugar concentration (osmotic effect), low water activity, and the presence of hydrogen peroxide produced by glucose oxidase.

In diluted form as a compost tea ingredient, honey provides simple sugars that feed beneficial bacteria without creating osmotic stress. The antimicrobial properties are reduced at dilution but may still provide some pathogen suppression.

Honey should not be used in high concentrations in compost tea, as the osmotic effect can kill beneficial organisms just as it kills pathogens.

## Integration with Other Ingredients

Biochar and seawater complement the standard compost tea ingredient suite rather than replacing it.

Biochar pairs well with humic acids — the humic molecules adsorb onto biochar surfaces, preventing the nutrient lockup effect and providing sustained food sources for colonizing microbes.

Seawater-derived minerals complement kelp (which provides similar minerals in organic-bound forms) and [[fish-hydrolysate]] (which provides nitrogen and amino acids).

The key principle is diversity: combining conventional and unconventional ingredients maximizes the range of nutrients, microbial foods, and habitat structures available in the finished tea.

## Key Takeaways

- Biochar provides massive surface area for microbial colonization and can be pre-inoculated during compost tea brewing.
- Raw biochar should always be matured or pre-inoculated before soil application to avoid nutrient lockup.
- Seawater provides a complete mineral spectrum but requires careful dilution (1:100 to 1:1000) to manage sodium content.
- Fresh seaweed is generally preferred over seawater for compost tea because it adds organic growth compounds and microbial diversity.
- Pathogen safety must be considered with seawater use — source from clean offshore locations and ensure adequate dilution.
- Honey at low dilution feeds beneficial bacteria; at high concentration it creates osmotic shock that harms all organisms.
