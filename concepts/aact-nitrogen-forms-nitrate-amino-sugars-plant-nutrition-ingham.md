## AACT Nitrogen Forms — Nitrate vs Amino Sugars in Plant Nutrition

## Overview

One of the most important yet least understood aspects of AACT ([[teaming-with-nutrients-natural-vs-synthetic-fertilizers]] that provide nitrogen primarily as nitrate or ammonium, AACT delivers nitrogen in multiple biological forms including amino sugars, amino acids, peptides, and microbial biomass. Dr. Elaine Ingham"s work emphasizes that the form of nitrogen available to plants profoundly affects plant health, disease resistance, and [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] forms:

- **Nitrate (NO₃⁻)**: The most common form in synthetic fertilizers; highly soluble and mobile in soil; readily taken up by plants but also easily leached into groundwater
- **Ammonium (NH₄⁺)**: A direct product of mineralization; available to plants but can become toxic at high concentrations; rapidly converts to nitrate under aerobic conditions

Inorganic nitrogen is immediately available to plants, which explains its popularity in conventional agriculture. However, this immediate availability comes at a cost: plants that receive primarily nitrate nitrogen tend to accumulate nitrates in their tissues, which has implications for both plant health and human nutrition.

### Organic Nitrogen Forms

AACT and healthy soil biology produce organic nitrogen forms that are fundamentally different from inorganic nitrogen:

- **Amino acids**: The building blocks of proteins; directly absorbable by plant roots
- **Amino sugars**: Components of microbial cell walls (chitin, peptidoglycan); released when bacteria and fungi are consumed by predators
- **Peptides**: Short chains of amino acids; intermediate breakdown products of protein
- **Proteins**: Larger molecular weight compounds; require further breakdown before plant uptake
- **Microbial biomass nitrogen**: Nitrogen contained within living and dead microbial cells

These organic forms represent a slow-release, plant-available nitrogen source that works in harmony with plant metabolism rather than overwhelming it.

## The Soil Food Web Nitrogen Pathway

Ingham's central thesis is that plants should receive nitrogen through biological cycling rather than direct chemical application. The pathway works as follows:

1. Organic matter (compost, plant residues, cover crops) enters the soil
2. Bacteria and fungi decompose this material, incorporating nitrogen into their biomass
3. Microbial predators (protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods) consume bacteria and fungi
4. Predators excrete excess nitrogen as ammonium — this is the "predator prey nutrient cycling" mechanism
5. Plants absorb ammonium and organic nitrogen forms from the rhizosphere
6. In aerobic soils with healthy biology, some ammonium converts to nitrate, but the process is moderated by biological demand

This pathway ensures that nitrogen is released gradually, matching plant demand rather than flooding the root zone with excess soluble nitrogen.

## Why Nitrogen Form Matters for Plant Health

### Nitrate-Feeding and Disease Susceptibility

Ingham's research demonstrates that plants fed primarily nitrate nitrogen are more susceptible to diseases and pests:

- Nitrate accumulation in plant tissue creates a nutrient-rich environment for foliar pathogens
- Plants grown with excessive nitrate develop thinner cell walls, making them easier for pathogens to penetrate
- Nitrate-fed plants tend to produce simpler amino acid profiles in their sap, which are more attractive to insect pests
- High nitrate levels in plant tissue correlate with reduced concentrations of defensive compounds (phenolics, tannins, alkaloids)
