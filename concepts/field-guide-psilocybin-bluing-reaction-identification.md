# Field Guide to [[psilocybin-bluing-reaction]] and Identification

The bluing reaction is the most reliable field identification tool for
psilocybin-containing mushrooms. This page covers the oxidation
mechanism, practical testing procedure, known exceptions, and the
comprehensive field identification checklist from F.C. Ghouled's 1972
field guide.

## The Oxidation Process

All psilocybin mushroom species described in this guide share a key
biochemical feature: an enzyme that co-occurs with the active
alkaloids psilocybin and psilocin. When the flesh of the
mushroom is damaged, this enzyme initiates an oxidation process that
produces a distinctive blue coloration. The reaction is a direct
indicator of the presence of the target alkaloids and serves as the
primary field test for identification.

The blue color is described as being similar to blue ink. If the
flesh of the mushroom has a yellowish tone, the resulting oxidation
color will appear blue-green rather than pure blue. This color
variation is normal and does not indicate a different or
non-psychoactive species.

## Timing of the Bluing Reaction

The oxidation process is not instantaneous. The guide specifies that
the reaction takes from **20 to 120 minutes** to fully develop after
the flesh is damaged. This timing range is important for the field
mycologist to understand:

- The reaction may begin as a faint discoloration within the first
  20 minutes, but a negative result at 15 minutes is not conclusive.
- Some specimens react faster than others. The inside flesh of a
  broken stem of [[psilocybe-cubensis]] typically yields the fastest
  bluing reaction.
- [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] shows the bluing most prominently in the
  stem rather than the cap.
- Patience is essential. The full color may take up to two hours to
  develop.

The guide notes that certain chemicals can accelerate the oxidation
process, but these reagents are unstable, difficult to work with in
the field, and generally not worth the trouble for most hunters.

## How to Perform the Bluing Test

Select a fresh specimen, crack or bruise the stem to expose the inner
flesh, and observe the damaged area over 20 to 120 minutes. The stem
is the best location to observe the reaction. The cap may also show
blueing with age or handling, particularly in [[psilocybe-caerulescens]],
but the stem crack test is more definitive.

## Reliability of the Bluing Reaction

The bluing reaction is a highly reliable indicator. All psilocybin
mushrooms in this guide turn blue, with the exception of one uncommon
species not included. Combined with other criteria (substrate, cap
shape, gill color, hollow stem), the guide states there is "no chance
for error" when all three primary criteria are met: growth on manure,
bluing reaction, and hollow stem.

## Non-Psilocybin Blueing Species: Exceptions

One non-psychoactive genus also produces a blue-staining reaction,
and the guide provides clear distinguishing characteristics to
prevent misidentification:

- These mushrooms are **large, bulbous, and very smooth** with a
  fat cap that cannot be confused with the smaller psilocybin
  species.
- Coloration is **yellow or yellow-brown** evenly over the entire
  surface. Blueing may appear on parts of the stem with age.
- The **underside of the cap has pores** instead of gills. These
  pores resemble an organic sponge.
- The **stem is proportioned like the cap**, quite solid and fleshy
  (not hollow).
- These species do **not** occur on dung but may be found in
  pastures and lawns after rain.

The guide notes that "to even the least observant person the
difference in physical appearance is extremely obvious." The pore
structure beneath the cap is the most definitive distinguishing
feature, as all three target psilocybin species have gills.

## Identification Prologue

The guide opens its identification section with a clear directive:
beginners must search cow pastures after rainstorms during months
with temperatures between 65 and 85 degrees Fahrenheit. Only
specimens that meet all three criteria should be considered:
growing on manure, turning blue when damaged, and possessing a
hollow stem. This triple filter is designed to be simple enough for
novices while eliminating all risk of misidentification with
dangerous species.

## Field Identification Checklist

The guide provides a structured identification sheet for use in the
field. The following categories should be evaluated for each
specimen collected:

1. **Age and condition** of the specimen
2. **Bluing reaction** (does it turn blue?)
3. **Cap:** coloration, shape and size, margin (incurved or
   regular)
4. **Gills:** coloration, shape and size, spacing, attachment to
   stem
5. **Stem:** coloration, shape and size, base (volva) present,
   hollow or solid
6. **Flesh:** coloration, texture, thickness, special color traits
7. **Climate and environment:** last rain (24 hours or longer),
   high and low temperatures (24 hours or past week), substrate
   the fungus is growing on
8. **Species determination** based on the above characteristics

## Safety Warnings

Several non-psilocybin species are dangerously poisonous, causing
symptoms ranging from severe cramping and vomiting to death. The
guide strongly warns that no mushroom collected by a beginner should
be eaten in the field. Specimens should be compared to descriptions
and photographs first. Even when confident in identification, only a
tiny test bite should be consumed initially. Six of the eight
alkaloids in the chemical structure are toxic, producing a slight
queasiness even from genuine psilocybin mushrooms.
