## 4. Wood Chips and Bark Mulch

This habitat type has become increasingly important as landscaping
practices have spread. Wood chips used in gardens, nurseries, and along
pathways create an ideal substrate for many lignicolous Psilocybe species.

Psilocybe cyanescens has famously established itself in wood-chip trails
that meander through gardenlike settings bordered by rhododendrons and
shade-providing shrubs. Psilocybe azurescens grows prolifically on
deciduous wood chips and in sandy soils rich in lignous debris along the
northern Oregon coast.

Psilocybe subaeruginascens grows on soils enriched with woody debris, in
wood chips, and in wood chips mixed with horse dung, frequently found along
trails or roadsides bordering deciduous forests.

Species found here: Psilocybe cyanescens, Psilocybe azurescens, Psilocybe
stuntzii, [[psilocybe-caerulescens]], Psilocybe zapotecorum, and Psilocybe
aztecorum are all tropical/subtropical species with deep cultural
significance.

In the subtropical Far East, including India, Thailand, Vietnam, and
Cambodia, P. cubensis is widespread. Psilocybe samuiensis was discovered
on the island of Koh Samui, Thailand, where it grows in seasonally
flooded rice paddies and water buffalo pastures.

Species found here: Psilocybe cubensis, Psilocybe mexicana, Psilocybe
caerulescens, Psilocybe zapotecorum, Psilocybe aztecorum, Panaeolus
tropicalis.

## Seasonal Considerations

In temperate regions, fall is the most productive season for psilocybin
mushroom hunting. However, many species have extended seasons. Along the
Pacific Northwest coast, fruiting can continue from September through
January. In tropical and subtropical regions, the rainy season determines
fruiting, with some species fruiting year-round in consistently humid
conditions.

The best time to hunt is after a period of warm, humid weather following
rainfall. Temperature, humidity, and substrate moisture are all critical
factors. Cold-tolerant species like Psilocybe azurescens can continue
fruiting well after the first frost.
