## The Mycophilia/Mycophobia Hypothesis

Wasson's investigation was prompted by the observation that his Russian wife Valentina loved mushrooms while he himself feared them. He developed the thesis that the world's peoples could be divided into mycophilic (mushroom-loving) and mycophobic (mushroom-fearing) cultures — a dichotomy he believed had ancient roots in a lost mushroom religion.

This hypothesis, once influential, has been thoroughly critiqued:
- The term "phobia" implies irrational panic, whereas mushroom fear is wholly rational given the lethal consequences of misidentification
- A Colorado study found only six poisoning cases per year in a non-foraging culture, while in the mycophilic Polish province of Poznan, fifty incidents per year occurred with 10% fatality — making the "mycophilic" culture more dangerous
- Patterns of mycophagy change with time (Britain had commercial wild mushroom markets in the 19th century)
- Wasson relied on the outdated Frazerian comparative method of cultural evolution

## The Velada and Maria Sabina

Wasson learned of a Mazatec mushroom ceremony — the velada — and traveled to Huautla de Jimenez in Oaxaca, Mexico. There he encountered Maria Sabina, a curandera who held mushroom veladas for healing and divination. He participated in ceremonies in 1955 and subsequently published his account in Life magazine.

Wasson described Sabina in exalted terms: "Religion Incarnate, hierophant, thaumaturge, psychopompos." She fit his preconceptions of what a priestess of an ancient mushroom cult should look like — devout, suffering, compassionate, generous, humble — a "most Mary-like figure" that slotted into his High Church expectations.

## Consequences of the Life Article

The 1957 article had profound and largely unintended consequences:

- Within months, psychedelic seekers were knocking on Sabina's door
- Oaxaca became awash with hippies on the "magic mushroom trail"
- Rock stars including Pete Townshend, John Lennon, and Bob Dylan reportedly made pilgrimages
- The trickle became a flood, transforming Huautla into an essential destination

For Sabina, the consequences were devastating. Her community blamed her for bringing unwanted attention. She was eventually imprisoned and her house burned down. Wasson had published her name in Russia and History (though not in Life), but the information quickly became public knowledge.

## Critique of Wasson's Methods

Letcher identifies several problems with Wasson's approach:

1. **Superficial fieldwork**: Lightning visits of only a few days, rather than the months and years needed to build trust and rapport
2. **Cultural bias**: He failed to distinguish between the taciturn Mazatec and their animated velada behavior; failed to recognize that many non-psychoactive mushroom species were considered powerful healing agents
3. **Confirmation bias**: He dismissed other curanderos as "second rate" — Aurelio Carreras and Matias Garcia were held in high regard locally, but Wasson rated them inferior
4. **Christian framing**: He pictured his ancient mushroom cult as a form of proto-Christian mysticism, with mushrooms foreshadowing the sacrament of bread and wine
5. **Hypocrisy**: Despite condemning hippy "profanation," Wasson arranged for Albert Hofmann to mail psilocybin pills to his California address, smuggled from the Sandoz factory in Switzerland
