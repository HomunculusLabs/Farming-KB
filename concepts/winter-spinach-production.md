# Spinach Production in Unheated Greenhouses

Spinach is the single most important leafy winter crop in the unheated greenhouse system. It is remarkably cold-hardy, produces over a long harvest window, and serves dual purposes as both a salad-mix ingredient and a bulk leaf crop. Many experienced winter growers consider it the one crop they would choose if limited to a single winter leafy green.

## Why Spinach Leads Winter Production

Spinach earns its place as the backbone crop of winter greenhouses for several reasons:

- **Exceptional cold tolerance**: Survives and continues slow growth even when outdoor temperatures drop well below freezing
- **Long harvest window**: A single September planting yields five or more harvests spanning from Thanksgiving through late March
- **Dual market**: Sells as both baby leaves in salad mix and as large leaves for bulk greens
- **High total yield per square foot**: With proper harvest technique, regrowth is strong and cumulative yield exceeds that of [[cut-and-come-again]] methods
- **Customer demand**: Spinach is universally popular and commands strong prices in winter when local supply is scarce

## Planting Schedule

For the standard winter greenhouse crop, plant outdoors during the **second to third week of September** in well-composted soil. The plants establish during the relatively mild fall weather and develop a robust root system before hard freezes arrive. A [[mobile-greenhouse]] is moved over the beds by late October to provide protection.

This timing produces the first harvest by Thanksgiving and supports four to five additional harvests per bed through late March or early April.

## Harvest Technique: Leaf-Only Cutting

The harvest method has a significant impact on both quality and total yield. Rather than cutting entire plants at the base (cut-and-come-again), individual leaves are harvested **without stems** using a very sharp small-bladed knife (bird's-beak type).

Advantages of leaf-only harvesting:
- **Superior regrowth**: The plant retains its growing point and continues producing new leaves
- **Greater total yield per square foot**: More harvests are possible from the same bed
- **Exceptional product quality**: Stemless leaves are tender and more pleasant to eat
- **Premium pricing**: The quality justifies a commensurate price

While leaf-by-leaf harvesting is slower work than shearing entire plants, experienced crews become very efficient with practice. The economic return from additional harvests and higher prices more than compensates for the slower harvest pace.

## Space Allocation

Spinach commands a significant share of total greenhouse area—nearly **half** of all winter greenhouse space in the Eliot Coleman system. This allocation reflects both the crop's economic importance and the reliable demand for winter spinach across all market channels.

## Soil Preparation

Well-composted soil is essential. Spinach is a moderately heavy feeder, and the long harvest season demands sustained fertility. Generous applications of finished compost at planting time provide the nutrient base. The compost also improves soil structure and moisture retention, both critical for the repeated harvest cycles.

## Uses in the Market

### Salad Mix Component
Small spinach leaves are cut at approximately three-quarters of full size and included in mesclun-style salad mixes. At this stage the leaves are tender, mild, and add both visual appeal and nutritional value to the mix.

### Bulk Leaf Sales
Full-sized leaves are sold by the pound for cooking greens. Chefs and home cooks prize greenhouse spinach for its tenderness compared to field-grown spinach available from distant sources during winter.

## Seasonal Timeline

| Period | Status |
|--------|--------|
| Mid-September | Seed sown outdoors |
| Late October | Greenhouse moved over beds |
| Late November | First harvest (Thanksgiving sales) |
| December–March | Four additional harvests at roughly 3-week intervals |
| Late March / Early April | Final harvest; plants begin bolting |

## Rotation Considerations

After the final spinach harvest in late March or early April, the bed is immediately available for warm-season crops. In the Coleman system, the spinach bed often transitions to tomatoes—the spinach is pulled just before it bolts, the bed is top-dressed with an inch of compost, and tomato transplants go in by early April. This seamless transition maximizes the number of harvests per bed per year.

## Varieties

While the source material emphasizes timing and technique over specific varieties, successful winter spinach production depends on selecting cold-hardy, slow-bolting varieties. Trial new varieties annually, as breeding programs continually improve winter spinach genetics.

## Key Management Points

- **Sharp harvest tools**: A bird's-beak knife minimizes damage to remaining leaves and crowns
- **Avoid harvesting when frozen**: Wait until leaves thaw to prevent tissue damage
- **Consistent moisture**: Even under greenhouse cover, winter spinach benefits from irrigation during dry spells
- **Monitor for bolting**: As day length increases in March, spinach will eventually bolt; time the final harvest accordingly
- **Pest pressure is minimal**: The cold largely eliminates insect pressure, one of the advantages of winter production
