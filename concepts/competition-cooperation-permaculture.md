# Competition and Cooperation in Permaculture

Holmgren's eighth principle ("Integrate Rather Than Segregate") opens into a deep analysis of the balance between competition and cooperation in natural and human systems. This analysis has profound implications for [[permaculture-design]], [[permaculture-community-scale-design]], and the transition from a [[consumer-versus-conserver-society]].

## Ecology of Competition and Cooperation

Holmgren synthesizes a key ecological pattern: "In nature, systems that are immature and growing rapidly, in a situation of surplus free energy, tend to be dominated by competitive relationships; mature ecosystems, in which there is little free or surplus energy, show a high degree of mutualistic and symbiotic relationships."

A rainforest clearing -- with abundant sunlight and fertility -- produces "a profusion of competing annual weeds, vines and trees." In the mature forest, adjacent plants develop "mutual or symbiotic relationships, such as trees that exchange nutrients via mycorrhizal fungi." The lesson for design is that cooperation is the hallmark of mature, energy-efficient systems.

This ecological pattern underpins a central argument of Holmgren's book: "Co-operative and symbiotic relationships will be more adaptive in a future of declining energy." Permaculture can be seen as part of a long tradition of concepts that emphasise mutualistic and symbiotic relationships over competitive and predatory ones. Declining energy availability will shift the general perception of these concepts from romantic idealism to practical necessity.

## Pioneer vs. Climax Communities

This ecological pattern parallels human social organization:
- **Pioneer communities** (rapidly growing, tapping new resources) tend toward competition -- whether colonial settlement or economic expansion
- **Stable traditional societies** (resources fully allocated) develop "defined roles, mutual obligations, gifting, taxation and other social mechanisms" that prevail over competitive ones

Some hunter-gatherer societies in harsh environments regarded competition between group members as evil. Inuit groups reportedly killed missionaries who introduced competitive team sports. The pattern connects directly to [[succession-in-permaculture]] -- pioneer and climax ecological stages mirror human social dynamics.

## Types of Ecological Relationships

Holmgren characterises ecological relationships on a scale from destructive to constructive:

- **Predatory relationships**: One organism lives by the death of another, but interdependence develops. Predators depend on the reproductive health of prey; prey depend on predators to cull the sick and weak.
- **Parasitic relationships**: Parasites feed off larger host organisms, generally reducing health without killing. This delicate balance tends to favour mildly debilitating parasites over lethal ones.
- **Competitive relationships**: Organisms with the same needs struggle for resources. Competition is more common between individuals of the same species.
- **Avoidance relationships**: Diversity and specialisation allow more efficient use of resources by avoiding competition. Different root depths, foraging times, and canopy positions let species coexist without conflict.
- **Mutualism**: Organisms with differing needs provide benefits to each other. Flocking behaviour, forest understorey dynamics, and shared food sources are examples.
- **Symbiosis**: Organisms become so interdependent they cannot live without each other. Lichen (alga + fungus) and nitrogen-fixing bacteria on legume roots are classic examples.

Holmgren notes that symbiotic integration is now thought to be one of the important mechanisms driving sudden evolutionary jumps. The nucleated cell, the building block of all complex organisms, likely originated from a symbiosis between two previously free-living life forms.

## Internal Cooperation, External Competition

Self-organizing systems tend to "internalise co-operation between member species and externalise competition with other ecosystems." Grasslands and forests compete for territory through fire, grazing, bird-distributed seed, and shading. "Mature ecosystems exploit immature ecosystems" -- the red gum floodplain forests catch nutrients eroding from steeper, fire-prone catchments; rainforests gain nutrients from smoke and ash of adjacent savannah fires.

Permaculture designs often "take advantage of the losses of unsustainable land use." Nutrients in catchment runoff are intercepted by dams; spoiled hay from farms that discard it is used to establish gardens. Wind-eroded soil and fertiliser drift from cropland catch in well-established shelterbelts and roadside vegetation. Holmgren notes the ethical complexity: "We are more likely to be able to decide the ethical course of action in any particular situation if we understand this tendency."

## Nature Determines Society

Tim Flannery has argued that Australian ecosystems and people have a strong disposition towards co-operative rather than competitive relationships because of the harsh and infertile environment. While fossil-fuel-based capitalism masked cooperative rural culture in Australia, the broader pattern holds: resource-rich environments foster more violent histories. America's greater natural wealth correlates with a more violent pioneer history. The Middle East, as the world's richest energy region, has been far from peaceful. As energy descent progresses, the competitive social structures of energy-rich environments will increasingly fail.

## Tribal Conflict and Global Interconnection

Holmgren analyzes tribal conflict as a pattern that was functional for most of human prehistory but has become "dysfunctional" in a globally interconnected world. Tribal hunter-gatherer societies had high degrees of internal co-operation, while competition between tribes resulted in occasional warfare that applied selection pressure on surplus young males without too much adverse impact on the tribe as a whole.

"The lack of new territory or resources to exploit, and the degree of global interconnection and integration, have dramatically shrunk the opportunities for functional competition and conflict in human systems." He describes current global conflicts as "a condensation of the ancient 'war between the tribes,' analogous to a boil on the skin which condenses and excretes a deep systemic disorder."

In the last 2000 years, humanity has been developing new patterns of collective identity and conflict sublimation (diplomacy, commerce, sport) but these solutions "will end up recreating the problem in new forms." The most obviously dysfunctional examples -- Rwanda, Bosnia, Palestine -- are recognisable as boils on the skin of humanity, but the forces driving these expressions of primitive behaviour are less obvious. The very solutions created to avoid larger-scale warfare may be major contributors to these outbreaks.

## Corporate Ecology

Holmgren extends the competition/cooperation framework to analyze corporations as "super-organisms driving human evolution." The global corporate ecosystem "has come into being based on the consumption of both the biological and fossil resources of the planet and the human resources of traditional cultures and values."

Traditional values that catalyzed capitalism's growth -- "frugality, household self-reliance, the work ethic, the rule of law, free markets" -- are undermined by that same growth, "just as the fertility of prime agricultural soils has been undermined by chemical monocultures." The shift from segregated local economies to a highly integrated global economy reflects the maturing of capitalism and signals that the energy flows that spawned capitalism are slowing.

While this global ecosystem is clearly born from human organisation and culture, Holmgren argues it can be described as "anti-people, anti-nature, and set on a course of self-destruction." He uses the term "alien" not to describe the people within these structures, but to emphasise the emergent potential of institutions that become self-organising without the constraint of human values and ethics.

## Materialism and Spirituality

The corporate capital version of an emergent hive mind races against limits to growth, but its real Achilles heel may be the denial of the spiritual side of humanity. Holmgren notes the nexus between fossil fuels and religious fundamentalism as a focal point drawing materialist and spiritual worlds together in an emergent destructive union.

Rather than struggling to keep spiritual and material in separate domains, we need to recognise that the gravitational pull toward integrated systems is always at work. "If we don't creatively design an ethical integration, then larger systemic forces will create one that is less sensitive to human values." The Yin-Yang symbol represents systemic thinking in which polar opposites form an integrated whole and each contains the seeds of the other.

## Implications for Design

The permaculture designer must:
1. **Design for maturity**: Create systems that favor cooperation over competition as they develop
2. **Understand competitive dynamics**: Don't simply declare competition "bad" -- understand its functions at different scales
3. **Foster internal cooperation**: Within designs, households, and communities, create mutualistic relationships
4. **Recognize the transition**: As energy descent progresses, competitive social structures will increasingly fail, and cooperative alternatives will become more viable
5. **Navigate ethics carefully**: Using the "losses" of unsustainable systems requires ethical judgment about exploitation vs. beneficial cycling
6. **Value diversity in relationships**: Just as ecological niches reduce competition, social and functional diversity in design reduces conflict
7. **Design for resilience**: Build systems with backup elements and complementary contributions that mirror natural redundancy
