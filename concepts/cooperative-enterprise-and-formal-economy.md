## Cooperative Enterprise and Formal Economy

## Overview

The formal economy encompasses all goods and services that operate under a legal umbrella with proper accounting, taxation, and regulatory compliance. In [[permaculture-design]], creating viable formal economic structures is essential for communities seeking self-reliance. Rather than retreating entirely from the monetary economy, [[permaculture]] advocates building ethical, cooperative enterprises that serve community needs while remaining integrated within the broader economic system.

## The Formal Economy

Every society requires a formal economic sector to handle transactions that demand legal recognition, contractual obligations, and transparent accounting. The formal economy provides the infrastructure through which communities can trade, save, invest, and plan for the future. Without formal structures, communities remain vulnerable to exploitation and unable to access the financial tools necessary for long-term stability.

In permaculture, the goal is not to abandon the formal economy but to reshape it so that it serves ecological and social well-being rather than extracting wealth from communities and ecosystems. A sound economy is one that operates within planetary limits and distributes benefits equitably among participants.

The [[informal-economy-and-community-currencies]] — barter, gift exchange, mutual aid — remains important and complements the formal sector. However, many essential transactions such as land purchase, equipment acquisition, and export of surplus production require formal legal and financial structures. Permaculture communities that fail to establish these structures risk remaining marginal and unable to scale their impact.

## Formal Structures Needed by Communities

Mollison identifies several formal structures that communities need to establish in order to build a robust and self-reliant local economy:

- **Cooperatives**: Member-owned and democratically controlled enterprises that distribute profits to members rather than external shareholders. Cooperatives can operate in any sector — agriculture, housing, manufacturing, retail, or services.

- **Community Savings and Loans**: Locally controlled financial institutions that pool member savings and provide affordable credit for community projects and individual needs. These keep capital circulating within the community rather than being extracted by distant banks.

- **Small Business Service Offices**: Shared administrative, legal, and accounting resources that reduce overhead for small enterprises. By pooling back-office functions, individual businesses can remain competitive without sacrificing independence.

- **Investment Funds**: Community-controlled pools of capital directed toward local enterprises, infrastructure, and regenerative projects. These funds ensure that community savings are invested in the community itself.

- **Leasing Companies**: Cooperative ownership of expensive equipment, vehicles, or facilities that individual members can lease as needed. This reduces the capital barrier to entry for small producers and businesses.

Together, these structures form an economic ecosystem that enables communities to retain wealth, create meaningful livelihoods, and fund the transition to sustainable systems.

## Cooperative Principles

The international cooperative movement has established a set of guiding principles that distinguish cooperatives from conventional businesses:

1. **Open Membership**: Cooperatives are open to all persons able to use their services and willing to accept the responsibilities of membership, without gender, social, racial, political, or religious discrimination.

2. **Democratic Organization**: Cooperatives are democratic organizations controlled by their members, who actively participate in setting policies and making decisions. Each member has one vote regardless of how much capital they have invested.

3. **Limited Interest on Share Capital**: Members receive only limited compensation on capital subscribed as a condition of membership. This prevents the accumulation of wealth through ownership alone and ensures that labor and participation are the primary sources of economic return.

4. **Surplus Belongs to Members**: Any surplus or profit generated by the cooperative is distributed among members in proportion to their participation — not their capital investment. Surpluses can also be allocated to reserves, community projects, or member education.

5. **Education and Training**: Cooperatives provide education and training for their members, elected representatives, managers, and employees so that they can contribute effectively to the development of their cooperatives.

6. **Cooperation Between Cooperatives**: Cooperatives serve their members most effectively and strengthen the cooperative movement by working together through local, national, regional, and international structures.

These principles align closely with [[permaculture-ethics]] of earth care, people care, and fair share. Cooperatives represent a practical application of these ethics in the economic sphere.

## The Mondragon Model

The Mondragon Corporation in the Basque region of Spain stands as one of the most successful examples of large-scale cooperative enterprise. At the time of Mollison's writing, the Mondragon network comprised 96 cooperatives with approximately 17,000 worker-members, making it one of the largest industrial groups in Spain.

Key features of the Mondragon model include:

- **Entry Investment**: New worker-members contribute approximately $5,000 as their share of entry capital, which gives them both ownership and voting rights in the cooperative.

- **Capital Account Structure**: The capital accounts of each cooperative are split 20% collective and 80% individual. The collective portion serves as a shared reserve that strengthens the entire cooperative network, while the individual portion tracks each member's accumulated equity.

- **Inter-Cooperative Support**: The network includes its own bank (Caja Laboral), research center, technical school, and social welfare system. When one cooperative faces difficulty, the network can redirect resources to support it, preventing job losses and business failures.

- **Democratic Governance**: Every worker-member has one vote. Major decisions are made collectively, and management is accountable to the workforce rather than to external investors.

- **Wage Solidarity**: The ratio between the highest and lowest paid workers within a cooperative is limited, preventing the extreme inequality characteristic of conventional corporate structures.

Mondragon demonstrates that cooperative enterprise can compete effectively in global markets while maintaining democratic control and equitable distribution of wealth. The model has since grown significantly, yet its foundational principles remain instructive for any community seeking to build cooperative economic structures.

## True Democracy and Peace

Mollison connects cooperative economics to a broader philosophy of true democracy and peace. Genuine democracy extends beyond periodic voting in political elections — it requires that people have meaningful control over the economic institutions that shape their daily lives. When workers own and manage their workplaces, democracy becomes a lived practice rather than an abstract ideal.

Peace, in this framework, is not merely the absence of war but the presence of justice, equity, and ecological harmony. Economic systems that concentrate wealth and power inevitably generate conflict — both social and ecological. Cooperative economics, by distributing power and wealth broadly, creates the material conditions for lasting peace.

Mollison suggests that true peace cannot be legislated or imposed from above. It must emerge from economic arrangements that give every person a stake in the system and a voice in its governance. When people are secure in their livelihoods and empowered in their workplaces, the frustrations and resentments that fuel violence lose their economic basis.

## The Lifetime Concept

The lifetime concept emphasizes that economic decisions should be evaluated over the full lifespan of their consequences — not merely over quarterly profit cycles. Investments in soil health, forest systems, community infrastructure, and human education may take decades to yield their full returns. A lifetime perspective requires patience, long-term planning, and economic structures that are not pressured by short-term shareholder demands.

Cooperatives are naturally aligned with the lifetime concept because their members are invested in the long-term health of the enterprise and the community, not in extracting maximum profit in the shortest possible time. Worker-owners who plan to spend their careers in the cooperative have every incentive to manage resources sustainably and invest in future productivity.

## Ethical Banking

Ethical banking refers to financial institutions that operate according to social and ecological principles rather than purely profit-maximizing criteria. Community banks, credit unions, and cooperative banks channel deposits toward local enterprises, affordable housing, renewable energy, and other productive investments rather than speculative activities.

In the cooperative economy, ethical banking serves as the circulatory system — connecting savers with borrowers, recycling capital within the community, and ensuring that financial resources flow toward regenerative rather than extractive activities. Without ethical banking, even well-intentioned cooperatives may find themselves dependent on conventional banks that do not share their values. Mollison saw ethical banking as a non-negotiable component of community self-reliance — a financial system rooted in place, accountable to people, and oriented toward the long-term health of both communities and ecosystems.

## Changing Ourselves

Mollison argues that building a cooperative economy requires not just structural change but personal transformation. We must change ourselves — our habits of consumption, our relationship to money, our willingness to cooperate rather than compete — in order to live in harmony with life processes. The shift from individualism to cooperation, from extraction to regeneration, from short-term thinking to lifetime perspective, is fundamentally a cultural and psychological shift.

This inner work is inseparable from the outer work of building cooperatives, community institutions, and sustainable economies. The two reinforce each other: cooperative structures provide the material basis for ethical living, while ethical commitment provides the motivation to sustain cooperative structures through difficult times. Permaculture therefore demands both inner and outer transformation — a simultaneous redesign of landscapes, economies, and consciousness.

## Sound Economy vs. Unlimited Growth Capitalism

A central tension in [[pdc-right-livelihood-and-permaculture-economics]] is the conflict between sound economy and unlimited growth capitalism. A sound economy operates within ecological limits, distributes benefits fairly, and builds durable wealth over time. Unlimited growth capitalism, by contrast, treats the economy as separate from ecology, externalizes environmental and social costs, and requires perpetual expansion on a finite planet.

Cooperative enterprise represents a path toward sound economy. By removing the imperative to generate returns for external shareholders, cooperatives can prioritize worker well-being, environmental stewardship, and [[permaculture-community-resilience]]. They can choose to grow slowly or not at all, to invest in [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]], and to accept lower but more stable returns. The cooperative model proves that an economy can serve people and planet without sacrificing productivity or innovation.

## Personal Independence and Cooperative Society

There is no contradiction between personal independence and cooperative society. In fact, permaculture argues that genuine personal independence is only possible within a cooperative social framework. Isolated individuals are vulnerable to economic forces beyond their control. Cooperative membership provides economic security, democratic participation, and access to shared resources — all of which enhance rather than diminish individual autonomy.

The cooperative model reconciles the human need for freedom with the equally human need for community. Each member retains ownership of their individual capital account and their personal skills, while participating in collective decision-making and sharing in collective prosperity. This balance between individual and collective is the foundation of a sustainable and just economy.

Personal autonomy is not undermined but rather strengthened by the security that cooperative membership provides. When basic needs are met through cooperative structures, individuals are freer to pursue creative, educational, and entrepreneurial endeavors without the constant anxiety of economic precarity.
