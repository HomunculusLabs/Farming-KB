# Dual Plant Vision System: Blue Light Phototropism and Red Light Photoperiodism

## Overview

Plants do not have a single visual system but rather employ at least two distinct light-sensing pathways that serve fundamentally different purposes. As described by Daniel Chamovitz in "What a Plant Knows" (2012), plants use **blue light** primarily to determine the direction of light (phototropism) and **red light** primarily to measure the length of the dark period (photoperiodism). This dual system represents a sophisticated division of labour that allows plants to simultaneously orient their growth toward light sources and regulate their developmental timing based on seasonal day-length changes.

## Blue Light: Directional Sensing and Phototropism

### Discovery

Julius von Sachs discovered in 1864 that blue light is the primary colour inducing phototropism in plants. Darwin and his son Francis later demonstrated (1880) that the site [[plant-photoreceptors-and-mechanism-of-light-perception]] for phototropism is at the shoot tip (coleoptile apex), not the bending region itself. Their five-condition experiment with canary grass seedlings proved that covering the tip with a lightproof cap prevented phototropic bending, while covering the middle section did not.

### Mechanism

Blue light is detected by photoreceptor proteins called **phototropins**. These photoreceptors are most sensitive to wavelengths around 450 nm (blue light). When blue light strikes the phototropins at the shoot tip, a signal cascade is initiated that causes the plant to redistribute the growth hormone auxin. Auxin accumulates on the shaded side of the stem, causing cells on that side to elongate more than cells on the illuminated side, producing the characteristic bending toward light.

Blue light phototropism operates on a short timescale—plants begin bending within hours of directional light exposure. It functions as a [[plant-gravitropism-spatial-orientation]] system, telling the plant which direction the strongest light is coming from so it can optimise its photosynthetic surface area.

## Red Light: Day-Length Measurement and Photoperiodism

### Discovery

The discovery that plants use red light to measure day length emerged from research on the [[garner-allard-maryland-mammoth-tobacco-experiment]] strain. Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard (1918) found that this strain flowered only when exposed to short days, establishing the concept of photoperiodism. Subsequent experiments around World War II showed that a brief flash of red light in the middle of the night could prevent short-day plants from flowering or trigger flowering in long-day plants.

### The Red/Far-Red Switch

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made the remarkable discovery that far-red light (wavelengths slightly longer than red, ~730 nm) could completely cancel the effect of red light on flowering. This led Warren L. Butler to identify a single photoreceptor responsible for both effects: **phytochrome**.

Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:
- **Pr** (P660): Absorbs red light (~660 nm) and converts to Pfr
- **Pfr** (P730): Absorbs far-red light (~730 nm) and converts to Pr

The Pfr form is biologically active and promotes flowering in long-day plants (or inhibits flowering in short-day plants). Red light converts Pr to Pfr (turning the switch "on"), while far-red light converts Pfr back to Pr (turning the switch "off"). In nature, the last light at dusk is far-red, signalling to the plant that night is beginning.

## The Critical Difference: Measuring Night Length

A key insight from photoperiodism research is that plants do not measure the length of the day—they measure the **length of the continuous dark period**. This was proven by night-interruption experiments:

- A few minutes of red light in the middle of a long night prevents short-day plants (chrysanthemum, soybean) from flowering
- The same brief red flash in the middle of a short night triggers flowering in long-day plants (iris, barley)
- Blue or green light flashes have no effect on photoperiodic responses

This means the plant is essentially "counting" how long Pfr (the active phytochrome form) has been absent. When Pfr levels remain low for long enough (a long night), short-day plants flower. When Pfr is replenished by a red flash before the critical dark period is reached, the plant perceives a short night.

## Different Sensory Organs for Different Functions

A surprising finding from photoperiodism research is that the sensory organ differs between the two systems:

- **Phototropism**: The "eye" is at the shoot tip (coleoptile apex). Removing or covering the tip eliminates phototropic bending.
- **Photoperiodism**: Any single leaf can perceive the red/far-red signal and regulate flowering for the entire plant. If all leaves are removed but the stem tip remains, the plant cannot perceive photoperiodic cues at all.

This means [[plant-phototropism-and-photoperiodism]] use completely different anatomical structures for light detection, even though both ultimately regulate plant [[cytokinin-plant-growth-and-development]]. In photoperiodism, phytochrome in the leaf initiates a mobile signal (now known to include the FLOWERING LOCUS T protein, FT) that travels to the shoot apex and triggers the floral transition.

## Agricultural Applications of the Dual System

Understanding the dual plant vision system has numerous practical applications:

### Greenhouse Lighting
Commercial greenhouse operators use the photoperiodism pathway to control flowering timing. Chrysanthemum growers keep lights on for a few minutes each night to prevent premature flowering, then stop the night interruptions two weeks before the desired harvest date to trigger uniform flowering across thousands of plants simultaneously.

### Shade Avoidance
Plants growing under canopy shade receive a lower red-to-far-red ratio (because chlorophyll in the canopy leaves absorbs red light but transmits far-red). Phytochrome detects this shift and triggers stem elongation, enabling the plant to grow through the canopy. This shade avoidance response is exploited in high-density planting to control plant architecture.

### Indoor Cultivation
Indoor growers use specific light spectra to manipulate plant responses. Blue-dominant lighting promotes compact, bushy growth (strong phototropic response). Red-dominant lighting during the dark period can influence flowering timing. Full-spectrum LEDs that include both blue and red wavelengths support both phototropic orientation and photoperiodic regulation.

## Evolutionary Significance

The dual system likely evolved because directional light sensing and seasonal timing impose different selective pressures. Phototropism requires rapid spatial resolution—knowing which direction light comes from—favouring short-wavelength (blue) light that scatters readily and provides directional cues. Photoperiodism requires reliable measurement of day-night transitions, which are most consistently marked by the dramatic shift in red-to-far-red ratio at sunset.
