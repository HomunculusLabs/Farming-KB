# Capoeira

## Overview
Capoeira is an Afro-Brazilian art that combines martial movement, music, dance, ritual, play, and cultural memory.
It developed among enslaved Africans and their descendants in Brazil, especially in urban and port settings.
A capoeira encounter is called a jogo, or game, rather than simply a fight.
The game takes place inside a circle of participants called a roda.
Players exchange kicks, evasions, sweeps, feints, acrobatics, and expressive gestures in dialogue with music.
The basic low, rocking movement is called the ginga.
Capoeira is not reducible to combat technique because timing, deception, rhythm, etiquette, and theatricality are equally central.
Its movements can look playful while carrying real tactical intent.
Its songs preserve names, places, warnings, jokes, histories, and coded lessons.
Capoeira is a living example of cultural survival under conditions of slavery, policing, racism, and social marginalization.
It has also become a global practice taught in academies, community centers, universities, and public spaces.
The art is significant because it resists neat categories: it is fight and dance, sport and ritual, folklore and strategy.
Capoeira's ambiguity is one of its powers.
It allowed practitioners to train, communicate, and resist in forms that could be disguised as play.
Today it functions as physical education, performance, heritage practice, self-defense training, music tradition, and social network.

## Key Aspects
The roda is the central social and ritual form of capoeira.
Participants form a circle while musicians set the rhythm and players enter the center.
The berimbau, a musical bow with a gourd resonator, usually leads the roda.
Other instruments may include pandeiro, atabaque, agogo, reco-reco, and hand clapping.
The lead berimbau determines rhythm, mood, speed, and sometimes the kind of game expected.
Common rhythms include Angola, Sao Bento Grande, Sao Bento Pequeno, Iuna, and Cavalaria.
Songs often begin with a ladainha, a solo narrative or invocation.
The chula or louvação may follow as a call-and-response praise section.
Corridos are shorter call-and-response songs that accompany active play.
The ginga keeps the body in constant motion and prevents a fixed target.
Basic attacks include meia-lua de frente, martelo, queixada, armada, benção, chapa, and meia-lua de compasso.
Defensive movements include esquiva, negativa, cocorinha, queda de quatro, and role.
Sweeps and takedowns include rasteira, vingativa, banda, and tesoura.
Acrobatic movements include au, macaco, queda de rins, and mortal, though their use depends on style and context.
Malicia is the strategic intelligence of deception, timing, misdirection, and reading intention.
Mandinga refers to charm, ritual power, unpredictability, and embodied cunning.
Chamada is a ritualized call or pause especially associated with Capoeira Angola.
Volta ao mundo is a walk around the roda that can reset energy, strategy, or etiquette.
Axé names the vital energy, spirit, or force generated by the roda.
Capoeira values dialogue more than domination, though games can become intense and dangerous.
A good player may show superiority by control, humor, timing, and restraint rather than by injury.
Etiquette includes entering properly, respecting the instruments, listening to the berimbau, and honoring elders.
Ranks, uniforms, and graduation ceremonies vary by school and lineage.
The mestre is a master teacher responsible for technical instruction and cultural transmission.
Capoeira Regional, Capoeira Angola, and contemporary capoeira are broad categories rather than rigid boxes.
Angola is often associated with lower, slower, more ritualized, and strategic play.
Regional is associated with Mestre Bimba's systematized teaching, athletic sequences, and public legitimization.
Contemporary capoeira often blends elements of both while adding global performance and sports influences.

## History and Context
Capoeira emerged from the African diaspora created by the Atlantic slave trade.
Enslaved Africans in Brazil came from many regions, including West Central Africa, West Africa, and Mozambique.
Their languages, cosmologies, combat games, dances, and musical forms contributed to new Afro-Brazilian practices.
Scholars debate exact origins because written sources are often hostile, fragmentary, or produced by police and elites.
The word capoeira may refer to brush, cleared vegetation, baskets, or urban contexts depending on etymological interpretation.
In the nineteenth century, capoeira was strongly associated with Rio de Janeiro street groups, dockworkers, freed people, and marginalized youth.
Practitioners known as capoeiras appeared in police records, newspapers, and political conflicts.
Some capoeiras were feared as fighters, bodyguards, gang members, or political agents.
After Brazilian abolition in 1888, capoeira remained stigmatized by racialized policing.
The Brazilian Penal Code of 1890 criminalized capoeira practices.
This criminalization pushed the art into coded, hidden, or negotiated forms.
In Salvador da Bahia, capoeira maintained strong links with Afro-Brazilian neighborhoods, music, and ritual culture.
Mestre Pastinha became the most famous twentieth-century defender of Capoeira Angola.
He emphasized tradition, ritual, philosophy, and the old style of play.
Mestre Bimba created Luta Regional Baiana, later known as Capoeira Regional.
Bimba systematized sequences, teaching methods, graduation practices, and public demonstrations.
He helped secure official recognition by presenting capoeira as a disciplined national art rather than a criminal disorder.
The Vargas-era search for Brazilian national identity created both opportunities and constraints for Afro-Brazilian cultural forms.
Capoeira was gradually rebranded as folklore, sport, heritage, and symbol of Brazil.
This legitimation did not erase racial inequality or the struggles of practitioners.
In the late twentieth century, capoeira expanded internationally through migration, tourism, workshops, performances, and cultural exchange.
UNESCO recognized the capoeira roda as intangible cultural heritage in 2014.
Globalization has created new communities while raising questions about commercialization, appropriation, lineage, and language.
The art continues to evolve while maintaining deep respect for ancestry and oral transmission.
Its history is not a straight path from oppression to acceptance, but an ongoing negotiation over power, memory, and belonging.

## Applications and Significance
Capoeira develops mobility, coordination, rhythm, balance, flexibility, and spatial awareness.
Its training builds both physical skill and social perception.
Because the game is improvised, players learn to read intention under uncertainty.
This makes capoeira a useful model for embodied cognition and interactive decision-making.
The art teaches tactical deception without reducing practice to violence.
It can support youth development by combining discipline, music, community, and identity.
Community capoeira groups often provide mentorship and cross-generational belonging.
In Brazil, capoeira remains tied to Afro-Brazilian pride and anti-racist cultural politics.
Outside Brazil, it can connect students to Portuguese language, Brazilian history, and diaspora studies.
Capoeira is used in dance, theater, film, and contemporary performance.
Its circular structure makes it useful for participatory education and group facilitation.
Music training teaches rhythm, call-and-response, memory, and collective listening.
Songs serve as informal archives of teachers, places, warnings, and values.
Capoeira also offers a lens for studying how oppressed communities transform surveillance into expressive strategy.
The roda models conflict without necessarily resolving it through domination.
Players may challenge, mock, invite, evade, or reconcile within shared rules.
This gives the art ethical depth as well as athletic complexity.
Capoeira has influenced mixed movement cultures, breakdance conversations, parkour aesthetics, and martial arts performance.
However, its value is not only in spectacular kicks or acrobatics.
The deepest skill lies in relation: to music, opponent, community, ancestry, and the moment.
As heritage, capoeira demonstrates how culture can encode resistance in pleasure.
As practice, it shows that play can be serious and that seriousness can be playful.
As scholarship, it links movement studies, African diaspora history, musicology, anthropology, and political memory.
