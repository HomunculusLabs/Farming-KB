# Magic Mushrooms in European Folklore

updated: 2026-04-24

The fly-agaric, Amanita muscaria, with its milk-white gills, its stem
and wart-covered scarlet cap, is the iconic, archetypal mushroom. It
appears in children's books, cartoons, and illustrated fairy tales as a
shorthand signifier of otherworldliness, enchantment and the uncanny. It
adorns Christmas tree baubles and kitsch china. Its genuinely bizarre
psychoactive properties have made it a potent catalyst for the human
imagination for at least three hundred years, and its story is
inseparably intertwined with that of psilocybin-containing mushrooms.

## Fairy Rings and Enchanted Circles

Mushrooms of all kinds have proved an enduring source of inspiration
for writers, poets, artists and musicians throughout Western history.
Whether it is their sudden appearance, their ephemerality, their
association with rot and decay, or just their sheer otherness, mushrooms
have always stirred and troubled our imaginations. They seem to us
living repositories of all that is weird, enchanted, otherworldly and
uncanny.

Cast up in "beguiling and enchanted-looking fairy rings," mushrooms
have long been associated with supernatural forces. Kipling's Puck of
Pook's Hill (1906) was accidentally summoned in a fairy ring.
Shakespeare placed "demy-puppets... whose pastime is to make midnight
mushrumps" into the magical universe of The Tempest. Jules Verne and H.
G. Wells imagined forests of giant mushrooms growing at the centre of
the earth and on the moon respectively, as if no other plant could
capture the strangeness of these imaginary places.

## The Fly-Agaric in Folklore

The fly-agaric has generated a spectacular array of myths and legends
about its supposed role in the origins of shamanism and religion. It is
widely believed that the shamans of Siberia undertook their spirit-
journeys buoyed up by the mushroom; that Siberian tribespeople drank
each other's urine -- and that of their reindeer -- to prolong the
mushroom's effects; that Santa Claus (with his red-and-white coat and
flying reindeer) is nothing less than a folk memory of fly-agaric
shamanism; that Viking berserkers entered their frenzied battle trance
with the mushroom; and that the mysterious Soma praised in the Rig Veda
was the fly-agaric.

The cultural history of the fly-agaric properly begins in the late
seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, when travellers' tales about
the so-called primitive peoples living to the east of the Russian empire
began to filter into Western society. These tales were shocking, for
they revealed that lying just over the edge of the civilised Western
world were people quite other in their religion.

## Siberian Shamanism and the Urine-Drinking Controversy

The most influential account came from the Swedish colonel Philip von
Strahlenberg, whose Description of the North and Eastern Parts of
Europe and Asia was translated into English in 1736. He described
members of the Koryak tribe in Kamchatka getting "drunk" upon the
mukhomor mushroom (the fly-agaric). Von Strahlenberg noted that when
the highly prized mushrooms were in short supply, the poor would loiter
outside dwellings where a mushroom feast was taking place, waiting for
guests to come out and relieve themselves. They would collect the urine
in wooden bowls and drink it, "having still some Virtue of the mushroom
in it."

It was this unusual act that ensured the fly-agaric myth made a splash
in the West. Its scatological character intoxicated the Western popular
and literary imagination. Oliver Goldsmith seized upon the urine-
drinkers in his Citizen of the World (1762), using the conceit to
satirise the British class system. Stephan Krasheninnikov's Description
of Kamchatka Land (1755) described dried fly-agaric consumed at feasts,
and the signs of intoxication: twitching of limbs, the urge to run and
jump and dance, followed by stupor and strange hallucinatory dreams.

## Shakespeare and the Mushroom Metaphor

In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, it was more usual to hurl
the word "mushroom" as an insult. The mushroom's impudent and
unexpected arrival, its equally rapid decay, its lack of a substantial
root and its inherent love of ordure made it the perfect derogatory
metaphor for lambasting the emerging mercantile middle class. John
Taylor wrote in 1639: "Consider this thou new made Mushroom man, / Thy
Life's a Blast, a Bubble, and a Span."
