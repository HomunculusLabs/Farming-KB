# Social Hierarchy and Pattern Structures

## Overview

Human social behaviour can be patterned to represent aspects of society. A set of patterns derived from studies made in Tasmania from 1969 to 1974 illustrates how occupational status hierarchies follow consistent geometric forms across diverse organisations. These patterns reveal the relationship between resources, sex ratios, and social mobility within human populations.

## The "Boat" Pattern of Social Stratification

### Structure

The central pattern represents the orders or castes of occupational level (status) along its long axis. Key structural features include:

- **Seldom more than seven major occupational levels**, even in rigorously-stratified hierarchies like the army
- **Width represents the numbers of people at each level**, summed from several organisations sampling approximately 35,000 people
- **Organisations studied**: local army, a multinational company, several churches, and many small businesses
- The overall form resembles a **"boat" shape** — broad in the middle, narrowing at top and bottom

### Genetic Streaming

Within the general boat pattern, arrows represent **genetic streaming** (by marriage or sexual congress) between occupational levels. The imbalance of sex ratios in these strata ensures a genetic turnover or diffusion between classes — a streaming of genetic materials between levels over generations.

## The Four Occupational Strata

### Level 1: Low Occupational (Resource) Area — Manual and Unskilled Urban

**Characteristics:**
- General dearth of material resources
- Low social status
- Part-time occupations predominate
- **Remarkable preponderance of male births and survived male children**: approximately 140 males per 100 females
- Large families
- Serial polyandry is common or acceptable

The excess male births at this level serve as a genetic pressure mechanism, pushing surplus males upward through the social hierarchy in search of mates, facilitating genetic mixing between strata.

### Level 2: The Central or Most Populated Level — The "Middle Class"

**Characteristics:**
- Adequate resources
- Nine-to-five employment patterns
- Some job tenure
- **"Normal" birth ratio**: approximately 104 males per 100 females
- Mixed white-collar and skilled technical workers
- Average family sizes
- Monogamy as an ideal, but often expressed as **serial monogamy**

This is the most populous stratum, forming the broad middle of the boat pattern. It represents social equilibrium where birth sex ratios approach natural baselines.

### Level 3: The Upper Levels

**Characteristics:**
- Few people relative to lower levels
- Extensive resources
- Flexible and often self-set working times
- **High proportion of female children**: approximately 100 females to 70 males or less
- Urban professionals or managers typify the group
- Small families
- **Effective polygyny via concubines or mistresses**

The reversal of the sex ratio at upper levels is striking — the scarcity of male children at this stratum, combined with the surplus of males at the lowest level, creates a systemic pressure for upward genetic mobility.

### Level 4: Very High Levels

**Characteristics:**
- Executive directors and landed nobility
- Variable family sizes
- Preponderance of female children (similar to Level 3)
- **Lateral intermarriage** for economic alliances
- Facilitated by exclusive schools and resorts

The narrowest tier of the boat pattern, this level maintains itself through strategic marriage alliances and the concentration of economic and social capital within closed networks.

## The Sex Ratio Mechanism

The most revealing aspect of this pattern is the systematic variation in birth sex ratios across occupational strata:

| Level | Resources | Male:Female Ratio | Family Pattern |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|----------------|
| Low | Scarce | ~140:100 | Large, serial polyandry |
| Middle | Adequate | ~104:100 | Average, serial monogamy |
| Upper | Extensive | ~70:100 | Small, effective polygyny |
| Highest | Vast | ~70:100 | Strategic intermarriage |

This creates a **genetic conveyor belt**: surplus males born at the lower levels migrate upward seeking mates, while surplus females born at upper levels may partner with upwardly-mobile males from lower strata. The result is continuous genetic diffusion across social boundaries, preventing complete reproductive isolation between classes.

## Pattern Implications for Design

### Land Division Patterns

A bird's-eye view of centralised and disempowered societies reveals a strictly **rectilinear network** of streets, farms, and property boundaries — as though the earth were patterned to suit survey instruments rather than to serve human or environmental needs. These straight-line patterns prevent most sensible landscape planning strategies and result in neither aesthetically nor functionally satisfactory landscapes.

### Traditional Bioregional Patterns

A far more sensible approach was developed by **Hawaiian villagers**, who took natural ridgelines as their boundaries. Because the area was contained in one water catchment, they achieved very stable and resource-rich landscapes reaching from dense cloud-forests to outer reefs. The conic and radial volcanic landscape with radial water lines suits such land division perfectly.

Similarly, **Zulus** and **American Indians** adopt circular or zonal modes in their plains settlements. Such models can be studied and adopted by future bioregional societies as sane and caring people become the majority in their regions and set about landscape rehabilitation.

### Sensible Land Division as Social Foundation

Sensible land division is identified as a long-delayed but essential precursor to a stable society. The connection between geometric pattern in land use and social pattern in human hierarchy suggests that the physical patterns we impose on the landscape shape — and are shaped by — the social patterns of the communities that inhabit them.
