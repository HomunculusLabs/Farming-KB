# Phytohormone Activity on Fungi: The Brassinosteroid Literature Gap

## Overview

Prior to the 1990 Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt study on *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]*, the scientific literature on brassinosteroids was entirely focused on higher plants. Despite the identification of approximately 30 brassinosteroid members across Angiospermae, Gymnospermae, and green algae, no published research had investigated whether these steroidal plant-growth regulators possessed biological activity in the fungal kingdom. This represented a significant blind spot in phytohormone research, as the structural and biochemical parallels between plants and fungi suggested potential cross-kingdom activity.

## The Phytohormone Classification

Brassinosteroids were recognized as a distinct class of steroidal plant-growth regulators following their initial discovery in *Brassica* pollen. By 1990, approximately 30 distinct brassinosteroid compounds had been identified and characterized across a wide taxonomic range of the plant kingdom, including:

- **Angiospermae** (flowering plants) — the largest represented group, with brassinosteroids detected in numerous crop species and model organisms across monocots and dicots
- **Gymnospermae** (cone-bearing plants) — conifers and allied groups showing brassinosteroid presence, suggesting broader evolutionary conservation within vascular plants
- **Green algae** — the most phylogenetically distant group in which brassinosteroids had been identified, indicating ancient origins of brassinosteroid biology predating the colonization of land

Their classification as a genuine phytohormone group was supported by their ubiquity across plant lineages and their potent physiological effects at extremely low concentrations (parts-per-million range). This broad phylogenetic distribution raised the question of whether brassinosteroid signaling might predate the divergence of plants and fungi, or whether convergent evolution had produced similar steroid-mediated regulatory systems in both kingdoms independently.

## Known Physiological Mechanisms in Plants

The established growth-promoting activity of brassinosteroids in plants involved complex, multifaceted physiological effects that went beyond simple stimulation of biomass accumulation:

- **Cell elongation promotion** — brassinosteroids directly stimulated longitudinal cell expansion, particularly in shoots and internodes, through effects on cell wall loosening, extensibility, and acid growth mechanisms
- **Cell division enhancement** — increased rates of mitotic division in meristematic tissues, accelerating overall growth rates in developing organs
- **Membrane integrity effects** — brassinosteroids influenced plasma membrane properties, including proton pump activation (H⁺-ATPase stimulation), ion transport regulation, and membrane fluidity
- **Gene expression regulation** — brassinosteroid-responsive genes were identified in multiple plant species, encoding proteins involved in cell expansion, photosynthesis, stress responses, and secondary metabolism
- **Interaction with other hormones** — brassinosteroids exhibited significant crosstalk with auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins, suggesting an integrative role in the plant hormonal signaling network rather than functioning in isolation
- **Vascular differentiation** — brassinosteroids promoted xylem differentiation and vascular bundle development, contributing to overall plant architecture and transport capacity

These mechanisms were understood to be mediated through specific brassinosteroid receptor proteins (later identified as BRI1 kinases) in plant cell membranes. The existence of these dedicated receptors raised the question of whether fungi possessed analogous binding proteins, or whether brassinosteroid effects in fungal systems would proceed through entirely different molecular mechanisms.

## Why Fungi Were Overlooked

Several factors contributed to the historical neglect of fungi in brassinosteroid research, creating a gap that persisted for over a decade after brassinosteroids were first described:

1. **Taxonomic distance** — fungi belong to an entirely separate kingdom from plants, and most phytohormone research was conducted within the context of plant physiology and agronomy, with little cross-disciplinary interaction with mycologists
2. **Assumption of kingdom specificity** — the receptor-mediated mechanism in plants suggested that brassinosteroids might be plant-specific signaling molecules with no relevance to fungal biology, as fungi lack obvious BRI1 homologs
3. **Practical focus** — brassinosteroid research was primarily driven by agricultural applications (crop yield improvement, stress resistance), not mycological inquiry, creating a narrow research agenda
4. **Sterol biosynthesis differences** — while both plants and fungi produce sterols as essential membrane components, their biosynthetic pathways diverge significantly; fungi primarily produce ergosterol while plants produce sitosterol, stigmasterol, and campesterol, suggesting fundamental differences in steroid chemistry
5. **Lack of obvious endogenous brassinosteroids in fungi** — no brassinosteroid compounds had been isolated from fungal tissues, suggesting fungi neither produced nor utilized these molecules in their native biology
6. **Funding priorities** — research funding for brassinosteroid work was overwhelmingly directed toward crop science applications with clear economic returns, leaving mycological investigations unfunded and unpublished

## Agricultural Context Motivating Cross-Kingdom Inquiry

The practical motivation for investigating brassinosteroids in fungi came from the agricultural sector. By 1990, promising field results had been reported for brassinosteroid application to crops, generating commercial interest in extending these applications:

- Increased yields in various crop species across multiple plant families, with reported gains of 5–30% depending on species and growing conditions
- Anti-stress effects under suboptimal growing conditions, including [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], temperature extremes, and salinity stress resilience
- Enhanced resistance to pathogens and environmental stressors that normally reduced crop productivity
- Improved seed germination rates and seedling vigor when brassinosteroids were applied as seed treatments or foliar sprays

The possibility that brassinosteroids might similarly enhance fungal cultivation — particularly for commercially or scientifically important fungal species used in food production, biotechnology, or pharmaceutical manufacturing — provided practical justification for expanding the research scope beyond the plant kingdom. If a compound that improved plant growth could also accelerate fungal biomass production, the implications for commercial [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] and industrial mycology could be substantial.

## The Pioneering Fungal Study

The Gartz et al. (1990) study represented the first systematic investigation of brassinosteroid effects on fungal growth. The choice of *Psilocybe cubensis* as the test organism was significant for several reasons:

- It was a well-characterized subtropical basidiomycete with established cultivation protocols, allowing reproducible experimental conditions across multiple trials
- The Gartz laboratory had previously developed stable strains with reliable fruiting behavior, eliminating strain variability as a confounding factor
- Both agar-based and dung/grain substrate cultivation systems were available for comparative testing, providing cross-validation of results across different nutritional environments
- The species produced a clear, quantifiable fruiting response that could be measured in terms of timing, yield (dry mass), and morphological characteristics
- The subtropical origin of the species meant it grew robustly at room temperature, simplifying experimental logistics

The study design included ten replicate runs per condition, providing reasonable statistical power for detecting growth differences between treated and control groups across both substrate systems.

## Broader Context of Cross-Kingdom Hormone Research

The question of whether plant hormones affect fungi was part of a larger scientific discourse about cross-kingdom biochemical signaling that had been explored intermittently for decades:

- **Auxins** had been reported to influence fungal morphology and growth patterns in some species, though results were inconsistent across studies
- **Ethylene**, a gaseous plant hormone, was known to affect fungal sporulation and enzyme production in certain phytopathogenic fungi
- **Cytokinins** had demonstrated effects on fungal cell division rates in certain contexts, though physiological relevance was debated
- **Abscisic acid** showed some effects on fungal stress responses, potentially indicating shared stress-signaling pathways

However, these reports were scattered and often inconsistent, making it unclear whether they represented genuine hormonal signaling interactions or nonspecific pharmacological effects at supra-physiological concentrations. The brassinosteroid study was notable for using concentrations (10⁻² ppm) that were well within the physiological range for plant hormone activity, lending credibility to the biological relevance of the observed effects.

## Implications for Myco-Phytohormone Research

The discovery that brassinosteroids promoted fungal growth opened several important research avenues that extended well beyond the immediate practical applications to mushroom cultivation:

- **Evolutionary questions** — if brassinosteroids affect both plants and fungi, what does this imply about the evolutionary history of steroid signaling? Did an ancestral steroid signaling system exist before the plant-fungal divergence approximately one billion years ago?
- **Receptor conservation** — do fungi possess brassinosteroid-binding proteins homologous to plant receptors, or is the mechanism entirely different? Structural similarities between ergosterol and plant sterols suggest the possibility of shared binding sites
- **Endogenous production** — do fungi synthesize their own brassinosteroid-like compounds, or do they only respond to exogenous sources? The detection of brassinosteroids in green algae suggested the biosynthetic capacity might exist outside the plant kingdom
- **Commercial applications** — could brassinosteroid supplementation improve cultivation yields for edible mushroom species of commercial importance, such as *[[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]*, *Pleurotus ostreatus*, or *Lentinula edodes*?
- **Stress tolerance** — if the anti-stress effects observed in plants translated to fungi, brassinosteroids might help mushroom cultures resist contamination and environmental fluctuations during commercial cultivation operations

## Steroid Signaling: Convergence vs. Homology

The question of how brassinosteroids affect fungal cells touches on a fundamental question in evolutionary biochemistry: does shared sensitivity to a molecular signal reflect common evolutionary ancestry (homology) or independent evolution of similar responses (convergence)? Several lines of evidence bear on this question:

- **Sterol receptor conservation** — both plants and fungi possess nuclear receptor superfamily proteins that bind steroidal molecules, though the specific ligand specificities differ
- **Membrane sterol similarities** — despite producing different dominant sterols (ergosterol vs. sitosterol), both kingdoms share the fundamental isoprenoid [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway-and-enzymology]] up to the point of divergence
- **Functional analogy** — sterols serve structural roles in both plant and fungal membranes, and brassinosteroids could potentially modulate membrane properties in both kingdoms through direct physicochemical effects rather than receptor-mediated signaling

The distinction between direct physicochemical membrane effects and specific receptor-mediated signaling is crucial for understanding whether brassinosteroid effects on fungi represent genuine hormonal signaling or merely pharmacological disruption of membrane dynamics at low concentrations. Resolving this question would require isolation of brassinosteroid-binding proteins from fungal cells and characterization of downstream signaling cascades.

Furthermore, the possibility of indirect effects must be considered: brassinosteroids might alter substrate chemistry or [[fukuoka-microbial-ecology-decomposition]] in ways that secondarily benefit fungal growth, rather than acting directly on fungal cells.

## Limitations of the Initial Study

While groundbreaking, the Gartz et al. study had several limitations that warranted further investigation:

- **Single species tested** — results from *P. cubensis* could not be generalized to other fungi without additional testing across diverse taxonomic groups
- **Single brassinosteroid compound** — only 22S,23S-homobrassinolide was tested, leaving open the question of whether other brassinosteroids showed similar or different potencies
- **No dose-response curve** — only one concentration (10⁻² ppm) was used, making it impossible to determine optimal dosing or establish EC₅₀ values
- **No mechanism investigation** — the study demonstrated effects but did not investigate the underlying molecular mechanism of brassinosteroid action in fungal cells
- **Morphological changes unexplained** — the loss of the membranous annulus in treated fruit bodies was an unexpected finding with no proposed mechanistic explanation

These limitations provided a clear roadmap for subsequent research, though the apparent scarcity of follow-up studies in the published literature suggests that the mycological community did not broadly pursue these questions.
