# Mycoremediation of Pharmaceutical Contaminants

## Overview

Pharmaceutical compounds are increasingly recognized as emerging environmental contaminants. Drugs from human and veterinary medicine enter the environment through wastewater effluent, agricultural runoff, improper disposal, and manufacturing waste. While conventional [[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]] makes them suitable for attacking diverse steroid structures

### Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)

Compounds such as diclofenac, ibuprofen, naproxen, and ketoprofen contain aromatic and heterocyclic structures amenable to fungal enzymatic attack.

### Pesticides with Pharmaceutical-Related Structures

Several classes of pesticides share structural features with pharmaceuticals and have been extensively studied for fungal degradation:

#### Phenylamide Herbicides (Acetanilides, Phenylureas, Phenylcarbamates)

- **Alachlor**: Degrading white rot fungi Ceriporiopsis subvermispora, Phlebia tremellosa, P. chrysosporium showed ~10% degradation, suggesting co-metabolic transformation (Ferry et al., 1994)
- **Metolachlor**: Co-metabolized by bacteria and fungi, including white rot fungi, with dechlorination to varying extent (Liu, Freyer & Bollag, 1991)
- **Linuron, isoproturon, diuron**: [[botrytis-cinerea]], Cladosporium cucumerinum, and Fusarium culmorum (Deas & Clifford, 1982; Deas, Clark & Carter, 1984)
- **Anilazine**: P. chrysosporium aided biodegradation and remobilization in humic soil fractions (Liebich, Buranel & Fuhr, 1999)

## Mechanisms of Pharmaceutical Transformation

### Oxidative Degradation

The primary mechanism involves ligninolytic enzymes:

1. **Laccase**: Direct oxidation of phenolic pharmaceuticals; with mediators, extends to non-phenolic compounds
2. **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)**: One-electron oxidation generating cation radicals; unique in oxidizing non-phenolic aromatics
3. **[[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] (MnP)**: Via Mn³⁺ and lipid peroxidation pathways

### Detoxification Through Polymerization

Fungal laccases can polymerize toxic intermediates of pharmaceutical/pesticide decomposition into less mobile, less toxic oligomers. T. versicolor laccase polymerized 4-chloroaniline (a metabolite of multiple phenylamide herbicides) to dimers through pentamers (Hoff, Liu & Bollag, 1985). This oxidative coupling may serve as a detoxification mechanism.

### Co-metabolic Transformation

Most pharmaceuticals are not used as sole carbon/energy sources by fungi but are co-metabolized. The ligninolytic system is produced for other purposes (secondary metabolism) and incidentally transforms pharmaceutical compounds. This is both an advantage (broad substrate range) and a limitation (requires appropriate induction conditions).

### Mycorrhizal Degradation

Mycorrhizal fungi can degrade pharmaceuticals and pesticides through incorporation of compound carbon into fungal tissue rather than decomposition (Donnelly, Entry & Crawford, 1993). Ectomycorrhizal fungi compete better with indigenous populations than introduced saprophytic fungi and have potential for in situ treatment.

## Challenges

- **Low environmental concentrations**: Pharmaceuticals occur at trace levels (ng/L-μg/L) that may not induce fungal enzyme production
- **Complex matrices**: Wastewater contains many competing substrates and potential inhibitors
