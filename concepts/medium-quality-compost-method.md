# Medium Quality Compost Method

The once-a-year heap is the most practical [[comparison-bokashi-vs-hot-composting-method]] for home vegetable gardeners. By accumulating organic materials throughout the year and building one large, well-managed compost heap in early autumn, you can produce medium-quality compost that will significantly improve soil health and grow most vegetables — especially when supplemented with [[complete-organic-fertilizer]] (COF) for high-demand crops.

## The Annual Cycle

### Accumulation Phase (Autumn to Autumn)

Starting in late autumn, steadily accumulate all new vegetative wastes and kitchen garbage into one great stack of plant material. Encourage it to dry out over the following eleven months. This is critical because dry material stores without rotting and is much easier to manage when building the heap.

### What to Accumulate

- Pea vines and spent vegetable plants
- Kitchen scraps and garbage
- Trimmings (outer leaves of lettuce, carrot tops, etc.)
- Old cabbage and cauliflower plants from seed-saving
- Autumn leaves (if you have deciduous trees)
- Grass clippings (spread thinly to dry into "hay" first)

### What to Exclude

- **Nothing woody** — no tree trimmings, no hedge trimmings, nothing with even thin bark
- **No sawdust** — C/N is way too high, will degrade [[aact-compost-quality-standards-ingham]]
- **No thick, woody stems** — they take years to decompose

Woody materials should be composted separately outside the vegetable garden. The resulting product is suitable as mulch under ornamentals or fruit trees, but never in the vegetable patch.

## Building the Heap

### Materials Needed

| Material | Quantity | Purpose |
|----------|----------|---------|
| Dry vegetation | Year's accumulation (see below) | Carbon source |
| Water | ~100 gallons (400 liters) for a 2,000 sq ft garden | Moisture |
| Garden soil | ~5% by volume of heap | Nitrate capture |
| Strong nitrogen source | See below | Lower C/N to under 25:1 |

### The Layering Process

1. **Base**: Spread dry vegetation in a rectangle at least 5-7 feet (150-210 cm) across, 5+ feet long, about 8 inches (20 cm) thick
2. **Soil layer**: Cover with a half-inch (1.25 cm) layer of garden soil
3. **Strong stuff**: Cover with about 2 gallons (8 liters) of seedmeal, OR 2 gallons of [[chicken-manure]], OR a 2.5 cm layer of horse/cow manure
4. **Repeat**: Another 8-inch layer of dry vegetation, water thoroughly
5. **Continue** until all material is layered into a heap about 5 feet (150 cm) high (max 6 feet / 180 cm)
6. **Cap**: Spread a thin layer of soil over the entire outside of the heap

### Sizing Guidelines

- **Minimum width**: 5 feet (150 cm) — narrower heaps don't heat properly
- **Ideal height**: 5 feet (150 cm) — taller is fine up to 6 feet
- **Length**: As long as needed — no maximum
- **Don't make it too wide or tall**: The core needs oxygen; exceeding these dimensions makes the center airless

## The Three Key Amendments

### Water

Thoroughly moisten the entire mass. Every particle should be damp — like a wrung-out sponge. If the heap's core is dry, decomposition stalls.

### Soil (The Nitrate Capturer)

Soil in a compost heap fills the same function as moderator rods in a nuclear reactor — it slows the process slightly and captures nitrates. When the heap starts working, proteins in the decomposing vegetation are broken down into ammonia gas. Without soil, ammonia escapes into the atmosphere (enormous nutrient loss). Soil contains bacteria that rapidly convert ammonia into nitrates, which are not gases.

**Use enough soil** to make up a generous 5% by volume. Too much soil is far better than too little. Use fertile garden soil, preferably from where last year's compost heap was (it's already inoculated with appropriate microorganisms).

### Strong Nitrogen Source

The finest thing to use is truly fresh ruminant manure without bedding. Horse and cow manure is usually not high enough in nitrogen, so a large quantity is needed — which means significantly less finished compost. If fresh, it also contains quantities of still-active digestive enzymes that help speed decomposition.

| Material | Amount | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| Fresh horse/cow manure (no bedding) | ~1/3 by volume of heap | Best if truly fresh |
| Manure with bedding (equal quantities) | Need additional strong material | Bedding raises average C/N |
| Dried poultry manure | 5-10% by volume | Collect and store in feed sacks |
| Seedmeal (cottonseed, soy, etc.) | ~50 lb (25 kg) sack for year's vegetation | About 2x potency of chicken manure |

## Temperature Management

### Optimal Range

- **Target**: 125-135°F (52-57°C) — this produces the most potent compost
- **Too hot** (>155°F / 68°C): Nutrient loss through ammonia volatilization
- **Too cool** (<100°F / 38°C): Decomposition is too slow

### The 10°F Rule

For every 10°F (5°C) increase in temperature, the speed of decomposition approximately doubles. This is geometric, not linear: 2x, 4x, 8x, 16x, 32x, 64x, 128x, 256x.

### Organic Enzyme Limits

Most organic enzymes are destroyed at around 120°F (50°C). A few, like those in the thermophilic compost heap, can handle temperatures as high as 155°F (68°C). Above that, enzymatic reactions slow dramatically and then stop.

## Turning the Heap

### The First Turn (at ~5 weeks)

1. Move the entire pile to the adjacent bare spot
2. **Outsides go to the core**: Material on the outside has decomposed the least — it goes to the center of the new heap
3. **Core goes to the outside**: The core has decomposed the most — it goes to the outside
4. **Remoisten** as you go
5. **Reshape** to about 5 feet high

The second heating will not be as hot nor last as long as the first. The heap will be smaller.

### The Second Turn (1 month later)

Same process. Each turn is easier because the material has broken down more — use a shovel more than a fork.

### The Third Turn (optional)

Depends on how much strong stuff and soil you used. In mild-winter climates, two turns are usually sufficient. In cold-winter climates, the heap may freeze between turns and need a third.

## Readiness

A compost heap that did NOT contain bark, twigs, or sawdust is done enough to use when:
- Dark brown in color
- Crumbly texture
- Pleasant earthy odor
- Contains nothing recognizable from the original materials
- May contain worms

A heap that DID contain woody materials might be cool after three turns but will still have a high C/N and might not be ready for another entire year.

## How Much Compost Do You Need?

### Application Rates

| Use | Amount | Frequency |
|------|--------|-----------|
| Soil amendment | ~1/4 inch deep | Annually |
| New garden bed establishment | 1-2 inches deep mixed into top 6 inches | Once |
| Top-dressing | 1/4 inch | Annually |

### The Closed-System Fallacy

A home garden cannot operate as a closed system that generates enough [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] to maintain itself — not unless you compost every opportunity and every bit of organic material generated. Even recycling everything from the garden (except humanure) back into it, plus importing lawn clippings and autumn leaves, may not be enough. For most gardeners, importing and [[comparison-vermicomposting-vs-composting-organic-garden]] matter at least twice the volume of the garden's own output is necessary.
