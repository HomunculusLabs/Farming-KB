## Siberian Shamanism and the Koryak Tradition

The ceremonial use of the fly agaric among Siberian peoples represents the
most thoroughly documented ethnographic tradition. Schultes and Hofmann trace
reports back to 1730, when a Swedish military officer imprisoned in Siberia for
twelve years recorded that primitive tribesmen employed the fly agaric as a
shamanistic inebriant. The custom persisted among scattered groups of
Finno-Ugrian peoples across Siberia.

A Koryak legend explains the origin of wapaq spirits (the fly agaric): the
culture hero Big Raven caught a whale but could not return it to the sea. The
god Vahiyinin (Existence) spat upon the earth, creating little white plants
with red hats and white flecks from the congealed spittle. After eating wapaq,
Big Raven gained extraordinary strength and commanded his people to learn
what wapaq could teach them. This origin myth underscores the sacred status of
the mushroom as a gift directly from the divine.

Siberian mushroom users had no other intoxicants until Russians introduced
alcohol. They dried mushrooms in the sun and ingested them alone or as an
extract in water, reindeer milk, or the juice of sweet plants. Women sometimes
rolled the mushroom into moistened pellets for men to swallow.

## Urine Recycling and the Soma Connection

Perhaps the most distinctive feature of fly agaric ethnobotany is the
practice of urine drinking, arising from the unique pharmacological property
that the active principles pass through the body unmetabolized or as still
active metabolites -- unusual among hallucinogenic plant compounds. An early
Koryak account describes poorer tribesmen waiting outside huts to collect and
drink the urine of the wealthy who could afford fresh mushrooms.
