# Stinkhorn Fungi (Phallus impudicus)

Phallus impudicus, commonly known as the common stinkhorn, is a conspicuous member
of the order Phallales within the phylum [[basidiomycota-and-basidiospore-biology]].
It is among the most recognizable and culturally significant fungi in the European
tradition, noted both for its striking morphology and its pungent odor. Stinkhorns
belong to a group of approximately 70 described species of phallic mushrooms that
have fascinated and repelled observers from antiquity to the present day. Nicholas
Money's *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard* devotes considerable attention to these organisms,
using them as a gateway to the broader wonders of [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]].

## Development: The Egg-to-Fruiting-Body Transformation

The life cycle of Phallus impudicus begins underground in an egg-like structure
called a "volva" or "peridium." This whitish, gelatinous egg measures roughly
3 to 5 centimeters in diameter and contains all the preformed tissues of the
adult [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] compressed within it. The development is analogous to the
egg-based development seen in [[cage-fungi-and-polyhedral-structures]]. Within
the egg, the receptacle (the stalk), the cap (the pileus), and the spore-bearing
gleba are already differentiated, folded and compressed, awaiting the moment
of expansion.

When conditions of temperature and moisture are favorable, the egg ruptures and
the receptacle expands rapidly through the absorption of water. The entire
process of expansion from egg to mature fruiting body can occur in a matter of
hours, making it one of the fastest-growing structures in the fungal kingdom.
The mature stinkhorn stands 10 to 25 centimeters tall, bearing a conical,
bell-shaped cap at its apex covered with a dark, olive-green, slimy gleba.
The receptacle itself is hollow and sponge-like, constructed from a network of
interwoven [[fungal-hyphae-and-mycelium]] that give it structural integrity
while remaining lightweight.

## Anatomy: Receptacle and Gleba

The receptacle of Phallus impudicus is a remarkable feat of fungal engineering.
It is a hollow column of reticulate tissue that expands through osmotic pressure
generated by the rapid uptake of water into the hyphal cells. The gleba, which
coats the cap, is the spore-bearing tissue. It is a viscous, malodorous paste
containing millions of basidiospores suspended in a gelatinous matrix. This
slime is deliberately attractive to insects; its consistency ensures that spores
adhere to the bodies of visiting flies and are carried to new locations.

The base of the mature fruiting body often retains remnants of the volva, a
cup-like structure at the ground line that is a remnant of the original egg.
This volva is a useful diagnostic feature for field identification, as it
persists after the receptacle has expanded fully.

## Chemical Attractants

The characteristic foul odor of the stinkhorn is produced by a cocktail of
[[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds that mimic the scent of decaying flesh or feces.
The principal chemical constituents include hydrogen sulfide (the compound
responsible for the smell of rotten eggs), formaldehyde, methylmercaptan, and
various short-chain fatty acids sometimes collectively referred to as phallic
acids. These compounds are potent attractants for carrion-feeding and
dung-feeding insects, particularly blow flies (Calliphoridae) and flesh flies
(Sarcophagidae). The chemical blend is exquisitely tuned to exploit the
olfactory sensitivities of these insects, ensuring reliable visitation and
effective [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] across considerable distances.

## Dispersal Vectors: Flies and Slugs

Stinkhorn spores are dispersed primarily by two groups of animals. Dipteran
flies are the most important vectors; attracted by the odor, they land on the
gleba and feed on the slime, becoming coated with spores in the process. When
they subsequently visit other substrates, spores are deposited. Slugs also
play a significant role, consuming the gleba directly and passing viable spores
through their digestive tracts. The dual-vector strategy ensures broad
dispersal across a range of microhabitats, from forest floors to garden beds.

## The Mechanism of Erection
