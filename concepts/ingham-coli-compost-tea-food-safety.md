# Coliforms, E. coli, and Compost Tea Food Safety

## Types of Coliform Bacteria

There are total coliform bacteria, fecal coliform bacteria, fecal
E. coli, and then the truly dangerous organism, E. coli O157:H7.
These categories represent increasingly specific levels of concern.
When you think about these bacteria, you need to be aware that the
surface of a vegetable is very different from milk or meat. Milk and
meat are sterile (or should be) when in the animal, while vegetables
naturally grow with all kinds of [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]] on their surfaces.

## Coliforms Are Normal in Soil

Coliforms can be present in soil, in water, in compost, and are
normal in these places. Coliforms are just a typical set of
bacteria, and most are not pathogenic. If you have these on the
surface of your vegetables, it is generally acceptable. Humans need
to consume some of these organisms for a healthy digestive system.
Total coliform counts include the E. coli found in human digestive
systems, which you need for proper digestion.

## Why Food Surfaces Are Different

Milk is high in sugars (lactose) and protein, so organisms grow very
fast, use up oxygen quickly, and cause spoilage. But on vegetable
surfaces, there is not enough sugar or protein to allow rapid
microbial growth. A few of these organisms at a time is perfectly
acceptable. The situation with plant surfaces is fundamentally
different from sterile food products.

## Fecal E. coli Indicates Possible Problems

Fecal E. coli are the specific coliforms that indicate there is a
possibility that human pathogens (microbes that cause typhus,
cholera, food poisoning) could be present. You need to find the
source of contamination and end it. If the contamination was from
someone sick with E. coli O157, or from an animal carrying this
bacterium, then this human pathogen could be present. You must track
that source down immediately.

## E. coli in Compost

If [[aact-compost-quality-standards-ingham]] is properly composted, it will have no E. coli. By making
compost correctly, getting it to temperature and keeping it [[ingham-anaerobic-vs-aerobic-compost-tea]],
there will be no E. coli surviving. If you do not have E. coli in
the compost, it will not be in the tea, no matter how much molasses
you add. In all tea makers tested, no E. coli in the compost means
no E. coli in the tea, even up to 5% molasses concentration.

## E. coli Disappearance Rates

With a good food web in the manure, E. coli disappears within three
months. With not-so-good a food web, E. coli took longer to
disappear. If the pile is anaerobic, E. coli persists. If you
contaminate a pile of good compost that has an active, complete food
web with high numbers of aerobic organisms, even a billion E. coli
added to the pile will be gone within 24 hours. If the compost is
anaerobic and the food web is not present, E. coli will remain
detectable for months.

## Molasses Concentration and E. coli

Data shows that at 0.1%, 0.5%, and 5% [[ingham-molasses-types-brewing-effects]] concentrations, no
E. coli was found. Some was found at 1% concentration. The 0.5% to
3% range is the worst concentration range because [[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea]]
will drop oxygen rapidly, creating conditions where E. coli could
potentially grow. At concentrations above 5%, [[ingham-antibiotics-osmotic-shock-compost-tea]]
suppress bacterial growth including potential pathogens.

## Testing Protocols

Testing should be done to establish a history of no problems, and
then samples taken periodically depending on production volume. If
fecal E. coli are found, testing should become more frequent until
you can be assured the source of contamination was removed. You
need to be aware of what kind of coliform people are talking about.
If someone says coliform, ask if it is a coliform of concern.

## Pragmatic Risk Perspective

Pathogens in [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] should not be disregarded, but the risk should be
kept in perspective. You are perhaps hundreds of thousands of times
more likely to die in a car accident on the way to the store than
from pathogens on your vegetables. The pathogen problem on produce
is as controllable as we all feel driving is controllable. If we
just keep our eyes open, and watch for problems, we can manage the
risk. Children playing in the vegetable garden need to be taught to
wash vegetables before eating them. A bird soiling the garden last
night is much more likely to be a problem than the compost.

## Recovery from Pathogen Contamination

If bad tea is produced, put it on an area that will not be harmed by
potentially present toxins, such as an area of weeds or bare soil.
Recovery typically requires 120 days if the soil is quite poor. If
the food web is in good shape, recovery (pathogens no longer
detectable) can occur within three days. The key is maintaining a
complete, active [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] that can outcompete and consume any
pathogens that might be introduced. See also [[aact-pathogen-safety-ingham]].
