## Sweet Winter Carrots

Sweet winter carrots represent one of the most acclaimed crops in the unheated winter greenhouse system. Unlike conventional carrots that lose sweetness after harvest, winter carrots stored in-ground under cold protection develop progressively sweeter, more complex flavors as the season advances. This section covers the complete system for producing, storing, and marketing premium winter carrots.

## The Winter Carrot Concept

The "Sweet Winter Carrot" is not a specific variety but a production method. Carrots left in the soil under greenhouse protection through the coldest months undergo a natural concentration of sugars. The cold temperatures convert starches to sugars as a frost-protection mechanism in the root, resulting in a product so sweet and crunchy that children call them "candy carrots." The last carrots harvested in late February are noticeably sweeter than the first ones dug in October.

This five-month season—from October through February—produces a product that cannot be replicated by any storage method. Once top growth resumes in March, the carrots begin drawing down their sugar reserves and lose their characteristic sweetness.

## Recommended Variety

**"Napoli'** is the most successful variety identified for winter in-ground storage. It produces a small but intensely full-flavored root that holds quality through months of cold soil conditions. The small size is actually an advantage—it allows dense planting and quick digging even in frozen or near-frozen soil.

For spring carrots (a separate crop sown in winter for May harvest), **'Nelson'** is used. While deliciously sweet as a spring carrot, it cannot match the acclaim of the winter 'Napoli' crop.

## Planting Schedule

- **October harvest**: Sow during the last week of July
- **Later harvests** (December–February): Sow during the first week of August
- **Spring crop** (May harvest): Sow in late December following fall lettuce

The planting window is narrow. Sow too early and the carrots will be oversized by fall; sow too late and the roots won't reach adequate size before cold halts growth.

## Soil Preparation

Before sowing carrots, turn under a soil-improving [[green-manure]] of **oats and peas** approximately one month prior to the sowing date. This:

- Adds organic matter and nitrogen from the pea component
- Improves soil structure with oat root mass
- Provides a clean seedbed after incorporation and decomposition

The green manure crop is cut and turned under while still green, allowing about four weeks for partial decomposition before carrot sowing.

## Planting Density

Plant in standard 30-inch-wide beds. With careful spacing, large areas can be covered efficiently. The beds are covered with [[mobile-greenhouses]] in late October, before hard freezes begin.

## Harvest Method

Carrots are **dug fresh from the soil** throughout the season:

- **October–November**: Dug from outdoor beds before greenhouse covering
- **December–February**: Dug from greenhouse-covered beds

The carrots are never bulk-harvested and stored. Each digging yields freshly harvested roots. The green tops remain alive under the inner layer of protection, so carrots are always sold with **1.5 inches of green top** attached. This serves two purposes:

1. Creates an attractive, premium appearance
2. Identifies the product as freshly harvested rather than from storage

This presentation allows growers to charge a premium price and distinguish their product from conventional stored carrots.

## Extending the Season — What Doesn't Work

Several attempts to extend the harvest window have failed:

- **Insulation layer over beds** (applied mid-January): Prevented the daily influx of solar warmth, causing carrots and soil to freeze solid for weeks. Carrots were of poor quality when thawed.
- **Spring-regrowth varieties** (planted late September/early October): Varieties selected for ability to resume root growth in spring produced disappointing flavor compared to properly wintered crops.

The conclusion is that the sweetness is a product of dormancy in cold soil—any technique that promotes continued growth or disturbs the cold-soil storage degrades quality.

## Marketing

Sweet winter carrots develop an almost legendary popularity in local markets. Demand consistently exceeds supply. The seasonality is part of the appeal—this crop is at its best for only five months, creating urgency among customers.

Key marketing points:
- "Candy carrots" appeal to children and families
- Freshly dug with green tops signals premium quality
- Local greenhouse-grown during winter commands attention
- Limited season creates scarcity value

## Economics

The return per square foot is high because:
- Carrots occupy the beds for only the late summer through winter period
- No post-harvest storage infrastructure needed
- Premium pricing for fresh-dug, green-topped product
- High demand allows reliable sales with minimal marketing effort

The in-ground cold-soil storage system eliminates the costs and quality losses associated with conventional root cellaring or refrigerated storage.

## Integration with Greenhouse Rotation

Winter carrots fit naturally into the greenhouse rotation schedule:
1. Green manure (oats and peas) through mid-summer
2. Carrot sowing late July / early August
3. Mobile greenhouse placed over beds late October
4. Harvest October through February
5. Bed becomes available for spring crops in March

This makes efficient use of the mobile greenhouse, which covers the carrots during the coldest months and can then move to protect other crops.
