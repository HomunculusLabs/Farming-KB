# Mushroom Bed Preparation

Mushroom bed preparation is the foundational skill in
cultivated mushroom production. The process involves selecting
and preparing manure, constructing the bed to correct depth,
managing fermentation temperatures, applying [[mushroom-spawn]],
and finishing with a proper layer of [[mushroom-soil-mould]].
[[james-cuthill]]'s 1870 treatise provides detailed guidance
refined over thirty years of commercial and private gardening.

## Manure Selection

Horse dung forms the basis of all mushroom beds. Cuthill did
not specify strict requirements for the condition of dung in
the lower part of the bed, provided the upper six inches
consisted of fresh droppings and unheated dung for the
surface. When quite fresh dung is trodden very hard, it
prevents overheating and retains heat longer, preserving
ammonia and other salts that nourish the spawn.

### Collecting Droppings

For cottagers without stable access, Cuthill recommended
employing children to collect horse-droppings along the
highway. Mixed with road-sand, the droppings are improved as
sand prevents nutrient loss through heavy rains. These should
be deposited in a heap during summer and trodden firmly.
The harder they are pressed, the less they overheat.

### Winter Droppings

Droppings collected during winter must be heaped and allowed
to heat gently to eighty or ninety degrees Fahrenheit, then
turned twice daily to release excess heat and steam. Neglect
of this turning destroys the natural spawn present in the
droppings through excessive fermentation.

## Bed Depth

All of Cuthill's beds were from one foot to one foot six
inches deep. The cottager was advised to provide strawy dung
for the foundation, ensuring the finished depth was not less
than one foot. This substantial depth provides adequate
thermal mass for sustained winter fermentation.

## Temperature Control

Temperature management is critical throughout bed preparation.
The two greatest enemies of spawn are excessive heat and
excessive moisture.

### The Trial Stick

A stick inserted into the bed served as a temperature probe.
If too hot for the hand to bear, the heat would destroy the
spawn. The ideal target was milk heat (approximately 98 to
100 degrees Fahrenheit) at spawning time.

### Letting Out Heat

When the bed was too hot, Cuthill made holes six inches deep
and one foot apart in regular rows. Once heat reduced to
eighty degrees, holes were filled to within three inches of
the surface with droppings, and [[mushroom-spawn]] inserted
in each. This ensured even heat distribution.

### Surface Temperature

The surface of a finished bed should be at September heat,
approximately sixty to seventy degrees. Dung near the surface,
four inches deep, should never exceed blood heat, or the
spawn or embryo mushroom will be destroyed.

## Construction Sequence

### Foundation Layer

The bed begins with a foundation of strawy stable dung,
tamped firmly. For cottagers using highway droppings, this
foundation ensures adequate depth and initial heat.

### Surface Droppings

Fresh droppings are applied over the foundation. By this
stage they should ideally be one mass of natural [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-spawn-grain-production]], with a grey, mouldy, thready appearance and a
mushroom-like smell, having developed spontaneously through
proper summer management.

### Firm Compaction

The entire bed must be pressed very hard. Treading firmly at
every stage prevents excessive fermentation, retains heat
longer, preserves ammonia and salts, and creates the firm
texture that spawn prefers.

### Spawning

Spawn is inserted one foot apart each way near the surface.
If using artificial spawn, merely insert it into the surface
with a light covering of droppings. Spawn planted too deep
never thrives, as it delights in being near the surface.

### Moulding

Immediately upon spawning, unsifted mould is applied four
inches thick and trodden down hard. The surface is watered
and compacted with the back of a spade for a plastered
finish. See [[mushroom-soil-mould]] for soil selection.

### Covering

A slight hay covering prevents surface cracking. This must
be removed periodically to dry, as mouldy hay can spoil the
bed. In September, hay and mat coverings also maintain the
desired temperature.

## The Improved Method

In his appendix, Cuthill described an improved method:
rather than waiting for the bed to cool before spawning, he
spawned as soon as heat reached 100 degrees or more. By
placing cold mould on the surface, treading hard, watering
and plastering, the heat was driven downwards. By morning it
had subsided to the bottom, injuring no spawn. The ammonia
and manure strength were preserved, and the latent heat could
be revived by covering, producing crops two weeks earlier.

## Bed Longevity

A properly made bed bears for three or four months or more,
lasting three to four times longer than field mushrooms. One
pound of well-grown bed mushrooms was worth three pounds of
field ones for any purpose.
