# British Adoption of French Intensive Gardening

The transfer of French intensive [[container-gardening-techniques]] to Britain
in the late 19th and early 20th centuries illustrates both the
power of agricultural innovation and the pitfalls of hype-driven
adoption.

The story involves a garden writer, an anarchist prince, a soap
millionaire, and hundreds of failed mini-farms.

## William Robinson — First English Advocate

In 1869, British garden writer William Robinson became the first
English-speaking outsider to publicize the Parisian maraîcher
system in his book Parks and Gardens of Paris, chiding his
countrymen for failing to adopt these superior techniques.

Despite Robinson's prominence, no one paid much attention.

## Kropotkin's Endorsement

The breakthrough came when Prince Peter Kropotkin praised the
Parisian maraîchers in his 1899 book Fields, Factories and
Workshops, lending the system unexpected ideological appeal as
a model empowering individual growers over large landowners.

He dismissed [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] as "pompously labeled and
unworthy drugs." The gardening world finally took notice.

## The 1905 British Delegation to Paris

In 1905, British market gardeners visited Paris to investigate
how French growers in the same climate exported salads to Britain
all winter while the British grew none.

They returned speaking glowingly about French productivity.
British interest surged.

## The Social Reform Context

Widespread concern existed about helping unemployed factory
workers return to self-supporting life on the land. Intensive
[[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] seemed perfect — minimal land, high output,
year-round cash flow.

A newspaper published an inexpensive how-to: The French Garden:
A Diary and Manual of Intensive Cultivation.

## Joseph Fels and the Mayland Experiment

Joseph Fels, the wealthy American Fels-Naptha soap businessman,
purchased 600 acres at Mayland, Essex, to subdivide into mini-
farms for intensive French-style production.

His foreman Joseph Smith wrote French Gardening (introduction
by Kropotkin). Other books followed, including Gold Producing
Soil — a title hinting at unrealistic expectations.

## Hype and Failure

The promotional literature gave the impression that "easy riches
were to be gained through French gardening."

As with other horticultural "miracle" schemes, the hype proved
detrimental to long-term outcomes.

Many French-style gardens in England failed through incompetence
or did not live up to economic expectations.

The critical missing elements in the promotional literature were
the high level of expertise needed for success and the long hours
of hard work involved — somehow lost in the glowing descriptions.

One commentator claimed the system would never succeed in Britain
because British growers would never work as hard as French
peasants.
