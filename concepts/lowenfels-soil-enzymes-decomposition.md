## How Bacterial Enzymes Work

Bacteria use enzymes both to break the bonds holding organic chains together and
to digest their food. All enzymatic digestion occurs outside the bacterial cell
before ingestion. Untold numbers of enzymes are employed by bacteria, which have
adapted over millennia to attack all manner of organic and even inorganic
matter. It is remarkable that bacteria can employ enzymes to break down organic
matter while simultaneously not damaging their own cell membranes. Specialized
bacteria like *Cellulomonas* carry cellulose-breaking enzymes that they release
only when in contact with cellulose, unlike other bacteria that release enzymes
in a hit-and-miss fashion.

## Cellulose Decomposition

Cellulose is a complex carbohydrate made of long chains of carbon-based glucose,
and it constitutes half the mass of plant bodies. Breaking down cellulose is one
of the most important decomposition tasks in soil.
[[lowenfels-fungi-role-soil]] excel: they produce phenol
oxidase, a strong enzyme that dissolves even lignin. Fungi are the primary decay
agents for the most recalcitrant carbon compounds in the soil food web. Their
enzymes allow them to penetrate not only lignin and cellulose in plants but also
the hard chitin shells of insects and even the protein of nails. Compared to
fungi, bacteria are in what Lowenfels and Lewis call the minor leagues of
decaying ability.

## The Apical Growth Advantage

Fungi release their powerful enzymes through apical growth, meaning growth at
the [[gadd-hyphal-tip-growth-and-branching-mechanisms]]. As new cells are pushed into the tip and along sidewalls,
elongating the hyphal tube, vesicles loaded with construction supplies are
transported to the advancing tip. Enzymes capable of dissolving all but the most
recalcitrant carbon compounds are released as new cells form. These enzymes
convert lignin, cellulose, and other tough organic matter into simple sugars and
amino acids, yet remarkably do not decay the chitin cell walls of the fungi
themselves. Fungi can grow up to 40 micrometers per minute, covering far more
ground than bacteria.

## Actinomycetes and the Earthy Smell

Actinomycetes deserve special mention for their enzymatic contributions.  These
filamentous bacteria produce enzymes that include volatile chemicals giving soil
its clean, fresh, earthy aroma, a smell every gardener recognizes. The genus
*Streptomyces* is particularly important, producing enzymes that decompose
cellulose, lignin, chitin, and protein. These organisms grow filaments almost
like fungal hyphae and can connect soil particles, making themselves too large
for protozoan predators. They are adapted to live in a wider pH range than other
bacteria, from acidic to alkaline conditions, making them versatile decomposers
in diverse soils.

## Chitin Decomposition

Chitin is a tough polysaccharide found in fungal cell walls, arthropod
exoskeletons, and insect shells. Its decomposition requires specialized enzymes
called chitinases. Actinomycetes are particularly adept at breaking down chitin,
along with certain fungi and [[lowenfels-soil-arthropod-macrofauna]] that physically grind up chitinous material. The release of
chitinases has an important secondary benefit: some of these enzymes also affect
pest insects, as chitin is a structural component of their exoskeletons. Adding
chitin-rich materials like crushed shrimp shells to soil can stimulate
chitinase-producing microbes, potentially helping to suppress soil-dwelling pest
insects.
