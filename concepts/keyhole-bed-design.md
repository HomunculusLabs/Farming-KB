# Keyhole Bed Design

Keyhole beds are one of the most practical and elegant space-saving garden layouts available to the home gardener. By bending a rectangular raised bed into a horseshoe shape with a central access path, keyhole beds dramatically reduce the area lost to paths while creating a more productive, accessible, and beautiful garden.

## The Problem With Paths

Paths are a necessary evil in any garden. After working hard to build up fluffy, fertile soil, it frustrates gardeners to pound a high percentage of it into hardpan under their feet. Every bit of path is unusable real estate that could be devoted to crops. The most basic garden layout -- single rows with paths between each row -- sacrifices about half of the soil area to walkways. Raised beds cut this to about 30 percent. Keyhole beds do far better.

### Space Efficiency by Numbers

For a typical 50-square-foot planting bed, the path requirements break down as follows:

- **Single rows**: ~40 square feet of path -- 80% wasted to access
- **Raised beds**: ~10 square feet of path -- 20% wasted to access
- **Keyhole beds**: ~6 square feet of path -- only 12% wasted to access

The keyhole bed puts nearly 88% of the garden area into production, making it ideal for [[sheet-mulching-lasagna-gardening]]. This builds soil while creating the bed.

## Zone-Based Planting

The keyhole bed can be planted using the zone system -- placing the most frequently picked plants closest to the center:

- **Inner ring** (closest to center path): Herbs, salad greens, and other daily-harvested vegetables
- **Middle ring**: Tomatoes, peppers, bush peas and beans, eggplants -- plants picked regularly through the season
- **Outer ring**: Long-term crops and single-harvest plants -- potatoes, carrots, root crops, cabbage, cauliflower

For outer-row plants that may be hard to reach, stepping on the bed once per season (or standing on a board to limit compaction) won't annihilate soil porosity.
