## The Best Loam

The ideal loam was rich, fresh, mellow soil such as florists eagerly
sought for potting and greenhouse purposes. The mushroom bed preparation process
depended heavily on quality loam. Falconer's preferred material
was fresh sod loam -- the top spit from a pasture field, gathered in early
fall and stacked without added manure. A healthy [[substrate-preparation|Substrate preparation]] in modern mushroom growing follows similar principles of material selection.

## Roadside Dirt

Roadside dirt, whether loamy or gritty, could be used with good results
if free from weeds, sticks, stones, and rough drift. The [[fungal-ecology|Fungal ecology]] determines the suitability of any soil for mushroom growing. Sandy soil from water-shed drifts along roads and fields was "much
inferior to stiffer and more fibrous earth." Rich dark soil from slopes
and dry hollows in woods could be used with success, though mushrooms do
not naturally grow in woods. Peat soil or swamp muck composted for two or
three years failed to give good returns -- mushrooms came up through it
but did not "take kindly to it." This aligns with the broader understanding of [[mushroom-nutrition-manure-fermentation|mushroom nutrition research]].

Falconer could not join this denunciation. When he was connected with the
London market gardens, the large growers in the Fulham Fields -- Steele,
Bagley, Broadbent, and others -- cased all their beds with common garden
soil, "perhaps the most manure-filled soil on the face of the earth," and
spurious fungi never troubled them.

He argued that the rich earth of vegetable gardens had no more to do with
producing toadstools than any other good soil, and old manure had far
less to do with it than fresh manure. Hotbeds in spring were apt to
produce toadstools when their heat declined, but this tendency disappeared
when beds were "spent." Spurious fungi were most numerous two to three
weeks before mushrooms appeared, and also appeared on manure piles in
yards after strong heat had subsided.
