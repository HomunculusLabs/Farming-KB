# Braggot Brewing Guide

Braggot is a hybrid fermented beverage that combines the grain-based tradition of beer with the honey-based tradition of mead. Essentially a malt and honey brew, braggot occupies the space between beer and mead, drawing characteristics from both. It is one of the oldest styles of fermented beverage, predating the clear distinction between beer and mead in many [[entheogens-in-ancient-cultures]].

## What Is Braggot?

Braggot (also spelled bragget or bracket) is defined as a fermented beverage made from both malted grain and honey. The proportions can vary widely -- some braggots are essentially beer with honey added, while others are mead with grain components. The BJCP style guidelines describe braggot as a beverage where both malt and honey contribute significantly to the character.

Historically, the boundary between beer and mead was often blurry. Many ancient [[comparison-mead-vs-kvass-fermented-beverages]] used whatever fermentable materials were available -- grain, honey, fruit, and herbs. The distinct categories of "beer" and "mead" are somewhat modern, and braggot represents the continuation of a more integrated, ancient approach to fermentation.

## Historical Context

Braggot has deep roots in European brewing history. In medieval England, braggot was a common beverage, particularly in monasteries that brewed both ale and mead. The Welsh tradition of bragawd was a prestigious fermented drink made from barley and honey, sometimes further enhanced with spices and herbs.

In Celtic and Anglo-Saxon tradition, braggot was often associated with celebrations and important occasions. It was considered a finer drink than ordinary ale due to the cost and labor involved in producing honey. The combination of grain and honey provided both the sustenance of beer and the refined sweetness of mead.

The word "braggot" likely derives from the Welsh "bragawd" or the Irish "bracch," both referring to fermented beverages combining malt and honey. Medieval records show braggot being served at feasts, given as gifts, and used in diplomatic exchanges.

## Ingredients

### Malt

The malt component of braggot provides body, flavor complexity, and a foundation of fermentable sugar. Common choices include:

- Pale malt as a base for most braggots
- Munich or Vienna malt for a richer, maltier character
- Crystal/caramel malts for sweetness and body
- Roasted malts for dark braggots with coffee and chocolate notes
- Wheat malt for head retention and a lighter body

The grain bill typically ranges from 3 to 10 pounds of malt for a 5-gallon batch, depending on the desired balance between malt and honey character.

### Honey

Honey contributes sweetness, aroma, and fermentable sugar. The choice of honey has a significant impact on the final character of the braggot:

- Wildflower honey: Complex, varied floral character, widely available
- Clover honey: Mild, sweet, neutral -- a good starting point
- Orange blossom honey: Distinctive citrus and floral notes
- Buckwheat honey: Dark, malty, earthy -- excellent for dark braggots
- Meadery blends: Often designed specifically for fermentation

Honey quantities typically range from 2 to 6 pounds for a 5-gallon batch. More honey produces a stronger, more mead-like braggot. Less honey produces a beer-like beverage with honey character.

### Hops and Herbs

Braggot can be hopped like beer, or flavored with herbs like traditional gruit ales. Hop bitterness should be balanced with honey sweetness. British or noble hop varieties pair well with honey flavors. For herbal braggots, consider heather, chamomile, rosemary, or ginger.

### Yeast

Yeast choice is critical for braggot because the fermentation medium is different from both standard beer wort and mead must. Considerations include:

- Ale yeast: Suitable for lower-alcohol braggots with beer-like character
- Mead yeast: Better for higher-alcohol braggots where honey dominates
- Wine yeast: Works well for strong braggots but may produce a drier finish than desired
- A combination approach: Start with ale yeast, then add a more alcohol-tolerant strain if fermentation stalls

English ale strains and Belgian strains tend to pair well with honey. American strains can work but may produce a character that clashes with delicate honey flavors.

## Brewing Process

### Method One: Beer-Forward Braggot

1. Mash the grain as you would for an all-grain beer batch
2. Collect wort in the boil kettle
3. Boil with hops or herbs as desired
4. After the boil, add honey to the hot wort (pasteurization occurs above 160 degrees F)
5. Cool and pitch yeast
6. Ferment at appropriate temperature for the chosen yeast

### Method Two: Mead-Forward Braggot

1. Prepare a grain tea by steeping specialty grains in hot water (no base malt, just crystal and roasted malts for flavor)
2. Dissolve honey in the grain tea
3. Boil briefly with hops or herbs
4. Cool and pitch mead yeast or wine yeast
5. Ferment and age as you would mead
