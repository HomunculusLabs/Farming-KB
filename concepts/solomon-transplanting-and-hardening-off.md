# Solomon on Transplanting and Hardening Off

From [[steve-solomon]].

## Solomon's Preference: Direct Seeding Over Transplanting

Solomon strongly advocates direct seeding as the primary method for most vegetables, arguing that the nursery/seedling industry developed primarily to serve two markets: (1) people who garden as a minor hobby and are willing to accept poor results, and (2) market gardeners in cool maritime climates where some crops simply cannot mature without a head start.

### Problems with Transplants
- **Shock**: Even hardened-off seedlings experience transplant shock, losing days or weeks of growth
- **Root disturbance**: Most vegetables have root systems that resent disturbance; cutting or circling roots from container growing permanently damages the plant
- **Succulence**: Greenhouse-grown seedlings are always lush and succulent, having been pushed for maximum growth rates with high fertility and regular watering. This makes them:
  - More attractive to pests (especially flea beetles)
  - Less drought-tolerant once in the garden
  - Less able to develop deep root systems
- **Cost**: Buying transplants is far more expensive than buying seed
- **Variety limitations**: Nurseries stock only a few varieties; seed catalogs offer hundreds
- **Quality uncertainty**: The seedling may not actually be the variety marked on the label, or the variety may be one bred to look good at 4 weeks old rather than to produce well in the garden
- **Fragility**: Seedlings that cost a few dollars each are hard to be dispassionate about when pests knock them over, unlike seeds that cost fractions of a cent each

### When Transplants Are Necessary
- Very long-season crops in short-season climates (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant in cool regions)
- Perennial crops started from seed (asparagus, etc.)
- Where growing seasons are genuinely too short for direct-seeded crops to mature

## Hardening Off

If you must use transplants, hardening off is essential. Tender greenhouse seedlings must be gradually acclimated to outdoor conditions:

1. **Start gradually**: Begin by setting seedlings outside for a few hours in a sheltered spot, increasing exposure over 7-10 days
2. **Reduce water**: Gradually reduce watering to toughen the plants; don't let them wilt severely
3. **Increase sun exposure**: Move from shade to partial sun to full sun gradually
4. **Reduce temperature**: If grown on heat mats or in warm indoor spaces, gradually reduce temperature
5. **Wind exposure**: Introduce seedlings to wind gradually so stems strengthen

Without hardening off, seedlings suffer severe shock and may lose weeks of growth or die entirely.

## Root Systems and Why They Matter

Solomon's argument against transplanting rests on understanding root development:

- Each root tip is the only part capable of efficiently absorbing water and nutrients, and this capability extends only a fraction of an inch behind the tip
- Within days of forming, root tips become suberized (bark-covered) and can no longer absorb effectively
- The plant feeds by continuously creating new root tips in an ever-expanding network
- When a root system is cut (as during transplanting), the plant loses its existing absorptive surface and must regenerate from the remaining root tips
- Direct-seeded plants never have their root development interrupted

## The Greenhouse Trap

Seedlings grown in the controlled environment of a greenhouse develop differently than those grown in the variable conditions of a garden:
- They are pushed with warmth and constant moisture, creating succulent tissue
- Their root systems are confined to small containers, leading to circling roots
- They never develop the depth or breadth they would have if grown from seed in open ground
- When transplanted out, the contrast between greenhouse conditions and field conditions is enormous

## Direct Seeding Advantages

- No transplant shock -- the plant never knows it was "moved"
- Root system develops from day one in its permanent position, reaching deep into subsoil
- Much lower cost (seeds vs. seedlings)
- Enormous variety choice from seed catalogs
- Plants are naturally hardened by growing in actual outdoor conditions from the start
- If conditions are unfavorable, seeds cost pennies -- you can easily resow
- In dryland conditions, direct-seeded plants develop deeper, more drought-resistant root systems

## Seedling Clusters vs. Single Plants

When direct seeding, Solomon recommends sowing several seeds per station (3-5 for most crops, up to 10 for tiny-seeded species like celery). After emergence, progressively thin to the best single plant. This provides insurance against germination failure and allows selection of the most vigorous individual.

# Raised Beds vs Double Digging

Steve Solomon explicitly rejects the intensive gardening orthodoxy of double-digging raised beds to 24 inches (60 centimeters) deep. He argues this practice is unnecessary, labor-intensive, and produces little additional benefit for the effort expended.

## The Case Against Double Digging

Most intensivists say raised beds need to be double-dug 24 inches deep. Solomon disagrees entirely. Digging 12 inches (30 centimeters) is plenty; loosening the second foot produces little additional benefit in exchange for a heap of effort. This is a critical distinction because Solomon wants gardening to be sustainable and enjoyable, not an exhausting chore that discourages people from continuing.

Beyond the effort issue, double-digging can actually be counterproductive. Over time the second foot will become looser without any extra effort on your part as worms transport organic matter you put into the surface deeper into the soil, and as plant nutrients leach into the subsoil. This chemical loosening of clay through nutrient leaching is called "flocculation" - a natural process that deepens the fertile zone without human labor.

## The Real Purpose of Raised Beds

Solomon believes there is no one best way to arrange plantings. Raised beds are useful for some crops where and when there is irrigation available. Crops like Europe-type small cabbage and sweet corn do better "on the flat" or in hills. Unirrigated gardens should mainly use hills and single rows except for small-sized vegetables.

The choice between beds, rows, and hills depends on:
- Whether you have irrigation available
- The size and type of crop being grown
- Your soil type and climate
- How much labor you are willing to invest

## Why Raise Beds at All

The primary reasons for slightly raising beds are practical, not aesthetic:

### Seed-Sprouting Facilitation

It is far easier to get small seeds to sprout if the planting areas are slightly raised. The seeds don't care about their position relative to the paths; they have no awareness of anything except temperature, humidity, and the total frustration they must feel when a big heavy lump of impenetrable stuff sits above them. In that case, the seed dies. Large seeds like beans, peas, and corn can push harder against obstacles, and if you sow extra seeds, enough of them will usually emerge to make a row even in rough ground. However, tiny seeds like lettuce need what is termed a "fine seedbed" to come up in.

### Creating a Fine Seedbed Efficiently

Try using a rake to make a zone of fine soil on flat ground after spading it up. After much time and effort you'll end up with a shallow valley of fine soil, edged on both sides by ridges of clods that can fall back and smother your emerging seedlings. But if you raise the beds a few inches, you can rapidly rake the clods and lumps off the top and put them down into the paths, where they will break down over the next months as you walk around the garden.

### Compaction Prevention

Raised beds grow better plants because they tend to stay looser. People don't step on them. Walking on the root systems of growing vegetables compacts soil and makes it more difficult for the vegetables to develop roots in the first place. This is critical because root development directly determines plant health and productivity.

Raising the growing areas a few inches also reminds people to keep their feet off.

## Dimensions

Raised beds should not be so wide that a person standing on the path next to them has trouble reaching the center while bending over or squatting. For most people, four feet (120 centimeters) wide is about the limit. If you are inflexible or have short arms, the width can get down to as little as three feet (90 centimeters). But the narrower the beds become, the less efficiently the overall space will be used because there will be more paths. Also, if the bed gets much narrower than four feet, it is no longer possible to have two parallel rows of large plants running down it lengthwise. For someone quite flexible with long arms, a bed might be 4.5 feet (140 centimeters) wide.

The paths between beds are about one foot (30 centimeters) wide.

Unless you have become so inflexible that you can no longer squat or bend over, there is no point in raising the beds more than two to three inches (five to eight centimeters). This is easily accomplished.

## Making Raised Beds

Immediately after digging a new garden, using a combination shovel, move along what will be the paths, scoop up about two inches (five centimeters) of soil, and toss it atop what will be the growing bed or row. Stretching string lines or pegging the corners might help when these beds are made. In following years you simply remake the beds and wide raised rows the same way.

## What Goes on Raised Beds

Small-sized vegetables - beets, carrots, lettuce, bush beans, bush peas - do well on raised beds or raised wide rows. Larger plants such as Swiss chard, many varieties of cucumber, cabbage, kale, and Brussels sprouts also grow well on raised beds and in wide raised single rows.

The exceptions are all species that have large, vigorously sprouting seeds or that grow big, widely sprawling plants, such as squash, melons, and some kinds of tomatoes. Sweet corn, which needs to grow in a large cluster to pollinate, also does better "on the flat."
