# Falconer Spawning the Beds

Chapter XI of William Falconer's *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891)
provides detailed guidance on the critical operation of inserting spawn
into prepared mushroom beds. Correct spawning technique and timing
directly determined whether a bed would produce a good crop, a poor one,
or fail entirely.

## Temperature for Spawning

After the bed was made up, it should warm to 110 to 120 degrees within a
few days. Falconer's cardinal rule: never spawn a bed when the heat is
rising or when warmer than 100 degrees, but always when declining and
under 90 degrees. "In this there is perfect safety." A ground thermometer
kept plunged in the bed was essential. Straight smooth stakes stuck into
the bed at intervals served as tactile indicators -- pulling them out and
feeling them with the hand gave a close approximation of bed temperature.

## Temperature Effects on Spawn

Falconer conducted careful experiments on spawning at different
temperatures:

- Spawn planted when bed heat later ran to 123 degrees yielded nothing;
  the mycelium was killed.
- Where heat never exceeded 115 degrees, spawn put in at bed-making and
  molded the same day yielded only a small crop.
- A bed spawned at 110 degrees (heat declining) bore a very good crop.
- Beds spawned at 100 degrees and under down to 65 degrees all produced
  good crops, with several days' delay in bearing at the lowest
  temperatures.

Despite these results, Falconer advised all beginners to wait until heat
declined to at least 90 degrees before inserting spawn.

## Preparing Brick Spawn

Brick spawn (standard size) was cut into ten or twelve pieces with a
sharp hatchet, avoiding the crumbs produced by hammers or mallets.
Extra-large pieces produced large clumps of mushrooms that were apt to be
undersized, and in gathering the grower could not pluck them all out
cleanly enough to avoid leaving part of the "root" to poison the balance
of the clump.

## Inserting Brick Spawn

Brick spawn lumps were planted about one inch deep under the manure
surface and about ten inches apart each way. If the spawn showed abundant
mycelium and the lumps were large, they were planted farther apart than
when mycelium was sparse and lumps small. A dibber was never used; holes
were made with the fingers, the lump inserted and covered at once, then
the bed firmed well all over.

## Inserting Flake (French) Spawn

French flake spawn was broken into pieces about three inches long by an
inch thick, and laid on its flat side in the hole (not pushed in) and
covered immediately. Spacing was similar to brick spawn.

## Deep vs. Shallow Planting

Many growers planted spawn much deeper than Falconer recommended, but he
had never found any advantage in deep planting. In moderately warm beds
likely to retain heat, shallow planting was better. When molding over was
planned soon after spawning, shallow planting was recommended. However,
in cool beds (75 to 80 degrees), deep planting was preferable because the
genial temperature deeper in the bed gave mycelium a better start.

## Timing for Molding Over

If there was any likelihood of surface manure getting wet from condensed
atmospheric moisture, beds were covered with hay or straw until molding
time. If the bed was a little sluggish (cool), the covering helped retain
warmth. Outside beds should be molded over in three to four days after
spawning; inside beds in eight to ten days.

## Steeped Spawn

Falconer tested steeping hard, dry brick spawn in tepid water before
planting. Pieces were dipped, or soaked for half an hour, one hour, five
hours, and ten hours. "The effect was prejudicial in every instance and
ruinous in the case of the long-soaked pieces."

## Transplanted Working Spawn

Some French growers transplanted pieces of actively growing (working)
spawn from one bed to another. Mr. Henshaw recommended breaking brick
spawn into pieces about two inches square and mixing them in a gently
fermenting heap of manure for about three weeks, producing "flake spawn"
that appeared two to three weeks earlier than brick spawn. Falconer
tested this method carefully and found results inferior to plain brick
spawn planted directly.

He concluded that any disturbance of spawn in active growth -- breaking,
exposing, or arresting the threads of mycelium -- had a weakening
influence. He was satisfied that he got better crops and larger mushrooms
from beds spawned with dry spawn than from beds planted with working
spawn transplanted from other beds.
