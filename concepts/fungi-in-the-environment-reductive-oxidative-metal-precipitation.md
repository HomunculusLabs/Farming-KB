# Reductive and Oxidative Metal Precipitation by Fungi

Fungi possess remarkable abilities to transform metal species through both
reductive and oxidative precipitation — processes that alter the
solubility, mobility, and toxicity of metals in terrestrial and aquatic
environments. These transformations are central to fungal roles in
biogeochemical cycling, mineral formation, and bioremediation of
contaminated sites. This concept is drawn from the chapter on fungal
involvement in metal transformations in *Fungi in the Environment* (Gadd,
Watkinson & Dyer, 2006).

## Reductive Precipitation

Reductive precipitation involves the reduction of soluble metal ions to
insoluble lower-valence states. This process converts mobile, often toxic
metal species into immobile precipitates that are far less bioavailable.
Fungi achieve this through enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms.

### Enzymatic Reduction

Several groups of fungi enzymatically reduce metals as part of their normal
metabolism. The most studied examples involve uranium and other actinides:

**Uranium (VI) to Uranium (IV)**: Soluble hexavalent uranium (UO₂²⁺,
uranyl) is reduced to insoluble tetravalent uranium (UO₂, uraninite) by
fungi including *Aspergillus niger*, *Penicillium* species, and *Rhizopus*
species. The precipitated uraninite forms nanocrystalline deposits
associated with fungal biomass, often at the cell wall or within
extracellular polymeric substances.

**Chromium (VI) to Chromium (III)**: Highly toxic and mobile chromate
(CrO₄²⁻) is reduced to relatively insoluble and less toxic chromium (III).
*Aspergillus niger* and other fungi accomplish this through both enzymatic
mechanisms (chromate reductases) and indirect chemical reduction by
metabolites including organic acids.

**Manganese (IV) to Manganese (II)**: Reduction of insoluble manganese
oxides to soluble manganese (II) is important in soil manganese cycling.
This process can solubilize manganese from mineral forms, making it
available for fungal nutrition but also potentially mobilizing associated
metals.

### Non-Enzymatic Reduction

Fungi produce a wide array of metabolites that can chemically reduce metals
without direct enzymatic involvement. Citric, oxalic, and other fungal
organic acids can act as reducing agents for certain metal species — oxalic
acid is particularly important, acting as both a chelating agent and a
reductant. Sulfate-reducing conditions created by some fungi produce
hydrogen sulfide, which precipitates metals as insoluble metal sulfides.
Glutathione and other sulfur-containing thiol compounds can reduce metals
through direct chemical interaction.

## Oxidative Precipitation

Oxidative precipitation converts soluble lower-valence metals into
insoluble higher-valence forms. Fungi oxidize metals through enzymatic
action and through indirect chemical pathways involving reactive oxygen
species and metabolites.

### Enzymatic Oxidation

**Iron oxidation**: Certain fungi, particularly acidophilic species found
in acid mine drainage environments, oxidize ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) to ferric
iron (Fe³⁺). The resulting ferric iron precipitates as ferric hydroxide
(Fe(OH)₃) or as jarosite-type minerals in acidic conditions. *Acontium
cylindrosporum* is a well-studied example of a fungal iron oxidizer.

**Manganese oxidation**: Fungal laccases, peroxidases, and tyrosinases
oxidize manganese (II) to manganese (III/IV) oxides. These manganese oxides
are highly reactive and serve as powerful oxidants for other soil
constituents. Manganese-oxidizing fungi include *Acremonium* and
*Cladosporium* species, which form dark manganese oxide deposits on their
hyphae and in surrounding media.

**Arsenic oxidation**: Some fungi oxidize arsenite (As(III)) to arsenate
(As(V)). While arsenate is actually more mobile than arsenite, the
oxidation facilitates subsequent removal strategies and alters arsenic
bioavailability.

### Indirect Oxidation Through Reactive Oxygen Species

Fungal metabolism generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) including
superoxide (O₂⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and hydroxyl radicals (OH·).
These powerful oxidants can oxidize metals non-specifically. While ROS
production is often considered a stress response, it also contributes to
metal transformation under natural conditions.

## Mycogenic Mineral Formation

Metal precipitation by fungi frequently leads to the formation of
crystalline mineral phases — a process termed mycogenic mineral formation.
Fungi can induce precipitation of oxalates, carbonates, phosphates, and
oxides/hydroxides of various metals.

### Metal Oxalates

Oxalic acid is one of the most common fungal metabolites and plays a
central role in metal precipitation. Metal oxalates formed by fungi include
weddellite (calcium oxalate dihydrate), whewellite (calcium oxalate
monohydrate), and metal oxalates of copper, zinc, lead, and rare earth
elements. These crystalline deposits form within and around fungal biomass
and can persist in soils as stable mineral phases.

### Metal Carbonates

Fungal metabolic activity, particularly CO₂ production through respiration,
can drive precipitation of metal carbonates. In carbonate-rich
environments, fungal calcium carbonate precipitation is widespread and
contributes to soil carbonate formation, rock weathering, and pedogenesis.

## Bioremediation Applications

The metal transformation abilities of fungi have practical applications in
environmental remediation. Reductive precipitation of uranium provides a
potential strategy for immobilizing radionuclide contamination at mining
and nuclear sites. Oxidative precipitation of manganese and iron can be
harnessed for metal recovery from waste streams. Fungal biomass itself
serves as an effective biosorbent, with metal binding occurring at cell
wall sites containing chitin, glucans, and associated proteins.

## Biogeochemical Significance

In natural environments, fungal metal transformations influence the
distribution and speciation of metals in soils and sediments. They affect
the mobility of toxic metals in contaminated sites, the availability of
essential trace metals to plants and microorganisms, and the formation of
authigenic mineral phases. The ability of fungi to transform metals through
both reductive and oxidative pathways gives them a unique role in
biogeochemical cycling that complements the activities of bacteria and
archaea.
