# Cannabis and Insomnia

**Source:** The Cannabis Health Index, Uwe Blesching, PhD (2017)
**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Evidence-Based Therapeutic Potential

- **Total Studies Reviewed:** 1
- **CHI Value:** 5

## Overview

Insomnia is difficulty or inability to fall or stay asleep. Its preceding symptoms mirror its effects: mental and physical performance impairment, moodiness, anxiety, depression, low energy, fatigue, hallucinations, lowered immunity, and hormone disruption. If insomnia follows acute pain, jet lag, or schedule changes, it is usually self-correcting. Insomnia from anxiety or substance use requires more active intervention.

Orthodox treatments include benzodiazepines (addictive), sedative-hypnotics like Ambien (addictive), opiates (addictive), and antidepressants (serious side effects). None cure chronic insomnia, which typically returns when medication stops.

Chronic insomnia affects approximately 10–15% of the adult population and is associated with significant morbidity including increased risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, obesity, depression, and impaired cognitive function. The condition often co-occurs with other disorders such as chronic pain, anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, creating complex symptom interactions that are difficult to manage with conventional pharmacotherapy alone.

## Cannabis and Insomnia

Most studies on cannabis and sleep date from the 1970s and 1980s. These early studies revealed cannabis had a varied impact on sleep — acting like a sedative in some ways but also reducing deep sleep. The Farnborough study (2004, UK) revealed an apparent self-balancing mechanism between CBD and THC: dose-dependent THC appears to have sedative effects, while dose-dependent CBD appears to have some alerting properties.

## Mechanisms of Action

The [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] is integral to normal sleep regulation.

- **Pain relief and sleep:** For many patients, insomnia is secondary to chronic pain. THC and CBD both provide analgesic effects through CB1 and CB2 receptor activation. By reducing pain, cannabinoids may improve sleep quality in patients with [[cannabis-for-mental-health]] research.

## Additional Research Developments

Research on cannabis and sleep has expanded significantly:

- A 2014 study of 163 adults using medical cannabis found that patients reported significant improvements in sleep quality, with the majority reporting falling asleep faster and experiencing less sleep disturbance.

- A 2018 large-scale survey of cannabis users found that insomnia was among the top five conditions for which people used cannabis, and that users reported significant improvement.

- Research has shown that the relationship between cannabis and sleep may change with long-term use, as tolerance to the sedative effects of THC can develop. This highlights the importance of [[cannabis-minor-cannabinoids]], has gained attention as a potential sleep aid. While clinical evidence is limited, anecdotal reports and some pre-clinical data suggest CBN may have sedative properties, particularly when combined with THC.

## Dosage and Administration

For insomnia patients considering cannabis therapy, several factors are relevant:

- THC-dominant preparations taken 30–60 minutes before bedtime may reduce sleep onset latency
- Indica strains are generally preferred for nighttime use due to their relaxing and sedating properties
- [[cannabis-methods-of-consumption]] is relevant — vaporization provides rapid onset for patients who have difficulty falling asleep
- Patients should consult [[cannabis-drug-interactions-and-contraindications]] with sedatives, benzodiazepines, and other sleep medications

## Strain-Specific Considerations

Patients using cannabis for sleep report indica and indica-dominant strains with their lower THC:CBD ratios encourage sedation, relaxation, and grounding effects. This aligns with the finding that THC has sedative properties while CBD is alerting — indicas generally have relatively less THC and more CBD compared to sativas.

Strains with high levels of myrcene (a sedating terpene) and linalool (anxiolytic) in their [[cannabis-minor-cannabinoids]] like CBN may provide additional sedative effects.

## Mind-Body Considerations

Insomniac patients commonly exhibit poor stress management and cognitive-emotional hyperarousal (exaggerated mental-emotional responses). The condition is frequently linked to past traumatic events. Common threads include inability to let go, excessive thinking, fear-based beliefs creating need for control, guilt inducing imagined punishment, and deprecating self-talk in endless loops. Regular, deep sleep usually returns once the person learns to trust the process of life.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the gold standard non-pharmacological treatment and may complement cannabinoid therapy. CBT-I addresses the cognitive and behavioral factors that perpetuate insomnia, including dysfunctional beliefs about sleep, poor sleep hygiene, and conditioned arousal.

## Limitations of Current Research

The evidence base for cannabis and insomnia is limited, with few controlled clinical trials. Long-term use of THC for sleep may lead to tolerance, requiring dose escalation that increases side effect risk. The impact of regular cannabis use on sleep architecture, particularly deep (slow-wave) sleep, is not fully understood. Abrupt discontinuation of regular cannabis use can cause sleep disturbance as a withdrawal symptom. Cannabis should be used as part of a comprehensive sleep management approach, not as a standalone solution.
