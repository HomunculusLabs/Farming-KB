# [[ayahuasca-tourism]] Ethnography

Ayahuasca tourism refers to the growing practice of Western travelers visiting South America, particularly the Peruvian Amazon, to participate in traditional ayahuasca ceremonies led by indigenous or mestizo shamans. Drawing from ethnographic fieldwork in the Ucayali region of Peru (R. Stuart, 2002), this concept page examines the cultural, economic, and ethical dimensions of this phenomenon.

## Historical and Cultural Context

Ayahuasca has deep roots in Amazonian spiritual practice, serving as both a religious sacrament and an herbal tonic among indigenous and mestizo communities across Peru, Brazil, Ecuador, and Colombia. The brew, prepared from the [[banisteriopsis-caapi]] vine and [[psychotria-viridis]] leaves (or the [[diplopterys-cabrerana]] alternative), has been used for centuries in healing rituals, divination, and community ceremonies.

In Peru, ayahuasca occupies a unique legal and cultural position. Unlike the United States, where the brew exists in a legal grey area regulated through religious exemptions (notably for the União do Vegetal and Santo Daime churches), Peruvians regard ayahuasca as an herbal tonic rather than an illegal drug. The government sponsors ayahuasca festivals, the brew and its raw materials are openly sold in markets, and even Peru's then president Alejandro Toledo participated in an ayahuasca ceremony. This permissive environment has made Peru the primary destination for ayahuasca tourism.

## Western Spiritual Seeking and the Appeal of Shamanism

Westerners have a long history of seeking alternative spiritual traditions when discontent with Judeo-Christian heritage. Each decade has seen a different focal point: Zen Buddhism among the Beats in the 1950s, Hindu gurus in the 1960s, cult movements in the 1970s, Tibetan Buddhism among New Agers in the 1980s, and shamanism in the 1990s and beyond. Ayahuasca tourism is the latest expression of this pattern, with North Americans and Europeans traveling to South America to find wisdom and experiences through indigenous practices.

This spiritual tourism intersects with the broader entheogenic renaissance, where psychoactive plants are sought not merely for recreational use but for perceived healing, self knowledge, and spiritual growth. The exoticism of the Amazon setting amplifies the perceived authenticity of the experience, even when the reality may be more commercially mediated.

## Key Destinations and Tourism Models

Ayahuasca tourism has developed along several distinct models, each with different implications for cultural authenticity and tourist experience.

### Iquitos, Peru

Iquitos has been the primary hub for ayahuasca tourism in Peru for over a decade. Its accessibility (reachable by air from Lima) and established tourist infrastructure make it the most common entry point. The surrounding region hosts numerous lodges, retreats, and independent shamans catering to foreign visitors.

### San Francisco (Shipibo Town)

The Shipibo town of San Francisco, reachable by taxi from Yarina near Pucallpa, has branded itself the "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca" as proclaimed by an enormous sign at the town entrance. The Shipibo community has a strong shamanic tradition, with at least a couple of ayahuasqueros in almost every Shipibo family. Many practitioners are willing to host ceremonies for both Peruvians and foreign visitors.

### [[brazilian-ayahuasca-churches]]

Some tourists visit Brazilian ayahuasca churches such as the Santo Daime, which offer a syncretic blend of indigenous ayahuasca practice with Christian and Afro-Brazilian elements. These churches provide a more structured, group-oriented ceremonial setting compared to the more individualized shaman-led ceremonies in Peru.

### American-Led Retreats

Higher-end American-led retreats offer a more comfortable and curated experience, sometimes described as "four-star ayahuasca resorts." While these may feel overly contrived, they also provide greater safety, English language support, and integration of Western therapeutic frameworks. Tours led by experienced guides like Peter Gorman are noted for offering particularly rich and interesting experiences.

## Ceremonial Practices

### The Ceremony Structure

Ayahuasca ceremonies typically take place at night in a ceremonial hut or maloca. Participants gather in darkness, and the shaman administers the brew. The ceremony unfolds through the singing of icaros, the sacred songs that shamans use to guide the experience, call protective spirits, and invoke healing. The icaros are considered one of the most important elements of the ceremony, and skilled practitioners develop extensive repertoires.

### Dosage and Safety Considerations

In commercialized ceremonies for tourists, dosages tend to be mild. Shamans generally err on the side of caution, preferring to give too little rather than too much, given the linguistic barriers and cultural misunderstandings that complicate communication with foreigners. This is a prudent approach, as adverse reactions can be serious.

### Medical Risks

A critical safety concern involves drug interactions. Most shamans are unfamiliar with Western pharmaceuticals, so tourists bear responsibility for understanding that ayahuasca is a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) and can have dangerous interactions with certain prescription medications, particularly those used to treat AIDS, depression, and psychiatric disorders. One documented case involved an AIDS patient who died during a ceremony in Bolivia despite being sufficiently healthy to dance at the beginning of the service. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and other serotonergic drugs pose especially severe risks when combined with ayahuasca.

## The Ayahuasqueros: Character and Commercialization

Stuart's ethnographic encounters with multiple ayahuasqueros reveal significant variation in practitioner character, ethics, and motivation. This diversity has important implications for tourists seeking authentic experiences.

### Case Studies from the Field

**"B" (Yarina):** An elderly shaman near Pucallpa who hosts nightly ceremonies attracting up to two dozen visitors. Despite his ceremonial role, B was described as a frequent liar, a shoplifter, and someone who solicits money or gifts at every opportunity. His family's religious practice was directed by his son, who was giving workshops in the United States.

**"E" (San Francisco):** Widely regarded as an honorable man in his community. He was observed singing icaros in the darkness of his home during private services for his family. He charged approximately ten dollars to give ayahuasca to a tourist, a modest fee reflecting community-oriented practice.

**"A":** A practitioner whose invitations for rituals were declined after he swindled the ethnographer on the price of a tobacco pipe.

**B's Nephew:** An enterprising shaman earning a couple hundred dollars per month catering to tourists, while his neighbors could not afford a thirty cent taxi ride. He charged thirty dollars to give ayahuasca to a tourist, compared to the going rate of about two dollars for a Peruvian.

### Economic Disparities

The pricing differential between foreign tourists and local Peruvians is substantial. Foreigners may pay fifteen to thirty dollars per ceremony while locals pay around two dollars. For shamans catering to tourists, earnings can reach a couple hundred dollars per month, a significant income in communities where neighbors cannot afford basic taxi fare. This economic incentive creates a powerful commercial dynamic that can distort the traditional shaman-patient relationship.

## Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### Authenticity and Depth

Unless a tourist spends significant time getting to know a practitioner, the character of a commercialized ayahuasca ceremony is likely shallower than a ritual conducted solely for the benefit of the shaman's relatives and community. In commercial settings, participants can observe ritual procedures and enjoy the icaros, but the deeper relational and communal dimensions of the practice may be absent. The ceremony becomes a performance rather than an integrated healing event.

### Christian Syncretism and Hidden Practice

Christian missionaries, particularly Pentecostal, Adventist, and to a lesser extent Catholic, have gained many converts in indigenous communities. While these converts publicly disavow shamanism, some continue to privately consume ayahuasca and maintain native religious practices. This reveals the complex, layered nature of spiritual practice in the region, where indigenous traditions persist beneath the surface of Christian observance.

### Tourism as Industry

Ayahuasca tourism is explicitly a thriving business in Peru. While many shamans undoubtedly come to their profession to help others, tourists should be aware that they will likely be treated as customers. The commercial dimension does not necessarily negate the spiritual value of the experience, but it introduces dynamics of commodification, price negotiation, and service provision that are absent from traditional community-based practice.

## Sustainability and Long-Term Impacts

### Economic Sustainability

Ayahuasca tourism generates significant income for participating communities and individual practitioners. For some shamans, tourist income provides a livelihood far beyond what local economies would otherwise support. However, this creates economic dependencies and may incentivize practitioners to prioritize tourist-facing services over community healing work.

### Cultural Sustainability

The commodification of ceremonial practice raises concerns about cultural dilution. When ceremonies are adapted for foreign audiences with milder doses, translated explanations, and shortened formats, the traditional form and depth of the practice may erode over time. The distinction between authentic community practice and tourist performance can become blurred, potentially undermining the cultural integrity of the tradition.

### Ecological Sustainability

The growing demand for ayahuasca has implications for the plants used in its preparation. Banisteriopsis caapi and Psychotria viridis (or Diplopterys cabrerana) must be harvested from increasingly pressured Amazonian ecosystems. While ayahuasca cultivation is common and the plants are not currently endangered, the expansion of tourism could contribute to broader ecological pressures through supporting infrastructure development in sensitive areas.

### Legal and Regulatory Sustainability

The permissive legal environment in Peru contrasts sharply with the criminalization framework in consuming countries. The isolated imprisonment of American ayahuasquero Alan Shoemaker in Peru for distributing ayahuasca suggests that even in relatively permissive jurisdictions, legal risk exists. As tourism grows, regulatory responses from both source and destination countries may shift, affecting the viability of the practice.

## Critiques and Counterpoints

### The Case for Home Brewing

Stuart provocatively suggests that for those interested primarily in the ayahuasca experience itself rather than cultural immersion, it would be more cost-effective to home-brew a batch with ingredients ordered from an ethnobotanical supplier. With an experienced friend as a sitter, one could have an intense entheogenic experience in the safety and comfort of home or in an isolated natural setting. This do-it-yourself approach could potentially be more enlightening than traveling to South America, though it completely removes the cultural and communal context.

### The Value of Cultural Immersion

Conversely, for those interested in learning about many different aspects of another society, the challenges of navigating Peru's infrastructure can be part of a meaningful cross-cultural experience. The ayahuasca ceremony becomes one element within a broader engagement with Amazonian life, rather than the sole purpose of travel.
