## Permaculture Principle 3: Obtain a Yield

*"You can't work on an empty stomach"*

This principle reminds us that any system should be designed to provide self-reliance by using captured and stored energy effectively to maintain itself and capture more energy. Without immediate and truly useful yields, designs will wither while elements that generate yield will proliferate. A yield functions as a reward through positive feedback that encourages, maintains, and/or replicates the system that generated it.

## Models from Nature

### Competition and Cooperation

All organisms obtain a yield adequate to sustain them; those that fail disappear. Darwin's emphasis on competition reflected Victorian industrial England, but Peter Kropotkin provided a refutation with diverse examples of cooperative and [[fungi-in-the-environment-symbiotic-relationships]]. Competition in nature tests vigor and fitness. Predation removes weaker individuals. In human systems, comfort and excessive protection lead to self-satisfied, lazy, and eventually dysfunctional behavior.

## The Maximum Power Law

### The Engine Analogy

A heat engine converts fuel to mechanical energy. Running free with no load: zero power, zero efficiency. Loaded to stalling: 100% efficiency, zero power. Maximum power is achieved when roughly half the energy converts to mechanical power and half dissipates as waste.

This pattern applies to all biological and self-organizing systems: a balance between mindless energy dissipation and obsessive pursuit of maximum efficiency.

### Consumer vs. Conserver Values

- **Consumer economy** — consuming energy for its own sake (engine running free, no load)
- **Conservation ethic** — maximum efficiency (engine loaded to near-stalling, efficient but not useful)
- **Maximum power** — balance between dissipation and efficiency giving greatest useful output

### Odum's Characteristics of Successful Systems

Systems that maximize power to meet survival needs:
1. Develop storages of high-quality energy
2. Feed back work from storages to increase inflows
3. Recycle materials as needed
4. Organize control mechanisms keeping system adapted and stable
5. Set up exchanges with other systems for special energy needs
6. Contribute useful work to surrounding systems

This list provides abundant scope for ethical and cooperative behavior.

## Positive Feedback

Positive feedback amplifies processes, especially energy harvesting:
- Bushfire heat preheats vegetation → more combustion → massive updrafts → more oxygen
- Plants grow more leaves using solar energy → capture more energy
- Reed growth in billabong slows water → more sedimentation → more reeds

In human society, law, religion, and marketplace distribute rewards encouraging more valued outcomes. In well-adapted systems, positive feedback is the accelerator toward freely available energy; negative feedback is the brake preventing scarcity from overuse.

## Staple Foods and Net Energy

Traditional societies focused energy on staple carbohydrate crops providing clear net energy yield. New Guinea [[swidden-agriculture]] showed very high net energy gains from food gardens, but raising pigs was a net energy loss — yet pigs provided needed protein, social functions, and ecologically functional warfare. This cross-subsidizing of important functions from net energy supply operates at all levels.

### Hardy Self-Reliant Species

Hardy, locally adapted, self-reproducing plants minimize maintenance resources. Their competitive advantage comes from minimal demands, relative efficiency, or associated symbiotic microbes. The obsession with control in gardens limits developing systems less dependent on non-renewable inputs. Farm landscapes where no tree/shrub removal is needed are generally degrading landscapes.

### Increasing Fertility

Using hardy species and increasing fertility are complementary strategies. The area needing highest fertility for food is small — concentrate soil-improving in gardens while adapting to conditions on larger areas. Plants adapted to low fertility generally grow better with higher balanced fertility.

## Timing, Flexibility, and Efficiency Traps

### Timing Is Critical

Natural systems go through growth phases leading to seasonal abundance. Fruit tree yields can all ripen in 1-2 weeks. Flexibility in harvesting, cooking, and preserving is essential.

### Drip-Feed Culture

Weekly wages and shopping are out of step with declining energy realities. The skills of poverty — flexibility and opportunism — have been lost. "No-one is as disadvantaged as the poor without the skills of poverty."

### Just-in-Time Madness

Elimination of materials inventories through just-in-time manufacturing shows extreme pursuit of efficiency at the cost of self-reliance. "Loose systems last longer and work better." Specialization costs flexibility; generalization costs efficiency.

### The Efficiency Trap

Chasing efficiency beyond maximum power is seductive. Commercial farms leave huge quantities unharvested due to market fickleness. In gardens, there are limits — harvesting marble-sized potatoes might happen once but not again. Permaculturalists can become obsessed with collecting more stuff than they can use.

## Accounting and Measurement

### Numeracy

Financial records and accounting skills are important and complementary to observation and design. Money may not adequately measure value, but accounting itself has value. "Accountants are not the enemies of sustainability; they just need appropriate numbers to add up."

### Ecological Footprint

Converts all consumed resources to land area required. Global average: 2.9 hectares/person, but only 2.2 available. US: 12.2 ha/person. Australia: 8.5 ha/person. [[melliodora]]: 3.1 ha/person — one-third Australian average without deprivation.

### EMERGY Accounting

[[howard-odum]]'s methodology — the most holistic but complex environmental accounting system. Uses universal energy laws and energy symbol language.

**EMERGY Yield Ratio:** Compares EMERGY value of a resource with economic feedback required. Value > 1 = net gain. Value > 4 = high-value source.

| Biomass Source | Replacement Time (years) | EMERGY Yield Ratio |
|---------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Rainforest timber, Brazil | 300 | 12.00 |
| Spruce | 90 | 4.10 |
| Slash pine, Florida | 25 | 2.40 |
| Eucalypts, Brazil | 7 | 2.20 |
| Radiata pine, NZ | 24 | 2.10 |
| Willow, Sweden | 6 | 1.34 |
| Sugar cane alcohol | 1 | 1.10 |
| Corn | 1 | 1.10 |

**Key insight:** The more work left to nature, the greater the net yield but the longer the time required. Annual crops have ratios barely above 1; 300-year rainforest yields 12.

## Voluntary Frugality

Unlike real poverty (no choices, constant self-comparison), voluntary frugality allows learning to optimize [[mycelial-foraging-resource-allocation]]. [[permaculture]] jacks-of-all-trades develop intuitive grasp of resource allocation patterns. Skills from small-scale self-reliance can overview and direct large-scale systems — Permaculture thinking is one of the most efficient ways to develop these capabilities.

## The Problems of Success

### Rebound Effect

Design changes saving energy tend to be spent in other sectors, further stimulating demand. Energy-efficient cars get driven further; [[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] house savings fund European airfares.

### Co-optation

Creative, ethically driven people in large organizations tend to be co-opted. Quality of change is traded for quantity of influence.

### Solutions

- Progressive holistic approach — when savings feed into local organic produce or [[ethical-investment-and-earth-banking]], rebound reduces
- Large-scale systems need only lose a small proportion of sales before reducing production
- Second-hand goods dilute demand for new goods

## Social Yield

If relationships are based only on emotional benefits without practical concrete yields, they're hard to sustain. Depending on family and friends for real needs (house maintenance, food supply) makes conflict resolution more likely. Permaculture is "remedial wholistics" necessary because of generations of industrial affluence.

## Key Takeaways

1. Maximum power is the balance between mindless dissipation and obsessive efficiency
2. Positive feedback accelerates systems toward energy; negative feedback prevents scarcity
3. Cross-subsidizing important functions from net energy supply operates at all system levels
4. Hardy self-reliant species should be prioritized in farm landscapes
5. Timing and flexibility matter more than efficiency in energy descent
6. EMERGY yield ratios show long-rotation natural forests provide vastly more net value than annual crops
7. Voluntary frugality develops intuitive resource allocation skills transferable to large systems
8. The rebound effect means efficiency savings must be directed toward low-energy alternatives
9. Practical interdependence in relationships sustains them better than emotional bonds alone
10. Permaculture thinking and practice efficiently develop skills to direct large systems toward humane ends
