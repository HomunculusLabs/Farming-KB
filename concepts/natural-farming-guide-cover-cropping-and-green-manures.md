# Cover Cropping and Green Manures

Cover crops and green manures are essential tools in natural farming for protecting and improving soil between main crop cycles. They prevent erosion, fix nitrogen, suppress weeds, and add organic matter when turned under or mulched. Any soil, not just bad soil, should be improved by planting cover and green manure crops or adding composted animal manure.

## The Role of Cover Crops in Soil Health

Soil that has been cleared most likely needs extra help because minerals have been leaching out. The super soil strategy emphasizes several principles: stop topsoil from flying away or eroding, rehabilitate the soil with pioneer plants before doing anything else, test soil and drainage and choose the right species, use permanent crops and no-till methods, avoid clearing the soil (use mulch instead), do not drain wetlands (which are packed with nutrients), and choose edible plant species for their nutritional value.

## Common Cover Crop Species

### Clover (Trifolium species)

White Clover (T. repens), Bush Clover (Lespedeza bicolor), and Red Clover (T. pretense) are the primary varieties. Clover enjoys partial shade to full sun and is a helpful nitrogen-fixing ground cover. It is also valuable as animal fodder and very hardy to foot traffic. Clover is easy to grow and spreads to form a mat. It can be planted in early spring on new hugelkultur beds to fix nitrogen before planting root vegetables like potatoes, carrots, and radishes.

### Vetch (Vicia species)

Hairy Vetch (V. villosa), Bitter Vetch (Lathyrus linifolius montanus), American Vetch (V. americana), Wood Vetch (V. caroliniana), Tufted Vetch (V. cracca), Sweet Vetch (Hedysarum boreale), and Milk Vetch (Astragalus glycyphyllos). Vetch is often grown with clover and alfalfa as ground cover and sometimes becomes part of a pasture that animals forage in. Despite toxicity concerns for animals eating too much over long periods (causing nervous system disorder), vetch is the most cold-hardy [[hemenway-cover-crops-guide]] available.

### Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum)

Although eaten as a grain, buckwheat is not a grass or cereal. It grows very quickly, ripening in 10-13 weeks, making it great for cooler climates. It does not tolerate frost and should be planted late in the season to ripen in early September. It requires regular watering and soil that is at least loose. Because of its short growing season, it should be double-cropped with another grain such as winter wheat, oats, or flax. Buckwheat serves as bee forage, an edible grain, and a cover crop. Millet should be fed with buckwheat for full animal nutrition.

As a cover crop, buckwheat effectively chokes out weeds. The seeds have a hard outer hull that must be removed before use. To harvest, cut the buckwheat down when it is almost all brown but still has a couple of green leaves or flowers. Cut as you would grain, by mowing and swathing. Thresh, remove chaff, and allow seeds to dry. Use a grain mill on the largest setting to de-hull, running through a few times to crack open all hulls. Sift until all hulls are removed (save hulls for pillows or other projects). The seed can be used whole or ground as flour for noodles and pancakes. It has no gluten and can only be used in small amounts when making bread. If earlier grain crops failed, buckwheat can be planted as an emergency food source.

### Sesbania

A large shrub that serves as a common green manure and animal forage crop worldwide, high in protein. It can tolerate terrible soil, flooding, and short periods of drought. Hardy to light frost, but heavy frost will kill it. Cut when at least 3 feet (1 meter) tall for storage as animal fodder, processed the same way as hay. May not be the best field forage for goats, which can kill it.

### Gliricidia

A leguminous tree that enjoys shade and warm temperatures. Leaves, shoots, and pods provide high-quality protein-filled cattle fodder. The tree is useful for live fencing, firewood, and green manure. It is the second most useful leguminous tree after Leucaena. Grows quickly to 10 feet (3 meters) and is often used as a shade plant for other gardens. The leaves are toxic to horses and other non-cattle livestock despite being excellent for cattle.

### Rye

Very cold hardy and commonly used as a winter cover crop. In no-till grain systems, rye is sown alongside rice and white clover. It is harvested in late spring, and the straw is spread on fields for mulch. If you have more than one plot, spread the oat straw in the rye field and vice versa to maintain soil diversity.

### Fava Beans

An excellent nitrogen fixer. In mild climates, plant in fall for winter growth. At the base of fruit trees, fava beans serve as part of plant guilds. Large pods near the base of the plant can be left on the ground to dry, mulched with straw in late summer, and they will sprout in fall.

### Field Peas

A leguminous green manure that fixes nitrogen. Should be plowed into soil or mulched before flowering, which is when the beneficial nitrogen is used up.

### Barley and Oats

Good winter cover crops in cooler climates. Oat straw makes the most nutritious hay of all and prefers cooler weather. In cold climates where rice cannot grow, spring wheat can be planted in spring with oats or barley as the winter crop.

## When to Plant Cover Crops

Cover crops are typically planted after harvesting main crops, usually in winter. In the ideal Zone 1 garden, a green manure crop is grown in winter and turned over to return precious nutrients to the soil. After harvesting main crops, it is a good idea to plant a cover crop or green manure to protect the soil.

## Managing Green Manures

Leguminous green manures like clover, vetch, and field peas can be plowed into the soil or mulched before they flower, which is when the beneficial nitrogen peaks. Rye, clover, buckwheat, barley, oats, and vetch are all common cover crops that can also be harvested for grain or animal feed.

## Integration with No-Till Grain Systems

Masanobu Fukuoka's no-till method grows grain and legumes together continuously. The four principles of natural farming are: no cultivation, no fertilizer, no weeding, and no pesticides. In this system, rice and white clover grow together. Clover is sown at 1 pound per acre alongside 6-14 pounds of grain seed and 5-10 pounds of rice seed. In early summer, the paddy is flooded for about a week until the clover turns yellow (without killing it). The clover recovers and continues fixing nitrogen while the rice grows.

Seed can be broadcast and covered with straw, or made into seed balls by mixing seeds with mud and pressing through wire mesh, or by wetting seeds and rolling in fine clay dust (potter's clay, compost, and sometimes paper mush).

## Cover Crops in Orchard Systems

Under fruit trees, clover and leguminous plants cover the ground and create nitrogen in the soil. The orchard understory is developed by first establishing nitrogen-fixing leguminous plants such as clover or shrubs, then planting orchard trees among them. These should not be in rows for personal use (rows are for income-generating orchards). Intercropping under trees can include green manure, nitrogen-fixers, forage crops for chickens or sheep, insect-repelling plants, flowers and herbs for bees, or vegetables until the trees get too big.

## Hay Production from Cover Crops

Alfalfa is one of the best hay choices, especially mixed with bromegrass, producing at least 5 tons per acre for 3 cuttings per year. Red clover and timothy mixed hay produces 2.5 tons per yearly harvest but avoids the alfalfa weevil. Millet grows quickly (30 days to feed), produces 5 tons per acre, and must be fed with buckwheat for full nutrition.

To plant hay, broadcast (toss seeds evenly by hand) on snow in February or on frozen ground in March. Cut clovers with a scythe or mower just as they begin to blossom. Let hay dry in the field for about 2 days (rain destroys hay). Rake into windrows. Test by twisting a 2-inch bunch: it should break on the third twist. Load with a pitchfork into a hay wagon and take to the barn.

## Leguminous Trees as Green Manure

Several tree species serve as perennial green manure sources. Leucaena is rated as the most useful nitrogen-fixing legume, native to the Americas with 24 species across various climates. It prefers warm climates and regular watering, does not tolerate frost (though frost will not completely kill it), and is an excellent animal forage and quick-growing shade tree. Seedlings must be protected from weeds and animals, but once established are fairly hardy.

## Cover Crops for Pasture and Forage

The pasture and pens should have trees and shrubs such as pigeon pea, berries, fruits, acacias, clovers, grasses, chicory, comfrey, and dandelions. Animals can be allowed to graze before the grass is harvested for hay or whatever cover crop is growing there. Clover is also listed among the many chicken forage plants that can be planted in pens and pastures. Other chicken forage plants include alfalfa, amaranth, autumn olive, barley, chickweed, fava beans, fennel, fruit and nut trees, honey locust, oak, oats, quinoa, Russian olive, rye, Siberian pea shrub, stinging nettle, sunflower, Swiss chard, vetch, and wheat.

## Nitrogen Fixation and Rhizobium

Certain plants are nitrogen fixers, living in a symbiotic relationship with unique bacteria called rhizobium. Almost all legumes (the pea family) and leguminous trees are nitrogen fixers, as are some other plants like alder. The rhizobium converts nitrogen in the air to a chemical form and releases it into the soil where it becomes usable by plants. Without rhizobium and the nitrogen fixers, nothing on earth would grow. This is why leguminous cover crops like clover, vetch, and field peas are so critical for soil health.

## Cover Crops in Erosion Recovery

Land that has experienced extreme erosion needs gentle treatment. Avoid grazing animals for a long while. Plant crops with deep roots such as daikon radish, chicory, and leguminous trees. Deep-rooted trees pull nutrients from the deepest soil layers, and their leaves can be used as mulch to return those nutrients to the soil. The goal is to cover all exposed soil with quick-growing local species of trees and shrubs to prevent further erosion.

## Cover Crops in Coastal and Salty Soils

In coastal areas, salt-tolerant cover crops are essential. Sea buckthorn, saltbush, and certain clover varieties can tolerate salty conditions. These plants not only protect soil from wind erosion but also help remediate salty soil over time by drawing salts up from lower soil layers. Beach grass is a natural pioneer for sandy coastal soils, though it is not typically used as a green manure.

## Crop Rotation with Cover Crops

A simple three-year rotation using cover crops maintains soil health without synthetic inputs:

Year 1: Plant a heavy-feeding crop (tomatoes, corn) with clover as a living mulch
Year 2: Plant a light-feeding crop (root vegetables, beans) and leave the clover from the previous year to continue fixing nitrogen
Year 3: Plant a grain or grass crop; after harvest, plant a winter cover crop like rye or vetch

This rotation prevents soil depletion, breaks pest and disease cycles, and reduces or eliminates the need for external fertilizer inputs.

## Winter Cover Crop Management

When the last of the summer and fall crops are harvested, the winter cover crop goes in. Rye and vetch are the most common choices for cold climates. In mild climates, fava beans and clover can overwinter. Before spring planting, the cover crop is either mowed and left as mulch (no-till approach) or turned into the soil with a fork (traditional approach). The no-till method is preferred in permaculture as it preserves soil structure and microbial life.
