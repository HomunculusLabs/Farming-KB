# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi Species Characterization

Characterizing species of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi is a meticulous process requiring healthy spore specimens, careful microscopic observation, and experimental verification of mycorrhizal function. The procedures were formalized largely through the work of Joseph B. Morton and colleagues at INVAM (International Culture Collection of Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi), and remain the standard approach for AM fungal taxonomy. Species-level diagnosis depends almost exclusively on spore morphology, as mycorrhizal structures differ only at the family level and above.

## Obtaining Specimens for Description

The first task in describing a species is obtaining healthy, fresh spores with all pertinent morphological characters present and intact. This is a rarity in field soils with a highly active soil microbiota. Spores showing any evidence of parasitism or degradation should be discarded, because species differences in some genera (such as *Glomus*) are small and misidentification of degraded specimens is likely.

Two approaches are used to obtain enough spores for an accurate description:

1. **Seasonal field collection**: Samples collected from a site at different times or seasons, especially just after a period of sustained root growth in the plant community. This takes advantage of natural sporulation cycles.
2. **Trap cultures**: Using field-collected soil, roots, or both to establish pot cultures with a suitable host plant. This approach is often critical for three reasons: (a) providing experimental verification that the fungi sporulating actually form mycorrhizae, (b) obtaining spores with all diagnostic characters intact, and (c) producing inoculum for establishment of monospecific cultures.

A second trap culture cycle—set up by reseeding the host plant into the first pot culture after host-plant shoots have been removed—can double or triple the number of species sporulating. A third cycle can lead to even higher fungal-species richness, although results vary considerably from site to site (Bever et al. 1995; Morton et al. 1995).

## Spore Mounting and Microscopy

After spores are extracted from soil and cleaned, all observable macromorphological and micromorphological features must be described accurately. Spore size and color can be determined from spores stored on filter paper or, preferably, in water where variation due to age, degradation, or parasitism is more discernible. Size is measured with an ocular micrometer, and reflected color should be measured by comparison with a color chart at the same illumination, best accomplished with a dual-beam fiber-optic unit.

### Slide Preparation Protocol

A minimum of 75 spores should be mounted, distributed across three preparation types:

- **One-third** mounted whole in water for natural-state observation
- **One-third** mounted and broken in PVLG (polyvinyl-alcohol-lactic acid-glycerol) and water
- **One-third** mounted and broken in a mixture of PVLG + Melzer's reagent (1:1 v/v)

The number of slides generated depends on how many spores are placed under one coverslip. Generally, no more than 5–10 spores should be mounted together when spores exceed 200 μm in diameter; as many as 20–30 spores can be mounted together when spores are less than 150 μm in diameter. Fewer spores on more slides are recommended for novices.

Slides of whole spores should be examined the day they are made, but slides of broken spores should be stored for at least 72 hours to clear spore contents and allow delineation of subcellular structures.

### Optical Requirements

Most layers of spore cell walls are less than 10 μm thick, and layers of flexible inner walls can be less than 1 μm thick. Inner walls fold on each other, stick together, or separate completely from the spore, or sometimes expand with pressure (Morton 1988). Layers of the spore wall vary in their continuation from the subtending hyphal wall—some are in both, others are unique to the spore wall. Recognition and description of these characters requires a microscope with excellent optics. Standard light microscopes may be adequate with excellent optics, but differential interference contrast (DIC) systems enhance contrast between layer boundaries, making thin transparent structures easy to see.

## Mycorrhizal Structural Characters

While mycorrhizal structures differ only at the family level and are not particularly informative for species description, they are important for experimental evidence of Glomales membership and for defining variation within a suborder or family. They should not be ignored if a fungal isolate can be cultured.

### Arbuscules

Arbuscules are the pivotal site of communication between host and fungus (Smith and Gianinazzi-Pearson 1988). Each is composed of fine, dichotomously branched hyphae developing from a trunk hypha following penetration of a host cortical cell wall. A unique interface forms between the arbuscule cell wall and the host plasmalemma (Gianinazzi 1991).

In species of the suborder **Glomineae**, arbuscule branches appear incrementally thinner than the arbuscule trunk. Those of some *Glomus*, most *Acaulospora*, and *Entrophospora* species stain only faintly in roots and may be hard to detect.

In species of the suborder **Gigasporineae**, the first-order branches decrease abruptly in width from the much thicker arbuscule trunk and typically stain dark in cleared roots. Staining intensity varies among families (darkest to faintest): Gigasporaceae > Glomaceae > Acaulosporaceae. Archaeosporaceae and Paraglomaceae stain very faintly or not at all (Morton and Redecker 2001).

### Intraradical Hyphae

Intraradical hyphae vary considerably in morphology and architecture within Glomales families (Abbott and Gazey 1994):

- ***Glomus***: Relatively straight hyphae ramifying in parallel along the root cortex long axis, often producing characteristic "H" branches resulting in simultaneous growth in two directions (Abbott 1982).
- ***Acaulospora***: More irregularly branched and looped; thin-walled with highly variable staining intensities.
- ***Gigaspora* and *Scutellospora***: Wide hyphae with inflated and constricted regions producing knobs and projections; dense loops or coils in entry cells and many cortical cells.

### Vesicles and Auxiliary Cells

**Vesicles** are produced only by taxa in the suborder Glomineae. These thin-walled saclike intercalary or terminal structures vary in shape by genus: globose to ellipsoidal in *Glomus*, irregularly shaped to lobed in many *Acaulospora* and *Entrophospora* species. Vesicles can develop concurrently with arbuscule formation but often increase in abundance as arbuscular colonization declines. In some *Glomus* species, vesicles become thick-walled and indistinguishable from extraradical spores. The extent to which thin-walled vesicles are infectious remains unclear.

**Auxiliary cells** are formed only by taxa in Gigasporineae. These thin-walled clusters on branching external hyphae have noticeable spiny ornamentations in *Gigaspora* and knobby to almost smooth surfaces in *Scutellospora*. They often form on germination tubes prior to mycorrhiza formation and develop concurrently with early mycorrhizal development. They appear to decline as sporulation progresses. Evidence suggests auxiliary cells are not infective propagules (Biermann and Linderman 1983).

### Extraradical Hyphae

Extraradical hyphae vary in form and function, with form correlating to some extent with function (Friese and Allen 1991). Fine "absorptive" hyphae grow beyond the root zone for nutrient uptake and translocation back to host roots; coarser "runner" hyphae extend along roots and initiate new colonization sites. Hyphal length appears inversely correlated with host root length (Miller et al. 1995).

Infectivity varies with taxonomic group: hyphal fragments of *Glomus* are highly infective, whereas *Gigaspora* species lose all infectivity when mycorrhizal roots are present and all spores have died.

### Spore Formation

Mode of spore formation differs among families and genera. Most species-level diagnostic characters are found in phenotypic properties of spore wall layers (including size and shape). Spores are produced asexually, occurring singly on a sporogenous hypha, in aggregates, or within organized sporocarps. Spores are multinucleate: direct counts range from 2,600 to 3,850 nuclei per spore in *Gigaspora* species (Cooke et al. 1987). Spores germinate in various ways to infect and colonize new roots.

## The Seven-Step Identification Protocol

Because 90% or more of published AM fungal descriptions are vague or incomplete—including many redescribed taxa—and because type specimens are often in poor condition (missing, parasitized, or degraded characters; sometimes morphologically altered by preservatives such as lactophenol), the following protocol is recommended:

1. Establish as close a match as possible between specimen and species description.
2. Note all shared characters and any characters that conflict with the species description.
3. Assign a species name equivocally; prepare permanent vouchers consisting of whole spores preserved in 0.05% sodium azide, broken spores mounted in PVLG alone and PVLG + Melzer's reagent, and photographs or digital files.
4. Contact an experienced taxonomist for verification, especially if the identification is important to an experiment.
5. Seek specimens from living reference cultures of diagnosed species from INVAM or BEG for comparison.
6. If no match exists, designate the organism as undescribed and define it by an accession number.
7. If established in culture, register with INVAM or BEG for maintenance and distribution.

## Microhabitat Documentation

All fungal hyphae contact soil directly, so soil factors strongly impact spore germination, hyphal growth, and nutrient uptake. As many soil parameters as possible should be measured: chemical and physical properties (especially pH and major cation concentrations) are most important.

Host species identity is important for several reasons: knowledge of host provides information about fungal-host association duration (annual vs. perennial), mycorrhizal dependency, tolerance to soil conditions, and root architecture. Anatomical features of the host root significantly influence mycorrhizal appearance (Brundrett and Kendrick 1990). Plant community structure and density around the sample host provide clues about sporulation patterns and density-dependent interactions (Koide et al. 1994). Climatological data (temperature, light, elevation, rainfall, seasonal patterns) influence soil conditions, host physiology, spore germination, and hyphal extension. Microbiological assays for general microbial activity should be carried out when possible.

## Specimen Archiving

### Live Specimens

Viable spores can be extracted from soil, washed 3–5 times, placed on filter paper, and dried slowly. The filter paper is then folded and stored in a Petri dish or glassine envelope. Spores often collapse with desiccation but return to original shape with added water. Both spores and sporocarps can be stored dry at 4°C or room temperature for 1–2 years, though viability differs considerably among species. Propagules in pot cultures can be stored in liquid nitrogen (Douds and Schenck 1990), but success is variable. Pot contents must be dried slowly and without disturbance before transfer to cryovials; propagules exposed to liquid do not survive freezing.

### Preserved Specimens

Whole spores are stored in 2-ml plastic or glass vials with 5% glutaraldehyde, 3.5% formalin, or 0.05% sodium azide. Sodium azide causes the fewest morphological changes; glutaraldehyde causes the greatest. All preservatives fail to prevent deterioration of some features. Older preservatives such as FAA and lactophenol cause major subcellular changes and should be avoided (Morton 1993).

Spores should be broken on slides to different degrees using localized pressure with a narrow blunt-tipped instrument (such as a pencil) to expose inner flexible walls. Slides prepared with PVLG can be stored for 5+ years. Melzer's reagent mixed 1:1 with PVLG provides permanent mounting and histochemical information (the most informative being the staining response to Melzer's reagent).

Roots stained with 0.05% trypan blue, chlorazol black E, or acid fuchsin can be mounted permanently in PVLG-based media. Critical specimens (type material) should be photographed in color or black and white within 2 weeks of preservation.

## Future Directions

Key unresolved questions include:

- **Taxonomic vs. functional diversity**: Whether glomalean fungi grouped by morphology also share genealogically conserved functional characters.
- **Fecundity and mutualism**: Whether the most fecund fungi obtain the greatest resources and confer the greatest benefit to host plants.
- **Molecular tools**: Techniques to detect individual species within roots (Clapp et al. 1995; Di Bonito et al. 1995) and potentially in bulk soil, though efficacy depends on understanding intra-organismal heterogeneity within multinucleate spores.
- **Sampling refinement**: Accounting for patchy spore distributions, autocorrelation of samples, and standardization of techniques for cross-site comparisons.
- **Indicator taxa**: Identifying species sensitive to edaphic and environmental changes for ecosystem health monitoring.
