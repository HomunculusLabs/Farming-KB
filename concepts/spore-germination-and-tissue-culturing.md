## Spore Germination and Tissue Culturing

The two fundamental pathways for starting fungal cultures: germinating spores for
[[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] or cloning tissue for genetic identicality, including detailed
step-by-step procedures for [[cultivator-sterile-technique-and-agar-culture]].

## Two Pathways to Starting Cultures

### Spore Germination

Spore germination produces **genetically variable offspring**. Mushrooms cultured
from spores can be identical to or remarkably different from the parent in shape,
taste, and [[growing-gourmet-fruiting-parameters]]. This makes spore germinations ideal for:

- **Breeding** new strains
- **Creating region-specific** strains adapted to local substrates or climates
- **Selectively culturing** specimens that express desired traits
- **Genetic banking** — spores remain viable for over a year when kept cool and dry

### Tissue Culturing (Cloning)

Cloning involves cutting or peeling a mushroom to reveal clean inner tissue and
removing a small fragment — essentially compressed mycelium. Cloned fungi are
**genetically identical** to their parent mushroom. Cloning is preferred for:

- **Preserving exceptional specimens** (unusual size, substrate, or ecological
  uniqueness)
- **Building strain libraries** — isolating the same species from different climates,
  seasons, or substrates
- **Maintaining proven genetics** for consistent production
- **Rapid propagation** of known performers

## Spore Germination: Culturing from Spores

### Spore Collection

Spores are collected by making a spore prints on aluminum foil or glass slides,
or by using a sterilized tool to lightly rake the gills or pores of a fresh
mushroom. Always take a [[spore-print]] when finding an unusual mushroom — it provides
more genetic material than mycelium alone.

### Agar Plate Preparation

Prepare agar plates with consideration for the target species. Supplementing agar
with a small amount of the mushroom's preferred substrate (powdered wood, soil, etc.)
can significantly improve germination rates, especially for wild or picky species.

### Tools Required

| Tool | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Nitrile or latex gloves | Hand protection and [[cervantes-cannabis-contamination-prevention]] |
| Freshly poured agar plates | Growth medium |
| Scalpel or inoculation loop | Spore transfer |
| Alcohol lamp | Tool sterilization |
| Tape or Parafilm | Plate sealing |
| Ziplock bags | Plate storage |
| Spray bottle with 70% [[isopropyl-alcohol]] | Surface sanitization |
| Spore print on foil or glass (sealed bag) | Spore source |

### Pre-Transfer Preparation

1. Turn on the [[laminar-flow-hood]] **one hour** before starting
2. Sanitize the entire interior of the hood with 70% isopropyl alcohol, multiple
   times
3. Wipe down all tools
4. Bring prepoured petri plates and wipe down their packaging bags
5. Leave the room, shut the door, and allow the [[hepa-filter]] to clean the air
6. After one hour, return, shut the door, and ensure all tools are handy
7. Light the alcohol lamp

### Spore Streaking Step-by-Step

**Step 1 — Organize the work area:**
Arrange materials from sterile (closest to the HEPA filter face) to not sterile
(closest to you). Place agar plates at the inside edge against the filter face where
air is most sterile. Keep the spore print at the front of the work space or just
outside the hood, stepping back to reach it.

**Step 2 — Sterilize the transfer tool:**
Hold the inoculation loop, scalpel, or wire in the alcohol lamp flame until the tip
glows red. Remove from flame, lift a petri plate lid about an inch, and dip the hot
tip into the agar several times until it stops popping from heat and is cool. Close
the lid.

**Step 3 — Collect spores:**
The tool now has wet agar on its tip, which acts as a spore carrier. Scrape the
spore print with the tool tip — spores will stick to the moist agar.

**Step 4 — Streak the plate:**
Lift the petri plate lid at an angle and drag the tool tip through the agar in a
wide **Z pattern**. Close the lid. Keep the lid open for **no more than 3 seconds**
to minimize contamination.

**Step 5 — Seal the plate:**
Wrap the seam with tape or Parafilm to keep the lid in place and prevent
contaminant entry.

**Step 6 — Label and incubate:**
Label with date, agar formula, spore type, and other tracked information. Most fungi
prefer **77°F (25°C)**. Beginners can use a small cabinet with a heating pad.
Incubation is faster in darkness; most species show fuzzy mycelial growth within 7–10
days.

**Step 7 — Monitor for growth:**
Most [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]] is white. Molds appear as circular masses producing powdery
spore rings in brown, green, black, or other colors. If mold appears, discard the
plate or attempt to rescue the desired mycelium.

**Step 8 — Transfer and expand:**
When mycelial growth is symmetrical and radiating outward after several petri-to-
petri transfers, make multiple backup plates for storage and use some to create
grain spawn for cultivation.

### Why Streak in a Z Pattern

Streaking keeps spores in a tight line, helping them find suitable mating partners
nearby. It also allows observation of **sectoring** — different strains (ecotypes)
of the same mushroom separating and diverging on the plate. The healthiest, most
efficient strains surge outward faster than inferior ones that mated but never
thrived on the agar.

## Tissue Culturing: Cloning a Mushroom

### When to Clone

Clone mushrooms that are:
- Growing on a peculiar or unusual substrate
- Exceptionally large or productive
- Ecologically unique or rare
- From different climates, seasons, or growing conditions (for strain library
  building)

### The Science of Cloning

A [[mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis]] is essentially a dense bundle of mycelial tissue that has
taken shape and color according to its species' genetic instructions. By taking a
small piece of the interior tissue — which is pure, compressed mycelium — and placing
it on agar, the mycelium "wakes up" and resumes vegetative growth, expanding
radially across the plate.
