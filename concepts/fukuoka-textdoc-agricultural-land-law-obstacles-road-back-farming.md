# Agricultural Land Law Obstacles and the Road Back to Farming

## Overview

Fukuoka identifies the legal system as the primary structural obstacle preventing people from returning to farming. Japan's Agricultural Land Law, City Planning Law, and National Land Use Law have created a web of restrictions that make farmland inaccessible to non-farmers while artificially inflating land prices. He argues that abolishing unnecessary laws rather than creating new ones is the only path to making land available for those who genuinely wish to farm. This chapter examines the legal barriers, their historical origins, and Fukuoka's vision for a society of quarter-acre farmers.

## The Two Obstacles to Returning to Farming

Fukuoka identifies two fundamental obstacles blocking the return to the land:

**The first obstacle is people — the obstacle within yourself.** Many who profess to love nature and wish to return to farming lack genuine resolve. They love the thought of themselves laboring in fields rather than the earth itself. If you have a genuine love for nature and wish to return to farming, the way will open with great ease. But if your love for nature is superficial and what you do amounts to making use of farming for your own purposes, the road will be closed off to you.

The foremost cause for the discord and contradictions of human society is that everyone acts independently and in their own interest without seeing things clearly. People all claim to love nature, but each pushes his interests without feeling the least contradiction. The painter who sketches mountains appears to love nature, but his real love is sketching. The farmer who works the earth merely loves the thought of himself laboring. The agricultural scientist believes he loves nature, but he really only loves the study of nature.

**The second obstacle is land** — the legal and economic barriers to obtaining farmland. With 120 million people in a small island nation and land prices soaring, purchasing farmland appears next to impossible.

## Enough Land for All: The Arithmetic

Japan has about 15 million acres of farming land, which works out to about a quarter-acre per adult. If Japan's land were divided evenly among 20 million households, this would give each household three-quarters of an acre of farmland plus two and a half acres of mountain and meadow land. With total reliance on natural farming, all it takes to support a household of several people is a quarter-acre.

On this amount of land, one could build a small house, grow grains and vegetables, raise a goat, and even keep several chickens and a hive of bees. If everyone were capable of being content with the life of a quarter-acre farmer, then this would not be impossible to achieve. Everyone has a right and a duty to live their lives within narrow bounds. This is the basic condition for achieving an ideal life.

## The Legal Trap: Agricultural Land Law

Legally, a "farmer" is defined as someone who owns at least one and one-quarter acres of farmland. Non-farmers can neither buy land nor formally lease and work it as tenant farmers. Not one square yard of land that goes by the name of farmland is available for purchase by city people. The Agricultural Land Law has brought a halt to the transfer of farmland.

Unless someone from the city buys up at least one and one-quarter acres at once, he cannot become a farmer. In fact, non-farmers can neither buy land nor formally lease and work it as tenant farmers. But there are always loopholes. If earth is carried onto farmland, or if the land is gradually turned into a lumberyard or planted with flowers and trees, then with time it can be converted into "miscellaneous land" and then sold or built upon.

Even so, in sparsely populated areas, unused land is left abandoned because the use category cannot be changed. The mountains, forests, and wilderness accounting for about eighty percent of Japan's land area are tied up by titles and laws preventing practical use. If even a small portion were freed for agriculture, homesteading would begin immediately.

## The City Planning Law and Housing Land Inflation

The law breaks land up into zones: forests, farmland, housing land. The City Planning Law was enacted, based upon which lines were drawn and farmland divided into areas within urban planning zones, land adjustment zones, and areas outside the lines. Conversion of farmland to housing was prohibited, forcing a sharp reduction in housing land supply that pushed up prices.

A mountain hut or shed used by a woodcutter is permitted. But if someone erects a small house with tatami mats, a lantern, and water pipes, the land must be classified as housing land — requiring a thirteen-foot-wide road and plumbing for tap water and sewage. The prospective homeowner must buy expensive developed land and build a costly house satisfying all standards and codes.

Rises in land prices over the past several years were triggered by massive purchases of land for housing and public use, arising from a perception that land in Japan is a limited resource, and the convergence of people on cities following false rumors of economic growth. The truth is that no matter how much the population grows, there will always be more than enough land to build houses on. Land aplenty exists, but "housing land" has become a life-threatening cancer.

## The Farmland Price Distortion

Until recently, the price of farmland was always stable, remaining fixed at roughly 110 bushels of rice per quarter-acre. Farmers used this as a yardstick whenever they bought and sold land to each other. Prices and taxes on farmland became unjustly high when farmland was assessed on the same scale as housing land by local government — designed to drive farmers off their land through taxes too high to afford with meager earnings.

Support was drummed up among city dwellers with the argument that freeing farmland for housing would reduce prices. But this turned out to be wishful thinking; land released this way never came within the common person's reach. The oases of green left in towns and cities are no longer farmlands and are vanishing from the farmer's reach.

## The Farmer's Traditional Land Wisdom

No one knows more about farmland than farmers. Had things been left in their hands, there would have been no need for any laws. The farmer would have passed land into the hands of his children or grandchildren when the time came. If necessary, he would have resigned himself to the inevitable and passed his fields to his neighbor smoothly and without trouble.

When people can do without a law, it is best not to have that law. Only the barest minimum of laws is needed — to create a world that can get along without laws. Were it necessary to have a single law, it should be: "One shall build one's house at least sixty feet from one's neighbor."

## The "Farming for All" Vision

The "Farming for All" program envisions a return of all people to the garden of God to farm. This would not be a return to primitive society but a way of life in which one constantly reaffirms the source of life — "life" being another name for God. Man must turn away from a world of expansion and extinction, and place his faith in contraction and revival.

The earth is not merely soil, and the blue sky is more than empty space. The earth is the garden of God, and the sky is where He sits. The farmer who chews well the grain harvested from the Lord's garden, and raises his face to the heavens in gratitude, lives the best and most perfect life possible. Farming was the simplest and also the grandest work allowed of man. There was nothing else for him to do and nothing else he should have done.

If people scattered out and built a small house on a quarter-acre wherever they pleased, the food problem would take care of itself, water and sewage lines would not be needed, and pollution would be licked. That is not all — this would also be the quickest path to making our lands a paradise on earth. It is not that land is unavailable. For individuals burning with a desire to farm the open land and willing to learn basic skills, farmland exists everywhere. There is no limit to the places one can live.
