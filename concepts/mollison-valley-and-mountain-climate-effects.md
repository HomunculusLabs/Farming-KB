## High Altitude Effects

Mountains are not strictly "latitude equivalents" because the air is more
rarefied, air pressure is less, and radiation is therefore higher. On
very high mountains of 4000 m and more, people may experience oxygen
deficiency (mountain sickness), snow or radiation blindness, and suffer
from the extremes of day-night temperature fluctuations.

In the Peruvian Andes, day temperatures remain at 16-19 degrees C all
year, but night temperatures fall rapidly to -10 degrees C. Shade
temperatures are lower than at sea level due to the less effective heat
transfer and insulation effects of rarefied air. Water boils at lower
temperatures due to low atmospheric pressure, and snow may sublime
directly to water vapour rather than melting.

### Valley Moisture Patterns
Valleys in tundra and desert support tree populations absent from the
surrounding plains, but the reasons differ. Tundra valleys are likely
protected by driven deep snow cover, which preserves warm or sub-lethal
soil temperatures in winter and provides excess summer melt moisture.
Desert valleys remain moist due to deep detritus filling their floors;
the shaded soils lose less moisture to evaporation, and lethal soil
temperatures are avoided by partial shading.
