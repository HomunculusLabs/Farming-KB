# Valley and Mountain Climate Effects in Design

Mountains, valleys, and altitude create dramatic local climate variations
that the permaculture designer must understand and utilize. Mollison
explains how altitude, slope aspect, valley shape, and daily wind cycles
create a mosaic of microclimates within short distances, offering
opportunities for diverse plantings and settlements.

## Altitude as a Climate Modifier

An average measure of temperature fall with altitude is 9.8 degrees C
per km in rainless or dry air, or 4-9 degrees C per km in humid and
saturated conditions. As a rough approximation, every 100 m of altitude
is equivalent to 1 degree of latitude, so that at 1000 m on the equator,
temperatures are about equivalent to a climate 10 degrees off the
equator with the same humidity.

This altitudinal factor is crucial to design strategies for homes and
gardens. Altitude effect alone enables us to grow a wide range of plant
species on a high island, using the area from ocean to mountain-top. In
the subtropics, even temperate-area plants can be grown on high islands
or hills.

## High Altitude Effects

Mountains are not strictly "latitude equivalents" because the air is more
rarefied, air pressure is less, and radiation is therefore higher. On
very high mountains of 4000 m and more, people may experience oxygen
deficiency (mountain sickness), snow or radiation blindness, and suffer
from the extremes of day-night temperature fluctuations.

In the Peruvian Andes, day temperatures remain at 16-19 degrees C all
year, but night temperatures fall rapidly to -10 degrees C. Shade
temperatures are lower than at sea level due to the less effective heat
transfer and insulation effects of rarefied air. Water boils at lower
temperatures due to low atmospheric pressure, and snow may sublime
directly to water vapour rather than melting.

## Valley Climate Patterns

### Valley Winds
Valley winds follow a daily cycle. Downslope and down-valley winds
occur at dawn as [[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]] drains from upper elevations. Upslope and
up-valley winds develop by day as slopes heat. Many bird species use
these winds to follow a daily migration (downhill at dawn, to ridge
forests at evening).

In large valleys, especially in cool moist climates, the upslope wind
may generate a chain of cumulus clouds at the valley head, trailing off
as a succession of clouds from mid-morning to evening. In more tropical
humid climates, the cloud may be continuously held on the mountain tops,
forming part of the standing cloud of high islands.

### Forest Effects on Valley Climate
Cloud and rainfall effects are accentuated by forest on the valley sides
and ridges, as trees actively humidify the air streams by transpiration
in hot weather. Removing forests from valley sides dramatically alters
local precipitation and temperature patterns.

### Valley Moisture Patterns
Valleys in tundra and desert support tree populations absent from the
surrounding plains, but the reasons differ. Tundra valleys are likely
protected by driven deep snow cover, which preserves warm or sub-lethal
soil temperatures in winter and provides excess summer melt moisture.
Desert valleys remain moist due to deep detritus filling their floors;
the shaded soils lose less moisture to evaporation, and lethal soil
temperatures are avoided by partial shading.

## Slope and Aspect Effects

As great as the effect of altitude is, the effect of slope is even more
pronounced. Slopes of 5 degrees towards the poles "reduce soil
temperatures as much as 168 km distance" towards the poles. Even a
gentle slope away from the sun creates very much cooler conditions
locally. The effect of cold ravines in near-permanent shadow is extreme.

In sun-facing wider valleys sheltered from winds, hot air builds up
rapidly, soils are drier, and strong thermal winds develop. These
contrasting conditions within a single valley create multiple
microclimate zones suitable for different species.

## Snow Cover Dynamics

Snow cover may serve as an insulating blanket, preventing [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]]
thawing or even autumn freezing if it covers unfrozen ground. Snow cover
also causes intense reflection, raising air temperatures just above the
snow by day. At night, radiation from snow causes an extremely cold
ground air layer, so that plants protruding from snow suffer these
extremes of diurnal temperature.

## Design Applications

The designer can exploit altitude and valley effects to:

- Grow temperate species at high elevations in tropical regions.
- Use valley floors for frost-sensitive crops where cold air drainage
  prevents frost.
- Plant moisture-loving species in shaded valley bottoms.
- Place heat-loving species on sun-facing slopes.
- Use daily valley wind patterns for ventilation of settlements.
- Exploit the altitude gradient to grow a wide variety of species on
  a single property.

## Key Principles

1. Altitude changes climate as effectively as latitude -- 100 m equals
  approximately 1 degree of latitude.
2. Cold air drains like water: valley bottoms are frost pockets while
  mid-slopes are thermal belts.
3. Sun-facing slopes create dramatically warmer conditions than shaded
  slopes.
4. Forest cover on valley sides enhances local rainfall and humidity.
5. Snow cover both insulates and creates extreme cold at the surface.
6. A single mountain property can support an extraordinary range of
  species across its altitude gradient.
