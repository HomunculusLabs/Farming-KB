# Rainwater Catchment Systems

Rainwater catchment (also called rainwater harvesting) collects and stores rainwater from roofs and other surfaces for irrigation, livestock watering, and household use. On a homestead, rainwater capture reduces dependence on wells or municipal water, provides a backup water supply, and reduces stormwater runoff.

## System Components

A complete rainwater catchment system consists of:

1.  **Catchment surface** — Roof (metal is ideal), greenhouse, or purpose-built collection surface
2.  **Conveyance** — Gutters and downspouts to direct water to storage
3.  **Pre-filter / First flush diverter** — Removes debris and the initial contaminated runoff
4.  **Storage tank** — Primary water storage vessel
5.  **Distribution** — Pump, gravity feed, or hose to deliver water where needed
6.  **Overflow** — Directs excess water away from foundation and toward useful purposes

## Roof Catchment

The roof is the primary catchment surface for most systems. Considerations:

-  **Metal roofing** — Ideal. Smooth surface, no absorption, no chemical leaching, long-lasting.
-  **Asphalt shingles** — Acceptable for irrigation only. Can leach petroleum compounds and granules. Not recommended for drinking water.
-  **Clay/concrete tile** — Good catchment surface but porous — absorbs some water. First flush is critical.
-  **Treated wood shake** — Poor choice. Absorbs significant water and can leach preservatives.
-  **Collection efficiency** — Metal roofs capture 90-95% of rainfall. Asphalt shingles capture 75-85%.

### Gutter Sizing

Gutters must handle peak rainfall intensity. For every 100 square feet of roof area, you need approximately 1 square inch of gutter cross-section. Standard 5-inch K-style gutters handle most residential roofs adequately. Ensure gutters slope 1-2% toward downspouts for proper drainage.

## First Flush Diverter

The first flush diverter captures the initial rainfall that washes contaminants off the roof surface. This "first flush" contains dust, pollen, bird droppings, and accumulated debris.

### How It Works

1.  Rain enters the diverter pipe from the downspout
2.  A floating ball rises as the diverter fills
3.  Once full, the ball seals the diverter pipe
4.  Clean water then flows past the diverter into the storage tank
5.  A slow-release valve at the bottom drains the diverter between rain events

### Sizing

A general rule: capture the first 0.5 mm (0.02 inches) of rainfall per square meter of roof. For a 100 sq m roof, this means diverting approximately 50 liters. Commercial first flush diverters are available in various sizes, or DIY versions can be built from PVC pipe and fittings.

### Alternative: Leaf Eater Screens

Mesh screens installed in downspouts catch leaves and large debris. Use in combination with a first flush diverter for best results. Clean screens after every significant rainfall.

## Tank Sizing

Tank size depends on roof area, rainfall, and water demand. Basic calculation:

    Catchment (liters) = Roof area (m2) x Annual rainfall (mm) x Runoff coefficient (0.8-0.9 for metal roofs)

For example, a 150 m2 metal roof in an area receiving 1000 mm annual rainfall:
    150 x 1000 x 0.85 = 127,500 liters annual potential

Tank sizing strategies:

-  **Dry season demand** — Size tank to carry you through the longest dry period. If your garden needs 200 liters/day and the dry season is 60 days: 200 x 60 = 12,000 liters minimum.
-  **Monthly rule of thumb** — 1 mm of rain on 1 m2 of roof = 1 liter of water. A 100 m2 roof in a month with 50 mm rainfall collects 5,000 liters.
-  **Common homestead sizes** — 1,000-5,000 liters for garden irrigation supplement; 10,000-50,000 liters for primary water supply.

### Tank Types

-  **Polyethylene (poly tanks)** — Most common. Lightweight, UV-stable, available in 200-25,000+ liter sizes. 15-25 year lifespan. Cost: $0.50-1.50 per liter.
-  **Metal (galvanized steel)** — Durable, fire-resistant, available in large sizes (up to 100,000+ liters). Liner required. Cost: $0.30-1.00 per liter.
-  **Concrete** — Extremely durable, 50+ year lifespan. Can be poured in place or purchased pre-cast. Heavy — not movable. Cost: $0.20-0.80 per liter.
-  **Ferro-cement** — DIY-friendly. Wire mesh and cement mortar over a frame. Very strong, inexpensive for large volumes. Labor-intensive.
-  **IBCs (Intermediate Bulk Containers)** — 1,000 liter food-grade plastic cubes in metal cages. Excellent budget option at $50-100 each. Stack up to 3 high. Modify with bulkhead fittings for connection.

## Filtration

The level of filtration depends on end use:

-  **Irrigation** — First flush diverter and leaf screen usually sufficient
-  **Livestock watering** — Add a 100-micron sediment filter before the trough
-  **Household non-potable** (toilet flushing, laundry) — Sediment filter plus carbon filter
-  **Drinking water** — Sediment filter, carbon filter, and UV sterilization or boiling. Test water quality regularly. Local regulations may require additional treatment.

## Pump Systems

### Gravity Feed

If the tank is elevated 3+ meters above the point of use, gravity provides 0.3 bar (4.3 psi) of pressure — adequate for drip irrigation but not sufficient for hose-end sprayers or pressure-demand fixtures. A 10-meter elevation provides 1 bar (14.5 psi).

### Pump Options

-  **Submersible pump** — Sits inside the tank. Quiet, efficient, and self-priming. Best for below-grade or ground-level tanks. Connect to standard garden hose or irrigation tubing.
-  **Surface pump** — Sits outside the tank, draws water through an intake hose. Easier to maintain. Must be primed before use.
-  **Booster pump** — Increases pressure from gravity-fed systems. Useful when tank elevation is marginal.
-  **Solar-powered pump** — Runs on photovoltaic panels. Ideal for off-grid homesteads. Pumps only during daylight hours unless paired with battery storage.

## System Maintenance

- Clean gutters twice yearly (spring and fall, more if trees are nearby)
- Empty and clean first flush diverter monthly during rainy season
- Check tank screens and overflow outlets regularly
- Inspect tank for sediment accumulation annually — drain and clean if needed
- Winterize in freezing climates: drain pipes, disconnect pumps, or heat-trace critical components
- Test water quality annually if used for livestock or household purposes

## Integration with Homestead Systems

Rainwater catchment complements multiple homestead systems:

- Supplement [[drip-irrigation-basics]] and [[passive-irrigation-systems]] with harvested rainwater
- Top off [[aquaponics-fish-species]] tanks and [[backyard-pond-building]] during dry periods
- Provide emergency water supply for livestock in [[chickshaw-and-poultry-tractors|poultry]] operations
- Reduce load on well pumps, extending well life in drought-prone areas

Rainwater is naturally soft (low mineral content) and free of chlorine, making it superior to municipal water for irrigating gardens and filling aquaponic systems.
