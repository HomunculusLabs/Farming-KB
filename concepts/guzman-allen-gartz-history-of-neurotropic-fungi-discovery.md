# History of Neurotropic Fungi Discovery

The study of neurotropic (hallucinogenic, psychotropic, magic, sacred, psychedelic
or entheogenic) fungi spans centuries, from indigenous ceremonial use to modern
scientific investigation. The worldwide distribution paper by Guzmán, Allen and
Gartz (1998/2000) provides a comprehensive historical framework for understanding
how knowledge of these organisms evolved through five distinct periods.

## Early Indigenous Use (Pre-Scientific Period)

The earliest use of neurotropic fungi dates to ancient peoples across multiple
continents. In Mesoamerica, the Aztec and other indigenous groups used
teonanácatl ("divine mushroom"), first documented by Sahagún (1569-1582). In
Siberia, the Chukchee and Koryak peoples used [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]],
[[psilocybe-zapotecorum]], respectively.

## Schultes and the Initial Scientific Inquiry

Richard [[richard-evans-schultes-updated]] (1939) was among the first modern scientists to study
Mexican neurotropic fungi. He initially concluded that the hallucinogenic
mushrooms of Mexico belonged only to Panaeolus campanulatus var. sphinctrinus,
a position later followed by Singer (1949). This early identification proved
incomplete, as the true diversity of neurotropic fungi in Mexico was far
greater than either researcher initially recognized. Schultes's pioneering
work nonetheless opened the door to systematic investigation and set the stage
for the transformative discoveries of the 1950s.

## Wasson, Heim, and the Rediscovery Era (1950s)

The watershed moment in neurotropic fungi research came with the studies of R.
Gordon Wasson, Roger Heim, and Rolf Singer between 1954 and 1958. Wasson and
Wasson (1957) brought international attention to the sacred [[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]]
of Mexico's indigenous peoples. Wasson also highlighted [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]] as a
sacred fungus among Siberian peoples. Roger Heim conducted extensive taxonomic
work, formally describing many new species, while Singer contributed
significant mycological expertise. Their collaborative publications, including
the landmark Les Champignons Hallucinogènes du Mexique (Heim & Wasson, 1958),
established the scientific foundation for all subsequent research.

## Impact on Mexican Mycology

Guzmán (1990a, 1990b) divided the development of Mexican mycology into two
periods: before and after the Wasson-Heim-Singer studies of 1954-1958. The
attention generated by these specialists' work on hallucinogenic fungi produced
widespread interest in studying all fungi in Mexico, transforming the country's
mycological landscape. The focus on neurotropic species served as a catalyst
for broader [[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]] research across the nation and throughout
Latin America.

## Expansion and Periods of Study

Stamets (1978, 1996) proposed four historical periods in the study of
neurotropic fungi, which Guzmán, Allen and Gartz expanded to five:

1. **Ancient use** by indigenous peoples in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Mesoamerica,
   Siberia, and New Guinea.
2. **Uncertain or erroneous studies** in the early 20th century, typified by
   Schultes's initial identification and Singer's incomplete early work.
3. **Scientific investigation** beginning with Wasson's studies in the 1950s,
   building on Schultes's foundation.
4. **Recreational use period** in the 1960s, involving degeneration of
   traditional practices and the imposition of legal restrictions by many
   governments worldwide.
5. **Recapitulation period** (current), involving renewed description of new
   species, refined chemical analysis, and conservation of indigenous
   knowledge.

## From Twenty to Over Two Hundred Species

In the late 1950s, only about 20 species of neurotropic fungi were recognized,
belonging to genera Psilocybe (the majority), Conocybe, Stropharia, Panaeolus,
Cordyceps, Claviceps, and Amanita. By the time of Guzmán, Allen and Gartz's
comprehensive 1998/2000 publication, 216 species were documented across a far
wider range of genera, reflecting decades of taxonomic refinement, chemical
analysis, and field exploration. This exponential growth underscores how much
remained unknown during the early periods and how much potential diversity may
still await discovery.

## Legacy and Continuing Research

The collaborative framework established by Schultes, Wasson, Heim, Singer,
and later Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz continues to guide modern research. Their
work demonstrated that neurotropic fungi are not merely curiosities but
represent a significant component of global fungal biodiversity with deep
cultural roots. The historical progression from indigenous knowledge through
scientific rediscovery to modern systematic study provides a model for
ethnobotanical research across disciplines. As noted in
[[guzman-allen-gartz-global-psilocybe-biogeography]], the uneven exploration of many tropical and temperate regions
suggests that the full diversity of neurotropic fungi has yet to be documented.
