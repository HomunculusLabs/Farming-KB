# Dr. Cho Han Kyu

Dr. Cho Han Kyu (also known as Cho Han-kyu) was a South Korean agricultural scientist and the founder of **Global [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] (GNF)**, a comprehensive system of sustainable agriculture that has spread to more than 15 countries worldwide. He is widely regarded as one of the most influential post-modern agriculture thinkers, ranked alongside Masanobu Fukuoka, Rudolf Steiner, and Bill Mollison for his contributions to [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy-and-practice]] and practice.

## Biography

Dr. Cho was born in the 1930s in South Korea and grew up during a period of rapid agricultural modernization following the Korean War. He trained as an agricultural scientist, but his observations of the ecological and social costs of the Green Revolution — soil degradation, farmer dependency on expensive chemical inputs, declining crop resilience, and the deterioration of rural communities — led him to question the dominant paradigm of industrial agriculture from an early stage in his career.

In the 1960s, he began developing what would eventually become Global Natural Farming, a system rooted in the observation that healthy soil ecosystems produce healthy crops without the need for synthetic fertilizers or pesticides. He founded the **Janong Natural Farming Institute** in South Korea, which became the primary center for research, training, and dissemination of his methods. The institute served as both a working farm and an educational facility where farmers could learn natural farming techniques through direct hands-on experience.

Dr. Cho continued his work into the 2010s, dedicating more than five decades to refining and teaching his natural farming system. His influence extended well beyond South Korea, with practitioners and institutions adopting his methods across Asia, the Pacific, Africa, and the Americas. Throughout his career, he trained thousands of farmers directly and authored numerous publications that codified his techniques for wider audiences. He was known for his tireless energy and his commitment to making natural farming knowledge freely available to all who sought it.

## Early Influences

Several formative experiences shaped Dr. Cho's thinking about agriculture. Growing up in rural South Korea, he was exposed to traditional Korean farming practices that had sustained communities for centuries without chemical inputs. These traditional methods — including composting, crop rotation, and the use of fermented plant materials — provided a foundation that he would later systematize and refine through scientific observation.

His formal training as an agricultural scientist gave him the tools to understand the biological and chemical processes underlying these traditional practices. Rather than dismissing traditional knowledge as unscientific, Dr. Cho approached it with curiosity and rigor, seeking to understand *why* these methods worked and how they could be improved. This combination of traditional wisdom and scientific methodology became a hallmark of his approach.

He was also deeply influenced by his observations of the negative consequences of the Green Revolution in South Korea. As [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] and pesticides were widely adopted in the 1950s and 1960s, he witnessed firsthand the decline of soil health, the loss of biodiversity, and the increasing economic vulnerability of farmers who became dependent on purchased inputs. Many farmers who initially saw higher yields from chemical inputs soon found their soils depleted and their debts mounting, trapped in a cycle of escalating input costs. These observations strengthened his resolve to develop a viable alternative that would restore both ecological health and economic independence to farming communities.

## Development of Global Natural Farming

Dr. Cho's system emerged from decades of careful observation of natural ecosystems and traditional farming practices. He spent years studying forest floors, undisturbed grasslands, and traditional Korean [[natural-farming-methods-comparison]], seeking to understand how natural landscapes maintain soil fertility and plant health without any human intervention. He then translated these observations into practical farming techniques accessible to ordinary farmers working at any scale.

Central to his approach was the insight that **soil biology** — the community of microorganisms, fungi, and invertebrates living in healthy soil — is the foundation of agricultural productivity. Rather than feeding plants directly with soluble nutrients (as conventional agriculture does), Dr. Cho's system focuses on feeding the soil ecosystem so that it can, in turn, nourish plants through natural biological processes. He often described healthy soil as a living digestive system that processes organic matter into plant-available nutrients.

His methodology is notable for being **low-cost and locally adaptable**. All inputs in the GNF system are made from materials that farmers can source within their own communities — rice straw, fallen leaves, local plants, animal manure, and the microorganisms already present in their environment. This makes the system especially accessible to smallholder farmers who cannot afford expensive commercial inputs, and it eliminates the dependency cycles that characterize conventional chemical agriculture.

Dr. Cho also placed great emphasis on the role of **fermentation** in agriculture. He observed that natural fermentation processes — governed by beneficial microorganisms — are the mechanism by which nutrients in natural systems become available to plants. By harnessing and directing these fermentation processes through controlled techniques, farmers could create potent biological inputs that improve soil health and crop vitality without any synthetic chemicals whatsoever.

## Persecution by the Chemical Lobby

Dr. Cho's advocacy for natural farming methods brought him into direct conflict with powerful interests in South Korea's agricultural sector. The chemical fertilizer and pesticide industry, along with government agencies committed to Green Revolution policies, viewed his work as a direct threat to the established economic and political order. The promotion of chemical inputs was deeply embedded in government policy, agricultural extension services, and corporate profit structures that had developed over decades.

He was **jailed multiple times** for promoting alternatives to chemical agriculture. Government authorities pressured him to stop teaching natural farming, and chemical industry lobbies actively worked to discredit his methods through public campaigns and regulatory obstacles. Farmers who adopted his techniques sometimes faced sanctions or were denied access to government support programs and agricultural subsidies. The persecution was not merely professional disagreement — it was a systematic effort to suppress an approach that challenged entrenched industrial interests worth billions of dollars.

Despite this sustained persecution spanning decades, Dr. Cho refused to abandon his work, continuing to develop and teach his system with remarkable resilience and determination. His perseverance is a testament to his conviction that natural farming was not merely an alternative technique but a necessary correction to the ecological and social damage caused by industrial agriculture. The eventual vindication of his approach — as soil degradation, water pollution, and farmer indebtedness became widely recognized global crises — has cemented his legacy as a visionary who was far ahead of his time.
