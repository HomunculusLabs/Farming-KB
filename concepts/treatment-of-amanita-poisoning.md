# Treatment of Amanita Poisoning

## Overview

The treatment of Amanitine poisoning represents one of the most challenging problems in medical toxicology. As Palmer documented in 1879, physicians throughout history had confessed their utter inability to find an antidote for this poison. The alkaloid acts insidiously — absorbed independently of the physical fungus material — making conventional gastrointestinal decontamination largely ineffective once symptoms begin.

## Why Conventional Remedies Fail

### The Absorption Problem
The fundamental difficulty in treating Amanitine poisoning is that the poison is absorbed through the intestinal villi independently of the mushroom tissue itself. By the time symptoms appear (typically 8-15 hours after ingestion), the system may be fully charged with poison even if every trace of the fungus has been expelled. Palmer noted that a dog poisoned with Amanitine was given vinegar as an antidote, and his symptoms were "fearfully aggravated" — the acid simply aided the system in absorbing more of the toxin.

### Emetics and Cathartics Are Worse Than Useless
By the time Amanitine poisoning is diagnosed, emetics and purgatives may actually worsen the patient's condition. They cause inflammation, cramps, colic, and straining — but these are secondary effects of the already-absorbed poison, not the cause of death. Expelling the physical fungus at this stage does not remove the alkaloid that has already entered the bloodstream and tissues.

Palmer cited cases where patients had been treated with emetics and purgatives until every trace of the mushroom was removed. Stimulants and alleviatives apparently built up the system, but sixty hours later, pre-cordial distress, faintness, coldness of extremities, and other symptoms manifested — proving that in rejecting the fungus, the system had not discharged itself of the poison.

### The Frog Experiment
A few drops of Amanitine injected into the back of a frog produced death within one hour, without leaving on the body the slightest indication of inflammation. This demonstrated that the poison kills through a mechanism entirely distinct from gastrointestinal irritation — it acts systemically on the nervous and muscular systems.

## The Atropine Antidote

### Discovery Through Independent Reasoning
In the October 1876 case, the consulting physician suggested using belladonna — specifically, a few drops of the tincture of belladonna injected hypodermically. He had never heard of Schmiedeberg and Koppe's advocacy of atropine (essentially the same agent). Instead, he arrived at this treatment from a totally independent consideration: watching the effect of the fungus on the patient, and comparing its action with that of belladonna.

### The Frog Reversal Experiment
Schmiedeberg demonstrated a remarkable physiological antagonism between muscarine (the Amanitine from *[[amanita]] muscarius*) and atropine:

- The throbbing heart of a frog is silenced by a single drop of muscarine
- Action is restored by a single drop of atropine
- Animals who have been hypodermically injected with atropine are unharmed by a fatal dose of muscarine

This reciprocal antagonism suggested that atropine could counteract the effects of Amanitine in human patients.

### Clinical Results
The patient in the 1876 case seemed to improve under the influence of belladonna. Although it was not administered until the fourth day of illness, his life was prolonged by twelve hours. Palmer concluded that had the attending physician continued the treatment, the case might not have terminated fatally — unless the system had already absorbed so much Amanitine that completely counteracting it would only have poisoned the patient by atropine.

### The Double-Poison Danger
This caveat is critically important: there exists a point at which the accumulated Amanitine in the system is so great that the dose of atropine required to counteract it would itself be toxic. The physician must balance between insufficient treatment (allowing the Amanitine to kill) and excessive treatment (killing with atropine).

## Recommended Treatment Protocol

Palmer, while not a medical man, outlined the following approach based on his observations:

### Primary: Subcutaneous Atropine Injections
The most promising treatment is subcutaneous injection of atropine (the active principle of *[[atropa-belladonna]]*). This delivers the antidote directly into the system, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract where absorption is unpredictable.

### Secondary: Tobacco
Palmer also suggested tobacco as a potential hypodermic agent. Not being a user of tobacco himself, he based this suggestion on his own experience of its effects in very slight doses and his corresponding knowledge of Amanitine's action. The Solanaceae family (nightshades) — which includes both tobacco and belladonna — furnishes the most trustworthy antidotes.

### Supportive Measures

#### Enemata and Intestinal Lavage
Rather than harsh purgatives, Palmer recommended injections of warm water, soap and water, perhaps mixed with oil of sweet almonds or oil of olives. These relieve tenesmus and assist the bowels in expelling foreign substance in a natural manner, without the violent action of chemical cathartics.

#### Sweet Oil
When the poisoned dog was given sweet oil after the failed vinegar treatment, it vomited the fungus mixed with whitish mucus and, on a milk diet, recovered. Palmer considered sweet oil a valuable supportive treatment.

#### The Vinegar Exception — Before Ingestion Only
Vinegar applied to *Amanitas* before eating can neutralize the alkaloid. In the 1876 case, the sole survivor had put vinegar on the toadstool in her plate before mastication. The acid neutralized or fused with the alkaloid strongly enough to cause only unusual bowel action. However, administering vinegar *after* ingestion is dangerous — it aids absorption of the poison.

## The Importance of Timing

The single most important diagnostic factor is the time between the mushroom meal and the onset of symptoms. The physician should:

1. **Inquire carefully** about the elapsed time
2. **Short delay** (immediate to several hours): indicates acrid or irritant poisoning — treat with emollients and expulsion
3. **Long delay** (eight hours or more): indicates Amanitine poisoning — begin atropine treatment immediately, avoid emetics and cathartics

The longer the delay before symptoms, the more poison has been absorbed, and the worse the prognosis. Every hour matters in Amanitine cases, and the physician must act decisively with subcutaneous injections rather than relying on oral remedies that must pass through the already-compromised digestive tract.

## Animal Sensitivity Differences

Palmer noted that animal susceptibility to fungal poisons varies enormously and cannot be used as a guide for human safety:

- Pigs ate a stew of *Amanitas* without ill effects
- Swine are proof against the poisonous properties of *Amanita muscarius*
- Rabbits swallow unharmed some non-esculent fungi
- Conversely, *[[agaricus-melleus]]* killed a dog in twelve hours
- A cat died in terrible convulsions from *[[marasmius-oreades]]* (an edible mushroom)
- A dog became very sick from biting off the tops of *[[coprinus-comatus]]* (also edible)

This extreme variability in animal responses underscores why human testing and careful species identification remain essential.
