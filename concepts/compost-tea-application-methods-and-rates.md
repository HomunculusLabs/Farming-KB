## Overview

Applying compost tea effectively requires matching the delivery method to the target environment, timing applications to plant growth stages, and using appropriate rates. The method of delivery affects which organisms survive application and reach the intended site. This page covers the practical application strategies for [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) as described by Elaine Ingham and the [[soil-foodweb-institute]].

## Delivery Methods

### Spray Application

Compost tea can be delivered through any system that applies water, including conventional spray equipment. This is the most common method for foliar applications and is also effective for soil drenching when coverage of the soil surface is desired. Spray application delivers organisms directly to leaf surfaces and the upper soil profile.

### Drip Irrigation

Adding compost tea into drip irrigation systems delivers organisms directly to the root zone and to whatever leaf surfaces can be reached by the system. This method is particularly useful for perennial crops and orchards where drip infrastructure is already in place. Beneficial insects are required in the broader system to complement the biology delivered through drip.

### Mulch Application

Mulch helps select for fungi because it creates spatial separation between carbon sources and soil nutrients — conditions that favor fungal growth over bacterial. Mulch also protects organisms from desiccation and UV exposure.

### Plug Transplants

Fill planting trays with 100% aerobic compost containing vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) spores mixed in. Plant bare-root seedlings or seeds directly into these trays to allow root colonization before transplanting into potentially unhealthy field soil. This ensures seedlings have established mycorrhizal partnerships before facing field conditions.

### Soil Cores for Perennials

Perennial plants lacking mycorrhizal colonization need one to four soil cores (depending on tree size) pulled from the root system. The cores are filled with 100% compost containing mycorrhizal spores. Roots grow toward these amended areas in compacted soil, become inoculated, and begin building soil structure from these colonization points. Turf systems may require soil cores punched into the turf to both aerate and establish VAM colonization on grass roots.

### Incorporation

When there is risk that wind or water may remove surface mulch, or in very hot dry climates where compost may desiccate rapidly, incorporation into the soil may be necessary. In extremely compacted soil, incorporation protects organic matter and provides immediate contact between inoculum and soil.

## Feeding Beneficial Organisms

After inoculation, beneficial organisms must be fed to maintain their populations and activity:

- **Cover crops** — provide root exudates and residue
- **Compost** — slow-release food resources for bacteria and fungi
- **Compost tea** — both inoculum and food source
- **[[molasses-and-sugars-in-compost-tea-brewing]]** — bacterial food resources
- **Humic acid and fulvic acid** — fungal food resources
- **Fish hydrolysate/emulsion** — mixed bacterial and fungal food
- **Citric products and garlic oil** — additional food resources
- **Herbs and [[biodynamic-preparations]]** — specialized food sources

## Application Rates

### Compost

- **5 to 30 tons per acre** applied in fall and spring
- Add VAM spores if mycorrhizal colonization is insufficient
- **Crops:** broadcast application (inoculum and non-soluble nutrients)
- **Vines:** under-row application (inoculant, nutrients, soil protection)
- **Trees:** 2-inch application to drip line with vertical mulching

### Compost Tea

- **Foliar:** 5 gallons per acre for each 6 feet of canopy height
- **Soil drench:** 20 gallons per acre, applied in fall and spring

## Timing of Applications

### Annual Crops

Apply at three critical stages:
1. **First true leaf** — establish biology early
2. **Flower bud** — support reproductive growth
3. **Post seed-set** — maintain nutrient cycling through maturation

### Vines and Trees

- **2 weeks before budbreak** — preemptive protection
- **Every 7 to 14 days** if disease is present (active management)
- **Once per month** if no disease is present and no disease history exists (maintenance)
- Use appropriate fungal/bacterial understory plants to help support root zone biology

## Sticker-Spreaders for Foliar Application

Beneficial organisms applied to leaf surfaces need help adhering. Without sticker-spreaders, organisms can be washed off by rain or irrigation before they establish. Several options are effective:

- **Aloe vera** — natural polysaccharides help organisms stick to leaf surfaces
- **Saponin and Nu-Film** — commercial sticker-spreader products
- **Unsulphured molasses** — provides both adhesion and bacterial food
- **Bacteria and fungi** — if the tea has good organism growth, many will naturally adhere to leaf surfaces without additional help
- **Fish hydrolysates** — useful but must be applied carefully to avoid phytotoxicity at high concentrations

### Avoid These Products

- **Dormant oils** — not compatible with living compost tea organisms
- **Sulphured molasses** — sulphur can be detrimental to beneficial biology

### Rain Considerations

Rain within several hours of application can wash organisms off leaf surfaces. Plan applications when dry weather is expected for at least 6-12 hours after spraying. If rain does wash off the tea, reapplication will be needed.

## Leaf Coverage Requirements

Complete leaf coverage is the goal, but it does not require high magnification to verify. The key is ensuring that sufficient organisms reach the leaf surface to occupy infection sites and out-compete potential pathogens. Minimum coverage thresholds should be met across all treated surfaces.

## Temperature Effects on Application

Temperature significantly affects both organism survival and reproduction rates:

- **Optimal application temperatures** vary by crop and organism type
- **Fungi** have different temperature preferences than bacteria — fungal-dominated teas may perform differently depending on ambient temperature
- **Rates of reproduction slow** when temperatures are colder, meaning organisms establish more slowly in cool conditions
- **Avoid application during extreme heat** — high temperatures can desiccate organisms on leaf surfaces before they establish

## Reapplication Reasons

Multiple applications of compost tea are often necessary because:

- Organisms may not survive long enough on leaf surfaces to provide lasting protection
- Competition from existing [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]] may limit establishment
- Environmental stress (heat, cold, UV) reduces organism viability
- Nutrient cycling organisms need to be replenished as they are consumed by higher trophic levels
- In degraded soils, it takes multiple inoculations to shift the biological balance

## Monitoring

Soil samples should be sent to SFI Labs periodically to monitor the foodweb. This allows assessment of whether the biology is establishing properly, whether organism ratios are balanced, and whether additional inoculation or feeding is needed. A simple testing protocol can track progress over time and guide adjustments to [[ingham-compost-tea-application-rates-and-timing]] timing.

## Important Considerations

- Application timing should align with plant phenology for maximum benefit
- Avoid spraying during blossom to prevent interference with pollination
- Multiple applications are typically needed to shift the soil biology from degraded to healthy, especially in fields with a history of chemical inputs
- The goal is to establish self-sustaining biological communities, after which application frequency can be reduced to maintenance levels
- Compost tea is not a magical cure-all — it works within the context of overall soil health management including proper tillage, cover cropping, and avoidance of detrimental chemicals
- Pesticide use can kill the beneficial organisms that compost tea introduces, undermining the entire program
- Always test the first batch of tea from any new compost source to verify organism levels before scaling up application
