# Rainwater Harvesting
# Permaculture Designers Manual - Water Conservation and Storage

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Rainwater harvesting is a fundamental permaculture strategy for water self-reliance. The Designers Manual emphasizes that the only long-term insurance of good water supply to a settlement is by rigorous control of a forested catchment. As there are few such clean areas left in the world, house roof tanks must serve for the foreseeable future. The manual outlines comprehensive strategies for capturing, storing, and distributing rainwater at scales from individual households to entire communities.

The aims of water catchment design are summarized as:
- To store fresh water for cooking and drinking
- To divert sheet flow and wastewater to gardens
- To infiltrate water into soils, and to produce plant growth on that site
- To give rainwater runoff time to soak into the landscape

## Roof Catchment Systems

### The Case for Roof Water

Every family roof should catch its own drinking water in tanks of from 20,000-50,000 litres (5,000-12,000 gallons), depending on family size and regularity of general supply. Only 1cm of rain produces 1,000 litres for a roof of 100 square metres, or 100,000 litres per hectare of sealed runoff.

### Tank Design and Construction

Domestic tanks are built at about 20,000 litres (up to 100,000 litres for small settlements). Any multiple of these tanks can be built of concrete, using a central vertical sliding form in which to pour the concrete. It is easy enough to scale up or down from these figures when estimating tank or swale capacity needed for dwellings.

### Tank Siting

Tanks can stand on or above the soil surface, and are fully sealed and usually roofed. Key siting principles:
- Sites are usually chosen above houses or settlements
- An accessory windmill (or solar-powered pump) lifts clean groundwater from wells to tanks in any period of higher demand
- On very flat plains, tanks are raised on a stand with the windmill lifting to the tank for house and stock water
- Large tanks have a roof which is itself a catchment to the tank

### Roof Types for Catchment

The cheapest roof for water catchment is a bare rock slab in granite, or a natural sealed cap on hills, around which a gutter (rock-cut or cemented) runs to storage cisterns or tanks. Other options:
- Metal roofing with gutter systems
- Concrete or tiled roofs
- In some areas, hill slopes sealed with concrete, wax, bitumen, or plastic to create "ironclad" [[water-catchment-permaculture]] areas

For larger-scale water storage, the Designers Manual integrates rainwater harvesting with [[permaculture-designers-manual-dam-construction]] and [[earthworks-for-water-management]].
- Road surfaces as large compacted collection areas

### Public Cisterns

Many dryland settlements in Australia have public cisterns. These are used for domestic or agricultural water, depending on the quality of runoff. In a few sensible towns:
- All roof areas are run by public pipeways to large storage tanks
- All road runoff goes to reservoirs for use on public gardens
- Other settlements run all road water to [[permaculture-designers-manual-swale-design-detailed]] where trees and fruit crops are growing

## Swale-Based Rainwater Harvesting

### Swale Design

The cheapest water storages for trees are in permeable earth swales (of any shape) alongside roads. A swale is a dead-level hollow built on contour (although the base need not be level) which creates temporary pools. Swales are:
- Ripped, gravelled, sanded, and planted at the base to assist rapid water infiltration
- Sized to take all tank or storage overflow, pavement runoff, and additional harvested overland water flow
- May need capacity of 200% of village runoff, which then infiltrates locally as soil water storage to 3-5m down

### Swale Performance

- Water penetrated soils to 6m (19 feet) deep after a few years of operation
- Swales become self-shaded after 3-4 years of tree growth
- In Hawaii and central Australia, swales produced fast growth in trees in volcanic cinder and sandy soils
- In Australian drylands, swales consistently grew larger and healthier water runoff-fed trees than open plantings

### Interswale Spacing

The distance between swales (the runoff or mulch-planted surface) can be from three to twenty times the average swale width (depending on rainfall). Given a useful swale base of 1-2m (4-6 feet):
- Interswale space of 3m (12 feet): rainfall exceeds 127cm (50 inches)
- Interswale space of 18m (60 feet): rainfall is 25cm (10 inches) or less

## Domestic Water Conservation

### Water-Saving Strategies

Every roof tank overflow, and some greywater wastes can be led to swales (if boron detergents are not used). Additional conservation measures:
- Using the least possible water in gardens (shrubberies instead of lawns, sub-surface trickle irrigation, mulch, and shade) - saves about 50% total water use
- Saving water on toilet flushing (hand-basin and raised-floor shower water fed to low-head, low-flush cistern) - saves 40% of remaining water
- Bathwater diverted to pre-soak trough for clothes
- Laundry water diverted to garden
- Water-saving shower heads and timer taps (save 14-20%)

### Simple Systems

Much simpler but still quite hygienic systems use:
- Rope and pulley showers
- Basins for hands and laundry (often under a lean-to)
- Earth-closet toilets
- These systems work well and save a great deal of water, usually a fraction of that caught on roof areas

### Household Rationing

Two approaches to water conservation in homes:
1. **Strict ration**: Fit float valves to all home roof tanks (of fixed dimension), and pump them full only once a week
2. **Metered use**: Strictly meter use with a sliding increase scale on excess use, so waste costs much more than conservation

## Rock Slab Catchment

Even in very low rainfalls of 10cm or so, large rock slab or slope runoff surfaces will supply all the drinking water that we have tanks to store it in. A rooted cistern can store rock slab water for houses or wildlife. Some towns create an "ironclad" area by rooting or concreting on hillslopes.

## Water Quality

The 30-40 additives commonly introduced into water supplies are often pollutants in themselves to that increasingly sensitive sector of society developing allergies to modern pollutants. These additives represent the end point of the technological fix: pollution is "fixed" by further pollution. For these reasons, roof catchment of clean rainwater remains the preferred drinking water source.
