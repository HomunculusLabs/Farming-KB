# Tassili-n-Ajjer and the Saharan Eden

The Tassili-n-Ajjer is a vast plateau in southern Algeria featuring an extraordinary concentration of Neolithic rock art that provides what McKenna considered the strongest archaeological evidence for his theory of a prehistoric [[mckenna-dominator-vs-partnership-society-model]].

He proposed that the Tassili was the historical Eden whose loss gave rise to humanity's persistent mythological nostalgia for paradise.

The rock art documents a culture centered on cattle cults, Goddess worship, and the ritual use of [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] that flourished in the Sahara between 9,000 and 20,000 years ago.

## Geological Setting

The Tassili-n-Ajjer is a curious geological formation in the Sahara Desert, resembling a vast badlands of stone escarpments cut by wind into many perpendicular narrow corridors.

Aerial photographs give the eerie impression of an abandoned city.

During the period when its rock paintings were created, the Sahara was significantly wetter than today, supporting extensive grasslands, abundant wildlife, and the pastoral human communities that produced the art.

The eminent geographer Carl Saur felt there was no such thing as a natural grassland, suggesting all grasslands were human artifacts resulting from the cumulative impact of seasonal burning by fire-using populations.

## The Rock Paintings

### Mushroom Shamans

The most significant paintings from the perspective of McKenna's thesis depict dancing shamans with fists full of mushrooms and mushrooms sprouting from their bodies.

At sites including Matalen-Amazar and Ti-n-Tazarift, the figures clearly hold mushrooms in their hands and have them emerging from their bodies.

In one notable instance, the bee-faced mushroom shaman of Tassili-n-Ajjer runs joyfully surrounded by geometric structures that appear to represent the visual phenomena of hallucinogenic experience.

The pictorial evidence for ritual mushroom use is unambiguous and represents the earliest known depictions of shamans with large numbers of grazing cattle.

Images similar to those of the Tassili occur in pre-Columbian Peruvian textiles, though with the Tassili frescoes the case is far clearer.

### The Round Head Period

The Round Head Period, named for its distinctive style of human figure depiction unknown from any other site, is believed to have begun very early and ended before the seventh millennium B.P.

Henri Lhote estimated it lasted several thousand years, placing its beginning near the start of the ninth millennium.

### The Great Horned Goddess

A painting from Inaouanrhat in the Tassili depicts a dancing woman with outstretched arms and horns extended horizontally on either side of her head, embodying the Great Horned Goddess.

Her discoverers saw a relationship with the Egyptian goddess Isis, mythical protector of [[miniculture-rye-grain-cultivation-method-for-psilocybe-cubensis]].

This image is central to McKenna's argument that a Goddess-worshipping partnership culture flourished in the Sahara long before the rise of Egyptian civilization.

## The Migration Hypothesis

McKenna proposed that the pastoral peoples who produced the Tassili paintings gradually migrated out of Africa over a long period spanning twenty to seven thousand years ago.

The Red Sea was landlocked during much of this time due to lower sea levels, creating land bridges at both ends that African pastoralists used to enter the Fertile Crescent and Asia Minor.

By twelve thousand years ago, the pastoral mode with its cattle cult and Goddess religion was well established across the ancient Near East.

Wherever the pastoral peoples went, their lifestyle went with them.

## The Egyptian Connection

A puzzling feature of the Tassili paintings is their unmistakable Egyptian influence in content and style, despite being created at a time when the Nile valley was nearly deserted.

McKenna concluded that motifs associated with ancient Egypt were first introduced into Egypt by dwellers of the Western Desert.

If correct, this would indicate the central Sahara as the source of what later became the high civilization of pre-Dynastic Egypt.

The implications of this suggestion would fundamentally reshape our understanding of the origins of one of humanity's greatest civilizations.

## The Natufian Culture

In the middle of the tenth millennium B.C., Palestine witnessed the sudden appearance of the remarkably advanced Natufian culture.

Its crescent-moon flints and elegantly naturalistic carved bonework were unrivaled anywhere on the planet.

James Mellaart described a love of art in the Early Natufian that was "worthy of Upper Paleolithic France."

Skeletal evidence from Jericho shows the inhabitants were of Eurafrican stock, fairly robust with long skulls.

The ceramic evidence supports an [[psilocybe-cubensis-cattle-association-african-origin-guzman-1998]]: dark, burnished monochrome pottery known as Sahara-Sudanese ware found at Natufian sites has also been found near the Egyptian-Sudanese border and in the Tassili-n-Ajjer.

The Natufian culture is particularly notable for its intense involvement with plant resources. The emphasis on plant resources allowed for a storable surplus that fundamentally influenced settlement patterns and social organization.

McKenna identified the Natufian as a transitional culture directly linking the Round Head civilization of the Sahara with the later flowering at Qatal Huyuk.

The Palestinian sequence provides "nothing at all convincing for an ancestor" for the Natufian, which "makes its first appearance apparently full-grown with no traceable roots in the past."

## Qatal Huyuk as Culmination

Qatal Huyuk in central Anatolia represents the Neolithic and eastern culmination of the partnership culture that McKenna traced back to the Tassili.

Called "a premature flash of brilliance and complexity" and "an immensely rich and luxurious city," it spread over thirty-two acres and accommodated over seven thousand people at its peak.

Despite less than three percent of the site being excavated, Qatal Huyuk yielded religious art and symbolism "three or four thousand years ahead of its time."

The site featured elaborate shrines with cattle bas-reliefs and heads of now-extinct aurochs covered with ocher designs, directly connecting it to the cattle cult documented in the Saharan rock art.

Mellaart observed that the plant husbandry of Qatal Huyuk must have a long prehistory elsewhere, presumably beginning with the Natufian of Palestine.

## Eden as History

McKenna proposed that the Tassili-n-Ajjer of approximately 12,000 B.C. was the historical Eden, the partnership paradise whose loss created one of humanity's most persistent mythological motifs.

There the partnership society arose and flourished, hunter-gatherer culture slowly gave way to domestication, and psilocybin-containing mushrooms were encountered, consumed, and deified.

Language, poetry, ritual, and thought emerged from the darkness of the hominid mind. Eden was not a myth. For the prehistoric peoples of the high plateau of the Tassili-n-Ajjer, Eden was home.

The Genesis story of expulsion from Eden was interpreted as a cultural memory of successive drought episodes that disrupted the Saharan paradise.

The fruit of the Tree of Knowledge was identified with *[[mckenna-stropharia-cubensis-extraterrestrial-hypothesis-spore-dispersal]]*, the psilocybin mushroom that catalyzed and maintained the Tassili partnership Eden through a religion of boundary-dissolution into the presence of the Great Goddess.

## Twilight-State Thinking

John Pfeiffer's concept of "twilight-state thinking" from his analysis of Upper Paleolithic cave art provides additional support.

Characterized by loss of objectivity, temporal distortion, and mild hallucinations, twilight-state thinking is essentially a gloss for egoless psychedelic arousal.

Pfeiffer argued that the prevalence of this susceptibility "argues for its evolutionary importance" and that "there must have been an enormous selective premium on the [[mckenna-food-gods-twilight-state-thinking]] during prehistoric times."
