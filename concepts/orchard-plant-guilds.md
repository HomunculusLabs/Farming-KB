# Orchard Plant Guilds

Orchard plant guilds are integrated assemblages of beneficial plants and organisms designed to support pome and stone fruit trees in permaculture systems. Drawing from [[symphytum-officinale]]*) — the quintessential permaculture accumulator plant; its deep taproot mines potassium, calcium, and other minerals; leaves can be cut several times per season for nutrient-rich mulch
- **Dandelion** (*Taraxacum officinale*) — dynamic accumulator with a deep taproot that breaks compacted soil; flowers provide early nectar for pollinators
- **Globe artichoke** (*Cynara scolymus*) — edible ornamental with an enormous taproot; produces a substantial food crop in addition to its soil-building function

These spike-root plants provide dense ground cover that suppresses grass, encourage earthworm activity through constant leaf litter deposition, and yield both mulch material and edible crops.

### Insectary Plants

Flowering plants that attract predatory and parasitic insects form the biological pest-management backbone of the orchard guild. The most effective insectary plants belong to two groups: the **Umbelliferae** (now Apiaceae) and small-flowered composite species.

Umbelliferous plants produce flat-topped flower clusters (umbels) that serve as ideal landing platforms for predatory insects. Their small, accessible flowers provide nectar and pollen to tiny parasitic wasps whose offspring attack orchard pests.

Key insectary species:

- **Fennel** (*Foeniculum vulgare*) — tall, long-blooming umbellifer; attracts parasitic wasps and hover flies
- **Dill** (*Anethum graveolens*) — umbellifer that hosts swallowtail butterflies and predatory insects
- **Queen Anne's lace** (*Daucus carota*) — wild carrot; excellent generalist insectary
- **Tansy** (*Tanacetum vulgare*) — repellent properties against certain pests; attracts ladybirds
- **Carrot and parsnip flowers** — when left to bolt, these umbellifers become potent insectary plants

The predatory insects attracted by these plants include:

- **Parasitic wasps** — attack codling moth larvae, aphids, and various caterpillars
- **Robber flies** — generalist aerial predators of flying insects
- **Ladybirds** (ladybugs) — voracious consumers of aphids, scale insects, and mites
- **Jewel beetles** — predators of specific orchard pests
- **Pollinators** — bees, hover flies, and other species that ensure fruit set

### Nitrogen and Nutrient Crops

Nitrogen-fixing plants supply the tree roots with biologically available nitrogen, reducing or eliminating the need for synthetic fertilizers. Other nutrient crops contribute specific soil-improving functions.

- **Clovers** (*Trifolium* spp.) — low-growing legumes that fix nitrogen in the root zone; white clover serves as a living mulch
- **Tagasaste** (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) — fast-growing leguminous shrub that can be coppiced for mulch and nitrogen
- **Acacia** species — nitrogen-fixing trees that also provide windbreak shelter and nectar
- **Crotalaria** (rattlebox) — leguminous cover crop that controls root-knot nematodes through allelopathic compounds
- **Marigolds** (*Tagetes* spp.) — roots release thiophenes that fumigate the soil against nematodes and other soil-borne pathogens

## Pest Reduction Through Biological Strategy

One of the most significant claims in Mollison's framework is that orchard guilds can reduce pest species to **4–7% of the levels found in conventional monoculture orchards**. This dramatic reduction is achieved not through any single intervention but through the synergistic effect of multiple biological strategies:

- **Disease-resistant rootstock** — selecting tree varieties with inherent resistance to common diseases
- **Minimal pruning** — reducing wound entry points for fungal pathogens
- **Predation by natural enemies** — birds, frogs, lizards, and predatory wasps maintained by habitat provision
- **Interplanted leguminous trees** — nitrogen availability increases tree vigor and pest resistance
- **Windbreaks and mulch** — microclimate moderation reduces environmental stress on trees
- **Ground foraging by domestic animals** — chickens, pigs, and geese consume fallen fruit and overwintering pests

## Orchard Layout and Physical Design

The physical layout of a permaculture orchard integrates the guild concept with earthworks and shelter systems:

- **Mound-planted fruits** — trees planted on mounds improve drainage and raise root zones above potential waterlogging
- **Swales above plantings** — water-harvesting earthworks on contour slow, spread, and sink runoff into the root zone
- **Mixed hedgerows** — multi-species shelterbelts surrounding the orchard provide wind protection, additional forage, and wildlife habitat; crops are sheltered between these hedgerows

## Animal Integration in the Orchard

Domestic animals play a complementary role in orchard management when introduced at appropriate densities and timing relative to tree age:

| Animal | Stocking Rate | Function | Timing |
|--------|--------------|----------|--------|
| **Chickens** | 120–240 per hectare | Scavenge windfalls, control codling moth and fruit fly by scratching over pupating larvae in leaf litter | All ages, with care for young trees |
| **Pigs** | Variable | Clean up windfall fruit, till and fertilize soil, consume overwintering pest pupae | Trees aged 3–7 years; removed before root damage risk |
| **Sheep** | Managed grazing | Graze understory vegetation, cycle nutrients via manure | Trees aged 7–20 years |
| **Cattle** | Managed grazing | Larger-scale nutrient cycling and vegetation management | Trees aged 7–20 years, mature enough to withstand rubbing |

The integration of animals must be carefully timed: young trees (under 3 years) are vulnerable to browsing and root damage, so poultry is the only appropriate animal in the earliest years. As trees mature, larger livestock can be introduced for broader ecosystem management.

## Design Principles Summary

Successful orchard plant guilds are built on several interconnected principles derived from Mollison's work:

1. **Eliminate grass** beneath the canopy and replace it with cooperative plant communities
2. **Maximize floral diversity** across the growing season to support predatory insects and pollinators
3. **Stack functions** — every guild member should perform at least two roles (e.g., comfrey as mulch producer, mineral accumulator, and bee forage)
4. **Integrate animals** as biological management tools at appropriate stocking rates and tree ages
5. **Design for succession** — use early-flowering bulbs, summer perennials, and leguminous shrubs in temporal sequence
6. **Reduce external inputs** — the guild's biological functions replace the need for synthetic fertilizers and pesticides
7. **Create habitat complexity** — layered vegetation provides niches for a full spectrum of beneficial organisms

When these principles are applied together, the orchard guild becomes a self-regulating polycultural system that produces fruit, herbs, vegetables, forage, and mulch while maintaining soil health and ecological balance.
