## Stropharia Cubensis as the Original Tree of Knowledge

## Overview

[[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]*), a psilocybin-containing mushroom that grows on
cow dung in tropical and subtropical grasslands, may have been the real
"Tree of Knowledge" referenced in the Genesis account of Eden. This
hypothesis forms a central pillar of McKenna"s argument that
[[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]]
could have produced significant effects on early hominid brain development
and social organization:

- **Low doses** (found in dried specimens or as contaminants in the diet)
  could increase visual acuity, making hunting more effective
- **Moderate doses** could increase sexual arousal and social bonding
- **High doses** could produce visionary experiences, dissolution of ego
  boundaries, and ecstatic states

These effects, McKenna suggested, acted as a "mutation-inducing
influence" that pushed early hominids out of the stream of animal
evolution and into the fast-rising tide of language and culture.

### Language Emergence

McKenna linked psilocybin use directly to the emergence of language. He
argued that the [[allegro-garden-of-eden-and-delight]]** represents the African grassland ecosystem where
  hominids coexisted with [[forbidden-fruit-cannabis]]** represents the conscious ingestion of
  the mushroom, which granted self-awareness and knowledge of good and
  evil (moral consciousness)
- **Expulsion from Eden** represents the transition from mushroom-using
  partnership societies to [[partnership-and-dominator-cultures-in-human-history]] that suppressed the
  original sacrament

### The Fall as Cultural Transformation

The "Fall" in McKenna's framework was not a moral failure but a cultural
catastrophe: the loss of the direct human-mushroom relationship as
climate changed, grasslands contracted, and mushroom-using cultures were
overwhelmed by pastoral and agricultural societies that did not maintain
the original sacramental practice.
