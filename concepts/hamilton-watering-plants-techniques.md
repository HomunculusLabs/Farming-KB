# Watering Plants — Techniques and Best Practice

Plants are entirely dependent on water for their survival. While natural
rainfall cannot be guaranteed, the gardener can supplement rainfall
during dry periods to keep plants healthy and productive. Good watering
practice is not simply about applying water — it is about applying the
right amount at the right time, in the right way, to encourage deep
rooting and efficient water use.

## When to Water

The guiding principle of organic watering is to water thoroughly but
infrequently rather than giving frequent light sprinklings. Light
watering encourages shallow surface roots that are vulnerable to drying
out, whereas a thorough soaking drives water deep into the soil,
encouraging roots to grow downward in search of moisture.

Water in the early morning or evening to minimise evaporation. Watering
during the heat of midday wastes a significant proportion of the water
through evaporation before it reaches the roots. Evening watering is
acceptable for most plants but avoid wetting the foliage of crops prone
to fungal diseases, as damp leaves overnight encourage problems like
[[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] and mildew.

## Watering Different Garden Areas

### The Vegetable Garden

Most vegetables need consistent moisture, especially when the plants
are young and establishing. Seed beds should be kept moist until
germination, after which watering can be reduced to encourage deep
rooting. [[hamilton-root-vegetables-growing]] like
carrots and parsnips need even moisture — fluctuating between wet and
dry causes splitting.

Leafy crops such as lettuce and spinach suffer badly from drought,
becoming tough and prone to bolting. Fruiting vegetables like tomatoes
and [[hamilton-tomato-growing-organic]] need regular water
while the fruit is developing. Inconsistent watering of tomatoes leads
to blossom end rot and splitting fruit.

### Container Plants

Container plants demand the most attention. Only very heavy rain will
provide enough water for a container, so regular watering is essential.
In high summer, containers may need watering twice daily. Always water
thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes — a splash on the
surface is worse than nothing because it encourages surface roots.

Use rainwater where possible, since most tap water contains lime which
can make the compost increasingly alkaline over time. Stand pots on
trays of moist gravel to increase humidity around the plants.

### The Ornamental Garden

Newly planted trees, shrubs, and [[hamilton-perennial-flowers-organic]]
need careful watering in their first growing season while their root
systems establish. Once established, many ornamental plants are
remarkably drought-tolerant, especially if the soil has been improved
with [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]
and protected with [[hamilton-mulching-materials-and-techniques]].

[[hamilton-annual-flowers-organic]] need regular watering
when first planted and during dry spells. [[hamilton-climbing-plants-organic]]
generally need watering only in very dry weather once established,
provided the soil was well prepared at planting time.

### Lawns

A newly sown or turfed lawn is very vulnerable in dry weather. Water
using a lawn sprinkler at regular intervals, applying a fine spray.
Established lawns rarely need watering — they recover quickly from
drought and the water is better used elsewhere in the garden.

## Water Conservation

Mulching is the single most effective water conservation measure. A
layer of [[hamilton-mulching-materials-and-techniques]]
spread over the soil surface dramatically reduces evaporation while
also suppressing weeds that compete for moisture.

Adding plenty of organic matter to the soil improves its water-holding
capacity. Sandy soils drain quickly and dry out fast, but generous
applications of compost or manure help them retain moisture. Even
heavy clay soils benefit, as organic matter opens up the structure
and allows water to penetrate rather than running off the surface.

Choose drought-resistant plants for dry areas and group thirsty plants
together so that watering can be concentrated where it is needed most.
Collect rainwater from greenhouse and shed roofs in water butts — this
free supply is better for plants than treated tap water.

## Watering New Plantings

Newly planted specimens of all types need special attention. When
planting trees and shrubs, fill the planting hole with water and allow
it to drain before placing the plant. After planting, water thoroughly
to settle the soil around the roots. Continue watering regularly through
the first growing season, soaking the root ball thoroughly at each
watering rather than giving frequent light sprinkles.

For bare-root plants, ensure the roots never dry out between purchase
and planting. Heel them into temporary ground or wrap the roots in damp
sacking if planting is delayed.

## Summary

Effective watering is about understanding what each plant needs and
delivering it efficiently. Water deeply and infrequently to encourage
deep rooting, use mulches to conserve moisture, and prioritise
newly planted specimens and container-grown plants. Collect rainwater
and incorporate plenty of organic matter into the soil to maximise
its water-holding capacity. Good watering practice is fundamental to
successful organic gardening, complementing the benefits of
[[hamilton-mulching-materials-and-techniques]],
[[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], and
[[hamilton-soil-drainage-improvement]].
