## Historical Evidence for Flying Ointments

Multiple historical accounts document the use of hallucinogenic ointments by accused witches. The Somerset witches in 1664 used a "greenish" oil in transporting themselves to their meetings. Elizabeth Style testified: "Before they are carried to their meetings, they anoint their Foreheads and Hand-wrists with an Oyl the Spirit brings them (which smells raw)."

Lady Alice Kyteler's case in 1324 revealed "a Pipe of ointment, wherewith she greased a staffe, upon the which she ambled and galloped through thick and thin." The fifteenth-century account from Bergamo describes witches who "anoint a staff and ride on it to the appointed place or anoint themselves under the arms and in other hairy places."

The use of a staff or broom was "undoubtedly more than a symbolic Freudian act, serving as an applicator for the atropine-containing plant to the sensitive vaginal membranes as well as providing the suggestion of riding on a steed, a typical illusion of the witches' ride to the Sabbat."

## The Physician's Testimony

Andrés Laguna, physician to Pope Julius III, provided a remarkable account in 1545. He obtained a jar of green ointment confiscated from accused witches in Lorraine and applied it to the wife of the hangman, who suffered from insomnia. Upon being anointed, she fell into a profound sleep lasting thirty-six hours. When awakened, her first words were: "Why do you wake me at such an inopportune time? I was surrounded by all the pleasures and delights of the world."

Laguna concluded: "All that which the wretched witches do is phantasm caused by very cold potions and unguents: which are of such a nature as to corrupt the memory and the imagination."

## Twentieth-Century Confirmations

Modern experimenters have confirmed the effects of the flying ointments. Karl Kiesewetter made a sample and experienced a dream of flying in spirals. Professor Will-Erich Peukert of Göttingen made a flying ointment of belladonna, henbane, and Datura using a seventeenth-century formula; he and his colleagues fell into a twenty-four hour sleep "in which they dreamed of wild rides, frenzied dancing, and other weird adventures."

Gustav Schenk, inhaling the smoke of burning henbane seeds, reported: "My teeth were clenched, and a dizzy rage took possession of me... a peculiar sense of well-being connected with the crazy sensation that my feet were growing lighter, expanding and breaking loose from my body... I experienced an intoxicating sensation of flying."

Carlos Castaneda, studying under a Yaqui shaman, applied a Datura ointment to his body and reported: "The momentum carried me forward one more step... And from there I soared. I saw the dark sky above me, and the clouds going by me. My speed was extraordinary. I enjoyed such freedom and swiftness as I had never known before."

## Lycanthropy and Hallucinogens

Harner also proposed a hallucinogenic explanation for lycanthropy — the belief that humans can transform into wolves or predatory animals. A psychiatrist colleague administered harmaline to a subject who first believed he was a bird flying through the air, then a fish, then a tiger: "I moved as a tiger in the jungle, joyously, feeling the ground under my feet, feeling my power." The subject had to be physically restrained from attempting to bite the neck of a woman who had appeared to him as a deer.

The clinical symptoms of atropine poisoning — dryness of the throat and mouth, difficulty in swallowing, great thirst, impaired vision, and staggering gait — closely match the classical description of lycanthropy from Paulus Aegineta (fourth-seventh century A.D.), which describes pale victims with feeble vision, dry tongue, stopped saliva flow, thirst, and ulcerated legs from falls.

## The Sabbat

The Sabbat, the nocturnal rendezvous of witches with the Devil and each other, involved orgiastic dancing, feasting, and sexual activities. Harner suggested that the anti-social sexual promiscuity reported at the Sabbat may also have a pharmacological basis in the solanaceous alkaloids, which in low doses are known to have aphrodisiac properties — belladonna was, after all, the "beautiful lady" whose pupils dilated attractively.
