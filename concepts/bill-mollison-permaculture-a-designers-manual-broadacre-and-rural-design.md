# Broadacre and Rural Design

Broadacre and rural design applies permaculture principles to large-scale
farming and ranching operations. Bill Mollison recognized that the majority of
the world's productive land is managed as broadacre agriculture and that
transforming these systems is essential for global ecological sustainability.
In *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, he demonstrated that the same
principles guiding [[permaculture-design]] in small gardens can be
scaled to farms, ranches, and regional landscapes. Broadacre permaculture
integrates [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]], [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]],
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]], and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] into
comprehensive whole-farm designs that are both productive and regenerative.

## Whole Farm Planning

Mollison advocated for whole-farm planning as the starting point for
broadacre permaculture design. Rather than treating each farm enterprise as
an independent operation, the designer considers the entire property as an
integrated system. [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] is applied at farm scale,
with Zone 1 around the homestead, Zone 2 containing intensive crops and
orchards, Zone 3 supporting main crops and pastures, Zone 4 providing
forage and timber, and Zone 5 preserving native ecosystems. Sector analysis
maps water flow, wind patterns, fire risk, and sun exposure across the
entire property. This comprehensive approach ensures that every element
supports every other element.

## Agroforestry Systems

Agroforestry, the integration of trees with crops and livestock, is a
cornerstone of broadacre permaculture. Mollison described several agroforestry
models suitable for large-scale application. Alley cropping plants rows of
timber or nut trees with crops grown between the tree rows. Silvopasture
combines trees with pasture for livestock grazing. Forest farming grows
specialty crops such as mushrooms, ginseng, or ramps under a managed forest
canopy. Riparian buffers of trees and shrubs along waterways protect water
quality while producing harvestable products. These systems increase total
farm productivity per unit area while building soil, sequestering carbon, and
providing habitat for beneficial species.

## Rotational Grazing Systems

Mollison strongly advocated for rotational grazing as an alternative to
continuous set-stocking. In rotational systems, livestock are moved frequently
through a series of paddocks, allowing each paddock an extended rest period
for recovery. High stock density for short durations achieves thorough grazing
of desirable plants, trampling of weeds and old growth, and even distribution
of manure. The rest period allows plants to rebuild root reserves and energy
stores, resulting in more vigorous regrowth. Mollison recommended adapting
rotation speed to season and growth rate, moving faster during rapid spring
growth and slower during drought or dormancy.

## Cell Grazing and Holistic Management

Mollison drew on the work of Allan Savory and others in describing intensive
cell grazing systems. These systems divide pastures into many small paddocks
with livestock moved daily or even multiple times per day. This creates a
grazing impact pattern that mimics the behavior of wild herbivores in
grassland ecosystems, stimulating biological activity, breaking soil crusts,
incorporating litter, and promoting diverse plant communities. The result is
improved [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]], increased water infiltration, and
higher carrying capacity per hectare compared to conventional grazing.

## Keyline Farm Planning

The keyline design principles developed by P.A. Yeomans, described in
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]], are applied at farm scale through
keyline farm planning. The farm is divided into land units based on keyline
contours, and tree planting, road placement, and fencing all follow keyline
patterns. This ensures that water is distributed from ridges into valleys,
that tree belts follow contour for maximum water capture, and that farm
infrastructure works with the natural shape of the land rather than against
it. Keyline plowing of pastures and croplands fractures compacted subsoil
layers, improving root penetration and water infiltration without disturbing
the valuable topsoil.

## Contour Strip Cropping

On sloping land, Mollison recommended contour strip cropping where different
crops are planted in alternating strips along the contour. Perennial strips
such as grasses or legumes alternate with annual crop strips, creating
barriers that slow runoff, reduce erosion, and trap sediment. The perennial
strips also provide habitat for beneficial insects and can be harvested for
hay or grazed by livestock during fallow periods. This system integrates crop
production with [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] and erosion control.

## Farm Forestry and Woodlots

Every broadacre design should include farm forestry plantings according to
Mollison. Woodlots provide timber, firewood, fence posts, and specialty wood
products while serving as windbreaks, wildlife corridors, and water table
regulators. Mollison recommended planting a diverse mix of species including
fast-growing pioneers for early harvest and valuable climax species for long-
term timber production. Woodlots are typically located in Zone 4 where they
require minimal management but still contribute to the overall farm system.
They connect to [[cannabis-seed-banks-and-genetics]] through the
preservation of locally adapted tree provenances.

## Water Infrastructure on Farms

Farm-scale water infrastructure includes dams, reticulation systems, and
irrigation distribution. Mollison recommended designing water systems to use
gravity wherever possible, with storage located high on the property and
distribution flowing downhill to points of use. Multiple water sources
including dams, bores, and roof catchment provide redundancy. Irrigation
should be designed for efficiency, using drip systems, micro-sprinklers, or
flood irrigation from contour channels rather than wasteful overhead
sprinklers. Farm dams should be designed as multi-purpose systems combining
water storage with [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]], fire protection,
recreation, and wildlife habitat.

## Paddock Design and Fencing

Paddock design in permaculture considers not only livestock management but
also shelter, water access, and ecological function. Mollison recommended
fencing paddocks to include shelter belts of trees on exposed sides and
permanent water access in each paddock. Lane fencing should be oriented to
allow livestock movement along contour rather than up and down slope,
reducing erosion on tracks. Electric fencing provides flexible, cost-effective
paddock subdivision that can be adjusted as management needs change. Fences
should also serve as wildlife corridors and windbreaks where possible.

## Diversified Farm Enterprises

Mollison argued that diversified farms with multiple enterprises are more
resilient and profitable than monoculture operations. A broadacre permaculture
farm might combine grazing, cropping, orchards, timber, poultry, bees, and
aquaculture in an integrated system where waste products from one enterprise
become inputs for another. This diversification reduces financial risk, smooths
income throughout the year, and creates ecological connections that reduce the
need for external inputs. The diversified farm becomes a self-regulating
system that becomes more productive and stable over time, embodying the
[[permaculture-design]] of biological resource use and energy cycling.
