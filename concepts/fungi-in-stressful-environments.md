# Fungi in Stressful Environments
## Overview
Fungi occupy virtually every habitat on Earth, including environments considered
extreme by human standards. Stressful environments are those with abiotic
conditions that restrict or prevent the growth and reproduction of most
organisms. These include habitats with extreme temperatures, pH, salinity,
water availability, or nutrient concentrations. Importantly, what constitutes a
"stressful" environment is relative to the organism in question -- conditions
extreme for one species may be optimal for another.
## Ecological Strategies
Fungal responses to stressful environments are framed within three primary
life-history strategies, adapted from concepts originally developed for higher
plants (Grime 1977, 1979):
- **Competitive (C-selected)**: Long-lived, persistent fungi capable of
  defending captured resources. Predominate when stress is reduced and
  resources are available.
- **Stress-tolerant (S-selected)**: Slow-growing, specialized fungi with
  high enzymatic competence. Characterized by slow biomass increase, temporal
  persistence, and slow germination. Favored when stress limits competition.
- **Ruderal (R-selected)**: Fast-growing, rapidly reproducing fungi with high
  reproductive capacity and short life spans. Favored by abundant soluble
  carbohydrate-rich resources and absence of stress.
Many fungal species display combinations of these strategies, and different
strategies may be adopted under different environmental conditions or during
different life cycle stages.
## Thermotolerant and Thermophilic Fungi
Most fungi are mesophilic, growing between 5-35 degrees C with optima at
20-30 degrees C. Thermophilic fungi grow only above 20 degrees C and up to
50 degrees C or beyond. Thermotolerant species grow below 20 degrees C but
also at 40-50 degrees C. These fungi are ubiquitous in soils worldwide and
are commonly isolated from compost piles, mushroom beds, municipal waste
deposits, bird and alligator nests, coal tips, volcanic hot springs, and soils
of arid and [[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]]. Notable genera
include Thermomyces, Talaromyces, Thermoascus, Scytalidium, Rhizomucor, and
Myriococcum. Recent estimates put the number at approximately 70 species.
Thermophilism is strongly developed in Zygomycota, Ascomycota, and mitosporic
ascomycetes but absent from Basidiomycota.
## Psychrotolerant and Psychrophilic Fungi
Cold-adapted fungi are widespread since 80% of the biosphere exists at -3 to
-7 degrees C and 90% of marine waters are below 5 degrees C. True psychrophiles
have optimum growth up to 16 degrees C and maximum at 20 degrees C, but also
grow at 0 degrees C. Psychrotolerant species have maximum growth temperatures
above 20 degrees C but grow at 10 degrees C.
Common psychrophilic and psychrotolerant genera include Alternaria,
Cladosporium, Mortierella, Mucor, Penicillium, and Typhula. Snow molds such as
Microdochium nivale, Myriosclerotina borealis, and Typhula ishikariensis
damage grasses and cereals at low temperatures.
## Xerotolerant, Halotolerant, and Acidophilic Fungi
Xerotolerant and xerophilic fungi tolerate low water activity (below 0.85 aw).
Most are Ascomycota or their anamorphs, frequently associated with arid
ecosystems, stored cereal grains, and dried foods. Aspergillus species are the
most xerotolerant taxa. Pitt (1975) reported 44 species that spoil foodstuffs
as xerophilic, spanning genera Aspergillus, Chrysosporium, Eremascus,
Paecilomyces, Penicillium, Wallemia, and Xeromyces.
No extreme halophiles (requiring 2.5-5.2 M NaCl) have been found among
eukaryotes. Halotolerant fungi, including Alternaria, Aspergillus,
Penicillium, and Trichoderma, are commonly isolated from arid soils. Some
species like Basipetospora halophila and Hortaea werneckii grow optimally at
2.5 M NaCl salinity. See also [[fungal-species-richness-and-diversity-indices]] and [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]].
