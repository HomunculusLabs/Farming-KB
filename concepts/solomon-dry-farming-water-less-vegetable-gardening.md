# Dry Farming and Low-Irrigation Vegetable Gardening

**Source:** Gardening When It Counts — Steve Solomon (2005)

## Core Concept

Dry farming is the practice of growing crops without supplemental irrigation,
relying entirely on soil-stored moisture from winter rains. Before the 1930s,
when few farms had electricity or piped water, over 90% of [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]
in North America were grown without running water. John Widstoe's 1911 book
*Dry Farming* defined drought not as a weather phenomenon but as a human
failure: "Drouth may be defined as a condition under which crops fail to
mature because of an insufficient supply of water. Providence has generally
been charged with causing drouths, but...man is the cause."

## Historical Context

Before 1880, approximately 90% of North Americans lived on family farms or in
small villages where every household maintained [[query-how-do-i-plan-and-start-a-vegetable-garden-from-scratch]]. These
gardens were routinely grown without any irrigation infrastructure. The concept
of "drought" as understood by modern gardeners — a condition requiring
watering to prevent crop failure — would have been foreign to these farmers.
They understood that proper soil preparation and appropriate plant spacing
could carry vegetables through months without rain.

The transition to irrigated gardening was driven by suburbanization rather
than agricultural necessity. As people moved to smaller lots, they adopted
[[row-gardening-vs-intensive-methods-solomon]] developed for market gardens, not realizing that these
methods were designed for commercial profit rather than food self-sufficiency.
The intensive approach requires daily watering, which in turn demands close
plant spacing, which demands super-fertile soil, which demands expensive
amendments — a cycle of dependency that Solomon explicitly rejects.

## Four Principles of Moisture Conservation

Widstoe identified four fundamental principles that underpin successful dry
farming and low-irrigation gardening:

### 1. Deep Soil Preparation
Deep cultivation stores winter precipitation in the root zone. When soil is
loosened to two feet or more, its capacity to hold water increases
dramatically. This stored moisture is then available to plants during dry
periods. The key insight is that most garden soils, even without irrigation,
contain sufficient water to grow vegetables — if that water is captured and
retained through proper soil management.

### 2. Soil Surface Management
Maintaining a loose soil surface (dust mulch) interrupts capillary channels
that would otherwise wick moisture to the surface where it evaporates.
After watering or rain, allowing the surface to dry and then lightly
cultivating breaks these capillary connections. This is counterintuitive to
many gardeners who believe a moist surface indicates adequate soil moisture.

### 3. Appropriate Plant Spacing
Wide spacing reduces competition between plants for limited soil moisture.
Intensive raised-bed gardening, with plants spaced inches apart, requires
enormous water inputs. Solomon recommends spacing based on the plant's mature
size, often two to four times wider than intensive methods suggest. This
means each plant has access to a larger volume of soil water.

### 4. Timing of Operations
Planting and cultivation must be timed to maximize soil moisture retention.
Early spring operations should be minimized to avoid losing winter-stored
moisture. Working soil when it is too wet destroys structure and reduces its
water-holding capacity. Working soil when too dry creates clods that resist
re-wetting.

## Intensive vs. Extensive Spacing Systems

Solomon contrasts four spacing approaches, ranked by water efficiency:

### System 1: Intensive Raised Beds
- Plants spaced very closely (inches apart)
- Soil loosened to 60cm deep
- Requires daily watering in warm weather
- High yield per square foot but low yield per unit of water
- Best suited to very small gardens with abundant water and fertilizer

### System 2: Moderately Spaced Rows
- Traditional row gardening with moderate spacing
- Requires watering every 2-3 days in warm weather
- More water-efficient than intensive beds
- Requires moderate soil fertility

### System 3: Wide Row Spacing
- Rows spaced 3-4 feet apart with generous in-row spacing
- May only need watering once per week or less
- Each plant accesses a large soil volume
- Lower yield per square foot but higher per gallon of water used
- Requires good soil preparation but less ongoing input

### System 4: Dry Farming (No Irrigation)
- Widest possible spacing based on mature plant size
- No supplemental irrigation after establishment
- Entirely dependent on soil-stored moisture
- Lowest yield per area but zero water input after planting
- Requires the deepest soil preparation and most careful [[coleman-winter-greens-variety-selection]]

## Watering Frequency and Soil Temperature

Solomon strongly emphasizes that frequent watering harms germination and
early growth. Every watering event lowers soil temperature, which slows seed
germination and seedling growth. Cool, wet soil also promotes damping-off
fungus, which kills seedlings at the soil line. Additionally, powdery mildew
on cucurbits thrives in damp, cool conditions.

The practical implication is that gardeners should water less frequently but
more deeply when they do water. A deep watering that reaches the root zone
is far more effective than daily shallow sprinkling. Seeds and seedlings are
better served by maintaining a warm, moderately moist seed zone than by
keeping the surface constantly wet.

## The Economics of Water in Gardening

Solomon makes a crucial economic argument: [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] methods were
developed for market gardeners who sell produce for profit. The high inputs
of water, fertilizer, and labor are justified by cash returns. For home
gardeners growing food for self-sufficiency, especially in hard times, these
methods are economically irrational. Spending money on water infrastructure,
pumping costs, and the fertility needed to support intensive spacing makes
no sense when wider spacing and proper soil preparation can produce the same
total food with far less input.

His own garden in Tasmania produces approximately $4,000 worth of vegetables
(measured by supermarket prices at harvest time) for about $300 in annual
fertility inputs. This return is achieved through extensive spacing, minimal
irrigation, and careful variety selection — not through intensive methods.

## Practical Recommendations

1. **Prepare soil deeply** before planting season, incorporating organic
   matter [[mollison-designers-soil-ph-and-mineral-amendments]] while the soil is workable
2. **Space plants widely** according to their mature size, not their
   seedling size
3. **Water infrequently but deeply** when irrigation is used, targeting the
   root zone rather than the surface
4. **Maintain a dust mulch** by lightly cultivating the soil surface after
   rains or watering to break capillary channels
5. **Choose drought-adapted varieties** when available, particularly for
   crops like corn, beans, and squash
6. **Accept lower per-square-foot yields** in exchange for lower inputs and
   more reliable production without irrigation infrastructure
