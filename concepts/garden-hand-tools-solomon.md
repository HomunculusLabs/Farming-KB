# [[steve-solomon]], Chapter 3

The right tools make low-input gardening feasible. Solomon emphasizes that the goal is to grow a significant portion of one's food with minimal financial investment and maximum self-reliance. This requires durable, well-maintained hand tools rather than power equipment.

## Essential Digging Tools

### The Spade (Not Shovel)
A spade is the gardener's primary tool. Unlike a shovel (which is scooped and meant for moving loose material), a spade has a flat blade and is designed for cutting into and lifting soil. Solomon recommends:

- A forged steel blade, not stamped
- A handle length that reaches from ground to the user's wrist bone
- A D-handle (short cross-handle) for control when digging in tight beds
- A long straight handle for leverage when digging deeply

The spade is used for: turning soil, double-digging, edging beds, cutting roots, trenching, and harvesting root crops.

### The Garden Fork
A four-tined fork is essential for soils that are not excessively stony. The fork:

- Loosens soil without inverting it (preserving soil structure)
- Aerates compacted ground
- Harvests root vegetables without damage
- Incorporates amendments into soil
- Breaks up clods after initial spade work

A forged fork with tines that don't bend easily is worth the investment. Cheap forks with thin tines will deform on the first encounter with a stone.

### The Hoe
Solomon considers the hoe the most important tool for ongoing maintenance. The two essential types:

- **Draw hoe (common hoe)**: Pulled toward the body. Used for forming furrows, hilling up plants, and chopping weeds. The blade should be kept sharp.
- **Dutch hoe (push hoe)**: Pushed ahead of the user. Cuts weeds just below the soil surface. Less effective in heavy soils or against established weeds.

Hoe maintenance: Keep the blade sharp with a file or whetstone. A dull hoe bruises weeds rather than cutting them, allowing regrowth.

## Bed Preparation Tools

### The Iron Rake
A sturdy bow rake (also called a garden rake, not a leaf rake) with rigid steel tines is used to:

- Level and smooth prepared beds
- Break up soil clods after digging
- Create a fine tilth for seed sowing
- Incorporate fine amendments into the surface

### The Trowel
For transplanting and small-scale work. A forged trowel with a substantial blade is preferred over thin stamped models.

## Seed Sowing and Thinning Tools

### The Seed Row Marking Tool
A simple implement for making uniform furrows at consistent depth. Can be as simple as a stick with a notch cut at the desired planting depth, or a purpose-made tool with adjustable depth.

### The Dibber
A pointed stick or purpose-made tool for making holes for transplanting seedlings or large seeds. The depth of the hole can be marked on the dibber to ensure consistent planting depth.

## Weeding Tools

### The Hand Cultivator (Three-Pronged)
A small hand tool with three curved tines, useful for:

- Weeding close to crop plants where a hoe cannot reach
- Breaking soil crust after rain
- Scratch-weeding tiny weed seedlings

### The Collinear Hoe
A narrow-bladed hoe with the blade aligned with the handle (developed by Eliot Coleman). Allows very precise weeding close to crop plants while standing upright.

## Watering Tools

### The Watering Can
A galvanized or heavy plastic watering can with a rose (fine-sprinkling head). Essential for:

- Watering newly sown seed beds without washing seeds away
- Watering transplants gently
- Applying liquid fertilizer

### Hose and Watering Wand
For larger gardens, a hose with an adjustable watering wand allows efficient watering with control over flow rate and pattern.

## Tool Maintenance

Keeping tools in good condition is essential:

- **Clean after use**: Remove soil to prevent rust and rot of wooden handles
- **Oil metal parts**: A light wipe with mineral oil prevents rust
- **Sharpen cutting edges**: Hoes, spades, and trowels work far better sharp
- **Store properly**: Keep tools in a dry place, ideally hanging rather than standing on their edges
- **Repair handles promptly**: A loose handle can cause injury and makes work inefficient

## Tool Purchase Philosophy

Solomon's key insight: buy the best quality tools you can afford once, rather than cheap replacements repeatedly. A well-made tool that lasts decades is far cheaper per year of use than a cheap tool replaced every few seasons. The same principle applies to all gardening equipment.

## Minimal Tools, Maximum Skill

Solomon challenges the garden-center approach that says you need many expensive gadgets. To successfully grow a vegetable garden, you only need a few hand tools used properly. He positions himself as the "gardening grandfather" who will teach you what your grandfather should have taught you -- how to sharpen shovels and hoes, how to use them efficiently, and how to read the earth.

## The Spade (Shovel)

The primary digging tool. Solomon emphasizes buying quality: a forged blade, strong handle of good wood or fiberglass. The shovel should be sharp -- most people have never seen a sharp shovel, but a properly sharpened one cuts through soil with far less effort.

Keep your feet off the growing beds at all times except when actively digging. Compacting the soil by walking on beds destroys the air spaces that roots need.

## The Hoe

Solomon considers the hoe the second most essential tool after the shovel. A properly sharpened and wielded hoe makes weeding efficient. The type of hoe matters: a narrow-bladed hoe gets between close plants better than a wide one.

After every five or ten minutes of use, run the blade over a sharpening stone. A dull hoe makes you work twice as hard. The stone will also hone a dull shovel in an emergency.

## The Bow Rake

Solomon says he could not start small-sized, delicately sprouting seeds directly in their growing beds unless he had a bow rake. The bow rake accomplishes four things at once when preparing a seedbed:

1. Level the surface precisely
2. Uniformly blend manure or compost into the top inch
3. Break up less-solidly cemented clods
4. Comb out lumps from the surface layer
