Paul Stamets' concept of "mycopermaculture" represents a framework for
integrating mushroom mycelium into permaculture design and ecological land
management. Rather than treating mushrooms solely as crops to be grown in
isolation, mycopermaculture positions fungi as essential partners in landscape
restoration, water management, soil building, and [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]]
bacteria and other microorganisms. Soils rich in mycelial networks resist
erosion, retain moisture through drought periods, and support more diverse
plant communities than fungal-poor soils.

The model operates on multiple scales. At the smallest scale, individual
mushroom patches in garden beds process organic waste and produce food. At the
landscape scale, fungal networks installed along waterways filter runoff, along
roadsides stabilize embankments, and in forest margins support mycorrhizal
partnerships with timber and food-producing trees.

## Mycofiltration: Filtering Water with Mycelium

Mycofiltration is the use of mycelial mats to filter pathogens, sediments, and
chemical contaminants from water. Stamets has demonstrated that dense mats of
Stropharia rugoso-annulata mycelium can intercept and destroy coliform
bacteria, including E. coli, from agricultural runoff. The mycelium produces a
dense three-dimensional network that physically traps particulate matter while
simultaneously secreting antimicrobial compounds that kill or inhibit pathogenic
bacteria.

In practice, mycofiltration installations consist of beds of wood chips or
straw inoculated with Stropharia spawn, placed in the path of water flow. As
water percolates through the mycelial mat, contaminants are removed. The
technique has been applied to farm runoff, stormwater management, and the
protection of sensitive waterways from upstream pollution sources.

Stamets reports that Stropharia mycelial mats can reduce coliform counts by
several orders of magnitude as water passes through them. The mechanism
involves both physical filtration and biological activity through antimicrobial
compounds secreted by the fungus. The mycelium continues to grow and maintain
filtration capacity as long as it has a food supply, making mycofiltration a
self-sustaining and self-repairing system.
