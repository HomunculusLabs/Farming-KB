## Green Manure Decomposition and Carbon-to-Nitrogen Timing

The timing of turning in [[coleman-winter-harvest-planting-schedule]] collapses by weeks. Steve Solomon treats this topic with unusual precision because he has seen gardeners lose entire seasons to poor green manure timing.

## The Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio Shift

Green manure crops start life with a relatively low carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio. Young, leafy, actively growing vegetation contains plenty of nitrogen relative to its carbon content. When such tender material is dug shallowly into moist soil, it decomposes rapidly and the land can be replanted in roughly two weeks.

The critical transition happens when the plant begins flowering or forming seeds. At that point [[mushroom-compost]], hairy vetch, and field peas contribute fixed atmospheric nitrogen to the soil in addition to [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] if the material is turned in too late.

Non-legume green manures such as cereal rye, winter wheat, and buckwheat contribute [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]], root penetration, and nutrient capture from deep soil layers that would otherwise leach away.

## The Heavy Soil Trap

Green manure timing problems are compounded enormously on heavy clay soils, particularly in regions with wet springs. The scenario Solomon describes is common and devastating:

1. An overwintered cover crop such as cereal rye or winter wheat emerges from snow in excellent condition
2. Spring rains persist, keeping the soil too wet to work
3. By the time the soil dries enough for tillage, the rye is waist-high or chest-high and forming seed heads
4. The gardener must now turn in woody, high-C/N material
5. This triggers the four-to-five-week decomposition delay on top of the already-late start
6. Cool-weather autumn crops may become impossible

On heavy soils, [[query-how-do-i-use-cover-crops-in-a-no-till-cannabis-garden]] autumn that will be killed by winter cold. Their root systems decompose in early spring without requiring any tillage of living, above-ground biomass. This is much safer for gardeners in cold-winter areas.

- **Frost-tender species**: Use species that reliably die in autumn in your climate zone. Their residues protect the ground through winter and decompose readily in spring.

- **Manual removal**: Rather than digging in a cover crop that has gotten too mature, pull or cut it and move it to the compost heap. The stubble and root system can then be turned in with far less biomass to decompose.

- **Brassica green manures**: Fodder kale, oilseed radish, tyfon, rape, and field turnips make excellent autumn green manures. Their huge taproots open up the soil, and if they freeze out, the dead residues are easy to handle in spring. In mild climates where they overwinter, they can be yanked by hand when blooming begins.

- **Annual legumes**: Crimson clover, hairy vetch, and similar legumes are mainstays for overwintering in regions where they will not freeze out. Perennial legumes like red clover grow more slowly their first year and may be outcompeted.

## Semi-Arid Warnings

Gardeners in semi-arid regions without irrigation face an additional risk. A green manure crop may extract so much moisture from the soil that the ground becomes too dry for the subsequent vegetable crop. In dry winters followed by rainless springs, a cover crop can effectively steal the water reserve needed to establish vegetables.

## Allelopathy Risk

Some cover crop species release [[ingham-manure-antibiotics-chemical-residues-composting]] into the soil that inhibit the germination or growth of subsequent crops. This phenomenon, called allelopathy, is the opposite of what the gardener intends. It is important to research allelopathic interactions before selecting a cover crop species.
