# Soil Fungal Diversity

## Overview

Soil is the most complex fungal habitat on Earth. Fungi are the most abundant soil microbes by biomass and play central roles in decomposition, [[soil-edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]], and their composition is an outstanding indicator of environmental similarity.

## Community Composition

### Characteristic Genera

Domsch, Gams, and Anderson (1980) cataloged 153 common soil genera. Barron (1968) documented 202 genera of soil mitosporic fungi. Expected species recovery from a few hundred isolates typically ranges from 50 to 100 species. Even small numbers of intensively examined soil samples yield hundreds of taxa.

### Biome-Specific Assemblages

Different vegetation types are characterized by distinctive assemblages of soil fungi:

- **Arctic/alpine/boreal**: Cylindrocarpon, Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, certain Penicillium species, sterile fungal strains, yeasts, and yeastlike forms
- **Cold bogs and coniferous swamps**: Tolypocladium, Trichoderma, sterile dematiaceous forms, certain penicillia and eurotialean fungi
- **Grasslands/agricultural**: Acremonium, Arthrinium, Cladosporium, Drechslera, Fusarium, Idriella, Periconia, Microdochium bolleyi, Phoma eupyrena
- **Beech forests**: Strikingly similar principal saprobic species in [[fungal-lichen-stress-tolerance-and-poikilohydry]] without relying on taxonomic identification. Metatranscriptomics captures the expressed genes of the fungal community, providing a snapshot of which metabolic pathways are actively functioning at the time of sampling. These functional approaches complement taxonomic surveys and are particularly valuable for understanding how soil fungal communities respond to environmental changes such as drought, warming, or management interventions.

## Global Diversity Patterns and Biogeography

Soil fungal diversity varies predictably with climate, latitude, and vegetation type. Tropical forests generally support the highest species richness, though the relationship between latitude and fungal diversity is less pronounced than for plants and animals. Arid and semi-arid soils harbor surprisingly diverse communities of melanized fungi adapted to desiccation and UV stress. Polar and alpine soils, despite their low temperatures, support distinct communities of cold-adapted fungi including psychrotolerant basidiomycetes and yeasts. The global distribution of soil fungi is influenced by both contemporary [[fungal-dispersal-limitation-biogeographic-barriers]] appears to play a greater role in fungal biogeography than was previously assumed: many soil fungal species have restricted geographic ranges, and the distance-decay relationship (the decline in community similarity with increasing geographic distance) is as strong for soil fungi as for plants and animals. Island biogeography principles apply to soil fungal communities, with larger landmasses and closer proximity to continental source pools supporting higher diversity.
