# Soil Fungal Diversity

## Overview

Soil is the most complex fungal habitat on Earth. Fungi are the most abundant soil microbes by biomass and play central roles in decomposition, nutrient cycling, plant nutrition, and soil structure. Soil fungal communities are extraordinarily diverse, regulated strongly by climatic and edaphic factors, and their composition is an outstanding indicator of environmental similarity.

## Community Composition

### Characteristic Genera

Domsch, Gams, and Anderson (1980) cataloged 153 common soil genera. Barron (1968) documented 202 genera of soil mitosporic fungi. Expected species recovery from a few hundred isolates typically ranges from 50 to 100 species. Even small numbers of intensively examined soil samples yield hundreds of taxa.

### Biome-Specific Assemblages

Different vegetation types are characterized by distinctive assemblages of soil fungi:

- **Arctic/alpine/boreal**: Cylindrocarpon, Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, certain Penicillium species, sterile fungal strains, yeasts, and yeastlike forms
- **Cold bogs and coniferous swamps**: Tolypocladium, Trichoderma, sterile dematiaceous forms, certain penicillia and eurotialean fungi
- **Grasslands/agricultural**: Acremonium, Arthrinium, Cladosporium, Drechslera, Fusarium, Idriella, Periconia, Microdochium bolleyi, Phoma eupyrena
- **Beech forests**: Strikingly similar principal saprobic species in North America and Europe
- **Desert soils**: Low propagule density but high species diversity; melanized fungi (Alternaria, Ulocladium, Curvularia, Periconia, Papulaspora); Aspergillus may account for up to 20% of species
- **Tropical forest soils**: Aspergillus, Penicillium, Trichoderma abundant; many rare tropical genera associated with overlying litter

### Indicator Assemblages

At the biome level, certain arrays of taxa from among the 30 or more principal microfungal species in soil are unmistakable indicator assemblages. The presence of Fusarium species, Paecilomyces lilacinus, Penicillium restrictum, Mortierella alpina, and Pyrenochaeta species in temperate grassland soils, and Mortierella vinacea, M. isabellina, Paecilomyces carneus, and Oidiodendron species in conifer forest soils, is predictable irrespective of geographic location (Christensen 1981a). An experienced microfungal taxonomist reading the list of the 10-20 most abundant fungi in a soil sample can guess with great accuracy the prevailing vegetational and climatic regimens of the sample's area of origin.

### Chytridiomycetes

Chytrids are widely distributed in soils from the Arctic to the tropics and appear abundantly when specialized enrichment methods are used. Species diversity increases along a gradient from higher latitudes toward equatorial regions. Distributions are sensitive to moisture, ion content, pH, and temperature. Different soil types share common species but harbor totally different assemblages of taxa. Chytrids are typically extracted from the top 5 cm of soils.

## Species Richness Estimates

Species richness in soil fungal communities varies widely with the extent and intensity of the survey. Comprehensive studies across vegetation types have documented remarkable diversity: northern upland conifer-hardwood forests in Wisconsin yielded more than 476 taxa from 8,061 isolates, while a Quercus-Pinus forest in New York produced 267 species from 15,770 isolates. Intuitively, one might expect tropical soil to be more species rich than an equal volume of temperate soil, but recent evidence indicates that species diversity in tropical soils is similar to that in temperate soils, and that the species richness of fungi in humid tropical soils is comparable to that of fungi in temperate forests.

## Factors Promoting High Diversity

The primary factors promoting high species diversity in soil fungal communities are: (1) microhabitat heterogeneity; (2) intermingling of primary, secondary, and tertiary decomposers; (3) faunal grazing; (4) persistence of organic matter that promotes niche partitioning; (5) temporal changes in climate and on-site vegetation; and (6) the remarkable capacity of the cells and mycelia of fungi to behave individualistically. Species richness increases through both primary and secondary succession in plant communities. Biotic interactions may equal or exceed abiotic factors as effectors of composition and diversity in soil fungal communities.

## Vertical Distribution

Fungal abundance and diversity are highest in the A1 horizon (upper mineral soil surface) in areas of moderate to abundant precipitation. The litter layer is a transition zone tied closely to plant community composition. In humid tropical forests, species richness declines rapidly with increasing depth in mineral soil. In temperate regions, species succession occurs downward through litter and soil horizons. Relict fungal communities from past climatic conditions (e.g., Pleistocene periglacial fungi) may persist at depths of 1 m or more.

## Seasonal and Temporal Dynamics

Relative abundances of common soil fungi vary seasonally in forests and grasslands. Seasonal variation is less conspicuous in agricultural soils and some boreal conifer forests. Soil mycobiota change over successional time as vegetation develops and organic matter accumulates. In the Wisconsin surveys, calcium content of litter (irrespective of tree species) correlated with the first axis of community ordination, while dry-to-mesic sequences of deciduous and coniferous forests correlated with subsequent axes.

## Isolation and Study Methods

### Standard Methods

Three principal soil plating methods are recommended: Warcup soil plates, suspension plating, and particle filtration. Species-isolate curves indicate that 600-1000 isolates per site should be examined to capture the majority of species obtainable. For comparative quantitative studies, 180-250 isolates per site provide repeatable data on the principal species. Because species-isolate relationships are nonlinear, simple species-to-isolate ratios cannot be used to compare richness among different studies; rarefaction is the appropriate technique for projecting richness from unequal sample sizes.

### Selective Techniques

Selective isolation extends the species list by targeting specific groups: ethanol pasteurization, soil steaming, addition of dichloran (botran) to medium, and high-temperature incubation. Selective media originally developed by phytopathologists for targeted pathogens can be adapted for inventory work.

### Collection Protocol

Soil is collected from the A1 horizon by removing litter and humus, sterilizing tools in 70% ethanol, and collecting 10-30 g plugs in sterile bags. In warm weather, a cooler (0-10 degrees C) should be used for transport. Samples should be processed within hours or a few days, or air-dried for long-distance transport.

## Practical Considerations

The maximum number of isolates manageable by two experienced full-time researchers at one time is approximately 2000-3000. This practical constraint must be balanced against the goal of comprehensive inventory. New species continue to be encountered indefinitely with increased sampling, and determination of all taxa present in a given soil type seems impossible. Multiple isolation methods detect only a portion of organisms present, and slight variations in technique can result in different perceptions of fungal activity.

## Modern Molecular Methods for Soil Fungal Diversity

### DNA Metabarcoding
DNA metabarcoding using high-throughput sequencing has revolutionized the assessment of soil fungal diversity. The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of ribosomal DNA is the standard barcode locus for fungi, adopted as the official fungal barcode by the mycological community. Metabarcoding studies consistently reveal that culture-based methods recover less than 5 percent of the fungal species present in a soil sample. Environmental DNA extracted from soil captures the total community, including unculturable species, dormant spores, and mycelial fragments that would never appear on agar plates. This approach has revealed that soil fungal diversity is orders of magnitude greater than previously estimated, with individual soil samples often containing hundreds to thousands of fungal operational taxonomic units (OTUs) or amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The challenge of DNA metabarcoding lies in the difficulty of linking sequence data to ecological function, since most soil fungal sequences cannot be assigned to named species and functional annotation relies on phylogenetic placement within known groups.

### Phospholipid Fatty Acid (PLFA) Analysis
PLFA analysis provides a culture-independent measure of total soil fungal biomass and the fungal-to-bacterial ratio, which is a key indicator of soil food web structure. Fungal PLFAs, particularly 18:2 omega-6,9, serve as biomarkers for fungal biomass in soil. While PLFA analysis does not provide species-level resolution, it offers rapid, quantitative assessment of how land management practices affect the overall balance between fungal and bacterial decomposition pathways in soil. Soils with higher fungal-to-bacterial ratios tend to have slower nutrient cycling, greater carbon storage, and more stable aggregate structure, all of which are associated with sustainable soil management practices.

### Next-Generation Sequencing Approaches
Shotgun metagenomics goes beyond metabarcoding by sequencing all DNA in a soil sample, providing information about the functional gene content of the soil fungal community. This approach can identify genes involved in nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, and stress tolerance without relying on taxonomic identification. Metatranscriptomics captures the expressed genes of the fungal community, providing a snapshot of which metabolic pathways are actively functioning at the time of sampling. These functional approaches complement taxonomic surveys and are particularly valuable for understanding how soil fungal communities respond to environmental changes such as drought, warming, or management interventions.

## Global Diversity Patterns and Biogeography

Soil fungal diversity varies predictably with climate, latitude, and vegetation type. Tropical forests generally support the highest species richness, though the relationship between latitude and fungal diversity is less pronounced than for plants and animals. Arid and semi-arid soils harbor surprisingly diverse communities of melanized fungi adapted to desiccation and UV stress. Polar and alpine soils, despite their low temperatures, support distinct communities of cold-adapted fungi including psychrotolerant basidiomycetes and yeasts. The global distribution of soil fungi is influenced by both contemporary environmental factors and historical biogeographic events. Dispersal limitation appears to play a greater role in fungal biogeography than was previously assumed: many soil fungal species have restricted geographic ranges, and the distance-decay relationship (the decline in community similarity with increasing geographic distance) is as strong for soil fungi as for plants and animals. Island biogeography principles apply to soil fungal communities, with larger landmasses and closer proximity to continental source pools supporting higher diversity.

## Land Use Effects on Soil Fungal Diversity

Land use change is the most significant driver of soil fungal diversity loss globally. The conversion of natural ecosystems to agriculture typically reduces fungal species richness by 30 to 50 percent, with the greatest losses occurring among ectomycorrhizal fungi and specialized saprobes. Intensive tillage disrupts hyphal networks, accelerates organic matter decomposition, and favors rapid-growing r-selected fungal species over slower-growing K-selected specialists. Monoculture cropping reduces the diversity of organic substrates entering the soil, narrowing the niche space available for fungal decomposers. The use of fungicides, particularly in cereal crops, has direct toxic effects on non-target soil fungi and can shift community composition toward resistant taxa. In contrast, conservation tillage, cover cropping, organic fertilization, and crop rotation all support higher soil fungal diversity than intensive conventional management. Agroforestry systems, which combine trees with crops or livestock, maintain fungal diversity at levels approaching those of natural ecosystems because the diverse plant community provides a continuous supply of varied organic inputs.

## Functional Guilds in Soil Fungal Communities

Soil fungal communities can be categorized into functional guilds based on their ecological roles:

- **Decomposers (saprotrophs)**: The most diverse guild in most soils, responsible for breaking down dead organic matter including leaf litter, wood, and root residues. Decomposers produce a wide array of extracellular enzymes and are critical for carbon and nutrient cycling.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi**: Form mutualistic associations with plant roots. [[symbiotic-fungi-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-mechanisms]] dominate in grasslands and agricultural soils, while ectomycorrhizal fungi are characteristic of forest soils, particularly under coniferous and hardwood trees. Mycorrhizal fungi influence plant community composition and ecosystem productivity.
- **Plant pathogens**: Soil-borne pathogens including Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Pythium, and Verticillium species cause root diseases that reduce crop yields. The abundance and activity of pathogenic fungi is moderated by competition with non-pathogenic community members and by soil suppressiveness.
- **Endophytes**: Fungi that colonize living plant tissues without causing disease. Some endophytes confer benefits to their host plants including drought tolerance, herbivore resistance, and pathogen protection.
- **Nematophagous fungi**: Predatory fungi that trap and consume nematodes, playing a role in biological control of plant-parasitic nematodes in agricultural soils.

## Conservation of Soil Fungal Diversity

Soil fungal conservation has received far less attention than the conservation of above-ground biodiversity, despite the critical ecosystem services that soil fungi provide. The primary threats to soil fungal diversity are habitat destruction through land use change, soil pollution, climate change, and the introduction of invasive plant species that alter the quantity and quality of organic inputs to soil. Protecting soil fungal diversity requires maintaining diverse plant communities, minimizing soil disturbance, reducing chemical inputs, and preserving natural habitat patches within agricultural landscapes. Soil fungal inocula from diverse reference ecosystems can be used to restore degraded soils, though the establishment of introduced fungal communities depends on matching the inoculum to local environmental conditions. The concept of "soil health" as articulated by [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] ecology is fundamentally a concept of soil biological diversity, and management practices that promote fungal diversity are increasingly recognized as essential for sustainable agriculture.
