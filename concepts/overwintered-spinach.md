## Overwintered Spinach

Overwintered spinach is the practice of sowing spinach in late summer or early fall, allowing it to establish root systems before winter, and then harvesting it continuously through the winter and early spring under protective structures. At Four Season Farm in Maine, overwintered spinach has become the single most important winter green crop, providing reliable harvests from November through March with minimal inputs and no artificial heating.

## Why Spinach Excels as a Winter Crop

Spinach possesses several characteristics that make it uniquely suited to winter harvesting:

### Continuous Winter Growth

Unlike many cold-hardy crops such as kale, which stop producing new leaves during the cold months, spinach continues to produce new leaves throughout the Persephone months (the period when days are shorter than ten hours). While growth is much slower than in summer, the plant does not go fully dormant. This means that once spinach reaches a harvestable size in late fall, it can be harvested repeatedly — typically four times at roughly monthly intervals — rather than being a single-harvest crop.

### Exceptional Cold Hardiness

Spinach is among the most cold-tolerant of common vegetable crops. Established plants can survive temperatures well below freezing, especially when protected by the double-layer system (unheated greenhouse with inner row cover). The plant's leaves may freeze solid overnight but thaw and recover once temperatures rise above freezing during the day.

### Strong Market Demand

Spinach is enormously popular as a winter crop, with demand consistently exceeding supply. This makes it one of the most economically valuable winter greenhouse crops. Customers particularly appreciate the sweetness and tenderness of cold-grown spinach, as cool temperatures cause the plant to accumulate sugars as a natural antifreeze.

### Ideal Rotation Timing

Mid-September-sown outdoor spinach is hardy enough to survive without protection until the mobile greenhouse is freed from summer crops. This creates a natural sequence:

1. Summer greenhouse crops are harvested and cleared
2. The mobile greenhouse is moved over the outdoor spinach beds
3. Protection begins just as outdoor conditions become too harsh for unprotected growth

This timing eliminates the need for dedicated fall greenhouse space for the spinach crop, maximizing overall greenhouse utilization.

## Planting and Growing Timeline

### Sowing

- **Optimal sowing date**: Mid-September at 44°N latitude
- **Location**: Outdoors, in prepared beds where the mobile greenhouse will eventually be placed
- **Soil preparation**: Beds should be well-prepared with mature compost before sowing
- **Goal**: Plants must reach a substantial size before the Persephone period begins (early November at 44°N)

### Fall Growth Phase (September–October)

- Spinach grows normally during the long days of September and early October
- The crop can be harvested from the field through the end of October
- Additional harvests come from another greenhouse during November
- Plants develop deep root systems that will sustain them through winter

### Greenhouse Transition (Late October)

- The mobile greenhouse is moved over the outdoor spinach beds during the second half of October
- Protection typically becomes necessary as nighttime temperatures regularly drop below freezing
- The double-layer system (greenhouse + inner row cover) provides the equivalent of a Zone 8 climate

### Winter Harvest Period (Thanksgiving through March)

- **First greenhouse harvest**: Begins around Thanksgiving (late November)
- **December**: Heavy harvesting to meet holiday demand; the crop is cut back substantially
- **Recovery period**: After heavy December harvest, spinach needs approximately one month to regenerate before the first new-year harvest in late January
- **Late January–March**: Monthly harvests continue, with each bed providing approximately four total cuttings
- **Late March**: Final harvest before spinach begins to bolt (go to seed)

### Spring Transition (Early April)

- Beds are cleaned up after the final harvest
- Soil is prepared for the earliest summer crop
- The cold house becomes a warm house as a propane heater (dormant all winter) is activated to maintain 60°F night temperatures
- Tomato seedlings are transplanted, beginning the summer rotation

## Harvesting Method

Spinach is harvested as a cut-and-come-again crop. The outer leaves are cut above the growing point, leaving the center of the plant intact to produce new leaves. Key considerations:

- **Harvest height**: Cut above the apical meristem to allow regrowth
- **Harvest frequency**: Approximately every 4 weeks during winter, less frequently during the slowest growth period (late December through mid-January)
- **Post-harvest handling**: Spinach should be cooled quickly and kept cold to maintain quality

## Variety Selection

While Coleman does not specify particular varieties in the passages covered here, the general principles for winter spinach selection include:

- **Smooth-leaf types**: Often preferred for winter production because they are easier to clean after freezing
- **Cold-hardy varieties**: Selected for their ability to withstand repeated freeze-thaw cycles
- **Slow-bolting types**: Important for the late-winter harvests when increasing day length triggers flowering

## Comparison with Other Winter Greens

| Crop | Winter Growth | Harvest Frequency | Hardiness | Market Demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Spinach** | Continuous (slow) | Monthly, 4× per bed | Excellent | Very High |
| **Kale** | Stops in winter | Once | Very high | Moderate |
| **Mâche** | Very slow | 1–2× | Excellent | Moderate |
| **Claytonia** | Very slow | 2–3× | Excellent | Low |
| **Tatsoi** | Slow | 2–3× | Very good | Moderate |
| **Swiss chard** | Very slow | 1–2× | Good | Moderate |

Spinach's combination of continuous growth, multiple harvests, cold hardiness, and market demand makes it the standout choice for winter green production.

## Soil and Nutrient Management

Because spinach is the primary winter green crop and the beds transition directly from winter spinach to summer tomatoes (a heavy-feeding crop), soil fertility management is critical:

- **Fall preparation**: Beds receive generous applications of mature compost before the spinach is sown
- **Winter maintenance**: No additional fertilization during winter; the slow growth rate means nutrient demand is low
- **Spring transition**: Additional compost or organic fertilizer may be needed before tomatoes are planted
- **Rotation**: The spinach-to-tomato sequence should not repeat on the same bed year after year; a minimum four-year rotation is recommended

## Economic Significance

Overwintered spinach is the backbone of the winter harvest system's profitability:

- It is the only multiple-harvest overwintered green crop in the simplified winter production system
- It fills the critical gap when other greens are either unavailable or in short supply
- Its popularity ensures strong sales even during the slower market period of late December through January
- By reducing the winter operation to primarily spinach (plus stored carrots and leeks), labor costs during the vacation period are minimized

## Growing in Different Climates

The overwintered spinach system can be adapted to a wide range of climates by adjusting the sowing date based on the local Persephone period:

- **Colder climates (higher latitude)**: Sow earlier (late August to early September) to ensure adequate establishment before the longer Persephone period
- **Milder climates (lower latitude)**: Sow later (late September to early October) since the Persephone period is shorter and less severe
- **Below 32°N**: Winter day length never drops below ten hours, so growth continues through winter; sowing dates are more flexible
