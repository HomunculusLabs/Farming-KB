# Minoan Crete and the Eleusinian Mystery

In Chapter 8 of *Food of the Gods*, McKenna examines two of the
most important examples of psychedelic plant use in ancient
Mediterranean civilization: the Minoan culture of Crete and the
[[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-kykeon-and-eleusinian-mysteries]] of ancient Greece. He argues that both
represent remnants of the [[mckenna-dominator-vs-partnership-society-model]]'s plant-based
spirituality, gradually being suppressed by the rising dominator
culture.

## Abandonment of the Mystery

McKenna opens by noting the progressive abandonment of direct
[[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]] as a source of religious authority in the
ancient world. The transition from partnership to dominator culture
involved the systematic replacement of experiential religion with
dogmatic religion — from direct encounter with the divine through
plants to mediated access through priests and scriptures.

The fall of Çatal Höyük and the rise of kingship mark this
transition in the archaeological record. As dominator values
spread from the Indo-European heartland, the last bastions of
partnership culture were progressively overwhelmed.

## Minoan Mushroom Fantasies

The Minoan civilization of Crete (roughly 3000-1450 BCE) represents
for McKenna one of the last great flowering of partnership culture.
Minoan art frequently depicts mushrooms, particularly in religious
contexts:

- The "Mushroom Goddess" figurines from various Cretan sites show
  female figures holding or wearing mushroom-like objects
- Frescoes from Knossos and other Minoan palaces feature botanical
  motifs that may represent psychoactive fungi
- The famous "sacred knot" and "horns of consecration" symbols may
  encode mushroom imagery
- The prominence of the snake goddess suggests a connection to
  entheogenic plant use (snakes are widely associated with
  psychoactive plants in world mythology)

McKenna suggests that the Minoan civilization was organized around
a mushroom-based mystery religion similar to the later Eleusinian
Mysteries. The absence of defensive architecture and the prominence
of female deities support the identification of Minoan culture as
partnership-oriented.

### The Myth of Glaukos

The myth of Glaukos, a Cretan child who fell into a jar of honey
and died, only to be revived by the seer Polyidos using a specific
herb, is interpreted by McKenna as a coded reference to the
preparation and use of a psychoactive substance. The honey motif
appears repeatedly in connection with entheogenic preparations
in ancient Greek mythology.

## Honey and Opium

McKenna explores the connection between honey and opium in ancient
Greek and Minoan culture. Opium poppies were cultivated in the
Mediterranean from at least the Neolithic period, and honey was the
primary medium for preserving and delivering plant-based medicines.
The combination of honey and opium — or honey and other psychoactive
plant extracts — may represent one of the earliest forms of
pharmacological preparation.

## The Dionysus Connection

Dionysus (Bacchus), the Greek god of wine, ecstasy, and the
theater, represents for McKenna the survival of shamanic plant
religion within Greek culture. Despite his association with wine,
Dionysian rituals involved forms of ecstasy that went far beyond
alcohol intoxication:

- The Maenads, female followers of Dionysus, achieved states of
  ritual frenzy that suggest psychoactive plant use
- Dionysian mysteries involved nocturnal ceremonies in wilderness
  settings, parallel to shamanic practices worldwide
- Dionysus himself was associated with plant life, particularly the
  vine, but also with other psychoactive botanicals
- The Roman suppression of the Bacchanalia (186 BCE) mirrors the
  broader pattern of dominator culture suppressing ecstatic religion

McKenna argues that Dionysian religion was the popular, experiential
counterpart to the increasingly abstract and intellectualized
religion of the Greek elite — and that its suppression was
essential to the consolidation of patriarchal authority.

## The Mystery at Eleusis

The Eleusinian Mysteries, conducted annually at Eleusis near Athens
for nearly 2,000 years (roughly 1500 BCE to 392 CE), represent the
most important surviving example of ancient psychedelic religion.
Initiates underwent a multi-day ceremony culminating in a visionary
experience that was universally described as life-changing.

### A Psychedelic Mystery?

The central question is whether the Eleusinian kykeon — the sacred
beverage consumed by initiates — contained a psychoactive ingredient.
McKenna examines several theories:

**The Ergotized Beer Theory:** The kykeon may have been prepared from
barley infected with *[[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]* (ergot), which contains
ergotamine — a precursor compound to LSD. Water-soluble ergot
alkaloids could have been extracted into the kykeon through a simple
brewing process, producing an LSD-like psychedelic experience.

**Graves's Psilocybin Theory:** Robert Graves proposed that the
kykeon contained [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]]. While the pharmacology is
more consistent with the reported effects, the identification is
more speculative since *Psilocybe* species are less reliably found
in the Attic environment.

McKenna favors the ergot theory on the grounds of ecological
plausibility — ergot is endemic to Mediterranean grain crops —
and pharmacological consistency with the visionary intensity
described by initiates.

## A Historical Watershed

The closing of the Eleusinian sanctuary by the Christian emperor
Theodosius in 392 CE represents for McKenna a historical watershed:
the final suppression of the last institutionalized form of
psychedelic religion in Western civilization. With its closing,
the direct experiential knowledge of the divine was replaced
entirely by faith-based, authority-mediated Christianity.
