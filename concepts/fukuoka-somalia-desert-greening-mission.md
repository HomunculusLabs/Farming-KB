# Somalia Desert Greening Mission

updated: 2026-04-18

Masanobu Fukuoka's forty-day journey to Africa, particularly his time
in Somalia, represented the practical application of his vision for
using [[fukuoka-desert-greening-techniques-detailed]]. The trip was inspired by a United Nations invitation and
driven by seven years of concern about global desertification.

## Origins of the Mission

The dream began seven years earlier when Fukuoka first boarded an
airplane and was astounded to see the American continent from the air.
He started advocating for revegetation efforts in Somalia and running
some tests. News reached the United Nations, and he was invited by an
officer involved in the Plan of Action to Combat Desertification, who
asked if Fukuoka could come up with ideas for greening the deserts.

An NHK television program entitled "Sowing Seed in the Desert"
featured his ideas, and some housewives in Japan -- joking all the
while at his quixotic vision -- wasted no time in collecting funds to
cover air fare and decided to fly to Africa.

## Plans and Preparation

Fukuoka's intention was to take 600 kg of daikon donated by the Murata
Nursery in nearby Matsuyama, another several hundred kilograms of
vegetable seeds sent by individual contributors, and several hundred
seedlings of Japanese fruit trees, and try what would grow in the
desert.

## Bureaucratic Obstacles

When he attempted to carry out this plan, he found he had nowhere to
go. Although aid to Africa in the form of food, clothing, and other
articles was being offered at refugee quarters, no institution existed
for providing direct agricultural guidance to farmers and peasants.

After searching all over Tokyo, he learned that several young volunteers
from Japan were trying to set up a 75-acre farm for food assistance near
Ethiopian refugee camps in a remote area of Somalia. But he had to wait
a long time for a visa. Donations and aiming for peasant
self-sufficiency through natural farming ran counter to the national
policy of the Somali government, which was trying to encourage the
production of cash crops on large plantations.

In the eyes of the government, what Fukuoka proposed amounted to
rebellion. He received warnings and threats: "Giving seeds to a
nomadic people and telling them to become farmers will be a blatant act
of contempt. If the secret police find that you've taken even one
photo, you won't be allowed to return to Japan."

## Arrival in Somalia

Fukuoka transferred through several points and eventually arrived in
Mogadishu, the capital of Somalia. From there he boarded a small
Cessna for the backcountry.

From the air, a splendid panorama of the vast savanna dotted here and
there with thorny bushes opened up. The sight of the isolated round
huts of nomads shared with camels and goats made him feel as if he had
wandered into a fairy-tale world.

## Observations About the Land

Fukuoka was especially happy to note that wherever he looked from the
air, he could see stream and river beds. There was at least one live
spring within any field of view. Surprising as it seemed, there was a
good deal of ground water even in the savanna. Two great rivers at
least a thousand kilometers long flowed around or through the desert,
which reportedly had less than 300 millimeters of annual precipitation.
The desert was green at the banks of these rivers.

Most importantly, the desert sand contained clay. Fukuoka told the
young people of Somalia: "This earth is young. It is sleeping. The
land must be awakened from its slumber with natural farming."

## The Refugee Camps

In the region Fukuoka visited, hundreds of thousands of refugees from
Ethiopia were living in temporary huts. These barely qualified as huts,
appearing to be little more than bird's nests made of thorny branches
propped up against one another, covered with dried leaves or a single
battered rag. The refugees poked fun at themselves by calling it
"housing."

The food in the camps consisted entirely of wheat from the U.S., old
rice from Japan, and macaroni from Italy. There were no vegetables. A
typical hut had just one pan, one knife, and one cup as tableware. This
was often the sum total of a family's possessions.

Fukuoka did not come across the sort of pitiful scenes commonly shown
on television. He found healthy, bright-eyed children running about and
playing. The refugees had a clear view of food aid from abroad:
"America's generosity is aimed at turning us into bread-eating people.
As for Italy, it figures that by giving us free macaroni for three
years, we'll turn into macaroni eaters," they laughed.

## The French Farm and Its Problems

The sole exception to the barren landscape was a French farm where
tomatoes and melons had created green fields. But this was located on
land with large trees, and those actually growing the crops were
Somalian farmers. One farmer holding a bunch of melons approached with
concern on his face -- the plants were swarming with mites.

Japanese volunteers working at the farm had experienced repeated
failures when the oil needed for running the water pumps ran out. The
large-scale irrigation approach used high levees built from the river
with irrigation canals dug in the top, pumping river water onto fields.
This resulted in the same salt buildup as when water is sprayed onto
a field, making the method unsustainable in the long term.

## Plant Irrigation Alternative

If Fukuoka were to set up a natural farm in the desert, he would use a
method he called "plant irrigation." Instead of making canals, he would
create a green belt consisting of various types of trees, using the
roots of these trees to draw in river water underground and cause it
to gradually penetrate dry ground away from the riverside. Or he would
employ special plants to draw up and store this ground water so as to
make it available for use.

While in Somalia, he was able to gather samples of several plants that
could be of help in combating desertification and removing salt from
the topsoil.
