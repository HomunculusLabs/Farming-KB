# AACT vs Anaerobic Brews: Mechanisms and Benefits Compared

## Overview

Dr. Elaine Ingham's Field Guide draws a sharp distinction between [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] Compost Tea (AACT) and anaerobic or fermentative brews. While both methods extract organisms and nutrients from compost, their mechanisms of action, benefits, and risks are fundamentally different. Understanding these differences is essential for anyone using biological amendments in agriculture or horticulture.

## Definition of Beneficial

Ingham defines five potential benefits of compost brews:

1. **Plant protection** — reducing disease and pest pressure
2. **[[biodiversity-of-fungi-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]]** — converting immobilized nutrients into plant-available forms
3. **Improved soil nutrient levels** — adding soluble nutrients to the soil system
4. **Soil structure building** — creating and maintaining soil aggregates and porosity
5. **Toxin decomposition** — breaking down harmful [[ingham-manure-antibiotics-chemical-residues-composting]]

The key question: which of these benefits are actually delivered by aerobic teas versus anaerobic brews?

## Mechanism 1: Plant Protection

### Aerobic Compost Tea (AACT)

AACT protects plants through **competition**, not toxicity. The mechanisms are:

- **Competition for food resources**: Beneficial bacteria and fungi consume exudates on leaf surfaces and in the rhizosphere faster than pathogens can access them
- **Competition for space**: Beneficial organisms physically occupy infection sites, preventing pathogen establishment
- **Consumption of disease organisms**: [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] in the tea consume [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] pathogens directly

This is a mechanical, ecological approach — analogous to cats controlling mice. Ingham emphasizes that compost tea should **not** be considered a pesticide.

### Anaerobic Brews

Anaerobic brews protect plants through **toxic inhibition**:

- They produce alcohols, organic acids, and other compounds that inhibit or kill organisms
- These toxic compounds can suppress both pathogens and beneficial organisms
- The smell of anaerobic brews (sulfur, putrefaction) signals nutrient loss

Ingham argues that anaerobic brews could be classified as pesticides and should theoretically require EPA registration, given that their mode of action is chemical toxicity rather than biological competition.

## Mechanism 2: Nutrient Cycling

### Aerobic Compost Tea

AACT contains the complete soil foodweb trophic cascade:

- **Bacteria and fungi** immobilize nutrients in their biomass
- **Protozoa** (flagellates and amoebae) consume bacteria, releasing plant-available nitrogen
- **Nematodes** consume fungi and bacteria, releasing additional nutrients
- If no plants are present to take up released nutrients, other bacteria and fungi consume them, maintaining the cycle

This nutrient loop prevents leaching and makes nutrients available at rates plants require.

### Anaerobic Brews

Anaerobic brews fail at nutrient cycling for multiple reasons:

- They lack beneficial fungi, so cannot immobilize significant quantities of nutrients
- They lack protozoa (flagellates, amoebae) and nematodes entirely
- Nutrients become trapped in bacterial biomass with no higher organisms to release them
- Nitrogen is lost as ammonia gas or nitrous oxide; sulfur is lost as hydrogen sulfide gas
- The pH drops into acidic range due to organic acid production

The result: nutrient cycling is "stopped cold" and cannot benefit plant growth.

## Mechanism 3: Soil Nutrient Levels

### Aerobic Compost Tea

AACT adds both organisms and soluble nutrients:

- Soluble nutrients are extracted from the compost during brewing
- Additional nutrients come from amendments (kelp, fish hydrolysate, yucca)
- These nutrients feed both the soil foodweb and plants
