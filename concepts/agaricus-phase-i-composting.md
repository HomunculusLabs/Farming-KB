# Agaricus Phase I Composting

Phase I composting is the outdoor stage of converting raw organic materials into a nutritious substrate for [[mushroom-cultivation-in-kenya]]. This process transforms straw, manure, and supplements into what Stamets calls a "nitrogen-rich-lignin-humus-complex" — a source of protein that mushroom mycelium can efficiently colonize and convert into fruitbodies. Phase I composting is both a biological and chemical process that determines the ultimate yield potential of the crop.

This content draws from Paul Stamets' The Mushroom Cultivator, which provides the most detailed English-language treatment of Agaricus composting for cultivators.

## The Two-Phase System

The composting process is divided into two stages. Phase I is outdoor composting — the mixing and primary decomposition of raw ingredients in long stacks or ricks. Phase II is indoor conditioning — pasteurization and microbial conversion of ammonia in a controlled environment. Together, these phases convert approximately 40% of the starting dry weight into microbial biomass and metabolic products, leaving a substrate optimized for mushroom growth.

Phase I is the more variable and less controllable stage, depending heavily on ingredient quality, ambient temperature, and the cultivator's skill at managing the composting process. Getting Phase I right makes Phase II considerably easier.

## Base Ingredients: Horse Manure vs. Synthetic Compost

Two fundamental compost types exist, distinguished by their base ingredient.

Horse manure compost uses material gathered from horse stables — material that is actually about 90% straw and 10% [[animal-manure-guide]]. Despite the name, the straw component is the primary substrate, while the manure provides nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and crucially, the microorganisms that accelerate decomposition. Horse manure from race tracks is preferred by commercial farms because it is typically clean, well-mixed, and contains active microbial populations.

Synthetic compost uses no horse manure. Straw, sometimes mixed with hay, serves as the base ingredient. Because straw is lower in potassium and phosphorus than horse manure compost, these elements must be provided by supplementation. Chicken [[animal-manure-guide]] is the standard nitrogen additive for synthetic composts.

No composts are made purely from straw or purely from manure. Both approaches require fortification with specific supplements to create a balanced substrate. The choice between horse manure and synthetic compost depends primarily on local availability and cost.

## The Role of Supplements

Supplements provide the nitrogen and carbohydrates that fuel microbial activity during composting. They are not optional — by themselves, neither horse manure nor straw alone produces a nutritious compost.

Key supplement categories include nitrogen sources (blood meal at 12% N, poultry manure, cottonseed meal at 6.5% N), carbohydrate sources (molasses, brewer's grain), and minerals (gypsum at 50-100 lbs per dry ton).

A supplement formula is used to determine how much supplementation is necessary for a given amount of base material. The formula is based on the nitrogen content needed, targeting approximately 1.5-1.7% nitrogen for the initial mix. Cottonseed hulls are noted as an excellent structural additive at 1% N, and gypsum should always be added to neutralize sulfuric acid groups and improve texture.

## The Composting Process

Phase I composting follows a characteristic temperature profile driven by microbial succession.

The process begins with thorough mixing of all ingredients. Hay is useful for boosting initial temperatures in synthetic composts because it contains substantial readily available carbohydrates. The initial mix should be homogeneous.

Within 24-48 hours, thermophilic microorganisms ([[thermophilic-fungi]]) raise the pile temperature to 140-170°F (60-77°C). This heat is generated by microbial metabolism — the same organisms that are consuming the organic materials and converting them into mushroom food.

The pile is turned periodically (typically every 2-4 days) to reintroduce oxygen, redistribute moisture, and ensure all material is exposed to the thermophilic zone. Each turn homogenizes the compost and promotes even decomposition. Turning also releases accumulated CO2 and ammonia, preventing the pile from becoming anaerobic.

The production of ammonia is essential to the composting process. Just as carbohydrates fuel microbial growth, ammonia production indicates active protein synthesis by the microbial community. The smell of ammonia should be evident throughout Phase I, reaching a peak at fill time when the compost is moved to the Phase II room.

## Critical Variables in Phase I

Several variables determine compost quality and must be managed carefully.

Moisture content should be maintained at 65-72% throughout Phase I. Too dry, and microbial activity slows; too wet, and the pile goes anaerobic, producing foul odors and inhibiting beneficial microbes. Water is added during turns as needed.

Pile dimensions affect heat retention and oxygen penetration. Piles that are too small cannot maintain thermophilic temperatures; piles that are too large develop anaerobic cores. Typical dimensions are 4-6 feet wide and 4-5 feet tall.

Composting time ranges from 7 to 21 days depending on ambient temperature, ingredient quality, and turning frequency. The compost is ready for Phase II when it has darkened to a rich brown color, straw fibers are soft but still visible, the pile is pliable and moist (not sticky), and a strong ammonia smell is present.

Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio should start around 30:1 and narrow to approximately 20:1 by the end of Phase I. This narrowing indicates that microbial populations have incorporated nitrogen into their biomass while consuming carbon as an energy source.

## Common Phase I Problems

Anaerobic conditions develop when piles are too wet, too compacted, or turned too infrequently. Signs include foul sulfurous odors, dark slimy areas, and failure to reach proper temperatures. Affected areas should be spread out to aerate, and moisture adjusted.

Failure to reach thermophilic temperatures indicates insufficient nitrogen, poor microbial inoculation (especially in synthetic composts), or excess moisture. Adding nitrogen-rich supplements or active compost from a previous batch can restart the process.

Uneven composting results from poor mixing. Patches of undecomposed straw or manure indicate that those areas were never exposed to the thermophilic zone. Additional turns improve homogeneity.

## Transition to Phase II

When Phase I is complete, the compost is moved to the [[phase-ii-room-design]] — a specially designed space for pasteurization and conditioning. At this point, the compost still contains free ammonia that is toxic to mushroom mycelium. Phase II will convert this ammonia into protein-rich compounds that the mycelium can use as food.

The timing of the Phase I to Phase II transition is important. Moving too early means the compost has not developed sufficient microbial biomass and nutritional complexity. Moving too late means excessive dry matter loss and reduced yield potential. The experienced composter learns to recognize the optimal moment by the compost's appearance, smell, and texture.
