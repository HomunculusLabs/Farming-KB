# Chitin and Chitosan — Structural Biopolymers of Fungal Cell Walls

## Overview

Chitin and its deacetylated derivative chitosan are the primary structural polysaccharides of fungal cell walls, analogous to cellulose in plant cell walls and collagen in animal tissues. Chitin is a linear polymer of β-(1→4)-linked N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) residues, providing mechanical strength and rigidity to fungal cell walls. Chitosan, produced by partial deacetylation of chitin, is found in the cell walls of certain fungi (particularly Zygomycetes) and has distinct [[soil-physicochemical-properties-cannabis-microbiome-assembly-winston]] that make it valuable in biotechnology and medicine. Together, these biopolymers are fundamental to [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]], ecology, and their interactions with other organisms.

## Chemical Structure

### Chitin
Chitin is a homopolymer of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc) linked by β-(1→4) glycosidic bonds. This structure is identical to cellulose (β-(1→4)-linked glucose) except that the hydroxyl group on carbon-2 of each sugar residue is replaced by an acetamido group. Chitin exists in three crystalline allomorphs:

- **α-chitin**: the most common form, found in fungi, crustacean shells, and insect exoskeletons. Antiparallel chain arrangement allows extensive hydrogen bonding, resulting in a tightly packed, highly crystalline structure with maximum mechanical strength.
- **β-chitin**: found in squid pens and some diatom spines. Parallel chain arrangement with less inter-chain hydrogen bonding, making it more reactive and easier to process.
- **γ-chitin**: a mixture of α and β arrangements, found in some insect and crustacean cuticles.

Fungal chitin is predominantly the α-allomorph, organized into microfibrils embedded in a matrix of β-glucans and glycoproteins.

### Chitosan
Chitosan is produced by deacetylation of chitin, converting some N-acetylglucosamine residues to D-glucosamine. The degree of deacetylation (DD) determines the proportion of free amino groups and profoundly affects chitosan's properties:

- **DD < 50%**: chitin-like, insoluble in most solvents
- **DD 50–85%**: partially soluble in dilute acids, mixed properties
- **DD > 85%**: fully soluble in dilute acids, strongly cationic, biologically active

Chitosan's amino groups carry positive charges at acidic pH, making it the only naturally occurring cationic polysaccharide. This polycationic nature underlies many of its biological activities.

## Fungal Cell Wall Architecture

The fungal cell wall is a dynamic, multi-layered structure approximately 100–200 nm thick. Chitin and chitosan are integrated into this complex architecture alongside other polymers:

### Basidiomycota and Ascomycota
In the majority of fungi (Dikarya), chitin constitutes 1–15% of the cell wall dry weight. The cell wall architecture is:
1. **Innermost layer**: chitin microfibrils covalently linked to β-(1→3)-glucan, forming a rigid skeletal network
2. **Middle layer**: β-(1→3)-glucan with β-(1→6)-glucan branches, providing tensile strength
3. **Outer layer**: mannoproteins (glycoproteins with mannose-rich oligosaccharides) that determine surface properties, mediate cell-cell interactions, and provide protection

Chitin microfibrils are cross-linked to β-(1→3)-glucan through glycosidic bonds, creating an integrated chitin-glucan scaffold. This scaffold is synthesized at the plasma membrane by membrane-bound enzyme complexes called chitosomes (in some fungi) or by chitin synthase complexes embedded in the plasma membrane.

### Zygomycota (Mucoromycota)
Zygomycete fungi have a distinct cell wall architecture with significantly more chitosan (up to 30–40% of wall dry weight). In these fungi:
- Chitin is synthesized as chitin and then enzymatically deacetylated to chitosan by chitin deacetylases (CDAs)
- Chitosan replaces much of the β-glucan content found in Dikarya
- The cell wall contains chitosan-chitin complexes with β-(1→3)-glucan, mannoproteins, and polyglucuronic acid
- This chitosan-rich wall is more susceptible to certain antifungal agents

## Biosynthesis

### Chitin Synthesis
Chitin is synthesized at the plasma membrane by chitin synthase (CHS) enzymes using UDP-N-acetylglucosamine as substrate. The process involves:
1. **UDP-GlcNAc synthesis**: produced from fructose-6-phosphate via the hexosamine pathway (glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate amidotransferase, GFAT, is the rate-limiting enzyme)
2. **Transport**: UDP-GlcNAc is transported to the cytoplasmic side of the plasma membrane
3. **Polymerization**: CHS catalyzes the transfer of GlcNAc from UDP-GlcNAc to the growing chitin chain, extruding the polymer into the extracellular space
4. **Crystallization**: chitin chains spontaneously assemble into microfibrils through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions

Fungi typically possess multiple chitin synthase genes (CHS1–CHS8 or more), each with distinct roles:
- **Class I–II CHS**: involved in septum formation and cell division
- **Class III CHS**: responsible for [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] and hyphal extension
- **Class IV–V CHS**: specialized roles in [[light-role-spore-formation-geotropic-sensitivity-psilocybe-cubensis]], [[epinephrine-stress-response-endocannabinoid-modulation-blesching]], or pathogenicity

### Chitosan Synthesis
In fungi that produce chitosan (primarily Zygomycota), chitosan is generated by enzymatic deacetylation of newly synthesized chitin:
1. Chitin is synthesized normally by chitin synthases
2. Chitin deacetylases (CDAs), secreted into the periplasmic space, remove acetyl groups from chitin
3. The degree and pattern of deacetylation are regulated and influence cell wall properties

## Biological Functions

### Structural Integrity
Chitin microfibrils provide tensile strength and shape to fungal cells. Without chitin, fungal cells would lyse due to internal [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] (typically 0.5–1.0 MPa in [[environmental-sensing-filamentous-fungi-read]]). Chitin content increases during [[gadd-hyphal-tip-growth-and-branching-mechanisms]], septum formation, and spore development — all situations requiring additional [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]].

### Hyphal Growth and Morphogenesis
Chitin synthesis is essential for hyphal tip extension. Chitin synthases are concentrated at actively growing tips, where they deposit new wall material. The pattern of chitin deposition influences branching patterns, hyphal shape, and overall colony morphology. Mutations in chitin synthase genes often result in morphological defects, reduced growth rate, and lysis.

### Cell Wall Remodeling
During growth and differentiation, the fungal cell wall must be continuously remodeled. Chitinases (enzymes that hydrolyze chitin) work alongside chitin synthases to allow wall expansion, septum formation, and hyphal fusion (anastomosis). This dynamic remodeling is essential for:
- Vegetative hyphal growth and branching
- Septum formation during cell division
- [[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]] and germ tube emergence
- Formation of specialized structures ([[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]], appressoria, haustoria)

### Defense Against Environmental Stress
Chitin and chitosan contribute to resistance against:
- **Osmotic stress**: the rigid chitin-glucan scaffold prevents cell lysis in hypotonic environments
- **Antifungal agents**: chitin modification can alter sensitivity to echinocandin antifungals (which target β-glucan synthesis)
