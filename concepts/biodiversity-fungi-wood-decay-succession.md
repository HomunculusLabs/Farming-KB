# Fungal Succession During Wood Decay

Wood decay is a major pathway of carbon cycling in forest ecosystems, driven by complex successional communities of fungi. The process involves a predictable sequence of fungal species as wood passes through different stages of decomposition, each characterized by distinct chemical and physical properties.

## Decay Types

Wood-inhabiting fungi cause three principal types of decay:

- **Brown rot**: Primarily degrades cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving lignin largely modified but not removed. Produces brown, crumbly residue. Brown-rot residues can be a major soil component in forests
- **White rot**: Degrades all wood components including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. Produces a light-colored, bleached wood residue
- **Soft rot**: Typically caused by ascomycetes and deimatiaceous hyphomycetes, particularly in wet or waterlogged wood

## Successional Patterns

Fungi colonize wood in an organized successional sequence as the substrate deteriorates:

- **Initial colonizers**: Include fungi that were in or on the wood when the tree fell (pathogens and nonpathogenic endophytes of living tissue, heart rots), as well as early-arriving saprobes
- **Primary decomposers**: Colonize freshly dead wood, often competing vigorously for the resource
- **Secondary and tertiary decomposers**: Replace primary species as substrate quality changes and simple carbon compounds are depleted

The correlated factors of log age, decay stage, and contact with soil, together with microenvironmental variables such as exposure to sun, moss cover, and soil moisture, are the most strongly correlated with community structure (Heilmann-Clausen 2001).

## Species Examples by Successional Stage

Research in boreal and temperate forests has identified characteristic fungal species associated with each stage of wood decomposition:

### Initial Colonizers (Decay Class 1-2: Fresh to Recently Dead)
- **Hymenochaete tabacina** and **H. corrugata**: Common on recently dead angiosperm branches, forming crust-like basidiocarps
- **Stereum hirsutum**: Among the first conspicuous fungi to appear on dead hardwood, producing tough, leathery brackets
- **Trametes versicolor** (Turkey tail): Rapidly colonizes freshly cut hardwood stumps and logs, competing aggressively for substrate
- **Schizophyllum commune**: A ubiquitous early colonizer of dead angiosperm wood worldwide, producing small, split-gilled fruiting bodies even in dry conditions
- **Bjerkandera adusta**: Colonizes dead hardwood and produces a white rot, commonly found on fallen branches
- **Chondrostereum purpureum**: A significant pathogen that transitions to saprotrophic decay after killing branches

### Primary Decomposers (Decay Class 2-3: Early to Mid Decomposition)
- **Fomes fomentarius** (Tinder conk): A white rot fungus producing large perennial bracket fungi on birch and other hardwoods
- **Fomitopsis betulina** (Birch polypore): Causes brown rot in birch, typically appearing on standing dead trees or fallen trunks
- **Ganoderma applanatum** (Artist's conk): Large perennial brackets on hardwoods, causes extensive white rot over many years
- **Piptoporus betulinus**: Another significant birch decomposer, causes brown cubical rot
- **Laetiporus sulphureus** (Chicken of the woods): A vigorous brown rot fungus on hardwoods and occasionally conifers

### Secondary Decomposers (Decay Class 3-4: Mid to Advanced Decomposition)
- **Phellinus igniarius**: Slow-growing white rot fungus appearing on well-decayed hardwood
- **Trametes ochracea**: Common on older deciduous wood in advanced stages of decay
- **Antrodia serialis**: A brown rot fungus of conifer wood in later decomposition stages
- **Phlebia radiata**: A white rot fungus that thrives on partially decomposed wood, often replacing earlier colonizers

### Tertiary Decomposers (Decay Class 4-5: Highly Decomposed to Humus Formation)
- **Mycena species**: Small, delicate mushrooms that fruit from heavily decayed wood and leaf litter, including *Mycena galericulata* and *M. haematopus*
- **Marasmius species**: Including *M. rotula*, which fruits from well-decoded hardwood twigs
- **Coprinellus species**: Ink cap mushrooms that appear on very rotten wood approaching soil incorporation
- **Xylaria species**: Carbonaceous, club-like fruiting bodies appearing on well-decayed wood, including the "dead man's fingers" *X. polymorpha*

## Resource Partitioning

Different groups of wood-inhabiting fungi use quite different volumes of substratum for sporocarp production:

- **Small debris** (twigs, branches <1 cm): Support diverse communities of small ascomycetes and resupinate heterobasidiomycetes
- **Medium debris** (1–15 cm diameter): Sampled effectively with circular plots
- **Large woody debris** (logs, standing trees): Support distinct macrofungal communities; best sampled using individual logs or trees as sample units rather than plots

Large-diameter wood is easily undersampled if collecting is confined to small subplots because of the low density of large pieces and because few will be in the right decay stage to support fungal fruiting.

## Species-Substrata Relationships

In Scandinavian studies, the species-substrata curve did not reach an asymptote even after many logs were surveyed, indicating that many more species would be discovered with additional sampling (Lindblad 1998). Aggregating samples from different habitats produces steadily increasing species-effort curves, but stratifying by diameter class, decay class, or tree species may produce flattened curves within strata.

Gymnosperm hosts generally support a more restricted mycota than angiosperm hosts. The number of wood-inhabiting fungal species in an area depends more on the range of decay classes of substrata than on the number of plant species present.

## Climate Effects on Succession Rates

Wood decomposition rates vary dramatically with climate. In tropical forests, a fallen log may reach advanced decomposition within 3-5 years, while the same process can take 20-50 years in boreal forests. Temperature, moisture, and fungal community composition all interact to determine decomposition speed:

- **Tropical systems:** High temperatures and consistent moisture accelerate both fungal growth and decomposition. Succession proceeds rapidly, with species turnover occurring in months rather than years. Termite activity also contributes significantly to wood breakdown in the tropics.
- **Temperate systems:** Seasonal temperature fluctuations slow decomposition. Succession typically requires 10-25 years from initial colonization to complete incorporation into soil organic matter.
- **Boreal systems:** Cold temperatures and short growing seasons make decomposition extremely slow. Large logs can persist for many decades. Brown rot fungi tend to dominate because lignin degradation by white rot fungi is temperature-limited.
- **Mediterranean systems:** Summer drought creates moisture limitations that slow fungal activity. Decomposition pulses occur during wet seasons. Wood-inhabiting fungi in these regions show adaptations for desiccation tolerance.

## Dead Wood as Wildlife Habitat

Coarse woody debris serves as critical habitat beyond its role in fungal ecology. Standing dead trees (snags) provide nesting sites for cavity-nesting birds such as woodpeckers, owls, and nuthatches. Fallen logs shelter amphibians, reptiles, and small mammals. The invertebrate communities supported by wood-decay fungi — including fungus gnats, beetles, and springtails — form the base of food webs that support higher trophic levels. In managed forests where dead wood is routinely removed, these dependent species populations decline sharply. Conservation guidelines in Scandinavian countries now recommend retaining a minimum of 20-30 cubic meters of dead wood per hectare to maintain biodiversity.

## Forest Management Implications

The relationship between dead wood and fungal biodiversity has significant implications for sustainable forestry:

- **Retention forestry:** Leaving dead trees, fallen logs, and high stumps on harvest sites maintains habitat for wood-decay fungi. In Scandinavian countries, "green tree retention" and "dead wood creation" are standard practices on certified forest lands.
- **Dead wood targets:** Fennoscandian forestry standards recommend 5-10 m³/ha of coarse woody debris on production lands, and 20-30 m³/ha on conservation lands. Many European forests currently fall below these targets due to historical management practices.
- **Species richness correlations:** Studies consistently show that the number of wood-inhabiting fungal species in a forest stand correlates more strongly with the diversity of decay classes and diameter classes of dead wood than with total forest area or living tree diversity.
- **Threatened species:** A disproportionate number of red-listed fungal species in Europe are dependent on large-diameter, late-decay-stage dead wood — precisely the resource that intensive forestry removes most completely.

## Measurement Methods

Researchers employ several methods to study wood-decay fungal succession:

- **Visual decay classification:** Most commonly using a 5-class system (1 = sound wood, 5 = completely decomposed). Assessors record wood hardness, texture, and moisture by probing with a knife or auger. This method is fast but subjective.
- **Sporocarp surveys:** Counting and identifying fruiting bodies on logs. This is the most common method but underestimates species that rarely fruit or produce inconspicuous basidiocarps. Surveys should be repeated throughout the growing season.
- **Wood sampling and culturing:** Bringing wood samples to the laboratory for isolation and culture of fungi. More comprehensive than sporocarp surveys but selects for fast-growing species that colonize culture media.
- **Molecular methods:** DNA metabarcoding of wood samples using high-throughput sequencing (Illumina, Oxford Nanopore) provides the most complete picture of fungal community composition, including species that never produce visible fruiting bodies. This approach has revealed that the true diversity of wood-inhabiting fungi may be 2-5 times higher than sporocarp surveys suggest.
- **Density loss measurement:** Measuring wood density changes over time using displacement methods or gravimetric analysis provides a quantitative measure of decomposition progress independent of subjective decay class ratings.

## Pathogenic and Endophytic Origins

In standing trees, decay fungi can be root-, butt-, or heart-rot organisms, which are generally mutually exclusive. These fungi cause decay of roots and/or stems, predisposing trees to wind throw or trunk breakage. Many wood-decay fungi begin their association with the tree while it is still alive, as endophytes or latent pathogens that become active upon tissue death or stress.

## Ecological Questions

Key unanswered questions about wood decay fungal succession include:

- How do species composition and richness change through the course of decay?
- How do natural disturbances (hurricanes, fires) perturb composition and richness?
- How do anthropogenic disturbances (logging, wood removal) affect diversity?
- Does wood-inhabiting fungal richness follow latitudinal gradients?
- How does sporocarp production vary within and between years?

## Sampling Considerations

- Many resupinate and inconspicuous basidiomycetes are not detectable or recognizable in the field; laboratory examination of collected wood is required
- No assumptions should be made regarding species identity of small ascomycetes — all separate patches should be collected
- Plots should be sampled throughout the growing season for complete assessment
- In tropical forests, diameter class and microclimate are among the most important factors influencing community composition
