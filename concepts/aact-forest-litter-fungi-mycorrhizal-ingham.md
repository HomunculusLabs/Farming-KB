# Forest Litter Fungi and Mycorrhizal Considerations in Compost Tea

## Overview

Forest soils contain complex fungal communities that can serve as inoculum sources for compost tea and soil restoration projects. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide addresses the use of forest litter fungi, mycorrhizal fungi, and actinobacteria in compost tea systems, providing guidance on when and how to incorporate these organisms into AACT programs.

## The "O" Horizon — Forest Litter Layer

### Definition

The "O" (organic) horizon is the uppermost soil layer in forest ecosystems, consisting of plant debris in various stages of decomposition. As litter breaks down and forms humus, it creates this distinct organic layer. The boundary between the O horizon and the A horizon (mineral soil) occurs when sand, silt, or clay becomes mixed into the organic material.

### Indigenous Beneficial Fungi

Healthy forest soils contain indigenous beneficial fungi adapted to local conditions. When plant debris is placed on the soil surface beneath trees, these fungi grow upward into the debris and begin the decomposition process. The critical management principle: **leave plant material on the soil surface to develop more fungi.** Mixing debris into the soil shifts the microbial community toward bacterial dominance.

### Collecting Forest Litter for Inoculation

Ingham suggests collecting fungi from the O horizon during walks in the woods. Handfuls of fungal material from the litter layer can be brought home and used to inoculate compost or directly applied to soil. This low-cost approach maximizes local fungal diversity and adapts organisms to regional conditions.

### Alaska Humus as an Alternative

Alaska humus (AH) is commercially available and typically high in fungal biomass. It provides a convenient source of beneficial fungi when local forest material is not accessible. Ingham recommends using both local forest material and Alaska humus together to maximize fungal diversity, combining the convenience of a commercial product with the local adaptation of indigenous fungi.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in AACT Systems

### Critical Warning: Do Not Brew Mycorrhizal Spores

One of the most important practical points in Ingham's guidance: **mycorrhizal spores should not be added to compost tea during the brewing process.** Unlike Trichoderma and Gliocladium, mycorrhizal spores are too delicate just after germination. The agitation and aeration of the tea brewing process damages the newly germinated mycorrhizal structures.

### Correct Application Method

Mycorrhizal spores should be added:
- Just before spraying the tea onto soil
- Directly to the soil alongside the tea application
- To compost that will be spread on planting areas

The tea serves as a carrier for even distribution, but the spores should not be subjected to the brewing cycle itself.
