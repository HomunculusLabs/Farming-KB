# Photoperiodism in Plants

Photoperiodism is the physiological response of plants to the relative lengths of light and dark periods. It is one of the most important environmental cues governing plant development, particularly the transition from vegetative growth to flowering. The [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] to day length:

**Short-day plants** flower only when the length of the uninterrupted dark period exceeds a critical threshold. These typically flower in late summer or autumn as days shorten. Examples include chrysanthemums, soybeans, poinsettias, and cannabis (most photoperiod-sensitive varieties). In [[blesching-cannabinoid-pharmacology]] is the standard method for inducing flowering — typically by switching from 18/6 or 24/0 light/dark to 12/12.

**Long-day plants** require a minimum period of daylight to flower and typically bloom in spring or early summer. Examples include irises, barley, spinach, and lettuce. These plants will not flower during the short days of winter even if temperatures are favorable.

**Day-neutral plants** flower regardless of day length, responding instead to other cues such as temperature or plant age. Examples include tomatoes, rice, and cucumbers.

Some plants exhibit more complex responses, such as **quantitative short-day** or **quantitative long-day** behavior, where flowering is accelerated by the appropriate day length but can eventually occur under non-optimal conditions.

## Plants Measure Night, Not Day

A crucial insight from photoperiodism research was that plants measure the length of the continuous period of darkness, not the length of daylight. This was demonstrated through experiments where scientists could manipulate flowering by briefly turning lights on and off in the middle of the night.

For a short-day plant like soybean, keeping it from flowering in short days was as simple as turning on the lights for a few minutes in the middle of the night. Conversely, a long-day plant like iris could be forced to flower in the middle of winter if lights were turned on briefly during the night. These experiments proved unambiguously that what a plant measures is not the length of the day but the length of the uninterrupted dark period.

This discovery has enormous practical significance. Flower farmers can keep chrysanthemums from flowering until just before Mother's Day by turning on lights for a few minutes each night throughout autumn and winter. Then, two weeks before the desired harvest, they stop the night lighting and all the plants begin flowering simultaneously.

## Phytochrome: The Molecular Switch

The photoreceptor responsible for photoperiodic responses is called **phytochrome** (from Greek: phyton = plant, chroma = color). Phytochrome is a pigment protein that exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr** (P660): the inactive form that absorbs red light (~660 nm wavelength)
- **Pfr** (P730): the active form that absorbs far-red light (~730 nm wavelength)

The switching mechanism works as follows:
1. Red light converts Pr to Pfr (the active form)
2. Far-red light converts Pfr back to Pr (the inactive form)
3. In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr naturally

This creates a biological light-activated switch. Red light turns on flowering
in long-day plants (or inhibits it in short-day plants), while far-red light
reverses the effect. Only a few seconds of illumination are sufficient to
trigger the switch, making it an extremely sensitive detection system. The
speed of the response reflects the fact that phytochrome operates through
conformational changes in a protein, not through slow biochemical cascades.

Ecologically, this makes perfect sense. In nature, the last light any plant sees at the end of the day is enriched in far-red wavelengths (red light is scattered by the atmosphere at dusk). This far-red signal tells the plant that darkness is coming, allowing it to "turn off." In the morning, the red-rich light of dawn converts phytochrome to the active form, "waking up" the plant's photoperiodic measurement system.

## Where Photoperiodism is Sensed

Darwin's studies of phototropism showed that the "eye" of a plant for directional light sensing is in the shoot tip. Surprisingly, the photoperiodic "eye" is located in a different place entirely.

If a beam of light is shone on different parts of a plant in the middle of the night, it is sufficient to illuminate any single leaf to regulate flowering in the entire plant. If all leaves are removed, leaving only the stem and the apex, the plant becomes completely blind to photoperiodic light cues, even if the entire remaining plant is illuminated.

This means that phytochrome in the leaves receives the light cues and initiates a mobile signal — a protein called **FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT)** — that propagates through the phloem from the leaves to the shoot apex. At the shoot apex, this signal triggers the floral transition. The mobile flowering signal has been called **florigen**, the hypothetical hormone whose existence was predicted decades before its molecular identity was discovered.

## Red and Far-Red: The Reversibility Experiment

The red/far-red reversibility of phytochrome was demonstrated in a classic series of experiments. If irises (long-day plants) are given a pulse of red light in the middle of the night, they flower as if they experienced a long day. But if far-red light is applied immediately after the red pulse, it is as if they never saw the red light — they will not flower.

If red light is applied after the far-red, flowering is induced again. Far-red after that reverses it. And so on, back and forth. This toggle-like behavior demonstrated that a single molecular switch governs the photoperiodic response, and that the plant effectively "remembers" the last color of light it perceived.

On a more philosophical level, this reversibility shows that plants possess a
form of short-term memory — the ability to retain information about recent
light conditions and integrate that information into developmental decisions.
This memory persists for at least several hours, as evidenced by the ability
of a brief light pulse in the middle of a long night to reset the photoperiodic
clock. The concept of plant memory extends beyond photoperiodism to include
other phenomena such as vernalization (remembering winter cold), stress
priming (remembering previous stress exposure), and epigenetic changes that
persist across generations.

## Photoperiodism and Agriculture

The practical applications of photoperiodism research have been transformative
for agriculture and horticulture:

- **Controlled flowering:** Greenhouse growers can precisely time flowering
  for market demand by manipulating day length with [[plant-signaling-pathways]], including the circadian clock, temperature sensing (through
vernalization), and hormone signaling (particularly gibberellins). This
integration allows plants to make sophisticated developmental decisions based
on the convergence of multiple environmental cues, ensuring that flowering
occurs under optimal conditions for reproductive success.
