## Permaculture Village Governance and Public Financing

The governance and financial systems of a [[permaculture]] village are designed to ensure that common resources are managed for the long-term benefit of all residents, that public services are sustainably funded, and that economic activity within the village generates wealth that stays within the community. These systems draw on proven models from cooperative movements worldwide, particularly the [[mondragon-cooperatives]] of Spain.

## Financing Public Services Through Land Trusts

The primary mechanism for financing public services in a permaculture village is the **allocation of 30% of all titles to the village trust (Trust A)**. When these titles are sold, the generated capital and ongoing interest yield fund [[village-development-financing-and-trust-structures]] and public services.

### How the 30% Allocation Works

When all titles sell, the monies generated by the sale of the 30% of titles vested in the village trust ensure a substantial annual interest yield for village development. This revenue stream effectively:

- **Funds all necessary public services**: Roads, fences, water supply, fire control, and other infrastructure
- **Eliminates land cost for residents**: Over a 20-year period, residents have effectively paid nothing for their land, as the trust-funded services represent a return on their collective investment
- **Accelerates development**: As soon as sales exceed 60% of total titles, either capital or interest becomes available for public services, enabling infrastructure development well before the village is fully occupied

### Using Trust Capital Productively

At approximately year 5 of development, when residents have a clear understanding of future needs, the capital itself (not just the interest) can be deployed to install **wealth-producing assets** for village employment. These may include:

- Glasshouses for commercial food production
- Computers and communication infrastructure
- Machinery for [[mollison-village-enterprises-occupations]]
- [[holzer-alternative-energy]] systems
- Schools and workshops

### The Credit Union Connection

If a credit union has been established within the village, much of the trust capital can be transferred to a loans account, enabling residents to draw on it at **low interest** for local occupational development. Conscientious use of a credit union by village residents greatly increases capital flow to village enterprises. Housing loans may also be available, though a building cooperative is often more effective than individual loans for this purpose.

### Community Morale

The result of having capital and interest flowing into the Trust is a significant increase in **village morale**. Every resident can see long-term plans being fairly rapidly achieved through shared investment. Well-managed, the capital should actually increase over time, providing a growing annual fund for village use.

## Ongoing Trust Revenue

The Trust requires ongoing income for maintenance of roads, fences, water supply, fire control, and other site infrastructure. Revenue is raised through several mechanisms:

- **Lease charges**: Small charges for leases to residents using common properties
- **Equipment hire**: Hourly rates for the lease, use, or hire of machines and shared facilities
- **Cooperative levy**: A 10% levy on net profits of locally-funded cooperatives

The 10% cooperative levy mirrors the system used by the Mondragon cooperative network in Spain, which uses this levy to fund their banks, schools, and research facilities. Importantly, this levy applies only to **net profits** of trust-funded cooperatives, not to gross revenue.

## The Community Services Council

A **Community Services Council** is elected at any new community or village with common lands. Its responsibilities include:

- Administering policy on publicly owned or common assets
- Collecting lease monies on utility plots
- Administering funds for schools and medical services
- Ensuring that rates are used in maintaining roads, water supplies, and other public services
- Balancing income-producing and income-consuming activities

### Composition and Structure

The Council should be comprised of people who are **actively involved** in the service areas it oversees, not an uninvolved group of general representatives. Each area of action has a basically independent management sub-group that:

- Reports to Council regularly
- Appoints one of its members to represent that group on Council
- Handles everyday business within its budget and allotted areas
- Can raise its own funds independently (as well as access public funds)
- May have an active business management role in income-producing areas
- Supplies workers to carry out businesses

The Council meets monthly, or even less frequently, with sub-groups handling everyday business. The Council can call for and act on submissions or reports relating to specific policies and strategies.

## Community Services Scope

"Community Services" in a permaculture village encompasses a wide range of activities:

- **Fire control services**: Prevention, equipment, and response
- **Land leasing**: Of common properties for business use
- **Public reserves and sports grounds**: Maintenance and development
- **Ambulance and health services**: Including cemetery administration
- **Education and educational facilities**: Schools, workshops, libraries
- **Community centre**: Use and hire for events and gatherings
- **Power and water supply**: Village-scale energy and water infrastructure
- **[[mollison-course-coppice-forest-management]]**: Sustainable harvesting, replanting, fire prevention
- **Tourism and promotion**: If the community desires visitor engagement

Some of these services are income-producing, some receive state aid or tax immunity, and some are income-consuming. The Services Council must balance these costs, allot land and assets for income to service groups, and ensure that common property and rights are fairly assigned and well managed.

## Models for Generating Community Income

Several complementary models exist for generating the income needed for continued upgrading of public services:

### Levy System
A yearly levy on each family or property, collected by the Community Council. This is probably in addition to normal Shire Council rates and may create hardship on some households. It is the simplest but least equitable model.

### Tithe System
Individuals, groups, and businesses tithe 10% of net profit to public services. All Mondragon cooperatives use this system to fund schools, libraries, churches, and recreational grounds. The key advantage is that rates are on a **sliding scale** — those who earn more contribute more, rather than a flat rate regardless of earning power.

### Local Currency
The Community Services Council can print its own currency, **backed by a valuable community resource** such as firewood or clean bottled water, to fund projects and new buildings. Local currency keeps wealth circulating within the community rather than leaking to external economies. A timber forest planted early and harvested at various stages of maturity can produce most of the income needed for subsequent years, as demonstrated by some modern towns that have developed social forestry.

### User Charges
Charges by the school, community centre, and health services can offset costs on a user-pays basis for tuition, facility use, and medical needs. This model should be used in conjunction with another model, as it covers only public facilities that not everyone uses. Universally used services are usually prorated across all residents.

## Optimal Combination

The most resilient financing model combines multiple revenue streams: a tithe on cooperative profits for fairness, charges for specific services to cover direct costs, local currency backed by real community assets for development funding, and social forestry for long-term income generation. This diversified approach ensures that no single revenue failure can cripple the village's public service capacity.

## Village Size and Governance

Human settlements vary in their ability to provide resources, develop self-reliance, and maintain social cohesion according to population size:

- **~100 income-producing people**: A significant financial institution can be village-based
- **~500 people**: All people can know each other if social affairs are encouraged
- **~2,000 people**: Theft and competitiveness become more common; sects form in opposition; ecumenical alliances are lost

The recommended approach is to **start small** at about 30 adults, build to 200-300 people, and proceed slowly and by choice to 500. Beyond this, the village should "calve" into new neighborhoods or new villages rather than continuing to grow.

Alliances of 200-500 household-size hamlets can form viable manufacturing or trading alliances while maintaining safe [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] and social cohesion. Many traditional tribes of approximately 200 people confederated into alliances of 3,000-7,000, sharing special products by trade and arranging out-marriages.

The **Mondragon Cooperatives** of Spain initially grew to 3,000-5,000 members but later deliberately reduced to cooperatives of 300-500 to preserve individual identity. Nevertheless, a group of such small cooperatives can manufacture any vehicle or machine if each produces a part — demonstrating that economic capacity does not require social scale.

The **neighborhood factor** — knowledge of each other's names — is identified as a primary survival factor. During the 1967 Hobart fires, casualties in "anonymous" commuter suburbs were many times higher than in neighborhoods where people knew and cared for others. This finding underscores the permaculture emphasis on human-scale settlement.
