## Selecting Wild Mushroom Patches for Transplantation

Stamets describes the practice of locating healthy, vigorous wild mushroom
patches as candidate sources for mycelium transplantation. Ideal patches exhibit
robust fruiting, indicating active, healthy [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]], where mycelial density is highest. These substrate blocks are
then transported to the prepared outdoor bed and buried at appropriate depth.

A more refined approach involves taking tissue cultures from wild mushrooms and
expanding them on sterile media in a laboratory setting before using the
resulting spawn for outdoor inoculation. This method offers superior genetic
consistency and reduced contamination risk but requires access to sterile
[[agar-culture-techniques-detailed]]. Stamets notes that direct transplantation of substrate
blocks, while less controlled, can be remarkably effective for hardy species
like King Stropharia that tolerate competition from soil microorganisms.

## Inoculating Outdoor Substrates with Pure Cultured Spawn

For cultivators with access to laboratory-produced spawn, Stamets describes a
more controlled approach to outdoor bed inoculation. Pure cultured grain or
sawdust spawn is thoroughly mixed into prepared wood chip substrate at rates
typically ranging from 5–15% by volume, depending on species vigor and
substrate sterility. The inoculated substrate is then packed into prepared beds
and covered with a protective layer of straw, leaves, or additional wood chips
to retain moisture and buffer temperature fluctuations.

Stamets emphasizes that outdoor inoculation with cultured spawn dramatically
improves colonization speed and consistency compared to wild transplantation.
The pre-colonized grain or sawdust particles act as numerous inoculation points,
ensuring rapid mycelial expansion through the substrate matrix. This method
also reduces the risk of introducing competing fungi or pathogens that may be
present in wild-collected substrate.

## Seasonal Timing of Outdoor Inoculation

Timing is critical for successful outdoor bed establishment. Stamets recommends
inoculating in early spring or early fall, when moisture levels are naturally
high and temperatures are moderate. Spring inoculation allows the mycelium to
establish through the warm summer months, with first fruiting typically occurring
in the following spring or fall. Fall inoculation takes advantage of autumn
moisture and cooling temperatures, with mycelium slowly colonizing through the
winter and fruiting the following spring.

Summer inoculation is generally discouraged due to heat stress and rapid
drying of exposed substrate, though deep-shaded locations with reliable
irrigation may permit successful summer establishment. Winter inoculation is
possible in mild climates but carries the risk of mycelium entering dormancy
before adequate colonization is achieved.
