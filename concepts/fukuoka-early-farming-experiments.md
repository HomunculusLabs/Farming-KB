# Early Farming Experiments

When Masanobu Fukuoka returned to his family farm on Shikoku Island around 1938, he began a series of [[fukuoka-road-to-natural-farming]] that would eventually lead to his revolutionary [[fukuoka-natural-vs-scientific-farming]] method. His [[fukuoka-personal-journey]] from agricultural scientist to natural farmer was marked by decades of patient trial and observation.

## Work at the Research Station

Before returning to the farm, Fukuoka worked at a plant disease and pest research section. He described himself as "a heretic" who had the freedom of working within science and exploring farming techniques that refute science and technology. He did his work with just the right measure of diligence and was thus able to live within his dreams.

However, as the war situation intensified, increasing food production became a more urgent priority than basic scientific research. All researchers at the laboratory were mobilized for this purpose. The directives stated that starch production was to be increased, even if this meant cutting the production of other crops.

## The Kochi Prefecture Rice Borer Campaign

Fukuoka was sent to the agricultural testing station in Kochi Prefecture, where a bold new plan was implemented: the eradication of the yellow rice borer through post-season rice cultivation. Because post-season cultivation made collective use of the most advanced rice growing technology of the day, knowing something about this method gave one a good idea of where scientific farming stood technically at the time.

Rice cultivation practices throughout Kochi Prefecture were everywhere different. Farmers on the centrally located Kacho Plain double-cropped their rice, while farmers in other areas variously practiced early-season, midseason, or late-season cropping. In spite of its warm climate, which seemed ideal for rice production, Kochi had the second lowest rice yields of any prefecture in Japan.

Fukuoka remembers commenting on how there wasn't "a single healthy rice plant on the Kacho Plain," an indiscretion for which he was roundly criticized. But facts are facts, and there was no disputing that to increase production in Kochi, the first step would have to be curbing production losses by diseases and pests.

## The Post-Season Cultivation Program

All the scientists and technicians in the prefectural crop production, agricultural testing, and agricultural cooperatives divisions joined in a common effort to guide the prefecture's farmers in carrying out this post-season cultivation program. The program was to be carried out in phases, covering a different part of the prefecture during each of three successive years.

They took advantage of the fact that the yellow rice borer does not feed on plants other than rice. The idea was to eliminate the rice borers through starvation by ensuring the absence of all rice plants during the first period of rice borer emergence. Farmers in each area were forbidden from planting rice until July 8 of that year.

Before the prefectural edict was issued, local farmers lodged a hundred objections against post-season rice cultivation, but once the policy was set, the farmers of Kochi Prefecture made a full about-face and gave their total, undivided cooperation. It was an enterprise carried out on a grand scale.

## Second Thoughts on Post-Season Rice Cultivation

The outcome of the Kochi post-season cultivation program was mixed: the yellow rice borer was completely eliminated, but they were unable to increase crop production. This result became a turning point in Fukuoka's thinking.

First, he examined how well the real extent of rice borer damage had been investigated and understood initially. Damage by rice borers always tends to be overestimated since white heads of grain due to post-heading damage stand out in the field. This degree of damage is often mistakenly assumed to translate directly into harvest losses.

Even when the crop seems a total loss, damage is generally at most about thirty percent and actual losses are no more than twenty percent. The overall rate of damage over a wide area then is usually grossly overestimated. Science prefers to overlook those fields that have been spared and focus instead on severely infested fields.

Natural farming, on the other hand, devotes its attention to the fields that escape damage. This observation became foundational to Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-vegetable-disease-insect-resistance]] and his understanding of [[fukuoka-bushman-world-ecological-balance]].

## The Sacrificial Area Principle

Fukuoka observed that if one small section of a large rice field contains rice grown with lots of fertilizer, rice borers congregate on this soft, vulnerable rice. The farmer could take advantage of this behavior by collecting the insects in one area and destroying them, but what would happen if he left them alone?

Although one might expect them to spread out to surrounding fields and cause extensive damage, this just does not happen. Damage would be limited to the small sacrificial area -- maybe no more than one percent of the field under cultivation.

During the fall, sparrows gather about the ripening heads of grain, causing serious damage. If one puts out scarecrows to chase the birds away, then the farmer in the next field feels he has to put scarecrows out too. This snowballs and before you know it everyone in the village is busy chasing away sparrows. Does this mean that if no one did anything the sparrows would devastate the fields? Certainly not.

## The Initial Conditions on the Farm

Fukuoka's father gave him a hillside citrus orchard on the slopes above the family farm. The trees had been cultivated conventionally -- pruned, fertilized, and sprayed with chemicals. The soil was typical of Japanese orchards: compacted, chemically dependent, and biologically impoverished.

Fukuoka's first decision was to stop all chemical inputs. He stopped spraying pesticides, stopped applying synthetic fertilizers, and stopped pruning the trees. The reaction was immediate and dramatic. The trees, accustomed to artificial support, struggled.

## The Difficult Transition

The first few years were discouraging. The citrus trees showed signs of decline. Pests attacked the fruit. Local farmers and agricultural experts pointed to Fukuoka's orchard as proof that his methods were foolish. "If you don't spray, the trees will die," they told him.

But Fukuoka persisted. He observed carefully, watching how the ecosystem responded to the removal of human interventions. Gradually, he noticed something unexpected: the natural processes of the orchard were beginning to reassert themselves. Birds that had been driven away by pesticides returned and began feeding on insects. Weeds that had been suppressed by cultivation grew, but their root systems began to loosen and aerate the soil.

## The Rice and Barley Revolution

Fukuoka's most important experiments were with rice and winter grains. He developed a method that eliminated plowing, transplanting, and flooding:

1. In the fall, after the rice harvest, he scattered rice straw on the field

2. He broadcast clover seeds, which grew as a living mulch through the winter

3. He scattered barley or wheat seeds directly onto the field

4. In the spring, the barley grew to maturity while the clover was temporarily suppressed

5. After the barley harvest, he scattered rice seeds in clay pellets directly onto the standing clover

6. The clover was eventually shaded out by the growing rice, and nitrogen was fixed in the soil for the next grain crop

## The 1950 Breakthrough

By 1950, Fukuoka had achieved complete no-tillage cultivation of rice and winter grains. This was the key breakthrough that demonstrated the viability of natural farming. His yields were comparable to or better than those of conventional farmers, while requiring a fraction of the labor and no chemical inputs.

The technique was deceptively simple: scatter straw, scatter seeds, let nature do the rest. But behind this simplicity lay decades of careful observation and a deep understanding of [[fukuoka-why-tillage-destroys-soil]].

## The Orchard Ecosystem

Simultaneously, Fukuoka was developing his orchard into a multi-layered ecosystem. He planted a border of trees and shrubs to serve as a windbreak and habitat for beneficial insects. He allowed weeds to grow freely, observing which species appeared and how they interacted with the fruit trees.

Over time, the orchard became a self-sustaining ecosystem that produced fruit, eggs, and vegetables without any external inputs. More than twenty types of trees and several hundred kinds of weeds grew together in a complex, interdependent community.

## Learning from Failure

Fukuoka was not afraid of failure. Every setback was an opportunity to learn. When a crop failed, he asked not "how can I prevent this?" but "what is nature trying to tell me?" This attitude of openness to nature's feedback was the key to his success. He never forced a solution but waited for nature to reveal it.
