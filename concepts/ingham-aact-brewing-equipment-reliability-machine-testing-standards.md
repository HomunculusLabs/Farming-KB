# AACT Brewing Equipment Reliability and Machine Testing Standards

## Overview

Not all  brewers produce equal results. Dr.  emphasizes that the reliability and consistency of  equipment is critical for achieving predictable   s-effects-soil-biology benefits. Her framework for evaluating tea machines focuses on reproducibility, extraction efficiency, and the ability to maintain the full soil food web — bacteria, fungi, protozoa,  — through the brewing process.

## The Core Problem: Testimonials Are Not Evidence

Ingham is blunt about the inadequacy of current machine evaluation:

- "A few testimonials are not adequate to be able to say a machine makes good tea."
- Many manufacturers claim their machines produce effective compost tea based on anecdotal reports from users.
- Testimonials cannot distinguish between the effects of the tea and other variables (weather, , crop variety, application timing).
- Without controlled testing, there is no way to know whether a machine is extracting beneficial organisms or merely producing nutrient-rich water.

## What Every Machine Manufacturer Should Be Able to Answer

Ingham identifies critical questions that any compost tea machine manufacturer should be able to address:

1. **What organisms does the machine extract?** Does it extract only bacteria, or does it also extract fungi, protozoa, and nematodes?
2. **Is the machine honest about its limitations?** If a machine only extracts bacteria, the manufacturer should say so.
3. **How reliable is the machine under identical conditions?** Can it produce the same quality tea when exactly the same conditions are used two, three, or four times in a row?
4. **How good must the compost be?** What [[aact-compost-quality-standards-ingham]] is required to achieve decent levels of bacteria in the tea?
5. **Can it prevent specific diseases?** If the manufacturer claims effectiveness against black spot or other diseases, do they have data?

## Reliability Testing Protocol

### Reproducibility Under Identical Conditions

The fundamental test of any brewing machine is consistency:

- **Same compost**: Use compost from the same batch, divided into identical portions.
- **Same water**: Identical water source, temperature, and volume.
- **Same ingredients**: Identical quantities of kelp, molasses, fish hydrolysate, and other additives.
- **Same brew time**: Identical brewing duration.
- **Same aeration**: Same air pump, same air stone configuration, same flow rate.

Under these identical conditions, run the brew multiple times (minimum 3-4 repetitions) and assess the resulting tea using direct microscopy. Key metrics to compare between runs:

- Total bacterial biomass (µg/ml)
- Total fungal biomass (µg/ml)
- Active vs. total organism ratios
- Protozoa counts (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates)
- Nematode presence
- [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] levels throughout the brew
- pH at start and end of brew

### Single-Species vs. Whole-Community Extraction

Different machines have different extraction capabilities:

- **Bacterial extraction**: Most machines can extract bacteria from compost. This is the minimum requirement.
- **Fungal extraction**: Many machines fail to extract  intact. Shearing forces in pumps and impellers can fragment fungal networks.
- **Protozoa extraction**: Protozoa are delicate and may be killed by turbulence or aeration patterns.
- **Nematode extraction**: Very few machines successfully extract nematodes. These require gentle extraction conditions.

Ingham notes that "if a machine makes tea that only extracts bacteria, then the machine manufacturer ought to tell you that." The failure to distinguish between bacterial tea and full food web tea leads to inconsistent results in the field.

## Compost Quality and Machine Performance

### The Compost-Machine Relationship

Machine performance depends critically on compost quality:

- **How good does the compost have to be?** Different machines require different compost quality thresholds to produce good tea.
- **Compost diversity matters**: "Well-made, local compost supplies tens of thousands of species of bacteria" plus fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. The machine must be capable of extracting this diversity.
- **Thermal, worm, and static composts**: All three types can have great organism communities if properly made. The machine should work with any of them.

### Water Quality Requirements

- **Chlorine**: Municipal water with chlorine will kill organisms. Water must be dechlorinated (aeration for 1+ hour or allowed to sit overnight).
- **Mineral content**: Very pure water (RO or distilled) may lack minerals needed by organisms.
- **Temperature**: Water temperature affects both organism growth during brewing and dissolved oxygen solubility.
- **pH**: Starting pH should be neutral (6.5-7.5). Significant pH shifts during brewing indicate problems.

## AACT Standards Development

### SFI's Approach to Standards

Ingham's Soil Foodweb Inc. worked to establish Actively [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] Tea standards "using solid scientific approaches":

- **Minimum organism thresholds**: Minimum levels of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes needed for [[aact-disease-suppression-mechanisms-ingham]].
- **Fungal biomass minimum**: 2 µg/ml fungal biomass is the cutoff for reliable [[cervantes-pest-disease-prevention-overview]] (mostly mildew and blight). "Prevention always works above 2 microgram, not always below."
- **More is better for fungi**: Ingham confirms "Yep" when asked if more fungal biomass is better. As high as 800 µg/ml has been recorded and could be diluted from 5 gallons per acre to 2 gallons per acre while maintaining effectiveness.
- **Pathogen screening**: Standards include testing for E. coli and other human pathogens.

### The Importance of the Whole Food Web

Ingham repeatedly emphasizes that the whole food web is needed:

- "The WHOLE food web is needed to prevent the easy-to-prevent-diseases as well as the harder-to-prevent-diseases."
- "Bacteria can account for only a few of the preventative mechanisms that work in soil."
- "You will not continue to see benefit over time" if only one or two species are added — "the improvement stalls because you have to build the food web back all the way."
- "Where people have had failures with compost tea, it is always where they were not getting the full set of organisms extracted from the tea."

## Extract vs. AACT: A Critical Distinction

Ingham distinguishes between simple extracts and true actively aerated compost tea:

- **Extracts**: Compost soaked or stirred in water, then strained. Organisms are present but not actively growing. Benefits are short-lived.
- **AACT**: Compost brewed with aeration and food sources for 12-24 hours. Organisms multiply during brewing. Benefits are more substantial and longer-lasting.
- **Nutrient extraction is not tea**: If the process extracts soluble nutrients but does not support organism growth, "then it isn't compost tea." The organisms are the active ingredient, not the nutrients.

## Practical Recommendations for Buyers

1. **Demand test data**: Ask for SFI test results or equivalent showing organism levels in tea produced by the machine.
2. **Test yourself**: Brew tea multiple times with identical inputs and send samples to a lab.
3. **Compare organisms, not just nutrients**: A nutrient-rich tea without the right organisms will not provide disease suppression.
4. **Check dissolved oxygen**: The machine must maintain DO above 6 mg/L throughout the entire brew.
5. **Evaluate the full food web**: Ensure the machine can extract and maintain bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and ideally nematodes.
6. **Beware of marketing claims**: Testimonials and anecdotal evidence are not substitutes for controlled testing.
