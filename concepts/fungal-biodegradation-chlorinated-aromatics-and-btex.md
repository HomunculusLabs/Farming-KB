## Overview

Chlorinated monoaromatic compounds and BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene isomers) represent major classes of soil and groundwater contaminants. Fungi, particularly white-rot basidiomycetes, offer promising degradation capabilities through both ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic enzymatic pathways. These contaminants arise primarily from petroleum product releases, industrial solvents, herbicides, and wood preservatives.

## Sources and Environmental Significance

### Chlorinated Monoaromatics

Chlorinated aromatic compounds enter the environment from multiple sources:
- **Herbicides**: 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T (phenoxy herbicides), chlorpropham
- **Wood preservatives**: Pentachlorophenol (PCP), extensively used for treating timber
- **Solvents**: Chlorobenzene derivatives used in industrial processes
- **Fumigants**: Chlorinated compounds used in soil treatment
- **Dye intermediates**: Chlorinated aromatic precursors in manufacturing

### BTEX Compounds

BTEX contamination primarily originates from:
- Petroleum leaks from underground storage tanks
- Solvent-based paints and coatings
- Manufactured gas plant sites
- Gasoline spills and refinery operations

These compounds are water-soluble, mobile in groundwater, and raise significant human health concerns due to their toxicity and carcinogenic potential.

## Bioavailability and Soil Interactions

The bioavailability of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) in soil depends on both the chemical nature of the pollutant and soil parameters. Key factors include:

- **Sorption**: Hydrophobic compounds like PCP sorb strongly to soil organic matter (humic and fulvic acids), reducing bioavailability. At high pH, PCP becomes a water-soluble salt, increasing availability.
- **Aging**: Longer contact time allows pollutants to diffuse into soil micropores (< 1 μm), rendering them inaccessible even to extracellular enzymes.
- **Soil organic matter**: Humic and syringic acid decrease PCP transformation by *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* in liquid culture (Stevens, Badkoubi & Murarka, 1996).

## Methylation as a Detoxification Route

Two independent methylation mechanisms in *P. chrysosporium* transform chlorinated phenols:
1. **Chloromethane-dependent**: Using chloromethane as methyl donor
2. **S-adenosylmethionine-dependent**: A purified O-methyltransferase catalyzes 4-O-methylation

The substrate specificity of these enzymes extends to xenobiotics — 2,4-dichlorophenol and 2,4-dibromophenol were methylated almost as readily as natural substrates. Methylation of 2,4-D degradation intermediates may regenerate substrates susceptible to LiP and MnP attack (Valli & Gold, 1991).

## Field Applications and Limitations

### Soil Remediation

Field studies demonstrate that white-rot fungi can degrade chlorinated monoaromatics in soil under both sterile and non-sterile conditions. However, complete mineralization in non-sterile soil does not occur. Typical degradation levels are:
- 14% for PAHs (Martens et al., 1999)
- 9–29% for PCP (Ruttimann-Johnson & Lamar, 1997; Tuomela et al., 1999)

The remaining pollutant fraction becomes bound to soil organic matter through covalent complexation catalyzed by fungal oxidative enzymes.

### Toxicity Considerations

Toxic intermediates can be produced during remediation. PCP-contaminated soil treated with *P. chrysosporium* showed compounds toxic to *Bacillus megaterium* after 6 weeks (McGrath & Singleton, 2000). This underscores the need for combined chemical and ecotoxicological assessment of remediated soils.

### Complexation as Remediation Endpoint

Complexation of POPs to soil organic matter reduces bioavailability and toxicity, even without complete mineralization. Soil remediated by fungal inoculants may pose lower risk than total contaminant concentrations suggest, particularly for [[white-rot-fungi-bioremediation]] — ligninolytic enzyme systems
