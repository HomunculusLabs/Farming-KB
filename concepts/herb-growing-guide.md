## Overview

Herbs are among the most rewarding plants to grow, offering fresh flavor, fragrance, and medicinal benefits with relatively low effort. Many herbs are compact, pest-resistant, and adaptable to containers, making them ideal for any growing situation. This guide covers perennial vs annual herbs, culinary uses, preserving methods, and indoor growing.

## Perennial vs Annual Herbs

Understanding the lifespan of herbs is essential for planning your garden and expectations.

### Perennial Herbs
Return year after year in appropriate climate zones. Some are evergreen; others die back to the ground in winter and regrow in spring.

| Herb | Hardiness Zone | Notes |
|------|---------------|-------|
| Rosemary | 7-10 | Tender; overwinter indoors in colder zones |
| Thyme | 4-9 | Low-growing, woody stems, very hardy |
| Oregano | 5-10 | Spreads vigorously; excellent for drying |
| Sage | 4-8 | Woody shrub; replace every 3-4 years |
| Mint | 3-9 | Extremely invasive — always container-grow |
| Chives | 3-9 | Clump-forming, divide every 3 years |
| Lavender | 5-9 | Needs excellent drainage; short-lived in humidity |
| Lemon Balm | 3-9 | Mint family, can be invasive |
| Tarragon (French) | 4-9 | Dies back in winter; reliable returner |
| Winter Savory | 5-8 | Hardy, peppery flavor |
| Fennel | 5-9 | Perennial in mild zones; self-seeds freely |

### Annual Herbs
Complete their life cycle in one growing season. Must be replanted each year, though some self-seed readily.

| Herb | Notes |
|------|-------|
| Basil | Heat-loving; blacken at 40F; most popular culinary herb |
| Cilantro/Coriander | Bolts quickly in heat; sow every 2-3 weeks |
| Dill | Self-seeds prolifically; attracts swallowtail butterflies |
| Parsley | Biennial (treated as annual); produces leaves year one |
| Summer Savory | Lighter flavor than winter savory |
| Borage | Edible flowers; self-seeds freely; attracts pollinators |
| Chamomile (German) | Annual; excellent tea herb; self-seeds |
| Calendula | Edible flowers; medicinal; self-seeds freely |

### Biennial Herbs
- **Parsley**: Leaves best in year one; flowers and sets seed in year two
- **Caraway**: Harvest seeds in second year

## Culinary Herbs by Use

### Mediterranean / Italian
- Basil, oregano, rosemary, thyme, parsley
- Essential for pasta sauces, pizza, grilled meats
- Basil is the standout annual; oregano and thyme are perennial staples

### French / fines herbes
- Chives, tarragon, parsley, chervil
- Delicate flavors added at end of cooking
- Chervil is harder to find but worth growing

### Asian
- Cilantro, Thai basil, lemongrass, garlic chives, perilla (shiso), Vietnamese coriander
- Lemongrass is perennial in zones 9+; grow in containers elsewhere
- Cilantro bolts in heat — see succession tips below

### Mexican / Latin American
- Cilantro, epazote, Mexican oregano (different plant than Mediterranean oregano), culantro
- Epazote: strong, acquired flavor; used with beans to reduce gas

### Herbal Teas
- Chamomile (German annual, Roman perennial), lemon balm, peppermint, spearmint, lemon verbena, bee balm, holy basil (tulsi), anise hyssop

### Medicinal Herbs
- Echinacea, calendula, yarrow, valerian, holy basil, elderberry, goldenseal
- Many have deep traditional use and growing pharmacological evidence
- Research specific herbs thoroughly before medicinal use

## Growing Requirements

### Soil
- Most herbs prefer well-draining soil
- Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano, lavender) prefer lean, sandy soil — do not over-fertilize
- Basil and parsley prefer richer, more fertile soil
- pH 6.0-7.0 for most herbs
- Mint thrives in moist soil; lavender demands sharp drainage

### Sun
- Most herbs need 6+ hours of direct sun
- Shade-tolerant herbs: mint, lemon balm, chervil, sweet cicely, cilantro (in heat)
- Indoor herbs need a bright south-facing window or grow lights

### Water
- Established Mediterranean herbs are drought-tolerant once rooted
- Basil demands consistent moisture — wilts dramatically when dry
- Mint prefers consistently moist soil
- Lavender and rosemary prefer to dry out between waterings
- Overwatering is the most common cause of indoor herb death

## Drying and Preserving

### Air Drying
- Harvest herbs in mid-morning after dew dries but before heat of day
- For best flavor, harvest just before flowering (peak essential oil content)
- Tie small bunches (4-6 stems) and hang upside down in a warm, dry, dark, well-ventilated area
- Dry until stems snap easily — usually 1-3 weeks depending on humidity
- Herbs that dry well: oregano, thyme, rosemary, sage, lavender, mint, dill, savory

### Dehydrator
- Set to 95-105F for leafy herbs, 125F for seeds
- Dries faster and more consistently than air drying
- Good for basil (air drying can cause mold due to high moisture content)
- Typically 4-8 hours depending on herb and thickness

### Freezing
- Best for herbs that lose flavor when dried: basil, cilantro, chives, dill, parsley
- Chop herbs, pack into ice cube trays, cover with water or olive oil, freeze
- Pop cubes out and store in freezer bags for up to 6 months
- Alternatively, freeze whole leaves in bags

### Herb-Infused Oils and Vinegars
- Pack clean, dry herbs into sterilized jars
- Cover with olive oil or vinegar (wine or cider vinegar)
- Store in a cool, dark place
- Use within 2-3 months for best quality
- Herb vinegars are excellent for salad dressings
- Garlic and herb oils must be refrigerated and used within 1 week (botulism risk)

### Herb Butter
- Mix softened butter with finely chopped fresh herbs
- Roll into logs, wrap in wax paper, freeze
- Slice off patties as needed
- Combinations: parsley-lemon, basil-garlic, dill-chive

## Indoor Growing

Growing herbs indoors extends the harvest year-round, especially for tender perennials and annuals.

### Best Herbs for Indoors
- Basil, chives, mint, parsley, oregano, thyme, rosemary, cilantro
- Avoid: dill and fennel (too tall), lavender (needs specific conditions)

### Light Requirements
- Minimum 6 hours of bright, direct light daily
- South-facing window is ideal in northern hemisphere
- Supplement with full-spectrum LED grow lights if natural light is insufficient
- Use a timer for 12-14 hours of light per day with supplemental lighting
