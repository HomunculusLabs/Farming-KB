## Swales Design and Construction

## Overview

Swales are one of the most versatile and under-used [[permaculture]] and ecological land management. They are long, level excavations built on contour that intercept overland water flow, hold it temporarily, and allow it to infiltrate into the soil as groundwater recharge. Swales transform runoff into infiltration, erosion into soil building, and barren slopes into productive, tree-planted landscapes. Their applications range from small garden ridges to broad agricultural [[swale]] is a **level ditch or broad drain** intended to first pool, then absorb all surplus water flow. Unlike drains, swales are **never intended to flow** — their function is to hold water until it infiltrates into the soil. They are the antithesis of conventional drainage, which aims to remove water from the landscape as quickly as possible.

### Physical Characteristics

Swales can vary greatly in width and treatment:

- **Small ridges** in gardens
- **Rock-piles** across slopes
- **Deliberately excavated hollows** in flatlands and low-slope landscapes
- **Broad earthworks** spanning tens to hundreds of meters in agricultural settings

## How Swales Work

### The Infiltration Process

1. Overland flow from rainfall or runoff enters the swale
2. Water pools in the level excavation, which may be 1–2 meters (4–6 feet) wide at the base
3. The swale base is specifically designed to promote infiltration — ripped, gravelled, sanded, loosened, or dressed with [[swale-design]]
5. Infiltrated water becomes groundwater recharge, available to tree root systems

### Why Trees Are Essential

Trees are **not optional** components of swale systems — they are essential. Without trees, several problems develop:

- **Waterlogging** — absorbed water has no pathway for evapotranspiration
- **Local rainfall deficit** — water stored in soil but not transpired means less moisture returning to the local atmosphere
- **Salt concentration** — in arid areas, evaporation from bare swale surfaces concentrates salts

Trees perform multiple critical functions in swale systems:

- **Evapotranspiration** — returning stored soil water to the atmosphere as moisture, supporting local rainfall cycles
- **Overshading** — cooling swale soils and reducing evaporation losses
- **Root penetration** — creating channels for deeper water infiltration over time
- **[[humus]] accumulation** — leaf litter builds soil organic matter, further improving water retention
- **Wind protection** — sheltered microclimates within the swale and spoil bank

### The Successional Nature of Swales

Swales are inherently **temporary features** in the long-term successional development of a landscape:

- Trees eventually supplant the water-holding function of swales as they mature
- Swales are **precursors to the rehabilitation of normal forests** in their region
- In humid forests (e.g., Tasmania), natural swales generate much larger trees, provide level access ways, and support thick humus and specialized plants on swale floors
- Orchids, fungi, and ginseng do better in the humid, shaded environment of forest swales

## Construction Guidelines

### Contour Alignment

All swales are built on **contour or dead-level survey lines**. This is the single most critical construction requirement. Even slight deviations from level will cause water to flow to one end, creating erosion at the low point and dry conditions at the high point.

### Base Treatment

The swale base must be treated to maximize infiltration:

- **In sandy or volcanic soils:** Widen and shallow the swale — these soils infiltrate quickly but hold less per unit depth
- **In clay-fraction soils:** Narrow and deepen the swale — these soils infiltrate slowly but hold water longer
- **Never compact or seal** swale bases or banks (unlike dams)
- **Can be gravelled, ripped, or loosened** to actively assist water infiltration
- **Base can be uneven** in width and may rise and fall, provided the lip remains level
- **Gypsum** can be applied to clay soils to improve infiltration

### Spoil Bank Placement

The excavated material (spoil) from swale construction is:

- **Normally mounded downhill** from the swale
- **In flat areas**, spoil can be spread rather than mounded
- The spoil bank creates a planting surface above the wet swale base
- Trees requiring better drainage (chestnuts, citrus) should be planted on the spoil bank rather than in the swale base

## Spacing Between Swales

The distance between swales (the run-off or mulch-planted surface between them) depends on rainfall:

| Rainfall | Swale Width (Base) | Interswale Distance | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| > 127 cm (50") | 1–2 m (4–6") | 3–18 m (12–60') | 3:1 |
| ~ 75 cm (30") | 1–2 m (4–6") | 6–12 m (20–40') | 6:1 |
| ~ 25 cm (10") | 1–2 m (4–6") | 18–36 m (60–120') | 18:1 |
| < 25 cm (10") | 1–2 m (4–6') | 18–60 m (60–200') | up to 20:1 |

As a general rule, the interswale distance ranges from **3 to 20 times** the average swale width, depending on rainfall. In humid areas, the interswale is fully planted with hardy or mulch-producing species. In very dry areas, it may be fairly bare, existing mainly to direct water into swales.

## Integration with Other Systems

### Urban and Suburban Applications

Every sub-humid and arid townscape can benefit from fitting roads and paved areas with swales:

- **Road runoff** is captured and infiltrated rather than contributing to stormwater
- **Tree lines** along swales shade pavement and reduce urban heat islands
- **Fuel, mulch, and food** are produced by swale-planted trees
- **Roof tank overflow** can be led to swales
- **[[gaias-garden-water-harvesting-swales-and-greywater|Greywater]]** can be directed to swales (if boron detergents are not used)

The **Village Homes** development in Davis, California (designed by [[michael-corbett]]) is a landmark example:

- Swales accept all road and excess roof runoff
- Hundreds of productive trees are supported within the settlement
- Water penetrated soils to 6 meters (19 feet) deep after a few years
- Swales were self-shaded after 3–4 years of tree growth

### Livestock Integration

Animals can be integrated with swale systems:

- **Chicken houses** positioned over swales allow manure to fertilize swale plantings directly
- **Manures from animals** placed in swales provide nutrients via water transport for crops and associated trees
- **Canals feeding wet terraces** can carry nutrient-rich water from animal areas to swale crops

### Mulch and Nutrient Pits

Two pit systems within swales are particularly useful:

1. **Mulch and manure pits** — filled with organic matter for heavy nutrient feeders (yams, bananas, etc.)
2. **Sealed water reserves** — oil drums, plastic liners, or tyre ponds for watering young plants; these can be planted with lotus, kangkong, watercress, Chinese water chestnut, and similar crops

## Planting Strategies

### Windbreak Swales

On windy sites, every **sixth to tenth swale** can be designated as a windbreak:

- **Plant on the downslope side** of the swale to allow mulch collection in the swale base
- **Use hardy, useful species:** Casuarina, Acacia, Leucaena, silky oak, pine, cypress
- **Ridges** should always have windbreak and condensation plants of hardy species

### Access Integration

Keeping the swale width to the **tractor, donkey cart, foot track, or wheelbarrow** access width planned for the site allows:

- Open access along the swale for maintenance and harvesting
- Regular widening at intervals for plant assemblies, ponds, or mulch pits
- Flexible placement of trees with higher water needs at widened sections
