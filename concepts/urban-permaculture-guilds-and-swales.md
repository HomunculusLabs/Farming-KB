## Guild Design in Practice

Designing an effective guild requires understanding the specific needs and outputs of each component species. The "three sisters" guild (corn, beans, and squash) is the classic example: corn provides a trellis for beans, beans fix nitrogen for corn, and squash suppresses weeds and conserves moisture. In urban settings, guilds can be adapted for container gardens, rooftop plantings, and small courtyard spaces. A simple urban guild might combine a dwarf fruit tree (apples or figs) with nitrogen-fixing ground cover (clover), pest-repelling herbs (chives, garlic), and accumulator plants (comfrey) that mine nutrients from deep soil.

## Urban Microclimate Considerations

Urban environments create unique microclimates that affect both guild composition and swale performance.
The urban heat island effect — where paved surfaces, buildings, and reduced vegetation raise temperatures 2-5°C above surrounding rural areas — extends growing seasons but increases water demand and heat stress on plants.
Tall buildings create shade patterns that shift throughout the day and across seasons, making careful sector analysis essential for guild placement.
Wind tunnel effects between buildings can desiccate plants and accelerate evaporation from swales; strategic placement of shrubs and small trees as windbreaks mitigates this effect.
Rooftop and balcony environments add wind exposure, shallow soil depth, and reflected heat from surrounding surfaces as additional design considerations.
Reflective surfaces (light-coloured walls, windows) can increase light intensity for shaded gardens but also increase heat stress.
Understanding these microclimatic factors allows the urban permaculture designer to select appropriate species for each niche and position water-catching earthworks where they will be most effective.
