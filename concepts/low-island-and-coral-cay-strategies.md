# Low Island and Coral Cay Strategies

Low island and coral cay strategies encompass the specialized permaculture design approaches needed for sustainable human habitation on atolls, coral sand cays, and sandy alkaline coasts within 28° of the equator. These fragile environments demand careful resource management, particularly regarding freshwater, soil fertility, hurricane protection, and food production.

## Overview

Many peoples live on atolls, where the unique combination of small land area, alkaline coral soils, limited freshwater, and exposure to tropical cyclones creates extreme design challenges. Successful strategies must simultaneously address water security, soil improvement, food diversity, coastal protection, and energy independence while working within the severe constraints of these environments.

## Environmental Conditions

### Soils
- **pH 8.0–9.5** (strongly alkaline)
- Thin coralline sand layer over caliche/calcrete hardpan
- Sand abrasion from wind
- **Mineral deficiencies**: especially iron, zinc, molybdenum, and boron
- Calcrete is worsened by superphosphate application

### Freshwater
- Sole natural sources: biological storages (baobabs, coconuts) and the freshwater lens
- About **12 coconut trees** provide one person's annual drinking water
- The freshwater lens floats on seawater beneath the sand, 0.75–1.0 m down, 1.0–2.0 m deep as a saturated sand layer
- The beach berm sits 2–4 m above sea level; the inner atoll 2–3 m — making the water lens extremely vulnerable to pollution

### Risks
- Hurricane erosion and damage to plantations and coasts
- Water lens pollution from human activities
- Poor nutrition from limited diet (typically high in carbohydrates and oxalic acids)

## Soil Improvement Strategies

### pH Reduction
To counteract alkalinity in garden soils and planting holes:
- Apply **elemental sulphur**
- Apply **iron sulphates**
- Add **humus** in generous quantities

### Humus Sources
Native and pioneer vegetation that yield abundant litter for gardens and tree planting holes:
- Palm fronds
- Coconut husks
- Tree trunks
- Leaf litter from:
  - *Casuarina equisetifolia* (ironwood/whistling pine)
  - Sea grape (*Coccoloba unifera*)
  - Coastal shrubs: *Scaevola*, *Tournefortia*, *Pemphis*
  - Mangroves
  - *Barringtonia* trees

### Phosphate Management
- Seabird guano usually provides sufficient phosphates if colonies of terns, gulls, boobies, frigate birds, and shearwaters are protected or encouraged
- Where seabirds are absent, domestic poultry can substitute: pigeon, quail, pheasant, geese, ducks, and chickens (feral predators are usually absent on islands)

## Planting Trees on Coral Cays

### Pit Preparation

To plant trees on coral cays:
1. Clear a patch of topsoil
2. Break open the caliche
3. Dig a pit approximately **40 × 40 cm** and **60–80 cm deep**
4. Create a humus pile of domestic and plant wastes
5. Add sulphur and trace mineral elements
6. Plant a coconut or other seedling tree

### Root Development
Tree roots spread out above the caliche layer, while feeder roots penetrate below the caliche to access the water lens. Trees are thus well-anchored and easily obtain water.

## Pit Garden Systems

### Design

The best approach for atoll gardens is a pit garden approximately **8 m × 15 m**, sloped for stability to about **2 m deep**. The base is often damp or wet. Sloped sides are stabilized as steps with coconut logs or caliche, and the excavated spoil is banked around the rim. Thick mulch is applied to the base and behind the stepped terraces.

### Vertical Zoning

Plants are arranged from base to crown according to moisture requirements:

| Zone | Elevation | Species |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Base** (damp, mulch) | Ground level | Watercress, parsley, chives, Brassica, taro, kangkong, salad greens |
| **First Terrace** | 18–25 cm above base | Tomatoes, peppers, taro, sweet corn, beans, peas, taller crops |
| **Second Terrace** | 25–60 cm above base | Banana, papaya, sweet potato, cassava (all provide mulch) |
| **Third (highest) Terrace** | Top | Cassava, sweet potato, banana, dry-tolerant vegetables, mulch trees |

### Mulch Trees for Upper Terraces
- *Leucaena leucocephala*
- *Glyricidia*
- *Tipuana tipu*
- *Moringa oleifera*
- Local tree and shrub legumes

These provide leaf mulch and partial shade. Palms supply frond mulch and high shade. Vines (passionfruit, four-winged bean) climb on the support trees.

### Alternative: Log Box Gardens

Under light palm-legume canopies, boxes made from palm logs hold thick mulch and household waste for surface gardens. These beds support:
- Potato
- Yam
- Sweet potato
- Normal vegetable crops

The thicker the mulch, the less watering needed.

### Essentials for Atoll Gardens
The four essentials for productive atoll gardens are:
1. **Log boxes** or pit structures for soil containment
2. **Pits** for moisture retention
3. **Thick mulch** for soil improvement and moisture conservation
4. **High shade canopy** for microclimate moderation

### Staple Foods
- Coconut
- Root crops (taro, cassava, sweet potato, yam)
- Fruits (banana, papaya, breadfruit)
- Salad vegetables
- Fish and shellfish from lagoons and coasts

## Water Lens Protection

### Zoning Principle

The critical design principle for atoll settlements: **keep polluting activities to the periphery and preserve the interior for natural vegetation.**

- Settlement, toilet areas, processing, livestock, and gardens → **periphery**
- Dense natural stands of food and native trees → **interior**
- Interior must be free of pesticides, industrial pollutants, and fuels

A polluting source at the center of the island can affect the entire water lens, all plants, and all people.

### Increasing Water Supply

1. **Roof run-off collection** into tanks (above or below ground), constructed from reinforced cement-coral-sand mix
2. **Deep mulches** combined with abundant leaf-producing plants (coconut, banana, arrowroot, papaya, legumes)
3. **Leaf ferment seals (gleys)** in coral pits:
   - Line a gently-sloped coral pit with 20 cm of shredded banana, papaya, and soft green leaf material
   - Cover with plastic until fermenting (4–5 days)
   - Fill with water pumped from the freshwater lens
   - Start with small-scale trials, then scale up

### Health Precautions

- Surface ponds can be sources of cross-infection in children
- **Swim in the lagoon** (not in freshwater ponds)
- **Drink tank water** (collected from roofs)
- Seawater serves for many toilet uses

## Hurricane Damage Prevention

### Reef Entry Design

When creating gaps in reefs for boat access, great care must be taken:
- Reef gaps should be cut **on the slant** through the reef
- Position entries in the **east or west quarters** (most sheltered sectors)
- South of the equator, winds blow southeast to northwest; north of the equator, northeast to southwest
- Gaps should be **just wide enough** (6–10 m) to admit a vessel or barge

### Airstrip Design

- Airstrips should be aligned about **20° off prevailing winds**
- **Both ends and sides** should be lined with tall palms and trees
- Coastal borders especially need dense tree plantings
- Light planes drop in using rudders to straighten up below tree crown level
- **Critical warning**: Airstrips carelessly made have destroyed whole islands when hurricane winds cut them in two following the line of the airstrip

### Coastal Shelterbelt Sequence

The sandy coasts of all atolls and cays need a sequence of perennial shelterbelts to hold the shoreline against hurricanes. From beach to interior:

1. **Beach level**: *Convolvulus* (*Ipomoea pes-caprae*) and beach pea
2. **Beach berm**: Dense shrubbery of vines, *Tournefortia*, *Scaevola*; in sheltered bays, mangroves
3. **Back beach**: 5–6 tree deep layer of:
   - Coconut palm
   - *Casuarina equisetifolia*
   - *Coccoloba* (sea grape)
   - *Barringtonia*
   - Other hardy beach trees

This dense frontline shelterbelt protects the interior settlement, gardens, and freshwater lens from hurricane-force winds and storm surge.

## Design Priorities Summary

The additional design inputs needed for sustainable low island habitation are:

1. **Extend vegetable and fruit crop** diversity beyond staples
2. **Extend water storage and conservation** through tanks, mulches, and gleys
3. **Conserve natural vegetation** and unique birds, reptiles, and lagoon/reef fauna
4. **Use shallow marine waters** for aquaculture and pond fish
5. **Develop energy resources locally** (solar, wind, coconut oil, wave)
