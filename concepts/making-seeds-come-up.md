## Making Seeds Come Up

Reliable seed germination outdoors should not be difficult. The only conditions in which it should be hard to sprout seeds are the still-chilled soils of early spring and the high heat of midsummer when the earth can dry out rapidly. Even these stressful conditions can be surmounted with the right techniques. The key is understanding what seeds need and creating conditions that match those needs as closely as possible.

## The Laboratory Standard

The best way to understand seed germination is to look at how a germ lab does it. [[solomon-seed-sprouting-conditions-laboratory-germination-technique]] is accurate and duplicatable — two different labs testing samples from the same bag of seed should produce the same result. The protocol for each species specifies:

- **[[query-what-is-the-ideal-temperature-range-for-growing-cannabis]]**: Usually constant, occasionally cycling (16 hours higher, 8 hours lower, mimicking daytime heating and nighttime cooling)
- **Moisture level**: Pre-moistened sterile media at a precise level — all surplus water squeezed out
- **Days allowed**: Each species has a maximum number of days; seeds emerging after this cutoff are considered too weak to survive field conditions
- **Media type**: Usually sterile blotting paper or fine soil mix

The batch that germinates quickest also germinates at the highest percentage. Speed and completeness of germination are both indicators of seed vigor.

## The Three Enemies of Germination

### 1. Overwatering

Every time you water, the soil temperature drops, slowing the seed's progress. Worse, wet, cool soil enhances **damping-off** — a fungus disease that invades and kills seedlings before and after emergence. When the stems of tiny seedlings pinch off at the soil line, you are seeing damping-off. This disease does not thrive in dryish soil.

**[[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]]** is another moisture-related disease that attacks cucurbits (squash, zucchini, melons, cucumber) in particular. It thrives in damp, cool conditions and can attack cucurbit seedlings before emergence.

**Key insight**: It is wise to avoid watering [[solomon-sprouting-seeds-and-early-season-nutrition]] unless they actually need it.

### 2. Temperature Too Low

When the soil is too cold, the seedling is slowed while soil-dwelling fungi and diseases that thrive in cold conditions attack it. The seedling dies before it can emerge. This is the single most common cause of seed failure in early spring.

**Watering makes this worse** because every watering lowers soil temperature.

### 3. Soil Crusts and Compaction

Crust formation and soil compaction create physical barriers that the fragile emerging shoot cannot push through. The solution is proper [[soil-tilth-and-seedbed-preparation]] — see [[soil-crust-formation-and-tilth]].

## The Ready-to-Till Moisture Test

How do you know if soil needs watering before sowing? Use the ready-to-till test:

When soil is loosened with a shovel, tiller, or fork, its capillary channels are broken. Moisture cannot rise from below to replace what evaporates from the surface. This means moisture evaporates quickly from loose, recently worked soil.

**The footprint test**: Walk across a newly worked bed. If your footprints stay moist (dark), the soil has adequate moisture for sowing. If the footprints dry out quickly (light-colored), the soil is too dry and needs watering.

**Important**: Water the bed a day or two before sowing, not immediately before. This allows the soil temperature to recover and excess moisture to drain before seeds go in.

## Watering Less Frequently

The natural fear is that if seeds dry out while sprouting, they will die. But the reality is more nuanced:

- Seeds sown into properly moist soil at the right temperature will sprout without additional watering
- The embryo sends down a root first — once the root is working, the seedling can draw its own water from deeper soil
- Additional surface watering cools the soil and promotes fungal diseases
- Overwatering kills more seeds than underwatering

### When Watering Is Actually Needed

- In very sandy soils that drain and dry rapidly
- During unusually hot, dry, windy weather after sowing
- For seeds planted very shallowly that may desiccate before roots reach moisture
- In midsummer when evaporation is extreme

## Making a Furrow

The proper technique for sowing in rows uses the hoe to create a furrow by pressing down into loose, raked soil rather than by cutting into compacted earth:

1. Rake the bed smooth and level
2. Use the hoe blade to press down into the loose soil, creating a V-shaped furrow
3. The depth of the furrow can be held quite uniform with a bit of practice
4. Sow seeds at the proper spacing in the bottom of the furrow
5. Use the flat of the hoe blade to push soil back over the seeds — either on the push or pull stroke
6. Gently firm the soil over the seeds (but do not compact it)

### Depth Guidelines

- **Small seeds** (lettuce, carrots, celery): 1/4 to 1/2 inch (6-12 mm) deep
- **Medium seeds** (beets, spinach, brassicas): 1/2 to 3/4 inch (12-20 mm) deep
- **Large seeds** (beans, peas, corn, squash): 1 to 2 inches (25-50 mm) deep

## Pre-Soaking Seeds

For seeds with hard coats or in dry conditions, pre-soaking can speed germination:

- Soak seeds in room-temperature water for 8-12 hours
- Do not soak longer than 24 hours or the seeds may drown
- Plant immediately after soaking — do not let pre-soaked seeds dry out
- Particularly helpful for peas, beans, corn, and squash

## Succession Sowing for Continuous Harvest

Rather than sowing all seeds of one crop at once, sow small amounts at regular intervals:

- **Lettuce**: Every 2-3 weeks
- **[[cabbage-family-pests]]**: Every 2 weeks for continuous heads
- **Root vegetables**: Every 3-4 weeks
- **Beans**: Every 2-3 weeks until midsummer

This ensures a continuous supply rather than a single glut, and spreads the risk — if one sowing fails due to weather, the next one may succeed.

## The Fundamental Principle

To get the best outdoor germination: **start with vigorous seeds and create soil conditions that match laboratory conditions as closely as possible.** This means:

- Warm enough soil (check temperature before sowing)
- Proper moisture (damp but not wet — use the footprint test)
- Fine seedbed (no crusts or clods)
- Correct depth (not too deep, not too shallow)
- Minimal disturbance after sowing (don't water unless necessary)
