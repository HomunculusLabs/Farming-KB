# Fungal Heavy Metal Accumulation and Bioremediation

Fungi occupy a central position in controlling heavy metal pollutants in
terrestrial ecosystems. They can immobilize metals within their biomass,
translocate them to other parts of the ecosystem, and alter the chemical
state of pollutants during these processes. The activities of
saprotrophic fungal species can be reduced by pollutants that suppress
enzyme expression or chemically interfere with enzyme activity, but
specific fungal species exhibit tolerance manifested in their survival
or rate of growth.

## Mechanisms of Metal Accumulation

Fungi accumulate heavy metals through both biosorption and metabolic
uptake. Biosorption relies on ion exchange between metal ions and
reactive groups of the cell wall. Both live and dead fungal mycelium can
function similarly to ion exchange resins for the cleanup of effluent
solutions. The uptake capacity varies considerably by metal and fungal
species: copper uptake ranges from 0.25 to 1.9 mmol per gram of
biomass, while zinc ranges from 0.12 to 15.3 mmol per gram. Uranium
uptake capacity ranges from 0.12 to 1.3 mmol per gram of biomass.

In living fungal tissue, metal ion accumulation results from both
metabolic uptake and [[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]] but a
significant negative effect on eukaryotic (presumed fungal) biomass as
measured by substrate-induced respiration. This suggests fungi are more
sensitive to heavy metals than bacteria.

Kuperman and Carreiro (1997) showed that total and fluorescein
diacetate active (FDA) [[psilocybin-psilocin-flush-accumulation-patterns]]
differ markedly between species and among individual basidiocarps, making
generalizations difficult. Rizzo et al. (1992) showed that despite
melanized outer cortex, rhizomorphs of Armillaria spp. can concentrate
elements 50 to 100 times more than surrounding soil. Concentrations of
Al, Zn, Fe, Cu, and Pb in rhizomorphs reached up to 3440, 1930, 1890,
15, and 680 mg per gram, respectively.

## Metal Transformation

Fungi can transform heavy metals through biochemical processes.
Byrne et al. (1997) discovered dimethylarsenic acid (DMA) in the
ectomycorrhizal basidiomycete [[sarcodon-imbricatus]] and Agaricus
