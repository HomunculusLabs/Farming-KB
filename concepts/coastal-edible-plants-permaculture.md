# Coastal Edible Plants for Permaculture Food Systems

Coastal environments, despite their challenges of salt spray, sandy soils, and strong winds, host a remarkable diversity of edible plants. From salt-tolerant vegetables and fruits to honey-producing trees and grain crops, coastal permaculture systems can produce substantial food yields when the right species are selected. Many of these plants have been used by Indigenous peoples and early settlers for centuries and offer exciting possibilities for modern coastal food production.

## Coastal Vegetables and Leafy Greens

### Tetragonia tetragonioides (Warrigal Cabbage, New Zealand Spinach)

The most significant coastal vegetable, warrigal cabbage is the **only plant native to Australia to be cultivated internationally as a vegetable**. This shrubby, creeping herb is common to sheltered beaches, saltmarshes, arid woodlands, and plains, extending into central Australia, and is also native to the coast of New Zealand.

**Culinary use**: Leaves are cooked like spinach (raw leaves contain oxalates and should be blanched before eating). The succulent young leaves can also be eaten raw in salads in moderation. The flavour is similar to spinach with a slightly salty, earthy quality that complements coastal cuisine.

**Cultivation**: Easily propagated from cuttings or seed. Spreads readily as a ground cover, making it both a food crop and a sand-stabilising plant. Prefers well-drained sandy soils and full sun to partial shade.

### Sesuvium portulacastrum (Sea Purslane)

A fleshy-leaved, shrubby herb native to beaches and mudflats of northern Australia and New South Wales. The leaves are reported to make an **excellent vegetable and pickle ingredient**. The succulent leaves have a slightly salty, crisp texture suitable for salads, stir-fries, and pickling.

### Suadea australis (Sea Blight)

A fleshy-leaved plant native to most of the Australian coast, growing as a shrubby herb (15–30 cm) on saltflats, inland saltpans, and sandbanks behind mangroves. Early settlers used the plant as a vegetable and in pickles. The leaves have a salty flavour that works well in cooked dishes.

### Apium prostratum (Sea Celery)

A small, squat herb growing on coastal headlands, dunes, margins of brackish swamps, and saline stretches of river banks throughout southern Australia. It has a striking resemblance to *Apium graveolens* (the wild ancestor of celery from Europe and Asia). Sea celery was used historically as a parsley substitute to treat and prevent scurvy in the eighteenth century. While the stems are too small to use as conventional celery, it has significant potential to be **bred as a vegetable**. The leaves and stems have a strong celery-like flavour suitable for seasoning soups, stews, and seafood dishes.

### Sarcocornia quinqueflora (Chicken Claws, Beaded Glasswort)

Forms extensive colonies on tidal mudflats with succulent, jointed, leafless stems rarely exceeding 50 cm. The fleshy stems absorb the flavour of pickles, making them a traditional pickle ingredient. The woody centre within each stem is a nuisance in culinary use — the English native *Sarcocornia stricta* is preferred for cooking. The young, tender tips can be blanched and used as a vegetable or garnish.

### Carpobrotus spp (Pigface, Karkalla)

Native to southern Australia, pigface has thick, fleshy, triangular leaves often with a reddish tinge, purple flowers, and purplish-red fruits. It is described as **one of Australia's tastiest fruits, tasting somewhat like salty apples**. The leaves were traditionally eaten cooked by Aboriginal people to salt their meat, and the dried fruit was also consumed. In Queensland, the mucilaginous juice from the leaves treats midge bites and jellyfish stings. *Carpobrotus rossii* and *C. modestus* extend well inland, broadening their utility.

### Arctostaphylos uva-ursi (Bearberry, Kinnikinik)

A trailing or creeping shrub (rarely exceeding 75 cm) of the northern temperate region, hardy to frost. The red berries are fairly bland and mealy when raw, but are usually eaten cooked, preserved, or made into jams, jellies, marmalades, sauces, and pastes. The dried leaves are used as a tea in some parts of Russia. The name "kinnikinik" comes from its use in Native American smoking mixtures.

## Coastal Fruits

### Carissa grandiflora (Natal Plum)

A thorny coastal hedge plant with edible fruit. The fruit is reddish and can be eaten fresh or used in jams, jellies, and preserves. The thorny habit makes it an effective barrier plant that also produces food — a dual-purpose species ideal for coastal permaculture boundary plantings.

### Coccoloba uvifera (Sea Grape)

A large bush or small tree to 6 meters with scarlet-coloured foliage, native to Florida and the Caribbean. It is an extremely hardy tree able to withstand wind and salt spray, and can grow with its roots partly in the sea. The fruit is reddish-purple to off-white and may be eaten out of hand or used in jellies, jams, drinks, syrups, and wines. Sea grape is dioecious, so both male and female plants are needed for fruit production. Fairly tender to frost.

### Chrysobalanus icaco (Cocoa Plum, Icaco Palm)

An extremely hardy shrub or tree to 12 meters native to south Florida and the Caribbean. It grows in dense clumps on the seashore and in both fresh and saline swamps, extending well inland into mountain regions (suggesting frost tolerance). The fruit was an **important food of the Seminole Indians**, eaten raw, stewed with sugar, dried like prunes, or made into jellies. Seed kernels are roasted and eaten. In preserves, the fruit is pierced right through to the kernel, allowing the fruit flavour to penetrate the seed, which is then eaten from the shell.

### Echyalaena tomentosa (Ruby Saltbush, Sturt's Saltbush)

A small shrub to 1 meter found throughout arid Australia in nearly all habitats. Well-adapted to saline and marshy conditions. The fruit is salty-sweet and can be eaten fresh. Leaves can be boiled and eaten as a vegetable. In the Macdonnell Ranges, Aboriginal people soaked the fruit in water and drank the liquid like a sweet tea — a traditional beverage from a widespread coastal and inland species.

### Elaeagnus angustifolia (Russian Olive, Bohemian Olive)

Yellow, sweet berries are eaten fresh or made into pies, sauces, jams, jellies, sherbets, and wines. The berries also provide poultry forage in late summer. This nitrogen-fixing tree is extremely cold-hardy (to -34°C) and grows right to the sea, making it one of the most versatile coastal fruit producers for [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

### Nyssa sylvatica (Black Tupelo, Pepperidge)

A frost-hardy tree to 30 meters with edible raw fruit useful in preserves. The tart fruit can be made into jelly and is valued by wildlife. Related species *Nyssa aquatica* and *Nyssa ogeche* are also worthy of trial in coastal settings.

### Persea borbonica (Red Bay, Bull Bay)

An evergreen, frost-hardy tree of low coastal areas along the eastern USA. A relative of avocado, its leaves are used as flavouring similar to bay leaves and can also be made into tea. While not a direct food crop, it provides an important culinary herb for coastal kitchens.

## Honey and Nectar Producing Coastal Plants

Coastal environments support significant honey production through adapted flowering plants:

- **Leptospermum laevigatum** (coast tea tree): A good honey plant; dried leaves used as tea.
- **Melaleuca spp** (paperbarks): Many species produce honey and essential oils. *Melaleuca lanceolata* produces light amber honey in summer.
- **Hakea suaveolens**: A noted honey plant for coastal gardens.
- **Banksia integrifolia** (coast banksia): Produces good dark honey in autumn to winter — valuable for filling the winter honey gap.
- **[[euphorbia-tirucalli]]**: Probably a good honey plant for tropical coastal areas.
- **Correa alba** (Cape Barren tea): Provides nectar and its leaves serve as a tea substitute.

## Coastal Grain and Forage Crops

### Distichlis palmeri (Palmer Saltgrass)

A promising salt-tolerant grain crop. This perennial grows to about 0.5 meters in tidal flats and salty marshlands off the Gulf of California, thriving with regular tidal inundation by seawater. The seed is traditionally harvested by the Seri Indians — it falls to the water and is washed ashore for collection. Once established, the plant should not need replanting, as it spreads by rhizomes and is extremely dense. Field tests with hybrid cultivars are producing **1 tonne of grain per hectare**, a promising yield for a crop that requires no fresh water irrigation.

### Distichlis spicata (Salt Grass)

Distinct inland and seashore ecotypes exist. Approximately **20,000 hectares of salt flats** near Mexico City are under cultivation for cattle fodder using this species, demonstrating its viability as a large-scale forage crop on saline land.

### Salsola iberica (Russian Thistle)

A salt and drought-tolerant annual with crude protein of 15–20% and a similar amino acid breakdown to alfalfa. While considered a weed in many contexts, its nutritional profile makes it a valuable forage species in saline environments where conventional fodder fails.

### Leptochloa fusca (Kallar Grass)

A highly salt-tolerant perennial forage species growing best on permanently wet soils in tropical and southern Africa. Its ability to produce forage on saline, waterlogged soils makes it valuable for coastal and estuarine livestock systems.

## Traditional and Indigenous Coastal Food Knowledge

The edible plants of coastal zones have sustained human populations for thousands of years:

- **Aboriginal Australian** use of pigface fruit, sea celery for scurvy prevention, ruby saltbush fruit tea, and mangrove seeds as avocado substitutes.
- **Seri Indian** harvesting of Palmer saltgrass grain from tidal flats — one of the few traditional grain harvests from a halophytic crop.
- **Seminole Indian** use of cocoa plum fruit as a staple food, prepared in multiple ways.
- **Early European settlers** used sea blight and sea purslane as vegetables and pickle ingredients, and bearberry for jams and teas.

This traditional knowledge provides a foundation for modern coastal food production and should guide species [[falconer-mushroom-cooking-selection-and-preparation]] methods in contemporary permaculture design.

## Designing a Coastal Food System

A productive coastal food garden integrates multiple layers:

1. **Canopy**: Russian olive (fruit, [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]]), New Zealand Christmas tree (windbreak, nectar), sea grape (fruit).
2. **Understory**: Cocoa plum (fruit), natal plum (fruit, hedge), red bay (flavouring herb).
3. **Shrub layer**: Ruby saltbush (fruit, vegetable), coastal tea tree (honey, tea), pigface (fruit, medicinal).
4. **Ground cover**: Warrigal cabbage (spinach substitute), sea purslane (vegetable, pickle), sea celery (seasoning), glasswort (pickle).
5. **Edge/forage**: Palmer saltgrass (grain), kallar grass (fodder).

By selecting species from each layer, a coastal permaculture system can produce vegetables, fruits, honey, tea, grain, and forage while simultaneously providing wind protection, [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], soil building, and [[amphibian-habitat-creation]].
