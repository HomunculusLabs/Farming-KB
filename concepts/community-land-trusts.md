## Community Land Trusts

Community land trusts (CLTs) are democratic, locally governed organisations that hold land in perpetuity for the benefit of a defined community. The CLT model separates land ownership from building ownership, allowing individuals to own homes, businesses, and other improvements while the land itself is held in trust. Bill Mollison addresses land access and trusteeship extensively in Chapter 14 of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, arguing that the concept of land ownership is fundamentally flawed and that trusteeship is a more appropriate and sustainable relationship between people and land.

## Philosophical Foundation

Mollison argues that our own lifetimes are, in terms of soils, trees, or climate, as ephemeral as snowflakes. For a little while, we have the use of the earth, and our time here is bounded by birth and death. Thus the very concept of land ownership is ludicrous, and we need only to use what is needed for the brief time that we are here. The law clearly distinguishes between ownership or entitlement to a resource, and the rights of the use of it.

Laws of ownership are relatively modern, and are foreign to tribal or clan law. Laws of trusteeship are ancient, philosophical, and realistic. Ownership gives the titleholder a right to exploit in the short term. Trusteeship governs any resource for the very long term, with no right to exploit resources beyond essential needs or replacement time.

The way that land passed from clan management to personal ownership is well documented. Since the year 1400 or thereabouts, methods used included enclosure of commons, forced sale of clan lands, colonisation and appropriation of indigenous territories, and the conversion of communal land systems to private title systems. Most people live on lands once tribal, now owned by states, churches, or corporations, with very few people having rights to share the resources of such land.

## How Community Land Trusts Work

A community land trust is a non-profit organisation that acquires and holds land permanently. The CLT owns the land and leases it to individuals, families, cooperatives, or other organisations through long-term ground leases, typically 99 years. The leaseholders own the buildings and other improvements on the land but not the land itself.

When a leaseholder sells their home or business, the CLT retains the right to purchase the improvements at a price that keeps the housing or business affordable for the next occupant. This removes land from the speculative market and ensures long-term affordability. The CLT can set resale formulae that allow leaseholders to build equity while maintaining affordability for future generations.

## CLT Governance

Community land trusts are governed by a tripartite board consisting of three equal groups. The first group is leaseholders, the people who live or work on CLT land. The second group is community representatives, people who live in the surrounding neighbourhood or bioregion. The third group is public interest representatives, including government officials, non-profit organisations, and other stakeholders.

This tripartite governance structure ensures that the CLT is accountable to its direct beneficiaries while also serving the broader community interest. No single group can dominate the board, and decisions reflect the needs and perspectives of all stakeholders. This democratic governance model aligns with permaculture principles of fair share and distributed decision-making.

## Land Access for Permaculture

CLTs provide a practical mechanism for permaculture practitioners to gain long-term access to land. Many people who want to practice permaculture cannot afford to purchase land at market prices. A CLT can acquire land and provide affordable long-term leases to permaculture farmers, gardeners, and community groups.

The CLT model also protects permaculture investments in land improvement. When leaseholders invest in soil building, tree planting, water management, and other land improvements, the CLT ensures that these improvements benefit the community over the long term rather than being captured by speculative land price increases when the leaseholder leaves.

## CLTs for Village Development

Mollison integrates the CLT concept into his village development model. A village development trust acquires land, develops infrastructure and house sites, and establishes a community land trust to hold the common land and manage the community assets. Individual homes and businesses are sold or leased on CLT land, with resale restrictions maintaining affordability.

The village CLT retains ownership of roads, common buildings, forests, water systems, [[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]], watershed protection, and ecosystem restoration. In the United Kingdom, CLTs can be registered as community benefit societies. In Australia, CLTs operate under various state and territory laws. The specific legal form should be adapted to local legal requirements and should provide for permanent land stewardship.

## Challenges

CLTs face several challenges including the high cost of land acquisition in desirable areas, complex legal requirements for establishment and operation, the need for ongoing organisational management and governance, balancing individual leaseholder rights with community interests, and building public understanding and support for the trust model.
