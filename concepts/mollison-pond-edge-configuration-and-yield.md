# Mollison: Pond Edge Configuration and Yield Maximisation

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 13, Section 13.5)

## The Edge Effect in Pond Design

Mollison presents a detailed mathematical argument for maximising pond edge to increase natural food availability for fish. He compares three ponds, all of 75 square metres surface area, equal depth, and containing the same volume and quality of water, but differing only in their ground plan configuration.

- Pond A (circular, 5 m radius): approximately 32 m of edge or margin.
- Pond B (37.5 m long and 2 m wide): 81 m of margin.
- Pond C (1 m wide and 75 m long): 153 m of margin.

The implications are dramatic. If pondside vegetation grows over the pond edge for a modest 0.5 m, this affects only about 25 square metres of pond A, reaches halfway across pond B, and crosses clear across pond C. The vegetation provides insects, leaf fall, fruit, and root exudates that omnivorous fish consume directly or indirectly through the food chain.

Pond B provides 2.6 times and pond C provides 5 times the natural food of pond A to omnivorous fish. Beyond the food factor, pond C is easily compartmentalised for parallel flow, easily partitioned with screens, and can be stepped and stacked on slopes where circular ponds become inaccessible and expensive to build.

## Partitioning and Complex Ponds

Mollison proposes that ponds can be partitioned using simple 1 metre square sieves or graded gravel and boulder mounds to create multiple environments within a single water body. A complex pond design can include:

- Separate acid and alkaline water zones for different species.
- Shallow marshy areas for frogs, scuds, and marsh plants (A1 and B1 zones).
- Bait fish ponds and predator ponds separated by one-way screens.
- A system where top minnows feed sunfish, which in turn feed largemouth bass (A3 to B3 to C), each eating at different life stages.

All screens are one-way, permitting downstream migration while preventing upstream escape. This creates a self-feeding trophic cascade within a single integrated water body.

## The Zone of Edge Effect

Mollison defines the "zone of edge effect" as the halo around any pond, including the bank area. This zone is critical for production because a large proportion of the plants around ponds can either be eaten by, or will host organisms eaten by, the omnivorous fish in the ponds.

The landscape mosaic ideal for sub-humid or humid areas includes approximately:
- 15 percent pond surface.
- 15 percent contiguous marsh.
- 30 to 60 percent forest.
- 10 to 40 percent meadow, crop, or pasture.

Forest should be upstream of, downstream from, and between ponds. Marshes should be upstream of and within the ponds. Pasture or prairie serves as downstream patches where trees are difficult to grow.

## Specific Edge Species Assemblies

Mollison describes a detailed pond system set in a 2-hectare field planted to tamarack, which grows very well near water but poorly away from it. Between the tamaracks and the pond edge, blueberries thrive, arching over the water and reaching a metre or two from the pond edge to the trees. They benefit from both the water and the acid tamarack mulch. Through them and above them, grape hybrids climb in the conifers. At the edge of the lake, rainbow trout feed on blueberries knocked down by birds and on insects coming to the plants. Blackbird manure from birds visiting the blueberries encourages a bloom of phytoplankton much appreciated by the trout.

This assembly of tamarack, blueberry, grape, and trout demonstrates how edge plant selection directly drives pond productivity.

## Long Narrow Ponds on Slopes

Channel-shaped ponds are appropriate for establishing aquaculture on slopes fed by a spring, where clay is present in the soils, or as part of a total canal connector system. They can be stepped and stacked, unlike circular ponds which become more inaccessible as slopes steepen. On well-sealed clay sites, linear ponds have no significant water loss disadvantage over circular ponds, and evaporation may actually be less in shaded narrow ponds.

The drawback to linear ponds on leaky sites is potentially greater water loss than circular ponds. However, sealing a linear pond with a plastic liner is also simple. Long narrow ponds can fit easily on slopes as troughs.

## Circular Ponds and Tanks

Circular ponds and tanks are most appropriate to intensive fish rearing with a water flow at head, or pumped. The usual arrangement sets water intake jets at an angle to both aerate and induce current flow in a round tank. Most intensive rearing tanks have a central water regulating and drainage system to facilitate harvest.

Heat-welded or rivet-silicone tanks of clear plastic or fibreglass serve multiple functions: growing dense algal food, storing heat, rearing fish (Tilapia is popular), and serving as a hydroponic and eventually terrestrial nutrient source for plants whose roots are pruned by fish. These indoor tanks are integrated with glasshouse and crop to give multiple benefits.

## Climate and Pond Orientation

Mollison outlines orientation criteria analogous to house siting:

- Cold climates: oxygenation less important than heating. Ponds need shelter from cold winds and maximum solar exposure.
- Cold winter winds with warm summers: oxygenation needed in summer, protection in winter. Wind scoops can be designed to direct cooling winds across water surfaces in summer.
- Hot most seasons: oxygenation is the primary need, shade necessary for shallow ponds. Trellis and high shade hedgerow reduce overheating, with deeps on shaded edges.
- Variable (continental) climate: different needs in any of four seasons, requiring flexible designs that can be adjusted seasonally.

## Yield Comparison Across Pond Configurations

Aquaculture yields should exceed terrestrial crop yields in the same region by factors of 10 to 20 (more in arid areas). There is no better use of land than pond-and-forest systems. Of all endeavours, aquaculture and its polyculture accessories show greatest promise for the reduction of land areas in present use and the repair of damage caused by badly managed pastoralism and monocrop systems.
