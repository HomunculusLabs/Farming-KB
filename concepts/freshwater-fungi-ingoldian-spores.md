# Freshwater Fungi and Ingoldian Spores

## Overview

Freshwater habitats harbor a diverse mycota that plays critical roles in [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]] of organic matter and [[dighton-fungal-roles-in-nutrient-cycling]] in aquatic ecosystems. The fungal communities of freshwater systems include truly aquatic species, terrestrial species that periodically enter aquatic environments, and species restricted to specific freshwater niches. This page covers the diversity, ecology, and sampling of [[freshwater-fungi-biodiversity]], with emphasis on Ingoldian (aqua-aquatic) [[dighton-aquatic-hyphomycete-conidia-community-dynamics]]l fungi.

## Types of Freshwater Fungi

### Ingoldian Fungi

[[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] (named after C.T. Ingold, who pioneered their study) are aquatic hyphomycetes that produce characteristic tetraradiate (four-armed) or sigmoid (curved) conidia. These spore shapes are adaptations for:

- Enhanced buoyancy in water
- Efficient attachment to substrata
- Prolonged suspension in flowing water
- Increased probability of contacting new substrata

### Aero-Aquatic Fungi

Aero-aquatic fungi produce conidia or spore-bearing structures that trap air bubbles, enabling them to float at the water surface and colonize surface films and foam. They are typically found in:

- Stagnant or slow-moving water
- Leaf litter in ponds and marshes
- Tree holes and water-filled cavities

### True Aquatic vs. Terrestrial Input

The distinction between truly aquatic fungi and terrestrial fungi entering aquatic systems is important:

- **Obligate freshwater fungi**: Complete life cycle in water; many cannot grow on standard mycological media
- **Facultative freshwater fungi**: Primarily terrestrial but able to grow and sporulate in water
- **Terrestrial transients**: Fungal propagules washed into streams; may germinate but not complete life cycle

## Ecological Roles

### Decomposition

Freshwater fungi are the primary decomposers of [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]] and other plant material in streams and rivers:

- Fungi colonize submerged leaves within days to weeks
- Softening and conditioning of leaf material makes it palatable for invertebrate shredders
- Enzyme production (cellulases, pectinases, ligninases) breaks down complex plant polymers
- Fungal biomass can exceed bacterial biomass on decomposing leaves
- Fungal contribution to leaf litter decomposition is often greater than that of bacteria

### Food Web Connections

- Fungal hyphae and conidia are important food sources for aquatic invertebrates
- Shredder invertebrates preferentially consume fungus-conditioned leaf litter
- Collector-gatherers may feed on fungal conidia entrapped in fine particulate matter
- Fungal production supports higher trophic levels in stream food webs

### Nutrient Cycling

- Mineralization of nitrogen and phosphorus from organic matter
- Uptake and retention of dissolved nutrients
- Influence on carbon flux through aquatic ecosystems

## Habitat Distribution

### Stream Order and Position

Fungal communities vary with stream characteristics:

- Headwater streams: High canopy cover, leaf litter input, diverse fungal communities
- Mid-order streams: Mix of allochthonous and autochthonous inputs
- Large rivers: Primarily autochthonous production; different fungal assemblages
- riffles vs. pools: Different flow regimes select for different fungal species

### Substratum Types

- Leaf litter: Most studied substratum; species composition varies with leaf species
- Wood: Slower decomposition; distinct fungal communities
- Sediments: Diverse microfungi including many mitosporic species
- Macrophytes: Living and dead aquatic plants host distinct fungal communities

### Seasonal Patterns

- Fungal sporulation peaks associated with leaf fall in autumn (temperate regions)
- Temperature influences growth rates and enzyme production
- Discharge events can reset community succession
- Dry-wet cycles in intermittent streams select for resistant structures

## Diversity Estimates

The total number of freshwater fungal species is poorly known:

- ~300-600 species of Ingoldian fungi described
- Many more await description, especially from tropical regions
- Molecular methods revealing previously undetectable diversity
- Species accumulation curves from tropical studies rarely plateau
- True diversity may be several times the number of described species

## Sampling Methods

### Conidial Trapping

- **Foam sampling**: Collect natural foam from stream surfaces; concentrate conidia by filtration or centrifugation
- **Artificial substrata**: Baiting with sterile substrata (cellophane, balsa wood, leaf discs) immersed for defined periods
- **Congo red staining**: Enhances visibility of hyaline conidia in microscopic examination
- **Sedimentation chambers**: Allow conidia to settle for quantitative estimates

### Direct Examination

- Examine submerged leaf litter and wood under dissecting microscope
- Scrape surfaces and prepare slide mounts
- Culture on weak nutrient media to induce sporulation

### Molecular Methods

- DNA extraction from environmental samples
- PCR amplification with fungal-specific primers
- Clone libraries or high-throughput sequencing
- Reveals species not detectable by traditional methods

### Cultivation

- Standard mycological media often too rich; use diluted media
- Incubation at stream temperature
- Longer incubation periods than for terrestrial fungi
- Many species never cultured

## Taxonomic Composition

### Dominant Groups

- **[[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages]]**: The majority of described freshwater species; include both sexual and asexual stages
- **[[basidiomycota-classification-major-clades]]**: Less common but include some wood-inhabiting species
- **[[chytridiomycota-phylogeny-diversity]]**: Important parasites of algae and other aquatic organisms
- **Oomycota**: Include significant pathogens (Saprolegnia, Aphanomyces) and saprobes

### Conidial Morphology

Ingoldian spore types include:
- Tetraradiate (four arms): e.g., Tricladium, Tetracladium, Flagellospora
- Sigmoid (curved): e.g., Anguillospora, Lemonniera, Trichocladium
- Branched: Various patterns of branching and appendage formation
- Filiform (threadlike): Long slender conidia adapted for flowing water
