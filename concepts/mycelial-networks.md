# Mycelial Networks

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a vast network of
branching hyphae that thread through soil, wood, and other substrates. A
mycelial network is the living, growing portion of the fungal organism — the
part that actively explores, decomposes, absorbs nutrients, and communicates.
The mushroom (sporocarp) is merely the reproductive structure; the mycelium is
where the real work happens. Mycelial networks represent one of the most
ancient and pervasive biological systems on Earth.

## Structure and Growth

Individual hyphae are tubular cells typically 2-10 micrometers in diameter,
surrounded by a rigid cell wall composed primarily of chitin and glucans.
Hyphae grow at their tips through a process called tip extension, where
vesicles containing cell wall precursors fuse with the plasma membrane at the
apex, adding new material as the hypha pushes forward. Growth rates vary
enormously — some species extend less than 1 mm per day, while others can grow
several centimeters per day under optimal conditions.

As hyphae branch and fuse (anastomose), they form an interconnected network
with both geometric and topological complexity. Research has shown that
mycelial networks share structural properties with other biological and
transportation networks, including optimized connectivity, redundancy, and
resource distribution pathways. The slime mold *Physarum polycephalum* has
