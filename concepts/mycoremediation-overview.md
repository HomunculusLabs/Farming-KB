# Mycoremediation Overview

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade or remove toxins from the environment. Fungi are adept as molecular disassemblers, breaking down many recalcitrant, long-chained toxins into simpler, less toxic chemicals. Mycoremediation also holds promise for removing heavy metals from the land by channeling them to the fruitbodies for removal.

## Mechanisms

The powerful enzymes secreted by certain fungi digest lignin and cellulose, the primary structural components of wood. These digestive enzymes can also break down a surprisingly wide range of toxins that have chemical bonds like those in wood. Mushrooms that degrade wood are classified into two subgroups:

- **Brown rot fungi** (~7% of mushrooms): Extracellular enzymes break down the white, pulpy cellulose, leaving behind brownish lignin. They cause checkered cubical cracking in wood, commonly seen on downed conifer trees. Examples: sulphur shelf (Laetiporus sulphureus), velvet polypore (Phaeolus schweinitzii), agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis), split-gill polypore (Schizophyllum commune).

- **White rot fungi** (more numerous): Produce enzymes that break down the recalcitrant brown fiber in wood, leaving cellulose largely intact. Examples: oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus), maitake (Grifola frondosa), shiitake (Lentinula edodes), turkey tail (Trametes versicolor), reishi (Ganoderma lucidum).

## How It Works

Mycoremediation practices involve:

1. Mixing mycelium into contaminated soil
2. Placing mycelial mats over toxic sites
3. A combination of techniques in one-time or successive treatments

Each mushroom species secretes unique mixes of extracellular enzymes and acids. Using a plurality of species creates a synergistic effect for more complete degradation of toxins than could be achieved with one species alone. The art of this emerging science is in the selection of species and their timely introduction.

## Surveying the Habitat

Before deploying mycoremediation, a damaged habitat should first be surveyed for its resident species -- nature's recommendations for habitat restoration. Mushrooms already growing in a contaminated area tolerate the toxin and may metabolize it as a nutrient. Cultures of these native strains can be tested using standard agar well assays for antimicrobial activity or toxin sensitivity.

If native mushrooms are not found, appropriate species can be imported. Species vary substantially in their ability to adapt to specific toxic loads. Strains can be trained by titrating dilutions of the toxin into media-filled culture dishes, selecting strains that thrive on the toxin as their sole nutrient source.

## What Fungi Can Remediate

Fungi have demonstrated the ability to break down or sequester:

- Petroleum hydrocarbons (oil, diesel, gasoline)
- Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
- Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs)
- Dioxins
- Pesticides and herbicides
- Heavy metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury)
- Chemical warfare agents (VX nerve agent surrogates demonstrated)
- Coliform bacteria and pathogens
- Explosives (TNT, perchlorate)

## Key Research

Stamets collaborated with Battelle Marine Science Laboratories to demonstrate that oyster mushrooms could break down petroleum hydrocarbons. In another project, a fungus was identified that broke down dimethyl methylphosphonate (DMMP), a key ingredient in the deadly neurotoxin VX, metabolizing it into unstable nontoxic subderivatives.
