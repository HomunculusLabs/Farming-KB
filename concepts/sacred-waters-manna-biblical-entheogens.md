## Sacred Waters and Manna in Biblical Tradition

Throughout biblical scripture, a recurring cluster of symbols—living waters, manna, the tree of life, hidden manna, the white stone, flesh, blood, and the Fountain of Living Waters—represents a single, tangible substance interpreted within this framework as an entheogenic mushroom. These symbols appear across both Old and New Testaments, forming what the author argues is a coherent sacramental language that has been progressively dematerialized into abstract symbolism by institutional religion.

## The Fountain of Living Waters

The declaration "I am the Fountain of Living Waters" is interpreted literally as describing a mushroom: alive, made of water, and in the similitude of a fountain. The "waters of life" is an ancient term linked to immortality and transcendence, synonymous with **Soma** and **Amrta** in Hindu/Buddhist tradition, and with the blood of God in sacramental analysis. The churning of the milky ocean in Hindu mythology is a metaphor for producing this substance.

In **Revelation 21:6**, Jesus promises to give the living waters to those who are athirst. The author argues that living waters, blood, manna, and flesh are all representations of the same thing: a phenomenal substance that produces multi-dimensionality, spiritual learning, and communion with God from within.

## Jeremiah's Broken Cisterns

**Jeremiah 3:13** delivers what may be the most pointed critique of sacramental substitution in the Bible: "My people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water."

The "two evils" are: (1) abandoning the actual entheogenic sacrament, and (2) replacing it with a non-functional substitute. The "broken cisterns that can hold no water" are analogous to a broken light bulb—something that cannot perform its intended function. If a substance that is supposed to produce specific spiritual effects does not produce those effects, it is "broken and actually worthless." The author argues this condemns the replacement of the real sacrament with a placebo.

## The Hidden Manna and White Stone

**Revelation 2:17** promises the overcomer "hidden manna" and "a white stone, and in the stone a new name written." The word manna in ancient tongue means mushroom. The mushroom is also described as a small white stone—directly matching its physical appearance. The mushroom is thus simultaneously the manna, the white stone, the comforter, and the "door" that one can enter in and out of, which will "show you all things."

## Flesh and Blood as Literal Substance

**John 6:53-56** contains some of the most explicit sacramental language in the New Testament: "Except ye eat the flesh of the son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you... For my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed. He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth me in and I in him."

The author argues this language is deliberately physical, not metaphorical. The statements "my body is flesh indeed" and "my blood is drink indeed" and "when you eat, it is inside of you" leave little room for purely symbolic interpretation. The admonition that "unless you eat and drink you have no life in you" is read as a literal requirement for consuming a tangible substance—not a phantom symbol. The author explicitly rejects the idea that this refers to cannibalism.

## Faith Without Works

**James 2:14-16** poses the question: "What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he have faith, and have not works? Can faith save him?" The "works" in this context are interpreted as tangible substances—the actual sacrament. Faith alone, without the physical ingestion of the entheogen, is argued to be insufficient.
