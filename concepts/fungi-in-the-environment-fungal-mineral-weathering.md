# Fungal Mineral Weathering

Fungi are among the most important biological agents of rock and mineral weathering. Through their combined biomechanical and biochemical activities, they dissolve minerals and mobilize metals, releasing essential nutrients into bioavailable forms and driving biogeochemical cycles on local and global scales. Their role in geomycology is increasingly recognized as central to understanding Earth surface processes.

## Bioweathering Defined

Bioweathering is the erosion, decay and decomposition of rocks and minerals mediated by living organisms. One of the most important bioweathering processes is weathering mediated by microorganisms, including fungi. Near-surface weathering of rocks and minerals often involves an interaction between physical (mechanical), chemical and biological processes.

While bacteria have historically dominated geomicrobiological studies owing to their extensive metabolic versatility, there is increasing evidence that fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at higher pH values and over a wider redox range more efficiently than bacteria. Fungal bioweathering of basaltic outcrops in sub-polar areas is believed to be chronologically the first process of weathering, followed by subsequent cryogenic processes. Alkaline (basic) rocks are generally more susceptible to fungal attack than acidic rocks.

Along with other organisms, fungi are believed to contribute to the weathering of:

- Silicate-bearing rocks: mica and orthoclase
- Iron- and manganese-bearing minerals: biotite, olivine and pyroxene
- Carbonate rocks: limestone, marble, dolomite
- Artificial materials: concrete, cement, medieval and antique glass

## Fungi as Weathering Agents

Fungi are exceptionally well suited as weathering agents for several reasons that together give them capabilities exceeding those of other microbial groups:

- They can be highly resistant to extreme environmental conditions (metal toxicity, UV radiation, desiccation)
- They adopt diverse growth, metabolic and morphological strategies
- They exude protons and metal-complexing metabolites that dissolve mineral lattices
- They form mutualistic symbioses with plants, algae and cyanobacteria that enhance their weathering capabilities
- Their filamentous growth allows exploration of heterogeneous environments with ability to increase or decrease surface area
- They can adopt exploration or exploitation strategies and form linear organs of aggregated hyphae for protected fungal translocation
- They translocate nutrients within mycelial networks, important for exploiting heterogeneous environments
- Their hydrated mucilaginous sheath creates a reactive microenvironment at the hypha-mineral interface

Many oligotrophic fungi scavenge nutrients from air and rainwater, enabling growth on rock surfaces. In the sub-aerial rock environment, they use organic and inorganic residues on mineral surfaces, waste products of other microorganisms, decaying plants and insects, dust particles, aerosols and animal faeces as nutrient sources.

## Rocks and Minerals Degraded by Fungi

Fungi have been documented weathering an extensive range of rock types and artificial materials:

**Natural rocks and minerals:**

- Sandstone, granite, soapstone, andesite, basalt
- Gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite, quartz
- Limestone, marble, dolomite

**Specific minerals dissolved:**

- Olivine, dunite, serpentine, muscovite
- Feldspar, spodumene, kaolin, nepheline
- Biotite, orthoclase, microcline, chlorite

**Artificial materials:**

- Concrete and cement
- Natural glass
- Artificial antique and medieval glass

**Specific fungal species documented as weathering agents:**

- Aspergillus niger: degrades olivine, dunite, serpentine, muscovite, feldspar, spodumene, kaolin, nepheline and basalt
- Penicillium expansum: similar range of minerals
- Penicillium simplicissimum: releases aluminium from aluminosilicates
- Scopulariopsis brevicaulis: releases aluminium from aluminosilicates

## Symbiotic Fungi in Mineral Weathering

### Lichens

Lichens are commonly thought of as pioneer colonizers of fresh rock outcrops and possibly one of the earliest life forms on Earth's land surfaces. An estimated 6% of the Earth's land surface is covered by lichen-dominated vegetation. Lichens play important biogeochemical roles in:

- Retention and distribution of nutrient and trace elements
- Soil formation through combined mechanical and chemical weathering
- Rock weathering through acid production and metal complexation
- Accumulation of metals (Pb, Cu, radionuclides) and formation of metal-organic biominerals, especially on metal-rich substrates

### Mycorrhizas

Nearly all land plants depend on mycorrhizal fungi. Two main types of mycorrhiza participate in mineral weathering:

- **Endomycorrhizas** (ericoid and arbuscular): colonize the interior of host plant root cells, forming arbuscules or coils within cells
- **Ectomycorrhizas**: remain outside root cells, forming a sheath and Hartig net between cortical cells

Mycorrhizal fungi are involved in:

- Proton- and ligand-promoted metal mobilization from mineral sources
- Metal immobilization via biosorption and accumulation within biomass
- Extracellular precipitation of mycogenic toxic metal oxalates

Ectomycorrhizal mycelia respond to different soil minerals by regulating growth, colonization, carbon allocation and substrate acidification. Carbon allocation within the mycelium was significantly greater in Hebeloma crustuliniforme/Pinus sylvestris ectomycorrhizas colonizing potassium feldspar patches than quartz patches, demonstrating that mycorrhizal fungi actively sense and respond to mineralogy.

During their growth, mycorrhizal fungi excrete low-molecular-mass carboxylic acids (malic, succinic, gluconic, oxalic), contributing to "heterotrophic leaching." In podzol E horizons under European coniferous forests, weathering of hornblendes, feldspars and granitic bedrock has been attributed to organic acid excretion by ectomycorrhizal hyphae producing micro- to millimolar concentrations at micropores (3-10 micrometres) in weatherable soil minerals.

## Environmental Significance

Fungal mineral weathering has critical environmental implications spanning ecology, agriculture and geochemistry:

- Releases essential nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Zn) into bioavailable forms for plants and microorganisms
- Mobilizes non-essential toxic metals (Cs, Al, Cd, Hg, Pb) during lithospheric weathering
- Contributes to soil formation and pedogenesis, particularly in primary succession
- Influences global biogeochemical cycling of elements, including carbon through coupled carbonate precipitation
- Has potential for bioremediation of contaminated land and recovery of metals from low-grade ores and industrial wastes
- Can deteriorate building materials including concrete, cement and stone, raising concerns for cultural heritage preservation and nuclear waste containment
