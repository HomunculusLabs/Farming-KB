# JADAM Ultra-Low-Cost Technology

JADAM (Jayonul Sasang Gua Damunhoe) is a South Korean organic farming
system developed by [[cho-seawater-and-fermented-seawater]] that pursues ultra-low-cost agriculture
through farmer-made inputs. The system represents a philosophical and
practical alternative to both conventional chemical agriculture and
expensive commercial organic farming, emphasizing that farming should be
accessible to anyone regardless of financial resources.

## Four Aims: The SESE Principle

JADAM promotes four foundational aims, summarized as SESE: Simple
(principles are simple), Easy (easy to make), Scientific (methodology
is scientific), and Effective (process is highly effective and low-
cost). Only when a farming system achieves all four criteria will
farmers embrace it voluntarily in significant numbers. JADAM argues
that modern organic farming has been shaped by manufacturers,
distributors, and retailers of farm inputs, creating a system where
farmers must "buy expensive things from afar instead of valuing what
is near and cheap."

## Core Philosophy: The Good and the Bad Are One

JADAM's philosophical foundation is the principle that "the good and
the bad are one." This rejects the dualistic view that divides
organisms into beneficial and harmful categories. In agriculture, this
means abandoning the practice of separating microorganisms into "good
guys" and "bad guys." The truth is that science cannot make such
distinctions: less than one percent of estimated one million soil
microorganism species have been studied, and the relationship between
different species remains largely unknown.

This principle has practical implications. Oxygen is necessary for life
but excessive intake creates harmful active oxygen. Water is essential
for plants but too much drowns them. Soil nematodes are not always bad,
as adequate numbers help maintain soil health. Ladybugs are not always
good; too many become pests. Technology, JADAM argues, is rooted in
philosophy, and the dualistic thinking promoted by corporate interests
has stripped farmers of their traditional knowledge and confidence.

## The JADAM Input System

JADAM provides recipes for a complete suite of farmer-made inputs:

### JADAM Indigenous Microorganism Solution (JMS)
The primary microbial input, made by culturing microorganisms from
local leaf mold. One gram of leaf mold contains approximately 2 billion
to 10 billion microorganisms across over one million different species.
Laboratory data shows 1 gram contains 30,000 protozoa, 50,000 algae,
400,000 fungi, and billions of bacteria. These indigenous
microorganisms, adapted to local conditions, are superior to factory-
cultured commercial products from distant origins.

### [[jadam-liquid-fertilizer]] (JLF)
Made from locally available organic materials including wild grasses,
crop residues, and food waste. Multiple specialized formulations exist
for nitrogen, calcium, potassium, phosphate, chitosan, and mineral
needs. These replace expensive commercial fertilizers entirely.

### JADAM Natural Pesticide (JNP)
A natural pest control solution made with JADAM herb solution (JHS)
and JADAM wetting agent (JWA). JHS is produced by boiling indigenous
weeds and herbs. JWA replaces chemical surfactants with natural
alternatives.

### Other Inputs
JADAM mineral water (JMW), JADAM sulfur (JS), [[jadam-water-softener]]
(JWS), and food waste liquid fertilizer. All are made from materials
freely available in the local environment.

## Ultra-Low-Cost Vision

JADAM's ultimate objective is to bring farming back to farmers. Through
JADAM's methods, farming can become ultra-low-cost, completely self-
sufficient, and high-yielding. The system recognizes that historically,
farming required almost no money. Farmers used what was freely available
around them: materials from nature, byproducts of farming, and any
organic matter. A person with no means of living should at least be
able to live off the land, but modern agriculture requires significant
capital investment before any farming can begin.

## Challenges Driving JADAM's Relevance

JADAM identifies several critical challenges facing agriculture in the
coming decades: global free trade expanding competition for agricultural
products; energy-dependent systems facing rising oil and electricity
costs; grain prices rising during food crises; and rising sea levels
threatening farmland. Ultra-low-cost agriculture addresses all of these
by reducing dependence on purchased inputs and fossil fuels.

## Traditional Farming as Foundation

JADAM traces its roots to thousands of years of Korean organic farming
history, citing Franklin Hiram King's 1909 observation that Korea's
traditional farming method was the best in Asia. The ancient Korean
state of Gojoseon, established in 2333 BCE, had an agricultural tax
system demonstrating sophisticated farming practices long before
chemical inputs existed. JADAM argues that the famous Masanobu
Fukuoka's farming method has deep precedents in traditional Asian
agriculture.

## Soil Foundation Method

The most important JADAM technology is the soil foundation method:
intensively applying JMS combined with sea salt, phyllite solution, and
wild grass JLF before transplanting or flowering. This should be done
when crops are not growing so unlimited water can be used, allowing
microbes to swim deep into the soil. Early root settlement determines
harvest success. JMS should be applied at 500 liters per quarter-acre
to 8 acres, with sea salt and phyllite water at 500 grams per
quarter-acre.
