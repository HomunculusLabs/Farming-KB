# Anaerobic Sewage Digestion and Methane Production

## Heavy Metal Removal

One of the most significant benefits of multi-stage pond treatment is its capacity for heavy metal removal. Unlike conventional chemical precipitation, the pond system achieves metal sequestration through entirely biological pathways, requiring no chemical additives.

The system achieves the following removal rates through a combination of sulphide precipitation, biological uptake, and sedimentation:

| Metal | Removal Rate |
|-------|-------------|
| Copper (Cu) | 97% |
| Zinc (Zn) | 97% |
| Lead (Pb) | 95% |
| Chromium (Cr) | 87% |
| Iron (Fe) | 85% |
| Cadmium (Cd) | 70% |
| Nickel (Ni) | 65% |

Sulphur-loving bacteria play a central role in this process. By converting H₂S to elemental sulphur, these organisms create binding sites that sequester dissolved metals. At pH levels above 7, metal sulphides precipitate and settle into the sludge, removing them from the water column. Zooplankton in the facultative and aerobic ponds also accumulate metals in their tissues, with measured concentrations reaching Fe 1200, Zn 152, Cu 37, and Cr 28 mg/L in dry weight. This biological concentration means that even dissolved metals remaining after sulphide precipitation are progressively extracted from the water column and locked into organic biomass.

## Case Study: Werribee, Victoria

The Werribee sewage treatment facility in Victoria, Australia, provides a large-scale demonstration of pond-based sewage treatment:

- **Area:** 1.5 million m² of treatment ponds
- **Population served:** 2.7 million people
- **Land requirement:** 1 hectare per 1,800 inhabitants

This land-intensive approach is offset by minimal energy inputs, low maintenance requirements, and the production of useful outputs including biogas, treated water, and nutrient-rich biomass. The Werribee system demonstrates that pond-based treatment is viable at municipal scale, relying on natural biological processes rather than energy-intensive mechanical aeration or chemical dosing. The treated effluent from such systems can support pasture irrigation, tree plantations, or aquaculture, turning waste disposal into productive land use.

## Seasonal and Temperature Effects

Decomposition rates are strongly influenced by temperature and season. The difference between summer and winter performance is substantial:

- **Summer BOD decomposition:** 1,034 kg/ha/day
- **Winter BOD decomposition:** 495 kg/ha/day

This seasonal variation — nearly a two-fold difference — means that system sizing must account for the reduced winter processing capacity. In cold climates, additional pond area or supplementary heating may be necessary to maintain year-round treatment performance. The optimal temperature range of 25–30°C for anaerobic digestion highlights the advantage of warm climates for passive, unheated systems. Designers in temperate regions should oversize pond area based on winter decomposition rates to ensure continuous treatment performance.

## Design Considerations

When designing an anaerobic sewage digestion system, several factors must be balanced:

1. **Land availability:** Pond systems require significant area but demand minimal infrastructure and energy
2. **Climate:** Temperature directly affects decomposition rates and biogas production; winter sizing is critical
3. **Scale:** The self-regulating nature of sludge digestion scales well from household to municipal systems
4. **Metal loads:** Industrial inputs with high heavy metal concentrations may require pretreatment to protect the biological processes
5. **Energy recovery:** Covered digesters with gas capture transform treatment from a cost center to an energy producer
6. **pH management:** Each stage has an optimal pH range; monitoring ensures proper biological function across the treatment chain
7. **Retention time:** The 20-day minimum retention at 25–30°C must be maintained even during cold periods

The integration of anaerobic digestion with facultative and aerobic ponds creates a resilient, low-maintenance treatment chain that produces valuable outputs while achieving high purification standards. By harnessing natural microbial communities rather than mechanical systems, permaculture sewage treatment designs offer sustainable alternatives to conventional energy-intensive approaches. The key advantage is that these systems work with biological processes rather than against them, using succession — from anaerobic through facultative to aerobic — as a design principle that mirrors natural decomposition pathways.
