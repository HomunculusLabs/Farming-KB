# Desert Soils and Mulching Strategies

Soil management in drylands presents unique challenges. The extreme temperature fluctuations, low organic matter, and tendency toward salinity and alkalinity require specific strategies for building and maintaining productive garden soils.

## The Problem with [[humus]] can rapidly decompose to nitrates with heat and water, giving a sometimes lethal flush of nitrate to new seedlings. Dry cultivated soils exacerbate this effect. The consequences are severe:

- **Soil cracking** occurs in dry conditions, damaging feeder roots
- **Rapid temperature gains** cook feeder roots at the surface, so that in subsequent rains there are fewer roots to absorb water
- **Fire** is destructive of the protective litter layer that prevents these effects
- After fire and cultivation, most of the soil nitrogen, sulphur, and phosphorus is lost
- Even a cool fire loses plant nutrients to soil water and leaching

### The Role of Aboriginal Burning

When more is understood about the effects of fire in drylands, methods other than fire (slashing, rolling, even light grazing) should be used to reduce fire litter to soil mulch. Aboriginal burning has likely:

- Gravely depleted soil nutrients
- Caused a breakdown in soil structure
- Been in great part responsible for the saltpans that preceded agriculture
- Agriculture itself is a monstrously effective way to speed up this process and intensify it

## Soil Treatments in Dryland Home Gardens

### Non-Wetting Sands

Where free-draining or non-wetting sand is the problem, **bentonite** (a volcanic fine clay which swells up and holds water) is a great help in flood-irrigated beds.

### Heavy Clays

Where clay is causing problems with water penetration, the addition of **gypsum** enables water to penetrate further into the clay particles.

### Salted Soils and Salty Water

Where salted soils or salty waters are a problem:
- Garden beds must be **mounded up or raised**, allowing salt to wash down into the paths and low places
- Rain run-off and roof water must be used to flush salts if local water exceeds 800 ppm

### Difficult Soil Remediation Techniques

Several heroic methods exist for dealing with severely degraded soils:

- **Calcrete layers**: Crack open the hard calcrete, dig pits of humus and organic wastes to allow roots to reach lower damp sands
- **Cracking clay soils**: Fill with sand-gypsum mix before planting seedlings
- **Alkaline sites**: Mix gels with trace elements and sulphur before planting
- **Deep sands**: Use plastic liners with holes to hold minerals, nutrients, and gels
- **Hard shales**: Small explosive charges can shatter hard pans
- **Hard pans for tree lines**: Ripping opens hard pans for tree lines and water infiltration

## Swale Design and Management

Water use is efficient only if all water soaks in quickly, and watering is stopped once the soil is saturated. Mulch greatly helps this process of fast soakage. Swale management principles:

- **Width**: Swales should be about 2–4 m (6–12 feet) wide, so that trees grown along them can overshade the swale base and help prevent evaporation
- **Soil treatments**: Same as for garden beds — bentonite for sandy soils, gypsum for clays, ripping to improve infiltration
- **Surface management**: Where swales are used as tracks, gravel mulch is appropriate; coarse sand (raked to keep swales weed-free) also helps
- **Salt management**: As swales develop, salt is carried down to groundwater level
- **Water sourcing**: Every desert settlement of 5 cm rainfall or less can direct all road and roof run-off and tank overflow into swales

## Saharawi Soil Rehabilitation Methods

The [[saharawi-people]] of North Africa, as refugees in Algeria, have developed two methods of soil treatment under irrigation that leaches salt. Both involve heroic or total soil rehabilitation:

### Method 1: Shingle Base (where pebbles are available)
Salt soil is removed to 1–2 m deep, a layer of shingle is laid down, and non-salted soil is carted in to fill the bed. This creates a perched water table above the drainage layer.

### Method 2: Sand Replacement and Deep Drains (where no stones are available)
Topsoil is removed (if clayey) to 20 cm, salt-free sand is brought in, and deep canals are cut to drain off surplus salt with the irrigation water.

## Desert Mulch Sources

Organic matter is as invaluable in deserts as elsewhere. Sources include:

- **Flood detritus**: Brought down in flood, which can be arrested on strong mulch-trap fences
- **Wind-blown material**: Tumbleweeds and wisps of plants, which settle in pits, swales, or are trapped on fences
- **Grown mulch**: Produced within gardens and orchards
- **Dedicated mulch plants**: Casuarina, bamboo, tamarisk, comfrey, some Acacia species, forage grasses and legumes planted for their mulch value or nitrogenous soil fixation
- **Desert vine trimmings**: Provide good mulch from regular pruning
- **Hedge species**: Regular clipping provides material
- **Household and storage wastes**: Kitchen scraps, paper, cardboard
- **Grazers on range**: If regularly penned, provide mulch-manure resources

## Categories of Mulches

### Domestic Mulches
Old skins, blankets, cotton or wool clothes, cardboard boxes, newspapers, hardboard, and thin planks. All of these, soaked, can be used to line sandy pits or tiled over the ground in raised beds before mulch is added.

### Collected Materials
Ashes, bones, dog, cattle, or horse manure — kept dry or pitted in circle pits for in-ground compost. Ashes and dry herbivore manure can be shredded or pounded.

### Fines
Chaff, bark, leaves, tea leaves, coffee grounds, flood detritus, rice or grain hulls, rotten wood, sawdust. These are ideally combined with shredded manures, sand, and ashes to fill up raised beds. They can be laid as a top or coarse mulch.

### Coarse Mulch
Logs, twigs, dry straw, thick bark, old wood. These are used as bed edges, or a 4–6 cm layer over which mixed soils and "fines" are spread for 18–20 cm thick.

### Toppings
Seagrass, woodchips, pine or Casuarina needles, cocoa bean husks. These form a cosmetic layer spread over the top of the earlier layers.

## Building a Mulched Garden Bed

A complete mulched garden bed is built in layers:

1. **Coarse mulch base** (4–6 cm): logs, twigs, dry straw, thick bark
2. **Mixed soil and fines** (18–20 cm): shredded manures, chaff, leaves, compost, sand, ashes
3. **Top mulch or cosmetic layer**: woodchips, pine needles, seagrass
4. **Planting methods** vary by type: seedlings in soil pockets, fine seeds on a soil lens, tubers pushed into mulch

These "instant" gardens can be made in drylands within hours. All stages are watered as completed. Mulch is built up progressively.

## Green Mulch Production

Good green mulches for grapes and tree crops can be gathered from:

- **Catch crops** of lupins, fava beans, and coppiced Acacia or mallee eucalypt — cut after rains and stored as silage or in earth pits
- **Lucerne** establishes in swales and damp areas
- **Casuarina leaf** or pine leaf can be regularly gathered from trees in the field

## Mulch and Soil Chemistry

We must largely rely on mulch to:

- **Bring down the pH** of alkaline soils
- **Make minerals available** that are locked in alkaline conditions

In mulch, free-living nitrogen-fixing organisms supply plant nutrients at a steady rate, and leaching is delayed or prevented. In tropical drylands, the greatest losses are in bare soil agriculture, when nitrogen, calcium, and potassium are leached to streams and groundwater.

### Nutrient Considerations

Wherever deep humus, mulch, or green manures are used, **dolomite** should be provided, as nitrates prevent copper and manganese mobility if no calcium or manganese is supplied.

## The Termite and Ant Principle

When mulching in deserts, we need to emulate the **ant and the termite**, who bury their organic materials out of the sun. Wherever possible:

- Place a **layer of stone, sand, or soil** over desert mulches
- Create **hollows** where trapped leaves will later be covered with sand
