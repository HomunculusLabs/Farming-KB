# Growing by Chunking

## The Strategy of Incremental Expansion

One of the most practical and important strategies
in [[ecological-garden-design]] is what [[hemenway-accelerated-succession]]
calls "growing by chunking." [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] attempting
to transform an entire landscape at once, the
gardener starts small, establishes one thriving
area, and then expands outward from that nucleus of
success. This approach dramatically reduces the risk
of failure and builds momentum as the garden
develops.

## Why All-at-Once Design Fails

Imposing an arbitrary, large, and untested pattern
on a landscape all at once is a recipe for disaster.
Hemenway warns that this approach often results in:

- **Unsuitable plantings** that don't match local
  conditions because the gardener hasn't had time
  to observe and understand the site.

- **Disconnected elements** that don't work well
  together because their interactions haven't been
  tested at a manageable scale.

- **Overwhelming maintenance demands** that
  discourage the gardener and lead to neglect of
  critical areas.

- **Expensive failures** when large plantings die
  because the gardener couldn't keep up with
  watering, weeding, and [[fukuoka-disease-pest-management]].

The result is often an unsatisfactory garden that
requires constant rescue efforts, or worse, a
complete abandonment of the ecological approach.

## The Chunking Approach Explained

Growing by chunking reverses this pattern. The
gardener starts small and close to the house,
finds out what works, gets one area growing
successfully, and then repeats the successful
pattern with appropriate variations for new sites.

In time, these many small patches of fertility and
thriving plants link up, bonding into a resilient,
healthy whole. The name comes from the ecological
concept of "chunking" -- building ecosystems one
functional unit at a time.

## The Roxanne Swentzell Example

Hemenway illustrates growing by chunking through
the experience of Roxanne Swentzell, who created a
remarkable garden in the harsh New Mexico desert.
When she first moved to her site, the land was
barren gravel desert. Everything they planted
died. The plants would cook in summer and freeze
or dry out in winter.

Her strategy, developed with permaculture designer
Joel Glanzberg, was to find sheltered spots, mulch
them, and plant pioneer species, usually native and
exotic nitrogen fixers like New Mexico locust,
Russian olive, and Siberian pea shrub. In the shade
of these pioneers, they planted fruit and nut trees.

As Joel explained: "We'd work on creating a
favorable spot, concentrating our resources there,
and then grow out from those nuclei. And those
nuclei started to link up." This strategy of
duplicating small successes rather than trying to
do everything at once pares down the chance of
failure by building on past successes.

## Practical Steps for Chunking

### Step 1: Start at Zone 1

Begin with the area closest to the house. This is
where you visit most frequently, where you can
monitor progress daily, and where the garden will
provide the most immediate benefit. A small,
intensively managed zone near the back door is
the ideal starting point.

### Step 2: Observe and Adapt

Before expanding, take time to observe what works
and what doesn't in your initial planting. Note
which species thrive, how water moves across the
site, where shade falls at different seasons, and
what insects and birds arrive. This observation
period is invaluable for designing the next chunk.

### Step 3: Replicate Success

Once a chunk is established and thriving, use it
as a template for the next expansion. Copy the
patterns that worked, modify the species selection
for the new site conditions, and maintain the
connections between chunks so they eventually
merge into one continuous ecosystem.

### Step 4: Link the Chunks

As each new area matures, ensure it connects
physically and functionally to existing plantings.
Shared wildlife corridors, connected mulch beds,
overlapping root zones, and interplanted insectary
species all help the chunks merge into a cohesive
whole.

## How Chunks Connect Over Time

The magic of growing by chunking becomes apparent
as the garden matures. Each chunk is not an island
but a node in an expanding network. Birds carry
seeds between chunks. Insects migrate from one
planted area to another. Soil organisms spread
through connecting mulch beds. Water harvested
in one chunk benefits adjacent areas.

Eventually, the individual chunks become
indistinguishable, having merged into what
appears to be a single, designed landscape. But
unlike a design imposed all at once, this
landscape was built on proven success at every
stage.
