## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungal communities undergo predictable successional changes during forest development, driven by shifts in the quality and quantity of organic resources available in the soil. As forests mature, litter inputs shift from high-quality (low C:N ratio, readily decomposable) materials in young stands to increasingly recalcitrant (high C:N ratio, lignin-rich) materials in older stands. These changes in resource quality select for different mycorrhizal fungal species with varying degrees of enzyme competence and physiological function.

## The Concept of Mycorrhizal Succession

**Fleming et al. (1986)** first proposed the concept of mycorrhizal succession based on their observation of **concentric bands of different ectomycorrhizal fungal species** around the base of aging birch trees. The pattern they described shows:

- **Outermost ring**: "Early-stage" [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] — species adapted to high-quality, readily available nutrients
- **Inner rings (near tree base)**: "Late-stage" fungi — species with greater enzyme competence suited to accessing recalcitrant organic matter

This spatial pattern reflects a temporal succession as the tree and its associated soil environment change over time.

## Succession of Fruiting Body Communities

**Dighton et al. (1986)** surveyed ectomycorrhizal fruit body production in Sitka spruce and lodgepole pine stands of different ages and revealed a general pattern of mycorrhizal succession that was subsequently linked to changes in:

- **Forest floor resource quality** (Dighton and Mason, 1985)
- **Physiological function of the mycorrhizal fungi** (Last et al., 1987)

The general pattern agrees with Read"s (1991a) observation that mycorrhizal species with higher enzyme competence dominate in older forest stands where greater amounts of recalcitrant organic material accumulate.

**Caveat**: There is ongoing debate about the reliability of using fruit body surveys as an index of mycorrhizal abundance and dominance compared to direct measures of mycorrhizal root tip abundance (Termorshuizen and Schaffers, 1989; Egli et al., 1993; Yamada and Katsuya, 2001).

## Resource Quality Drives Fungal Succession

The parallel between mycorrhizal succession and saprotrophic fungal succession is clear. **Ponge (1990, 1991)** showed that ectomycorrhizal invasion of decomposing pine needles occurs during the **latter stages of decomposition**, when:

- Saprotrophic fungal and faunal activity has rendered the litter matrix more penetrable by roots and mycorrhizal fungi
- Nutrients have become more available in inorganic form
- The remaining organic material is increasingly recalcitrant

These successions vary depending on the nature of the initial resource. In general, the pattern follows:

1. **Early decomposition** (high-quality resources): Dominated by [[saprotrophic-fungi]] and fast-growing "early-stage" ECM fungi
2. **Mid decomposition** (mixed resource quality): Transition zone with both early and late-stage ECM species
3. **Late decomposition** (recalcitrant organic matter): Dominated by "late-stage" ECM fungi with high enzyme competence

## Nutrient Patch Exploitation and Local Successions

**Sagara (1995)** documented dramatic fungal successions on localized nutrient-rich patches arising from urine, feces, and dead animal bodies in pine forests in Japan. The temporal sequence on these patches follows a clear pattern:

### Dead Cat Substrate
| Time (days) | Species | Functional Role |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | *Ascobolus denudatus*, *Tephrocybe tesquorum* | Early saprotrophs |
| ~100–200 | *[[hebeloma-spoliatum]]* | Ammonium-tolerant ECM |
| ~300–600 | *[[lactarius-chrysorrheus]]*, *Mitrulia sp.* | Later ECM species |
| ~1700 | *Lactarius chrysorrheus* | Late-stage ECM |

### Aqueous Ammonium Treatment
| Time (days) | Species | Functional Role |
|---|---|---|
| 0–100 | *Ascobolus denudatus*, *Amblyosporium botrytis* | Early colonizers |
| ~100–300 | *Coprinus echinosporum*, *Tephrocybe* spp. | Mid-succession |
| ~300–600 | *Peziza* spp., *Tephrocybe ambusta* | Transition species |
| ~300–1700 | *[[laccaria-bicolor]]*, *Hebeloma* spp. | Late-stage ECM with ammonium affinity |

Notably, **Hebeloma radicosum** specifically exploits subterranean mole middens and can "defend" its site of occupancy against the more common *H. spoliatum*, suggesting that local environmental variables (nutrient availability) drive competitive outcomes.

## Mycorrhizal Mats: Dense Networks in Forest Soils

Many ectomycorrhizal species with enzyme activity produce rapid hyphal growth and large mycelial networks, often aggregated into **cords or rhizomorphs** that allow translocation of nutrients from distal parts of the extraradical mycelial network to the root (Read, 1991a; Rayner et al., 1985; Cairney, 1992).

In some forests, these mycelial networks become so dense they are termed **"mats"**:

- Mats may form **10–20% of the top 10 cm** of soil in temperate forest ecosystems (Cromack et al., 1988)
- They account for **45–55% of total soil biomass** (Cromack et al., 1979)

**Aguilera et al. (1993)** showed that mat-forming ectomycorrhizal communities in Douglas fir forests are increasingly important in removing organic nitrogen from the soil pool and immobilizing it into high C:N ratio fungal tissue as forest growth progresses. This creates a paradox: the forest soil becomes enriched with organic nitrogen as it matures, but this N becomes **increasingly less available** to plant growth.

## Hyphal Plasticity and Foraging Strategy

**Tibbett (2000)** demonstrated that extraradical hyphae of both ericoid and ectomycorrhizal symbioses exhibit significant **morphological and physiological plasticity** (Bending and Read, 1995a, b; Cairney and Burke, 1996), making them ideally suited for exploiting patchily distributed nutrient resources.

**Dickie and Koide (1998)** showed that hyphal foraging strategy responds to nitrogen availability:

- **Low nitrogen concentrations** (inorganic or organic): Production of **less dense but more extensive** hyphal growth, maximizing soil exploration
- **High nitrogen concentrations**: Denser, more compact hyphal growth

This response mirrors that of saprotrophic fungi (Ritz, 1995; Rayner, 1991) and represents an adaptive strategy for exploiting heterogeneous nutrient distributions.

## The PACER Concept: Phosphate Cycling in Tropical Forests

**Newbery et al. (1997)** proposed the **Phenological and Climatic Ectomycorrhizal Response (PACER)** model based on studies of tropical legume trees (cesalps) with ectomycorrhizal associations. Key elements:

- ECM fungal communities on roots of some tropical legumes enable exploitation of **phosphorus in deeper soil layers** than those colonized by surface feeder roots
- This keeps phosphorus cycling within the biotic components of the forest, minimizing leaching losses
- There is a strong interaction among:
  - Phosphate acquisition capacity of mycorrhizae
  - Environmental controls on phosphate release
  - **Seasonal P demands by trees**, especially during mast years

## Root Zone Positioning and Direct Nutrient Uptake

**Répač (1996)** showed that ectomycorrhizal colonization of tree roots **increases in the presence of organic matter**. The juxtaposition of roots, fungal hyphae, and nutrient-rich organic material in upper humic soil horizons provides the optimal arrangement for:

1. Mineralization of organic nutrients
2. Direct uptake of mineralized nutrients by roots
3. Minimization of leakage losses to drainage water

Much of the fine root system and its associated mycorrhizae in temperate forests occurs in the **upper organic humic soil horizons**, where this direct coupling is most effective.

## Differential Effects of Mycorrhizal Types

**van der Heijden (2001)** demonstrated that in willow (*Salix repens*), which can associate with either arbuscular or ectomycorrhizal partners, the two types have functionally distinct effects:

| Parameter | Arbuscular (*Glomus mosseae*) | Ectomycorrhizal (*Hebeloma leucosarx*) |
|---|---|---|
| Root colonization | Low | High |
| Short-term shoot growth | Significant effect | Less pronounced initially |
| Long-term growth benefit | Limited | Significant improvement |
| Shoot P uptake | Higher | — |
| Seasonal sensitivity | Higher response in December vs. March | No difference between dates |

These differential effects likely reflect the seasonal availability of nutrients and the fundamentally different physiological strategies of the two mycorrhizal types.

## Forest Ecosystem as a Fungal-Permeated Network

**Rayner (1998)** and **Vogt et al. (1991)** envision the forest ecosystem as permeated by fungal mycelia acting as a **plumbing system** that conveys carbon and nutrients among ecosystem elements. The traditional view of nutrient cycling — saprotrophic decomposition followed by mineralization and plant uptake aided by mycorrhizae — has been replaced by an understanding of **synergistic interactions** between saprotrophic and mycorrhizal communities, with mycorrhizal fungi playing a much more active role in the decomposition process itself.

## Summary

Mycorrhizal succession is driven by predictable changes in forest floor resource quality as ecosystems develop. Early-stage ECM fungi dominate young forests with readily available nutrients, while late-stage fungi with greater enzyme competence dominate mature forests with recalcitrant organic matter. The formation of dense mycorrhizal mats, hyphal foraging plasticity, and the PACER response all represent adaptations that optimize nutrient cycling efficiency in forest ecosystems.
