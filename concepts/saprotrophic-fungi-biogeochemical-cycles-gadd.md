# Saprotrophic Fungi and Biogeochemical Cycles

## Overview

Geomycology encompasses the interactions between fungi and rocks, minerals,
metals and organic matter, and their significance in element cycling and
environmental change. Rocks and minerals represent a vast reservoir of
elements essential to life, including bulk biological metals, essential
trace metals, and phosphorus. All these elements must be released into
bioavailable forms through weathering via physical, chemical and biological
processes. Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities
since the Ordovician period, approximately 480 to 460 million years ago.

Their filamentous growth habit, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, ability to
translocate nutrients within mycelial networks, and capacity to form
hydrated mucilaginous sheaths ensure that fungal processes are integral to
biogeochemical change. Fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at
higher pH values and over a wider redox range more efficiently than
bacteria, making them key agents in terrestrial [[soil-protozoa-nutrient-cycling-and-mineralization]] soil
formation.

## Fungal Weathering Mechanisms

Two synergistic actions drive fungal mineral degradation: biomechanical
and biochemical weathering. Biomechanical weathering is direct through
extensive hyphal penetration into decayed rocks and tunnelling along crystal
planes and grain boundaries, facilitated by thigmotropic reactions and
lubrication with mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating
metabolites. Hyphal turgor pressure provides the mechanical force for
penetration. Indirect biomechanical weathering occurs through shrinking
and swelling of hydrated mucilage produced by many fungi.

Biochemical weathering is considered more important and operates through
three principal mechanisms. Acidolysis (proton-promoted dissolution) occurs
when fungi acidify their microenvironment through excretion of protons,
organic acids and carbonic acid. Complexolysis (ligand-promoted dissolution)
involves excretion of metal-complexing metabolites including carboxylic
acids, amino acids, siderophores and phenolic compounds. Redoxolysis
involves reduction and oxidation of metal species. These mechanisms are
enhanced by metal accumulation in and around fungal biomass.

## Carbon, Nitrogen and Phosphorus Cycling

Fungi play central roles in cycling the major elements. As dominant
decomposers of lignin and cellulose, fungi mineralize organic carbon and
release carbon dioxide. Their filamentous growth enables exploration of
[[fungal-mycelial-foraging-heterogeneous-environments]] and access to physically or chemically protected
organic matter within soil aggregates. Natural abundance of stable isotopes
such as carbon-13 and nitrogen-15 provides tracers for tracking fungal
nutrient pathways and distinguishing fungal from bacterial contributions.

Phosphorus mobilization is one of the most important functions of
mycorrhizal fungi. Mycorrhizal hyphal tips produce micro- to millimolar
concentrations of organic acids including oxalic, citric, succinic and
malic acids, associated with micropores in weatherable soil minerals.
The ectomycorrhizal fungus Piloderma extracted potassium and magnesium
from biotite, microcline and chlorite to satisfy nutritional
requirements. Ericoid and ectomycorrhizal fungi dissolve cadmium, copper,
zinc and lead minerals including metal phosphates, demonstrating that
fungal weathering extends to toxic metal tolerance.

## Secondary Mineral Formation

Fungi precipitate [[gadd-mycogenic-mineral-precipitation-secondary-minerals]] through metabolism-independent and
dependent processes. Microbial carbonate precipitation coupled with
silicate weathering provides an important carbon dioxide sink. Many
limestones and calcic soil horizons are cemented with calcite and
whewellite (calcium oxalate monohydrate) of fungal origin. Fungi produce
metal oxalates with calcium, cadmium, cobalt, copper, manganese, zinc,
nickel and lead, with calcium oxalate being the most common form.

Precipitation of carbonates, phosphates and hydroxides increases soil
aggregation. Hyphae enmesh soil particles, alter alignment and release
organic metabolites enhancing aggregate stability. Chitin in fungal cell
walls serves as a nucleation substrate for calcite. During hyphal
decomposition, calcite crystals act as sites for further precipitation.
Reduced [[staycare-fungal-biomethylation-of-metals-and-metalloids]] such as elemental silver, selenium and
tellurium can be precipitated within and around fungal cells.

## Mycorrhizal Symbioses in Nutrient Cycling

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic partnerships with nearly all land
plants, representing a remarkable adaptation for exploiting soil and rock
environments. They are involved in proton- and ligand-promoted metal
mobilization, metal immobilization via biosorption and accumulation, and
extracellular precipitation of toxic metal oxalates. Their activities
enhance weathering of soil minerals and release metal cations.

Ectomycorrhizal mycelia respond to different soil minerals by regulating
growth, colonization, carbon allocation and substrate acidification.
Carbon allocation in [[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]] was greater when colonizing
potassium feldspar than quartz patches. Zinc phosphate dissolution studies
showed phosphate acquisition and zinc accumulation depended on mycorrhizal
status, fungal zinc tolerance and [[phosphorus-availability]]. These
interactions are context-dependent and may vary with biotic and abiotic
factors.

## Environmental Significance

Fungal responses to heterogeneous nutrient distribution parallel
macroorganism behavioural flexibility. Fungi adopt exploration or
exploitation strategies, form linear aggregated organs for protected
translocation, and dynamically reconfigure network architecture. Rock-
dwelling communities are classified as epilithic, hypolithic, endolithic,
chasmolithic, cryptoendolithic or euendolithic, representing specialized
colonization strategies.

The ability to transform toxic metals has potential for bioremediation.
Fungal immobilization via biosorption, accumulation and precipitation
detoxifies metals. However, fungal mineral-solubilizing activity may also
transform remediation products, such as phosphate-induced metal
stabilization minerals, highlighting the need to account for fungal
activity in long-term remediation planning. Fungi also contribute to
deterioration of building materials including concrete, raising concerns
for infrastructure including nuclear waste containment.

## Key Points

- Fungi dissolve minerals more efficiently than bacteria across wider pH
  and redox ranges through biomechanical and biochemical mechanisms.
- Three biochemical mechanisms operate: acidolysis, complexolysis and
  redoxolysis, enhanced by metal accumulation.
- Mycorrhizal fungi are critical for phosphorus mobilization from
  insoluble mineral sources.
- Fungi precipitate secondary minerals including carbonates and oxalates
  that influence soil structure and act as carbon sinks.
- Fungal weathering extends to toxic metal tolerance and immobilization.
- Fungal responses to heterogeneous environments parallel macroorganism
  exploration and exploitation strategies.
