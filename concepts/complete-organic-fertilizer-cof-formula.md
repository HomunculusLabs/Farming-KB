# Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) Formula

The Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) is Steve Solomon's signature recipe for a balanced, slow-release organic fertilizer blend that provides all essential plant nutrients. Detailed in *Gardening When It Counts*, COF is designed to produce highly nutritious vegetables while maintaining soil health. Unlike chemical fertilizers that provide only NPK and some secondary nutrients, COF delivers a complete spectrum of minerals and trace elements.

## Why Complete Organic Fertilizer?

Solomon argues that simply spreading manure and compost is no guarantee of making garden soil that will produce highly nutritious vegetables. Manure quality varies enormously depending on what the animals were fed. If the soils in your area do not contain a perfect balance of minerals, then even adding large amounts of manure and compost may not correct the deficiency. COF fills this gap by providing known quantities of every essential nutrient.

The concept is rooted in the work of William Albrecht, who demonstrated that the nutritional quality of food is directly linked to soil mineral balance. Data from the USDA shows that the mineral content of American vegetables has declined about 2 percent over the past 25 years, and the protein content of wheat has also steadily decreased.

## The COF Formula

All materials are measured out by **volume** (scoops, bucketfuls, jarfuls, etc.). Proportions may vary plus or minus 10 percent of the targeted volume and will work out close enough. Do not attempt to make this formula by weight.

### Base Mix

Choose **one** of the following nitrogen sources:

**Option A (standard):**
- 4 parts any kind of seedmeal **except** cottonseed meal
- OR 4 parts less-potent cottonseed meal, supplemented with 1 part tankage to boost nitrogen content

**Option B (higher nitrogen, slightly better for leafy crops in spring):**
- 3 parts any seedmeal except cottonseed meal
- AND 1 part "tankage" (sometimes called "blood-and-bone" or "meatmeal")

### Lime Blend

Add the following lime components:

- 1/2 part ordinary agricultural lime (best finely ground)
- 1/2 part gypsum (if you don't use gypsum, double the quantity of agricultural lime)
- 1/2 part dolomite lime

### Phosphorus Source

Add **1 part** of any one of these phosphorus sources:

- Finely ground rock phosphate (there are two kinds: "hard" or "soft")
- Bonemeal
- High-phosphate guano

### Trace Minerals

Add **1 part** of either:

- Kelpmeal
- OR basalt dust

These are rich in trace elements and provide growth regulators, natural hormones, and resistance to cold, frost, and other stresses.

## Understanding the Ingredients

### Seedmeals

Seedmeals are byproducts of making vegetable oil, mainly from linseed (flax), sunflowers, cotton, soybeans, canola (rapeseed), and safflower. When chemically analyzed, most seedmeals show an NPK of about 6-4-2, although cottonseed meal (from which the oil is pressed out and used mainly for soap) is about one-third weaker. Cottonseed meal has one advantage: coconuts are perennial, so the meal is free of pesticide residues common in other oilseeds.

Seedmeals also contain calcium, magnesium, and trace nutrients, and these vary from kind to kind and from purchase to purchase. Because seedmeals are not classified as fertilizers, they are labeled by their protein percentage rather than NPK. The general rule is that for each 6 percent nitrogen, there is roughly 6 percent protein, so buy whichever seedmeal offers the most protein for the least cost. Store dry and protected from moisture in a tight container.

### Tankage

Tankage is a byproduct of slaughterhouses — the whole animal minus the meat, hide, and bones, cooked down and dried. Solomon's preferred tankage analyzes at 10-4-0. It has been a standard agricultural fertilizer for more than 20 years. If you cannot find tankage, the only consequence is that your COF will be a bit lower in nitrogen, requiring you to use a bit more of it to get the same result.

### Limes

There are three types of lime, all ground natural rock containing various amounts of calcium:

- **Agricultural lime**: Relatively pure calcium carbonate
- **Dolomitic lime**: Contains both calcium and magnesium, usually in roughly equal amounts
- **Gypsum**: Calcium sulfate

Do not use quicklime, burnt lime, hydrated lime, or other chemically processed limes. If you have to choose only one kind, agricultural lime is probably the best choice, but you would get a far better result using a mixture of the three types. Limes are inexpensive when bought in large sacks from agricultural supply dealers.

### The pH Myth

Solomon strongly advises readers to forget about adjusting soil pH. Liming to adjust soil pH may be standard practice in chemical farming, but is not of concern in an organic garden. If a soil test shows low pH and you are advised to lime to correct it — don't. Each year just add what the COF formula calls for. Over time the pH will correct itself, more because of the added organic matter than from adding calcium and/or magnesium. If your soil pH tests as acceptable, use the full lime recommendation anyway because vegetables still need calcium and magnesium, and in the right balance.

With routine use of COF, there will never be any need to lime the garden separately. COF is formulated so that when applied at the recommended rate, it automatically distributes about the right amount of calcium per 1,000 square feet per year.

### Kelpmeal and Basalt Dust

Kelpmeal may seem costly per pound, but it adds considerable fortitude to the plants. Basalt dust, which is mineralized and contains a broad and complete spectrum of trace elements, may be substituted for kelpmeal. Either should be applied at least once a year, preferably in spring.

### Rock Phosphate, Bonemeal, and Guano

All three of these boost the phosphorus content. They are rich in trace elements and sulfur, though the phosphorus content of some deposits has steadily decreased. They are becoming increasingly expensive. For the best results, use finely ground rock phosphate or high-phosphate guano.

## Application Rates

Apply four to six liters (about one gallon) of COF atop each 100 square feet of raised bed or down each 50 feet of row, 12 to 18 inches wide. Work the fertilizer in with a hoe or spade. If you practice no-dig gardening, just spread it on top — earthworms will incorporate it. When making hills (for squash, corn, etc.), mix an additional amount of COF into each hill.

This base application provides enough fertility for low-demand vegetables (carrots, parsnips, beans, peas) to grow to their maximum potential and will usually adequately feed all medium-demand vegetables. For high-demand vegetables, additional side-dressings of COF or concentrated fertilizer may be needed during the growing season.
