# Solomon Planting and Transplanting Guide

## Direct Seeding vs Transplanting

Solomon has a clear preference for direct seeding over transplanting for most vegetables, with some important exceptions. His reasoning is based on root system development: transplanting destroys the taproot and converts the plant into more of a surface feeder. Direct-seeded crops endure rainless spells better because their deeper root systems can access subsoil moisture.

## Seed Starting

### Seed Quality

Solomon emphasizes buying quality seed from reputable suppliers. Important qualities of seed:
- High germination rate (seed must pass germination regulations)
- Vigor (ability to emerge strongly under less-than-ideal conditions)
- Genetic purity (especially important for OP varieties)
- Disease-free

Defective seed can often be identified visually. Seed that is cracked, discolored, or excessively small compared to normal should be discarded.

### Planting Depth

A general rule: plant seed at a depth of 2-3 times the seed's diameter. However, this varies by soil type:
- In sandy soils, plant slightly deeper (moisture is lower near the surface)
- In heavy/clay soils, plant slightly shallower (emergence is harder through dense soil)
- In hot weather, plant slightly deeper (moisture retreats deeper)

### Presprouting (Chitting)

Presprouting seeds before planting can speed germination and improve success rates, especially for slow-germinating species. Presprouting is particularly recommended for:
- Large seeds of vining crops (squash, cucumbers, melons)
- Peas in cool soil
- Parsnips and other slow germinators

Method: Place seeds between layers of moist paper towel in a sealed container at room temperature. Check daily. Plant immediately when the first root (radicle) emerges -- do not allow the sprout to grow long, as it is fragile and easily damaged during planting.

## Thinning

Proper thinning is essential for the extensive spacing system. Solomon's approach:

1. **Initial emergence:** When seeds first germinate, lightly thin the densest clumps (for multi-seed crops like beets, where each "seed" is actually a fruit containing multiple seeds)
2. **At 2-4 inches tall:** Postpone the final precise thinning until plants are about 4 inches (10 cm) tall, because there will usually be a fair number of mysterious seedling disappearances
3. **Final thinning:** Thin to whatever spacing you want the mature plants to be growing at

For beets: After initial thinning, thin at 4 inches tall to the final spacing. Well-formed beets require careful thinning.

For corn: Plant several seeds per spot, thin to one plant when they are about 4 inches tall and securely established. If you get an odd failure, grow a replacement in the adjoining spot.

## Transplanting Tips

### When to Transplant

Some crops benefit from or require transplanting:
- **Onions:** Especially in shorter-season areas, start indoors and transplant. Fortunately, onions transplant easily. The trick is to use a variety that matches your latitude and to direct-sow if the season is long enough
- **Celery/Celeriac:** Usually started as seedlings. A single row of seedlings produces enough to transplant for the garden. However, where water is short, Solomon suggests direct-seeding small clusters of seeds in their final growing positions and thinning each clump progressively to a single one
- **Peppers and eggplants:** These need a long season and are usually started indoors
- **Tomatoes:** Started indoors for early harvest, but can also be direct-seeded in long-season areas

### Transplanting Technique

When transplanting seedlings:
1. Water the seedling flat thoroughly before transplanting
2. Dig the hole first, then gently remove the seedling with as much root ball as possible
3. Set the seedling at the same depth it was growing (except tomatoes, which can be planted deeper -- they will root along the buried stem)
4. Firm soil gently around roots to eliminate air pockets
5. Water immediately after transplanting
6. If plants show signs of stress after transplanting (wilting), provide temporary shade

### Hardening Off

Seedlings grown indoors or in greenhouses must be gradually acclimated to outdoor conditions:
- Begin 1-2 weeks before planned transplant date
- Start by setting seedlings outside for a few hours in a sheltered location
- Gradually increase exposure to sun and wind
- Reduce watering slightly during the hardening-off period
- Bring plants indoors if frost is expected

### Reasons Not to Use Transplants

Solomon provides several reasons to avoid transplants when possible:
1. Transplanting destroys the taproot, converting deep-rooted plants into shallow feeders
2. Transplants require more water until their root systems recover
3. The cost of transplants (if purchased) is high compared to seed
4. Direct-seeded plants are more drought-resistant
5. Transplant shock can set plants back significantly, especially in hot weather
6. The labor of raising transplants is significant if done at home

## Succession Planting

For crops that produce over a limited harvest window (radishes, lettuce, bush beans), make repeated sowings at 2-3 week intervals to extend the harvest period. However, Solomon notes that with his extensive spacing system, many crops produce for much longer than intensive plantings, reducing the need for frequent succession sowings.

For crops that produce continuously (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, indeterminate beans), a single well-spaced planting will produce over a much longer season than closely spaced plants.

## Seed Storage and Viability

- Store seed in a cool, dry place
- Properly dried seed at moderate temperatures lasts far longer than moist seed
- Beet and chard seed: 6-10 years
- Brassica seed: 4-5 years
- Legume seed: 3-5 years
- Solanum seed: 3-5 years
- Allium seed: 1-2 years (shortest viability of common vegetables)
- If seed sprouts poorly, it was either stored poorly or was already old when purchased
