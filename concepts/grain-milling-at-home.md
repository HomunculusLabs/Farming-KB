## Overview

Home grain milling transforms whole grains into fresh flour at the point of use. Freshly milled flour retains the full spectrum of nutrients, oils, and flavor that commercial flour loses during processing and storage. For off-grid and self-sufficient homesteads, milling provides independence from commercial flour supplies and access to the full nutritional value of whole grains.

## Why Mill at Home

### Nutritional Advantages

Commercial whole wheat flour has lost significant nutritional value by the time it reaches the consumer:

- **Vitamin E degradation** — Wheat germ oil contains vitamin E, which oxidizes rapidly once the grain is crushed. Commercial whole wheat flour typically loses 50-90% of its vitamin E within days of milling
- **Enzyme activity** — Fresh flour contains active enzymes (amylase, protease) that improve dough development and bread quality
- **Flavor** — Fresh flour has a complex, slightly sweet, nutty flavor that commercial flour lacks
- **Complete nutrition** — The bran, germ, and endosperm remain intact and balanced

A whole grain kernel contains:
- **Bran** (14% by weight): Fiber, B vitamins, minerals, protein
- **Germ** (3% by weight): Healthy fats, vitamin E, B vitamins, antioxidants
- **Endosperm** (83% by weight): Starch, protein (gluten), some B vitamins

Commercial white flour removes the bran and germ entirely. Commercial whole wheat flour includes them but the oils in the germ begin oxidizing immediately.

### Shelf Life Comparison

| Product | Shelf Life at Room Temp |
|---------|------------------------|
| Whole grain berries | 6-12 months (cool, dry storage) |
| Freshly milled flour | 1-3 days (full nutrition) |
| Freshly milled flour | 1-2 weeks (usable, declining quality) |
| Commercial whole wheat flour | 2-3 months |
| Commercial all-purpose flour | 6-12 months |

## Grain Mills: Stone vs Steel

### Stone Mills (Burr Mills)

Stone mills crush grain between two circular stones — one stationary, one rotating with grooves that move grain from center to edge.

**Advantages:** Lower milling temperatures (preserves nutrients), broader [[vermiculite-particle-size-water-retention-calibration-pf-tek]] range, quiet, can mill oily grains and seeds.

**Disadvantages:** Slower (1-10 cups/min depending on manual/electric), stones need periodic dressing, may leave trace stone particles.

**Notable models:** Mockmill ($250-300, attaches to KitchenAid), WonderMill Junior Deluxe ($300-350, hand crank), Komo Classic ($500-700, electric).

### Steel Burr Mills

Steel burr mills use toothed steel plates instead of stones:

**Advantages:**
- Faster milling than stone
- Adjustable from coarse meal to fine flour
- Easier to clean than stone mills
- Durable plates last many years

**Disadvantages:**
- Higher milling temperatures than stone (some nutrient degradation)
- Less consistent flour texture
- Steel plates wear over time and require replacement
- Can produce a metallic taste if plates become worn
### Impact Mills (Micronizers)

Impact mills use high-speed stainless steel blades (20,000+ RPM) to pulverize grain.

**Advantages:** Very fast (up to 20 cups/min), very fine consistent flour, no stones to dress.

**Disadvantages:** Highest milling temperature (nutrient degradation), cannot mill oily grains (clogs), loudest type, no hand-crank option, cannot produce coarse meal.

**Notable models:** WonderMill Electric ($250-300), NutriMill Classic ($300-350), Blendtec Kitchen Mill ($250-300).

### Hand-Cranked Mills

For off-grid use: throughput 1-3 cups/min. Country Living Grain Mill ($450-500, lifetime warranty) is the gold standard. Budget option: Victorio ($50-80) for occasional use. Many hand-cranked mills can be retrofitted with electric motors.

## Grains for Home Milling

### Wheat Berries

The primary grain for most home millers:

**Hard Red Wheat:**
- Protein content: 11-15%
- Best for: Bread, rolls, pizza dough
- Produces hearty, rustic loaves with good rise
- The most common choice for bread flour

**Hard White Wheat:**
- Protein content: 11-14%
- Best for: Bread, all-purpose baking
- Milder flavor than red wheat, lighter color
- Good all-around choice if you want one wheat

**Soft White Wheat:**
- Protein content: 8-10%
- Best for: Pastries, cakes, cookies, muffins
- Lower protein means less gluten, tender crumb
- Produces lighter, softer baked goods

**Soft Red Wheat:**
- Protein content: 8-10%
- Best for: Pastries, flatbreads, crackers

**Durum Wheat:**
- Protein content: 13-15%
- Best for: Pasta, couscous
- Very hard grain — requires a sturdy mill
- High gluten strength with golden color

### Other Grains

- **Spelt** — Ancient wheat relative, nutty flavor, works well in bread (lower gluten, may need less water)
- **Rye** — Low gluten, distinctive flavor, requires different dough handling. See [[homestead-bakery-guide]]
- **Oats** — Groats can be flaked in a roller mill or milled into flour. High oil content; best milled in stone burr mills
- **Corn** — For cornmeal and polenta. Steel burr or stone mill. Can be milled to various textures from coarse grits to fine cornmeal
- **Barley** — Mild flavor, low gluten. Often mixed with wheat for bread
- **Buckwheat** — Not a true grain (a pseudocereal). Gluten-free, strong flavor, good for pancakes and soba noodles
- **Rice** — [[brown-rice-flour-substrate]] can be milled into flour for gluten-free baking

## Grinding Fresh Flour: Practical Tips

### Before Milling

1. **Clean the grain** — Spread berries on a sheet and pick out any debris, small stones, or weed seeds. A grain cleaner or simple sifter helps
2. **Measure the grain** — 1 cup of wheat berries produces approximately 1.5 cups of flour by volume, or 4.5 ounces by weight
3. **Check moisture** — Grains should be dry (10-12% moisture). If grains are damp, dry in a low oven (150F) for 30-60 minutes before milling

### During Milling

1. **Set the grind consistency** — Start coarser and adjust finer until the desired texture is achieved
2. **Mill in batches** — Do not overload the mill. Feed grain steadily for best results
3. **Sift if desired** — For finer pastry flour, sift fresh flour through a fine mesh sieve. The bran that remains can be used in granola or added to [[fukuoka-textdoc-plowing-ruins-soil-compaction-bread-dough-analogy]]
4. **Let flour cool** — If milling produces warm flour, let it cool to room temperature before using (warm flour ferments too quickly in sourdough)

### After Milling

1. **Use immediately or store briefly** — Fresh flour is at peak nutrition within hours of milling
2. **Short-term storage** — Room temperature in a paper bag or breathable container for 1-2 weeks
3. **Longer storage** — Refrigerate for up to 1 month, freeze for up to 3 months
4. **Do not store fresh flour in sealed containers at room temperature** — The oils in the germ will go rancid
