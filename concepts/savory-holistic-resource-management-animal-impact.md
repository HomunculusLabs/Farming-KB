# Animal Impact and Herd Effect

Animal impact is one of the six ecosystem management tools in Allan Savory's
holistic management framework. It refers to the physical effects that large
hooved animals have on soil surfaces and vegetation through trampling,
dunging, and urination. The concept of herd effect, a specific form of
animal impact, is critical to understanding how [[savory-holistic-resource-management-brittle-vs-nonbrittle-environments]] evolved and how they can be restored.

## The Discovery

Savory's university training taught that large animals damaged land and
that only keeping numbers low and scattering stock widely would prevent
destructive trampling. Years of field observation led to the opposite
conclusion: relatively high numbers of heavy, herding animals, concentrated
and moving as they naturally do in the presence of predators, support the
health of the very lands we thought they destroyed.

Thousands of hours tracking people across all sorts of country in Zimbabwe
sharpened Savory's observational skills. He noticed vast differences between
land where wildlife herded naturally, where people herded domestic stock,
and where stock was merely fenced in without herding. Compared to areas
without large animals, the differences were startling. Where animals were
present, plants were green and growing. In areas without animals, plants
were often grey and dying.

## How Herding Animals Behave

When feeding, herding animals tend to spread out, walk gently, and place
hooves beside coarse plants rather than on top of them. They place their
full weight on their hooves, compacting soil below the surface but hardly
disturbing the surface itself. While feeding, they have remarkably little
impact on plants and soil beyond the removal of forage.

Once feeding is over and the herd begins to move, or when predators
threaten, behavior changes dramatically. Animals bunch together and kick
up dust. While bunched as a herd, each animal steps recklessly. Coarse
plants are trampled down, providing soil cover. Trotting and galloping
hooves chip and break the soil surface like a gardener's hoe. The herd
beats down steep gully edges, creating more gradual slopes.

## Stock Density vs Herd Effect

Two distinct guidelines govern the application of animal impact:
- **Stock density**: The concentration of animals on the unit of land where
  they actually are at a given moment (not to be confused with stocking
  rate, which is the number of animals continuously supported by a unit
  of land).
- **Herd effect**: The excited movement of crowded, hooved animals when
  under threat or attack by predators, in full migration, being driven,
  or competing for food.

Normally, grazing animals place their hooves carefully and barely break the
soil surface. When herd effect occurs, the same animals break down coarse
plants, raise dust, chip soil surfaces, and open the soil to aeration. The
distinction is critical and easy to identify in the field.

## Partial Rest

Land sustains partial rest when animals are present but never have cause
to produce herd effect. They may slightly disturb the soil cover but seldom
stimulate a successional shift to more complex communities. The millions
of acres in America deteriorating under the combination of partial rest and
overgrazing support this conclusion.

## Why Animal Impact Is Essential in Brittle Environments

As [[savory-holistic-resource-management-brittle-vs-nonbrittle-environments]] evolved over
millions of years, predators and their herding prey were the only things
that could realistically have both created the necessary soil disturbance
to provide a good seedbed for new plants and protected bare soil by
trampling down old plant material. Both functions are critical to the health
of brittle environments, and the world's large populations of herding
animals appear to have evolved mainly in such areas.

The destruction of predator populations and wild herds in America
precipitated the decline in the environment we see today. This has been
exacerbated by spreading relatively few domestic animals over large areas,
which explains why land deterioration has occurred more rapidly in America
than in Africa.

## The Practical Effects of Animal Impact

Periodic high animal impact in brittle environments:
- Promotes [[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]] on
  bare, gullied, and eroding ground
- On dense grassland, retards the shift to woody communities
- Improves [[savory-water-cycle-management]] and [[holmgren-mineral-balance-soil-fertility]] more effectively than
  fire or machinery
- Increases [[savory-energy-flow-and-solar-capture]]
- Removes old oxidizing plant material and scatters it as litter
- Breaks soil surface capping and algal communities between plants
- Provides compaction enabling grass seedlings to establish

Low animal impact, typically associated with partial rest:
- Produces bare ground by disturbing algal communities without advancing
  succession
- Allows plant spacings to increase
- Reduces mineral and water cycles below the land's potential
- Reduces energy flow, especially when compounded by overgrazing

## Resilience to Extreme Impact

Trampling carried to extremes can cause temporary damage, but when the time
factor is controlled, most environments show astounding resilience. Two
thousand cattle concentrating during a heavy rainstorm created an
impressive quagmire, but eighteen months later the location could no
longer be identified. Continuous trampling around gates and water points
does not allow recovery, but such areas are usually insignificant compared
to the total land area.

## Traditional Knowledge

Many cultures recognized the connection between hooves and land health.
Scottish shepherds referred to the "golden hooves" of sheep. Navajo
medicine men warned that reducing sheep numbers would harm the soil.
South African oldtimers had a saying: "Hammer veld to sweeten it",
meaning literally to hammer the land with herds of livestock to improve
forage quality. These observations were rejected by the scientific
community of the time.
