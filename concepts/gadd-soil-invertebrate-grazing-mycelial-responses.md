## Soil Invertebrate Grazing Effects on Fungal Mycelia

## Overview

Fungal mycelia in soil and leaf litter face [[bloomfield-what-lies-beneath-mycelial-architecture-hyphal-biology-subterranean-fungus]], [[fungal-decomposition-processes]]. Rather than being merely destructive,
grazing regulates fungal growth strategies and functional diversity. Fungal
responses to invertebrate grazing reveal sophisticated adaptive mechanisms with
striking analogies to plant-herbivore interactions.

## Grazing-Induced Morphological Changes

### Shift from Dense Exploitative to Explorative Growth

When mycelia are grazed, they undergo dramatic morphological changes
representing a fundamental foraging strategy shift. Ungrazed decomposer
basidiomycetes typically exhibit dense, highly branched cord systems
intensively exploiting local resource patches. Grazing disrupts these
networks, removing hyphal tips and severing connections. In response, many
fungi shift to explorative growth: faster extension, thinner hyphae, wider
branch spacing, and reduced cord investment. This plasticity allows escape
from high grazing pressure and discovery of new resource patches beyond
current herbivore reach.

### Species-Specific Grazing Effects

Different grazers affect mycelia in distinct ways reflecting feeding apparatus,
behavior, and dietary preferences. Folsomia candida, a generalist Collembola,
consumes broad fungal species ranges but prefers thin hyphae over thick or
melanized ones. Hypogastrura species are more selective, targeting specific
fungal taxa with localized damage. Proisotoma minuta shows intermediate
selectivity but achieves substantial consumption at high densities. Multiple
grazer species with different strategies create complex selective landscapes
maintaining fungal diversity by preventing single-species dominance.
