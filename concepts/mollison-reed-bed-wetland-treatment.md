# Reed Bed and Wetland Water Treatment

## Overview

Mollison describes reed beds and constructed wetlands as the final
treatment stage in biological sewage systems, recommended by scientists
at the Max Planck Institute in Switzerland and already in use in
Holland. Waters polluted with metals, biocides, or sewage can be
cleaned by travelling through reed beds of Scirpus, Typha, and Juncus,
or by harvesting off floating plants such as water hyacinth. The rushes
and sedges can be mown and removed periodically for mulch or
cellulose.

## Species and Their Functions

Mollison provides a specific list of species and their treatment
capabilities:

### Phragmites communis and species, Typha species
- Flocculate colloids
- Dry out sludges
- Eliminate pathogens
- These are the primary reed bed species used worldwide

### Schoenoplectus species
- Take up copper, cobalt, nickel, and manganese
- Exude mould antibiotics
- Particularly effective for heavy metal removal

### Scirpus species
- Break down phenols, including toxic pentachlorophenol
- Important for industrial waste treatment

### Additional Pathogen-Removing Species
Also active in pathogen removal (species must be tested and selected
for specific problems):

- Alisma plantago-aquatica
- Mentha aquatica
- Juncus effusus
- Schoenoplectus lacustris
- Spartina species
- Iris pseudacorus

### Chlorinated Hydrocarbon Treatment
For chlorinated hydrocarbons (herbicides and pesticides), rush types
with large pith cells (Aerenchyma) are used:

- Juncus species, especially Juncus effusus
- Schoenoplectus species

Cyanide compounds, thiocyanates, and phenols are treated in fairly
short flow times (7+ hours) with Juncus.

## System Design

### Holding Times
- Untreated sewage: 10-12 days necessary
- Travel through a series of maze-like gravel-filter canals with
  floating weeds and sedges
- Swimming pools and less polluted systems: a pumped cycle through
  ferns, rushes, and watercress suffices to remove urine and leaves

### Gravel Bed Specifications
Such natural pools need a 23-30 cm (9-12 inch) coarse river gravel
base, with intake pipes below, and a skimming notch for leaves.

### Flow Design
Final treatment can be released via a sinuous, sealed canal of a
variety of rushes and floating water plants. Water can flow through a
gravel base planted to purifying species, or for longer rest times,
passed through lagoons and ditches.

## Pathogen Elimination Results

Low to zero populations of E. coli, coliform bacteria, Salmonella, and
Enterococci are found after water is treated via the above species.
Virus and worm eggs are also eliminated. This surpasses the safety
levels achieved by chlorination without producing carcinogenic
byproducts.

## Water Hyacinth Systems

Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) is a floating plant that removes
residual metals, surplus nutrients, and the coli group of bacilli from
waste water. It has been used as a taro mulch in Hawaii, and Pistia
(water lettuce) has been successfully used in Africa for similar
purposes. Both plants can be harvested and used as [[mollison-tropical-mulch-systems-and-materials]] or fed to biogas
systems.

However, water hyacinth must be carefully managed as it can become
invasive. Harvesting is essential both for nutrient removal and for
preventing the plant from blocking waterways.

## Bank Stabilisation Species

For pond edges and waterway banks, stabilisation species include:

- Juncus (rushes)
- Scirpus (bulrushes)
- Various grasses and clovers
- Phyla nodosa (Lippia)
- Bamboo and pampas grass clumps

Pond surface stabilisers include water lilies, Azolla, and water
hyacinth.

## Comfrey for Waste Absorption

Domestically, a comfrey bed is one way to absorb the faecal products
of animals, where wash-water from yards or pens is available. Comfrey
can stand heavy inputs of raw faeces in solution, and the crop may
then be used for fodder or trenched for "instant compost" under other
species such as potatoes. Flow-through systems for methane production
take little plant nutrient from faecal matter, and comfrey or algae
ponds deal with the residues while producing useful by-products for
compost and stock feed.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Reed beds fit naturally into the broader waste treatment cascade:

1. Initial settlement in [[mollison-sewage-lagoon-pond-treatment]]
2. [[mollison-biogas-and-methane-production-from-sewage]] for energy
3. Reed bed and wetland treatment for final polishing
4. Discharge to [[mollison-aquatic-polyculture-and-pond-culture]] or
   irrigation

In Peru, whole cultures based on reed beds derive forage, boats,
housing, bread, and meat from Typha, Phragmites, Cyperus, and rice
reeds. This demonstrates the productive potential of treatment
wetlands far beyond mere water purification.
