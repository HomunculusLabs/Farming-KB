## Desert [[permaculture-design]] for desert cooling draws on centuries of traditional knowledge combined with thermodynamic principles to create comfortable living spaces that can reduce ambient indoor temperatures by 10–15°C below outside conditions.

## Sources of Cool Air for Desert Houses

Five primary approaches provide cool air for daytime use in desert dwellings:

### 1. Internal Courtyards

Internal courtyards are the cornerstone of [[desert-housing-design]] across cultures from the Middle East to India to the Mediterranean. Effective courtyard design principles include:

- Preferably **latticed or shaded overhead** to block direct sun
- More effective when **two or more storeys high**, naturally shaded by the surrounding building walls
- **Narrow and tall** to preserve maximum shade throughout the day
- Can incorporate **water features** (fountains, ponds, unglazed pots of water) for additional evaporative cooling
- **Mulched floors** with trickle irrigation maintain cool, humid microclimates
- Hanging ferns or house plants aid cooling through transpiration
- A water tank within the courtyard provides both storage and evaporative cooling

### 2. Enclosed Vine Arbours

Fully enclosed vine arbours serve as living cooling systems integrated with the house:

- Need to be approximately **30% of the total floor area** to provide effective cool air
- Feature mulched floors and trickle irrigation to maintain humidity
- Suit single-storey dwellings particularly well
- Very small courtyards with shade can also be fitted into single-storey houses
- Function as both the summer living area and the primary cool-air source
- Hanging ferns or house plants within the arbour aid in cooling through transpiration

### 3. Earth Cooling Tunnels

Earth tunnels represent the ultimate passive cooling device for desert houses, pantries, and living areas:

- **Minimum dimensions**: 1 m deep and 20 m long
- **Ideal configuration**: slopes downhill with air intake on the upslope
- **Evaporative elements** placed inside the tunnel:
  - Large unglazed pots filled with water
  - Pans of wet coke or charcoal
  - Curtains of coarse fibreglass weave, drip-fed
  - Beds of coarse bark kept damp
- Cool humid air continually falls through the tunnel to the house rooms
- In swampy areas, tunnels can be mounded above the surface soil
- Provides **unlimited cool air** supply without energy input

### 4. Down-Draught Systems

Down-draught devices capture prevailing winds and direct them downward into living spaces:

- **Sails, slats, or wind-scoops** mounted on roof areas
- Can be **fixed** or **self-steering** to follow wind direction
- At their outlets in rooms, down-draught inlets can be fitted with:
  - Damp hessian (burlap) screens
  - Coke evaporation beds
  - Unglazed pots full of water
- These add considerable cooling capacity and humidify indoor air
- Towers, vanes, and airscoops are traditional ventilation elements in Iranian, Afghan, and Rajasthani architecture

### 5. Induced Cross-Ventilation (Solar Chimneys)

Solar chimneys create reliable airflow by harnessing thermal convection:

- **Black-painted sheet-metal solar chimneys** fitted to open from ceilings or roof ridges
- As the chimney heats up in sunlight, it draws air out of the rooms
- This creates negative pressure that pulls cool air in from courtyards, arbours, or tunnels
- Can create cross-draughts strong enough to blow out a candle
- Work passively without any mechanical assistance
- Can be fitted with dampers to control airflow rate

## The Complete Cooling System

The most effective desert house cooling combines multiple strategies into an integrated system:

**Sunward side**: A small solar chimney or attached greenhouse draws hot air up and out.

**Shade side**: A fully enclosed, totally vined shadehouse provides cool, humid air.

**Cross-flow**: Cool air from the shadehouse enters living areas by a fairly direct route while the solar chimney exhausts warm air, creating continuous cool and humid cross-flow.

**Water supply**: Some water must be available to supply vines and evaporative cooling elements.

This combination of shade, vines, and induced airflow can cool air to **10–15°C below ambient temperature** — comparable to mechanical air conditioning but without energy costs.

## Evaporative Cooling Surfaces

Multiple evaporative cooling elements can be placed in the through-draught path:

- **Unglazed pottery**: Water seeps through porous clay walls, evaporating and cooling the air
- **Coke mounds**: Wet charcoal/coke beds provide large surface area for evaporation
- **Ferns in bark**: Transpiring plants in moist bark mulch
- **Thick vines**: Dense foliage transpires continuously in shade
- **Wet hessian (burlap) screens**: Simple, replaceable, and highly effective evaporative surfaces

## The Desert House as Cool Refuge

The design philosophy emphasises that no desert house should be planned or built without its integral trellis and garden systems. These vegetative systems may:
- Save most or all climatic energy use (eliminating air conditioning)
- Provide food and shelter simultaneously
- The attached shadehouse must be planned as integral to house design and as the **summer living area**
- The winter (indoor) kitchen must open onto the shadehouse (the summer kitchen)

## Massive Walls and Thermal Mass

Thick, massive walls are fundamental to desert cooling:
- **White-painted mud walls** absorb heat slowly during the day and release it slowly at night
- The thermal mass buffers temperature extremes, creating a time lag between peak outside heat and interior warmth
- In hot deserts with warm nights, very thick, white, fully-shaded walls maintain comfort
- In hot deserts with consistently hot days and nights, lighter, screened, or matted walls allow more rapid cooling

## Winter Heating Integration

The same design elements that cool in summer can provide winter warmth:

- **Closing the house** at night or on cold days retains heat from glasshouse or solar gain
- **Solar chimneys** can be dampened to prevent warm air escape
- **Glasshouses** on the sunward side provide winter heat while venting hot air for summer cross-ventilation
- Designs following these principles save up to **80% of fuel energy**
- At high latitudes (30°–60°), extensive glazing is needed
- At lower latitudes (0°–30°), approximately 20–25% of the sun-facing wall needs glazing

## Shaded Streets and Settlement Design

Cooling extends beyond individual houses to entire settlement patterns:

- **Close-clustered dwellings** with long axes along east-west alignment
- **Common or close-spaced walls** prevent heat and wind penetration
- **Narrow streets** with 2–3 storey buildings provide shade
- **Cool air pools** in shaded narrow "wells" of courtyards and streets
- **Roof-level vents** draw cool air into dwellings from below
- In Davis, California, at least 60% of paved areas must be shaded by law
- White canvas-covered markets (as in Istanbul) exemplify good commercial area design

## Economic Benefits

Properly designed passive cooling has significant economic implications:

- Energy can constitute 30–40% of total household expenditure for low-income desert families
