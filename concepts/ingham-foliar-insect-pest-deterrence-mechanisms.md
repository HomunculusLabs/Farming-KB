# Foliar Insect Pest Deterrence Through AACT Mechanisms

One of the most consistently reported — yet least formally documented — effects of [[elaine-ingham]]'s field guide identifies four potential mechanisms by which AACT application deters insect feeding on plant foliage, while emphasizing the need for rigorous entomological study to formally document these observations.

## The Observed Phenomenon

Nearly every practitioner working with Ingham's Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) reports that foliar insects "go away" when they begin spraying with AACT. Despite the consistency of these anecdotal reports across a wide range of crops, growing conditions, and geographic locations, hard scientific data documenting the reduction and disappearance of insect pests through compost tea application remains limited.

Ingham explicitly calls for entomological collaboration to formally document these observations. The consistency of practitioner reports across diverse crops and regions suggests a real biological effect, but the absence of controlled, replicated studies leaves the scientific basis unproven. This gap between field experience and formal evidence is a recurring theme in Ingham's work — she consistently advocates for rigorous documentation of effects that practitioners observe routinely.

## Four Proposed Mechanisms

Ingham identifies four non-exclusive mechanisms that may explain why AACT helps foliar pests exit from plants. These mechanisms likely operate simultaneously, with their relative importance varying by crop, pest species, environmental conditions, and tea quality.

### 1. Surface Biology as a Physical and Chemical Deterrent

The beneficial organisms applied to the leaf surface may be physically or chemically repugnant to insect pests, preventing them from landing on treated leaves. This could involve:

- **Textural changes** — microbial films alter leaf surface topology
- **Taste deterrents** — microbial metabolites make leaf tissue unpalatable
- **Olfactory cues** — microbial VOCs signal an inhospitable environment for egg-laying or feeding

This mechanism operates at the level of initial contact — the insect never lands, or lands and immediately departs. The microbial film creates an environment that insects actively avoid, not because it is toxic but because it does not match the sensory profile of a suitable host plant.

### 2. Elevated CO2 Disrupts Pest Orientation

Microbial respiration on leaf surfaces increases the local concentration of carbon dioxide in the leaf boundary layer atmosphere. Insects use chemical cues including CO2 gradients to locate suitable host plants. Elevated CO2 from a healthy [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds (VOCs) that serve as attractant signals for insect pests. The microorganisms in compost tea may consume these alcohols, effectively masking the plant's stress signal. Without the chemical beacon that indicates a weakened, vulnerable host, insects are not attracted to the plant.

This mechanism operates at the level of long-range attraction — the insect may approach but cannot locate the specific stressed tissue it would normally target. The protective organisms act as a biological cloak, hiding the plant's stress signals from pest detection.

The relationship between plant stress chemistry and insect behavior is well-documented in entomology. Many herbivorous insects use volatile cues not just to locate host plants but to assess plant quality — stressed plants with elevated alcohol and free amino acid emissions are preferentially selected for oviposition because they represent easier feeding for developing larvae.
