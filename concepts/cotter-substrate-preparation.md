# Substrate Preparation for Mushroom Cultivation

The growing substrate is the material on which mushroom mycelium feeds and eventually produces fruiting bodies. Proper preparation through pasteurization or sterilization is essential for successful cultivation. From Cotter's "Organic Mushroom Farming and Mycoremediation."

## Primary vs. Secondary Decomposers

Mushrooms are categorized by their role in decomposition:

- **Primary decomposers** (oyster, shiitake, king stropharia): Break down raw lignin and cellulose in fresh wood and plant material. Can be grown on raw substrates with minimal processing.
- **Secondary decomposers** (Agaricus/button, paddy straw, shaggy mane): Require substrates that have already been partially broken down by bacteria and other organisms. Need composted or microbe-enriched substrates.

## Common Substrate Materials

### Wood-Based Substrates

- Hardwood sawdust (oak, maple, beech, birch): Primary substrate for shiitake, oyster, lion's mane, reishi
- Wood chips: For outdoor cultivation; used in garden beds, stump inoculation, and path mulching
- Supplement with wheat or rice bran at 5% (dry weight) for enhanced yields
- Avoid coniferous wood for most species (exceptions exist for some specialist fungi)

### Agricultural Waste Substrates

- Wheat straw: The most common substrate for oyster mushroom cultivation
- Cotton hulls: Excellent nitrogen supplement; used in compost cultivation
- Shredded wheat straw: Carbon source for second pasteurization dunk
- Cornstalks, bean hulls, beet pulp, sugarcane bagasse: Regional agricultural by-products
- Spent coffee grounds: Free, nitrogen-rich substrate for oyster mushrooms

### Composted Substrates

- Composted livestock manure mixed with cereal straws: For Agaricus species
- Composted hardwood leaves: For blewits and other compost-loving species
- Compost must reach internal temperatures of ~140°F (60°C) during the composting process

## Pasteurization vs. Sterilization

### Pasteurization

- Heats substrate to 165-175°F (74-79°C) for 1-2 hours
- Kills most competitors (molds, bacteria) but leaves beneficial thermophilic organisms alive
- Preferred for substrates with supplements (wheat bran, etc.) since beneficial microbes help defend against contamination
- Best for straw, compost, and agricultural waste substrates
- More forgiving; does not require a pressure vessel

### Sterilization

- Heats substrate to 250°F (121°C) at 15 PSI for 1-2 hours
- Kills all organisms including beneficial microbes
- Required for supplemented sawdust substrates used indoors
- Necessary for grain spawn production
- Creates a blank slate that the mushroom mycelium must colonize alone

## Hot Water Immersion Pasteurization

This is Cotter's recommended method for home-scale pasteurization:

1. Use 55-gallon food-grade drums
2. Heat water to 175°F (79°C)
3. Substrate is placed in wire baskets or mesh bags
4. Immerse substrate and maintain 165-175°F (74-79°C) for 1-2 hours
5. Remove and drain; cool to below 90°F (32°C) before spawning

### The Two-Dunk Rule

A critical principle for hot water pasteurization:

- Maximum 2 batches of substrate per barrel of water
- First dunk: nitrogen source (cotton hulls + wheat bran) - nutrients leach into water
- Second dunk: carbon source (shredded wheat straw) - less nutrient leaching
- After 2 dunks, nutrient buildup in the water inhibits mushroom yields
- Cool nitrogen and carbon batches separately, then mix before spawning

### Equipment and Costs

- Home-scale pasteurization setup: $200-$1,500
- Custom aluminum basket hoist system: ~$400 to build
- 55-gallon food-grade drums: Readily available, often free or low-cost
- Wire baskets or mesh bags: For holding substrate during immersion

## Substrate Hydration

- Substrate must be properly hydrated before pasteurization or sterilization
- For straw: Soak for 12-24 hours, then drain; target field capacity (squeezes without dripping)
- For sawdust: Mix with water until it holds together when squeezed but no water drips out
- For grain spawn: Boil or soak grains until fully hydrated but not split or bursting
- Over-hydrated substrates promote bacterial contamination and anaerobic conditions

## Supplementation

Adding nutritional supplements to substrates increases yields but also increases contamination risk:

- Wheat bran: Most common supplement at 5-10% dry weight
- Rice bran: Alternative to wheat bran, similar performance
- Cottonseed meal: High nitrogen content
- Gypsum: Adds calcium, improves texture, buffers pH
- Limestone: Raises pH for Agaricus species (target pH 7.0)
- Supplements must be mixed thoroughly and evenly through the substrate

## Cropping Containers

Substrate is packed into containers for colonization and fruiting:

- Autoclave bags: Most common for indoor sawdust cultivation
- Polypropylene or polyethylene bags with filter patches for gas exchange
- Drill holes or cut slits in bags for fruiting sites
- Containers should be filled loosely to allow for gas exchange during colonization
- For Agaricus: Fill trays 8-10 inches deep, poke spawn holes in diamond pattern every 6-8 inches
- Cover spawned holes with wet compost to protect spawn from drying and insects

## Outdoor Substrate Preparation

For outdoor cultivation, substrate preparation is often simpler:

- Fresh logs (cut within 1 month) require no treatment beyond inoculation
- Wood chip beds: Fresh hardwood chips, inoculated with spawn, kept moist
- Cardboard layers: Soaked corrugated cardboard with spawn between layers
- Compost piles: 5 feet square, 3-4 feet high, alternating green/brown layers, turned 1-2 times per week for 3-4 weeks
- Spent mushroom substrate: Excellent for garden beds, soil building, and as animal litter

## Water Quality

- Water quality affects substrate preparation and mushroom quality
- Chlorinated municipal water can be left to off-gas for 24 hours before use
- Avoid water with high heavy metal content
- Well water is generally excellent for mushroom cultivation
- Rainwater collection is ideal for organic operations
