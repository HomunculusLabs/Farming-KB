## Interceptor Banks & Soil Rehabilitation

[[soil-collapse]],
desertification, and [[dryland-salting-mollison]] in agricultural landscapes. Pioneered by the WISALT group on
West Australian hill farms, this approach addresses land degradation at its source — the hilltops —
rather than attempting remediation further downslope where damage has already compounded. The system
integrates deep contour earthworks, subsurface aquifer management, and strategic revegetation into
a comprehensive land-repair strategy.

## The Problem: Collapsed Soils

Soil collapse is a severe form of land degradation that creates deeply inhospitable conditions for
plant roots. In collapsed soils, pore spaces are drastically reduced, cutting off the air and water
channels that roots depend on for gas exchange and moisture transport. Carbon dioxide concentrations
in the soil profile can reach lethal levels of approximately 5%, compared to just 1% in healthy,
well-structured soils. This stifling, oxygen-deprived environment promotes the growth of anaerobic
bacteria, which produce methane and sulphur dioxide — toxic byproducts that further poison the soil.

Plant roots in collapsed soils face a compounding set of stresses beyond the toxic soil atmosphere.
Surface evaporation accelerates drought conditions at the root zone, while rising salt water from
shallow aquifers creates osmotic stress that prevents roots from absorbing moisture even when water
is physically present. Together, these forces — toxic gases, drought, and salinity — create a
landscape where trees die, pastures fail, and desertification advances.

## Why Traditional Contour Banks Fail

Conventional contour banks — typically shallow ditches about 0.5 metres deep — have proven
inadequate against the full spectrum of degradation affecting hill country. While they may slow
some surface runoff in the short term, they fail to address wind erosion, gully formation, and
the progressive death of trees on slopes as saline water tables continue to rise.

The WISALT group recognised that managing these problems further downslope was too late. By the
time water and salt have travelled to the valley floor, the damage above is already severe. The
solution must start at the hilltops, where overland flow and subsurface throughflow originate.

## WISALT Interceptor Bank Design

The core element is a deep ditch — approximately 2 metres deep and 3–5 metres wide — bulldozed
precisely on contour, positioned 1–3 metres below the ridgeline. This positioning captures the
maximum volume of both surface runoff and subsurface throughflow before either can gain destructive
momentum on the slope below.

### Construction Method

Construction follows a careful, sequenced process:

1. **Topsoil removal** — The valuable topsoil layer is pushed aside first and preserved for later
   redistribution, ensuring the microbiologically rich surface layer is not buried during earthworks.
2. **Subsoil breaking** — The exposed subsoil is ripped with a bulldozer blade to shatter compacted
   layers and impermeable horizons, improving future infiltration and root penetration.
3. **Bank ramming** — The clayey or stony subsoil is rammed against the downhill side of the ditch
   to form a sealed barrier bank that functions as an underground dam when properly sealed.

If the downslope bank is properly sealed, all overland flow stops and subsurface throughflow is
intercepted. Higher (uphill) banks are constructed as swales to hold and infiltrate water, while
downslope banks are graded at 1:3000, directing overflow toward natural stream beds.

## Spacing and Layout Guidelines

Interceptor banks are spaced at every **3 metres of vertical height** downhill from the ridgeline.
Layout varies with terrain:

- **Steep slopes (10–15°):** Continuous ditches run uninterrupted from ridge to foothills.
- **Gentle slopes (5° or less):** Maximum 300 metres spacing; ditches terminate at natural
  waterways to prevent excessive water concentration.

## Managing Subsurface Salt Aquifers

A critical component of the WISALT system is managing shallow aquifers that carry salt through deep
sand seams. These hidden aquifers transport enormous quantities of salt laterally, surfacing
downslope as saline seeps that kill vegetation.

### Detection and Blocking

Aquifers in sand seams can be detected by backhoe excavation, satellite radar, or dowsing. Where
deep sand seams extend to 6 metres and carry saline water, they are blocked by vertical plastic
sheets set into clay backfill — an impermeable barrier preventing saline groundwater migration.

### Repurposing Sand Seams for Freshwater Storage

Once saline throughflow is blocked, deep sand seams function as freshwater "tanks" — underground
reservoirs holding clean water in their pore spaces. Interceptor drains remain water-filled
year-round as permanent canals, redistributing fresh water across the rehabilitated landscape. This
transforms a destructive salt transport system into a productive freshwater distribution network.

## Salt Lake Management

Where salt lakes exist, early wet-season floods (carrying the highest salt loads) are diverted
away. Later fresh run-off is directed to fill the lakes, progressively diluting salinity and
allowing eventual revegetation of lake margins with salt-tolerant species.

## Results and Recovery Timeline

### Immediate Effects (Within Months)
- Wind erosion ceases immediately as bank barriers interrupt surface wind flow.
- Flooding of lower slopes stops as water is retained and infiltrated on contour.
- Crops and pastures regain health as water is retained rather than running off with topsoil.

### Medium-Term Recovery (2–3 Years)
- Grasses regenerate naturally between banks, stabilising soil and rebuilding topsoil.
- Dying trees recover canopy health as water tables stabilise and salt declines.
- New tree seedlings establish in improved moisture and soil conditions.

### Long-Term Rehabilitation (Up to 10 Years)
- Full soil profile recovery including pore spaces, microbial communities, and organic matter.
- Tree-lines 5–8 trees wide below each bank mature as windbreaks stopping desert winds.
- The landscape transitions to a self-sustaining productive system.

Farmers estimate **5–7 years of improved cash returns** pay for all earthwork costs, making the
investment economically viable before full ecological recovery is achieved.

## The Broader Context: Dryland Salting

In Victoria, rising groundwaters from deforested aquifers drive salinisation. Deep sand aquifers
contain 30,000–42,000 ppm salt — lethal to most crops and pastures. Forested areas do not
experience salting; the problem is created by agricultural clearing and wood-chipping that remove
deep-rooted vegetation and the "vegetative pump" that keeps water tables in check.

Western Australian soils carry enormous salt reserves to 20 metres depth — 3,400 to 19,000 tonnes
per hectare. Once vegetation is cleared, rising water tables mobilise this salt to the surface,
devastating soils and creating salt deserts. Interceptor banks combined with reforestation represent
one of the few proven strategies for halting and reversing this process.

## Key Principles

- **Treat the problem at its source** — hilltops and ridgelines, not valley floors.
- **Intercept both surface and subsurface flow** — overland runoff and throughflow aquifers.
- **Block saline aquifers** while repurposing sand seams for freshwater storage.
- **Design for gentle overflow** — graded banks at 1:3000 prevent catastrophic breach.
- **Combine earthworks with revegetation** — tree-lines provide windbreaks and deep-rooted water
  management complementing physical earthworks.
- **Plan for economic viability** — the system pays for itself within 5–7 years.
