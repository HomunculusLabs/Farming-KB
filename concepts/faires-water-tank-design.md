# Water Tank Design

Water for human consumption is stored in tanks. Proper tank selection and placement is critical for reliable water supply, especially in off-grid or homesteading situations.

## Sizing

- **Single person:** At least 1,000 gallons (3,700 liters)
- **Family:** Minimum 2,500 gallons (9,500 liters)
- Water comes from roof runoff or pumped from dams/springs

## Tank Materials Comparison

| Material | Pros | Cons |
|----------|------|------|
| **Galvanized steel** | Durable, compact, 20-year lifespan | Needs concrete base, can't be in-ground, more expensive, possible metal leaching |
| **Polyethylene** | All shapes/sizes, less expensive, no concrete base needed, durable, 25-year lifespan | Possible chemical leaching from plastic |
| **Concrete** | Less expensive, no chemical leaching | 15-year lifespan, more metal (rebar) than steel tank, takes more space |
| **Earthbag** | Cheapest option, 20+ year lifespan | Takes up the most space |

## General Tank Principles

- Cover and screen to repel mosquitoes
- Thick green algae will grow on sides — this is beneficial (cleans the water)
- Set outlet pipe 3 inches (7.6 cm) above bottom so algae remain undisturbed
- Place dams or tanks at top of hill for gravity-fed distribution
- A large water tank on a hilltop can serve as a building foundation
- The building roof can then collect rainwater into the tank below

## Mosquitofish Warning

Despite recommendations, mosquitofish (gambusia) do more damage than good in water tanks:
- Questionable if they eat more larvae than other fish
- Breed prolifically (hundreds of fry, short 2-year lifespan)
- Other fish avoid eating them — they can choke out populations
- Should NOT be introduced to aquaculture ponds
- Frogs and birds are more effective mosquito control

## Earthbag Cistern Construction

A very inexpensive water tank option using 50-lb polypropylene bags (at least 17" wide × 30" long):

1. Dig a smooth hole at least a quarter of the way into the ground, sized for your tank with room for earthbags
2. Line the hole with pond liner or heavy polyethylene
3. Build walls with earthbag courses, laying two strands of barbed wire between each layer for grip
4. Waterproof the walls: plaster with concrete/stucco, or use pond liner/heavy polyethylene
5. Cover the top with an earthbag dome or concrete slab

## Cold Climate Considerations

- Water will freeze, making some supply unusable
- Several feet of ice significantly reduces available water
- Bury pipes from tank to house at least 3 feet deep to prevent freezing/bursting
- Leaving pipes running all winter is wasteful and drains the tank
- Best solution: put water tank underground, possibly under the barn, uphill from the house

## Strategic Placement

Position water storage at the highest point on your property. The pond or lake can be at the bottom of the hill, serving as both a firebreak and drought reservoir. Gravity does the work of distribution — no pumps needed.

## Sizing Calculations for Water Tanks

Proper sizing requires estimating daily water demand and matching it to available supply. A conservative baseline is 50 gallons (190 liters) per person per day for drinking, cooking, hygiene, and limited irrigation. For a family of four over a 30-day dry period, the minimum tank volume would be approximately 6,000 gallons (22,700 liters). Adding a 20% safety margin raises this to about 7,500 gallons. In areas with pronounced dry seasons, plan for the full length of the drought period rather than relying on average rainfall data.

Roof catchment potential can be estimated from the formula: catchment (gallons) = roof area (sq ft) × annual rainfall (inches) × 0.623 × runoff coefficient. Metal and tile roofs have a runoff coefficient of 0.85–0.95, while gravel or thatch roofs may be as low as 0.15–0.25. This calculation helps determine whether a tank can be sustained by roof runoff alone or requires supplemental sources such as spring water or well pumping.

## Integration with Rainwater Harvesting Systems

Water tanks are typically the central storage component of a [[faires-water-sources-and-catchment]]. First-flush diverters should be installed to prevent debris, bird droppings, and concentrated pollutants from the first rainfall from entering the tank. Leaf screens on gutters reduce organic matter load. For potable systems, a multi-stage filtration approach is recommended: coarse screening at the gutter, sediment settling in the tank, and point-of-use filtration (ceramic filters, UV treatment, or slow sand filters) before consumption.

Multiple smaller tanks can be chained in series for both redundancy and staged filtration — the first tank acts as a rough settling basin while downstream tanks store cleaner water. This approach also allows maintenance on one tank without losing the entire water supply.

## Tank Material Selection Guide

Beyond the basic comparison, material choice should account for site-specific conditions. In areas with acidic rain (pH below 5.5), galvanized steel tanks may corrode faster than rated. Polyethylene tanks degrade under prolonged UV exposure unless shielded or painted with UV-protective coatings. Concrete tanks are excellent for underground installation and benefit from thermal mass — keeping water cooler in summer and slower to freeze in winter — but they require careful waterproofing and can crack in freeze-thaw cycles if not properly reinforced.

For potable water, FDA-approved or food-grade polyethylene is the safest plastic option. Stainless steel (304 or 316 grade) offers the durability of steel without corrosion concerns but comes at a premium price. Ferrocement — a technique using wire mesh and cement mortar over a frame — offers a middle ground between concrete and metal: it is inexpensive, can be formed into any shape, and has been used successfully in developing countries for decades.

## Frost Protection and Winterization

In cold climates, frost protection extends beyond burying pipes. Above-ground tanks should be insulated with rigid foam board (minimum R-10) or wrapped in purpose-built tank blankets. In extreme cold zones, a small heat trace cable on the outlet pipe prevents freezing at the draw point. Passive solar heat gain can be harnessed by painting dark-colored tanks or placing them against a south-facing wall.

For underground tanks, the frost line depth varies widely — from 12 inches in mild climates to over 6 feet in northern regions. The tank should be placed below the local frost line entirely. In areas with permafrost or prolonged deep freezing, insulated berms around the tank can reduce heat loss. Alternatively, a wet system that keeps water moving (even at a trickle) prevents freezing, though this requires reliable power or gravity flow. See [[faires-pond-design-and-aquaculture]] for related water management in cold climates.

## Maintenance and Water Quality

Regular tank maintenance ensures long-term water quality and system reliability. Sediment should be cleaned from the tank bottom every 1–2 years, taking care not to disturb the beneficial algae layer on the walls. Inlet screens and first-flush diverters require periodic clearing of leaves, twigs, and debris. Gutters should be inspected before each rainy season to prevent blockages that could reduce catchment efficiency.

Water quality testing is recommended at least annually, checking for coliform bacteria, pH, turbidity, and total dissolved solids. If bacterial contamination is detected, treatment options include chlorination, UV sterilization, or boiling. For systems using roof catchment, be aware that treated lumber, asphalt shingles, and lead flashing can leach contaminants — metal or tile roofs are preferred for potable water systems.

When designing a multi-tank system, consider elevating secondary tanks above the primary tank to allow gravity flow between them. A float valve connecting tanks enables automatic equalization and ensures the primary tank can overflow into secondary storage during heavy rainfall events. This passive management approach reduces the need for pumps or monitoring while maximizing total storage capacity across the system.
