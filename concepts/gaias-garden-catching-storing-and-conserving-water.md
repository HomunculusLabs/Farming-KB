# Catching, Storing, and Conserving Water

In conventional garden design, water appears only by fortunate rainfall or by the gardener's intervention through hoses and sprinklers fed by distant wells and pumps. In an ecologically based design, water is not an externally caused event -- it is designed in, naturally present, naturally abundant. The well-designed garden spontaneously cycles toward lush growth even when the gardener is absent and skies are cloudless.

## The Fivefold Path to Water Wisdom

Following the ecological design principle that "each function is supported by many elements," a water-wise garden relies on multiple complementary techniques. If one system fails, others sustain the garden.

### 1. High Organic Matter Content

The cheapest place to store water is in the soil. Humus and organic matter act as a sponge, swelling to hold several times their weight in water. One foot of rich, moist soil blanketing a backyard holds as much water as a 3-inch-deep lake of the same size.

Research shows that soil with as little as 2 percent organic matter can reduce irrigation needs by 75 percent compared to poor soils with less than 1 percent organic matter. Organic matter also adds fertility, stores nutrients, boosts soil life, and fluffs soil structure.

### 2. Deep Mulching

Mulch slows evaporation, cools soil, adds fertility as it decomposes, boosts soil life, and smothers weeds. A thick mulch layer can dramatically reduce water loss from the soil surface.

### 3. Water-Conserving Plants

Using plants suited to available water includes:
- **Native plants** adapted to local rainfall regimes
- **Mediterranean-climate plants** (from regions with moist winters and dry summers) that handle wet/dry shifts better than pure desert species. Examples: almond, aloe, barberry, blackberry, borage, fig, lavender, olive, rosemary, thyme
- **Proper placement**: Station water-hungry plants in naturally wet spots -- near downspouts, under unguttered eaves, at low spots, near faucets. Place drought-tolerant species in dry zones.

### 4. Dense Plantings

Dense plantings shade the soil, slow evaporation, and smother weeds. The canopy of densely planted beds creates a microclimate that retains moisture far longer than bare soil between widely spaced plants.

### 5. Soil Contouring

Sculpting the land to catch and direct water is one of the most powerful water-harvesting techniques.

## Swales

A swale is a shallow trench laid out dead level along the land's contours, typically 1 to several feet across and about a foot deep. The excavated soil is piled on the downhill side to form a berm. Water runs into the swale, spreads along its length, and slowly percolates into the soil, forming an underground lens of moisture that aids plant growth for tens of feet downslope.

**Key principles for swale construction:**
- Swales must be truly level so water infiltrates evenly
- Spacing: 18 feet apart in areas with 40-50 inches of rainfall, increasing to 50 feet apart for 15-inch rainfall areas
- On steep slopes or compacted soils, bring swales closer together
- Plant the berm with trees and shrubs whose roots will hold it in place
- Partially fill with mulch to increase water absorption and reduce visibility

**Variations:**
- **Straw-filled swales**: For formal areas, a trench 1-3 feet deep packed with straw, topped with thin sandy topsoil
- **Fishscale swales**: Short, overlapping swales used when long swales are blocked by existing trees or obstacles

Swales work in wet climates too. Even in the Pacific Northwest, a single swale can hold moisture weeks longer than untreated ground, creating a welcoming microclimate where humus builds and new wildflower species spontaneously appear.

## The Synergistic Effect

The Zemach garden in Los Alamos, New Mexico demonstrates the power of combining all five techniques. Designed by permaculturist Ben Haggard, this high-desert oasis can last months between waterings yet supports fruit trees, delicate ferns, herbs, and abundant wildlife. The plantings survived a five-month drought at only three years old.

The synergy comes because the techniques do more than just save water: they protect plants from drought by keeping water in the soil, boost plant growth through organic matter, and protect soil from erosion during fierce downpours.

## Greywater and Ponds

Beyond the five core techniques, ecological gardens can incorporate greywater systems (draining household wash water to mulch basins and constructed wetlands) and ponds for water storage, wildlife habitat, and microclimate creation. These extend the water web of connections linking the pieces of the garden together.

Greywater systems typically divert water from laundry, bathroom sinks, and
showers (never from toilets or kitchen sinks with food waste) into mulch-filled
basins planted with thirsty vegetation. The mulch acts as a biological filter,
trapping particles and supporting microbial communities that break down soaps
and organic matter. Over time, these mulch basins become fertile planting zones
where fruit trees, berries, and ornamentals thrive on the consistent moisture
and nutrients. Simple branched-drain systems with no moving parts are the most
reliable and require the least maintenance.

Ponds serve multiple functions in the ecological garden. A well-placed pond
reflects light into nearby growing areas, moderates temperature extremes by
absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night, and provides habitat
for amphibians, beneficial insects, and birds that contribute to pest control.
Even a small pond of 50 to 100 square feet can significantly influence the
microclimate within a 30-foot radius. The pond edge, or riparian zone, is
often the most productive planting area in the entire garden, supporting
species that bridge the gap between aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems.

## Keyline Design and Broad-Scale Water Harvesting

For properties larger than a typical backyard, the principles of keyline
design offer a broader framework for water management. Developed by P.A.
Yeomans in Australia, keyline design uses the natural topography of the land
to guide the placement of dams, channels, and cultivation patterns that
maximise water infiltration across the entire landscape. The keyline itself is
a contour line identified at the point where the valley floor begins to
flatten out on the slope. Cultivation parallel to this keyline, rather than
along the contour, gently directs runoff from valleys toward ridges,
distributing moisture more evenly across the landscape. This approach
