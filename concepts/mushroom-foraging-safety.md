# Mushroom Foraging Safety

Foraging for wild mushrooms is rewarding but carries real risk. A single mistake in identification can be fatal. This guide covers the safety principles every forager must follow, with special attention to the deadliest species and their look-alikes.

## The Golden Rules

1. If you are not 100% certain of identification, do not eat it. No exceptions.
2. Identify the mushroom to species level, not just genus. Many deadly species have close edible relatives.
3. When learning a new edible species, have it confirmed by an experienced forager or mycologist before eating.
4. Always cook wild mushrooms before eating. Raw wild mushrooms can cause gastrointestinal distress even when the cooked mushroom is safe.
5. Eat a small amount (a few bites) the first time you try any new species. Wait 24 hours before eating more. Some people have individual sensitivities.
6. Never forage in polluted areas -- mushrooms bioaccumulate heavy metals and other contaminants.
7. Keep a detailed journal with photos, habitat notes, spore prints, and location data for every find.

## The Deadly Species

### Amanita Genus
The genus responsible for the vast majority of mushroom fatalities worldwide.

- Death Cap (Amanita phalloides): responsible for most mushroom deaths globally. Olive-green cap, white gills, white stem with a skirt (ring), growing from a cup-like volva at the base. Looks similar to several edible species including the paddy straw mushroom and some edible Amanitas. Contains amatoxins that destroy liver cells. Symptoms may not appear for 6-24 hours after ingestion. By the time symptoms occur, organ damage may be irreversible.

- Destroying Angel (Amanita virosa, A. bisporigera, A. ocreata): pure white, elegant appearance. One of the most poisonous mushrooms known. Common in North America and Europe. Often mistaken for edible white mushrooms by beginners. Same amatoxin mechanism as the death cap.

- Fly Agaric (Amanita muscaria): the iconic red cap with white spots. Rarely fatal to adults but causes delirium, nausea, and disorientation. Can be dangerous to children and pets.

Key Amanita identification features: white gills that do not touch the stem, a skirt/ring on the stem, a cup (volva) at the base of the stem (may be underground -- dig carefully), white spore print.

### Galerina marginata (Deadly Galerina)
Contains the same amatoxins as Amanita species. Brown cap, brown gills, brown spore print. Frequently grows on wood, often in the same habitat as the psychedelic Psilocybe species. One of the most dangerous look-alikes for beginning mushroom foragers. Size and habitat overlap with several edible Galerina species. Must be positively identified and excluded from any collection.

### Gyromitra Species (False Morels)
- Gyromitra esculenta (False Morel): contains gyromitrin, which converts to monomethylhydrazine (rocket fuel) in the body. Causes nausea, vomiting, neurological symptoms, and can be fatal. Some people parboil and eat them (common in Scandinavia) but this is risky -- toxin levels vary by region and preparation method. Not worth the risk.

- Gyromitra caroliniana: large false morel of eastern North America. Same gyromitrin risk.

## Common Dangerous Look-Alikes

| Edible Species | Dangerous Look-Alike | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Chanterelle | Jack O'Lantern (Omphalotus) | Jack O'Lantern has true gills; chanterelles have false gills (forked ridges). Jack O'Lantern grows in clusters on wood; chanterelles are solitary on ground. Jack O'Lantern glows in the dark. |
| Morel | False Morel (Gyromitra) | Morels are hollow when cut lengthwise with a pitted cap. False morels have chambered, cottony interiors and wrinkled or brain-like caps. |
| Paddy Straw | Death Cap (A. phalloides) | Death Cap has white gills and a volva at base. Paddy Straw has pink gills and no volva. |
| Honey Mushroom | Galerina marginata | Galerina has a brown spore print; Honey Mushroom has a white spore print. Galerina has a more prominent ring. |
| King Bolete | Devil's Bolete (Rubroboletus satanas) | Devil's Bolete has red pore surface (underside of cap) that turns blue when cut. King Bolete has white to yellowish pores. |

## Spore Prints

A spore print is a fundamental identification tool. Place the mushroom cap gills-down on half white, half black paper. Cover with a bowl and wait 4-24 hours. The color of the spore deposit is a critical identification feature:

- White spore print: Amanita species (warning flag), many boletes
- Brown spore print: Galerina, many agarics
- Pink spore print: Volvariella (paddy straw)
- Rusty brown: many boletes (edible group)
- Dark purple-brown: Psilocybe, Agaricus (store mushrooms)

## When to Seek Expert Help

- You are unsure about any identification, even after consulting field guides
- Someone has eaten an unidentified mushroom
- Symptoms appear after eating wild mushrooms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, confusion, jaundice)

If mushroom poisoning is suspected, call Poison Control immediately (1-800-222-1222 in the US) and go to the emergency room. Bring a sample of the mushroom (whole, including base) and any remaining cooked portion. Do not wait for symptoms if a deadly species may have been consumed -- early medical intervention (activated charcoal, liver transplant) can be lifesaving with amatoxin poisoning.

## Safe Foraging Practice

- Learn 5-10 easily identified, distinctive species before branching out
- Join a local mycological society for guided walks and expert mentors
- Use multiple field guides -- never rely on a single source
- Learn habitat and season: when and where does the species grow?
- Use a basket, not a bag -- allows spores to spread as you walk
- Carry a knife, hand lens, and field guide on every foray
