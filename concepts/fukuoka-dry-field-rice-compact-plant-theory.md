[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s method of growing rice in dry fields contradicts centuries of Japanese
agricultural practice. Rather than flooding paddies, he maintains soil moisture at 60 to 80
percent of water-holding capacity, producing compact plants with a remarkably high grain-to-straw
ratio. This approach is central to the [[fukuoka-no-till-grain-production-method|direct seeding non-cultivation method]] and represents a radical departure from the assumptions of the
[[conventional-vs-organic-vs-regenerative|Green Revolution]].

## The Problem with Flooded Rice

For centuries, Japanese farmers have believed rice cannot be grown any other way than in flooded
paddies. Farmers have been growing rice in water for so many centuries that most people believe it
cannot be grown any other way. The main reason for flooding is actually weed control, not the
plant's preference. Flooded fields create an environment where only a limited variety of weeds
survive, but those that do must still be pulled by hand or uprooted with a hand-weeding tool. This
time-consuming and backbreaking job must be repeated several times each growing season.

Cultivated "wet-field" rice varieties are relatively strong in flooded conditions, but it is not
good for the plant. Rice plants grow best when the water content in the soil is between 60 and 80
percent of its water-holding capacity. When the field is not flooded, plants develop stronger
roots and are extremely resistant to attacks by disease and insects.

## Fukuoka's Minimal Water Management

Fukuoka's approach to water management is minimal. In June, during the monsoon season, he holds
water in the field for about one week. Few dry-field weeds can survive even so short a period
without oxygen. The clover also withers and turns yellow, but the idea is not to kill the clover,
only to weaken it to allow the rice seedlings to establish. When the water is drained as soon as
possible, the clover recovers and spreads to cover the field's surface again beneath the growing
rice plants.

After that, he hardly does anything in the way of water management. For the first half of the
season there is no irrigation at all. Even in years with very little rain, the soil stays moist
below the layer of straw and green manure. In August, fresh water is let in a little at a time but
never allowed to stand.

## The Compact Plant Principle

Visitors to Fukuoka's fields are always sceptical toward the end of July. The rice plants in the
neighbours' fields are waist high, while his are only about half that size. His guiding principle:
"Keep the head small, do not over nourish the plants, and let them grow true to the natural form
of the rice plant."

Typical rice plants three or four feet tall produce luxuriant leaves that seem to promise high
yield, but most energy goes into vegetative growth rather than grain production. Starch production
is great but efficiency is low, and so much energy is expended on leafy stalks that not much is
left for the grains. Tall plants yielding 2,000 pounds of straw produce only 1,000 to 1,200 pounds
of rice.

Fukuoka's small plants achieve the reverse: 2,000 pounds of straw yields 2,000 pounds of rice. In
a good harvest, the rice reaches 20 percent heavier than the straw, approximately 2,400 pounds per
quarter acre. This inversion of the normal grain-to-straw ratio is one of the most remarkable
results of the dry-field method. The principle applies equally to wheat, rye, buckwheat, oats,
millet, and other grains.

## Leaf Physiology and Yield

One square inch of leaf produces about six grains of rice. Three or four small leaves are more
than enough to produce a hundred grains per head. Fukuoka sows thickly, achieving 250 to 300
grain-bearing stalks (20 to 25 plants) per square yard. Sunlight is received uniformly, reaching
the base of the plants and lower leaves, because the plants are short.

Any experienced farmer can identify Fukuoka's rice by its appearance: the ideal shape, natural
sprouting rather than transplanting, no flooded field growth, and no chemical fertilizer. The
overall form, root shape, and joint spacing on the main stem all reveal the growing method. If you
understand the ideal form, it is just a matter of how to grow a plant of that shape under the
unique conditions of your own field.

## The Matsushima Debate

Fukuoka disagreed with Professor Matsushima's theory that the fourth leaf from the tip should be
the longest for optimal yield. In Fukuoka's experience, sometimes the second or third leaf being
longest produces the best results. When growth is held back while the plant is young, the top leaf
or second leaf often becomes longest and a large harvest is still obtained.

Fukuoka attributed this difference to their fundamentally different approaches. Matsushima's
theory derived from experiments using fragile rice plants grown with fertilizer in nursery beds
and later transplanted. Fukuoka's rice was grown in accordance with the natural life cycle, "just
as though it were growing wild." He waited patiently for the plant to develop at its own pace. The
Matsushima debate illustrates a deeper point: Professor Matsushima's theory applies only to plants
grown under specific conditions that Fukuoka considers unnatural. When the conditions change, the
optimal leaf configuration changes as well. Nature is everywhere in perpetual motion; conditions
are never exactly the same in any two years.

## The Old Southern Glutinous Variety

In later years Fukuoka experimented with an old variety of glutinous rice from southern Japan.
Each seed sown in fall produced an average of 12 stalks with about 250 grains per head. With this
variety he believed he could approach the greatest theoretically obtainable harvest from the solar
energy reaching the field. Some areas already yielded 27.5 bushels (1,650 pounds) per quarter
acre.

This variety represented his ideal: a plant close to its wild form, producing abundantly without
human intervention. The variety had evolved over generations in conditions similar to those of his
[[fukuoka-no-till-grain-production-method|natural farming]] system. He had been growing rice in
this manner for over twenty years, and the yields continued to increase and the soil became richer
every year.

## Disease Resistance Through Growing Conditions

When Fukuoka first began, his red clay soil was weak and unsuited for rice. Brown spot disease
occurred frequently. As the field grew in fertility through [[fukuoka-straw-healing-the- earth|straw mulch]] and [[cover-cropping-natural-farming|cover cropping]], brown spot disease
decreased and eventually disappeared entirely.

The three great rice diseases, stem rot, rice blast, and bacterial leaf blight, could all but
disappear if farmers stopped using weak improved varieties, stopped adding too much nitrogen to
the soil, and reduced the amount of irrigation water so that strong roots could develop. Chemical
sprays would become unnecessary. The approach connects directly to [[fukuoka-five-principles- natural-farming|Fukuoka's four principles]]: no cultivation, no chemical fertilizer, no weeding by
tillage or herbicides, and no dependence on chemicals.

## The Seasonal Cycle

The annual cycle begins in early October when white clover and winter grain seed are broadcast
among ripening rice. Rice is harvested in November, straw spread, and rice seed sown in clay
pellets. In May the winter grain is harvested, straw scattered, and water held briefly. The cycle
so closely follows natural patterns that "it could be considered a natural process rather than an
agricultural technique." It takes only one or two hours for one farmer to sow seed and spread
straw across a quarter acre. Fukuoka considered it "probably no easier, simpler method for growing
grain," though it took him over thirty years to reach this simplicity.

## The Critique of Modern Research

Modern scientific agriculture has no vision of developing a method close to nature. Research
wanders about aimlessly, each researcher seeing just one part of the infinite array of natural
factors that affect harvest yields. Furthermore, these natural factors change from place to place
and from year to year. Modern research divides nature into tiny pieces and conducts tests that
conform neither with natural law nor to practical experiences.

The results are arranged for the convenience of research, not according to the needs of the
farmer. Even if you can explain how metabolism affects the productivity of the top leaf when the
average temperature is eighty-four degrees, there are places where the temperature is not eighty-
four degrees. Moreover, if the temperature is eighty-four degrees this year, next year it may only
be seventy-five. The geography and topography of the land, the condition of the soil, its
structure, texture, and drainage, exposure to sunlight, insect relationships, the variety of seed
used, the method of cultivation, a truly infinite variety of factors must all be considered.
