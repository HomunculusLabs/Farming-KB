## Low Islands (Atolls and Cays)

### The Water Table Problem
Low islands have very limited water catchment. Fresh water sits on top of a saltwater base, often only three to five feet below the surface with the bottom only four to five feet below that. The freshwater lens is finite and easily polluted -- even two or three septic tanks can contaminate the entire water table.

### The Platen Problem
Calcareous sand combined with phosphate deposits (accumulated sea bird manure) creates a calcium triphosphate "concrete" layer about 18 inches down. This platen prevents rain from penetrating and supports only shallow-rooted agriculture. Mangroves are being degraded worldwide, leading to declining offshore fisheries.

### Breaking Through: Grow-Hole Technology
To establish productive forest on low islands:
1. Shovel away sand and break up the platen to reach brackish groundwater
2. Fill the hole with all available humus
3. Plant a coconut (or other tree) -- the tree keeps the platen open, creating its own groundwater system
4. Repeat to expand the forest

Within twenty years, trees are in production.

### Ring (Circle) Gardens
The "magical ring garden" is ideal for atoll vegetable production:
- Break up platen, heap sand around the edge
- Fill the circle with mulch, plant vegetables
- Single drip system at center creates a wick-watering effect
- Within a four-foot circle, twelve running feet of row crop fit within reach of a single drip point
- Maximum efficient use of space and water

### Peripheral Settlement
Settlements should be placed on the island periphery, drawing water from central areas. This keeps waste from the main water lanes. Building a large village at the center could be disastrous. If there are too many people, the beaches become unusable from pollutants dashing up in the sparkling green waters.

## High Islands (Volcanic)

### Wet and Dry Sides
Islands more than five degrees off the equator have a wet and dry side. The wet side has rain forest, while the dry side can be quite arid. A 2,000-foot-high island effectively gains 7-12 degrees of latitude from top to bottom, allowing descent from temperate species (plum, deciduous) at the top to ultra-tropical equatorial species at sea level.

### The Hawaiian Ohana System
Mollison calls the ancient Hawaiian land division "a nice concept" where watershed, people, nutrient flow, animals, and everything form "a single, indivisible unit." Key features:
- Land divisions followed natural ridges between valleys, from mountain ridge to reef
- The Hawaiians independently invented the keyline system, building massive stonewalls at the keyline and directing water from upper valleys to ridges
- Forests were taboo areas (source of water), with foot traffic confined to ridge tops
- Nutrient runoff enclosed by rock wall at the sea -- the sea would grab nutrients and fix them as algae, feeding millions of pounds of mullet in enclosed ponds
- The whole nutrient flow: paddy fields on ridges to fish ponds at sea and back again

### Cloud and Mist Effects
Mist often reproduces the shape of the island in the air above it. The forest condensation and protection of soil from evaporation "win over transpiration." As forests extend down slopes, they create additional precipitation. Springs dry up or recommence flowing depending on whether forests are extending or being removed.

### Natural Hazards
Oceanic islands face tidal waves (periodicity 12-15 years), volcanic activity with mud flows (speed 400 mph), cyclones, and earthquakes. Design rules:
- Keep clients out of valley bottoms without offshore reefs
- Perch on sub-ridges within major valleys
- Reduce house mass to minimum
- Stay inland from the first elevation above the shoreline
- Study paths of mud flows, tidal waves, and cyclone damage

## Coastal Defense Systems

### Front-Line Plants
Plants that will stand directly in the sea (15 feet high): Pemphis, Tournefortia, Scaevola. Behind them: palms, date palms (which grow as well as coconut palms on coasts despite being considered oasis palms). The highest vegetation can build an island is about six feet -- do not tamper with shoreline vegetation without other defenses ready.

### Mangrove Systems
Mollison calls mangroves "perhaps the highest biomass turnover of any system." They lay thick leaf mulch that feeds shrimp, anthropoids, and diatoms, supporting the offshore shrimp fisheries. Aegicera is a superb honey plant. He warns that when people cut down mangroves, "they get washed away when they do it."

### Wind and Erosion
On all oceanic islands there are two sets of wind (NE-SW, NW-SE) and two periods of calm. Remove a strip of coastal vegetation and you get much broader waves with greater force. An airstrip without vegetation will be "cut to pieces" by the sea. Contain airstrips within vegetation, preferably at an angle to winds.

## Granitic Tropical Landscapes

Granitic landscapes present challenges of deep permeable sand (up to 100 feet) where organic matter washes straight down. Key strategies:
- Emphasize tree crops (palms, figs, grapes, olives) over annual crops
- No lawns -- takes 90 inches of water per year
- Use rocks for heat storage, ripening, and evaporative cooling
- Work crevices and shelving areas with mulch for niche planting
- Small gley ponds and modest bore water use

## Island Aquaculture Potential

Mollison argues that desert and island settlements, being essentially isolated "islands" with no connection to river systems, are ideal for experimental aquaculture. With sufficient water volume, even a five-mile lagoon in desert landscape is one of the richest potential aquaculture areas. You can move in lagoon rushes, water lilies, root crops in marshes, fish, mussels, and crayfish.
