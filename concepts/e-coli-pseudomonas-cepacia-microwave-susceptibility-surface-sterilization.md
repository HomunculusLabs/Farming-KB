# Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas cepacia Microwave Susceptibility in Surface Sterilization

## Overview

The NASA [[challenge-microorganisms-microwave-surface-sterilization]] study (MSC-22484) included
*Escherichia coli* and *Pseudomonas cepacia* as challenge organisms
alongside the spore-forming *[[bacillus-pumilus-radiation-resistance-surface-decontamination]]*. These two Gram-negative
bacteria represent common surface contaminants in biological systems
and provide a contrast to the more resistant bacterial spore form.
Their inclusion in the mixed-contaminant kill curve reveals the
relative susceptibility spectrum of the [[coaxial-power-splitter-waveguide-microwave-sterilization]]
method.

## Organism Profiles

### Escherichia coli

*E. coli* is a Gram-negative rod-shaped bacterium and one of the most
studied organisms in microbiology. As a challenge organism for
[[microbial-kill-curves-sterilization-validation]], it represents:

- **Enteric contamination**: *E. coli* originates from fecal matter and
  is a classic indicator of sanitation failure. Its presence on surfaces
  suggests fecal contamination or poor hygiene.
- **Moderate environmental resistance**: As a vegetative bacterium,
  *E. coli* is more susceptible to heat and chemical agents than spore-
  formers. It typically dies within minutes at 60°C.
- **Rapid growth**: *E. coli* doubles every 20–30 minutes under optimal
  conditions, meaning even small residual populations can regrow
  quickly if sterilization is incomplete.

### Pseudomonas cepacia (Burkholderia cepacia)

*Pseudomonas cepacia* (now reclassified as *Burkholderia cepacia
  complex*) is a Gram-negative rod bacterium known for its environmental
  resilience. As a challenge organism it represents:

- **Environmental contamination**: *B. cepacia* is found in soil, water,
  and plant rhizospheres. It is a common contaminant in water systems,
  pharmaceutical solutions, and medical devices.
- **Enhanced resistance**: Compared to *E. coli*, *B. cepacia* shows
  greater intrinsic resistance to disinfectants, antibiotics, and
  environmental stresses. It produces biofilms that protect embedded
  cells.
- **Opportunistic pathogen**: *B. cepacia* is an opportunistic pathogen
  in immunocompromised individuals, particularly cystic fibrosis
  patients. Its elimination from medical and laboratory surfaces is
  critical.

## Relative Susceptibility in Microwave Kill Curves

The NASA data shows a clear hierarchy of susceptibility among the three
challenge organisms:

1. ***Pseudomonas cepacia***: Despite its reputation for disinfectant
   resistance, *P. cepacia* showed relatively rapid kill in the
   microwave system. This is likely because microwave sterilization
   operates primarily through thermal mechanisms (steam generation at
   the cell surface), which bypass the specific biochemical resistance
   mechanisms that protect *P. cepacia* from chemical disinfectants.

2. ***Escherichia coli***: *E. coli* showed intermediate susceptibility.
   Its thin peptidoglycan layer and lack of spore structures make it
   vulnerable to thermal disruption.

3. ***Bacillus pumilus*** (spores): The most resistant organism by a
   significant margin. Bacterial spores survive the longest and
   determine the total exposure time required for complete sterility.

## Mechanism of Microwave Kill in Gram-Negative Bacteria

Gram-negative bacteria like *E. coli* and *P. cepacia* have cell walls
consisting of a thin peptidoglycan layer sandwiched between an inner
cytoplasmic membrane and an outer membrane containing
lipopolysaccharide. Microwave sterilization disrupts these organisms
through:

- **Thermal denaturation**: The microwave-induced steam at the cell
  surface rapidly raises local temperature above the thermal death
  point (typically 65–80°C for these species), denaturing proteins and
  disrupting membrane integrity.

- **Membrane disruption**: The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria
