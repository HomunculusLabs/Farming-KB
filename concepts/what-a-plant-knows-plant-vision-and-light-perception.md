## Plant Vision and Light Perception

Plants monitor their visible environment continuously and with surprising
sophistication. They see if you come near them, know when you stand over them, can
distinguish if you are wearing a blue or a red shirt, and know if you have moved
their pots from one side of the room to another. While plants do not see in pictures
as humans do -- they cannot discern between a slightly balding middle-aged man with
glasses and a smiling little girl with brown curls -- they perceive light in many
ways and colors that humans can only imagine. Plants see the same ultraviolet light
that causes sunburn and infrared light that produces heat. They can tell when there
is very little light (from a candle), when it is the middle of the day, when the
sun is about to set, if light comes from the left, the right, or above, if another
plant has grown over them blocking their light, and how long the lights have been on.

## What Constitutes Vision

Vision, broadly defined, begins with the ability to discriminate between light and
shadow. Imagine a person born blind, living in total darkness, who gains the ability
to differentiate night from day and inside from outside. These new senses would
definitely be considered rudimentary sight and would enable new levels of function.
Now imagine this person being able to discern color -- blue above and green below.
This fundamental change from total blindness to seeing color would qualify as vision.
By this standard, plants clearly possess a form of sight: they detect light,
discriminate its direction, measure its duration, and differentiate its colors.
