# Wasson's Mexico Expeditions

Between 1953 and the mid-1960s, [Robert Gordon Wasson made ten
successive trips to Mexico in pursuit of indigenous psychoactive mushroom use. These
expeditions resulted in the introduction of [psilocybin mushrooms to Western awareness and indirectly catalysed the psychedelic revolution
of the 1960s.

## Background and Origins

Wasson's interest in mushrooms had been stimulated by the contrasting reactions to fungi
he shared with his Russian-born wife valentina pavlovna wasson. Their
mycophobia-mycophilia hypothesis had been elaborated in their monumental work
Mushrooms, Russia and History (1957).

In 1952, Wasson received two letters that would change the course of his life. Both
arrived virtually in the same post, alerting him to the existence of the Mesoamerican
mushroom stones and to the fact that the Aztec teonanacatl ("flesh of the gods") had
been a mushroom rather than, as scholars had assumed, a cactus or other plant.

Wasson wrote to the Austrian anthropologist Richard Evans Schultes's colleague Roberto Weitlaner and to the Czech engineer Blasius
Reko, both of whom had reported on indigenous mushroom use in Oaxaca. Reko forwarded
Wasson's letter to Eunice Pike, a missionary resident in the Mazatec town of Huautla
de Jimenez. Pike confirmed that mushrooms were indeed used in curing rituals and
added that the local people believed the mushroom to possess a "voice," which spoke
through the curanderos (healers) to reveal the cause and cure of illnesses.

## The First Expedition (1953)

In the late summer of 1953, the rainy mushroom season, Wasson travelled to Mexico for
the first time, accompanied by his daughter Masha. Reko had died in the interim, but
the Wassons were assisted by Weitlaner, who provided invaluable local knowledge and
acted as translator.

The journey was arduous. They travelled by car over appalling roads and then with
donkeys over steep and vertiginous mountain passes to reach the remote town of Huautla
in the Sierra Mazateca of Oaxaca.

After days of fruitless searching, during which the taciturn locals proved reticent
about mushrooms, they discovered that their local guide, Aurelio Carreras, was himself
a curandero. After some persuasion, he agreed to hold a velada (mushroom vigil) to
determine the health of Wasson's son Peter.

Only Aurelio consumed mushrooms at this first ceremony, but Wasson was at last able to
witness an authentic indigenous mushroom ceremony and make detailed records of it. He
politely humoured Aurelio when the mushroom-assisted divination revealed Peter to be
not in New York (where Wasson believed him to be) but in a state of emotional turmoil.

On returning home, Wasson found things rather as Aurelio had predicted: the
enterprising Peter had taken advantage of his father's absence to hold a party in
their New York apartment.

## The Second Expedition (1954)

Wasson's second trip took him to the mountainous Mixe region of north-east Oaxaca.
This expedition generated useful ethnographic data on indigenous mushroom use but is
less well known than the trips that preceded and followed it.

## The Third Expedition (1955): The Maria Sabina Encounter

Wasson's third trip, in 1955, proved the most momentous. Accompanied by society
photographer Allan Richardson (and later joined by Valentina and Masha), Wasson
returned to Huautla and arranged his now-infamous meeting with the curandera
[Maria Sabina (1894-1985).

Sabina was not only a locally respected and renowned healer but, most importantly,
she agreed to let both Wasson and Richardson eat the sacred mushrooms. They thus
became the first known Westerners to intentionally consume psilocybin mushrooms in
an indigenous ceremonial context.

The experience was transformative. Wasson described seeing "brilliant colours, gold
and onyx and ebony, all most harmoniously and ingeniously contrived, in richest
magnificence extending beyond the reach of sight." He felt himself a "disembodied
eye" hovering above strange new landscapes, and then a witness to what seemed like
the Platonic realm of forms.

## The Life Article and Its Aftermath

Wasson's account of his experiences was published in the popular American magazine
Life on 13 May 1957, under the title "Seeking the Magic Mushroom." The article was
read by millions and played a pivotal role in kick-starting the psychedelic
revolution.

Key figures of the movement, including timothy leary, experimented with
psychedelics as a direct result of reading the Life article. The trickle of mushroom
seekers visiting Huautla in the early 1960s became a flood, until Oaxaca was awash
with hippies following the magic mushroom trail.

Rock stars including Pete Townshend, John Lennon and Bob Dylan were rumoured to have
made the pilgrimage, lending the trip an aura of celebrity endorsement that made
Huautla an essential countercultural destination.

## Consequences for the Mazatec

The impact of Wasson's publications on the Mazatec community was profound and largely
negative. The influx of Western seekers disrupted traditional practices and placed
enormous pressure on local curanderos.

For many hippies arriving in Huautla, the mushrooms were "psychedelic" commodities,
bound up with notions of authenticity, freedom and rebellion. They did not wish to
be bound to the curandero-led velada or to the animist peasant culture that
surrounded it. They expected to buy and consume mushrooms as and when they wished,
irrespective of local sensibilities.

The situation contributed significantly to [Maria Sabina's later difficulties, as the traditional knowledge she
guarded became exposed to commercial exploitation and cultural appropriation.

## Scientific Collaboration

During these expeditions, Wasson worked with the French mycologist Roger Heim, who
identified several new species of psychoactive mushrooms. Heim sent specimens to
albert hofmann in Switzerland, who in 1958 successfully isolated and named the
active compounds psilocybin and psilocin.
