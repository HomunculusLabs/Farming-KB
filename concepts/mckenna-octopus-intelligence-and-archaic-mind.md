# Mckenna Octopus Intelligence and the Archaic Mind

In Food of the Gods, McKenna uses the octopus as a provocative example of an alien intelligence that evolved independently of the vertebrate lineage, arguing that the existence of such radically different forms of cognition on our own planet suggests that intelligence and consciousness are properties of matter itself, not unique to the human species.

## The Octopus as Alien Intelligence

McKenna draws attention to the octopus as a being whose form of intelligence is utterly alien to our own, yet whose cognitive abilities are undeniable. The octopus is a mollusk -- a lineage that diverged from our own vertebrate ancestors over 600 million years ago. Its nervous system is organized on entirely different principles from ours.

The octopus has no centralized brain in the vertebrate sense; instead, its neurons are distributed throughout its body, with concentrations in the arms and in a ring-shaped brain surrounding the esophagus. Each arm can act independently, solving problems and processing sensory information without direction from the central brain.

This distributed model of intelligence is radically different from our own vertebrate model, yet it produces behaviors that we recognize as intelligent: problem-solving, tool use, learning, and memory. The octopus can open jars, navigate mazes, and escape from enclosures -- all signs of a sophisticated cognitive system.

## Implications for Understanding Consciousness

The existence of the octopus challenges our assumptions about the relationship between brain structure and consciousness. If intelligence can arise in a nervous system organized on principles so different from our own, then consciousness may be a much more general property of complex biological systems than we have been willing to admit.

This is the point that McKenna wants to make. Consciousness is not a unique gift to humanity, not a special dispensation from a divine creator, but a natural property of complex, self-organizing systems. The octopus is proof that there is more than one way to build a mind.

## The Archaic Mind Before Language

The octopus also serves as a model for what McKenna calls the "Archaic mind" -- a form of cognition that existed before the emergence of language and the ego, and that persists in the depths of the human psyche as the unconscious.

The Archaic mind is not primitive in the sense of being inferior; it is archaic in the sense of being prior, more fundamental, more deeply rooted in the structure of reality. It is the mind that we share with all living beings, the mind that is in constant communication with the environment through [[dmt-self-transforming-machine-elves-mckenna]] of hyperspace." These beings, he suggests, may represent a form of intelligence that is as different from our own as the octopus's, but that exists not in physical space but in the dimension of consciousness itself.

## Connection to the Stoned Ape Theory

The octopus also connects to McKenna's stoned ape theory in an important way. If consciousness is a general property of complex biological systems, then the emergence of human consciousness may not have been a unique event but a specific instance of a general phenomenon.

The stoned ape theory suggests that psilocybin catalyzed the emergence of human consciousness by activating neural circuits that were already present in the primate brain but that had not yet been fully developed. In other words, the potential for consciousness was already there; psilocybin simply accelerated its development.

The octopus provides a parallel example. Its form of intelligence emerged independently of the vertebrate lineage, through entirely different evolutionary mechanisms. This suggests that consciousness is not a uniquely human achievement but a general possibility that evolution can realize in many different ways.

## The Horror of the Dominator Mind

The dominator mind, which sees nature as a resource to be exploited and other beings as competitors to be defeated, cannot tolerate the idea that intelligence exists in forms radically different from its own. The octopus, with its alien intelligence, is therefore a kind of horror to the dominator mind -- a reminder that the world is richer, stranger, and more complex than the dominator worldview allows.
