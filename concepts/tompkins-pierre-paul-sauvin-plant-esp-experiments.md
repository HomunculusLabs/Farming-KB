# Pierre Paul Sauvin and Plant ESP Electronics

## Overview

Pierre Paul Sauvin, an electronics specialist from West Paterson,
New Jersey, was among the most technically sophisticated researchers
to investigate Cleve Backster's discovery of [[tompkins-cleve-backster-primary-perception]] in plants. An assiduous investigator of ESP and the
phenomenon of remote hypnotism, Sauvin combined his engineering
expertise with parapsychological interests to build advanced
instrumentation capable of detecting and utilizing plant responses
to human thought and emotion at a distance.

## Background and Inspiration

Sauvin trained and worked for several large corporations, including
Aerospace and International Telephone and Telegraph (ITT). When Long
John Nebel, a professional skeptic, pressed Backster on his radio
program to describe practical uses for [[plant-perception]], Backster
suggested instrumenting a plant to activate a small electric train
on no other command than that of human emotion. This notion, though
singularly impractical, fired Sauvin's imagination and he turned his
bachelor quarters overlooking the Passaic River into what amounted
to a laboratory filled with electronic equipment.

Sauvin claimed that many of his insights and ideas for inventions
came to him in psychic flashes, as if he were merely acting as a
medium. He would sometimes get factual data necessary for an
invention without fully understanding the principle, and must get
further details by questions addressed to "levels beyond."

## The Electric Train Experiment

Sauvin's first dramatic demonstration was getting a toy electric
train running around a track and reversing its direction through
nothing but his thought and emotion relayed to a plant. He was able
not only to demonstrate the phenomenon before an audience of sixty
in Madison, New Jersey, but to make the train start and stop at will
under the klieg lights of a television studio.

The mechanism was ingenious: as the engine moved around the track
it would activate a switch leading to Sauvin's body, giving him a
sharp electric shock. Just ahead on the track, another switch was
wired to a galvanometer attached to an ordinary philodendron. As
the philodendron picked up Sauvin's emotional reaction at being
shocked, the galvanometer needle would jump and throw the switch,
reversing the train. Eventually Sauvin found that he merely needed
to remember or re-feel a shock for his plants to pick up the signal,
even from as far away as his holiday cottage eighty miles north of
his West Paterson lab.

## Advanced Instrumentation

Perusing trade journals at ITT, Sauvin was struck by articles by L.
George Lawrence on unusual electronic circuits. From one of Sauvin's
government contract jobs he salvaged phase-loop-locked discriminators
pressed into microelectronic silicon wafers. With these "chips" he
built a Wheatstone bridge for measuring electrical potential with
alternating instead of direct current, and an automatic gain control
circuit. The sensitivity achieved was one hundred times greater than
Backster's galvanometer and eliminated enormous amounts of electronic
"noise."

What Sauvin was now measuring was no longer voltage amplitude but
phase shift, the fine lag between two running voltages. Sauvin
hooked his plants to an oscilloscope whose loops changed shape as
the current from a plant varied. Simultaneously, a varying tone was
produced by current run through an amplified tone oscillator which
enabled Sauvin to hear minute changes in vibrations. A bank of tape
recorders kept a permanent record along with a WWY international
time-signal broadcast. With a stopwatch Sauvin could monitor the
effect he was having on his plants from a distance.

## Remote Monitoring Systems

Sauvin's Merlin equipment included a complex system of automatic
phone-answering and recording devices. By means of a small radio
transmitter strapped to his leg and a battery of automated and
preprogrammed tape machines at home he could communicate via his
home phone, receiving messages and giving answers, all from his desk
at ITT. This Rube Goldberg equipment served Sauvin admirably for
remote-controlled communication with his plants. He could call his
own number and speak to his plants directly, monitoring the tones of
their response via the amplified audio-oscillator.

## Emotional Range of Plant Responses

Like Backster, Sauvin found that his plants reacted most strongly to
the death of living cells in their environment, and most consistently
to the death of human cells. He also found that the simplest signal
he could transmit to his plants extrasensorily was to give himself a
light electric shock. His plants several miles away would react with
an instant surge.

These experiments led Sauvin to wonder if plants might not react
just as well to emotions of pleasure and joy as to pain and shock.
He soon found that his plants did react to joy and pleasure, but
with wave patterns that were not sharp enough to trigger a switch
reliably. During a holiday with a girlfriend at his lakeside cottage
he established that his plants, eighty miles away, would react with
very high peaks on the tone oscillator to the acute pleasure of
sexual climax, going right off the top at the moment of orgasm.

## The Triple-Plant Circuit

To address the problem of environmental false positives, Sauvin wired
three plants, each set in a different room, to a single circuit which
could only be activated if all three plants reacted synchronously. By
keeping the plants in separate environments Sauvin hoped the required
stimulus would be synchronous only when it came from him, wherever
he might be. This represented a step forward in creating a reliable
plant-based bioelectric detection system.
