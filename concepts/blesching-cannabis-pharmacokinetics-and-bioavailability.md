# Cannabis Pharmacokinetics and Bioavailability

Pharmacokinetics describes how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolizes, and
excretes a substance (ADME). Understanding the pharmacokinetics of cannabinoids
is essential for optimizing therapeutic outcomes. *The Cannabis Health Index*
addresses these principles throughout its discussions of
[[blesching-cannabis-administration-methods]],
[[blesching-cannabis-dosing-strategies]], and the
[[blesching-cannabis-therapeutic-window-methodology]] concept.

## Routes of Administration and Absorption

The method by which cannabis enters the body dramatically affects its
pharmacokinetic profile, including onset time, duration of effect, and overall
bioavailability (the proportion of the administered dose that reaches systemic
circulation).

### Inhalation (Smoking and Vaporization)

Inhalation delivers cannabinoids directly to the lungs, where they rapidly
cross the alveolar membranes into the bloodstream. This route provides the
fastest onset of effects, typically within minutes. Bioavailability through
smoking is estimated at 10-35%, with significant variability due to smoking
technique, depth of inhalation, and duration of breath-holding.

Vaporization heats cannabis to release cannabinoids and terpenoids without
combustion, avoiding the production of many harmful compounds found in smoke.
This method preserves bioavailability while reducing respiratory risks.

The rapid onset of inhaled cannabis makes it suitable for conditions requiring
immediate relief, such as acute pain, nausea, or anxiety. However, the duration
of effects is typically shorter (2-4 hours) compared to oral administration.

### Oral Administration

Oral ingestion through edibles, capsules, or tinctures produces a markedly
different pharmacokinetic profile. Cannabinoids absorbed through the
gastrointestinal tract undergo first-pass metabolism in the liver, where they
are converted to metabolites. This process significantly reduces bioavailability
(estimated at 5-20%) but produces longer-lasting effects (4-8 hours or more).

A critical consideration with oral cannabis is the delayed onset of effects,
typically 30 minutes to 2 hours. This delay can lead to overconsumption if
patients re-dose before feeling the initial effects, potentially resulting in
uncomfortable intoxication.

The liver converts THC to 11-hydroxy-THC, a metabolite that is more
psychoactive than THC itself and crosses the blood-brain barrier more readily.
This conversion explains why oral cannabis often produces more intense
psychoactive effects compared to inhaled cannabis at equivalent doses.

### Sublingual and Topical Administration

Sublingual application allows cannabinoids to be absorbed through mucous
membranes, partially bypassing first-pass liver metabolism. This offers a
middle ground with faster onset than oral (15-45 minutes) and moderate
bioavailability. Tinctures and sprays provide more predictable dosing than
inhaled methods while avoiding the delayed onset of edibles.

Topical preparations (creams, balms, oils) act primarily on CB2 receptors in
skin and peripheral tissues with minimal systemic absorption, making them ideal
for localized pain and inflammation without psychoactive effects.

## Distribution

Once absorbed, cannabinoids are distributed throughout the body. THC is highly
lipophilic (fat-soluble), readily crossing cell membranes and accumulating in
fatty tissues. This has several implications:

- **Blood-brain barrier:** THC readily crosses into the brain, enabling its
  psychoactive and neuroprotective effects. CBD also crosses this barrier.

- **Fat storage:** Cannabinoids accumulate in adipose tissue, serving as a
  long-term storage reservoir. This explains detection weeks after last use.

- **Receptor distribution:** CB1 receptors in the brain and CNS, and CB2
  receptors in immune and peripheral tissues, determine where cannabinoids
  exert their effects after distribution.

## Metabolism

Cannabinoid metabolism occurs primarily in the liver through cytochrome P450
(CYP450) enzyme systems, particularly CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4. This
metabolic pathway has important clinical implications:

- **Drug interactions:** The CYP450 enzymes metabolize many common medications.
  Cannabis can either inhibit or induce these enzymes, potentially altering the
  metabolism and effectiveness of pharmaceuticals taken concurrently.

- **Individual variation:** Genetic polymorphisms in CYP450 enzymes create
  significant inter-individual variation in cannabinoid metabolism, explaining
  why the same dose can produce markedly different effects in different people.

- **Metabolite activity:** As noted, the conversion of THC to 11-hydroxy-THC
  produces a more potent metabolite. CBD is metabolized to 7-hydroxy-CBD and
  other compounds with their own pharmacological profiles.

## Excretion

Cannabinoids and their metabolites are primarily excreted through feces (65%)
and urine (20%), with minor amounts eliminated through sweat, hair, and breath.
The elimination half-life of THC varies widely, from approximately 1.3 days for
occasional users to 5-13 days for chronic users, due to accumulation in fat
tissue and slow release.

## Factors Affecting Bioavailability

Multiple factors influence cannabinoid bioavailability beyond the route of
administration:

- **Strain composition:** Different [[blesching-cannabis-sativa-indica-classification]] produce different ratios of THC, CBD, and other
  compounds, affecting the overall pharmacokinetic profile.

- **Decarboxylation:** Raw cannabis contains cannabinoid acids (THCA, CBDA)
  that must be heated (decarboxylated) to convert to their active forms.
  [[blesching-cannabis-decarboxylation-and-raw-cannabis]] consumed
  as juice retains the acid forms, which have different pharmacokinetic
  properties and lower bioavailability in the traditional sense.

- **Food effects:** Taking oral cannabis with food, particularly high-fat meals,
  can significantly increase absorption and bioavailability.

- **Individual factors:** Body composition, metabolism, tolerance, and
  [[blesching-endocannabinoid-system-overview]] function
  all affect how an individual processes cannabinoids.

## Clinical Implications

Understanding pharmacokinetics is essential for finding the
[[blesching-cannabis-therapeutic-window-methodology]], the
optimal dose range where therapeutic effects are maximized while adverse
effects are minimized. The variability in cannabinoid pharmacokinetics between
individuals and between administration methods underscores the importance of
personalized dosing and careful titration.
