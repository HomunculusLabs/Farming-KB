# Winter Salad and Braising Mix Composition

Eliot Coleman's Four [[coleman-four-season-farm]] has developed two signature winter
products through decades of trial and refinement: a baby-leaf salad
mix and a braising mix. Both are composed exclusively of cold-hardy
varieties grown in unheated greenhouses, harvested during the coldest
months of the year in coastal Maine.

## The Winter Salad Mix Philosophy

The salad mix is composed of whole baby leaves that are washed, mixed,
and packed directly after harvesting. Ideally, leaves are no more than
three inches in length. Coleman believes a quality mesclun must have
leaves that easily fit onto a fork and into the mouth — overgrown leaves
are better consigned to a braising mix or sold bunched as cooking greens.

The standard mix includes a carefully chosen combination of: red and
green lettuces (red oak-leaf, green oak-leaf, crisp, and romaine),
broadleaf arugula, sylvetta arugula, 'Bianca Riccia' endive, narrow-stem
chard, claytonia, minutina, spinach, mâche, watercress, and 'Bull's
Blood' beet leaves.

Based on USDA nutritional data, this salad blend contains at least 5
times more calcium, 4 times more iron, 12 times more vitamin A, and 6
times more vitamin C than a salad of head lettuce.

## Quality Standards and Decision-Making

Quality is the primary concern. Coleman works hard to set rather than
follow quality standards. The farm does not sell salad ingredients that
are too large, bitter, tough, stringy, or frost damaged. If not completely
satisfied, that ingredient is left out until conditions improve.

These evaluations have led to important operational adjustments:

- Moving claytonia from colder edge beds to warmer inner beds for
  midwinter harvest
- Increasing succession plantings of minutina and narrow-stem chard
  for more new young leaves rather than relying on regrowth
- Cutting back red-oak lettuce earlier because regrowth is hardier
  than initial leaves
- Continual trial plantings of standard and potential crops to identify
  better cultivars and discover new flavors, shapes, and textures

## Variety Selection Details

The farm does not chop mature plants into small pieces like the cut-up
endive and radicchio seen in supermarket mixes. Instead, they grow
'Indivia Bianca Riccia da Taglio' (white curly endive for cutting), sold
by Johnny's Seeds as 'Bianca Riccia', which has nice light-colored
frilly leaves at the baby-leaf stage.

For red color, 'Bull's Blood' beet leaves are used rather than radicchio.
Cool [[coleman-aphid-management-winter-greenhouse-nitrogen-flushing]] temperatures intensify the beautiful deep maroon,
and the flavor lacks radicchio's bitter edge (which under poor conditions
can become so bitter as to ruin the whole salad).

Cool temperatures keep arugula and watercress from becoming too strong
and biting. The hardy oriental greens are deliberately excluded from the
salad mix because customers prefer a milder flavor. Many parents report
this is the only mixed salad their children will eat.

## Temperature Management Challenges

Three components — lettuce, endive, and arugula — do not consistently
meet quality standards once temperatures get too cold for too long.
These traditionally receive extra protection in the [[coleman-cool-house-minimal-supplementary-heat]] during
January and February.

Some lettuce varieties, principally the oak-leaf types, are very cold
hardy at the baby-leaf stage, but their quality during parts of January
and February is better from the cool house than under the inner covers
of the cold houses. The same is true for 'Bianca Riccia' endive and
standard arugula.

Wild arugula sold as 'Sylvetta' is perfectly hardy, but the farm is
still learning about its soil-fertility needs and planting timing.
Despite occasional inconsistency, the farm prefers selling a consistent
product and continues the search for hardier cultivars.
