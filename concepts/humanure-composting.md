# Humanure Composting

Safe composting of human manure following thermophilic methods. Based on Joe Jenkins' *The Humanure Handbook* (4th ed.) and university extension guidelines. Humanure, when properly managed, becomes a valuable soil amendment; when mishandled, it is a pathogen vector.

## Safety and Pathogen Concerns

Thermophilic composting (internal temps 120-160°F / 49-71°C) destroys pathogens. Key time-temp relationships:

| Pathogen | Temp to Kill | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Salmonella spp. | 131°F / 55°C | 1 hour |
| E. coli | 131°F / 55°C | 30 min |
| Ascaris eggs (helminth) | 104°F / 40°C | Days at 122°F / 50°C recommended |
| Taenia (tapeworm) | 131°F / 55°C | Minutes |
| Shigella | 131°F / 55°C | 1 hour |
| Entamoeba histolytica | 122°F / 50°C | Minutes |
| Vibrio cholerae | 131°F / 55°C | Minutes |

Critical rule: **retention time of 1-2 years after active composting**. Even if thermophilic phase is incomplete, time and microbial competition eliminate remaining pathogens.

## Carbon Cover Materials

Cover after every use to prevent odor and flies. Material must be carbon-rich and fine:

- **Sawdust** (best): high C, fine particles, absorbs moisture. C:N ~400:1. Hardwood preferred.
- **Peat moss**: excellent moisture control, slightly acidic. More expensive.
- **Shredded leaves**: free, but coarser. Mix with finer material.
- **Straw/hay**: use shredded or chopped. Coarser — may allow odor.
- **Coir (coco coir)**: excellent, consistent. Store compressed blocks.
- **Wood shavings**: acceptable if fine. Avoid large chips.

Ratio: aim for 1 part humanure to 3+ parts cover material by volume.

## Composting Process

1. **Collection**: deposit in bucket/bin, cover with carbon material after every use
2. **Transfer**: empty bins to outdoor [[mushroom-cultivator-compost-pile-management]] (minimum 4x4x4 ft for thermophilic activity)
3. **Build**: alternate layers — humanure + carbon cover + kitchen scraps + straw/leaves
4. **Thermophilic phase**: pile should reach 120-160°F within days. C:N ratio 25-35:1. Moisture like a wrung-out sponge. Maintain for months.
5. **Curing**: after heating subsides, let sit **1-2 years**. Cover to shed rain.
6. **Application**: use on non-food crops first if cautious; on food crops after full curing

Target C:N ratio: 25-35:1. Humanure is ~5-10:1, cover materials 100-400:1. Layer generously.

## Using Finished Compost

| Use | Safety Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit trees (non-grafted root zone) | Highest | Apply in fall, root uptake minimal |
| Ornamental plants, shrubs | Highest | No food chain concern |
| Berry bushes | High | Avoid harvest-season surface application |
| Grain crops | Moderate | Pathogens don't accumulate in grain |
| Root crops (potatoes, carrots) | Moderate | Avoid if pile didn't reach thermophilic temps |
| [[solomon-leafy-greens-growing]] eaten raw | Lowest risk | Only use fully cured (2+ years) compost |

## Legal Considerations

Laws vary widely by jurisdiction. In the US, no federal regulation — it's state/local:

- **Permissive states** (few restrictions): Colorado, Oregon, Washington, Vermont, Maine allow composting [[composting-toilet-systems]] with minimal permitting
- **Restrictive states**: some require NSF/ANSI 41 certified systems or prohibit use on food crops
- **Building codes**: many jurisdictions accept composting toilets under IRC Appendix Q or as alternative systems via variance
- **International**: Sweden and parts of Scandinavia are permissive; many EU countries require approved systems; Australia has state-level variation
- **Practical approach**: most rural homesteads operate under the radar with DIY bucket systems. Urban areas face more scrutiny.

Check [[homesteading-legal-considerations]] for general permitting guidance.

## Integrating with Existing Compost Systems

- Humanure compost bins should be **separate** from kitchen/garden compost
- Position: Zone 2-3, downwind of living areas, accessible by wheelbarrow
- Dual-chamber system: fill one while the other cures (minimum 2 bins)
- Can blend finished humanure into garden compost as a nutrient boost
- Volume: one person produces ~400 lbs/year of compostable material

## Urine Diversion and Use

Urine contains ~11-1-2.5 NPK (g per liter). It is essentially sterile when fresh from a healthy person.

- **Direct application**: dilute 1:10 with water, apply to soil around plants. Avoid foliage contact.
- **Compost activator**: add to carbon piles to boost nitrogen and moisture
- **Storage**: store in sealed container up to 1 week (urea converts to ammonia over time)
- **Volume**: average person produces 1-2 liters/day — significant nitrogen source
- **Cautions**: medications pass through urine; don't apply to food crops within 30 days of harvest

## Graywater Systems Overview

Graywater (sink, shower, laundry — NOT blackwater/toilet) can irrigate landscapes:

- **Simplest**: laundry-to-landscape (L2L) — diverter valve, 1" poly tubing, mulch basins
- **Moderate**: branched drain system — gravity-fed to multiple mulch basins
- **Advanced**: [[comparison-constructed-wetland-vs-backyard-pond]] — gravel/plant filter for indoor reuse or discharge
- **Soap selection**: use biodegradable, low-phosphate, no bleach/borax
- **Legal**: many states allow L2L without permit (CA, AZ, NM, TX, others)
- Integration with [[homestead-water-systems]]

## Odor Management

Odor only occurs when aerobic conditions fail:

- Cover every deposit immediately with carbon material
- Keep bins sealed when not in use (tight lid)
- Adequate cover material is the single most important factor
- Vent compost pile (passive pipe or active fan for enclosed systems)
- If odor appears: add more carbon, ensure drainage, turn pile
- No odor = working correctly. If it smells, fix the process — not with chemicals.
