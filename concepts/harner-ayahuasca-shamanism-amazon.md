## The Jivaro System: "The Sound of Rushing Water"

Harner's own chapter, "The Sound of Rushing Water," is the volume's most detailed account of ayahuasca shamanism. Its evocative title comes from the sound that fills the shaman's ears when he drinks natem and knows he possesses the power of Tsungi, the first shaman. Among the Jivaro Indians of the Ecuadorian Amazon, the normal waking life is considered a "lie" or illusion, while the true forces determining daily events are supernatural and accessible only through hallucinogenic drugs. This reality view "creates a particularly strong demand for specialists who can cross over into the supernatural world at will to deal with the forces that influence and even determine the events of the waking life."

Approximately one in four Jivaro men becomes a shaman, a remarkably high proportion facilitated by the widespread availability of natem. Women rarely become shamans, but when they do they are thought to be particularly powerful because they are believed to possess special tsentsak (spirit helpers). Any adult who wishes to become a shaman simply presents a gift to a practicing shaman, who administers the drink and transfers some of his own power.

### The Tsentsak (Spirit Helpers)

Jivaro shamans operate with tsentsak, which are the main supernatural forces believed to cause illness and death in daily life. These spirit helpers, or "darts," are invisible to the non-shaman and even to shamans except under the influence of natem. Each tsentsak has a dual nature: a natural aspect (an ordinary material object as seen without the drug) and a supernatural aspect revealed through the brew.

In their supernatural form, the tsentsak appear as demons in various forms--giant butterflies, jaguars, monkeys, snakes, and birds. Almost any small object, including living insects and worms, can become a tsentsak if small enough to be swallowed by a shaman. The greater the variety of these objects a shaman has in his body, the greater his ability. Different types of tsentsak are used to cause different kinds and degrees of illness.

A special class called pasuk serves only a single shaman as a specialized bewitching assistant, appearing under natem as "covered with iron except for its eyes." The curing shaman can kill this pasuk only by shooting a tsentsak into its eyes, the sole vulnerable area. To the person who has not taken the hallucinogenic drink, the pasuk usually appears to be simply a tarantula.

### Bewitching and Curing

Bewitching is carried out against specific individuals, usually to avenge a particular offense. The shaman drinks natem, secretly approaches the victim's house, drinks green tobacco juice to regurgitate a tsentsak, and throws it at the target. If the dart passes completely through the body, the patient dies and "there is nothing for a curing shaman to suck out." If it lodges within, a curing shaman may be able to suck it out. The curing shaman's work is complementary to the bewitcher's: he takes natem, sees the intruding object in the patient's body, and sucks it out.

Shamans also create supernatural animals from their tsentsak: a pointed armadillo bone becomes an anaconda that overturns a victim's raft; a snake tooth becomes a poisonous serpent; a tsentsak shot into a river creates a jaguar or puma. About five years after receiving their tsentsak, bewitching shamans undergo a test by attempting to split a tree with a dart. If they fail, they must purchase new power from a stronger shaman, drinking large quantities of natem, tobacco juice, and piripiri extract daily until they can afford the transaction.

### Shamanic Training and Discipline

The training process involves the donor regurgitating a brilliant substance containing spirit helpers, cutting part off with a machete, and giving it to the novice to swallow. The recipient experiences pain and stays in bed for ten days, drinking natem every evening. The donor periodically blows and rubs all over the novice's body to increase the transfer's power.

Sexual abstinence is required for at least five months to achieve killing or curing power; a full year is considered necessary for real effectiveness. During this period, the new shaman collects insects, plants, and other objects to convert into tsentsak. At the end of the first month, a tsentsak emerges from the mouth. If the novice can control the tremendous desire to bewitch that follows--said to come "with a strength and frequency similar to that of hunger"--and reswallows the dart, he becomes a curing shaman. If he casts it, he becomes a bewitcher.

## Other Amazonian Traditions

### Cashinahua (Kensinger)
Kenneth Kensinger documents Banisteriopsis use among the Peruvian Cashinahua, including the concept of a "good trip" (nixi pae waken bai wai pe). This linguistic parallel to modern Western drug culture is noted by Harner as evidence for cross-cultural patterns in the hallucinogenic experience.

### Sharanahua (Siskind)
Janet Siskind records visions and curing practices involving ayahuasca among the Sharanahua, contributing to the broader picture of Amazonian plant-mediated shamanism in the western Amazon.
