# Mycelial Network Dynamics

[[mycelial-networks]] represent one of the most remarkable biological systems in
terrestrial ecosystems. Formed by filamentous fungi, these networks can extend
over metres or even hectares, connecting and redistributing resources across
vast areas of soil and organic matter. The study of mycelial network dynamics
encompasses nutrient translocation, architectural plasticity, and the
coordinated physiological responses of fungal colonies to heterogeneous
environments.

## Basidiomycete Mycelial Networks

Basidiomycetes are the major agents of decomposition and nutrient cycling in
forest ecosystems, occurring as both [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs]] and
[[gadd-fungal-symbiosis-mycorrhizae]]. The mycelium can scavenge and sequester
nutrients from soil, concentrate nutrients from decomposing organic matter,
relocate nutrients between different organic resources, and ultimately make
nutrients available to plants to maintain primary productivity.

Hyphae of both saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes that ramify
through soil often aggregate to form rapidly extending, persistent,
specialized high-conductivity channels termed cords. These cords form complex
networks that can extend for considerable distances in the natural environment.
The distribution of resources is extremely heterogeneous and unpredictable in
space and time, and these fungi have developed species-specific strategies to
search for new resources and to capitalize on resources landing on their
mycelial systems.

## Network Architecture and Plasticity

The architecture of the mycelial network is not static, but is continuously
reconfigured in response to local nutritional or environmental cues, damage,
or predation, through a combination of growth, branching, fusion, or
regression. At this stage it is not clear whether specific global mechanisms
exist to couple local sensory perception and responses over different length
scales specifically to maximize the long-term success of the whole colony,
or whether such collective behaviour is an emergent property arising solely
from local interactions of individual hyphae.

Embedded within the physical structure of the network is an equally complex
set of physiological processes that contribute to uptake, storage, and
redistribution of nutrients throughout the network in an apparently well
coordinated manner. As the colony grows out from a resource base, nutrient
translocation would be expected to be predominantly towards the growing
margin. If additional resources are found, then re-distribution back to the
base can also occur, although not necessarily by the same transport system at
the same time.

## Transport Mechanisms

The precise mechanisms underlying nutrient translocation in fungi are not yet
fully known but are thought to include mass flow, diffusion, generalized
cytoplasmic streaming, and specific vesicular transport. The highly dynamic
pleiomorphic vacuolar system present in filamentous fungi of all the major
taxonomic groups examined so far might have a role in long-distance
translocation over millimetres or centimetres.

The structure of the vacuole within a single hypha develops from a complex
reticulum of fine tubes interspersed with small spherical vacuoles at the
tip, to a series of larger, more spherical, adherent vacuoles interconnected
with fine tubes. The lumen of the vacuole can be labelled with fluorescent
dyes, providing a non-specific marker for any movement of the lumenal
contents.

## Vacuolar Transport Modelling

Using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), researchers have
measured the vacuolar diffusion coefficient in vivo. Values compared
favourably with theoretical and experimental values for fluorescein in pure
water, suggesting that fluorescent probes were freely diffusible in a largely
aqueous vacuole. With a known value of the diffusion coefficient, it was
possible to estimate the functional tube diameter in vivo between two vacuoles
of defined size and separation.

To estimate the transport characteristics of an entire septal compartment,
diffusion coefficients and median tube diameters were combined with measured
distributions of vacuole length, width, and separation to construct an in
silico vacuole system. Diffusion was found to be a sufficient mechanism to
explain observed transport in the various regions of the vacuolar organelle.

## Scale-Dependent Transport Phenomena

Transport operates across multiple scales within the mycelial network:

### Micrometre to Millimetre Scale
At this scale, individual hyphae take up nutrients through membrane
transporters. The vacuolar system within individual hyphae provides a pathway
for longitudinal translocation. An unbranched hypha possessing a continuous
tubular vacuole system could sustain growth over a transport distance of
around 12 to 24 mm, whereas diffusion alone in a maximally branched system
would operate over only a few millimetres.

### Millimetre to Centimetre Scale
Radiolabelled compounds have been used to track movement through mycelial
networks. A novel non-invasive technique using photon-counting scintillation
imaging (PCSI) was developed to track movement of 14C-labelled nitrogen
compounds in foraging mycelial networks in contact with an inert scintillation
screen.

### Colony-Scale Dynamics
Time series analysis of radiolabel distribution patterns has revealed that
colony development occurs in two phases: an initial phase of symmetrical
growth, followed by a transition to sparser, more asymmetric growth. Added
cellulosic resources induced marked nitrogen accumulation and asymmetric
growth tightly focused on the new resource.

## Pulsatile Transport

A strong pulsatile component has been found to be associated with rapid
nutrient transport, particularly through corded systems. Analysis using
Fourier techniques from discrete regions of interest revealed that the
assimilatory mycelium on the inoculum and the foraging mycelium both pulsed,
but were out of phase with each other. This pulsatile behaviour suggests a
mechanism for coordinated resource distribution across the network.

## Ecological Implications

Mycelial network dynamics have profound implications for ecosystem
functioning:

- **Resource sharing**: Networks can connect multiple resource patches,
  enabling fungi to buffer against local resource depletion
- **Carbon cycling**: Through translocation, fungi redistribute carbon
  within soil systems, affecting [[gadd-fungal-carbon-sequestration]]
- **Plant-fungal interactions**: [[mycorrhizal-networks]] connect individual
  plants, potentially facilitating nutrient transfer between them
- **Competition**: Network architecture influences competitive outcomes
  between different fungal species occupying the same substrate
- **Decomposition**: The ability to reallocate nutrients influences rates
  and patterns of [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs]]

## Methodological Advances

The study of mycelial network dynamics has benefited enormously from modern
imaging techniques. Confocal laser scanning microscopy combined with
fluorescent probes allows visualization of intracellular transport. Photon-
counting scintillation imaging provides real-time, non-invasive tracking of
radiolabelled nutrients. Automated image analysis using contrast-limited
adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) enables quantitative measurement of
colony area from scintillation images.
