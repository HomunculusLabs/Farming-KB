Village energy systems represent a [[permaculture]] approach to meeting community energy needs
through small-scale, decentralised, and environmentally responsible technologies. Rather than
relying on large centralised power infrastructure, village-scale systems emphasise local
resource use, community ownership, and long-term ecological health. The design of such systems
requires careful consideration of conversion efficiency, social acceptability, economic
viability, and [[fertilizer-pollution-environmental-impact]].

**Criteria for Energy Technology Selection** — Permaculture establishes a clear set of
criteria that any energy technology must satisfy before it can be considered appropriate for
village-scale adoption. First, the technology must be non-polluting, meaning it must not
degrade air, water, or soil quality during operation or at end of life. Second, it must be
socially acceptable and locally benign, fitting within the cultural and practical context of
the community it serves without disrupting established social structures or ways of living.

Third, the technology must be affordable, with amortisation periods under ten years so that
communities are not burdened by long-term debt for basic energy needs. Fourth, the technology
should ideally be locally produced and maintained, reducing dependence on distant supply chains
and external technical expertise that may become unavailable or prohibitively expensive.

A foundational principle underpinning these criteria is the "economics of extinction" — any
technology whose widespread adoption destroys the soil, water, or air quality upon which all
life depends must be firmly rejected, regardless of its apparent efficiency or convenience.
This principle recognises that short-term energy gains achieved at the expense of fundamental
ecological capital represent a catastrophic trade-off that undermines human survival and
community well-being.

**Low Efficiency Systems (2–15%)** — Several commonly used energy technologies operate at
surprisingly low conversion efficiencies, yet remain in widespread use due to accessibility
and simplicity. Amorphous silica solar cells convert only 2–5% of incoming [[albedo-and-solar-radiation]]
into electricity, making them among the least efficient photovoltaic options available, though
their low cost and flexibility have historically driven their adoption in certain applications.

Burning cow dung as direct fuel achieves approximately 7% efficiency, a practice widespread in
rural areas of many developing nations yet extremely wasteful of both energy and organic
material that could otherwise return to soils as valuable fertiliser and soil conditioner. The
loss of nutrients from agricultural cycles represents a compounding cost beyond the immediate
energy inefficiency of this practice.

Crystalline photovoltaic panels perform considerably better at 13.5%, though this still
represents a modest conversion rate relative to the solar energy incident upon them. Open
fires, the most ancient and universally accessible energy technology, operate at roughly 15%
efficiency, with the vast majority of heat energy lost to the surrounding environment rather
than directed toward useful work such as cooking or space heating.

**Moderate Efficiency Systems (18–40%)** — A broad middle range of technologies achieves
moderate conversion efficiencies representing practical improvements over the lowest-tier
systems. Solar ponds, which capture and store thermal energy in salt-gradient bodies of water,
reach around 15% efficiency while offering the advantage of built-in [[coleman-soil-heat-mass-passive-thermal-storage]]. Humphrey
pulse jet engines operate in the 10–20% range, finding niche applications where simplicity of
mechanical construction outweighs their modest thermal performance.

Undershot water wheels — historically significant and still used in appropriate settings where
low head and moderate flow exist — achieve 18–20% efficiency. More advanced photovoltaic
cells reach 25–29%, representing the practical upper bound of commercially available solar
electric conversion under standard conditions. Stirling engines, which operate on closed-cycle
external combustion using temperature differentials, typically achieve 20% efficiency but can
theoretically reach higher values with optimised designs.

Solar flat plate collectors show a wide efficiency range of 10–80% depending on design
quality, temperature differential, and application, with well-engineered systems performing
remarkably well for water and space heating purposes. Improved cookstoves reach approximately
30%, a significant improvement over open fires that also reduces indoor air pollution and
associated respiratory health problems. Gas turbines achieve around 30% efficiency in typical
installations, making them more suited to larger scale applications than individual village
use. Diesel engines convert 30–40% of fuel energy to mechanical work, straddling the boundary
between moderate and higher efficiency categories.

**Higher Efficiency Systems (60–93%)** — The most efficient energy conversion technologies
available at village scale occupy the upper performance range. [[biogas-digesters-and-anaerobic-digestion-systems]], which
decompose organic waste anaerobically to produce methane, achieve approximately 60% efficiency
and simultaneously provide fertiliser as a valuable by-product, closing nutrient loops within
agricultural systems.

Thermal mass heaters — masonry stoves and rocket mass heaters that store heat in dense
materials such as stone, cob, or brick for slow radiant release — reach 80–90% efficiency,
making them among the most effective heating technologies known for temperate and cold
climates. Their ability to provide steady warmth from a single, efficient burn cycle
distinguishes them from conventional heating approaches.

Pelton wheels, the impulse turbines used in high-head micro-hydro installations, similarly
achieve 80–90% efficiency and can provide reliable continuous electricity generation where
suitable water resources exist. Diesel co-generation systems, which capture waste heat from
electricity generation for space or water heating, push combined efficiency to 90–93% by
utilising energy that would otherwise be rejected to the environment. Wood gasification, which
converts solid biomass into a combustible producer gas through controlled partial combustion,
achieves 75–90% efficiency depending on system design and feedstock quality, allowing biomass
resources to fuel internal combustion engines for electricity generation or mechanical power
at village scale.

**Amplified Efficiency Systems** — Certain technologies appear to produce more energy than
they consume by exploiting ambient environmental energy rather than generating it from fuel.
Heat pumps achieve approximately 350% efficiency by moving existing thermal energy from air,
ground, or water sources into buildings for heating, effectively amplifying electrical input
by exploiting temperature differentials that exist naturally in the environment. Compressed
air heaters reach 500–600% effective efficiency through similar principles, concentrating and
converting ambient thermal energy into useful heat. These figures represent coefficient of
performance rather than true conversion efficiency, as the systems rely on external thermal
reservoirs, but they demonstrate the enormous potential of technologies that work with natural
energy flows rather than against them.

**Diverse Energy Approach** — Permaculture advocates for a diverse portfolio of energy sources
matched to local site conditions rather than dependence on any single technology or fuel
source. Wind energy is appropriate where consistent wind patterns exist and can be harnessed
without significant ecological disruption. Tidal and wave energy can serve coastal communities
with suitable geography and tidal ranges.

River and stream power through micro-hydro installations provides reliable baseline energy
where topography and water flow permit, often serving as the most dependable renewable source
available to village communities. Solar energy, both thermal and electric, serves as a
universal resource across most inhabited regions, with different collection technologies
suited to different climatic conditions and energy end-uses. Methane production from organic
wastes through [[anaerobic-digestion-biogas-production]] provides both energy and waste management benefits, turning
a disposal problem into a resource. The key principle is that each technology should be
deployed where it is naturally advantaged by local conditions, creating resilient and redundant
energy systems that do not depend on distant fuel supplies or centralised infrastructure.

**Rejection of Centralised Polluting Systems** — A central tenet of the permaculture energy
philosophy is firm opposition to giant coal, nuclear, and oil-powered energy systems. These
centralised technologies concentrate power, wealth, and environmental risk while disconnecting
communities from understanding and managing their own energy needs.

Fission-based nuclear power is specifically excluded due to its radioactive by-products, which
create waste management problems persisting for thousands of years and pose catastrophic risk
through accident or proliferation. Coal, as currently extracted and burned, is rejected for its
massive contributions to air pollution, atmospheric carbon loading, acid rain, and landscape
destruction through both mining and combustion. Mineral petroleum is excluded beyond limited
initial and transition use, recognising that fossil fuel dependency is inherently unsustainable
and that continued extraction undermines the transition to genuinely renewable energy systems.
The permaculture preference is firmly for renewable, decentralised systems that communities
can understand, control, and maintain without external dependence.

**Mechanical Efficiency vs. Holistic Assessment** — While [[energy-conversion-efficiency]] is a
useful metric for comparing technologies in purely thermodynamic terms, [[permaculture-design]]
philosophy holds that mechanical efficiency is actually the least important concept in energy
system selection. A technology with high conversion efficiency may still be entirely
inappropriate if it is polluting, socially disruptive, unaffordable, or dependent on supply
chains beyond community control. What matters far more is whether the energy process is
appropriate to its context — whether it is non-polluting, affordable within the community's
economic means, locally maintainable, and supportive of rather than destructive to the broader
ecological systems upon which the community depends.
