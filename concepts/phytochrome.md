# Phytochrome

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein found in plants that detects red
and far-red light. Named from the Greek "phyton" (plant) and "chroma" (color),
it functions as a biological light-activated switch that governs numerous
developmental processes including seed germination, shade avoidance, flowering
time, and stem elongation. The discovery of phytochrome by Warren L. Butler
and colleagues in the early 1950s was a landmark in plant biology, providing
the first molecular explanation for how plants measure the duration of darkness
and regulate their life cycles accordingly.

## The Red and Far-Red Switch

The defining characteristic of phytochrome is its reversible response to red
light (approximately 660 nm) and far-red light (approximately 730 nm). This was
first demonstrated through experiments with photoperiodism in plants like iris
and chrysanthemum.

When a long-night plant such as iris, which normally does not flower during
long nights, receives a brief pulse of red light in the middle of the night,
it initiates flowering as if it had experienced a short night. However, if
far-red light is applied immediately after the red pulse, the effect is
completely cancelled and the plant does not flower.

The process is fully reversible:

- Red light triggers flowering
- Far-red light cancels it
- Red light triggers it again
- Far-red cancels again, and so on

Only a few seconds of illumination with either color are sufficient to toggle
the switch. On a deeper level, this means that the plant "remembers" the last
color of light it perceived. Ecologically, this makes intuitive sense: the
last light a plant perceives at the end of the day is far-red (present in the
long-wavelength-rich light of dusk), which signals the plant to "turn off."

In the morning, red light predominates, and the plant "wakes up." In this way,
a plant measures how long ago it last saw red light and adjusts its growth
accordingly.

## Discovery Through Photoperiodism

The discovery of phytochrome emerged from studies of photoperiodism, the
phenomenon first described by Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard in 1918
at the US Department of Agriculture. They studied the Maryland Mammoth tobacco
strain, a mutant tobacco plant discovered in 1906 in the valleys of southern
Maryland.

This remarkable plant could reach four and a half metres in height, produce
almost a hundred leaves, and would only stop growing when the frosts set in.
On the surface this seemed a boon to tobacco farmers, but like the two-faced
Roman god Janus, it rarely flowered, meaning farmers could not harvest seed for
the next year's crop.

Garner and Allard planted Maryland Mammoth in pots and left one group outside
in the fields. The other group was put in the field during the day but moved
to a dark shed every afternoon. Simply limiting the amount of light the plants
saw was enough to cause them to stop growing and start flowering.

This phenomenon, called photoperiodism, gave the first strong evidence that
plants measure how much light they take in. Plants are classified as short-day
plants (flower when nights exceed a critical length, e.g., chrysanthemums,
soybeans) or long-day plants (flower when nights are shorter than a critical
length, e.g., irises, barley).

Around the time of World War II, scientists discovered that they could
manipulate flowering by briefly turning lights on and off in the middle of the
night. These experiments proved that what a plant measures is not the length of
the day but the length of the continuous period of darkness.

Scientists then investigated which color of light was effective. The plants
only responded to a flash of red light during the night. Blue or green flashes
had no influence on flowering, but a few seconds of red light was sufficient.
Plants were using blue light to determine which direction to bend in (via
phototropism) and red light to measure the length of the night (via
phytochrome).

## Molecular Mechanism

Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr** (red-absorbing): the inactive form
- **Pfr** (far-red-absorbing): the biologically active form

Pr absorbs red light and converts to Pfr. Pfr absorbs far-red light and
converts back to Pr. The Pfr form triggers downstream gene expression changes.
Pfr is also thermally unstable and slowly reverts to Pr in darkness, providing
the plant with a built-in timer that measures the duration of uninterrupted
darkness.

The phytochrome protein consists of a covalently bound chromophore called
phytochromobilin, an open-chain tetrapyrrole related to the heme group in
hemoglobin and the bilin pigments of algae. Light absorption causes a
conformational change in the chromophore, which propagates through the protein,
leading to translocation of the Pfr form to the nucleus where it interacts
with transcription factors including PIFs (phytochrome-interacting factors).

These interactions alter the expression of hundreds of genes, coordinating the
plant's developmental program with the ambient light environment.

## Leaf Perception and Florigen

Unlike phototropism, where the "eye" of the plant resides in the shoot tip,
phytochrome-mediated photoperiodic responses are perceived primarily in the
leaves. If a single leaf of a plant is illuminated with red light during the
middle of the night, the entire plant responds as if fully illuminated.

Conversely, if all leaves are removed, leaving only the stem and apex, the
plant is blind to photoperiodic light cues. Phytochrome in the leaves generates
a mobile signal that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex and induces
flowering. This mobile signal, long hypothesized and sought by plant biologists,
was identified in 2007 as the protein FT (FLOWERING LOCUS T), sometimes called
florigen.

## Agricultural Applications

Understanding phytochrome has had major agricultural significance. Chrysanthemum
farmers use supplemental lighting to extend the photoperiod through autumn and
winter, preventing flowering until just before market dates like Mother's Day.
Two weeks before the target date, they discontinue the nighttime lighting, and
the plants flower simultaneously, ready for harvest and shipping.

Controlled-environment agriculture manipulates red-to-far-red ratios to
regulate plant architecture, flowering, and yield in greenhouse production.
The phytochrome system is also relevant to indoor cultivation of fungi and
plants, where the spectral composition of artificial lighting can influence
developmental timing and morphology.

## Phytochrome Gene Families

Modern research has revealed that phytochrome is not a single protein but a
small gene family. In Arabidopsis thaliana, five phytochrome genes (phyA
through phyE) have been identified, each with distinct but overlapping roles:

- **phyA**: primary photoreceptor for germination and de-etiolation under
  continuous far-red light
- **phyB**: dominant receptor for shade avoidance and flowering responses
- **phyC, phyD, phyE**: more specialized roles in specific light conditions

This diversity allows plants to mount nuanced responses to complex light
environments including canopy shade, where the red-to-far-red ratio drops
significantly because chlorophyll in overlying leaves absorbs red light but
transmits far-red.

## Ecological Significance

The phytochrome system is ecologically critical because it allows plants to
detect and respond to competition from neighboring vegetation. When a plant is
shaded by other plants, the ratio of red to far-red light decreases because
the canopy above absorbs most red light while transmitting far-red.

Phytochrome detects this shift and triggers shade avoidance responses: stem
elongation accelerates, leaf angle changes to capture more light, and flowering
may be accelerated. This ability to "see" neighboring plants through spectral
quality changes is a sophisticated form of environmental awareness that
operates continuously throughout a plant's life.
