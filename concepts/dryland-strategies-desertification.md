## [[dryland-salting-strategies|Salting]] of Waters and Soils**: Poor irrigation practices and the clearing of deep-rooted vegetation cause water tables to rise, bringing dissolved salts to the surface. Evaporation concentrates these salts, rendering soil toxic to most plant life. This salinization is one of the most insidious forms of land degradation.

Together, these forces convert productive drylands into expanding deserts. The process is often slow enough to escape notice until the damage is severe, yet fast enough to affect human generations. Satellite data confirms that desert margins worldwide are advancing, driven by the compounding effects of deforestation, overgrazing, and inappropriate agricultural practices. Once a threshold of degradation is crossed, natural recovery becomes extremely difficult without active intervention.

## Desert Types

Not all deserts are the same. Three major categories are recognized:

- **Hot Deserts**: Classic arid landscapes with high daytime temperatures, minimal cloud cover, and extreme diurnal temperature variation. Examples include the Sahara, Arabian, and Australian deserts. Surface temperatures can exceed 70°C while nighttime temperatures may drop below 10°C.
- **Montane Hot-Cold Deserts**: High-altitude arid regions that combine intense solar radiation with cold nights and seasonal frosts. The Tibetan Plateau and parts of the Andes fall into this category. Vegetation here must cope with both drought stress and freezing conditions.
- **Polar Cold Deserts**: Ice-free regions of Antarctica and the high Arctic where precipitation is minimal despite low temperatures. These deserts lack liquid water availability rather than warmth, presenting unique biological constraints.

Each type demands distinct strategies for design and rehabilitation. A technique effective in a hot desert may fail entirely in a montane or polar desert due to the interaction of aridity with temperature extremes.

## Rainfall Classifications

Drylands are classified by annual rainfall into four categories:

| Classification | Annual Rainfall |
|---|---|
| Hyperarid | 0–2 cm |
| Extremely Dry | 2–5 cm |
| Arid | 5–15 cm |
| Semi-arid | 15–40 cm |

These are not rigid boundaries but zones of transition. Even within a single landscape, rainfall can be extremely patchy — a phenomenon known as **mosaic rainfall patterns**, where one valley receives runoff while an adjacent one remains dry. This unpredictability is a defining feature of dryland systems and must be central to any design approach. A single thunderstorm may deliver a year's worth of rain to a few hectares while leaving surrounding areas untouched.

## Key Ecological Features of Drylands

### Cryptogamic Crust

One of the most critical yet overlooked features of desert soils is the **cryptogamic crust** — a living surface layer composed of fungi, algae, and lichens. This biological crust:

- Stabilizes soil against wind and water erosion
- Fixes atmospheric nitrogen, making it available to plants
- Captures and retains moisture from dew and light rain
- Provides a seedbed for higher plants

Disturbing this crust through vehicle traffic, overgrazing, or plowing can set back soil recovery by decades. Protecting cryptogamic crust is one of the first priorities in any dryland restoration effort. In some deserts, the crust may be the only living thing covering the soil surface, and its destruction can trigger rapid erosion.

### Endorheic Drainage

Many dryland regions feature **endorheic drainage** — closed basin systems where water flows inward toward salt pans or ephemeral lakes rather than reaching the ocean. This means salts accumulate over geological time, making these basins inherently prone to salinization. Design strategies must account for this closed hydrology rather than assuming water will flush salts away.

### Episodic Recruitment

Plant reproduction in deserts is not a steady process. Many species rely on **episodic recruitment**, producing seedlings only when rare favorable conditions align — sometimes intervals of 7 to 20 years between successful germination events. This makes desert ecosystems extremely vulnerable to disturbance: a single bad grazing season can eliminate decades of accumulated reproductive potential. Management must therefore protect not just mature plants but the entire seed bank and the conditions for future recruitment events.

## Plant Strategies for Dryland Survival

Plants in arid environments employ two broad categories of strategy:

### Drought-Evading Species

These plants complete their life cycle rapidly during brief periods of moisture availability. Annual ephemerals germinate, flower, and set seed within weeks of a rain event, then persist as dormant seeds in the soil for years. They are the desert's opportunists, banking on the statistical probability that some rain events will be sufficient.

### Drought-Adapted (Drought-Resistant) Species

Perennial plants survive extended dry periods through a range of physical and physiological adaptations:

- **Wax Coatings**: Thick cuticular wax layers on leaves reduce cuticular water loss and reflect excess solar radiation.
- **Reduced Transpiration**: Small leaf surface area, sunken stomata, leaf hairs (pubescence), and vertical leaf orientation all minimize water loss through transpiration.
- **Water Storage Organs**: Succulent stems, leaves, or roots (caudiciform plants) store water internally for use during dry periods. Cacti, baobabs, and bottle trees are classic examples.
- **Deciduous Habits**: Many dryland trees and shrubs shed leaves during drought, entering dormancy to eliminate transpiration entirely until rains return. This is distinct from temperate deciduousness, which is triggered by cold rather than drought.

A robust dryland design incorporates species from both categories to ensure that some vegetation is active under any moisture regime.

## Animal Survival Strategies

Desert animals have evolved a complementary set of strategies:

- **Aestivation**: A summer dormancy analogous to hibernation, where animals enter a state of reduced metabolic activity to survive extreme heat and aridity. Snails, frogs, and some reptiles are well-known aestivators.
- **Migration**: Many species move seasonally across vast distances to track rainfall and available forage. Nomadic birds and ungulates follow green flushes across the landscape.
- **Nocturnal Browsing**: Activity shifts to nighttime to avoid daytime heat stress and reduce water loss. Many desert rodents, foxes, and herbivores are exclusively nocturnal.
- **Burrowing**: Many species escape surface extremes by retreating underground. Remarkably, **termites in arid regions have been documented tunneling to depths of 40 meters**, accessing deep moisture and creating extensive subterranean ecosystems that improve soil structure and water infiltration. These termite mounds become focal points of fertility and moisture in otherwise impoverished landscapes.

## Species Diversity in Deserts

Contrary to popular perception, deserts harbor significant biodiversity:

- The **Sonoran and Great Basin deserts** support approximately **450 edible plant species**, many of them highly nutritious and drought-resistant. Indigenous peoples of these regions developed sophisticated knowledge of this flora over millennia.
- The **West Australian wheat-belt** alone contains **936 species of woody plants**, of which approximately **45% are at risk** from land clearing and agricultural expansion.

This diversity represents an irreplaceable genetic resource for dryland agriculture and restoration. The loss of even a fraction of these species narrows the options available for re-humidification and sustainable land management. Each species lost is a potential tool for landscape rehabilitation that can never be recovered.

## Water Resources in Drylands

### Exotic Rivers

Some deserts are crossed by **exotic rivers** — rivers that originate in humid regions and flow through arid lands. The Nile, Colorado, and Indus are examples. These rivers are ecological lifelines but are also heavily exploited, often failing to reach their termini due to upstream extraction.

### Oases

Oases form where groundwater reaches the surface, typically along fault lines or at the base of mountains where aquifers are recharged. They are critical nodes of biodiversity and human settlement, and their protection is essential for maintaining ecological corridors through arid landscapes.

### Aquifers

Deep fossil aquifers store water accumulated over millennia. While they represent a massive resource, most are being drawn down far faster than they can recharge, making them a non-renewable resource on human timescales. Sustainable dryland design must treat aquifer water as a precious capital to be invested in permanent landscape improvement rather than consumed for annual crops.

## Salt Accumulation and the Role of Trees

A critical insight for dryland design is the relationship between tree cover and salt dynamics:

- **Bare soil** under intense solar radiation undergoes high rates of **evaporation**, which draws moisture upward from the water table. As water evaporates at the surface, dissolved salts are left behind and concentrated in the root zone.
- **Tree-covered land** intercepts solar radiation with canopy shade, reducing surface soil temperatures. Trees use water through **transpiration** rather than allowing it to evaporate at the soil surface. Transpiration pulls water through the soil profile in a controlled manner and does not concentrate salts at the surface to the same degree.

This distinction is fundamental: replacing bare, evaporating surfaces with transpiring tree cover is one of the primary mechanisms for preventing and even reversing soil salinization. It also explains why clearing trees in drylands frequently triggers salinity problems that did not previously exist.

## The Re-Humidification Strategy

The overarching permaculture strategy for drylands is **re-humidification** — the systematic restoration of the local water cycle through tree planting and vegetation establishment. The logic is as follows:

1. **Plant pioneer and nitrogen-fixing trees** adapted to arid conditions to establish initial canopy cover and shade.
2. **Reduce bare soil evaporation** by increasing ground cover, mulching, and establishing windbreaks.
3. **Trees transpire moisture** into the atmosphere, contributing to local humidity and potentially influencing regional rainfall patterns.
4. **Root systems break hardpan**, improve infiltration, and cycle deep nutrients to the surface.
5. **Leaf litter and organic matter accumulation** improve soil water-holding capacity and feed [[soil-food-web]].
6. **As the system matures**, microclimates improve, more species can establish, and the landscape progressively shifts from desert toward productive dryland forest or savanna.

This is not a rapid process, but it is a proven one. Every tree planted in a dryland is an investment in long-term landscape rehabilitation. The key is to start with the toughest, most adapted species and allow succession to proceed naturally once initial conditions are established.

## Design Principles for Drylands

- Protect existing cryptogamic crusts and remnant vegetation at all costs
- Design for episodic rainfall — capture and infiltrate water when it comes
- Use swales, contour banks, and gabions to slow runoff and allow infiltration
- Select species from multiple drought strategy categories for resilience
- Plan for 7-20 year recruitment cycles — maintain seed banks and protect young plants
- Address salinity through tree planting, not through drainage alone
- Work with endorheic hydrology rather than against it
- Use exotic river water to establish permanent tree systems, not annual crops
