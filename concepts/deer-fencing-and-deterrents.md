# Deer Fencing and Deterrents

Deer are among the most persistent challenges for homesteaders. A single deer can devastate a garden overnight, and once they establish a feeding pattern on your property, breaking that habit requires a multi-layered approach. This page covers fencing options, repellents, deer-resistant plant strategies, and seasonal pressure management.

## Understanding Deer Pressure

Deer pressure varies by season, region, and food availability. In spring and early summer, deer browse on tender new growth. In late summer and fall, they consume larger quantities to build fat reserves. Winter is the highest pressure period when natural forage is scarce.

Key factors influencing local deer pressure:
- Proximity to wooded cover and travel corridors
- Severity of the previous winter
- Local predator population (coyotes, wolves, mountain lions)
- Agricultural development reducing natural habitat
- Hunting season pressure pushing deer into safe zones

## Physical Fencing Options

### 8-Foot Woven Wire Fence

The gold standard for deer exclusion. Deer can jump 8 feet from a standstill, but a properly constructed 8-foot fence with no horizontal footholds prevents this.

**Materials:**
- 8-foot metal T-posts or wooden posts every 8-10 feet
- 96-inch woven wire fencing (2x4 inch mesh)
- 12.5-gauge wire minimum
- Corner bracing and tension wire at top and bottom

**Installation tips:**
- Bury bottom wire 6 inches or secure with a ground stake to prevent deer from pushing under
- Add a single strand of electric wire at the top for extra deterrence
- Angle the top 12 inches outward if jumping is a persistent issue
- Use drive caps on T-posts for safety

**Cost:** $2-4 per linear foot for materials

### Electric Fence

More affordable than woven wire and effective when maintained properly. Works best when deer have an established food source elsewhere — if they are desperate, they will push through.

**Types:**
- Three-wire sloped fence (wires at 10, 20, and 30 inches, sloped outward)
- Five-wire vertical fence (wires at 6, 12, 18, 24, and 42 inches)
- Temporary poly-tape or poly-wire with step-in posts

**Installation tips:**
- Vegetation must be cleared from wire lines to prevent grounding
- Bait the fence with peanut butter on aluminum foil strips to train deer
- Use a charger rated for at least 1 mile of fence
- Check voltage regularly — minimum 3,000 volts at the furthest point

**Cost:** $0.50-1.50 per linear foot

### Double Fence (Penn State Design)

Two parallel fences spaced 3-5 feet apart, each 4-5 feet tall. Deer cannot jump both fences simultaneously and will not attempt the wide gap.

**Pros:**
- Less visually intrusive than an 8-foot fence
- Easier to step over for humans
- Very effective based on research

**Cons:**
- Requires double the footprint width
- Double the post and wire costs

## Repellent Strategies

### Contact Repellents

Applied directly to plants, making them taste bitter or irritating:
- Eggs-based sprays (rotten eggs + water + soap)
- Capsaicin sprays (hot pepper wax)
- Bitter-tasting commercial products (Bitrex-based)
- Must be reapplied after rain and new growth

### Area Repellents

Create an odor barrier around the garden perimeter:
- Predator urine (coyote, wolf)
- Soap bars hung from stakes (Irish Spring)
- Human hair in mesh bags
- Milorganite (processed sewage) as a fertilizer and repellent
- Garlic and putrescent egg solids

### Effectiveness Notes

No repellent works 100% of the time. Deer in high-pressure areas become accustomed to repellents. The most effective approach is rotation — alternate between different repellent types every 2-3 weeks so deer never adapt.

## Deer-Resistant Plants

No plant is completely deer-proof, but many are strongly avoided. Incorporating these into your landscape reduces overall browsing pressure:

**Highly resistant (rarely browsed):**
- Lavender, rosemary, thyme, sage
- Daffodils, foxglove, bleeding heart
- Yarrow, catmint, Russian sage
- Ornamental grasses (Miscanthus, [[pennisetum]])
- Ferns, hellebores, lungwort

**Moderately resistant (occasionally browsed when food is scarce):**
- Peonies, iris, black-eyed Susan
- Coneflower, bee balm, columbine
- Asparagus, rhubarb, garlic, onion

**Frequently browsed (avoid near fence lines):**
- Hostas, daylilies, tulips
- Apple trees, fruit trees (young)
- Beans, lettuce, strawberries, sweet corn

## Seasonal Pressure Management

**Spring (March-May):**
- Inspect and repair winter fence damage
- Apply repellents to emerging shoots
- Install temporary electric fences around new garden beds

**Summer (June-August):**
- Maintain electric fence voltage
- Rotate repellent types monthly
- Monitor fruit trees as fruit ripens

**Fall (September-November):**
- Peak pressure — reinforce all fencing
- Harvest crops promptly
- Apply heavy repellent applications around orchard areas

**Winter (December-February):**
- Heavy browsing on evergreens and woody plants
- Wrap young tree trunks with hardware cloth
- Stack brush piles near fence lines to provide alternative forage
- Consider temporary supplemental feeding (where legal) to divert pressure

## Integration with Homestead Design

- Place high-value crops (vegetables, orchard) in the most protected zone
- Use deer-resistant plants as a buffer zone around the garden perimeter
- Combine fencing with [[homestead-permaculture-design]] zone planning — intensive zones get the best fencing
- [[cover-crop-quick-reference]] with deer-resistant species like winter rye or crimson clover
- Integrate livestock guardian dogs into your [[goat-husbandry]] plan for both deer and predator deterrence
