# Phytoremediation with Mycorrhizal-Assisted Plant Uptake

Phytoremediation employs plants to remove, stabilize, or degrade contaminants
from soil and water. When combined with mycorrhizal fungal symbionts, the
efficacy of phytoremediation is substantially enhanced through expanded root
absorption surfaces, improved plant nutrition, and direct fungal involvement in
detoxification pathways.

## Mycorrhizal Mechanisms in Phytoremediation

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF) and ectomycorrhizal fungi extend the
effective rooting zone through extensive extraradical mycelial networks. This
hyphal exploration greatly increases the soil volume from which plants can
absorb both nutrients and contaminants. The fungal mantle and Hartig net
(ectomycorrhiza) or arbuscules (AMF) serve as selective interfaces regulating
element transfer between soil and plant.

AMF contribute to [[singh-mycorrhizal-fungi-in-heavy-metal-phytostabilization]]
through several mechanisms: (1) hyphal absorption and translocation of metals
from bulk soil beyond the root depletion zone, (2) intracellular compartmental
ization within fungal vacuoles, (3) binding to cell wall chitin and
glucuronic acid residues, and (4) extracellular precipitation as polyphosphate
granules or oxalate crystals.

## Phytoextraction Enhancement

Phytoextraction relies on hyperaccumulator plants capable of concentrating
metals in aboveground tissues at levels far exceeding normal physiological
ranges. Mycorrhizal colonization can enhance phytoextraction yield by improving
plant biomass production and, in some cases, increasing metal translocation
to shoots. Key hyperaccumulator genera include Alyssum (Ni), Thlaspi (Zn, Cd),
Pteris (As), and Brassica (various metals).

The extent of mycorrhizal benefit depends on fungal and plant species
compatibility, soil metal concentration, and bioavailability. At low to
moderate contamination levels, AMF generally promote metal uptake alongside
enhanced phosphorus nutrition. At extremely high metal concentrations,
mycorrhizal colonization may decline, reducing the symbiotic benefit.

Mycorrhizal-assisted phytoextraction represents a form of singh plant fungus  co evolution in [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] applied to contaminated land
management. The fungal partner alters rhizosphere chemistry through organic
acid exudation, lowering soil pH locally and increasing metal solubility for
plant uptake.

## [[phytostabilization]] Mechanisms

Phytostabilization immobilizes contaminants in place, reducing their
bioavailability and preventing migration to groundwater or entry into food
chains. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] contribute through hyphal binding of soil particles,
glomalin production (see [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]]),
and direct metal sequestration within fungal tissues.

Ectomycorrhizal fungi are particularly effective in phytostabilization of
metal-contaminated mine tailings. Species such as [[pisolithus-tinctorius]],
[[suillus-luteus]], and [[paxillus-involutus]] tolerate high metal concentrations and
promote revegetation of otherwise barren substrates. The fungal mantle acts as
a barrier, reducing metal translocation to host plant shoots while
stabilizing metals in the rhizosphere.

## Metal Speciation and Bioavailability

Mycorrhizal fungi alter metal speciation in the rhizosphere through pH
modification, organic acid exudation, and redox changes. Low molecular weight
organic acids (citrate, oxalate, malate) exuded by extraradical hyphae
solubilize metal-bearing minerals, increasing the bioavailable fraction for
plant uptake during phytoextraction. Conversely, during phytostabilization,
fungal-induced precipitation as metal-phosphate or metal-oxalate minerals
reduces bioavailability.

The dual role of AMF in simultaneously enhancing and reducing metal
bioavailability creates apparent paradoxes. The outcome depends on the target
metal, fungal species, soil chemistry, and plant-microbe interaction context.
Understanding and manipulating these processes is key to designing effective
mycorrhizal-assisted phytoremediation strategies.

## Rhizosphere Interactions

The mycorrhizosphere hosts diverse microbial communities that collectively
contribute to contaminant transformation. Mycorrhizal exudates including
organic acids, amino acids, and sugars fuel rhizobacterial growth, some of
which possess metal-resistant or organic-degrading capabilities. This
three-way interaction among plant, fungus, and bacteria amplifies
phytoremediation outcomes.

## Limitations and Field Considerations

Field deployment of mycorrhizal-assisted phytoremediation faces challenges
including inoculum establishment in disturbed soils, competition with native
microflora, seasonal variability in colonization, and the slow pace of
phytoremediation relative to engineering solutions. Soil physicochemical
properties (pH, organic matter, texture, moisture) strongly influence both
mycorrhizal function and phytoremediation efficiency.

Combining phytoremediation with [[singh-composted-fungal-biomass-heavy-metal-immobilization]] as soil amendments can create synergistic remediation
strategies addressing both organic and inorganic contaminants simultaneously.
Harvest and disposal of metal-laden biomass from phytoextraction also requires
careful management to prevent contaminant redistribution.
