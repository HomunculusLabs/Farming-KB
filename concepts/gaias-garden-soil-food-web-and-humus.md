# Soil Food Web and Humus Formation

Toby Hemenway's detailed explanation of how soil organisms decompose organic matter, create humus, build soil structure, and make nutrients available to plants. From Gaia's Garden, Chapter 4: "Bringing the Soil to Life."

## The Decomposition Cascade

When a leaf falls to the ground, it enters a multi-stage decomposition process involving thousands of species of soil organisms.

### Stage 1: Primary Decomposers

Bacteria, fungi, algae, and other microbes begin the work. They first attack the easiest compounds:

1. **Sugars and starches** — converted quickly to energy, CO2, or more organisms
2. **Celluloses and some proteins** — harder to digest, require special enzymes; chains of tightly linked molecules decompose more slowly
3. **Lignins** (wood strength), **chitins** (insect armor), **waxes** — toughest polymers; only specialized fungi can break these down

### Stage 2: Secondary Decomposers

Mold mites, springtails, and certain beetles feed on the primary decomposers.

### Stage 3: Tertiary Decomposers

Centipedes, ground beetles, pseudoscorpions, predatory mites, ants, and spiders feed on secondary and primary decomposers. The hierarchy is not strictly linear — it is a set of nested cycles with extensive interconnections.

## How Humus Is Made

As the leaf is shredded, chewed, and chemically dissolved, some parts decompose faster than others. The resistant compounds — lignins, chitins, waxes — are worked over by microbes in a poorly understood process that converts them into **humus**.

### Properties of Humus

Humus is a stable, complex group of carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen molecules bonded in ways that resist further decomposition. Key characteristics:

- **Moisture holding:** Absorbs 4-6 times its weight in water
- **Soil structure:** Swells when wet (heaving soil upward), shrinks when dry (leaving air spaces) — acting like gentle tilling
- **Nutrient holding:** Surface bristles with negatively charged oxygen atoms that attract positively charged nutrients (K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, NH4+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Mn2+)
- **Accumulation:** Decomposes so slowly it builds up in healthy soil where fresh organic matter arrives as fast as old humus breaks down

### Warning Signs

If humus levels are dropping, it means the soil is in very bad shape — easily digested organic matter is gone, and soil organisms are "burning down the house to keep warm." Wise gardeners keep soil rich in humus.

## Soil Structure Building

Soil organisms build the physical structure of soil:

- **Bacteria** secrete gums, waxes, and gels that hold tiny earth particles together into mini-clumps
- **Fungal hyphae** surround soil crumbs and bind them together
- These mini-clumps give microbially rich soil its **good tilth** — the loose, crumbly structure gardeners strive for
- Microbial byproducts protect soil from drying and allow it to hold huge volumes of water
- Without soil life, earth dries up and blows away, or clumps into root-thwarting clods after rain

## Nutrient Cycling: Two Pathways

### Direct Pathway

Plant roots secrete very mild acids that break bonds holding nutrients onto humus. Nutrients wash into soil moisture, creating a rich broth. Plants absorb as much as they need. Evidence suggests plants stop acid secretion once they have supped enough, to avoid depleting humus.

### Indirect Pathway (Microbial Middlemen)

1. Roots release sugars and vitamins — ideal food for beneficial bacteria and fungi
2. Microbes thrive in huge numbers close to roots, in the moisture film surrounding them
3. Microbes produce acids and enzymes that release humus-bound nutrients
4. Microbes share these nutrients with the plants
5. Microbes excrete additional food for plants in their waste
6. Many fungi secrete antibiotics that protect plants from disease
