## Deduction from Nature

Deduction from nature is a [[masanobu-fukuoka]], whose remarkable journey to [[acacia]] provide sites for birds to roost, initiating soil changes that allow clumps or coppices of forest to form. Designers can use the same techniques with allied species of more direct use to pioneer food forests. Many indigenous peoples evolve scattered forest nuclei based on pioneer trees, termite mounds, and compost heaps. Designers can provide perches for birds to drop pioneer seeds, establishing plant nuclei in degraded lands around simple perches placed on disturbed sites.

#### Biological Pest Control

Nest holes attract owls to control rodents, purple martins to reduce mosquitoes, and woodpeckers to control codling moth. Nurse plants allow insect predators to overwinter, feed, or shelter within gardens, while small ponds support frogs and rock piles shelter lizards. Some natural pest controllers are remarkably effective — woodpeckers alone reduce codling moth by 40-60%.

### Landscape

Gullies, ridgetops, natural shade, the sides of multi-storey buildings, and exposed sunny sites all demonstrate different opportunities, just as various velocities and grades of streams or rockfalls present specific niches. Each special site can serve as an aid to food storage, food dehydration, an energy source, or a habitat for particular plants or animals. Designers also create opportunities over time as they grow groves of trees, raise earthbanks, build houses, or excavate caves.

Microclimate creation is where natural diversity and richness increase most rapidly. Every clump of trees invites new species, every shaded area provides refuge from heat, and every stone pile creates a moist, shaded soil site. These evolutions can be planned in advance, planting to take advantage of anticipated conditions using data derived from close observation of natural systems.

## Succession as a Design Tool

Nature imposes successions and limits on every species. Once the rules of succession are understood, they can be used to limit or exclude problem species:

- **Soft vines smother prickly shrubs**: Browsed or cut out, they allow trees to permanently shade out the shrub or rot its seeds in mulch
- **Barrier plants block invasive grasses**: Kikuyu grass is blocked by low hedges of comfrey, lemongrass, arrowroot (Canna spp.), or nasturtiums — species that can be deployed at tropical garden borders or around young fruit trees
- **Smothering rampants**: Rampant species like Lantana can be smothered by vines such as chayote (Sechium edulis) and succeeded with palm/legume forests
- **Protective zones for trees**: Where rampant grasses threaten trees, a protecting zone of "soft" barrier plants (comfrey, nasturtium, or locally observed species that "beat the grass") surrounds mulched gardens

## Design by Analogy

The overarching principle is design by analogy: selecting analogous or botanically-allied species for trials based on natural observations. If thistles grow around a rabbit warren, then disturbing soil, supplying urine and manure, and sowing seed may produce globe artichokes (a confirmed observation). Penning goats or sheep on a site, then excluding them and planting, inspired the concept of chicken or pig "tractors" to remove stubborn weeds like nut-grass, Convolvulus, onion-weed, and twitch before planting new successions of useful plants.

Fences or pits can trap wind-blown debris (dried leaves, rabbit and sheep manures, seagrasses) for garden use. Hundreds of such botanical lessons exist — looking long enough, the methodologies of nature become clear.

## Philosophy of Natural Design

Life is not merely survival in a stable ecosystem. By designing well and then observing system evolution, designers gain contemplative and celebratory time. In celebration, the myths and skills important to future generations can be incorporated. In contemplation, more refined, profound, or subtle insights into good procedures emerge.

Implementing and managing a constructed or natural system leads inevitably to a more revelationary lifestyle, greater satisfaction, and a sense of one's place in nature. To become a good designer is to search for understanding of nature and to be content with the search itself — designing by natural example, becoming aware, taking notes, sitting long in one place, watching wind and trees, feeling soil for moisture (always more moist on the shade side of tussock grasses), and becoming sensitive to the processes and sights about you.

The huge information store that is nature is itself a primary reason for its preservation. No equivalent education system can ever be afforded that operates without cost or bureaucratic involvement. In microcosm and macrocosm, the world offers the very best lessons to adopt.
