# Windbreak Design and Livestock Shelter

Windbreaks (also called shelterbelts) are one of the most cost-effective and multi-functional elements in [[permaculture-design]]. They provide shelter for livestock, increase crop yields, conserve soil moisture, prevent wind erosion, and can be designed to yield forage, timber, fuelwood, and mulch simultaneously.

## Effects on Livestock Production

The impact of wind on livestock is significant and measurable. In New Zealand, sheep raised in unsheltered fields weigh **15% less** than those in sheltered areas. Australia attributes **20% of all lamb losses** to wind chill factors, and the country issues regular wind chill warnings at shearing time to prevent adult sheep deaths.

Cattle fed winter rations on exposed sites consume **16% less food**, meaning winter hay and concentrates must be fed in shelter for animals to obtain full benefit. Both heat and cold similarly reduce weight gain. Shelterbelts are therefore one of the most effective strategies for increasing livestock production and conserving rations.

### Design Considerations for Livestock

When designing for livestock, several factors need sensible placement:

- **Fences** should be positioned to direct animals toward sheltered areas
- **Shelter access** must be available from all grazing paddocks
- **Feeding and watering points** should be placed in sheltered locations so animals are not exposed to extreme temperatures

In tropical and subtropical regions, a ridge planting of **pines or Casuarinas** with a wind gap left below the crowns provides both shade and an induced breeze that discourages flies and mosquitoes. These ridges also serve as rich mulch sources for lower slopes.

## Civil Construction Applications

Windbreaks serve important roles beyond agriculture:

- **Snowdrift control** on highways is more effectively and permanently blocked by hedgerows of hardy **Caragana** and **Eleagnus**, estimated to be **50% cheaper** than stout fences and outlasting them
- **Juniper** in high country grows better in areas of snow drift (below sharp ridges where snow forms cornices), and swales at such places enable more snow melt and root moisture for trees
- **Wind shear** on exposed highways or at caravan parks can cause casualties and property damage, requiring designed windfast median strips and highway shelterbelts, especially on mountain passes and near exposed coasts subject to gales

## Cropland and Orchard Shelter

For croplands, a matrix of shelterbelt species **10–16 m in height** spaced **33–66 m apart** (using Casuarina, poplar, Matsudana willow, or trimmed eucalypt) provides wind protection for crops like kiwifruit and avocado while giving the greatest yield increases and reducing wind damage to fruit and leaf.

### Measurable Yield Improvements

- **Citrus** culled as damaged: 50% in unsheltered areas vs. 18.5% in shelterbelt systems
- **Cotton** yields: 17.4% higher within 5× the height of the shelterbelt, falling to 7.9% advantage at 10× the height
- **Avocado**: up to 100% yield increase
- **Corn**: 45% increase
- **Alfalfa**: 60–70% increase
- **Wheat**: 30% increase
- **Lettuce and other low crops**: 7–18% gains

### Compound Benefits

Effects of shelterbelts are compound and include:

1. More meltwater from snow capture
2. Much greater fruit or seed set in bee-pollinated crops (bees fly more freely in shelter)
3. Preservation of good tree shape, hence less pruning required
4. Reduced mechanical damage to leaves and fruit

## Species Selection Criteria

Shelterbelt species should be selected based on multiple criteria that assist the farm enterprise:

- **Nitrogen fixation** or good mulch potential from leaves and trimmings
- **Hosting predatory insects or birds** that control crop pests
- **Minimal moisture competition** with the crop (roots can be ripped or trenched at the crop edge)
- **Excellent forage yields** or concentrated foods for livestock
- **Natural barriers** to livestock (thorny plants or woven hedge)

## Shelterbelt Succession

Windbreaks are planted as a succession from tall grass to a taller legume, eventually maturing into a long-term, tall, windfast hedge of species such as Casuarina, poplar, willow, eucalypt, oak, or chestnut. The entire complex can be established at once and managed as it evolves to maturity.

Quickset (by cuttings) hedges of **poplar** and **Erythrina** are popular for their fast windbreak effect, but species must be chosen to suit the particular climate.

## Windbreak Configurations

Where space is ample and winds strong, the windbreak profile can be carefully streamlined with up to six rows of trees and tall grass lines, yielding a mixed production of forage, timber, fuel, mulch, honey, and shelter. In constricted areas, a matrix of single-tree lines is effective if close-spaced.

There is no standard shape or windbreak — different configurations are needed for different sites, functions, enterprise types, wind strengths, and wind loads (salt, sand, dust).

## Soil Moisture Conservation

Windbreaks are very effective in snowy areas, increasing soil moisture 4% to four times the height of the break, to a depth of 1.2 m in soils. This moisture reserve is particularly valuable in cold dry climates.

In foggy climates or facing sea coasts, the effect of **sea air condensation** adds 80–300% of rainfall as leaf drip. In hot deserts, advected hot winds are the major factor in soil moisture loss, produced over large treeless areas of dry grain crop as well as in deserts.

Effects of windburn and seed shattering in hot winds are insignificant for up to **18 times the height** of the windbreak.

## Soil Erosion Prevention

Very serious soil losses of up to **100 tonnes/hectare/day** in duststorm episodes (usually followed by torrential rain) are prevented by windbreaks combined with soil pitting using tussock grasses. Approximately 50–70% of dusts settle out of the air 100 m into tree clumps.

On coasts, removal of mangroves and coastal dune vegetation causes sudden acceleration of wind erosion on beaches and coastal soils. Following deforestation, up to **30% more silt per annum** flows into and reduces the useful life of water storages.

## Accessory Functions

Beyond shelter, windbreak species can provide:

- **Crop mulch**: Prosopis, Acacia, Erythrina, Melia, Canna
- **Fodder**: All the above plus Leucaena, Fig, Pennisetum
- **Nitrogen and phosphorus fixation** or recycling
- **Trace element mining**: Casuarina, Banksia, [[eucalyptus-camaldulensis]]

Dry or cold-deciduous species provide natural leaf fall in crop, automatically adding growth elements. In every crop and orchard, interplanting leguminous trees for mulch, soil building, and in-crop windbreak or frost cover is advisable.

### Frost Protection Through Canopy

Trees like avocado and papaya can be grown on subtropical frosty sites if there is a high canopy of hardy palms or light-crowned legumes (e.g., Butia palm, Jacaranda, Tipuana tipu). Such sites do not frost because there is no bare-ground radiation at night, and advected frost is impeded.

## Area Allocation

Well-chosen and designed windbreaks can occupy up to **30% of the total area** of any site without reducing crop yields. If windbreak species are chosen to aid the crop itself, there will be an increase in total yield, soil quality, and available moisture.

Shelterbelt species must be carefully selected for multiple uses — to either **assist** the crop yield or **add to** the end-use yield (e.g., forage trees in pasture). This ensures the area occupied by shelterbelts adds to total yields rather than deducting from them.

Where severe sea or desert winds greatly reduce all yields, salt-resistant or sand-blast-resistant windbreak species must be selected regardless of the shelterbelt's intrinsic yields, as survival of the whole system depends on the frontline protection.
