# Fukuoka Natural Pest Balance

Masanobu Fukuoka's approach to pest and [[plant-disease-management-solomon]] in [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] creates the very conditions that make pest outbreaks inevitable, then responds with chemicals that further degrade the system.

## The Spider Phenomenon

Fukuoka described a remarkable event that illustrates the complexity of natural pest control. One autumn, after rice harvest, the rice stubble and low-lying grasses became "completely covered with spider webs, as though with silk. Waving and sparkling with the morning mist, it was a magnificent sight." Several species of spiders, millions of them, appeared in densities of "several in every square inch." After one or two days, strands of web broke off and the young spiders sailed away on the wind like dandelion fluff.

This phenomenon -- great swarms of spiders appearing in autumn and vanishing overnight -- is still not understood by scientists. "No one knows where they come from, how they survive the winter, or where they go when they disappear." Yet it is clearly part of an essential [[fukuoka-textdoc-animal-manure-human-waste-natural-cycle-heretic]] that chemicals destroy in an instant.

A Kochi Prefecture insect specialist came to investigate why rice leafhoppers were scarce in Fukuoka's unsprayed fields. He found not only that leafhoppers were as scarce as in heavily sprayed fields, but that their natural predators (spiders, frogs, toads) were far more numerous. The professor "acknowledged that if my method were generally adopted, the problem of crop devastation by leafhoppers could be solved" -- then got in his car and drove away. Despite this finding from a government research station, five or six years later there had been few changes in Kochi Prefecture's agricultural practices.

## The Dynamic Predator Web

Natural pest control shifts from year to year in ways no single research program can capture:

- **One year:** spiders are the primary leafhopper predators
- **Another year:** toads dominate
- **Another year:** frogs are most prevalent
- **Rain patterns** affect which predators thrive; when it's dry, neither leafhoppers nor frogs may appear at all
- **Four or five different spider species** inhabit the fields, each filling a different ecological niche

"Methods of [[mushroom-pests-insect-control-stamets]], which ignore the relationships among the insects themselves, are truly useless." Research on one predator at one time "within the intricacy of insect interrelationships" cannot capture this dynamic system. To truly understand it would require not just an entomologist but "a frog professor," experts on rice, water management, and "philosophers, men of religion, artists and poets."

## The Ash Experiment: How Fragile the Web Is

Fukuoka once scattered wood ash from the fireplace onto his fields, thinking it harmless. Two or three days later, the field was completely bare of spiders -- the ash had caused the strands of web to disintegrate. "How many thousands of spiders fell victim to a single handful of this apparently harmless ash?" This incident illustrates that "applying an insecticide is not simply a matter of eliminating the leafhoppers together with their natural predators. Many other essential dramas of nature are affected."

## The Pine Bark Weevil Cascade

Fukuoka used the example of Japanese red pine weevil blight to illustrate the futility of chemical intervention. Weevils do not directly infest the trees; they follow upon mediating nematodes, which breed within the trunk and block water/[[mycelial-network-nutrient-transport-imaging-gadd]]. The nematodes feed on a trunk fungus. An unknown microbe always accompanies the fungus, and a virus toxic to the fungus is also present. "Effect following effect in every direction, the only thing that can be said with certainty is that the pine trees are withering in unusual numbers."

Foresters responded with helicopter spraying of insecticides -- effective in the short run, but addressing none of the root causes. "People cannot know what true cause of the pine blight is, nor can they know the ultimate consequences of their remedy." Spraying only "sows the seeds for the next great catastrophe."

## The Fusol Lesson: Exterminating Predators Backfires

The common orchard pests -- ruby scale and horned wax scale -- have natural enemies that normally keep them under control. At one time, the insecticide Fusol was widely used in Japan. It completely exterminated the natural predators, and "the resulting problems still survive in many prefectures." This experience taught most farmers that "it is undesirable to eliminate predators because in the long run greater insect damage will result."

## The Stem Borer Paradox

While at the Kochi [[fukuoka-straw-scattering-testing-station-resistance]] as Head Researcher of Disease and Insect Control, Fukuoka conducted experiments on stem borers (insects that enter rice stems, causing stalks to wither and turn white). He compared a field sprayed with insecticide against an untreated field. Results: "the untreated field with many withered stalks had the higher yield."

What happened was that stem borers attacked the weakest plants, producing a natural thinning effect. The remaining plants received more sunlight, grew stronger, sent up more grain-bearing stalks, and produced more grains per head. "When the density of stalks is too great and insects do not thin out the excess, the plants look healthy enough, but in many cases, the harvest is actually lower."

This counterintuitive result was checked as an "experimental discrepancy" and discarded. "Reports of lower yields after spraying rarely appear in print."

## What Destroys Natural Balance

### The Problem with "Harmless" Organic Sprays

Fukuoka did not advocate even organic sprays like salt-garlic solution or machine oil emulsion. He did occasionally use machine oil diluted 200-400 times for orchard scales, but only as a transitional measure. His fundamental position: "Trees weaken and are attacked by insects to the extent that they deviate from the natural form." Fix the form and the environment, and the pests take care of themselves. If [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] and pest balance is direct: plowing compacts soil and creates conditions for weak root growth; chemical fertilizer produces lush but vulnerable foliage; flooding weakens plants; and "improved" varieties bred for chemical support lose their natural vigor. Each unnatural practice increases susceptibility to pests, creating the apparent need for chemicals.
