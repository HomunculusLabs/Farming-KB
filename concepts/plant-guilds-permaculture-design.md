# Plant Guilds in Permaculture Design

A guild is a harmonious assembly of species clustered around a central element (plant or animal). This assembly acts to assist the health of the central element, aid human work in management, and buffer adverse environmental effects. Guild design is the primary methodology for creating productive polycultures in [[permaculture]], relying on species interaction rather than spatial configuration alone.

## Definition and Concept

In the natural world, assemblies of plants or animals of different species are often observed occurring together over their range. Closer examination reveals a set of mutual benefits arising from such convivial togetherness—benefits that offer help or protection to the whole assembly. One bird species may act as "lookout" for another, or defend others from hawks.

When designing plant guilds in a polyculture, the goal is to **maximize the benefits of each species to the others** while also incorporating factors of convenience to humans or which save inputs of fertilizer and pesticides.

## Reasons for Species Association

### Root Competition Reduction

Almost all cultivated food trees thrive in herbal ground covers rather than grasses. Reducing root competition from invasive grasses is one of the primary reasons to establish guild understories.

### Pest Control Through Multiple Mechanisms

Pest control in guilds operates through several distinct pathways:

- **Anti-feedants**: Plants providing bitter or unpalatable browse or chemical deterrents. Nasturtium roots supply root chemicals to tomatoes and gooseberries that deter whitefly. Many plants, fermented or in aqueous extraction, deter pests or act as anti-feedants when sprayed on leaves of target species.

- **Root parasite killing**: *Crotalaria* captures nematodes that damage citrus and solanaceous roots. *Tagetes* marigolds "fumigate" soils against grasses and nematodes.

- **Predator hosting**: Almost all small-flowered plants—especially *Quillaja*, many *[[acacia]]* species, tamarisk, Compositae (daisy family), and Umbelliferae such as dill, fennel, carrot, and coriander—host robber-flies and predatory wasps.

### Soil Surface and Mulch Provision

Comfrey and globe artichokes allow tree roots to feed at the surface, unlike grasses which compete with tree roots. Spring bulbs (daffodils) or winter-grown wild *Allium* species, whose tops die down in mid-spring, do not compete with deciduous tree roots during summer dry periods and do not intercept light rains.

### Free Nutrient Provision

Woody or herbaceous legumes fix nitrogen or other essential nutrients via root associates, stimulate soil bacteria or fungi, and benefit associated trees. Examples include:

- **Clovers** and other groundcovers
- **Trees**: *Acacia*, *[[casuarina]]*, *Pultenaea*
- **Sugar-providing grasses** (sugar cane)
- **High humus producers** (bananas)

Many can be slashed or trimmed to produce rich mulch below trees or between rows.

### Physical Shelter

Hardy windbreak species of equal or greater height provide multiple benefits as edge windbreaks or in-crop crown cover: excluding frost, nullifying salty or hot winds, providing mulch, and moderating the environment. Examples include bamboo, cane grasses, *Casuarina*, hardy palms, and tamarisks.

In-crop shade shelter from legumes is needed by crops like avocado, citrus, cocoa, and coffee. In-crop trees can eliminate frost effects in marginal frost areas.

## Culinary Associates

Keeping common culinary associates together reduces work by enabling simultaneous harvesting for cooking, salads, or processing:

- **Tomatoes with parsley and basil**
- **Potatoes with mint**
- **Dill with cucumbers** (also dill with apples, raw or cooked)
- **Dill** is one of the Umbelliferae that host predatory wasps below apple trees

## Vertebrate Guild Members

Vertebrates that assist crop species form an important part of total guilds:

### Ground Foragers

Pigs or poultry specifically used to clear fallen fruit that hosts fruit fly or larval pest forms. Foragers can be run in orchards during the relatively short period when fruit is falling and rotting, or used to eat reject fruit and deposit manure.

### Insectivores

Birds that search bark crevices (woodpeckers, honey-eaters) for resting larvae and egg masses. To encourage these, plant scattered flowering shrubs and herbaceous plants such as *Kniphofia*, *Banksia*, *Salvia*, *Buddleia*, and *Fuchsia*—all providing insect and nectar foods for insectivorous birds.

### Mollusc Control

Snails and slugs are almost totally controlled by a duck flock on range. Several large lizards (*Tiliqua* spp.) also feed primarily on snails. Ducks can be ranged seasonally (autumn to spring) in plant systems and on marshlands in summer. Ducks will eat seedlings, so appropriate scheduling is essential.

### Guard Animals

A small number of guard dogs, fed and kennelled in orchards, provide sufficient control for fox predation on orchard poultry foragers. Dogs reared with domestic poultry do not attack the flocks themselves. Guard dogs also deter deer, rabbits, and other vertebrate pests.

### Hawk Kites

Suspended over berry crops or flown as light model planes over extensive grain crops, hawk kites deter all flock-bird predators. They are more dependable than natural hawks but must be removed when not needed so birds do not become accustomed to them.

## The Apple-Centered Guild

The classic example of guild design is the apple-centered guild (Figure 3.12 in the source), where most accessory species enhance the health and productivity of the apples while floristic elements are maximized. This guild incorporates:

- Nitrogen-fixing tree legumes
- Pest-deterring understory plants
- Predator-hosting flowers
- Mulch-producing comfrey
- Root-protecting bulbs
- Culinary associates like dill
- Poultry for fallen fruit cleanup
- Insectivore-attracting shrubs

## Analytic Approach to Guild Selection

### Species Interaction Categories

Research suggests three broad categories of species interaction:

- **~80%** of all plant species can co-mingle without ill effect
- **10–15%** of species greatly assist others in one or more ways (positive placement)
- **~5%** of species show antagonistic behavior toward others

### The Interaction Symbol System

Mollison proposes a simple analytic notation:

- **+**: Beneficial result of interaction (yield above monoculture baseline)
- **0**: No change as a result of interaction
- **−**: Reduction in yield or vigor from interaction

For two species, this yields a 3×3 matrix with nine possible interaction outcomes: three beneficial, three neutral, and three antagonistic. A peasant farmer may accept a mixed (+/−) effect to ensure at least getting a crop, even if one species underperforms. It is always safer to mix crops than to depend on a single main crop.

### States of Action

Three distinct states of action exist between species pairs:

1. **Co-action (unilateral action)**: One species acts on the other
2. **Interaction (mutual action)**: Both species act on each other
3. **Inaction (neutrality)**: Neither species acts on the other

### Symbiosis (++)

The (++) interaction is called **symbiosis**—common both in nature and in society. It represents a "win-win" situation ideally suited to guild development. The mycorrhizal associates of higher plants exemplify this state, where mutualism or fair trade occurs between a plant and its root associate.

## Design Principles for Guilds

Every designer and gardener can plan guilds for specific target species, specific pests and weed control needs, and specific garden beds or orchards. The key is to:

1. Identify the central element (crop tree, animal, or system)
2. Select species that provide multiple benefits to the central element
3. Ensure functions are served by multiple species (redundancy)
4. Incorporate human convenience factors
5. Observe and adjust based on actual performance rather than theory alone

As Mollison cautions: be guided by analyses but study reality. The vagaries of weather in any given year mean that theoretical models must always be validated by field observation.
