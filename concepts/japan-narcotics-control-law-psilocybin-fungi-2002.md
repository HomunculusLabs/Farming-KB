## International Context and Comparative Legislation
Japan's 2002 decision to regulate psilocybin-containing fungi placed it among a growing number of nations
that had specifically addressed mushroom-derived psychedelics in their controlled substances legislation,
reflecting a broader international trend toward closing legal loopholes regarding naturally occurring
psychedelic substances.
The United Kingdom had already enacted the Drugs Act 2005 (with earlier provisions under the Misuse of Drugs
Act 1971) to control fresh and dried psilocybin mushrooms, while the Netherlands, long known for its tolerant
drug policies, banned the sale of fresh psilocybin mushrooms in 2007 following widely publicized incidents
involving foreign tourists.
The United States had controlled psilocybin and psilocin under the Controlled Substances Act since 1970
(placed in Schedule I), and the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971) had listed
psilocybin and psilocin in Schedule I, though the convention's coverage of the fungi themselves was less
explicit than Japan's targeted legislation.
Japan's approach was notable for its specificity in targeting the fungal organisms rather than only the
isolated chemical constituents, a legal strategy designed to eliminate any ambiguity regarding the
permissibility of possessing, cultivating, or selling the raw mushroom material itself.
