# Food Forest Layers in Gaia's Garden

> In a forest garden, the yard is a parklike grove of spreading fruit trees, walnuts, chestnuts, and other useful trees. In the bright openings are smaller persimmon trees, plums, cherries, pawpaws. Catching the sunlight farther down, dancing with birds, are flowering shrubs and berry bushes. Occasional honeysuckle and hardy kiwi vines wind up tree trunks, leaving a trail of blossoms and fruit. -- Toby Hemenway, Gaia's Garden

## Overview

The food forest (or forest garden) is the most mature expression of ecological gardening -- a multistoried edible landscape that mimics the structure and function of a natural woodland. Rather than planting in separate, disconnected elements (a vegetable patch here, a fruit tree there, flowers over there), the forest garden integrates all productive layers into a smoothly working whole. Toby Hemenway identifies up to seven distinct vegetation layers in a fully developed food forest, each offering unique growing conditions, ecological functions, and food-producing potential.

## The Basic Three Layers

A simple forest garden contains three fundamental layers:
- **Trees** (the canopy/overstory)
- **Shrubs** (the middle level)
- **Ground plants** (herbs, vegetables, flowers)

But for those who want to take full advantage of every planting opportunity, a deluxe forest garden can contain as many as seven tiers of vegetation.

## The Seven Layers of the Forest Garden

### 1. The Tall-Tree Layer (Overstory/Canopy)

This is an overstory of full-sized fruit, nut, or other useful trees, with spaces between to let plenty of light reach the lower layers. Dense, spreading shade trees such as maple, sycamore, and beech do not work well because they cast deep shadows over a large area. Better choices are multifunctioned fruit and nut trees:

- Standard and semi-standard apple and pear trees
- European plums on standard rootstocks such as Myrobalan
- Full-sized cherries
- Chestnut trees (especially Chinese chestnuts, generally not as large as American types), pruned in open, light-allowing shapes
- Walnut trees, especially naturally open, spreading varieties such as heartnut and buartnut
- Nut-bearing stone pinyon pines
- Nitrogen-fixing trees: black locust, mesquite, alder, and in low-frost climates, algoroba, tagasaste, and carob

The canopy trees define the major patterns of the forest garden, so they must be chosen carefully. Plant them with regard to their mature size so enough light falls between them to support other plants.

### 2. The Low-Tree Layer

Here are many of the same fruits and nuts as in the canopy, but on dwarf and semi-dwarf rootstocks to keep them low. Plus, naturally small trees:

- Apricot, peach, nectarine, almond, and mulberry
- Shade-tolerant fruit trees such as persimmon and pawpaw
- In a smaller forest garden, these may serve as the canopy
- Flowering trees: dogwood and mountain ash
- Nitrogen fixers: golden-chain tree, silk tree, and mountain mahogany

Both large and small nitrogen-fixing trees grow quickly and can be pruned heavily to generate plenty of mulch and compost.

### 3. The Shrub Layer

This tier includes flowering, fruiting, wildlife-attracting, and other useful shrubs:

- Blueberry, rose, hazelnut, butterfly bush, bamboo, serviceberry
- Nitrogen-fixing Elaeagnus species and Siberian pea shrub
- Dozens of other options emphasizing food, crafts, ornamentals, birds, insects, native plants, exotics, or biodiversity

Shrubs come in all sizes, from dwarf blueberries to nearly tree-sized hazelnuts. Shade-tolerant varieties can grow beneath the trees, sun-loving types in the sunny spaces between.

### 4. The Herb Layer

"Herb" is used in the broad botanical sense to mean nonwoody vegetation:
- Vegetables, flowers, culinary herbs, cover crops
- Mulch producers and other soil-building plants
- Emphasis on perennials, but choice annuals and self-seeding species are included
- Shade-lovers beneath taller plants, sun-worshiping species in open spaces
- At the edges, more traditional garden beds of full-sun plants

### 5. The Ground Cover Layer

Low, ground-hugging plants that snuggle into edges and spaces between shrubs and herbs:
- Strawberries, nasturtium, clover, creeping thyme, ajuga
- Prostrate varieties of phlox and verbena
- They play a critical role in weed prevention, occupying ground that would otherwise succumb to invaders

### 6. The Vine Layer

Climbing plants that twine up trunks and branches, filling the unused vertical dimension:
- Food plants: kiwifruit, grapes, hops, passionflower, vining berries
- Wildlife plants: honeysuckle and trumpet-flower
- Climbing annuals: squash, cucumbers, and melons
- Some perennial vines can be invasive or strangling, so use sparingly and cautiously

### 7. The Root Layer

The soil provides yet another layer; the third dimension goes both up and down:
- Shallow-rooted crops: garlic and onions
- Easy-to-dig types: potatoes and Jerusalem artichokes
- Deep-rooted varieties like carrots don't work well because digging disturbs other plants
- Daikon (Asian radish) seeds can be sprinkled in open spots -- the long roots can often be pulled with one mighty tug, and if unharvested, the blossoms attract beneficial insects and the fat roots add humus as they rot

## How Nature Uses Layers

In a natural forest, layers develop in a time-delay sequence. In spring, the first plants to leaf out are tiny herbs and ground covers. Once these have gathered sunlight for a few weeks, building sugary sap and healthy roots, the shrubs begin to burst their buds. The low trees follow, and last come the spreading forest giants. By the time summer heat arrives, the canopy is nearly closed. Yet everything is in full leaf, with branches arrayed in alternating whorls to catch shifting rays and efficiently convert solar energy.

Once the forest canopy closes, the air and soil beneath stays far more moist than in the open. Desiccating sunlight cannot reach in to dry the air and earth, and temperature stays cool, further slowing moisture loss. This multilayered approach to water conservation is a major benefit of the forest garden model.

## Light Management in the Food Forest

Proper tree spacing and shade-tolerant plants keep lack of light from being a problem. In northern gardens where the sun is weaker, fruit yields and flower density won't be as large in the shady parts of the understory as in full sun. However, shade-tolerant bush fruits like currants and gooseberries still bear well with less direct sun, require less water, and have lusher foliage. The shrub-filled, sun-dappled margin under trees offers far more ecologically, aesthetically, and gastronomically than a lawn.

Northern gardeners need a more open array of trees to allow sunlight to reach ground layers, while southerners may desire dense spacing for needed shade.

## Design Principles

Key considerations for designing the forest garden:
- Trees and woody plants go in first, since they take longest to establish
- Include plenty of nitrogen-fixing and other soil-building plants early on
- Choose canopy trees with careful regard to their mature size
- Leave sunny edges for sun-loving vegetables and flowers
- Consider U-shaped or asymmetric layouts oriented toward the sun to create sun-traps
- Every site offers opportunities for creative stacking of layers

## Benefits of the Forest Garden

- Maximum wildlife habitat in three dimensions
- Largest possible crop yields for the space
- Greatest possibilities for edge and diversity
- Reduced water needs through thick vegetation cover
- Self-mulching and natural soil building
- Mostly perennial and self-seeding plants, needing no tilling and little seasonal replanting
- Reduced pest problems as biodiversity provides natural controls
