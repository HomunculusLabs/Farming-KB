# Holzer Terraced Landscape Design

Terraces are a central feature of [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]]. Without terraces, Holzer considers it would have been impossible to cultivate the otherwise unproductive and inaccessible steep slopes of his farm in Lungau, Austria (1,100--1,500m above sea level).

## Functions and Benefits

Terraces serve multiple simultaneous functions in Holzer's system:

- **Erosion prevention**: Making steps in steep slopes stops valuable [[small-scale-intensive-food-systems-mollison]].
- **Water retention**: Terraces store and hold moisture so that plants have access to rainwater and meltwater for longer.
- **Access**: They are more pleasant to work and far easier to access than steep slopes, and can even accommodate medium-sized machinery.
- [[holzer-raised-bed-techniques-hugelkultur]]). Keeping terrace and path lines rounded and winding prevents wind tunnels; curved shapes create sheltered niches. In particularly exposed places, Holzer also makes hills and hollows to lessen weather effects.

### Continuous Belts

Every terrace should form a continuous belt of land, minimising dead ends, so terraces can be worked using the least amount of energy. Dead ends waste time and energy and should be avoided. Paths connect terraces with shortcuts so workers need not travel the full length.

### Dual-Use Terraces and Paths

Holzer designs terraces to serve simultaneously as paths and provide access for mechanised farming equipment. By combining the two uses, he has both a terrace that is a path and a path that is a terrace. However, no single terrace should be used as a path for too long, or the soil becomes compacted and crops suffer. It is still possible to access terraces while under cultivation by keeping to the embankments.

### Width Considerations

The entire terrace should be cultivable or harvestable in a single trip. Managing two terraces each five metres wide is easier and more profitable than one terrace ten metres wide. Steeper slopes require narrower terraces; shallower slopes allow wider ones. Extra care is needed with fine, loamy soil on very steep slopes due to erosion danger.

### Gradient

Terrace gradient should be as low as possible, no more than 15--20 percent. Terrace embankments can be 1:1 if soil is stony; on sandy or loam soils, gradients of 1:1.5 to 1:2 work best.

## Construction Methods

### Soil Separation Technique

On the Krameterhof, deeper soil layers (two to three metres) consist of distinct coarse and stony material. When making terraces, Holzer digs the earth and shakes it into tall mounds using an excavator. Fine material stays in the middle; coarse material falls to the sides. Coarse material supports and secures structures, while fine material forms fertile terrace soil.

### Loam Soils

On loam soils, material separation is unnecessary (except for the humus layer). The challenge is stabilising walls of deeper structures. Loam's high water retention means heavy soils should not be shaken into tall heaps, as they will sink and crack under pressure. Separated material must stabilise over time before further work.

### Embankment Construction

To stabilise embankments, risers are constructed by removing topsoil and underlying layers until reaching solid, stable material. The riser forms the foundation, angled slightly into the hill. The embankment is then built up replacing excavated layers, with topsoil as the uppermost layer. [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] and bushes establish deep roots into the embankment, contributing greatly to its stability.

### Working with Water

In low-rainfall areas, Holzer angles terraces slightly into the hill to retain water. When securing against heavy rainfall, alignment must not channel water. On heavy loam soils, surface water must be drained over a large area by creating terraces with a slight downward angle. Ditches between terraces raise moisture levels in surrounding soil by storing surface water and runoff.

### Gradual Stabilisation

Large-scale projects are carried out gradually over a long period. In the first year, terraces are made at the top, middle, and bottom of the slope and planted. In the second year, more terraces are created between the original ones once completely stable. On steep slopes, work begins at the lowest point and moves upward. New terraces are sowed and planted immediately, as erosion danger is greatest right after construction.

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Holzer Terraced Landscape Design requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
