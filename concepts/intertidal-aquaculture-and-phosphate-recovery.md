# Intertidal Aquaculture and Phosphate Recovery

The intertidal zone—the dynamic edge between land and sea that alternates between exposure and submersion with each tidal cycle—represents one of the most productive and designable environments in [[permaculture]]. [[bill-mollison]] drew extensively on traditional coastal management practices from Ireland, Hawaii, Japan, and elsewhere to demonstrate that intertidal aquaculture can produce food, materials, and fertility at scales that rival or exceed terrestrial agriculture.

## Tidal Physics and Habitat Creation

### Intertidal Ponds and Lagoons

Areas that are ponds at low tide and slightly flooded at high tide create ideal habitat for a range of [[marine-aquaculture-and-intertidal-design|marine]] organisms. The key design variable is the period of still water—not too much tidal run. Sub-surface dams (leaky rubble walls rather than true dams) can create these conditions by partially restricting tidal flow while allowing water exchange.

When such structures are installed on open coastlines, a cascade of [[ecological-succession]] follows:
1. Mussels colonize the walls
2. Shrimp move into the sheltered spaces
3. Fish aggregate in the scour holes
4. Detritus accumulates, providing substrate for further colonization

### Scour Hole Design

Complex aquaculture systems can be designed by combining natural drift events with constructed elements. Barrier fences, drift fences, and pond scour holes work together to create self-maintaining systems that concentrate fish traps, still-water growing areas, and material accumulation zones. Mollison recommends beginning at miniature scale—placing small logs in the shoreline zone, observing effects, building small fences, and only scaling up once the [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]] are verified.

The principle of working with natural forces is critical. Designers should observe where reefs end, where logs strand, and where material naturally accumulates, then amplify these patterns rather than fighting against tidal energy.

## Octopus Cultivation

Octopus require sheltered breeding locations in intertidal zones rich with mollusk prey. The design solution is remarkably simple: placing ordinary clay pots in intertidal ponds provides exactly the shelter octopus seek. The animals apparently swim continuously over browsing lands looking for suitable habitat, and will colonize provided pots immediately.

The behavioral pattern is entirely predictable: octopus emerge from their pots at high tide to feed on mollusks, then return to the pots at low tide. These same intertidal pond systems also support sponge cultivation, creating polyculture potential.

## Irish Stone Fields

The Irish developed intertidal "fields" that remain visible from the air as enormous underwater acreages. These consist of rows of stone placed across hard ocean bottoms to support seaweed growth.

### The Smaller-Field Advantage

A counterintuitive finding from Donegal demonstrated that smaller stone fields produce more than larger ones. The reason is thermal: stone walls generate radiant heat, creating warmer microenvironments that boost seaweed growth. By deliberately making fields smaller and increasing the density of stone walls, the Irish maximized thermal benefit per unit area.

### Kelp Harvesting and Processing

The Irish developed sophisticated techniques for harvesting and processing kelp:

1. **Cutting and bundling** — Kelp is cut and ropes tied around bundles of 10-12 tons
2. **Tidal transport** — Bundles are floated to shore using the incoming tide, deposited in bog channels
3. **Stem processing** — Stems are stacked and dried like firewood, then fired with peat to extract potash for field fertilization
4. **Frond application** — Fronds are laid directly as mulch

This system uses tidal energy for transporting enormous weights without mechanical assistance. The Irish could also handle very large boulders in the sea by floating them alongside boats, drawing them aboard, and rolling them off at designated locations.

## Estuary Polyculture

Estuaries provide unique opportunities because they simultaneously offer cold fresh water, warm salt water, and graduated [[temperature-salinity-and-flow-in-aquaculture|salinity]] zones. By designing adjoining ponds with different salinity levels, a single system can support species ranging from freshwater trout through brackish-water species to marine grey mullet and eels.

The design leverages both the fresh water inflow from upstream and the twice-daily warm saltwater tidal intrusion. Swap-offs between these water types can be managed through channels, sluices, and pond configurations.

## Raft Culture Systems

### Salmon Raft Culture

When wild salmon runs collapsed in Ireland due to river "improvement" (bulldozing riverbeds destroyed oxygenating salmon weirs) and offshore gill net overfishing, the Irish developed inshore raft culture as a response. Giant floating nets were moored in quiet tidal areas behind islands, producing remarkable salmon yields.

The Irish also developed very large ring nets with four-foot fences to prevent salmon from jumping out, with workers walking boards around the perimeters for feeding and maintenance.

### Universal Raft Applications

Rafts are applicable across the entire spectrum of aquatic systems:
- **Micro-scale**: Tiny rafts on small ponds serving as insect attractants
- **Plant production**: Rafts maintaining plants at fixed root depths, including pot plants and daffodils grown on chicken wire frameworks
- **Shellfish and algae**: Rock cultures for mussels and oysters, ring nets for algae cultivation
- **Open-sea fish culture**: Ring nets deployed in the sea with net enclosures

Water culture on rafts is a highly developed technique in southwest Asia, where raft stability allows precise control of root depth for consistent growing conditions.

## Phosphate Recovery Through Sea Bird Management

### The Phosphate Island Concept

Sea birds concentrate marine phosphate and deposit it at roosting sites in quantities that can sustain productive agriculture. The phosphate island concept involves constructing artificial roosting platforms to capture this nutrient flow.

### The West African Model

A single documented installation on a desert coast of West Africa consisted of concrete pylons supporting a platform the size of a football field. This facility generates enough phosphate to make its operator an annual millionaire through two [[biodiversity-of-fungi-freshwater-survey-collection-methods]]:

- **Liquid collection**: Rainfall on the roosting surfaces is piped ashore and evaporated to concentrate phosphate
- **Solid collection**: Accumulated phosphate is shoveled into bags for direct distribution

### Roosting Site Design

Sea birds demonstrate species-specific roosting preferences. By observing gulls, cormorants, terns, and other species at existing roosting sites (old bridgeworks, natural islands), designers can create attractive artificial roosts. Mollison estimated that 200 terns consistently roosting in the right location could entirely supply an island or village with phosphate.

### The Dutch Bat Roost Model

The Dutch apply the same principle with bat roosts constructed throughout agricultural fields. These structures, described as "slatted like racks for drying towels," serve dual purposes:
- **[[backswimmer-mosquito-control-mollison]]** through insectivorous bat populations
- **Manure production** that is carefully collected and distributed as critical fertilizer

### Broader Significance

Phosphate is one of the most critically lacking minerals in developing regions. While many natural phosphate islands worldwide are being mined into depletion, very few new ones are being created. The potential for creating small phosphate islands serving villages or communities is described as "a very simple affair" requiring only attention to roosting site design and bird species preferences.

Mollison considered phosphate recovery from birds in any setting—lakes, sea coasts, or land—an achievement that "would be making a better strike than anybody else for local self-sufficiency."

## Sea Bird Colony Establishment

### Translocation Techniques

Large-scale transfer of sea birds in nesting cycles is possible by keeping adults captive until the nesting cycle begins on a target section of coast. When released, birds return to established nest sites, founding new colonies. This technique has been demonstrated with:

- **Mutton birds** (shearwaters) — a critically important food source for Tasmanians
- **Seals** — young seals kidnapped before they can swim, before they are being fed by mothers, will start new seal colonies

### The Seal-Fishery Connection

Mollison identified a critical ecological link that "nobody ever made": seals primarily eat spiny dogfish and whitefish, which have high manurial turnover on the seabed. This manurial activity in turn supports high-quality food fish populations. When seals were killed for fur, the inshore fisheries collapsed—not because seals competed with fishermen, but because eliminating seals destroyed the manurial system that sustained the fishery food web.

## Eelgrass (Seagrass) Systems

### Nutrient Absorption Capacity

Eelgrass beds absorb nutrients at extraordinary rates—Mollison cited calculations that a bag of super-phosphate placed in an eelgrass bed would be completely absorbed within three days. This makes eelgrass beds among the most efficient biological nutrient filters in marine environments.

### Insulation Material Production

When eelgrass washes ashore and is composted for approximately 10 days, it produces what Mollison considers the best insulation material available. The [[composting]] process:
- Burns off [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] that would otherwise create foul odors
- Leaves a chocolate-colored, fibrous frame material
- Creates no health risks (unlike mineral fiber insulation)
- Lasts indefinitely

The raw material is free and globally abundant. Eelgrass that isn't collected either blows inland (contributing silica skeletons to beach plant growth) or breaks down and returns as mud to channels, ultimately fertilizing offshore waters. Converting it to insulation represents permanent productive use at significant energy savings.

### Harvesting Access

Eelgrass typically washes ashore at predictable locations, making it straightforward to install simple catch fences. In many jurisdictions, individuals retain the right to harvest eelgrass regardless of intertidal zone ownership. If not collected, the material is essentially wasted.

### Mulch Application

Eelgrass can be applied directly as [[cotter-outdoor-mushroom-garden-mulch-beds]], preferably on sopping wet ground. In locations with below 20 inches of annual rainfall, salt content should be considered, though in most applications the rapid [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] of eelgrass makes salt concerns minimal. It should not be used where cattle graze, as they will consume it.

## Intertidal Title and Access Law

Understanding coastal property law is essential for intertidal design:

- **Ancient grant titles** may extend to the low tide mark
- **Freehold titles** (non-Crown charters) typically extend to high tide
- **Modern titles** may be set back 100+ feet from the beach as beach reserve
