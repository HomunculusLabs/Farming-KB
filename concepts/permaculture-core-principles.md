# Permaculture Core Principles

The foundational principles of permaculture, as articulated by [[permaculture-design]] prioritizes mutually beneficial relationships between components.

## The Three Ethics

The ethical basis of permaculture rests on three pillars:

1. **Care of the Earth**: Provision for all life systems to continue and increase
2. **Care of People**: Provision for people to access those resources necessary to their existence
3. **Setting Limits to Population and Consumption**: By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles

These ethics are not merely philosophical — they function as practical design criteria. Any design that degrades the earth, fails to provide for people, or exceeds sustainable consumption limits is fundamentally outside permaculture.

## Rules of Use of Natural Resources

- **Reduce waste**, hence pollution
- **Thoroughly replace** lost minerals
- **Do careful energy accounting**
- **Make a biosocial impact assessment** for long-term effects on society, and act to buffer or eliminate any negative impacts

## Life Intervention Principle

> **In chaos lies unparalleled opportunity for imposing creative order.**

Disturbance, change, and disruption — whether natural or human-caused — create openings for design intervention. The permaculture designer sees crisis as a catalyst for creative system redesign.

## Law of Return

> **Whatever we take, we must return.** Nature demands a return for every gift received. The user must pay.

This law establishes that all resource extraction carries an obligation of replacement. It applies to nutrients, water, soil, energy, and all other resources drawn from natural systems.

## Directive of Return

> **Every object must responsibly provide for its replacement.** Society must, as a condition of use, replace an equal or greater resource than that used.

This directive extends the Law of Return into the social and economic realm, requiring that systems of production include provisions for their own renewal.

## Ethics on Natural Systems

- **Implacable and uncompromising opposition** to further disturbance of any remaining natural forests
- **Vigorous rehabilitation** of degraded and damaged natural systems to a stable state
- **Establishment of plant systems** for our own use on the least amount of land we can use for our existence
- **Establishment of plant and animal refuges** for rare or threatened species

These ethics establish a clear hierarchy: first protect what remains, then restore what is damaged, then meet human needs with minimal land use, and finally create refuges for biodiversity.

## Thermodynamic Principles

The **Basic Law of Thermodynamics** (as restated by Watt): All energy entering an organism, population, or ecosystem can be accounted for as energy which is stored or leaves. Energy can be transferred from one form to another, but it cannot disappear, be destroyed, or be created. No energy conversion system is ever completely efficient.

As stated by Asimov (1970): The total energy of the universe is constant and the total entropy is increasing. These physical constraints underpin all permaculture design — energy efficiency is not optional but obligatory.

## Birch's Six Principles of Natural Systems

1. **Nothing in nature grows forever.** There is a constant cycle of decay and rebirth.
2. **Continuation of life depends** on the maintenance of global biogeochemical cycles of essential elements (carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, phosphorus).
3. **Extinction probability is greatest** when density is very high or very low. Both crowding and scarcity can reach extinction thresholds.
4. **Species survival depends primarily** on one or two key factors in the complex web of relations with the environment.
5. **Our ability to change the earth increases** at a faster rate than our ability to foresee the consequences of change.
6. **Living organisms are not only means but ends.** They have intrinsic worth beyond their instrumental value to humans.

## Mollisonian Permaculture Principles

Five core design principles attributed directly to Mollison:

1. **Work with nature**, rather than against natural elements, forces, pressures, processes, agencies, and evolutions — assist rather than impede natural developments
2. **The problem is the solution; everything works both ways.** It is only how we see things that makes them advantageous or not. A corollary: everything is a positive resource; it is up to us to work out how to use it as such
3. **Make the least change for the greatest possible effect.** Minimum intervention for maximum benefit
4. **The yield of a system is theoretically unlimited.** The only limit on the number of uses of a resource possible within a system is in the limit of the information and imagination of the designer
5. **Everything gardens**, or has an effect on its environment

## Practical Design Considerations

- Systems should **last as long as possible** and take least maintenance
- Systems, **fueled by the sun**, should produce not only their own needs but the needs of the people creating or controlling them — making them sustainable, as they sustain both themselves and their designers
- Energy may be used to construct systems, provided that in their lifetime they store or conserve more energy than was used to construct and maintain them

## Categories of Resources

Mollison classifies resources into five categories based on their response to use:

1. **Those which increase by modest use** (e.g., pastures grazed moderately, skills practiced)
2. **Those unaffected by use** (e.g., solar energy, wind)
3. **Those which disappear or degrade if not used** (e.g., annual crops unharvested, knowledge unshared)
4. **Those reduced by use** (e.g., fossil fuels, topsoil)
5. **Those which pollute or destroy other resources if used** (e.g., persistent poisons, radioactive materials)

## Resource Management Policy

A responsible human society bans the use of resources which permanently reduce yields of sustainable resources — pollutants, persistent poisons, radioactives, large areas of concrete and highways, sewers from city to sea.

## Principle of Disorder (Resource Oversupply)

> **Any system or organism can accept only that quantity of a resource which can be used productively.** Any resource input beyond that point throws the system or organism into disorder; oversupply of a resource is a form of chronic pollution.

This principle explains why fertilizer runoff, excessive irrigation, and overfeeding all create problems rather than benefits. Order and harmony produce energy for other uses; disorder consumes energy to no useful end. Neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness in the natural world often signify energy-maintained disorder.

## System Yield

**System yield** is defined as the sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by the design. Energy is in surplus once the system itself has available all its needs for growth, reproduction, and maintenance. Living things, including people, are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources and produce yield. Yield is not a fixed sum — it is the measure of the comprehension, understanding, and ability of the designers and managers.

## Dispersal of Food Yield Over Time

Practical strategies for spreading production across seasons:

- Selection of early, mid, and late season varieties
- Planting the same variety in early or late-ripening situations
- Selection of long-yielding varieties
- Increasing diversity so that leaf, fruit, seed, and root are all product yields
- Using self-storing species (tubers, hard seeds, fuelwood, rhizomes) that can be "cropped on demand"
- Preserving, drying, pitting, and cool storage
- Regional trade between communities, or utilizing land at different altitudes and latitudes

## Principle of Cyclic Opportunity

> **Every cyclic event increases the opportunity for yield.** To increase cycling is to increase yield.

[[cycles-in-nature]] are diversion routes away from entropic ends — life itself cycles nutrients, creating opportunities for yield and for species to occupy time niches.

## Types of Niches

- **Niche in space** ("territory"): nest and forage sites
- **Niche in time**: cycles of opportunity
- **Niche in space-time**: schedules and temporal-spatial patterns

Understanding these niche types enables designers to stack functions and species across multiple dimensions, maximizing both diversity and productivity.

## Policy of Responsibility

> **The role of beneficial authority is to return function and responsibility to life and to people; if successful, no further authority is needed.** The role of successful design is to create a self-managed system.
