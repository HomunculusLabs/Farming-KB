# Plant Olfaction and Chemical Sensing

## Overview

Plants possess a sophisticated ability to detect [[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]] in the air
— a capability that Daniel Chamovitz argues in *What a Plant Knows* can
legitimately be described as a form of olfaction, or smell. While plants lack
noses and olfactory bulbs, they achieve functionally similar results through
receptor proteins that bind volatile organic compounds (VOCs) dissolved in the
air. This chemical sensing allows plants to perceive the ripeness of nearby
fruit, detect herbivore attacks on neighboring plants, locate potential hosts
(as in the case of parasitic plants), and coordinate communal defense
responses. The study of plant olfaction reveals that plants are far more
responsive to their chemical environment than traditionally assumed. See also
[[phytochrome-photoreceptor-plant-vision]] for how plants sense light, and
[[thigmomorphogenesis-plant-touch-response]] for other sensory modalities.

## Ethylene and Fruit Ripening Signaling

The classic example of plant chemical sensing is the role of ethylene in fruit
ripening. Ethylene is a gaseous plant hormone — a volatile organic compound
that functions as both a hormone within the producing plant and a signal to
neighboring plants. When a fruit begins to ripen, it produces ethylene, which
accelerates the ripening process in that fruit and diffuses through the air to
trigger ripening in nearby fruits. This is why placing a ripe banana in a bag
with unripe avocados speeds up their ripening — the ethylene from the banana
is detected by the avocados and activates their ripening program. Chamovitz
describes how plants have specific ethylene receptor proteins that bind
ethylene molecules, initiating a signal transduction cascade that alters gene
expression. This is functionally analogous to how olfactory receptors in
animals bind odorant molecules, though the downstream responses differ. The
agricultural industry exploits this system extensively, using ethylene to
coordinate ripening during transport and storing fruit in ethylene-controlled
atmospheres to delay spoilage.

## Plant-Plant Communication Through Airborne Chemicals

One of the most fascinating aspects of plant olfaction is the evidence for
plant-to-plant communication through airborne chemicals. When a plant is
attacked by herbivores, it releases a bouquet of [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]] that serve
multiple functions. Some of these volatiles attract predators or parasitoids
of the herbivores — a phenomenon known as "crying for help." But these same
volatile signals are also detected by neighboring plants, which respond by
neighbors. Plants exposed to the volatile emissions of herbivore-attacked
neighbors activate defense-related genes more quickly and strongly when
subsequently attacked. This interplant warning system has been demonstrated
in numerous species including sagebrush, lima beans, and poplar trees.

## Methyl Jasmonate as a Signaling Molecule

Methyl jasmonate is one of the most important [[plant-olfaction-and-volatile-signaling]] molecules in
plant defense. It is a volatile ester derived from jasmonic acid, a fatty
acid-based plant hormone central to the plant's wound and defense response.
When a plant is damaged by herbivores, it rapidly produces methyl jasmonate,
which is released into the air and can be detected by neighboring plants.
Upon detection, methyl jasmonate is converted back to jasmonic acid in the
receiving plant, where it activates the same defense pathways that would be
triggered by direct herbivore damage. These pathways include the production
of protease inhibitors (which interfere with insect digestion), defensive
[[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] such as alkaloids and tannins, and additional volatile
emissions that may further propagate the warning signal through the plant
community. Chamovitz notes that the jasmonate signaling pathway is remarkably
conserved across the plant kingdom, underscoring its fundamental importance
in plant defense ecology. See also [[photoperiodism-flowering-plants]] for
how plants integrate chemical signals with light sensing.
