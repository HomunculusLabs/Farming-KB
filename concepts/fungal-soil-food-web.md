# Fungal Soil Food Web

Fungi are central nodes in soil food webs, serving as both decomposers of organic matter and as food resources for a wide range of soil organisms. The fungal channel in soil food webs represents a major pathway of energy and nutrient flow from dead organic matter to higher trophic levels, operating alongside the bacterial channel. The balance between fungal and bacterial energy channels has important consequences for nutrient cycling rates, carbon storage, and the stability of soil ecosystem processes.

## The Fungal Energy Channel

Soil food webs can be conceptually divided into fungal and bacterial energy channels, each supporting distinct consumer communities and contributing differently to ecosystem processes. The fungal channel is characterized by slower decomposition rates, more efficient carbon conservation, and support for microbivorous fauna with lower reproductive rates but larger body sizes (such as collembola and oribatid mites). The bacterial channel supports faster decomposition, more rapid nutrient turnover, and consumers with higher reproductive rates but smaller body sizes (such as protozoa and nematodes).

The dominance of the fungal versus bacterial channel varies among ecosystems and is influenced by soil pH, moisture, substrate quality, and management history. Acidic forest soils typically support fungal-dominated food webs, while neutral agricultural soils tend to be bacterial-dominated. This has implications for the efficiency of energy transfer through the food web, with fungal-based webs generally conserving more carbon in the soil system.

## Fungal Interactions with Soil Fauna

Fungi interact with soil fauna through multiple pathways. Bacterivorous nematodes and protozoa primarily consume bacteria but can indirectly affect fungi by altering the competitive balance between bacteria and fungi. Fungivorous nematodes, collembola, and mites directly graze fungal hyphae and spores, creating feedbacks that regulate fungal biomass and community composition. Predatory nematodes and mites at higher trophic levels regulate populations of fungivores, creating trophic cascades that affect fungal-mediated decomposition.

The grazing of fungal hyphae by microarthropods has been shown to stimulate fungal growth and decomposition rates in some studies, through compensatory growth responses similar to those observed in plant herbivory. Moderate grazing may increase branching density and enzyme production, accelerating decomposition. However, heavy grazing can damage mycelial networks, interrupt nutrient translocation, and reduce fungal biomass below levels needed for efficient decomposition.

## Fungal Biomass in Soil Food Webs

Fungal biomass in soil represents a substantial pool of carbon and nutrients in soil food webs. Estimates of fungal biomass in temperate forest soils range from 50 to 500 g dry weight per square meter, contributing significantly to total soil microbial biomass. The turnover time of fungal biomass varies from weeks to years, depending on the fungal species, environmental conditions, and grazing pressure. Fungal necromass (dead fungal biomass) is an increasingly recognized component of soil organic matter, with the chitin and melanin in fungal cell walls contributing to stable carbon pools.

The production of fungal biomass from decomposing organic matter represents the conversion of detrital carbon into microbial carbon that is more accessible to soil fauna. This conversion, sometimes called the fungal loop, creates a pathway from plant litter to soil animals that bypasses the need for direct consumption of detritus. The efficiency of this conversion depends on fungal carbon use efficiency and the palatability of fungal tissue to consumers.

## Trophic Interactions and Nutrient Cycling

Trophic interactions in fungal-based soil food webs have important consequences for nutrient cycling. When fungivorous fauna consume fungal biomass, they excrete nitrogen and phosphorus in more plant-available forms than the original fungal tissue. This trophic transfer accelerates the mineralization of nutrients immobilized in fungal biomass, creating a pathway from decomposition to plant nutrient availability.

The intensity of faunal grazing on fungi can shift the balance between nutrient immobilization and mineralization. Under low grazing pressure, fungi may immobilize nutrients from the surrounding soil to support their growth on nutrient-poor substrates. Under high grazing pressure, nutrients are released through faunal excretion, potentially increasing nutrient availability for plants. These grazing-mediated nutrient dynamics connect above-ground plant communities to below-ground fungal and faunal communities.

## Fungi as Pathogens in Food Webs

Entomopathogenic fungi occupy a unique position in soil food webs, acting as regulators of invertebrate populations. Fungal pathogens including Cordyceps (Ophiocordyceps) species, Beauveria bassiana, and Metarhizium anisopliae can cause epizootics in insect populations, creating density-dependent population regulation. The reduction of herbivorous insect populations by fungal pathogens can have cascading effects on vegetation, plant-fungal mutualisms, and ecosystem productivity.

Fungal pathogens of soil nematodes, including nematode-trapping fungi and endoparasitic fungi, represent another trophic link between fungi and soil fauna. These predatory fungi contribute to the regulation of nematode populations, including plant-parasitic species, and represent a biological control mechanism that operates within the soil food web.

## Fungal-Fauna Coevolution

The long evolutionary history of interactions between fungi and soil fauna has led to numerous adaptations on both sides. Fungi produce toxic secondary metabolites and defensive structures (such as melanized hyphae and oxalate crystals) to deter grazing. Soil fauna have evolved detoxification mechanisms, specialized mouthparts, and behavioral adaptations to exploit fungal resources. These coevolutionary dynamics have shaped the diversity and functional characteristics of both fungal and faunal communities in soil.

## Management Implications

Agricultural management practices that favor fungal-dominated soil food webs (such as reduced tillage, organic amendments, and diverse crop rotations) tend to enhance nutrient cycling efficiency, carbon storage, and disease suppression. The fungal pathway conserves more carbon in soil and releases nutrients more gradually than the bacterial pathway, which is beneficial for sustainable agricultural production. Conservation of fungal diversity and biomass in agricultural soils is therefore an important goal for maintaining soil health and ecosystem function.

## Ecological Significance

The fungal channel in soil food webs is a major determinant of ecosystem carbon storage and nutrient cycling efficiency. Fungal-dominated food webs conserve more carbon in soil than bacterial-dominated webs because fungal biomass turns over more slowly and fungal cell walls (chitin, melanin) are more resistant to decomposition than bacterial cell walls. This difference has profound implications for ecosystem carbon balance, as management practices that shift the fungal-to-bacterial ratio can significantly alter long-term carbon storage potential.

The trophic interactions mediated by the fungal soil food web connect decomposition processes to plant nutrition. When fungivorous soil fauna graze fungal biomass, they release nutrients in plant-available forms, creating a pathway from dead organic matter through fungal biomass to plant nutrient uptake. This fungal-mediated pathway is particularly important in acidic forest soils where fungal decomposition dominates and where the gradual nutrient release through fungal biomass turnover supports sustained plant productivity.

## Research Methods

Soil food web analysis employs phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) profiling to distinguish fungal and bacterial biomass, ergosterol quantification for fungal biomass estimation, and molecular gut content analysis to characterize feeding relationships among soil fauna. Stable isotope techniques using 13C and 15N allow tracing of carbon and nitrogen flow through food web pathways. Food web modeling approaches, including mass-balance models (Ecopath) and energetic models, integrate biomass and consumption data to quantify energy flow and nutrient cycling through the fungal channel.
