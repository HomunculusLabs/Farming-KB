Updated: 2026-04-18 Source: Permaculture Plants: A Selection (Nugent & Boniface, 2nd ed 2004) ## Overview - Family: Passifloraceae - 40+ edible species; mostly tropical to subtropical woody fruiting vines - Climb by tendrils; will climb trellis or trees - Can inhibit supporting tree growth by reducing sunlight — provide clump of trees so vine can spread - Fruit falls to ground when ripe - Named by Spanish who thought flowers resembled the passion of Christ ## Key Species ### Passiflora edulis (Purple Passionfruit / Granadilla) - Most widely known; native Brazil - Subtropical, fairly dense, evergreen vine; also red and black variations - Prefers moist, well-drained, fertile, neutral to slightly alkaline soil - Withstands occasional light frosts; long fruiting season; often two crops/year - Ripe aromatic fruits: raw, juiced, bottled, sauces, cakes, jellies; edible oil from seeds (comparable to sunflower/soybean) - Medicinal: juice used as digestive stimulant; treatment for [[lentinan-gastric-cancer-combination-therapy]] - Unripe fruit contains cyanogenic glycosides (potential toxicity) ### P. edulis var. flavicarpa (Yellow Passionfruit) - Less cold tolerant but resistant to Fusarium wilt and nematodes - More juice to pulp than purple; does well climbing tall acacia trees ### Passiflora mollissima (Banana Passionfruit / Curuba) - Native Andes; mid-level valleys 1800-3200m - Cold tolerant to -2°C; rampant vine; considered pest in Hawaii - Long elliptical yellow-orange fruit; thicker, stronger juice than purple - Best tasting juice of all passionfruit; prized wine; liqueur (crema de curuba) ### Passiflora incarnata (Maypop) - Most cold tolerant; native SE USA; dies back to ground in winter - Leaves eaten raw in salads and cooked; flowers made into syrup and eaten as vegetable - Contains passiflorine (sedative, tranquilliser) ### Passiflora ligularis (Sweet Granadilla) - Native central Mexico to western South America - Orange-yellow fruit with soft translucent perfumed pulp - Can shade and kill understorey; tolerates light frosts ### Passiflora quadrangularis (Giant Granadilla) - Native tropical America; fruit up to 15cm wide x 30cm long - Largest passionfruit; melon-like; requires warm days and nights - Unripe fruits used in soups, steamed and boiled; roots of old vines eaten like yams - Pulp contains passiflorine (sedative in large doses); extensive medicinal uses ## Growing Conditions Passionfruit vines require full sun (6+ hours daily) and well-drained soil with pH 5.5-6.5. They are shallow-rooted and benefit from thick mulch to retain moisture. In cooler climates, plant against a warm, north-facing wall. Vines need strong trellis support — a single wire at 1.5-2m height is standard for commercial plantings, while pergolas and fences work well for home gardens. ## [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]] Common pests include [[permaculture-fruit-tree-guide]]

## Cultivation Guide

### Site Selection and Climate
Passionfruit thrives in frost-free subtropical and tropical
environments but can be grown in temperate regions with careful
site selection and seasonal protection:
- Full sun exposure is essential for maximum flower production
  and fruit development; partial shade significantly reduces yield
- Protection from strong winds is important because the vine
  structure is relatively fragile and flowers can be damaged
  by desiccating winds during the critical pollination period
- Ideal temperatures range from 18-30°C; growth slows below 15°C
  and most species suffer tissue damage at temperatures below 0°C
- In marginal climates, planting against a warm south-facing wall
  with overhead protection can extend the viable growing season by
  several weeks in both spring and autumn
- Good air circulation around the trellis reduces disease pressure
  from fungal pathogens that thrive in humid, stagnant conditions

### Trellising and Support Structures
Passionfruit vines are vigorous climbers that require sturdy
permanent support structures to produce well over multiple years:
- A T-bar or wire trellis system with wires at 1.5-2m height
  provides ideal support for commercial production and easy
  harvesting from both sides of the trellis
- Individual posts should be spaced 3-5m apart with 2.5-3mm
  galvanized wire strung taut between them at the top
- The vine is trained up a vertical leader to the wire, then
  two permanent arms are allowed to run in opposite directions
  along the top wire with laterals hanging down for fruiting
- Fruit is produced on current-season growth arising from the
  permanent framework, so annual pruning of older laterals
  maintains productivity and prevents the vine from becoming
  an unmanageable tangled mass
- A strong fence, pergola, or large tree can serve as informal
  support in home garden settings, though harvest access may
  be more difficult with these structures

### Propagation Methods
Passionfruit can be propagated by seed or vegetatively, with
each method offering distinct advantages for different purposes:
- Seed propagation produces genetic variability that is useful
  for breeding programs but results in unpredictable fruit
  quality, yield, and disease resistance in the progeny
- Cuttings from mature wood root readily in 4-6 weeks under
  mist with bottom heat at 25°C, preserving exact genetic
  copies of superior parent plants
- Grafting purple passionfruit scions onto resistant yellow
  passionfruit rootstock combines the superior fruit quality
  of the scion with disease and nematode resistance of the
  rootstock, significantly extending orchard productive life
- Layering is a simple low-tech propagation method suitable
  for home gardeners; a low vine section is buried in moist
  soil and roots develop at the node within 2-3 months

### Nutrition and Irrigation
Regular nutrition and consistent moisture are critical for
sustained fruit production throughout the long growing season:
- Young vines benefit from high nitrogen fertilization to
  establish the vegetative framework during the first year
- Mature bearing vines require balanced NPK with emphasis on
  potassium for fruit quality and regular calcium to prevent
  skin disorders and improve post-harvest storage
- Mulching heavily around the root zone conserves moisture,
  suppresses weeds, and moderates soil temperature fluctuations
- Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone and
  reduces foliar disease compared to overhead sprinkler systems
- Water stress during flowering and early fruit development
  causes flower drop and produces smaller, poorly flavored fruit
