## Overview

- Family: Passifloraceae
- 40+ edible species; mostly tropical to subtropical woody fruiting vines
- Climb by tendrils; will climb trellis or trees
- Can inhibit supporting tree growth by reducing sunlight — provide clump of trees so vine can spread
- Fruit falls to ground when ripe
- Named by Spanish who thought flowers resembled the passion of Christ

## Key Species

### Passiflora edulis (Purple Passionfruit / Granadilla)
- Most widely known; native Brazil
- Subtropical, fairly dense, evergreen vine; also red and black variations
- Prefers moist, well-drained, fertile, neutral to slightly alkaline soil
- Withstands occasional light frosts; long fruiting season; often two crops/year
- Ripe aromatic fruits: raw, juiced, bottled, sauces, cakes, jellies; edible oil from seeds (comparable to sunflower/soybean)
- Medicinal: juice used as digestive stimulant; treatment for gastric cancer
- Unripe fruit contains cyanogenic glycosides (potential toxicity)

### P. edulis var. flavicarpa (Yellow Passionfruit)
- Less cold tolerant but resistant to Fusarium wilt and nematodes
- More juice to pulp than purple; does well climbing tall acacia trees

### Passiflora mollissima (Banana Passionfruit / Curuba)
- Native Andes; mid-level valleys 1800-3200m
- Cold tolerant to -2°C; rampant vine; considered pest in Hawaii
- Long elliptical yellow-orange fruit; thicker, stronger juice than purple
- Best tasting juice of all passionfruit; prized wine; liqueur (crema de curuba)

### Passiflora incarnata (Maypop)
- Most cold tolerant; native SE USA; dies back to ground in winter
- Leaves eaten raw in salads and cooked; flowers made into syrup and eaten as vegetable
- Contains passiflorine (sedative, tranquilliser)

### Passiflora ligularis (Sweet Granadilla)
- Native central Mexico to western South America
- Orange-yellow fruit with soft translucent perfumed pulp
- Can shade and kill understorey; tolerates light frosts

### Passiflora quadrangularis (Giant Granadilla)
- Native tropical America; fruit up to 15cm wide x 30cm long
- Largest passionfruit; melon-like; requires warm days and nights
- Unripe fruits used in soups, steamed and boiled; roots of old vines eaten like yams
- Pulp contains passiflorine (sedative in large doses); extensive medicinal uses

## Growing Conditions

Passionfruit vines require full sun (6+ hours daily) and well-drained soil with pH 5.5-6.5. They are shallow-rooted and benefit from thick mulch to retain moisture. In cooler climates, plant against a warm, north-facing wall. Vines need strong trellis support — a single wire at 1.5-2m height is standard for commercial plantings, while pergolas and fences work well for home gardens.
