# Psilocin Biosynthetic Delay and Accumulation Across Successive Flushes

## Overview

Bigwood and Beug (1982) discovered that [[psilocin]] is generally absent in the first
one or two flushes of *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* from a given culture, reaches maximum
concentration by the fourth flush, and then begins to decline. This delayed
accumulation pattern suggests that psilocin is not a direct [[stigmasterol-as-brassinosteroid-biosynthetic-precursor]]
to psilocybin but rather a metabolite that accumulates as the mycelium ages and
metabolic pathways shift across successive fruiting cycles.

## The Delay Pattern

The flush-by-flush data from three minicultures revealed a consistent pattern:

**Miniculture 1**: Psilocin 0.5 → 1.5 → 1.0 → 2.6 → 2.2 → 0.5 mg/g
**Miniculture 2**: Psilocin 0 → 0 → 1.7 → 2.9 → 2.2 mg/g
**Miniculture 3**: Psilocin 0 → 0 → 0.9 → 1.8 → 1.7 mg/g

Key features of the pattern:

- **Flush 1–2**: Psilocin is absent or near-zero in most cultures. This is the
  most striking finding, as psilocybin is present at substantial levels (5–13
  mg/g) during these same flushes.
- **Flush 3–4**: Psilocin rises sharply, reaching maximum levels by the fourth
  flush. This accumulation is progressive and consistent across cultures.
- **Flush 5+**: Psilocin begins to decline, though data beyond the fifth flush
  is limited because cultures typically stop fruiting after five flushes.
- **Psilocybin stability**: In contrast to psilocin, psilocybin levels show no
  consistent upward or downward trend across flushes, varying unpredictably
  between 3.2 and 13.3 mg/g.

## Biosynthetic Interpretation

The delayed appearance of psilocin has important implications for understanding
the biosynthetic relationship [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-psilocybin-and-psilocin]]:

### Psilocybin-First Hypothesis

The most widely accepted biosynthetic pathway proposes that psilocybin
(4-PO-DMT) is the primary product, synthesized from tryptophan through
tryptamine and 4-hydroxytryptamine intermediates. Psilocin (4-HO-DMT) is
then produced by dephosphorylation of psilocybin, either enzymatically
(phosphatase activity) or through acid hydrolysis during extraction.

Under this model, the flush-dependent pattern can be explained by:

1. **Early flushes**: The mycelium actively produces and accumulates psilocybin
   as the primary defense compound. Phosphatase enzyme activity is low, so
   little psilocin is generated.
2. **Later flushes**: As the mycelium ages and substrate nutrients are depleted,
   phosphatase activity increases, converting accumulated psilocybin to psilocin.
   Alternatively, degradative pathways may become more active.
3. **Decline phase**: By flush 5, total alkaloid production decreases as the
   mycelium exhausts its resources, causing both compounds to decline.

### Alternative: Independent Biosynthetic Pathways

An alternative explanation is that psilocybin and psilocin are produced by
partially independent pathways that are differentially regulated across the
fruiting cycle:

- Early in the fruiting cycle, the pathway favoring the phosphorylated form
  (psilocybin) is dominant.
- Later flushes see upregulation of the non-phosphorylated pathway (psilocin)
  or downregulation of the phosphorylation step.

This model is less parsimonious but cannot be ruled out by the available data.

## Phosphatase Activity Hypothesis

The phosphatase hypothesis proposes that the key variable controlling the
psilocybin-to-psilocin ratio is the activity of one or more phosphatase enzymes
in the mushroom tissue:

- **Young mycelium** (early flushes): Low phosphatase activity → high
  psilocybin/psilocin ratio, psilocin near zero.
- **Aging mycelium** (later flushes): Increased phosphatase activity → lower
  psilocybin/psilocin ratio, psilocin accumulates.
- **Senescing mycelium** (final flushes): Declining overall metabolism → both
  compounds decrease.

This hypothesis is consistent with the general observation that phosphatase
activity increases in aging fungal tissue as part of nutrient recycling and
autolytic processes.

## Strain-Specific Patterns

Analysis of cap and stem tissue in three strains (M.R., Equadorian, and Amazon)
provided additional detail:

- **M.R. strain**: Caps contained 6.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0 psilocin. Stems
  contained 4.2 mg/g psilocybin, 0.35 mg/g psilocin.
- **Equadorian strain**: Caps 7.6 mg/g psilocybin, 0 psilocin. Stems 4.7
  mg/g psilocybin, 0.4 mg/g psilocin.
- **Amazon strain**: Caps 5.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0.1 mg/g psilocin. Stems
  5.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0 psilocin.

The consistent absence of psilocin in first-flush caps across all strains,
combined with its presence in stems of two strains, suggests that phosphatase
activity is tissue-specific and developmentally regulated.

## Practical Implications

The delayed psilocin accumulation has implications for users and cultivators:

- **Harvest timing**: First-flush mushrooms contain almost exclusively psilocybin
  with negligible psilocin. Users seeking a specific psilocybin/psilocin ratio
  could theoretically select flush number to modulate their experience.
- **Potency assessment**: The common assumption that potency increases with
  flush number is not supported—psilocybin levels are variable but not
  trend-dependent. Only psilocin shows a clear flush-dependent pattern.
- **Drying and storage**: Psilocin is less stable than psilocybin and degrades
  more rapidly during drying and storage. The flush-dependent accumulation
  pattern may be partially obscured by post-harvest degradation.
