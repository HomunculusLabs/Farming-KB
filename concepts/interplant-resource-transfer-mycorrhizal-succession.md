## Interplant Resource Transfer and Mycorrhizal Succession

## Overview

One of the most revolutionary discoveries in mycorrhizal ecology is that mycorrhizal [[fungal-hyphae]] can interconnect individual plants, creating underground networks that facilitate the transfer of carbon, phosphorus, and other nutrients between plants. Simultaneously, mycorrhizal fungal communities undergo predictable successional patterns as forest stands develop. These two phenomena—interplant resource sharing and mycorrhizal succession—are intimately connected and have profound implications for understanding plant community dynamics and ecosystem stability.

## Interplant Carbon Transfer

### Seminal Studies by Finlay and Read
The landmark work of Finlay and Read (1986a,b,c) established that ectomycorrhizal mycelial strands can interconnect host plants and mediate bidirectional resource transfer:

- **1986a**: Demonstrated translocation of ¹⁴C-labelled carbon between plants interconnected by a common mycelium of [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]]
- **1986b**: Showed the uptake and distribution of phosphorus by mycelial strands interconnecting host plants
- **1986c**: Further confirmed phosphorus transfer capabilities of interconnecting mycelial strands

These studies provided the first conclusive experimental evidence that carbon fixed by one plant could move through shared mycorrhizal mycelium to benefit another plant.

### Earlier Evidence
Heap and Newman (1980a,b) provided earlier evidence for interplant nutrient cycling through arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae:

- **1980a**: Demonstrated physical links between roots by hyphae of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizas
- **1980b**: Showed the influence of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizas on phosphorus transfer between plants

### Specificity of Transfer
Eason et al. (1991) investigated the specificity of interplant phosphorus cycling, finding that the role of mycorrhizas in mediating this transfer varies depending on the plant species involved and their relative positions in the mycorrhizal network.

### Carbon Cost-Benefit Analysis
Fitter (1985, 1991) examined the cost-benefit economics of mycorrhizal associations, considering the carbon invested by the host plant against the nutrient benefits received. This framework is essential for understanding why interplant carbon transfer might be evolutionarily stable—it may not represent altruism but rather mutual benefit through network-level resource optimization.

## Mycorrhizal Fungal Succession

### Patterns of Successional Change
Mycorrhizal fungal communities change predictably as forest stands age:

- **Last et al. (1987)**: Described successions of sheathing (ectomycorrhizal) fungi and identified trends in species composition shifts over time
- **Dighton et al. (1986)**: Documented changes in occurrence of basidiomycete fruit bodies during forest stand development, with specific reference to mycorrhizal species
- **Dighton and Mason (1985)**: Studied mycorrhizal dynamics during forest tree development

### Post-Fire Succession
- **Baar et al. (1999)**: Showed mycorrhizal colonization of *Pinus muricata* from resistant propagules after stand-replacing wildfire, revealing how mycorrhizal communities reassemble after disturbance
- **Jonsson et al. (1999b)**: Studied ectomycorrhizal fungal communities in late-successional Swedish boreal forests and their composition following wildfire

### Continuity in Boreal Forests
Jonsson et al. (1999a) compared mycobiont diversity on seedlings and mature trees in self-regulating boreal *Pinus sylvestris* forests, demonstrating continuity of ectomycorrhizal fungi across generations—a key finding for understanding forest resilience.

### Defoliation Effects
Cullings et al. (2001) showed that defoliation affects the ectomycorrhizal community of mixed *Pinus contorta/Picea engelmannii* stands in Yellowstone Park, linking above-ground disturbance to below-ground community composition.

## Mycorrhization Helper Bacteria

### Discovery and Function
Garbaye and colleagues discovered that specific bacteria associated with the mycorrhizosphere significantly enhance mycorrhizal formation:

- **Garbaye (1991)**: Described biological interactions in the mycorrhizosphere
- **Garbaye (1994)**: Proposed "helper bacteria" as a new dimension to the mycorrhizal symbiosis
- **Garbaye and Bowen (1987, 1989)**: Showed that certain microorganisms stimulate mycorrhizal infection of *Pinus radiata*
- **Garbaye and Duponnois (1992)**: Demonstrated specificity and function of [[mycorrhization-helper-bacteria]] (MHB) in the *Pseudotsuga menziesii–Laccaria laccata* symbiosis
- **Duponnois and Garbaye (1990, 1991)**: Elucidated some mechanisms involved in growth stimulation of ectomycorrhizal fungi by bacteria

### Significance
MHB represent a third trophic level in the mycorrhizal symbiosis, potentially explaining why mycorrhizal inoculation sometimes fails in practice despite successful laboratory demonstrations.

## Host Plant Effects on Mycorrhizal Communities

### Species-Level Determinants
- **Eom et al. (2000)**: Showed that host plant species effects shape arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal communities in tallgrass prairie
- **Gehring and Whitham (1994)**: Compared ectomycorrhizae on pinyon pines across extremes of soil type and herbivory
- **Jonsson et al. (2001)**: Found context-dependent effects of ectomycorrhizal species richness on tree seedling productivity

### Functional Diversity
- **Cairney (1999)**: Discussed intraspecific physiological variation and its implications for understanding functional diversity in ectomycorrhizal fungi
- **Cairney and Burke (1996)**: Highlighted physiological heterogeneity within fungal mycelia as important for understanding ectomycorrhizal symbiosis
- **Miller (1995)**: Addressed functional diversity in fungi more broadly

## Soil Factors and Mycorrhizal Function

### Litter and Humus Manipulation
- **Baar and de Vries (1995)**: Studied effects of manipulating litter and humus layers on ectomycorrhizal colonization potential in Scots pine stands of different age
- **Baar et al. (1994)**: Found stimulatory and inhibitory effects of needle litter and grass extracts on the growth of some ectomycorrhizal fungi

### Salinity and Mycorrhizae
- **Allen and Cunningham (1983)**: Examined effects of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae on *Distichlis spicata* under three salinity levels
- **Juniper and Abbott (1993)**: Studied vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizas and soil salinity interactions

### Water Relations
- **Augé (2001)**: Reviewed water relations, drought, and vesicular-[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] comprehensively
- **Cruz et al. (2000)**: Showed effects of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] on tree growth, leaf water potential, and ethylene levels under water-stress conditions

### Waterlogging Effects
Gadgil (1972) documented the effect of waterlogging on mycorrhizas of radiata pine and Douglas fir, highlighting how soil physical conditions influence mycorrhizal function.

## Resource Competition Between Plants and Microbes
Kaye and Hart (1997) reviewed competition for nitrogen between plants and soil microorganisms, establishing the theoretical framework within which interplant resource transfer through mycorrhizal networks must be understood. The balance between plant and microbial nitrogen demand determines how much nitrogen is available for interplant transfer through mycorrhizal hyphae.
