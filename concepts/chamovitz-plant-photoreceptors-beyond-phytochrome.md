# Plant Photoreceptors Beyond Phytochrome

While phytochrome is the most famous plant photoreceptor, plants possess a sophisticated array of light-sensing proteins that allow them to perceive their environment with remarkable nuance. Beyond the red/far-red phytochrome system, plants use at least four [[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages]] of photoreceptors, each tuned to different wavelengths and serving distinct physiological functions.

## The Photoreceptor Families

Plants detect light across the [[electromagnetic-spectrum-plant-light-perception]] using several families of photoreceptor proteins. These include:

- **Phytochromes** — detect red and far-red light (600–750 nm), regulating photoperiodism, [[cannabis-seed-germination-methods]], [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]], and flowering
- **Cryptochromes** — detect blue and UV-A light (320–500 nm), involved in [[plant-circadian-rhythms]], de-etiolation, and flowering time
- **Phototropins** — detect blue light (390–500 nm), mediating phototropism, chloroplast movement, and stomatal opening
- **UVR8** — detects UV-B light (280–315 nm), triggering protective UV-responses
- **ZEITLUPE family** — blue-light receptors involved in circadian clock regulation

Each photoreceptor family uses a different chromophore (light-absorbing molecule) to capture photons. Phytochromes use an open-chain tetrapyrrole called phytochromobilin. Cryptochromes are related to DNA photolyase enzymes and use flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) as their chromophore. Phototropins bind flavin mononucleotide (FMN).

## Cryptochromes: The Blue-Light Timekeepers

Cryptochromes were first identified in [[arabidopsis-thaliana]] through mutants that showed altered flowering time. The name derives from the Greek "kryptos" (hidden) because these receptors were cryptic — they were present but their function was hidden until genetic screening revealed them.

Cryptochromes serve two fundamental roles in plants:

1. **Circadian clock entrainment** — Plants, like animals, have internal 24-hour biological clocks. Cryptochromes help synchronize this clock to the external light/dark cycle. Without cryptochromes, the plant's clock drifts, leading to mistimed physiological processes.

2. **De-etiolation** — When a seedling emerges from soil into light, it must rapidly transition from skotomorphogenic (dark-growth) to photomorphogenic (light-growth) development. Cryptochromes help inhibit stem elongation, promote chlorophyll synthesis, and open cotyledons.

Cryptochromes are evolutionarily related to photolyase, an enzyme that repairs UV-damaged DNA using blue light energy. This shared ancestry suggests that cryptochromes may have evolved from an ancient DNA repair mechanism into a light-signaling role.

## Phototropins: The Directional Sensors

Phototropins are the photoreceptors responsible for phototropism — the bending of plants toward light. Charles Darwin first demonstrated that the tip of the plant "sees" light and sends a signal to the stem to bend. The molecular basis of this perception involves phototropins.

Phototropins are unique among photoreceptors because they undergo autophosphorylation — they add phosphate groups to themselves upon absorbing blue light. This self-modification triggers a signaling cascade that ultimately causes differential [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]] on the shaded versus illuminated sides of the stem.

Two phototropins exist in most plants: phot1 and phot2. Phot1 is sensitive to low-intensity blue light and mediates the primary phototropic response. Phot2 responds to higher light intensities and controls chloroplast relocation — moving chloroplasts to optimize light capture under low light or avoid photodamage under high light.

The phototropin signaling pathway involves:

1. Blue light absorption by the FMN chromophore
2. Conformational change in the phototropin protein
3. Autophosphorylation of the receptor
4. Activation of downstream signaling components
5. Redistribution of auxin (the plant hormone) to the shaded side
6. Differential cell elongation causing the bend

## UVR8: The UV-B Sensor

UV-B radiation (280–315 nm) can damage DNA, proteins, and lipids. Plants have evolved a specific photoreceptor called UVR8 to detect UV-B and activate protective responses. UVR8 is unusual because it does not use a traditional chromophore — instead, it uses intrinsic tryptophan amino acids as light-absorbing pigments.

Upon UV-B absorption, UVR8 undergoes a conformational change, monomerizing from a dimer to an active monomer. This active form moves to the nucleus and activates genes involved in:

- Flavonoid biosynthesis (UV-absorbing pigments)
- DNA repair [[ligninolytic-enzyme-production-bioremediation]]
- Antioxidant synthesis
- Cell wall thickening

The discovery of UVR8 resolved a long-standing question in photobiology: how do plants specifically perceive UV-B radiation separately from UV-A and blue light?

## ZEITLUPE and the Circadian Clock

The ZEITLUPE (ZTL) family of blue-light photoreceptors plays a specialized role in circadian clock regulation. ZTL proteins contain both a blue-light sensing domain (LOV domain, similar to phototropins) and an F-box domain that targets proteins for degradation.

ZTL functions as a blue-light-regulated timer: it uses light information to time the degradation of TOC1, a central component of the circadian oscillator. By controlling when TOC1 is removed, ZTL helps set the pace of the plant's [[chrysanthemum-coronarium]].

## Cross-Talk Between Photoreceptor Systems

Plant photoreceptors do not operate in isolation. They form an interconnected signaling network with extensive cross-talk:

- Cryptochromes and phytochromes co-regulate flowering time through shared downstream targets
- Phototropins and cryptochromes both respond to blue light but activate different pathways
- UVR8 signaling interacts with both phytochrome and cryptochrome pathways
- The circadian clock, regulated by multiple photoreceptors, feeds back to modulate photoreceptor sensitivity

This integration allows plants to produce coherent responses to the complex spectral quality of natural light. Sunlight contains all wavelengths simultaneously, and the plant must interpret this composite signal to make appropriate developmental decisions.

## Photoreceptor Evolution

The photoreceptor families found in plants have deep [[tryptamine]]:

- Phytochromes exist in bacteria, algae, and all land plants
- Cryptochromes are found in plants, animals (where they function in circadian rhythm), and some fungi
- Phototropins are primarily found in green algae and land plants
- UVR8 appears to be unique to the plant lineage
