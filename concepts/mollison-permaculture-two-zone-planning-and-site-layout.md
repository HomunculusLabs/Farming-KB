## Mollison Zone Planning and Site Layout in Permaculture Two

The whole key to efficient energy planning, which is in fact efficient economic planning, is the zonation and radial or sectoral placement of plants, animal ranges, and structures. Permaculture Two provides a detailed framework for zone-based planning on real landscapes with slope.

## The Importance of Starting at the Doorstep

There is no doubt at all that the place to start is at the doorstep. If you do not have the doorstep controlled, there is not much hope for the back paddock, or the back fence. Zoning, measured as distance from centre, is decided on two factors:
(a) the number of times you need to visit the plant, animal or structure
(b) the number of times the plant, animal or structure needs you to visit it

## Zone Characteristics

### Zone I: House Climate and Domestic Intensive Gardens
Main design for house climate, domestic water, and intensive gardens. Features complete sheet mulch, intensive pruning (cup or espalier), selected dwarf or multi-graft trees. Water provision through rainwater tanks, wells, bores, and reticulation. Structures include house and glasshouse integration and storage.

### Zone II: Small Domestic Sufficiency
Main design for small domestic stock and orchard. Features spot mulch and tree guards for establishment, pyramid and built trellis pruning, grafted varieties. Water through earth tanks and fire control dams. Structures include greenhouse and barns, poultry sheds, and field shelter.

### Zone III: Main Cropping
Main design for main crops, forage, stored food, and forestry. Features soil conditioning and green mulch for establishment, unpruned and natural trellis trees, selected seedlings for later grafts. Water storage in soils and dams. Structures include feed store and field shelter grown as hedgerow and woodlot.

### Zone IV: Gathering and Forage
Main design for gathering, forage, forestry, and pasture. Features soil conditioning only for establishment, seedlings thinned to selected varieties, or managed by browse. Water from dams, rivers, bores, and wind pumps. Structures are field shelter.

## Landscape Profile and Placement

On a property with reasonable slope, the placement strategy follows the landform:

### High Ground (Ridge and Plateau)
Storage dams (turkey nests) to take overflow from high tanks. Diversion channels around high ridges. Covered tanks can be built as basement or foundation of buildings, forming heat/cold buffers. Fire protection water storage. Dry-country permaculture plants needing only spot watering for establishment.

### Midslope (Keyline Area)
The most productive and versatile zone. Bulk domestic water from high dams on the keyline. Dwelling sites behind lower dams for fire protection. Waste water systems leading to small pondings for algae and nutrient recycling. Access tracks embodying drainage to midslope dams. Barn-to-house garden using gravity to move mulch and manures downhill.

### Low Ground (Plains and Valleys)
Large shallow dams for cheap water storage. No-tillage crops, copses, and hedgerows. The plains are most resistant to water damage except for evaporated salt. Scattered leguminous trees at about 10 to 12 per hectare greatly assist in nutrient recycling and soil stability.

## What is Worth Main Cropping

Only a few plant species are worth extensive main cropping. Three main considerations apply:
1. Main crop which needs little attention after establishment (potatoes, corn, pumpkin)
2. And which is easy to harvest, store and use
3. Also may form a staple in diet

Commercially, additional considerations include:
4. High economic value even if difficult to harvest (strawberries, raspberries)
5. Hard to keep crops (melons, peaches)
6. Rare but in wide demand (ginseng, asparagus)
7. Particularly suited to site (cactus, fig, water chestnut, cranberry)

## Tree Crop Selection by Zone

The same criteria apply to tree crops, which can always be interplanted with annual crops while time passes:
1. Low attention: plums, hardy fruits and vines
2. Easy to store: nuts and easily dried fruits
3. Staple food value: nuts again and high food value fruits
4. High economic value: cherries, crocus for saffron
5. Local soft fruits: paw-paw, raspberries
6. Special products: spices, medicines, dyes, and oils
7. Site-specialized: sugar maple, cider gum, pistachios

## Settlement Between Two Economies

Slope gives immense planning advantages; there is hardly a viable human settlement not supported by the failing petrol economy that is not sited on those critical junctions of two natural economies, between foothill forest and plains, or on the edge of plain and marsh, land and estuary, or some combination of all of these.

Successful and permanent settlements have always been able to draw from the resources of at least two environments. Similarly, any settlement which fails to preserve natural benefits, and for example clears all forests, is bent on eventual extinction.

The easy, rounded ridges of non-eroded lower slopes and their foothill pediments are a prime site for settlement. They allow filtration of wastes, inseparable from large populations, through lowland forest and lake, and the conversion of toxic wastes into useful timber, trees, fruits, and aquatic life.

## Design Criteria for Layout

The initial placement of structures, earthworks, and plants should be so arranged that any other alternative energy system is still available for later inclusion. In short, don't cut out the sun as a source of energy, and keep the water running downhill; store it in the soil, and release it clean. Let heated air and water rise, as they will, and forget about pumps to force the reverse of natural flows.

The simple way to check on any design is to ask: Why did you put that structure or plant there?
