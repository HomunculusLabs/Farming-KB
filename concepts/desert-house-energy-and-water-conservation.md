## Desert House Energy and Water Conservation

Energy and [[permaculture-design]] for desert dwellings integrates energy harvesting, water recycling, and conservation into the fabric of the house itself, drawing on both traditional techniques and modern technology to minimise waste and maximise self-reliance.

## Home Energy Conservation

### Solar Water Heating

Producing hot water in deserts is straightforward given the abundant solar radiation. Three effective approaches exist:

1. **Solar attic heater**: A water tank in an insulated attic space with a glass cover and aluminium foil over thick insulation. This acts as a bulk water heater, providing reliable hot water from solar gain alone.

2. **Grid pipe in soot-covered sand**: A grid of plastic pipe laid in soot-covered sand, itself placed under glass. The soot absorbs maximum solar radiation, heating the water in the pipes. This is a low-cost, highly effective approach using readily available materials.

3. **Roof-mounted pipe coil**: A simple coil of pipe on a metal roof provides enough warm to hot water for household use. The metal roof acts as the solar collector, and the coil configuration maximises heat transfer.

### Solar Ponds for Community Heating

For groups of 5–10 houses, a **solar pond** provides a shared heating solution:

- Dimensions: approximately 4–5 m per side, 1–2 m deep
- The pond operates over a wide climatic range
- Highly efficient for heat capture
- Supplies both hot water and house heating
- Space heat and hot water together constitute approximately 80% of modern housing energy needs
- Low efficiency for electricity generation via turbine
- Needs reliable domestic-scale models for deployment in undeveloped desert areas

### Electricity from Photovoltaics

- A solitary photovoltaic cell or bank of cells provides the modest electricity needed for lights and electronics
- Desert solar radiation levels make photovoltaics particularly effective
- Cooking typically remains via oil, gas, or modest fires

### Firewood from Wastewater

All desert settlements should, as a matter of legislated planning, divert wastewater to firewood plantations:

- Water from washing, toilets, and sinks in a modern town such as Alice Springs could provide all firewood for cooking
- Careful use in trickle [[irrigation-systems]] is essential
- Stored in non-leaking, covered cisterns
- Hardy desert trees on a broader scale establish long-term firewood reserves
- This closes the loop between water use and energy production

## House Water Conservation

### Greywater Recycling

Modest water use is achievable through simple greywater recycling systems:

- **Efficient shower heads** reduce water consumption for washing
- **Shower and handbasin water** is diverted first to:
  - The flush tank of toilets (where sewage systems are provided), or
  - The garden (where dry pit toilets are used)
- The shower and handbasin can be raised a few steps above floor level to gravity-feed a low-level toilet cistern
- This eliminates the need for fresh water to flush toilets

### Rainwater Harvesting from Roofs

Wherever possible, spouting from all roof areas should run to covered concrete tanks:

- These tanks are common in Australia but rare elsewhere globally
- A rare isolated house in dryland Australia typically boasts at least 5,000 litres of tank capacity
- Located on the shade side of the house under trellis, such tanks provide cool drinking and cooking water
- Covered tanks prevent evaporation and contamination
- In areas of slight rainfall, flat parapet concreted roof areas maximise collection area

### Dry Pit Toilets

The pit toilet popular in central Australia works well for years without water:
- Simple excavation with composting
- Downdraught ventilation and solar chimneys prevent odour
- Deep drill-hole variants (20–30 m) in soft rock areas last for years
- Best occasionally pumped out to establish trees around the site
- Eliminates water use for sanitation entirely

## Roof and Parapet Design

In areas of slight rainfall, flat parapet concreted roof areas are preferred for their multiple functions:

### Built-In Roof Features

- **Pigeon lofts**: For eggs, squabs (young pigeons), and manure production
- **Laundry troughs and drying lines**: Taking advantage of sun and airflow
- **Header tanks**: For 1–2 weeks water supply, fitted with float valves
- **Wind towers**: With scoops, slats, lattice, sails, or aerofoils to catch local winds for cooling
- **Evening rest areas**: Outdoor passive recreation spaces with hammocks
- **Grain drying flats**: Drying racks, bulk food stores, or bins
- **Winter hay storage** (in cold climates): Heat and partial composting radiates through the ceiling to rooms below

### The Caspian Haystack Model

In the severe winters of the Caspian area, peasants make haystacks on the roof:
- Protects hay from goats
- Provides thick insulation for winter warmth
- Compost heat radiates from the ceiling to rooms below
- Demonstrates the multi-functional potential of roof spaces

## Insulation Strategies

### Floor Insulation

- **Edge-insulated concrete slabs** absorb and store solar heat during the day, releasing it at night
- With rigid foam insulation, trenches cut at or below foundations (to 0.5–1 m deep) effectively insulate the whole floor/earth mass under the house
- This simple strategy buffers both heat and cold extremes without firewood
- Plastic bags of straw or sawdust have been used for ground insulation in Nepal

### Trombe Walls

Where insulated slabs were not built into the original dwelling:
- A thick vertical wall of mud brick standing 0.5 m inside a room
- Faced with a glass window
- Acts as a heater long after dark through the Trombe wall effect
- Thermal mass absorbs daytime heat and releases it slowly through the night

### Wall and Ceiling Insulation

- Straw, thatch, wool, feathers, cork, woven materials, paper, and cardboard are adequate insulators
- Thick vines over walls and roofs serve as **active cooling systems** — affordable by any person
- Insulated ceilings or roof areas are essential (can be trellised or carpeted with thick vines)
- White-painted exterior walls reflect excessive heat

## The Sun-Side Glasshouse

A glasshouse on the sunward (equator-facing) side of a desert dwelling serves multiple functions:

### Summer Function
- Vents hot air to create cross-ventilation through the house
- Draws cool air in from shade-side courtyards or tunnels

### Winter Function
- Creates a winter heat source by trapping solar radiation
- Can be closed off to retain heat inside the house

### Agricultural Function
- Starts spring plants early in the year
