# Goat Dairy and Cheese Production

Goats are among the most versatile and productive animals in permaculture
systems. They are efficient converters of browse and pasture into milk, meat,
and manure. Mollison values goats for their ability to thrive on marginal land,
their preference for browsing (which complements grazing livestock), and their
relatively small scale, which suits homestead and small-farm systems. Goat
dairy production integrates [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]]
with good quality pasture and supplementary feeding. Relatively susceptible to
heat and cold stress.

**Toggenburg:** Chocolate-brown Swiss breed with good milk production (2 to 4
litres daily) and greater hardiness than Saanen. Adaptable to varied climates
and terrain. Excellent forage converters.

**Anglo-Nubian:** Large, distinctive breed with long pendulous ears. Produces
rich, high-butterfat milk (2 to 3 litres daily) ideal for cheese and yoghurt.
Heat-tolerant and well-suited to subtropical and tropical climates.

**British Alpine:** Black and white breed with reliable production (2 to 3.5
litres daily) and good temperament. Hardy and adaptable to a range of
conditions.

**Miniature breeds:** Nigerian Dwarf and Pygmy goats produce 1 to 2 litres
daily and are well-suited to small homesteads. Their smaller size requires
less feed and housing space while still providing meaningful dairy production.

## Milking Routines

Consistent milking routines are essential for maximum production and animal
health. Does are milked twice daily, ideally at 12-hour intervals. Milking
should occur in a clean, calm environment. Does produce milk for approximately
305 days after kidding, followed by a 60-day dry period before the next
kidding. Staggered kidding dates ensure year-round milk supply.

Before milking, the udder is cleaned with warm water and dried. Sanitary
milking practices include washing hands, cleaning equipment, and filtering
milk immediately. Milk should be cooled to 4 degrees Celsius within one hour
of milking to maintain quality and extend [[psilocybin-shelf-life-stability]]. Stainless steel equipment
is preferred for ease of cleaning and sanitation.

## Housing Design

Goat housing must provide shelter from rain, wind, and temperature extremes
while allowing adequate ventilation. Mollison recommends housing oriented to
face the morning sun, with solid walls on the prevailing wind side and open or
mesh walls on the sheltered side. Minimum space requirements are 2 to 3 square
metres per adult animal indoors, with additional outdoor access.

Raised flooring (slatted timber or mesh) keeps goats clean and dry, reducing
mastitis and hoof problems. A separate kidding pen provides a safe, warm
environment for newborn kids. Feed troughs and water containers should be
designed to prevent contamination with manure. Hay racks keep feed off the
ground. A milking stand with head restraint facilitates safe, efficient
milking. Housing should be located on well-drained ground, uphill from
waterways and food production areas.

## Forage Systems for Dairy Goats

Goats are natural browsers, preferring leaves, twigs, and shoots over grass.
This browsing behaviour makes them ideal for integrating with [[permaculture-design]], dairy goat forage systems combine: managed browsing in
orchards and woodlots (strip grazing with electric fencing to prevent bark
damage to valuable trees), [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] on mixed pastures (see
[[permaculture-tagasaste]], leucaena, mulberry, willow, poplar,
comfrey, and various leguminous shrubs for browse. Pasture species such as
clover, chicory, plantain, and lucerne provide high-protein grazing. Grain
supplementation during peak lactation supports milk production when forage
quality is insufficient.
