# Iconic Types of Mushroom Mycelium in Culture

Each mushroom species produces a recognizable type of mycelium whose variations fall within a defined range of expressions. Within a species, multitudes of strains can differ dramatically in their appearance. In culture, mushroom strains reveal much about the portion of the mushroom life cycle that is invisible to the mere forager of wild mushrooms. The range of characteristics — changes in form, color, rate of growth, fragrance, and even volunteer fruitings — defines the strain's "personality" and provides critical information to the cultivator.

## Linear Mycelium

Linear mycelium is arranged as diverging, longitudinal strands. Typically, the mycelium emanates from the center of the petri dish as a homogeneously forming mat. This is one of the most common mycelial types.

**Species examples:**
- **Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes) — classic linear pattern
- **Oyster mushrooms** (Pleurotus ostreatus) — initially linear
- **Morels** (Morchella spp.) — produce a rapidly growing, finely linear mycelium that is so fine during the first few days that it is nearly invisible, detected only by tilting the petri dish back and forth to see the fine strands on the reflective sheen of the agar medium. Morel mycelium eventually thickens with time.

## Rhizomorphic Mycelium

Often called "ropey," rhizomorphic mycelium is composed of braided, twisted strands of varying diameters. This type of mycelium supports primordia formation and is considered a sign of vigorous, fruiting-capable mycelium.

**Key characteristics:**
- Its presence is encouraged by selecting rhizomorphic zones for further transfer
- The disappearance of rhizomorphs is an indication of loss of vigor and genetic decline
- Some types take on a highly reflective, silk-like sheen

**Species examples:**
- **Lion's Mane** (Hericium erinaceus)
- **King Stropharia** (Stropharia rugoso-annulata)
- **Button Mushrooms** (Agaricus brunnescens, A. bitorquis)
- **Psilocybe** species (P. cubensis, P. cyanescens)
- **Clustered Woodlovers** (Hypholoma capnoides, H. sublateritium)

Rhizomorphic mycelium often diverges from cottony mycelium soon after spore germination, as the dikaryotic mycelium establishes itself and begins organizing into fruiting-capable structures. The transition from cottony to rhizomorphic growth is a hallmark of dikaryotization — the point at which compatible monokaryons fuse and form the fertile, fruiting-capable mycelium.

## Cottony (Tomentose) Mycelium

Looking like tufts of cotton, cottony mycelium is nearly aerial in its growth. The mycelium is fluffy and raised above the agar surface. Mycologists commonly call this type tomentose.

**Species examples:**
- **Oyster Mushrooms** (Pleurotus species)
- **Shaggy Manes** (Coprinus comatus)
- **Hen-of-the-Woods** (Grifola frondosa)

**Important relationship to strain health:** When a rhizomorphic mycelium degenerates with age, tomentose (cottony) formations typically take over. The transition from rhizomorphic to cottony growth is a warning sign of genetic decline and reduced fruiting potential. [[growing-gourmet-strain-senescence-degeneration|Strain senescence]] often manifests first as a loss of rhizomorphic character.

## Zonate Mycelium

Cottony mycelium often shows concentric circles of dense and light growth, or zones. Zonate mycelium is characteristic of natural changes in the age of the mycelial mat.

**Pattern characteristics:**
- The newest mycelium, on the peripheral edge of the culture, is usually light in color
- The more aged mycelium, towards the center of the culture, becomes strongly pigmented
- Zonations can also be a function of natural circadian cycles, even when cultures are incubated in laboratory conditions
- These rings of growth and pigmentation provide a visual record of the culture's developmental history

## Aerial Mycelium

Aerial mycelium rises significantly above the surface of the agar medium, forming fluffy, three-dimensional structures. It is most pronounced in vigorously growing young cultures. The degree of aerial growth is a useful indicator of strain vitality and is one of the 28 features used in [[growing-gourmet-strain-evaluation-28-features|strain evaluation]].

## Mycelial Color Changes

As mycelium ages, predictable color changes occur that serve as diagnostic indicators:

- **Agrocybe aegerita** (Black Poplar) — white at first, soon becoming spotted brown, eventually tan brown
- **Hypholoma sublateritium** (Kuritake) — white and cottony, soon linearly rhizomorphic with a silk-like sheen, developing tawny or rusty brown discolorations with age
- **Pholiota nameko** (Nameko) — whitish and radial at first, becoming light orangish or tawny from the center
- **Pleurotus djamor** (Pink Oyster) — white at first, developing strong pinkish tones as primordia form, especially at sites of primordia formation
- **Morchella** species — initially gray, soon thickening to gray-brown with brown orangish to golden nodules (micro-sclerotia)

These color changes are not random — they reflect metabolic activity within the mycelium, including the accumulation of pigments, the production of secondary metabolites, and the formation of specialized structures like [[growing-gourmet-sclerotia-formation-biology-mushrooms|sclerotia]].

## Metabolite Production

Many strains produce visible metabolic by-products during growth that serve as additional identification markers:

- **Oyster species** — may exude a yellowish-orange metabolite and form thick, peelable mycelial mats
- **Hypsizygus tessulatus** — does not exude the yellowish-orange metabolite typical of Pleurotus
- **Morel mycelium** — stains the nutrified agar media dark brown as it matures, visible from underneath the petri dish
- **Pink Oyster** — a milky gray metabolic exudate collects at the bottom of incubation containers

## Fragrance Signatures

Each species and strain produces a characteristic scent that experienced cultivators use for identification:

- **Agrocybe aegerita** — mealy, farinaceous, not particularly pleasant
- **Hypholoma sublateritium** — pleasant, sweet, reminiscent of freshly rained-on forest, similar to King Stropharia
- **Pholiota nameko** — musty, farinaceous, not pleasant
- **Hypsizygus tessulatus** — sweet, rich, oyster-esque but not anise-like
- **Pleurotus djamor** — initially acrid with a peculiar "bite," developing a rotting fish-like odor when stored
- **Morchella** species — pleasant, smelling like crushed fresh Morel mushrooms

Fragrance should always be assessed by opening the petri dish and wafting the air towards the nose. Direct sniffing of culture dishes is discouraged as it may introduce contaminants and poses a small risk of inhaling spores of contaminant molds.

## Rate of Growth as a Diagnostic Feature

Growth rate is one of the most quantifiable mycelial characteristics. Each strain establishes a characteristic radial growth rate (measured in millimeters per day) that serves as a baseline. Deviations from this baseline are among the earliest indicators of [[growing-gourmet-strain-senescence-degeneration|strain senescence]] or contamination. Cultivators should record growth rates at each [[growing-gourmet-stamets-p-value-strain-age-system|P-value]] transfer to build a historical profile.

## Practical Significance for Cultivators

Understanding iconic mycelial types is essential for several aspects of cultivation:

1. **Contamination detection:** Knowing what healthy mycelium looks like allows immediate identification of foreign growth
2. **Strain selection:** Rhizomorphic sectors should be selected for transfer as they correlate with higher fruiting potential
3. **Senescence monitoring:** The shift from rhizomorphic to cottony growth signals genetic decline
4. **Species verification:** Mycelial appearance can confirm species identity before committing to large-scale spawn production
5. **Volunteer primordia:** Some strains spontaneously form miniature mushrooms on agar, indicating strong fruiting genetics
