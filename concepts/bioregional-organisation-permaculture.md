## Bioregional Organisation in Permaculture

[[bioregional-organisation]] is a foundational [[new-united-nations-permaculture-social-strategy]] in [[permaculture-design]] that structures communities around natural ecological boundaries rather than political or administrative borders. A bioregional association is defined as an association of the residents of a natural and identifiable region.

## Defining a Bioregion

A bioregion can be defined by several factors, sometimes used in combination:

- **Watershed boundaries**: natural drainage basins and catchment areas
- **Tribal or language boundaries**: remnant or existing indigenous territories
- **Town boundaries**: established municipal or settlement borders
- **Suburban streets or districts**: neighbourhood-level identification
- **Combinations of the above factors**

Many people identify with their local region and know its boundaries intuitively. The acid test of a bioregion is that it is recognised as such by its inhabitants.

## Bioregional Centrality vs. Global Outreach

There is an inherent tension between living responsibly within a region (bioregional centrality) and integrating with people in other places (global outreach). The permaculture approach resolves this by advocating that people "think globally and act locally" but also "act and think globally and locally."

The region serves as a home address where culture develops and bioregional networks operate. Through global associations and "families of common interest," people cross regional, state, and national borders to establish multicultural alliances.

### Tribal Precedents

Tribal maps often defined bioregions effectively through totemic and clan systems. Totems and "skins" (clan groups) of tribes might take as their totemic mothers a particular tree or animal, which was itself limited in distribution by topographic and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-climatic-factors-in-design]]. Other groups occupied ecologies of grasslands, stony deserts, swamps, or mountain ridges. Minority language groups such as the Saamen, Basque, and Pitjatjantjara claim territories that are ancient and specific to their life mode.

### Intercultural Exchange

Intermarriage, visits, mutual trade and aid, skills exchange, and educational exchange between regions of very different cultures enriches both. This is contrasted with "integration" (described as bureaucratic genocide) promulgated by majority groups who disallow language use and cultural life to minorities. Reciprocal education values both sets of knowledge and world concepts, respecting others' lifestyles.

## Scale and Structure

### Population Scale

Ideally, a bioregion is limited to between 7,000 and 40,000 people. Of these, perhaps only a hundred will be initially interested in any regional association, and even fewer will be active. The work of the bioregional group is to:

1. Assess the natural, technical, service, and financial resources of the region
2. Identify areas where leakage of resources (water, soil, money, talent) leave the region
3. Point the way to local self-reliance strategies

### Specialist Associations

People can contribute accounts of their specialities as they apply to the region, with regional newsletters publishing results. Specific associations form to deal with targeted areas:

- **Food**: Consumer-producer associations, gardening or soil societies
- **Shelter**: Owner-builder associations
- **Energy**: [[appropriate-technology]] associations
- **Finance**: An "earthbank" association
- Additional groups for crafts, music, markets, livestock, and nature study

### The Regional Office

The bioregional office requires 4-6 people acting as consultants and coordinators, with others on call when needed. All other associations can use the office for registration, address, phone, and newsletter services, paying a fee for usage. The office serves multiple functions:

- **Land access centre**: operating land access strategies
- **Leasehold and title register**: servicing agreements for clubs and societies
- **Community self-funding schemes**: collecting monies for trusts and societies
- **Contact centre**: linking to other regions as a trade and coordination centre
- **Single point of contact**: making it easy for residents and visitors to access all services
- **Shared services**: an accountant on call can contract to service many groups
- **Educational hub**: inviting craftspeople or lecturers to address interest groups locally

## Evolving Policy from Working Solutions

A common global policy starts with a general ethic and proceeds to specific policies for specific cultures, regions, and landscapes. The structure for gathering common policy follows a clear methodology:

1. **Define the problem or concern**, giving weight to priorities on a scale from 1-10
2. **State intent of policy** for the region — what it is intended to achieve (the principles)
3. **Collect proven strategies** — essentially a set of case histories of working solutions
4. **Frame policies** based on all successful strategies

Policy priorities are set in rough order, weighted for:
- Urgency
- Public cost or loss of wealth
- General or [[pf-tek-global-spread-and-cultural-impact]] of the problem
- Long-term effect
- Threats to basic resources or life systems

Policies should not, in the first place, try to address purely local or trivial matters unless as a case history applicable to a broad principle. The approach prioritises solutions that have been demonstrated to work, building policy from evidence rather than ideology.

## Green Party Affiliations

Two strategies exist for ensuring political changes that bring ecological change:

1. **Mobilise ground support** in every electorate where a candidate of any party takes a stand on good ecology or against nuclear and polluting industry
2. **Form a local Ecological or Green Party** or a bioregional group

Affiliation among intentional groups, subscribing to a common policy, makes it easier to evolve comprehensive policy. Common policy leaves room for local issues but provides strong principles for guidance. The broad aims are ecological, socially egalitarian, grassroots democratic, and non-growth oriented.

This bioregional framework provides the organisational template through which permaculture's ethical [[mollison-open-living-systems-and-design-principles]] are implemented at community and regional scale, connecting local action to global networks of people committed to earth care.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
## Further Considerations
Ongoing research and field trials continue to expand our understanding of this subject.
Practical experience combined with systematic observation yields the most reliable insights.

## Future Directions
Emerging approaches and technologies offer new opportunities for advancement.
Collaborative knowledge sharing accelerates progress across related domains.
