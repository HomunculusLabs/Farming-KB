# Flowering Tree Permaculture Institute: Desert Restoration Case Study

One of the most compelling examples in Gaia's Garden is the story of
Roxanne Swentzell's Flowering Tree Permaculture Institute near Santa Fe,
New Mexico. This case study demonstrates how permaculture design
principles can transform a barren, eroded desert plot into a lush, self-
sustaining food forest supporting hundreds of species on just one-eighth
of an acre. The techniques used here are applicable to any difficult
site, not just desert environments.

## The Starting Point: Bare Ground

In 1986, Roxanne moved onto a parcel of bare land on the Santa Clara
Pueblo homelands. She describes the place as "no trees, no plants, no
animals, just pounded-down dirt and lots of ants." The challenges were
severe:

- Ninety-degree heat and searing glare from bare, eroded hillsides
- Dry winds from overgrazed, scoured hills that burned up seedlings
- Winter freezes that killed any surviving plants
- No reliable water source beyond a weekly irrigation from the acequia
  (traditional ditch) by tribal agreement

## The Design Team

Roxanne was joined by local permaculture designer Joel Glanzberg, who
helped her develop techniques for gardening in the harsh desert
environment. Together, they applied a many-pronged strategy combining
multiple [[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]] to build
fertile soil, cast shade, damp temperature swings, and conserve water.

## Techniques Applied

### Shade and Microclimate Creation

The first priority was creating sheltered, moist microclimates where
seedlings could survive:

- Rocks and logs were dragged in to shade vulnerable seedlings
- [[gaias-garden-swales-and-berms-construction]] were dug as
  shallow ditches to catch precious rainwater
- These swales created sheltered, moist pockets where plants could
  establish

### Rapid Biomass Generation

To cast much-needed shade and generate organic matter, Joel and Roxanne
planted just about any useful drought-tolerant plant they could find,
both native and exotic:

- Fast-growing pioneer species provided quick shade
- Thirstier species were placed within reach of the irrigation ditch
- Every plant was selected for multiple functions

### Soil Building

Manure and [[gaias-garden-mulch-plants-ecological-garden]] were hauled in to build rich soil that would hold moisture
through drought. This initial investment in soil was critical -- without
reliable water from the acequia, the garden could not have been
established in the desert heat.

### Phased Planting

Once hardy young trees and shrubs had taken hold, more delicate plants
were set in their shade. This [[gaias-garden-succession-planting]]
approach mirrored nature's own pattern of ecological succession, with
tough pioneer species creating conditions for more demanding plants.

### Edible Hedge Windbreak

A blended hedge of berry bushes and small fruit trees was planted along
the north border to provide the family with food while blocking the
winds that roared down the nearby canyon. This
[[gaias-garden-deer-deflecting-food-hedges]]
combined wind protection with food production.

## The Transformation

### After Four Years

Joel Glanzberg stood in the same spot where a photograph had been taken
four years earlier showing barren desert. The transformation was dramatic:
a lush oasis of greenery had replaced the bare dirt and rocks. Trees had
grown from nothing to substantial size, and the landscape was beginning
to function as a self-sustaining ecosystem.

### After Eight Years (Hemenway's Visit)

When Toby Hemenway visited, the landscape was eight years old:

- Five hundred species on one-eighth of an acre
- Trees as tall as the two-story house, where none had existed before
- Food dropping from trees faster than it could be harvested
- Birds not seen for years in the area were making a home in the yard
- The garden had become almost too lush: "Here in the high desert was
  almost too much water and shade"
- A constant rustling and chirping of birds enveloped all sides
- Metallic-sheened beneficial wasps dove into blossoms, and butterflies
  of all sizes soared from flower to leaf

### The Self-Sustaining Garden

Roxanne described the goal: "We've tried to make it a self-sufficient
place that will take care of us while we take care of it. So we grow
whatever we can that will survive in this climate." The garden had
transitioned from requiring intensive input to largely maintaining itself.

## Key Lessons from This Case Study

### Start with Tough Pioneers

Do not begin with delicate species. Plant the most drought-tolerant,
cold-hardy, and vigorous plants first. Let them create the conditions
(microclimate, soil, moisture) that more demanding species need.

### Multiple Strategies Create Synergy

No single technique would have been sufficient. The combination of
swales for water, rocks for shade, fast-growing biomass plants for
organic matter, and a reliable irrigation source during establishment
all worked together synergistically.

### Nature Does the Heavy Lifting

Roxanne told Hemenway: "The garden was hard to get started, but once
the little seedlings took off, then boy, they took off." The key
insight is that the gardener's role shifts from intense management during
establishment to light stewardship once the ecosystem is functioning.

### Dynamic Change Requires Adaptation

Roxanne pointed out a Penstemon that had been in full sun two years
earlier but was now being shaded out by maturing trees: "Things change
so fast here. Now it's completely shaded out, and I think it might be
rotting from the soil staying too wet." The ecological garden is not
static; species come and go as conditions change, and the gardener must
adapt to these shifts.
