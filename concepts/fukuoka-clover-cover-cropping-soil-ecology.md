## Weed Suppression

Clover functions as a living mulch that suppresses unwanted vegetation through
competition for light, space, and soil surface resources. The dense, low-growing mat
of clover leaves shades the soil surface, preventing weed seed germination and
establishment. This biological weed control replaces the mechanical weeding, hand
pulling, or herbicide application required in conventional farming.

Unlike bare-earth weed management, which creates a continuous ecological vacuum that
must be defended repeatedly, the clover cover occupies the niche that weeds would
otherwise fill. Weeds that do establish in the clover are typically shade-tolerant
species that do not compete aggressively with the grain crops. Fukuoka observed that
nature "does not arbitrarily call one plant a weed and try to eradicate it" — the
clover system works with this principle by establishing a diverse but managed ground
cover rather than pursuing the impossible goal of a weed-free field.

## Soil Structure and Erosion Prevention

The permanent clover cover protects the soil surface from raindrop impact, which is the
primary mechanism of soil erosion in arable lands. In conventional farming, bare soil
between crop rows is exposed to rainfall, causing surface crusting, reduced infiltration,
and accelerated runoff that carries away topsoil. The clover canopy intercepts raindrops,
reducing their kinetic energy and allowing water to infiltrate gradually.

Clover roots contribute to soil structure by creating channels through the soil profile
as they grow, die, and decompose. This root channel network improves water infiltration,
aeration, and the habitat for soil organisms. In Fukuoka's untilled fields, this
biological soil preparation entirely replaces the mechanical soil working that
conventional farming achieves through plowing.

The combination of surface protection and root channel creation means that Fukuoka's
fields, despite thirty years without tillage, maintained excellent soil structure and
drainage. This contradicts the conventional assumption that tillage is necessary to
prevent soil compaction and maintain tilth.
