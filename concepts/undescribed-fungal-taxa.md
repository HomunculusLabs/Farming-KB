## Undescribed Fungal Taxa

Undescribed fungal taxa represent the vast majority of fungal species on
Earth. With an estimated 2.2 to 3.8 million fungal species and only
approximately 150,000 formally described, between 93% and 96% of fungal
diversity exists as unnamed and uncharacterized biological entities.
These undescribed taxa include species from all major fungal groups and
span every ecosystem on the planet, from deep ocean sediments to arctic
tundra and [[fungal-taxonomic-impediment]] affecting all areas of
mycological research.

The formal process of species description is time-consuming and
resource-intensive. It requires expert comparison with all previously
described species, detailed morphological documentation, deposition of
voucher specimens in herbaria, and publication in peer-reviewed
journals. Some fungi cannot be cultured with current techniques, leaving
them effectively impossible to describe under traditional frameworks.

## Categories of Undescribed Fungi

### Environmental Sequences

The largest category by far. DNA sequences from soil, water, or air
samples that do not match any described species but exist only as
genetic data. Environmental sequencing has detected over one million
putative fungal species lacking formal names. These are sometimes
referred to as dark taxa because they are detectable only through
molecular signatures.

### Unvouchered Observations

[[cryptic-fungal-species]] are morphologically identical but genetically
distinct lineages that cannot be distinguished by traditional methods.

## Innovative Approaches to Description

Several innovative approaches are accelerating the description of
undescribed fungi. DNA-based taxonomy offers a faster path for taxa that
cannot be easily cultured. The concept of candidate species, where
genetic data provide provisional recognition pending formal description,
has gained acceptance. Automated species delimitation algorithms using
machine learning are being applied to large sequence datasets.

Citizen science initiatives contribute significantly. Platforms allowing
non-specialists to document fungal observations generate vast amounts of
distributional data guiding taxonomic research. Fungal forays and
bioblitz events organized by amateur mycological societies continue to
produce new species discoveries, particularly in poorly surveyed areas.
