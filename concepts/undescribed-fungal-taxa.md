# Undescribed Fungal Taxa

Undescribed fungal taxa represent the vast majority of fungal species on Earth.
With an estimated 2.2 to 3.8 million fungal species and only approximately
150,000 formally described, between 93% and 96% of fungal diversity exists as
unnamed and uncharacterized biological entities. These undescribed taxa include
species from all major fungal groups and span every ecosystem on the planet,
from deep ocean sediments to arctic tundra and tropical forest canopies. The
challenge of documenting and describing this hidden diversity is one of the
grand challenges in modern biology, with significant implications for ecology,
medicine, agriculture, and conservation science.

## The Taxonomic Impediment

The taxonomic impediment refers to the combination of factors that slow the
description of new species. For fungi, these factors are particularly acute.
The number of practicing fungal taxonomists worldwide has declined over the
past several decades as funding has shifted toward molecular and genomics
approaches. Training new taxonomists requires years of specialized education
and mentorship, and career prospects in taxonomy are limited in many countries.
The result is a shrinking workforce facing an ever-growing backlog of
undescribed species, with the gap between known and unknown diversity widening
each year rather than narrowing despite advances in technology and DNA
sequencing capabilities.

The formal process of species description is itself time-consuming and
resource-intensive. It requires expert comparison with all previously described
species, detailed morphological documentation including microscopic features
and spore characteristics, deposition of voucher specimens in herbaria, and
publication in peer-reviewed journals. For many fungi, the most challenging
step is obtaining a living culture that can be used to study the complete life
cycle and reproductive structures. Some fungi cannot be cultured at all with
current techniques, leaving them effectively impossible to describe under
traditional taxonomic frameworks that require living material for study.

## Categories of Undescribed Fungi

Undescribed fungal taxa can be grouped into several categories based on how
they are known to science. Environmental sequences represent the largest
category by far. These are DNA sequences obtained from soil, water, or air
samples that do not match any described species but exist only as genetic data
without associated physical specimens or morphological descriptions. It has
been estimated that environmental sequencing has detected over one million
putative fungal species that lack formal names. These sequence-based taxa are
sometimes referred to as dark taxa because they are detectable only through
their molecular signatures and cannot be linked to physical organisms using
current knowledge and reference databases.

Unvouchered observations constitute another large category of undescribed
fungi. These include fungal fruiting bodies that have been photographed or
collected in the field but never formally described by a taxonomist. Citizen
science platforms such as iNaturalist and mushroom observer websites have
accumulated large numbers of such observations, many of which likely represent
undescribed species that could be formally named with additional study.
Collections in herbaria that were collected but never fully identified or
described represent another source of undescribed taxa waiting to be studied
by future researchers with access to molecular tools unavailable when the
specimens were originally collected and preserved.

## Innovative Approaches to Description

Several innovative approaches are being developed to accelerate the description
of undescribed fungi. DNA-based taxonomy, or taxonomy using sequence data as
the primary diagnostic character, offers a faster path to formal recognition
for taxa that cannot be easily cultured. The concept of candidate species,
where genetic data provide provisional recognition pending formal description,
has gained acceptance in some research communities as a practical compromise.
Automated species delimitation algorithms using machine learning approaches are
being applied to large sequence datasets to identify clusters that likely
represent distinct species worthy of formal description and conservation
attention.

Citizen science and community science initiatives are contributing
significantly to the discovery of new fungi. Platforms that allow
non-specialists to document and share fungal observations generate vast amounts
of distributional data that can guide taxonomic research toward the most
promising areas for new species discovery. Fungal forays and bioblitz events
organized by amateur mycological societies continue to produce new species
discoveries, particularly in poorly surveyed areas and during unusual weather
conditions that trigger fruiting of rarely seen species.

## Urgency of Documentation

The urgency of documenting undescribed fungal taxa cannot be overstated.
Habitat destruction, particularly in tropical forests which harbor the greatest
fungal diversity, is eliminating species before they can be described or even
detected by molecular methods. Climate change is altering the distributions
and abundances of fungal species, potentially driving some to extinction before
they are known to science. The loss of undescribed fungal species represents
not just a reduction in biodiversity but also the loss of potentially valuable
genetic resources, including novel enzymes, pharmaceuticals, and plant
growth-promoting organisms that could benefit humanity in ways we cannot yet
anticipate or fully understand.

## Economic Value of Undescribed Diversity

Undescribed fungi represent an enormous reservoir of unexplored biological
and economic value. Fungi have already provided humanity with antibiotics
such as penicillin, immunosuppressants like cyclosporine, and industrial
enzymes used in manufacturing and food processing. Given that the vast
majority of fungal species remain undescribed, it is reasonable to assume
that many more valuable compounds and applications await discovery. Fungal
enzymes from extremophilic species found in unusual environments are
particularly promising for biotechnology applications.
