# Oss & Oeric: Spore Syringe Preparation

Spore collection and germination constitute the critical first step
in the Oss & Oeric cultivation methodology. The 1976 guide describes
two methods for obtaining spore prints and two approaches for
germinating them, along with an alternative tissue isolation
technique that bypasses spores entirely. Proper spore handling is
foundational to successful cultivation and requires careful attention
to sterility.

## Spore Print Collection: Paper Method

The standard method begins with one or more fresh mushroom specimens
with caps fully open. Using a sharp knife, the stipe is cut off as
close to the gills as possible, and the cap is placed gill-side down
on a clean sheet of white paper. The caps may be covered with a
small bowl to retard desiccation during the 24-hour deposition period.

When the caps are removed, a dark purplish, radially symmetrical
deposit of spores will remain on the paper where the gills contacted
it. A single spore print contains tens of millions of spores,
sufficient for hundreds of germination attempts. The paper should be
folded and sealed in an envelope to prevent contamination by airborne
spores of other species.

## Spore Print Collection: Sterile Slide Method

A more sterile variation uses four standard flat microscope slides,
swabbed with alcohol and flamed. The slides are arranged side by side
and end-to-end on a surface swabbed with Lysol, creating a larger
platform. The fresh cap is placed in the exact center of the slides
so approximately half of the cap covers each pair. After 24 hours,
each slide end will be covered with spores, and the slides can be
sealed individually or together in plastic or paper.

This method enhances sterility by using flame-sterilized glass
surfaces rather than paper, reducing the chance of contaminant
spores being present on the collection surface. The resulting spore
prints are also easier to work with under sterile conditions.

## Spore Germination on Agar

The spore print is inoculated onto sterile nutrient agar (PDA or
MEA) in petri plates. Using a flame-sterilized knife, inoculating
loop, or similar implement, spores are scraped lightly from the
print and transferred to the agar surface by touching it at one or
more points. The transfer must be done rapidly to minimize the time
the petri plate is open and exposed to airborne contaminants.

A liquid inoculation variation involves scraping spores into 10ml
of sterilized water, shaking vigorously, then diluting to 100ml with
sterile water. Using a sterilized pipette or syringe, 2-3ml of the
diluted spore solution is placed at two or three separate points on
the plate. This method distributes spores more evenly and allows
for better separation of individual germination events.

## Syringe Inoculation Method

The authors describe an alternative inoculation approach suggested
by a mycologist colleague. Sterile water is prepared in a flask
sealed with aluminum foil and sterilized in the pressure cooker
alongside the rye jars. Using a disposable pre-sterilized plastic
syringe, approximately 10cc of sterile water is drawn up and
injected onto the surface of an agar mycelial culture. The culture
is resealed and swirled vigorously until mycelial fragments become
visible suspended in the water. The mycelial suspension is then
drawn back into the syringe and used to inoculate each mason jar
by injecting 1-2cc per jar.

This syringe method is noted as a standard approach in mycological
work and seemed promising for increasing the sterility of the
procedure. The needle can be flamed over an alcohol lamp before
each inoculation. This technique is a direct precursor to the
modern spore syringe that became standard in later cultivation
methods.

## Tissue Culture Alternative

For those with fresh mushroom specimens, the subcutaneous isolation
method bypasses spores entirely. The stipe is removed and the cap
is fastened gills-down to cork-board with needles. The cap surface
is swabbed with tincture of iodine using a sterile cotton swab. A
flame-sterilized scalpel removes a section of outer cuticle, then
resterilized, and a small piece of subcutaneous flesh is transferred
to a sterile nutrient plate. Since the flesh is woven from dikaryotic
mycelium, it grows out as a single strain, eliminating the need to
isolate individual strains from a multi-strain spore germination.

## Historical Context of Spore Syringe Development

The syringe method described by Oss & Oeric represents a direct
precursor to the modern spore syringe that became standard in
mycological cultivation. By the 1990s, pre-made spore syringes
were widely available through underground channels, allowing
cultivators to bypass the spore print stage entirely. The basic
principle, however, remained the same: suspend spores in sterile
water and distribute them via injection into sterile substrate.

The development of the spore syringe was significant because it
dramatically simplified the inoculation process for beginners.
Rather than working with open petri dishes and exposed spore
prints, cultivators could inject spore solution directly through
a small hole in the jar lid, greatly reducing contamination risk
and eliminating the need for an inoculation hood in some cases.

The modern spore syringe typically contains millions of spores in
10-12cc of sterile water, providing enough inoculum for dozens of
jars. Quality syringes are prepared under laboratory conditions
and can remain viable for months or even years when stored in a
cool, dark location. The syringe method is now the most common
starting point for home cultivation of gourmet and medicinal
mushrooms as well as psilocybin-containing species.
