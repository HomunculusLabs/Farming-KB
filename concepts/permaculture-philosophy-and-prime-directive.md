# Permaculture Philosophy and Prime Directive

## The Prime Directive

The foundational statement of permaculture philosophy is the **Prime Directive**: *The only ethical decision is to take responsibility for our own existence and that of our children.* This is not merely a slogan but a practical imperative that shapes all [[permaculture-design]] and practice. It demands that individuals and communities move from passive consumers to active participants in providing for their own needs.

## The Crisis of Modern Civilization

Mollison frames permaculture within a sharp critique of modern industrial civilization. He argues that humanity is in danger of perishing from its own stupidity and lack of personal responsibility to life. The key observations include:

- Our consumptive lifestyle has led to the brink of annihilation
- We have expanded our right to live on earth into an entitlement to conquer the earth
- "Conquerors" of nature always lose
- To accumulate wealth, power, or land beyond one's needs in a limited world is truly immoral

This critique extends to all levels of society: the wage-slave, peasant, landlord, and industrialist alike are portrayed as equally captive in the destructive systems they participate in. Both "warders and prisoners are equally captive in the society in which we live."

## The Principle of Cooperation

A central philosophical tenet is the **Principle of Cooperation**: *Cooperation, not competition, is the very basis of existing life systems and of future survival.* Mollison draws on lewis thomas (1974) to note that even bacteria "live by collaboration, accommodation, exchange, and barter." Life is fundamentally cooperative rather than competitive, and life forms of very different qualities may interact beneficially with one another and with their physical environment.

This stands in direct opposition to the competitive paradigm dominant in both economics and biology. The shift from competition to cooperation as a design principle has profound implications for how settlements, food systems, and economies are structured.

## The Gaia Hypothesis and Earth as Living System

Mollison draws heavily on [[james-lovelock]]'s [[gaia-hypothesis]], which sees the earth as a self-regulating, self-constructed, and reactive system — a thought process that creates and preserves the conditions that make life possible, actively adjusting to regulate disturbances. However, he warns that humanity, "in its present mindlessness, may be the one disturbance that the earth cannot tolerate."

The Gaia hypothesis is presented as an alternative to both the view of nature as a primitive force to be subdued and the picture of Earth as a "demented spaceship, forever travelling, driverless and purposeless." It reframes the human relationship with the planet from one of domination to one of participation in a living system.

## Aboriginal Wisdom and the Egg of Life

Mollison draws a striking parallel between scientific understanding and Aboriginal Australian cosmology. For every scientific statement on energy, he says, Aboriginal tribespeople have an equivalent statement on life. Life, they say, is a totality neither created nor destroyed — it can be imagined as an egg from which all tribes (life forms) issue and to which all return.

The ideal way to spend one's time, in this worldview, is in the perfection of the expression of life — to lead the most evolved life possible and to assist in and celebrate the existence of life forms other than humans, for all come from the same egg.

Mollison argues that unless modern society adopts sophisticated aboriginal belief systems and learns respect for all life, we lose our own potential — not only as a lifetime but as any future opportunity to evolve. He contrasts the meaningful daily existence of tribal peoples with the "closed, artificial, and meaningless lives" of technological civilization.

## The Rainbow Serpent and Pattern

The book's cover story invokes the [[rainbow-serpent]], a creation figure shared by Australian and American Aboriginal peoples. The Serpent wound across the flat earth, its body heaping up mountains and digging troughs for rivers — a metaphor for the patterning forces that shape landscapes. Within the body of the Rainbow Serpent is contained the tree of life, expressing the general pattern of life forms, with roots in earth and crown in rain, sunlight, and wind.

This mythological framing serves to connect the abstract design principles discussed later in the book (particularly in the Pattern Understanding chapter) to a deep, culturally rooted understanding of how landscapes are formed and sustained.

## The Two Fundamental Questions

Mollison identifies two fundamentally different approaches to interacting with land and people:

1. *"What can I get from this land, or person?"*
2. *"What does this person, or land, have to give if I cooperate with them?"*

The former leads to war and waste; the latter to peace and plenty. He argues that most conflicts arise not from the answers to questions but from how the questions are asked. The task of permaculture design is to reframe questions — to look for the right questions rather than answers, and to "rephrase or refuse the wrong question."

## Unity Through Shared Ethics

Mollison argues that unity among people comes from common adherence to a set of ethical principles. Each person may go their own way, at their own pace, and within the limits of their resources, yet all leading to the same goals — that of a living, complex, and sustainable earth. Those who agree on such ethics, philosophies, and goals form what Mollison calls a "global nation."

This concept of a global nation is not a political entity but a voluntary association of people committed to earth care, people care, and the ethical limitation of consumption. It is decentralized, with no central structure but a strong sense of shared work.

## "This is Heaven, Right Here"

The chapter concludes with a story about a young woman who, after a lecture on concepts of afterlife, offered her view: "This is heaven, right here. This is it. Give it all you've got." Mollison writes: "I couldn't better that advice. The heaven, or hell, we live in is of our own making."

This pragmatic, present-focused ethic — investing fully in the world as it exists rather than deferring to hypothetical futures or afterlives — is a hallmark of the permaculture philosophy.

## Information, Specialization, and Integration

Mollison traces the evolution of human understanding from distant observation (astronomy, astrology) through naming and categorizing things, to understanding how they function. This analysis resulted in specialized sciences, disciplines, and technologies — "a welter of names and the sundering of parts; a proliferation of specialists; and a consequent inability to foresee results or to design integrated systems."

The present shift, he argues, is toward understanding how parts interact, how they work together with each other, and how dissonance or harmony in life systems or society is achieved. Permaculture is positioned as part of this shift — a discipline dedicated to integration rather than specialization.
