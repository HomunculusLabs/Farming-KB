Masanobu Fukuoka's philosophy of natural diet extends his principles of
natural farming into the realm of human nutrition and culture. Just as
natural farming consists of adapting to true nature understood with
non-discriminating knowledge, a true natural diet is a way of eating
where one feeds with an undiscriminating attitude on food taken from
the wild, crops grown by natural farming, and fish caught naturally.
The philosophy holds that flavor and nutrition are inseparable, that
eating should follow the seasons, and that food culture arises from
nature rather than from human invention.
## Eating with the Seasons
The food that farmers and fishermen have taken locally for thousands
of years provides a splendid example of natural diet in accordance
with the laws of nature. The seven herbs of spring: Japanese parsley,
shepherd's purse, cudweed, chickweed, bee nettle, wild turnip, and
wild radish, emerge early in the new year from the slumbering earth.
As the farmer enjoys their flavors, he meditates joyfully on having
survived a harsh winter. Nature provides shellfish alongside the
herbs as a brown food appropriate to the season.
A little later, young cherry, persimmon, peach, and Chinese yam
leaves become edible. Bamboo shoots pair deliciously with rockfish.
Red sea bream and grunt are excellent at barley harvest time. All
fruits ripen at the right time of year, and that is when they are
most delicious. Loquats glistening in early summer rains are a fruit
the body craves. Under the midsummer sun, one may eat melon, drink
milk, and lick honey in the cool shade.
In early autumn, cereal grains, soybeans, and adzuki beans become
available. Millet dumplings under the moonlight, taros and green
soybeans, chestnuts and rice in late autumn all make sense. Most
welcome are the ripened grains of rice that have absorbed the yang
of summer, providing calories for winter. Barley, harvested in
spring, suits the palate as the appetite lags under summer heat.
Autumn brings heavy catches of yang fish such as yellowtail and
tuna, never more delicious than during the yin season. Through
winter, wild game with welsh onions warms the body, while pickled
vegetables from the fall provide a fragrant finish to meals. The
Japanese historically took foods of the seasons near at hand, and
while savoring their distinctive flavors, saw the providence of the
heavens in the modest fare on which they lived.
## Critique of Modern Dietetics
Fukuoka offered a pointed critique of Western dietetics as mechanical
and disconnected from nature. Western dietetics teaches that health
requires a balanced diet of starch, fat, protein, vitamins, and
minerals in carefully calculated proportions. Because it is built
upon scientific reasoning, its pronouncements are accepted at face
value, but this carries the potential for disaster.
First, dietetics lacks awareness of man as a living, breathing
creature. Menus give the impression of supplying energy to
mechanical humans cut off from the source of life. There is no
attempt to conform to natural cycles. Because it relies so heavily
on the human intellect, dietetics appears useful in the development
of anti-natural man isolated from nature.
Second, we have forgotten that man is a spiritual animal that cannot
be fully explained in organic, mechanical, and physiological terms.
His body and life are extremely fluid and undergo great physical and
mental vicissitudes. The food that man eats is linked with human
emotion, so a diet devoid of feeling is meaningless.
Third, Western dietetics understands things only within a narrow
temporal and spatial framework. No matter how the scientist assembles
ingredients, this will never approach a complete diet. The powers of
the intellect succeed only in creating an incomplete diet removed
from nature. Unmindful that the whole is greater than the parts,
modern science commits blunder after blunder. Man can dissect a
butterfly and examine it in detail, but he cannot make it fly.
## Flavor and Nutrition as One
Fukuoka insisted that taste and nutrition should not be separated.
What is nourishing and good for the human body should stimulate the
appetite of its own accord and serve as delicious food. Flavor and
nutrition must be one. Farmers in his area once enjoyed simple meals
of barley and rice with unrefined soy sauce and pickled vegetables,
which gave them strength and long life. Working hard made one hungry,
which is why coarse fare tasted delicious. A strong body draws
sustenance from a simple diet.
People today chase after flavor because they have lost it. Natural
man gathers food without discrimination, his instincts intact, so he
eats properly; everything is delicious, nourishing, and therapeutic.
Modern man bases judgments on mistaken knowledge and searches with
five deranged senses. The problem is that man has become spiritually
alienated from food. True flavor can be perceived only with the five
senses, the mind, and the spirit.
## Food and Culture
Fukuoka linked diet directly to the question of human culture. Culture
is often seen as a human product created by separating man from
nature. However, the culture associated intimately with daily
existence always originates in a return to the source of nature,
forming when nature and man fuse into a single whole.
A culture born of human vanity and divorced from nature cannot become
a true culture. True culture arises from within nature, and is pure,
modest, and simple. Were this not so, man would be destroyed by that
culture. When mankind forsakes a natural diet for a civilized diet,
he turns away from true culture and sets off on the road to decline.
## The Seasonal Food Mandala
Fukuoka arranged foods in mandala diagrams to illustrate the harmony
of seasonal eating. One mandala systematically shows the vast variety
of foods available for human consumption. Another shows foods
available during each month of the year, demonstrating that as long
as man accepts divine dispensation, a complete natural diet arises of
itself without needing to ponder the principle of yin and yang.
The mandala drew partly on George Ohsawa's application of yin and
yang. Summer is yang (red, orange), winter is yin (indigo, purple).
In summer one should eat yin foods, and in winter yang foods.
Vegetables are green, seaweed is blue, grains are yellow, meat is
red. But Fukuoka cautioned that too much concern over yin and yang
leads back into discriminating knowledge. Those at the perimeter of
spiritual enlightenment need not differentiate between plants or
animals; all may become exquisite fare. Only man, having alienated
himself from nature, cannot partake directly of its bounty.
