## Cannabis Microbiome Alpha Diversity Gradient from Bulk Soil to Endorhiza

## Overview

Alpha diversity — the diversity of microbial species within a single sample — follows a
consistent and significant gradient across the plant-soil interface in Cannabis. Diversity
is highest in bulk soil, decreases slightly in the rhizosphere, and drops dramatically within
the endorhiza (root interior). This pattern reflects progressive ecological filtering as
microbes transition from open soil into the nutrient-rich but selective root zone and
ultimately into root tissue itself. (see [[cannabis-alpha-diversity-gradient-bulk-soil-rhizosphere-endorhiza]]).
In the second experiment of Winston et al. (2014), bulk soil samples demonstrated the
highest chao1 alpha-diversity estimates across both soil types tested. The Mo-Bio (MB)
soil bulk samples had a mean chao1 of 5597 (SD = 89), while Orange County (OC) soil bulk
samples had a mean chao1 of 4296 (SD = 85). This high diversity reflects the complex and
heterogeneous nature of soil as a microbial habitat, where numerous niches support a wide
range of taxa including Acidobacteria, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes,
Verrucomicrobia, and many others.

### Rhizosphere: Moderate Diversity Reduction
The rhizosphere — the narrow zone of soil directly influenced by root exudates — showed
a slight but measurable reduction in alpha diversity compared to bulk soil. MB rhizosphere
samples had a mean chao1 of 4859 (SD = 286), while OC rhizosphere samples had a mean chao1
of 3913 (SD = 290). This reduction occurs because root exudates (sugars, amino acids,
organic acids) create a nutrient-enriched environment that favours fast-growing copiotrophic
bacteria over the diverse oligotrophs that dominate bulk soil. The rhizosphere effect acts
as the first filter, selectively enriching certain taxa while excluding others.

### Endorhiza: Dramatic Diversity Collapse
The most striking diversity change occurs at the transition from rhizosphere to endorhiza.
Endorhiza alpha diversity dropped dramatically: MB endorhiza had a mean chao1 of 3325
(SD = 517), and OC endorhiza had a mean chao1 of 3311 (SD = 112). This represents
roughly a 30% reduction from rhizosphere diversity and a 40% reduction from bulk soil
diversity. The endorhiza imposes the strongest selective filter, as only bacteria capable
of colonizing plant tissue — resisting or evading plant immune responses, competing for
internal niches, and tolerating the biochemical environment of root cells — can persist.

## Comparison Between Experiments

The first experiment, conducted eight weeks post-harvest, showed the same directional
pattern but with much lower absolute diversity values. Despite shallower sequencing depth,
bulk soil had a chao1 of 2010.7 (SD = 146.2), rhizosphere 1837.2 (SD = 114.0), and
endorhiza 916.1 (SD = 161.7). The extremely low endorhiza diversity in the first
experiment was attributed to early stages of root decay following harvest, as the samples
were collected long after the living plant had been removed. This finding underscores that
alpha diversity in the endorhiza is heavily influenced by [[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]] including nitrogen content, salinity, organic carbon, and water content.
However, once bacteria colonized the endorhiza, diversity converged between soil types.
This convergence supports the two-tier selection model: soil determines the available
species pool (Tier 1), but host genotype-dependent selection (Tier 2) overrides soil
differences within the root interior.

## Taxonomic Changes Along the Diversity Gradient

The decline in alpha diversity is accompanied by predictable taxonomic shifts:
- **Acidobacteria** decrease dramatically from bulk soil to endorhiza, as these
  oligotrophic organisms are poorly adapted to the carbon-rich root environment.
- **Proteobacteria** increase in relative abundance, particularly Gammaproteobacteria
  and Alphaproteobacteria, which are common endophytic taxa.
- **Actinobacteria** also increase within the endorhiza, reflecting their known
  endophytic associations.
- Specific orders like Rhizobiales become enriched, suggesting nitrogen-fixing or
  plant-growth-promoting roles within root tissue.
