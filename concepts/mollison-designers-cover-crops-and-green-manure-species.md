---# Cover Crops and Green Manure Species

[[cover-crops]] are living plants grown primarily to benefit the
soil rather than for harvest. Mollison treats them as essential components of
any permaculture system, serving multiple functions simultaneously: nitrogen
fixation, organic matter addition, [[mycorrhizal-networks]] and [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]].
Above-ground biomass acts as a mulch layer when terminated, conserving moisture
and moderating soil temperature. Diverse cover crop mixtures support a wider
range of soil organisms than monocultures, building more resilient soil food
webs.

## Leguminous Cover Crops

Legumes are the primary nitrogen-fixing cover crops. Their effectiveness
depends on proper inoculation with the appropriate Rhizobium strain, adequate
soil pH, and sufficient phosphorus and molybdenum for [[mushroom-compost]], red clover, white clover, subterranean
clover, hairy vetch, field peas, faba beans, and lucerne (alfalfa). Crimson
clover and hairy vetch are excellent autumn-sown covers that produce abundant
biomass before being terminated in spring. Subterranean clover self-seeds
readily and persists as a living mulch.

**Tropical and subtropical species:** Cowpea, mung bean, lablab, sunn hemp,
siratro, centro, and pigeon pea. Cowpea and lablab are fast-growing summer
covers that produce large amounts of biomass in warm conditions. Sunn hemp
reaches 2 metres in 60 days and is resistant to root-knot nematodes. Pigeon
pea grows as a perennial shrub in frost-free zones, producing edible seeds
and abundant nitrogen-rich mulch.

**Arid and semi-arid species:** Sweet clover (Melilotus spp.), medics
(Medicago spp.), and tagasaste. These deep-rooted legumes access subsoil
moisture and nutrients unavailable to shallow-rooted species.

## Non-Leguminous Cover Crops

Non-legumes provide biomass, scavenge nutrients, and break compaction without
fixing nitrogen. They are often mixed with legumes to maximise total biomass
production while adding nitrogen fixation.

**Cereals and grasses:** Winter rye, oats, barley, wheat, annual ryegrass,
and sorghum-sudangrass. Cereal rye is the most cold-hardy winter cover,
establishing in autumn and producing extensive root systems that suppress
weeds and [[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]]. Sorghum-sudangrass produces enormous biomass
in summer and its roots penetrate compacted subsoil layers.

**Brassicas:** Daikon radish (tillage radish), mustard, and forage radish.
Daikon radish produces a thick taproot that penetrates compacted soil layers,
creating channels for water infiltration and subsequent root growth of cash
crops. Upon decomposition, the taproot leaves voids that improve soil
aeration. Mustard produces glucosinolate compounds that suppress soil-borne
pathogens and nematodes through biofumigation.

**Broadleaves:** Buckwheat, phacelia, and sunflower. Buckwheat grows rapidly
in warm conditions, suppressing weeds and attracting [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]].
Phacelia is an excellent bee forage that also produces good biomass. Sunflower
roots access deep soil nutrients and the tall plants create wind protection.
