## Principles of Solar Desalination

Solar stills operate on the fundamental principle that saline water, when
heated by solar radiation, evaporates leaving salts and impurities behind. The
water vapor then condenses on a transparent or translucent surface that is
cooler than the evaporating basin. Gravity directs the condensed freshwater
to collection channels or troughs. The efficiency of any solar still depends
on the temperature differential between the evaporating surface and the
condensing surface, the angle of incidence of solar radiation, and the
quality of insulation beneath the basin to minimize heat loss to the ground.

Mollison emphasizes that solar desalination is most effective in climates
with high insolation and low cloud cover. Even modest stills can produce
2 to 5 liters of fresh water per square meter per day in favorable
conditions. The output varies seasonally and must be matched to demand
through careful sizing during the design phase, particularly when integrated
with [[water-management]]
on arid properties.

## Types of Inclined Solar Stills

### Basin Stills

The conventional basin still consists of a shallow, insulated basin filled
with saline water, covered by a sloped glass or plastic canopy. Solar energy
passes through the transparent cover, heats the water, and the evaporated
moisture condenses on the inner surface of the cover, running down to a
collection trough. Basin stills are simple to construct and operate but have
relatively low efficiency, typically 30 to 40 percent, due to heat losses
and the difficulty of maintaining a large temperature differential.

### Inclined Evaporators

Inclined or wick-type solar stills improve efficiency by spreading saline
water as a thin film over an inclined, dark, absorbent surface. Capillary
action in a wick material (such as canvas, hessian, or synthetic fabric)
draws saline water from a reservoir to the evaporation surface. The thin
film heats rapidly and evaporates efficiently, with condensed water collected
from the transparent cover above. These systems are lighter, respond faster
to solar input, and can be mounted at an optimal angle for the latitude of
the site.

### Multi-Effect Stills

Multi-effect stills use the latent heat of condensation from one stage to
drive evaporation in a subsequent stage, significantly improving output per
unit area. While more complex, they can achieve efficiencies of 50 to 60
percent. Mollison notes that for permaculture applications, the added
complexity must be justified by the water demand of the system.

## Design Considerations

### Sizing and Output

A general rule of thumb from Mollison is to allow 1 square meter of still
area per person per day for minimum drinking water needs, recognizing that
actual output depends heavily on local solar conditions. For a family of four
in a high-insolation coastal environment, 4 to 6 square meters of basin still
area or 3 to 4 square meters of inclined wick still area should provide
adequate drinking water. Additional area is needed if the water supplies
irrigation for [[mollison-designers-sewage-aquaculture-systems]] where brackish estuarine water serves as both the aquaculture medium
and the desalination feedstock. The waste heat from stills can warm adjacent
seedling propagation areas or animal shelters.

Mollison also suggests coupling desalination with [[mollison-designers-roof-water-catchment]] systems so that rainwater provides the primary supply during
wet seasons while solar stills maintain production during dry periods. The
brine concentrate from desalination can be directed to salt-tolerant plantings
such as mangrove nurseries or saltbush pastures, closing the nutrient loop
rather than creating a waste disposal problem.

## Summary

Inclined solar desalination provides a passive, low-technology method for
producing freshwater from saline sources. By careful attention to still type,
sizing, materials, orientation, and system integration, permaculture designers
can supply reliable drinking water in arid coastal zones without external
energy inputs. The technology is scalable from single-household basin stills
to community-scale multi-effect installations.
