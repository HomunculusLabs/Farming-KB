# Mycorrhizal Fungi Biome Surveys

Mycorrhizal fungi surveys across biomes reveal the fundamental
importance of plant-fungal symbioses in ecosystem structure
and function.

## Mycorrhizal Types Across Biomes

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)
The most widespread type, found in tropical forests (extremely
high diversity), temperate grasslands (AMF dominate), agricultural
fields (reduced by tillage), and arid lands (critical for water
uptake). Hundreds of species in Glomerales await description.

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (EMF)
Associated with forest trees in temperate and boreal biomes:
boreal forests (Suillus, Lactarius, Russula dominate), temperate
deciduous forests (mixed AMF/EMF), Mediterranean woodlands (high
endemism under oaks), and tropical montane forests. EMF surveys
show strong host specificity and seasonal fruiting patterns.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizal Fungi
Found in heathlands, boreal forests, and alpine meadows.
Associated with Ericaceae, enabling access to organic nitrogen
in acidic, nutrient-poor soils.

## Survey Methodology
Multiple complementary methods are employed:
- Fruiting body surveys: seasonal recording; biased toward
  visible species
- Root tip analysis: direct microscopic examination of roots
- Molecular identification: DNA from roots or soil; detects
  non-fruiting species
- Spore extraction: soil washing and counting for AMF
- Trap plant cultivation: bait plants in soil samples

## Biome-Specific Patterns

### Tropical vs. Temperate Diversity
Tropical forests show highest overall mycorrhizal diversity.
Tropical lowlands are AMF-dominated; tropical montane and
temperate forests show increasing EMF importance.

### Elevation Gradients
AMF diversity generally decreases with elevation while EMF
diversity may peak at mid-elevations. Ericoid mycorrhizae
increase in importance at high elevations.

### Successional Changes
Early successional sites show lower mycorrhizal diversity.
Late successional forests accumulate diverse networks.
Disturbance reduces diversity; recovery takes decades.

## AMF Functional Roles
Beyond nutrient exchange, AMF provide phosphorus uptake from
micropores, enhanced drought tolerance, pathogen protection,
soil aggregate stabilization via glomalin, and carbon
sequestration through deep hyphal carbon deposition.

Conventional tillage reduces colonization 40-70% versus
no-till. Phosphorus fertilization suppresses colonization
by removing plant dependency on fungal nutrient uptake.

## EMF Community Dynamics
Host specificity ranges from generalist to specialist.
Fruiting is highly variable year-to-year. Below-ground
diversity exceeds sporocarp surveys 2-10x. Molecular
methods reveal far more species than visual surveys.

## Molecular Tools in Surveys
Modern surveys rely increasingly on DNA-based methods:
- NGS of root and soil samples provides community profiles
- ITS region sequencing detects unculturable species
- Metagenomics reveals functional gene diversity
- qPCR quantifies AMF abundance across gradients

Challenges include incomplete reference databases, primer
bias, and difficulty linking sequences to functional traits.

## Climate Change Impacts
Warming shifts EMF communities toward warm-adapted species.
Altered precipitation affects AMF sporulation. Elevated CO2
may increase mycorrhizal dependency. Host range shifts
create compatibility gaps with local fungal communities.
