# Keyline Pattern Plow Technique

The keyline system, developed by P.A. Yeomans in Australia and extensively
referenced by Bill Mollison in Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, is a
landscape management approach that uses patterned subsoil cultivation to
redistribute water across slopes, improve soil structure, and increase
pasture and tree growth without the need for earthworks like dams or
swales.

## Origins and Principles

P.A. Yeomans observed that the natural water flow pattern on undulating
land follows a characteristic keyline, the point where a valley (concave)
transitions into a ridge (convex) formation. By cultivating parallel to
this keyline and extending the pattern across the entire slope, water that
would naturally concentrate in valleys is instead spread laterally toward
ridges, creating more uniform soil moisture across the landscape.

Mollison adopted keyline as a core permaculture technique because it
requires no ongoing energy input after the initial cultivation and works
with natural gravity-driven water movement rather than against it.

## The Keyline Geometry

On any hillside with valleys and ridges, Yeomans identified these features:

- Keypoint: the point in a valley where the slope changes from steep (above)
  to flat (below), typically where the valley floor begins to widen. This
  is where water naturally slows and begins to spread.
- Keyline: the contour line that passes through the keypoint. This line
  marks the transition from convex to concave landform.
- Above the keyline: the land slopes down toward the keypoint; water
  collects and concentrates in valleys.
- Below the keyline: the land slopes away from the keypoint; water
  disperses from valleys into the broader landscape.

## Cultivation Pattern

The keyline cultivation pattern differs above and below the keyline:

Above the keyline: plow parallel to the keyline but NOT on contour. Since
the keyline follows a contour across the valley floor, lines parallel to
it above the keyline will be slightly off-contour, rising toward the ridge
crests. This pattern directs water away from valley centres and toward
ridges, spreading moisture across the full slope width.

Below the keyline: plow parallel to the keyline contour. Below the keyline,
the parallel lines naturally spread water outward from valleys toward
ridges because the ridge flanks diverge at lower elevations.

The result is that water which would concentrate and potentially erode in
valley bottoms is instead distributed across the entire slope surface,
reaching ridge areas that would otherwise be dry.

## The Yeomans Plow (Keyline Plow)

The keyline plow (or Yeomans plow) is a specialised subsoil cultivator
that differs fundamentally from a mouldboard plough:

- It does not turn the soil over; it only fractures the subsoil below the
  cultivation layer
- Shanks (typically 3-5) penetrate 15-40cm below the surface depending on
  soil conditions
- Shanks are fitted with narrow points or winged shares that lift and
  fracture compacted subsoil without inverting the soil profile
- The plow leaves the surface largely undisturbed, preserving existing
  pasture or crop cover
- It can be drawn by a tractor or, for smaller areas, by animals

The key innovation is that subsoil fracturing improves infiltration and
root penetration while maintaining the soil's biological surface layer
and existing vegetation. No topsoil is lost, no weed seeds are brought to
the surface, and the soil structure is improved rather than degraded.

## Subsoil Ripping Benefits

Keyline cultivation addresses several soil and water problems:

- Hardpan and plough pan: compacted layers that restrict root growth and
  water infiltration are fractured, allowing roots to penetrate deeper
  and water to percolate into the subsoil
- Surface water runoff: improved infiltration means rainfall enters the
  soil profile rather than running off, reducing erosion and increasing
  available soil moisture
- Soil aeration: subsoil fracturing introduces oxygen into deeper soil
  layers, stimulating microbial activity and root growth
- Pasture improvement: existing pasture responds dramatically within
  1-3 seasons as roots access deeper moisture and nutrients
- Tree growth: trees planted in keyline-cultivated soil grow faster and
  develop deeper root systems, improving their drought resilience

## Integration with Swales and Dams

Mollison emphasises that keyline cultivation works in combination with
other [[pdc-water-harvesting-earthworks-design]] earthworks:

- Above dams: keyline cultivation of the catchment area above farm dams
  increases runoff into the dam while simultaneously improving the soil
  and pasture in the catchment
- Between swales: keyline cultivation of the inter-swale zone improves
  water distribution between swales, extending the moisture lens
- On steep slopes: keyline is preferred over swales where slopes exceed
  15%, as it avoids the erosion risk of earthworks on steep ground
- In established orchards: keyline cultivation between tree rows improves
  moisture availability without disturbing tree roots or surface mulch

## Timing and Frequency

Keyline cultivation timing affects its effectiveness:

- Cultivate when soil moisture is moderate: too dry and the shanks cannot
  penetrate; too wet and the soil smears rather than fractures
- In seasonal rainfall areas, cultivate at the start of the wet season so
  that the first rains infiltrate into the fractured subsoil
- On pasture land, cultivate every 3-5 years as needed; the soil will
  gradually re-compact, especially under grazing pressure
- In arid areas, a single cultivation may last many years if the soil is
  not heavily trafficked by livestock or vehicles

## Limitations

- Keyline does not store water; it only improves infiltration and
  distribution. In very dry climates, supplementary water storage
  (dams, tanks) is still needed.
- Very rocky or shallow soils may not allow deep subsoil penetration
- The technique requires understanding of contour and landform to
  identify the correct keyline and cultivation pattern
- On very gentle slopes (< 2%), the lateral water distribution effect
  is minimal as water moves slowly in any direction
