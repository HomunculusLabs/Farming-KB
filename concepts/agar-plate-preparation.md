## Agar Plate Preparation

Preparing agar plates is one of the foundational skills in [[mushroom-cultivation]] and mycological laboratory work. An agar plate is a petri dish containing agar—a gelatinous seaweed extract commonly used as a substrate for tissue cultures. The process involves preparing a nutrient-rich gel, sterilizing it, pouring it into sterile petri plates, and optionally supplementing it with additives tailored to specific mushroom species or experimental goals.

## Understanding Agar

Agar serves as the growth medium on which mushroom spores germinate and [[mycelium]] expands. The basic formula provides nutrients, while supplements can be added to improve growth rates, select against contaminants, or simulate natural growing conditions. Potato-based agar is the standard beginner formula because it is difficult for bacteria to grow on, making it forgiving for cultivators still developing sterile technique. As skills advance, alternative bases and enrichment strategies can improve performance for specific species.

## Step 1: Preparing the Agar Gel

The simplest agar formula begins with potato broth. A small potato is sliced into small chunks or shredded (no need to peel) and placed in a pot with 3–4 cups of water. The mixture is boiled for one hour, monitored to prevent foaming or boiling over. The resulting liquid resembles "potato tea" and does not thicken. If water level drops significantly, more is added and boiling continues.

The broth is strained and measured, then poured into an Erlenmeyer flask or wide-bottomed glass container. Agar powder is added at a rate of approximately 1 teaspoon (5 grams) per 2 cups of broth. The agar must be added while the broth is still hot to prevent clumping—cold water causes instant clumping. Professional labs use hot plate stirrers for homogenization, but swirling the flask by hand works adequately.

## Step 2: Sterilizing the Agar

[[sterilization]] requires a pressure cooker rated for 12–15 PSI. Lower PSI means longer cook times and lower maximum sterilization temperature. Pressure cookers with metal-to-metal seals are superior to those with rubber gaskets, but gasketed models are adequate for skill development before investing in professional-grade equipment. The cooker must be tall enough to accommodate the agar flask.

The cooker is filled with water per manufacturer's directions, and the flask is placed upright in the inner pan. If tipping is a risk, the flask can be stabilized in a wire basket or metal bowl with aluminum foil wads. The flask's open top is plugged with cotton or polyester fill, covered with aluminum foil, and compressed tightly.

When the cooker reaches 15 PSI, a timer is started for 20 minutes, then heat is turned off. The cooker must not be opened until internal pressure drops above 0 PSI but below 2–3 PSI. Opening below 0 PSI creates a vacuum that sucks contaminated air into the cooker, requiring resterilization. Opening well above 0 PSI causes the agar to boil out of the flask, wasting supplies. Ideally, the cooker is opened in a clean room or in front of the [[laminar-flow-hood]].

The hot flask is removed using an alcohol-saturated paper towel as a mitt or a sanitized jar lifter. Bare hands must never touch the flask, as skin oils can harbor bacteria that compromise subsequent transfers.

## Step 3: Pouring the Agar

The sterilized agar cools from approximately 200°F (93°C) to a pouring temperature of around 100°F (38°C). An infrared temperature gauge (available for approximately $30) is invaluable for monitoring this process, allowing the cultivator to swirl the agar periodically while cooling. If the temperature window is missed and the agar gels, it can be reheated to melt. Antibiotics, if used, are added between 105–110°F (41–43°C).

Sterile petri plates are prepared at the laminar flow hood. The outer plastic sleeve is wiped with rubbing alcohol, and sanitized scissors trim the bottom off. The stack is inverted upright on the work surface, the sleeve is gently tugged upward and rolled for potential reuse, and the stack is divided into four or five smaller groups for efficient pouring.

When the agar reaches pouring temperature, the stack is pulled forward. One hand holds the flask while the other grasps the lid of the bottom plate and lifts straight up—lifting the entire stack while leaving the base of the bottom plate on the tabletop. Agar is slowly poured until the base is approximately half full, and the stack is lowered to replace the lid. This process repeats for each plate in the stack. Filled stacks are gently slid aside to prevent sloshing. Two cups of agar typically suffices for 20–25 plates. With practice, an entire sleeve can be poured in one to two minutes.

After approximately one hour, plates are checked for gelling by tilting them. Properly gelled agar should be firm and elastic. Wet or improperly gelled agar is prone to contamination and difficult for making clean spore streaks.

## Step 4: Labeling, Using, and Storing

Plates are labeled with the date, recipe, and any additives. Because space is limited, color-coding systems are efficient—for example, green marker for penicillin G, with the number of marks indicating concentration (one mark for 1 ml, two for 2 ml, etc.). Entire stacks can be coded quickly by dragging a colored marker up the side once gelled.

Cultured plates are sealed at the base-lid seam with tape or Parafilm. Unused plates are stored in new ziplock bags in packs of three or four (the interior of plastic bags is very clean due to heat-injection manufacturing), labeled with date and formula code, and refrigerated. Refrigerated plates keep for one to two months or longer. Extra plates are invaluable for unexpected field collections.

Before use, stored plates are inspected at the laminar hood for contamination—any circular green, yellow, or black powdery growth means the entire bag should be discarded unopened.

## Alternative Agar Bases

As cultivators expand their skills, alternative bases can replace or supplement [[potato-agar]]:

- **Powdered brewer's malt** — Rich in nutrients suitable for many species
- **Dog food or rabbit food** — Readily available, nutrient-dense
- **Tomato juice** — Provides vitamins and minerals
- **Cattle feed** — Economical bulk nutrient source
- **Cornmeal** — Good base for many cultivated species
- **Beet pulp** — Provides sugars and minerals

Different ecotypes of the same species can vary significantly in their enzymatic ability to break down compounds, so customizing agar formulas based on observed preferences improves culture health and performance. Cultivators should maintain a journal recording which species perform best on which formulas. Supplementation increases the risk of attracting contaminants, so the goal is finding the range that yields optimal mycelial growth while preventing molds and bacteria from outcompeting mushroom cultures.

## Enrichment Medium (Substrate Agar)

Adding a mushroom's preferred fruiting substrate to the agar creates an enrichment medium or substrate agar, helping picky fungi or hard-to-clone specimens "feel at home." Dried and powdered samples of growing substrates—finely ground sawdust, screened wheat straw powder (sieved to the smallest particle obtainable)—are kept in jars for on-demand use. Adding a tablespoon of appropriate substrate powder to the agar formula helps cultures adapt and express needed enzymes.

Substrate agar plates also serve as experimental mini-trials to test whether a strain can grow on an unusual substrate (e.g., shiitakes on wheat straw). Pollutants can be added instead of substrate to gauge a fungus's bioremediation potential. Alternating media across successive lab expansions keeps mycelial enzyme production strong.

## Split Gallery Plates

Split plates create transitional or behavioral galleries where fungal responses to different agar concentrations are observed. One side contains a familiar formula (such as potato agar), while the other has a more challenging medium. The base of the petri dish is set at an angle (resting one end on a dowel), and enough agar poured to fill half. Once gelled, the base is flipped and the other half filled with the second formula. Mycelium is transferred to the "safe side," allowing it to expand on familiar medium before reaching the transition zone where its reaction to the new medium can be observed. Additive concentrations can be increased with consecutive transfers to determine a strain's tolerance limits.

## Adding Antibiotics to Agar

Antibiotics minimize or eliminate bacterial contaminants during early cultivation stages. Common options include:

- **Ampicillin and streptomycin** — Broad-spectrum bacterial control, available as powders requiring rehydration, vortexing, centrifugation, and syringe filtration
- **Premixed antibiotic agar** — Heat-stable formulations that can be autoclaved, but expensive
- **Sterile injectable penicillin G** (livestock formulation) — The most practical option; added below 110°F (43°C) during cooling, swirled for even distribution

Antibiotics are destroyed above 110°F (43°C) or by autoclaving, so timing is critical. A sterile syringe can be reused if both the antibiotic jar and syringe are wiped with alcohol before first use and stored together in a ziplock bag in the refrigerator. Antibiotics can also be added to liquid cultures for longer-term storage to prevent bacterial growth in nutrient suspensions used for repeated transfers.
