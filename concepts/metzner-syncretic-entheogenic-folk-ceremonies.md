## Native American Church and Peyote

The use of peyote by the Huichols of Mexico follows the traditional
shamanic healing model, involving careful apprenticeships and a group
pilgrimage to Wirikuta to find the sacred cactus. Native American tribes
in the United States legally incorporated the Native American Church
(NAC) using peyote as sacrament. Though legal protection has eroded in
recent court decisions, the NAC enjoyed legal access for most of the
[[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]].

NAC ceremonies follow a consistent format: a "roadman" presides,
assisted by a drummer, firekeeper, and cedar-man. Participants sit on
the ground in a circle around a blazing central fire in a tipi. The
ceremony lasts all night with almost constant singing of traditional
peyote songs. A staff and rattle are passed around. There is virtually
no discussion of healing processes or visions, although individuals may
have private experiences. NAC ceremonies are legally limited to persons
with 25% or more Native American ancestry, though some leaders have
opened ceremonies to non-Indians.

## Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches

In Brazil, three organized churches use [ayahuasca
as their main sacrament: Santo Daime, Uniao do Vegetal (UDV), and
Barquinia. Each was founded in the 1950s by rubber tappers in the Amazon
who encountered the vine through mestizo or Indian ayahuasceros. Each
founder reported a vision instructing them to organize a church using
the brew as sacrament.

Members come from all walks of life in both urban and rural Brazil.
Each church has several thousand members, and two have satellite centers
in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] and Europe. The churches are officially recognized and
ayahuasca use is legal in Brazil within that framework. Research by
Charles Grob and Dennis McKenna confirmed that long-term participation
is associated with no adverse health effects and with a reduction of
addictions and other psychopathologies.

### Uniao do Vegetal (UDV)

The UDV is the largest and most formal of the Brazilian churches.
Participants sit in rows in straight-backed chairs, listening to sermons
