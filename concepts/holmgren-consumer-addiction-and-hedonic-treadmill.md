# Consumer Addiction and the Hedonic Treadmill

[[david-holmgren]] extends the concept of addiction beyond pharmacological
dependence to analyse the systemic addictions of modern industrial
society. His framework treats widespread consumption patterns as
addictive behaviours that undermine both personal wellbeing and
ecological sustainability.

## Addiction as a Design Problem

Holmgren describes Permaculture (only partly tongue-in-cheek) as "aid
for addicts." He uses the growing awareness of the structure and
processes of pharmacological addiction to understand widespread addictions
in modern life to shopping, television and other media, driving cars and
other forces of unsustainability.

The term "hedonic treadmill" refers to the phenomenon in affluent
societies where continuous increases in material wealth are now needed to
maintain societal happiness measures, but overall improvements in
happiness do not seem achievable. Holmgren argues this is addiction by
another name.

## Identifying Needs Versus Wants

Perhaps more fundamental than how we provide for our needs is how we
identify them as distinct from "wants," and the degree to which those
wants have become dysfunctional needs (addictions). Central to this
process is recognising the role of addiction in the modern world and in
ourselves.

This connects directly to the permaculture ethic of "set limits to
consumption and reproduction." The statistics on growth in human
consumption are sobering: per capita consumption in the United States
has been declining in wellbeing since 1978, despite huge rises in
material consumption, because much new wealth offsets the adverse effects
of overconsumption.

## Market Economics and Addiction

Market economics have for several generations rewarded materially
advantageous innovations, creating a cultural climate that sees at least
some innovation favourably. But Holmgren suggests that market economics
also systematically reward and reinforce addictive consumption patterns.
The consumer economy constantly replaces non-monetary self-reliance with
economically mediated goods and services.

Voluntary frugality threatens to undermine the foundations of the
consumer economy. For this reason, the mainstream treats self-reliant
lifestyles as threatening rather than admirable. Holmgren argues that
self-reliance works as a more generalised and invisible consumer boycott,
undermining the market share and psychosocial dominance of centralised
and large-scale economies.

## Lessons from Addiction Recovery

Holmgren draws specific lessons from dealing with drug addiction that
apply to systemic design problems:

- Acknowledge our own addictive behaviours and admit they are barriers
  to a better life and a more sustainable world
- Recognise the emotional and other benefits or yields we get from the
  addiction
- Avoid guilt and blame of others, including our parents
- Disconnect from reinforcing relationships with addicts unwilling to
  acknowledge or deal with the addiction
- Connect with ex-addicts who understand, and form self-help groups of
  addicts willing to change

## Systemic Addiction in Institutions

Beyond personal addictions, Holmgren identifies systemic addiction
within organisations and institutions. A well-recognised example is
government dependence on revenue from gambling as well as alcohol and
tobacco taxes. High-yield agriculture is addicted to inputs that maintain
those yields while destroying the future productive base. Simply stopping
the input can result in a "cold turkey" of collapsing yield.

## Self-Reliance as Political Action

Holmgren argues that self-reliance tends to work as a more generalised
and invisible consumer boycott, undermining the market share and
psychosocial dominance of centralised and large-scale economies that
support and maintain addictive and dysfunctional behaviour. At the same
time, it tends to foster and stimulate new local forms of economic
activity.

The amorphous and informal nature of self-reliance makes it hard to
identify how effective it is in undermining the power of dysfunctional
economic systems. This invisibility can slow recognition of the power in
people's hands, but it also confounds the efforts of media, corporations
and government to track, control and subvert processes that might
undermine their interests.

## Voluntary Frugality

After three decades of personal commitment to voluntary frugality,
Holmgren concedes disadvantages in what some describe as a poverty
mentality. The biggest contradiction is that accumulated knowledge must
always be given a modest value in small-scale self-reliant systems. Such
systems are not wealthy enough to support a top-heavy design and
management structure.

However, the same skills can overview complex systems and grasp the
driving forces characterising the transition from growth to descent.
Holmgren is convinced that Permaculture thinking and practice are among
the most efficient ways to develop skills for directing large-scale
systems toward more appropriate ends.
