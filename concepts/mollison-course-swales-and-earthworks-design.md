# Swales and Earthworks Design

Swales are one of the most versatile and important earthworks in
[[permaculture-design]]. They serve multiple functions simultaneously:
harvesting water, reducing erosion, building soil fertility, providing
fire protection, and creating productive growing environments.

## How Swales Work

Swales are level trenches dug on contour across a slope. They collect and
hold water, allowing it to infiltrate into the soil rather than running
off and causing erosion. As water infiltrates, it creates a plume of
moisture below the swale that can sustain trees and other vegetation even
during dry periods.

In an existing forest, many leaves will arrive in a swale, and they rot
quickly there. It is a moist site. Your little salamanders run around in
there. You can deliberately add to the leaves in the swale. It is a long
composting system on site. Occasionally, you can take from the swale for
the garden. The older the swales get, and the more the tree roots
penetrate down into the swale, the better they get rid of water.

## Mulch Systems in Swales

Swales greatly decrease the risk of forest fire because they collect a
lot of fuel and rot it very quickly. Swales make for a far more moist
forest than existed before. It is amazing how few trees you have to remove
to run a swale in an existing forest. However, it is a good idea to swale
a forest before you plant it as a forest. Some trees can stand in the
swales.

You can swale out from your garden and mulch into your swales. Now you
decide the sort of mulch you bring in, because you plant trees above the
swale to give you the mulch you want. We get alkaline mulch from western
cedar, acid mulch from oaks, and so on. So you treat your garden from a
continuing input from the mature system, thus reversing the axiom that
maturity exploits immaturity.

## Swales in Urban Areas

Swales have a particular application in urban areas. In an isolated place
where there is no chance that you are going to be able to bring in mulch
material for your garden, you can swale out from your garden, and mulch
into your swales. They collect runoff from off-site and get rid of it on
your site. These swales do not have to be renewed. They become
self-maintaining systems that improve with age.

## Net and Pan Systems

On stony and steep slopes where you cannot possibly run shelves along
them, not even shallow shelves, you go to very small scale systems. Here
is a slope that we will describe as net and pan. You set up very shallow
guide lines for drainage. You can hand cut the drains. You clear the
slope of vegetation at the junctions, and make little flat planting
platforms. This is particularly effective in very rocky country that you
would normally not use for orchards.

A group of five or six people did a fairly large orchard in three days on
a little system like this. They cut little flats in the slope, which
provided a tree on, and they had a little water roll around it, soaking
in. When it overflowed, it came down and soaked in. This is a small scale
system that works well on slopes up to about 19 degrees.

## Slope-Based Design Systems

Slopes give us a very great advantage. When we have this gravitational
advantage, it is possible to do all sorts of things. If our slope is not
an extreme slope, we can put a pond right here in front of the house.
That is a great advantage. It is also a fire barrier, and it is a light
reflector; it also makes for a pleasant environment.

You can grow blueberries in the swales below a pine forest. You can raise
ginseng in the swales. Your swales are obviously ideal sites for certain
useful plants that like this moist, rich, highly mulched situation. There
are many techniques you can use with water in landscapes.

## Gley: Sealing Leaky Dams

When you have a dam that will not hold water, the gley technique is the
best solution. You put green sappy material right across it, six inches
thick. Gather mowings and anything you can obtain. Pack it down. Chip
green leaves and sappy material, second cut hay. Cover all this with sand
or plastic or old carpets or a combination. Then it starts to ferment.
As soon as it goes slimy, you fill it with water and it fills without any
trouble, and will never leak again.

## Biological Dam Structures

We take some of our excavation material and make an island in our lake.
We have increased our shoreline. If we know that we are going to be
drawing quite a lot of water down from this, we throw up low dams across
easily dammed sections that flood at high water. In this way, even when
the other water is four feet down, these dams hold and preserve the
shoreline flora. Many small animals that live along the shore continue to
have refuge.

When you draw off water, your island sticks out a bit higher. Your
shallows are mud-dammed, almost at water level, so that the main water
rises over them a bit, going through them in pipes near the surface. When
it falls, your little mud walls come out and hold the shallows. You don't
bother about sub-surface dams where you have constant level productive
water. Your larger fish cannot get into some places that are too shallow
and too weedy. They provide refuge for quite a lot of fry.
