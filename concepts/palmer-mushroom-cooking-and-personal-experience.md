## Mushroom Cooking Methods and Personal Poisoning Experience

A detailed account of [[julius-auboineau-palmer]]'s personal methodology for testing mushroom edibility, his preferred cooking techniques, a firsthand poisoning incident, and his observations on how environmental conditions affect the edibility and flavor of fungi.

## Overview

Palmer combined empirical rigor with culinary pragmatism in his approach to wild mushrooms. Having personally tested approximately forty varieties, he came to eat more than half of them on a regular basis. His experience spans not only identification and preparation but also a significant poisoning event that reinforced his theories about cross-contamination and the role of preparation methods in neutralizing fungal toxins.

## Palmer's Testing Methodology

Palmer's approach to determining whether an unknown mushroom was edible followed a careful, incremental protocol:

1. **Broil a specimen** on a wire gridiron using only salt and butter — deliberately omitting pepper, which could mask the natural taste.
2. **Eat a very small piece** on an empty stomach and wait to observe any adverse effects.
3. **Gradually increase the quantity** consumed in subsequent sessions, continuing to monitor for symptoms.

Through this process, Palmer found that non-edible fungi consistently revealed themselves through three warning signs:

- **Nauseous slime** — an unpleasant, viscous texture upon cooking.
- **Grassy odor** — a smell reminiscent of cut grass rather than the earthy, nutty, or meaty aromas of edible species.
- **Unpleasant taste** — acrid, bitter, or otherwise offensive flavors even after cooking.

Palmer regarded these sensory indicators as reliable enough that no amount of culinary preparation could make a truly noxious fungus palatable.

## Cooking Methods

### Broiling

Palmer's preferred method for most mushrooms, especially grassy or "tasting" varieties such as the elm-tree mushroom and the honey-colored mushroom (*[[agaricus-melleus]]*). He recommended:

- Using a **wire gridiron** (not a flat pan) to allow fat to drip away.
- Applying **salt and butter only** — no pepper, no strong sauces.
- Cooking until the flesh was tender but not reduced to mush.

He considered broiling essential for mushrooms with stronger or more vegetal flavors, as it concentrated their natural taste without drowning it.

### Stewing in Milk

For milder species, Palmer advocated stewing mushrooms in milk in the manner of an oyster stew. This method produced a rich, creamy dish that he found particularly suited to the common mushroom and other tender-fleshed varieties.

### Drying for Winter Use

Palmer also described the practice of drying mushrooms for winter consumption, noting the Chinese tradition of using dried fungus in cuisine. He regarded dried mushrooms as an effective meat substitute, capable of providing substantial and savory nutrition when fresh provisions were scarce.

## The Coprinus Deliquescens Poisoning Incident

Palmer's most serious encounter with mushroom poisoning occurred not from eating a toxic species directly but through **cross-contamination**. He had purchased common mushrooms that were shipped in a box containing a large number of *[[coprinus-deliquescens]]* (inky cap) specimens. These deliquescent fungi were in a state of advanced decomposition.

### Symptoms

Within a short time after consuming the contaminated mushrooms, Palmer experienced:

- **Dizziness**
- **Nausea**
- **Purging** (vomiting and diarrhea)
- **Perspiration**
- **Alternating cold spells**

The onset was rapid and the symptoms severe, but Palmer credited prompt treatment with preventing a more dangerous outcome.

### Treatment and Recovery

Palmer treated himself with a mixture of **whiskey and sweet-oil in equal parts**. This remedy neutralized the poison within a matter of hours, and he recovered fully. He attributed the effectiveness of this treatment to the sweet-oil's ability to counteract the alkaline poisons produced by the decomposing *Coprinus deliquescens*.

### The Italian Practice

Palmer noted that Italian foragers had long employed a similar principle — eating dangerous fungi with sweet-oil or preserving them in pickle — which they believed neutralized alkaline poisons. His own experience lent empirical support to this traditional practice.

## Environmental Influence on Edibility

One of Palmer's most important observations was that the **growing environment** of a mushroom could fundamentally alter its edibility, flavor, and even its safety. He documented several striking examples:

### Horse-Mushrooms Forced with Potash

Horse-mushrooms that had been forced with potash (potassium carbonate) as a growth accelerant cooked "like soap" and produced a liquor resembling lye. The chemical treatment rendered them unpalatable despite being a normally edible species.

### Boletus Astivalis

Specimens of *Boletus astivalis* growing near decaying fish remains became impregnated with the odor of the decayed fish, making them unfit for consumption despite the species' general edibility.

### Boletus Pachypus and Potato Cross-Contamination

In a curious case, *[[boletus-pachypus]]* stored in a refrigerator communicated a distinct nutty flavor to nearby potatoes through proximity alone — demonstrating that fungal volatiles can permeate and alter the taste of other foods.

### Coprinus Comatus: Lawn vs. Waste Ground

The shaggy ink cap (*Coprinus comatus*) provided the most dramatic example of environmental influence:

- Specimens gathered from **lawns** were delicious and entirely wholesome.
- Specimens from **dust-heaps and brick-yards** were either flavorless or actively deleterious.

Palmer used this as a cautionary illustration: a mushroom's identity alone does not guarantee its safety; where it grew matters equally.

## The Honey-Colored Mushroom: *Agaricus melleus*

The honey-colored mushroom presented a particularly instructive case in Palmer's experience:

- **Raw**, it was acrid and unpleasant — its acridity serving as a natural deterrent.
- **Cooked**, it was entirely harmless and, in Palmer's judgment, quite good eating.
- The mycologist Greville had reported that *A. melleus* killed a dog, yet Palmer ate it regularly without ill effect.

Palmer reconciled this discrepancy by noting that the dog may have consumed the mushroom raw, or that environmental conditions may have affected the specimen in Greville's case.

## Growth Timeline

Palmer provided rough growth estimates for various fungi:

- **Common mushroom**: approximately **10 days** from pin-head stage to mature disk.
- **Coprinariae** (inky caps): less than **5 days** from emergence to full deliquescence.
- Most fleshy fungi: roughly **10 days** from initial appearance to full development.

These timelines were relevant both to foraging (knowing when to return to a patch) and to understanding the rapid decomposition that could cause cross-contamination in harvested collections.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Incremental testing** — eating small quantities on an empty stomach with minimal seasoning — is a safe method for evaluating unknown mushrooms.
2. **Non-edible fungi betray themselves** through slime, grassy odor, and unpleasant taste even after cooking.
3. **Cross-contamination** from decomposing fungi can poison otherwise safe mushrooms; storage conditions are critical.
4. **Sweet-oil** (and similar fatty emollients) can neutralize alkaline fungal poisons, a principle supported by both personal experience and Italian tradition.
5. **Growing environment** fundamentally affects edibility — the same species can be delicious or dangerous depending on where it grew.
6. **Cooking matters** — many mushrooms that are acrid or unpleasant raw become perfectly wholesome when properly prepared.
