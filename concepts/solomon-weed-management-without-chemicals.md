# Weed Management Without Chemicals

Steve Solomon advocates a systematic, non-chemical approach to weed control
that relies on timing, sharp tools, and understanding weed biology. The core
principle is that no weed in creation can survive if its leaves are
repetitively removed before they can manufacture food through photosynthesis.

## The Weekly Weeding Cycle

If the garden is weeded once a week during the growing season, there will
never be large, resistant weeds to chop out. All of them will be killed
rapidly and with little effort when they are small and tender. Solomon
reports that he can weed 2,000 square feet (200 square meters) of growing
beds and the paths between them in about one concentrated hour, done once
a week during peak growing season.

The key insight is that weeds that come back from tubers or bulblets may
require cutting six or eight times over six or eight weeks. But persistence
always wins. Even tough perennial weeds like horseradish or comfrey
eventually succumb to repeated defoliation.

## Hoeing Techniques for Different Soil Conditions

Solomon describes two primary hoeing techniques depending on soil
conditions:

**Weeding soft ground:** Pull the hoe toward you, half an inch (1.25
centimeters) below the surface. This cuts off weeds as though with a
knife. Most weeds will die within hours of being cut. The odd one that
does not die because it was not completely separated from its root system
will almost surely die when you cut it again a week later.

**Weeding compacted soil (including paths):** Hold the hoe entirely
differently. Keep the handle slightly below horizontal, grip about
halfway down the handle, and use this hand as a pivot while the other
hand pulls the sharp blade toward you. Because it is sharp, it cuts off
small weeds efficiently. This action is essentially scraping the surface.

## The Sharp Hoe: Essential Tool

A sharp hoe is the single most important tool for chemical-free weed
management. When you bring a hoe home from the store, it is never sharp.
Most gardeners never properly sharpen their tools after buying them. In
Solomon's gardening classes, the tool-sharpening session is consistently
rated the second-most-popular activity, after the hands-on bed
preparation demonstration.

The difference between a sharp and dull hoe is dramatic. Try chopping
weeds with a new hoe straight from the store and after two minutes you
will be dripping with sweat, tired out for the rest of the day. A
properly sharpened hoe makes the same task effortless.

## Preventing Seed Set

The most important long-term strategy is preventing weeds from setting
seed. A new garden is usually full of weed seeds. When you turn the soil
over in late summer, a lot of those weeds sprout in autumn; the next
spring, a lot more of them sprout. But before they have a chance to get
large, you dig again and kill them off wholesale.

This is one of the great advantages of autumn digging: it stimulates
weed seeds to germinate, and the subsequent winter frost or spring
digging eliminates them before they can reproduce.

## Extensive Spacing Reduces Weed Pressure

One of Solomon's key arguments for wider plant spacing is that it makes
weed control far easier. Because the rows are so far apart in his
recommended extensive spacing system, hoeing weeds is simple. He grew
his variety trials grounds using wide spacing and during the main growing
season, when weeds were growing their fastest, he kept a half acre
weed-free with only one short morning's hoeing each week.

## Crop Canopy as Natural Weed Suppression

Once vegetable plants grow large enough, their leaves strongly shade the
soil below. Even if a weed should sprout beneath the canopy, the shade
prevents it from growing much. Only the odd weed will show itself, and
this one you can yank out by hand. This natural suppression means that
the most intensive weeding is needed only during the first weeks after
planting.

## Surface Cultivation for Moisture Conservation

In gardens without irrigation, surface cultivation serves double duty.
After intense summer thunderstorms, if there is a crust or hard layer
on the surface, rain may run off. A day or so after such a rain, going
out with a hoe to loosen up the surface of every accessible part of the
garden helps the soil fully accept the next chance shower and also helps
keep the moisture now in the earth from evaporating.

This practice is especially vital when gardening with extensive spacing
and limited or no irrigation. The surface dust mulch created by regular
shallow hoeing acts as an insulating layer that dramatically reduces
evaporative moisture loss from the underlying soil.

## Grass Sod Elimination

When starting a new garden in grass sod, the initial weed challenge is
formidable. Solomon describes several approaches:

1.  **Rotary cultivation:** A rear-tined tiller of at least seven or
    eight horsepower can eliminate about 1,000 square feet of sod per
    hour, but only to a depth of about five inches (12 centimeters).
    Multiple passes are required.

2.  **Smothering with compost and amendments:** After mowing the grass
    short (or removing tall grass clippings), spread basic amendments
    and then dig or till them in. This alone will not kill all the grass,
    so repeated cultivation or hoeing is needed.

3.  **Autumn preparation:** Turning sod in late summer or autumn allows
    weed seeds to germinate before winter. Many will be killed by frost
    or by spring digging before they can set seed.

## Dealing with Tough Perennial Weeds

Some of the most challenging weeds in a new garden are perennial grasses
and broadleaf weeds with deep root systems or rhizomes. These may
require repeated cutting over many weeks. The strategy remains the same:
never let the leaves regrow enough to replenish the root's energy
reserves. Consistent weekly attention will eventually exhaust even the
most stubborn perennial.

Solomon notes that once the garden has been established for a few years
and the soil has been improved with organic matter and proper
fertilization, the vegetable crop itself becomes increasingly competitive
against weeds, further reducing the ongoing weeding burden.
