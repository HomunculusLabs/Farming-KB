# Toadstool Eating and Amateur Mycology

Julius A. Palmer's 1877 article "Toadstool-Eating," published in the Popular
Science Monthly, represents one of the first serious American attempts to
encourage safe, personal investigation of wild fungi for food. Written from the
perspective of a self-described "amateur toadstool-eater," the article combined
practical field advice with philosophical arguments against popular superstition
and misinformation.

## The Mushroom-Toadstool Distinction

Palmer opened with a fundamental observation that contradicted common belief:
"Every mushroom is a toadstool, and every toadstool is a mushroom." He argued
that applying the term "toadstool" to all fungi except the cultivated
mushroom was as illogical as calling a beet a "vegetable" while classifying
every other garden plant as something else entirely. The popular distinction
had no botanical basis and served only to perpetuate ignorance and fear of
fungi.

## State of American Mycological Knowledge

Palmer painted a stark picture of mycological knowledge in 1870s America. He
noted that, excepting the writings of Dr. Curtis of South Carolina, he had
found no original American contributions to the literature on edible and
poisonous fungi. The ideas of the four leading British mycologists — Berkeley,
Cooke, Smith, and Badham — were occasionally reprinted by professional
writers but were of limited utility because they described European, not
American, species.

Even the available works presented serious difficulties. Descriptions were
inexact, classifications were not harmonious (generic names varied between
authorities from Sowerby onward), and critically, placing a specimen in its
proper family did not define its edibility — one mushroom could be edible while
its nearly identical "twin brother" was poisonous.

## Debunking Popular Myths

Palmer devoted considerable space to demolishing the folk wisdom and newspaper
advice that passed for mushroom identification guidance. He systematically
contradicted each commonly held belief:

**"Mushrooms should peel like a potato."** Palmer agreed that many fungi peel
readily, but noted that this was true of both edible and poisonous species.

**"The stem should come out like an umbrella handle."** Whole classes of
fungi, many of them noxious, have free gills that cause the stem to separate
easily from the cap.

**"All found in damp, dark places are poisonous."** Palmer countered that
the very best edible mushrooms grow in damp, dark places.

**"Thin-capped mushrooms should be avoided."** The edible fairy ring
mushroom (*Marasmius oreades*) has a notably thin cap.

**"Mushrooms with stems growing from the side are dangerous."** The elm-tree
mushroom (*Agaricus ulmarius*) has an eccentric stem and is edible.

**"All milky mushrooms are dangerous except the orange one."** The brown
milk-mushroom (*Lactarius volemus*) and several others are edible.

**"Equal-length gills indicate poison."** The entire Russula family has
equal gills, and many are edible.

**"Mushrooms that turn to dark liquid are poisonous."** The delicious
shaggy mane (*Coprinus comatus*) literally dissolves into ink.

**"Acrid-tasting mushrooms are poisonous."** The honey mushroom (*Agaricus
melleus*) is very acrid raw but harmless when cooked.

Palmer concluded that no test based on form, shape, color, basis of growth,
or experimental action would reliably distinguish healthful from harmful fungi.

## The Broiling Test

Palmer advocated a practical method for testing unknown fungi. After tracing a
specimen to its family, he would broil it with salt and butter (no pepper) and
eat a small piece on an empty stomach. He would then increase the quantity in
successive experiments until feeling safe or experiencing unpleasant symptoms.

Usually, the non-edible fungus would reveal its character during cooking:
nauseous slime would weep from the stem, a grassy and disagreeable odor would
arise, or the taste would be so unpleasant that there would be no desire for
another bite.

## The Taste Theory

Palmer advanced a significant proposition: "no fungus which, unspiced, being
plainly cooked with dry heat, commends itself to the taste, can be, in the
least, dangerous to human life." He elaborated that mushrooms are often
disguised by gravies, batter, spices, and other seasonings, which mask the
natural warning signals that taste provides. The stinging sensation of a
harmful fungus affects the tongue slightly but the throat and tonsils more,
and has its greatest effect on the stomach and bowels.

## Notable Species Described

Palmer provided detailed descriptions of several edible species suitable for
beginners:

- **Shaggy mane (*Coprinus comatus*)**: Ovate like a turtle's egg, with a
  brown smooth top and white mane-like fringe. Turns to ink after maturity.
  Has no poisonous counterpart. Must be eaten before gills blacken.

- **Elm-tree mushroom (*Agaricus ulmarius*)**: Grows on elm in fall, thick
  solid stem (sometimes lateral), broad white gills, light yellow cap that
  becomes spotted and cracked with age.

- **Spreading hydnum (*Hydnum repandum*)**: Yellow, firm-fleshed, with
  distinctive teeth or spines under the cap. Safe for beginners.

- **Giant puffball (*Lycoperdon giganteum*)**: Smooth skin, pure white inside.
  If the knife leaves a yellow stain, it is too old. No poisonous look-alike.
