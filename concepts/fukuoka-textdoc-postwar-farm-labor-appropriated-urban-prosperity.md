# Japanese Postwar Prosperity Built on Appropriated Farm Labor

Masanobu Fukuoka argues that Japan's celebrated postwar economic miracle was fundamentally built on labor drained from the farming population. The skyscrapers, highways, and subways of Japanese cities were constructed not through abstract economic genius, but through the systematic appropriation of farm workers displaced by mechanization. This analysis reframes Japan's "high growth" period as a story of rural destruction enabling urban construction.

## The Seedbed of the Japanese People

No country experienced such a sudden and dramatic transformation as Japan following World War II. The country rose rapidly from the ruins of war to become a major economic power. During this process, the farming and fishing populations — described by Fukuoka as "the seedbed of the Japanese people" — fell from fifty percent of the overall population at the end of the war to less than twenty percent.

This is an extraordinary demographic shift. In the span of a single generation, nearly two-thirds of the people who had sustained Japan's food supply for centuries left the land. They did not leave voluntarily in most cases — they were pushed off by economic forces and pulled toward the cities by the promise of wage labor.

Fukuoka insists that without the dexterous, hard-working farmer, the infrastructure of Japan's metropolises would never have materialized. Japan owes its current prosperity to the labor it appropriated from the farming population and placed at the service of urban civilization. The cities consumed the countryside's most productive asset: its people.

## The Official Narrative vs. the Farmer's Interpretation

Japan's rapid postwar growth is generally attributed to good fortune and wise leadership. Economists point to industrial policy, export-led growth, and technological innovation. Fukuoka offers a sharply different interpretation from the farmer's perspective, one that grounds the economic miracle in the dispossession of [[fukuoka-textdoc-energy-efficiency-agricultural-labor]].

Changes in the self-image of the farming population led to the adoption of new agricultural methods. As farming became less labor-intensive through mechanization, surplus manpower poured out of the countryside into the towns and cities, bringing prosperity to the urban civilization. The city's gain was the country's loss.

But far from being a blessing, this prosperity has made things harder on the farmer. In Fukuoka's vivid phrase, the farmer "tightened the noose about his own neck." The very modernization that was supposed to liberate the farmer from drudgery instead liberated him from his land.

## The Transport-Tiller as Turning Point

The first critical step was the arrival of the motorized transport-tiller in the farming village — a major turning point in [[fukuoka-breakdown-japanese-agriculture]]. This was rapidly followed by three-wheeled vehicles and trucks. Before long, ropeways, monorails, and paved roads stretched to the furthest corners of the village, completely altering the farmer's notions of time and space.

The transport-tiller was the thin edge of the wedge. It promised to reduce labor and increase efficiency, and it delivered on that promise — but the labor it reduced was labor that the farmer valued as part of his identity. The temporal rhythms of agricultural life — governed by seasons, sunrise and sunset, the growth cycles of crops — were replaced by the mechanical rhythms of engines and schedules.

## The Mechanization Cascade

The wave of change from labor-intensive to capital-intensive farming brought the replacement of the horse-drawn plow with tillers, and later, tractors. Methods of pesticide and [[cervantes-fertilizer-application-strain-feeding]] underwent major revisions, with motorized hand sprayers being abandoned in favor of helicopter spraying. Traditional farming with draft animals was abandoned and replaced with methods involving the heavy application of [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] and pesticides.

Each step in this cascade reduced the need for human labor on the farm, freeing workers to migrate to cities. Each step also increased the farmer's dependence on purchased inputs — machinery, fuel, chemicals — creating an economic trap from which escape became increasingly difficult. The farmer who once produced most of what he needed now bought everything from the industrial economy.

The cascade was not accidental. Each innovation created the conditions for the next. The tiller required fuel, which required supply chains. The chemicals required application equipment, which required maintenance and replacement. The entire system was designed to be self-reinforcing: the more a farmer mechanized, the more he needed to mechanize. What appeared as progress was, in ecological terms, a progressive deepening of dependency.

## Mechanization as Industrial Foundation

The rapid mechanization of agriculture lit the fires for the revival and precipitous growth of the machine industry, while the adoption of pesticides, [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], and petroleum-based farming materials laid the foundation for development of the chemical industry.

This is the crucial insight: agriculture was not merely a sector that benefited from industrialization — it was the engine that drove industrialization. The demand created by millions of farmers purchasing machinery, chemicals, and fuel provided the initial market that launched Japan's manufacturing boom. By the mid-1950s, agriculture had become a captive market for industrial products, and the farmer had become a consumer of the very industries his displaced labor was building.

## The Betrayal of the Late 1960s

The situation changed completely in the late sixties and early seventies. Stability of food supply had been achieved and the economy overflowed with vigor. Politicians and businessmen started thinking of how to bring the large number of farmers and their land into the picture.

Once food surpluses started to arise, the farmers became a weight around the government's neck. The food control system set up to ensure an adequate food supply began to be regarded as a burden on the nation. The [[fukuoka-textdoc-basic-agriculture-law-japan-food-self-sufficiency-crisis]] Law of 1961, rather than serving as a foundation for farmers, established controls over the farmer and passed the reins of control to the financial community.

The food control system that had once guaranteed farmers a fair price for their rice became an instrument of control. Production quotas were imposed, acreage reduction programs were mandated, and the prices paid to farmers were squeezed to keep urban food costs low. The farmer who had been encouraged to increase production now found himself penalized for doing exactly what he had been told to do. The policies that were supposed to support farmers had been inverted into policies that controlled and constrained them.

## Farmers as Selfish Monopolizers

The general public started thinking that [[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]] could be put to better use in industry and housing than for food production. City dwellers even began to see farmers, who were reluctant to part with their land, as selfish monopolizers of land. Laborers and office workers joined in the effort to drive farmers off their land, and taxes as high as those on housing land were levied on farmland.

This represents a complete inversion of values. The people who produced the nation's food were now seen as obstacles to progress. The land that had sustained generations was now viewed as raw material for development.

## The Backfire of Increased Production

The effort by farmers to raise food production appeared to have backfired against them. Even though Japan's food self-sufficiency dropped below thirty percent, farmers were unable to speak up because the public was under the illusion that the farmland reduction policy was in the interest of the consumer. Somewhere along the way, the farmer lost both his land and the freedom to choose the crops he wished to raise.

Farmers have simply gone with the flow of the times. Today, most of them lament that they can't make a decent living off farming. The experience of Japanese farmers over the thirty postwar years is unprecedented and poses very grave problems for the future. A generation of farmers was sacrificed to build an industrial economy that now regards their descendants as an inconvenience.

## The Psychological Dimension

Fukuoka's analysis includes a psychological dimension that is often overlooked. The mechanization of agriculture did not merely change how farmers worked — it changed how farmers thought about themselves and their relationship to the land. The farmer who once identified as a steward of the land came to see himself as a businessman managing inputs and outputs. The spiritual connection to the land was severed along with the connection to traditional methods.

This psychological transformation made it easier for farmers to accept policies that were against their long-term interests. A farmer who sees himself as a businessman will respond to economic incentives differently than a farmer who sees himself as a steward. The businessman will sell the land when the price is right; the steward will hold it regardless of price. Japan's [[fukuoka-japan-postwar-agricultural-transformation-mechanization-trap]] policies systematically cultivated the businessman mindset in order to facilitate the transfer of land and labor to the industrial sector.

## The Hidden Cost of Urban Prosperity

Fukuoka's analysis forces a reckoning with the true cost of Japan's economic miracle. The prosperity of the cities was purchased at the price of rural destruction. The food self-sufficiency ratio dropped from nearly one hundred percent before the war to below thirty percent. Japan became one of the world's largest food importers, dependent on global supply chains for the sustenance of its population.

A nation that cannot feed itself is not truly prosperous — it is merely wealthy enough to buy food from others. And that wealth was built on the labor of the very farmers whose displacement created the food dependency in the first place.

## Mechanization and the Loss of Rural Culture

The displacement of farm labor did not merely move people from rural to urban areas — it destroyed a way of life. Rural communities that had existed for centuries, with their own traditions, knowledge systems, and social structures, were dismantled as young people migrated to cities. The elderly were left to manage farms they could no longer work alone. Traditional festivals, craft skills, and ecological knowledge were lost as the rural population aged and shrank.

This cultural loss is invisible in economic calculations but represents a genuine impoverishment of Japanese society. The knowledge of how to read weather patterns, select seed varieties, manage irrigation, and maintain soil fertility without chemicals was accumulated over centuries and discarded in a single generation. Fukuoka's work represents one of the few systematic attempts to preserve and transmit this traditional agricultural wisdom.
