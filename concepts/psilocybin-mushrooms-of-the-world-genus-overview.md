About 30,000 mushroom species have been documented worldwide; roughly one in 300 is known to contain psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, or nor-baeocystin. The species producing psilocybin are clearly concentrated in the genus Psilocybe, which has more than 80 active species (Stijve 1995). A few psilocybin mushrooms occur in other genera, including Panaeolus, Pluteus, Gymnopilus, Conocybe, and Inocybe. Although the vast majority of species in these minor genera are not active, more than half of the species in the genus Psilocybe contain psilocybin.

## The Major Genera

### Genus Psilocybe

The most important genus for psilocybin-containing mushrooms, with more than 80 active species out of approximately 180 total taxa. Guzman's monographs (1983, 1995) are the most thorough taxonomic treatments. Psilocybe mushrooms are saprophytes found in a wide range of habitats: dungs, mosses, soils, grasslands, and decaying wood debris. When moist, most have viscid, deep-brown caps that fade to yellowish brown when drying (hygrophanous). Active species, particularly those high in psilocin, bruise bluish where injured. Gills are usually dark brown with whitish edges. Spore deposits are purplish brown to black.

No known poisonous species exist in this genus, making it the safest for identification. Many mycologists advocate an expanded Psilocybe sensu lato that would include most Stropharia and Hypholoma species. Within the genus, species can be grouped by habitat: dung-inhabiting (P. cubensis, P. coprophila), grassland (P. semilanceata, P. mexicana), and wood-decomposing (P. cyanescens, P. azurescens, P. baeocystis).

The most potent Psilocybe species include P. azurescens (up to 1.78% psilocybin), P. bohemica (up to 1.34%), P. semilanceata (up to 1.71% in one specimen), and P. cyanescens (up to 1.68%). These are all wood-inhabiting or grassland species from temperate regions.

### Genus Panaeolus

Species are recognized by their mottled or spotted gills, caused by uneven ripening of spore-producing cells (basidia). Many grow in dung, while others prefer grassy or rarely woodland habitats. They typically have hemispheric caps, relatively long stems, and produce black spore prints. Under an expanded concept (Panaeolus sensu lato), this genus includes the subgenera Panaeolina, Copelandia (tropical bluing species including P. cyanescens, P. cambodginiensis, and P. tropicalis), and Anellaria.

Several species consistently produce psilocybin and/or psilocin. None have proven poisonous. Notable active species include P. cyanescens (a tropical species, not to be confused with [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] is one of the most widely distributed psilocybin-containing species, found across much of the temperate world. [[conocybe-cyanopus]] contains up to 1.01% psilocybin -- making it one of the most potent species in any genus. Other reported active species include C. smithii and C. kuehneriana.

Beware: the deadly Pholiotina filaris (formerly Conocybe filaris) also belongs here and contains amatoxins similar to Amanita phalloides. Several Galerinas resemble Conocybes. Stamets recommends that collectors avoid the genus Conocybe until sufficiently skilled at identification.

### Genus Gymnopilus

Medium to large mushrooms with rusty orange to yellow-orange spore prints, dry caps, and well-developed veils. Approximately 75 species occur in North America and fewer than 150 worldwide. Ten species have confirmed psilocybin activity: G. aeruginosus, G. braendlei, G. intermedius, G. luteoviridis, G. liquiritiae, G. luteus, G. purpuratus, G. spectabilis, G. validipes, and G. viridans.

The "big laughing mushroom" (G. spectabilis) shows vast regional variation in chemical content -- active in Japan, Ohio, Michigan, Massachusetts, and eastern Canada, but inactive in Europe. Caps can reach 5-40 cm broad, making it one of the largest psychoactive mushrooms. This extremely bitter mushroom varies substantially in its psilocybin content from one geographical region to the next.

G. purpuratus, with its distinctive reddish purplish brown caps, has been reported from Europe and south Australia. Gartz (1994) reported 0.25% psilocybin, 0.33% psilocin, and 0.03% baeocystin from cultivated specimens. All Gymnopilus collectors should familiarize themselves with the deadly genus Galerina, which shares rusty spore color.

### Genus Inocybe

The most dangerous genus for psilocybin hunters. Species are among the most difficult of all mushrooms to identify, even for experts. Of the estimated 400-600 species, approximately 150 are recognized. Five species have tested positive for psilocybin: I. aeruginascens, I. corydalina var. corydalina, I. corydalina var. erinaceomorphia, I. coelestium, and I. haemacta.

However, 34 out of 39 species tested in one Russian study contained toxic muscarine. Collecting Inocybes for recreational purposes is described by Stamets as "mycological Russian roulette." The presence of psilocybin in some Inocybe species is of academic interest only; the risks of misidentification are simply too great. Stamets recommends avoiding this genus entirely.
