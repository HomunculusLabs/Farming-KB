## Overview

Fukuoka extends his [[fukuoka-critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] agriculture to [[fukuoka-natural-livestock-farming]],
framing the difference between scientific and natural approaches as a
fundamental philosophical divide. "[[fukuoka-illusions-scientific-farming-centrifugal-centripetal-model]] is content to think of
conditional truth as the truth, but [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] makes every effort to
discard all premises and conditions and seek out a truth without conditions."
This page explores how Fukuoka applies this distinction to the raising of
animals, arguing that true [[holzer-natural-animal-husbandry]] requires abandoning the very
concept of raising livestock entirely.

## Scientific Method Versus Natural Observation

The contrast between scientific and natural approaches to livestock is
sharply drawn in how each studies [[query-how-do-i-start-black-soldier-fly-farming-for-composting-and-animal-feed]]. To study a particular feed,
"[[fukuoka-illusions-of-scientific-farming]] will give various formulations to cows chained in a
barn, representing a certain set of environmental conditions, and judge the
mixture producing the best results to be superior to the others." This is
what Fukuoka calls [[fukuoka-textdoc-intuitive-reasoning-vs-inductive-experimentation]], a method that yields only
conditional truth.

[[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]] does not follow this type of reasoning. "Because its goal
is unconditional truth it begins by examining the cow from a standpoint
that disregards environmental conditions, by asking how the cow lives in
open nature." But it does not immediately analyze what the cow eats when
and where. Rather, it takes a broader perspective and looks at how a cow
is born and grows in its natural environment.

## Beyond Food Alone

By paying too much attention to what the cow feeds on, "we lose a broader
understanding of how it lives and what its needs are." More is required to
sustain life than just food. Nor are problems of sustenance resolved by
food alone. Many other factors relate to life: weather, climate, living
environment, exercise, sleep, and more.

Even on the subject of food, "what a cow does not eat, dislikes, or has
low nutritive value is generally thought worthless, but may actually be
indispensable in certain cases." Fukuoka argues that we must find a way,
within the broad associations between man, livestock, and nature, of
rearing animals that leaves them free and unrestrained. The scientific
method of isolating variables in a barn fundamentally distorts our
understanding.

## The Notion of Raising Should Not Exist

"The very notion of raising livestock should not even exist in natural
farming." Nature is the one that raises and grows. Man follows nature; all
he needs to know is with what and in what manner cattle live. When a farmer
designs and builds a barn or a chicken coop, he should not rely on his
human reasoning and feelings about what animals need.

Even if the scientist conducts independent studies on temperature and
ventilation, "it is only natural that his results will show that these
should be raised under cool conditions in summer and warm conditions in
winter." The conclusion that an optimum temperature is needed is a natural
consequence of the method used, and "certainly is not an immutable truth."

## Hot and Cold Do Not Exist in Nature

Fukuoka makes a striking philosophical claim about the nature of
temperature. "Although high and low temperatures exist in nature, the
notions of hot and cold do not." One will never be wrong in starting with
the assumption that the [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]] are everywhere and at all
times just right for the animals living there.

The size, height, frame, construction, windows, and floor of animal
enclosures have been improved on the basis of diverse theories, but "we
have to return to the starting point and try making a fundamental
turnabout." Without hot and cold as problems to be solved, the barn is no
longer necessary. All that is needed, for the convenience of man, is the
smallest of sheds.
