## Overview

P-glycoprotein is an ATP-dependent efflux transporter encoded by the ABCB1 gene. It sits in cell membranes and
pumps a broad range of hydrophobic or amphipathic compounds out of cells. In pharmacology it is usually
abbreviated P-gp and is also called MDR1, reflecting its discovery in multidrug-resistant cancer cells.

The transporter matters because it changes where drugs go, how long they remain in tissues, and whether they
reach pharmacological targets. It can limit oral absorption, protect the brain, enhance biliary and renal
elimination, and reduce intracellular drug accumulation in tumors or pathogens.

P-gp is not a metabolic enzyme. It does not chemically transform a drug; it moves the drug across a membrane.
Nevertheless, transport and metabolism are tightly linked because efflux can determine how much substrate
reaches enzymes and how quickly metabolites leave cells.

## Molecular Mechanism

P-gp belongs to the ATP-binding cassette transporter family. It contains transmembrane domains that recognize
substrates within the lipid bilayer and nucleotide-binding domains that bind and hydrolyze ATP. Energy from
ATP hydrolysis drives conformational changes that move substrate from the inner leaflet or cytosolic side
toward the extracellular space.

Its substrate binding is broad rather than exquisitely specific. Many P-gp substrates are bulky, lipophilic,
and cationic or weakly basic, although the range is not limited to one chemical class. This polyspecificity
lets the transporter function as a general chemical defense system.

Because the transporter works in the membrane, apparent affinity can depend on membrane partitioning as much
as on simple aqueous concentration. A compound that accumulates in lipid can be efficiently intercepted even
if its free cytosolic concentration is low.

## Tissue Distribution

P-gp is expressed in tissues that control barriers and excretion. Important sites include intestinal
epithelium, liver canalicular membranes, kidney proximal tubules, the blood-brain barrier, placenta, testes,
and some immune cells. The orientation of the transporter usually favors movement away from protected
compartments.

In the intestine, P-gp can pump absorbed drug back into the lumen. In the liver, it can move compounds into
bile. In the kidney, it contributes to tubular secretion. At the blood-brain barrier, it reduces penetration
of many central nervous system-active or potentially neurotoxic compounds.

This distribution means that P-gp can affect both systemic exposure and tissue-specific exposure. A plasma
concentration may look adequate while the brain or tumor concentration remains low because efflux is locally
strong.

## Cancer and Multidrug Resistance

The name MDR1 comes from cancer cells that became resistant to multiple structurally unrelated
chemotherapeutic agents. Overexpression of P-gp can lower intracellular concentrations of cytotoxic drugs,
allowing tumor cells to survive concentrations that would otherwise be lethal.
