# Teaming with Microbes — Classic Soil Science

In *Teaming with Microbes*, Lowenfels and Lewis present the physical and chemical foundations of soil science as essential background for understanding the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]]. They argue that soil is "the house in which all the organisms of the soil food web live."

## What Is Soil?

Soil is "all the loose, unconsolidated, mineral and organic matter in the upper layer of the earth's crust." Using the apple analogy: if the Earth were an apple, the usable soil would be thinner than the skin.

## Soil Formation Through Weathering

Soil is primarily a product of weathering — the sum impact of natural forces that decay rocks:
- **Physical weathering**: wind, rain, snow, sun, cold, glacial grinding, water action
- **Chemical weathering**: carbonic acid and other chemical reactions dissolving rock minerals
- **Biological weathering**: plant roots, microorganisms, and other life forms breaking down rock

## Soil Texture: Sand, Silt, and Clay

### Sand (0.0625 to 2mm)
- Visible to the naked eye
- Large pore spaces — water drains quickly, mostly gravitational water
- Gritty texture when rubbed between fingers
- Too much sand = poor water and nutrient retention

### Silt (0.004 to 0.0625mm)
- Requires a microscope to see individual particles
- Moderate pore spaces — holds more capillary water than sand
- Feels like flour when rubbed between fingers

### Clay (less than 0.002mm)
- Requires an electron microscope to see individual particles
- "Plastic" and slippery when wet
- Tiny particles but enormous total surface area
- If a clay particle were a marigold seed, a sand grain would be a garden wheelbarrow
- Holds tremendous nutrients but can become waterlogged

### Loam: The Ideal
- Roughly equal parts sand, silt, and clay with 5-10% organic matter
- Has the surface area of silt/clay (nutrient holding) plus the pore space of sand (drainage)
- Good garden soil: 30-50% sand, 30-50% silt, 20-30% clay, 5-10% organic matter

## Water in Soil

Three types of water in soil:
- **Gravitational water** — moves downward through large pores; drains away quickly
- **Capillary water** — held in smaller pores by surface tension; available to plants; can move uphill
- **Hygroscopic water** — thin film on particle surfaces; not available to plants; critical for microbial survival

## Soil Structure

Soil structure — how particles aggregate — is more important than texture:
- Good structure means adequate drainage plus water retention
- [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]] (from fungi) and bacterial slime are the "glues" that bind aggregates
- Soil organisms of all sizes create pores as they move through soil
- Good structure "withstands torrential rains, the drying of desert-like droughts, herds of animal traffic, and deep freezes"

## Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)

Clay and humus particles carry negative electrical charges that attract positive nutrient cations (Ca++, K+, Na+, Mg++, NH4+). This CEC is the soil's nutrient-holding ability. Organic matter dramatically increases CEC.

## Organic Matter and Humus
Organic matter, while typically comprising only 3-6% of soil volume, is
the biological engine of soil fertility. It exists in three forms: fresh
residues (recently added plant and animal material), active humus
(partially decomposed matter undergoing rapid microbial processing), and
stable humus (long-lived, dark-coloured material that persists for
decades to centuries). Stable humus is the most important component for
long-term soil health -- it provides the [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]] that feeds microbial communities, improves soil
structure through aggregation, and dramatically increases CEC. Humus can
hold 80-90% of its weight in water, making it crucial for drought
resilience. In the soil food web framework, organic matter is the energy
source that drives the entire system: bacteria and fungi decompose it,
protozoa and nematodes eat the decomposers, and their wastes become
plant-available nutrients.

## Soil pH and Nutrient Availability
Soil pH (the measure of hydrogen ion concentration on a logarithmic
scale from 0-14) dramatically affects nutrient availability. Most garden
plants prefer a slightly acidic pH of 6.0-6.8, where the widest range of
nutrients are available for root uptake. At low pH (below 5.5), aluminium
and manganese become toxic while phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium
become locked in unavailable forms. At high pH (above 7.5), iron,
manganese, boron, and copper become deficient. The pH of soil is
primarily determined by the parent rock material and the biological
activity within it. Bacterial decomposition tends to produce alkaline
conditions, while fungal decomposition produces acidic conditions. This
is one reason why [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] is connected to the pH preferences of
different plant communities -- grasslands and vegetables tend to prefer
the more bacterial (neutral to alkaline) soils, while trees and shrubs
often prefer the more fungal (acidic) soils.

## Soil Profiles and Horizons
Mature soils develop distinct horizontal layers called horizons. The O
horizon is the organic layer of leaf litter and decomposing material on
the surface. The A horizon (topsoil) is the dark, mineral-rich layer
where most biological activity occurs and where plant roots are
concentrated. The E horizon (eluviation layer) is a light-coloured zone
where minerals and organic matter have been leached downward. The B
horizon (subsoil) accumulates the materials leached from above -- clay,
iron, aluminium, and dissolved organic compounds. The C horizon is the
partially weathered parent rock material, and the R horizon is the
underlying bedrock. In agricultural and garden contexts, the A horizon
is the most critical zone. Deep-rooted plants and [[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]] processes can help rebuild thin topsoil over
time, while tillage and erosion can destroy it in a single season.

## Redox and Anaerobic Soil Conditions
Soil oxygen availability affects both chemistry and biology. Well-aerated
soils have oxidising conditions that favour beneficial aerobic microbes,
efficient decomposition, and healthy root function. Waterlogged or
compacted soils develop reducing (anaerobic) conditions where oxygen is
depleted. Under anaerobic conditions, denitrifying bacteria convert
available nitrate (NO3-) to nitrogen gas (N2), which escapes to the
atmosphere -- a major mechanism of nitrogen loss from waterlogged
agricultural soils. Anaerobic conditions also produce toxic compounds
such as hydrogen sulphide, ferrous iron, and organic acids that can
damage plant roots. The relationship between soil structure, water
management, and aeration is central to [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] in both garden and farm
contexts.

## Practical Applications

The principles outlined on this page connect directly to hands-on work in gardens,
farms, and homesteads. Integrating these concepts with complementary techniques from
related disciplines can significantly improve outcomes. Start with small-scale
implementation and scale up as understanding deepens through direct observation.
Key practices derived from these soil science principles include regular organic matter
addition through [[teaming-with-microbes-mulch-and-soil-food-web]], minimizing
soil disturbance to preserve aggregate structure, managing pH through appropriate amendments,
and ensuring adequate drainage to maintain aerobic conditions that support the
[[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]].
