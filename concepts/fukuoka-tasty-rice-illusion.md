# Fukuoka's Tasty Rice Illusion

## Overview

Fukuoka's essay on "Tasty Rice" dissects one of the most widespread delusions of modern agricultural society: the belief that distinct varieties of rice possess inherently superior taste, and that pursuing "tasty rice" through breeding, cultivation techniques, and market mechanisms is a worthwhile endeavor. This argument is a specific application of his broader [[fukuoka-natural-diet-philosophy]] and reveals how consumer delusion drives agricultural dysfunction.

## The Campaign for Tasty Rice

More than thirty years have passed since the days of famine and hunger in Japan following the end of World War II. Today, those times appear as but a bad dream. With bumper crops of grain seen over the last dozen years or so, rice surpluses have formed and there is no longer enough warehouse space to store all the old grain.

Dissatisfied consumers are furious, complaining that the price of rice is too high, that they have no need for "bad-tasting" rice, that they want to eat "good-tasting" rice, and that new and more palatable varieties had better be produced. Politicians, traders, and agricultural cooperatives have added their voices, pounding desks and huddling together to come up with ideas. Agricultural technicians have been ordered to encourage farmers to stop growing "bad-tasting" rice and grow "tasty" varieties instead.

Fukuoka argues this controversy is possible only when people have no idea of the true nature of the food problem. The debate over good-tasting rice gives a clear view of the world of fantasy in which modern consumers and agricultural institutions live.

## The Minimal Difference Between Varieties

Fukuoka does not deny that "tasty" and "untasty" rice exist, but points out that the difference between varieties is very small. Even were a farmer to select a good-tasting variety of rice and, sacrificing yields, willingly devote himself wholeheartedly to perfecting techniques for growing good-tasting rice, just how delicious would the rice be? No rice would win unanimous praise by a panel of samplers. And even if it did, the difference with other varieties would be very, very minor.

## The Instability of Taste Through the Chain

Tasty rice cannot always be produced from a tasty variety. It is far too simplistic to think that the original difference in taste between varieties will be sustained right up to the dinner table. The inherent taste is subject to reversal at every stage:

**Growing conditions**: Depending on the land on which it is grown, the method of cultivation, and the weather, poor-tasting varieties may approach tasty varieties in flavor, while tasty rice, when hit by bad weather and heavily attacked by disease and pests, is often less palatable than poor-tasting rice. The minor differences in taste between varieties are always subject to reversal.

**Processing**: Even when it appears as if tasty rice has been produced, the taste may deteriorate during harvesting, threshing, or processing. The dealer grades rice from various farming districts, processes it by milling to various degrees, and mixes it in given proportions to create hundreds of varieties with distinctive flavors. Tasty rice can be converted into tasteless rice, and tasteless rice into tasty rice.

**Cooking**: When the rice is cooked at home, whether one soaks the rice overnight in water and drains it, how much water one uses, how high the flame, the type of fuel, and even the quality of the rice cooker can all have an effect on the taste. The difference between good- and bad-tasting varieties and between old and new rice can fall either way depending on processing and cooking.

## The One-in-a-Thousand Probability

Fukuoka calculates that even considering just a few of the production conditions, the chances that a tasty variety of rice will be grown, properly processed, and cooked skillfully to give rice of outstanding taste is not more than one in a thousand. This means that, even with the best of luck, someone may encounter truly tasty rice perhaps once in every two or three years. And if that person does not happen to be very hungry at the time, all will have been for naught.

## Who Benefits?

This campaign for tasty rice has placed a great burden on the farmer and forced the housewife to buy high-priced rice without knowing what is going on. The only one likely to benefit from all this is the merchant. Bitten by the illusion of slightly tasty rice, people today are all floundering about in a sea of mud and toil.

## The Deeper Philosophical Point

The tasty rice debate illustrates a fundamental principle of Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-natural-food-philosophy]]: taste is not an inherent property of food but a product of the relationship between the eater and the eaten. The farmer, the dealer, and the housewife all participate in creating the experience of taste, and in a deeper sense, no one creates tasty rice -- it arises from conditions that no single actor controls.

One could say that it is the farmer, the rice dealer, and the housewife who create tasty rice. But in a sense, no one creates tasty rice. This paradox reflects Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy-and-practice]]: the most important things in life are not produced by individual effort but emerge from the proper relationship between all elements.

## Hunger as the True Spice

Underlying Fukuoka's analysis is a simple truth that modern consumers have forgotten: the best seasoning for food is hunger. A person who is truly hungry finds any rice delicious. The modern consumer, surrounded by abundance and divorced from the experience of genuine hunger, seeks to manufacture delight through variety selection and processing -- a doomed project that only enriches the intermediary.

## The Illusion of Progress in Taste

The tasty rice campaign represents a broader pattern in modern society: the substitution of consumer choice for genuine quality. Just as scientific farming substitutes technological intervention for natural productivity, the tasty rice campaign substitutes variety selection and processing for the simple experience of eating food grown in healthy soil by someone who cares about the land.

## Relevance to Modern Food Systems

The tasty rice illusion parallels many modern food phenomena:

- The pursuit of "craft" or "artisanal" products as inherently superior
- The marketing of specific varieties as premium
- The complex supply chains that transform food between farm and table
- The consumer's loss of connection with the source and reality of food
- The agricultural industry's exploitation of consumer desires to drive demand

Fukuoka would argue that all of these represent movement away from a [[fukuoka-natural-diet-theory]] and toward a food system driven by delusion and commerce.
