## Fungal Biodiversity in Marine and Coastal Ecosystems

## Overview

Marine fungi inhabit environments ranging from intertidal zones to deep-sea
sediments, hydrothermal vents, and the open water column. Once considered
depauperate relative to terrestrial ecosystems, marine fungal diversity is now
recognized as substantial and largely undescribed. Molecular surveys have
revealed that the ocean harbors unique fungal assemblages, with many lineages
apparently endemic to marine habitats. Current estimates suggest over 10,000
marine fungal species, of which only approximately 1,100 have been described.

## Marine Fungal Habitats

Coastal zones experience the most dynamic environmental conditions in the marine
realm, with regular immersion, exposure, temperature fluctuations, and salinity
variation. Intertidal wood supports rich communities of marine Ascomycota
including Lulworthia and Halosphaeriaceae. Driftwood and mangrove detritus are
particularly productive fungal substrates, with mangrove ecosystems alone
harboring hundreds of species.

Marine sediments from shallow coastal waters to abyssal plains contain diverse
[[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]] produced by marine
Ascomycota. This decomposition drives carbon and [[fungal-lichen-stress-tolerance-and-poikilohydry]] and disease
resistance. Parasitic marine fungi infect diatoms, other fungi, and marine
invertebrates. Fungal parasites regulate marine phytoplankton populations and
influence marine food web dynamics.

Marine yeasts including Aureobasidium pullulans, Rhodotorula species, and
Debaryomyces hansenii are cosmopolitan in surface waters. Recent metabarcoding
surveys have revealed unexpected fungal diversity in deep pelagic waters,
including Basidiomycota and early-diverging fungal lineages previously unknown
from marine settings.
