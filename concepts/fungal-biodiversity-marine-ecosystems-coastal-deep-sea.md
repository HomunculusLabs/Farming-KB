# Fungal Biodiversity in Marine and Coastal Ecosystems

## Overview

Marine fungi inhabit oceanic and coastal environments ranging from
intertidal zones to deep-sea sediments, hydrothermal vents, and the
open water column. Once considered depauperate relative to terrestrial
ecosystems, marine fungal diversity is now recognized as substantial
and largely undescribed. Molecular surveys have revealed that the ocean
harbors unique fungal assemblages distinct from terrestrial and
freshwater communities, with many lineages apparently endemic to marine
habitats. Current estimates suggest over 10,000 marine fungal species,
of which only approximately 1,100 have been formally described.

## Marine Fungal Habitats

### Intertidal and Supralittoral Zones

Coastal zones experience the most dynamic environmental conditions in
the marine realm, with regular immersion, exposure, temperature
fluctuations, and salinity variation. Intertidal wood, a globally
distributed substrate, supports rich communities of marine
Ascomycota including Lulworthia, Halosphaeriaceae, and numerous
perithecium-forming species. Driftwood and mangrove detritus are
particularly productive fungal substrates, with mangrove ecosystems
alone harboring hundreds of described and undescribed species.

### Subtidal Sediments

Marine sediments from shallow coastal waters to abyssal plains contain
diverse fungal communities dominated by Ascomycota and Basidiomycota
yeasts. Sediment fungal diversity generally decreases with depth but
remains detectable at hadal zone depths exceeding 10,000 meters.
Anaerobic sediment layers support fermentative yeasts and microaerophilic
filamentous fungi adapted to low-oxygen conditions.

### Coral Reefs and Hard Substrates

Coral reef ecosystems harbor fungal communities associated with living
coral tissue, coral skeleton, algal surfaces, and reef sediments. Endolithic
fungi bore into coral skeletons and carbonate substrates, contributing to
bioerosion. Some marine Ascomycota form intimate associations with
macroalgae and seagrasses, while others live as saprotrophs on dead coral
and algal detritus. Coral-associated fungi are of particular interest due
to their potential roles in coral disease and reef health.

### Deep-Sea and Hydrothermal Vent Environments

Hydrothermal vents support chemosynthetic ecosystems that include
thermophilic and barophilic fungi. Species of Aspergillus, Penicillium,
and Cladosporium have been isolated from vent fluids and surrounding
sediments at temperatures exceeding 30 degrees Celsius. Cold-seep
ecosystems and methane-rich sediments harbor distinct fungal assemblages
that may participate in carbon cycling and anaerobic methane oxidation.

### Pelagic Zone

The open ocean water column contains both particle-associated and free-
living fungal communities. Marine yeasts including Aureobasidium
pullulans, Rhodotorula species, and Debaryomyces hansenii are
cosmopolitan in surface waters. Recent metabarcoding surveys have
revealed unexpected fungal diversity in deep pelagic waters, including
Basidiomycota and early-diverging fungal lineages previously unknown
from marine settings.

## Functional Roles

### Organic Matter Decomposition

Marine fungi are primary decomposers of refractory organic matter in
coastal ecosystems. Lignocellulose from driftwood, mangrove litter,
and seagrass debris is degraded by a suite of cellulolytic and
ligninolytic enzymes produced by marine Ascomycota. This decomposition
drives carbon and nutrient cycling in coastal food webs and contributes
to the biological carbon pump.

### Symbiosis and Parasitism

Marine fungi form endophytic associations with seaweeds, seagrasses,
and mangrove roots, potentially enhancing host stress tolerance and
disease resistance. Parasitic marine fungi infect diatoms, other fungi,
and marine invertebrates. The oomycete Lagenidium and the chytrid
Oblongichytrium are examples of fungal-like and true fungal parasites
that regulate marine phytoplankton populations.

### Biogeochemical Cycling

Marine fungi participate in nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur cycling
in sediments and the water column. Their ability to degrade complex
polymers including chitin, cellulose, and lignin makes them important
agents of organic matter transformation in marine carbon budgets.
Fungal mycelial networks in sediments may also facilitate nutrient
translocation analogous to mycorrhizal networks in terrestrial soils.

## Biogeographic Patterns

Marine fungal communities exhibit biogeographic structure at multiple
scales. Tropical and subtropical coasts harbor the highest species
richness, with mangrove ecosystems and coral reefs as diversity
hotspots. Temperate and polar marine fungal communities are less
diverse but include cold-adapted and psychrotolerant species not found
elsewhere. Endemism appears higher in marine fungi than previously
assumed, with some species restricted to specific substrate types or
geographic regions.

### The Latitude-Diversity Gradient

Like many marine organisms, fungal diversity generally decreases with
latitude. However, the gradient is less steep for fungi than for
plants or animals, possibly reflecting the dispersal capacity of
airborne and waterborne fungal spores and the cosmopolitan nature of
many marine yeasts.

## Knowledge Gaps and Challenges

### Cultivation Bias

Standard marine fungal isolation uses seawater-based media supplemented
with organic substrates. These methods favor fast-growing Ascomycota and
yeasts while under-sampling slow-growing Basidiomycota, early-diverging
lineages, and obligate symbionts. Many marine fungi may have specific
growth requirements related to salinity, pressure, temperature, or
chemical signals from host organisms that are not replicated in culture.

### Sequencing and Reference Database Gaps

Marine fungal metabarcoding studies often recover large proportions of
sequences that do not match described species in reference databases.
The UNITE database, while comprehensive for terrestrial fungi, contains
relatively few marine reference sequences. This reference gap inflates
the proportion of "unassigned" sequences in marine surveys and hampers
ecological interpretation.
