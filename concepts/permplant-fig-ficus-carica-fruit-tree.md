# Fig as Ancient Fruit Tree in Permaculture

The fig ([[ficus-carica]]) is one of the most ancient plants in
civilisation, existing in as many as 700 varieties of the species. A
deciduous tree growing 5-10 metres with a spread up to 25 metres, it
is a foundational element of [[permaculture-energy-systems]] and [[mollison-designers-olive-fig-and-mediterranean-tree-crops]]. Figs belong to the family Moraceae and are valued
for their fruit, medicinal properties, and ornamental qualities.

## Climate Adaptability

The fig grows best in warm temperate and subtropical regions and is
frost tolerant to -10 degrees C. Very young trees may be injured by
early or late frosts and should be protected. Figs can live for 100
years and begin to bear fruit at just 2-3 years of age, making them one
of the fastest-bearing fruit trees available.

## Soil Requirements

Fig trees do well in a wide range of soils, although they dislike
alkaline or salty soils. The soil should not be too rich in nitrogen and
periodic applications of lime are beneficial. They are tolerant of
drought but produce better quality fruit when water is plentiful. Figs
often produce a better crop if their roots are restricted, making them
an excellent tree for container growing in small gardens.

## Fruit Production

Fruits are produced singly or in pairs in the leaf axils and vary
considerably in shape and skin colour between varieties. Some cultivated
varieties require no pollination and produce only female flowers. In
some climates, figs produce two crops: a larger early summer crop and a
smaller but more numerous late summer to early autumn crop. The fruit
only matures on new or recently ripened wood.

Figs can be eaten fresh, frozen, or dried, with sugar content rising
from 12% to 50% on drying. The best drying method is to let them
partially dry on the tree and drop naturally, then finish drying on
trays.

## Nutritional Composition

Fresh fig per 100 grams:
- Water: 77.5%, Energy: 80 calories
- Carbohydrate: 20.3 g, Fat: 0.3 g, Protein: 1.2 g
- Calcium: 35 mg, Potassium: 194 mg
- Vitamin A: 80 I.U., Vitamin C: 2 mg
- Digestion time: 2.25 hours

Dried fig per 100 grams:
- Water: 23%, Energy: 274 calories
- Carbohydrate: 69.1 g (about 50 g is dextrose)
- Protein: 4.3 g, Calcium: 126 mg
- Potassium: 640 mg, Iron: 3.0 mg
- Digestion time: 2.5 hours

## Medicinal Uses

Figs have a long history of medicinal use. They provide a mild laxative
effect and are often mixed with senna, rhubarb, and other stronger
laxatives. Demulcent decoctions are prepared from figs and used in the
treatment of catarrhal infections of the nose and throat. Figs can be
roasted and split open to provide a poultice for dental abscesses, gum
boils, and circumscribed tumours. The milky latex of the fig is applied
to warts, causing them to dry and fall off.

## Pruning and Training

Little pruning is required unless the tree is to be kept as a dwarf.
Pruning consists mainly of cutting out dead, deformed, or weak branches.
The tips of young shoots can be pinched out to ensure strong growth.
Figs can be trained to grow against walls or along fences, making them
suitable for small-space [[gaias-garden-urban-permaculture-design]].

## Propagation

Propagation is by layering or cuttings. Layers should root in a season
and are detached when the pot is filled with roots. Cuttings should be
selected from short-jointed shoots of the previous year's growth and
taken in spring. The 15-25 cm cuttings root best in sandy soil with
warmth.

## Notable Cultivars

- Brown Turkey: medium fruit with mahogany brown skin, cold hardy
- Celeste: violet coloured with light-coloured, firm, juicy flesh
- Conadria: vigorous, adapted to warmer climates, best for drying
- Desert King: widely adapted, large dark green fruit
- Genoa (White Genoa): best for cooler areas, hardy, excellent fresh
- Kadota: vigorous, does best in hot climates, popular for canning
- Mission (Black Mission): dependable, purplish black skin, versatile
- Osborn Prolific: prefers cooler areas, strong flavour
- Texas Everbearing: good for short growing seasons

## Fire Retardant Properties

The Ficus genus is generally considered to have good fire retardant
properties. This makes fig trees useful as components of [[mollison-designers-fire-resistant-landscape-design]], particularly in Mediterranean climates where
wildfire risk is significant.

## [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]]

Some species in the Ficus genus (including curtain fig, Moreton Bay fig,
and strangler fig) can start growing in the canopy of a host tree and
engulf it entirely. This should be considered in species selection. The
milky latex from broken leaves, stems, or unripe fruit is used to treat
infections such as ringworm and warts.

## Role in Food Forests

In a [[comparison-food-forest-design-vs-backyard-orchard-design]], figs serve as a productive mid-canopy
element. Their early bearing age and long productive life make them an
excellent investment. The fruit provides a concentrated energy source
during late summer and autumn, complementing other fruit tree harvests.
The dried fruit stores well, providing a year-round food resource.
