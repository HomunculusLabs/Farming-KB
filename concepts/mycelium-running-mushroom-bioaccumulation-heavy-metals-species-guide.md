Paul Stamets documents how mushrooms selectively concentrate [[staycare-fungal-biosorption-mechanisms-for-heavy-metal-removal]] contaminants from their environments, with bioaccumulation factors ranging from negligible to more than 10,000 times background levels. This capacity has profound implications for ecological remediation and foraging safety.

## Overview of Heavy Metal Bioaccumulation

Mushrooms concentrate many metals from their surroundings. Stamets notes that biologists do not fully understand why certain species concentrate specific [[staycare-fungal-biosorption-mechanisms-for-heavy-metal-removal]] elements such as arsenic, cadmium, cesium, lead, mercury, and copper. Just as biologists do not know why Psilocybe mushrooms manufacture hallucinogenic phosphorus-based compounds, the reasons other species concentrate heavy metals remain unclear. Determining the level of toxicity of heavy metal exposure is described as a work in progress. The bioaccumulation factor indicates how many times more concentrated a metal is compared to the background level in the environment.

Stamets presents a preliminary and admittedly simplistic chart sampling research and presenting a guide for further comparisons. He cautions that considerable variation has been found, and is to be expected, between samples. Several factors affect bioaccumulation of heavy metals, making broad generalizations difficult.

## Arsenic Accumulation

arsenic is used as a wood preservative and was once widely used in treated lumber for home construction. The EPA banned its use in residential lumber and lowered acceptable drinking water levels to 10 micrograms per liter (ppb) in 2001, down from the previous 50 mcg per liter. Most arsenic ingestion occurs through drinking water. Excessive exposure can cause lung, bladder, and other cancers.

Mushrooms that accumulate arsenic include:
- **Tricholoma magnivelare** (North American matsutake), a mycorrhizal species
- **Coprinus comatus** (shaggy mane), a saprotrophic species

A Canadian study in the Northwest Territories measured 494 mcg/g (dry weight) of arsenic in shaggy manes growing near a mine. Consuming approximately 100g fresh (10g dry weight) of these mushrooms would deliver about 4,940 mcg of arsenic — roughly 250 times the EPA limit for drinking water.

## Cadmium Accumulation

The [[agaricus-aurora]] genus stands out for concentrating acutely toxic cadmium. Cadmium hyperaccumulation has been a concern for the button mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) industry for many years. The maximum acceptable level for cadmium intake is 2 ppb per kg of body weight per day. Intakes as low as 1 mcg per day can harm the neurological and immune systems and may cause cancer.

Agaricus blazei (now Agaricus brasiliensis) specimens from China contained 2.75 mg/kg of cadmium, compared to 0.90 mg/kg from California and 0.53 mg/kg from Washington — likely reflecting industrial air pollution differences. If the commercial mushroom industry uses cadmium-contaminated substrate, such as high-nitrogen chicken manure, their produce will concentrate cadmium.

One complicating issue is that many analytical laboratories use tests not sensitive to occurrences below 1 ppm, and hence cadmium, although possibly present, is not detected. This is problematic because cadmium is toxic at less than 1 ppm.

## Lead Accumulation

Most mushrooms do not actively concentrate lead above the environmental level, with a few exceptions. The morel proliferates in burned habitats where fire increases baseline metals. Lead is made less soluble in soils near neutral pH; adding lime (calcium carbonate) significantly reduces the solubility of lead, cadmium, and other heavy metals. In contrast, acidic soils allow lead to be easily absorbed by plants and mushrooms. Saprophytic mushrooms absorb more lead than mycorrhizal ones. The European Community limit for lead in food is 2 mg per kg in dry weight. The U.S. standard for drinking water is less than 15 mcg per liter, assuming consumption of 2 liters per day.

Stamets notes he does not see any practical mycoremediation method for removing lead from polluted lands since most mushrooms do not hyperaccumulate this metal.

## Mercury and Selenium

Mercury is a highly toxic metal that dramatically impairs human and environmental health. The maximum amount allowed in drinking water is 1 ppb or 0.001 mg per liter. Turkey tail (Trametes versicolor) and oyster mushroom (Pleurotus pulmonarius) mycelia have been shown to remove mercuric ions from aquatic systems.

Selenium bonds with mercury in a biologically inactive form, making it nearly non-toxic. However, excessive selenium bonding depletes the body's selenium levels, affecting the immune system. A placebo-controlled clinical study published in the Journal of the American Medical Association found that 200 mcg of selenium per day reduced lung, colorectal, and prostate cancer. Few foods are rich in selenium — fish, Brazil nuts, meat, and mushrooms are notable sources.

## Mycorrhizal vs. Saprophytic Species in Metal Uptake

Stamets highlights a critical distinction between mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi in metal accumulation:

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
- Live in close association with plant roots as symbionts
- Penetrate deeper below the surface over time
- Can reside on a site for decades
- Longer colony contact with contaminated soil means greater absorption
- Younger colonies bioaccumulate less than older, larger colonies
- Serve as a transport system from areas deeper underground
- Examples: Gomphidius glutinosus, Paxillus atrotomentosus, Suillus species

### Saprophytic Fungi
- Grow primarily on or above the ground on debris fields
- Thrive relatively briefly compared to mycorrhizal fungi
- More useful for cleaning up recently deposited or surface contaminants
- Oyster mushrooms can flourish in environments heavily contaminated with cadmium and mercury while still degrading [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]]
- Examples: Coprinus comatus, Pleurotus ostreatus, [[calvatia-gigantea]]

## Bioindicator Species

Stamets proposes that certain mushroom species can serve as bioindicator organisms for environmental contamination:

- **Shaggy mane** (Coprinus comatus) for arsenic and potentially lead
- **Agaricus species** for cadmium — recommended to be avoided entirely due to high bioaccumulation properties

Shaggy manes are frequently found in lawns rich in organic debris and in disturbed habitats with upturned, mineral-rich subsurface soil. Prolific fruitings in these disturbed settings can number in the thousands.

Obst et al. (2001) surveyed wild mushrooms around the North Great Slave Lake region in the Northwest Territories of Canada. They recommended that authorities advise the public not to harvest mushrooms from contaminated sites including mine sites, tailings, crushed waste rock fill, landfills, roadsides, parking lots, disturbed areas, and cities. Similar species-specific bioaccumulations have been found in Eastern Europe, China, and India.

## Practical Foraging Safety Implications

Stamets provides several key recommendations for foragers:

- Avoid all mushrooms found along roadsides where exhaust fumes and asphalt supply arsenic
- Avoid mushrooms of the Agaricaceae family (including Agaricus genus) due to high cadmium bioaccumulation
- Be cautious about mushrooms from areas near industrial sites, steel smelters, and mines
- Mushrooms growing where leaded gasoline was used should not be eaten
- Monitor metal concentrations in all edible mushrooms, especially species known for hyperaccumulation
- One should not be unduly concerned about eating shaggy manes unless they occur on or near arsenic-contaminated land

## Oyster Mushroom Resilience to Heavy Metals

Oyster mushrooms are notable for their resilience [[singh-fungal-biofilms-in-contaminated-environments]]. They can have twice the concentration of cadmium in their flesh compared to the surrounding soil mass (Favero et al. 1990). Yet oyster mushrooms can flourish in environments heavily contaminated with cadmium and mercury, typically where petroleum products have spilled, while still degrading [[staycare-fungal-degradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]]. Baldrian and others (2000) found that only when cadmium levels reached 100 to 500 ppm and mercury 50 to 100 ppm did oyster mycelia die back. In a more recent study, Baldrian and Gabriel (2003) found that the cellulose- and hemicellulose-degrading abilities of oyster mushrooms were limited by cadmium levels. Pleurotus sajor-caju (Pleurotus pulmonarius) mycelium concentrates cadmium but not lead (Purkayastha et al. 1994).

## Broader Ecological Significance

The fact that mushrooms selectively concentrate heavy metals may benefit organic habitat restoration. Few other avenues of treatment are available for in situ restoration. Once mycelium upchannels heavy metals into mushrooms, the fruiting bodies can be picked and transported away. If heavy metal-laden mushrooms are not removed, bacteria and other fungi decompose them and return metals to the soil.

In theory, tainted mushrooms could be collected and their metals further concentrated and recycled. But as long as we pollute, heavy metal contaminants will continuously accumulate in the food web. When this effect is compounded over time, biological populations intolerant to heavy metals will die. As understanding of hyperaccumulation rates and selectivity factors improves, mushrooms could be put to work mining metals from contaminated environments. Stamets envisions a future where trained mushroom harvesters could use specific species for gradual environmental detoxification.
