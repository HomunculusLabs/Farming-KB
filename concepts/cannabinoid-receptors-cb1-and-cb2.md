# Cannabinoid Receptors CB1 and CB2

Cannabinoid receptors are G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) that serve as the primary molecular targets of endocannabinoids, phytocannabinoids, and synthetic cannabinoids. Two main cannabinoid [[cannabinoid-receptors-cb1-cb2-tissue-distribution-blesching]] receptors have been identified and characterized: CB1 (cannabinoid receptor type 1) and CB2 (cannabinoid receptor type 2). Together with their endogenous ligands (anandamide and 2-AG) and the enzymes responsible for their synthesis and degradation, these receptors form the core of the endocannabinoid system, a widespread neuromodulatory network critical to homeostasis.

## CB1 Receptor: Structure and Distribution

### Molecular Characteristics

The CB1 receptor [[cannabinoid-receptor-agonists-antagonists-synthetic-reference-blesching]] was first cloned in 1990 and is encoded by the CNR1 gene located on human chromosome 6. As a class A G protein-coupled receptor, CB1 consists of seven transmembrane alpha-helices connected by extracellular and intracellular loops. The receptor primarily couples to Gi/o proteins, which inhibit adenylyl cyclase and reduce cyclic AMP (cAMP) production upon activation. Additionally, CB1 activation modulates ion channels, including inhibition of N-type and P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels and activation of inwardly rectifying potassium channels (GIRKs).

The binding pocket of CB1 is located within the transmembrane domain, where both endogenous and exogenous ligands interact. THC, the primary psychoactive component of cannabis, acts as a partial agonist at CB1 with moderate efficacy. Anandamide (AEA) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) serve as the endogenous full and partial agonists respectively. The binding affinity and functional activity of different cannabinoids at CB1 account for the wide variation in psychoactive effects among cannabis preparations.

### Central Nervous System Distribution

CB1 receptors are among the most abundant G protein-coupled receptors in the central nervous system, with particularly high densities in:

- **Basal ganglia**: Involved in motor control, explaining the effects of cannabinoids on movement and coordination
- **Cerebellum**: Critical for balance and fine motor control, consistent with cannabinoid-induced motor impairment
- **Hippocampus**: Essential for memory formation, accounting for the well-documented short-term memory effects of cannabis
- **Cerebral cortex**: Associated with higher cognitive functions, perception, and consciousness
- **Hypothalamus**: Regulating appetite and metabolic processes, correlating with cannabinoid-induced hunger stimulation

This distribution pattern explains why CB1 activation produces the characteristic psychoactive effects of cannabis, including euphoria, altered perception, impaired memory, increased appetite, and motor coordination deficits. The high receptor density in brain regions controlling reward and pleasure also underlies the abuse potential of CB1 agonists.

### Peripheral Distribution

Beyond the central nervous system, CB1 receptors are found in peripheral tissues including:

- **Adipose tissue**: Playing a role in lipogenesis and energy metabolism regulation
- **Liver**: Modulating gluconeogenesis and lipid metabolism
- **Gastrointestinal tract**: Influencing motility, secretion, and visceral pain sensation
- **Skeletal muscle**: Affecting energy expenditure and glucose uptake
- **Reproductive organs**: Involved in fertility and early embryonic development
- **Cardiovascular system**: Modulating blood pressure and cardiac function

The peripheral CB1 receptors contribute to the metabolic effects of cannabinoids and have become important therapeutic targets for conditions like obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, while avoiding the central psychoactive effects.

## CB2 Receptor: Structure and Distribution

### Molecular Characteristics

The CB2 receptor was cloned in 1993 and is encoded by the CNR2 gene on human chromosome 1. Like CB1, it is a G protein-coupled receptor with seven transmembrane domains, but shares only approximately 44% amino acid sequence identity with CB1. CB2 also primarily couples to Gi/o proteins, inhibiting adenylyl cyclase and modulating ion channel activity, though its downstream signaling cascades differ in important respects from those of CB1.

CB2 was initially considered an "orphan" receptor with unknown function, but its role in immune modulation has since become well established. The receptor shows distinct ligand binding properties compared to CB1, with certain compounds showing selectivity for one receptor over the other. This selectivity has enabled the development of CB2-selective agonists that produce therapeutic effects without psychoactivity.

### Immune System Distribution

CB2 receptors are predominantly expressed in immune cells and tissues, including:

- **B lymphocytes**: Modulating antibody production and B cell proliferation
- **T lymphocytes**: Influencing T cell activation, proliferation, and cytokine release
- **Natural killer cells**: Affecting cytotoxic activity and immune surveillance
- **Monocytes and macrophages**: Regulating phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and inflammatory mediator release
- **Dendritic cells**: Modulating maturation and T cell priming capacity
- **Microglia**: The resident immune cells of the central nervous system, where CB2 expression is upregulated in neuroinflammatory conditions

This immune-focused distribution makes CB2 a particularly attractive therapeutic target for inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, as CB2-selective agonists can modulate immune function without producing the central psychoactive effects associated with CB1 activation.

### Emerging Peripheral Roles

Beyond immune cells, CB2 receptors have been identified in an expanding range of peripheral tissues:

- **Bone**: Osteoblasts and osteoclasts express CB2, and receptor activation promotes bone formation while inhibiting bone resorption, suggesting therapeutic potential for osteoporosis
- **Skin**: Keratinocytes and hair follicles express CB2, and the receptor modulates sebaceous gland function, wound healing, and inflammatory skin conditions
- **Gastrointestinal tract**: CB2 in the enteric nervous system and gut epithelium influences inflammation, motility, and visceral pain
- **Liver**: Hepatic CB2 expression increases during liver injury, where activation appears to reduce fibrosis and inflammation

## Receptor Signaling and Downstream Effects

### Gi/o Protein Coupling

Both CB1 and CB2 primarily signal through Gi/o proteins, which mediate several key downstream effects:

- **Inhibition of adenylyl cyclase**: Reduces intracellular cAMP levels, modulating protein kinase A (PKA) activity and downstream phosphorylation cascades
- **Activation of MAPK pathways**: Including ERK, JNK, and p38 MAPK, influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival
- **Modulation of PI3K/Akt pathway**: Affecting cell growth, metabolism, and survival signals
- **Regulation of nitric oxide production**: Through modulation of endothelial and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity

### Biased Signaling

Recent research has revealed that cannabinoid receptors exhibit biased signaling, meaning that different ligands can activate distinct downstream pathways even when binding to the same receptor. A ligand that potently activates Gi/o coupling may weakly recruit beta-arrestin, and vice versa. This pharmacological nuance has important therapeutic implications, as it may be possible to develop ligands that produce beneficial effects through one signaling pathway while avoiding adverse effects mediated by another.

### Receptor Desensitization and Internalization

Prolonged cannabinoid receptor activation leads to desensitization and internalization through mechanisms involving GRK-mediated phosphorylation and beta-arrestin recruitment. Chronic cannabis use results in CB1 downregulation, which contributes to the development of tolerance. This adaptive response is reversible, with receptor density typically returning to baseline levels following a period of abstinence.

## Therapeutic Implications

### CB1-Targeted Therapies

- **Pain management**: CB1 agonists reduce neuropathic and inflammatory pain, though psychoactive side effects limit clinical utility
- **Appetite stimulation**: Dronabinol (synthetic THC) is approved for AIDS-related anorexia and chemotherapy-induced nausea
- **Spasticity**: Sativex (THC:CBD oromucosal spray) is approved for multiple sclerosis spasticity in many countries
- **Glaucoma**: CB1 agonists reduce intraocular pressure, though duration of effect limits practical application
- **CB1 inverse agonists**: Rimonabant was developed for obesity but withdrawn due to psychiatric side effects, highlighting the importance of tonic CB1 signaling for mood regulation

### CB2-Targeted Therapies

- **Inflammatory conditions**: CB2-selective agonists reduce inflammation in models of arthritis, colitis, and dermatitis without psychoactivity
- **Neuroprotection**: CB2 activation reduces neuroinflammation and neuronal damage in models of stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegenerative diseases
- **Bone health**: CB2 agonists show promise for treating osteoporosis by promoting bone formation
- **Pain**: Peripheral CB2 activation reduces inflammatory and neuropathic pain without central effects
- **Fibrosis**: CB2 agonists reduce liver and kidney fibrosis in preclinical models
