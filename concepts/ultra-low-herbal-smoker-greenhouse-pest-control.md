# Herbal Smoker for Greenhouse Pest Control

The herbal smoker is a simple, low-cost pest control method particularly
effective in greenhouses. By smoldering pesticidal plant materials to
produce smoke that fills the enclosed space, farmers can control tiny
pests that are difficult to reach with spray applications. The method
is especially valuable in winter greenhouses where water-based pesticide
applications would raise humidity to harmful levels.

## Why Smoke Works in Greenhouses

Greenhouses present unique pest control challenges. Many serious
greenhouse pests, such as spider mites, silverleaf whitefly, greenhouse
whitefly, and leaf miners, are extremely small and hide on the
undersides of leaves, in leaf crevices, and in plant joints. Spray
pesticides often fail to reach these hidden locations. Smoke, however,
fills the entire enclosed space, seeping into the tiniest holes and
crevices, reaching pests that sprays cannot.

In winter, applying water-based pesticides inside greenhouses raises
humidity levels, which promotes fungal diseases like botrytis and
powdery mildew. The herbal smoker avoids this problem entirely because
it introduces no water into the greenhouse environment. This makes it
particularly useful during cold months when humidity management is
critical.

## Effective Plant Materials

Several plants have demonstrated pesticidal properties when burned:

- **Tobacco**: The most commonly used herb for smoking. Contains
  nicotine, a potent insecticide. However, tobacco smoke can leave
  residue on fruits, so it should not be used near harvest time.

- **Ginkgo**: Known for strong pesticidal effects. Readily available
  in many regions. The leaves produce effective insecticidal smoke.

- **Peppermint**: The essential oils in peppermint are released when
  heated, creating a pest-repellent smoke with a pleasant aroma.

- **Clove**: Contains eugenol, a natural insecticide and fungicide.
  Clove smoke has powerful pesticidal properties.

- **Korean mint**: Aromatic herb whose oils are released in smoke,
  providing pest-repelling effects in enclosed spaces.

- **Japanese pepper (Zanthoxylum piperitum)**: Contains strong
  insecticidal compounds that become volatile when heated.

## Making a DIY Smoker from an Oil Tin Can

A simple herbal smoker can be made from a readily available oil tin can
in about 15 minutes:

1. **Prepare the can**: Use a standard oil tin can. Clean it
   thoroughly. Remove any remaining oil residue.

2. **Punch ventilation holes**: Use the sharp tip of a hammer to
   punch small holes on all four sides of the can, near the top.
   These holes allow smoke to escape.

3. **Make bottom air holes**: Punch slightly larger holes on the
   bottom of the can to allow air intake for the smoldering process.

4. **Add a handle**: Attach a wire hanger to the can for convenient
   carrying and placement.

5. **Place fuel**: Put a coal briquette in the can and light the fire.
   Wait until the coal is glowing evenly.

6. **Load the herb**: Place herb material on top of the glowing coal.
   Spray water on the herb material before loading; adequately wet
   material produces more smoke. If the plant material is too dry,
   it will burn quickly instead of smoldering.

7. **Position the smoker**: Place the smoker on the floor of the
   greenhouse. As the coal burns, smoke is produced continuously
   for over 2 hours.

## Application Protocol

For effective pest control in greenhouses:

1. Smoke after 7 PM on a cloudy day. Smoke sinks on cloudy days,
   enshrouding the plants more effectively. On clear nights, warm air
   rises and smoke may not reach lower plant surfaces.

2. Place one or two smokers per 330 square meters (0.08 acres) of
   greenhouse space.

3. Before smoking, close all greenhouse windows and remove any
   beehives. Bees are sensitive to smoke and must be protected.

4. Light the smokers, place them on the floor, and exit the
   greenhouse quickly.

5. Leave the greenhouse closed overnight. The smoke will fill every
   corner of the space.

6. Next morning, open the windows to ventilate and release the smoke.
   Return beehives to the greenhouse after ventilation.

## Managing Tobacco Residue

Tobacco smoke can leave a noticeable smell and residue on fruits. To
prevent this, avoid tobacco smoking within 2 to 3 weeks of harvest. If
tobacco was used and residue is a concern, spray [[jadam-jms-pesticide]]
on the plants; this will help the smell dissipate quickly. Using
non-tobacco herbs like ginkgo, peppermint, or clove near harvest avoids
this issue entirely.

## Potential and Future Development

JADAM considers the herbal smoking method to have "enormous potential."
Building on this method, greenhouse farming can go completely
chemical-free with ease. The method is described as "a very interesting
and promising area that remains to be studied." Farmers worldwide are
encouraged to discover and test local plants with strong pesticidal
scents for use in smokers.

## Cost

The herbal smoker costs virtually nothing to build and operate. An old
oil can, some coal, and locally available herbs are the only
requirements. This makes it one of the most cost-effective pest control
methods available, perfectly aligned with the ultra-low-cost philosophy.
