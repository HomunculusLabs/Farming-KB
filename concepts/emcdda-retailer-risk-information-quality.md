The quality and completeness of risk information provided by retailers
selling hallucinogenic mushrooms varies considerably across Europe.
A snapshot survey of 21 online shops conducted in January 2006 by the
EMCDDA found that while most retailers include some warnings, the
information is systematically biased toward positive effects and
rarely addresses negative outcomes or dosage variability.

## Smartshop Labels and Warnings

Smartshops in the Netherlands, the primary retail channel for
hallucinogenic mushrooms in Europe, typically provide warning labels
on fresh mushroom products. An example from a pack of Psilocybe
cubensis purchased in an Amsterdam smartshop in February 2006 warned
that persons under 18 should not use the product, as well as those
who are pregnant, taking medication, or suffering from mental illness.

The label also warned against driving or operating machinery and
against combining the product with alcohol. Notably, the label
displayed the number of a UK Drug Helpline, suggesting that the
product was actively being marketed to UK customers despite the
imminent change in legal status in that country.

The Dutch Risk Assessment Report from 2000 noted that information
leaflets typically provided no data on maximum shelf life, possible
side effects, or the proportion of active substances present (CAM,
2000).

## Online Shop Warning Coverage

The EMCDDA snapshot of 21 online shops found that the majority warned
users not to consume hallucinogenic mushrooms when taking medication
or in combination with alcohol or other drugs such as stimulants.
However, only two thirds of shops recommended against use when the
user suffers from depression or psychosis, a significant omission
given the known mental health risks associated with psilocybin use
in vulnerable populations.

About half of the surveyed sites provided information on dosage and
included guidance on safe use practices such as consuming on an empty
stomach, drinking liquids during the experience, and consuming in a
quiet, safe environment. Most sites provided information on sought
after effects, yet very few provided guidance on how to enhance
positive effects or manage challenging experiences.

## Bias Toward Positive Effects

The most significant finding from the retailer information analysis
was the systematic bias toward positive effects. The vast majority of
online shops excluded information on possible negative side effects.
Only a minority of sites explicitly mentioned nausea and sickness,
which are among the most commonly reported acute effects, with over a
quarter of recent users in UK clubbing surveys reporting nausea or
vomiting.

This bias is understandable from a commercial perspective, as
retailers have an economic interest in promoting their products.
However, it creates a significant information asymmetry for consumers
who may rely on retailer-provided information as their primary or
sole source of guidance before using hallucinogenic mushrooms for the
first time.

The extent of overlap between pro-mushroom lobby groups and
commercial retailers remains unclear, but the shared interest in
minimising negative perceptions of hallucinogenic mushrooms likely
contributes to the consistently incomplete risk information found
across retail channels.

## Official Prevention Materials

Specialised prevention materials relating specifically to
hallucinogenic mushroom use were relatively rare across EU Member
States as of 2006. Many general drug prevention programmes included
information on hallucinogenic drugs alongside other substances, but
dedicated mushroom-focused resources were limited.

Notable examples of targeted prevention materials included a flyer
about the effects of psilocybin published by the Trimbos Institute
of the Dutch Ministry of Health, an information brochure on
hallucinogenic mushrooms by the Berlin Drug Working Group
(Landesarbeitsgemeinschaft Drogen Berlin), and a publication by the
UK organisation Lifeline titled "Magic mushrooms - frequently asked
questions."

The relatively low levels of reported harm together with indications
that patterns of frequent or intensive mushroom use are rare probably
account for the lack of developments in specialised prevention
materials. This stands in contrast to the more extensive prevention
infrastructure surrounding drugs like heroin, cocaine, and ecstasy.

## Internet as an Alternative Information Source

Beyond retailer-provided information, users increasingly access
dedicated internet websites for more detailed guidance on
hallucinogenic mushroom effects, dosages, chemistry, and personal
experience reports. Sites such as Erowid, the Shroomery, and
Mycotopia provide trip reports, dosage calculators, and community
forums where users share practical advice.

However, as with retailer information, the quality and reliability
of internet-sourced information is highly variable. Some sites
provide comprehensive, evidence-based harm reduction guidance, while
others promote inaccurate claims about mushroom effects or
downplay known risks. The decentralised and largely unmoderated
nature of these information sources makes it difficult for users to
distinguish reliable from unreliable guidance.

## Implications for Consumer Safety

The information gap identified by the EMCDDA has direct implications
for consumer safety. Users who receive only positive framing from
retailers are less prepared for adverse effects such as panic attacks,
which the Mixmag survey found affected nearly a quarter of recent
users. The absence of dosage standardisation information further
compounds risks, as potency varies significantly between species,
growing conditions, and individual specimens.

The combination of commercially biased retailer information, limited
official prevention materials, and variable internet resources means
that consumers of hallucinogenic mushrooms in Europe faced a
fragmented and often incomplete information environment during the
peak of the smartshop trade in the early to mid-2000s.
