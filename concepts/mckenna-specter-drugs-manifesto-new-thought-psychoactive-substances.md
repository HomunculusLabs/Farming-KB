## Overview

The introduction to [[terence-mckenna]]'s *Food of the Gods* opens with a
deliberately provocative echo of Marx: "A specter is haunting planetary
culture — the specter of drugs." This manifesto frames the global drug
crisis not as a [[cervantes-grow-room-security-law-enforcement]] problem but as a cultural and
existential one — a failure of collective imagination that has
collapsed the vast diversity of human-plant relationships into a
binary of prohibition and addiction.

## The Satanic Marriage

McKenna's opening metaphor describes the convergence of three forces:

- **Modern pharmacology**: the ability to isolate, concentrate, and
  synthesize psychoactive compounds
- **Marketing**: [[four-groups-neurotropic-fungi-global-distribution-guzman-1998]] systems that deliver substances
  to every market
- **High-speed transportation**: the infrastructure that moves both
  drugs and drug cultures across borders

Together these have produced what McKenna calls "a satanic marriage
with modern pharmacology, marketing, and high-speed transportation."
The framing is theological — "satanic" implies not merely harmful but
existentially opposed to human flourishing — yet the mechanism is
thoroughly material and technological.

## The Cartel Paradox

McKenna describes a historical irony in the evolution of drug cartels:

- International narcotics cartels were originally "the obedient
  creations of [[mckenna-drugs-governments-and-intelligence-agencies]] agencies" seeking
  "invisible money" for covert operations
- The cartels subsequently "evolved, through the unprecedented rise
  in the demand for cocaine, into rogue elephants before whose power
  even their creators have begun to grow uneasy"
- Drug wars are "waged by governmental institutions that usually are
  paralyzed by lethargy and inefficiency or are in transparent
  collusion with the international drug cartels they are publicly
  pledged to destroy"

This analysis positions the drug war as a self-reinforcing system in
which prohibition creates the conditions for cartel power, and cartel
power justifies further prohibition.

## Habit, Obsession, Addiction — A Spectrum of Free Will

McKenna traces a conceptual trajectory from habit through obsession
to addiction, framing each as a diminishing of free will:

- **Habit**: "a settled tendency or practice" — the substrate of all
  culture, largely unexamined
- **Obsession**: habits whose "devotion exceeds the culturally
  defined norms" — the sense that "the uniquely human dimension of
  free will has somehow been violated"
- **Addiction**: obsession with [[guzman-magic-mushrooms-chemical-constituents]] of foods and
  drugs, labeled as disease, reducing the role of free will "to the
  vanishing point"

McKenna notes that the concept of addiction has shifted across
centuries: from demonic possession ("opium fiend") through moral
failure to medical disease — each reframing progressively removing
individual agency from the equation.

## The Cornucopia of Synthetics

McKenna identifies a qualitative shift in the drug landscape driven by
[[pericyclic-reactions-organic-chemistry]]:

- Since the mid-nineteenth century, [[mckenna-distillation-and-the-birth-of-synthetic-drugs]] have become
  "more powerful, more effective, of greater duration, and in some
  cases, many times more addictive than their natural relatives"
- [[shulgin-designer-drugs-analysis]] like MDMA and anabolic steroids are "harbingers of
  an era of ever more frequent and effective pharmacological
  intervention in how we look, perform, and feel"
