## Dryland Garden Irrigation Systems

Dryland garden irrigation represents one of the most critical design considerations in [[humus]] pit waters a circle of vegetables, exploiting the porous earthenware to seep water slowly to surrounding soil.

### Home Slot-Pipe System

The top of the pipe (6–5 cm diameter) has slots cut to one level, wrapped with nylon mesh or old stockings. Wastewater is directed in from handbasins or sinks past a grease trap or crude sieve to catch solid particles. These are excellent for small-fruit beds, with slots at correct spacing for plants. Pipes should be laid to one side of the plants for periodic inspection, as they eventually block up.

## Nutrient Film and Injection Techniques

Several techniques exist to prevent any water loss by soil absorption; both are essentially hydroponic-intensive:

- **Nutrient drip**: In Israel, direct infusion of water and nutrients into tree or vine stems is in use.
- **Plastic film technique**: In India, a plastic film technique grows Napier grass (*[[pennisetum]]*) for forage, with part of the root mass in earth and part in a nutrient solution in plastic gullies.
- **Wick systems**: Slotted pipe can be used with hessian or nylon wicks to deliver water to nearby plant roots, with little or no water lost to soil.

## Low-Technology Irrigation Methods

At a modest level, several simple but effective methods serve to keep special trees alive through severe drought or establish windbreaks in deserts:

- **Unglazed earthen jars** placed central to small circle gardens
- **Inverted bottles** with leaky tops
- **Short tubes of pebbles** (hand-filled)
- **Bucket systems**
- **Plastic "pillows"** placed as part-mulch, part-drip at valuable trees, some designed as circular windshields and small glasshouses

## Automatic Irrigation

In areas where electronic sensors are impractical or expensive, mechanical apparatus can permit automatic watering and self-regulate in rain. Such systems use evaporation measurement to switch on manifolds of trickle irrigation lines, with feedback accounting for rainfall. A set period of irrigation then resets the system.

## Sea and Brackish Water Irrigation

Sea or brackish water can be used at high tide level in lagoons for garden irrigation. Extremely salty seawater will evaporate under plastic to provide root-zone water to plants via sand and peat layers. This approach is warranted only in extreme conditions and in areas where no fresh water can be spared.

## Key Design Principles

- Place water directly at plant roots to minimize evaporation
- Use subsurface delivery whenever possible in arid climates
- Filter all drip systems to prevent clogging
- Monitor salt accumulation in drip zones
- Integrate household wastewater into irrigation where feasible
- Select irrigation technology appropriate to available labor and capital
- Design for establishment phase — mature dryland-adapted species may need no irrigation at all
