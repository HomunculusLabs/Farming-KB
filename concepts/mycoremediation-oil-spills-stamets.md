## Overview

Paul Stamets' pioneering work with Battelle Pacific Northwest Laboratories demonstrated that oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus) mycelium can effectively break down petroleum hydrocarbons, offering a biological alternative to expensive and environmentally damaging conventional cleanup methods for oil-contaminated sites.

Source: [[mycelium-running-techniques]]

***

## The Problem: Petroleum Contamination

Petroleum hydrocarbons — including oil, coal, tar, gasoline, and diesel — are among the most pervasive environmental contaminants. Current policy prescribes burning, hauling, and/or burying toxic waste. These methods:
- Leave a lifeless, ecologically crippled environment
- Cost up to $1,000 per ton of contaminated soil
- Often merely relocate rather than eliminate toxins
- Create secondary environmental damage through transport and incineration

***

## Why Fungi Can Degrade Petroleum

The key insight is that many bonds holding plant material together are similar to the bonds found in petroleum products. The same lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases that white rot fungi use to decompose wood can also break down hydrogen-carbon bonds in petroleum hydrocarbons. See [[query-difference-between-white-rot-and-brown-rot-fungi]].

Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) are particularly effective because:
- They are aggressive colonizers of woody substrates
- Their white rot enzymes target hydrogen-carbon bonds
- They grow rapidly in a wide range of conditions and temperatures
- They are primary saprophytes adapted to breaking down complex organic materials

***

## The Battelle Experiment

### Setup

In the late 1990s, Stamets worked with Battelle Pacific Northwest Laboratories in Sequim, Washington. The experiment involved:
- Using strains of Pleurotus ostreatus to test petroleum degradation
- Creating mesocosm (midscale) tests with diesel-contaminated soil
- Mixing sawdust spawn with soil and unsterilized alder chips
- Combining with bunker C oil

### Results

The results were dramatic:
- Oyster mushrooms fruiting from oil-soaked substrate reached "mammoth sizes," testament to the nutrition they found in the petrochemicals
- As the oil-soaked straw was metabolized by the mycelium, it lightened in color — a direct reflection of petroleum hydrocarbon reduction
- The contaminated pile "regreened, becoming an oasis of life" while control piles remained lifeless
- Near the end of the trial, as mushrooms rotted away, plants appeared

### The Multi-Kingdom Effect

The experiment demonstrated the [[mycelium-running-multi-kingdom-bioremediation]]:
1. Fungi break down complex hydrocarbons into simpler compounds
2. Insects attracted by mushroom spores enter the recovering habitat
3. Bacteria use rotting mushrooms as a base for growth, further freeing nutrients
4. Plant communities emerge as biological conditions improve

## Hair Mats: An Innovative Delivery System

### Using Human Hair

Human hair naturally soaks up petroleum and can serve as a substrate for mycelial growth:
- Hair is placed in contact with oil floating on water
- Once saturated with oil, the hair is saporphytized (colonized) by mycelium
- The mycelium darkens with absorption, then metabolizes the oil and becomes white again after digestion
- This approach combines oil absorption with biological decomposition

### Practical Advantages

The hair-mat approach is:
- Low-cost (hair is widely available from salons)
- Biodegradable (the entire assembly decomposes after use)
- Non-toxic (unlike chemical dispersants)
- Can be deployed in sensitive environments (wetlands, shorelines)

## Supporting Research

Multiple studies have demonstrated fungal petroleum degradation:
- Bhatt et al. (2002), Cajthaml et al. (2002), Eggen and Sasek (2002), Thomas et al. (1999), Moder et al. (2002) — all showed petroleum hydrocarbon degradation by white rot fungi
- Enviros, a Seattle company, confirmed that oyster mushrooms fruit from tubs filled with Alaskan crude oil
- Fungi adapt their enzymatic pathways to use petroleum as a food source when other nutrients are limited

## Cost Comparison

| Method | Cost per Ton of Contaminated Soil |
|--------|-----------------------------------|
| Incineration | ~$1,000 |
| Hauling and burial | $500-$800 |
| On-site mycoremediation | ~$50 |

Mycoremediation could cost as little as $50 per ton compared to nearly $1,000 per ton for incineration.

***

## Heavy Metal Absorption

Mycorrhizal fungi can also absorb heavy metals, preventing their entry into the food chain:
- They take up arsenic, copper, zinc, iron, lead, cadmium, nickel, mercury, chromium, and aluminum
- Some fungal tissues can filter radioactive materials such as cesium
- Shiitake mycelium (Lentinula edodes) exposed to heavy metals in dye-enriched effluents increased laccase production, decolorizing toxic dyes while absorbing heavy metals
