## The Neglected Science of Mycology

## A Science Overlooked by Schools and Public Alike

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]] Jr., writing in late 19th-century Boston, lamented the near-total absence of [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] in American life.

"Yet, right under our feet are a race of beings about which the schools tell us nothing," he wrote. The child who kicked over a fungus called it a toadstool; the cook who broiled it called it a mushroom. Between those two words lay the full extent of popular knowledge about fungi — a vast and diverse kingdom of organisms that numbered in the thousands even within the bounds of a single New England state.

Palmer"s frustration was not merely academic. He saw real consequences of this ignorance:

- Edible fungi rotting uncollected in fields and pastures
- Families poisoned by mistaken identification
- An entire class of nutritious food shunned due to unwarranted fear

His mission was to bridge the gap between the meticulous work of professional mycologists and the practical needs of the ordinary person who might wish to gather and eat wild mushrooms safely.

The scale of the neglect was staggering. While American universities and public schools taught botany, chemistry, and zoology, the fungi — an entire kingdom of life — were virtually absent from the curriculum. No American textbook on edible fungi existed. No popular guide had ever been published. The knowledge that did exist was locked in technical monographs written in Latin, accessible only to specialists.

## Early American and English Authorities

Palmer acknowledged a small cadre of dedicated authorities who had advanced the science of mycology, noting that their numbers were lamentably few given the vastness of the field:

- **Cooke, Berkeley, Smith, Badham, and Hussey** — the principal English mycologists who built the foundation of [[fungal-taxonomy-and-classification]] through decades of patient work
- **Dr. [[moses-ashley-curtis]]** of South Carolina — an American expert who assembled a collection of hundreds of fungal specimens across a lifetime of study
- **Mr. Ravenal** — also of South Carolina, noted for his regional expertise in southern fungal species
- **[[charles-james-sprague]]** of Boston — who contributed valuable original correspondence and foreign works to the library of the Boston Society of Natural History
- **[[charles-frost]]** of Vermont — described by Palmer as a living authority whose ongoing work continued to expand the boundaries of American mycological knowledge

Beyond these figures, Palmer observed with some bitterness that American literature on fungi consisted largely of professional writers weaving together other men's researches into diverse magazine articles, without making original contributions. The science was being popularized without being advanced, and the popularization itself was reaching only a small circle of readers.

## No Magic Test Exists

A central and recurring theme in Palmer's writing is that **no universal test** — no simple rule of thumb, no folk remedy, no kitchen trick — can distinguish edible fungi from poisonous ones. This point was so important that he returned to it repeatedly across multiple articles.

The species commonly eaten — the common mushroom of commerce — could be propagated from horse manure, and so it became the single cultivated variety. Everything else was indiscriminately lumped under the name "toadstool." Yet Palmer estimated there were roughly one thousand visible fungal species in Massachusetts alone, many of which were no less edible than their cultivated cousin.

The term "toadstool" had no scientific meaning whatsoever; it was merely a word for any mushroom that the speaker did not know how to identify. This linguistic confusion was one of the greatest obstacles to public education about fungi. By creating a false dichotomy between "mushrooms" (good) and "toadstools" (bad), popular language prevented people from understanding that the real question was whether a specimen was a specifically identified species known to be safe.

Each of the thousand visible species had its own distinct characteristics, its own edibility or toxicity, its own preferred habitat and season — just as each species of bird or flower had its own identity.

## The Boleti Family

Palmer singled out the **Boleti** as a family largely used by Italian-Americans for food, noting that Italian immigrants brought with them a rich tradition of fungal cuisine that native-born Americans had largely abandoned out of ignorance and fear.

These fungi are distinguished by a spongy, porous substance beneath the cap (a tubular hymenium) rather than the blade-like gills of the common mushroom. When the cap is cut open, the underside reveals thousands of tiny tubes packed tightly together.

Palmer offered a practical rule of thumb for the Boleti, one of the few general guidelines he was willing to endorse:

- **Poisonous Boleti** tend to be very brightly colored — vivid reds, yellows, and blues
- **Edible Boleti** are usually drab, brown, or pale green — understated colors

He framed this observation memorably: "Here beauty would be linked with cruelty, and Quaker simplicity with innocence."

The colorful Boleti were among the most visually striking fungi in the forest, yet their beauty concealed danger. The drab, unassuming species were the ones that could be safely gathered and brought to the table. This was one of the few instances where Palmer allowed a general visual principle, though he qualified it carefully.

## Puffballs as Safe Food

Among the safest fungi for the beginner, Palmer noted the common **puffballs** (*Lycoperdon* species). These distinctive round fungi, which release clouds of spores when mature and are often kicked by children walking through fields, were characterized by Palmer as perfectly safe "when pure white inside and out."

This straightforward visual criterion — internal whiteness indicating freshness and edibility — makes puffballs an ideal starting point for anyone interested in eating wild fungi, requiring no specialized botanical knowledge beyond the ability to cut one open and observe its interior.

A puffball that is pure white throughout when sliced can be prepared for the table with confidence. One that shows any trace of yellow, purple, or dark coloration inside should be discarded, as this indicates the beginning of spore maturation or decomposition.

Palmer"s endorsement of puffballs was significant because he was otherwise extremely cautious about recommending any general rule for fungal identification. The fact that he singled out puffballs as safe reflects both their genuinely low risk profile and his desire to give the public at least one accessible entry point into [[emcdda-wild-mushroom-foraging-misidentification-hazards]].

## The Amanita Warning

Even as he encouraged public engagement with mycology, Palmer was careful to warn of genuine dangers. He described a family of white-gilled fungi with caps of various colors, always covered with scurfy warts that rub off easily, as containing many very poisonous members.

These were the **Amanitas**, a genus that Palmer would later identify as responsible for virtually all fatal mushroom poisonings in his experience and in the reports he received from physicians.

The dangerous element in these fungi, he wrote, "resembles in effect hasheesh or opium" — a narcotic poison rather than a merely irritant one. He cited a New Jersey case in which three out of four people who ate them died, calling these among the most dangerous organisms in the American landscape.

The poison was tasteless, odorless, and invisible — there was no sensory warning that could protect the unwary. A person eating an Amanita would notice nothing unusual about the flavor or texture of the mushroom until the poison had already begun its deadly work.

## Mushroom Reproduction: Spores and Spawn

Palmer explained fungal reproduction in accessible terms designed to reach a popular audience that had no background in botany. Mushrooms possess reproductive bodies analogous to seeds, produced by the gills on the underside of the cap.

If one places a fully open mushroom cap gills-down on white paper, within hours the paper will be covered with fine powder composed of minute bodies, usually the color of the gills. This spore print is a basic tool of mycological identification still used today, more than a century after Palmer described it.

However, Palmer noted a crucial difference between spores and true seeds: these spores contain no trace of an embryo, no miniature plant waiting to develop. One may plant them in any number with no assurance of producing a single mushroom. The reproduction of the cryptogams (from Greek *kryptos*, hidden, and *gamos*, marriage) was little understood even by men of science.

The common mushroom is instead propagated by the **mycelium** or **spawn** — the subterranean part of the fungus, corresponding to the vine of a fruiting plant, while the part eaten represents the fruit. This white, thread-like substance spreads through prepared beds of rich manure and ultimately produces mushrooms above ground.

## The Agaricus Campestris

The common meadow mushroom, *Agaricus campestris*, was the benchmark edible fungus of Palmer"s era. It was the one variety that could be reliably propagated by artificial means, which is why it became the sole "mushroom" of commerce.

Everything else — the hundreds of other edible species, many of superior flavor and nutritional value — was excluded from markets and kitchens by default. Palmer noted that its description had been often attempted, but that almost any single characteristic of the common mushroom could also be found in some other fungus.

The pink gills that darken to brown, the white cap, the ring on the stem — all of these features appear in other species as well, some edible and some deadly. This observation underscored his central thesis that species-specific knowledge, not general rules, was the only path to safe [[bloomfield-angel-of-death-poisonous-mushroom-identification-toxinology]].

## New England"s Fungal Diversity

Palmer estimated approximately three thousand varieties of fungi in New England. Of these, about one-third were "bodies of size and substance" — visible, tangible mushrooms and toadstools large enough to be noticed and potentially collected for food.

The balance were microscopic, gelatinous, or otherwise outside the practical scope of the amateur mycologist interested in edible species. From this thousand visible species, Palmer believed that any person of good judgment and discernment could readily learn to distinguish some fifty edible varieties.

These fifty species "now rot in our fields and pastures" — an enormous waste of natural food resources that could be easily prevented with proper education. The loss was not merely nutritional; it was cultural, representing a cuisine that Americans had never developed because of irrational fear born of ignorance.

## Palmer"s Plea for Illustrated Popular Guides

Palmer's most passionate and repeated appeal was for the creation of an illustrated popular guide to American fungi. He proposed making use of **chromolithography** — then a widely available and affordable color printing technology — to produce accurately colored images of fungi at natural size.

A small book with explanations in popular language could accompany the pictures.

"One glance at such a work would acquaint the ordinary mortal with the chances of death or life by toadstools more surely than volumes of technical description," he wrote. No American work on edible fungi had ever been issued at the time of his writing. The existing technical classifications were so exclusively scientific as to be of little benefit to the general public.

Palmer announced his intention to produce such a work himself, soliciting specimens from interested persons and providing practical guidance for contributors: mushrooms are best gathered in the afternoon when dry, then laid in damp grass to maintain freshness.

Specimens for identification should be young, fresh, free from maggots (which infest fungi in large quantities as soon as they become stale), and ideally gathered in large lots of a single kind rather than miscellaneous collections that make identification more difficult.

## The Broader Significance

Palmer's advocacy for accessible mycological education was part of a larger vision: that the American public could safely enjoy a rich and varied fungal cuisine that already grew abundantly in their fields and forests. The gap was not nature's — it was education's.

The fungi were there, waiting to be known. His work stands as an early example of [[citizen-science-fungal-biodiversity-discovery]] advocacy, arguing that natural history knowledge should not be the exclusive province of specialists but should be made accessible to anyone willing to learn.

Palmer was also generous in acknowledging those who had helped his research. He praised the Boston city forester Mr. John Galvin for permission to collect specimens from city grounds, the custodians of the libraries at Horticultural Hall and the Natural History Rooms for free use of valuable works, and the many strangers who had sent him specimens and information from across New England.

The mushrooms, Palmer believed, deserved better than to be crushed underfoot in ignorance. They deserved to be known, appreciated, and when appropriate, brought to the table.
