# Desert Basins, Pans, Scalds, and Gully Erosion

The flat depressions, claypans, scalds, and eroded gullies of arid landscapes represent both the most degraded desert environments and some of the most responsive to [[permaculture]] restoration. Understanding the formation, behaviour, and restoration potential of these features is essential for desert reclamation work.

## Depression Types

### Tectonic Basins

The largest-scale flat areas or near-circular depressions are called **tectonic basins**, formed by geological subsidence. It is in these great tectonic depressions that salt lakes and dune seas (draas) form. Their enormous scale means they are generally managed at regional rather than site level.

### Deflation (Wind) Hollows

Next in scale are fairly large **deflection (wind) hollows**, formed where persistent winds have removed surface material down to harder substrates.

### Pans

Depressions with flat floors are called **pans**. Smaller depressions gather run-off and develop typical base materials of suspended silts or salts carried by water and deposited as the water evaporates. Two main types exist:

- **Claypans**: Clay-based evaporation areas where clay settles out after silty waters pool and evaporate.
- **Salt pans**: Salt-based evaporation areas where dissolved salts concentrate as water evaporates.

### Gilgais

Small depressions called **gilgais** form as a result of the swelling and shrinking of plastic clays. They are circular in outline and function as natural water-capture systems where erosion has left them as hollows. When sand-filled, however, they cease to function as water harvesters.

## Clay Soil Management

Clay soils in desert environments have distinctive properties that can be either harnessed or problematic:

### Sealing Properties

Clays can be treated with [[gypsum]] to **decrease** their capacity to infiltrate water (useful for directing run-off to storage). Alternatively, they can be treated with sodium carbonate or bentonite to **seal** them where they are to be used for water storage.

Natural montmorillonite or smectite clays swell and form impermeable surfaces after a short period of rain. This property greatly impedes infiltration and is the same mechanism that makes deliberately sealed clays useful for leading run-off water to tanks.

### Behaviour When Wet

Heavy, compacted, or cracking clays become notoriously sticky when wet, preventing all vehicle movement. This is a critical practical consideration for any desert project involving clay substrates.

## Scalds

### What Are Scalds?

A **scald** is a bare clay-pan area where a duplex soil (originally light sandy loams over a sharply-defined transition to deep clay below) has lost its upper soil layer. The remaining clays are often solonetic (with a high content of sodium ions).

### The Scald Problem

When first wetted by rain, solonetic clays tend to "melt," effectively sealing the surface and preventing infiltration. The wet crust so formed is almost impermeable—the same effect is used deliberately to seal clays for leading run-off water to tanks. Unlike claypans, scalds are not hollows but have flow into and out of the area.

### Restoration Strategy

Scald restoration uses a progressive banking approach:

1. **Grade a bank** up to 10-15 cm high, with the cut on the lower side. Water backs up a considerable distance behind this low bank.
2. **Let the backed-up distance equal one-third** of the total area to be treated, allowing two-thirds of bare soil as run-off.
3. **Pre-sow seed** on the surface where water will pool. The seed germinates in the moist zone behind the bank.
4. The low bank eventually "melts" flat (as solonetic clays do), and the next one-third can be treated as a successional strategy.
5. **Over 2-3 years**, a complete vegetative cover can be re-established.

### Critical Design Notes

- If banks are made too high or too solid, the plants will drown.
- Spiral earth-casting and ridges sown at their apex are also successfully used to seed claypans.
- Circular or spiral banks can be made on large claypans.

### Ecological Importance of Scald Restoration

Revegetation of scalds is important because plague locusts lay their eggs and hatch most successfully in the narrow shaded edges of scalds. Since most scalds are produced by over-grazing, the desert grazier effectively inflicts plague on whole regions through poor management. Healing scalds thus has far-reaching ecological benefits beyond the immediate site.

## Claypans

### Characteristics

Unlike scalds, claypans rarely overflow. They receive silty waters from clay soils, and after pooling for a period, clay settles out and water evaporates. They function as ephemeral wetland systems with distinct wet and dry phases.

### Wet Phase Ecology

During the wet phase:

- Tall canegrass (*Eragrostis australasica*) grows vigorously.
- Duck and waterfowl nest in the temporary swamp.
- The system provides critical habitat for aquatic species.

### Dry Phase Ecology

As the swamps dry out:

- Marsupials and rodents take refuge in the drying vegetation.
- The area serves as foraging ground for predators.
- Aboriginal peoples traditionally burnt these areas in autumn as canegrass dried out, knowing they would regenerate in the next wet period.

## Gully Erosion

### Causes and Characteristics

Active gully erosion represents one of the most dramatic forms of land degradation in arid and semi-arid regions. Gullies form when concentrated water flow removes soil faster than it can be replaced, creating deep, steep-sided channels that continue to expand.

### Small Gully Treatment

Small gullies can be addressed with relatively simple measures:

1. **Fill** the gully with available material.
2. **Fence** to exclude livestock.
3. **Plant** with appropriate species.
4. Monitor for stabilisation.

### Large Gully Treatment

Large gullies require multiple, coordinated strategies:

1. **Diversion of headwaters** away from the gully head to stop further extension.
2. **Swales** to hold water on side walls and prevent lateral erosion.
3. **Gabions** (wire cages filled with rock) on the gully floor to slow water velocity and trap sediment.
4. **Fencing** to exclude livestock from the gully system and surrounding area.
5. **Tree planting** throughout the treated area to stabilise soil with root systems.
6. **Spreader banks** to distribute headwaters gently downstream rather than concentrating them.

### The Importance of Upstream Intervention

The most critical intervention for gully erosion is addressing the headcut—the point where the gully is actively extending upstream. Diverting or spreading the concentrated flow that drives headward erosion is the single most effective measure. Without this, downstream treatments (gabions, plantings) will eventually be undermined or outflanked.

## The Desert Profile

A typical desert profile integrates all these features: fold mountains shedding water to foothills, wadis draining to alluvial plains, dune fields storing freshwater, and basins collecting the final run-off into pans and evaporative depressions. Understanding how these elements connect hydrologically allows the designer to intervene at the most effective points in the system—stabilising erosion at its source, harvesting water at points of concentration, and restoring degraded areas through the sequential strategies outlined above.
