# Shamanic Healing Three Forms: Extraction, Soul Retrieval, and Dismemberment

## Overview
In his 1998 article "Hallucinogenic Drugs and Plants in Psychotherapy and
Shamanism," Ralph Metzner identifies three main variations of shamanic healing
experiences that occur with entheogenic plants and other methods of accessing
nonordinary states of consciousness. These three forms—extraction of toxins or
sorcerous implants, retrieval of split-off psychic fragments or "soul," and
the experience of dismemberment followed by reconstitution—represent the core
therapeutic mechanisms of shamanic healing systems worldwide. Unlike Western
psychotherapy, which typically frames healing in terms of insight, emotional
processing, and cognitive restructuring, shamanic healing operates through
dramatic experiential events that directly address the spiritual and energetic
dimensions of illness.

## The First Form: Extraction of Toxins

### Sorcerous Implants
The first form of shamanic healing involves the extraction of a toxin that may
have been implanted by means of sorcery. In many indigenous shamanic traditions,
illness is understood not merely as a biological malfunction but as the result
of a spiritual intrusion—a foreign object or energy that has been introduced
into the patient's body or energy field by a sorcerer, by malevolent spirits,
or through exposure to polluted or dangerous places. The shaman, using
entheogenic plants or other methods of entering nonordinary reality, perceives
the location and nature of the intrusion and performs an extraction to remove
it.

### Shamanic Sucking Methods
In traditional Amazonian ceremonies with ayahuasca, shamans sometimes employ
"sucking" methods of extracting toxic psychic residues or sorcerous implants.
The shaman physically sucks at the site of the intrusion, drawing out the
offending object or energy. This practice is documented across numerous
Amazonian indigenous and mestizo traditions and is considered one of the most
direct and dramatic forms of shamanic healing. The shaman may then show the
extracted object to the patient and community, providing tangible evidence of
the healing that has taken place.

### Parallels in Other Traditions
Similar extraction practices are found in shamanic traditions worldwide. In
Siberian shamanism, the shaman may extract illness-causing objects from the
patient's body. In indigenous North American traditions, healers may remove
"spirit arrows" or other intrusive objects. The universality of this healing
form suggests that it addresses a fundamental dimension of human experience of
illness that is not captured by purely biomedical models.

## The Second Form: Soul Retrieval

### The Concept of Soul Loss
The second form of shamanic healing involves the retrieval of a split-off
psychic fragment or "soul." In shamanic cosmology, the soul is not a unitary,
indivisible entity but can be fragmented through trauma, fright, grief, or
other overwhelming experiences. When a piece of the soul becomes detached, the
person may experience a range of symptoms including depression, dissociation,
chronic illness, addiction, or a sense of emptiness and meaninglessness. The
concept of "soul loss" as a cause of illness is found in virtually all shamanic
traditions worldwide.

### The Retrieval Journey
To perform a soul retrieval, the shaman enters a trance state—often using
entheogenic plants, drumming, or other methods—and journeys to the place where
the soul fragment is believed to reside. This may be in the "lower world" (a
realm accessed through a hole in the earth), the "upper world" (reached by
climbing a cosmic tree or mountain), or some other nonordinary landscape. The
shaman negotiates with the beings who hold the soul fragment and brings it back
to the patient, reintegrating it into the patient's being.

### Modern Applications
Soul retrieval has been adapted into various contemporary healing practices,
particularly in the neo-shamanic and transpersonal psychology communities. The
concept resonates with Western psychological ideas about dissociation, trauma,
and the fragmentation of the self, while adding a spiritual and energetic
dimension that conventional psychotherapy typically does not address. Some
practitioners have found that combining soul retrieval with conventional trauma
therapy can produce results that neither approach achieves alone.

## The Third Form: Dismemberment and Reconstitution

### The Death-Rebirth Experience
The third and most dramatic form of shamanic healing involves the experience
of being dismembered or destroyed, and then reconstituted with a healthier,
stronger "body." This form of healing is closely related to what Stanislav
Grof, working within the psychedelic psychotherapy tradition, called "perinatal
matrices"—experiences of ego death and rebirth that occur when the psyche
opens to levels deeper than biographical material. In shamanic contexts, the
dismemberment experience is often described in vivid physical terms: the
shaman's body is torn apart by animals, boiled in a cauldron, ground to powder,
or otherwise destroyed before being reassembled.

### The Initiation Function
Dismemberment-reconstitution experiences frequently occur in the context of
shamanic initiation rather than healing per se. The aspiring shaman undergoes
a symbolic death that destroys their old identity and creates space for a new,
shamanic identity to emerge. This process is analogous in some respects to the
psychedelic therapy concept of the "psychedelic peak experience"—a moment of
ego dissolution that can lead to lasting personality transformation. However,
in the shamanic context, the dismemberment is understood as a literal spiritual
event, not merely a psychological metaphor.

### Cross-Cultural Parallels
The dismemberment-reconstitution motif appears in mythological and religious
traditions worldwide. The Egyptian god Osiris is dismembered by Set and
reassembled by Isis. The Norse god Odin hangs from the world tree for nine
nights, sacrificing himself to himself. In the Tibetan Buddhist chöd practice,
the practitioner visualizes offering their body to demons. In the Christian
tradition, the death and resurrection of Christ follows a similar pattern of
destruction and renewal. The universality of this motif suggests that it
encodes a fundamental psychological and spiritual process.

## The Underlying Shamanic Paradigm

### Spirit Beings as Co-Healers
In all three forms of shamanic healing, the healing is experienced as being
done by or with the assistance of spirit beings—also referred to as "allies,"
"power animals," "guardians," or "helpers." The shaman does not heal through
personal power alone but acts as an intermediary between the patient and the
spirit world, channeling healing energy and information from nonordinary
sources. This collaborative model of healing stands in contrast to the Western
medical model, in which the physician is the sole agent of healing.

### Multiple Worlds
All three forms presuppose the existence of multiple worlds or dimensions of
reality. The shaman travels to these worlds to perform the healing work,
accessing realms that are not available in ordinary waking consciousness. This
cosmological assumption is one of the two features that Metzner identifies as
most challenging to the Western worldview (the other being the reality of
spirit beings). Yet it is precisely this assumption that makes the three forms
of healing intelligible and practicable within the shamanic framework.

## Contrast with Western Psychotherapy

### Different Models of Illness
Where Western psychotherapy understands illness primarily in terms of
psychological dynamics—unconscious conflicts, maladaptive patterns, cognitive
distortions—shamanic healing understands illness in terms of spiritual and
energetic events: intrusions, soul loss, and the need for death-rebirth
transformation. These are not competing explanations of the same phenomenon but
fundamentally different frameworks for understanding the nature of illness and
the meaning of healing.
