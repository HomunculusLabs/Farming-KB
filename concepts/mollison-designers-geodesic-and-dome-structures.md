## Geodesic Geometry Fundamentals
A geodesic dome is based on the subdivision of an icosahedron (a regular
polyhedron with 20 equilateral triangular faces) onto the surface of a
sphere. The key concept is "frequency" -- the number of times each edge
of the icosahedron is subdivided:
- 1V (frequency 1): The basic icosahedron -- 20 triangular faces, 30
  edges, 12 vertices. Too faceted for practical shelter.
- 2V: Each icosahedral edge divided once -- 80 triangles. Still quite
  angular but usable for small structures.
- 3V: Each edge divided twice -- 180 triangles. A common frequency for
  practical domes, offering a good balance between structural complexity
  and smoothness.
- 4V: 320 triangles. Smoother appearance, more pieces to cut and
  assemble.
- 5V and higher: Increasingly smooth approximations of a sphere with
  correspondingly more structural elements.
As frequency increases, the number of strut lengths also increases. A 3V
dome typically uses two or three different strut lengths; higher
frequencies may require five or more. Each strut length must be cut and
labelled precisely.
## Strut Calculations
Mollison provides the basic geometry for calculating strut lengths in a
geodesic dome. For a sphere of desired radius R:
- The chord factor for each strut type depends on the frequency and the
  specific position of that strut in the subdivision pattern
- Strut length = chord factor x R (for the desired dome radius)
- Struts are typically made from timber, steel tubing, or bamboo
For a 3V dome (5/8 sphere, the most common practical configuration):
- The "A" strut (longest) has a chord factor of approximately 0.3486
- The "B" strut has a chord factor of approximately 0.4035
- The "C" strut (shortest) has a chord factor of approximately 0.4124
These chord factors are multiplied by the desired radius to give actual
strut lengths. Mollison notes that precise calculation is essential --
small errors accumulate across hundreds of struts and can prevent proper
assembly.
## Hub and Connection Systems
Connecting struts at vertices (hubs) is the critical engineering detail.
Mollison describes several approaches:
- Metal hub plates: Flat steel plates with holes drilled at the
  appropriate angles, through which bolts pass to connect strut ends.
  Commercial systems use precision-fabricated hubs.
- Timber hub blocks: Carved wooden blocks that receive strut ends in
  pre-drilled holes. Lower tech but require skilled woodworking.
- Pipe-flange method: Steel pipe segments welded into hubs, with strut
  ends flattened and bolted.
- Bolted plate hubs: Simple flat plates with bolts, suitable for lower
  frequency domes where angular tolerances are less critical.
The connection system must resist both tension and compression forces
that develop under wind and snow loads. Mollison emphasises that dome
structures, while materially efficient, concentrate forces at hub points
and require careful engineering of connections.
## Covering Materials
Dome covering presents specific challenges due to the curved surface:
- Tensioned fabric: PVC-coated polyester or canvas stretched over the
  frame. Effective but requires regular replacement (10 to 15 year
  lifespan for most fabrics).
- Sheet metal: Corrugated iron or aluminium panels, cut and bent to fit
  the triangular facets. Durable but labour-intensive to install and
  prone to leaks at joints.
- Shingles: Timber, slate, or metal shingles applied to a plywood or
  board substrate. The substrate follows the dome curvature.
