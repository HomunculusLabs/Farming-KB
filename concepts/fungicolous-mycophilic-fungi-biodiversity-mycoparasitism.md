## Fungicolous and Mycophilic Fungi: Mycoparasitism and Biodiversity

## Definition and Scope

[[fungicolous-fungi]] are fungi consistently found in association with other fungi,
encompassing mycoparasites (deriving nutrition from living fungal hosts),
commensals (benefiting without harming the host), and saprobes (colonizing dead
or dying fungal tissue). The term "mycophilic" is sometimes used
interchangeably. These organisms play significant roles in natural population
control, nutrient cycling, and maintenance of fungal community structure across
virtually all terrestrial ecosystems.

## Diversity and Species Counts

The true diversity of fungicolous fungi has proven difficult to quantify.
Rudakov (1978) catalogued approximately 1,700 nonlichenicolous fungicolous
species, establishing one of the earliest comprehensive benchmarks. Hawksworth
(1979, 1981) identified roughly 1,100 conidial (asexual) species associated
with approximately 2,500 host species. The [[lichenicolous-fungi]] — those
colonizing lichen thalli — represent an additional substantial component. Early
surveys identified 550+ lichenicolous species, though current estimates suggest
1,500+ as taxonomic study has intensified. Together these figures underscore
that fungicolous fungi represent a major yet frequently overlooked dimension of
global fungal biodiversity.

## Trophic Relationships: Biotrophy vs. Necrotrophy

Fungicolous fungi engage in a spectrum of trophic strategies. The two primary
modes are biotrophy and necrotrophy. Biotrophic fungicolous fungi maintain a
balanced relationship with their hosts, deriving nutrition without causing
immediate harm or cell death, often establishing long-term associations. In
contrast, necrotrophic fungi actively kill host cells through secretion of
lytic enzymes, toxins, or both, then exploit dead tissue as a nutritional
resource. Between these extremes, intermediate forms are common. Many
fungicolous fungi display facultative behavior, shifting between biotrophic and
necrotrophic modes depending on environmental conditions, host identity, or
physiological state of the host. This plasticity complicates classification and
reflects the ecological adaptability of the group.

## Rudakov's Six-Group Classification System

Rudakov (1978) proposed a six-group classification based on trophic
relationships with hosts:

1. **Biotrophs**: Obligate biotrophs maintaining living host tissue; cannot
   complete their life cycle on dead fungal material.
2. **Facultative biotrophs**: Primarily biotrophic but capable of limited
   survival on dead or senescent fungal tissue when living hosts unavailable.
3. **Necrotrophs**: Obligate necrotrophs that kill host tissue as a
   prerequisite for colonization and nutrition.
4. **Facultative necrotrophs**: Normally kill host cells but can occasionally
   establish biotrophic-like relationships under certain conditions.
5. **Semisaprophytic mycophiles**: Colonize senescing or partially dead fungal
   structures, positioned between true parasites and free-living saprobes.
6. **Saprophytic associates**: Found consistently on fungal substrata but
   functioning primarily as saprobes on dead fungal tissue.

## Behavioral Plasticity: Hypomyces chrysospermus

*Hypomyces chrysospermus* illustrates the complexity of fungicolous trophic
behavior. This species is invariably necrotrophic on mushroom fruiting bodies,
rapidly colonizing and destroying host tissue with characteristic yellow-to-
brown coloration. However, the same species behaves as a biotroph in
interactions with *Botrytis cinerea* and *Trichothecium roseum*, establishing
balanced relationships without immediately killing the host. This dual behavior
challenges rigid classification and highlights the importance of understanding
host-specific and condition-dependent variation in mycoparasitic interactions.

## Five Types of Mycoparasite-Host Interfaces

The physical interface between mycoparasite and host varies across groups:

1. **Contact necrotrophic (e.g., *Arthrobotrys*)**: Kills host tissue through
   diffusible toxins or enzymes without direct penetration, lysing hyphae at
   a distance before colonizing dead remains.
2. **Invasive necrotrophic (e.g., *Trichoderma*)**: Physically penetrates host
   hyphae using coiling behavior and enzymatic degradation, killing tissue
   from within.
3. **Haustorial (Zygomycota, Tremellales)**: Specialized haustoria penetrate
   host cells to extract nutrients without immediately killing them.
4. **Fusion via micropores (e.g., *Gonatobotrys*)**: Cytoplasmic continuity
   established through micropores or direct hyphal fusion for nutrient
   exchange between parasite and host.
5. **Intracellular (Chytrids, Oomycetes)**: The entire parasite lives within
   host cells, completing its life cycle inside as an endoparasite.

## Hyphal Interference Phenomenon

Hyphal interference is a widespread phenomenon whereby proximity of an
antagonistic fungus triggers deleterious changes in the host. When an
interfering hypha approaches a sensitive host within approximately 50
micrometers, the host hyphae exhibit rapid vacuolation — expansion of
vacuoles that disrupt cytoplasmic organization. This response occurs before
physical contact and is mediated by diffusible metabolites. The resulting
disruption can arrest host growth, trigger cell death, or render the host
vulnerable to subsequent colonization, functioning as chemical warfare at a
distance distinct from direct parasitism.

## Host Defense: Callosity Formation and Wall Thickening

Fungi targeted by mycoparasites have evolved various defenses, the most
prominent being callosity formation — localized cell wall thickenings at the
point of contact with a potential parasite. These create physical barriers
impeding penetration and restricting haustorial development. Wall appositions
and papillae, composed of polysaccharides and sometimes phenolic compounds,
serve similar functions. Some hosts produce antifungal secondary metabolites
or upregulate chitin-degrading enzymes. The evolutionary arms race between
fungicolous fungi and their hosts has driven diversification in both offensive
and defensive morphological and chemical strategies.

## Role as Biocontrol Agents

The antagonistic properties of fungicolous fungi, particularly necrotrophic
mycoparasites like *Trichoderma harzianum* and *T. viride*, have been
exploited in biological control of plant pathogenic fungi. These agents
suppress diseases by parasitizing pathogen hyphae, producing antibiotics,
competing for nutrients and space, and inducing systemic resistance. Commercial
*Trichoderma* formulations are widely used against soilborne pathogens
including *Rhizoctonia*, *Fusarium*, *Pythium*, and *Sclerotinia*.

## Detoxification of Substrata

Beyond direct mycoparasitism, fungicolous fungi detoxify substrata colonized
by toxic fungi. Many species degrade mycotoxins produced by their hosts,
including aflatoxins and trichothecenes, facilitating succession by allowing
secondary colonizers to establish after chemical modification of the
environment. This capacity enables fungicolous fungi to occupy niches
chemically inhospitable to most microorganisms, with broad implications for
nutrient cycling and community dynamics in terrestrial ecosystems.
