# Windbreak Design and Shelter in Permaculture

## Why Windbreaks Matter

Wind chill - the removal of heat from surfaces and evaporation of
fluids - creates cool to [[query-best-kiwifruit-varieties-for-cold-climates]] even in the tropics at
lower altitudes than adiabatic factors alone would predict.

This chill factor retards plant growth and lowers the efficiency
of solar devices and insulation. In cyclonic or hurricane areas,
catastrophic winds may become the overriding design modification
around which all other factors must be arrayed.

The effects of shelterbelts are compound and extend far beyond
simple wind reduction: increased snow meltwater for soil moisture,
greater fruit and seed set in bee-pollinated crops, preservation
of tree shape (reducing pruning), reduced livestock weight loss,
and significant [[fukuoka-hotel-analogy-crop-yield-model]] increases.

## Species Selection

Local lists of windbreak species are available from forestry and
agricultural departments. Key criteria for selection:

**Pioneer species**: Easily grown, fast-establishing plants that
tolerate exposure. Quickset hedges of poplar and Erythrina are
popular for fast windbreak effect.

**Penetrability**: Front-line species should have 40-50% wind
penetrability. Solid barriers create turbulence on the lee side;
semi-permeable barriers provide more effective and extensive
shelter zones.

**Wind-resistant plant traits**: Fibrous stems (palms), fleshy
leaves (aloes, agaves), hard needle-like leaves (pines,
tamarisks, Casuarinas), or hairy/waxy leaf covers (Coprosma,
eucalypts, some Acacias).

**Multi-functional species**: Chosen for [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]]
(Acacia, Casuarina, Leucaena), mulch production (Prosopis,
Erythrina, Melia, Canna), livestock fodder, hosting predatory
insects or birds, minimal moisture competition with crops, and
[[fukuoka-natural-livestock-farming]] barriers (thorny plants or woven hedges).

## Establishment Methods

Initial protection for young windbreak plantings includes
individual plastic bags around stakes, earth mounds or banks
of greater length than the tree line, brush or wire-mesh fences
with 40% penetrability, and tussock grass to windward.

Earth mounds are better streamlined and less sensitive to
windthrow. The hollow from mound excavation can hold water or
protect young plants.

On coasts, mesh fences with heavy top rails serve as the basis
for fedges (fence-hedges) of thick-leaved vines, mounded with
semi-succulents like Rhagodia, Tetragonia, Carpobrotus, or
Mesembryanthemum. Substantial trellis - sinuous (brick) or
zigzagged (timber) - provides more immediate shelter.

Tyre walls are cheap, removable, and warm inside, though
scarcely aesthetic unless regularly arranged and planted.

## Design Patterns

**Predictable wind sites**: Plan directional, patterned
windbreaks of earthbank and trees aligned to prevailing wind.
Buildings, gardens, and animal shelters face sun while
sheltered from wind.

**Multi-directional wind sites** (deserts): Impose a close
rectangular or network pattern of windbreaks, as severe winds
may arrive from any direction.

**Coastal windbreaks**: Tree canopies on dry salt-wind coasts
gain only ~46 cm height per 1 m width. It is not until after
the fourth or fifth tree row that wind-prone fruit or nut
trees will yield. Small openings of 6-9 m provide garden
shelter and admit light.

**Streamlined profiles**: Where space is ample and winds
strong, up to six rows of tree and tall grass lines yield
forage, timber, fuel, mulch, honey, and shelter
simultaneously. In constricted areas, close-spaced
single-tree lines are effective.

## Effects on Houses

Wind chill removes 60% of heat from glazed areas and hot
water collectors. Shelterbelt around a house achieves 20-30%
savings on heating fuels in moderate to severe winters.

In cold areas, earthbanks plus shelterbelt plus sun-facing
aspect form a critical design strategy. In deserts,
shelterbelt trees reduce ground temperatures up to 15 C
through shade and wind reduction.

## Effects on Livestock

Blizzards kill livestock and newborn lambs; even hardy
animals can lose 30% of bodyweight in 3 days. Fences must
not form downwind traps - herds pile up and smother in
fenced corners.

Moorland fences should allow easy escape to woodlots and
sheltered valleys. Sheep weight in unsheltered fields is
15% less than sheltered (New Zealand data). Australia
attributes 20% of lamb losses to wind chill.

Cattle on exposed sites eat 16% less winter feed. In the
tropics, ridge plantings of pines or Casuarinas with a
wind gap below crowns afford shade and induced breeze
that discourages flies and mosquitoes. Such ridges are
also rich mulch sources for lower slopes.

## Effects on Croplands

A matrix of shelterbelt species 10-16 m tall and 33-66 m
apart (Casuarina, poplar, Matsudana willow, trimmed
eucalypt) protects crops like kiwifruit and avocado.

Citrus culled as wind-damaged: 50% in unsheltered areas
versus 18.5% in shelterbelt systems. Cotton yields are
17.4% higher within five times shelterbelt height, 7.9%
at ten times height. Grain windburn is insignificant
for up to 18 times shelterbelt height.

## Soil Moisture and Erosion

Windbreaks increase soil moisture 4% to four times the
break height, to 1.2 m depth. In foggy climates, sea air
condensation provides 80-300% of rainfall as leaf drip.

Soil losses of up to 100 tonnes per hectare per day in
duststorms are prevented by windbreak and tussock grasses;
50-70% of dust settles out 100 m into tree clumps.
