# Mycorrhizal Plant Defense

How mycorrhizal and endophytic fungi protect plants from diseases, pests, and environmental stress. Based on [[paul-stamets]]' *Mycelium Running* (2005). See also [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], [[endophytic-fungi]], [[plant-defense-mechanisms]], and [[fungal-symbiosis-types]].

## The Root Armor Concept

Stamets describes mycorrhizal fungi as creating a living "armor" around plant roots:

- **Physical barrier**: Dense mycorrhizal hyphal mantle physically blocks pathogen access to root surfaces
- **Chemical defense**: Fungi secrete antimicrobial compounds that inhibit pathogens
- **Biological competition**: Mycorrhizal fungi compete with pathogens for root colonization sites and nutrients
- **Immune priming**: Mycorrhizal colonization primes the plant's own immune system for faster response
- **Biofilm formation**: Fungal-bacterial biofilms on root surfaces create a protective micro-ecosystem

## Defense Mechanisms

### 1. Physical Protection

- **Hyphal mantle**: Ectomycorrhizal fungi form a dense sheath around root tips (Hartig net), physically blocking soil-borne pathogens
- **Hyphal density**: A single gram of ectomycorrhizal root tip can contain meters of fungal hyphae
- **Root colonization**: Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi colonize root cortical cells, leaving no space for pathogens
- **Soil aggregation**: Mycelial networks bind soil particles around roots, creating stable microenvironments that resist pathogen invasion

### 2. Chemical Defense

- **Antibiotic secretion**: Mycorrhizal fungi produce antibiotics and antifungal compounds:
  - **Ectomycorrhizal species**: Secrete compounds inhibitory to Pythium, Phytophthora, Fusarium, and Rhizoctonia
  - **Stropharia rugoso-annulata**: Produces antibacterial compounds lethal to E. coli and other coliforms
  - **Trametes versicolor**: Secretes PSK/PSP with immunomodulatory and antitumor activity
- **Phenolic compounds**: Mycorrhizal roots contain higher levels of phenolics, which are toxic to many pathogens
- **Phytoalexin induction**: Mycorrhizal colonization triggers the plant to produce its own defense compounds (phytoalexins, pathogenesis-related proteins)

### 3. Systemic Acquired Resistance (SAR)

- Mycorrhizal colonization can trigger SAR — a whole-plant immune response
- Once activated, the plant becomes resistant to a broad range of pathogens, not just those at the root zone
- SAR involves salicylic acid signaling and production of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins
- The effect is systemic: leaves and stems become more resistant even though the mycorrhizae are on roots

### 4. Induced Systemic Resistance (ISR)

- Similar to SAR but mediated by jasmonic acid and ethylene signaling pathways
- Primarily effective against necrotrophic pathogens and herbivorous insects
- Mycorrhizal fungi trigger ISR through root-to-shoot signaling
- Often works synergistically with plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR)

### 5. Nutrient-Mediated Defense

- Well-nourished plants are inherently more disease-resistant
- Mycorrhizal fungi improve phosphorus, zinc, copper, and other micronutrient uptake
- Adequate phosphorus is particularly important for disease resistance
- Healthier plants allocate more resources to defense compounds

## Specific Disease Protection

### Root Pathogens

| Pathogen | Disease | Mycorrhizal Protection Mechanism |
|----------|---------|--------------------------------|
| Phytophthora spp. | Root rot, damping-off | Physical barrier, antibiotic secretion, competition |
| Pythium spp. | Damping-off, root rot | Biofilm formation, nutrient competition |
| Fusarium spp. | Wilt, root rot | Phytoalexin induction, SAR triggering |
| Rhizoctonia spp. | Damping-off, root rot | Physical barrier, ISR induction |
| Verticillium spp. | Wilt | SAR/ISR, improved plant vigor |
| Armillaria spp. | Root rot | Competition, though Armillaria IS a basidiomycete itself |

### Foliar Diseases

- Mycorrhizal colonization has been shown to reduce severity of:
  - Powdery mildew (reduced by 20-50% in some studies)
  - Downy mildew
  - Leaf spot diseases
  - Rust infections
- Mechanism: SAR/ISR signaling from roots to leaves primes foliar defenses

### Nematode Protection

- Mycorrhizal fungi can reduce nematode damage through:
  - Physical barrier preventing nematode root penetration
  - Chemical deterrence (fungal exudates repel nematodes)
  - Improved plant tolerance (better nutrition = better ability to withstand damage)
  - Some mycorrhizal fungi trap and kill nematodes (though this is more common in predatory fungi like Arthrobotrys)

## Endophytic Defense

### Endophytes as Internal Protectors

Stamets discusses endophytic fungi that live INSIDE plant tissues:

- **Clavicipitaceous endophytes**: Found in grasses; produce alkaloids that deter herbivores (insects, grazing mammals)
- **Non-clavicipitaceous endophytes**: Found in all plant groups; provide disease resistance, drought tolerance, heat tolerance
- **Piriformospora indica**: Root endophyte that provides broad-spectrum disease resistance and growth promotion
- **Endophytic Trichoderma**: Live inside root tissues; produce antibiotics and induce systemic resistance

### Cannabis Endophytes

- See [[endophytic-fungi]] for Stamets' discussion of fungal endophytes in cannabis
- Endophytic fungi in cannabis may contribute to terpene and cannabinoid production
- Potential for using endophytes to enhance cannabis disease resistance

## Fungal-Bacterial Biofilms

### The Biofilm Defense System

- Mycorrhizal hyphae serve as scaffolding for bacterial biofilms
- These biofilms contain plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR)
- The combined fungal-bacterial community provides more effective defense than either alone
- Specific PGPR partnerships include:
  - **Pseudomonas fluorescens**: Produces siderophores and antibiotics; suppresses Fusarium
  - **Bacillus subtilis**: Produces antifungal lipopeptides; suppresses Rhizoctonia
  - **Bacillus amyloliquefaciens**: Produces iturin and fengycin; broad-spectrum pathogen suppression

## Practical Applications

### Mycorrhizal Inoculation for Disease Control

1. **At planting**: Apply mycorrhizal inoculant to roots or planting hole
2. **As seed treatment**: Coat seeds with mycorrhizal spores
3. **As soil drench**: Apply liquid mycorrhizal inoculant to soil
4. **In compost tea**: Include mycorrhizal spores in AACT applications (though spores may not survive brewing)
5. **See [[mycoforestry-forest-restoration]] for forest-scale inoculation techniques**

### Species Selection for Defense

| Goal | Recommended Fungal Group | Notes |
|------|-------------------------|-------|
| General disease resistance | AM fungi (Glomus, Rhizophagus) | Broad host range, easy to inoculate |
| Forest tree protection | ECM fungi (Pisolithus, Suillus) | Essential for conifer health |
| Vegetable garden protection | AM fungi + Trichoderma | Combination approach |
| Pasture improvement | Endophytic fungi in grasses | Also deters insect pests |
| Contaminated site restoration | Mycorrhizae + saprophytes | Combined remediation + plant support |
