# Common Homestead Weeds

Detailed profiles of the most common weeds found on a regenerative homestead. Covers identification, edible/medicinal uses, ecological roles, and management strategies. See [[weed-management-strategies]] for prevention, control methods, and seasonal calendar.

## Edible and Useful Weeds

### Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*)

**Identification**: Low rosette of deeply toothed leaves; yellow composite flowers on hollow stems; round seed head (puffball). Found everywhere — lawns, gardens, fields, roadsides.

**All parts edible**:
- Young leaves: salads, slightly bitter (less bitter before flowering); cook like spinach
- Flowers: wine, fritters, jelly; petals raw in salads
- Roots: roast and grind as coffee substitute; also medicinal (liver tonic, diuretic)
- Flower buds: capers substitute when pickled

**Ecological role**: Deep taproot (6-15 feet) mines calcium, potassium, and trace minerals — a key [[dynamic-accumulators]]. Early spring nectar source for bees and pollinators.

**Management**: Don't fight it — harvest regularly. Use as accumulator mulch. Remove from intensive garden beds if desired; allow in lawns and pathways.

### Purslane (*Portulaca oleracea*)

**Identification**: Prostrate succulent with reddish stems; fleshy paddle-shaped leaves; tiny yellow flowers; forms dense mat. Thrives in warm, moist garden soil.

**Culinary**: Excellent raw in salads or sautéed — mild, slightly tangy, crunchy texture. High in omega-3 fatty acids (alpha-linolenic acid), vitamin A, vitamin C, iron, calcium, potassium. Used in Mediterranean, Mexican, and Middle Eastern cuisines.

**Ecological role**: Ground cover that retains soil moisture and protects soil surface. Seeds persist in soil for decades.

**Management**: Eat it. If removing, don't compost — fragments re-root easily. Mulch heavily to suppress.

### Lamb's Quarters (*Chenopodium album*)

**Identification**: Upright (2-6 feet), triangular-toothed leaves with whitish mealy coating underneath. Tiny green flowers in clusters. Found in rich garden soil and disturbed areas.

**Culinary**: Nutritious spinach substitute — high in iron, protein, vitamins A and C, calcium. Young leaves and tender tips best. Blanch or cook (contains oxalates). Seeds are quinoa relatives — edible when threshed and cleaned.

**Ecological role**: Bioaccumulator of nitrogen — thrives in fertile soil, indicating good fertility. Can produce 70,000+ seeds per plant.

**Management**: Harvest young plants. Pull or cut before seed set (critical).

### Plantain (*Plantago major*, *P. lanceolata*)

**Identification**: *P. major*: broad oval leaves with prominent parallel veins from base; *P. lanceolata* (narrow-leaved): lance-shaped leaves in basal rosette. Both have characteristic seed stalks.

**Edible**: Young leaves (salads, cooked — older leaves are fibrous). Seeds contain psyllium mucilage — can be ground as fiber supplement.

**Medicinal**: "Nature's bandage" — crush fresh leaves and apply to insect bites, stings, cuts, splinters (drawing action). Leaf tea for coughs and digestive issues. Traditional poultice herb worldwide.

**Ecological role**: Dynamic accumulator of calcium, magnesium, sulfur, potassium. Tolerates foot traffic — good pathway plant.

**Management**: Allow in pathways and lawns. Remove from garden beds. Harvest for medicine and mulch.

### Chickweed (*Stellaria media*)

**Identification**: Low, spreading mat with bright green oval pointed leaves. Tiny white star-shaped flowers (5 petals, deeply divided — appears 10). Single line of hairs on stem between leaf joints.

**Culinary**: Mild, tender green — excellent raw in salads or sandwiches; cook like spinach. High in iron, zinc, potassium, magnesium, and vitamins A, C, D.

**Medicinal**: Cooling and soothing — poultices for skin irritation, rashes, burns. Traditional spring tonic.

**Ecological role**: Winter annual — germinates in cool, moist conditions. Indicates fertile, moist soil. Seeds persist 5+ years.

**Management**: Easy to pull (shallow roots). Mulch to suppress. Eat it before it goes to seed.

### Nettles (*Urtica dioica*)

**Identification**: Upright perennial (2-6 feet) with opposite, serrated heart-shaped leaves covered in stinging hairs. Square stems (mint family). Dense patches spread by rhizome.

**Culinary**: Blanch 1-2 minutes to neutralize sting — excellent cooked green. High in protein, iron, calcium, vitamins A and C. Dry for tea. Traditional spring tonic in many cultures.

**Soil building**: Excellent compost activator — [[compost-tea-aact]] ingredient. [[fermented-plant-extract-fpe]] ingredient in [[knf-preparations]]. Dynamic accumulator: N, Fe, K, Ca, Mg, S.

**Ecological role**: Host plant for butterfly larvae (question mark, red admiral, tortoiseshell). Wildlife habitat.

**Management**: Contain with root barriers (rhizomes spread aggressively). Harvest regularly for tea, compost, and FPJ. Wear gloves.

### Amaranth / Pigweed (*Amaranthus retroflexus*)

**Identification**: Tall (3-6+ feet), sturdy stem with alternate diamond-shaped leaves. Green flower spikes at stem tips and leaf axils. Related to cultivated amaranth.

**Culinary**: Young leaves and stems cooked like spinach (high in vitamins A, C, iron, calcium). Seeds are edible — highly nutritious grain (protein, lysine). Thresh and winnow like grain.

**Ecological role**: Extremely competitive — shades out other plants. Indicates nitrogen-rich soil.

**Management**: Pull before seed set (one plant = 100,000+ seeds). Easy to pull when young. Must prevent seed set.

## Problem Weeds

### Bindweed (*Convolvulus arvensis*)

**Identification**: Trailing or climbing vine with arrowhead-shaped leaves. White/pink morning-glory-like flowers. Deep rhizome system (20+ feet).

**Why it's a problem**: Wraps and strangles crop plants. Regrows from any root fragment. Extremely difficult to eradicate.

**Management**: Smother with 6+ inches of mulch or landscape fabric for a full growing season. Repeated pulling depletes root reserves (takes 3-5 years). Never till (spreads root fragments). Solarization can help in hot climates. Accept that control, not eradication, is realistic.

### Quackgrass (*Elymus repens*)

**Identification**: Creeping grass with distinctive clasping auricles where leaf meets stem. Aggressive underground rhizomes with sharp points.

**Why it's a problem**: Spreads rapidly underground, competing with all crops. Rhizome fragments regrow.

**Management**: Smother with cardboard + thick mulch (6+ inches) for a full season. Solarize. Repeated digging to remove rhizomes. Improve soil to favor desired species.

### Canada Thistle (*Cirsium arvense*)

**Identification**: Upright perennial (2-5 feet) with spiny leaves. Small purple/white flowers in clusters. Spreads by creeping roots and wind-blown seed.

**Why it's a problem**: Noxious weed — spreads by root (horizontal roots can extend 15+ feet). One plant can produce 5,000+ seeds. Deep taproot.

**Management**: Cut/mow before flowering to prevent seed set (repeated cutting weakens roots). Dig out taproot where feasible. Maintain dense cover crops to compete. Accept long-term management.

### Bull Thistle (*Cirsium vulgare*)

**Identification**: Biennial — flat rosette first year, tall flowering stalk (2-6 feet) second year with large purple flower heads and sharp spines.

**Management**: Easier than Canada thistle — biennial lifecycle means cutting before seed set in year two eliminates that generation. Dig out rosettes in year one.
