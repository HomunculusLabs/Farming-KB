## Overview

Protozoa are among the most important drivers of [[phosphate-accumulators-sustainable-agriculture]] and soil health. Research has demonstrated that protozoan grazing can increase
plant nitrogen uptake by 30–70% compared to soils where protozoa are excluded, highlighting the practical importance of
maintaining healthy protozoan populations for crop production. Understanding the mechanisms by which protozoa mineralize nutrients
is essential for developing management practices that optimize soil biological fertility.

## Nitrogen Mineralization by Protozoan Grazing

Nitrogen mineralization by protozoa is driven by the stoichiometric imbalance between protozoan consumers and their bacterial
prey. Bacteria typically have carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratios of 4–6:1, while protozoa have higher C:N ratios of approximately
8–12:1. When protozoa consume bacterial biomass, they cannot incorporate all the nitrogen contained in their prey because their
carbon requirements are proportionally higher. The excess nitrogen is excreted primarily as ammonium (NH₄⁺), which is immediately
available for plant uptake or nitrification.

Quantitative studies have measured nitrogen mineralization rates of 0.5 to 5.0 μg N per gram of soil per day attributable to
protozoan grazing in agricultural soils. The actual rate depends on protozoan abundance, bacterial productivity, and environmental
conditions. Under favorable moisture and temperature conditions, protozoan-mediated nitrogen mineralization can supply 30–100 kg N
per hectare per growing season, equivalent to a substantial fraction of typical crop nitrogen requirements.

The nitrogen use efficiency of protozoan grazing is remarkably high compared to direct microbial turnover. While bacteria alone
mineralize nitrogen slowly through endogenous respiration and cell death, protozoan grazing accelerates nitrogen release by 5 to
20 times. This acceleration occurs because protozoa selectively consume the most active bacterial cells—those with the highest
rates of nutrient uptake and [[fungal-roles-in-phosphorus-solubilization]] cycling. Protozoa
contain phosphorus-rich compounds including nucleic acids, phospholipids, and polyphosphate granules, and their grazing activities
release both inorganic phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and organic phosphorus compounds into the soil solution. Protozoan-mediated phosphorus
mineralization can increase plant-available phosphorus by 15–40% compared to ungrazed soils.

Protozoa influence [[phosphorus-availability]] is naturally limited.

The interaction between protozoan grazing and mycorrhizal fungi creates additional phosphorus cycling pathways. Protozoa grazing
on bacteria in the mycorrhizosphere can enhance mycorrhizal hyphal growth and activity, indirectly increasing phosphorus uptake
through the mycorrhizal pathway. Conversely, protozoan consumption of bacterial biofilms on root surfaces may reduce bacterial
competition for phosphorus at the root-soil interface, facilitating direct phosphorus uptake by root hairs.

## Carbon Flow Through Protozoan Channels

Protozoa occupy a central position in carbon flow through the soil food web, channeling carbon from bacterial production toward
higher trophic levels and respiratory CO₂ loss. The carbon use efficiency of protozoa—the proportion of consumed carbon
incorporated into new protozoan biomass—typically ranges from 30 to 50%, with the remaining 50–70% respired as CO₂. This
relatively low growth efficiency means that protozoan grazing accelerates carbon turnover and increases total soil respiration.

The carbon channeled through protozoan populations follows two main pathways. A portion is transferred to higher trophic levels
when protozoa are consumed by nematodes, microarthropods, and other soil predators. This trophic transfer supports larger soil
organisms and contributes to the overall energy flow of the soil food web. The larger portion is respired as CO₂ during protozoan
metabolism, representing a significant source of soil CO₂ emissions. Protozoan respiration can account for 10–30% of total soil
respiration in grassland and forest soils.
