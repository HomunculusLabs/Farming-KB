# Nitrogen Deposition and Ectomycorrhizal Community Structure

Atmospheric nitrogen deposition, primarily from fossil fuel combustion and agricultural emissions, fundamentally reshapes ectomycorrhizal fungal communities. The effects are complex, operating through changes in soil chemistry, plant-fungal carbon allocation, and direct toxicity, with consequences that cascade through forest ecosystems.

## The Scale of the Problem

Nitrogen deposition rates have increased dramatically across Europe and North America since the industrial revolution. In experimental studies, researchers have applied nitrogen at rates ranging from ambient levels (typically 10–35 kg N ha⁻¹ y⁻¹ in European forests) up to extreme loading of 790 kg N ha⁻¹ to simulate cumulative deposition effects over time.

## Effects on Mycorrhizal Fruiting

### General Decline Patterns

The most dramatic and consistently observed effect of nitrogen addition is the reduction in ectomycorrhizal fungal fruit body production:

- **Termorshuizen (1990)** showed that nitrogen addition significantly reduced the number of fruit bodies of basidiomycete ectomycorrhizal species
- **Rühling & Tyler (1991)** found that adding 3× and 9× ambient N deposition (260 and 790 kg ha⁻¹ N) to beech woodlands caused near-complete cessation of mycorrhizal fungal fruiting after 3 years
- **Arnolds (1989a,b)** documented declines in ectomycorrhizal fruit body appearance in Dutch forests associated with combined acidifying pollutants and particularly nitrogen deposition

### Contrasting Increase in Saprotrophs

While mycorrhizal fungal fruiting declines, many saprotrophic fungal species increase fruiting under elevated N:
- Species of *Mycena*, *Clitocybe*, *Lepista*, *Agaricus*, and *Lycoperdon* all increased in N-fertilized beech plots
- A possible explanation: increased N availability stimulates decomposition of recalcitrant leaf litter, providing more resources for saprotrophs

### Species-Specific Responses

Not all ectomycorrhizal species respond equally to nitrogen addition. In the Swedish NITREX experiment (**Brandrud, 1995**):

| Response Pattern | Species |
|---|---|
| **Decreased by N** | *Cortinarius* spp., *Russula* spp. |
| **Little change** | *Lactarius* spp. |
| **Increased by N** | *Paxillus involutus*, *Lactarius rufus* |

This species-specific variation suggests that nitrogen deposition acts as a selective filter, promoting opportunistic species while suppressing sensitive taxa. The functional consequences of this community restructuring remain poorly understood.

## Fruiting vs. Root Colonization: A Critical Disconnect

One of the most important findings in this field is the poor correspondence between aboveground fruiting patterns and belowground root colonization:

### The NITREX Experiment

**Jonsson (1998)** used molecular analysis of ectomycorrhizal community structure on roots in the Swedish NITREX experiment and found:
- Only 1–4% correspondence between species of fruit bodies observed aboveground and mycorrhizae identified on roots belowground
- Fruit body surveys showed more dramatic shifts in species composition than root tip analyses
- This was true in both control plots and those receiving nitrogen additions

### Implications for Monitoring

This disconnect has profound implications:
1. **Fruit body surveys may overestimate** the magnitude of community change, as fruiting is more sensitive to N than root colonization
2. **Root tip analyses may underestimate** change, as molecular methods may not detect all species present
3. **Both methods are needed** for comprehensive assessment, and neither alone gives a complete picture
4. **Functional assessment** (enzyme production, nutrient uptake rates) is largely missing from most studies

## Belowground Community Responses

### Root Colonization Changes

The effects of nitrogen on actual root colonization vary among studies:

- **Arnebrant & Söderström (1992):** Total mycorrhizal colonization of Scots pine roots reduced from 70% to 55% after 13 years of fertilization with 1700 and 950 kg N ha⁻¹
- **Kårén & Nylund (1997):** Ammonium sulfate addition to Norway spruce decreased fine root biomass but not the degree of ectomycorrhizal colonization — suggesting that while total root area is reduced, the remaining roots maintain colonization
- **Esher et al. (1992):** In US pine forests, acid precipitation (pH 4.3 and 3.6) significantly reduced fine root length, dry mass, and number of short ectomycorrhizal roots of both loblolly and longleaf pine

### Community Structure Shifts on Roots

Molecular approaches have revealed changes in belowground community structure that fruit body surveys miss:

- **Jonsson et al. (1999a,b)** combined fruit body, morphological, and molecular analyses to provide the most comprehensive picture of ectomycorrhizal community response to nitrogen
- **Kårén & Nylund (1997)** suggested that while the degree of colonization may not change, the structure of the ectomycorrhizal community on roots does shift under elevated N

## Mycorrhizal Diversity and Function

### The Diversity-Productivity Relationship

The question of how mycorrhizal species diversity affects ecosystem function has received increasing attention:

- **Van der Heijden et al. (1998)** showed that arbuscular mycorrhizal diversity promotes higher plant productivity
- **Baxter & Dighton (2001)** constructed laboratory communities with one, two, or four ectomycorrhizal species and found that diversity per se was a better predictor of birch seedling nutrient content than species composition or colonization rates — with stronger effects on phosphorus than nitrogen uptake

### Functional Consequences of Community Change

The critical unanswered question is: what are the ecophysiological consequences of pollutant-induced community change? Evidence suggests that not all ectomycorrhizae function identically:

- Different species vary in enzyme production, nutrient uptake rates, and degree of host plant protection from root pathogens (**Dighton, 1983; Abuzinadah & Read, 1986, 1989; Dighton et al., 1990**)
- Loss of sensitive species (e.g., *Cortinarius*, *Russula*) that may perform unique functions could reduce overall ecosystem process efficiency
- The replacement of diverse communities by N-tolerant generalists like *Paxillus involutus* may fundamentally alter nutrient cycling pathways

## Nitrogen Saturation and Nutrient Imbalance

### Liebig's Law of the Minimum

The principle that growth is limited by the most scarce resource applies to nitrogen-saturated forests:

- **Harrison et al. (1995)** showed that potassium and phosphorus became increasingly limiting for Sitka spruce growth as nitrogen addition increased
- Chronic N addition shifts ecosystems from N-limitation to limitation by other nutrients
- The change in relative availabilities of inorganic and organic forms of phosphorus, along with N:P ratios, may strongly influence mycorrhizal community structure by:
  - Promoting fungal species with high phosphatase-producing potential
  - Suppressing species capable of acquiring nitrogen from organic sources

### Carbon-Nutrient Balance Theory

Under elevated N, plants allocate less carbohydrate to mycorrhizal partners because:
1. N is no longer limiting, reducing the benefit of the mycorrhizal N-scavenging function
2. Plant demand for other nutrients (P, K) may increase, but the specific fungal species best suited for acquiring these nutrients may have been lost
3. The net effect is reduced plant investment in the mycorrhizal symbiosis

## Phosphorus Cycling Under Nitrogen Enrichment

**Carreira et al. (2000)** proposed that acidifying pollutants combined with nitrogen saturation induce phosphate nutritional stress through two pathways:

1. **Soil acidity effects:** Changes in soil pH affect both inorganic and organic P cycling, increasing P sorption and decreasing labile P
2. **N saturation effects:** Increased P demand induced by luxury N uptake depletes available P pools

Acid phosphatase activity in soil decreased under combined acid mist and N addition, suggesting reduced fungal involvement in organic P mineralization. This creates a potential positive feedback: reduced P availability increases plant stress, further altering carbon allocation to mycorrhizae.

## Regional Variation and Cautions

The effects of acidifying pollutants and nitrogen deposition are not uniform across regions:

- **Sæstad & Jenssen (1993)** found decreased saprotrophic species richness and increased dominance of some ectomycorrhizal species in response to simulated acid rain in Norway — the opposite pattern to many European studies
- They attributed differences to local conditions (soils, climate) and cautioned against broad generalizations about pollutant effects on fungal communities
- The context-dependency of responses means that management recommendations must be regionally calibrated

## Conservation Implications

The sensitivity of ectomycorrhizal communities to nitrogen deposition has conservation consequences:
- **Arnolds (1989b, 1997)** developed "red data" lists for threatened fungal species
- **Watling (1999)** advocated for inclusion of fungi and non-vascular plants in UK species conservation lists
- Loss of [[mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]] may impair forest ecosystem resilience, nutrient cycling efficiency, and tree regeneration capacity
