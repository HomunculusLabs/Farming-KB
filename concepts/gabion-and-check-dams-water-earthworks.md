# Gabion and Check Dams

[[gabion-dams-and-check-dams]] are permeable or semi-permeable water-harvesting structures built across valleys, gullies, and drainage lines to control water flow, reduce erosion, and capture silt. They are foundational elements of permaculture water management, particularly in drylands and eroding landscapes.

## Types of Dams and Their Functions

### Gabion Dams

Gabions are wire mesh baskets filled with rocks or shingle. In dryland environments, permeable gabion barriers placed across eroding valleys create **silt fields** and spread water across the landscape. The typical scale for farm construction ranges from 0.6 to 1.8 meters (2–6 feet) high. The purpose is not to store free surface water, but rather to create flat areas where silt loads deposit usefully, forming **absorption beds** during flood conditions. Over time, these silt deposits build fertile ground that supports vegetation.

### Check Dams

Check dams serve several specific purposes depending on their design:

- **Silt Check Dams**: Built from earth, concrete walls, or gabions to hold silt fields, spread water, reduce silt load in streams, and trap sediment. They are essential for watershed restoration.
- **Diversion Check Dams**: Divert intermittent flow to ridges, storages, or canals constructed on contour. These redirect water from watercourses to productive use.
- **Concrete Flood Check Dams**: Allow normal flow to pass while retarding floods and preventing rapid flood discharge downstream. They provide flood moderation without obstructing base flow.
- **Check Dams for Ram Pumps or Water Wheels**: Create sufficient head (height difference) to enable mechanical energy use for pumping, lifting water, or diversion to canals.

### Ring and Turkey's Nest Dams

Ring dams and turkey's nest dams store water pumped in by windmills and provide low-head water storage at higher elevations. Landscapes can be pipe-tiled from large roofs or parking areas to feed these storages, making them useful in areas where gravity-fed systems are needed.

## Landscape Zonation

Mollison conceptualizes the landscape—sliced into layers through contours—as a set of functional zones:

1. **Ridges and skylines** — the primary catchment zone where most precipitation falls
2. **Upper slopes** — areas of concentrated flow
3. **Mid-slopes** — transition zones between catchment and storage
4. **Lower slopes and valley floors** — storage and usage zones
5. **Drainage lines** — revitalization zones where water, biological activity, and nutrient cycling are concentrated

Understanding this zonation is critical for placing dams and earthworks where they will be most effective.

## Building Earth Dams

### Site Selection Criteria

Storage dams should be carefully located with respect to several factors:

- **Soil type**: Core out a sample pit to assess clay fractions; soils with better than 50% clay fractions can form waterproof barriers up to 3.6 m (12 feet) high
- **Grade behind wall**: Lower slopes provide the greatest water capacity for the least earth moved
- **Downstream safety**: A key factor for all dams, especially large ones—structures and houses below must be protected
- **Height above use points**: Gravity flow is always desirable over pumped systems
- **Available catchment or diversion**: The water source must justify the dam's construction

### Construction Standards

**Tamped earth** with adequate clay fractions is the primary material. Practical depths for small earth dams are 4.5–6 m (15–20 feet). Slopes to the crest should be **concave**, and compaction is critical:

- Every 25 cm (10 inches) of earth should be rolled or tamped by machinery
- Boulders, logs, grass clumps, and topsoil must be excluded from the dam wall
- Earth should be neither so dry as to crumble nor so wet as to slump under the roller
- Shrinkage of well-compacted dams is less than 1%

**Structural requirements:**

- A **key** (trench) must be cut to prevent shear failure and cut off base seepage on all walls 1.8 m (6 feet) or higher
- Safe slope ratios: 3:1 (inner/upstream face) and 2 or 2.5:1 (outer/downstream face)
- Freeboard: 0.9 m (3 feet) above expected water level
- Key depth: 0.6–0.9 m (2–3 feet)
- In suspect soils, the entire core can be constructed from carted clay

### Dam Efficiency

The efficiency of a dam depends on the **flatness of the area behind the wall**. A V-valley, U-valley, plateau, or flat field provides the greatest storage per unit of wall construction. The key metric is the ratio of water backup length to dam wall length—when back-up exceeds wall length, the dam is increasingly cost-effective. Short dams at constricted sites with broad, flat valleys behind them are among the most efficient.

## Spillways and Outlets

The **spillway base** should be carefully surveyed at 1 m below the crest and positioned away from the wall or fill. A **siphon** or **base outlet pipe** fitted with baffle plates draws off water. These ensure the dam can safely release excess water without eroding the wall.

## Enhancing Dam Ecosystems

Small dams become valuable landscape features when:

- **Fenced and planted** with 30–60 m of forest and fruit tree surround for biological filtration
- **Topsoil returned** and edges planted with appropriate species to reduce turbidity
- **Cattle, sheep, and other animals** watered at spigots or troughs rather than directly at the dam
- **Troughs treated** with copper sulphate crystals to kill snails and parasitic hosts
- **Fish stocked** in the dam to control parasites
- **Crests gravelled** and used as road crossings over valleys or bogs
- **Special features** incorporated: deep areas, islands, peninsulas, shelves, and benches for wildlife habitat

## Stabilizing Eroding Gullies

Eroding gullies are best stabilized using strong wire baskets (gabions) containing stones or shingle. These structures resist flood forces well and gradually trap sediment, allowing vegetation to establish and ultimately healing the gully system.
