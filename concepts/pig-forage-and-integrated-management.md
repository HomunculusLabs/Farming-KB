# Pig [[urban-permaculture-and-integrated-land-management|permaculture]] system. When
properly managed with forage systems and integrated rotational strategies, they provide
soil cultivation, weed control, and fertilization while substantially reducing feed costs.
Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual* outlines approaches that transform
pigs from resource-intensive livestock into active contributors to landscape regeneration.

## Forage Species Selection

A well-designed pig forage system draws on tree crops, root vegetables, leafy greens,
and fruits. The goal is always year-round or near-year-round availability of
high-calorie, nutrient-dense forage appropriate to the local climate.

### Tree Forage

Tree crops provide long-term, low-maintenance fodder. In temperate zones, oak (acorns),
carob, honey locust (pod-bearing), mulberry, fodder beech, and chestnut are excellent
choices. Chestnuts provide a high-starch autumn harvest; mulberry is valued for prolific
leaf production and high protein. In the tropics, [[leucaena-leucocephala]], gliricidia,
and moringa offer protein-rich browse that pigs readily consume.

### Root Crops

Root crops are the backbone of pig forage, offering dense caloric value. Jerusalem
artichoke is perhaps the single most important — it produces enormous tuber yields, is
perennial, and pigs will root them out themselves. Other key root forages include chicory,
mangel wurzel (fodder beet), sweet potato (tropics and subtropics), cassava/manioc
(tropics), yams, potatoes, and parsnips.

### Greens and Ground Covers

Leafy forage supplements the diet with protein, vitamins, and minerals. Comfrey is
outstanding — pigs eat both leaves and roots, and it regrows rapidly after grazing.
Kudzu, clover, alfalfa, and various brassicas (kale, cabbage, rape) provide excellent
grazing. Duck potato (Sagittaria) and water chestnut serve dual purposes in aquatic-edge
pig systems.

### Fruits

Fruit drops and windfalls form a significant seasonal feed source. Persimmon, fig,
pomegranate, loquat, apple, pear, and plum all contribute. In the tropics, guava,
papaya, and banana are extremely productive, though see the tropical considerations
section below regarding pen placement relative to these species.

## Stocking Densities and Land Requirements

Careful stocking rates prevent overgrazing while achieving the desired cultivation effect:
- **Plowing density**: 20 pigs per 4,000 sq m is recommended for effective soil
  cultivation and weed root-up.
- **Large-scale operations**: 100 pigs in 2-hectare pens can plow roughly 40 hectares
  over an 18-month rotation cycle.
- Stocking must be adjusted seasonally — higher during active plowing, reduced during
  recovery and planting phases.

## Pig Pen Layout and Design

The physical layout of pig pens determines the success of the integrated system.

- **Land allocation**: Allocate approximately 1 hectare per breeding sow when the system
  includes forage production within the pen area.
- **Number of pens**: 15 to 18 pens is considered ideal, enabling a rotation cycle that
  allows each pen sufficient recovery time between pig occupations.
- **Fencing**: Electric fencing is essential, particularly around newly planted tree crops
  within or adjacent to pens. A single hot wire at pig-nose height prevents ring-barking
  and root exposure of young trees.
- Pens should include shelter, wallows (in warmer climates), and access to fresh water.
- The **rubbing post** should be a central feature — a stout wooden post wrapped in
  Hessian (burlap) and soaked in non-drying oils mixed with neem oil. This serves both as
  a parasite control measure and as behavioral enrichment, reducing stress and aggression.

## Succession and Rotation Systems

The power of integrated pig management lies in the sequential use of animal labor to
prepare ground for planting, followed by grazing animals that maintain the system.

### The Basic Rotation

1. **Pigs plow**: Pigs root and cultivate the soil, consuming roots, rhizomes, and
   weeds. Their rooting action breaks compacted soil and incorporates organic matter.
2. **Sow crops**: After pigs are moved, the freshly cultivated ground is direct-seeded
   or planted with root crops, pasture mixes, or tree seedlings. Root crops are
   particularly effective — [[orchard-plant-guilds-and-integrated-pest-management|plant]] them as pigs are moved to the next pen so they
   establish before the pigs return.
3. **Cattle graze**: Cattle are introduced to graze the established pasture and
   crop residues, further fertilizing the ground with manure and maintaining vegetation.
4. **Pigs return**: After the pasture and root crops have matured (typically 1-3 years),
   pigs return to harvest root crops, cultivate again, and begin the cycle anew.

### Weed and Scrub Removal

Pigs are extraordinarily effective at eliminating invasive woody species. Gorse,
blackberry, thistle, and other tough perennials are systematically destroyed by rooting.
A season or two of pig occupation can clear land that would require years of mechanical
or chemical control, yielding fertile, well-cultivated ground ready for planting.

## Time to Develop Full Forage Systems

Establishing a complete pig-forage system takes **3 to 5 years** from initial planting.
Tree crops need time to mature, root crop patches must be established across the
rotation, and soil biology must recover from prior degradation. During the establishment
phase, [[savory-supplemental-feeding-and-winter-management]] is necessary, but costs decrease each year as the forage
system matures toward self-sufficiency.

## Pest and Parasite Control

[[diatomaceous-earth-and-natural-pest-management]] is preferable to chemical treatments in integrated systems:
- **Neem oil**: Applied as a spray or used in rubbing posts, neem effectively controls
  mites, lice, and other external parasites.
- **Pyrethrum**: Useful as a broad-spectrum insecticide for topical application or
  shelter treatment.
- **Diatomaceous earth**: Spread in sleeping areas and dust-bathing spots, it provides
  mechanical control of external parasites by damaging their exoskeletons.
- The combination of the oiled neem rubbing post, clean bedding rotation, and regular
  pen moves significantly reduces parasite loads without pharmaceuticals.

## Integration with Orchards

Pigs are highly beneficial in **mature orchards**, where they consume fallen fruit
(reducing disease carryover), control groundcover, and fertilize trees. However, pigs
must be excluded from orchards with young trees, as they will ring-bark and uproot
saplings. Once trees are beyond pig reach (typically 3-4 years), pigs can be rotated
through orchard alleys during [[azurescens-fruiting-seasons-and-conditions]] with excellent results.

## Tropical vs. Temperate Considerations

Tropical systems have specific constraints that differ markedly from temperate designs:

- **Banana and papaya must be planted outside pig pens** in the tropics — pigs will
  destroy these soft-stemmed species.
- In temperate systems, hardier tree forages can be planted within pens behind electric
  fencing, as pigs do less damage to mature woody stems.
- Tropical forage systems mature faster due to longer growing seasons, potentially
  reducing establishment from 5 years toward 3. Wallows are essential for thermoregulation
  in humid tropics; in temperate zones, deep-bedded straw shelters are more appropriate.
