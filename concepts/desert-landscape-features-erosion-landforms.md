# Desert Landscape Features and Erosion Landforms

Desert landscapes present a dramatically different set of landforms compared to humid areas. In drylands, erosion landforms are more significant, conspicuous, and numerous in type than in humid regions. Understanding these features is essential for any [[permaculture]] designer working in arid environments.

## Primary Desert Pavement Classifications

True desert has three broad primary landscape pavement classifications:

- **Erg**: Sandy desert, further divided into dune types and formations. Erg is composed of **sandplain** (near-level sand sheets over various substrates, also called sand fields) and dunes of several types.
- **Hamada**: Rock and boulder pavement.
- **Reg**: Gravel surfaces, generally areas from which sand and silt has been removed. Erg may lie over reg or hamada bases.

## Major Desert Landform Types

### Mountains and Valleys

Extensive deserts may show ranges of mountains with complex long valleys and shear-sided gorges draining these. These are among the most productive features in desert landscapes, providing catchment, shade, and microclimate variation.

### Inselbergs

Isolated or grouped granitic domes that rise steeply out of the desert plain. Great domes such as Uluru (Ayers Rock) in Australia and similar massifs of granitic or metamorphosed sandstones rarely develop deep caves but do have some rock shelter. They dip steeply into loose sandy soils around the base.

### Scarps and Wadis

Complex scarp and pediment landscapes of fault-lines, mesas, and wadis (box canyons) are characteristic features of the desert. These develop from periods of uplift or faults that fracture desert peneplains, creating long cliff-lines that may run many tens of kilometres.

### Badlands

Complex, eroded, softer sediments that develop on unconsolidated areas. Sharp-sided and much-branched gulches develop on steep slopes within badland formations.

### Basin and Range Topography

Series of folds produce basin and range topography over most desert borders near mountains. This is a characteristic feature of the Great Basin deserts of North America.

### Dune Systems

Dunes can take characteristic forms depending on wind intensity, duration, streamlines and velocity, and sand supply:
- **Transverse dunes**: Regular ridges perpendicular to prevailing wind
- **Oblique dunes**: Formed at an angle to wind direction
- **Longitudinal dunes**: Aligned with the wind
- **Barchans**: Crescent-shaped dunes with horns pointing downwind
- **Sand seas**: Wave-like forms with lobed advance edges

## Reading the Desert Landscape

Occupied and fire-managed arid lands present a total mosaic of vegetation with very different changes obvious to the traveller. To "read" such a landscape mosaic, a designer needs to note:

1. **Process**: Whether wind, water, or infiltration is active locally
2. **Rock and soil type**: These decide local response to process and produce characteristic landforms
3. **Aspect**: Even slight shading by hills changes opportunities and promotes growth
4. **Fire frequency**: And the time since the last fire
5. **Date of last heavy rain**: More than 12 mm may have triggered specific age-groups of plants (recruitment of species)

## Desert vs. Humid Landscapes

In total, desert landscapes are angular and actively eroding. Humid areas, especially those clothed with grasslands and forests, have softer and more rounded outlines with rare cliff faces appearing only at recent fault-lines or shorelines.

## Water Quality Considerations

Water in desert environments is particularly vulnerable to contamination. Deep bore or well waters must be rigorously tested for excessive mineral and radioactive pollutants. Water in particular must be subject to frequent analysis for pollution and salts, as poor management can put whole populations at risk.

## Design Implications

The lower slopes, basins, and playas of overgrazed and eroded drylands need attention, as do the dry river beds and aquifers that lie below the surface sediments. Each landform type presents unique opportunities for water harvesting, settlement, and productive land use. The angular, actively eroding nature of desert landscapes means that earthworks, structures, and plantings must be designed to work with rather than against these powerful natural processes.
