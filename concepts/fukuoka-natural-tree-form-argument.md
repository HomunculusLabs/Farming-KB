# Fukuoka Pruning Phyllotaxy Argument

Updated: 2026-04-19

Masanobu Fukuoka presents a detailed argument against the necessity of pruning fruit trees in *The Natural Way of Farming*, grounded in the botanical principle of phyllotaxy — the fixed mathematical patterns governing leaf and branch arrangement on plants.

This argument is one of the most technically precise in Fukuoka's body of work, using botanical science itself to challenge horticultural assumptions. It demonstrates that [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] was not anti-knowledge but rather pro-nature, using careful observation to reveal truths that human intervention obscures. See [[fukuoka-nature-as-teacher]] and [[fukuoka-observation-based-farming]].

## The Core Argument

Fukuoka asks: if pruning is discontinued on a fruit tree, the form becomes confused, branches entangle, and foliage grows dense. But this is the form of a tree that has been pruned haphazardly and then abandoned — not the form of a tree that has grown in its natural state from the start.

He elaborates: "Would it be reasonable to expect the tree to put out leaves and branches not touched by the sun? Would it seem normal for lower and inner branches to die back? For fruit to form only at the ends of branches? This is not the form that a natural tree takes, but one most commonly seen in trees that have been pruned haphazardly then abandoned."

This distinction is fundamental. Critics of no-pruning methods point to tangled, messy abandoned orchards as evidence that pruning is necessary, but they conflate two entirely different scenarios: a tree interfered with then abandoned, versus a tree never interfered with at all.

## Phyllotaxy: The Fixed Law of Plant Form

Fukuoka invokes phyllotaxy as evidence that natural tree forms are orderly and self-regulating:

- Leaves always grow either alternately or oppositely

- The direction and angle at which a leaf grows is always the same

- In peach, persimmon, mandarin orange, orange, and cherry trees, the sixth leaf is always located directly above the first, and the eleventh directly above the sixth

- If the distance between consecutive buds is one inch, the distance from one leaf to the next directly above it is always five inches

- Two leaves will not overlap within any five-inch length along the branch

Fukuoka provides even more precise botanical detail: "The direction and even the angle at which a leaf grows is always the same; never is there even the slightest deviation. If the angle between one leaf on a fruit tree branch and the next leaf is 72 degrees, then the next leaf and all the other leaves too will emerge at respective angles of 72 degrees."

The direction, angle, and divergence of a shoot or branch is regular and orderly. Never does one branch cross over another; lower and upper branches maintain the same distance over their entire length, never overlapping. This is why the branches and leaves of natural plants all receive equal ventilation and sunlight.

## Natural vs. Pruned Form

Fukuoka compares fruit trees to mountain pines and cedars:

- Natural forest tree trunks never branch or twist as long as not cut or harmed

- Branches on right and left sides do not run up against each other or cross

- No dense lower branches die back in natural trees

- Every leaf, shoot, and branch grows in an orderly and regular arrangement

He describes the mountain pine in vivid detail: "The central trunk rises straight and true, putting out branches at equal vertical spacings in a radial arrangement. One can clearly make out the chronology of branch emergence, the spacing and angle of the branches being also regular and orderly. Never does one branch grow too long or cross with another branch."

The same applies to bamboo, cryptomeria, Japanese cypress, camphor tree, camellia, Japanese maple, and all other trees — each observes the phyllotaxy and divergence specific for that species.

He argues: "Not a single wasted leaf, not a single branch lacking — that is the true form of a plant."

## Why Pruning Creates Problems

Pruning is necessary only because the tree has been shaped by human intervention for the convenience of the grower — to facilitate tillage, weeding, fertilization, spraying, and harvesting. Once the grower eliminates operations like weeding and tillage, pruning is no longer needed for anything other than fruit-picking.

Fukuoka describes the cascade of problems caused by pruning: "When a pine is transplanted into a garden and pruned with clippers, the arrangement of branches undergoes a dramatic change, taking on the contorted 'elegance' of a garden tree. This is because, once it has been pruned, the pine no longer sends out normal shoots and branches. Instead, branches grow irregularly, crisscrossing every which way, bending, twisting, and overlapping with each other."

By merely nipping the buds at the tips of a few shoots, conical citrus trees that had until then grown straight fork into a three-leader arrangement or assume a wineglass shape. Each intervention creates new problems that demand further intervention.

## Misconceptions About Natural Form

Fukuoka identifies a critical confusion: orchardists have never really tried growing fruit trees in their natural form. Most have never given thought to what the natural form is. The scientific literature contains no discussion of pruning based on phyllotaxy of citrus trees or the natural divergence angles that give each species its characteristic shape.

"Not a single book or report has been published which discusses pruning based on such basic factors as the phyllotaxy of a citrus tree, or which explains that a divergence of so much gives such-and-such a natural form with primary and secondary scaffold branch angles of X degrees."

Many have a vague idea of the natural form as something akin to the shape of a neglected tree. But there is a world of difference between the two. In a sense, the true natural form of a tree may be unknowable to man.

## The True Natural Form

A pine tree produces pine cones without any pruning. If a fruit tree is grown under natural conditions from the start, there should never be any need for pruning. The tangled, crowded, dying-back form that growers cite as evidence that pruning is necessary is actually the result of human interference followed by abandonment.

"Just as no one is so foolish as to strike his left hand with his right, no persimmon or chestnut tree has branches on the right that compete with those on the left and eventually have to be cut back because they grow too long."

A branch on the east side of the tree does not wander over to the south side, cutting off light. And what tree grows inner branches only to have them die off because they receive no light?

## Connection to Natural Farming Principles

This argument connects directly to Fukuoka's broader critique in [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] and [[fukuoka-five-principles-natural-farming]] — specifically the principle of no pruning. It also connects to the deeper philosophical point in [[fukuoka-philosophy-of-mu-and-nature]] that nature is self-organizing and self-regulating when left to its own devices.

If trees can grow in their natural form, the energy savings from eliminating pruning operations are significant. Fukuoka's approach requires starting with trees grown from seed in their natural location rather than transplanted grafted nursery stock. See also [[fukuoka-nature-as-teacher]].

## Practical Implications

The practical implication is that orchardists should study the phyllotaxy of their tree species and learn to recognize the natural divergence angles. Rather than imposing a shape through pruning, the farmer should observe what shape the tree naturally assumes and design the orchard accordingly. This may require different spacing, different planting patterns, and acceptance of trees that look different from conventional orchard trees.
