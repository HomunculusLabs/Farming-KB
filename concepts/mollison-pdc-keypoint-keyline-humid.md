Bill Mollison's detailed treatment of keypoint and keyline systems for water storage and landscape hydration in humid landscapes (rainfall >30 inches). From the 1981 [[dam-construction]] is possible
- The deepest dam on the property — gathers all water from the hilltop to that point
- Can be made as large as affordable
- Water exits via siphon or lock-pipe into a contour drain

### Irrigation with Flags

- A simple canvas/plastic flag on a chain controls water flow in the drain
- Peg the flag down to hold back flow; when the drain fills, pull the flag and water sheets across the hillside
- "One person can water hundreds of acres this way with no effort at all"
- For fire control, walk backwards with the flag to douse the hillside

## Saddle Dams (Ridge Storage)

### Location

The little saddles in the ridges mark points of weakness in the landscape — where rivers start coming down on both sides of the ridge. These saddles have enormous catchments above them.

### Construction

- Build walls on either side (or one side) of the saddle
- Create broad semi-circular bowls
- Sheet water runs across the saddle

### Advantages

- **Highest water storages** possible on any property
- **Perched 400+ feet** above gardens — can run sprinklers, turbines, mechanical power
- **Fill first** during rain — buffer erosion by taking the first shock of water
- **Moderate flow** — continue feeding the water table as it dries out
- **Wildlife habitat** — seed-eating birds (grouse, quail) need water daily within 24 hours
- **Fire protection** — 1,200 gallons up high with sprinklers covers 2 acres
- **Hydroelectric potential** — 400 feet of fall is maximum for thick-walled plastic pipe

## Contour Dams

- Choose the least sloping site (flattish area up high)
- Build an earth wall; run diversion drains as usual
- Shallow dam with large surface area — very cheap for the amount of water stored
- Effects: increased water table in surrounding area, better absorption, more time on landscape
- Overflow via spillway contoured away from dam, graded to taper to nothing; plant spilldown area with shrubs

## The Thermal Belt

### How Frost Behaves

"Frost is not water; frost is treacle." It moves over tree tops like fog, pushing warm air downward. The air at the bottom of the forest is several degrees warmer than air entering at the top.

### The Thermal Belt Location

Between the frost above the keypoint and the valley floor below lies the **thermal belt**:

- First buds of spring break here
- Last area of autumn productivity
- Several degrees warmer than ridgetop or valley floor
- With evergreen forest above the keyline, even in snow, a warm downdraft occurs in the thermal belt

### House Siting

"Within this thermal belt, just below the keypoint, is where we site our clients."

**Why not the ridgetop (Berchtesgarten syndrome):**
- Ridgetops are frosty and cold
- Fire sweeps with fantastic rapidity uphill — two fronts hit from both sides simultaneously
- No water above (thirst)
- No thermal advantages

## Soil as the Greatest Reservoir

"The greatest reservoir that we will have on the farm is the soil. It is the billion-gallon reservoir."

### Wallace Soil Conditioner

The Wallace soil conditioner is a farmer's machine for storing water in soil:

- A sharp disc cuts the soil surface
- Followed by a shank with a shoe at the base
- Creates thousands of grooves running faintly across slope (parallel to keyline)
- Penetrates 9 inches deep without turning soil
- Only a couple teaspoons of earth emerge per slit

### Results

- Water initially follows the million little drains
- Heavy rain fills them to capacity; water overflows to charge grooves below
- Water is very quickly absorbed — massive increase in absorption surface
- Soils hold water to 9+ inches depth
- Interstitial water moves out at about 1 inch per hour, or 10-20 feet per day
- Once conditioned, soil never needs re-treatment unless heavily restocked with cattle

### What the Soil Conditioner Is NOT

- Not a subsoiler (which is a cultivation tool that brings enormous earth to the surface)
- Not a chisel plow
- Designed specifically for water storage within the soil

### The Bunyip Level

A simple surveying tool for establishing keylines:

- 80 feet of half-inch hose
- Clear, stiff plastic uprights at each end, rigidly fixed to stakes
- Fill hose with water; bring stakes together and mark the level
- Walk around the hill, raising or lowering stake until water reaches level
- Mark with a stake — for 1:2,000 contour drop, bring down proportionally to distance walked
- "Two kids can run keylines all over the landscape in half an hour"
