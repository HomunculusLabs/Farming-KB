## Livestock Energy Conversion Ratios

Fukuoka provided striking data on the thermodynamic inefficiency of modern livestock production, expressed as the ratio of edible energy output to feed energy input:

- **Broilers**: 50% energy conversion efficiency
- **Pork**: 20% efficiency
- **Milk**: 15% efficiency
- **Beef**: 8% efficiency

Raising beef cattle cuts recoverable food energy from land tenfold. People who eat beef consume ten times as much energy as people on a rice-based diet. These figures represent gross efficiency; when accounting for the energy costs of feed production, transport, processing, and refrigeration, net efficiency drops further.

Genetically improved White Leghorns raised in cages lay one large egg daily but require feed grain having twice the caloric value of the eggs produced. Such artificial methods are fundamentally counterproductive — instead of increasing calories, they actually cut the number of calories in half. Restoration of wastes to the land is not easy, and soil fertility is depleted to the extent of the caloric loss.

## Mechanization: Apparent Gains, Real Losses

Fukuoka argued that mechanization creates a deceptive illusion of increased productivity. A 2-acre farmer who purchases a 30-horsepower tractor does not automatically become more productive — the land under cultivation is still the same size, and the tractor's fuel, maintenance, and depreciation costs often exceed the value of any labor savings. The farmer is driven to seek off-farm income to pay for equipment, effectively exchanging agricultural work for factory labor.

Distribution costs compound the energy inefficiency. In Japan, food distribution costs are five times those in the United States and twice those in West Germany. The retail price of farm produce reflects not the cost of production but the infrastructure built to support mechanized, petroleum-dependent agriculture.

## The Minimum Human Calorie Requirement

Fukuoka's young apprentices in the citrus orchard demonstrated that a person's minimum daily calorie requirement is approximately 1,000 calories for a "hermit's diet" of brown rice with sesame seeds and salt, and 1,500 calories on a diet of brown rice and vegetables. This is sufficient to perform a farmer's work — equivalent to about one-tenth of a horsepower. By contrast, the fossil fuel energy consumed per capita in industrialized societies dwarfs this biological minimum by orders of magnitude.
