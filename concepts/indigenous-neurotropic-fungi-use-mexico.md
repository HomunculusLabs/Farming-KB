# Indigenous Use of [[richard-evans-schultes]] in 1939, who reported that the neurotropic
fungi in Mexico belonged to *[[psilocybe-zapotecorum]]*.

- **Apipiltzin** ("kid or little boy"): An indigenous name.

- **Atkad** ("mayor or leader"): An indigenous name.

- **Di nizé taaya** ("fungus of the genius"): An indigenous name.

- **Shi thó** ("that eruptions thing"): An indigenous name.

- **Teotlaquilnanácatl** ("divine fungus"): An indigenous name.

## Indigenous Groups and Species

The most well-documented ethnic groups using neurotropic fungi reside in
the subtropical humid forests of Mexico. Key groups include:

**Mazatec** (Oaxaca): Perhaps the most famous practitioners. María
Sabina, a Mazatec curandera, became internationally known after Wasson's
participation in a velada (night ceremony) in 1955. The Mazatec use
*[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]]*, *P. caerulescens*, and other species in healing
ceremonies. They sometimes confuse *[[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]]* and
*L. marginatum* (now classified as *Vascellum qudenii* and *L.
candidum*) as narcotic fungi. Ott et al. (1975) later found these to
be common edible puffballs with no neurotropic properties, suggesting the
Mixtec confused them with traditional psilocybin-containing species. The
Mixtec also used at least six additional "sacred" puffball species
interchangeably, along with the poisonous *Scleroderma verrucosum*.

**Chinantec** (Oaxaca): Use *[[dictyophora-indusiata]]* (with its three
forms) as special narcotic fungi for divination purposes. These fungi
are unusual in that they belong to the Phallales (stinkhorns), not the
typical agarics associated with psilocybin use.

**Trique** (Alta Mixteca, Oaxaca): Use species of *Cordyceps*
(including *C. capitata* and *C. ophioglossoides*) in special nocturnal
ceremonies. These are eaten either with *Psilocybe muliercula* or alone.
A specimen of *Elaphomyces* (the host fungus of these *Cordyceps*) is
placed at the center of the ceremony room as a "director." The Trique
also use *Elaphomyces* species to help heal wounds and "rejuvenate the
organism."

**Nahua and other groups**: *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* is widely used across
multiple indigenous communities in central and southern Mexico.

## The Church of Ganoderma

A remarkable example of fungal veneration was documented in Chignahuapan,
Puebla, where a church was built in honor of *[[guzman-ganoderma-lobatum-church-cult-chignahuapan-puebla]]*. The
Indians of the region regard the fungus as a saint. The fungus features an
arresting sketch on its inner surface portraying Christ with a sun and moon
on each side. While *Ganoderma lobatum* has no known neurotropic properties,
its cult probably relates to the use of neurotropic *Psilocybe* species
commonly found in the region.

## Ceremonial Practices

Indigenous [[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]] (veladas) are nocturnal rituals typically
led by a curandero or curandera (healer). The ceremonies involve:

- Consumption of fresh or semi-dried fungi in a darkened room
- Chanting and singing of icaros (magical curing songs)
- Guidance of participants through the visionary experience
- Diagnostic and therapeutic applications for physical and spiritual
  ailments

Guzmán personally experienced passive hallucination from exposure to
large quantities of fresh neurotropic *Psilocybe* specimens stored in a
closed room, suggesting that psilocybin and psilocin may be volatile
compounds capable of producing effects through inhalation.

## Post-Contact Recreational Degeneration

Guzmán identifies five historical periods in the relationship between
humans and neurotropic fungi. The fourth period, beginning in the 1960s,
involved the utilization of the fungi as recreational drugs and a
degeneration of traditional indigenous use. This led many governments to
prohibit the use and commerce of these fungi, driving an underground
market and illegal trade. The tropical species *Psilocybe cubensis* is
frequently reported in Europe, but almost exclusively as illegally
cultivated or dried specimens imported from tropical countries.
