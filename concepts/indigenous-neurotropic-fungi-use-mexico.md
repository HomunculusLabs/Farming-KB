## Indigenous Groups and Species

The most well-documented ethnic groups using neurotropic fungi reside in
the subtropical humid forests of Mexico. Key groups include:

**Mazatec** (Oaxaca): Perhaps the most famous practitioners. María
Sabina, a Mazatec curandera, became internationally known after Wasson's
participation in a velada (night ceremony) in 1955. The Mazatec use
*[[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]]* and
*L. marginatum* (now classified as *Vascellum qudenii* and *L.
candidum*) as narcotic fungi. Ott et al. (1975) later found these to
be common edible puffballs with no neurotropic properties, suggesting the
Mixtec confused them with traditional psilocybin-containing species. The
Mixtec also used at least six additional "sacred" puffball species
interchangeably, along with the poisonous *Scleroderma verrucosum*.

**Chinantec** (Oaxaca): Use *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* is widely used across
multiple indigenous communities in central and southern Mexico.

## Post-Contact Recreational Degeneration

Guzmán identifies five historical periods in the relationship between
humans and neurotropic fungi. The fourth period, beginning in the 1960s,
involved the utilization of the fungi as recreational drugs and a
degeneration of traditional indigenous use. This led many governments to
prohibit the use and commerce of these fungi, driving an underground
market and illegal trade. The tropical species *Psilocybe cubensis* is
frequently reported in Europe, but almost exclusively as illegally
cultivated or dried specimens imported from tropical countries.
