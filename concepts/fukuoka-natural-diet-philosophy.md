## Fukuoka's Natural Diet and the Philosophy of Food

Masanobu Fukuoka extended his [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]]—both reflected the same fundamental error of imposing human intellect over natural wisdom.

## The Four Types of Food

Fukuoka classified food into four categories based on its relationship to human health and the natural environment:

### 1. Complete Natural Food

The most ideal food is gathered from the wild in its natural state. Fungi, wild vegetables, and natural cereals eaten raw or prepared simply, without the addition of oil, salt, or seasoning, constitute the highest category. A person subsisting on this diet acquires a strong constitution and high resistance to disease. This is the diet of the original human being.

### 2. Partial Natural Food

This includes food grown by natural farming, such as rice, barley, vegetables, and fruits produced without chemical inputs. While slightly less ideal than wild foods, these retain their natural vitality and nutritional completeness. Fukuoka's own farm produced this category of food.

### 3. Processed Natural Food

Foods of natural origin that have been altered through processing—such as brown rice polished into white rice, or raw sugar refined into white sugar—fall into this category. The processing removes essential nutrients and disrupts the natural balance. White rice requires supplementary vitamin B1 precisely because the polishing has removed it.

### 4. Artificial Food

Synthetic foods, heavily processed products, and items made from petrochemical feedstocks represent the lowest category. These bear no relationship to natural food and provide only fragmented nutrition—calories without wholeness. Fukuoka saw the proliferation of artificial foods as both a symptom and a cause of the modern health crisis.

## The Wholeness Principle

Central to Fukuoka's food philosophy is the principle that **food is whole or it is not food at all**. Scientific nutrition breaks food down into its components—proteins, fats, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals—then attempts to reassemble health from these fragments. This approach, Fukuoka argued, is as misguided in nutrition as it is in agriculture.

Just as a living ecosystem cannot be reconstructed from its dissected parts, the health-giving properties of natural food cannot be reconstructed from isolated nutrients. The relationship between the whole and its parts is one-directional: the whole contains the parts, but the parts do not contain the whole. When scientists identify a "nutrient" in a natural food and then synthesize it industrially, they have isolated a fragment that functions differently outside its natural matrix.

## Rice: The Case Study

Fukuoka used rice as his primary example of how processing destroys food wholeness:

- **Brown rice** is a complete food containing all the nutrients needed for human health. It can serve as the staple of a healthy diet.
- **White rice** is nutritionally incomplete—polishing removes the bran and germ, which contain fiber, B vitamins, minerals, and essential fatty acids. The polished grain becomes "fragile, disease-prone, and delicate," much like the rice plants produced by [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] produce weak rice plants that need pesticides, polished rice produces weak humans who need vitamin supplements.

He noted that eating brown rice was the path to good health, but that even this benefit was secondary: the more important question was whether one ate food with gratitude, recognizing the entire chain of natural processes that produced it.

## Critique of Modern Food Systems

Fukuoka traced the modern food crisis to the same logic that created agricultural problems:

1. **Fragmentation**: Science analyzes food into nutrients, losing sight of the whole
2. **Substitution**: Processed foods replace whole foods in the name of convenience
3. **Dependency**: Nutritional deficiencies created by processing are treated with supplements rather than by returning to whole foods
4. **Economic distortion**: The price of food reflects distribution costs and industrial profit, not nutritional value

He observed that in 1979, the retail price of rice in Japan was calculated to support manufacturers of agricultural equipment—not the cost of growing food naturally. Distribution costs in Japan were five times those in the United States. The consumer, believing they were demanding "cheap, tasty rice," was actually eating "weak rice, polluting rice grown with lots of pesticides."

## The Sensory Connection

Fukuoka emphasized that humans once possessed the ability to sense which foods were beneficial and which harmful through their natural instincts. When people ate only natural foods, their bodies developed an intuitive discrimination. Modern processed foods, with their artificial flavors and refined textures, have overridden this natural sensory wisdom.

A person who eats natural food gradually recovers their innate ability to distinguish good from bad—not through intellectual knowledge of nutrition, but through direct bodily sensation. The tongue knows what the mind has forgotten. This recovery of instinct was, for Fukuoka, one of the most important but overlooked benefits of returning to a natural diet. It represented not merely a dietary change but a restoration of the fundamental relationship between human beings and the natural world.

## Food and Spiritual Life

For Fukuoka, food was inseparable from spiritual practice. Eating was an act of communion with nature—a recognition that one's body is built from the soil, water, air, and sunlight that produced the food. When food is produced naturally and eaten with gratitude, it nourishes not only the body but the spirit.

He contrasted this with the modern attitude toward food as mere "nutrition"—a commodity to be analyzed, optimized, and consumed efficiently. When the consumer can no longer distinguish between "food as the staff of life and food as merely nutrition," the entire relationship between humanity and nature has been corrupted.
