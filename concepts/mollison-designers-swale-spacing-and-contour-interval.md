## Swale Fundamentals

A swale is a level ditch excavated on contour (the horizontal line across
a slope at constant elevation) with the excavated material piled on the
downhill side to form a berm. Swales intercept surface water runoff, slow
its flow, and allow it to infiltrate into the soil profile rather than
running off the landscape.

The swale-berm combination creates an underground water lens that persists
for weeks or months after rainfall, providing moisture to tree crops and
other plantings on and below the berm.

## Contour Interval

The contour interval is the vertical distance between adjacent swales,
which determines how much of the landscape each swale can effectively
hydrate. Mollison provides the following guidelines:

- Gentle slopes (1-3%): contour interval of 1.5-3 metres between swales
- Moderate slopes (3-8%): contour interval of 1-2 metres
- Steep slopes (8-15%): contour interval of 0.5-1 metre
- Very steep slopes (above 15%): swales are not recommended; use terraces
  or [[mollison-designers-keyline-pattern-plow-technique]] to break
  hardpan and improve infiltration

## Water Holding Calculations

The water holding capacity of a swale system can be estimated:

  Volume per metre of swale = (width x depth) / 2  (triangular cross-section)

For a swale 2m wide and 0.5m deep:
  Volume = (2 x 0.5) / 2 = 0.5 cubic metres per metre length

Each cubic metre holds 1000 litres, so each metre of this swale holds
500 litres. On a 10% slope with 10m horizontal spacing, each metre of
swale drains a 10m x 1m = 10 square metre catchment. A 50mm rainfall
event on this catchment produces 500 litres, exactly matching swale
capacity.

This calculation must include a safety factor of 1.5 to 2x for extreme
events, plus provision for overflow spillways on non-erodible surfaces.

## Design Process

Mollison's recommended design sequence:

1. Survey the site to determine slope, aspect, existing drainage, and
   soil types across the landscape
2. Mark contour lines using an A-frame level, water tube level, or laser
   level; mark contours at the calculated vertical interval
3. Adjust contour positions to avoid obstacles (rocks, existing trees,
   buildings) and to connect logically across the landscape
4. Stake the swale line and excavate by hand or machine; pile spoil on
   the downhill side to form the berm
5. Compact the berm and immediately plant with hardy pioneer species
6. Install overflow spillways at the lowest point of each swale to direct
   excess water safely to the next swale below
7. Plant the berm with productive tree crops and the inter-swale zone
   with appropriate understory species

## Common Mistakes

- Spacing swales too far apart, leaving dry zones between them
- Spacing swales too close together, wasting effort and creating
  saturated conditions
- Failing to provide overflow spillways, leading to berm breach and
  erosion during heavy rain
- Not planting the berm immediately, allowing erosion before vegetation
  establishes
- Building swales on slopes above 15% without additional erosion control
