# Mycofiltration: Fungal Water Filtration

## Overview

Mycofiltration is the practice of using fungal mycelium to filter contaminated water, removing pathogens, silt, heavy metals, and chemical pollutants. Pioneered by mycologist Paul Stamets and detailed in his seminal work *Mycelium Running* (2005), mycofiltration harnesses the extraordinary biological capabilities of fungal networks to perform water purification that rivals or exceeds conventional mechanical and chemical treatment methods.

The technique represents a convergence of mycology, ecology, and ecological engineering, offering low-cost, sustainable alternatives for water treatment across agricultural, residential, and industrial contexts. Unlike conventional filtration systems that require energy-intensive operation and chemical inputs, mycofiltration leverages the self-renewing biological activity of living organisms to achieve continuous water treatment with minimal external inputs.

## How Mycelial Mats Filter Water

### Physical Filtration

Mycelium forms dense, interwoven networks of hyphae — thread-like cellular structures that create a matrix capable of trapping particulate matter. As water percolates through a mycelial mat, suspended solids including silt, clay, and organic debris are physically captured within the hyphal network. The sheer density of hyphal filaments in a mature mycelial mat is staggering: a single cubic inch of substrate may contain miles of hyphal tissue, creating an extraordinarily fine filter with immense surface area.

The three-dimensional structure means that water follows tortuous paths through the substrate, increasing contact time and filtration efficiency. This physical filtration capacity is analogous to a biological sand filter but with far greater surface area and the added benefit of ongoing enzymatic activity. As the mycelial mat matures and thickens, its physical filtration capacity increases, making older, well-established mats more effective than newly installed ones.

### Enzymatic Degradation

Beyond physical straining, mycelium secretes a remarkable array of extracellular enzymes that break down complex organic molecules. These enzymes — including lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, laccases, and various cellulases — are capable of decomposing lignin, cellulose, hydrocarbons, and even some synthetic chemicals. White-rot fungi, in particular, produce enzymes that can degrade a wide spectrum of environmental pollutants, including [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), certain pesticides, and [[mycoremediation-petroleum-hydrocarbons]].

This enzymatic capacity means mycofiltration does not merely trap contaminants but actively transforms them into less harmful compounds, often breaking them down into simpler molecules that can be assimilated by the fungal organism or by other members of the soil food web.

### Antibiotic and Antimicrobial Exudation

One of the most significant discoveries in mycofiltration is the ability of certain fungi to secrete antibacterial and antiviral compounds directly into the water passing through their mycelial networks. Stamets' research demonstrated that mycelia of species such as *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) produce antibiotics that kill *Escherichia coli*, *Salmonella*, and other pathogenic bacteria.

The mycelium effectively creates a biological kill zone, reducing bacterial concentrations by several orders of magnitude as water moves through the substrate. This antimicrobial exudation is continuous and self-renewing, unlike chemical disinfectants such as chlorine that must be repeatedly applied and that can create toxic byproducts. The specificity of fungal antimicrobials is also noteworthy: they tend to target pathogenic organisms while sparing beneficial bacteria.

### Bioaccumulation and Competitive Exclusion

Mycelium can bioaccumulate certain contaminants, particularly heavy metals, binding them within the fungal tissue and removing them from the water column. Additionally, the dense mycelial network creates a competitive environment that excludes pathogenic microorganisms. By colonizing the substrate completely, beneficial fungi deny pathogens the space and resources they need to establish themselves, a process known as competitive exclusion.

## Construction of Mycofiltration Barriers

### Materials and Methods

Mycofiltration barriers are constructed using simple, accessible materials. The basic design involves inoculating a bulk substrate — typically straw, wood chips, or sawdust — with fungal mycelium and containing this inoculated substrate within a permeable structure. Burlap sacks filled with mycelium-inoculated straw are the most commonly described barrier type in Stamets' work.

These "mycobags" are placed in the path of water flow — across drainage ditches, at the base of slopes, or along the edges of agricultural fields. The burlap allows water to pass through while containing the substrate, and it eventually decomposes, becoming part of the organic matrix. The construction process follows a straightforward sequence:

1. **Substrate preparation**: Straw or wood chips are pasteurized to reduce competing organisms.
2. **Inoculation**: The cooled substrate is thoroughly mixed with fungal spawn.
3. **Colonization**: The inoculated substrate is allowed to colonize fully, typically 2-4 weeks.
4. **Deployment**: Fully colonized substrate is placed in burlap sacks and positioned at the target site.
5. **Maintenance**: Barriers are monitored and refreshed periodically as the mycelium depletes its food source.

### Sizing and Placement Considerations

Barrier dimensions depend on the volume and flow rate of water being treated. A typical drainage ditch barrier might consist of a row of mycobags spanning the full width of the channel, stacked one or two bags high. Placement is critical: barriers must be positioned where they intercept flowing water without causing unwanted pooling or diversion of flow upstream. The substrate depth should ensure adequate contact time — generally a minimum of 6-12 inches of fully colonized material.

### Mycelial Blankets and Pond Integration

Beyond burlap sack barriers, mycofiltration can be implemented as mycelial blankets — large mats of inoculated substrate spread across the ground surface to filter overland flow. These are particularly useful for construction sites and mining operations where large areas of exposed soil generate contaminated runoff. In pond and wetland settings, mycelium-inoculated substrates can be placed along inflow channels or integrated into [[comparison-constructed-wetland-vs-backyard-pond]] designs, providing biological filtration as part of a multi-stage treatment system.

## Applications

### Agricultural Runoff and Erosion Control

Agricultural runoff carries sediment, excess nutrients, pesticides, herbicides, and animal waste pathogens. Mycofiltration barriers placed at field edges and along drainage ways can significantly reduce all of these pollutant categories simultaneously. The mycelium captures silt physically, enzymatically breaks down agricultural chemicals, and its antimicrobial exudates kill *E. coli* and other pathogens.

On steep terrain, mycofiltration barriers serve a dual purpose, stabilizing soil and filtering water in a single installation. Stamets documented dramatic reductions in coliform bacteria and turbidity in water passing through experimental mycofiltration installations on his property in Washington State.

### Graywater and Stormwater Systems

Mycofiltration can be integrated into graywater treatment systems for residential or community-scale applications. Water from sinks, showers, and laundry can be directed through mycofiltration beds before being released into the environment or used for subsurface irrigation.

Stormwater runoff from parking lots and roads — often contaminated with petroleum hydrocarbons and heavy metals — can be treated through mycofiltration installations in bioswales, rain gardens, and detention basins. The integration of mycofiltration into green infrastructure represents one of its most promising large-scale applications, particularly as urban areas seek nature-based solutions for stormwater management.

### Pathogen Filtration

The antimicrobial properties of mycofiltration are particularly relevant for applications involving *E. coli*, coliform bacteria, and protozoan pathogens such as *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*. These organisms are common contaminants in agricultural runoff and surface water supplies, and conventional treatment methods (particularly chlorination) are not always effective against protozoan cysts. Mycofiltration offers a biological alternative that can reduce pathogen loads without creating harmful disinfection byproducts.

### Old-Growth Forests as Natural Filtration Models

Old-growth forests demonstrate the capacity of fungal networks to maintain water quality at a landscape scale. The forest floor is underlain by vast mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungal networks that filter water as it percolates through soil horizons. Streams emerging from old-growth forests are notably cleaner than those from younger or managed forests, a difference attributable in significant part to the maturity and complexity of the fungal networks.

These natural systems serve as models for engineered mycofiltration, illustrating the scale and longevity of fungal water purification when given the conditions to fully develop over decades and centuries. The loss of old-growth forests therefore represents not just a loss of biodiversity and carbon storage but also a loss of natural water treatment infrastructure — a service that is enormously expensive to replicate with engineered systems.

### Erosion Control and Slope Stabilization

Mycofiltration barriers serve a dual purpose in erosion-prone areas. The mycelial network binds soil particles together through physical entanglement and the production of glomalin-related soil proteins, which act as biological glues. On steep slopes, road cuts, and construction sites, mycofiltration installations can reduce sediment loss by 50-90 percent while simultaneously filtering contaminants from the runoff that does occur. This dual function makes mycofiltration particularly cost-effective for land management applications where both erosion control and water quality protection are needed.

## Limitations and Considerations

Mycofiltration is not a universal solution. Its effectiveness depends on water temperature, flow rates, the specific contaminants present, and the health and species of the fungal inoculant. Key limitations include:

- **Flow rate**: Very rapid water flow can overwhelm filtration capacity, reducing contact time
- **Maintenance**: Barriers require periodic replenishment as mycelium consumes its substrate
- **Species selection**: No single species is optimal for all contaminants; matching species to target pollutants is essential
- **Seasonal variation**: Cold temperatures reduce fungal [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] and filtration efficiency
- **Nutrient loading**: Excessive nutrient inputs can favor bacterial overgrowth that competes with mycelium
- **Longevity**: Mycofiltration barriers have finite effective lifespans, typically requiring renewal every 1-3 years
- **Heavy metals**: While mycelium can capture dissolved heavy metals, it does not destroy them — accumulated metals require proper disposal

Extreme pH, high salinity, or very rapid water flow can overwhelm mycofiltration barriers. While mycelium can degrade many organic pollutants, some inorganic contaminants such as dissolved heavy metals are captured but not destroyed, eventually accumulating in the fungal biomass and requiring proper disposal.

## Future Directions

Research into mycofiltration is expanding rapidly, with growing interest from municipalities, watershed management agencies, and the agricultural industry. Emerging areas of investigation include the use of genomics and selective breeding to develop optimized fungal strains for specific filtration applications, the integration of mycofiltration with [[constructed-wetlands-wastewater-treatment]] bioretention systems, and the development of standardized monitoring protocols to quantify filtration performance under field conditions. As [[medicinal-mushroom-cancer-regulatory-frameworks]] for nature-based water treatment mature, mycofiltration is likely to move from experimental practice to mainstream implementation.
