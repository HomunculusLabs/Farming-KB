# Mycofiltration: Fungal Water Filtration

## Overview

Mycofiltration is the practice of using fungal mycelium to filter contaminated water, removing pathogens, silt, heavy metals, and chemical pollutants. Pioneered by mycologist Paul Stamets and detailed in his seminal work *Mycelium Running* (2005), mycofiltration harnesses the extraordinary biological capabilities of fungal networks to perform water purification that rivals or exceeds conventional mechanical and chemical treatment methods.

The technique represents a convergence of mycology, ecology, and ecological engineering, offering low-cost, sustainable alternatives for water treatment across agricultural, residential, and industrial contexts. Unlike conventional filtration systems that require energy-intensive operation and chemical inputs, mycofiltration leverages the self-renewing biological activity of living organisms to achieve continuous water treatment with minimal external inputs.

## How Mycelial Mats Filter Water

### Physical Filtration

Mycelium forms dense, interwoven networks of hyphae — thread-like cellular structures that create a matrix capable of trapping particulate matter. As water percolates through a mycelial mat, suspended solids including silt, clay, and organic debris are physically captured within the hyphal network. The sheer density of hyphal filaments in a mature mycelial mat is staggering: a single cubic inch of substrate may contain miles of hyphal tissue, creating an extraordinarily fine filter with immense surface area.

The three-dimensional structure means that water follows tortuous paths through the substrate, increasing contact time and filtration efficiency. This physical filtration capacity is analogous to a biological sand filter but with far greater surface area and the added benefit of ongoing enzymatic activity. As the mycelial mat matures and thickens, its physical filtration capacity increases, making older, well-established mats more effective than newly installed ones.

### Enzymatic Degradation

Beyond physical straining, mycelium secretes a remarkable array of [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), certain pesticides, and [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]* (oyster mushroom) produce antibiotics that kill *Escherichia coli*, *Salmonella*, and other pathogenic bacteria.

The mycelium effectively creates a biological kill zone, reducing bacterial concentrations by several orders of magnitude as water moves through the substrate. This antimicrobial exudation is continuous and self-renewing, unlike chemical disinfectants such as chlorine that must be repeatedly applied and that can create toxic byproducts. The specificity of fungal antimicrobials is also noteworthy: they tend to target pathogenic organisms while sparing beneficial bacteria.

### Bioaccumulation and Competitive Exclusion

Mycelium can bioaccumulate certain contaminants, particularly heavy metals, binding them within the fungal tissue and removing them from the water column. Additionally, the dense mycelial network creates a competitive environment that excludes pathogenic microorganisms. By colonizing the substrate completely, beneficial fungi deny pathogens the space and resources they need to establish themselves, a process known as competitive exclusion.

## Applications

### Agricultural Runoff and Erosion Control

Agricultural runoff carries sediment, excess nutrients, pesticides, herbicides, and animal waste pathogens. Mycofiltration barriers placed at field edges and along drainage ways can significantly reduce all of these pollutant categories simultaneously. The mycelium captures silt physically, enzymatically breaks down agricultural chemicals, and its antimicrobial exudates kill *E. coli* and other pathogens.

On steep terrain, mycofiltration barriers serve a dual purpose, stabilizing soil and filtering water in a single installation. Stamets documented dramatic reductions in coliform bacteria and turbidity in water passing through experimental mycofiltration installations on his property in Washington State.

### Graywater and Stormwater Systems

Mycofiltration can be integrated into graywater treatment systems for residential or community-scale applications. Water from sinks, showers, and laundry can be directed through mycofiltration beds before being released into the environment or used for subsurface irrigation.

Stormwater runoff from parking lots and roads — often contaminated with petroleum hydrocarbons and heavy metals — can be treated through mycofiltration installations in bioswales, rain gardens, and detention basins. The integration of mycofiltration into green infrastructure represents one of its most promising large-scale applications, particularly as urban areas seek nature-based solutions for stormwater management.

### Pathogen Filtration

The [[medicinal-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties]] of mycofiltration are particularly relevant for applications involving *E. coli*, coliform bacteria, and protozoan pathogens such as *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*. These organisms are common contaminants in agricultural runoff and surface water supplies, and conventional treatment methods (particularly chlorination) are not always effective against protozoan cysts. Mycofiltration offers a biological alternative that can reduce pathogen loads without creating harmful disinfection byproducts.

### Old-Growth Forests as Natural Filtration Models

Old-growth forests demonstrate the capacity of fungal networks to maintain water quality at a landscape scale. The forest floor is underlain by vast mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungal networks that filter water as it percolates through soil horizons. Streams emerging from old-growth forests are notably cleaner than those from younger or managed forests, a difference attributable in significant part to the maturity and complexity of the fungal networks.

## Mycofiltration System Design Considerations
Designing an effective mycofiltration system requires attention to several
key parameters. Flow rate must be matched to the biological activity rate
of the fungal species — too fast and contaminants pass through untreated;
too slow and the system becomes anaerobic. Substrate depth, porosity, and
surface area all affect contact time and filtration efficiency. Stamets
recommends a minimum contact time of 2-3 minutes for basic particulate
and biological filtration, with longer retention times needed for chemical
pollutant removal.

## Species Selection for Specific Contaminants
Different fungal species exhibit different filtration capabilities:
- *Pleurotus ostreatus*: Excellent for bacterial and particulate removal;
  moderate chemical pollutant degradation
- *Stropharia rugosoannulata*: Particularly effective for *E. coli* and
  coliform bacteria; tolerant of varying temperatures
- *Ganoderma lucidum*: Strong enzymatic activity for chemical pollutant
  degradation; produces antimicrobial compounds
- *Trametes versicolor*: Powerful lignin-degrading enzymes effective
  against PAHs, dyes, and pharmaceutical contaminants

## Maintenance and Longevity
Mycofiltration systems require periodic maintenance to remain effective.
As the mycelium consumes available nutrients and the substrate compacts,
filtration capacity gradually declines. Systems typically remain effective
for 6-18 months depending on contaminant load, flow volume, and species.
After the active lifespan, spent mycelial substrate can be composted or
incorporated into soil as an organic amendment.
