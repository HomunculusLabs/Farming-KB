## Cool Temperate System Establishment

Establishing productive permaculture systems in cool temperate climates requires strategic use of pioneer species, nurse crops, and creative microclimate management. Cool temperate regions present challenges including frost, short growing seasons, strong winds, and slow soil development, but they also offer unique opportunities for integrating livestock, tree crops, and innovative small-scale growing systems.

## Pioneer Species and Succession Planting

In cool temperate regions, rampant pioneer species such as gorse (Ulex europaeus) can be transformed from a perceived weed problem into a powerful ally for system establishment. On the Collingwood River in New Zealand, Dick Nichols manages 280 hectares of gorse-covered land by rolling lines down or slashing the gorse and planting eucalyptus, pines, and acacia. The process unfolds in stages:

1. **Zone 1** is fully cut and planted with pioneer trees
2. **Years 2-3**: Remaining gorse is slashed, and mature rainforest species are slip-planted into the slash
3. **Years 5-6**: The gorse has rotted down and rainforest establishes under the acacia nurse crop, which is then felled
4. **Ongoing**: Native birds carry in rainforest seed, accelerating natural regeneration

Dick Roberts in Nelson, New Zealand uses gorse as "rough mulch" to protect fruit and forage trees in steep sheep pasture. The reduction of weed species protects the succeeding crop. Tree crops eventually shade out the gorse when it is thickly planted as a nurse, demonstrating how pioneer species can be deliberately employed to create protected microsites for desirable later-succession species.

## Tagasaste as a Multi-Purpose Nurse Crop

Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus proliferus) is one of the most valuable multi-purpose plants for cool temperate systems. At Jim and Miriam Tyler"s farm in Taheke, New Zealand, an avocado thrives in a hard frost area when planted within a clump of tagasaste. Kikuyu grass is slashed to provide additional mulch, and the tagasaste yields up to 7 meters of mulch trimmings per annum.

In DSIR trials by Doug Davies on the Banks Peninsula, tagasaste planted at 1 meter in-row spacing with 2 meters between rows provides summer forage from coppice and short-period browsing by sheep. Grasses thrive between the rows, creating a productive integrated system. On the Canterbury Plain, Matthew Carpenter drills tagasaste with turnip for future sheep forage — seedling trees establish through the brassica crop, which provides both nurse protection and an immediate harvest.

Pampas grass combines well with tagasaste strips, providing fast shelter for lambing ewes and serving as preferred forage for most livestock. It is propagated by divisions and complements the tagasaste with its own rapid growth and wind protection capabilities.

## Microclimate Creation and Protected Growing

### Keyhole Beds and Ziggurat Herb Gardens

At Stanley, Tasmania, tomatoes grow in a keyhole bed sheltered by a windbreak of sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke) planted in old tyres on a cold, windswept site. The same location features a herb spiral in the form of a ziggurat — 2 meters across and 1 meter high — providing ample culinary herbs at the kitchen door. The stepped design creates varied drainage conditions and aspects that suit most cool-area herb species within a compact footprint.

### Willow Coppice Systems

Willow coppice near Christchurch, New Zealand demonstrates remarkable versatility. The coppice crop intended for basketry also yields medicinals, forage, stickwood for radiant (mass) heaters, active charcoal filters, artist's charcoal, and more. "Willow water" — fresh willow chips soaked in water — provides gibberellic acid for promoting root growth in the striking of cuttings, making willow an invaluable propagation aid.

At Perth, Tasmania, willows are pollarded for propagation cuttings at a nursery. Matsudana willow provides strict windbreak for kiwifruit vine crops on the plains near Nelson — its columnar form allows minimal space loss on the ground and needs little maintenance to control shape.

## Small Livestock Integration

### Rabbit and Chicken Tractors

At Michael Mangevelakis"s farm in Forest, Tasmania, a rabbit cage tractor cleans clover and grasses from mounded strawberry crops. Rabbits serve as part of the yield of the weed management system and provide manure for the strawberries. At Lake Chelan, Okanogan, USA, Michael Pilarski (Friends of the Trees Coordinator) uses chicken tractors to manage grasses around small fruit crops — chickens weed small fruits over the autumn period and provide manure.

### Multi-Function Hedges

Lycium ferocissimum (boxthorn) hedges carry vines of hops and banana passionfruit while sheltering main crop potatoes. Its berries are loved by poultry, and being a sharp thorny shrub (used historically to keep out lions in Africa), it provides ideal shelter for vulnerable plantings.

### Poultry and Tree Forage Systems

At Wilton, New Hampshire, turkeys forage below autumn olive (Elaeagnus umbellata) with its abundant berries. Siberian pea shrub (Caragana arborescens) and autumn olive are identified as important tree forage legumes of cool and cold climates, providing both browse and nutritious seeds.

### Chicken-Heated Greenhouses

In Aspen, Colorado, a chicken-heated greenhouse combined with a solar-heated seedling cloche enables early planting in short-season high mountain areas. The body heat from chickens supplements solar gain, creating a microclimate warm enough to start transplants weeks before the outdoor season allows.

## Bramble-Apple Guilds

Geoff Wallace in Kiewa, Victoria, observed free-seeded apples establishing in blackberries on his farm. His cattle demolished the brambles to reach the apples. He now deliberately plants apples in the brambles — a pattern of a natural guild in England — where they prosper in their protected hole, fruit well, and the brambles are eventually trodden down by cattle eager for sweet apples. This demonstrates a three-way guild: brambles protect young apples from browsing, apples provide high-value fruit, and cattle manage the brambles while receiving nutrition.

## Forest Edge Berry Production

Near Cle Elum, Washington, forest clearings in fir stands maintain high production of berry fruits, fungi, and ferns — all important crops to the Salish Indians. Stumps of firs grow many berry species, and slabs from stumps, fibers, and logs generate berry clumps on lawns or in clearings, as do fallen fir logs. Birds bring seed and manure to stumps, where salmonberry, black raspberry, huckleberry, and wild strawberry thrive protected from deer browsing.

## Jean Pain Composting Method

Neil Dauce and Jason Alexandra at Tumbarumba, NSW, reduce forest fuel loads using the Jean Pain method: chips are mulched, composted, and fermented to methane. Mechanical removal of forest fuel reduces fire risk and enriches soils, providing an alternative to "cool burns" which impoverish soils and often lead to wildfire. This approach turns a hazard (excess forest fuel) into multiple yields: compost, mulch, and energy.

## Integrated Design Principles for Cool Temperate Systems

1. **Use pioneer species as nurses**: Gorse, tagasaste, and other fast-growing species create protected microclimates for slower-growing desired species
2. **Integrate livestock with crops**: Chicken tractors, rabbit tractors, and managed grazing provide fertility, pest control, and weed management
3. **Create microclimates**: Windbreaks, earth-sheltered structures, and thermal mass extend growing seasons
4. **Design multi-functional elements**: Every plant and animal should serve multiple purposes
5. **Plan for succession**: Design initial plantings with the intention that they will be replaced by climax species
6. **Use [[coppice-and-pollard-systems]]**: Renewable harvest of wood, forage, and propagation material from managed trees
7. **Leverage guild relationships**: Combine species that support each other's growth and productivity
