# Saprotrophic Decomposition

Saprotrophic decomposition is the process by which fungi and other organisms
break down dead organic matter, converting complex biological molecules into
simpler compounds that re-enter nutrient cycles. Fungi are the dominant
saprotrophs in terrestrial ecosystems, responsible for decomposing the vast
majority of plant-derived carbon, particularly recalcitrant materials like
lignin and cellulose that resist bacterial attack.

## The Decomposition Process

Decomposition proceeds through a series of enzymatic stages:

### Recognition and Colonization

Fungal spores germinate in response to chemical cues from dead organic
matter — volatile organic compounds, soluble sugars, and amino acids.
Hyphae grow toward the substrate using chemotropic gradients and establish
a mycelial front that physically penetrates the material.

### Enzymatic Depolymerization

Fungi secrete extracellular enzymes (exoenzymes) that break large polymers
into oligomers and monomers:

- **Cellulases** (endoglucanases, exoglucanases, β-glucosidases) hydrolyze
  cellulose into glucose.
- **Hemicellulases** (xylanases, mannanases) degrade hemicellulose into
  pentose and hexose sugars.
- **Ligninolytic enzymes** (laccases, peroxidases) oxidatively cleave
  lignin's phenylpropanoid polymer.
- **Proteases** and **chitinases** release nitrogen from protein and
  chitin substrates.

### Absorption and Assimilation

Small molecules diffuse or are actively transported across hyphal cell walls.
Fungi preferentially absorb nitrogen and phosphorus, often immobilizing these
nutrients during early decomposition stages. Carbon assimilation is
constrained by the carbon-use efficiency (CUE) of the fungal species,
typically ranging from 0.3 to 0.6 — meaning 40-70% of decomposed carbon is
released as CO₂ through fungal respiration.

## White-Rot vs. Brown-Rot Strategies

The two major wood-decay strategies represent fundamentally different
biochemical approaches:

### White-Rot Fungi

White-rot basidiomycetes (Phanerochaete, Trametes, Pleurotus) are the only
organisms capable of substantial lignin mineralization. They employ an
enzymatic suite including lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase
(MnP), and laccase, often with the aid of low-molecular-weight mediators
that penetrate lignin's dense, non-enzymatic-accessible regions. The result
is a bleached, cellulose-rich residue — hence "white rot."

White-rot decomposition is relatively complete, leaving little residual
organic matter. These fungi are critical for carbon turnover in forest
ecosystems.

### Brown-Rot Fungi

Brown-rot basidiomycetes (Serpula, Gloeophyllum, Postia) employ a
non-enzymatic strategy based on Fenton chemistry. They generate hydrogen
peroxide and reduce ferric iron (Fe³⁺) to ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), producing
hydroxyl radicals (·OH) through the Fenton reaction. These extremely
reactive radicals fragment cellulose and modify lignin without fully
mineralizing it. The result is a brown, lignin-rich, crumbly residue.

Brown-rot fungi are responsible for enormous economic damage to wooden
structures but play essential roles in coniferous forest ecosystems where
their incomplete decomposition contributes to durable soil carbon pools.

## Factors Controlling Decomposition Rates

Decomposition speed depends on interactions between substrate quality,
environmental conditions, and fungal community composition:

- **Lignin-to-nitrogen ratio**: High lignin content slows decomposition;
  adequate nitrogen accelerates it by supporting fungal enzyme production.
- **Moisture**: Optimal decomposition occurs at 50-70% water-holding
  capacity. Both drought and waterlogging suppress fungal activity.
- **Temperature**: Decomposition follows a modified Arrhenius relationship,
  roughly doubling for every 10°C increase (Q₁₀ ≈ 2), up to thermal
  optima around 25-30°C for most temperate species.
- **Fungal diversity**: Mixed-species fungal communities often decompose
  substrates faster than monocultures due to complementary enzyme
  production and reduced competitive inhibition.

## Carbon Fate After Decomposition

Decomposed carbon follows three primary pathways:

1. **CO₂ release**: Fungal respiration returns 40-70% of decomposed carbon
   to the atmosphere.
2. **Fungal biomass**: 10-30% is incorporated into living fungal tissue
   (mycelium, spores, fruiting bodies).
3. **Soil organic matter**: 10-40% persists as residual organic matter,
   eventually forming humus and mineral-associated organic matter (MAOM)
   that can resist further decomposition for centuries to millennia.

The balance among these pathways determines whether an ecosystem functions
as a net carbon source or sink.
