# Saprotrophic Decomposition

Saprotrophic decomposition is the process by which fungi break down dead
organic matter, converting complex biological molecules into simpler
compounds that re-enter nutrient cycles. Fungi are the dominant
saprotrophs in terrestrial ecosystems, responsible for decomposing the
vast majority of plant-derived carbon, particularly recalcitrant
materials like lignin and cellulose that resist bacterial attack.

## The Decomposition Process

Decomposition proceeds through a series of enzymatic stages:

### Recognition and Colonization

Fungal spores germinate in response to chemical cues from dead organic
matter, including [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds, soluble sugars, and amino
acids. Hyphae grow toward the substrate using chemotropic gradients and
establish a mycelial front that physically penetrates the material.

### Enzymatic Depolymerization

Fungi secrete extracellular enzymes (exoenzymes) that break large
polymers into oligomers and monomers:

- **Cellulases** (endoglucanases, exoglucanases, beta-glucosidases)
  hydrolyze cellulose into glucose.
- **Hemicellulases** (xylanases, mannanases) degrade hemicellulose
  into pentose and hexose sugars.
- **[[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]]** (laccases, peroxidases) oxidatively cleave
  lignin phenylpropanoid polymers.
- **Proteases** and **chitinases** release nitrogen from protein and
  chitin substrates.

### Absorption and Assimilation

Small molecules diffuse or are actively transported across hyphal cell
walls. Fungi preferentially absorb nitrogen and phosphorus, often
immobilizing these nutrients during early decomposition. Carbon
assimilation is constrained by carbon-use efficiency (CUE), typically
0.3-0.6, meaning 40-70% of decomposed carbon is released as CO2.

## White-Rot vs. Brown-Rot Strategies

### White-Rot Fungi

White-rot basidiomycetes (*Phanerochaete*, *Trametes*, *Pleurotus*)
are the only organisms capable of substantial lignin mineralization.
They employ [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]] (LiP), [[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] (MnP), and
laccase, often with low-molecular-weight mediators that penetrate
lignin dense regions. The result is a bleached, cellulose-rich residue.
These fungi are critical for carbon turnover in forest ecosystems. See
[[singh-lignin-peroxidase-lip-fungal-degradation]] for details on
lignin peroxidase mechanisms.

### Brown-Rot Fungi

Brown-rot basidiomycetes (*Serpula*, *Gloeophyllum*, *Postia*) employ
a non-enzymatic [[bioremediation-fenton-chemistry-brown-rot-fungi]] strategy. They generate hydrogen
peroxide and reduce ferric to ferrous iron, producing hydroxyl radicals
that fragment cellulose and modify lignin without fully mineralizing it.
The result is a brown, lignin-rich, crumbly residue. Brown-rot fungi
contribute to durable soil carbon pools in coniferous forests.

## Soft-Rot Fungi

Soft-rot fungi (primarily Ascomycota and some Deuteromycota) represent
a third decay strategy. They degrade cellulose in wet environments
where basidiomycete activity is limited, producing characteristic
cavity formation within wood cell walls. Soft-rot is significant in
waterlogged wood and in tropical environments.

## Factors Controlling Decomposition Rates

- **Lignin-to-nitrogen ratio**: High lignin slows decomposition;
  adequate nitrogen accelerates it by supporting [[ligninolytic-enzyme-production-bioremediation]].
- **Moisture**: Optimal at 50-70% water-holding capacity. Both drought
  and waterlogging suppress fungal activity.
- **Temperature**: Roughly doubles for every 10C increase (Q10 ~ 2),
  up to optima around 25-30C for most temperate species.
- **Fungal diversity**: Mixed-species communities often decompose
  faster than monocultures due to complementary enzyme production.

## Carbon Fate After Decomposition

1. **CO2 release**: Fungal respiration returns 40-70% to the atmosphere.
2. **Fungal biomass**: 10-30% is incorporated into living tissue.
3. **Soil organic matter**: 10-40% persists as humus and mineral-
  associated organic matter (MAOM) for centuries to millennia.

The balance among these pathways determines whether an ecosystem
functions as a net carbon source or sink. See
[[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] and [[fungal-ecology]] for broader
context on fungal contributions to ecosystem carbon dynamics.
