# Stamets' Mycoforestry Case Studies

Practical demonstrations of using fungi to restore forest landscapes, recover damaged roads, and establish sustainable woodland management practices. These case studies bridge the theoretical framework of [[mycoforestry-principles-stamets]] with field-validated results.

## Tahuya River Road Restoration (Washington State)

The most detailed mycoforestry case study in Stamets' work, conducted in collaboration with the Washington Department of Natural Resources (DNR).

### Site Description

DNR set aside a swath of upland road destined for habitat recovery beside salmon habitat on the Tahuya River, a salmon spawning ground. Runoff saturated with silt flowed directly into the river, threatening the aquatic ecosystem. The project site was a hundred yards from the salmon habitat.

### Method

1. Wood chips were dumped on the road surface, inoculated with oyster mushroom (*Pleurotus ostreatus*) mycelium
2. Straw was spread over the inoculated wood chips
3. 20 pounds of Regreen (a nonseeding wheat approved for erosion control) was broadcast over the straw using a broadcast seeder
4. The seeder was stocked with 1 pound of Mycogrow, a mycorrhizal inoculum
5. Work completed in mid-April during intermittently heavy rainfall
6. One week later: seeds sprouting and habitat in first stages of restoration

### Species Selection

*Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) was selected as the keystone species because:
- It is a primary saprophyte -- indigenous, aggressive, and adaptable
- Grows under a variety of conditions and temperatures
- Has demonstrated mycoremediation capabilities against petroleum products
- Future trials will use a matrix of white rot, brown rot, and other mycorrhizal species (no parasitic species contemplated)

### Three-Year Results

After 3 years, the reclaimed road showed a mantle of nearly contiguous mycelia at the point where wood chips and gravel meet. The mycelium overlaid and held the gravel together, adding structural resilience to the road's subsurface and providing porosity and microcavities that flourished with microbial life. An uplifted rock had rested upon a mantle of mycelium underneath it -- Stamets hypothesizes the mycelium became resident in this subterranean zone, feeding upon the soupy, nutrient-rich water flowing from above.

### Ecological Cascade

The project demonstrated a full ecological recovery cascade:
- Mycelium infiltrates wood chips, retaining moisture
- Mushrooms grow, mature, and release spores
- Rotting mushrooms attract insects (fly larvae, grubs)
- Insects attract animals from lizards to birds
- Wheat grass climaxes and dies, wood chips decompose
- Rich soil created (estimated 1-2 inches of soil per 12 inches of wood chips after 4 years)
- Native species recover in the enriched soil

### Lessons

Wood chips are the "ecological currency" that should be banked for preventing erosion. Once a mycofiltration habitat is constructed, ecological recovery unfolds and nature guides the course.

## Fungus Gnat-Phorid Fly-Fire Ant Matrix

While not a forestry case study per se, this field observation has direct implications for forest pest management:

- Certain mycelia attract fungus gnats
- Mycelia also attract phorid flies, which are predators of fire ants (*Solenopsis invicta*)
- Some entomopathogenic mycelia emit a fragrance attracting phorid flies AND another compound that kills fire or leaf-cutting ants
- A matrix of 2 fungal species can be "mycoengineered" for a double-pronged treatment
- This is one of hundreds of tactical predator-prey combinations in Stamets' mycopesticide patents

## Roads as Ecological Problems

Stamets frames degraded logging roads as a primary ecological problem in forest management. Roads create erosion pathways that deliver sediment to streams, damage salmon habitat, fragment wildlife corridors, and provide vectors for invasive species. The Tahuya River project demonstrated that mycoforestry techniques can transform these damaged areas into green, foot-friendly pathways for hikers and fauna.

Through this simple, direct, and practical approach, ugly roads -- the source of numerous ecological problems -- are transformed into green, foot-friendly pathways for hikers and other fauna. Mycorestoration practices also offer a complementary management strategy for long-term sustainability of our forestlands in general.

## Spored Oils for Forestry Equipment

Stamets proposes inoculating chainsaw bar oil, chipper lubricants, and other cutting equipment with mushroom spores. When trees are cut, the oil delivers spores directly to the cut surfaces, giving beneficial saprophytes a head start over parasitic pathogens. This low-cost intervention could dramatically reduce tree losses to parasitic fungi in managed forests.

## Mycoforestry Principles Applied

These case studies demonstrate several core [[mycoforestry-principles-stamets]]:

1. **Use native species when possible** -- *P. ostreatus* is indigenous to the Pacific Northwest
2. **Start with keystone saprophytes** -- primary decomposers jumpstart the ecological cascade
3. **Layer substrates** -- wood chips (structural/fungal food) + straw (erosion control) + mycorrhizal inoculant (plant support)
4. **Let nature take over** -- once established, the mycological community drives its own recovery
5. **Multiple species matrices** -- future trials will combine white rot, brown rot, and mycorrhizal species

## Implications for Large-Scale Forest Management

Stamets argues that mycoforestry practices offer a complementary management strategy for long-term sustainability of forestlands. The approach is low-cost, scalable, and works with natural ecological processes rather than against them. The key inputs -- wood chips, straw, native grass seed, and mycorrhizal inoculant -- are inexpensive and readily available.
