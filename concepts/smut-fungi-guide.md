# Smut Fungi Guide

Smut fungi (order Ustilaginales, phylum [[basidiomycete-phylum-guide|Basidiomycota]]) are plant pathogenic fungi characterized by the production of dark, sooty masses of teliospores that replace plant tissues, particularly reproductive structures. While less visually dramatic than [[rust-fungi]], smuts cause significant economic losses in cereal crops worldwide. The name "smut" refers to the soot-like appearance of the spore masses.

## Defining Characteristics

- Obligate biotrophs (most species) or facultative saprotrophs
- Produce teliospores (often called "smut spores") that are dark brown to black
- Teliospores replace host tissue, especially kernels, ovaries, or anthers
- Most are internally infecting: spores germinate to produce a yeast-like phase that infects seedlings systemically
- No asexual uredinial stage (unlike rust fungi), so disease progression is slower
- Basidiospores (from teliospore germination) are the primary infective propagules
- Can survive in soil and seed for many years as dormant teliospores

## Key Genera and Species

### Ustilago (Covering Smuts)

The largest genus of smut fungi. "Covering smuts" destroy the entire grain/kernel, replacing it with a mass of teliospores enclosed by a thin membrane that eventually ruptures.

#### Ustilago maydis (Corn Smut / Huitlacoche)
- One of the most economically important smut fungi
- Infects maize ([[zea-mays]]) and its wild relative teosinte
- Systemic infection: enters through young tissue, grows throughout the plant
- Produces tumor-like galls on any above-ground plant part (leaves, stems, ears, tassels)
- Galls are filled with dark teliospores; young galls are edible (huitlacoche)
- Huitlacoche is considered a delicacy in Mexican cuisine, selling for premium prices
- In the US, corn smut is primarily a disease; in Mexico, it is a valued crop
- Causes yield losses of 2-15% in commercial corn; can be devastating in sweet corn
- Lifecycle: teliospores overwinter in soil, germinate in spring to produce basidiospores, infect through wounds or young tissue
- Requires mating between two compatible mating types (bipolar heterothallism)

#### Ustilago hordei (Covered Smut of Barley)
- Infects barley through germinating seeds
- Systemic infection: fungus grows within the plant without visible symptoms until heading
- Replaces entire grain with spore mass covered by persistent membrane
- Membrane usually remains intact until harvest, limiting spread
- Primarily seed-borne; seed treatment is the main control method
- Yield losses can reach 100% in severely affected fields

#### Ustilago tritici (Loose Smut of Wheat)
- Infects wheat through germinating seeds
- Systemic infection: replaces entire grain head with dark spore mass
- "Loose" because the membrane covering the spores ruptures early, releasing spores during flowering
- Spores are wind-dispersed to flowers of neighboring plants, infecting the embryo
- The fungus survives in the embryo of infected seed, perpetuating the cycle
- Not controlled by foliar fungicides since infection occurs before emergence
- Primary control: seed treatment with systemic fungicides (carboxin, triadimenol)

### Urocystis (Flag Smuts)

#### Urocystis tritici (Flag Smut of Wheat)
- Produces long, dark streaks on leaves, stems, and leaf sheaths
- Streaks contain spore balls (groups of spores surrounded by sterile cells)
- Primarily soil-borne; spores survive in soil for years
- More important in dryland wheat areas
- Control through resistant varieties and seed treatment

### Sphacelotheca (Sorghum Smuts)

#### Sphacelotheca reiliana (Head Smut of Sorghum)
- Infects sorghum and maize
- Systemic infection that replaces the entire inflorescence with a spore mass
- Soil-borne; teliospores survive in soil for several years
- Can cause severe yield losses, especially in susceptible hybrids
- Control through resistant varieties and seed treatment

#### Sphacelotheca sorghi (Loose Kernel Smut of Sorghum)
- Seed-borne, infecting sorghum kernels
- Replaces individual kernels with spore masses
- Control through seed treatment

### Tilletia (Bunt / Stinking Smut)

#### Tilletia caries and T. foetida (Common Bunt of Wheat)
- Infects wheat kernels, replacing them with spore masses
- Spores have a distinctive fishy odor (hence "stinking smut")
- Can contaminate grain, reducing quality and marketability
- Both soil-borne and seed-borne
- Control through seed treatment and resistant varieties

#### Tilletia controversa (Dwarf Bunt of Wheat)
- More serious form of bunt, causes stunting of infected plants
- Requires cold, wet conditions for infection
- Teliospores have a longer dormancy period than common bunt
- Primarily a problem in high-elevation or high-latitude wheat areas
- Quarantine organism in some countries

## Disease Cycle (Generalized)

Most smut fungi follow a similar pattern:

1. Teliospores persist in soil or on seed
2. Teliospores germinate in response to moisture and temperature, producing basidia
3. Basidia produce basidiospores (or in some species, the basidium itself acts as the infective structure)
4. In Ustilago, basidiospores of opposite mating types must fuse (plasmogamy) before infection
5. Infection occurs through seedling tissue (most common) or through flowers
6. The fungus grows systemically within the plant (intercellularly)
7. At flowering or maturity, the fungus replaces reproductive structures with teliospores
8. Teliospores are released and persist until the next growing season

## Control Methods

### Seed Treatment
- Systemic fungicide seed treatments are the most effective control for seed-borne smuts
- Carboxin and triadimenol are the standard chemicals
- Must be applied before planting; they protect during the critical seedling infection period
- Not effective for soil-borne infections

### Resistant Varieties
- Genetic resistance is available for many smut diseases
- Resistance is often race-specific, requiring monitoring of pathogen populations
- Durability varies: some resistance genes have remained effective for decades

### Cultural Practices
- Crop rotation reduces soil-borne smut inoculum (most effective with non-host crops)
- Planting date adjustment: avoid conditions favorable for infection
- Deep plowing can bury teliospores below the infection zone
- Clean seed: use certified seed from smut-free fields

### Biological Control
- Some bacterial and fungal antagonists show promise against smut fungi
- [[bacillus-subtilis]] and [[trichoderma]] species have been investigated
- Not yet widely adopted commercially

## Economic Impact

- Corn smut: estimated global losses of hundreds of millions of dollars annually
- Loose smut of wheat: historically caused losses of 1-3% in North America; more severe in developing countries
- Common bunt: can cause 1-20% losses depending on severity
- Karnal bunt (Tilletia indica): quarantine disease that affects wheat trade, even at low incidence levels
- Total smut-related losses to cereals globally are estimated in the billions of dollars

## Smut Fungi as Model Organisms

[[ustilago-maydis]] has become an important model system for studying:
- Plant-microbe interactions and fungal pathogenicity
- Mating type and sex determination in fungi
- Filamentous growth and dimorphism
- Signal transduction pathways
- Genome evolution in pathogenic fungi
