# Plants Without Mycorrhizal Associations

Most plants form [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] associations, but a notable minority do not. These [[non-mycorrhizal-plants]] have evolved alternative strategies for acquiring nutrients, including specialized root structures and unusual adaptations.

## Non-Mycorrhizal Plant Families

Plants that do not form mycorrhizae are the exceptions, but they are well-represented across several major families:

- **Brassicaceae** — mustards, kales, cabbages, broccoli, cauliflower, radish, turnip, arugula, horseradish, rapeseed

- **Caryophyllaceae** — pinks and carnations

- **Chenopodiaceae** — amaranths and goosefoot

- **Polygonaceae** — knotweed and buckwheat

- **Portulacaceae** — purslane

- **Proteaceae** — proteas

- **Amaranthaceae** — beets, spinach

These plants usually have extensive and quick-growing specialized root systems that make the most of limited nutrients in poor soils such as sand. Their root strategies eliminate the need for fungal symbiosis.

## Root Adaptations of Non-Mycorrhizal Plants

### Cluster Roots

Proteas (Proteaceae) and lupines (*Lupinus* spp.) have cluster roots—closely spaced lateral roots that are densely covered with root hairs. These roots form near the soil surface so they can feed in the duff layer.

To obtain phosphorus without mycorrhizal fungi, cluster roots support large populations of phosphate-solubilizing bacteria in their rhizospheres. Their exudates are full of organic acids that decay organic matter, releasing nutrients that the roots can absorb directly.

### Dauciform Roots

Some members of the Cyperaceae (sedges) have dauciform roots—swollen, carrot-shaped lateral roots that are densely covered in root hairs. Like cluster roots, these structures exude organic acids that help solubilize and take up nutrients from the soil.

### Root Mats and Sand-Binding Roots

Capillaroid plants such as rushes (Restionaceae) form a mat of root clusters densely covered in long root hairs at the soil surface. These roots pump out large quantities of organic acid exudates to help them take up nutrients.

Some sedges, rushes, and other monocots in Western Australia have sand-binding roots, in which sand literally sticks to and completely covers the roots. Scientists hypothesize that the attached sand enhances nutrient uptake, though the exact mechanism is still under study.

### Carnivorous, Epiphytic, and Parasitic Strategies

Some non-mycorrhizal plants have developed carnivorous capabilities, obtaining nutrients from aboveground organisms such as insects. Others, such as epiphytes, grab nutrients from the air. Still others have evolved into parasites, drawing nutrients directly from host plants.

## Multi-Mycorrhizal Plants

Though most plants associate with only one type of mycorrhizal fungus, some can form associations with multiple types. See [[mycorrhizal-types-diversity]] for a full overview.

Most poplars (*Populus* spp.) can form either ectomycorrhizal or arbuscular mycorrhizal relationships depending on the species, but *P. tremuloides* can be associated with both types simultaneously. This is also the case with other trees in the willow family (Salicaceae), as well as alder (*Alnus* spp.) and eucalyptus. The same is true, though far less often, for ericaceous plants.

This phenomenon is not often studied because of its rarity. It is possible that a host plant may be testing different fungi for future use.

## Mycorrhizal Succession

See [[mycorrhizal-succession-primary-secondary]] for detailed treatment of succession dynamics.

Determining the succession of mycorrhizal fungi and plants in soil can be a chicken-or-egg situation: the type of mycorrhizal fungi present affects plant distribution and diversity, but plants may also control local fungal distribution by associating or failing to associate with particular species.

The general succession pattern follows this sequence:

1. **Nonmycorrhizal plants** grow where no mycorrhizal fungi are present in the soil.

2. **Facultative mycorrhizal fungi**, which can function as parasites or saprobes, move in next.

3. **Semidependent host plants** become established and eventually take over.

4. **Obligatory mycorrhizal fungi** colonize the area along with completely dependent host plants as decay increases.

Studies show that in response to the presence of mycorrhizal fungi, plant diversity usually increases. The presence of a particular mycorrhizal fungus influences which plants can live in particular soils, and the ability of mycorrhizal fungi to change the environment means some plants may slow down or cease to grow as conditions shift.

## Geographic Distribution by Ecosystem Type

[[dighton-mycorrhizal-diversity-ecosystem-function]] varies dramatically by geography, driven by soil type, nutrient availability, and climate.

### Subarctic and Tundra Regions

Soils in subarctic regions have extremely low pH levels. Peat dominates, as do ericoid mycorrhizae. Ascomycete fungal partners penetrate root cortex cells but do not send much mass into the soil. They mineralize nutrients near the roots that the host plant could never access alone, producing the inorganic ions needed by the host.

Some tundra mycorrhizal fungi provide organic nitrogen directly to their host—a remarkable feat that bucks the norm of inorganic nitrogen uptake. If this process could be translated into agricultural plants, it could transform global food production.

### Conifer and Hardwood Forests

In conifer and hardwood forests with warmer temperatures, higher pH, and more available nutrients, ectomycorrhizal fungi and their associations predominate. See [[ectomycorrhizae-forest-life]] for more detail.

These fungal hyphae send out extensive hyphal mass, bringing back nutrients to hosts while mineralizing tremendous amounts of organic material, making it available to other plants and fungi.

### Temperate Grasslands

In temperate grasslands with warmer temperatures, higher soil pH, and nitrates as the predominant form of nitrogen, arbuscular mycorrhizae prevail. The huge increase in surface area available from mycorrhizal networks enables host plants to meet their nutrient needs. See [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] for more on these associations.
