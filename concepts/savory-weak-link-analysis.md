# Weak Link Analysis

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]],
the weak link guideline is one of the key [[savory-testing-and-management-guidelines]] used in [[savory-holistic-decision-making]]. It directs investment toward the
constraining factor in any production chain, ensuring that resources are
not wasted on non-problems. The concept applies to both financial and
biological planning contexts.

## The Chain from Sunlight to Cash

Three links generally span the distance from sunshine to cash:

1. Energy conversion, in which sunlight energy becomes useful plant
   material
2. Product conversion, in which plants are rendered into a marketable form
   (crops for humans, fodder for livestock, wildlife, fish, etc.)
3. Marketing, in which the product returns solar dollars to the enterprise

According to the guideline, only investment in the weak link will result in
more wealth at the end. No amount of advertising (marketing link) expense
will profit an operation that turns out a poor product. Nor will a better
breed of cattle (product conversion link) help when grass to feed them is
lacking.

## The Ranch Example

Consider a twenty-thousand-acre ranch in a [[savory-brittle-environments]] with fifteen inches of rainfall. The ranch has
carried a conventional [[savory-stock-density-vs-stocking-rate]] for many years. Serious erosion, soil exposure, and brush
encroachment have caused severe gullying and nearly constant drought
symptoms. The debt on the land is crushing.

An extension agent offers a 50 percent cost-sharing program to eradicate
brush and cure gullies. On paper it looks cost-effective. But at the
present stocking rate, the rancher is not using approximately 50 percent
of the available forage each year. Despite the erosion and brush, the
rancher is not short of forage or energy conversion. The weak link is
product conversion: not converting all the grass into a marketable product.

Money spent increasing grass would go to waste since there are insufficient
cattle through which to market it. The correct action is to buy more
cattle, not to invest in land improvement.

## Identifying the Weak Link

Weak link analysis requires honest assessment of each link in the chain:

Energy conversion weakness shows up as shortage of forage for livestock
and/or wildlife. In farming, poor crop yields indicate poor energy
conversion, but exorbitant input costs may be a more common symptom in the
era of mechanized, chemical agriculture.

Product conversion weakness shows up when animals cannot use available
forage, or when a farmer can produce a crop but cannot market it well due
to transport, storage, or harvest limitations.

Marketing weakness appears when the producer fails to meet market needs,
when available markets remain untapped, when the product is poorly
presented or promoted, or when supply is erratic or out of sync with peak
demand.

## Common Misapplications

The most common mistake in ranching is investing in land improvement
(fencing, water development, brush clearing, erosion control) when the
weak link is actually product conversion or marketing. Many people attending
HRM training courses fail to grasp this point because the prospect of
radically improving their landscape through high [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] and long [[savory-recovery-and-grazing-periods]] tends to short-circuit clear thinking.

Similarly, many farmers go under because the expense of overcoming a
damaged natural energy flow keeps climbing. Low prices, high interest
rates, and overproduction may be blamed, but no relief there will save a
farmer who must import all fertility from fossil energy to create solar
dollars.

## The Cost-Sharing Illusion

The level of government cost-sharing has no bearing on the logic of the
decision. Even a 90 percent subsidy means the 10 percent invested would
return less than nothing, because of the opportunity cost in not putting it
where it would directly increase yield of solar dollars.

This is a critical insight: subsidies cannot overcome a fundamental
misallocation of resources. If the weak link is not addressed, no amount
of investment in other links will produce returns.

## Application to Financial Planning

In [[savory-ranch-financial-planning]], the weak
link analysis is applied annually to guide reinvestment decisions. The
annual reinvestment cycle shows how solar dollars flow through the three
links and where the constraint lies. The goal is to minimize the use of
mineral and paper dollars (nonrenewable wealth) and maximize the
generation of solar dollars (renewable wealth from sunlight capture).

Every dollar of reinvestment should go to the weakest link. If energy
conversion is weak, invest in [[savory-livestock-grazing-planning]] and land health. If product conversion is weak, invest in
livestock numbers or equipment. If marketing is weak, invest in market
research and product development.

## Biological Applications

The weak link concept also applies to biological planning. When managing
for [[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]],
the weak link might be soil surface condition, seed availability, moisture,
or any of the [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]]. Addressing the biological weak link ensures that management
effort produces the greatest ecological benefit.

## Not All Enterprises Have All Links

Not all enterprises involve all three links. Hay sold from the field does
not undergo much product conversion compared to boxed beef or processed
orange juice. A guided elk hunt enterprise may have very different link
structures than a cattle operation. Yet one always must consider where
value may be added to a calorie of solar energy.

## The Discipline of the Guideline

The weak link guideline requires discipline. Once the weak link has been
identified, it must be addressed. It is not merely desirable or important.
Any actions or investments must pass other [[savory-testing-and-management-guidelines]] as well to assure they really do address
the situation. Perceived problems that do not represent the weak link are
nonproblems.
