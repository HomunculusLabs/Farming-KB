# Agar and Tissue Culture Techniques

## Overview

Working with agar plates is the foundation of [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]. Agar is a
gelatinous [[comparison-seaweed-extract-vs-fish-hydrolysate]] used as a substrate for tissue cultures. Petri plates
(available in glass for reuse or disposable plastic) hold the agar. Agar
powder can be purchased from mycological supply companies or most Asian markets.

There are two primary pathways for starting a culture: from spores (genetically
variable offspring) or by cloning (genetically identical to the parent). Spore
germinations are ideal for breeding or creating region-specific strains. Cloning
is easier and preserves exact parent characteristics.

## Making Agar Plates

### Step 1: Preparing Agar Gel

Slice a small potato (unpeeled) into chunks or shred with a grater. Boil in 3-4
cups of water for one hour. Strain the potatoes and measure the hot broth. Pour
into an Erlenmeyer flask. Add 1 teaspoon (~5 grams) of agar powder per 2 cups
of broth while hot (never use cold water -- agar clumps instantly).

### Step 2: Sterilizing the Agar

Use a pressure cooker capable of maintaining 12-15 PSI. The best cookers have
metal-to-metal seals. Fill the cooker with water per manufacturer's directions.
Plug the flask top with cotton or polyester fill, cover with aluminum foil, and
process at 15 PSI for twenty minutes.

Critical: Open the cooker only in a clean room or in front of the [[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]]
hood, when internal pressure is still slightly above 0 PSI. If pressure drops
below 0, a vacuum forms and sucks in contaminated air. If opened too hot, agar
boils out of the flask. Sanitize the work area thoroughly before opening.

### Step 3: Pouring Agar

Monitor temperature with an infrared gauge ($30 hardware store). The pouring
temperature is around 100 degrees F (38 degrees C). If adding antibiotics, do
so between 105-110 degrees F.

Prepare petri plates at the laminar flow hood. Wipe the outer sleeve with
rubbing alcohol. Invert the stack on your work surface, remove the sleeve, and
divide into groups of 4-5 plates. Pour agar into each petri base until half
full, replacing the lid after each. Two cups of agar fills 20-25 plates. Allow
to cool for about one hour, then check for gelling by tilting.

Label plates with date, recipe, and additives. Cotter uses colored permanent
markers as codes (e.g., green marks denote penicillin G).

### Step 4: Storing Agar Plates

Seal cultured plates with tape or Parafilm. Store extra plates in ziplock bags
(3-4 per bag) in the refrigerator for one to two months. Inspect for
contamination (green, yellow, or black growth) before use.

## Alternative Agar Bases

Potato agar is difficult for bacteria to grow on, making it ideal for beginners.
For picky species, substitute other bases: powdered brewer's malt, dog and
rabbit food, tomato juice, cattle feed, cornmeal, or beet pulp. Track formulas
in a journal to learn which mushrooms perform best on each medium. Keep a
library of dried, powdered substrate samples in the lab.

## Enrichment Medium (Substrate Agar)

Adding a tablespoon of the mushroom's preferred [[pf-tek-maximum-fruiting-substrate-formula]] (finely
ground sawdust or screened wheat straw powder) to agar helps cultures adapt and
express needed enzymes. This "enrichment medium" is useful for hard-to-clone or
picky fungi.

### Split Gallery Plates

Set the petri dish base at an angle on a dowel and pour enough agar to fill
half. After gelling, flip and fill the other side with a different formula.
Transfer mycelium to the "safe side" to observe how it reacts when reaching the
challenging medium. Increase additive concentration with each consecutive
transfer to test the strain's tolerance.

## Adding Antibiotics to Agar

Professional labs add antibiotics to minimize bacterial contaminants:
- Ampicillin and streptomycin limit bacterial growth
- Sterile injectable penicillin G (livestock grade) is the most practical
  option -- add below 110 degrees F (43 degrees C) as agar cools
- Antibiotics can also be used in liquid cultures for longer storage

## Spore Germination: Culturing from Spores

Collect spores via spore print or by lightly raking gills/pores with a sterile
tool. Prepare agar plates supplemented with the mushroom's preferred substrate.
Streak spores across the agar surface. Compatible spores will mate and
germinate on the plate, allowing selection of desirable strains.

## Cloning: Tissue Culture

Cloning creates genetically identical fungi to the parent. Cut or peel the
mushroom to reveal clean inner tissue, remove a small fragment (compressed
mycelium), and transfer to agar. This is ideal for building a library of strains
from different climates, seasons, or substrates. Wild-collected mushrooms
already adapted to their environment have "done the hard work."

## Rescuing Cultures from Contamination

If mycelium is growing on a contaminated plate at some distance from the
contaminant, attempt a "still-air" transfer -- never near the laminar flow
hood. Set up in a room with no airflow (or a glove box), disinfect all surfaces
and tools, carefully retrieve healthy mycelial tissue, transfer to fresh agar,
and seal in a ziplock bag.

### Antibiotic Sandwich for Bacteria

For bacteria-contaminated cultures, pour antibiotic-supplemented agar plates,
place contaminated mycelium on one side, then flip a large wedge of antibiotic
agar on top to sandwich it. The mycelium drills through while bacteria cannot
follow. Purified mycelium fuzzes up on the outside for transfer.

### Sectoring: Separating Strains

When a spore streak produces mixed strains of the same species, the different
morphologies develop as competing islands. Where islands collide, they build
walls. To "sector" a promising strain, excise a small bit of the outermost
hyphal tips and transfer to a fresh plate. Even, symmetrical growth indicates a
purified strain.
