# Structural Forests and Forest Product Management

Structural forests are managed specifically for timber, poles, and construction materials. In permaculture, the strongest structural timber is round, uncut timber from pioneer and edge species. These forests provide fence posts, building poles, coppice products, and a wide range of materials while simultaneously performing [[fungal-melanin-biosynthesis-ecological-functions]] such as soil building, windbreak, and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]].

## Round Timber vs. Saw Log

A fundamental principle of [[permaculture-forestry-systems]] is that round pole timber is of far more use to the smallholder or community than saw logs. Only a small number of trees may be needed to rebuild a house. The emphasis on saw logging serves the interests of large-scale timber operations rather than self-reliant communities. When comparing economic returns, pole timber managed on short rotation (4-6 years for black locust posts) generates income far sooner than waiting 40 years for plank timber.

Australian builders have demonstrated that an entire house framework can be constructed using approximately nine two-inch poles, then filled in with mud, brick, stone, wattling, or chicken wire and cement. The complete pole framework costs around $800. Chicken wire and cement are highlighted as particularly effective [[gadd-fungal-biodegradation-concrete-building-materials]], producing beautiful and durable homes.

## Black Locust: The Premier Structural Tree

Black locust (*[[robinia-pseudoacacia]]*) stands as perhaps the most valuable structural tree in North America:

- **Durability**: Posts have been documented lasting 90-100 years while still near maximum strength
- **Impact strength**: The highest of any timber known — it is the traditional mallet head wood
- **Growth rate**: Fence post-sized stems in only 4-6 years
- **Coppicing**: The more you cut, the more you get — it freely regrows from cut stumps
- **Soil building**: As a leguminous pioneer, it improves soil fertility
- **Chicken forage**: Provides excellent browse for poultry, increasing forest nutrient cycling
- **Stocking density**: 4,000-5,000 stems per acre is achievable
- **Economic value**: Fence posts heading toward $5-$8 each (in 1980s Australian dollars)

Black locust also serves as an excellent buffer tree between incompatible species groups, tolerating proximity to both nut trees and fruit trees without crop reduction.

## Bamboo: Multi-Purpose Structural Plant

Bamboo offers numerous domestic and structural uses but requires patience:

- **Time to harvest**: Generally 10 years before a clump can be cut
- **Propagation**: Primarily vegetative; two to four North American bamboos are heavy seeders
- **Structural quality**: A large bamboo growing to 60 feet with 4-inch diameter is suitable for North American climates
- **Concrete reinforcement**: When cut at 2-4 years and dried for 8 months, bamboo provides two-thirds the strength of steel per diameter — and superior bonding in concrete since it doesn't rust
- **Additional uses**: Cups, knives, plates, gutters, downpipes
- **Food value**: Bamboo shoots are edible (with caution regarding cyanide in raw shoots)

Bamboo cannot be propagated from seed as quickly as from root mass division. Convincing someone to allow their clump to be broken up and root masses dug out is the fastest path to establishment.

## Coppice Systems

Coppicing is the practice of cutting trees at ground level to stimulate regrowth from the stump. A whole set of plants are cut-and-come-again species:

### Coppice Species
- **Willow**: The classical coppice tree, used for basketry, furniture, and handles
- **Poplar**: Good for forage, splitting, and inside work (not durable outside)
- **Ash**: Used for woven fences and tool handles
- **Hazel and oak**: Traditional woven fence materials
- **Tea-tree** (not native to America): Extremely thick growth, weaves well, produces high-value oil from leaf distillation, lasts 30-40 years in fences at 30 cents per stick

### Coppice Management Strategies

Coppice can be managed for different products:

- **Bark production**: Start coppice at specific heights for bark harvest
- **Timber production**: Standard coppice rotation for pole timber
- **Forage**: Start coppice above cow height to allow cattle browsing without damage to regrowth points
- **Basketry**: Start below ground level by burying billets in wet sites; the Tasmanian basket makers maintained a half-acre patch through five generations using this method

### Preventing Coppice Regrowth

To stop a stump from coppicing, simply exclude light — throw a piece of carpet or other light-blocking material over the cut stump. Alternatively, applying road salt to the stump prevents regrowth.

### Forage Value

A quarter acre of willow coppice can carry cattle through droughts. The system requires monitoring of browse damage — either cut and carry the forage, or turn cattle in for limited periods depending on observed impact. In extensive cattle areas, 5-6 acres of coppice forage provides critical drought insurance.

## Forest Management Strategies

### Three Management Approaches

For any forested area, multiple management strategies should be applied simultaneously:

1. **Continual-product pole forest**: Continually fell the largest trees as they reach a certain diameter, providing a steady production of round timbers
2. **Eventual-product forage forest**: Cut out all small and weak trees, eventually leaving high-quality saw log trees
3. **Mixed approach**: Where more than 7-8 acres are available, manage some areas for poles and others for forage/saw logs

### Management Zones

In a large design, forest management should be zoned by function:

- **Inner zones**: Maximum productivity for human and animal forage; species selection favors high forage-drop trees like oak, cherry, and apple
- **Reflective species**: White birch screens near gardens create micro-habitats for food production
- **Secondary zones**: Structural forests, bamboo clumps, and shelter plantings
- **Tertiary zones**: Future plank timber areas managed for long-term production
- **Undisturbed reserves**: Patches of forest left entirely to themselves as ecological controls and wildlife habitat — these may reveal practices that prevent mistakes in managed areas

### Dry Distillation: Maximizing Wood Value

Dead and dying trees represent an opportunity for value-added processing through dry distillation. A simple brick-lined container system produces:

- **Charcoal**: Excellent cooking fuel, readily saleable
- **Methane**: Cooking gas — 4% of output can pump the rest to storage
- **Methanol**: Fuel for tractors and machinery
- **Creosote**: Wood preservation and proofing — can turn birch into rot-resistant timber
- **Heat**: Massive thermal mass for home heating and hot water

The system works by loading wood into a brick-lined container, lighting a fire underneath (separate from the wood being processed), and steaming out all [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]]. Flue gases passed through pond water precipitate [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]], which can be returned to fields. The French historically used a double-chamber brick system — one for fire, one for cooking timber.

This approach transforms firewood worth more than a cord into multiple high-value products while providing home heating and cooking gas.

### Nest Boxes and Wildlife Habitat

Old hollow logs should be salvaged as nest boxes rather than burned. Forest bird populations are often limited by the lack of suitable nesting cavities, not by food supply. Analysis of local bird species typically reveals missing hole-nesters. Key principles:

- Nest boxes placed in the open attract sparrows; woodland nesters prefer forest interior placement
- Hollow logs can be left standing upright or laid flat in the forest
- Reference books provide critical entrance sizes for different bird species
- The practice provides both ecological benefit and a potential saleable product

### Forest Fire Management Priorities

The highest priority in any forest is removing fallen trunks from contact with live trees. These "ladder fuels" allow ground fires to reach and scar the bases of living trees. Old chestnut logs, in particular, lodge against standing trees and resist rotting — in fire-prone areas like North Carolina, they eventually contribute to wildfire rather than decomposing.

### Wild Rose Hedges and Livestock

Wild rose hedges serve as effective livestock barriers while producing rose hips for vitamin C. Geese can be trained to run along wild rose hedges for pest control, creating an additional productive interaction.
