# Principle 2: Catch and Store Energy (Detailed Analysis)

From "Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability" by David Holmgren.

## Core Definition

We live in a world of unprecedented wealth resulting from the harvesting of the enormous storages of fossil fuels created by the earth over billions of years. In financial language, we have been living by consuming global capital in a reckless manner that would send any business bankrupt.

We need to learn how to save and reinvest most of the wealth that we are currently consuming or wasting so that our children and descendants might have a reasonable life.

This principle deals with the capture and long-term storage of energy, that is, savings and investment to build natural and human capital.

Holmgren's use of financial language here is deliberate and illuminating. Just as a prudent investor lives off the interest from their capital while preserving the principal, a sustainable society must live off the annual flows of solar energy while preserving the planetary capital -- the soils, forests, wetlands, and biodiversity -- that capture and store that energy. Our current civilization is doing the opposite: burning capital as if it were income.

## Energy Laws

Throughout the universe, energy is always spreading from centres of concentration to vacant regions. In addition, energy of high quality degrades into lower-quality forms, thus reducing its power to affect change. This tendency to disorder and eventual death is called entropy.

However, self-organising systems (primarily living ones), can capture and transform a limited proportion of energy they absorb. This energy is then held in storages of varying form and durability for use in self-maintenance, growth and capture of more energy. This stored energy is generally higher in quality than the source from which it was derived.

For living systems, available energy flows are mostly erratic, limited in quantity, and low in quality. Living systems that are "designed" to optimise the efficiency of energy transformation and storage tend to prevail through evolution.

The second law of thermodynamics (entropy) is often cited as evidence that sustainability is impossible -- that all systems must eventually run down. Holmgren acknowledges entropy but points out that living systems have evolved sophisticated strategies for temporarily reversing it: capturing solar energy and storing it in increasingly concentrated and useful forms.

A tree is a perfect example: it captures diffuse sunlight and stores it as concentrated chemical energy in wood. A forest captures the energy of thousands of trees and stores it in soil humus. A civilization that manages forests wisely can draw on these storages for centuries without depleting them -- provided it reinvests enough energy to maintain the system's capacity for renewal.

## The Modern Pattern

In modern affluent societies, energy capture and storage has ceased to be a major concern. No food or fuel is stored in the house and purchasing power is dependent on credit, which itself is dependent on permanent employment.

Economic rationalism has led to the decline of large-scale stores of food, fuels, materials, essential spare parts and permanent employment in the interests of economic efficiency, which in turn increases the likelihood of disruption and even disaster.

The "just in time" delivery systems that dominate modern supply chains are exquisitely efficient but extremely fragile. They assume continuous availability of cheap energy, stable political conditions, and functioning financial systems. Remove any of these assumptions, and the system collapses within days, as the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated when empty supermarket shelves appeared almost overnight.

Holmgren's principle urges us to rebuild the storages that modern efficiency has eliminated: pantries, root cellars, woodsheds, seed banks, water tanks, and the biological storages of fertile soil and diverse ecosystems.

## Sources of Energy

Important sources of energy currently poorly used but commonly available:

- Solar energy: simple drying of wood and crops, passive solar design, water heaters and photo-electric panels

- Wind energy: pumping and power generation

- Biomass: sustainable management of trees and forests for fuel and construction materials

- Run-off water: irrigation, aquaculture and power generation

We are surrounded by wastes from agriculture, forestry and industry that can be used for soil improvement, fuel and animal feed. The slogan "the problem is the solution" helps us recognise opportunities to harvest sources of energy specific to our site and situation.

The principle of catching and storing energy connects directly to [[keyline-design]] and alexander shulgin practices that capture and store water in the landscape. Water is a form of stored solar energy -- it arrives as rain (powered by the sun's heat engine) and can be stored in soil, ponds, and aquifers for later use. Every drop of water retained on the land is a unit of energy captured and stored.

Similarly, [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] represents the capture and storage of nutrients that would otherwise be lost to leaching or volatilization. A healthy soil food web acts as a nutrient bank, holding fertility in biological forms that are stable in the soil and available to plants on demand.

## Design Strategies for Energy Storage

Effective application of this principle requires thinking in terms of multiple scales and timeframes. At the household scale, it means storing food, water, seeds, and fuel. At the landscape scale, it means building soil, planting trees, creating ponds, and designing water-harvesting earthworks. At the community scale, it means developing local food systems, renewable energy infrastructure, and shared resource banks.

The key insight is that energy stored in biological systems (trees, soil, perennial plants) is more resilient than energy stored in technological systems (batteries, fuel tanks, silos). Biological storages are self-maintaining, self-replicating, and capable of recovering from disturbance. They represent the most durable form of capital available to human societies.

This principle is foundational to the entire framework of [[permaculture-water-harvesting-earthworks]] and is elaborated in [[permaculture-water-harvesting-earthworks]]. It serves as the economic foundation upon which all other permaculture principles rest: before we can produce, we must store; before we can share, we must save.

## Key Points

1. This principle from David Holmgren's permaculture framework highlights how thoughtful design can create self-sustaining systems.
2. The principle connects directly to broader permaculture ethics: earth care, people care, and fair share.
3. Applying this principle requires careful observation of natural patterns before implementing design changes.
4. Energy cycling and resource conservation are core themes embedded in this design principle.
5. The principle encourages designers to think in terms of systems and relationships rather than isolated elements.
6. Small, slow solutions are preferred over large-scale interventions — this principle embodies that approach.
7. Integration rather than segregation of elements increases system efficiency and resilience.
8. Edge effects and marginal spaces often hold the greatest potential when this principle is properly applied.
9. The principle supports the broader goal of reducing dependence on non-renewable resources and external inputs.
10. Real-world application of this principle has been demonstrated across diverse climates and contexts worldwide.
