# Garden Seed Sourcing and Storage

**Garden seed sourcing and storage** encompasses the practices of selecting, purchasing, evaluating, and preserving seeds for successful vegetable gardening. As covered in Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts*, these topics are critical to food-production gardening, where reliability and economy directly influence yields and self-sufficiency.

## The Commercial Seed Industry

The commercially produced seed business operates on a large scale, with most garden seeds grown by specialized farms and distributed through retail packets and mail-order catalogs. Solomon emphasizes that seed quality varies considerably between suppliers, and gardeners should not assume uniformity across brands. The unpredictability of seed packet contents is a recurring concern: germination rates, seed counts, variety purity, and even correct identification can differ from what the packaging suggests.

This unpredictability is especially pronounced among discount and mass-market seed packets, where older stock may be relabeled or where variety mixing can occur during packaging. Gardeners who depend on specific variety characteristics—such as days to maturity, disease resistance, or flavor profile—should be wary of seed from unknown sources and prioritize companies with reputations for accuracy and freshness.

Key considerations when buying seeds include:

- **Germination standards**: Commercial seed in the United States is subject to germination regulations. Federal and state laws require that seed packets display minimum germination percentages and test dates. Solomon provides germination charts showing expected rates for common vegetables, which often differ from the optimistic figures printed on packaging.
- **Seed trials**: Conducting personal seed trials helps gardeners identify which varieties perform best in their specific climate and soil. Solomon recommends small-scale comparative plantings before committing to a variety at scale.
- **Identifying defective seeds**: Visual inspection can reveal problems such as cracked coats, discoloration, mold, or insect damage. Seeds that appear shriveled or unusually small often have reduced viability.

## Selecting a Seed Company

Solomon advises careful selection of seed suppliers. Notable companies referenced include **SESE (Southern Exposure Seed Exchange)** and **Territorial Seed Company**, both recognized for regional adaptation and reliable germination. When evaluating a seed company, gardeners should consider:

- Regional suitability of offered varieties
- Germination guarantees and customer feedback
- Breadth of open-pollinated vs. hybrid offerings
- Willingness to provide detailed sowing and growing instructions
- Seed packet freshness and turnover rates

Mail-order seed companies remain a primary sourcing channel for serious food gardeners, as they offer wider variety selection than typical retail outlets and often provide varieties better suited to intensive or survival gardening contexts. Solomon encourages gardeners to seek out smaller regional companies that trial their own stock, as these tend to offer better-adapted varieties than large national distributors.

## Seed Quality and Hybrid vs. Open-Pollinated Varieties

Understanding the distinction between **hybrid seed** and **open-pollinated (OP) seed** is fundamental. Hybrid seeds are produced by controlled cross-pollination of two inbred parent lines; they offer vigor and uniformity but do not breed true—saved seed from hybrids will not reliably reproduce the parent characteristics. Open-pollinated varieties, by contrast, stabilize over generations and allow gardeners to save their own seed.

Solomon discusses **self-sterile varieties**, which require cross-pollination with another plant of the same variety to set viable seed. This has implications both for seed saving and for planting arrangements in the garden. Isolating self-pollinators from related varieties is important to maintain genetic purity when saving seed.

**Growing your own seed** is presented as an advanced but valuable skill, particularly for gardeners interested in long-term self-sufficiency or survival gardening. The process requires knowledge of each crop's pollination biology, isolation distances, and seed harvesting and drying techniques.

## Importing Seeds

Importing seeds from other countries introduces additional regulatory and practical considerations. Phytosanitary requirements, customs inspections, and potential restrictions on certain species or varieties can complicate international sourcing. Solomon notes that imported seeds may also carry risks of introducing new pests or diseases, making careful inspection essential upon receipt. Gardeners considering imported seed should verify that the variety is legal in their jurisdiction and that the supplier complies with all applicable quarantine regulations.

## Seed Storage

Proper **storage of seeds** is essential for maintaining viability from one season to the next, and potentially for several years. Solomon outlines the following principles:

- **Temperature and humidity**: Seeds store best in cool, dry conditions. Lower temperatures slow metabolic activity and extend storage life. Ideal storage environments are consistently below 50°F (10°C) with low relative humidity.
- **Moisture control**: Seeds must be thoroughly dried before storage. Excess moisture promotes fungal growth and premature germination. Airtight containers with desiccant packets are recommended for long-term storage.
- **Darkness**: Exposure to light can degrade some seed types over time. Opaque containers or dark storage locations are preferable.
- **Longevity varies by species**: Some vegetables (e.g., onions, parsnips) lose viability quickly—often within one to two years—while others (e.g., tomatoes, beans) can remain viable for five years or more under proper conditions.

**Storage of produce over winter and seeds** is discussed together, as both require careful environmental management. For survival gardening contexts, maintaining a viable seed bank is as important as preserving the harvest itself.

Solomon also notes that seed viability declines gradually rather than abruptly. A germination rate of 80% at purchase may drop to 60% after one year of suboptimal storage, meaning gardeners should increase sowing density proportionally as seed ages. Keeping a simple log of purchase dates and observed germination rates helps calibrate future plantings and avoid costly under-seeding.

For gardeners pursuing survival gardening strategies, Solomon recommends maintaining a diverse seed bank that includes staple calorie crops alongside vegetables. Rotating seed stock—using older seed first and replacing it with fresh purchases—ensures the bank remains viable without waste.

## Germination and Presprouting

**Soil temperature** is a primary factor governing germination speed and success. Each crop has an optimal soil temperature range; planting too early in cold soil leads to delayed, uneven emergence and increased losses to rot and soil-borne pathogens. Solomon provides guidance on reading soil temperature and timing sowings accordingly.

**Presprouting**—germinating seeds under controlled conditions before planting them out—can improve reliability, especially for crops with erratic germination or when soil conditions are marginal. Sprouting in the lab (or on a damp paper towel at home) allows the gardener to identify viable seeds before committing them to garden beds. Only sprouted seeds are planted, eliminating the uncertainty of blank spots in rows.

Solomon cautions that presprouted seeds require gentle handling. The emerging radicle is fragile and easily damaged during transplant. Presprouting works best with larger seeds (peas, beans, squash) and is less practical for very fine seed like lettuce or carrots, which are difficult to handle individually once sprouted.

## Sowing Techniques

Successful **sowing** requires attention to several variables:

- **Depth in the seedbed**: Planting depth is critical and varies by seed size. A general rule is to plant at a depth roughly two to three times the seed's diameter. Planting too deep prevents emergence; planting too shallow leaves seeds vulnerable to drying and birds.
- **Overseeding and resowing**: Because germination is never 100%, gardeners typically overseed slightly and thin later. If emergence is poor, prompt resowing can recover the planting window.
- **Raised beds**: Raised beds warm faster in spring, improving germination for early plantings. They also provide better drainage, which benefits seedling emergence in wet conditions.
- **Watering newly planted seeds**: Consistent moisture is essential during germination. The seedbed should be kept evenly moist but not waterlogged. Drip irrigation and fine-nozzle watering are preferred over heavy spraying, which can displace small seeds.

## Plant Spacing

Solomon provides a detailed **plant spacing chart** and explains the rationale behind recommended distances. Proper spacing ensures adequate light interception, root room, air circulation, and nutrient access for each plant.

Two broad approaches exist:

- **Intensive (close) spacing**: Maximizes yield per unit area but demands high soil fertility, consistent watering, and careful management. More common in raised-bed and market-garden systems.
- **Separated (wide) row spacing**: Easier weeding and cultivation, better drought tolerance per plant, and lower input requirements. Solomon generally favors wider spacing for low-input, self-sufficient gardening.

**Uniform rows** facilitate efficient weeding, thinning, and harvesting. **Isolating self-pollinators** at appropriate distances prevents unwanted cross-pollination when seed saving is a goal.

## Thinning

**Thinning** is the practice of removing excess seedlings to achieve proper spacing. Solomon stresses its importance for root crops such as beets and carrots, where crowded plants produce small, deformed roots. The thinning method matters: pulling disturbs remaining roots; cutting at soil level with scissors is often preferable for sensitive crops. Thinned produce from crops like beets can be consumed as microgreens, recovering some value from the thinning process.

Timing of thinning is also important. Initial thinning should occur once seedlings have developed their first true leaves, with a second pass a week or two later to reach final spacing. Delaying thinning too long causes root competition that stunts all plants in the row, even those that remain.

## Saving on Seed Costs

For gardeners managing tight budgets, Solomon recommends several strategies to reduce seed expenses:

- Buy from reputable mail-order companies offering bulk pricing
- Save seed from open-pollinated varieties
- Practice presprouting to avoid wasting garden space on non-viable seed
- Store leftover seed properly for use in subsequent seasons
- Share orders with other gardeners to meet minimum purchase quantities
