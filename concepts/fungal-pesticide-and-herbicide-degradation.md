# Fungal Pesticide and Herbicide Degradation

Fungi play important roles in the degradation of agricultural pesticides and herbicides in soil environments. Their mycelial growth maximizes both physical/mechanical and enzymatic contact with the environment, giving them advantages over bacteria for sorption-limited contaminants. The extensive hyphal networks produced by fungi can penetrate soil aggregates and access contaminants that are otherwise poorly bioavailable to smaller microbial cells.

The significance of fungal degradation of pesticides has grown as regulatory pressure has increased on pesticide residues in soil and water. Many synthetic pesticides are structurally related to natural lignin-derived compounds, making them susceptible to the broad-specificity oxidative enzymes produced by ligninolytic fungi. See [[mycoremediation-pesticides]] for broader coverage of this topic.

## Pyrethroid Insecticide Degradation

Photostable synthetic pyrethroids constitute about 25% of total pesticides used worldwide. They are relatively nonpersistent in mineral soils (half-life 5-90 days) but more persistent in less-fertile soils and under anaerobic conditions. Ester cleavage is the first major transformation step, generating non-insecticidal polar products. Soil culture enrichments with radiolabeled permethrin have been used to isolate degradative microorganisms. Fusarium oxysporum possesses esterase-amidase activity relevant to pyrethroid transformation. Saccharomyces cerevisiae degrades residual fenvalerate and permethrin, producing 3-phenoxybenzyl alcohol and dihalovinyl acid metabolites.

The esterase-mediated pathway for pyrethroid degradation is particularly important because it represents a detoxification mechanism that generates relatively benign products. In contrast, some bacterial degradation pathways produce intermediate compounds that may retain insecticidal activity or exhibit other toxicological concerns.

## Chlorinated Phenoxyacetate Herbicides

2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, and MCPA have been widely used since the 1940s. Several fungi, including Aspergillus and Penicillium spp., degrade 2,4-D through ester hydrolysis and ring hydroxylation. Mycorrhizal fungi degrade 2,4-D through incorporation of herbicide carbon into tissue rather than decomposition (Donnelly, Entry and Crawford, 1993).

Phanerochaete chrysosporium degrades 2,4,5-T in both nitrogen-limited liquid cultures and contaminated soil supplemented with corn cob mixture. P. chrysosporium also degrades mixtures of 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T at higher rates than when tested individually, demonstrating broad degradative ability relevant to contaminated site remediation.

MCPA is metabolized by hydroxylation to 4-chloro-5-hydroxy-2-methylphenoxyacetic acid by Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium spp. Consortia of bacteria and fungi can break down MCPA aerobically, though toxic 5-chloro-o-cresol accumulates under anaerobic conditions.

The ability of P. chrysosporium to degrade herbicide mixtures more efficiently than individual compounds has practical implications for field remediation, where contaminated sites rarely contain single contaminants. This synergistic degradation may reflect induction of broader enzymatic capabilities when multiple substrates are present.

## Phenylamide Herbicides

### Acetanilides

Chaetomium globosum uses alachlor as sole carbon and energy source. Rhizoctonia solani degrades alachlor only under co-metabolic conditions. White-rot fungi Ceriporiopsis subvermispora, Phlebia tremellosa, and P. chrysosporium achieve low (~10%) co-metabolic alachlor transformation. Metolachlor is dechlorinated by white-rot fungi to varying degrees without complete degradation. Propachlor is degraded by Aspergillus ustus, Fusarium solani, F. oxysporum, Penicillium sp., and Trichoderma viride.

### Phenylureas

About 25 phenylurea herbicides are marketed, including linuron, diuron, monuron, neburon, and chlorbromuron. They persist 4-18 months and are specific photosynthesis inhibitors. Many fungi use these herbicides as sole nitrogen or carbon sources:

- Aspergillus nidulans: most effective for linuron degradation
- Cunninghamella echinulata: oxidative dealkylation of five phenylureas including diuron
- Rhizoctonia solani: over 70% degradation of chlortoluron, isoproturon, and diuron in liquid culture

The major detoxification mechanism involves successive dealkylation or N-dealkoxylation, producing 3,4-dichloroaniline as the principal metabolite.

## Organophosphorus Insecticides

Organophosphorus compounds such as chlorpyrifos, diazinon, and parathion are widely used insecticides that can be degraded by various fungal species. Key degradation pathways include:

- **Hydrolysis**: Cleavage of the phosphoester bond by fungal phosphotriesterases, producing less toxic dialkyl phosphates and phenolic derivatives
- **Oxidative degradation**: Cytochrome P450 enzymes in fungi can oxidize organophosphorus compounds, altering their structure and toxicity
- **Co-metabolic transformation**: White-rot fungi can transform organophosphorus compounds as secondary substrates during ligninolytic activity

Trichoderma species have shown particular effectiveness against organophosphorus insecticides, with some strains capable of using these compounds as phosphorus sources under nutrient-limiting conditions.

VX and Russian VX (RVX), and the insecticide analog diisopropylamiton (containing phosphothiolate P-S bonds) were rapidly and completely oxidized by P. ostreatus in the presence of ABTS mediator.

## Triazine Herbicides

Atrazine and simazine are widely used triazine herbicides that persist in soil for months to years. Fungal degradation of triazines involves:

- **Dealkylation**: Removal of alkyl side chains from the triazine ring by fungal cytochrome P450 systems
- **Dechlorination**: Reductive removal of chlorine substituents, primarily under microaerophilic conditions
- **Ring cleavage**: Complete mineralization is rare but has been demonstrated in some fungal-bacterial consortia

P. chrysosporium degraded 48% of atrazine after 14 days in nitrogen-limited medium. Ethyl carbons were mineralized to CO2; ring-labelled atrazine was largely unaffected. Products: hydroxylated and N-dealkylated metabolites. Pleurotus pulmonarius produced a novel metabolite: 2-chloro-4-ethylamino-6-(1-hydroxyisopropyl)amino-1,3,5-triazine.

Phanerochaete chrysosporium and Trametes versicolor have demonstrated atrazine transformation capabilities, though complete mineralization typically requires complementary bacterial activity.

## Key Principles

- Fungi oxidize herbicide C-C and C-Cl bonds non-specifically while growing with other carbon co-substrates
- Acylamidase enzymes cleave phenylamide herbicides, forming chlorinated aniline used for growth
- The wide variety of herbicide structural groups requires different catabolic enzyme systems not usually found within a single organism
- Fungal mycelial networks provide advantage in accessing sorbed contaminants
- Bioavailability limits degradation; sorption and partitioning in soils readily occurs
- Ligninolytic enzyme systems (laccase, MnP, LiP) provide non-specific oxidation capabilities that attack diverse pesticide structures
- Co-metabolic degradation is common and may require supplementation with primary substrates

## Chlorinated Insecticides

Despite bans or restrictions, organochlorine insecticides persist in soils, water, and sediments due to bioaccumulation and chemical stability:

- **Lindane**: P. chrysosporium degraded up to 23% of radiolabelled lindane to CO2 in 30 days in liquid cultures and 60 days in soil-corn cob cultures. Degradation attributed to P450-monooxygenase, not lignin-modifying enzymes.
- **Chlordane**: Up to 23% degradation to CO2 by P. chrysosporium under the same conditions.
- **Heptachlor**: Extensively degraded by P. chrysosporium, P. eryngii, P. florida, and P. sajor-caju.
- **Aldrin and mirex**: Did not undergo appreciable mineralization but showed substantial biotransformation with intermediate metabolites detected.

## DDT Degradation

DDT is one of the most persistent environmental pollutants. Its recalcitrance is attributed to the trichloromethyl group:

- P. chrysosporium was one of the first organisms shown to degrade DDT (Bumpus et al., 1985)
- Substantial mineralization of radiolabelled DDT to CO2 observed
- First metabolite: DDD (1,1-dichloro-2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)ethane), which further degraded on continued incubation
- Soil-corn cob systems supported DDT degradation; cellulose served as growth substrate
- DDT degradation, similar to lignin degradation, required an additional carbon source

## Role of Cytochrome P450 vs. Ligninolytic Enzymes

A key distinction in fungal pesticide degradation:
- **Lignin-modifying enzymes (LiP, MnP, laccase)**: Non-specific oxidation of aromatic structures
- **Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases**: More specific oxidation; involved in lindane and chlordane degradation
- Many pesticide transformations involve both systems working together or sequentially

## Soil Application

- Soil-corn cob cultures supported fungal growth and pesticide degradation
- DDT degradation required cellulose as a growth substrate
- Amendment with straw, hay, or wood improved colonization and degradation
- Field application requires formulation approaches discussed in [[fungal-formulation-in-situ-bioremediation]]

## Environmental Factors Affecting Degradation

Several environmental variables influence the rate and extent of fungal pesticide degradation:

- **Soil organic matter**: Higher organic matter increases pesticide sorption, reducing bioavailability but potentially providing co-substrate for co-metabolic degradation
- **Moisture content**: Optimal moisture supports fungal growth and enzyme production; waterlogged conditions shift degradation toward anaerobic pathways
- **Temperature**: Mesophilic fungi perform optimally at 20-30°C; temperature extremes reduce enzymatic activity
- **pH**: Affects both fungal physiology and pesticide speciation, with implications for degradation kinetics
