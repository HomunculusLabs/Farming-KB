# Earth Resources and Materials

Whenever earth is moved during construction of dams, ponds, terraces, or foundations, excavation invariably reveals materials already sorted by nature into specific and useful categories. To mix them indiscriminately is to set back the clock thousands of years. The supervising designer or property owner should follow every excavator and carefully set aside each material type, as the excavation may pay for itself on any one of the resources recovered.

## Topsoil

Topsoil is the often dark, root-filled living surface of the earth — the most valuable material uncovered during any excavation. It must be carefully stripped off, piled aside, and later returned over other material onto banks, over paddocks and fields, and even below water in ponds.

Topsoil is typically only 6–18 cm deep. Where deeper deposits are found, the excess can be spread on poorer soils to improve their productivity, or pocketed (placed in planting holes) for trees in poor soil areas. Topsoil is the foundation of all terrestrial productivity and should never be buried or wasted.

## Peat

Excavation in marshes, bogs, or lowlands may reveal 1–9 m (3–30 feet) of semi-compacted fibrous plant material. Peat should be stockpiled, with about 0.6 m (2 feet) returned to the surface as topsoil. The peat stockpile has multiple valuable uses:

- **Soil improvement** — mixing with sands and loams creates additional topsoil
- **Nursery [[cervantes-green-algae-growing-medium]]** — an excellent fine-textured medium for [[permaculture]] systems:

- **Road construction** — heaped gravel makes good roads and driveways
- **Drainage** — gravel-filled drains provide reliable water movement
- **Soft surfaces** — superior to lawns for sun-bathing areas (no green stains)
- **Concrete** — angular gravel produces strong concrete
- **Water filtration** — smooth gravel makes good water filters and enzyme columns
- **Tree mulch** — gravel (along with cinder and crushed pumice) serves as an effective mulch
- **Heat storage** — 19 mm-size angular gravel is optimal for [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] heat stores, where air from solar attics or glasshouses is blown through a wall or tank filled with gravel

## Shingle

Shingle (coarser than gravel) has specific uses:

- **Road base** — good underlayer beneath gravel on roads
- **Drainage** — effective in drainage systems
- **Coarse filtration** — for filter beds and [[mollison-designers-natural-swimming-pools-and-water-purification]]
- **Desert water condensation** — an excellent mulch for condensing atmospheric moisture in arid environments
- **[[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]]** — a refuge for snails and decomposer organisms
- **Natural pool filtration** — filter beds for chemical-free swimming pools

## Slate

Slate is a versatile [[bamboo-as-building-material]] from any rock that can be split:

- **Flooring** — durable, attractive floor surfaces
- **Roofing** — traditional and long-lasting roof material
- **Billiard tables** — the premium playing surface
- **Walls and houses** — split stone for structural and decorative use
- **Garden paths** — natural, slip-resistant pathway material
- **Floor tiles** — interior and exterior applications

## Boulders

Boulders provide multiple functions in designed landscapes:

- **Coarse mulch** — large rocks moderate soil temperature and reduce evaporation
- **Wildlife refuge** — habitat for lizards, insects, and small mammals
- **Walling and windbreaks** — structural and wind-protection roles, gaining and radiating heat
- **Tools** — some boulders serve as pounders, mortars and pestles, weights, anchors, and ballast

## The Designer's Role During Excavation

A bulldozer or excavator is a source of information, and making a pond is a rare chance to explore, explain, and store useful information about the site. On complex sites (old volcanoes, shorelines, swamps, or desert pediments), excavation allows confirmation or denial of theories about geological formation and history.

Experience on many sites develops predictive capacity for similar sites, building confidence in finding the right locations for ponds, excavated houses, silos, silage pits, and earth resources. The designer should never leave the site when earth is being moved — it is very expensive to bring machines back for fine adjustments or to sort out mixed materials. Excavation can pay for itself through any one of the materials recovered.

## Tracks and Signs

Freshly moved earth and puddled clays reveal a cross-section of earth life. After rain is an ideal time to observe footprints, scrapes, and tracks of species on site, from beetles to elephants. A species list obtained this way adds to basic site information resources and may warn of future troubles from ground foragers. Many cryptic animals avoid traps but can be detected by their tracks in fresh earth.

## Shards and Fossils

Under the earth lie the stories of prior life — camps of ancestors revealed by shells, tools, shards, ashes, and bones. Buried trees provide growth rings that tell stories of older climates and air quality. Pottery shards may encode astronomical or technological data, and bones reveal past diseases and conflicts. These materials have more lessons for understanding landscape history than any futurology, since we can only look forward by first understanding where we have been.
