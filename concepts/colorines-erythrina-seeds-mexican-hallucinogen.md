# Colorines (Erythrina spp.)

Colorines is the name given to several species of Erythrina that may be used
as hallucinogens in some parts of Mexico. The bright red beans of these
plants resemble mescal beans ([[mescal-bean-sophora-secundiflora-vision-dance]]), long used as a narcotic
in northern Mexico and the American Southwest. Both types of beans are
sometimes sold mixed together in herb markets, and the mescal bean plant is
sometimes called by the same common name, colorin. Some species of Erythrina
contain alkaloids of the isoquinoline type, which elicit activity resembling
that of curare or arrow poisons, but no alkaloids known to possess
hallucinogenic properties have yet been found in these seeds.

## The Colorines-Mescal Bean Confusion

One of the most significant aspects of the colorines story is the persistent
confusion between Erythrina seeds and true mescal beans (Sophora
secundiflora). Both types of seeds are bright red, visually similar in
size and shape, and sold in the same herb markets in Mexico. This similarity
has led to overlapping common names, with both plants sometimes being called
"colorin," and has created considerable confusion in the ethnobotanical
literature about which plant is actually being used in traditional practices.

The mescal bean ([[sophora-secundiflora-entity]]) has well-documented use as a
narcotic and hallucinogen in the Red Bean Dance of at least 12 tribes in
northern Mexico, New Mexico, and Texas. Archaeological evidence traces this
use back to at least 1500 B.C. The alkaloid cytisine in mescal beans is
highly toxic, causing nausea, convulsions, and death from asphyxiation.
The visual similarity between mescal beans and Erythrina colorines raises
the possibility that some historical or ethnographic accounts of "mescal
bean" use may actually refer to Erythrina seeds, or vice versa.

## Botanical Description of Erythrina

The genus Erythrina comprises approximately 50 species belonging to the bean
family, Leguminosae (also known as Fabaceae). These plants grow in the
tropics and subtropics of both hemispheres and include both [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]].
Many Erythrina species are known for their striking red flowers, which have
earned the genus common names such as coral tree and flame tree. The seeds
are typically bright red with a black spot at the base, making them visually
distinctive and easily recognized.

Erythrina species are widely planted as [[hamilton-ornamental-trees]] in tropical and
subtropical regions worldwide, valued for their spectacular floral displays.
Several species also have important uses in traditional agriculture, where
they serve as shade trees, windbreaks, nitrogen fixers, and fodder plants.
The genus has a broad distribution across Africa, the Americas, Asia, and
the [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]].

## Chemistry: Isoquinoline Alkaloids

Some species of Erythrina contain alkaloids of the isoquinoline type. These
compounds elicit activity resembling that of curare or arrow poisons, which
is significant because it indicates that the seeds possess genuine
pharmacological activity, even if that activity is not specifically
hallucinogenic. Isoquinoline alkaloids are a large and diverse class of
plant compounds that include both medically important drugs (such as
morphine and codeine from opium poppy) and potent toxins (such as the
curare alkaloids used in arrow poisons).

The specific isoquinoline alkaloids found in Erythrina species include
erythrina alkaloids such as erythrinine, erythrartine, and others that are
structurally distinct from the classical hallucinogenic alkaloids. The
curare-like activity of these compounds suggests they act primarily on the
neuromuscular system rather than on the central nervous system pathways
associated with hallucinogenesis.

## The Question of Hallucinogenic Activity

Despite the traditional association of colorines with hallucinogenic use in
some parts of Mexico, Schultes explicitly states that no alkaloids known to
possess hallucinogenic properties have yet been found in Erythrina seeds.
This is an important caveat that distinguishes colorines from many other
plants discussed in the Golden Guide. The possibility remains that the
hallucinogenic reputation of colorines derives from confusion with true
mescal beans (Sophora secundiflora) rather than from genuine psychoactive
properties of Erythrina seeds.
