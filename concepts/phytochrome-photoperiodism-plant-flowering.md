# Phytochrome and Photoperiodism in Plant Flowering

## Overview

[[phytochrome-photoperiodism]] is a photoreceptor pigment mediating plant responses to red and
far-red light, central to [[photoperiodism-in-plants]] — the mechanism by which plants
measure night length to determine flowering time. Discovered through study of
the mutant tobacco strain [[chamovitz-maryland-mammoth-photoperiodism-discovery]], photoperiodism revealed that
plants measure continuous darkness rather than [[coleman-winter-day-length-and-persephone-months]], using a molecular
light-activated switch toggled by alternating red and far-red light.

## Discovery: Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a tobacco strain that grew to 4.5
meters with nearly a hundred leaves but rarely flowered. Named [[chamovitz-maryland-mammoth-photoperiodism-discovery]], it prevented seed harvest for the next crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the USDA discovered that
simply limiting light exposure — moving plants to a dark shed each afternoon —
was enough to trigger flowering. Exposed to long summer days, Maryland Mammoth
grew vegetatively; given shorter days, it flowered. This phenomenon, called
[[photoperiodism-in-plants]], provided the first evidence that plants actively measure
light to regulate developmental transitions.

## Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Garner and Allard's work led to classifying plants by flowering response:

**Short-day plants** flower when dark periods exceed a critical threshold:
chrysanthemums, soybeans, poinsettias, strawberries, and Maryland Mammoth
tobacco. They naturally flower in late summer or autumn as days shorten.

**Long-day plants** flower when dark periods are shorter than a critical
threshold: irises, barley, spinach, lettuce, and radishes. They flower in
spring or early summer as days lengthen.

**Day-neutral plants** (tomatoes, corn, cucumbers) flower based on age or
temperature regardless of photoperiod.

## Measuring Night, Not Day

Experiments around World War II revealed that plants measure continuous
darkness, not day length. Scientists could manipulate flowering by briefly
flashing lights during the middle of the night:

- Short-day plants kept from flowering by a few minutes of light at midnight
- Long-day plants induced to flower in winter by a midnight light flash

This "night break" technique proved the continuous dark period is what plants
measure. Commercially, chrysanthemum farmers use this to delay flowering:
lighting plants for minutes each night through autumn, then stopping two weeks
before desired harvest for simultaneous blooming.

## Red and Far-Red: The Molecular Switch

Scientists found plants responded only to red light during the night. Blue or
green flashes had no effect, but seconds of red sufficed to disrupt night
measurement. In the 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues discovered that
far-red light (wavelengths slightly longer than red, barely visible at dusk)
could completely cancel the red light effect:

1. Red pulse at night → long-day plant flowers
2. Far-red pulse after → plant does NOT flower
3. Another red pulse → plant flowers again
4. Far-red pulse → no flowering

A few seconds of either color sufficed. This red/far-red reversibility acts as
a molecular toggle switch. On a philosophical level, the plant "remembers" the
last color it saw.

## Phytochrome: The Receptor

By the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler demonstrated that a single photoreceptor
mediates both effects. Named [[phytochrome-photoperiodism]] ("plant color"), it exists in two
interconvertible forms:

- **Pr**: Inactive form absorbing red light (660 nm), converts to Pfr
- **Pfr**: Biologically active form absorbing far-red light (730 nm), converts
  back to Pr

signaling the plant to deactivate. Morning red light reactivates it. Pfr slowly
reverts to Pr in darkness over several hours — this natural decay is how plants
