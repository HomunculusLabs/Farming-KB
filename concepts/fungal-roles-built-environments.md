# Fungal Roles in the Built Environment [[fungal-adaptations-environmental-gradients]] [[fungal-biogeochemistry]]

Fungi are major agents of biodeterioration in human-built structures, causing billions of dollars in damage annually to buildings, ships, and stored materials. The study of these organisms bridges mycology, materials science, and building physics. Content drawn from Chapter 14 by N.P. Money (Miami University), adapted from "Carpet Monsters and Killer Spores" (Oxford UP, 2004).

## Dry Rot Fungi

### Serpula lacrymans
The most notorious building-decay fungus, *Serpula lacrymans* (formerly *Merulius lacrymans*), causes "dry rot" -- a misnomer, as the fungus requires significant moisture (typically 20-30% wood moisture content). Key features include:

- **Strands**: Exploratory structures consisting of a parent hypha core surrounded by thinner hyphae and tendrils. Strands develop from nutrient exhaustion and disassemble at nutrient "oases."
- **Rhizomorphs**: Larger, waterproofed structures with a central pipe for water and nutrient transport. *S. lacrymans* rhizomorphs are among the most complex fungal transport structures, allowing the fungus to conduct water from moisture sources to dry wood. These rhizomorphs can pipe water across meters of dry building material, effectively bringing the moisture to the wood rather than requiring wet wood.
- **Air passage**: Rhizomorphs contain air channels that allow oxygen supply to avoid suffocation in anoxic wood, a critical adaptation for colonizing building timbers.
- **Brown cubical rot**: *S. lacrymans* digests cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving lignin largely intact, producing the characteristic cracking of wood into brown cubes.
- **Basidiome formation**: The fruiting body (basidiome) is a fleshy, sheet-like structure that produces tremendous numbers of spores. Basidiome development requires high humidity, and sporulation represents the primary mechanism for spreading to new buildings.

### Meruliporia incrassata
The sister species *Meruliporia incrassata* is a major structural pest in North America, particularly in California and the southeastern United States. It shares the rhizomorph-forming habit with *S. lacrymans* but has a different geographic distribution and evolutionary origin.

The Glaviano case in California exemplifies the destructive potential: Joseph and Jodeanna Glaviano's home was severely damaged by *Meruliporia* without their knowledge until their hardwood floor collapsed, revealing complete destruction of the sub-flooring. The couple successfully challenged Allstate Insurance in court, with the 9th Circuit Court ruling that losses due to "collapse caused by hidden decay" were covered (2002).

### Evolutionary Origins
Both *S. lacrymans* and *M. incrassata* are thought to have evolved from forest fungi that colonized human structures. *S. lacrymans* has been found on only two occasions in the wild (in the Himalayas and in California), suggesting it is now almost entirely a building-adapted species.

## Historical Impact on Naval Architecture

Dry rot has had an enormous impact on naval history:
- **Mary Rose** (sank 1545): Henry VII's flagship probably lost due to dry-rotted timber
- **Royal George** (sank 1782): Wood so rotten it failed to hold a nail, sinking with several hundred crew at Portsmouth
- **Ship lifespan decline**: Warships expected to last 25-30 years in the 17th century saw average life cut to 12 years by the 18th century, dwindling to "no duration" after the Battle of Trafalgar (1805)
- **Queen Charlotte**: The 110-gun battleship deteriorated so swiftly during construction that the navy rebuilt her in 1810 before she could sail, at a cost of GBP 287,837 (estimated US$2.5 billion at modern prices)
- **Nelson's Victory**: Like other ships from the Napoleonic Wars (1799-1815), was heavily colonized by wood-rotting fungi
- Ships effectively became disposable items, pleasing neither crews nor politicians

## Dry Rot Remediation History

### Kyan's Process (1832)
John Howard Kyan patented a treatment using corrosive sublimate (mercuric chloride) to preserve timber. Michael Faraday tested this at the Admiralty's "fungus pit" at Woolwich -- timber was submerged in mercuric chloride, dried, and left in the damp pit for 5 years. Faraday concluded the treatment rendered wood "as indestructible as charred timber" but raised concerns about toxic atmospheric effects and noted it would be "found useful in a far higher degree, in the construction of cottages and outhouses, than palaces." The method was not widely adopted.

### Creosote Treatment (1838)
John Bethell patented creosote as a wood preservative in 1838. A blend of thousands of compounds from coal tar (including trimethylbenzene, naphthalene, and pyrene), creosote proved effective against all wood-decay fungi and became the standard treatment for ships, fence posts, railway sleepers, and telephone poles.

### The Train Wrecker
*Lentinus lepideus* shows high tolerance to creosote and attacks insufficiently treated wood products. Probably implanted in softwood timber before treatment, it forms fruiting bodies when moisture increases through soil contact. The EU banned creosote in 2003; the US EPA banned chromated copper arsenate after 2003 but has not extended the ban to creosote. American creosote advocates argue a ban would lead to a new epidemic of wood decay.

## Modern Concerns

### Indoor Mould and Health
While dry rot fungi (*Meruliporia* and *Serpula*) do not produce toxins like indoor moulds such as *[[stachybotrys-chartarum]]*, their spores are allergenic and can cause hypersensitive pneumonitis -- an immune response involving gamma globulins. Documented cases include a 32-year-old schoolteacher who developed severe breathing difficulties, joint pains, and weight loss from *Serpula* spore exposure, with blood tests showing high levels of IgE and IgG antibodies to *Serpula* spores. The relationship between dry rot and allergic disease raises the possibility of personal injury lawsuits alongside property damage claims.

### Insurance and Legal Issues
Insurance companies use "dry rot" as a blanket exclusion in homeowners policies. Victims of *Meruliporia* have successfully challenged this in court by arguing that damage from this specific fungus represents a new and unforeseen disaster. The shift toward steel framing in home construction is partly driven by fungal decay concerns.

### Contemporary Targets
Steel ships are resistant to dry rot, but wood-hulled pleasure boats and recreational vehicles (RVs) remain vulnerable. Fungi can establish in wet locations within vehicles (e.g., flooring beneath plumbing leaks) and use rhizomorphs to colonize the rest of the structure. Stationary homes remain the primary target for dry rot fungi.

## Other Building Fungi

- **Gloeophyllum trabeum**: Causes substantial damage in California homes; implicated in the recent increase in wood rot in California. Little attention has been paid relative to *Meruliporia* despite its destructive potential
- **Coniophora puteana**: Tolerant of creosote-treated timber
- **Lentinus lepideus**: The "train wrecker"; tolerant of creosote and copper-arsenate treatments
- **Stachybotrys chartarum**: Indoor mould producing mycotoxins; prefers warmer temperatures than dry rot fungi

## Building Materials as Substrates

Fungi attack diverse building materials:
- **Timber**: Primary substrate for most building decay fungi
- **Concrete and mortar**: Colonized and degraded by fungi producing biogenic sulfuric and nitric acids
- **Glass**: *Penicillium simplicissimum* and *P. expansum* identified as bio-weathering agents
- **Plaster and insulation**: Can support fungal growth under moist conditions
- **Building stone**: Fungi contribute to biodeterioration through acid production, physical penetration, and mineral transformations

## Detection and Control

Detection methods include visual inspection, moisture measurement, and molecular identification. Control focuses on moisture management, removal of affected material, and chemical treatment. Cutting rhizomorphs can kill dry rot colonies in dry homes by severing their water supply. Understanding fungal biology is essential for effective prevention and remediation strategies.

## Indoor Airborne Fungi

### Sources and Dispersal

Indoor fungal spores originate from:

- Outdoor air (the primary source for most indoor environments)
- Indoor growth on damp materials (walls, ceilings, carpets)
- HVAC systems (heating, ventilation, air conditioning)
- Houseplants and potting soil
- Pets and human activity
- Food storage and preparation areas

### Common Indoor Genera

- **Cladosporium**: The most common airborne fungus worldwide; outdoors on dead plant material; indoors on damp surfaces
- **Penicillium**: Common on damp building materials, food, and textiles; some species produce mycotoxins
- **Aspergillus**: Ubiquitous; some species (A. fumigatus) are serious human pathogens
- **Alternaria**: Common allergen; associated with damp areas and plant material
- **Stachybotrys chartarum**: "Black mold"; associated with water-damaged buildings; produces mycotoxins
- **Chaetomium**: Indicator of chronic water damage; cellulolytic species
- **Trichoderma**: Common on damp wood and building materials

### Health Impacts

**Allergic responses**: Allergic rhinitis, asthma exacerbation, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, allergic fungal sinusitis.

**Toxic effects**: Mycotoxin exposure (especially from Stachybotrys, Aspergillus, Penicillium), sick building syndrome, trichothecene mycotoxins from Stachybotrys.

**Infections**: Invasive aspergillosis (A. fumigatus in immunocompromised patients), dermatophytosis, various opportunistic infections.

**Risk factors**: Dampness and moisture problems, poor ventilation, water damage, condensation on cold surfaces, inadequate building maintenance.

## Food Spoilage Fungi

### Xerophilic Spoilage Agents

Xerophilic fungi are the primary cause of spoilage in dried and intermediate-moisture foods:

- **Aspergillus species**: Major spoilers of cereals, nuts, spices, dried fruits
- **Penicillium species**: Spoilage of bread, cheese, fruits, cured meats
- **Wallemia sebi**: Extremely xerotolerant; found in dried fish, salted foods
- **Xeromyces bisporus**: Among the most xerophilic fungi known

### Mycotoxin Producers

- **[[aspergillus-flavus]]**: Aflatoxins (potent carcinogens) in peanuts, corn, tree nuts
- **Aspergillus parasiticus**: Also produces aflatoxins
- **Fusarium species**: Fumonisins, trichothecenes, zearalenone in cereals
- **Penicillium verrucosum**: Ochratoxin A in cereals
- **Aspergillus ochraceus**: Ochratoxin A

### Temperature-Related Spoilage

- **Psychrotolerant fungi**: Spoilage of refrigerated foods
- **[[thermophilic-fungi]]**: Spoilage in heat-processed or stored foods
- **Mesophilic fungi**: Most common spoilage agents at ambient temperatures

## Fungi in Extreme Built Environments

### Hospitals and Healthcare

- Nosocomial (hospital-acquired) fungal infections are a serious concern
- Aspergillus species are the primary agents
