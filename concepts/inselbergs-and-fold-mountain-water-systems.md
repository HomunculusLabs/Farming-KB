# Inselbergs and Fold Mountain Water Systems

## Overview

Inselbergs (isolated rock domes) and fold mountains are among the most significant desert landforms for water harvesting and settlement potential. While inselbergs are relatively simple systems, fold mountains are the most extensive features in many deserts, combining characteristics of inselbergs and scarps but with far greater complexity. Both offer unique opportunities for permaculture design in arid environments.

## Inselbergs and Residual Domes

### Characteristics
Great domes such as Uluru (Ayers Rock) in Australia and many similar massifs of granitic or metamorphosed sandstones have distinct features:

- Rarely develop deep caves (but do have some rock shelter)
- Rarely have pronounced valley entries
- Usually dip steeply into loose sandy soils around the base
- Occur sometimes in series, separated by great fissures

Despite their simplicity compared to wadi and scarp systems, inselbergs are very effective run-off areas, some covering many tens of hectares. Large trees and good humus soils will develop at the base of cliffs on the shade side.

### Water Harvesting from Domes
The basic ratio for dome water harvesting is **1 acre of field to 20 hectares of rock run-off**. Base floods can be led via fluming to such fields to grow both crops and fruit trees or palms. A few shallow mulch-filled valleys exist on these massifs where the soil is mainly humus, supporting trees, yams, and other vegetation.

Occasionally, steep and shaded clefts present sites for rock dams, but for the main part, partly sealed tanks and infiltration must suffice. The larger systems (100 hectares or more) have often been occupied in historical times by careful and conservative peoples, usually as base camps for long-range foraging after rains.

### Small Rock Slabs
Very small rock slabs (1–10 hectares) present a useful area for:

- A gutter and tank system
- A hectare or two of trees
- A wayside house
- Wildlife ramp-tanks for desert birds and small mammals

Some have been guttered using rock walls (slab on edge) or concreted drains to serve the needs of a house or steam trains for water in remote locations. Small tanks should have access ramps for pigeons, quail, small mammals, and/or frogs, with thatch to retard evaporation and exclude large animals.

### Occupancy Principles
Each inselberg has a special charm, and very sensitive and restricted occupancy is called for — developing and protecting natural resources rather than attempting extensive systems.

## Fold Mountains

### Geological Structure
Fold mountains are the most extensive features in many deserts. They are created by continental drift, often forming inside coastal ranges. Key structural elements include:

- **Synclines**: Down-flexed sediments that may erode to great canoe shapes, with a few river gorges cutting their way out
- **Anticlines**: May form great whalebacks, but rivers eventually form on their spines and produce long valleys in the hills, faced on each side by cliffs or steep tilted sediments

Series of folds create successions of desert basins. It is the anticlines that most commonly erode to form valleys; synclines are less easily eroded. Eventually, typical basin and range series evolve from folded sequences.

### Dam Opportunities
In many places, a dam at the exit of a river from these ranges has resulted in a large ovoid freshwater lake in the interior of the ranges. The permanence of this water depends on the proportion of run-off area to storage area, which must be **20–30:1 in deserts**. Key principles:

- **Modest and deep storages** are more likely to hold water all year than large shallow storages
- Such dams need careful hydrological assessment and engineering
- The opportunity to create successful freshwater dams is greater in fold mountains than in other desert landforms
- Wherever massive ranges back the fold mountains (as in Colorado, Nevada, and near Mt. Ararat in Turkey), foothill dams will hold water from snow melt and rain run-off

Settlements and roads need to be well off any flood path should dams burst. As few settlements exist in deserts, this is an open option, whereas large dams in well-settled areas tend to be built above existing valley settlements, with frequently disastrous consequences in war or earthquake.

### Modest Dam Systems
A great many more modest dams are available in fold systems:

- **Narrow rock-walled exit valleys** to foothill dams where exit waters are led to diversion dams
- **Complex freshwater dam systems** led off low-slope wadi-like streams as they flow to the plains
- A vigorous day hunting around village sites usually reveals many possible storages wherever the ranges are sufficiently large

Fold systems occur in large series and have correspondingly large catchments. They almost always yield a few permanent rockholes and streams.

## Palisade Rock Formations

All fold mountains of sedimentary or metamorphic rock show more or less marked palisades of harder rock series. These usually run along the slopes like sub-contours. Wherever vegetation exists, it is far taller, greener, and denser on the **upslope side** of such palisades, because run-off is slowed down and forced to infiltrate there.

These palisades are not as effective as swales, but they help. Some are wide-spaced, fairly well on contour, project well above the surface, and develop striking tree-lines. While less effective than constructed swales, they provide natural water infiltration points across the landscape.

Wherever series of these palisades are a feature, a horizontal bore will often tap sweet water trapped in the rock strata. Unlike quanats, such water can be sealed off and used only as needed, and can be regulated to the amount taken into the aquifer.

## Headwater Stream Diversion

A shallow, fast dry-stream system usually develops out of the upper foothill regions of folds, often of stream sequences only 4–15 metres wide with banks 0.5–2 metres high. The bed is usually rock-filled, and detritus along the banks shows the extent of flood levels. Flood periods are violent and sudden.

Rather than damming these violent flows directly (which would fill with boulder and silt in a few rains), the strategy is to **bleed off the torrent** and divert it to a more peaceful contour flow, and thence to a dam. By leading this flow along an oversized, wide, and shallow contour trench, silt falls out in the trench before ever reaching the dam. The trench itself presents a planting site and a silt trap that is easily cleaned out, whereas a dam is difficult to de-silt.

This fresh water in dams or swales is then used to aid downstream forests, tree lines, and settlements.
