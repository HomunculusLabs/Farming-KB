# Precision Seeders for Vegetable Production

Precision seeders are specialized tools that enable vegetable growers to sow multiple rows of seed at exact, consistent spacing, dramatically increasing yields per square foot of valuable greenhouse or field space. In intensive vegetable production systems — particularly for baby-leaf salad crops and root vegetables — precision seeding is one of the most impactful investments a grower can make, practically doubling yields compared to single-row or homemade multi-row alternatives.

In a well-managed greenhouse operation, space is the most expensive and limiting resource. Every square foot must produce maximum returns. Precision seeders allow growers to sow crops at 2.25-inch row spacing, fitting far more productive rows into each 30-inch bed than would be possible with hand broadcasting or single-row sowing. The improvement is so significant that Coleman credits precision seeders with practically doubling yields per square foot over the seeders used previously on his farm.

These seeders compare very favorably with larger multi-row greenhouse seeders available in Europe, which cost ten to fifteen times as much. For the small-scale grower, the precision seeder represents an ideal balance of cost, effectiveness, and simplicity.

## The Pinpoint Seeder

The Pinpoint Seeder is a hand-pulled precision seeder widely used by small-scale market gardeners. It is described as a "tool, not a machine" — the distinction being that its effectiveness depends heavily on the care and skill of the operator rather than on automated precision. The seeder operates on a simple but effective principle: a rotating axle with precisely sized seed holes passes beneath gravity-fed hoppers, dropping individual seeds at regular intervals as the operator pulls the tool along a prepared bed.

### Seed Selection Mechanisms

The seeder uses two complementary mechanisms to control seed delivery with a high degree of precision:

1. **Four hole sizes on a movable axle**: The holes are labeled A (smallest), B, C, and D (largest). The axle slides into position so the desired hole size is centered under the hoppers. Sizes A, B, and C have nine holes per rotation; size D has seven. With a wheel rolling circumference of approximately 9 inches, the smaller holes deliver roughly one seed per inch of travel — an ideal spacing for most baby-leaf and root crops.

2. **Adjustable brushes in each hopper**: Each hopper contains a small brush that sweeps excess seeds away from the seed hole, ensuring only one (or a controlled few) seeds drop through. Brushes are adjusted via thumbscrew to three settings — Tight (T), Medium (M), or Loose (L) — allowing fine control over sowing density. A tighter brush setting produces a lighter sowing rate; a looser setting allows more seeds through for slightly heavier sowing. These subtle brush adjustments allow for fine-tuning of seed rates that can make a meaningful difference in crop density and yield.

### Determining Optimal Settings

Before sowing any seed, growers should invest time in determining the ideal combination of hole size and brush setting. The testing process is straightforward but important:

- Fill one hopper with seed from the lot to be planted
- Turn the wheel manually one full rotation at a time
- Count how many seeds drop per turn
- Repeat several times to get a consistent average
- Adjust the brush tighter or looser and retest until the desired rate is achieved

The optimal combination is then recorded directly on the seed packet using a coded notation that allows rapid setup for future sowings. A common convention prefixes the code with the number of rows. For example, `4BT` indicates a four-row seeder, hole size B, brush set to Tight.

| Crop | Setting | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| Baby-leaf lettuce (greenhouse) | 4BT | Standard indoor rate |
| Baby-leaf lettuce (outdoors) | 4BM | Slightly heavier rate for field conditions |
| [[arugula]] (winter, low light) | 4AM | Adjusted for season |
| Arugula (summer) | 4BT | Standard rate |
| Radishes | 4 spacing | 2 square inches per plant |
| Carrots | 4 spacing | 4 square inches per plant |

Some crops need wider spacing for optimal development. Spinach, claytonia, and turnips are planted at 4.25-inch row spacing by filling only every other hopper on the seeder, effectively skipping alternate rows.

### Modifications for Very Small Seeds

Three of the seeds commonly sown in winter greenhouses — claytonia, minutina, and sylvetta — are so small that even the smallest hole (A) sows multiple seeds per drop. This is an inherent limitation of any mechanical seeder when dealing with seed sizes below a certain threshold. For these species, Coleman recommends setting the brushes down tightly, as the crops tolerate close growing conditions and the extra seeds have not proven problematic in practice.

A practical modification for tiny seeds involves sealing the seams of the seed hoppers with liquid solder. The original seed hoppers were not designed to hold such small seeds, and the seeds were falling through the seams between hopper sections. Carefully filling all seams with liquid solder eliminates this problem entirely and makes a good tool even better.

For larger seeds like beets and chard that are unsuccessful with standard hole sizes, an extra axle can be fabricated by a machine shop with deeper seed holes. Coleman had his custom axle made with hole D deepened by 0.125 inch and holes A, B, and C deepened by 0.0625 inch. The deeper version of hole D now successfully plants beet and chard seeds that were unsuccessful with the standard axle.

### Operating Technique

Proper operating technique is essential to realizing the full potential of a precision seeder. The following practices are recommended:

**Walking backwards** alongside the bed is the standard approach. This allows the operator to continuously monitor seeder function — watching the wheels turn and seeds drop. If the wheels stop turning or seeds stop dropping, the operator can immediately diagnose and fix the problem. Walking backwards also allows the operator to spot and remove rocks or compost clumps that could knock the seeder off line or clog the furrow openers.

**Depth control** is achieved by adjusting the handle angle relative to the ground. Raising the handle sows seeds more shallowly; lowering it sows more deeply. With practice this becomes second nature, but the operator must consciously think about whether a new depth is required each time a different seed variety is loaded.

**Soil preparation** must be meticulous. Rocks, undecomposed compost chunks, stringy weeds, or root residues will disrupt the seeder's operation and give the operator "fits." The extra production per square foot gained through precision seeding more than justifies the additional care taken in seedbed preparation. Beds should be finely raked smooth before seeding.

**Bed rolling** is critical for proper seeder function. A lightweight mesh-surfaced roller (preferred over smooth-surface rollers to avoid glazing the soil) is run over freshly prepared beds to create a firm, even surface. This prevents the seeder wheels from sinking too far into loose soil, which would cause the axle to bog down and stop turning.

**Furrow openers** on the seeder are designed so that soil falls back quickly after the seed drops. If additional coverage is desired, the bed can be lightly irrigated after sowing to wash more soil over the seeds. Alternatively, the same mesh roller used for bed preparation can be run over the bed to lightly firm in the seeds — this approach has the added benefit of leaving a smooth soil surface that facilitates the close cutting of mesclun salad crops at harvest time.

**Row alignment** between successive passes relies on the tooth marks left by the seeder wheels. The four-row model spaces rows 2.25 inches apart, with wheel tooth marks extending 2.25 inches beyond the outside rows. When making multiple passes with the four-row model, the wheel teeth will run directly over a previously sown row if spacing is even — this has never been found to cause damage to the earlier planting.

**Moisture management** is critical in cold, damp greenhouses. Condensation causes seeds to stick to seeder surfaces, leading to irregular or absent seed drop. Solutions include warming the seeder in front of a heater before use and, for conditions where drops of condensed moisture fall from the greenhouse roof, fabricating a form-fitting Plexiglas cover for the top of the seeder to protect the seed hoppers.

### Storage and Maintenance

Proper storage extends the life and maintains the accuracy of the seeder. After each use, brushes should be returned to a loose setting to prevent deformation from prolonged pressure against the seed holes. Seeds are tipped from all hoppers into a 9-inch-wide pan and poured back into the seed packet for future use. The seeder should be stored in a dry location — a closet or similar indoor space — to prevent corrosion of metal parts.

Over years of use, the wheel hubs may become harder to slide on and off the axle. Coleman's solution was to carefully file the hubs to a larger diameter and add a hose clamp over each wheel shank. Snugging each wheel in place by tightening the screw on its hose clamp is easier to work with than the original friction-fit and is recommended as a proactive modification even on new seeders.

## The Six-Row Seeder

The six-row seeder represents an evolution of the pinpoint design, adapted for larger-scale production while retaining the features that made the original effective. It is pushed rather than pulled and incorporates several significant efficiency improvements:

- **Integrated mesh rollers**: A front roller levels the soil ahead of the seed drop area, while a rear roller drives the seed axle and presses seeds into the soil after they drop. When pushing the tool, the operator must maintain pressure on the rear roller to ensure the seed axle turns properly. This integrated design eliminates the need for separate bed-rolling passes before and after seeding.

- **Drive pulley system**: Three different drive pulleys connected by a drive belt allow seed delivery at three different spacings — one seed per inch, one every two inches, or one every four inches — providing flexibility for different crops and growing strategies.

- **Six rows instead of four**: A standard 30-inch-wide bed can be fully planted in just two passes (one down and one back), compared to three or more passes with the four-row model.

- **Built-in seed-depth adjustment**: Incorporated into the front roller design and adjustable via a screw knob, this provides precise, repeatable depth control without the need to change handle angle.

The combined effect of these improvements is a dramatically faster seeding process. With no separate rolling required and only two passes per bed instead of three, the six-row seeder significantly reduces labor time per square foot planted. It transforms precision seeding from a careful, deliberate process into a more production-oriented operation suitable for larger growing areas.

## Economic Impact and Return on Investment

The economic case for precision seeding is compelling. The yield improvement alone — approximately double per square foot over less precise methods — would justify the investment. But when combined with reduced seed waste (precise placement means fewer seeds needed), faster operation (multiple rows per pass), and more uniform crop stands (which simplify harvesting and improve marketable yield), the return on investment is typically realized within the first season.

For small-scale growers competing in local markets, the ability to maximize production from limited greenhouse space is often the difference between profitability and loss. Precision seeders help close the yield gap between small intensive operations and much larger mechanized farms, making them an essential tool in the four-season grower's arsenal.

## Choosing Between Seeder Types

The choice between a four-row pinpoint seeder and a six-row push seeder depends on the scale of the operation and the grower's specific needs.

**The four-row model** is ideal for smaller operations where its lighter weight, lower cost, and simpler design are advantages. It excels in greenhouse environments where beds are already well-prepared and the pulling motion gives good visibility and control. The operator walks backwards alongside the bed, maintaining a clear line of sight to the seeder at all times. This model is also easier to maintain and modify, making it a good starting point for growers who want to learn precision seeding fundamentals.

**The six-row seeder** is better suited to larger operations where the time savings from integrated rollers and fewer passes per bed become significant. Its push design and wider footprint make it more efficient on larger field areas as well as in bigger greenhouses. The trade-off is higher cost and somewhat greater complexity, but for operations regularly planting more than a few thousand square feet per season, the labor savings justify the investment.

**Key decision factors:**

- Total square footage under production
- Frequency of planting cycles per season
- Availability of labor for seeding operations
- Whether the operation is primarily greenhouse or field-based
- Budget constraints and expected return timeline

## Precision Seeding in Context

Precision seeding is most effective when integrated into a complete system of intensive bed management. Proper soil preparation — including thorough compost incorporation, careful raking, and light rolling — is a prerequisite. Without a well-prepared seedbed, even the best seeder will deliver inconsistent results.

The seeder works in concert with other tools in the four-season grower's toolkit. The same mesh roller used for bed preparation serves double duty for firming seeds after sowing. Efficient weed control strategies, particularly preemergence flaming, protect the precisely placed crop seeds during the critical germination period. And the uniform crop stands produced by precision seeding make subsequent harvesting operations faster and more efficient, since the crop grows at a consistent density and height across the entire bed.

Growers new to precision seeding should expect a learning curve of a few plantings before achieving optimal results. The time invested in testing settings, recording codes, and developing a feel for the tool's operation pays dividends in increased yield, reduced seed waste, and more predictable crop performance throughout the season. Starting with forgiving crops like lettuce and arugula allows the operator to build confidence before moving to more demanding seeds.
