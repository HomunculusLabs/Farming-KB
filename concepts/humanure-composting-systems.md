## Overview

Humanure composting is the controlled biological decomposition of human excrement into safe, nutrient-rich humus. The most widely referenced method is the Jenkins method, developed by Joseph Jenkins and published in "The Humanure Handbook." This page covers the complete system from collection through final compost application.

## The Jenkins Method

Joseph Jenkins pioneered the modern approach to thermophilic humanure composting. His method is deliberately simple and has been used successfully for decades:

1. Collect waste in a sawdust toilet bucket (see [[sawdust-toilet-composting]])
2. Cover each deposit with a carbonaceous cover material
3. Transfer full buckets to a compost bin
4. Add bulky carbon material (straw, weeds, leaves) in layers
5. Allow the compost to thermophilically decompose
6. Cure for 12-24 months before garden application

The method requires no electricity, no water, and minimal equipment. Jenkins emphasizes that the process is fundamentally about microbial ecology — create the right conditions and beneficial organisms do the work.

## Carbon Material Requirements

The carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio is the most critical variable. Human excrement has a combined C:N ratio of roughly 5:1 to 15:1. For thermophilic composting, the target C:N ratio is approximately 25:1 to 35:1.

### Cover Material Estimates

A typical household produces about 1 gallon of humanure per person per week. Plan for roughly equal volumes of cover material to humanure by volume. For a family of four over one year:

- Humanure: approximately 200 gallons
- Cover material needed: approximately 200 gallons (roughly 10-15 cubic feet of bulk material)
- Additional bulking material for compost bin: 20-30 cubic feet of straw, leaves, or hay

### Sourcing Carbon Materials

- Sawdust from local sawmills (often free)
- Wood chips from tree services
- Straw from grain farms
- Fallen leaves collected in autumn
- Shredded newspaper (black ink only, no glossy inserts)
- Peat moss (non-renewable but effective)
- Coconut coir blocks (rehydratable, storable)

Stockpile carbon materials when they are abundant (autumn leaves, summer grass hay) to ensure year-round supply.

## Compost Temperature Management

### Target Temperatures

| Phase | Temperature Range | Duration |
|-------|------------------|----------|
| Mesophilic | 70-100F | 2-7 days |
| Thermophilic onset | 100-113F | 1-3 days |
| Active thermophilic | 113-160F | Several weeks |
| Pasteurization zone | 131-150F | Critical for pathogen kill |
| Cooling | Gradual decline | Weeks to months |
| Curing | Ambient to 100F | 6-24 months |

### Pathogen Destruction Thresholds

- **Bacteria** (Salmonella, E. coli, Shigella) — Killed at 131F for 1 hour
- **Helminth eggs** (Ascaris, Tapeworm) — Killed at 131F for 1-3 days; Ascaris eggs are the most heat-resistant and serve as the indicator organism
- **Protozoa** (Giardia, Entamoeba) — Killed at 122F for 30 minutes
- **Viruses** (Hepatitis A, Rotavirus) — Killed at 140F for 5-20 minutes

### Monitoring

A 36-inch compost thermometer with a probe is essential. Insert into the center of the pile at multiple points. Record temperatures every few days during the active phase. If the pile never reaches thermophilic range:

1. Check moisture (should be 50-60% by weight)
2. Increase nitrogen (add more green material or kitchen scraps)
3. Increase pile size (minimum 1 cubic yard recommended)
4. Ensure adequate carbon particle size (not too fine, not too coarse)

## Compost Bin Designs

### Single Bin System

Simplest approach: one bin that fills over a year, then sits undisturbed for a year of curing. Best for very small households (1-2 people). Drawback: you must stop adding to the bin while it cures.

### Dual Bin System

Two bins side by side. Fill one bin for a year while the other cures. At year-end, switch. This is the standard recommendation and works well for most households.

### Three-Bin System

Fill bin 1 in year 1, bin 2 in year 2, while bin 3 cures. Allows continuous filling with maximum curing time. Best for larger families or community-scale systems.

### Bin Construction Details

- Minimum volume per bin: 4x4x4 feet (64 cubic feet)
- Open bottom allows earthworm and beneficial organism access
- Wire mesh or slatted sides allow air circulation
- Removable front panels make harvesting easier
- Roof or tarp cover prevents excessive rain saturation
- Located at least 50 feet from any well or water source

## The Role of Microorganisms

Thermophilic composting relies on successive waves of microorganisms:

1. **Psychrophilic bacteria** — Active below 68F, initiate decomposition
2. **Mesophilic bacteria and fungi** — Active 70-100F, dominate early decomposition
3. **Thermophilic bacteria** — Active 105-170F, drive pathogen destruction. Key genera include Bacillus, Thermus, and Thermomonospora
4. **Thermophilic fungi** — Active up to 140F, contribute to cellulose breakdown
5. **Actinomycetes** — Bridge between bacteria and fungi, important in later stages of decomposition
6. **Earthworms and macrofauna** — Enter during cooling and curing, further process and stabilize the material

The microbial diversity in a well-managed compost pile rivals that of any natural ecosystem. This diversity is itself a safety mechanism — competition from billions of beneficial organisms suppresses any surviving pathogens.

## Curing and Maturation

Curing is the extended period after active composting where the material stabilizes. During curing:

- Remaining organic matter slowly breaks down
- pH neutralizes (finished compost pH is typically 6.5-7.5)
- Ammonia converts to stable organic nitrogen
- Earthworms and other macrofauna process the material
- Phytotoxic compounds degrade
- The material develops a pleasant earthy aroma

Minimum recommended curing time is one year. Two years provides an additional margin of safety and produces higher quality compost.

## Using Finished Humanure Compost

Finished humanure compost is a valuable soil amendment:

- Nutrient content comparable to other composts (NPK roughly 1-1-1)
- High in humic acids, beneficial microorganisms, and trace minerals
- Improves soil structure, water retention, and cation exchange capacity
- Application rate: 1-2 inches worked into garden beds, or top-dressed around perennials

Best practices suggest applying to fruit trees, berry bushes, and ornamental plants first. Application to root crops and leafy vegetables is safe after proper composting but may face greater social resistance.

## Common Misconceptions

- "It smells" — A properly managed system has no more odor than a regular compost pile
- "It"s illegal everywhere" — Regulations vary widely; many rural areas have no specific prohibition
- "It spreads disease" — Thermophilic composting is a proven pathogen destruction method used in municipal sewage treatment worldwide
- "It"s only for hippies" — Composting toilets are used in national parks, eco-villages, and off-grid homes across the socioeconomic spectrum
