# Fungi as Environmental Indicators and Biosensors

Updated: 2026-04-18

Fungi are increasingly recognized as valuable bioindicators in environmental pollution surveys. Their sensitivity to various pollutants, their ability to accumulate contaminants, and the development of molecular detection methods make them useful tools for environmental monitoring. The use of fungi as bioindicators draws on [[fungal-biology-fundamentals]] to interpret organismal responses in terms of underlying biochemical and ecological mechanisms, and connects to [[fungal-biomonitoring]] for systematic environmental assessment programs.

## Bioindicator Species

Several fungal species serve as indicators of specific environmental conditions:

- **Thelephora caryophyllea** -- accumulates metals in soil (Maurice & Lagerkvist, 2000)

- **Lycoperdon perlatum** (puffball) -- bioindicator of heavy metals and selenium in soil pollution (Quinche, 1990)

- **Scopulariopsis and Candida** -- specific environmental indicators of sand beach quality (Mendes et al., 1998)

- **Armillaria species** -- detected by molecular methods in forest soil; distribution indicates mycelium locations and soil health

Macrofungal fruiting bodies are particularly useful bioindicators because they integrate contaminant exposure over time and can be sampled non-destructively (except for the harvested fruiting body). The metal accumulation patterns in mushrooms often reflect both the total soil metal concentration and the bioavailable fraction, providing information that total soil metal analysis alone cannot deliver. Ectomycorrhizal species such as Amanita muscaria and Boletus edulis are among the most effective metal accumulators, with some species concentrating cadmium, mercury, and lead to levels 100-1000 times higher than surrounding soil.

## Lichen Communities as Pollution Indicators

Although lichens are symbiotic associations of fungi with algae or cyanobacteria, they are among the most widely used fungal-based bioindicators:

- **Epiphytic lichen diversity** -- decreases with increasing air pollution, particularly SO2 and nitrogen deposition

- **Lichen mapping** -- standardized surveys of lichen species on tree trunks provide spatial data on air quality gradients

- **Transplanted lichens** -- lichen thalli moved from clean to polluted sites accumulate contaminants and show physiological damage

- **Lichen biomonitoring networks** -- established across Europe for long-term air quality assessment

Lichen-based bioindication is particularly valuable because it provides a cost-effective, passive monitoring approach that requires no power or instrumentation. The sensitivity of different lichen species to specific pollutants allows semi-quantitative assessment of pollution levels based on species composition alone.

## Radioisotope Tracking

137Cs/134Cs activities measured in 350 fungal species and 250 soil samples in Bavarian forests serve as indicators of major mycelium locations. Two approaches -- static (measuring accumulation in fruiting bodies) and dynamic (measuring in mycelial samples) -- have advantages in estimating fungal mycelium distribution in forest soils (Ruhm et al., 1997).

Radioisotope tracking by fungi provides unique insights into belowground ecosystem processes. The differential accumulation of radiocesium among fungal species reflects variations in their mycelial depth distribution, hyphal exploration patterns, and physiological mechanisms for cesium uptake and retention. Saprotrophic fungi generally accumulate higher radiocesium levels than mycorrhizal species, and accumulation ratios (fungus-to-soil concentration) vary by more than two orders of magnitude among species. This methodology connects to [[fungal-ecology-forest-floor]] for understanding belowground fungal distributions.

## Mutagenicity and Carcinogenicity Testing

Yeasts are used in tests for determining mutagenic or carcinogenic action of environmental samples:

- **D7ts1 test** -- an enhanced Saccharomyces cerevisiae assay with increased cell permeability to detect mutagens and carcinogens in soil, air, and water samples

- The D7ts1 test detects all samples positive in the bacterial Ames test, plus additional positive samples the Ames test misses

- Simultaneous use of D7ts1 and Ames tests is beneficial as it detects hazards to human health where bacterial cells do not respond

The complementary use of yeast and bacterial mutagenicity assays provides a more comprehensive assessment of genotoxic risk because the two systems differ in their DNA repair pathways, metabolic activation capabilities, and cell wall permeability. Yeast assays are particularly valuable for detecting agents that require eukaryotic metabolic activation or that target eukaryotic-specific DNA structures.

## Molecular Detection Methods

Modern molecular assays allow species-level identification and quantification of fungi in environmental samples:

### Immunological Assays

- Monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies against whole-cell mycelial extracts and extracellular antigens

- ELISA, modified ELISA, immunofluorescence, immunoblotting, dipstick assays

- Used for detecting wood decay fungi (Serpula lacrymans, Postia placenta, Ophiostoma spp.)

- Quantitative detection of fungal biomass during solid-state fermentation

### Molecular Assays

- **PCR amplification** of rDNA sequences using universal primers for total fungal diversity

- **Fungal-specific primers** EF4f/EF3r and EF4f/Fung5r amplify wide range of major fungal groups

- **Denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis** (DGGE) of PCR products profiles fungal communities in soil

- **Terminal restriction fragment (TRF) pattern analysis** of ITS rDNA assesses fungal diversity

- **Real-time PCR** (TaqMan) with fluorescent dyes enables detection and quantification of specific fungi from soil DNA

- **RFLP analysis** differentiates species and subspecies (e.g., Trametes versicolor, Phanerochaete chrysosporium)

- **In situ hybridization** with DNA probes against 5S or 18S rRNA for identification in tissue sections

### DNA Extraction from Soil

The fungal DNA extraction method (FDM) efficiently lyses fungal spores or mycelia. Bead milling methods allow accurate estimation of fungal organisms in air and water samples. Multiplex PCR followed by agarose gel or microchip electrophoresis can identify 120+ fungal strains.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of fungal ITS regions has revolutionized environmental fungal community analysis, enabling detection of hundreds of species from single soil or water samples. Metagenomic approaches can assess both taxonomic composition and functional potential of fungal communities in contaminated environments. These molecular tools are essential for [[fungal-community-ecology]] studies that link community structure to ecosystem function and pollution response.

## Biosensor Applications

Whole cells of yeast and filamentous fungi can be incorporated into biosensor arrays on silicon chips and optic fibers for environmental pollutant sensing. Multicolored fluorescent proteins from yeasts and fungi have opened new possibilities for sensing systems. A biological oxygen demand (BOD) biosensor using immobilized yeasts monitors effluent from wastewater treatment plants.

Fungal biosensors exploit the specificity of enzyme-substrate interactions and the sensitivity of cellular stress responses to detect and quantify pollutants. Laccase-based amperometric biosensors detect phenolic compounds in water at micromolar concentrations. Yeast-based biosensors with stress-responsive promoters coupled to reporter genes (luciferase, GFP) provide real-time detection of heavy metals, organic pollutants, and endocrine-disrupting chemicals. The development of portable, field-deployable fungal biosensors is an active area of research with applications in [[mycofiltration-of-water-contaminants]] monitoring.

## Ecological Indicators and Soil Health

Fungal community composition is a sensitive indicator of soil health and ecosystem disturbance:

- **Fungal-to-bacterial ratios** -- shifts toward bacterial dominance indicate soil disturbance or nutrient enrichment

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization** -- reduced colonization of plant roots indicates soil compaction, pesticide exposure, or nutrient imbalance

- **Ectomycorrhizal diversity** -- species richness and evenness reflect forest stand age, management history, and air quality

- **Saprotrophic fungal diversity** -- changes in decomposer communities indicate organic matter quality, moisture regime, and pollution exposure

These community-level indicators provide integrated measures of ecosystem condition that complement chemical analysis of individual pollutants. The connection between fungal community indicators and broader [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] processes makes them valuable for holistic environmental assessment, linking fungal indicators to [[fungal-biodiversity-forest-ecosystems]] conservation.
