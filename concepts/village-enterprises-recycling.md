# Village Enterprises and Community Recycling

## Overview

A [[permaculture]] village derives its economic vitality from diverse local enterprises that serve both the village and surrounding districts. Mollison analyzes how the occupants of a 50-house (100 adult) village can achieve self-employment, identifying the major cost areas of food, energy, and transport as primary opportunities for local income generation. Complementing the productive economy, comprehensive [[community-recycling-systems]] can return a net cash benefit to households while eliminating waste.

## Essential Village Occupations

For a village of approximately 50 households (100 adults), the major cost areas — **food, energy, and transport** — represent the largest opportunities for local employment and self-reliance.

### Food Production (5–7 families)

About 20 adults can support 1 adult providing food supply, meaning 5 to 7 families can earn a living from food-related enterprises:

- **Open-air market gardens** — fresh produce for village and district
- **Glasshouse crops** — [[coleman-protected-cultivation]] for year-round production
- **Cooperative store** — local trade hub with both village-produced and imported goods
- **Domestic livestock and fishery** — chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, rabbits, aquaculture
- **Café and food processing** — value-added products from village-grown ingredients
- **Baked goods** — artisan breads and bakery items
- **Village market** — outlet for surplus sold to visitors and nearby residents

### Energy Services (2–5 families)

Energy needs include space heating, cooking, electricity, hot water, gas, and appliances. To establish energy-efficient systems:

- **House design and retrofit** — designing new dwellings and improving existing ones for energy efficiency
- **Contract and extension work** — serving the broader district beyond the village
- **Hardware and appliance sales** — supplying efficient appliances to village and district
- **Village energy system installation, repair, and maintenance** — solar, biogas, hydro
- **Local manufacturing** — over time, village manufacturers enable balanced trade in appliances

Most energy investments amortise in 3–7 years, making them economically sound despite requiring initial capital.

### Vehicle Services (2–4 families)

Vehicles are often the most costly item in household budgets. A planned approach is essential:

- **Service, repairs, and maintenance** — for village vehicles
- **Bulk fuel and oil supply** — economies of scale through collective purchasing
- **Fuel production** — growing, distilling, or fermenting fuels for engines
- **Fleet lease and insurance** — shared vehicle systems
- **Special vehicle and tractor lease** — heavy equipment sharing
- **Specialized manufacturing** — village alliances can establish engine or chassis production

### Financial Services (1–2 people)

Handling income, loans, accounting, and running a credit union is essential [[permaculture-village-infrastructure-and-dwelling-design]]:

- **Community credit union** — holding insurance money and incomes to fund enterprises such as energy systems, dairies, and food processing centres
- **Computer-based accounting** — modern accounting and managerial skills
- **Enterprise support** — funding new village businesses through low-interest loans

### Medical and Pharmaceutical Services (1–2 people)

Community health based on prevention of most illnesses:

- **Medical services** — prescription, massage, counselling, local treatment
- **Pharmaceutical production** — medications made and sold locally
- **Remedy cultivation** — specific remedies grown or manufactured on site
- **Community health** — prevention-focused wellness programs

### Building and Maintenance (1–2 people)

A plumber-builder-mechanic serves both village establishment and ongoing maintenance:

- **Construction** — new buildings and renovations
- **Furnishings** — useful items produced in the woodworking area
- **Sale items** — extra income from crafted furniture and goods

### Coverage Summary

These essential occupations cover shelter, food, economy, energy, transport, and health — initially funding about **20 of the 50 households**, with more employment as manufacturing develops.

## Extended Enterprise Opportunities

Beyond essential occupations, a range of additional village enterprises can develop from land resources and local skills:

### Land-Based Enterprises

| Enterprise | Description |
|---|---|
| Glasshouse crops | Protected cultivation for premium markets |
| Special crops | Herbs, pharmaceutical plants, rare varieties |
| Cut flowers | Floriculture for local and distant markets |
| Processed dairy products | Cheese, yogurt, butter from village dairy |
| Fish and aquaculture | Pond and tank-based fish production |
| Nursery | [[plant-propagation]] for village and district sales |
| Special forage crops | [[comfrey]], tagasaste, and other multi-purpose plants |
| Beekeeping | Honey, pollination services, wax products |
| [[orchard-management]] | Fruit production for fresh market and processing |
| Animal breeding | Special poultry, pigeon, sheep, or goat breeds (stud operations) |

### Skills-Based Enterprises

| Enterprise | Description |
|---|---|
| Teaching | Children's education, adult education, applied workshops |
| Consultancy | Architecture, landscape design, permaculture site planning |
| Computer services | Programming, databases, network services — can be home-based |
| Publishing | Computer-aided word processing and typesetting services |
| Physical therapy | Massage, paramedical services — high demand in rural districts |
| Craft production | Metalwork, woodwork, pottery, art |
| Trade and distribution | Distribution rights, wholesale, import-export, village trade networks |

### Visitor and Education Services

- Modest income from guests, visitors, and site tours (if the village concurs)
- Educational services and accommodation — much in demand
- Workshops and classes — a very reliable early income source
- [[permaculture-village-development]] as a special education centre

## The Job-Sharing Principle

It should be the long-term aim of any village to own and operate its own employment enterprises. In past times, a villager rarely held just one job — the banker was also a part-time barber, trader, and perhaps gardener.

### Benefits of Job-Sharing

- **Total failure is unlikely** — income is diversified across 2–3 enterprises
- **Unemployment is minimized** — work is available across multiple occupations
- **Holidays are manageable** — others can cover while one person takes time off
- **Wet days are productive** — indoor work is always available
- **Individual work is simple** — though the total village structure is complex

The analogy to a **plant in a polyculture** is apt: each individual fills a simple role, but the overall system is resilient and productive. In every occupation, job-sharing should be the rule, not the exception.

## Enterprise Dependencies

All village enterprises depend on two basic factors:

### Capital
- Enough money to start up and develop
- Sources include credit union loans, pre-sales, trust investment, personal savings
- Many small enterprises can pre-sell their wares for initial capital
- Many others need no capital to start but need time to develop

### Management
- Careful accounting and bookkeeping
- Forward planning and market research
- Product development and [[blesching-cannabis-contaminants-testing-quality-control]]
- Sensible costing and staffing
- Correct lease and rental agreements
- Appropriate legal structures
