## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Carbon Cycling

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of approximately 90% of terrestrial plant species. These associations are among the most important biological mediators of the terrestrial carbon cycle, influencing how carbon flows from the atmosphere into soils and how it is stored, processed, and eventually returned to the atmosphere. The chapter by Jonathan Leake in *Fungi in the Environment* (Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, 2007) provides a comprehensive treatment of these roles.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (phylum Glomeromycota) are the most widespread mycorrhizal type, associating with the majority of herbaceous plants, many shrubs, and some tropical trees. The defining feature is the formation of arbuscules — highly branched fungal structures inside root cortical cells that serve as the primary interface for nutrient exchange.

AMF are obligate symbionts: they cannot complete their life cycle without a host plant. In return for plant-derived carbon (primarily as sugars), AMF supply the host with mineral nutrients, particularly phosphorus, which they access through an extensive extraradical [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] in many ecosystems. This carbon allocation represents a significant cost to the plant but is offset by improved nutrient acquisition, [[soil-aggregate-formation-fungal-contributions]]:** Mycorrhizal hyphae physically bind soil particles into aggregates, protecting enclosed organic matter from decomposition
- **Root turnover promotion:** Mycorrhizal plants often have different root architecture than non-mycorrhizal plants, with more root tips and faster root turnover, contributing carbon through root exudates and decomposition

### Quantifying Mycorrhizal Carbon Contributions

Leake estimates that mycorrhizal mycelial networks represent one of the largest pools of living biomass in many terrestrial ecosystems. In forests, EMF mycelial biomass can exceed root biomass, and AMF networks in grasslands extend meters from individual plants.

Globally, mycorrhizal fungi are estimated to receive 5–10 gigatons of carbon annually from host plants — a flux comparable to total fossil fuel emissions. A significant fraction of this carbon enters relatively stable soil pools through the mechanisms described above, making mycorrhizal fungi critical players in terrestrial carbon sequestration.

## Mycorrhizal Influence on Decomposition

### The Gadgil Effect

Mycorrhizal fungi can suppress free-living decomposer communities, a phenomenon known as the Gadgil effect. By competing with saprotrophic fungi for nitrogen and other nutrients, mycorrhizal fungi can slow the decomposition of organic matter, particularly in nitrogen-limited ecosystems.

This effect is more pronounced with ectomycorrhizal associations than with arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, reflecting the broader enzymatic capabilities of EMF. The net result is that EMF-dominated forests tend to accumulate organic matter on the forest floor more rapidly than AMF-dominated ecosystems.

### Organic Matter Mining

Some mycorrhizal fungi, particularly ectomycorrhizal species, can directly access nutrients from organic matter through the production of extracellular enzymes such as proteases, phosphatases, and oxidative enzymes. This "organic matter mining" can accelerate the decomposition of specific organic compounds while simultaneously contributing to carbon cycling.

## Plant Community Composition and Carbon Cycling

Mycorrhizal type influences plant community composition, which in turn affects carbon cycling at the ecosystem level. AMF-associated plants tend to dominate in disturbed, early-successional, and grassland environments, while EMF-associated plants dominate in late-successional and forest environments.

This shift from AMF to EMF dominance during succession has important implications for carbon cycling:

- **Early succession (AMF-dominated):** Faster decomposition rates, more rapid nutrient cycling, less soil carbon accumulation
- **Late succession (EMF-dominated):** Slower decomposition, more organic matter accumulation, greater soil carbon storage

Understanding these dynamics is important for predicting how ecosystems will respond to disturbances such as deforestation, fire, and land-use change.
