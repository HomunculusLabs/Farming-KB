# Fukuoka's Natural Predator Protection in the Orchard

**Source:** [[one-straw-revolution-philosophy]] (Natural Farming Orchard section)
**Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Fukuoka's Discovery

When Fukuoka first stopped using chemicals in his citrus orchard, he found that while insect damage initially appeared, it was far less than in the chemically sprayed orchards of his neighbors. The reason: natural predators were far more numerous in his fields than in the sprayed fields.

## The Morishima Acacia and Ladybird Strategy

Fukuoka planted Morishima acacia trees in his orchard. These trees grow year round, putting out new buds in all seasons. The aphids, which feed on these buds, began to multiply in great numbers. Ladybirds fed on the aphids and soon they too began to increase. After the ladybirds had devoured all of the aphids, they climbed down to the citrus trees and started to feed on other insects such as mites, arrowhead scales, and cottony-cushion scales.

This created a self-regulating biological pest control system where:
1. The acacia provides a host for aphids (sacrificial crop)
2. Aphids feed ladybird populations
3. Ladybirds then protect the citrus trees from pest insects
4. No chemical sprays are needed

## The Leafhopper Incident

Fukuoka described a year when the leafhopper (a major rice pest) appeared in great numbers in the region. He had been growing rice without chemicals for many years, and his field had few leafhoppers, but the many natural predators kept them in check. That year spiders appeared in great numbers in his field. It is impossible for specialized research to grasp the role of a single predator in this complex web of relationships.

## The Folly of Chemical Intervention

In Japan, when insecticides were first developed, they were applied enthusiastically. The natural predators were completely exterminated, and the resulting power vacuum led to worse pest outbreaks than before. Scientists later realized that it is undesirable to eliminate predators because in the long run this actually increases pest problems.

The response was to develop a chemical relatively harmless to the predators, diluted 200 to 400 times and applied to the crops. But even this diluted chemical kills the predators. Some researchers then tried introducing foreign predator species into the orchard to control troublesome insects, but trees weakened by years of chemical use became susceptible to new problems.

## The Ecological Principle

Growing fruit without pruning, fertilizing, or using chemical sprays is possible only within a natural environment. When you stop interfering with the ecosystem, the natural balance of predators and prey re-establishes itself. The key insight is:

- Chemical insecticides kill both pests and their natural predators
- When predators die, pest populations explode unchecked
- Each chemical intervention creates a need for further intervention
- The only way out is to stop spraying and allow natural predator populations to recover
- This requires patience -- the ecosystem needs time to rebuild

## The Web of Life

Fukuoka described the interconnected food web: the hawk hunts the snake, the wolf attacks the hawk, a human kills the wolf, and later succumbs to a tuberculosis virus. Bacteria breed in the remains of the human, and other animals, grasses, and trees thrive on the nutrients made available by the bacteria's activity. Insects attack the trees and the frog eats the insects. Animals, plants, microorganisms -- all are part of the cycle of life, maintaining a suitable balance.

People may choose to view this world either as a model of the strong consuming the weak, or of coexistence and mutual benefit. Either way, it is an arbitrary interpretation, which causes wind and waves, brings about disorder and confusion. The natural world simply is.

## The Chalcid Fly Discovery

One of the most striking examples Fukuoka offered of natural predator protection involved a parasitic wasp called a chalcid fly. American researchers visiting his orchard collected specimens of a fly that was parasitizing a citrus pest. They told Fukuoka that with the help of these flies, they might be able to grow oranges in California without spraying pesticides. All those years of natural farming without pesticides had, without anyone planning it, created the conditions for a natural biological control to establish itself.

The fact that this chalcid fly existed in his orchard meant the ecosystem had returned to a natural state and was becoming self-supporting. After decades without pesticides, the soil had grown rich, the cover of vegetation had become denser, and the complex web of predator-prey relationships had reestablished itself. The fruit trees began to yield fine-looking fruit without any chemical inputs.

## The Danger of Interference

Fukuoka warned that even well-intentioned biological pest control -- importing natural predators to control specific pests -- was a form of the same interference that caused pest problems in the first place. The farmer who releases ladybugs to control aphids is still thinking in terms of control, still seeing the aphid as an enemy to be defeated. True natural farming meant accepting that insects are not enemies but participants in a complex system whose workings are beyond human understanding.

When a pest outbreak occurs in a natural farm, Fukuoka saw it not as a problem to be solved but as information about the state of the ecosystem. An aphid infestation might indicate that the soil is out of balance, that a particular crop is weakened by growing in the wrong place, or that the natural predator population has been disrupted. The response is not to attack the pest but to address the underlying condition that allowed it to flourish.
