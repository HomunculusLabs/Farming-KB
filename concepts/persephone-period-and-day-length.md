## Persephone Period and Day Length

The Persephone period is the portion of the year when day length falls below ten hours, causing plant growth to slow dramatically or effectively cease for most vegetable crops. Named by Eliot Coleman after the Greek myth of Persephone"s descent to the underworld, this period is the central biological constraint that drives all planning for [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]]. Understanding its timing, its effects on different crops, and its variation by latitude is essential for successful [[coleman-winter-harvesting-logistics-workflow]].

## The Ten-Hour Threshold

In Coleman's experience at Four Season Farm in Maine (44.33°N latitude), plant growth slows significantly when day length drops below ten hours in early November. Growth remains slow until early February, when days lengthen back past ten hours and overall light energy becomes adequate again for vigorous growth.

The ten-hour day provides a remarkably consistent yardstick across latitudes. The eminent British greenhouse researcher W. J. C. Lawrence confirmed this from his greenhouses at Merton near London (51°N), reporting that plant growth slowed from October 27 to February 16 — precisely the period when days are shorter than ten hours at his latitude.

## The Mythological Origin

The concept takes its name from the Greek myth of Persephone, daughter of the earth goddess Demeter. According to the story, Persephone was abducted by Hades to live as his wife in the underworld. Demeter, devastated by her daughter's absence, threatened to shut down all plant growth. Zeus brokered a deal whereby Persephone would spend only the winter months with Hades. During that time, Demeter made the earth barren.

This myth elegantly encodes an agricultural truth that [[ancient-greek-mystery-cults-comparison]] farmers understood intuitively: the sun's power and the length of the day are the principal influences on agriculture, and there is a period each year when growth ceases.

## Persephone Period by Latitude

The [[phenethylamine-duration-and-timing]] of the Persephone period vary significantly with latitude. At higher latitudes, the period is longer; at lower latitudes, it is shorter. South of the 32nd parallel (Savannah, Georgia; El Paso, Texas; San Diego, California), winter day length never drops below ten hours.

| Location | Latitude | Persephone Period Start | Persephone Period End | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anchorage, Alaska | 61°N | October 16 | February 24 | ~131 days |
| London, England | 51°N | October 27 | February 16 | ~112 days |
| Maine (Four Season Farm) | 44°N | November 5 | February 5 | ~92 days |
| Washington, D.C. | 39°N | November 17 | January 24 | ~68 days |
| Charlotte, NC | 35°N | December 1 | January 10 | ~40 days |
| Atlanta, GA | 33°N | December 7 | January 5 | ~29 days |
| Savannah, GA / San Diego, CA | 32°N | N/A | N/A | 0 days |

Coleman suggests that winter harvest could theoretically succeed as far north as Anchorage (61°N), though the extremely long Persephone period there would present significant challenges.

## The Cross-Quarter Days and Agricultural Calendar

The pagan agricultural calendar of the British Isles (the Wheel of Life) aligns closely with practical farming observations related to day length. While most people are familiar with the four quarter days (solstices and equinoxes), the four cross-quarter days — evenly spaced between them — have particular agricultural significance:

1. **Imbolc (February 1–2)**: Celebrates the growing of the light, the onset of lactation in ewes, and Brigid, goddess of fire and fertility. This date closely corresponds to the end of the Persephone period at many latitudes, when rapid spring growth begins in greenhouses. Traditionally, fires were lit to represent the increasing power of the returning sun.

2. **Beltane (May 1)**: Marks the beginning of the summer pastoral season when grasses for livestock begin growing again. Outdoor transplanted vegetables begin their season of rapid growth around this date.

3. **Lughnasadh (August 1)**: The first day of fall in the pagan calendar, marking the beginning of harvest season and the ripening of first fruits. Coleman notes this is truly bounteous harvest time, with nearly every crop in full production. It is also the start of the fall planting season for winter crops.

4. **Samhain (November 1)**: Celebrates the start of winter and the last of the harvest. This corresponds to the end of the outdoor-harvest season and the beginning of the next Persephone period when growth slows for winter.

Coleman observes that when farming with nature, the dates and meanings of these ancient Celtic celebrations fit closely with practical day-to-day farming experience.

## Effects on Plant Growth

### Growth Rate Changes

The pattern of growth rate changes through the year follows a consistent curve for most crops:

- **Summer (June–September)**: Normal days-to-maturity (e.g., 40 days from seeding to harvest)
- **Fall (October–early November)**: Gradual increase in days-to-maturity as days shorten
- **Persephone period (November–February)**: Days-to-maturity roughly doubles in a cool (minimally heated) house and triples in a cold (unheated) house compared to summer rates
- **Late winter/early spring (February–March)**: Gradual return to normal rates as days lengthen

### Planning Implications

For a crop that takes 40 days to mature for a September 1 or June 1 harvest:
- **Midwinter cool house**: ~80 days from sowing to maturity
- **Midwinter cold house**: ~120 days from sowing to maturity

This means that to harvest a crop on March 15 in a cold greenhouse, a grower would need to plant approximately 118 days earlier — around November 17.

Growers at different latitudes should adjust their calculations based on the ratio between their day length and that of the reference latitude (~45°N). Days to harvest will be longer at higher latitudes and shorter at lower latitudes.

### Cut-and-Come-Again Crops

Crops like spinach, Swiss chard, claytonia, and tatsoi continue productive regrowth after harvest throughout the Persephone months, provided they have well-established root systems. The key is that these crops must be established before the Persephone period begins:

- **Cutoff date for new sowings**: End of October
- **After the cutoff**: Seeds will germinate but grow very slowly, "sitting there waiting expectantly"
- **After ten-hour days return in February**: Growth accelerates, with earlier-sown crops maturing first

## Day Length and the Winter Sun Myth

A common misconception is that the eastern United States lacks sufficient winter sunlight for vegetable production. Coleman addresses this with a striking latitude comparison:

- **Portland, Maine** (44°N) shares its latitude with St. Tropez, France
- **New York City** (41°N) shares its latitude with Naples, Italy
- **Washington, D.C.** (39°N) shares its latitude with Majorca and Corfu
- **The southern U.S.** below 37°N shares day length with northern Africa

The British, by contrast, face genuine winter light challenges. The southern tip of Great Britain sits just above 50°N — the same latitude as Kiev, Ukraine; Moose Jaw, Saskatchewan; and Seal Cove, Newfoundland. All are some 70 miles north of the U.S.–Canadian border. The heartland of Britain (54°N) shares latitude with Great Whale River, Quebec, on the shores of icy Hudson Bay — truly "up there with the polar bears."

## Calculating Day Length

The National Weather Service provides day-length calculations for any location. The formula for adjusting planting dates based on latitude is:

> Days to harvest at your latitude = Days to harvest at reference latitude × (Your Persephone period duration ÷ Reference Persephone period duration)

Alternatively, growers can simply count backward from their desired harvest date using the appropriate days-to-maturity multiplier for their greenhouse type (2× for cool house, 3× for cold house) during the Persephone months.

## Practical Applications

1. **All winter crops must reach minimum harvestable size before the Persephone period begins** at the grower's latitude
2. **Planting dates must be calculated backward** from desired harvest dates, accounting for the growth-rate slowdown
3. **[[coleman-succession-planting]]** before the Persephone period ensures a spread of harvest dates during the slow-growth months
4. **True winter annuals** (mâche, claytonia, spinach) will germinate if sown during short days but will grow very slowly until February
5. **The Persephone period is not a barrier** to winter production — it is a biological reality that must be planned around, not fought against
