# Mushroom Grain Spawn Preparation

Grain spawn is the bridge between small-scale agar culture and full-scale substrate cultivation. Mycelium grown on sterile grain serves as inoculum for bulk substrates, providing thousands of inoculation points for rapid colonization.

## What is Grain Spawn?

Grain spawn consists of cereal grains (rye, brown rice, millet, wheat) that have been sterilized and inoculated with mushroom mycelium. The mycelium colonizes the grain, converting each kernel into a self-contained unit of nutrition and inoculum. When spawned into a bulk substrate, each grain kernel becomes a point from which mycelium can spread.

## Grain Types

### Rye Grain
The traditional and most widely recommended grain. Good moisture retention, individual kernel integrity, and nutritional profile. Used by Oss & Oeric and Stamets as the standard.

### Brown Rice
Used in PF TEK (as flour) and in research (whole grains). Higher contamination resistance in some applications but less ideal as whole grain spawn due to tendency to clump.

### Millet
Small grain with many individual kernels per volume, providing many inoculation points. Popular among experienced cultivators.

### Wheat
Good alternative to rye with similar properties.

## Grain Preparation

### Basic Rye Grain Method (from Stamets / Oss & Oeric)

1. **Measure grain**: Calculate the dry weight needed for your jars/bags
2. **Soak or simmer**: Soak grains in water for 12-24 hours, or simmer until grains are plump but not burst
3. **Drain**: Allow grains to drain thoroughly -- surface moisture is the enemy
4. **Load**: Fill jars or bags to 2/3 capacity (allows shaking)
5. **Seal**: Use lids with filter patches or polyfill for gas exchange
6. **Sterilize**: Pressure cook at 121C for 60-90 minutes (longer for larger volumes)
7. **Cool**: Allow to cool completely in a sterile environment before inoculation

### Moisture Content
- Target: 50-55% moisture content for most grains
- Too wet: bacterial contamination, grain clumping, anaerobic conditions
- Too dry: slow colonization, poor mycelial vitality
- Test: Grains should be plump and separate when shaken, not slimy

## Inoculation of Grain

### From Agar (Grain Master)
1. Under sterile conditions (laminar flow hood preferred), open the grain jar
2. Transfer a wedge of colonized agar (approximately 1cm2) into the jar
3. Close lid immediately
4. Shake jar to distribute the agar wedge
5. Incubate at appropriate temperature

### From Grain Master (Expansion)
Once a "grain master" jar is fully colonized, it can be used to inoculate many more jars:
1. Shake the colonized grain master to break up the mycelium
2. Under sterile conditions, open both the master jar and the receiving jar
3. Pour a portion of colonized grain into the new jar (typically 10% spawn rate)
4. Seal and shake the receiving jar
5. Expansion ratio of 1:10 is standard (one master jar inoculates ten new jars)

### Liquid Inoculation (LI)
Stamets describes liquid inoculation techniques using mycelium grown in nutrient broth. This allows very fast expansion but requires more equipment.

## Incubation

### Temperature
- P. cubensis: 70-80F (21-27C); incubation at ~70F is recommended by PF
- Panaeolus species: ~25C or slightly higher
- Woodlovers: ~20C
- Too high: mycelium "sweats," producing metabolites and sickening the culture
- Too low: slow growth, but never as problematic as too high
- Temperature fluctuations cause condensation on lids

### Colonization Time
- Typical grain spawn: 2-3 weeks for P. cubensis
- Panaeolus species: colonize grain less densely; 5-10 days on bulk substrate
- Wood chip spawn: 3-4 weeks at 20C

### Shaking
Grain jars should be shaken at 20-30% colonization to redistribute the mycelium and accelerate full colonization. A second shake at 50-70% can further speed things up.

## Spawn Storage

Colonized grain spawn can be stored in the refrigerator for several weeks to months, though viability decreases over time. For long-term storage, [[mushroom-agar-work]] cultures on slants are preferred (last at least 1 year refrigerated).

## Grain-to-Bulk Transition

Once grain spawn is fully colonized, it is mixed with a bulk substrate:

1. Prepare bulk substrate (see [[mushroom-substrate-recipes]])
2. In a sterile or semi-sterile environment, break up the grain spawn
3. Mix spawn with bulk substrate at a 10-30% ratio by volume
4. Transfer to fruiting container
5. Cover and incubate until fully colonized
6. Introduce fruiting conditions

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Sour/foul odor at inoculation | Bacterial contamination in grain | Increase sterilization time; verify grain isn't too wet |
| No growth after 2 weeks | Dead spores/mycelium; jar too hot | Cool jars before inoculation; use fresh cultures |
| Wet spot (Bacillus) | Insufficient sterilization | Extend pressure cooking time |
| Mold growth (green/black) | Contaminated inoculum or technique | Improve [[mushroom-sterile-technique]]; use control jars |
| Slow colonization | Low temperature; dry grain | Increase temperature slightly; verify moisture content |
| Grain clumping | Too wet; over-cooked grains | Reduce soak/simmer time; ensure thorough draining |

## Supplementation

Stamets notes that substrates can be supplemented at spawning or casing to increase yields. However, supplementation increases contamination risk and is generally not recommended for beginners.

The Gartz study demonstrated that adding 10^-2 ppm of the brassinosteroid 22S,23S-homobrassinolide to substrates significantly promoted mycelial growth (2-3x faster on agar) and accelerated fruiting (3-3.5 weeks vs 4-5 weeks for controls), while increasing total dry mass yield (3.4-3.9g vs 2.3-2.9g per 10g substrate).
