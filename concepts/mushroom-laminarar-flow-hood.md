## Overview
A laminar flow hood (LFH) is a piece of equipment that creates a continuous stream of HEPA-filtered air moving in parallel lines across a work surface. This sterile airflow prevents airborne contaminants from settling on exposed cultures during inoculation, agar work, and other sterile procedures essential for mushroom cultivation. The laminar flow hood is the single most important piece of equipment for reliable mushroom cultivation, as contamination is the primary cause of culture failure. The [[pf-tek-guide]] and [[pf-tek-guide|Stamets' Mushroom Cultivator]] both emphasize its importance for laboratory-grade results.

## How Laminar Flow Works
A blower or fan draws room air through a pre-filter (typically a MERV-rated furnace filter) that captures large particles — dust, hair, and debris. The air then passes through a HEPA (High Efficiency Particulate Air) filter, which removes 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns and larger, including bacteria (0.5-5 microns) and fungal spores (1-30 microns). The filtered air exits through the HEPA filter in parallel streams — laminar flow — that sweep contaminants away from the work area rather than creating turbulent eddies that could deposit particles on sterile surfaces. The work area must be at least 6 inches behind the filter face to ensure laminar flow has fully developed.

## Horizontal vs. Vertical Flow
Horizontal flow hoods blow filtered air directly toward the operator, creating a "wall" of clean air between the work surface and the room. The operator works with their hands between the filter and the cultures. Vertical flow hoods (also called clean benches) direct filtered air downward onto the work surface. Vertical flow is preferable when working with volatile chemicals or odors, as the air is directed away from the operator. For mushroom cultivation, horizontal flow is generally preferred because it provides better visibility and easier access to the work surface.

## DIY Construction
Building a laminar flow hood is a common project for serious cultivators. The basic components are: a box or frame (plywood or metal), a HEPA filter (typically 24x24x12 inches for a 2x2 foot work area), and a blower motor sized to provide the correct face velocity. The critical design parameter is face velocity — the speed at which air exits the HEPA filter. The standard target is 100 feet per minute (fpm), which provides sufficient air movement to prevent contaminant settling without creating turbulence that could disturb lightweight materials. Too fast (>120 fpm) creates turbulence; too slow (<80 fpm) allows contaminants to settle. The blower must be matched to the HEPA filter's static pressure rating.

## Operating Procedures
Before use, the hood should run for 15-30 minutes to flush the workspace with clean air. All tools, containers, and culture media must be sterilized before being placed in the hood — the LFH maintains sterility but does not sterilize items. The operator should wear clean clothing, a face mask, and gloves, and avoid talking or moving unnecessarily over the work area. Arms should be placed inside the hood slowly to minimize disruption of laminar flow. Work surfaces should be cleaned with 70% isopropyl alcohol before and after each session. Regular HEPA filter replacement (typically every 2-5 years depending on use) and pre-filter cleaning maintain performance.

## Alternatives to Laminar Flow Hoods
For cultivators without a laminar flow hood, a Still Air Box (SAB) — a simple container with arm holes — provides a draft-free environment for basic inoculation work. While far less effective than a LFH, a SAB significantly reduces contamination rates compared to open-air work. The [[mushroom-grow-equipment]] comparison shows that while SABs are adequate for grain-to-grain transfers and simple inoculations, they are not suitable for agar work, spore printing, or any procedure requiring extended sterile time. For commercial cultivation, a LFH is an essential investment that pays for itself through reduced contamination losses.
