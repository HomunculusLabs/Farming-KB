## Beyond Cooperation vs Competition

Peter Kropotkin was one of the first of a long line of critics of the social Darwinists. He provided extensive evidence from both nature and human history that cooperative and symbiotic relationships were at least as important as competition and predation. Kropotkin's work had a strong influence on Holmgren's early thinking in developing the Permaculture concept.

Charles Darwin's emphasis on competitive and predatory relationships in driving evolution was based on some excellent observations of wild nature, but he was also influenced by his observations of society around him. Early industrial England was a rapidly changing society, tapping new energy sources. Predatory and competitive economic relationships were overturning previous social norms. The social Darwinists used Darwin's work to explain and justify industrial capitalism and the free market.

## Symbiosis as Fundamental

The lichen, composed of an alga and a fungus living in mutualistic symbiosis, serves as an icon for the principle of integration. The alga, a photosynthesising producer of carbohydrate energy from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide, is partnered with a fungus which in return for food energy provides habitat and assists in the provision of mineral nutrients and water.

This mutualistic relationship is not merely cooperation -- it is a fusion of identities that creates something neither partner could achieve alone. The lichen is not an alga plus a fungus; it is a new organism with capabilities beyond either component. This model suggests that the most powerful integrations in human systems may similarly create emergent properties not predictable from the components.

## Guilds as Functional Assemblages

A guild is a "harmonious assembly of species clustered around a central element (plant or animal)." Guilds can be reliably demonstrated at a bioregional and local level. They represent the practical application of ecological integration in permaculture design.

The concept of guilds draws on both ecological science and traditional agricultural knowledge. Indigenous farming systems around the world have long combined species in ways that create mutually beneficial assemblages -- the Three Sisters of corn, beans, and squash being the most well-known example.

## The Spectrum of Ecological Relationships

Ecological relationships exist on a spectrum from strongly competitive to strongly cooperative:
- Mutualism: both partners benefit (lichen, mycorrhizae)
- Commensalism: one benefits, the other is unaffected (epiphytes)
- Predation: one benefits, the other is consumed
- Parasitism: one benefits at the expense of the other
- Competition: both are disadvantaged
- Amensalism: one is disadvantaged, the other unaffected

Understanding this spectrum prevents the oversimplification of assuming all natural interactions are cooperative. Predation, parasitism, and competition are all important ecological processes that maintain system health. The design challenge is to create systems where competitive and predatory relationships are balanced by cooperative ones, and where the overall system trend is toward increasing complexity and productivity.

## Competition as Creative Force

Competition in nature often drives innovation and specialisation. The competition for light among forest trees produces the vertical stratification that creates habitat diversity. The competition for pollinators among flowering plants drives the diversification of flower forms and blooming times. Without competition, systems tend toward stagnation.

The problem in human systems is not competition per se but the assumption that competition is the only or primary driver of progress. Industrial capitalism has elevated competition to a principle while suppressing the cooperative dimensions that are equally important in nature.
