## Early Summer: Abundance Begins

Spring brings walks along the seashore for seaweed, a blue food. Loquats glisten in
the early summer rains, a fruit the body craves because all fruits ripen at the right
time. Fukuoka emphasizes the principle of using the whole plant: not only can loquat
flesh be eaten, the seeds can be ground for coffee substitute and the leaves infused
for tea described as the best of all medicines. Peach and persimmon leaves yield a
potion for longevity.

The pickled green Japanese apricot (ume) season arrives alongside the bracing flavor
of pickled scallion. As the rainy season gives way to summer, one naturally hankers
for the fresh beauty and taste of peach, and the bitter-sour flavors of oleaster
berry, plum, and apricot. Under the hot midsummer sun, melon, milk, and honey in
cool shade revive the body, while rapeseed oil and sesame oil combat summer swelter.

## Autumn: The Harvest of Grains

Many fruits ripen in early autumn, when yellow foods such as cereal grains, soybeans,
and adzuki beans also become available. Millet dumplings under the moonlight; taros
and green soybeans in the pod; corn, red beans and rice, matsutake mushrooms and
rice, chestnuts and rice in late autumn. Most welcome of all are the ripened grains
of rice that have fully absorbed the yang of summer, providing a food staple rich in
calories for winter preparation.

Barley, slightly more yin than rice, is harvested in spring and eaten as iced or hot
noodles, suiting the palate as summer appetite lags. Buckwheat, harvested in late
summer and early fall, is a strongly yang grain yet most essential during summer.
