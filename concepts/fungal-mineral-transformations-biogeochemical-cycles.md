# Fungal Mineral Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycles

## Overview
Fungi play critical and often underappreciated roles in biogeochemical cycling — the
transformations and movement of chemical elements through the biosphere. The field of
geomycology, as articulated by geoffrey gadd and colleagues, encompasses the diverse
ways in which fungi interact with minerals and metals, mediating transformations that
affect soil fertility, environmental quality, and ecosystem productivity. These roles
extend far beyond simple decomposition to include mineral dissolution, precipitation,
oxidation-reduction reactions, and the formation of biogenic minerals.

## The Geomycological Perspective
Traditional biogeochemistry has focused primarily on bacterial-mediated transformations,
with fungi receiving comparatively little attention despite their dominance in many soil
ecosystems. In soil, fungi can comprise the largest pool of biomass, exceeding that of
other microorganisms and invertebrates. Their filamentous growth habit and exopolymer
production contribute to soil structure maintenance, while their metabolic versatility
enables them to drive transformations of rocks, minerals, and metals that profoundly
influence [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] and contaminant mobility.

Gadd and colleagues advocate for a "geomycological perspective" that recognizes fungi
as active geological agents — organisms that can weather rocks, solubilize minerals,
precipitate new mineral phases, and transform metal speciation. This perspective
challenges the traditional view of minerals as passive substrates and instead treats
the fungus-mineral interface as a dynamic biochemical system.

## Mechanisms of Mineral Transformation

### Acidolysis and Complexolysis
Fungi solubilize minerals through several complementary mechanisms. Acidolysis involves
the excretion of organic acids (such as oxalic, citric, and gluconic acids) that lower
pH at the fungal-mineral interface, directly dissolving acid-soluble minerals. Oxalic
acid is particularly important in [[lignin-degradation]], using a suite of extracellular oxidative enzymes
(lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases) to break down this
recalcitrant polymer. This capability gives fungi a unique and irreplaceable role in
the terrestrial carbon cycle.

Through their decomposer activity, fungi mineralize organic carbon to CO2, returning
carbon to the atmosphere and making nutrients locked in organic matter available for
plant uptake. [[saprotrophic-fungi]] with different
ecological strategies exhibit different patterns of nitrogen use, as revealed by
natural abundance measurements of 15N and 13C isotopes.

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] access organic nitrogen sources (proteins, peptides, amino acids)
directly from soil organic matter, bypassing the mineralization step that was
previously thought to be required before plants could access organic nitrogen. This
direct uptake pathway can supply a significant fraction of plant nitrogen demand,
particularly in nitrogen-limited ecosystems such as boreal and tundra forests.

## Fungi in the Phosphorus Cycle
The solubilization of phosphate from insoluble mineral forms is one of the most
practically significant fungal contributions to biogeochemical cycling. Many fungi
produce organic acids and siderophores that can dissolve [[bionutrients-lab-calcium-phosphate-calamansi]], iron
phosphate, and aluminum phosphate minerals, converting phosphorus into plant-
available forms. Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective depletion zone around plant
roots by orders of magnitude, accessing phosphorus from soil volumes that roots alone
cannot reach.
