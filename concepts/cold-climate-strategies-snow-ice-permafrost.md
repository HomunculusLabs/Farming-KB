# Cold Climate Strategies — Snow, Ice, and Permafrost

[[avalanche-phenomenon]] demand a specialised set of design techniques that exploit the unique properties of snow, ice, and frozen ground. While most literature focuses on heating, cold itself is a powerful tool for preservation, concentration, construction, [[climate-and-microclimate-management]].

## Useful Properties of Cold Phenomena

- **Albedo (reflective heating)**: Fresh dry snow reflects up to 100% of incoming light; muddy slush about 20%. Reflection is most effective at low sun angles — dawn, evening, and winter — precisely when heat is most needed
- **Snow insulation**: Snow thicker than 15 cm acts as an insulator for soils, buildings, and [[permafrost]], reducing heat loss dramatically
- **Ice preservation**: Ice frozen in winter and stored in insulated containers lasts through summer, providing refrigeration before mechanical systems existed
- **Ice as structural material**: Ice can be moulded into any shape, reinforced with fibres, and used for lenses, containers, and even towed transport
- **Freeze concentration**: Freezing concentrates sugars, alcohol, and salt solutions as efficiently as heating distils water. Maple sugar production exploits this — surface ice is removed daily until a sugar concentrate remains

## Snow as a Design Resource

### Heat Concentration
Earth structures shaped as reflectors or parabolic dishes, when covered with snow, produce intense reflected heat. Houses built within earth-formed reflectors, or designed as reflectors themselves, use snow for both heating and insulation. Low sun angles are "bounced" off snow surfaces, potentially adding 60–70% more heat to walls.

The Chinese use slanted bamboo and straw lean-tos to achieve early vegetable growth and [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] season. Stone walls backed by birch trees create early warm sites — snow melts rapidly around birch stems for about 4 metres. Semicircles of tyres facing the low sun, optionally topped with glass, create miniature grow-holes, especially when tyres are earth-filled or half-filled with water to store daytime heat.

### Water Storage
Snow is more durable in landscape than rain, making it easier to store. Compacted snow endures more than one season. Fences, hedgerows, and plant barriers catch and heap snow, directing it to melt into swales or cisterns in spring. Trees store snow as sheets, clumps, or drifts depending on species form.

### Wildlife Forage Systems
In fall, wildlife gathers acorns, filberts, and hickory nuts as winter stores. Field and pack rats bring wild rice from marshes. By providing artificial dens (hollow pipes, logs, nest boxes, wall cavities), foragers will collect and store seed — 85% of which can be harvested for human use if 15% is left for the workers. Grains and nuts so gathered feed poultry and form winter concentrates for other animals.

### Spring Forage
As snow melts, winter acorns sprout. Pigs and poultry released from winter quarters can forage these sprouted acorns, as can sheep and cattle. Sprouted seed food value exceeds unsprouted by factors of 10–100 times for vitamin and sugar content.

## Snow Physics

Snow is almost a "black body" for radiation, cooling rapidly at night with radiation values of 0.986–0.962. Thin snow cover chills soil to 35°C below air temperature. Snow thicker than 15 cm insulates effectively.

Light penetrates snow 10–15 cm in normal snow, but 30–60 cm in the hollow ice crystals of hoarfrost and dry snow. About 30% of outgoing radiation is heat (long wavelength), but snow is completely non-transparent to incoming long-wave radiation. Melting more commonly occurs from below due to earth heat — this basal melting causes poor snow-mass cohesion and may trigger snow creep or [[avalanche-phenomenon]].

### Snow Movement Modes
- **Microcreep**: Freeze/thaw cycles advance snow downslope a few centimetres per day
- **Snow boards**: Thick masses slip, covering metres per day
- **Snow mounds**: Streamflow effect from rocky uplands
- **Snow slides**: Velocities of 4–5 m/sec, arrestable by terraces
- **Avalanches**: Can reach 6.5 m/sec on long slopes; usually episodic (every 3–5 years)

## Avalanche Risk and Prevention

### Trigger Conditions
Avalanche risk exists on slopes steeper than 6° wherever snow exceeds 15 cm depth. Triggers include:
- Strong winds (>13–18 km/sec) breaking the crust
- Sudden freeze followed by thaw
- Warm winds causing snowmelt
- Spring thaw
- Meltwater saturating unstable layers over buried hoarfrost or ice
- A gunshot, collapsing cornice, or even a skier or rabbit

### Impact
Snow mass impact ranges from 4–54 tonnes per square metre. The compressed air blast preceding the mass travels at 330 m/sec — the real destructive force. Even snow-dust avalanches produce waves of 10–100 m/sec at 35 atmospheres pressure, sufficient to destroy trees and buildings.

### Prevention Strategies
- Preserve high-slope forests (their removal by acid rain creates new threats)
- Place essential services in tunnels
- Erect V-shaped barriers to split the avalanche front
- Prevent settlement in high-risk areas
- Snow barriers on plateaus above slopes hold snow and reduce the load on the slope below

### Indicators
Avalanche chutes are often delineated by pioneer species such as aspen. Downslope tree lean, lack of lower limbs, bare "trains" of different age classes, and blown-down forests radiating from avalanche snouts all reveal past instability. Eighty percent of all avalanches occur where old compact or dry snow is dislodged by condensation of moisture from air exceeding 70% humidity.

## Ice as a Tool

### Ice Lenses
Ice can be moulded as water into lens shapes and used as solar energy concentrators. Components of complex lenses are glued together with water (which freezes to bond them). These are cheap alternatives to glass lenses for directing heat to storages or machines.

### Ice Storage
Ice frozen inside insulated boxes in winter, stored off the ground in shaded locations, lasts through summer as a natural refrigerator. Before 1830, uninsulated ice pits packed with winter snow and earthed over provided chilled drinks — the precursor to mechanical refrigeration.

### Water Storage in Cold Climates
Ponds freezing to 15–90 cm render that depth unavailable for winter use. Storages must be left unfilled, slope-sided, or open-topped to accommodate expanding ice. Pipes must be buried to 1 metre depth or allowed to trickle continuously. The optimal placement for domestic water storage is within the house cellar structure, doubling as a [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] for heating.

## Permafrost

Permafrost occupies 47–50% of Canada, Russia, and Greenland. Peaty surfaces preserve permafrost by preventing melting and rapidly admitting cold. Removing peat through clearing, fire, or overgrazing triggers rapid thaw — thawed areas can reach 25–45 m deep within a few years. Solar thaw is about 5,000 times more effective than earth heat when peat is removed.

Much permafrost is fossil, reaching 400 m thick. Earth heat from below reduces thickness by only about 1 cm per year. Soils form through frost shattering of rock and are sorted by ice action and thaw (gelifluction).

### Loess Deposits
Windblown [[loess-soils]] (50–80% silica, 20–25% feldspar, low calcium) occur throughout Europe, Russia, China, and near continental glaciers. They support excellent forests, dry out quickly, have high porosity, and are stable as steep walls to 300 m high — making them ideal for underground housing, as used in China for millennia.

## Cold Climate Design Precautions

- Insulate and bury pipes below frost line
- Wells within insulated earth, preferably within building fabric
- Steep roof slopes in heavy snowload areas
- House design for heat storage and insulation
- Frost-heave management on bare soils (exploit for [[soil-tilth-and-seedbed-preparation]])
- Water storage in earth dams for summer gardens
- Avalanche site avoidance in settlement planning
