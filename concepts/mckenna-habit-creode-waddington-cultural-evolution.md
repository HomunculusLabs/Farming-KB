# Habit as Creode: Waddington's Developmental Pathways and Cultural Evolution

## Overview

In *Food of the Gods*, [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]] by early
hominids could have become an entrenched and self-reinforcing behavioral pathway. The creode,
literally a "necessary path," describes a channel of developmental activity that, once
established, becomes increasingly resistant to deviation. McKenna applies this biological
concept to cultural evolution, arguing that the habitual use of [[archaeology-of-psychedelic-mushrooms]] created a deep cultural channel through which human social and cognitive
evolution flowed for millennia. He describes the process as follows: once having experienced
the state of consciousness induced by the mushrooms, foraging humans would return to them
repeatedly in order to reexperience their novel effects. This process of repeated return
created what Waddington called a creode, "a pathway of developmental activity," which
McKenna equates with what is more commonly called a habit.

The significance of this framing lies in its implication that the mushroom habit was not
merely a behavioral preference but a developmental pathway that constrained and directed
subsequent cultural evolution. Just as a biological creode channels the physical development
of an organism, the mushroom creode channeled the cognitive and social development of early
human communities. The depth of this channel meant that deviation from the mushroom-using
pattern became increasingly difficult as the habit became more entrenched.

## The Mushroom Creode and Adaptive Advantage

The creode model helps explain how a behavioral pattern can become self-reinforcing even in
the absence of genetic programming. The mushroom-using groups enjoyed demonstrable adaptive
advantages: improved [[shamanic-ecstasy-and-healing]] group bonding from
high doses. These advantages created a positive feedback loop in which the benefits of
mushroom use reinforced the habit, while the habit itself deepened the creode and made
abandonment of the practice increasingly unlikely.

McKenna argues that this feedback loop operated on both the cultural and the biological level.
Culturally, the mushroom creode generated the rituals, social structures, and linguistic
practices that characterized partnership societies. Biologically, the repeated consumption
of psychoactive compounds changed the parameters of natural selection by favoring behavioral
patterns, such as language use and group cohesion, that were enhanced by the mushroom
experience. Over time, these cultural and biological channels merged into a single, deeply
entrenched developmental pathway.
