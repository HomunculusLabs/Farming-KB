# Plant Cellulose and Carbohydrate Chemistry

Carbohydrates are the most abundant organic molecules on Earth. In plants, they serve as structural materials (cellulose), [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]] glucose to release energy. It is the universal fuel of metabolism.

**Fructose (C6H12O6)**: An isomer of glucose -- same atoms but rearranged bonds. Much sweeter tasting. Found in fruits.

Note: Glucose and fructose have the same chemical formula but different molecular structures, demonstrating that arrangement matters as much as composition.

### Dimers (Two Units)

**Maltose (C12H22O11)**: Two glucose molecules linked together. Found in germinating seeds.

**Sucrose (C12H22O11)**: One glucose + one fructose. The primary transport sugar in most plants. Table sugar.

The linking of two monomers requires removing one water molecule (condensation reaction). This is why the formula loses one H2O compared to two separate monomers.

### Polymers (Multi-Unit Chains)

**Starch ([C6H10O5]n)**: The primary energy storage carbohydrate in plants. Long chains of glucose units stored in roots, tubers, and seeds. Potatoes, grains, and cassava are starch-rich foods.

**Cellulose ([C6H10O5]n)**: The most abundant organic polymer on Earth. Contains half of all [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]].

Starch and cellulose have the same basic subunit formula but different bond linkages. This difference is crucial:
- Starch bonds: easily broken by plant and animal enzymes (digestible)
- Cellulose bonds: cannot be broken by most animals (indigestible fiber)

## Cellulose Structure and Properties

### Chain Length

The "n" in cellulose's formula can be 500 to 14,000 or more. These enormously long chains of glucose molecules pack together in parallel arrays, forming strong, rigid microfibrils.

### Hydrogen Bonding

Cellulose chains are held together by thousands of hydrogen bonds between adjacent chains. This gives cellulose its remarkable tensile strength -- cotton fibers (nearly pure cellulose) are among the strongest natural fibers known. The same hydrogen bonding that makes cellulose strong also makes it water-resistant and chemically stable.

### Role in Cell Walls

Cellulose microfibrils are embedded in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin to form the plant cell wall. The wall provides:
- [[fungal-fruiting-body-mechanics-osmotic-pressure]] (preventing cells from bursting)
- A scaffold for other wall components

### Cellulose and the Carbon Cycle

Because cellulose is so chemically stable, it decomposes slowly. The carbon stored in wood (mostly cellulose) can remain locked up for centuries. Fungi are the primary decomposers of cellulose -- they produce cellulase enzymes that break the bonds animals cannot. This is why fungi are essential to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] in forests.

## Starch: Energy Storage

### Structure

Starch exists in two forms:
- **Amylose**: Unbranched chains, typically 200-1,000 glucose units
- **Amylopectin**: Branched chains with 10,000+ glucose units

Most plant starch is 20-30% amylose and 70-80% amylopectin.

### Storage Locations

- **Roots**: Carrots, beets, sweet potatoes
- **Tubers**: Potatoes, yams
- **Seeds**: Grains (wheat, corn, rice), legumes
- **Stems**: Sugarcane stores sugar (not starch) in stems
