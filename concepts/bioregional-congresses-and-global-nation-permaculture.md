## Bioregional Congresses and the Concept of a Global Nation

In Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14), Bill Mollison proposes a radical reimagining of international governance built around ecological ethics rather than political power. This concept challenges the legitimacy of the existing United Nations and envisions an alternative global body composed of peoples united by a shared commitment to earth care.

## Critique of the Existing United Nations

Mollison argues that the United Nations is "neither united nor represents nations." He points out that many true nations—defined as peoples with common ethics and cultural identity—are not represented, including the Iroquois Confederation, Basques, Tartars, Kurds, Palestinians, Hawaiians, Hopi, Tibetans, Pitjatjantjara, Misquito, Aranda, Basarwa, and Herrero. Furthermore, he contends that most member states of the UN themselves repress majority populations within their borders.

## Redefining "Nation"

The proposed redefinition of nationhood departs fundamentally from territorial or ethnic criteria. A nation, in this framework, is defined as a people subscribing to a common ethic and aspiring to a similar culture. Such nations may share neither a common land base nor a common language, but are united by ethical commitments that include:

1. **Care for the earth** — to repair and conserve natural systems
2. **Seeking peace** and guarding human rights everywhere
3. **Investing all capital, intelligence, goodwill, and labour** toward these ends

Mollison observes that thousands of organizations worldwide—affinity groups, tribes, bioregional associations, spiritual groups, and non-governmental organizations—already aspire to these beneficial ends. In every continent, a majority of people want peace, a clean and forested earth, cessation of torture and malnutrition, and the right to work toward these goals. He calls this the "ethical majority."

## The Practical Path to Alliance

The transition to a new body of united nations requires relatively little additional organization. Groups that already share the minimal ethic can:

- Meet together to count their numbers and recognize each other's rights
- Discuss and accept the minimal ethical framework
- Proceed to mutual recognition

Mollison notes that there are already more active organic gardeners whose life work seeks peace and plenty than there are paid-up members of political parties or oppressive societies. The foundation for a global ethical alliance already exists; it primarily needs coordination and recognition.

## Amerindian Initiatives

Concrete steps toward this vision have already begun, particularly among Amerindian groups subject to national political oppression in both North and South America. These groups have started issuing their own passports and adopting world citizen status, united by common aims rather than territorial claims.

## Decentralized Structure

Unlike the existing United Nations, Mollison's proposed body does not require a world centre or paid administrators. Instead, it operates through:

### Affinity Groups

Groups meet as affinity clusters at alternative economic summits, bioregional congresses, tribal conferences, and garden and farm design groups. Each addresses specific areas of interest while maintaining global scope through coordination.

### Avoidance of Central Pitfalls

- **No centralized administration** — prevents the formation of power blocs
- **No tax funding** — avoids bureaucratic inefficiency
- **Regional secretariats** funded by annual fees from participant groups

### Existing Networks as Foundation

Global seed exchanges, technology groups, gardening forums, and regional associations already meet and are increasing in cooperation. The concept of a global nation is already well-developed within these networks. The idea of war or oppression across racial, linguistic, or territorial lines is anathema to those already allied in practical good works.

A critical advantage of such alliances is that even geographically isolated people can find global affinities—a benefit not necessarily available through purely regional organizations.

## Bioregional and Tribal Congresses

Mollison identifies the bioregional and tribal congresses occurring at the time of writing as the first concrete moves toward a new body of nations united in earth care. These congresses serve as:

- **Meeting points** for diverse groups to share resources and strategies
- **Models of decentralized governance** that avoid the pitfalls of centralized power
- **Evidence** that global cooperation can emerge from local ecological commitment

## The Ethical Majority

The concept rests on an empirical observation: across cultures, races, and economic conditions, most people share basic desires for peace, environmental health, freedom from oppression, and meaningful work. The existing political structures do not represent this majority. A new global body, built from the ground up through ethical affiliation rather than political representation, could provide that voice.

## Implications for Permaculture Practice

For permaculture practitioners, this framework suggests:

- **Local action is globally meaningful** — bioregional work connects to a worldwide movement
- **Ethics precede politics** — shared values matter more than shared borders
- **Existing networks are the infrastructure** — seed exchanges, technology groups, and farming forums already form the skeleton of a global ethical nation
- **Decentralization is strength** — avoiding centralized power prevents the co-optation that has undermined other international bodies
