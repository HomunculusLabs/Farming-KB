# Citrus Planthopper and Leafhopper Pesticide (JADAM Recipe #8)

## Overview

JADAM pesticide recipe #8 specifically targets citrus flatid planthopper, leafhoppers, mulberry suckers, and spot clothing wax cicada. It uses [[jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] with ginkgo [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]], which is particularly effective against these sucking and jumping insects.

**Developed by:** Taehwa Bak, Hyunho Cha, Youngsang Cho

## Recipe

For 500 L (132 gal) of spray solution:

- [[jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] 10 L (2.64 gal)
- Ginkgo [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]] 15 L (4 gal)
- Water to make 500 L (132 gal)

## Target Pests

- Citrus flatid planthopper (adult and nymph)
- Ricanid planthopper
- Mulberry sucker
- Spot clothing wax cicada (adult and nymph)
- Rice green leafhopper
- Wooly apple aphid
- Slug moth larvae (secondary coverage)

## Herb Selection and Substitutions

- Use **ginkgo leaves** and/or **ginkgo fruit peels** for the JHS
- Bracken JHS can replace ginkgo
- Korean pasque flower root (KPFR) JHS can also replace ginkgo

## Application Instructions

### Standard Use
- Use good water ([[jadam-rice-water-weevil-control]]) for best wetting effect
- If JS is not used, you can spray every 1-2 days
- Add 0.5 kg (1.1 lb) of very fine red clay powder to increase effectiveness

### Soil Pesticide Application
- Halve the amounts of both JWA and ginkgo JHS for soil application
- This provides soil disinfecting effect against soil-dwelling pests

## Critical Cautions

- Do NOT continue use with JWA exceeding 10 L (2.6 gal)
- Strong JWA concentration can damage plants:
  - Damages the white powdery coating on fruits
  - Can stunt growth
- If you increase JWA and JHS, pesticide becomes stronger but risk increases

## Relative Strength

Recipe #8 is stronger than both recipe #7 (moth) and recipe #6 (aphid/mite). For the broadest coverage including stinkbugs and mealybugs, see [[jadam-stinkbug-mealybug-pesticide]] (recipe #9).

## Ginkgo Availability

Ginkgo trees are widely distributed globally, making ginkgo JHS one of the most accessible herbal solutions worldwide. Both leaves (collected spring to autumn) and fruit peels (collected autumn) can be used.

## Biology of Target Pests
Understanding the biology of the insects targeted by recipe #8 is essential for effective application timing. Citrus flatid planthoppers (Metcalfa pruinosa) are sap-sucking insects that feed on phloem tissue, extracting large quantities of plant sap and excreting honeydew that promotes the growth of sooty mould on leaves and fruit. The nymphs are covered in white, waxy filaments that give them a cottony appearance and provide some protection against contact pesticides. This waxy coating is one reason why the [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] is critical in recipe #8 — it penetrates the wax layer and allows the ginkgo-based [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]] to reach the insect's cuticle.
Leafhoppers (Cicadellidae family) are among the most economically important pests in agriculture worldwide. Both nymphs and adults feed by inserting their stylet into the vascular tissue of leaves and stems, causing stippling, chlorosis, and in severe cases, leaf curling and stunting. Many leafhopper species are also vectors of plant pathogens, particularly phytoplasmas that cause diseases such as aster yellows and rice tungro. The rice green leafhopper (Nephotettix virescens) is one of the most destructive pests of rice in Asia, transmitting rice tungro bacilliform virus and rice grassy stunt virus. JADAM's recipe #8 provides an affordable alternative to the synthetic insecticides that are commonly used against these vectors but are increasingly ineffective due to resistance development.

## Ginkgo Phytochemistry and Insecticidal Properties
The effectiveness of ginkgo [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]] against these pests is attributable to several classes of bioactive compounds found in ginkgo leaves and fruit peels. Ginkgolic acids (alkylphenol compounds) are the primary insecticidal constituents, exhibiting both contact toxicity and antifeedant properties against a range of insect species. Research has shown that ginkgolic acids disrupt mitochondrial function in insect cells, interfering with energy production and leading to cellular death at higher concentrations. At sublethal concentrations, they act as feeding deterrents, causing insects to stop feeding and eventually die from starvation.
Ginkgo also contains flavonol glycosides (quercetin, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin derivatives) and terpene lactones (ginkgolides and bilobalide) that contribute to the overall insecticidal activity. The flavonoids have been shown to interfere with insect detoxification enzymes, particularly the cytochrome P450 monooxygenase system that many insects use to metabolise plant defence compounds. By inhibiting these detoxification pathways, ginkgo flavonoids may actually enhance the toxicity of other compounds present in the JHS, creating a synergistic effect. The fruit peels of ginkgo contain higher concentrations of ginkgolic acids than the leaves, making them the preferred material when available, though leaves are more commonly used because they can be harvested without destroying the fruit.

## Timing and Application Strategy
Effective use of recipe #8 requires attention to the life cycle of target pests. Planthoppers and leafhoppers both have multiple generations per year in warm climates, with peak populations typically occurring in mid-summer when temperatures are highest. JADAM recommends beginning preventive applications in early spring when the first nymphs appear, rather than waiting until populations reach damaging levels. Early intervention with lower concentrations is more effective and less stressful on plants than attempting to control large established populations with higher doses.
For citrus crops, the critical application window is during the spring flush of new growth, when young leaves are most susceptible to planthopper feeding damage and most attractive to egg-laying adults. Spraying the new growth with recipe #8 at 7-10 day intervals during the flush period provides protection during this vulnerable stage. For rice, applications should begin at the seedling stage and continue through the tillering period, with particular attention to the base of the plant where leafhoppers congregate. The soil application method described in the recipe is particularly useful for rice, as it provides root-zone protection against soil-dwelling stages and reduces the need for foliar spraying in flooded paddies.

## Resistance Management and Rotation
JADAM emphasises the importance of rotating among different [[jadam-pest-control-recipes]] to prevent the development of insect resistance. Recipe #8 should not be used continuously throughout the season against the same pest population. Instead, JADAM recommends alternating between recipes with different modes of action — for example, rotating recipe #8 (ginkgo-based) with [[jadam-pesticide-for-aphids-and-mites]] (canola oil-based) and [[jadam-stinkbug-mealybug-pesticide]] (red clay-based) to create a multi-mode pest management programme.
This rotation strategy is grounded in the same principles that underpin conventional integrated pest management. When a single pesticidal compound is applied repeatedly, surviving individuals that possess genetic resistance traits reproduce preferentially, leading to a resistant population within as few as 5-10 generations. By rotating among compounds with different biochemical targets — ginkgolic acids that disrupt mitochondrial function, canola oil that suffocates by physical means, and red clay that desiccates through abrasion — the selection pressure for any single resistance mechanism is reduced, extending the useful life of all the materials in the rotation.

## Compatibility with Beneficial Insects
One of the advantages of JADAM's herbal pesticide system is its generally favourable profile toward beneficial insects compared to synthetic broad-spectrum insecticides. The ginkgolic acids in recipe #8 are primarily effective against soft-bodied sucking insects such as planthoppers, leafhoppers, and aphids, while having relatively low toxicity to predators such as ladybird beetles, lacewings, and predatory mites. This selective toxicity allows natural enemy populations to persist in the crop and provide supplementary biological control, reducing the need for repeated pesticide applications.
However, JADAM cautions that high concentrations of the [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] can be harmful to beneficial insects, particularly small parasitic wasps that are important natural enemies of planthoppers and leafhoppers. Keeping JWA concentrations at or below the recommended 10 L per 500 L of spray solution helps preserve parasitoid populations. JADAM practitioners are encouraged to monitor beneficial insect populations alongside pest populations and to adjust their spraying programme accordingly, increasing the interval between applications when parasitism rates are high.

## JHS Preparation from Ginkgo Materials
The preparation of effective ginkgo [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]] follows the standard JADAM herb solution protocol but with specific considerations for ginkgo materials. Fresh ginkgo leaves should be collected during the growing season (May through September), preferably in the morning after dew has dried but before the heat of the day reduces volatile oil content. The leaves are chopped into small pieces and packed into a fermentation vessel at a ratio of approximately 1 kg of fresh leaves per 10 litres of water. Brown sugar or [[jadam-animal-byproduct-liquid-fertilizer]] is added at a rate of 100g per litre of water to feed the fermentation microorganisms.
The fermentation period for ginkgo JHS is typically 7-14 days at ambient temperature, though longer fermentation can produce a stronger solution. The finished JHS should have a dark brown colour and a characteristic herbal-ginkgo aroma. Before use, the JHS is filtered through a fine mesh to remove plant debris that could clog spray equipment. Ginkgo fruit peels, collected in autumn when the fruits fall from the trees, produce a more potent JHS due to their higher ginkgolic acid content, but they require careful handling because the fresh peels can cause contact dermatitis (ginkgo poisoning) in sensitive individuals. Gloves should be worn when handling fresh ginkgo fruit, and the peels should be washed before fermentation to remove residual pulp that could interfere with the fermentation process.

## Red Clay as an Adjuvant in Recipe #8
The addition of 0.5 kg of very fine red clay powder to the recipe #8 spray solution is an important but sometimes overlooked component of the formulation. Red clay (iron-rich kaolinite or illite clay) acts as a physical adjuvant that enhances the pesticidal effect through multiple mechanisms. When the clay particles land on the insect's body, they abrade the waxy cuticle layer, creating micro-abrasions that increase the permeability of the insect's exoskeleton to the ginkgolic acids in the JHS. The clay also absorbs the moisture from the insect's body surface, contributing to desiccation.
In addition to its direct insecticidal effects, red clay improves the spray characteristics of the solution. The clay particles increase the viscosity of the spray, allowing it to adhere more effectively to plant surfaces and to the waxy bodies of target insects. This improved adhesion is particularly important for planthoppers and leafhoppers, which are highly mobile and may escape from spray droplets that do not adhere firmly to their bodies. The clay also creates a visible residue on sprayed surfaces, allowing the applicator to verify complete coverage of the target area — a practical advantage that is especially valuable for JADAM practitioners who may not have access to sophisticated spray equipment.
The red clay should be ground to a very fine powder (passing through a 200-mesh sieve) and mixed thoroughly with the water before adding the JWA and JHS. If the clay is too coarse, it will settle out of suspension during spraying and may clog spray nozzles. The combination of clay, [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]], and ginkgo JHS creates what is effectively a three-component pesticidal system: the JWA provides the surfactant action that penetrates the insect's waxy coating, the clay provides the physical abrasion and desiccation, and the ginkgo JHS provides the biochemical toxicity. This multi-modal approach makes it difficult for target pests to develop resistance through any single mechanism.

## Monitoring and Decision-Making for Recipe #8 Applications
Effective pest management with recipe #8 requires systematic monitoring of both pest populations and crop condition. JADAM recommends regular scouting of target crops at least twice per week during the growing season, paying particular attention to the undersides of leaves, the bases of stems, and the growing tips where planthoppers and leafhoppers prefer to feed and lay eggs. Yellow sticky traps placed at crop canopy height provide a simple, low-cost monitoring tool that captures flying adults and provides a quantitative measure of population density over time.
Decision thresholds for recipe #8 application depend on the crop, the growth stage, and the economic value of the damage. For high-value fruit crops such as citrus, even low populations of planthoppers justify treatment because the honeydew excretion and resulting sooty mould directly reduce fruit marketability. For field crops such as rice, the economic threshold is higher because the crop can tolerate moderate levels of leafhopper feeding without significant yield loss. JADAM recommends using the presence of honeydew on leaves as a practical indicator: when honeydew droplets are visible on more than 10-15% of leaves, treatment with recipe #8 is warranted.
Record-keeping is an important component of the JADAM pest management approach. Practitioners are encouraged to maintain a simple spray log that records the date, crop, growth stage, pest pressure level, recipe used, and any observed effects on both target pests and beneficial insects. Over successive seasons, this log becomes a valuable management tool that allows the practitioner to identify patterns in pest population dynamics, optimise spray timing, and evaluate the effectiveness of different rotation sequences among the various [[jadam-pest-control-recipes]]. This data-driven approach to pest management, combined with the low cost of JADAM inputs, makes it possible to achieve commercial-grade pest control with a fraction of the investment required by conventional synthetic pesticide programmes.

## Equipment and Spray Technique
The effectiveness of recipe #8 depends not only on the formulation but also on the equipment and technique used for application. JADAM recommends using a backpack sprayer with a cone nozzle that produces a fine mist rather than a coarse spray. The fine mist provides better coverage of plant surfaces and is more likely to reach the undersides of leaves where planthoppers and leafhoppers congregate. The spray pressure should be adjusted to produce a mist that drifts gently onto the plant surfaces rather than a directed stream that bounces off waxy leaf surfaces.
Spraying should be performed in the early morning or late evening when temperatures are moderate and wind is minimal. Spraying during the heat of the day can cause rapid evaporation of the spray solution before it reaches the target pests, and can also increase the risk of plant damage from the [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] component. The sprayer should be agitated frequently during application to keep the red clay particles in suspension and ensure uniform distribution of all components.
Complete coverage is essential for effective control. Planthoppers and leafhoppers are highly mobile and will quickly re-infest plants that have untreated areas. The applicator should spray both the upper and lower leaf surfaces, the stems, and the growing tips, paying particular attention to the sheltered areas where nymphs congregate. For large-scale applications, JADAM recommends spraying in a systematic pattern that ensures no areas are missed — for example, walking each row in both directions or using a flagging system to mark the boundaries of the sprayed area. After spraying, the sprayer should be cleaned thoroughly with clean water to prevent corrosion and residue buildup that could affect the accuracy of subsequent applications.
