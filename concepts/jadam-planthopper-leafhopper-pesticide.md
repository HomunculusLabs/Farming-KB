# Citrus Planthopper and Leafhopper Pesticide (JADAM Recipe #8)

## Overview

JADAM pesticide recipe #8 specifically targets citrus flatid planthopper, leafhoppers, mulberry suckers, and spot clothing wax cicada. It uses [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]], which is particularly effective against these sucking and jumping insects.

**Developed by:** Taehwa Bak, Hyunho Cha, Youngsang Cho

## Biology of Target Pests
Understanding the biology of the insects targeted by recipe #8 is essential for effective application timing. Citrus flatid planthoppers (Metcalfa pruinosa) are sap-sucking insects that feed on phloem tissue, extracting large quantities of plant sap and excreting honeydew that promotes the growth of sooty mould on leaves and fruit. The nymphs are covered in white, waxy filaments that give them a cottony appearance and provide some protection against contact pesticides. This waxy coating is one reason why the [[jadam-herb-solution-jhs]] to reach the insect's cuticle.
Leafhoppers (Cicadellidae family) are among the most economically important pests in agriculture worldwide. Both nymphs and adults feed by inserting their stylet into the vascular tissue of leaves and stems, causing stippling, chlorosis, and in severe cases, leaf curling and stunting. Many leafhopper species are also vectors of plant pathogens, particularly phytoplasmas that cause diseases such as aster yellows and rice tungro. The rice green leafhopper (Nephotettix virescens) is one of the most destructive pests of rice in Asia, transmitting rice tungro bacilliform virus and rice grassy stunt virus. JADAM's recipe #8 provides an affordable alternative to the synthetic insecticides that are commonly used against these vectors but are increasingly ineffective due to resistance development.

## Ginkgo Phytochemistry and Insecticidal Properties
The effectiveness of ginkgo [[jadam-pest-control-recipes]] to prevent the development of insect resistance. Recipe #8 should not be used continuously throughout the season against the same pest population. Instead, JADAM recommends alternating between recipes with different modes of action — for example, rotating recipe #8 (ginkgo-based) with [[jadam-stinkbug-mealybug-pesticide]] (red clay-based) to create a multi-mode [[cannabis-pest-management-guide]] programme.
This rotation strategy is grounded in the same principles that underpin conventional integrated pest management. When a single pesticidal compound is applied repeatedly, surviving individuals that possess genetic resistance traits reproduce preferentially, leading to a resistant population within as few as 5-10 generations. By rotating among compounds with different biochemical targets — ginkgolic acids that disrupt mitochondrial function, canola oil that suffocates by physical means, and red clay that desiccates through abrasion — the selection pressure for any single resistance mechanism is reduced, extending the useful life of all the materials in the rotation.

One of the advantages of JADAM's herbal pesticide system is its generally favourable profile toward beneficial insects compared to synthetic broad-spectrum insecticides. The ginkgolic acids in recipe #8 are primarily effective against soft-bodied sucking insects such as planthoppers, leafhoppers, and aphids, while having relatively [[fukuoka-low-toxicity-pesticide-deception]] to predators such as ladybird beetles, lacewings, and [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]]. The leaves are chopped into small pieces and packed into a fermentation vessel at a ratio of approximately 1 kg of fresh leaves per 10 litres of water. Brown sugar or [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]], and ginkgo JHS creates what is effectively a three-component pesticidal system: the JWA provides the surfactant action that penetrates the insect's waxy coating, the clay provides the physical abrasion and desiccation, and the ginkgo JHS provides the biochemical toxicity. This multi-modal approach makes it difficult for target pests to develop resistance through any single mechanism.

## Monitoring and Decision-Making for Recipe #8 Applications
Effective pest management with recipe #8 requires systematic monitoring of both pest populations and crop condition. JADAM recommends regular scouting of target crops at least twice per week during the growing season, paying particular attention to the undersides of leaves, the bases of stems, and the growing tips where planthoppers and leafhoppers prefer to feed and lay eggs. Yellow sticky traps placed at crop canopy height provide a simple, low-cost monitoring tool that captures flying adults and provides a quantitative measure of population density over time.
Decision thresholds for recipe #8 application depend on the crop, the growth stage, and the economic value of the damage. For high-value fruit crops such as citrus, even low populations of planthoppers justify treatment because the honeydew excretion and resulting sooty mould directly reduce fruit marketability. For field crops such as rice, the economic threshold is higher because the crop can tolerate moderate levels of leafhopper feeding without significant yield loss. JADAM recommends using the presence of honeydew on leaves as a practical indicator: when honeydew droplets are visible on more than 10-15% of leaves, treatment with recipe #8 is warranted.
Record-keeping is an important component of the JADAM pest management approach. Practitioners are encouraged to maintain a simple spray log that records the date, crop, growth stage, pest pressure level, recipe used, and any observed effects on both target pests and beneficial insects. Over successive seasons, this log becomes a valuable management tool that allows the practitioner to identify patterns in pest population dynamics, optimise spray timing, and evaluate the effectiveness of different rotation sequences among the various [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] component. The sprayer should be agitated frequently during application to keep the red clay particles in suspension and ensure uniform distribution of all components.
Complete coverage is essential for effective control. Planthoppers and leafhoppers are highly mobile and will quickly re-infest plants that have untreated areas. The applicator should spray both the upper and lower leaf surfaces, the stems, and the growing tips, paying particular attention to the sheltered areas where nymphs congregate. For large-scale applications, JADAM recommends spraying in a systematic pattern that ensures no areas are missed — for example, walking each row in both directions or using a flagging system to mark the boundaries of the sprayed area. After spraying, the sprayer should be cleaned thoroughly with clean water to prevent corrosion and residue buildup that could affect the accuracy of subsequent applications.
