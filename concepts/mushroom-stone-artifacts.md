# Mushroom Stone Artifacts in Plants of the Gods

Schultes and Hofmann document the archaeological discovery of mushroom stone
effigies across Mesoamerica, providing some of the strongest material evidence
for the great antiquity of hallucinogenic mushroom use in the New World.

## Discovery and Distribution

More than two hundred mushroom stone effigies have been discovered in
archaeological sites throughout Mesoamerica. The oldest date from the first
millennium BC, making them among the earliest archaeological evidence for
entheogenic plant use in the Americas. The majority are Guatemalan in origin,
but specimens have been unearthed as far afield as El Salvador and Honduras
to the south, and Veracruz and Guerrero in Mexico to the north.

The Mayan mushroom stone from Guatemala featured on the title page of
Plants of the Gods serves as a visual emblem for the work, underscoring the
central importance of these artifacts to the ethnobotanical narrative.

## Age and Chronology

Miniature mushroom stones approximately 2,200 years of age have been found in
archaeological sites near Guatemala City. The oldest specimens from the first
millennium BC establish that ceremonial mushroom use predates the Aztec
civilization by well over a thousand years. The artifacts span a considerable
chronological range, indicating a long-lived and evolving mushroom cult
tradition.

## Design and Iconography

The mushroom stones typically feature a cap or dome atop a columnar or
anthropomorphic base. Some specimens incorporate human or animal figures. The
design of the caps has been compared to cross-sections of psilocybin-bearing
mushrooms, and the overall morphology clearly represents fungal fruiting
bodies rather than any other natural or man-made form.

## Connection to the Popol Vuh

Schultes and Hofmann note that stone mushroom effigies buried with a Mayan
dignitary have been postulated to suggest a connection with the Nine Lords of
the Xibalba, described in the sacred Popol Vuh. This literary-archaeological
link, while not conclusively proven, provides a compelling narrative framework
connecting the material culture of mushroom stones to the mythological
traditions of the Maya. The Popol Vuh, the sacred book of the Quiche-Maya,
describes the underworld realm of Xibalba and its ruling lords. The
association of mushroom stones with burials of important individuals suggests
that these artifacts may have served as guides or protectors for the
deceased in their journey through the underworld.

## Functional Interpretation

The precise function of mushroom stones remains debated. Schultes and
Hofmann state that "whatever the use of these mushroom stones, they indicate
the great antiquity of a sophisticated sacred use of hallucinogenic
mushrooms." The artifacts may have served as ritual objects, funerary
offerings, ceremonial weights, or objects of veneration. Their consistent
fungal morphology across widely separated sites and time periods argues for a
shared cultural significance centered on mushrooms as sacred entheogens.

## Varieties and Regional Styles

The mushroom stones exhibit considerable regional variation in style and
craftsmanship. The earliest and simplest forms consist of plain domes on
columns. Later specimens become increasingly elaborate, incorporating human
faces, animal figures, and complex decorative elements. The most
sophisticated examples from the Guatemalan highlands feature full
anthropomorphic figures standing atop or emerging from mushroom caps. This
artistic evolution mirrors the development of Mesoamerican civilization
itself, from simple village cultures to complex urban societies with
specialized artisan classes.

## Prehistoric Mushroom Cult of Northwestern Mexico

In addition to the Guatemalan mushroom stones, evidence indicates a mushroom
cult flourished in prehistoric times -- from approximately 100 BC to about
AD 300-400 -- in northwestern Mexico, specifically in Colima, Jalisco, and
Nayarit. Funerary effigies with two "horns" protruding from the head are
believed to represent male and female "deities" or priests associated with
mushrooms. Traditions among contemporary Huichol Indians in Jalisco also
suggest the former religious use of these fungi "in ancient times."

## South American Parallels

While no mushroom stones per se have been documented in South America,
Schultes and Hofmann note anthropomorphic gold pectorals with two domelike
ornaments on the head found in Colombia's Darien style, mostly from the Sinu
area of northwestern Colombia and the Calima region on the Pacific coast.
These so-called "telephone spirits" may represent mushroom-associated
deities, suggesting a broader New World pattern of fungal iconography.

## Significance for Ethnobotany

The mushroom stones provide irrefutable archaeological evidence that
hallucinogenic mushrooms were culturally significant in Mesoamerica long
before the Spanish conquest. They bridge the gap between pre-Columbian
archaeology and the living mushroom traditions documented ethnographically
among the Mazatec and other contemporary indigenous groups, establishing
continuity across millennia of sacred mushroom use.
