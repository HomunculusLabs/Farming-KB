# Biomass Compost Greenhouse Heating Systems

Biomass is organic material that, when piled up, creates heat through
the action of bacteria breaking down the material. The act of piling
it up is not what generates the heat, but rather the microbial
decomposition process. This heat can be harnessed passively to warm
greenhouses, heat water, and extend growing seasons without any
external fuel inputs. As described in the Ultimate Guide to Natural
Farming and Sustainable Living, biomass compost heating is one of the
most practical and accessible renewable energy systems available to
the homesteader.

## The Science of Compost Heating

When organic materials decompose aerobically, microorganisms break
down complex carbon compounds, releasing heat as a byproduct of their
metabolic processes. A well-managed compost pile will reach
temperatures of 140 degrees Fahrenheit (60 degrees Celsius) and can
maintain that temperature for six months to a year, depending on the
size of the pile and the materials used. This heat is continuous,
reliable, and requires no external energy input beyond the initial
material gathering and pile construction.

## Passive Heat Exchange

The simplest application of biomass heating is passive heat exchange.
This involves placing the compost heap inside a building that needs
heating and running plastic piping full of water through the compost.
The compost reaches 140 degrees F (60 degrees C), and the water
circulating through the pipes absorbs this heat, which can then be
distributed through the building. The plastic piping can be thin-
walled PVC pipe, but garden hose may work even better because of its
thinner walls, which allow more efficient heat transfer.

## The Most Common Application: Greenhouse Heating

The most common and practical place to take advantage of biomass heat
is in the greenhouse, where the warmth supports plant growth during
cold months. Rather than using a wood compost bin, a ring made of
chicken wire or other mesh fencing is set up to contain the compost
pile. This ring is filled with alternating layers of compost material
and organic matter until full. Then, a coil of plastic pipe is
wrapped around and around the compost pile.

A second ring of wire fencing is set up at least four feet (one
meter) outside the first ring. More compost and organic material is
added to this outer ring until it is full. As both rings of compost
heat up through decomposition, they warm any water that is run
through the piping coiled between and around them.

## Water Heating System Integration

The heated water from the compost coils can be sent to several
destinations:

- A hot water tank for household use, reducing or eliminating the
  need for gas or electric water heating
- [[composting-toilets]] as Biomass Source

A composting toilet system is an ideal partner for biomass heating.
The solids from a composting toilet become part of the compost pile
that generates heat. The system creates a closed loop: human waste
becomes compost, the compost generates heat, the heat warms the
greenhouse where food is grown, and the food sustains the humans.
A typical suburban or rural home uses 3.5 gallons per flush, totaling
3,832 gallons per year for a family of three. A waterless composting
toilet eliminates this waste entirely while providing fuel for the
heating system.

## Seasonal Management

In spring, the heat output from winter compost piles can be used to
start seedlings early. As the weather warms, the remaining compost
can be spread on garden beds as finished fertilizer. New piles are
started in late summer to be ready for autumn heating needs. This
annual cycle ensures continuous soil fertility improvement while
providing seasonal heating exactly when it is needed most.

## Biogas Considerations

While biomass compost heating is practical at the homestead scale,
biogas production (methane from manure) is generally not recommended
for small properties. It takes about 240 pounds of fresh manure per
day to produce enough gas to fuel a stove. Since animals would
normally be foraging and fertilizing edible forests and pastures,
confining them for biogas collection breaks the nitrogen cycle.
Methane is also hazardous, as methane and oxygen mixtures can
explode. Biomass compost heating achieves similar energy goals more
