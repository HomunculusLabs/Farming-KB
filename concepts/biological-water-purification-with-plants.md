# Biological Water Purification with Plants

[[biological-water-purification]] using higher plants represents the most sophisticated and ecologically integrated approach to wastewater and polluted water treatment. Building on the foundation of anaerobic, facultative, and aerobic pond systems, specific genera of rushes, sedges, and floating plants can greatly assist with removal of heavy metals and human pathogens, and critically, some plants can break down halogenated hydrocarbons synthesised as herbicides and pesticides. [[eichhornia-crassipes]]*) removes residual metals, surplus nutrients, and *coli*-group bacteria (New Scientist, 4 Oct 1979, p. 29). Water hyacinth systems are effective final-stage polishers but must be carefully managed, as the plant is highly invasive and can clog waterways if not contained.

## Microwave Treatment

Microwave radiation has been found effective at breaking up algal mats and sterilising algal products, eliminating toxic aluminium salts that may have been introduced during algal harvesting and processing.

## Solar Pond Integration

Solar ponds used in conjunction with compact anaerobic ponds can supply the low-grade heat necessary for efficient sludge digestion. Methane from the digesters drives motors needed for both aeration and gas compression for sludge agitation. The entire processing system can be made very compact.

At the aerobic pond level, throughflow can be directed to:

- Firewood or fuel forest systems
- Irrigated grasslands (as at Werribee, Australia)
- Trickle [[irrigation-systems]] for crops in arid areas

## Reed Bed and Constructed Wetland Systems

Final treatment, used in Holland and recommended by scientists at the Max Planck Institute in Switzerland, routes water through a sinuous, sealed canal planted with a variety of rushes and floating water plants.

Waters polluted with metals, biocides, or sewage are cleaned by travelling through reed beds of *Scirpus*, *Typha*, and *Juncus*, or by harvesting floating plants such as water hyacinth. The rushes and sedges are periodically mown and removed for mulch or cellulose production.

### Treatment Parameters

- **Untreated sewage**: Holding time of 10-12 days necessary, or travel through a series of maze-like gravel-filter canals with floating weeds and sedges
- **Swimming pools and less polluted systems**: A pumped cycle of water through ferns, rushes, and watercress removes urine and leaves. These pools need a 23-30 cm (9-12 inch) coarse river gravel base with intake pipes below and a skimming notch for leaves

## Key Plant Species for Water Purification

### *Phragmites communis* and species, *Typha* species

These robust emergent macrophytes flocculate colloids, dry out sludges, and eliminate pathogens. They are among the most widely used plants in constructed wetland systems worldwide. Common reed (*Phragmites*) and cattail (*Typha*) create extensive root zone networks that support dense microbial communities responsible for the majority of pollutant removal.

### *Schoenoplectus* species (bulrushes)

These plants actively take up copper, cobalt, nickel, and manganese from contaminated water. They also exude mould antibiotics into the root zone, providing additional pathogen suppression in the treatment system.

### *Scirpus* species (club rushes)

Remarkably, *Scirpus* species break down phenols, including toxic pentachlorophenol — one of the most persistent and hazardous wood preservative chemicals. This ability to degrade halogenated hydrocarbons makes them invaluable for treating industrial wastewater contaminated with chlorinated compounds.

## Pathogen Reduction

After water passes through these biological treatment systems, low to zero populations of key indicator organisms are found:

- *E. coli*
- Coliform bacteria generally
- *Salmonella* species
- *Enterococci*

This level of pathogen reduction meets or exceeds many conventional treatment standards, achieved without chemical disinfection and its associated byproduct risks (such as trihalomethane formation from chlorination).

## Integrated Biological Treatment Schematic

A complete biological water treatment system combines multiple stages:

1. Sand filter (2 metres deep) from settling dam
2. Rush and freshwater canal bed for preliminary biological treatment
3. Settling tank with limestone base for pH adjustment
4. Household use with recirculation
5. Digester or septic tank (10-20 days retention), then to irrigation
6. Greenhouse pond with water hyacinth for nutrient stripping
7. Anaerobic pond for methane production and metal precipitation
8. Final aerobic polishing ponds
9. Rush bed filtration as the terminal treatment before release

This integrated approach produces clean water suitable for irrigation or stream release while simultaneously generating energy (biogas), protein (algae), wildlife habitat, and plant biomass for mulch or craft use — transforming what is conventionally treated as a waste disposal problem into a productive resource system.
