# Albedo, Heat Radiation, and Thermal Belts

The interaction between radiation, absorption, and heat transfer determines the thermal character of every site. In [[permaculture-design]], understanding albedo values, heat storage mechanisms, temperature inversion risks, and thermal belt positioning is essential for siting homes, gardens, and settlements effectively.

## Albedo and Absorption

**Albedo** is the reflected light value of a surface — the fraction of incoming radiation that bounces back into the atmosphere. Light reflected goes back into space or is absorbed by nearby surfaces and structures such as greenhouses. The light absorbed is converted into long-wave radiation and re-emitted as heat. A perfect reflector has an albedo of 100% (refuses all light); a perfect absorber is a black body that absorbs all light and converts it to heat (0% albedo).

### Albedo Reference Table

The following table indicates the reflected and absorbed percentages for common natural and artificial surfaces:

- **Perfect reflector** — 100% reflected, 0% absorbed (mirrors)
- **White smooth paint** — 96% reflected, 4% absorbed
- **Clean fresh snow** — 75–95% reflected, 5–25% absorbed
- **White gravel** — 50–93% reflected, 7–50% absorbed
- **Dense white clouds** — 60–90% reflected, 10–40% absorbed
- **Calm water** (sun ≤15° elevation) — 50–80% reflected, 20–50% absorbed
- **Adapted desert shrubs** — 30–38% reflected, 62–70% absorbed
- **Sand dunes** — 30–40% reflected, 60–70% absorbed
- **Sandy soils** — 15–40% reflected, 60–85% absorbed
- **Dry hay** — 20–40% reflected, 60–80% absorbed
- **Wood edges** — 5–40% reflected, 60–95% absorbed
- **High sun, rough water** — 8–15% reflected, 75–92% absorbed
- **Young oaks** — 18% reflected, 82% absorbed
- **Young pines** — 14% reflected, 86% absorbed
- **Dark soils** — 7–10% reflected, 90–93% absorbed
- **Fir forest** — 10% reflected, 90% absorbed
- **Perfect black body** — 0% reflected, 100% absorbed

## Fate of Radiation on Surfaces

The fate of incoming waves encountering an object or substance is one of four outcomes:

- **Reflected:** Turned away almost unchanged, as light off a flat mirror or a white wall.
- **Refracted:** Sharply bent or curved, as light in water, images in curved glass, or sea waves around a headland.
- **Absorbed:** Soaked in, as when a black object absorbs light. This changes the wavelength from light to heat (short to long wavelength). All absorbed light is emitted as heat.
- **Transmitted:** Passed through the object entirely.

Different substances pass on, or are transparent to, different wavelengths due to their molecular structure. Thus it is by the choice of materials, colours, or shapes of fabricated or natural components that designers manipulate energy on a site — redirecting, converting, or passing on incoming energy.

The Earth acts like a black body, accepting short wavelengths from the sun and emitting long wavelengths from its surface and atmosphere. Curiously, snow is also a black body in terms of heat radiation. Black objects such as crows or charcoal can become effective reflectors if their shiny surfaces are adjusted to reflect radiation at certain angles to incoming light.

## Heat Transfer Mechanisms

Heat is transferred through three grades of intensity:

### Low-Grade Heat: Conduction
Conduction is heat transmitted by solid-to-solid or solid-to-fluid contact. Examples include heating an entire floor or wall from one heated point — the basis of the efficiency of slab-floored houses where the floor is insulated from surrounding earth. Pipes buried in hot solid masses have heat conducted to their contents, and hot water pipes conduct heat to slab floors in which they are buried. Such heating is most efficient in homes. In open (uninsulated) systems, conduction effects are local as heat is fairly rapidly radiated from solids or soil surfaces.

### Medium-Grade Heat: Convection
Convection is heat transmitted by the movements of fluids or gases — air, wind, or water. The key principle: heated fluids rise. Draught-proofing conserves convective heat losses in buildings. Air is difficult to heat and stores little heat, making air convection an inefficient way to heat building interiors despite being the main engine of atmospheric circulation globally.

### High-Grade Heat: Radiation
Radiation is heat travelling by straight-line radiation across space — the effect by which the sun heats the Earth. Radiant heaters affect air temperature very little but heat other solids and liquids (like our bodies) directly. Thus we can keep very warm even in a draughty or cool room by using radiant electric, gas-fire, or wood-heated mass stoves — very efficient space heaters. As radiation crosses space and is non-directional, focused radiation can produce very intense local heat. Only reflective surfaces (dust-free mirrors) can conserve radiant heat.

## Temperature Extremes for Design

Understanding thermal limits is critical for site planning. Poultry and many wild birds do not survive temperatures greatly exceeding 43°C (109°F). Plants do not survive transplant shock from nursery stock when soil temperatures exceed 36°C (97°F), whether in deserts or in compost piles. Many plants are frost-affected at or below 0°C (32°F), and sustained periods of lower temperatures eliminate even hardy, well-established species.

The very widespread and sometimes economically disastrous **black frosts** that affect whole regions should be noted by site designers as much as flood periodicity. Livelihoods should not depend on broadscale plantings of frost-susceptible crops in frost-prone situations.

## Mean Temperatures and Lapse Rates

The mean temperature of the Earth is 5°C (41°F), of the air at or near ground level 14°C (57°F), and of the outer layers of the atmosphere -50°C to -80°C (-90°F to -144°F). Normally we lose about 1°C for every 100 m increase in altitude (3°F per 1,000 feet).

## Temperature Inversions

In many valleys or on plains surrounded by mountains, cool air from the hills or cold air generated by rapid radiation loss from soils creates a condition where layers of dense cooler air are trapped below warmer air — a **temperature inversion**. It is in such conditions that fog, smog, and pollution can build up over cities lying in valleys or plains where wind effect is slight. Such sites must be carefully analysed for potential pollutants before settlement.

## Thermal Belts

In hill country and mountains, the **thermal belt** is the midslope zone that is warmer than hilltops (exposed to chilling winds) and valley floors (where cold air pools at night). Sun-facing slopes not only absorb more heat but drain off cold air at night. They lie below the chilly hilltops and above the cold night air of valleys and plains.

Key elevation ranges for thermal belts:

- **Mountains:** Thermal belts at 1,000–5,000 m (3,280–16,400 feet)
- **Lower hills:** Thermal belts at 100–200 m (330–650 feet)
- **Hot deserts:** Frost levels may only reach 10–15 m (33–49 feet) up the slopes of mesas

Each situation needs specific information from local anecdotes, observation of existing plants, or trial plantings of frost-susceptible species. Siting houses on sun-facing slopes in the thermal belt is a critical energy-conservation strategy in all but tropical climates.
