## Fungal Spawn and Mycelium Growth

## Overview

The visible mushroom is merely the fruiting body of a far larger organism that lives underground. The subterranean network of white, thread-like fibers — the **mycelium** (called **spawn** in horticultural language) — is the true body of the fungus, analogous to the vine of a grape plant, while the mushroom itself represents the fruit. Understanding spawn and mycelium is fundamental to understanding how fungi grow, reproduce, and can be cultivated.

## What Is Spawn?

Spawn is the common name for the vegetative, spreading part of a fungus that exists below the soil surface. When the soil is broken open, spawn appears as a white, branching substance that divides and subdivides like the veins on the back of a hand. It emits a strong, characteristic odor detectable from a distance. This subterranean "white vine" is nothing less than the living body of the fungus, ramifying through the soil in search of nutrients.

## Mycelium as the True Fungal Body

The entire earth beneath our feet on a country walk is alive with mycelial vegetation to a great depth. The various vines — the thousand distinct varieties of mushroom-spawn — are just as specific and recognizable to the trained eye as the hop-vine, woodbine, ivy, and virgin's-bower that twine above ground. Where one variety of toadstool grew this year, the same plant will appear next year and in successive seasons, just as predictably as a farmer's wheat crop returns.

This regularity disproves the popular error that toadstools "spring up in a night" in unforeseen places for unknown causes. The mycelium is always present, growing steadily, and only fruits when conditions are favorable.

## Growth Patterns and Timing

### Not Overnight After All

The common belief that mushrooms appear overnight is largely an illusion. Many species form below the soil surface for days before becoming visible. A heavy dew or evening shower straightens the stem and expands the cap. The fungus breaks through the earth during the night, so a gatherer who saw nothing the day before finds white buttons in the morning. The apparent suddenness of mushroom growth has made it proverbial for superficiality — an undeserved reputation.

### Seasonal Succession

Different varieties of fungi succeed each other in rotation through the growing season, just as spring bloodroots and anemones give way to summer roses and fall cardinal flowers. These are not theories but the results of years of careful observation. The mushroom-lover can plan regular harvests with all the certainty of the farmer — with the same degree of uncertainty, no more and no less. Certain years favor certain fungal fruits, just as potato crops sometimes fail and apple orchards occasionally go barren.

### Permanence of Mycelial Beds

The author observed the same varieties fruiting in the same spots over a span of five years (1874–1879), demonstrating that fungal spawn can persist and fruit reliably for extended periods. However, spawn is not immortal — blight can strike fungal life just as it afflicts animals and plants, and under adverse conditions, a mycelial bed may weaken or become extinct.

## Mushroom Cultivation

### The Propagable Species

With a single exception, the conditions for propagating mushrooms artificially were unknown in the 19th century. That exception is the horse-mushroom (*Agaricus arvensis*), which follows the animal whose name it bears — it can be grown in and by horse manure, and in no other way. The closely related meadow mushroom (*Agaricus campestris*) is probably the same species in a less cultivated state. This is why the popular mind reserves the word "mushroom" for just one variety: it is the only one that can be reliably produced.

### The Cultivation Process

The cultivation process follows the principle of yeast in breadmaking. A piece of earth permeated by the white, cellular vine (spawn) is placed in prepared manure. The manure should be about two-thirds horse manure and one-third from other grazing animals or rich loam. It must be broken and turned until a thermometer shows a temperature of 60–80°F. Small blocks of spawn are then inserted. In about three weeks, the entire mass becomes filled with the white vine, and roughly ten days after that, mushrooms begin to appear.

### Common Causes of Failure

Many gardeners have found this process fails in practice, accounting for the high price of cultivated mushrooms ($1–2 per pound in the 19th century). Three principal causes of failure were identified:

1. **Bad spawn**: Much commercially sold spawn contains no trace of actual fungal material. It is far better to collect spawn from the edge of an old mushroom bed.
2. **Whole-bed spawning**: Planting the entire bed at once means a single picking followed by barrenness. The bed should be planted in sections, like a Pullman car, so that while one end is bearing, the other is just preparing.
3. **Atmospheric neglect**: Fungi are extremely sensitive to atmospheric conditions. Surface watering is worse than useless — moisture must come from beneath, like natural dew. Mushroom beds should have nothing between them and mother earth.

### The Steam Solution

In greenhouses, steam pipes can provide the necessary warm, humid atmosphere. Vents opened in steam-pipes beneath benches allow steam to collect and condense on the soil, keeping it moist and warm. With such a system, mushrooms can be grown with all the certainty of other crops.

## The Mystery of Spore-to-Spawn Reproduction

### Spores Are Not Seeds

Fungal spores differ fundamentally from seeds: they contain no trace of an embryo. Even the most powerful microscope fails to reveal any germ of a future plant within them. A spore print — made by placing a mushroom cap gill-down on paper — produces fine powder in white, brown, purple, rosy, or black, according to the species. Yet the most learned mycologist cannot determine the exact species from its spores alone; at best, they can identify which of five grand divisions of fungi (numbering seven hundred members) it belongs to.

### The Unsolved Problem

The true mystery of fungal growth lies in the propagation of spawn from spores. The author scattered the refuse of hundreds of fungi on a grass plot and the soil must have received millions of spores — yet not a single toadstool ever grew there. Spawn appears to be a collection of oblong cells united at their ends, with the whole plant seemingly a mass of these cells extended into interlaced chains. But what triggers their initial growth? How do certain points become fructified and develop into visible mushrooms? When different fungal species grow side by side and their mycelial beds interlace beneath the soil, why does one matrix produce wholesome food while another yields mortal poison?

### Hints of Sexual Reproduction

Some fungal spores show conjugation under the microscope, indicating a sexual differentiation. This suggests that fungal reproduction may be more analogous to animal than to vegetable nature — a hypothesis further supported by the taste, odor, and nutritional constituents of edible fungi, which resemble no form of vegetable food. The humble fungi crushed underfoot may be nearer in nature to animals than to the lofty oak above them.

## The Amanita Family

All or nearly all fatal mushroom-poisoning cases are attributable to the **Amanita** family. Ten varieties were found in the vicinity of Boston alone. They can be identified by three marks that must exist together:

- **Wrapper (volva)**: A white membranous cup around the base of the stem, close to the soil
- **Ring (annulus)**: A similar membrane in the form of a ragged veil encircling the stem about two-thirds up from the ground
- **Scurfy top**: Irregular collection of warts on the cap, quite defined in young plants but apt to wear off in older specimens

The gills and stem are always pure white, and the toadstool is often very beautiful — the top may be white, drab, golden, red, or greenish, soft and sticky when moist. The most common variety has a yellow spot in the center shading to pure white.

These mushrooms contain a subtle alkaloid not detectable by smell or taste, yet surer than arsenic in its fatal consequences. No antidote to fungal poison had been discovered at the time of writing, though the hypodermic injection of atropia (from the nightshade family) was identified as a potential — if still experimental — antidotal measure.

## Historical Context

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]] (1828–1905), a Boston lawyer and amateur mycologist, devoted years of leisure to the study of edible and poisonous fungi. His observations on spawn and mycelium were drawn from careful fieldwork in the Boston area during the 1870s. His description of finding the white mycelial network of *Coprinus comatus* (the shaggy mane) running through soil being excavated opposite the Brunswick Hotel provides one of the clearest contemporary accounts of mycelial structure visible to the naked eye. Palmer's work bridges the gap between the purely taxonomic mycology of his era and the practical, ecological understanding of fungi that would develop in the 20th century.

## Spawn in the Wild vs. Cultivation

Wild spawn exists everywhere in natural soils — in pastures, forests, lawns, and along roadsides. Rich lawns and well-kept gardens produce the finest mushrooms because their soils contain abundant organic matter and maintain consistent moisture. The wild spawn network is vast and ancient; individual mycelial mats of some species are now known to extend for hundreds of acres and live for centuries, though this was not understood in Palmer's time.

Cultivated spawn, by contrast, is a concentrated form of mycelium propagated on a substrate (traditionally horse manure, later grain or sawdust). The transition from wild-collected spawn to commercially produced pure-culture spawn would not occur until the early 20th century with the work of American mycologists like Duggar and San Antonio. In Palmer's era, spawn was collected from old mushroom beds or purchased in blocks — often of dubious quality, since there was no way to verify that commercial spawn actually contained living fungal tissue.

## The Cryptogam Problem

The very name "cryptogam" (from Greek *kryptos*, hidden, and *gamos*, marriage) reflects how little was understood about fungal reproduction. The "secret marriages" of these organisms and the means of their propagation were well concealed from science. Palmer's observation that millions of spores deposited on a single plot produced no mushrooms highlights the gap between spore production and successful mycelial establishment — a gap that modern mycology has only partially closed. We now understand that spore germination requires specific conditions of moisture, temperature, nutrients, and often the presence of compatible mating types, but many species remain recalcitrant to laboratory cultivation.

## Modern Understanding

Today we know that mycelium is a network of hyphae — individual tubular cells that grow at their tips and branch repeatedly. The mycelial network transports water, nutrients, and chemical signals across considerable distances. Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, extending the plant's effective root system by orders of magnitude. Saprotrophic fungi decompose dead organic matter, recycling nutrients back into the soil. These ecological roles, only dimly perceived in Palmer's time, are now recognized as fundamental to ecosystem function.

## Key Takeaways

- Spawn (mycelium) is the true body of the fungus; the mushroom is merely its fruit
- Fungi return to the same locations year after year, following predictable seasonal patterns
- Overnight appearance is an illusion — mycelium grows for days below ground before fruiting
- Only *Agaricus arvensis*/*campestris* can be reliably cultivated; all other species resist artificial propagation
- Successful cultivation requires proper spawn, sectioned planting, and careful atmospheric management
- Spore-to-spawn reproduction remains one of the great unsolved mysteries of biology
- The Amanita family (volva + ring + warts) is responsible for virtually all fatal mushroom poisonings
- Wild mycelial networks are vast, persistent, and ecologically indispensable
- The term "cryptogam" reflects how little remains known about fungal reproduction even today
