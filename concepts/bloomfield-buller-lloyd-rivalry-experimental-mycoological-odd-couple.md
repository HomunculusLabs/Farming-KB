## A. H. R. Buller: The Experimentalist

Arthur Henry Reginald Buller arrived in Winnipeg, Manitoba, in 1904, stepping
from a westbound Canadian Pacific Railway train at the age of thirty. The newly
minted Ph.D. from the University of Leipzig wore a fashionably drooping
moustache that concealed his pouty lips. He had been an undergraduate in London,
obtained his doctorate under the botanist William Pfeffer — one of the
preeminent figures in plant physiology — and conducted research in Munich and
Naples before teaching at the University of Birmingham.

### Why Winnipeg?

Winnipeg was undergoing rapid growth, but the prospect of an academic position
in the only university in a windswept province nearly three times larger than
Britain was a strange choice for an ambitious young scientist. One advantage
was the autonomy offered by a young institution lacking the usual herd of
disaffected faculty veterans. In England, an inexperienced professor climbing
the ranks would encounter nothing but friction unless his passage was greased
with an Oxbridge degree or two.

### Teaching and Public Science

Buller was appointed the first professor of botany at the University of Manitoba
and proved to be a superb teacher. He treated every lecture as a word-perfect
performance, leavening botanical details with humor and genuine interest in his
audience. Students nicknamed him "Uncle Regie" and many adored him. On Saturday
mornings, he would walk to the farmers' market, stand on a wooden crate, and
lecture to crowds about cereal crops and the fungal diseases that afflicted
them. In England he would have been ignored. In Winnipeg he was a star.

### Spore Discharge Research

After breakfast at a downtown restaurant, Buller hopped a streetcar at 6:00 A.M.
to work in his laboratory for a couple of hours before classes. His first
Winnipeg experiments investigated spore discharge — a phenomenon described since
the sixteenth century but whose mechanism remained mysterious. This was virgin
scientific territory.

He estimated spore release rates by collecting deposits beneath mushroom caps
and visualized the invisible spore rain with strong light beams. He recognized
that spore behavior might offer a critical test of Stokes' law of fluid
dynamics. To substitute spores for water droplets, Buller measured spore size
and density, then designed an ingenious method for determining fall velocity.
He enclosed mushroom slices in glass-sided chambers, viewed falling spores
through a horizontally tilted microscope calibrated with silk threads, and
connected an electric tapper to an ink pen on a spinning recording drum to
measure velocity precisely.

### The Discovery of Buller's Drop

In 1910, Buller observed the formation of a fluid drop at the base of each
basidiospore seconds before discharge. Once he found that the drop traveled
with the spore, the Stokes' law discrepancy was resolved — fluid clinging to

full explanation for seventy years, but his hypothesis proved critical to its
ultimate solution.

His seven-volume *Researches on Fungi* (1909–1934) remains a landmark of
experimental biology. He lived in a two-room suite at the McLaren Hotel for
forty years, unencumbered by family, devoting himself entirely to fungal science
with very keen eyesight and painstaking microscopic patience.

## Curtis Gates Lloyd: The Self-Taught Taxonomist

Curtis Gates Lloyd was Buller's polar opposite in nearly every respect. A
pharmacist in Cincinnati, Ohio, he funded his mycological obsession through his
business and built one of the largest private fungal herbaria in the world. His
*Mycological Notes* (1898–1925) became one of the field's most important
informal publications, describing new species, correcting professional
taxonomists' errors, and airing controversial opinions with characteristic
bluntness.

Lloyd reserved particular scorn for professional mycologists who spent too much
time in laboratories and not enough in the field actually looking at fungi. His
notes are peppered with sardonic commentary on the profession. An old bachelor,
he was, by his own cheerful admission, never as happy as when "rigged out in
full dress and in the presence of the ladies." His lack of formal credentials and his preference for informal publication
meant that he never received the institutional recognition that Buller enjoyed,
though his taxonomic work laid the foundation for much of modern fungal
systematics.

## The Broader Tension

Their relationship exemplifies the enduring divide between experimentalists and
naturalists. Buller sought to understand *how* fungi operated — spore discharge
mechanisms, hyphal growth physics, genetic material distribution. Lloyd sought
to catalogue *what* fungi existed — classification, diversity, geographic
distribution. Both approaches proved essential to the advancement of mycology. Without the
taxonomic foundation laid by naturalists like Lloyd, experimentalists would lack
organized knowledge of fungal diversity needed to select appropriate study
organisms. Without mechanistic insights produced by experimentalists, taxonomists
would lack the understanding of process needed to interpret the patterns they
observe in nature. The odd couple, in their very different ways, each pushed
the science of mycology forward, and their combined legacy reminds us that
the deepest understanding comes from the interplay of observation and
experiment.
