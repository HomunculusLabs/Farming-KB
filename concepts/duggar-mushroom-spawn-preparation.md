## Mushroom Spawn Preparation Methods

Spores cannot be used directly in propagation. Spawn of commerce is the only "seed" material practically employed in growing these fungi. Pure cultures are effective, but too expensive for practical purposes. "Brick" spawn is commonly used in England and the United States, and very few growers find it advisable to make their own; while in France "flake" spawn has a monopoly of the market, and most of the large growers prepare, by means of a "starter" of "virgin" spawn, all of the material they use.

## Types of Spawn

### Brick Spawn (English Type)
The compact commercial brick spawn seems to exhibit a better keeping quality than any other product on the market. This spawn has also the advantage of being easily handled. In making the bricks, manure or compost is compressed into a compact form, which, when dry, ordinarily measures about 5.5 x 8.5 x 1.5 inches.

### Flake Spawn (French Type)
French flake spawn was never in very great demand, and time has convinced most growers that this type rapidly loses vitality under the conditions of importation. It was the traditional French method, relying on "virgin" spawn as a starter.

### Virgin Spawn
From the earliest records regarding mushroom culture, the grower depended upon a "spontaneous" or "native" occurrence of spawn in pastures, in compost piles, or elsewhere. This was known as "virgin" spawn, presumably from the accidental germination of spores. In England, "mill-track" virgin spawn was at one time much sought, this being originally the spawn found in the well-trodden soil and manure of the horse path or course of the animal-driven mill.

## The Chance Method

The maker of spawn whose source was the pasture proceeded by finding a [[falconer-growing-mushrooms-in-sheds]], obtained under aseptic conditions, may be made the starting point for pure cultures of spawn. A small piece of the inner tissue of a fresh mushroom will, when placed on any suitable sterile nutrient medium, promptly develop a mycelium, thus yielding a culture of the spawn. This method was originated by the writer while at the Bureau of Plant Industry, U.S. Department of Agriculture, in 1902.

### Making Pure Cultures

The process requires:
1. Clean test tubes or wide-mouthed bottles, plugged with cotton batting
2. Sterilization by dry heat at about 150 degrees C for nearly an hour, or by steam pressure (15 lbs for 15-30 minutes)
3. Partial filling (about two-thirds) with manure or half-decayed leaves
4. Removal of small pieces of internal tissue from young, vigorous mushrooms
5. Transfer to sterile tubes using sterilized scalpels
6. Growth at room temperature for several weeks until mycelium spreads throughout the medium

### Selection

The tissue culture method permits selection. One should procure from a bed of mushrooms in full bearing specimens which represent the most desirable qualities. Size, quality, and prolificness must all be considered, as well as other characteristics in any special selection. One may desire, for instance, to select from a variety which yields throughout a long period or one which is resistant to higher temperatures.

## Commercial Brick Making

### Materials
The compost used is customarily a mixture of stable-manure, cow-manure, and sometimes a small quantity of loam. At least two-thirds should be stable manure free of heavy straw. A considerable admixture of cow manure gives a more compact brick and encourages a mold-like rather than a thread-like texture of the mycelium.

### Methods
Hand moulds may be made of iron or a strong oak frame lined with steel plates. Two methods are employed:

1. The compost may be thoroughly wet or puddled, compressed into the mold, and dried on boards in racks.
2. The compost may be used in a moist but not puddled condition, compressed into the brick with some force using a mallet.

## Storage Conditions and Shelf Life

Proper storage conditions are critical for maintaining spawn viability. Duggar's observation that spawn deteriorates after six to eight months holds true for most commercial spawn today, though refrigerated storage can extend viable life to approximately one year. The key factors in spawn storage are temperature (cool but not freezing, ideally 35-40 degrees F), humidity (low to prevent premature growth), and protection from contaminants. Spawn stored at room temperature loses viability rapidly as mycelium continues to metabolize available nutrients, eventually exhausting its food supply and dying. Spawn that has dried out excessively will not revive, while spawn that has been exposed to moisture may develop bacterial or mold contamination.

## Spawn Testing and Quality Control

Before committing to large-scale cultivation, growers should test spawn viability and vigor. A simple test involves inoculating a small quantity of sterilized grain or agar medium with a sample of the spawn and observing the rate and pattern of mycelial growth. Vigorous spawn will colonize fresh substrate rapidly with thick, white, ropey mycelium. Weak spawn shows slow, thin, or patchy growth and may produce off-colors (yellow, pink, or green indicating bacterial or mold contamination). For commercial operations, periodic testing for contamination by plating dilutions on nutrient agar provides quantitative data on spawn purity. Duggar's advice to order spawn well in advance remains sound: it allows time for testing and avoids the risk of being forced to use whatever is available at planting time.
