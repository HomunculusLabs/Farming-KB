# Mycorrhizal Fungi Application and Food Web Integration

## Terminology

Understanding the correct terminology for [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] is important for clear communication:

- **Mycorrhizal fungus** — refers to the fungus itself, not in association with the plant
- **A [[mycorrhiza]]** — a single association between a root and a mycorrhizal fungus
- **Mycorrhizae** — plural of mycorrhiza, meaning all associations of roots and fungi; literally translates to "fungus-root"

## Application Considerations

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are applied in many different ways depending on the plant species, the growing system, and the requirements for getting spores (the typical application form) next to the root. The critical factor is **spore-to-root proximity** — if spores never make it close to the roots, colonization will never establish.

There are environments where applying spores to the soil surface works perfectly well, with rapid and high colonization. Conversely, there are soils where surface application would be futile because the spores would never reach root zones. The key principles for successful inoculation are:

1. **Get the spores next to the root** without harming the roots or killing the spores
2. **Provide fungal foods** near the root zone to support initial colonization and growth
3. **Consider soil conditions** — compaction, organic matter levels, and existing biology all affect spore viability and movement

## The Whole Food Web Concept

A complete [[soil-food-web]] is needed to prevent both easy-to-prevent and harder-to-prevent diseases. While bacteria can account for some preventative mechanisms, the rest of the food web is needed for comprehensive protection.

### The "Stall" Effect

When only a subset of organisms (e.g., just bacteria) is added back into degraded soil, initial improvement is typically observed. However, improvement eventually **stalls** because the full food web must be rebuilt to reach a state where pesticides and [[ingham-inorganic-fertilizers-soil-biology-damage]] are no longer needed. This means compost teas must deliver the whole food web, not just bacteria.

A bacterial-only tea is not necessarily "bad," but it may lack the organisms needed for comprehensive disease prevention. Where failures with compost tea have occurred, they consistently trace back to not getting the full set of organisms extracted and applied.

### Practical Disease Prevention Strategy

When targeting a specific disease, consider which organisms are needed for control:

- **Bacteria** — compete with certain bacterial pathogens
- **Fungi** — essential for preventing fungal diseases (mildews, blights, root rots)
- **Protozoa** — consume bacteria and release plant-available nutrients
- **Nematodes** — control fungal and bacterial populations, cycle nutrients

The organisms must be delivered to both leaf surfaces and soil, and they must become established to provide ongoing protection.

## Fungal Biomass Minimums

Based on observed efficacy of compost tea in preventing disease (primarily mildew and blight), a minimum of **2 micrograms of fungal biomass** per unit has been established as the threshold. Above this level, prevention consistently works; below it, results are unreliable.

### Is More Better?

Yes — higher fungal biomass correlates with more reliable disease suppression. Exceptionally high levels (up to 800 micrograms of fungal biomass have been documented) allow for reduced application rates while maintaining efficacy. For example, a tea with 800 micrograms of fungal biomass could be applied at 2 gallons per acre instead of the standard 5 gallons per acre while still delivering excellent results.

## Organism Dormancy

Putting organisms into a dormant state (for storage or transport) involves inducing them to produce dormant stages without killing them. This is typically achieved by manipulating osmotic factors or pH, but carries significant trade-offs:

- Approximately **50% of species diversity is lost** during the dormancy process
- Organisms that lack dormant stages, or that take longer to enter dormancy, are lost
- This loss must be considered when evaluating the viability of stored inoculants

## The Importance of Diversity

Diversity is critically important for soil and compost tea health. Most quality composts contain a huge diversity of organisms, so even a single compost source is far from a monoculture situation.

### Assessment Methods

[[soil-foodweb]] Inc. (SFI) testing examines the morphology of bacteria and fungi to assess whether the community is well-balanced or skewed. This morphological approach is more useful than plate counts (which capture less than 1% of soil organisms) but less precise than molecular methods.

### DNA-Based Assessment

DNA testing can assess the number of unique DNA strands present in a soil or compost sample. Considerations include:

- There is debate about how many base-pair differences constitute a different species — some plant pathogens differ by only a single base from non-pathogenic relatives
- Accounting for plasmid DNA and multiple genomic copies, good healthy soil and compost contain approximately **25,000 unique DNA strands per gram**
- The species composition of compost closely mirrors that of soil when the organic matter source has had environmental contact (leaves, tree debris, grass clippings)

### Compost Source Considerations

The source material for compost significantly affects the resulting microbiology:

- Composting grocery store vegetables grown indoors without environmental exposure produces a **bizarre set of organisms** — lacking the natural [[edaphic-factors-soil-microbial-community-structure]]
- Materials that had contact with soil (leaves, tree debris, grass clippings from unsprayed sources) produce compost with more natural, balanced microbiology
- **Clopyralid-treated materials must be avoided** — this herbicide persists through composting and is not known to be decomposed by any common compost organisms
- Desert environments may require inoculation, but most [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] have sufficient indigenous biology
- Urban/downtown areas may have depleted [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]]

## Glomalin

Glomalin is a category of sticky compounds produced by many soil organisms (not just mycorrhizal fungi) that bind soil particles together. Key points:

- **VAM (vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal) fungi** produce glomalin, but so do many bacteria and other soil organisms
- The term "glomalin" serves as a general name for various compounds that aggregate soil particles
- Research by R.C. Foster in Australia ("The Ultrastructure of the Rhizosphere") documented these gluing compounds before the term "glomalin" was coined
- Bacteria also produce glues to attach themselves to soil particles and surfaces
- The full story of glomalin — its chemical structure, pH characteristics, and how bacterial and fungal versions differ — remains an active area of research

The recognition of these sticky biological materials is significant because they are fundamental to soil aggregation, water retention, and overall soil structure.
