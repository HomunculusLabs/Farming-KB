# Seed Storage for Maximum Viability (Solomon)

From [[solomon-gardening-when-it-counts-index]] by Steve Solomon.



## The Science of Seed Longevity

Seed storage life is governed by two interacting factors: temperature and moisture content. The rules are:

- For every 10 degrees F increase in temperature combined with a 1 percent increase in seed moisture content, storage life is **cut in half**
- For every 10 degrees F decrease in temperature combined with a 1 percent decrease in seed moisture content, storage life **doubles**

Standard conditions are assumed to be 70 degrees F (21 degrees C) with enough humidity that seed stabilizes at 13% moisture by weight. This is typical of temperate-climate indoor storage.

Examples of the multiplier effect:
- 60 degrees F and 12% moisture: seed lasts **twice** as long as standard conditions
- 80 degrees F and 14% moisture: seed lasts **half** as long
- Seeds in a cardboard box in a steamy greenhouse: may not last a year
- Seeds on a closet shelf in a dry, cool bedroom: probably last longer than standard
- 50 degrees F and 50% relative humidity (drying seed to ~10% moisture): seed lasts **four times** as long as standard



## What Shortens Seed Life

- High temperature (most common problem)
- High humidity / high seed moisture content
- Fluctuating temperature and moisture conditions
- Seed that was not fully mature when harvested
- Seed that was rained on during field drying
- Seed grown on infertile soil (less food reserve in embryo)



## Professional Seed Storage

When Solomon ran Territorial Seed Company, he built a climate-controlled storeroom holding seed at 50 degrees F (10 degrees C) with 50% relative humidity. At this RH, seeds dried to about 10% moisture, lasting four times longer than standard conditions. Johnny's Selected Seeds and Park Seed Company do this as well.



## DIY Seed Storage at Home

### Materials needed
1. Silica gel crystals (available at hobby/craft stores, ~$4 per kilogram). The crystals are blue when dry and change to light blue, then pink as they absorb moisture. Silica gel can be reused many times as long as it is not heated above 250 degrees F (120 degrees C).
2. Airtight containers -- one-gallon (four-liter) plastic storage boxes that seal nearly airtight
3. A cool storage location

### Setup instructions
1. **Recondition silica gel**: Spread crystals in a baking pan and bake at 230 degrees F for a few minutes until they turn blue again. Let cool.
2. Place reconditioned crystals into a cloth sachet or small open-topped container inside the storage box. Use about one cup (250 ml) of silica gel per half-gallon of seed storage volume.
3. Put seed packets in the container. Monitor the gel color -- if it turns pink, take it out, recondition, and return it.
4. Store the sealed container in the **refrigerator** (best option), root cellar (good option), or the coolest stable place available. The refrigerator addresses the cool-and-stable part of the equation.

### Solomon's practice
He keeps his large plastic tub in the refrigerator, claiming half a shelf. He notes this took negotiation with his partner. A root cellar is the next best option. Least ideal: an unheated room in winter and the coolest place available in summer (such as under the house).



## Understanding Purchased Seed's History

There is always more uncertainty about the storage life of seeds you purchase than those you grow yourself. Consider:
- How many years has the seed sat on a warehouse shelf?
- Under what storage conditions was it held?
- How good was the seed from the start?
- Did the weather cooperate the year it was grown? Seed may have failed to fully mature, or may have been rained on during drying
- Was the seed production field adequately fertile?

When you grow your own seed, you know a lot more about its history.
