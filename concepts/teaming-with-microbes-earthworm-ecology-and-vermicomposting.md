Earthworms are among the most recognizable and important animals in the
[[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]]. Their activities --
shredding organic matter, aerating soil, creating burrows, and producing
nutrient-rich castings -- make them indispensable to healthy garden
soils. Charles Darwin devoted an entire book to earthworms (The Formation
of Vegetable Mould Through the Action of Worms), arguing that every
particle of soil has passed through a worm at least once.

## Species and Classification

Earthworms are segmented worms (oligochaetes) with approximately 7,000
species worldwide. The most common genera in garden soils are
Aporrectodea, Eisenia, and Lumbricus. Key species include:

- **Night crawler** (Lumbricus terrestris): Dominant in gardens across
  North America; a European introduction that arrived with settlers
- **Red wiggler** (Eisenia fetida): The standard composting worm,
  favored for vermicomposting bins
- **Pot worm** (Enchytraeus doerjesi): Much smaller (millimeters to
  centimeters), inhabits acid forest soils where earthworms cannot
  survive; replaces earthworms in forest ecosystems

Most North American garden earthworms are actually European introductions,
arriving in ship ballast, potted plants, and nursery stock. They spread
across the continent in soils carrying fruit trees and other nursery
material. The only region where European worms have not thrived is the
warm desert Southwest.

## Population and Biomass

An acre of good garden soil contains 2 to 3 million earthworms,
equivalent to 10 to 50 per square foot. This population can move an
astonishing 18 tons of soil per year. By comparison, an acre of forest
soil contains only about 50,000 earthworms -- earthworms play a far
larger role in garden soil food webs than in forest systems.

Worms are hermaphroditic (carrying both sets of sexual organs) but
require a mate to reproduce. Each cocoon contains 15 or more baby
worms, which mature in three to four months. Some species live up to
15 years, breeding continuously throughout their lives.

## Feeding Biology

Despite their reputation as decomposers, earthworms lack the enzymes to
digest organic matter directly. They rely on bacteria in their intestines
to do the actual digestion. The worm's feeding process works as follows:

1. The prostomium (fleshy lip) and pharynx grab food and pull it inside
2. Strong muscles begin breaking food into smaller particles
3. Saliva moistens the material
4. Food passes to the crop (storage compartment)
5. The gizzard -- an extremely strong muscle partially filled with sand
   and small rocks -- grinds the food (serving as the worm's "teeth")
6. Ground food is mixed with liquid calcium carbonate before entering
   the intestine
7. Intestinal bacteria digest the finely ground organic matter
8. Nutrients from bacterial digestion are absorbed into the worm's
   bloodstream

Worms feed primarily on bacteria, which explains why soils with large
worm populations are usually bacterially dominated. They also consume
fungi, nematodes, protozoa, and the organic matter in which these
microorganisms live.

## Vermicastings: Nature's Fertilizer

Vermicastings (worm excrement) are a remarkable soil amendment:

- **50% higher in organic matter** than surrounding soil
- **7x richer in phosphate**
- **10x more available potash**
- **5x more nitrogen**
- **3x more usable magnesium**
- **1.5x higher in calcium** (from calcium carbonate added during
  digestion)

The worm's intestinal bacteria unlock chemical bonds that otherwise
tie up nutrients, making them plant-available. The increased organic
matter raises the soil's [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure]]
(cation exchange capacity), providing more charged surfaces for
nutrients to attach to.

Worms can deposit 10 to 15 tons of castings per acre annually on the
soil surface.

## Shredding and Decomposition

Earthworms are classified as shredders. They break down leaf litter,
greatly accelerating decomposition. By opening up leaves and organic
matter, they give bacteria and fungi better access to [[fungal-cellulose-decomposition-fungal-enzymes]] and [[fungal-decomposition-ecology]].
Without worm shredding, leaves require one to two years to decay;
with worms, decomposition takes only three months.

Worms both directly shred material and indirectly stimulate decomposition
by:
- Creating more surface area for microbial attack
- Mixing microbes into their fecal pellets (creating protected enclaves)
- Competing with fungi and bacteria for nutrients, stimulating microbial
  activity

## Burrowing and Soil Structure

[[biodiversity-of-fungi-biomass-carbon-soil-structure]] depends on the physical soil
improvements that earthworm burrows provide. Different worm species create
different burrow types:

- **Vertical burrowers**: Move up and down, sometimes reaching 12 feet
  deep; pull surface litter into burrows for later decomposition;
  deposit deeper soil on the surface
- **Horizontal burrowers**: Stay within the top 6 inches of soil but
  redistribute organic matter several feet laterally

Burrows provide critical soil functions:
- Water drainage pathways
- Air passage channels
- Root penetration pathways (roots grow into abandoned burrows,
  accessing deeper soil layers)
- Microbial transport (worms carry microorganisms on their bodies and
  in the litter they pull underground)

Worms can move rocks six times their body weight. Their burrowing
increases both soil porosity and water-holding capacity.

## Threats from Gardening Practices

Earthworms are remarkably vulnerable to common gardening practices:

- **Rototilling**: Destroys burrows and cuts worms into pieces that do
  not regenerate
- **Chemical fertilizers**: Salts irritate worms and drive them out of
  garden soils
- **Synthetic pesticides**: Kill worms directly and destroy their food
  sources

A noticeable worm population is a clear sign of a healthy [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]]. It indicates that organic matter, bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes -- all necessary to support worms -- are
present and functioning.
