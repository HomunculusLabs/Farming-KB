## Cross-Cultural Examples

The pattern McKenna identified is not abstract — it appears with remarkable specificity across distinct cultural traditions:

**Siberia:** Among the Evenki and other Tungusic peoples, the classic shamanic calling involves *shamanic illness* (*saman bōki*), a severe psychosomatic crisis that can last years. The afflicted person experiences seizures, fainting, delirium, and ecstatic states. The community recognizes these as signs that the spirits are "choosing" the person. If no elder shaman intervenes to guide the crisis, the person may die. The initiate is torn apart by spirits in vision — bones removed, organs replaced with crystal or iron — and reassembled as a new being. This dismemberment vision, documented extensively by Mircea Eliade, is the archetype of shamanic ordeal: death and reconstruction.

**Amazonia:** Among the Shipibo-Conibo of Peru, the shamanic calling often begins with a severe illness — fever, hallucinations, inability to eat — during which the plant spirits (*onán*) appear and offer their knowledge. The ill person is taken to a master shaman, who diagnoses the cause not as disease but as the plants calling. Ayahuasca ceremonies then become the primary training ground, with the initiate drinking repeatedly over months or years, each session deepening their relationship with specific plant spirits and expanding their healing repertoire. The illness is not cured in the Western sense — it is *completed* through apprenticeship.

**Native American (Plains):** Among the Lakota and related peoples, the calling may arrive through the vision quest (*hanbleceya*), a period of solitary fasting and exposure undertaken at a sacred site. But it can also arrive uninvited — through near-death on the battlefield, through the involuntary reception of a powerful dream, or through what looks to outsiders like a psychotic break. The person who receives a calling without seeking it is considered especially powerful, having been chosen rather than choosing. The vision's content — animal spirits, thunder beings, ancestral figures — provides both the mandate and the curriculum for their practice.

**Southern Africa:** Among the !Kung San, the healing trance dance (*n/um kxao*) serves as both calling and training. Individuals who enter deep trance report sensations of heat rising through their bodies, a feeling they describe as *!kia* — a potentially dangerous activation of spiritual energy that can cause illness or death if not properly managed. Those who survive and learn to work with *n/um* become healers. The trance state itself is the ordeal, repeated night after night until the healer can enter and exit at will.

## The Ordeal as Curriculum

The ordeal is not merely a selection mechanism — it is the first lesson. What the initiate learns during their crisis becomes the foundation of everything that follows. The near-death experience teaches the geography of the other world. The seizure teaches the feeling of leaving the body. The psychotic episode teaches the contents of the unstructured mind. The lightning strike teaches the overwhelming power of transpersonal forces. Each ordeal deposits specific knowledge into the initiate's being — knowledge that cannot be transmitted verbally and cannot be acquired by any other means. The shamanic training that follows does not replace this experiential knowledge but gives it language, technique, and ethical direction. The elder shaman's role is not to explain the experience but to recognize it, name it, and teach the initiate how to return to it intentionally.

## Gender and Shamanic Calling

Shamanic callings are not limited by gender, though gender dynamics vary significantly across cultures. In Siberia, female shamans (*udaghan*) were historically common and often considered more powerful than their male counterparts — their capacity for trance and boundary dissolution was linked to the transformative power of childbirth and menstruation, themselves experiences of ego-dissolution and liminal passage. In Amazonian traditions, both male and female shamans are common, with the Shipibo and Asháninka recognizing women as particularly gifted plant shamans. In some Native American traditions, Two-Spirit individuals hold unique shamanic roles, their gender liminality paralleling the liminal nature of the shamanic calling itself — existing between categories is itself a form of boundary dissolution.

## Implications for Modern Understanding

McKenna's analysis raises questions that modern psychology has barely begun to address. If the same phenomena — seizures, psychotic episodes, near-death experiences, spiritual emergencies — can produce either breakdown or breakthrough depending on the cultural container, then the framework of interpretation is not neutral. It actively shapes the outcome. The growing field of spiritual emergency, pioneered by Stanislav Grof and Christina Grof, represents one attempt to develop a clinical framework that can distinguish between pathological crises and transformative ones. [[sacred-mushroom-use-mexico-teonanacatl-indigenous-traditions]] have had this capacity for millennia — not through clinical sophistication but through the simple recognition that some experiences, however terrifying, are not diseases but callings.
