# Bioaccumulation and Biosorption of Heavy Metals by Fungi

Heavy metal contamination of soil and water from industrial activities
including mining, smelting, electroplating, and chemical manufacturing
represents a persistent environmental challenge. Unlike organic
pollutants, metals cannot be degraded and must be removed, transformed,
or immobilized. Fungi offer cost-effective and efficient mechanisms for
metal removal through biosorption (metabolism-independent binding to
cell surfaces) and bioaccumulation (active intracellular uptake requiring
metabolic energy).

## Mechanisms of Metal Uptake

### Biosorption (Passive Mechanisms)

Biosorption occurs independently of cellular metabolism and involves
binding of metal ions to functional groups on the fungal cell wall:

- **Ion exchange**: Metal cations displace lighter cations (Ca2+, Mg2+,
  K+, Na+) bound to cell wall functional groups. This is the dominant
  mechanism for most fungal biosorption systems.
- **Complexation**: Metal ions form coordination complexes with nitrogen,
  oxygen, and sulfur donors on cell wall polymers.
- **Electrostatic attraction**: Negatively charged cell wall groups
  (carboxylate, phosphate) attract cationic metal species.
- **Precipitation**: Metal ions may precipitate as hydroxides, carbonates,
  or sulfides on the cell surface or in the extracellular matrix.

### Bioaccumulation (Active Mechanisms)

Bioaccumulation is a metabolically dependent process involving:
- **Active transport**: Membrane-bound transport proteins actively import
  metal ions into the cytoplasm, often as a detoxification or nutritional
  acquisition mechanism.
- **Intracellular sequestration**: Metals are bound by intracellular
  ligands including metallothioneins, phytochelatins, glutathione, and
  organic acids, preventing toxic interactions with cellular components.
- **Vacuolar compartmentalization**: Sequestered metal complexes are
  transported into vacuoles for storage and isolation.
- **Efflux pumping**: Some fungi actively pump excess metals out of
  cells using P-type ATPases and other transporter systems.

## Cell Wall Composition and Metal Binding

The fungal cell wall provides the primary biosorption surface. Key
structural components and their metal-binding groups include:

- **Chitin**: Linear polymer of N-acetylglucosamine with amino and
  hydroxyl groups that coordinate metal ions.
- **Glucans**: Beta-glucans (beta-1,3 and beta-1,6 linkages) provide
  hydroxyl groups for metal complexation.
- **Chitosan**: Deacetylated chitin, present in some fungal cell walls
  (especially Mucorales), provides abundant free amino groups with high
  affinity for metal cations.
- **Proteins and glycoproteins**: Cell wall-associated proteins contribute
  carboxyl, amino, sulfhydryl, and imidazole groups.
- **Melanin**: Pigmented polymers in some fungal cell walls (e.g.,
  [[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]]) provide carboxyl, phenolic hydroxyl, and
  amine groups with high metal-binding capacity.

## Live vs Dead Biomass

### Live Biomass
- Advantages: Bioaccumulation adds to biosorption capacity; self-
  regenerating; can adapt to metal stress through inducible mechanisms.
- Disadvantages: Requires nutrients and controlled conditions; sensitive
  to toxicity at high metal concentrations; limited storage life;
  operational complexity in continuous systems.

### Dead Biomass
- Advantages: No nutrient requirements; not affected by metal toxicity;
  can be stored for extended periods; easier handling and disposal;
  can be regenerated by desorption; higher predictability of
  performance.
- Disadvantages: Limited to biosorption (no bioaccumulation); may
  require pretreatment (washing, drying, grinding); potential for
  cell lysis releasing intracellular contents.

Dead or non-viable fungal biomass is often preferred for industrial
applications due to operational simplicity and cost-effectiveness.
Common biomass preparations include heat-killed, acid-treated, and
lyophilized preparations.

## Biosorption Isotherm Models

Equilibrium biosorption data are analyzed using isotherm models to
characterize the metal uptake capacity of fungal biomass:

- **Langmuir isotherm**: Assumes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous
  surface with finite identical sites. Provides maximum adsorption
  capacity (qmax) and affinity constant (b). Fungal biosorption
  commonly fits the Langmuir model, with qmax values for heavy metals
  typically ranging from 10 to 200 mg/g dry biomass.

- **Freundlich isotherm**: Empirical model for heterogeneous surface
  adsorption with multilayer coverage. The Freundlich constant (Kf)
  and intensity parameter (n) indicate adsorption capacity and
  favorability.

- **Temkin isotherm**: Considers adsorbate-adsorbent interactions and
  assumes heat of adsorption decreases linearly with coverage.

- **Dubinin-Radushkevich (D-R) isotherm**: Distinguishes between
  physical and chemical adsorption based on the mean free energy of
  adsorption.

## Fungal Species for Metal Biosorption

Effective biosorbent fungi include:
- [[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]]: Widely studied; high biosorption capacity for
  Pb2+, Cd2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Ni2+, and Cr6+.
- [[rhizopus-arrhizus]] and rhizopus oryzae: Mucorales species with
  chitosan-rich cell walls, excellent for Pb2+ and Cd2+ removal.
- [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]]: Brewer's yeast biomass, available as
  industrial waste, effective for multiple metal ions.
- [[penicillium-chrysogenum]]: Pharmaceutical industry waste biomass
  with high metal-binding capacity.
- [[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]]: White-rot fungus combining metal biosorption
  with organic pollutant degradation capability.

## Factors Affecting Biosorption

- **pH**: Strongly influences metal speciation and cell wall charge;
  optimal pH typically 4.0-6.0 for cationic metals.
- **Biomass concentration**: Higher biomass increases total metal
  removal but decreases per-unit uptake (q).
- **Initial metal concentration**: Higher concentrations drive greater
  uptake until saturation.
- **Temperature**: Moderate effects; most biosorption is exothermic.
- **Contact time**: Equilibrium typically reached within 30-120
  minutes for most systems.
