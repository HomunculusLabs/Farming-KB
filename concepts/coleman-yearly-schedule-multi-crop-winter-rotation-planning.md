# Coleman Yearly Schedule and Multi-Crop Winter Rotation Planning

## Overview

Eliot Coleman's year-round production system at Four Season Farm represents one of the most thoroughly documented models for four-season [[jadam-vegetable-farming-techniques]]. The yearly schedule is built around the principle that winter production is not an extension of summer but an entirely distinct season with its own crops, timing logic, and management demands. The schedule revolves around three annual phases: the summer production season, the fall harvest and transition period, and the deep winter rest period. (see [[coleman-cool-house-vs-cold-house-economics-winter-production]]).

## The Annual Cycle

Coleman divides the year into distinct operational phases:

### Phase 1: Summer Production (June–October)
During summer months, the mobile greenhouses house heat-loving crops [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]]—tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, cucumbers, melons, and sweet potatoes. Simultaneously, outdoor fields produce artichokes, beans, cabbage, cauliflower, celery, peas, summer squash, and many of the same greens that will later fill the winter houses.

While the summer crops [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] occupy the greenhouses, the winter-harvest crops are sown outdoors in the fields over which the greenhouses will eventually move. This overlap is essential to the [[coleman-mobile-greenhouse-system]]: it means that when the summer crop season ends in mid to late October, mature or near-mature winter crops are already waiting in the field to be covered.

### Phase 2: Fall Transition and Harvest Peak (October 1–December 21)
This is the most [[mollison-designers-market-garden-intensive-production-design]] and sales period of the year. Coleman maximizes greenhouse production during these months for several reasons:
- **High demand**: Pre-holiday market demand for fresh, local produce is at its peak. (see [[coleman-greenhouse-summer-crops]]).
- **Good growth conditions**: Day length, while declining, is still sufficient for active plant growth. Temperatures are moderate. (see [[coleman-double-cover-microclimate-protection]]).
- **Economic focus**: November and December sales generate the highest revenue relative to production costs, as no heating is required and growth rates are still useful.

During this phase, the mobile greenhouses are moved from summer crop locations to cover the outdoor-sown winter crops. The first six weeks after the move are the most productive of the winter season.

### Phase 3: Deep Winter Rest (Late December–Late January)
Coleman schedules a four-to-five-week vacation for the crew during the period when plant growth, customer interest, weekly income, and energy levels are all at their lowest ebb. This is the period around the winter solstice when day length is at its minimum.

During the vacation, only [[coleman-overwintered-spinach-cold-house-production]] and leeks remain available for harvest, and these can be managed by a single person. This deliberate rest period prevents burnout and acknowledges the biological reality that plant growth is minimal during this window.

### Phase 4: Late Winter and Spring Reawakening (February–May)
As day length increases and temperatures moderate, overwintered crops resume active growth and new sowings begin producing. Spring crops from both cold and cool houses include arugula, carrots, baby beets, baby new potatoes, broccoli, fennel, kale, lettuce, turnips, and overwintered onions. By May 31, the winter harvest season concludes and the cycle begins again.

## The Mobile Greenhouse Rotation

The mobile greenhouse is central to Coleman's rotation planning. The system works as follows:

**Year 1, Site A:**
1. Winter: Greenhouse covers winter crops (spinach, carrots, Asian greens) sown outdoors.
2. Summer: After winter harvest, greenhouse moves to Site B. Site A is uncovered and planted to summer field crops or cover crops.

**Year 1, Site B:**
1. Summer: Greenhouse covers heat-loving crops (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers).
2. Fall: Greenhouse moves to Site A to cover newly sown winter crops. Site B receives winter crops or cover crops.

**Year 2:**
The pattern reverses—each site alternates between covered (greenhouse) and uncovered (field) years. This means every growing bed spends one year out of every two exposed to the cleansing effects of sun, rain, wind, and snow.

This alternation provides several benefits:
- **Soil restoration**: Exposure to natural weathering breaks down disease inoculum and excess salts.
- **Pest disruption**: Moving the greenhouse removes the protected environment that [[mushroom-pests-and-diseases]] may have established.
- **Nutrient balancing**: Field crops and cover crops in the uncovered year contribute to soil fertility in ways that greenhouse cropping alone cannot.

## Cold House Rotation Examples

Coleman provides specific rotation sequences used at Four Season Farm:

**Rotation A: Carrot–Potato–Melon–Spinach**
1. Winter: Carrot crop harvested through November–December.
2. March 15: First baby new potatoes planted.
3. Post-harvest (mid-June): Charantais melons transplanted.
4. Post-melon (September): Fall spinach sown.
5. Late November: Greenhouse moved to cover later spinach planting for spring.

**Rotation B: Stir-Fry Greens–Lettuce–Tomato–Red Clover**
1. Winter: Asian stir-fry greens (tatsoi, mizuna, pak choi) and early spring lettuce.
2. May 10: Six-week-old tomato plants set out, protected with inner layer initially.
3. September 1: Red clover green manure undersown in standing tomatoes.
4. Late October: Tomato vines unclipped, greenhouse moved to winter crop site.

## Cool House Rotation

The cool house (non-mobile, with minimal supplementary heat) follows a different pattern because it remains in place year-round. Coleman maintains high productivity in this fixed structure through meticulous attention to:

- **Soil preparation**: Deep incorporation of quality compost before each crop.
- **Crop rotation**: Alternating Compositae (lettuce family) with Brassicaceae (cabbage family) crops, plus alternating with warm-weather crops (cucumbers, peppers, eggplants) in summer.
- **Irrigation management**: Careful watering to prevent salt accumulation and maintain optimal soil moisture.
- **Compost quality**: Only the highest-quality compost is used in the permanent house to avoid introducing pathogens or weed seeds.
