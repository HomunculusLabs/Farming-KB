#### Saprotrophic Succession on Wood
The traditional hypothesis for saprotrophic fungal succession (Frankland, 1998) holds that each successive colonizer utilizes substrates that preceding species either could not use or could only partially degrade. However, stable isotope evidence suggests that at least some late colonizers may be specialized in using the mycelium of earlier colonizers as a nutrient source (Holmer et al., 1997). This mycophagous strategy could be distinguished using d15N and d13C analysis, since fungal mycelium and woody substrates differ significantly in their isotope signatures.

Many wood-boring beetles inoculate wood with spores of wood-decay fungi during egg laying, creating insect-fungal mutualisms. The beetles may benefit by feeding on more palatable decayed tissue and/or by consuming fungal mycelium directly. Stable isotope analysis of d15N and d13C in larvae, wood, and mycelium could help determine the relative importance of each food source.

#### Ectomycorrhizal Succession
Primary succession of ectomycorrhizal fungi has been documented on volcanic substrates at Mount Fuji (Nara et al., 2003). As vegetation establishes on barren volcanic deposits, ECM fungal communities undergo underground succession, with early colonizers facilitating conditions for later species. This underground succession parallels above-ground plant succession and is critical for ecosystem development on new substrates.

### Spatial Heterogeneity
Fungal communities in granite show distinct phylogenetic structure. Principal components analysis of community fingerprint data reveals that environmental variables (pH, moisture, organic matter) explain significant variation in community composition across microhabitats.

## Competition and Interaction

### Mycelial Combat
When fungal mycelia encounter each other, outcomes include:
- **Deadlock**: Neither mycelium advances; maintained confrontation
- **Replacement**: One species overgrows and replaces the other
- **Partial replacement**: Mutual penetration with territory exchange

These interactions determine community composition and resource partitioning.

### Mycorrhizal-Saprotrophic Antagonism
The interactions between mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi profoundly shape decomposition processes:
- **Gadgil effect**: ECM fungi suppress decomposition by outcompeting saprotrophs for nitrogen, increasing C:N ratios of remaining residues. This antagonism is driven by the much greater below-ground C allocation of ECM host trees compared to AM plants.
- **Nutrient transfer at mycelial interfaces**: When ECM and saprotrophic mycelia meet in soil, significant nutrient transfers can occur (Lindahl et al., 1999), further slowing organic matter decomposition.
- **ERM antagonism toward EM**: Ericoid mycorrhizal (ERM) fungi, combined with the allelopathic and nutrient-poor litter of their host plants, may directly suppress EM fungi, contributing to the exclusion of EM trees from boreal regions (Mallik, 2003).
- **AM-saprotroph relations**: Arbuscular mycorrhizal plants produce low-recalcitrance litter with higher N content that decomposes quickly, so there is little evidence of strong antagonism between AM fungi and saprotrophs.

### Mycoheterotrophy and Carbon Flow
Over 400 achlorophyllous plant species (mycoheterotrophs) depend exclusively on fungi for carbon, exploiting mycorrhizal networks of adjacent autotrophic plants. About 10% of all plant species depend on exploitation of fungal C at some stage. Most fully mycoheterotrophic orchids associate with EM fungi co-infecting roots of nearby green plants, creating tripartite carbon pathways. Pulse-labelling studies and microcosm experiments have confirmed carbon transport via co-linking mycorrhizal mycelium (McKendrick et al., 2000; Bidartondo et al., 2003). Mycoheterotrophs exploit a wide phylogenetic breadth of mycorrhizal fungi, from Glomeromycota to Basidiomycota and Ascomycota. This parasitic carbon flow has important implications for plant community composition and ecosystem functioning.

## Multilocus Typing of Communities
Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and multilocus microsatellite typing (MLMT) provide tools for characterizing population structure within fungal communities. The eBURST algorithm identifies clonal complexes and predicted founders, illuminating short-term evolutionary history. These methods reveal geographic structuring in many fungal species and can identify sources of infection in epidemiological studies.
