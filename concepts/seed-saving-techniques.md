## Seed Saving Techniques

Seed saving is the practice of harvesting, processing, and storing seeds from your garden for future planting. It is a foundational skill for self-sufficiency, genetic preservation, and the development of locally adapted crop varieties. Open-pollinated and heirloom varieties produce true-to-type seeds, while hybrids do not reliably do so.

## Why Save Seeds

- **Self-sufficiency**: Eliminate dependence on seed companies and supply chains.
- **Cost savings**: Seeds are free once you have the parent plants.
- **Local adaptation**: Over generations, saved seeds adapt to your specific climate, soil, and growing conditions, becoming more resilient.
- **Genetic preservation**: Maintain heirloom and heritage varieties that may be lost commercially.
- **Community sharing**: Seeds can be shared, traded, and distributed through seed libraries and exchanges.
- [[homesteading]] depends on seed sovereignty — the ability to grow your own food from saved seed year after year.

## Open-Pollinated vs Hybrid

- **Open-pollinated (OP)**: Varieties that breed true from seed. When you save seed from an OP variety and replant it, the offspring will closely resemble the parent. All heirloom varieties are open-pollinated.
- **Hybrid (F1)**: First-generation crosses of two distinct parent lines. Hybrids are bred for specific traits (vigor, disease resistance, uniformity) but do not breed true — their offspring will segregate into a range of unpredictable types.
- **Rule**: Only save seeds from open-pollinated or heirloom varieties unless you are actively breeding.

## Isolation Distances

To prevent cross-pollination and maintain varietal purity, different varieties of the same species must be isolated from each other. The required distance depends on the plant's pollination method:

| Crop Type | Examples | Isolation Distance | Notes |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|-------|
| Self-pollinating | Tomatoes, peppers, beans, peas, lettuce | 10-20 feet | Low risk of crossing; bagging flowers provides near-total isolation |
| Insect-pollinated | Squash, cucumbers, melons, onions, carrots | 1/4 - 1/2 mile | Bees travel far; hand pollination with bagging is the home-scale solution |
| Wind-pollinated | Corn, spinach, beets, chard | 1/2 - 1+ mile | Very difficult to isolate on small properties; hand pollinate corn ears and tassels |

### Home-Scale Isolation Techniques

When proper isolation distances aren't possible:

- **Bagging**: Cover individual flowers or flower clusters with breathable bags (organza, spun polyester) before they open. For insect-pollinated crops, hand-pollinate with a small brush, then re-bag.
- **Staggered planting**: Grow only one variety of a cross-pollinating species per season.
- **Caging**: Build fine-mesh screen cages around entire plants and introduce pollinators manually.
- **Time isolation**: Plant varieties that flower at different times so they can't cross.

## Wet Processing (Fleshy Fruits)

For seeds embedded in fleshy fruits: tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, squash, melons, eggplant, berries.

### Fermentation Method (Tomatoes, Cucumbers)

Fermentation removes the gelatinous coating around seeds that inhibits germination and can harbor disease.

1. **Extract**: Scoop seeds and surrounding gel into a glass jar. Add a small amount of water.
2. **Ferment**: Leave the jar at room temperature (not in direct sun) for 2-4 days. A white mold layer will form on top. This fermentation breaks down the germination-inhibiting gel.
3. **Rinse**: Add water, stir, and pour off the floating debris and mold. Viable seeds sink to the bottom. Repeat rinsing 3-4 times until water runs clear.
4. **Dry**: Spread clean seeds on a non-stick surface (glass plate, wax paper, coffee filter). Label immediately. Dry in a warm, well-ventilated area out of direct sunlight for 1-2 weeks.

### Simple Rinse Method (Peppers, Squash, Melons)

For seeds that don't need fermentation:

1. Remove seeds from the fruit.
2. Rinse under running water, rubbing to separate seeds from pulp.
3. Spread on a screen or paper towel to dry for 1-2 weeks.
4. For squash and melons: some growers skip rinsing and simply spread the wet seed mass to dry, then separate seeds from dried pulp.

## Dry Processing (Dry Seed Heads)

For seeds produced in dry pods or heads: beans, peas, lettuce, radish, carrot, onion, herbs, grains.

### Method

1. **Allow to mature**: Leave seed heads/pods on the plant until fully dry and brown. Seeds should rattle in pods.
2. **Harvest**: Cut entire seed heads or pull up whole plants. Hang upside down in a dry, well-ventilated area if not fully dry.
3. **Thresh**: Break seed heads open to release seeds. Methods:
   - **Hand threshing**: Rub seed heads between your hands or stomp on them in a bucket.
   - **Bag threshing**: Put seed heads in a pillowcase or burlap bag and beat gently with a stick.
   - **Screen winnowing**: Rub through a screen to separate seeds from chaff.
4. **Winnow**: Pour seeds from one container to another in front of a fan or in a breeze. The lighter chaff blows away; heavier seeds fall into the container. Repeat until clean.
5. **Dry**: Spread seeds in a thin layer for an additional few days to ensure they are completely dry before storage.

## Viability Testing

Before relying on saved seeds for your main planting, test germination rates:

1. Place 10 seeds on a damp paper towel.
2. Fold and place in a sealed plastic bag.
3. Keep at room temperature (or appropriate germination temperature for the species).
4. Check after 7-14 days and count sprouted seeds.
5. Germination rate = (sprouted / total) × 100%.

**Interpretation**:
- 90%+: Excellent — plant normally
- 70-89%: Good — plant slightly thickly
- 50-69%: Adequate — plant 1.5x normal rate
- Below 50%: Poor — use more seed or purchase fresh

## Seed Storage

Proper storage maximizes seed longevity:

- **Dry**: Seeds must be thoroughly dry before storage. Aim for 5-8% moisture content. A simple test: hit seeds with a hammer — if they shatter, they're dry enough. If they mash, they need more drying.
- **Cool**: Store at 35-50°F (refrigerator is ideal). Freezing extends viability further but seeds must be completely dry to avoid ice crystal damage.
- **Dark**: Light accelerates degradation. Use opaque containers.
- **Airtight**: Use glass jars with tight lids. Include a desiccant packet (silica gel) in humid climates.
- **Labeled**: Always label with variety, year, and any relevant notes.

**Expected seed viability** (under proper storage conditions):

| Species | Viability (years) |
|---------|------------------|
| Onion, parsnip | 1-2 |
| Corn, pepper | 2-4 |
| Bean, pea | 3-5 |
| Tomato, cucumber | 4-6 |
| Squash, melon | 4-6 |
| Lettuce | 3-5 |

## Heirloom and Heritage Varieties

Heirloom varieties are open-pollinated cultivars that have been passed down through generations — typically at least 50 years old. They offer:

- Unique flavors, colors, and textures not found in commercial hybrids
- Genetic diversity important for food security
- Adaptability when grown and seed-saved in the same location over multiple seasons
- Cultural and historical significance

Saving seeds from heirloom varieties preserves this genetic heritage. Seed libraries, seed exchanges, and organizations like Seed Savers Exchange help maintain and distribute these varieties.
