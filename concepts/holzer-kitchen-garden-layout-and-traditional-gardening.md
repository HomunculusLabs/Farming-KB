# Holzer Kitchen Garden Layout and Traditional Gardening

The nicest areas around houses were once reserved for kitchen gardens. There, farmers cultivated valuable fruit, vegetables, and medicinal and [[permaculture-plants-medicinal-herbs-healing]] like monkshood and foxgloves, which catch the eye with their beautiful flowers. These play an important role in interactions within nature and make a significant contribution to healthy soil life.

**By the garden gate (most accessible):** Lovage, chives, leeks, onions, and garlic -- the herbs the cook used most frequently.

**Sunny vegetable beds:** [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Parsley was kept away from the salad plants.

### The Recipe for Success
The areas around the fence and in the garden (sunny, shady, dry or wet soils) were given to the plants that were suited to them. Plants that are in the right places will thrive and are not as susceptible to disease. They also develop the highest nutrient content (essential oils, bitter substances) in the locations that they are naturally suited to.

## Cold Frame Seed Starting

A cold frame consists of a simple wooden box covered with a window or clear sheeting. In spring, a 30-cm-thick layer of straw and dung is placed at the bottom, covered with garden soil. The dung warms up through decomposition, functioning like underfloor heating for the bed. The glass or sheeting cover creates a greenhouse effect.

Location should be sheltered from wind and as sunny as possible to make best use of spring sun. Plants must be hardened off before planting out by gradually increasing the periods the cover is removed. Hardening off begins around Saint Joseph's Day (19th March). As soon as plants are large enough and overnight frosts are over, they are planted out in the garden.

Dry branches pushed into the soil serve as climbing supports for peas and beans.

## Vegetable Patch

In addition to the kitchen garden, many farmers also had a large fenced vegetable patch replanted each year:

**Crops grown:** White cabbage (for sauerkraut), turnips, chard, beetroot, swede, stock feed carrots, black radish. Turnips, chard, and stock feed carrots were used to feed the cattle.

**Autumn harvest:** Winter vegetables were pulled up, roots and leaves cut off with a knife (crops must not be damaged or they rot in the cellar), and stored in a frost-free earth cellar under the house, separated into rooms with larch posts.

**Soil management:** Dead flowers and stems were spread over the soil around plants and covered with soil. Plants growing too close together were thinned and either replanted elsewhere or given to neighbours. Larger "weeds" were pulled and left on a sunny day so roots dried out quickly.

## Companion Planting in Practice

The traditional interplanting at Krameterhof demonstrates natural [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] that also serve food crops.
- **Lovage isolated in its own corner:** Recognizing lovage's allelopathic tendencies — it inhibits growth of nearby plants through chemical suppression — Holzer assigns it a dedicated position rather than interplanting it. A single mature lovage plant supplies a family of four year-round, making its space requirement minimal relative to its yield.
- **Fruit bushes along the fence line:** Redcurrants, blackcurrants, white currants, and strawberries occupy the fence perimeter, producing food while serving as a wind-filtering barrier that protects more sensitive interior plants.
