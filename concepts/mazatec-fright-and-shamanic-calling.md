# Mazatec Fright (Susto) and Shamanic Calling

## Overview

Among the Mazatec Indians, the illness known as *fright* — equivalent to the widely documented Meso-American syndrome of *susto* (Spanish for "fright" or "shock") — represents a fundamental concept linking trauma, spiritual loss, and the shamanic vocation. As described by Henry Munn through the case study of the shaman Roman Estrada, *fright* is both the primary ailment that shamans treat and the initiatory crisis through which shamans themselves are called to their profession.

## The Concept of Fright (*Susto*)

### Etiology

The Mazatecs understand *fright* as a condition in which a traumatic event causes the soul or spirit to flee the body. Following beliefs shared with their Siberian ancestors (reflecting the broad distribution of soul-loss concepts across indigenous cultures), the Mazatecs hold that:

- The soul can be "frightened from one" by a sudden, overwhelming experience
- The spirit "goes" — leaving the person alienated from themselves or possessed by another
- The affected person "loses themselves" — experiencing depression, apathy, loss of will, and a sense of disconnection from life

This is not merely a psychological metaphor but an ontological claim: the person has literally lost a part of themselves. The illness is understood as a genuine absence — the spirit is somewhere else, wandering, lost, and must be found and returned.

### Environmental Context

The Mazatec landscape itself contributes to the prevalence of fright:

- **Precipitous terrain**: Walking along arduous mountain paths on different elevations, through clouds and sunlight, creates constant exposure to danger
- **Grottos and abysses**: Mysterious groves, deep caves, and sudden drop-offs populate the landscape with physical dangers
- **Supernatural inhabitants**: "The lad, the little people, mischievous dwarfs and gnomes" are believed to inhabit these spaces
- **Spirits of water**: Rivers and wells are inhabited by "spirits with powers of enchantment"
- **Dangerous winds**: At night, "winds whirl up from the depths, rush out of the distance like monsters, and pass, tearing everything in their path with their fierce claws"
- **Phantoms and the evil eye**: "Phantoms appear in the mists. There are persons with the evil eye."

In this world, "existence in the world and with others is treacherous, perilous: unexpectedly something may happen to you and that event, unless it is exorcised, can mark you for life." The environment itself generates the conditions for fright.

### The Shaman's Task

The shaman's role in treating fright is to:

1. **Locate the lost spirit**: Using mushroom-induced vision to find where the spirit has gone
2. **Retrieve it**: Bringing the spirit back to the patient
3. **Reintegrate the personality**: Restoring the patient to wholeness by returning their missing spiritual component
4. **Make payments**: If necessary, paying the powers that have appropriated the spirit by burying cacao beans (traditional exchange currency) wrapped in bark cloth at the place of fright, which the shaman has divined through vision

The medicine woman's prayer illustrates this: "Bring her spirit, her soul. Let her spirit come back from where it got lost, from where it stayed, from where it was left behind, from wherever it is that her spirit is wandering lost."

## The Case of Roman Estrada: Shamanic Calling Through Fright

### The Initiatory Illness

Roman Estrada's story illustrates the classic pattern of shamanic calling through personal illness. His vocation began with what was initially a physical ailment — appendicitis — that became a traumatic neurosis:

1. He was hospitalized and anesthetized with ether for an appendectomy
2. During the operation, "he gave up the ghost" — he experienced what felt like death
3. Upon recovery from anesthesia, he was "frightened and depressed, without any will to live"
4. Despite successful physical recovery (the wound healed), he remained apathetic and unresponsive
5. He lay "like a dead man with his eyes wide open, not saying anything to anyone"
6. He believed his life had been a failure and that "it was too late" to become the important man he had always aspired to be

This pattern — physical crisis leading to psychological collapse, failed treatment by conventional medicine, and restoration through shamanic intervention — is one of the most widely documented pathways to shamanic calling across cultures.

### Failed Conventional Treatment

Estrada sought help from multiple Western medical institutions:

- The Latin American Hospital
- Hospital in Cordoba
- Hospital in Mexico City
- Hospital in Tehuacán

None could help him because, as Munn observes, "there was nothing wrong with him physically" — his appendix had been successfully removed. The doctors "couldn't help him because there was nothing wrong with him physically; contrary to what he believed, he had survived the operation." The problem was spiritual: "he had been terrified by death and his spirit had flown away like a bird or a fleet-footed deer."

### The Shamanic Cure

Estrada was treated by a renowned shaman from the nearby village of San Lucas — a "short, slight, wizened old man" of 105 years. The cure proceeded through several stages:

**Symbolic Surgery**: Under the influence of the mushrooms, Estrada vividly relived his operation. The mushrooms "cut him open, arranged his insides, and sewed him up again." This visionary re-enactment allowed him to:

- Consciously experience what had happened to him under anesthesia
- Correct what he perceived as the mistakes of the "profane doctor"
- Assume the frightening event he had been unable to integrate
- Complete his physiological cure psychologically

As Munn describes: "It was as if he were operating upon himself, undoing what had been done to him, and doing it over again himself. The trauma was exorcised. By intensely envisioning with a heightened, expanded consciousness what had happened to him under anesthesia, he assumed at last the frightening event he had previously been unable to integrate into his experience."

**Revivification**: "The dead man came back to life, he wanted to live because he felt once again that he was alive and had the force to go on living: once exhausted and despondent, he was now invigorated and rejuvenated."

## The Transmission of Shamanic Vocation

### The Master's Approach

The elderly shaman of San Lucas did not merely cure Estrada — he also initiated him into the shamanic profession. The cure and the calling were inseparable:

> "The cure is successful because not only is his spirit awakened, but he is offered another future: a new profession that is a compensation for his humble one as a storekeeper."

The old shaman understood that Estrada's neurosis was caused by "the repression of his creative forces" and that genuine healing required the release of these forces through a new vocation.

### The Initiation Ritual

The initiation involved a carefully structured series of steps:

1. **The mushroom session**: Estrada was given the mushrooms three times to complete his cure
2. **The offering**: The old shaman left him "a table with ground tobacco on it and a cross underneath it"
3. **The choice**: Estrada was asked to choose between these objects — a test of his readiness
4. **The refusal**: Estrada initially refused, saying he only wanted to be cured, not to become a shaman
5. **The negation of will**: The shaman told him, "Here you don't give the orders... it is already the middle of the night." By negating the patient's will, the shaman aimed to arouse it and prepare him to accept the vocation
6. **The diploma**: "Whether you want to or not, you are going to receive your diploma" — invoking the prospect of recognition and authority
7. **The voice of the father**: During the mushroom experience, Estrada heard the voice of his dead father (deceased forty-three years) commanding him to accept the shamanic work
8. **The interpretation**: The old shaman identified the voice as that of Estrada's father, resolving the ambiguity of the experience
9. **Acceptance**: Estrada agreed to receive the teaching and was taught techniques including "sucking through space" — drawing sickness out of a patient from a distance using a hollow tube of cane

### Psychological Dynamics of Initiation

Munn's analysis of this initiation reveals several psychological mechanisms:

**Superego activation**: The voice of the dead father represents the activation of Estrada's superego — the internalized parental authority — in conjunction with the liberation of vitality produced by the mushrooms. The father's command releases the resistance that Estrada's conscious will could not overcome.

**Personality reintegration**: The acceptance of the new vocation becomes the organizing principle around which Estrada's fragmented personality is reconstituted: "he becomes an adept of the dimensions of consciousness where live the spirits; a speaker of mighty words."

**Compensation**: The shamanic role provides a "compensation for his humble one as a storekeeper" — addressing the life-narrative of failure that contributed to his depression.

**Transference of will**: The old shaman's technique of negating the patient's will ("It is no longer you who command") and then transferring his own will to the patient parallels the psychoanalytic concept of transference, but operates in reverse — the healer transfers healing intention to the patient rather than receiving the patient's projections.

## Symbolic Objects in Initiation

The objects presented during Estrada's initiation carry rich symbolic meaning:

- **The table**: An altar at which to officiate. Mazatec mushroom sessions are called "masses," and the shaman assumes a sacerdotal role even without formal ordination
- **The tobacco (San Pedro)**: Believed to have "powerful magical and remedial values" — a sacred substance in Meso-American shamanism
- **The cross**: Represents both a "crossing of the ways, an intersection of existential paths, a change" and the Christian symbol of crucifixion and resurrection — death and rebirth, appropriate for a man who has just symbolically died and been restored to life

## The Universal Pattern of Shamanic Calling

Estrada's story exemplifies a pattern found across cultures worldwide:

1. **The call comes through crisis**: The future shaman experiences a serious illness, trauma, or psychological breakdown
2. **Conventional healing fails**: Doctors, medicines, and normal remedies prove ineffective
3. **The shamanic cure succeeds**: A shaman treats the condition successfully using traditional methods
4. **The cure reveals a calling**: The patient discovers during treatment that they have been chosen for the shamanic role
5. **Resistance and acceptance**: The patient initially resists the calling but ultimately accepts it
6. **The dead speak**: Ancestral voices or spirits confirm the calling during the visionary experience
