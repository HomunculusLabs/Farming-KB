## Ethics as System Feedback

Holmgren frames ethics as feedback mechanisms that have evolved in cultures to regulate behaviour in ways that maintain the health of the larger system. In traditional societies, ethical rules and spiritual beliefs served as constraints on individual behaviour that prevented destruction of the resource base on which the community depended.

This framing connects ethics to [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]]. Ethics are not arbitrary rules imposed from above but functional adaptations that have evolved because they help maintain the health of the whole system, just as negative feedback loops in ecosystems prevent destructive overshoot.

## The Three Ethics as Functional

The three permaculture ethics — Care for Earth, Care for People, and Redistribute Surplus — are understood not as philosophical ideals but as practical cultural mechanisms:

- **Care for Earth** — a cultural mechanism to maintain the biological resource base that sustains society

- **Care for People** — a mechanism that ensures social cohesion and the well-being needed for collective action

- **Redistribute Surplus** — a mechanism to prevent elite capture and hoarding that destabilizes social systems

Each ethic addresses a specific systemic vulnerability: ecological degradation, social breakdown, and elite parasitism. Together they form a functional ethical framework that has parallels in many traditional cultures.

## Nature-Spirit Traditions

In indigenous and peasant societies, caring for the land beyond family or descendants was embedded in nature-spirit traditions which required gifts, work, or other expressions of devotion to maintain all of the living world. At a practical level, planting long-lived and valuable trees and forests was a traditional way of redistributing surplus time and resources for the benefit of future generations and the land itself.

## Decline of Institutional Ethics

Holmgren observes that traditional institutions of church and state are losing their authority while corporations and other powerful economic institutions have gained enormous power with little if any ethical constraint. People are finding a wide variety of ways to help others through overseas aid, social service clubs, philanthropic trusts, voluntary work, and the arts.

This institutional vacuum creates both danger and opportunity. Without functional ethical mechanisms, systemic feedback breaks down. But it also opens space for new ethical frameworks more suited to energy descent realities.

## Ethics vs. Atonement

Many social and environmental good works can be seen as atonement for collective sins rather than necessarily providing what people or nature really need. Sometimes giving is a disguised form of self-interest that creates dependency. In both social and natural domains, how to give in ways that show true altruism has always been problematic.

This insight connects to [[holmgren-ethical-philosophical-framework]] by suggesting that permaculture ethics emerge from the intersection of scientific understanding and spiritual awareness rather than belonging exclusively to either domain.

## Care for People as Expanding Circles

Care for People starts with the self but expands in widening circles to include families, neighbours, local and wider communities. In this sense it follows the pattern of almost all traditional (tribal) ethical systems. The greatest ethical concern is naturally focused close to the centre because that is where we have the greatest power and influence. To be able to contribute to a wider good, one must be healthy and secure.

## Redistribute Surplus

The ethic of Redistribute Surplus requires sharing surplus resources to help the earth and people beyond our immediate circle of power and responsibility. It requires exercising power to stimulate or fund the provision of needs beyond those of ourselves, family, community and region where there may be no mutual obligation or feedback mechanism to reward our benevolence.

## Cultural Mechanisms for Energy Descent

In the context of energy descent, Holmgren argues we need new cultural mechanisms — effectively new ethical frameworks — that recognize society's increasing dependence on nature. These must replace the economic rationalism that currently drives decisions.

## Population and Limits

The issue of population growth is a vexed one with many different perspectives. The world is probably already overpopulated for the long-term good of humanity and other species. The ecological footprint is one relatively simple method to audit and reorganise our personal demand on natural resources. Such methods help us to set limits and monitor our performance.
