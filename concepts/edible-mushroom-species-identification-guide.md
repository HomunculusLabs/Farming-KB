# Edible Mushroom Species Identification Guide

Drawing from Julius A. Palmer's field observations and personal consumption
experiments, this guide covers the major groups of edible fungi that he
identified and tested. Palmer personally tested approximately forty varieties
of toadstools and regularly ate more than half of them whenever found, making
daily additions to his fungal bill of fare during the mushroom season.

## Agarics with Gills

### Meadow Mushroom (*Agaricus campestris*)

The classic "mushroom" of English-speaking people. At first growth it resembles
a puffball, but soon a veil or web appears under the cap, which ruptures to
expose free gills showing a faint pink color. This pink deepens through purple
into black as the mushroom matures. The ring around the stem is perfect in
younger specimens. The cap ranges from pure white to deep brown. Though
difficult to describe from text, the taste and smell are so peculiar to the
species that deception is unlikely once one has made their acquaintance.

### Horse Mushroom (*Agaricus arvensis*)

So similar to the meadow mushroom that many observers ignore any difference
between them. Palmer mentioned the theory that the horse mushroom is propagated
from spores of the meadow mushroom after passing through a horse's digestive
system — a rational explanation for the name. It shares the same general
edibility profile and the same gill color progression from pink through purple
to black.

### Elm-Tree Mushroom (*Agaricus ulmarius*)

Found exclusively in fall growing on elm trees. Features a thick, solid stem
(sometimes set to one side of the cap), broad white gills, firm white flesh,
and a light yellow cap that is smooth at first but becomes spotted and cracked
with age. Older specimens develop yellow gills and a leathery cap surface. It
is most common in clefts or where limbs have been removed, and often reappears
yearly at the same spot. Because no other large gilled fungus with white flesh
commonly grows from elm, identification is relatively straightforward.

### Shaggy Mane (*Coprinus comatus*)

Perhaps the most easily recognized agaric. When fit for food, it is ovate,
shaped like a turtle's egg, alike at each end. The cap is brown and smooth,
but toward the base the outer skin ruffles up into a white mane or fringe of
concentric layers. The stem is hollow, and when cut resembles macaroni. A
ring around the stem is movable in well-developed specimens. Very soon after
reaching full growth the gill tips turn black, sometimes running through pink,
then the mushroom opens umbrella-like and dissolves into an inky fluid. This
was the first fungus Palmer dared to eat on his own recognition. It has no
poisonous counterpart but must never be eaten stale or with blackened gills.

### Sparkling Coprinus (*Coprinus micaceus*)

A small toadstool very common about old stumps. Initially condemned by Palmer
as non-esculent, he later corrected this error, acknowledging it as one of the
safest fungi. Its repeated crops make it important for the mycologist to
recognize.

### Honey Mushroom (*Agaricus melleus*)

A species with a controversial reputation among authorities. Greville noted
that while Fries quoted Trattinick as authority for its being freely eaten on
the Continent, Person and Paulet gave it a bad character, with Paulet's dog
dying twelve hours after consuming it. Palmer found it a favorite article of
diet, concluding that it may be noxious raw but that cooking kills the virus.

## Russulae (Brittle Fungi)

The Russula family is quite safe for beginners who observe certain rules. The
key distinguishing feature is brittleness: the gills break into small pieces
and the whole fungus snaps suddenly when bent, unlike the pliable flesh of
common mushrooms.

### Identification Rules

If milk or juice exudes when the flesh is cut, Palmer advised the amateur to
reject the specimen, as the lactarius group requires more experience. For
dry Russulae, tasting a small piece of the stem provides a reliable test: if
the specimen is not an edible russula, it will bite and sting the tongue,
while all esculent varieties are tasteless, mild, or have a chestnut-like
flavor.

### Red-Capped Russulae

The common variety (*[[russula-alutacea]]*) has a bright red cap, buff-colored
gills, and a stout short stem. Two very similar species (*R. emetica* and
*R. rosacea*) resemble it so closely that Palmer habitually tasted a small
piece of each stem while washing them to avoid any possibility of mistake.

## Boleti (Pore-Bearing Fungi)

The Boleti can only be confused with their twin brothers the Polyporei. The
edible boletus grows from the ground with a distinct stem and easily
separable tubes, while the polyporus grows mostly on wood with an abortive
stem and inseparable pores. Tubes brown, yellowish, or greenish with a russet
cap and white flesh indicate edibility; brilliant red or yellow coloring or
blue bruising suggests rejection; red tube orifices indicate probable poison.

## Hydnei (Teeth-Bearing Fungi)

### Spreading Hydnum (*Hydnum repandum*)

Usually yellow, sometimes reddish, always firm-fleshed. The stem is deformed,
indistinct, or eccentric, and one side of the cap is frequently much higher
than the point diametrically opposite. The teeth or spines, which rub off
easily in young specimens, are distinctive enough to prevent confusion with
other groups. Smith stated that all species of any size enjoyed a good
character.

## Puffballs

### Giant Puffball (*Lycoperdon giganteum*)

The safest mushroom for the beginner. When found with a smooth skin and
perfectly white inside, it is in an edible infantile state. Skin color varies
from pure white to nearly black. If the knife leaves a yellow stain, it is
too old. Palmer believed all white puffballs could be safely used if cooked
at once, as they change rapidly after gathering. No poisonous fungus resembles
them.

## Cooking and Preservation

Palmer insisted on only two proper [[comparison-biogas-digester-basics-vs-solar-cooking-methods]]: broiling on a fine-wire
gridiron with salt, pepper (for known edibles), and butter; or stewing in milk
like an oyster stew. He also dried mushrooms for winter, noting from experience
in [[san-francisco-shipibo-ayahuasca-ceremonial-center]], Honolulu, and Hong Kong that dried fungus was an excellent
meat substitute.
