# Ingham — Fungal Inoculum Sourcing and Identification

Updated: 2026-04-18

Dr. Elaine Ingham emphasizes that the most critical missing component in most agricultural soils is beneficial fungal biomass. Conventional tillage and chemical applications destroy fungal hyphae, and restoring them requires deliberate sourcing of high-quality fungal inoculum from compost, forest soils, or commercial products. Understanding how to identify, source, and apply beneficial fungi is central to the [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] and pesticides further reduce fungal populations by eliminating the organisms on which fungi depend or by directly killing sensitive species. Ingham notes that compost tea "provides the inoculum of fungi, since that is most often what is lost with conventional tillage and chemical applications."

The result is that most agricultural soils are bacterial-dominated -- 75% of the time, according to Ingham. While annual vegetables and row crops can tolerate bacterial soils, perennials, trees, and shrubs require fungal-dominated soil food webs for optimal health. Even annual crops benefit from elevated fungal biomass for disease resistance and [[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] live.

**Collection protocol:**
1. Find a mature forest with healthy trees showing deep root systems
2. Look for dark brown humus material in the O horizon
3. Seek material with visible thick white, yellow, pink, or tan fungal strands
4. Collect handfuls of this material
5. Avoid mixing the organic material into the mineral soil below -- that favors bacteria over fungi

### Alaska Humus and Commercial Sources

Alaska humus is noted as generally high in fungal biomass and can serve as a reliable inoculum source. However, Ingham recommends combining commercial sources with local forest material to maximize diversity: "If you are trying to choose between local forest material and AH, why not use them both, in order to maximize diversity?"

### Healthy vs Unhealthy Tree Soils

The source matters enormously. Ingham distinguishes between:

- **Healthy trees**: Deep roots going far into the soil, indicating adequate [[ingham-glomalin-biological-soil-glues]]. Beneficial saprophytes tend to have wider-diameter hyphae and are more efficient at using litter resources than disease organisms.

### Pathogenic Fungi

Pathogens use living organic matter and typically cause the death of the host. [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]], pathogens are generally outcompeted.

### Facultative Species

Some fungi can function as saprophytes or pathogens depending on conditions. When adequate dead organic matter and aerobic conditions are present, they function beneficially. When stress conditions favor them, they may shift to pathogenic behavior. This is why maintaining diverse, aerobic fungal communities is more effective than trying to target specific species.

### Parasitic vs Mutualistic

[[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]]. Ingham offers practical guidance:

- **Black walnut** contains juglone (a particularly effective terpene) that can suppress both weeds and beneficial fungi
- **Volatilization**: Chip woody material and let it sit for 4+ weeks to allow terpenes and phenols to volatilize
- **Fungal inoculation**: Apply compost tea with good fungi and add old wood chip pile material to accelerate decomposition of resistant compounds
- **Strategic use**: Some terpenes may provide desirable weed suppression -- test by applying treated and untreated material in separate areas
