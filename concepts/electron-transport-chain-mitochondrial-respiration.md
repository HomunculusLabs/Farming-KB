## Overview

The electron transport chain (ETC) is a series of protein complexes and
mobile electron carriers embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane that
couple the oxidation of NADH and FADH2 to the generation of a proton
gradient, which drives ATP synthesis. This process, collectively termed
oxidative phosphorylation, is responsible for producing approximately 90-95%
of the ATP in aerobic eukaryotic cells. The ETC is the final common pathway
of aerobic metabolism, integrating catabolic products from carbohydrates,
fatty acids, and amino acids. The entire system operates with remarkable
efficiency, converting the chemical energy of reduced cofactors into the
universal energy currency of the cell.

## Complex I — NADH Dehydrogenase

Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) is the largest and most
elaborate of the ETC complexes, consisting of 44 subunits in mammals.
It accepts electrons from NADH generated by the [[glycolysis-embden-meyerhof-parnas-pathway|glycolytic pathway]], the
[[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle|citric acid cycle]], and beta-oxidation of fatty
acids. Complex I transfers
two electrons through a flavin mononucleotide (FMN) cofactor and a chain
of seven iron-sulfur clusters to ubiquinone (coenzyme Q10), reducing it to
ubiquinol. This electron transfer is coupled to the translocation of four
protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, contributing significantly
to the proton-motive force.

Complex I is a major site of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production.
When electron flow through the chain is impaired, electrons can leak from
the FMN cofactor and reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide. This has
implications for aging, neurodegenerative diseases, and the [[oxidative-phosphorylation-mitochondrial-respiration|oxidative phosphorylation]] system overall.
Genetic defects in Complex I subunits
are the most common cause of mitochondrial respiratory chain disorders in
humans, presenting as Leigh syndrome, Leber hereditary optic neuropathy,
and various myopathies.

## Complex II — Succinate Dehydrogenase

Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) is unique among the ETC complexes
because it also functions as part of the citric acid cycle, directly
oxidizing succinate to fumarate. It contains a flavin adenine dinucleotide
(FAD) cofactor, iron-sulfur clusters, and a b-type cytochrome. Complex II
transfers electrons from FADH2 to ubiquinone but does not pump protons
across the membrane, which is why FADH2 yields less ATP per molecule than
NADH. The lack of proton pumping at Complex II reflects its dual role as
a TCA cycle enzyme constrained to the matrix face of the inner membrane.

Mutations in Complex II subunits are associated with hereditary
paragangliomas and pheochromocytomas, highlighting the connection
between mitochondrial function and tumorigenesis. The succinate
accumulation that results from Complex II deficiency also inhibits
prolyl hydroxylase enzymes, leading to stabilization of HIF-1alpha
and a pseudohypoxic cellular response that promotes angiogenesis and
tumor growth.

## Complex III — Cytochrome bc1 Complex

Complex III (cytochrome bc1 or ubiquinol-cytochrome c oxidoreductase)
transfers electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c, a small mobile
heme protein. It operates through the Q cycle, a sophisticated mechanism
in which ubiquinol is oxidized at one site (Qo) and ubiquinone is
reduced at another site (Qi), resulting in the net translocation of
four protons per pair of electrons transferred. The Q cycle doubles
the proton-pumping efficiency compared to a simple linear transfer and
is considered one of the most elegant energy-conservation mechanisms
in bioenergetics.

Cytochrome c is the only mobile ETC carrier that is a soluble protein
rather than a lipid-soluble molecule. It shuttles single electrons
from Complex III to Complex IV through the intermembrane space, and
its release into the cytoplasm is a key event in initiating the
intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway of apoptosis. Antimycin A, a
fungicidal antibiotic, inhibits Complex III at the Qi site and is
widely used as a research tool in mitochondrial studies.

## Complex IV — Cytochrome c Oxidase

Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) is the terminal enzyme of the ETC,
catalyzing the four-electron reduction of molecular oxygen to water.
This is the step at which oxygen is consumed in cellular respiration.
Complex IV contains two heme groups (heme a and heme a3) and two copper
centers (CuA and CuB) that facilitate the sequential transfer of four
electrons to oxygen, preventing the formation of partially reduced and
highly reactive oxygen intermediates. The reduction of oxygen is coupled
to the translocation of two additional protons across the membrane,
with two more protons consumed from the matrix in forming water.

## Proton-Motive Force and ATP Synthase

The combined action of Complexes I, III, and IV creates an electrochemical
proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, known as the
proton-motive force. This gradient has two components: a chemical gradient
(delta pH) and an electrical gradient (delta psi). ATP synthase (Complex V)
harnesses this proton-motive force to catalyze the phosphorylation of ADP
to ATP. As protons flow back through the F0 transmembrane channel of ATP
synthase, conformational changes in the F1 catalytic domain drive ATP
synthesis according to the binding change mechanism.

The P/O ratio describes the number of ATP molecules synthesized per atom
of oxygen consumed. Classical values of 3.0 for NADH and 2.0 for FADH2
have been revised downward to approximately 2.5 and 1.5 respectively based
on modern measurements, reflecting the actual proton stoichiometry of the
transport chain and the costs of substrate transport into the mitochondria.

## Inhibitors and Uncouplers

The ETC is a target of numerous natural and synthetic inhibitors.
Rotenone inhibits Complex I, antimycin A blocks Complex III at the Qi
site, and cyanide and carbon monoxide inhibit Complex IV by binding to
the heme iron. Uncouplers such as 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) dissipate the
proton gradient by carrying protons across the inner membrane without
passing through ATP synthase, causing electrons to flow through the
chain at maximum rate while ATP synthesis ceases. Energy is released as
heat instead, which is the basis of mitochondrial uncoupling in brown
adipose tissue thermogenesis through the action of uncoupling protein 1
(UCP1).
