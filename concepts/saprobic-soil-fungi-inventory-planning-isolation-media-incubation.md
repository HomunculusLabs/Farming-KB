# Saprobic Soil Fungi — Inventory Planning, Isolation Techniques, Media, and Incubation

## Evaluating Diversity — Preliminary Planning

A clear definition of habitats and an efficient sampling design are foundational to any soil fungal inventory. The investigator must explore the magnitude of species diversity and determine the minimum sample sizes needed to achieve the inventory's objectives. The scale of sampling is determined by the inventory goals: a comprehensive evaluation of a park or reserve requires a network of sites, whereas assessing land management impacts may call for more limited sampling with experimentally introduced variables.

If no prior data exist for a region, preliminary sampling is necessary to determine the effort level required for detecting fine-scale patterns. This preliminary phase helps calibrate the intensity and spatial arrangement of the full inventory.

### Horizon and Depth Considerations

In moderate-precipitation areas, the upper surface of the mineral soil (the A1 horizon) harbors the highest water content, organic matter, and fungal abundance and diversity. In arid and semiarid regions, the subsurface moist horizon may harbor a different assemblage altogether, and 2–3 depth levels should be sampled to capture this variation. Sampling at multiple depths is essential in such environments because moisture availability is the primary driver of fungal distribution.

Litter and humus each support distinct mycotas. The litter mycota is tied to the plant community and includes distinctive decay genera. The litter-humus intergrade at their interface is an exceptionally species-rich zone. In humid tropical forests, the litter–soil interface is abrupt: litter is species-rich while the soil is depauperate (Pfenning 1993; Bills and Polishook 1994).

This contrast underscores the importance of sampling both substrates separately and analyzing their transition zone, where the highest species turnover is often observed.

### Vertical Stratification and Seasonal Dynamics

A vertical succession of species occurs through litter and soil horizons (Wicklow and Whittingham 1974; Kjøller and Struwe 1982; Aoki et al. 1990, 1992). Vertical dissection via pit studies or core dissection is strongly recommended during preliminary investigations to characterize this stratification.

Stable organic compounds—waxes, lignins, and phenolics—can persist for hundreds of years in mineral soil, providing enduring substrate for specialized decomposers. In tropical soils, species richness declines rapidly with depth (Stotzky et al. 1962; Pfenning 1993). Remarkably, Beiswenger and Christensen (1989) recovered a periglacial relict mycota at 1 m depth in Wyoming, demonstrating that deep soil horizons can preserve communities from past climatic regimes.

Seasonal variation in relative abundances is well documented (Christensen 1969; Gochenaur 1978; Clarke and Christensen 1981; Widden 1986b), though it is less pronounced in agricultural soils and boreal conifer forests. Seasonal sampling should therefore be considered in ecologically variable habitats to capture the full complement of species active across the annual cycle.

In temperate regions, spring and autumn peaks in fungal activity are common, while summer drought and winter cold may suppress detectable populations.

### Sample Size Targets

The maximum number of isolates manageable by two full-time experienced researchers is approximately 2000–3000. Beyond this number, the logistics of isolation, purification, identification, and curation become prohibitive for most research programs.

Species-isolate curves reach asymptotes of 60–150 species for 600–1000 isolates, but new species continue to appear indefinitely, reflecting the open-ended nature of soil fungal diversity and the rarity of many taxa. Common taxa can be determined with 200–250 strains from 6–10 samples.

A minimum of 600–1000 isolates per site is recommended for recovering the majority of species, while 180–250 isolates suffice for quantitative comparative data. These targets should be adjusted upward for tropical or otherwise hyperdiverse regions where species accumulation curves plateau more slowly.

## Collection and Storage of Soil

Remove litter and humus from an area of approximately 20 cm². Sterilize the digging instrument in 70% ethanol between samples to prevent cross-contamination. Collect 6–10 soil plugs (10–30 g each) in labeled sterile bags; several bags should be taken from each homogeneous site to ensure adequate replication.

If field sterilization of tools is impossible, immerse tools and bags in the local soil repeatedly before use to reduce contaminant spores from previous sampling locations.

In warm weather, transport samples in a cooler at 0–10°C to slow microbial activity and prevent desiccation. Process samples as soon as possible—ideally within hours to days. If immediate processing is not feasible, use cold storage or freezing to arrest community changes.

For reconnaissance work, soil can be sprinkled, swabbed, or stamped directly onto media in the field, providing a rapid qualitative assessment of the mycota without the need for transport.

For long-distance transport, air-dry samples in breathable cotton or perforated Tyvek bags, or in paper envelopes. Plastic bags are acceptable if samples will be processed or frozen within 1–2 days, but condensation and anaerobic conditions may promote undesirable microbial growth. Air-drying should be performed in a clean room or laminar flow hood at 2–5°C.

Frozen storage at −10 to −20°C stops temporal changes in fungal communities for at least 9 months (Christensen et al. 1962; Christensen 1969). However, the effects of physical manipulation during transport and storage have not been adequately studied, and caution is warranted in drawing conclusions from stored samples.

## Media

### Selectivity of "Nonselective" Media

Commonly used "nonselective" media—including Czapek, malt extract agar, and potato dextrose agar (PDA)—are in fact highly selective. High sugar concentrations favor rapid germinators and fast-growing species, biasing community composition toward opportunistic taxa. As a result, the fungal assemblages recovered on these media represent only a fraction of the true soil mycota.

The ideal medium for comprehensive inventories would be either truly nonselective or a standard array of selective media applied in combination; neither has been fully developed.

### Multiple Media Required

Recovering the complete mycota of a soil sample requires multiple media and isolation conditions. No single medium captures the full spectrum of soil fungi. Nutritionally rich media are needed for slow-growing species, but interfering fast-growing species must be constrained.

This is achieved by adding sublethal doses of fungitoxic agents such as rose bengal, cyclosporin A, cycloheximide, and dichloran, used singly or in combination. Phytopathologists have developed specialized media for targeted pathogens (Tsao 1970), but few investigators have directly compared media effectiveness in general mycological inventories (Mahoney 1972; Andrews and Pitt 1986; Wildman 1991).

### Recommended Media Strategies

- **General inventories:** Low-nutrient media such as soil extract–rose bengal or CMC (carboxymethyl cellulose) agar simulate oligotrophic conditions and yield diverse assemblages by reducing the competitive advantage of fast-growing sugar-loving species.
- **Expanding inventory scope:** Supplement with low-temperature (0–10°C) or high-temperature (40–55°C) incubation to capture psychrophilic and thermotolerant species absent from standard plates.
- **Augmenting further:** Nutrient-rich media with colony-restricting agents (e.g., DRBC or malt-cyclosporin agar) combined with early weeding and low-temperature incubation promote slower colonizers.
- **Filamentous oomycetes:** P10VP medium supplemented with pimaricin (natamycin) selectively inhibits true fungi while permitting oomycete growth.
- **Cycloheximide:** Toxic to most filamentous fungi at 0.25–0.5 g/L; used to selectively isolate Ophiostomatales and other cycloheximide-tolerant groups.
- **Benomyl:** Toxic to ascomycetes but less so to basidiomycetes, Pleosporales, and Mucorales; useful for selectively enriching the latter groups.

## Incubation

Temperature profoundly affects the kinds of fungi that develop on isolation plates. Soil temperatures are naturally buffered by water evaporation and poor thermal conductivity. Forest soils typically range from 15–20°C; coastal rainforest soils may reach 15–25°C at 10 cm depth. Matching incubation temperatures to in situ soil conditions improves the ecological relevance of isolates recovered.

At the standard incubation temperature of 25°C, most colonies appear within 2–10 days, favoring fast-growing species. While efficient for high-throughput processing, this temperature biases recovery toward a subset of the community.

Incubation at 15–20°C causes colonies to appear sequentially over approximately 3 weeks, allowing better separation of slow-growing from fast-growing species and improving species discrimination. Cool temperatures favor terrestrial aquatic hyphomycetes and psychrophilic fungi. High temperatures (>40°C) reveal thermotolerant and thermophilic fungi that would be missed at ambient temperatures.

The effects of light on isolation have not been systematically investigated. Some media components photodegrade—notably, rose bengal becomes more toxic under illumination, potentially biasing results. Incubation in darkness is therefore advisable unless light effects are a specific subject of study.

## Selecting Colonies — Three Options

### Option 1: Quantitative Approach (Few Isolates)

Select 25–35 isolates per soil sample from 6–10 samples per site. Colonies are selected from low-density plates to minimize competitive overgrowth by dominant species. Approximately 300 ± 10 pairwise comparisons are possible, providing a quantitative snapshot of community composition suitable for statistical comparison among sites or treatments.

### Option 2: Thorough Community Characterization

Harvest all isolates from a limited number of plates (5–10 plates yielding 30–40 colonies each). Begin selection within 1–2 days of plating to capture early-colonizing species before they are overgrown. Transfer fast-growing species to slants for preservation so they do not overgrow slower colonies on the original plates.

Frequency is calculated as (isolates of a species / total isolates) × 100. Rarefaction analysis is recommended when comparing samples of unequal size.

### Option 3: Exploratory Approach for Rare and Slow-Growing Species

After principal species have been identified through Options 1 or 2, eliminate fast-growers by dissection or by burning them off with a soldering gun. Continue examining plates until no new colonies appear. Overselection is recommended to maximize recovery of uncommon taxa.

Intermittently lowering incubation temperature can further favor slow-growing and rare species that are outcompeted at standard temperatures. This approach is labor-intensive but essential for documenting the full diversity of a site, particularly for conservation assessments and taxonomic surveys where rare species are of particular interest.

## Principal Isolation Methods

### Suspension Plating

Soil is suspended in water, agar, dextrin, or CMC and pipetted onto the surface of an agar plate. Despite often being incorrectly called "dilution plating," this method does not necessarily achieve a true dilution.

Trial analyses should target 5–30 colonies per 100 mm plate for optimal resolution. Too many colonies per plate cause overgrowth and make species identification difficult; too few waste plates and effort.

### Warcup Soil Plates

Finely pulverized soil (0.2–5.0 mg) is placed in a Petri dish and cooled agar is poured over it. This method yields a spectrum of species similar to suspension plating but may additionally recover basidiomycetes, which are often underrepresented in suspension plating. Young colonies are harder to remove from the soil particles, however, which can complicate subculturing.

Despite this limitation, Warcup plates are widely used in soil mycology and remain a standard method for comparative studies.

### Drechsler's Method

Fresh soil or humus is placed directly on water agar or dilute cornmeal agar, creating microcosms in which fungi develop in situ. This method is particularly effective for isolating fast-growing oomycetes and cellular slime molds that may be lost or overgrown in other methods.

The microcosm approach preserves some of the natural interactions between soil organisms, though it is less suited to quantitative community analysis than suspension plating or soil washing.

### Rhizosphere and Rhizoplane Sampling

Tiny root fragments are placed in 5 mL of sterile water or 0.1% sodium pyrophosphate and agitated for 1 minute to dislodge attached fungal propagules. The resulting suspension is plated to isolate fungi closely associated with plant roots, including both pathogenic and commensal species.

Sodium pyrophosphate helps disperse soil aggregates and improve the release of root-associated hyphae and spores, increasing recovery efficiency.

### Particle Filtration ("Soil Washing")

This technique favors mycelial fragments over spores, revealing rarely seen species that are underrepresented in spore-based methods. Recommended particle sizes are 50–80 μm (Bååth 1988) or 105–210 μm (Bills and Polishook 1994).

Particle filtration provides data on relative density and frequency of colonization, makes microhabitat preferences detectable, and is adaptable to many substrata beyond soil. Because mycelial fragments represent actively growing hyphae rather than dormant propagules, this method provides insight into the in situ distribution of fungal mycelia and their relationship to soil particles of different sizes and compositions.
