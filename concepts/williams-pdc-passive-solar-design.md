# [[passive-solar-design]]

Passive design harnesses natural existing forces and
energies to modify the environment within a structure,
using the ambient energy that naturally exists in the
environment rather than relying on external fuel inputs.

## The Essence of Passive Design

For millennia human architecture excelled at passive design
to keep buildings cool in the heat and warm in the cold.
That is until the modern age when the International Style of
architecture embraced HVAC systems. With the proliferation
of HVAC dependence, modern humanity has all but forgotten
the art of passive design.

Permaculture Principle 2 calls for us to catch and store
energy, and this is the essence of passive design. Passive
designs catch, store, and harness ambient energy naturally
existing in the environment. While passive solar design is
an integral component, passive design is much more than just
solar. It is a whole systems approach.

## Understanding Heat Movement

There are three ways that heat moves: radiation, conduction,
and convection. Radiation moves heat via infrared and
microwaves. Conduction transfers heat through physical
contact between materials. Convection transfers heat through
the movement of a medium such as water or air.

Heat always flows from hot things into cold things, seeking
thermal equilibrium. When the designer understands these
forces they can design systems that move heat to where it is
needed most. As with water systems, one must concentrate heat
where wanted and bleed it off from where it is not wanted.

## The Four Key Elements

The four most important aspects to proper passive solar
design are orientation, insulation, glass, and mass.

Orientation: The Sun rises higher until the Summer Solstice
on June 21, then gets lower until the Winter Solstice on
December 21. A proper passive design keeps direct sun out
during summer and lets it in during winter. The structure
should have its longest wall facing solar south.

Solar altitude is the height of the Sun in the sky expressed
as an angle. The solar azimuth is the horizontal angle of
the Sun, measuring the angle of sunrise and sunset off of
solar south. The azimuth is always equal on both sides.

## Designing Overhangs

The length of a shadow equals the height of the structure
divided by the tangent of the solar altitude. Using this
formula you can design overhangs that keep the sun out in
summer and let it penetrate in winter. An overhang can be a
roof, an awning, or a window setback into a thick wall such
as in adobe buildings.

## Thermal Mass

A thermal mass is any material that absorbs and stores heat
energy. Stone, concrete, water, and earth make excellent
thermal mass. The purpose is to catch energy, specifically
in the form of heat, and store it for later use. By placing
a thermal mass in the path of direct sunlight it will absorb
heat during the day and bleed heat back into the space as
the temperature drops at night. For more on passive building techniques, see [[mollison-thermal-mass-passive-building]] and [[passive-solar-and-biomass-energy]].

As a rule of thumb, the thicker your thermal mass the more
heat energy it can store. A thermal mass should be about
four inches thick at minimum. Thicker masses store more heat
but take longer to absorb and release it, creating a time
lag effect that smooths temperature fluctuations over the
course of a day and night.

## Passive Design Limits and Judgment

You cannot perform miracles with passive design. In extreme
climates passive design may still have its limits. The
challenge of the permaculture designer is to distribute and
control as much heat as is environmentally possible. Only
when the limits of passive design are reached should the
designer turn to fireplaces, swamp coolers, or other
[[appropriate-technology]] to make up the difference. The
designer needs to be disciplined and imaginative enough to
not turn to these alternatives until their design has
reached its full passive design potential.

## Combining Heat Movement Strategies

A skilled designer combines all three methods of heat
movement in a single design. Radiation is captured through
properly oriented and glazed windows. Conduction is managed
through thermal mass materials that store and release heat.
Convection is controlled through ventilation design that
allows warm air to vent in summer while trapping it in
winter. Together these strategies create a comfortable
interior environment without mechanical heating or cooling
in most conditions.

## The Passive Design Process

Begin your passive design by researching the solar altitude
and solar azimuth for both the summer and winter solstice at
your latitude. Use this data to calculate overhang dimensions
and determine optimal window placement. Then identify where
thermal mass should be located to capture and store solar
gain. Finally, design ventilation paths that work with
natural convection currents. This systematic approach ensures
that your passive design captures the maximum available
ambient energy for your specific climate and site.
