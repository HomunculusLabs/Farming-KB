# Agar Culture Media

Agar media serve as the foundation of mushroom mycology, providing a solid
surface for isolating, maintaining, and propagating fungal cultures. The use
of agar in mycology traces back to early plant pathology, and it remains the
standard method for working with pure fungal cultures. Every cultivated
mushroom begins its journey on an agar plate.

## What Is Agar

Agar is a polysaccharide derived from red algae (primarily Gelidium and
Gracilaria species). It forms a gel at temperatures below approximately
42°C and melts at temperatures above 85°C, creating a convenient solid
growth medium that can be sterilized without decomposition. At
concentrations of 1.5--2.0%, agar provides a firm, stable surface that
retains moisture while allowing gas exchange. Importantly, most fungi
cannot metabolize agar itself, making it an inert structural component.

## Standard Agar Formulations

### Malt Extract Agar (MEA)

The most widely used general-purpose medium in [[mushroom-cultivation]]:

- **Malt extract**: 20 g (provides carbohydrates, amino acids, minerals)
- **Agar**: 15--20 g
- **Water**: 1000 mL

MEA supports vigorous, rhizomorphic growth for most cultivated species
including [[pleurotus-ostreatus]]. It is simple, reliable, and inexpensive.
The 2% concentration of malt extract provides sufficient nutrition without
encouraging bacterial overgrowth.

### Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)

The traditional standard in plant pathology:

- **Potato (peeled and diced)**: 200 g
- **Dextrose (glucose)**: 20 g
- **Agar**: 15--20 g
- **Water**: 1000 mL

Prepared by boiling potatoes in water, straining, and adding dextrose and
agar to the potato broth. PDA powder (dehydrated) is commercially available
for convenience. PDA supports slightly denser growth than MEA for some
species but is otherwise similar in performance.

### Malt Yeast Agar (MYA)

An enriched formulation for demanding species or slow-growing cultures:

- **Malt extract**: 10 g
- **Yeast extract**: 2--4 g
- **Agar**: 15--20 g
- **Water**: 1000 mL

The added yeast extract provides B vitamins and additional nitrogen,
promoting faster and more vigorous growth. MYA is preferred for isolating
from wild specimens and for maintaining finicky species.

### Oatmeal Agar

Used for species that benefit from more complex carbohydrates. Oats are
boiled, the broth strained, and used as the base with 15--20 g/L agar.
Oatmeal agar produces exceptionally rhizomorphic mycelium for many
[[pleurotus-species]].

## Selective Media

Antibiotics can suppress bacterial growth during isolation: gentamicin
(50 mg/L, heat-stable) or chloramphenicol (25--50 mg/L, added after
autoclaving). Low-pH agar (pH 4.5--5.0, adjusted with citric acid) provides
non-antibiotic bacterial suppression. Selective media are essential when
isolating from wild-collected specimens.

## Media Preparation

1. **Weigh ingredients** and combine in a clean flask or bottle. Use
   distilled or deionized water.
2. **Dissolve** by heating with stirring. Microwave heating is convenient
   for small batches (500 mL or less). Agar will not fully dissolve until
   the mixture reaches at least 85°C.
3. **Adjust pH** if needed. Most mushroom mycelium prefers pH 5.5--6.5.
   Test with pH paper or a meter.
4. **Dispense** into containers: Petri dishes (20--25 mL per plate), slant
   tubes (5--7 mL), or media bottles for storage.
5. **Sterilize** at 121°C (15 psi) for 20--30 minutes. Over-sterilization
   can caramelize sugars and darken the medium.
6. **Cool and pour**: For plates, pour in a laminar flow hood or still air
   box after the medium has cooled to approximately 50°C (hot to the touch
  but not burning). Cover plates and allow to solidify.

## Culture Techniques on Agar

### Tissue Culture

The simplest method for obtaining a pure culture from a fresh mushroom:

1. Select a young, healthy fruiting body. The interior tissue is more
   likely to be sterile.
2. Tear the mushroom open by hand (do not cut with a knife, which drags
   surface contaminants through the tissue).
3. Using a flame-sterilized scalpel, remove a small piece (2--3 mm) of
   internal tissue from the cap or the base of the stem.
4. Place the tissue sample onto the agar surface.
5. Incubate at the appropriate temperature. Mycelium should emerge from the
   tissue within 3--7 days.

### Spore Germination

Spores can be collected from a spore print and germinated on agar, but this
produces a genetically diverse culture (each spore is a unique individual).
Spore germination is used for breeding and strain development rather than
routine cultivation, where clonal propagation from tissue culture is
preferred.

### Agar-to-Agar Transfer

Routine maintenance involves transferring a small wedge of mycelium from a
colonized plate to a fresh plate every 30--60 days. This prevents
senescence (age-related decline in vigor) and provides an opportunity to
inspect for contamination. Each transfer is called a "passage" and the total
number of passages should be tracked.

## Storage

Cultures on agar slants or plates can be stored at 2--4°C for 3--6 months
with Parafilm to prevent desiccation. For long-term archival storage,
cultures can be preserved in sterile distilled water (viable for 5+ years)
or cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen at -196°C (indefinite viability). These
methods eliminate genetic drift from repeated subculture.
