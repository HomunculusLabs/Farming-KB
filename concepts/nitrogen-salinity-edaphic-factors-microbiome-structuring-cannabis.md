## Soil Type Variation in the Study

The two experiments examined soils with notably different edaphic profiles:

- **Mo-Bio (MB) soil**: Higher nitrogen (1.30–1.51%), higher salinity (5.12–7.44), lower organic carbon (3.02–5.00%), and lower water content (0.101–0.178).
- **Orange County (OC) soil**: Lower nitrogen (0.53%), much lower salinity (1.73), dramatically higher organic carbon (20.0%), and higher water content (0.371).

These differences produced significantly distinct microbial communities (ADONIS R² = 0.32, p = 0.001 for unweighted; R² = 0.21, p = 0.001 for weighted). Despite the extreme differences, particularly in organic carbon, nitrogen remained the strongest individual correlate with community structure across both soil types when pooled.

## Interaction with the Two-Tier Selection Model

Within the two-tier selection framework for root microbiome assembly, edaphic factors operate primarily at the first tier:

1. **Tier 1 (Soil → Rhizosphere)**: Soil properties (especially nitrogen and salinity) determine the composition of the bulk soil microbiota. When Cannabis roots begin secreting exudates, the available microbial pool from which rhizosphere communities are drawn is already heavily shaped by these edaphic variables. This explains why soil type accounted for 690 significant OTU differences (weighted analysis) compared to 71 for strain and 51 for sample type.

2. **Tier 2 (Rhizosphere → Endorhiza)**: Host genotype then fine-tunes community structure within the endorhiza. However, the available taxa for this second selection step are constrained by what the soil provided at tier 1. Endorhiza communities in different soil types share fewer OTUs (mean = 2,162) than endorhiza communities paired with their own soil (mean = 2,934, p = 1.209e-15).

## Edaphic Factors vs. Strain in OTU Abundance

An important distinction emerged from the statistical analyses: soil type showed the strongest influence over both OTU presence/absence (657 significant OTUs, unweighted) and OTU abundance (690 significant OTUs, weighted). Strain, by contrast, showed zero significant unweighted OTU differences but 71 significant weighted differences. This means that Cannabis cultivar primarily affects the relative abundance of microbes already present rather than determining which taxa are present at all. Nitrogen and salinity, as the dominant edaphic factors, thus control the foundational species pool from which cultivar-specific selection occurs.

## Practical Implications for Cannabis Cultivation

Understanding that nitrogen and salinity are the dominant edaphic drivers has actionable implications for both outdoor and indoor Cannabis cultivation:

- **Soil amendment strategies**: Targeted nitrogen supplementation can shift rhizosphere community composition toward beneficial Proteobacteria. However, excessive nitrogen may suppress Acidobacteria and other oligotrophs that contribute to disease suppression.
- **Water quality management**: Irrigation water salinity directly influences microbiome structure. In hydroponic or container-based Cannabis cultivation, monitoring electrical conductivity (EC) serves as a proxy for salinity management of the root microbiome.
- **Strain-specific soil pairing**: Because the same Cannabis strain grown in different soils develops significantly different endorhiza communities, cultivators could potentially optimize soil nitrogen and salinity profiles for specific cultivars to promote beneficial microbial associations.
- **Living soil approaches**: Organic cultivation methods that build soil nitrogen through composting and cover cropping may support more diverse microbial communities than synthetic nitrogen inputs, potentially improving plant resilience and terpene profiles.
- **Terroir considerations**: The concept of microbial terroir (borrowed from wine science) is directly supported by the dominance of edaphic factors. Cannabis grown in the same soil type will tend to share microbial community features regardless of cultivar.

## Comparison with Other Plant Systems

The dominance of nitrogen as a structuring factor aligns with findings from other crops. In maize rhizosphere studies, nitrogen form (ammonium vs. nitrate) has been shown to dramatically shift community composition, favouring different Proteobacterial taxa. In Arabidopsis, nitrogen availability modulates the balance between disease-suppressive and disease-conducive rhizosphere communities. The Cannabis findings extend this pattern and add salinity as a co-dominant factor, which may be particularly relevant for Cannabis cultivation in arid regions where saline irrigation is common.

In wine grape microbiome research, nitrogen and salinity similarly emerged as key drivers of both fungal and bacterial community composition on berries and in vineyard soils. The parallel between Cannabis and Vitis microbiome structuring by these factors reinforces the broader ecological principle that nitrogen availability and osmotic conditions are universal constraints on plant-associated microbial communities across diverse crop species.

## Temporal Dynamics of Edaphic Influence

The study design (sampling at a single time point per experiment) cannot capture how edaphic influence on the microbiome changes across the Cannabis growth cycle. Nitrogen demand varies dramatically between vegetative growth and flowering stages, and root exudate profiles shift accordingly. Salinity effects may also intensify during drought periods or be moderated by rainfall. A time-series study tracking edaphic variables and microbiome composition from seedling through harvest would clarify whether nitrogen and salinity maintain their dominance throughout the plant's life cycle or whether other factors become more influential at specific developmental stages.

## Methodological Considerations

The Mantel test approach used to correlate edaphic factors with community beta-diversity captures overall correlation strength but may conflate direct and indirect effects. For example, nitrogen's strong correlation could partly reflect its relationship with organic carbon (higher carbon often supports higher microbial nitrogen cycling). Multivariate approaches such as structural equation modeling or variation partitioning could disentangle these intercorrelated effects in future studies. Additionally, the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene used for sequencing provides genus-level resolution at best; species-level or strain-level differences in nitrogen metabolism may be masked by this taxonomic resolution.

## Limitations and Future Directions

The study examined only sandy loam soils from Southern California, limiting generalizability to other soil textures (clay, silt, peat). The five edaphic factors tested represent a subset of potentially relevant variables; additional factors such as phosphorus, potassium, micronutrient availability, and [[cation-exchange-capacity]] were not assessed. Future work should explore dose-response relationships between nitrogen/salinity gradients and specific endorhiza taxa to enable precision microbiome management in Cannabis agriculture.
