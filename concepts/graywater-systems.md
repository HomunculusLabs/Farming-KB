# Graywater Systems

Graywater is wastewater from household sources other than toilets (sinks, showers, bathtubs, washing machines). A graywater system captures, treats, and reuses this water for landscape irrigation, reducing municipal water consumption by 30-50% and returning nutrients to the soil. Graywater reuse is a key component of [[permaculture-design]] water management and [[homestead-water-systems]], closing the loop between household water use and landscape productivity.

## Graywater vs. Blackwater

Understanding the distinction is critical:

- **Graywater**: Wastewater from showers, bathtubs, bathroom sinks, laundry machines, and kitchen sinks (in some jurisdictions). Contains soap, hair, skin cells, food particles, and pathogens but is relatively low in nitrogen compared to blackwater.
- **Blackwater**: Toilet wastewater. Contains high levels of nitrogen, pathogens, and organic matter. Requires extensive treatment. See [[humanure-composting]] for safe handling of toilet waste.
- **Never mix**: Graywater systems must be completely separate from blackwater plumbing. Cross-contamination creates serious health risks.

## Legal Status

Graywater regulations vary enormously by jurisdiction:

- **Some states** (California, Arizona, Texas, New Mexico) have permissive graywater codes that allow simple residential systems without permits under certain conditions
- **Other states** require permits, professional installation, and specific design standards
- **Some municipalities** prohibit graywater reuse entirely regardless of state law
- **Rural properties** with no municipal sewer connection often have the most flexibility

Before designing a system, check [[homesteading-legal-considerations]] and consult your local health department or building department.

## System Types

### Branched Drain System

The simplest and most reliable graywater system. Household graywater plumbing is branched so each fixture drains separately to its own mulch basin or irrigation zone. Graywater flows through standard 1.5-2 inch drain pipe, splits at a junction using reducing fittings, and each outlet leads to a mulch basin where water percolates through wood chip mulch into the soil.

**Advantages**: No pumps, no filters, no moving parts — gravity-fed. Extremely reliable. Inexpensive (mostly standard plumbing parts). Each fixture has its own irrigation zone.

**Disadvantages**: Requires sufficient slope from house to landscape (minimum 2% grade). Requires 3-way valves at each fixture to divert to sewer during wet weather.

### Laundry-to-Landscape System

The most common and accessible DIY graywater system. Washing machine discharge hose is connected to a 1-inch HDPE pipe that runs to the landscape through a hole in the foundation wall, branching to multiple mulch basins. Each wash cycle sends 15-40 gallons to the landscape. A 3-way valve allows switching between landscape and sewer discharge.

**Advantages**: Simplest to install (can be done in a weekend). Washing machine pump provides pressure. Very low cost ($50-150). Legal without permit in many jurisdictions.

**Disadvantages**: Only handles laundry water. Mulch basins must handle surge volume of each wash cycle.

### Pumped and Surge Tank Systems

For properties where landscape is uphill from plumbing fixtures or where gravity flow is not possible:

- **Pumped system**: All fixture graywater drains to a collection tank (30-50 gallons). A sump pump activates when full and distributes water to irrigation zones through pressure lines. Works regardless of slope but requires electricity (integrate with [[homestead-renewable-energy]]) and more maintenance.
- **Surge tank system**: Intermediate approach — graywater flows by gravity to a covered tank that temporarily stores and slowly releases water to irrigation via gravity outlet. Handles surge volumes without flooding. Requires excavation and periodic tank cleaning.

## Soap and Product Considerations

The products you use directly affect graywater quality and soil health:

### Safe for Graywater

- Biodegradable, phosphate-free, low-sodium liquid detergents
- Natural soaps (Dr. Bronner's, homemade soap from [[candle-making]])
- Borax-free detergents (borax can build up in soil to toxic levels)
- Plant-based cleaning products

### Avoid in Graywater Systems

- **Bleach and chlorine compounds**: Kill soil biology, including beneficial microbes in the [[soil-food-web]]
- **Boron/borax**: Accumulates in soil to toxic levels
- **Fabric softeners**: Contain chemicals toxic to plants
- **Harsh drain cleaners**: Kill soil biology
- **Paint, solvents, automotive fluids**: Never discharge to graywater system
- **Water softener discharge**: Very high sodium content damages soil structure

### Salt Accumulation

The biggest long-term concern with graywater irrigation is salt accumulation:
- Many laundry detergents contain significant sodium
- Sodium builds up in soil over time, destroying soil structure
- Choose low-sodium or sodium-free detergents
- Test soil annually for sodium levels via [[soil-testing]]
- Alternate graywater irrigation with fresh water to flush salts

## Mulch Basins

The mulch basin is the heart of most graywater systems — the point where graywater meets soil:

- **Size**: 2-4 feet wide by 6-12 inches deep, sized to hold the surge volume from one fixture use
- **Fill**: Coarse wood chips (not bark mulch) to a depth of 6-12 inches
- **Plants**: Fruit trees, berries, ornamentals, and perennials planted around the basin perimeter
- **Function**: Mulch layer provides biofilter removing pathogens, hair, and soap residues. Fungi and bacteria break down organic matter. Water percolates into soil below.
- **Maintenance**: Top off mulch annually (connect to [[composting-systems]] for mulch supply). Flush distribution pipes if flow slows. Ensure drainage within 1-2 hours. Replace mulch every 2-3 years.

## Plant Selection

### Excellent Graywater Plants

- **Fruit trees**: Apple, pear, plum, cherry, fig, pomegranate, citrus
- **Berries**: Blackberry, raspberry, blueberry, elderberry
- **Ornamentals**: Rose, butterfly bush, canna, willow, bamboo (contained)
- **Perennials**: Comfrey, artichoke, asparagus, banana (frost-free zones)

### Plants to Avoid Near Graywater

- Root vegetables (carrots, potatoes, onions) — potential pathogen contact with edible root
- Leafy greens eaten raw (lettuce, spinach) — pathogen risk
- Acid-loving plants (blueberries, rhododendrons) — graywater tends toward alkaline
- Plants sensitive to sodium or boron

Graywater from the house can be the primary irrigation source for a [[food-forest-design]]: branched drain outlets at fruit tree locations, mulch basins under tree canopies, laundry-to-landscape irrigating berry bushes and understory plants.

## Maintenance

- **Monthly**: Check mulch basin levels; add mulch if needed; inspect for ponding
- **Quarterly**: Flush distribution lines; check 3-way valves; inspect for root intrusion
- **Annually**: Test soil for sodium and boron; top-dress mulch basins; clean surge tanks

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Odor from mulch basin | Anaerobic conditions; insufficient drainage | Add more mulch; improve drainage; ensure basin drains in 1-2 hours |
| Slow draining | Clogged outlet pipe; compacted mulch | Flush pipe; replace mulch; clear roots |
| Plant stress or death | Salt or boron accumulation; wrong plant choice | Test soil; switch detergents; replace with tolerant species |
| Graywater surfacing | Saturated soil; overloaded basin | Reduce volume; improve drainage; add sand/gravel under mulch |
| Mosquitoes | Standing water in basins | Ensure drainage within 48 hours; add BTI dunks |
| Pipe clogging | Hair, lint, soap scum buildup | Install lint filter; flush pipes regularly |
