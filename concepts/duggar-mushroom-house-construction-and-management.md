# Duggar Mushroom House Construction and Management

Duggar provided detailed guidance on structures for mushroom growing,
covering the full range from natural caves to purpose-built commercial
houses, with emphasis on American conditions.

## The Fundamental Requirements

The main environmental factors to regulate are temperature and
moisture. Any situation where these can be controlled to meet mushroom
requirements should be satisfactory. Mushrooms demand uniform
moderately low temperature (54-56 F optimum), moist atmosphere (60-75
percent relative humidity), and protection from direct drafts and
sudden fluctuations.

## French Cave Systems

The French industry was built on the subterranean limestone quarries
around Paris. The suburban caves ranged from a few feet to 50 feet
beneath the surface, 5-25 feet wide (exceptionally 50), and 5-20 or
more feet high. These cave systems were ventilated by chimneys or
chutes, with change of air facilitated by small charcoal fires beneath
the air chutes. Access was through open quarry faces or through holes
only a few feet in diameter, with ladders and windlasses for lifting
materials.

## American Mushroom Houses

In the United States, production was confined to special houses,
caves, and cellars. Near Philadelphia, Chicago, Boston, and other
centers, mushrooms were grown wholly in special mushroom houses,
which furnished the major part of the American market product.

### Construction Principles

For a permanent investment:

- **Drainage**: Perfect drainage was of first importance, complicated
  by the need for convenient heating and uniform temperature.
- **Excavation**: Houses were often constructed over an excavation
  several feet below natural surface level.
- **Height**: Houses high enough to accommodate beds in tiers of
  two to five gave greatest economy.
- **Spacing**: About 2.5 feet of free space between tier beds was
  necessary (beds being 8-10 inches deep), requiring somewhat more
  than 3 feet total height per bed.
- **Walls**: Better class houses used double walls packed with fresh
  shavings. More expensive houses used hollow tile or concrete.
  Hollow tile had much to recommend it due to moisture resistance.
- **Roof**: A sufficiently sloping roof with air space corrected the
  "sweating" problem from moisture-laden air contacting cold walls.
- **Ventilation**: Ventilators placed in the roof, avoiding direct
  drafts.
- **Bed supports**: Uprights for four or five tiers should be 2x6
  inch scantling placed about 4 feet apart. Commercial growers
  increasingly used large gas piping for permanency and sanitary
  advantages.
- **Bed dimensions**: Wall beds should seldom exceed 3 feet across;
  beds approachable from either side could be 6 feet. Aisles of 3
  feet were usually sufficient.

## Alternative Structures

### Abandoned Beer Cellars
Many antiquated beer storage cellars of limited capacity were in use
in various cities.

### Natural Caves
Limestone caves in Missouri had been used successfully. Natural caves
occurred in Kentucky, Indiana, Ohio, Virginia, and Arkansas. Dangers
included flooding, seepage, accumulation of spent bedding material,
and increased mushroom enemies over time.

### Coal Mines
Abandoned coal mines were generally unsuitable as left, but hill
coal mines in some sections might repay investigation.

### Temporary Structures
For temporary use, a simple boarded shed or unused barn with a heater
could suffice. Winter was the important season for mushroom work.

## Open Air Culture

In Southern England, modified hot beds or cold frames were employed
without glass, covered with sheet iron or boards and straw. Fresh
manure under the compost could force early spawn growth. Duggar
considered this not truly open air culture. For most regions, the
daily temperature variation, drying winds, and difficulty regulating
moisture made open air culture unpromising, though sections of
California with mean monthly temperatures of 50-60 F might prove
suitable.

## Temperature Management

The optimum air temperature was 54-56 F, with a workable range of
50-65 F. Growing continuously above 60 F or below 50 F was not
commercially successful. High temperature (72 F for 24 hours) was
fatal to pin heads, but this was due to stimulation of competing
organisms rather than direct thermal injury. Pure cultures grew well
up to 85 F, proving the problem was biological. Freezing was
tolerable for considerable periods; beds spawned in November, frozen
through winter, bore abundantly in March with unusually large
mushrooms.

## Light

Light had no directly injurious effect on quality or productiveness.
The supposed harm of light under glass was entirely a matter of
associated temperature increase. Mushrooms were grown successfully
in greenhouses both under and on benches when temperature and
moisture were controlled.

## Between Crops

After beds ceased production, all old compost, soil, and spent
material had to be removed. Wooden supports, boards, walls, and
floors required thorough cleaning -- white-washing with stone lime
or spraying with copper sulphate solution. Walls could be fumigated
with hydrocyanic acid or sulphur fumes. No old compost should be
reused, as it would increase disease and insect injury.
