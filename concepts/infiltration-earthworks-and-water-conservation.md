# Infiltration Earthworks and Water Conservation

Earthworks represent one of the most cost-effective and long-lasting
strategies for conserving water in agricultural and permaculture
landscapes. By shaping the land to capture, slow, and infiltrate
surface water, earthworks convert runoff into deep soil moisture
reserves, recharge groundwater, and create distributed water
availability across a property. This page covers the principal
earthwork strategies for infiltration and [[permaculture-design]].

## Why Infiltration Matters

Soil conditioning or "ripping," when followed by [[infiltration-earthworks-water-storage|storage]] directly
increases throughflow, baseflow, and water available to plants.

As water cycles more effectively via evapotranspiration and
rainfall, the total water budget of a landscape improves. Soil
treatments should precede [[fukuoka-textdoc-shelterbelt-design-environmental-tree-planting]] over almost every area that
has been used by contemporary agriculture, particularly barren areas
used for constant cropping in dryland regions.

## Primary Infiltration Strategies

### Pitting

Pitting involves creating small depressions across the landscape to
capture and hold surface runoff, allowing it time to infiltrate into
the soil. Pits are especially effective on gently sloping land and
in areas where overland flow is rapid but the soil has reasonable
infiltration capacity once water is held in place. They are
inexpensive, can be created with hand tools or machinery, and work
well in combination with tree planting.

### Swales

Swales are level or near-level channels excavated on contour across
a slope. They capture runoff, slow its movement, and allow it to
infiltrate along their entire length. Swales are one of the most
important [[permaculture-earthworks]], serving simultaneously as
infiltration devices, water distribution systems, and tree-planting
sites. The berm on the downslope side of a swale is an ideal location
for productive tree crops, which benefit from the concentrated
moisture.

### Water Spreading

Water spreading involves diverting concentrated flow from drainage
lines, roads, or paved surfaces across broad areas of land. This can
be achieved through [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]], spreader channels, or simple
earth banks that release water gradually over a wide area. Water
spreading is particularly effective for recharging sandy soils,
dune fields, and degraded pastures where concentrated flow would
otherwise cause erosion.

## Supporting Infiltration Features

### Diversion Drains

[[mollison-designers-diversion-drains-and-interceptor-banks]] their associated valves, slides, cross-walls,
intakes, and irrigation systems enable effective water harvesting,
dependable [[earthworks-water-conservation-and-storage|storage]], and fast emergency use. They can recharge sand
basins and swales from otherwise wasted overland flow, and can damp
down wildfire risk by keeping surrounding vegetation and soils moist.
Drains are typically cut at gradients of 1:250 to 1:500, ensuring
slow, non-erosive flow.

### Soakage Beds and Absorption Fields

Water can be directed into sand basins, swamps, soakage beds, and
earth-bermed fields to ensure resident water reserves for crops and
trees. These systems provide longer-term storages for use in dry
seasons and are especially valuable in areas where surface storage
evaporation is high.

## Storage Types

### Pond and Farm Dam Storages

Wherever precipitation exceeds the demands of transpiration and
evaporation, small dams, wetlands, and swamps can proliferate. In
humid areas, these water storages can occupy up to 20% of the
landscape with great benefit, providing fish, aquatic plants,
waterfowl, and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] while moderating drought and flood.
Dams and ponds are also potential aquaculture sites, and the
production of diverse plant and fish or waterfowl protein should be
considered during their construction.

Many Australian farms have become "drought-proof" through sensible
investment in Keyline or similar [[water-conservation]] systems. The
Keyline system, developed by P.A. Yeomans, is the pioneering modern
text on [[holzer-permaculture-landscape-design]] for water conservation and gravity-fed
irrigation.

### Biological Storages

Great forests and biological water storages in the form of fruits
and nuts (such as coconuts) are the basis for the proliferation of
life where no "free" water otherwise exists. On atolls and arid
islands of free-draining sands, biological reserves are the main
water reserves. Plants such as cactus, palms, and agaves have
specific tissues or organs to store water, and browsers, insects,
and fungi draw on these reserves year-round.

### Tank Storages

Water captured off roof areas, roads, and other paved surfaces can
be used for drinking, shower, or garden water. Roof water is least
polluted and most easily treated, while absorption beds are
sufficient for muddy or polluted runoff from roads and parking areas.

## Two Basic Strategies

There are two fundamental strategies for water conservation in runoff
areas:

1. **Diversion of surface water to impoundments** (dams, tanks) for
   later use — this creates visible, accessible storages but is
   subject to evaporation losses.
2. **Storage of water in soils** — this is the largest potential
   storage in any landscape, invisible but vast, and largely
   immune to evaporation losses.

Both strategies result in groundwater recharge and increased
baseflow. The most effective designs combine both approaches, using
earthworks to fill soil profiles while also creating modest surface
storages for immediate access.

## Key Principles

1. Soil is the largest water storage system in any landscape.
2. Earthworks convert destructive runoff into productive infiltration.
3. Swales on contour are the single most effective broad-scale
   infiltration tool.
4. Water spreading maximises the benefit of limited runoff events.
5. Combining earthworks with tree planting multiplies water storage
   capacity.
6. Diversion drains at gentle gradients move water without erosion.
