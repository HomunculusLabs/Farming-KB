## Mechanisms of Fungal Invasion

Fungal invasions occur through multiple pathways including international trade
in live plants and timber, movement of soil and plant material, and natural
dispersal across newly connected landscapes. The rapid evolution of introduced
plant pathogens by genetic change, induced by their new environmental
conditions, is of particular concern for devising effective control methods.

The impact of exotic fungal pathogens tends to be most severe when introduced
into regions where host plants have not co-evolved with the pathogen. In native
ecosystems where pathogens and hosts have evolved together, dramatic effects are
rarely observed. Current interactions represent the outcome of long-term
coevolution.

## Chestnut Blight: Cryphonectria parasitica

The chestnut blight fungus *Cryphonectria parasitica*, probably imported from
China, caused one of the most dramatic ecological transformations in North
American forest history. The blight spread through American chestnut (*Castanea
dentata*) populations at approximately 37 km per year, virtually eliminating
chestnut as a dominant canopy tree. The species now persists primarily as an
understory shrub, sprouting from root collars but rarely reaching reproductive
maturity before reinfection.

The loss had cascading ecological consequences. Chestnut was a major source of
mast (nuts) for wildlife, and its elimination affected populations of birds,
mammals, and insects. The replacement of chestnut by other tree species altered
forest composition, canopy structure, and leaf litter quality.

A notable feature is hypovirulence, caused by mycoviruses that infect *C.
parasitica* and reduce its pathogenicity. These fungal viruses have been
investigated as [[dighton-fungi-climate-change-feedback]] models predict increasing rainfall
would increase disease spread, though cold winters currently limit northward
expansion. Warming trends may gradually expand the pathogen's range.

## Phytophthora ramorum and Sudden Oak Death

*Phytophthora ramorum* emerged as a major forest pathogen in the late 1990s,
causing sudden oak death in coastal California and Oregon. The broad host range
and multiple modes of dispersal make *P. ramorum* particularly difficult to
manage.
