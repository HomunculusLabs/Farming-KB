The global movement of fungal pathogens has produced some of the most devastating biological invasions in recorded history.

Introduced fungal pathogens can rapidly transform forest ecosystems, eliminate dominant tree species, and cascade through food webs with consequences for biodiversity, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem structure.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Invasion

Fungal invasions occur through multiple pathways including international trade in live plants and timber, movement of soil and plant material, and natural dispersal across newly connected landscapes.

The rapid evolution of introduced plant pathogens by genetic change, induced by their new environmental conditions, is of particular concern for devising effective control methods.

The impact of exotic fungal pathogens tends to be most severe when introduced into regions where host plants have not co-evolved with the pathogen.

In native ecosystems where pathogens and hosts have evolved together, dramatic effects of fungal diseases on plant communities are rarely observed.

The extreme interactions may have already been played out during the development of plant communities, and current interactions represent the outcome of long-term coevolution.

## Chestnut Blight: Cryphonectria parasitica

The chestnut blight fungus Cryphonectria parasitica, probably imported from China, caused one of the most dramatic ecological transformations in North American forest history.

The blight rapidly spread through American chestnut (Castanea dentata) populations at approximately 37 km per year, virtually eliminating chestnut as a dominant canopy tree.

The species now persists primarily as an understory shrub, sprouting from root collars of killed trees but rarely reaching reproductive maturity before reinfection.

The loss of American chestnut had cascading ecological consequences.

Chestnut was a major source of mast (nuts) for wildlife, and its elimination affected populations of birds, mammals, and insects that depended on this food resource.

The replacement of chestnut by other tree species altered forest composition, canopy structure, and the quality of leaf litter entering the decomposer system.

A notable feature of the chestnut blight story is the role of hypovirulence, caused by mycoviruses that infect C. parasitica and reduce its pathogenicity.

These fungal viruses have been investigated as biological control agents, with some success in European chestnut populations but limited effectiveness in North America.

## Dutch Elm Disease: Ophiostoma ulmi and O. novo-ulmi

The fungus Ophiostoma ulmi caused extensive decline in elm populations across Europe and North America.

Like chestnut blight, Dutch elm disease was introduced from another region and encountered elm species with limited resistance.

The disease is transmitted by bark beetles that carry fungal spores from infected to healthy trees, creating a vector-driven epidemic.

Resistance of elm trees to the pathogen has been observed, arising through multiple mechanisms.

Some resistance is due to genetic variation in host plants producing actual resistance, but some apparent resistance is attributable to the presence of fungal pathogenic mycoviruses that reduce the effectiveness of the pathogen.

The interaction between host genetics and viral hypovirulence in the pathogen creates a complex evolutionary dynamic.

## Phytophthora cinnamomi and Oak Decline

The oomycete pathogen Phytophthora cinnamomi has been responsible for significant decline in evergreen oaks (Quercus suber and Q. ilex) across Mediterranean regions of Spain, Portugal, Tunisia, and Morocco.

This pathogen spreads through soil by virtue of motile spores that require wet or waterlogged soil for optimum dispersal.

Climate change models predict increasing rainfall in these regions, which would potentially increase the rate of disease spread.

Brasier (1996) suggests that the severity of cold winters in central and northern Europe would limit the northward spread of Phytophthora cinnamomi, but warming trends may gradually expand the pathogen's range.

The loss of oak species in Mediterranean ecosystems alters community structure, affects associated mycorrhizal communities, and reduces the input of characteristic leaf litter into soil food webs.

## Phytophthora ramorum and Sudden Oak Death

Phytophthora ramorum emerged as a major forest pathogen in the late 1990s, causing sudden oak death in coastal California and Oregon.

This pathogen affects numerous plant species and has spread to Europe through the nursery plant trade.

The broad host range and multiple modes of dispersal make P. ramorum particularly difficult to manage.

## Invasive Plants, Fungal Pathogens, and Community Change

The interactions between invasive plants, their associated fungal communities, and native ecosystems can be complex.

Walker et al. (1999) demonstrated that leaf litter of Rhododendron maximum, an invasive weed of southern Appalachian forests, dramatically affected mycorrhizal development of native hemlock.

Hemlock seedlings planted under rhododendron litter had one-third the intensity of ectomycorrhizal colonization, one-fourth the root ramification, and half the biomass compared to trees planted under hemlock litter.

The pathogen Phellinus weirii reduced development of Douglas fir in areas of pathogen abundance.

These infection centers were colonized more successfully by western hemlock, changing both species composition and canopy architecture of the forest.

Similarly, Discula destructans (dogwood anthracnose) selectively reduced dogwood populations in the Cumberland Plateau, shifting forest community dominance toward bird-dispersed species and reducing calcium cycling through the ecosystem.

## Pathogen-Facilitated Plant Invasions

The fungal pathogen flora of invasive plants changes significantly between native and exotic ranges.

Studies of tropical weed species show minimal overlap of fungal pathogen species between native and exotic ranges, typically only 4-7 species shared out of 15-33 total.

This reduced pathogen pressure in the exotic range is thought to contribute to the competitive success of invasive plants, a phenomenon known as the enemy release hypothesis.

This reduced pathogen load also suggests scope for selecting effective biocontrol pathogens from the plant's exotic range, where novel pathogen associations may exist that could be exploited for weed management.
