# Permaculture Ethics and Design Principles

## Overview

The ethical foundation and design principles of permaculture form the philosophical backbone of the entire design system. [[permaculture-design]]. They emerge from the observation of natural systems and the recognition that human survival depends on cooperation with, rather than domination over, natural processes.

## The Prime Directive of Permaculture

> The only ethical decision is to take responsibility for our own existence and that of our children.

This directive places the onus of survival squarely on individual and community action. It rejects dependence on external systems and insists that people design their own means of provision.

## The Three Ethics

### 1. Care of the Earth

Provision for all life systems to continue and increase. This means actively supporting the health, diversity, and productivity of all living systems — soil, water, plants, animals, and the ecological processes that sustain them. It is not passive preservation but active regeneration.

### 2. Care of People

Provision for people to access those resources necessary to their existence. This ethic ensures that the design system serves human needs — food, shelter, energy, community, and meaningful work — without compromising the first ethic.

### 3. Setting Limits to Population and Consumption

By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles. This is the least comfortable but most necessary ethic, recognizing that unchecked consumption and population growth undermine both earth care and people care.

## The Principle of Cooperation

> Cooperation, not competition, is the very basis of future survival and of existing life systems.

This principle directly challenges the dominant economic paradigm of competition. In nature, symbiotic relationships — [[permaculture-ethics]]:

1. **Nothing in nature grows forever.** There is a constant cycle of decay and rebirth. Systems that attempt perpetual growth are unsustainable.

2. **Continuation of life depends on the maintenance of global biogeochemical cycles** — carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, and phosphorus must continue to cycle.

3. **Extinction probability is greatest at very high or very low densities.** Both crowding and scarcity push populations toward extinction thresholds.

4. **Survival and reproduction depend primarily on one or two key factors** in the complex web of relationships between organism and environment.

5. **Our ability to change the face of the earth increases faster than our ability to foresee the consequences.** This principle demands precaution in all design interventions.

6. **Living organisms are not only means but ends.** They have intrinsic worth beyond their instrumental value to humans and other organisms.

## Mollisonian Permaculture Principles

Mollison distilled his approach into five core design principles:

### 1. Work With Nature

Work with nature, rather than against the natural elements, forces, pressures, processes, agencies, and evolutions, so that we assist rather than impede natural developments. This is the fundamental orientation of permaculture — alignment rather than resistance.

### 2. The Problem Is the Solution

> Everything works both ways. It is only how we see things that makes them advantageous or not.

A corollary: everything is a positive resource; it is just up to us to work out how we may use it as such. If the wind blows cold, use both its strength and its coolness to advantage. Pests indicate imbalances that can be corrected. Weeds reveal soil conditions.

### 3. Least Change for Greatest Effect

Make the least change for the greatest possible effect. This principle of efficiency directs the designer to find leverage points — small interventions that produce large, cascading benefits. It discourages brute-force solutions in favor of elegant, minimal interventions.

### 4. Unlimited Yield

> The yield of a system is theoretically unlimited. The only limit on the number of uses of a resource possible within a system is in the limit of the information and the imagination of the designer.

Yield is not a fixed quantity. It is limited only by the designer's comprehension and creativity. Every element can perform multiple functions; every function can be supported by multiple elements.

### 5. Everything Gardens

Everything gardens, or has an effect on its environment. Chickens till and fertilize soil. Trees modify microclimate. Water shapes landscapes. No element in a system is passive — all modify their surroundings, and the designer must account for these effects.

## Categories of Resources

Mollison classified resources into five categories:

1. **Those which increase by modest use** — information, some pasture, human skills.
2. **Those unaffected by use** — sunlight, wind, landscape views.
3. **Those which disappear or degrade if not used** — annual crops, seasonal water flows, skills that atrophy.
4. **Those reduced by use** — soil minerals, fossil fuels, old-growth timber.
5. **Those which pollute or destroy other resources if used** — persistent poisons, radioactives, large-scale concrete.

## Key Definitions

### System Yield

> System yield is the sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by the design. Energy is in surplus once the system itself has available all its needs for growth, reproduction, and maintenance.

### The Role of Life in Yield

Living things, including people, are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources on this planet and to produce a yield. The sum and capacity of life forms decide total system yield and surplus.

### Limits to Yield

> Yield is not a fixed sum in any design system. It is the measure of the comprehension, understanding, and ability of the designers and managers of that design.

## Principle of Disorder

> Any system or organism can accept only that quantity of a resource which can be used productively. Any resource input beyond that point throws the system or organism into disorder; oversupply of a resource is a form of chronic pollution.

This principle explains why fertilizer runoff, excessive irrigation, and overfeeding all produce negative outcomes. More is not better — the right amount is better.

Neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness signify an energy-maintained disorder in natural systems. Natural systems are messily productive; manicured systems are energetically expensive.

## Practical Design Considerations

- Systems should last as long as possible with the least maintenance.
- Systems fueled by the sun should produce not only their own needs but also the needs of the people creating or controlling them.
- Energy invested in construction should be repaid through the lifetime energy savings of the system.
- A policy of responsible authority: the role of beneficial authority is to return function and responsibility to life and to people. If successful, no further authority is needed.
