# Diversion Drains and Interceptor Banks in Permaculture Design

Diversion drains and interceptor banks are critical earthwork
features in permaculture water management. While [[mollison-pdc-swale-design-water-harvesting]]
store water in place, diversion drains actively transport water
from one location to another, and interceptor banks prevent
undesirable water movement across slopes. Together with dams,
swales, and [[mollison-pdc-keypoint-keyline-humid]] systems,
they form the complete toolkit for landscape water management.

## Diversion Drains: Purpose and Function

Diversion drains are gently sloping channels used to lead
water away from valleys and streams and into storages and
irrigation systems, or into [[faires-swales-and-water-conservation]]
or swales for absorption. They differ fundamentally from
swales in that they are built to flow after rain, carrying
overland flow or water from feeder streams to useful
destinations.

Diversion drains are the normal and essential connectors
of dam series built on the keyline systems, so that the
overflow of one dam enters the feeder channel of the next.
Even without a stream intake, diversion drains will gather
water from overland flow in as little as 1-1.5 cm (1/2 inch)
of rain over 24 hours. This means isolated dams are normally
fitted with diversion drains even in quite dry country.

## Construction and Design Parameters

Such diversion systems need careful planning and survey,
with drain bases sloping as little as 1:6000 in fine sands.
Dam crests and irrigation drains need equally careful
placement. The key principles include:

- Slope should be minimal but sufficient to maintain flow
  without erosion
- Drain bases must be stable and well-compacted where
  necessary
- Integration with dam spillways and irrigation layouts
  is essential
- In the Canary Islands, rock run-off is gathered from
  solid rock faces and led to underground cave storages
  or large open tanks

## Spill Gates and Flow Control

Simple sliding gates across or in the downhill banks of
diversion drains allow controlled flow, controlled
irrigation, and by-passing of dams during flood events.
Two main types are used:

- FIXED gates: concrete slide holders and aprons for
  permanent installations
- MOVEABLE "flags": plastic sheets weighted with a chain
  sewn into the foot, supported across the drain by a
  light pipe sleeved into the top

Slide gates can open on to ridge lines, where water then
spreads downhill. Plastic flags can be placed and removed
at any point along a drain. This enables one or two people
to water 200-240 ha (400-600 acres) in a morning. The
system also serves as an effective [[mollison-fire-control-design]] system in forested areas.

For sophisticated wildfire or irrigation control, both slide
gates and dam-base gate valves can be remotely operated by
radio signal and storage batteries or buried electrical
conduits, powering small motors or hydraulic slides. A
complete wildfire control can be achieved by dams and
sheet irrigation, using infrared sensors and automatic
spill gates.

## Mini-Swales in Diversion Drains

If low earth-walls are raised across the flow channels of
larger diversion drains, these then act as a series of
mini-swales for specific tree sites, while surplus water
flows on to storages. This integration of storage and
conveyance is a hallmark of permaculture water design,
where every element serves multiple functions.

Diversion drains can also be led to broad level swales in
drylands, or made of simple concrete or stone walls across
solid rock faces. This flexibility allows adaptation to
any landscape condition, from steep volcanic slopes to
flat alluvial plains.

## Interceptor Drains and Banks

Interceptor drains act in the opposite sense to diversion
drains. These earthworks are specifically designed (by
Harry Whittington of West Australia) to prevent overland
waterflow and waterlogging, which has the effect of
collapsing the dryland valley or downslope soils of a
desert soil catena.

Unlike diversion drains and swales, interceptor bank
construction always involves the ramming (by bulldozer
blade) of subsoil layers hard against the downhill bank.
This effectively prevents or impedes water seepage through
the downhill wall. Key specifications include:

- Depth: always 1.5-2.5 m (5-8 feet)
- Spacing: carefully calculated based on slope and
  soil conditions
- Effect: they totally intercept overland flow and
  direct it to streams or valley run-offs
- Salt management: they effectively stop not only
  overland flow but also salt water seepages in
  shallow sand seams

In effect, interceptor banks isolate large blocks of soil
from waterlogging and salt seepage, enabling the
rehabilitation of previously desertified soils through
subsequent [[mollison-designers-pioneer-species-for-exhausted-tropical-soils]].

Both diversion drains and interceptor banks work within the
broader framework of landscape water management that
