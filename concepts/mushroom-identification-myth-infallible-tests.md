### The Snail and Insect Test

The claim that poisonous mushrooms are avoided by insects, worms, and snails was repeatedly cited. Palmer noted he had never seen a snail on a mushroom, and that slugs were often cut out of edible specimens without affecting edibility. Pigs ate [[amanita]] "remains the purest white under all conditions." This myth causes gatherers to discard safe, colorful fungi while feeling safe with the most dangerous species.

## The "Poisonous Under Unknown Conditions" Theory

Encyclopedias and some botanical texts repeated the claim that the common mushroom might become poisonous under "certain unknown conditions," and that this was "so esteemed in Italy." Palmer called both claims "utter falsehoods." This theory had serious consequences: it absolved victims of their own failure to identify specimens, and it discouraged people from learning safe varieties by implying that no identification was reliable. If any mushroom could become poisonous at any time, study was pointless — which was precisely the attitude Palmer fought against.

## The Only Reliable Method

Palmer's answer to the question of safe identification was the same principle applied to all food:

> "You must get acquainted with these little strangers. You must know their habits, color, size, smell, taste, and general appearance, just as well as you know that of the ordinary kitchen vegetables."

He compared the task to a jeweler distinguishing genuine diamonds from glass, or a cook telling pears from quinces. The discrimination is acquired through practice and cannot be communicated in words. The eye and hand develop "a delicacy of analysis, impossible to communicate in words." A gentleman who once sent Palmer specimens from the use of which he claimed illness had brought mushrooms from three independent genera "as unlike in properties and appearance as currants from grapes," suggesting the illness arose not from any of them but from overeating or poor preparation.

## Palmer's Testing Protocol

For each new variety, Palmer followed a careful protocol:

1. **Identify the family** — trace the specimen to its proper taxonomic group
2. **Small initial taste** — chew a small piece of flesh on an empty stomach, first thing in the morning
3. **Gradual increase** — if no ill effects, increase the quantity in successive trials
4. **Whole specimen test** — eventually eat an entire mushroom
5. **Confirm permanence** — once proven edible, the quality never changes

He noted that constant use of edible fungi develops extreme sensitiveness to toxicological properties. The system does not become hardened to noxious elements but repels them. Broiling a test piece with salt and butter (no pepper) and eating it on an empty stomach served as his standard preliminary test. Non-edible fungi typically disclosed their character through nauseous slime, grassy odor, or unpleasant taste when cooked.

### A Variety Once Edible Never Changes

One of Palmer's most important conclusions, directly contradicting the prevailing theory attributed to Berkeley, was that an edible mushroom can never become poisonous. He tested this over five seasons by eating every variety of known edible mushroom from widely separated localities, propagating them, mixing their spawn, and making mixed stews of unrelated species. His conclusion: "You cannot make a noxious dish out of innoxious elements. You cannot pluck food and poison from the same plant."

## The Classification Problem

Part of the difficulty lay in the chaotic state of mycological classification in the 19th century. Generic names varied between leading authorities from Sowerby onward. A specimen could be placed in its proper family without defining its edibility, since "one mushroom may be esculent, and its twin brother poisonous." The descriptions in standard texts were imprecise, often based on European species that did not match American specimens.

## The Fundamental Distinction: Edible vs. Poisonous

Palmer proposed a practical three-tier classification:

1. **Agreeable varieties** — genuinely edible, tested by personal experience
2. **Disagreeable varieties** — acrid, bitter, slimy, or tough; unpleasant but not deadly
3. **Deadly varieties** — the Amanita family, containing tasteless amanitine

The second category is harmless once identified; the third is invariably fatal. The confusion arose because many authorities applied the word "poisonous" indiscriminately to both categories, leading people to believe all non-esculent mushrooms were equally dangerous.

This conflation had real consequences. Worthington G. Smith's chart labeled whole families as "poisonous" when they were merely disagreeable. Books by Cordier of France and Cooke and Berkeley of England all used the term loosely.
