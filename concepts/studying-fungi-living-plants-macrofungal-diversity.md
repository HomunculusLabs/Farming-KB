# Studying Fungi on Living Plants and Macrofungal Diversity Factors

## Overview

Investigating fungi that colonize living plants and understanding the diversity of terrestrial macrofungi requires a suite of specialized field and laboratory techniques, as well as an appreciation for the complex factors that drive fungal species richness. This concept page covers methods for studying leaf-colonizing fungi and twig microepiphytes, introduces the study of terrestrial and lignicolous macrofungi, and examines how seasonality and annual variation profoundly affect macrofungal diversity assessments.

---

## Studying Fungi on Living Plants

### Leaf Colonization Investigation

The colonization of living plant leaves by fungi has been investigated using a variety of techniques described by Dix and Webster (1995). With the exception of obligate biotrophs, many leaf-colonizing fungi—including downy mildews, rusts, black mildews, and powdery mildews—can be grown in culture for identification purposes. Culture methods are similar to those used for the study of [[endophytic-fungi]] (see Chapter 12 methods), with one critical distinction: parasites are generally isolated from the margins of lesions rather than from asymptomatic tissue. Care must be taken to include living plant tissue in the sample for isolation, because isolation from necrotic tissue usually yields a range of secondary colonizers rather than the primary pathogen.

### Direct Observational Methods

#### Moist Chamber Incubation

For fungi that sporulate readily, direct observational methods are effective. Leaf tissue is incubated in a moist chamber—a sealed Petri dish with moistened filter paper or a sealed plastic bag (Appendix I of the source text)—for 1 to 3 days. Humidity from the investigator's breath is usually sufficient; addition of water or damp paper towels is generally excessive. The incubated leaves are then examined under a dissecting microscope for the presence of fungal fruiting structures. This method is especially useful for detecting *Colletotrichum* species. Fruiting bodies may be overcome by aggressive saprobes, but if the sample is not contaminated by soil or damaged by insects, this method often works well.

#### Leaf Surface Impressions and Peels

Leaf surface impressions or peels are useful for examining fungal structures on leaf surfaces:

- **Nail varnish method**: The surface of a leaf is coated with nail varnish or similar material, allowed to dry, and then stripped off, taking many of the superficial fungal structures with it. The film is mounted on a microscope slide and examined with a compound light microscope.
- **Double-sided adhesive tape method** (Langvad 1980): An easy method for making leaf peels using double-sided adhesive tape.
- **Water-based mucilage method**: Water-based mucilage is applied as a thin, uniform coat to cover slips, allowed to dry until tacky, then lightly touched to the leaf surface. The cover slips are then mounted in water on a microscope slide for examination.

### Sooty Mold Examination

Sooty molds on leaves can be collected and examined using a specialized peel technique. A drop of 2% collodion/parlodion solution—prepared in a volume/volume mixture of [[diethyl-ether]] or absolute ethanol—or cellulose acetate is spread gently on the leaf colony using a glass rod. The solution dries to form a thin film in which the fungus is embedded (Stevens 1981). When the film is peeled from the leaf surface, it removes the intact colony. The "leaf peel" is placed on a microscope slide, and the collodion is removed with acetone. The intact [[sooty-mold]] colony can then be examined under a compound light microscope.

### Twig Microepiphyte Sampling

Pike et al. (1977) described a technique for sampling twig microepiphytes:

1. Edges of cylindrats from twigs are scored by turning the twig against two razor blades mounted 3.3 mm apart in a wooden block.
2. The bark surface is moistened with water, and the microepiphytes are scraped off using a flattened probe.
3. The material is dispersed on a slide in a drop of mounting medium (1:1 aqueous KOH and glycerin) and covered with a cover slip.
4. All fungal cells found in a 40-mm field are counted and identified.
5. The cover slip is divided into transects running vertically, horizontally, and diagonally.
6. Spore counts from each field are multiplied by a factor directly related to the distance of each field from the center of the cover slip to allow for uneven distribution under the cover slip.

### Fluorescence Microscopy

Bernstein et al. (1973) described methods for estimating foliar coverage of Douglas fir needle microepiphytes using fluorescence microscopy, providing a quantitative approach to assessing microepiphyte abundance on conifer foliage.

## Terrestrial and Lignicolous Macrofungi: Introduction

### Defining Macrofungi

Macrofungi are distinguished by having fruiting structures (sporocarps) visible to the naked eye. Most macrofungi are Ascomycota or Basidiomycota, but a few are Zygomycota. Most terrestrial macrofungi are saprobes or mycorrhizal symbionts, but some are pathogens of plants, animals, or fungi. Fungi fruiting on woody substrata are usually either saprobes or plant pathogens. The primary feature that distinguishes the ascomycetes treated as macrofungi from those treated as microfungi is their visibility on the substratum surface—all have fruiting structures visible with the naked eye, with a hand lens, or under a stereomicroscope. For the most part, a moist chamber is not used to observe or induce fruiting of these species.

### Scope and Exclusions

This treatment does not specifically consider lichenized fungi (Chapter 9), plant or animal pathogens (Chapters 7 and 19), Myxomycota, or hypogeous fungi (Chapter 10). Much of the information on microfungi covered elsewhere—such as limitations on their study and their taxonomic and biotic statuses—is equally relevant to the ascomycetes treated in this section.

### Global Knowledge Gaps

Although macrofungi have perhaps the longest history of diversity studies of any mycota, they are nevertheless understudied over most of the world. More data are available from Europe than from any other region, and many of those data have been reviewed in a book edited by Winterhoff (1992). Even for Europe, however, knowledge of macrofungal diversity is incomplete. Taxonomic obstacles and the absence of long-term studies prevent conclusively answering even basic questions about the number of species at a specific location or whether diversity is greater in one type of forest than in another.

### Prerequisites for Study

Before initiating a survey or monitoring program, an investigator should carry out preliminary background research. Essential materials include:

- Maps of the area
- Descriptions of its climate, geology, and vegetation
- Ability to recognize woody plant species and major plant associations
- Knowledge of anticipated macrofungal species diversity and distributions
- Color photographs of vegetation and landscape

Such background information increases understanding of habitat requirements, aids in delimiting representative plots, and helps in the design of an effective sampling regimen.

## Factors Influencing Species Richness: Seasonality and Annual Variation

### Climate-Driven Fruiting

Within a geographic region, fruiting is influenced by elevation and latitude and their effects on temperature and precipitation (Ohenoja 1993). A particular species may fruit at different seasons across wide geographic distances or along strong elevational gradients. Fungi fruit when temperatures are above freezing and moisture is available. Maximum richness of fruiting species occurs only during brief periods and differs among years. Environmental variables and ecological processes that affect the likelihood of recording a species during a study must be considered irrespective of the study's goals.

### The Scale of Annual Variation

Annual variation in presence of sporocarps can be enormous:

| Study | Location | Duration | Key Finding |
|-------|----------|----------|-------------|
| O'Dell and Ammirati (1994) | Olympic National Park, 8 sites | 2 years | Only 5–20% of ectomycorrhizal species fruited in both successive years at individual sites; ~30% detected in both years across all sites combined |
| Winterhoff (cited in Arnolds 1992) | 5 sites (Europe) | 5 years | Only 8–88% of total reported species detected in any given year |
| Lodge (1996) | Puerto Rico, wet subtropical forest | 13 years | Entolomataceae fruited every 2nd–3rd year; some species found only 1 year of the survey |
| Schmit et al. (1999) | Oak forests, Chicago region | 3 years | Significant annual difference in species composition |
| Straatsma et al. (2001) | Switzerland | 21 years | Some species fruited only during 1 particularly rich year; fruiting abundance and species richness were correlated; species richness estimators did not stabilize |

### Implications for Study Design

The challenge of measuring species diversity of macrofungi increases in years when many species fruit simultaneously at a given site. Unless a large, efficient workforce is available, specimens may decay before they can be adequately documented, resulting in significant loss of data. That situation, together with the fact that some species may fruit only 1 year out of 4 or more, underscores the **need for long-term studies—at least 5 years, preferably 10 years**.

The time scale of fungal succession is not well known, but the first observations of some species on a site may reflect their recent migration into the community rather than successional change. Annual variation in species recorded because of variable fruiting patterns is difficult to distinguish from true succession.

### Fruiting Season Patterns

In temperate regions with summer drought, spring and fall are the main fruiting seasons. The "fall" season is progressively later at lower latitudes, in some regions occurring after the winter solstice. In temperate regions with summer rain and at high elevations and latitudes, summer may be the most important season for fruiting. Unusual weather events can trigger unusual patterns; in regions with dry summers, fruiting of rarely observed species may follow a summer shower. Temperature also has a major impact—Ohenoja (1993) found that temperature interacted with habitat and ecological guild, e.g., warm summers increased mycorrhizal sporocarp production in spruce forests but had no effect in pine forests.
