The presence or absence of oxygen in soil is one of the most critical factors
determining soil health and the character of the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]]. In *Teaming With Microbes*, Lowenfels and
Lewis emphasize that beneficial soil organisms are overwhelmingly aerobic,
requiring oxygen to function. When soil becomes anaerobic, pathogenic organisms
take over and plant-toxic by-products are produced.  Understanding and
maintaining aerobic conditions is fundamental to successful soil food web
gardening.

## Aerobic Bacteria

Aerobic bacteria require oxygen to survive and are the dominant beneficial
bacteria in healthy garden soils. They are responsible for most of the
decomposition and nutrient cycling that supports plant life. Aerobic bacteria do
not normally produce bad smells; in fact, actinomycetes among them produce
volatile chemicals that give good soil its clean, earthy aroma. Aerobic
conditions support the full diversity of the soil food web, from bacteria and
fungi through protozoa, nematodes, and
[[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]], all of which require oxygen
to some degree. The metabolic activity of aerobic bacteria drives the
[[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]] process and produces
carbon dioxide as a by-product.

## Anaerobic Bacteria

Anaerobic bacteria can live without oxygen, and most cannot survive in its
presence. The genus *Clostridium* does not need oxygen and can invade and
destroy the soft tissue of decaying matter. By-products of anaerobic decay
include hydrogen sulfide (smelling of rotten eggs), butyric acid (smelling of
vomit), ammonia, and vinegar. The notorious *E. coli* and other bacteria found
in mammalian guts are facultative anaerobes, meaning they can tolerate aerobic
conditions but prefer anaerobic environments.  Most gardeners have smelled the
by-products of anaerobic decomposition, a clear warning sign that something is
wrong.

## Why Anaerobic Conditions Are Harmful

Anaerobic conditions foster pathogenic bacteria and kill off beneficial aerobic
bacteria. Some anaerobic bacteria produce alcohols that are toxic to plant life
and to other bacteria, with as little as one part per million capable of killing
plant cells. When soil becomes compacted or waterlogged, oxygen is displaced,
creating anaerobic zones where beneficial organisms cannot survive. The nitrogen
cycle is disrupted under anaerobic conditions, and plant-toxic compounds
accumulate. Gray soil colors often indicate anaerobic conditions, as microbes
use iron and manganese in the soil, rendering them colorless.

## Compaction and Oxygen Deprivation

[[lowenfels-soil-structure-aggregates]] is a primary cause of
anaerobic conditions. When soil particles are compressed together, the pore
spaces that hold air are eliminated. Roots, fungi, and larger organisms cannot
move through compacted soil. Water cannot drain properly, creating standing
water that displaces air. Compaction from foot traffic, vehicles, and heavy
equipment destroys the tunnels and burrows created by earthworms and other soil
organisms. Even light compaction from pets and children playing on lawns can
reduce soil oxygen over time.  Maintaining
[[lowenfels-tilling-no-till-soil-health]] and using
appropriate aeration techniques helps prevent compaction.

## Waterlogging and Poor Drainage

Excessive water in soil fills pore spaces that would otherwise hold air,
creating anaerobic conditions. Poor drainage, whether from heavy clay soils,
hardpans, or grading issues, prevents water from moving through soil profiles.
Plants roots need both water and oxygen; waterlogged soils suffocate roots and
encourage root rot pathogens. Proper [[lowenfels-soil-structure-aggregates]] with adequate aggregation creates macropores for drainage and
micropores for water retention, maintaining the balance of air and water that
soil organisms need.

## Anaerobic Soils and Plant Health

Plants growing in anaerobic soils face multiple stresses beyond nutrient
unavailability. Without oxygen, roots cannot carry out normal cellular
respiration, reducing their ability to take up water and nutrients even when
these are present. Root rot pathogens thrive in low-oxygen environments,
attacking weakened roots. Denitrifying bacteria convert available nitrogen back
into atmospheric nitrogen gas, literally causing fertilizer to evaporate away.
The toxic by-products of anaerobic metabolism, including alcohols and organic
acids, can directly damage root tissues. Plants in anaerobic soil show
yellowing, stunted growth, and increased susceptibility to disease.

## Maintaining Aerobic Conditions

Several practices help maintain aerobic soil conditions. Avoiding compaction by
minimizing foot traffic and using raised beds or designated paths preserves pore
space. Ensuring good drainage through proper grading and soil structure prevents
waterlogging. Adding organic matter improves soil aggregation and creates air
channels. [[lowenfels-tilling-no-till-soil-health]] preserves
existing soil structure and the burrows of soil organisms. In lawns, plug
aeration every three to four years relieves compaction. In
[[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]], regular turning
maintains aerobic conditions by introducing oxygen into the pile.

## Anaerobic vs Aerobic Compost Tea

The distinction between aerobic and anaerobic conditions is critical in
[[lowenfels-compost-tea-brewing]] production. Traditional compost
teas were often anaerobic, producing foul-smelling liquids that could contain
pathogens. Actively aerated compost teas (AACT) maintain oxygen throughout the
brewing process using air pumps and bubblers, ensuring that beneficial aerobic
organisms dominate. The difference is clear in the smell: good aerobic tea
smells fresh and earthy, while anaerobic tea smells foul. Only aerobic teas
should be applied to plants.

## Summary

Aerobic soil conditions support the full diversity of beneficial soil organisms,
while anaerobic conditions promote pathogens and produce plant-toxic compounds.
Maintaining oxygen in soil requires preventing compaction, ensuring drainage,
adding organic matter, and minimizing soil disturbance. The presence of earthy
smells, dark soil color, and active earthworm populations all indicate healthy
aerobic conditions, while foul odors and gray soil colors signal dangerous
anaerobic zones that need correction.
