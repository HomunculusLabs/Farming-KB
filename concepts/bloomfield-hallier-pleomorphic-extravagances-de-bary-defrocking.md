# Ernst Hallier and the Pleomorphic Extravagances

The Tulasne brothers' discovery that a single fungus could produce
radically different spore types across its life cycle was one of the
great revolutions in mycology. But every revolution attracts its
radicals, and the concept of pleomorphy was no exception. The story of
Ernst Hallier stands as a cautionary tale about how a valid scientific
insight can be stretched beyond all reason.

## The Tulasne Revolution

Louis-René and Charles Tulasne, two bachelor brothers in Paris, had
demonstrated through meticulous observation and illustration that the
asexual (anamorph) and sexual (teleomorph) stages of many fungi
belonged to the same organism. Their three-volume *Selecta Fungorum
Carpologia* established that fungi previously classified as separate
species were in fact different phases of a single life cycle.

This was a staggering insight. It meant that a vast number of fungi had
been described twice because the observer was privy only to one chunk of
a larger life cycle. Nothing in mycology was more perplexing than this
duality — the same organism wearing two utterly different faces.

## Hallier Takes the Concept Too Far

Ernst Hallier, professor of botany at Jena, Germany, absorbed the
Tulasne brothers' insight and ran with it — straight off a cliff.
Where the Tulasnes had shown that one fungus could produce two types of
spores, Hallier proposed that a single microbe could transform into
virtually any other fungus depending on its environment.

Hallier suggested that the yeast *Saccharomyces* turned into a
specialized insect pathogen when consumed by flies, and that it could
give rise to a bread mold in air, or a zoospore-producing oomycete if
the insect fell into water. In short, he subsumed a ream of unrelated
fungi into a single species by proposing a life cycle that — as
Nicholas Money observed — would rival the escapades of a worm capable
of modifying itself into an elephant or a sea snake.

## From Fungi to Bacteria

Hallier's extrapolations grew ever more audacious. He became convinced
that the bacterium causing gonorrhea was actually a stage in the life
cycle of a fungus that produced conidia when living outside the human
body. This was not merely wrong — it represented a fundamental
misunderstanding of the distinction between prokaryotes and eukaryotes,
a distinction that would not be formally articulated for another
century.

The idea that bacteria and fungi were merely different life stages of
the same organism reflected the confused state of microbiology in the
mid-nineteenth century. The theory of spontaneous generation — which
held that rodents could originate spontaneously from piles of garbage —
had not yet been disproved. Louis Pasteur's swan-necked flask
experiments would not be published until the 1860s.

## Context: The Pre-Pasteurian World

It is useful to consider the intellectual environment in which Hallier
formulated his theories. The boundaries between kingdoms of life were
poorly understood. Microscopes revealed a bewildering diversity of
microscopic organisms, but the tools for classifying them — genetic
analysis, electron microscopy, molecular phylogenetics — lay decades or
centuries in the future.

Without these tools, researchers relied on morphology and behavior, and
the polymorphic nature of fungi made them especially confusing. If a
single organism could look like a yeast in one environment and a mold
in another, who was to say it could not look like a bacterium in a
third? Hallier was pushing the logic of pleomorphy to its most extreme
conclusion.

## De Bary's Defrocking

Anton de Bary, the great German botanist and mycologist, put an end to
Hallier's excesses in his landmark book on fungi published in English
in 1887. De Bary discussed the work of the Tulasne brothers and accused
Hallier of "pleomorphic extravagances" — a phrase that has echoed
through mycological literature ever since as the definitive description
of overzealous application of a valid concept.

Hallier raised no defense against this public defrocking. Instead, he
abandoned science entirely and devoted himself to the study of
philosophy and aesthetics — a retreat that suggests he recognized the
collapse of his mycological theories but could not bear to engage with
the evidence against them.

## Lessons from the Hallier Affair

### The Perils of Overextension

Hallier's story illustrates a common pattern in science: a genuine
discovery is generalized beyond its domain of validity. The Tulasne
brothers had shown that some fungi are pleomorphic; Hallier assumed
that all microorganisms were phases of a single protean entity. The
error was not in the observation but in the extrapolation.

### The Importance of Skepticism

De Bary's intervention demonstrated the corrective function of the
scientific community. A single voice of rigorous skepticism, grounded
in careful observation, can cut through an entire edifice of
speculation. The phrase "pleomorphic extravagances" remains one of
mycology's most memorable put-downs precisely because it was earned.

### Identity and Metamorphosis

At a deeper level, the Hallier affair raises the question of fungal
identity. Even the Tulasne brothers' legitimate discoveries meant that
the identity of a fungus was always in question. As Money notes, the
identity of a fungus is almost always in question — an observation
that resonates far beyond the specific case of pleomorphy to the
fundamental nature of fungal biology.

## The Tulasne Brothers' Actual Achievement

While Hallier distorted their work beyond recognition, the Tulasne
brothers' actual contribution was profound. Louis-René wrote the text
of the Carpologia, drawing on scientific knowledge acquired after
quitting the legal profession for a research career at the Jardin de
Plantes in Paris. Charles created the luminous illustrations —
three-dimensional depictions whose information content far exceeds that
of any photograph.

Charles Tulasne's drawings allowed mycologists to see the fluid
interior of cells through transparent walls, sense the roughness of
spore surfaces, and imagine the tactile pleasure of running a finger
over a fruiting body. It is as if he shrank himself to the size of a
spore and drew the fungal forest that towered around him. These
illustrations remain among the finest scientific artworks ever produced.
