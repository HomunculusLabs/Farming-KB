## Mycorrhizal Succession and Functional Dynamics in Forest Ecosystems

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] undergo successional changes as forests develop, driven by
shifts in substrate quality, nutrient availability, and competitive
interactions. Understanding these dynamics is essential for grasping how
forests cycle carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus across decades of stand
development.

## Mycorrhizal Succession During Forest Development

### Early-Stage vs. Late-Stage Fungi

Fleming et al. (1986) proposed the foundational concept of **mycorrhizal
succession** based on concentric bands of different ectomycorrhizal (ECM)
fungal species radiating from the base of aging birch trees. The outermost
ring harbored "early-stage" fungi—species adapted to young, nutrient-rich
conditions—while species nearer the trunk represented "late-stage" fungi
associated with older, more resource-limited environments.

### Fruit Body Surveys and Stand Age

Dighton et al. (1986) documented a general pattern of mycorrhizal succession
through fruit body surveys in Sitka spruce and lodgepole pine stands of
different ages. Dominant mycorrhizal species shifted predictably with stand
age, linked to changes in available nutrient resources in the forest floor and
the physiological capacities of the mycorrhizal fungi (Dighton and Mason,
1985; Last et al., 1987).

### Enzyme Competency and Recalcitrant Litter

Read (1991a) hypothesized that late-stage ECM fungi in older stands possess
**greater enzyme competency** because they must access nutrients from
increasingly recalcitrant litter. During early development, litter is
high-quality and readily decomposed; as stands mature, accumulated organic
matter becomes chemically complex, selecting for fungi with stronger
oxidative and proteolytic enzyme systems.

## Succession on Decomposing Litter

Ponge (1990, 1991) demonstrated that ECM fungi invade pine leaves during the
**latter stages of decomposition**, after [[saprotrophic-fungi]] and fauna have
rendered the leaf matrix physically penetrable and nutrients more available
in inorganic forms. The juxtaposition of roots, mycorrhizal hyphae, and
decomposing organic matter creates optimal conditions for direct nutrient
transfer, minimizing leaching losses.

## Fungal Succession on Localized Nutrient Patches

Sagara (1995) documented clear temporal successions of fungi on localized
nutrient-rich substrates in Japanese pine forests:

| Substrate | Early (0–200 days) | Mid (200–400 days) | Late (400–1700 days) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human feces | *Ascobolus hansenii*, *Peziza* sp. | — | *[[laccaria-laccata]]* |
| Dead cat | *A. denudatus*, *T. tesquorum* | *[[hebeloma-spoliatum]]* | *L. chrysorrheus*, *Mitrulia* sp. |
| Aqueous ammonia (500 g/m² N) | *A. denudatus*, *A. botrytis* | *T. tesquorum*, *C. echinosporum* | *[[laccaria-bicolor]]*, *Hebeloma* spp. |

Late-successional *Laccaria* and *Hebeloma* species consistently appeared on
ammonium-enriched patches, reflecting their affinity for high ammonium. The
ECM fungus *Hebeloma radicosum* exploits mole middens and actively "defends"
its territory against the more common *H. spoliatum* through competitive
exclusion driven by differential nutrient exploitation.

## Root-Mycorrhizal Juxtaposition with Organic Matter

Répač (1996) showed that ectomycorrhizal colonization increases near organic
matter. Matsuda and Hijii (1998) documented spatial and temporal patterns in
ECM fruiting phenology in *Abies firma* forests of Japan, reflecting nutrient
availability and competitive interactions. Both findings underscore how root-
mycorrhizal juxtaposition with nutrient-rich organic material provides the
most efficient pathway for mineralization and direct uptake, minimizing
leaching losses.

## Tropical Ectomycorrhizae and the PACER Concept

Newbery et al. (1997) showed that ECM communities on tropical legume tree
roots (caesalps) enable exploitation of phosphorus in **deeper soil layers**
beyond surface feeder roots, keeping phosphorus cycling within biotic
components. They proposed the **PACER** concept (Phenological And Climatic
Ectomycorrhizal Response)—the tight interaction among phosphate acquisition
capacity, environmental controls on phosphate release, and seasonal tree
demand, especially during mast years.

## Mycorrhizal Mats: Dominant Soil Biomass Structures

ECM fungi with rapid hyphal growth form dense aggregations called
**mycorrhizal mats** (Griffiths et al., 1990), constituting **10–20% of the
top 10 cm of soil** (Cromack et al., 1988) and **45–55% of total soil
biomass** (Cromack et al., 1979). Aguilera et al. (1993) showed mat-forming
ECM communities in Douglas fir progressively remove organic nitrogen and
immobilize it into **high C:N ratio fungal tissue**—enriching soil organic N
but rendering it increasingly less available for direct plant uptake.

## Hyphal Foraging Plasticity

Patchy nutrient distribution has selected for significant morphological and
physiological plasticity in ECM extraradical hyphae. Dickie and Koide (1998)
showed that ECM fungi produce **less dense but more extensive hyphal growth
at low nitrogen concentrations**—a response analogous to saprotrophs (Ritz,
1995; Rayner, 1991)—allowing mycelia to exploit patchy resources effectively.

## Differential Effects of AM vs. ECM Associations

Van der Heijden (2001) compared AM and ECM associates on willow (*Salix
repens*). The AM fungus *Glomus mosseae* had low colonization but
**significant short-term effects on shoot growth and root length**. The ECM
fungus *Hebeloma leucosarx* achieved high colonization and improved growth
over a **longer term**. AM colonization enhanced shoot P uptake for December-
collected plants versus March, while ECM showed no seasonal difference—
suggesting complementary temporal and functional niches.

## Research Priorities and the Fungal Plumbing System

Vogt et al. (1991) identified four research priorities: (1) cost-benefit
analyses of mycorrhizal maintenance, (2) roles in nutrient and carbon
storage, (3) significance of inter-plant mycorrhizal linkages, and (4)
nutrient acquisition from organic sources. Rayner (1998) envisioned the
forest as permeated by fungal mycelia functioning as a **plumbing system**
conveying carbon and nutrients among ecosystem components. This supplanted
the traditional model (saprotrophs mineralize, then mycorrhizae assist
uptake) with recognition of **synergistic interactions** between saprotrophic
and mycorrhizal communities—mycorrhizal enzyme activity, carbohydrate
exudation stimulating helper bacteria, and organic acid secretion contributing
to a cooperative nutrient acquisition network (Fig. 3.11).
