# Biogeography of Austral Hemisphere Fungi

## Overview of Southern Hemisphere Distribution

The Austral hemisphere (Australia, New Zealand, and surrounding Pacific
Islands) hosts a distinctive neurotropic mycoflora that reflects both
ancient Gondwanan evolutionary history and more recent introductions
through human activity. The paper by Guzman, Allen, and Gartz documents
approximately 25-30 neurotropic species from the Austral region, including
several notable endemics. The distribution patterns in this region provide
important insights into [[fungal-biogeography]] and the processes shaping
global mycodiversity.

## Australia

Australia has a documented neurotropic mycoflora of approximately 15-20
species. The most significant indigenous species include:

- **[[psilocybe-subaeruginosa]]**: The most common and widely distributed
  Australian neurotropic species, found in the southern and eastern
  states (Victoria, New South Wales, South Australia, Tasmania). It
  fruits on wood chips, decomposing wood, and garden mulch, and is
  particularly common in urban and suburban areas. It is one of the
  most potent Psilocybe species, with significant psilocybin and
  psilocin concentrations.

- **[[psilocybe-cubensis]]**: Widespread in tropical and subtropical
  northern Australia (Queensland, Northern Territory), growing on
  bovine dung in pastures. It is likely an ancient naturalized species
  rather than a recent introduction.

- **[[psilocybe-australiana]]**: An Australian species found in New South
  Wales and Victoria, growing on dung and enriched soil.

- **[[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]**: Found in southern Australia, associated
  with dead wood. This species is shared with South America, suggesting
  a possible Gondwanan distribution.

- **Copelandia cyanescens**: Present in tropical northern Australia,
  particularly Queensland, growing on dung.

Additional Australian species include P. allenii (potentially endemic),
P. tasmaniana (from Tasmania), and several Panaeolus and Pluteus
species. The Australian mycoflora is considered undersampled,
particularly in tropical northern regions and the vast arid interior.

## New Zealand

New Zealand has a rich neurotropic mycoflora for its size, with several
endemic species:

- **[[psilocybe-aucklandii]]**: Endemic to New Zealand, found in the
  Auckland region. This species is restricted to the North Island and
  represents a unique component of New Zealand's mycoflora.

- **[[psilocybe-makarorae]]**: Another endemic species described from
  New Zealand, found in native forests. It is one of the more
  recently described species in the genus.

- **[[psilocybe-semilanceata]]**: Found in New Zealand pastures, this
  species represents a circumpolar distribution shared with Europe
  and North America.

- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa**: Also present in New Zealand, shared
  with Australia.

- **Psilocybe weraroa** (formerly Weraroa novae-zelandiae): A unique
  secotioid (pouch-like) fungus endemic to New Zealand. This species
  does not open its cap to release spores in the typical agaric
  manner, instead retaining them in a pouch-like structure. Its
  inclusion in Psilocybe represents a remarkable evolutionary
  transition.

- **Gymnopilus purpuratus**: Shared with Australia and South America.

New Zealand's isolation and long evolutionary history have resulted in
a distinctive mycoflora with significant endemism. The native forests
of both the North and South Islands support neurotropic species
associated with decomposing wood and forest litter.

## Pacific Islands

The Pacific Islands harbor neurotropic species that reflect a mix of
natural distributions and human introductions:

- **Hawaii**: Despite its extreme isolation, the Hawaiian Islands have
  a surprisingly rich introduced neurotropic mycoflora. Amanita
  muscaria (introduced with pine plantations), Copelandia cyanescens,
  [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]], and several Psilocybe species are
  documented. The introduction of these species is likely associated
  with the importation of livestock and plants.

- **Fiji and Samoa**: Copelandia cyanescens and Psilocybe cubensis
  are documented from these islands, likely spread through the
  movement of domesticated herbivores.

- **Solomon Islands**: Limited records exist, but Copelandia species
  have been documented.

- **New Caledonia**: Records of neurotropic species exist but the
  mycoflora remains poorly studied.

## Gondwanan Connections

One of the most interesting biogeographic patterns in the Austral
hemisphere is the shared distribution of Gymnopilus purpuratus between
South America (Chile, Argentina) and the Austral region. This suggests
an ancient Gondwanan distribution before the breakup of the southern
supercontinent. Similar patterns are observed in other fungal groups.

## Introduced vs Native Species

Distinguishing between native and introduced neurotropic fungi in the
Austral hemisphere is a significant challenge. Species like Psilocybe
cubensis and Copelandia cyanescens may be ancient naturalized species
or relatively recent introductions associated with human activity.
[[psilocybe-cyanescens]], which is native to the Pacific Northwest of
North America, has been widely introduced to Australia and New Zealand
through the movement of wood chips and garden mulch. Amanita muscaria
was deliberately introduced to New Zealand and Australia as an
ectomycorrhizal associate of introduced pine trees.

## Conservation and Research Needs

The Austral hemisphere neurotropic mycoflora remains significantly
undersampled. The endemic species of Australia and New Zealand are
particularly vulnerable to habitat destruction and deserve priority
in conservation efforts. International collaboration between
mycologists and indigenous communities is essential.
