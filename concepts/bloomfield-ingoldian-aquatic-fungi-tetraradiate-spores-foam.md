## Discovery and Significance

Ingold found the spores trapped in foam that collected at the base of waterfalls and
around half-submerged branches in fast-flowing creeks. The foam is a natural phenomenon
caused by fatty substances released from decaying leaves that act as detergents,
forming bubbles when water is churned violently. When Ingold described his spores at a
meeting of the British Mycological Society, a dismissive colleague from Cambridge
University suggested they were merely hairs from plant leaves. Sixty years later, the
discovery is recognized as comparable in importance to the first scientific reports of
African megafauna or Australian marsupials. A wholly new type of fungus had been found,
a rich mine of species that had always, but invisibly, fashioned their spores in
creeks and streams on every continent.

## Spore Morphology

Ingoldian conidia display an astonishing variety of shapes. Some are star-shaped with
thin limbs connected to a central hub, others are crescent-shaped or sigmoid, an
elongated S twisted into an extended helix. A few combine these features and resemble
the balloon animals created by sculptors at children's hospitals and carnivals. The
spores reach concentrations of 20,000 per liter of water in late fall and early winter,
exposing anyone splashing in a creek to millions of them. Fortunately, no human
infections by Ingoldian fungi have been reported.

## Actinospora: The Largest Fungal Spore

The development of the tetraradiate spores of Actinospora is particularly impressive. A
hyphal apex swells to form a central hub, from which four buds emerge and elongate into
arms simultaneously. At maturity, the spore spans 0.5 millimeters in diameter, making
single spores visible with a hand lens. This is the largest spore formed by any fungus.
Once mature, each star separates from its hyphal stalk and is carried off by the water,
arms outstretched to embrace the next submerged leaf.

## The Function of Tetraradiate Shapes

The most compelling explanation for the unusual spore shapes comes from search theory,
a branch of mathematics originally developed for antisubmarine warfare. Analysis shows
that the probability of hitting a target increases in proportion to the length of the
search vehicle. A star-shaped spore has a much higher probability of encountering a
submerged leaf than a spherical spore of equal mass. Because the appendages of
tetraradiate spores are no thicker than hyphae, these decorative structures span an
enormous area with a minimal investment of cytoplasm.

## The Three-Point Landing

When the tip of a star-shaped arm strikes a solid surface, water movement forces the
spore to pivot around this point of attachment until additional arms land on the
substrate to complete a three-point landing. Upon contact, the end of each arm swells
and secretes an adhesive that cements the tripod to the leaf. The swellings produce
infection hyphae that penetrate the leaf, so a single spore attacks its food from the
corners of a triangle. Sigmoid spores tumble in the water flow and roll sideways or
somersault until a firm attachment is made, then secrete adhesive at a second contact
point to prevent further disturbance.

## Why Not Slower Sedimentation?

Some mycologists contended that the advantage of extended spore forms might be slower
settling speed in water. However, meticulous experiments demonstrated that spores with
microscopic arms fall through the water column as fast as spherical spores of similar
mass. Water represents a viscous medium for microscopic particles, slowing descent to
millimeters per minute. If appendages were effective at slowing sedimentation, elaborate
shapes would be anticipated in terrestrial fungi, where the faster rate of descent in
air places an even greater premium on braking structures. But airborne spores are
always compact, confirming that appendages add unwelcome mass and speed descent.
