# John Raper and the Pheromone Biology of Achlya ambisexualis

John Raper, working at Harvard University in the mid-twentieth century,
made one of the most significant contributions to the understanding of
sexual communication in microorganisms when he discovered and
characterized the pheromone system of the water mold Achlya
ambisexualis.
His work demonstrated for the first time that sexual development in a
fungus was coordinated by specific [[fungal-roles-aquatic-environments]].
Achlya ambisexualis is unusual because it incorporates a family of
strains in which distinct males and females coexist with hermaphrodites
and "she-males" — organisms that can offer either oogonia or antheridia
depending on the gender of available partners.
This sexual plasticity made it an ideal subject for studying the chemical
signals governing mating behavior.

## The Antheridiol-Oogoniol System

The pheromone system involves two classes of steroid hormones.
The female initiates mating by releasing antheridiol from her mycelium
into the surrounding water.
Antheridiol is a steroid molecule, structurally related to the hormones
that regulate reproduction in animals.
If a male is growing sufficiently close, he detects the antheridiol and
responds by producing antheridia — specialized male reproductive
branches.

As these male branches grow toward the source of antheridiol, they
release a second pheromone called oogoniol, recognized by the female.
Sensing the success of her "seductive behavior," the female forms
oogonia (egg sacs) and secretes more antheridiol to attract every male
in the neighborhood.
She becomes entangled in a cage of male sex branches that clamber around
until they pinpoint the source of the antheridiol.
Once contact is made with the oogonium, the tips of the antheridia
flatten against its curved surface, stimulating egg formation.
Finally, the egg chamber is penetrated by stumpy hyphae from each
antheridium, and sperm nuclei are injected into one or more eggs.

## Parallel with Animal Endocrinology

The discovery that oomycetes use steroid hormones for sexual
communication was particularly significant because it revealed a
fundamental parallel between fungal and animal endocrinology.
In animals, steroid hormones enter target cells and bind to nuclear
receptors, where the hormone-receptor complex acts as a transcriptional
regulator directing the expression of specific genes.
The steroid pheromones of Achlya are structurally similar to animal
steroid hormones, suggesting an ancient evolutionary relationship.

## The Endoglucanase Connection

In 1967, Donovon des Thomas and John Mullins at the University of
Florida discovered that the appearance of male branches coincided with
the secretion of an enzyme called endoglucanase.
Since antheridiol elicited both a surge in enzyme activity and branch
formation, Thomas and Mullins proposed that the enzyme clipped polymers
within the hyphal wall, causing it to relax and allow branch emergence.

In normal hyphae, branches emerge some distance behind the growing tip.
But in the presence of antheridiol, male branches emerge as bumps close
to the apex, indicating a fundamental shift in where the cell wall can
be reshaped.
This observation was consistent with localized changes in hyphal wall
plasticity induced by the hormone.

The wall-loosening hypothesis was tested decades later by Terry Hill and
Nicholas Money, who measured both endoglucanase activity and wall
tensile strength from hyphae cultured under varying conditions.
Their experiments revealed a strong correlation between enzyme activity
and wall plasticity, supporting the conclusion that endoglucanase
secretion governs the emergence of male branches.
