# [[permaculture-ethics-earth-care-people-care-fair-share]] and Rules of Use

## The Three Ethical Foundations

[[permaculture]] is grounded in three ethical principles, distilled from research into [[fukuoka-sowing-natural-culture-community-and-green-earth-vision]] ethics as adopted by older religious and cooperative groups. Although many traditional systems contained as many as 18 principles, Mollison found that most could be subsumed under these three — and even the second and third arise from the first:

### 1. Care of the Earth

*Provision for all life systems to continue and multiply.*

This first ethic is the foundation upon which the others rest. It requires that human activities support the continued existence and proliferation of all living systems — not just human life, but the entire web of soil organisms, plants, animals, fungi, and microbial communities that make the earth productive. It demands that we design systems that enhance rather than degrade the biological capacity of the land.

### 2. Care of People

*Provision for people to access those resources necessary to their existence.*

This ethic focuses on ensuring that human communities can meet their needs for food, water, shelter, energy, and social connection. The emphasis on "access" rather than "ownership" is significant — it implies systems designed for sharing, common resource [[query-what-are-ec-and-tds-and-how-do-i-use-them-for-cannabis-nutrient-management]], and equitable distribution rather than private accumulation.

### 3. Setting Limits to Population and Consumption

*By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles.*

The third ethic recognizes that unlimited growth is incompatible with a finite planet. It calls for voluntary self-regulation of consumption and population, creating surpluses that can be reinvested in [[permaculture-ethics-of-earth-care-and-people-care]] community care. This is not a call for austerity but for intelligent management — reducing waste while maintaining quality of life.

## The Taoist Influence

Mollison acknowledges that his philosophy is close to Taoism, evolved from his experiences with natural systems. As stated in *Permaculture Two*, it is:

- A philosophy of **working with** rather than against nature
- Of **protracted and thoughtful observation** rather than protracted and thoughtless action
- Of **looking at systems and people in all their functions**, rather than asking only one yield of them
- Of **allowing systems to demonstrate their own evolutions**

This Taoist-influenced approach reframes the designer's role from one of imposing will upon the landscape to one of observing, understanding, and gently guiding natural processes toward productive ends.

## Self-Reliance and Cooperation

The ethical framework is coupled to a determination toward self-reliance: to be neither employers nor employees, landlords nor tenants, but to be self-reliant as individuals and to cooperate as groups. This does not mean isolation — cooperation is essential — but rather that the basic unit of production and decision-making should be the individual or small group rather than large hierarchical institutions.

## Rules of Necessitous Use

From extensive case histories, Mollison derives practical rules governing human interaction with natural systems:

**The Rule of Necessitous Use**: Leave any natural system alone until you are, of strict necessity, forced to use it.

This rule establishes a default posture of non-interference — natural systems are presumed to be functioning well on their own, and intervention requires genuine necessity. This contrasts sharply with the dominant paradigm of exploiting resources as soon as they are discovered.

## Rules of Conservative Use

Once necessity has been established and a natural resource must be used, the **Rules of Conservative Use** require every attempt to:

- **Reduce waste, hence pollution**: Every output of a system should be an input for another. Waste is a failure of design, not an inevitable byproduct.
- **Thoroughly replace lost minerals**: Nutrients removed in harvests must be returned to the soil [[query-how-do-i-use-knf-inputs-through-the-plant-growth-cycle]] composting, mulching, green manures, or mineral amendments.
- **Do a careful energy accounting**: Every energy input and output must be tracked. Systems should produce more energy than they consume.
- **Make an assessment of long-term, negative biosocial effects on society, and act to buffer or eliminate these**: Design must consider not only immediate productivity but also multi-generational social and biological consequences.

## The Evolution of Ethical Behavior

Mollison provides a framework for understanding how ethics evolve:

Humans are thinking beings with long memories, oral and written records, and the ability to investigate the distant past. It is therefore evident that behaviors in the natural world which seemed appropriate at one time later prove to be damaging to our own society in the long-term — for example, the effects of biocidal [[query-what-are-predatory-mites-and-how-can-i-use-them-for-pest-control]]s on soils and water.

Thus, people are led by information, reflection, and careful investigation to moderate, abandon, or forbid certain behaviors and substances that in the long-term threaten their own survival. Conservative and cautious rules of behavior evolve. This is a rational and sensible process, responsible for many taboos in tribal societies.

This evolutionary view of ethics means that ethical principles are not fixed dogmas but living guidelines that adapt as understanding improves. What seems acceptable today may be recognized as harmful tomorrow, and the ethical framework must accommodate this learning process.

## Forms of Accounting

The ethical system leads naturally to various forms of accounting for human actions. Mollison identifies several domains:

- **Fiscal accounting**: Tracking financial costs and [[query-how-does-cover-cropping-benefit-soil-and-when-should-i-plant-them]]s
- **Social accounting**: Evaluating impacts on community [[query-what-are-the-health-benefits-of-sea-buckthorn-and-how-do-i-grow-it]] and cohesion
- **Environmental accounting**: Measuring effects on ecological systems
- **Aesthetic accounting**: Considering beauty, meaning, and cultural value
- **Energetic accounting**: Calculating energy inputs and outputs

All forms of accounting are appropriate to human survival. A design that looks profitable financially but is destructive environmentally or socially fails the ethical test. Permaculture demands multi-dimensional accounting that recognizes the interconnection of all these domains.

## Interconnectedness with Nature

The rules of necessitous and conservative use lead, step by step, to what Mollison calls "the basic realisation of our interconnectedness with nature." This is not an abstract philosophical insight but a practical understanding that emerges from careful observation and energy accounting. When you truly account for all the inputs and outputs of a system, the interconnections become visible and undeniable.

## From Philosophy to Practice

The ethical framework is not merely theoretical — it is intended to illuminate everyday endeavors. Mollison writes: "This ethic is a very simple statement of guidance, and serves well to illuminate everyday endeavours." Every design decision, from choosing which plants to grow to deciding where to place a house, should be evaluated against these three ethical principles.

The system does not require perfection — only a genuine commitment to continuous improvement and an acceptance of personal responsibility for the consequences of one's actions on the earth and on other people.
