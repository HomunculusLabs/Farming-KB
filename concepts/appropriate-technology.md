# Permaculture Appropriate Technology

Permaculture appropriate technology is the selection, design, and application of tools, techniques, and systems that are small-scale, decentralised, energy-efficient, locally maintainable, and environmentally benign. The concept derives from E.F. Schumacher's philosophy of Small is Beautiful and is integral to [[permaculture-design]]. Bill Mollison integrates appropriate technology throughout Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, from water pumps and earthworking tools to energy systems and building techniques. Appropriate technology prioritises human labour and skill over fossil fuel energy and external inputs.

## Principles of Appropriate Technology

Appropriate technology follows several key principles. It should be small enough to be managed by individuals or small communities without requiring large organisations or bureaucracies. It should use energy efficiently, preferably from renewable sources. It should be maintainable and repairable using local skills and materials. It should be affordable and accessible to people with limited financial resources. It should be environmentally benign, not creating pollution or degrading natural systems. It should build local skills and self-reliance rather than dependency on external suppliers.

The appropriate scale for technology depends on the context. A technology appropriate for a village may not be appropriate for an individual household, and vice versa. The key criterion is whether the technology can be effectively owned, operated, and maintained by the people who depend on it.

## Water Technology

Water-related appropriate technologies are central to permaculture design. Hand pumps, treadle pumps, and simple wind pumps provide water lifting without fossil fuel energy. Ram pumps use the energy of flowing water to pump a portion of that water to a higher elevation without external power. Solar pumps powered by photovoltaic panels provide reliable water supply in sunny locations.

Water filtration systems using sand filters, ceramic filters, or biosand filters provide safe drinking water without chemical treatment. UV treatment systems powered by small solar panels offer another low-energy purification option. Rainwater harvesting systems, from simple guttering and tanks to more sophisticated first-flush diverters and filtration, capture and store roof runoff for domestic use.

Low-flush toilets, composting toilets, and dry toilets reduce water consumption for sanitation. Hand-basin-to-toilet diversion systems, described by Mollison, use hand-washing water to flush toilets, reducing domestic water use by 40 percent. Water-saving showerheads and timer taps reduce water waste.

## Energy Technology

Appropriate energy technologies in permaculture include solar water heaters, solar cookers, photovoltaic systems, small wind generators, micro-hydro systems, biogas digesters, wood-efficient stoves, and rocket stoves. These technologies provide energy services at a human scale using renewable resources.

Solar water heaters range from simple batch heaters made from recycled tanks painted black to more sophisticated thermosiphon systems. Solar cookers use concentrated sunlight for cooking, reducing fuelwood consumption. Photovoltaic systems provide electricity for lighting, communication, water pumping, and small appliances.

Biogas digesters convert organic waste, including animal manure, food waste, and human sewage, into methane gas for cooking and lighting. See [[biogas-digester-basics]] for details. The effluent from biogas digesters is a nutrient-rich fertiliser. Biogas systems integrate waste management, energy production, and soil fertility in a single technology.

## Earthworking Tools

Permaculture earthworking uses a range of appropriate tools depending on the scale of the project. A-frames and bunyip levels for contour identification can be made from locally available materials at minimal cost. Hand tools including mattocks, shovels, digging bars, and wheelbarrows are sufficient for small-scale swale and garden construction.

For larger earthworks, animal-powered equipment such as horse-drawn ploughs and scoops provides effective earthmoving without fossil fuels. Small tractors with attachments including chisel ploughs, rippers, and front-end loaders extend the scale of what small teams can accomplish. Laser levels and GPS guidance improve accuracy for larger projects.

## Building Technology

Appropriate building technologies include [[earth-building-permaculture]] methods such as cob, rammed earth, adobe, and compressed earth blocks. Straw bale construction uses agricultural waste as a building material. Bamboo construction uses rapidly renewable bamboo for structural elements. Recycled materials including tyres, bottles, and salvaged timber can be used creatively in building.

Natural insulation materials include sheep's wool, straw, cellulose from recycled paper, and hemp fibre. Natural finishes such as lime wash, earth plaster, and milk paint provide breathable, non-toxic surface treatments. Passive solar design, thermal mass, and natural ventilation reduce or eliminate the need for mechanical heating and cooling.

## Food Processing Technology

Appropriate food processing technologies preserve harvests without reliance on fossil fuel energy or industrial supply chains. Solar dehydrators use sunlight to dry fruits, vegetables, herbs, and meat. Root cellars and earth-cooled storage maintain low temperatures for winter food storage without refrigeration. Fermentation techniques including lacto-fermentation, vinegar-making, and alcohol production preserve food while adding nutritional value.

Hand-powered grain mills, oil presses, and food processors reduce dependence on electric equipment. Solar ovens and rocket stoves provide efficient cooking. Solar food dryers and smokers preserve seasonal abundance for year-round consumption.

## Communication and Information Technology

Appropriate communication technologies for permaculture communities include community radio for information sharing, two-way radio systems for coordination, and low-power computing powered by solar panels. The internet, when powered by renewable energy, provides access to information and connects communities with broader networks.

## Transport Technology

Appropriate transport technologies minimise fossil fuel dependence for local movement. Bicycle-based transport including cargo bicycles and bicycle trailers provide efficient local transport. Electric bicycles powered by solar-charged batteries extend the range and cargo capacity of human-powered transport. Animal-powered transport using horses, oxen, or donkeys remains appropriate in many contexts.

## Waste Management Technology

Appropriate waste management technologies close resource loops and eliminate the concept of waste. Composting systems, including hot composting, vermicomposting, and sheet mulching, convert organic waste into soil fertility. Greywater treatment systems using constructed wetlands and mulch basins recycle household wastewater for irrigation. Biogas systems convert sewage and organic waste into energy and fertiliser.

## Selection Criteria

Choosing appropriate technology requires assessing several factors. Is the technology suited to the local climate, culture, and resources? Can it be maintained and repaired locally? What are its environmental impacts over its full lifecycle? Does it build local skills and self-reliance? Is it affordable within the local economic context? Does it serve genuine needs rather than creating wants?

The most appropriate technology is often the simplest one that effectively meets the need. Complexity should be added only when simpler solutions are insufficient. This principle of appropriate complexity ensures that systems remain manageable, maintainable, and resilient.
