# The Two-State Phytochrome Model: Pr and Pfr Interconversion

The **two-state phytochrome model** describes the fundamental molecular mechanism
by which plants measure the duration of continuous darkness and regulate
flowering, seed germination, shade avoidance, and numerous other developmental
processes. At its core, phytochrome is a protein photoreceptor that exists in
two stable, interconvertible forms: **Pr** (which absorbs red light at
approximately 660 nm) and **Pfr** (which absorbs far-red light at approximately
730 nm). The dynamic equilibrium between these two states encodes information
about the plant's recent light environment and drives critical developmental
decisions.

## Historical Path to the Model

The conceptual path leading to the two-state model passed through several major
discoveries spanning more than four decades. The story begins with Garner and
Allard's discovery of photoperiodism in 1918, when they demonstrated that the
Maryland Mammoth tobacco measured day length to determine when to flower. Next
came the discovery of night-break experiments around the time of World War II,
showing that brief flashes of red light in the middle of the night could reset
the plant's dark-period measurement.

The critical breakthrough came when Harry Borthwick and his USDA colleagues
discovered that far-red light could reverse the effect of a red-light pulse.
This far-red reversal implied that a single molecular species could exist in two
interconvertible forms, one responding to red and the other to far-red. Warren
L. Butler and his colleagues formalised this model around 1960, demonstrating
the existence of the photoreceptor and naming it **phytochrome** (Greek:
*phyton*, plant; *chroma*, colour).

## The Pr Form: Red Absorbing

Pr is the ground state of phytochrome, the form that is synthesised by the
plant in darkness. It is named for its absorption maximum in the red region of
the spectrum, centred at approximately 660 nanometres. When a molecule of Pr
absorbs a photon of red light, it undergoes a rapid conformational change that
converts it to the Pfr form.

The photoconversion from Pr to Pfr involves a structural rearrangement of the
chromophore, the light-absorbing prosthetic group attached to the phytochrome
protein. The chromophore, a linear tetrapyrrole called phytochromobilin,
isomerises around a specific double bond when it absorbs red light, triggering a
series of protein conformational changes that alter the photoreceptor's
biochemical properties.

Pr is considered the biologically inactive form of phytochrome. It remains
predominantly cytoplasmic within the cell and does not interact with downstream
signalling partners to alter gene expression. Its primary function is to serve
as the substrate for red-light-driven activation.

## The Pfr Form: Far-Red Absorbing and Biologically Active

Pfr is the biologically active form of phytochrome. It has an absorption maximum
in the far-red region at approximately 730 nanometres. When Pfr absorbs a
photon of far-red light, it converts back to the Pr form, completing the
reversible cycle that gives the system its switch-like character.

Pfr differs from Pr in several critical ways. First, Pfr is capable of
translocating from the cytoplasm into the cell nucleus, where it interacts with
transcription factors called **PIFs** (PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS) to
regulate gene expression. Second, Pfr is less stable than Pr; in the absence of
light, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through a thermal relaxation process called
**dark reversion**, the rate of which varies among different phytochrome types
and environmental conditions.

The biological activity of Pfr is central to the phytochrome signalling pathway.
Pfr binds to PIF proteins in the nucleus, promoting their phosphorylation and
degradation, which in turn de-represses or activates hundreds of light-responsive
genes. This cascade alters virtually every aspect of plant development, from
seedling de-etiolation to flowering time regulation.

## Photoconversion Dynamics

The Pr-to-Pfr photoconversion is remarkably efficient. A few seconds of
red-light illumination are sufficient to drive the phytochrome pool strongly
toward the Pfr form. Similarly, a few seconds of far-red light can shift the
equilibrium back toward Pr. The efficiency of these conversions means that
plants can respond very rapidly to changes in their light environment.

The equilibrium between Pr and Pfr at any given moment depends on the spectral
composition of the light the plant is receiving. In pure red light, nearly all
phytochrome is driven to the Pfr form. In pure far-red light, nearly all
reverts to Pr. In natural daylight, which contains a mixture of wavelengths,
the equilibrium position reflects the red-to-far-red ratio of the ambient light
environment.

## Measuring Night Length Through Dark Reversion

The two-state model explains how plants measure the length of continuous
darkness, the key parameter in photoperiodic flowering. At dawn, sunlight rich
in red light rapidly converts phytochrome to the Pfr form. Throughout the day,
continued red-light exposure maintains high Pfr levels. At dusk, the last light
reaching the plant shifts toward far-red wavelengths (because atmospheric
scattering preferentially removes shorter wavelengths near the horizon), driving
some Pfr back to Pr.

During the night, no light is available, and the residual Pfr pool slowly
reverts to Pr through dark reversion. The plant effectively measures night
length by monitoring how much Pfr remains when dawn arrives. If the night was
long enough for nearly all Pfr to have decayed, the plant registers a long
night (short day) and triggers short-day flowering responses. If significant
Pfr persists at dawn, indicating a short night (long day), the plant initiates
long-day flowering responses.

A mid-night flash of red light creates a pulse of Pfr that resets the
dark-period timer, which is why night-break experiments are so effective at
manipulating flowering. A subsequent flash of far-red converts that Pfr back to
Pr, erasing the night-break effect and restoring the original dark-period
measurement. This elegant mechanism allows plants to respond to even brief
interruptions in their darkness period with remarkable precision.

## Ecological Significance: Shade Avoidance

The two-state model also explains one of the most important ecological behaviours
in plant biology: the shade avoidance response. In dense vegetation, the canopy
above absorbs most red light for photosynthesis while transmitting or reflecting
far-red light. A plant growing beneath the canopy therefore experiences a low
red-to-far-red ratio, which drives its phytochrome pool toward the Pr (inactive)
form.

This shift toward Pr mimics the end-of-day transition and triggers shade
avoidance responses. The plant elongates its stems, accelerates its growth rate,
reorients its leaves toward any available light gaps, and may flower earlier
than it would in full sun. These responses represent an adaptive strategy to
escape from shade and compete more effectively for light resources.

## Agricultural Applications

Understanding the Pr/Pfr model has enabled numerous agricultural innovations.
Greenhouse growers can manipulate the red-to-far-red ratio using different types
of supplemental lighting to control plant architecture and flowering.
Chrysanthemum farmers, for example, use night-break lighting with red-rich
sources to prevent premature flowering throughout autumn and winter, then cease
the treatment approximately two weeks before the desired harvest date to trigger
simultaneous blooming in time for seasonal markets.

In cannabis cultivation, the transition from vegetative growth to flowering is
controlled by reducing the daily light period from 18 hours to 12 hours. The
associated increase in continuous darkness allows Pfr levels to fall below the
threshold that maintains vegetative growth, triggering the flowering programme.
Supplemental far-red light can be used to accelerate this transition or to
manipulate plant height during the vegetative phase.

## Modern Extensions of the Model

While the original two-state model remains conceptually foundational, modern
molecular biology has revealed additional complexity. Higher plants possess
multiple phytochrome genes (phyA through phyE in Arabidopsis), each with
distinct absorption properties, stability profiles, and physiological roles.
PhyA is particularly important for sensing very low light levels and
far-red-enriched light environments, while PhyB is the primary mediator of
responses to red-to-far-red ratios in daylight conditions. Despite this genetic
diversity, every member of the phytochrome family operates through the same
fundamental principle of Pr/Pfr photoconversion that Butler and his colleagues
first described.
