## Global Precipitation Overview

The world average rainfall is approximately 86cm (34 inches) per year. Regions
receiving 50cm or less are classified as semi-arid, while arid or desert regions
receive 25cm or less annually. These thresholds are critical benchmarks for
selecting water storage strategies, conservation measures, and plant species.

Water storage strategies become essential as precipitation decreases. In dry
climates, evaporation routinely exceeds precipitation, so without active capture
and storage, water is a net loss. Designing for water retention is not optional
in arid and semi-arid zones — it is the central design challenge.

## Reading Rainfall Data: Beyond Averages

Average annual rainfall figures can be deeply misleading. Distribution and
intensity of rainfall are far more useful metrics than simple means. A region
receiving 100cm in 30 heavy storm events presents a vastly different challenge
than one receiving the same total spread evenly across 200 days. Flooding
histories are particularly important — understanding the frequency, severity,
and seasonal timing of flood events allows designers to position structures and
growing areas safely. Averages smooth out extreme events that cause the most
damage and offer the most design opportunities, through floodwater harvesting
and spreading channels that turn destructive flows into productive infiltration.

There is an inverse relationship between precipitation and available light. As
precipitation rises, cloud cover increases and solar radiation decreases. At the
dry end, clear skies maximize light but evaporation exceeds precipitation.
Designers must balance water availability and solar energy at any given site.

## Three Types of Rainfall

Rainfall is generated by three distinct meteorological mechanisms:

### Orographic Rainfall

Orographic rainfall occurs when air masses are forced upward over mountain
ranges. As air rises, it cools adiabatically, causing moisture to condense and
fall on windward slopes. Leeward slopes (rain shadows) receive significantly
less — sometimes less than a quarter of the windward total.

### Cyclonic or Frontal Rainfall

Cyclonic or frontal rainfall results from the overriding of cool and warm air
masses at weather fronts. When warm air meets cold, it is lifted over the cold
mass, cooling into broad, prolonged rainfall events. This type is widespread

average rainfall, captured entirely from fog. Lanai, Hawaii receives 127cm per
year from fog interception. In New Zealand, cloud forests are well documented:
trees on sea-facing slopes act as massive condensers, with moisture dripping
from needles, bark, and epiphytic mosses. The accumulated water can be so heavy
that large limbs fall from trees in still air, overloaded with intercepted
moisture — illustrating both the productivity and structural considerations of
fog forest environments.

## Design Implications

Understanding the full spectrum of precipitation inputs — from rainfall through
dew and fog — expands the designer's toolkit. In arid and semi-arid regions,
dew and fog may represent the difference between viability and failure. Even in
humid regions, fog interception on ridgelines and dew capture from well-placed
vegetation can supplement supplies and reduce irrigation demands. Effective
design requires mapping all precipitation inputs for a site, not just rainfall
