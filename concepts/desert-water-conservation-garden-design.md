## Desert Water Conservation and Garden Design

## Overview

[[water-conservation]] [[inselbergs-and-fold-mountains-in-desert-permaculture]] is not optional — it is the fundamental constraint upon which all other design decisions rest. [[bill-mollison]] presents a comprehensive system for [[desert-gardening]] built on three pillars: **mulching** (both organic and stone), **[[drip-irrigation]]**, and **physical barriers** that together create a self-contained growing environment capable of sustaining agriculture through multi-year droughts.

## The Three-Layer Desert Garden

The complete desert garden consists of three integrated components working together:

### Layer 1: The Barrier (Perimeter)
A moisture barrier surrounds the entire garden to prevent lateral water loss:

- **Vertical plastic sheets** dug into trenches at least 3 feet deep (or more)
- Alternatively, **clay tamped barriers** where plastic is unavailable
- The barrier ensures that drip irrigation water stays within the garden and travels vertically rather than spreading laterally into surrounding dry soil
- This is described as **critical** — without it, precious water is lost to the desert

### Layer 2: Mulch (Interior)
Mulch within the barrier serves [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]]:

- **pH buffering**: Humic acids in mulch prevent the rapid pH rise that occurs when water is applied directly to desert soil. Without mulch, [[soil-ph]] rapidly rises and becomes toxic to plants
- **Moisture retention**: Mulch reduces evaporation from the soil surface
- **Soil cooling**: Mulch moderates soil temperature extremes
- **Organic matter**: Gradually builds soil structure and fertility

### Layer 3: The Trellis (Canopy)
A high trellis constructed from **termite-proof wood** (black locust or honey locust) provides shade canopy:

- Grapes, melons, and vine crops grow on the trellis
- Normal vegetables grow underneath, protected from summer heat
- Plants receive plenty of light filtered through the trellis
- Heavy mulch at the base completes the system

The formula is simple and memorable: **barrier around, mulch within, trellis over it — that is the desert garden.**

## Mulch Strategies

### Organic Mulch
Organic mulch is non-negotiable in desert gardens. Without it, soil becomes toxic:

- You need approximately **3 acres of desert** to furnish sufficient mulch for 0.25-0.1 acre of garden
- High mulch production species should be planted as barrier plants in windbreaks on banks
- **Tamarisk** is one of the best mulch producers; it can be planted from cuttings soaked 3 days in water
- **Casuarinas** and **desert pines** also produce large quantities of mulch
- **Mesquites** have deep rooting systems (over 100 feet) and heavy pod production
- Desert burrs can be used to carpet around isolated plantings to deter jackrabbits

### Stone Mulch
Stone mulch works through two complementary mechanisms:

1. **Daytime**: Rocks gain heat rapidly, drawing moisture from surrounding soil and increasing evaporation at that spot
2. **Nighttime**: Rocks cool more rapidly than surrounding sand; during positive humidity periods, moisture condenses within the rocks and drips into the sand below

Evidence for stone mulch effectiveness:
- Aborigines use stones in pits with clay basins to collect moisture
- Desert figs in their natural habitat are always found in loose rock or boulder piles
- Citrus performs very well in rock piles
- St. Barbe Baker observed that even 3-4 stones around a tree can make the difference between survival and non-survival

### Mulch Collection Methods
- **Fence trapping**: Desert plants release whole seed heads that ball across the desert and settle against fences — easily accumulated in large quantities
- **Casuarinas and pines** deposit large quantities of mulch naturally
- **Aboriginal techniques**: Thatch water holes with thick spinifex cover; sweep desert floors and mound sweepings in lines around camps
- **Thatch cycling**: Old thatch from shelters serves as mulch — a continuous thatch-to-mulch cycle

## Drip Irrigation Systems

Drip irrigation is described as the **great secret of growing plants in the desert**. It ranges from extremely primitive to highly sophisticated:

### Primitive Forms
- **Ostrich egg** with a single hole bored near the plant; water leaks in little drips
- **One-gallon wine flagon** (inverted, with a small hole in the cap) — many litter desert camps

### Modern Forms
- **Israeli drip irrigation lines** — the sophisticated end of the spectrum

### Design Principles
- Drip water extends laterally approximately 2-2.5 feet
- Hardiest plants go at the furthest distance from water source
- Most water-demanding plants go closest to the water source
- Always drip into mulch, never directly onto bare soil

## Silt Traps and Absorption Systems

### Siphon-Linked Absorption Pans
A system of absorption pans linked by siphons provides progressive water infiltration:

- When one area fills during rain, water siphons to the next
- Fully charged soils in one impoundment pass surplus to the next
- In light rains, only 3 of many impoundments may be fully soaked
- This ensures maximum water infiltration across the largest possible area

### Brushwood Fences and Diversion Banks
- Slightly reinforced brushwood fences with low diversion banks across wadis
- These extend onto the desert to create absorption pans
- Combined with silt traps and formal dams, they can create permanent lagoons in large wadis

## Shade Strategies

### High Shade Structures
In very dry areas, high shade receives special attention:

- High trellises with termite-proof wood support vine crops
- Normal vegetables grow underneath, protected from extreme heat
- The trellis still allows sufficient filtered light for understory crops

### Natural Shade
- **Rock piles** provide shade that supports reptile populations and creates microclimates for plants
- **Desert trees** with heavy canopy (mesquites, tamarisk) create shade zones
- **Aboriginal thatch shelters** demonstrate simple effective shade construction

## Termite Management

Termites are the **primary decomposer** in desert ecosystems — equivalent to earthworms in humid lands. They are also one of the two foremost problems in deserts (along with lack of water).

### Problems
- Termites destroy certain trees (carob is a particular favorite)
- They live in mud tunnels that can be disrupted
- They reinfest from flying adults even after inundation

### Management Strategies
- **Chickens** scratch around, uncover and eagerly seek out termites, easily kicking apart mud tunnels
- **Flooding** (even infrequent) collapses tunnels and drowns termites in cultivated areas
- **Termite-proof wood** (black locust, honey locust) for construction
- **Marigolds** planted in gardens provide protection against eelworms (nematodes), following traditional practice across Southwest Asia and the Indian Deccan

## Frost Zone Management on Desert Hillsides

On escarpments and residual hills, the frost line is abrupt and consistent:

- Frost cuts off at a specific elevation, fluctuating no more than 6 feet
- Within 20 vertical feet, conditions can range from water lilies to walnuts
- Shade adjusts frost intensity
- Perennial tomatoes can grow above the frost line while annuals (peppers, melons) grow below
- A small tank at the frost line enables drip irrigation for the zone below

## Water Storage Calculations

- A 20-25 foot diameter windmill returns approximately **25,000 gallons per day**
- This supplies a settlement of about **500 people**
- Desert winds are typically soft and constant due to continental buffering
- Design for **three-year storage** capacity
- Run-off area should be **20 times the area cultivated** (20 acres of run-off per acre of living)
