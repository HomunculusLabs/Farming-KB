# Casing Soil Recipe and Sterilisation Debate in Small-Scale Mushroom Cultivation

The casing layer is a non-nutritive covering applied to fully colonised substrate
that triggers and supports mushroom fruiting. The composition and preparation
of casing soil significantly influence contamination rates, moisture retention,
and overall yield. The Guid method provides a specific recipe and sterilisation
approach that has been validated through repeated use in home cultivation.

## Standard Casing Soil Recipe

A proven casing mix for small-scale psilocybe cultivation uses three
ingredients in specific proportions:

- **10 parts peat**: Provides the organic base with excellent water-holding
  capacity. Peat is naturally slightly acidic, which is why a buffering
  agent is necessary for optimal mushroom fruiting conditions.
- **5 parts vermiculite**: Improves aeration, drainage, and moisture retention
  while adding physical structure to the mix. The expanded mineral creates
  pockets that help maintain proper gas exchange at the substrate surface.
- **2 parts limestone (marl)**: Buffers the pH upward to a range more suitable
  for mushroom fruiting. Without this amendment, the naturally acidic peat
  would create conditions unfavourable for many mushroom species.

The ingredients are mixed thoroughly in dry form before water is gradually
incorporated. The target moisture level is the maximum water the mix can
hold without becoming muddy. Achieving this precise moisture level requires
adding water gradually while stirring and testing the consistency by feel.
If oversaturated, additional dry ingredients are added to restore proper
consistency. The amount of water needed depends on the moisture content
of the peat being used.

## Casing Soil Sterilisation Versus Pasteurisation

A significant and ongoing debate exists in mushroom cultivation regarding
whether casing soil should be sterilised, pasteurised, or left untreated.
Each approach has proponents and specific drawbacks:

**Arguments against sterilisation**:
Some authors advise against sterilising casing soil because the process
kills beneficial microorganisms. These beneficial organisms are thought
to contribute to contamination suppression through competitive exclusion.
They may also provide biological triggers for fruiting initiation,
as certain bacteria and actinomycetes in the casing layer are known to
stimulate pin formation in commercial mushroom species like Agaricus bisporus.

**Pasteurisation as a compromise**:
Heating casing soil to approximately 60 to 70 degrees Celsius for
30 to 60 minutes is sometimes recommended as a middle ground.
This reduces pathogen loads while theoretically preserving heat-sensitive
beneficial microbes. However, the temperature sensitivity thresholds of
specific beneficial organisms are often not well characterised for
psychoactive species cultivation.

**Arguments for full sterilisation**:
The practical experience documented in this source strongly favours full
sterilisation. The author reports only bad experiences with untreated
or pasteurised casing soils, noting that unsterilised mixes introduced
unacceptable contamination rates in their workflow. This pragmatic
perspective suggests that for small-scale cultivators without access to
commercial-grade microbiological monitoring, the risk of contamination
from untreated casing outweighs any potential benefits from beneficial
microorganisms.

## Sterilisation Procedure

The casing soil is filled into oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags
(polypropylene), or jars and sterilised for one hour in a pressure
cooker. This is the same duration used for the grass seed substrate
itself. Larger volumes may require extended sterilisation times.
After cooling to room temperature, the soil is ready for application.

## Casing Application Technique

The casing process follows a specific sequence designed to maximise
fruiting success:

1. Fully colonised grass seed is shaken loose from the jar and poured
   into a 1-litre disposable plastic tray.
2. Any lumps in the seed are broken up with the clean rim of the jar
   to create an even surface.
3. The grain surface is levelled evenly across the tray.
4. A thin layer of casing soil (1.5 to 2.0 cm) is applied over the
   grain using a large spoon and fork.
5. The casing surface is kept deliberately rough, with small valleys
   and hills, while maintaining overall even depth.
6. The cased tray is covered with tin foil to retain moisture and
   placed in a clean location at 20 to 25 degrees Celsius.
7. Mycelium typically grows through the casing within a few days,
   at which point the foil is removed to introduce light and fresh air.

## Surface Texture Importance

The rough surface texture of the casing is a deliberate technique.
Small valleys and hills increase the effective surface area for pin
formation and create microclimates with varying humidity levels.
These microclimates encourage more even pinning across the tray surface
and can improve total yield compared to a perfectly smooth casing layer.

## Moisture Management After Casing

After the initial casing application, moisture management becomes the
cultivator's primary ongoing responsibility:

- The casing must never be allowed to dry out completely, as this halts
  pin formation and can damage developing primordia.
- Daily misting is required during the fruiting cycle to maintain
  surface moisture.
- Watering should be increased directly after a flush is picked, as
  maturing mushrooms pull significant moisture from the casing soil.
- Precise watering instructions are difficult to provide because the
  optimal amount varies with ambient humidity, temperature, and
  air circulation.
- Cultivators must develop a tactile sense for proper moisture levels
  through experience, checking the casing surface daily.

## Abort Management

Dead pinheads (aborts) should be removed from the casing surface promptly.
Left in place, they can become sites for mould contamination that spreads
across the casing layer. Regular removal of aborts is part of routine
casing maintenance throughout the fruiting cycle.
