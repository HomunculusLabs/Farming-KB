# Bioremediation of Petroleum Hydrocarbons

Petroleum hydrocarbons are among the most widespread environmental contaminants, resulting from oil exploration, transportation, refining, storage, and use.

The bioremediation of petroleum-contaminated environments relies on the metabolic activities of hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and yeasts, which can use various fractions of crude oil and refined petroleum products as carbon and energy sources.

This topic addresses the general principles of petroleum hydrocarbon biodegradation, complementing the fungal-specific discussions in [[fungal-oil-petroleum-bioremediation]], [[petroleum-hydrocarbon-fungal-biodegradation]], and [[biosurfactants-in-bioremediation]].

## Composition of Petroleum Contaminants

Crude oil is a complex mixture of thousands of hydrocarbon compounds classified into four main fractions: aliphatics (saturates), aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes.

The aliphatic fraction includes normal alkanes (n-alkanes), branched alkanes (isoalkanes), and cycloalkanes (naphthenes), ranging from methane to compounds with more than 60 carbon atoms.

The aromatic fraction includes benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene isomers (BTEX), as well as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) with two or more fused benzene rings.

Resins and asphaltenes are high molecular weight polar compounds containing nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen heteroatoms that are the most recalcitrant components of petroleum.

Refined petroleum products including gasoline, diesel fuel, jet fuel, lubricating oils, and heavy fuel oils have different hydrocarbon compositions and therefore different biodegradation characteristics.

Gasoline, with its high proportion of low molecular weight alkanes and BTEX compounds, is generally more readily biodegraded than heavy fuel oils or lubricating oils.

The concept of total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH) is used as a regulatory measure of overall petroleum contamination, but TPH encompasses compounds with vastly different biodegradation rates and toxicities.

## Biodegradation Pathways for Aliphatic Hydrocarbons

The biodegradation of alkanes proceeds through several enzymatic pathways depending on the chain length and the microorganism involved.

Short-chain alkanes (C1 to C4) are degraded primarily by methanotrophic and other specialized bacteria through monoxygenase-catalyzed oxidation.

Medium-chain alkanes (C5 to C16) are degraded by a wide range of bacteria and fungi through initial oxidation by alkane hydroxylases or cytochrome P450 systems to produce primary alcohols.

The primary alcohols are subsequently oxidized to aldehydes and then to fatty acids, which enter the beta-oxidation pathway and are ultimately mineralized to carbon dioxide and water, or incorporated into cellular biomass.

Long-chain alkanes (C17 and above) are degraded primarily by filamentous fungi and certain bacteria, often requiring initial attack by extracellular or cell-associated enzymes.

Fungal degradation of long-chain alkanes involves cytochrome P450 monooxygenases that hydroxylate terminal or subterminal carbon atoms, as described in [[fungal-cytochrome-p450-biodegradation]].

Isoalkanes and cycloalkanes are generally more resistant to biodegradation than their normal alkane counterparts because of their branched or cyclic structures.

Biodegradation of isoprenoid alkanes such as pristane and phytane provides useful biomarkers for assessing the extent of petroleum biodegradation in environmental samples.

## Biodegradation of Aromatic Hydrocarbons

BTEX compounds (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes) are among the most water-soluble and mobile components of petroleum and are of particular concern for groundwater contamination.

Aerobic degradation of BTEX compounds proceeds primarily through dioxygenase-catalyzed ring oxidation, with toluene and xylenes generally degraded more rapidly than benzene.

Pseudomonas, Achromobacter, and Burkholderia species are among the most well-characterized BTEX-degrading bacteria.

Under anaerobic conditions, BTEX compounds are degraded through fermentation, nitrate reduction, iron reduction, sulfate reduction, or methanogenic pathways, though these processes are generally much slower than aerobic degradation.

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are discussed in detail in [[pah-properties-toxicity-bioremediation]] and [[fungal-pah-degradation-mechanisms]].

Low molecular weight PAHs (two to three rings) are degraded through both bacterial and fungal pathways, while high molecular weight PAHs (four or more rings) are degraded primarily by fungi and actinomycetes.

White-rot fungi play a particularly important role in PAH degradation through their ligninolytic enzyme system, as described in [[fungal-pah-degradation-pathways]].

The co-occurrence of aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons in petroleum creates complex degradation dynamics because preferential metabolism of easily degraded compounds (such as n-alkanes) can repress the enzyme systems needed for degradation of more recalcitrant compounds (such as PAHs).

## Factors Affecting Petroleum Biodegradation

Numerous environmental factors influence the rate and extent of petroleum hydrocarbon biodegradation in natural and engineered systems.

Oxygen availability is often the primary limiting factor because the initial oxidation steps of both aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbon degradation are aerobic processes.

Nutrient availability, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus, frequently limits hydrocarbon degradation rates in marine and freshwater environments where these nutrients are scarce.

Temperature affects both the rate of microbial metabolism and the physical properties of petroleum, including viscosity, volatility, and water solubility.

Moisture content is critical for terrestrial biodegradation because microbial activity requires aqueous phase for nutrient transport and enzyme function.

Soil or sediment texture influences oxygen diffusion, water retention, and the surface area available for microbial colonization of hydrocarbon substrates.

Petroleum composition determines the inherent biodegradability of the contamination, with the ratio of labile to recalcitrant fractions being a key predictor of treatment outcomes.

The presence of heavy metals or other toxic co-contaminants can inhibit hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms and slow bioremediation rates.

## Bioremediation Strategies for Petroleum Contamination

Biostimulation involves the addition of nutrients, oxygen, or other growth-limiting factors to enhance the activity of indigenous hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms.

Nutrient amendments are commonly applied at C:N:P ratios of approximately 100:10:1 to support balanced microbial growth during petroleum biodegradation.

Oxygen can be supplied through tilling, sparging, peroxide addition, or the use of oxygen-releasing compounds for in situ treatment.

[[biostimulation-techniques]] applied to petroleum-contaminated sites include land farming, bioventing, and biosparging.

Bioaugmentation involves the introduction of exogenous hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms to supplement or enhance the indigenous microbial community.

[[bioaugmentation-strategies]] for petroleum contamination may include inoculation with consortia of characterized hydrocarbon degraders or with organisms possessing specialized capabilities such as surfactant production.

[[biosurfactants-in-bioremediation]] play an important role in petroleum biodegradation by increasing the bioavailability of hydrophobic hydrocarbons through micelle formation and reduction of interfacial tension.

Fungal-bacterial coculture systems, described in [[fungal-bacterial-coculture-bioremediation]], can enhance petroleum degradation through complementary metabolic activities.

[[mycoremediation-of-petroleum-hydrocarbons]] and [[fungal-bioremediation-organic-pollutants]] detail the specific contributions of fungi to petroleum hydrocarbon degradation.

## Field Applications and Monitoring

Petroleum bioremediation has been successfully applied at field scale for treatment of contaminated soil, groundwater, and marine shorelines.

Land farming involves spreading contaminated soil in thin layers and periodically tilling to aerate and mix nutrients, promoting aerobic biodegradation.

Bioventing applies vacuum or pressure to pull or push air through contaminated soil, delivering oxygen to hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms in the vadose zone.

Biosparging injects air into the saturated zone to deliver oxygen to groundwater contaminated with dissolved petroleum hydrocarbons.

Biopile systems combine soil excavation, nutrient amendment, and aeration in engineered piles for treatment of excavated petroleum-contaminated soil.

Marine oil spill bioremediation involves nutrient application to oiled shorelines, as was done extensively during the Exxon Valdez cleanup, with mixed but generally positive results.

[[bioremediation-monitoring-and-assessment]] for petroleum contamination includes measurement of TPH reduction, BTEX disappearance, PAH degradation, and changes in microbial community composition.

The ratio of pristane to phytane and the distribution of n-alkane chain lengths serve as diagnostic indicators of petroleum biodegradation progress.

Integration of chemical monitoring with toxicity testing and ecological assessment provides a comprehensive evaluation of remediation effectiveness.
