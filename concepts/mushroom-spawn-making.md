# Mushroom Spawn Making

Spawn is the living fungal mycelium propagated on a nutritious medium, used as the "seed" to inoculate larger substrates. Making quality spawn is the critical bridge between agar culture and fruiting. Poor spawn is the single most common cause of failed grows.

## What Is Spawn

Spawn is mycelium growing through a carrier material that provides nutrition and structure. The mycelium colonizes the grain or sawdust medium, creating a network of hyphae that can be expanded onto fruiting substrates. One jar of fully colonized grain spawn can inoculate 10-100x its volume in bulk substrate.

## Grain Types

### Rye Berries (Gold Standard)

- Preferred by most cultivators for most species
- Nutrient-dense, good moisture retention, kernels stay separate
- Available at health food stores and feed stores
- Cook to hydration: simmer 30-40 min until kernels are plump but not burst
- Target moisture: 50-55% by weight after cooking/draining

### Brown Rice

- Excellent for PF TEK jars; good for many species
- Available everywhere, inexpensive
- Tendency to clump if overcooked
- Cook: simmer 20-30 min or soak 24 hours

### Millet

- Small grain means many inoculation points per volume
- Fastest colonization due to high surface-area-to-volume ratio
- Popular for commercial spawn production
- Can be hard to find; try bird seed mixes
- Light, fluffy texture — excellent for shaking and distribution

### Wheat Berries

- Widely available, inexpensive at feed stores
- Good for many species, especially oyster and shiitake
- Harder kernels than rye — longer simmer time (45-60 min)
- Can develop bacterial contamination if over-hydrated

### Wild Bird Seed (WBS)

- Mixed millet, milo, sunflower seeds, safflower
- Inexpensive, available at any pet/garden store
- Remove floating sunflower seeds (they harbor contaminants)
- Good general-purpose grain spawn

### Sorghum

- Hard, round grains; very clean and separate
- Good alternative to rye; popular in commercial operations
- Longer prep time required

## Spawn Jar Preparation

### Hydration Methods

**Method 1: Simmer and Drain**
1. Rinse grain thoroughly to remove dust and debris
2. Cover with water (2:1 water to grain by volume)
3. Bring to boil, then reduce to simmer
4. Cook until grains are plump and soft in the center (bite-test)
5. Drain through a colander for 20-30 minutes; spread on towel to surface-dry
6. Grain should be moist but not wet — no water pools when shaken

**Method 2: 24-Hour Soak**
1. Cover grain in water for 12-24 hours at room temperature
2. Some growers add a splash of gypsum during soak
3. Drain and proceed to loading (skip simmering)
4. Less consistent hydration than simmering

**Method 3: Fractional Sterilization (Tyndallization)**
1. Load grain with measured water directly into jars (no pre-cooking)
2. Sterilize at 15 PSI for 60 min, wait 24 hours, repeat
3. Three cycles total
4. Useful if no pressure canner is available

### Loading Jars

- Use quart mason jars with modified lids (self-healing injection port + 0.22 micron filter or polyfil)
- Fill jars 2/3 full — leave headroom for shaking
- Wipe jar rims clean with alcohol before applying lids
- Tighten lids fingertip-tight (not wrench-tight) to allow pressure equalization

### Sterilization

**Pressure Canner (Required for Grain)**
- 15 PSI for 90 minutes (quart jars)
- 15 PSI for 60 minutes (pint jars)
- Natural pressure release — do not vent manually
- Elevate jars off canner bottom with a trivet

- Never skip the pressure canner for grain. Boiling water baths (212°F / 100°C) cannot kill bacterial endospores (Bacillus spp.) that survive at boiling temperature. They germinate during incubation and destroy your spawn.

## Inoculation

### Sources for Inoculum

- **Agar wedge**: Most reliable. Transfer a clean wedge from an isolated sector to the grain jar
- **Liquid culture**: Inject 2-5 cc of clean LC through the self-healing port
- **Spore syringe**: Least reliable (genetically diverse). Inject 1-2 cc; expect longer colonization and possible sectoring
- **Grain-to-grain (G2G)**: Transfer colonized grain to fresh jars. Fastest expansion but each transfer increases contamination risk

### Inoculation Procedure

1. Work in a still air box (SAB) or in front of a laminar flow hood
2. Flame-sterilize inoculation tool (scalpel, syringe needle) until red-hot
3. Cool briefly (touch agar or sterile surface — never blow on it)
4. Transfer inoculum quickly; minimize time the jar is open
5. Cover and shake immediately to distribute inoculum
6. Place jar in incubation

## Incubation

- **Temperature**: 75-80°F (24-27°C) for most species. Avoid above 83°F — bacterial contamination accelerates faster than mycelium above this threshold
- **Darkness**: Not required but prevents premature pinning. Most species colonize equally well in light or dark
- **Gas exchange**: Ensure filter lids allow breathing. Mycelium produces CO2; buildup slows growth
- **Shaking**: Shake jars at 20-30% colonization to redistribute mycelium and speed colonization. Shake again at 60-70% if desired
- **Timeline**: Most grain jars colonize in 2-4 weeks depending on species, grain type, and inoculation method

## Spawn Quality Assessment

### Visual Indicators of Good Spawn

- Bright white mycelium (for most species) growing uniformly
- Ropiness: grain kernels bound together by thick mycelial threads
- Clean grain smell (earthy, mushroomy)
- Even colonization from inoculation point outward

### Warning Signs

- **Wet spot / bacterial slime**: Grain looks wet, yellowish, smells sour. Toss immediately — bacteria can spread to other jars
- **Green mold (Trichoderma)**: Green or blue-green patches. Often appears as distinct spots. Toss — spores spread easily
- **Black mold (Aspergillus)**: Dark spots with powdery spore heads. Toss
- **Cobweb mold (Dactylum)**: Gray, fluffy, fast-growing. Can sometimes be treated with H2O2 spray but usually not worth saving
- **Slow/stalled colonization**: May indicate contamination, wrong moisture content, or spent inoculum
- **Metabolites**: Yellowish or brownish liquid pooling at jar bottom. Normal in small amounts; excessive metabolites indicate bacterial stress

### Smell Test

Open fully colonized jars briefly and smell:
- Clean, earthy, mushroomy = good
- Sour, rotten, sweet-rotten, feet = bacterial contamination
- Musty, dusty = mold contamination

## Spawn Storage

- **Short-term**: Store at 35-40°F (2-4°C) for up to 2-3 months
- **Long-term**: Transfer to fresh grain every 2 months to maintain vigor
- **Liquid culture storage**: Refrigerate LC at 35-40°F; lasts 6-12 months
- **Slant storage**: Agar slants at refrigeration temps for 6-12 months
- Do not freeze — ice crystals destroy hyphal cells

## Grain-to-Grain Expansion

G2G is the fastest way to multiply spawn:

1. Sterilize receiving jars as normal
2. In SAB or flow hood, open colonized donor jar
3. Shake donor jar to break up grain
4. Pour 1-2 tablespoons of colonized grain into each receiving jar
5. Shake receiving jar to distribute
6. Incubate as normal

- Each jar can inoculate 10 jars. Each of those can inoculate 10 more
- After 3-4 G2G transfers, senescence (genetic degradation) becomes a concern
- Always start G2G from the healthiest, fastest-colonizing jar

## Sawdust Spawn

Used primarily for outdoor inoculation and log cultivation:

- Substrate: hardwood sawdust (oak, maple, beech) + 10-20% wheat bran supplement
- Moisture content: 55-60%
- Sterilize at 15 PSI for 90-120 minutes (larger bags need longer)
- Inoculate with grain spawn or LC
- Slower colonization than grain (3-6 weeks) but stores longer and handles outdoor conditions better
