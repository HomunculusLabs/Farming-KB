# Mushroom Spawn Making

Mushroom spawn is the vegetative growth of the fungus ([[cotter-mushroom-life-cycle-mycelium-biology]])
propagated in a nutrient medium, serving as the seed material for
mushroom cultivation. Spores cannot be used directly in propagation;
spawn of commerce is the only practical seed material employed in growing
these fungi. The development of pure culture methods for spawn production
in the early 1900s revolutionized the American mushroom industry.

## Historical Development

From the earliest records of mushroom culture, growers depended upon
spontaneous or native occurrence of spawn in pastures, compost piles,
or elsewhere. This virgin spawn presumably came from accidental
germination of spores. In England, mill-track virgin spawn was
originally the spawn found in well-trodden soil and manure of the
horse path, later becoming merely a trade name.

For centuries, mushroom spawn was produced entirely by empirical methods.
No progress was made in maintaining varieties or developing improved
forms until about 1900. It was not possible to take varieties into
consideration until pure culture work was developed both in France and
the United States.

## The Pure Culture Method

The breakthrough came when B.M. Duggar at the Bureau of Plant Industry,
U.S. Department of Agriculture, developed a method of securing pure
cultures of [[agaricus-campestris]] and other mushrooms in 1902. The
tissue-culture method involves taking small pieces of internal tissue
from living mushrooms and transferring them to sterilized nutrient
tubes, yielding uncontaminated mycelial growth.

To prepare pure cultures, large test tubes or wide-mouthed bottles are
cleaned, plugged with cotton, and sterilized by dry heat at about 150
degrees C for nearly an hour. The tubes are partially filled (about
two-thirds) with manure or half-decayed leaves, then sterilized in an
autoclave at about 15 pounds pressure for 15 to 30 minutes. If a steam
boiler is used at atmospheric pressure, boiling for at least an hour on
each of two or three successive days is required.

## Tissue Culture Inoculation

Selection is a key advantage of the pure culture method. Mushrooms
representing the most desirable qualities are chosen from beds in full
bearing. Size, quality, color, and prolificness must all be considered.
Plants as young as possible, especially those with the veil still
intact, are most desirable.

With a sterilized scalpel, small pieces of internal tissue are removed
from the mushroom and transferred to the tubes without contacting any
unsterilized surface. It is advisable to wash the mushroom first to
remove dust. The plant may be broken open longitudinally and bits of
internal tissue readily removed. In several days slight growth becomes
evident; in three weeks the mold-like development of mycelium should
spread throughout the medium.

## Selection and Variety Preservation

The advantages of pure culture methods are substantial. Wherever the
species may be found, provided specimens are growing vigorously and free
from parasitic attacks, a culture may be made. Whether varieties vary in
color, quality, or productiveness, the method permits perpetuation of
desirable kinds. Any individual difference indicating the possibility of
a permanent desirable departure may be selected and propagated.

Continuous propagation through tissue cultures for five years has
consistently shown high yield and quality. Running out is likely due to
improper care of the pure cultures rather than any inherent weakness of
the method.

## Commercial Brick Spawn

Brick spawn exhibits better keeping quality than other products on the
market and is easily handled. In making the bricks, manure or
[[mushroom-training-button-mushroom-compost]] is compressed into a compact
form measuring about 5.5 by 8.5 by 1.5 inches when dry. Hand moulds of
iron or strong oak frame lined with steel plates are commonly employed.

The compost used is customarily a mixture of stable-manure, cow-manure,
and sometimes a small quantity of loam. At least two-thirds of the
material should be stable manure free of heavy straw. A considerable
admixture of cow manure gives a more compact brick and encourages a
mold-like rather than thread-like texture of the mycelium.

## Inoculation, Growth, and Drying

Two inoculation methods are practiced: (1) bricks are dried to slight
internal moisture, stacked with spawn pieces distributed between pairs
of bricks in successive layers; or (2) while bricks are still fairly
moist, spawn pieces are inserted in cuts toward each end. Growth occurs
at temperatures favorable for mushroom growing, approximately 55
degrees F.

The surface must be kept slightly moist during growth. Spraying the
brick or storage cellar walls with water may be required, but extremely
moist conditions are to be avoided. Well-penetrated bricks show mold-like
growth throughout. An abundance of coarse thread-like elements alone is
usually indicative of undesirable quality.

## Spawn Vitality

Mushroom spawn is unquestionably so badly injured after being stored
for one year as to be unfit for commercial purposes. No mushrooms have
been obtained from spawn two years old. It is preferable to use spawn not
more than six to eight months old. Under the same conditions, the
vitality of brick spawn is greater than that of flake spawn.
