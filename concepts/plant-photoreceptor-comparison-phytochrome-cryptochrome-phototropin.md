# Comparison of Major Plant Photoreceptor Families

Plants possess a remarkably sophisticated array of photoreceptor systems that
detect light across a broader spectral range than any [[rhodopsin-photopsins-animal-visual-pigments-plant-photoreceptor-parallels]] system. As
Daniel Chamovitz describes in "What a Plant Knows," plants "see" ultraviolet,
visible, and infrared light — a far wider range than human eyes, which are
limited to a narrow band between roughly 400 and 700 nanometers. Plant
photoreception is not about forming images; rather, it extracts biologically
critical information from light: its color (wavelength), direction, intensity,
and duration. Three major families dominate [[darwin-phototropism-discovery-plant-light-perception]]:
phytochromes, cryptochromes, and phototropins. Each has a distinct chromophore,
spectral sensitivity, and set of biological functions.

## Phytochrome: The Red/Far-Red Light Switch

Phytochrome is arguably the most versatile plant photoreceptor, and its
discovery in the mid-twentieth century transformed plant biology. First
identified by Sterling Hendricks, Harry Borthwick, and colleagues at the USDA's
Beltsville [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]] Center, phytochrome exists in two
interconvertible molecular forms. The inactive form, Pr, absorbs red light at
approximately 660 nanometers; upon absorbing a red photon, it isomerizes into
the active form, Pfr, which absorbs far-red light at approximately 730
nanometers. Exposure to far-red light converts Pfr back to Pr. This reversible
switch gives phytochrome the ability to function as a biological toggle.

The biological roles of phytochrome are extraordinarily diverse. In seeds, the
Pr-to-Pfr conversion serves as a germination cue: many seeds will only sprout
when sufficient red light has converted enough phytochrome to its active Pfr
form, indicating the seed is close enough to the soil surface for the seedling
to reach sunlight. If buried too deeply, the seed receives little red light but
some far-red light from soil reflections, keeping phytochrome inactive.

Phytochrome is also the primary photoreceptor governing photoperiodism — the
ability of plants to [[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]] and determine the season. As
Chamovitz explains, phytochrome acts as a molecular hourglass: during daylight,
Pfr accumulates; during darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through thermal
relaxation (dark reversion). The amount of Pfr remaining at the end of the
night tells the plant whether the night was long or short, controlling whether
a short-day or long-day plant will flower.

Additionally, phytochrome mediates [[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]]: when a plant is shaded by
neighboring vegetation, the ratio of red to far-red light drops because
chlorophyll in the shading leaves absorbs red light but transmits and reflects
far-red light. Phytochrome senses this shift and triggers shade-avoidance
responses, allowing the plant to grow toward unfiltered sunlight.

The phytochrome chromophore is a linear tetrapyrrole called phytochromobilin,
covalently attached to the apoprotein. The protein exists as a dimer, and the
conformational change triggered by photon absorption propagates through the
structure, enabling interaction with downstream signaling partners. Phytochrome
resides primarily in the cytoplasm, but upon activation, Pfr translocates to
the nucleus, where it directly regulates gene expression. In the context of
flowering, phytochrome in the leaves produces a [[chamovitz-phytochrome-mobile-signal-leaf-to-flowering]] — subsequently
identified as the protein FT (florigen) — that travels through the phloem to
the shoot apex and triggers the floral transition.

## Cryptochrome: The Blue Light Receptor for Development and Timekeeping

Cryptochromes are blue-light photoreceptors central to de-etiolation, circadian
rhythm regulation, and inhibition of stem elongation. They were first
identified through genetic screens in [[arabidopsis-thaliana]] in the 1990s,
although the underlying phenomenon — blue light inhibiting hypocotyl elongation
in dark-grown seedlings — had been observed much earlier. Cryptochromes use a
flavin-based chromophore, specifically flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), to
absorb light in the blue and UV-A regions (approximately 320–500 nanometers).

Upon absorbing blue light, the flavin chromophore undergoes a redox reaction
triggering a conformational change that activates the cryptochrome protein.
Activated cryptochrome interacts with COP1 (CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1),
an E3 ubiquitin ligase that in darkness targets positive regulators of
photomorphogenesis for degradation. Blue-light activation of cryptochrome
disrupts this interaction, stabilizing these regulators and enabling the
light-growth program to proceed.
