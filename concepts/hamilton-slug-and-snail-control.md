# Slug and Snail Control in the Organic Garden

Slugs and snails are among the most persistent and damaging pests in
any garden, capable of destroying seedlings overnight and disfiguring
mature plants. While [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]]
offers no single magic solution, a combination of physical barriers,
natural predators, trapping techniques, and biological controls can keep
their numbers at acceptable levels without resorting to toxic chemicals.

## Understanding the Enemy

There are several types of slugs, and understanding which cause damage
is the first step toward effective control. The very large slugs that
live on fungi and dead organic matter are largely harmless to living
plants. The real culprits are the smaller brown or black slugs, some of
which live underground and are extremely difficult to catch. These
destructive species come to the surface mainly in midsummer, and this
is the prime time to attack them.

Snails pose problems very similar to those caused by slugs. They eat
seedlings and all parts of mature plants, hiding and breeding under
stones or garden debris during the day and emerging at night to feed.
Their silvery slime trails make their presence easy to detect.

## Physical Barriers and Deterrents

Traditional physical methods remain among the most effective slug
control measures. Surrounding vulnerable plants with a ring of lime,
soot, or wood ash creates a barrier that slugs are reluctant to cross.
The thin covering of lime will not radically alter soil pH, but should
be kept away from acid-loving plants such as rhododendrons and
camellias. A mulch of ornamental pine bark is also effective as a
deterrent.

Plastic bottle cloches offer excellent protection for individual
seedlings. Cut the bottoms off plastic bottles and place them over
young plants. For larger plants, remove both ends of a plastic can and
place it over the plant. Generally, protection is only necessary in the
early stages of growth — once plants are larger they can withstand some
slug damage and show little ill effect.

## Trapping and Hand Picking

One of the oldest and most reliable methods is to go out into the
garden at night with a torch when slugs are actively feeding, pick
them up by hand, and drop them into a jar of paraffin. This nocturnal
patrol is surprisingly effective, even against the underground-dwelling
species that surface to feed. Regular nightly forays during midsummer
can dramatically reduce populations.

Beer traps are another well-established technique. Bury a container
such as a yoghurt pot so its rim is at soil level and fill it with
beer. Slugs are attracted to the fermenting smell, fall in, and drown.
Empty and refill the traps regularly. For snails, watch for their slime
trails on plants and paving, then pick them off and dispose of them in
the same way.

## Encouraging Natural Predators

The organic garden should actively encourage creatures that prey on
slugs and snails. Hedgehogs are perhaps the most valuable ally — a
family of hedgehogs will devour huge numbers of slugs along with
cutworms, woodlice, millipedes, and wireworms. Provide hedgehog
habitat by leaving piles of logs or maintaining dense [[hamilton-hedges-and-windbreaks]]
where they can shelter during the day.

Frogs and toads are another excellent means of slug control. A garden
[[hamilton-garden-pond-maintenance]] is perhaps the ideal
environment for them, though they really only need water for breeding
purposes. Birds also eat large numbers of slugs and their eggs, so
incorporate food tables, bird baths, and nest boxes into the garden
plan. Ground beetles are active slug predators that can be encouraged
by growing [[ground-cover-plants]].

## Biological Control

For gardeners seeking additional help, biological control using the
nematode Phasmarhabditis hermaphrodita offers a modern organic solution.
This microscopic parasitic worm is watered onto the ground, where it
penetrates slugs and snails, usually preventing them from feeding
within three days and ultimately killing them within about a week.
The nematode is most effective in moist, warm conditions and is
applied as a soil drench according to the manufacturer's instructions.
Apply in the evening to moist soil for the best results, and repeat
the treatment if slug populations remain high. The nematodes are safe
for other wildlife and pose no risk to pets, children, or beneficial
insects.

## Copper Barriers and Other Methods

Copper tape or rings placed around pots and raised beds provide
another effective physical barrier. Slugs and snails receive a mild
electric shock when their moist bodies contact the copper, deterring
them from crossing. This method is particularly useful for protecting
container-grown plants and individual prized specimens.

Crushed eggshells, sharp grit, or diatomaceous earth scattered around
vulnerable plants create an abrasive surface that slugs find difficult
to traverse. These materials need to be renewed after heavy rain but
provide a useful additional line of defence alongside other methods.

## Summary

Effective slug and snail control in the organic garden relies on an
integrated approach. Combine physical barriers like lime rings and
plastic cloches with regular night-time patrols, beer traps, and
predator-friendly gardening practices. Biological nematodes provide a
useful additional weapon. The key is persistence — no single method
will eliminate slugs entirely, but consistent effort across multiple
strategies will keep populations at manageable levels and protect your
plants from serious damage. For more general pest management
strategies, see [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]]
and [[hamilton-wildlife-gardening-biodiversity]].
