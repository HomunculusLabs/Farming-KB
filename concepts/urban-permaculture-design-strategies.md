## Urban Permaculture Design Strategies

Urban [[permaculture]] applies the same design principles used for broadacre systems to tiny spaces, but the constraints and opportunities shift dramatically. In urban areas, the main strategies revolve around maximizing vertical space, making optimal plant choices, leveraging social organization, and creating productive links between city and countryside.

## Spatial Optimization

### Vertical Growing and Trellising

In restricted urban spaces, the vertical dimension becomes the greatest area available. Trellising should be maximized wherever possible. Tall walls can support substantial crop production on vertical surfaces. Additional vertical strategies include:

- **Modest turf roofing** on small roofs for herbs and shallow-rooted plants
- **Reflecting surfaces** using old mirrors and aluminum foil to light up dark corners and shade situations
- **Glasshouses within roofs** where southern exposure is limited, functioning as both growing spaces and active heating systems (requiring fans to circulate air to heat stores)

Low-yielding and slow-bearing plants like bush peas and bush beans should be replaced with high-producing, long-yielding crops such as lettuce, zucchini, and peppers on trellises.

### Glass and Protected Growing

Encouraging maximum glassed-in area provides far greater continuity and controllability of production. Wind shielding is critical in urban settings. Boxed trellises on wooden walls protect plants from wind, and ivy grown on brick or masonry walls provides both shade and external insulation.

Ivy alone can prevent 70% of heat gain in a house during spring and autumn in variable climates, and is about 40% effective at preventing radiant heat escape. Its primary function is checking the rapid lateral flow of cold winds along walls.

## Optimal Lot Sizes

Research suggests that suburban lots between a quarter acre and a half acre produce about 28% more than the equivalent area of standalone crop land. The quarter-acre lot appears to be ideal for garden production. Key observations:

- Lots that are too large tend to be converted to lawns
- Lots that are too small discourage gardening altogether
- Poultry (excluding roosters and peacocks) can be integrated on quarter-acre suburban lots

## Social Strategies for Land Access

### The Allotment System

The allotment system, typically using one-eighth acre plots, provides land access through [[urban-permaculture-community-building-social-design|community]] gardening organizations (in America) or public lands programs (in Britain). Britain's system is particularly sophisticated: post offices maintain listings of people seeking land matched with those who have land available. This has successfully connected younger gardeners with older residents no longer able to maintain their allotments.

The British convention is that gardeners using someone else's land do not pay rent but provide vegetables in exchange—a simple but effective arrangement.

### Neglected Lot Reclamation

In Australia, where land often reverts to local authorities when absentee owners fail to pay rates and taxes, [[bill-mollison]] have developed a systematic approach:

1. **Identify** neglected, boarded-up lots owned by the local council
2. **Consult** local residents about interest in a garden
3. **Perform initial landscaping** including stone block beds and paths
4. **Organize a community planting day** involving council, residents, and garden enthusiasts
5. **Present the completed garden** to residents in a ceremony (often with the mayor present)

While no formal title transfer occurs, the political reality is that the council would face electoral consequences if they attempted to reclaim the land. Mollison reported that after three to four years, all four projects he helped establish were still thriving in industrial urban neighborhoods.

### Garden Clubs

Garden clubs work particularly well for low-income groups, including high-rise residents. They require a dedicated organizer and operate successfully in land-scarce places like the Netherlands. The club model involves a large group purchasing a farm with formally issued shares—potentially a $100,000 farm acquired through a hundred $1,000 shares with $100 down and the rest on time.

Critical requirements include:
- **Public transport accessibility** (train or bus routes)
- **Negotiated agreements** with local authorities for overnight camping and central toilet facilities
- **Regular weekend use** with caravans or salvaged sheds

## City Farms

### Australian City Farms

The 18-acre Melbourne city farm demonstrates comprehensive [[urban-permaculture-design]]. An unemployed group supplies hardware and labor, while the site functions as a demonstration area where city people learn practical skills. Features include:

- Attached glasshouses constructed from salvaged factory materials
- Complete herb gardens planted by the cooperative
- Retail operations selling everything from stock ponds to windmills
- Park walkways integrated into a larger parkway development
- Picnic areas for public use
- A retrofitted ordinary house demonstrating sustainable living
- Multiple ponds and diverse demonstration systems

### British City Farms

Britain's 46 city farms are smaller, located directly within cities, and primarily educational. They aim to give urban children—many of whom have never visited a farm—direct contact with livestock and food production. These farms typically include:

- Milk cows being milked and giving birth
- Goats and geese
- Rabbit production
- Worm beds
- [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]

While they could be self-supporting, they require at minimum a manager's wage. Many London city farms are voluntarily staffed.

## Urban-Rural Linkages

### Farm Link Systems

[[farm-link]] programs connect small growers with 20-30 urban families on a personal basis—a precursor to what became the [[community-supported-agriculture-csa]] Agriculture (CSA) model. The arrangement includes:

- **Guaranteed purchase** of the farmer's crop at above-wholesale prices
- **Coordinated production planning** with accurate harvesting estimates
- **Scheduled distribution** so urban families are prepared to process produce when ready
- **Below-retail prices** for urban participants
- **Weekend labor exchange** at critical periods (planting, hilling, harvesting)
- **Mutual services**: urban families can order spare parts and groceries for the farmer

This system evolved beyond simple produce exchange to include:
- Small shelters on farms for urban family holidays
- Assistance with tree planting
- Access to salvaged [[gadd-fungal-biodegradation-concrete-building-materials]] from urban construction sites

### WWOOF (Working Weekends on Organic Farms)

Originating in Britain and spreading to Australia and many other countries, WWOOF addresses the finding that approximately 68% of people want to leave cities but are uncertain about their rural capabilities. Participants pay to visit and work on organic farms, learning practical skills including fencing, firewood cutting, crop management, harvesting, and machinery maintenance.

### Surplus Material Exchange

Urban construction sites generate enormous quantities of useful materials—glass, solid fencing, building materials, doors, and windows—at scrap prices that would be expensive in rural areas. Scouting groups can locate and transport these materials to farms. Mollison noted this as one of many "beneficial linkages possible" between urban surplus and rural need.

## Urban Foraging and Resource Recovery

### Urban Fruit Trees

Melbourne and San Francisco each contain approximately half a million citrus trees in residential back yards, most of whose fruit falls to the ground unused. Mollison described an entrepreneur who identified good chestnut trees throughout Melbourne for grafting, realized he had catalogued thousands of productive trees, and built a $70,000 annual retail business from harvested chestnuts. He subsequently bought a farm, began selling grafted trees, and developed innovative grafting techniques (matching nut color to stock color) that dramatically improved success rates.

This model—identifying, cataloguing, and contracting urban fruit trees—can be applied to any productive urban tree species.

### Sheep for Urban Weed Management

In Adelaide, an entrepreneur runs 9,000 sheep as a mobile grazing service at $6 per week per sheep. Property owners fence the sheep, which reduce fire hazard by consuming wild grass on rough ground. The service provides more effective vegetation management than a human worker at the same price point, with the sheep delivered, sheared, drenched, and collected by the operator.

## Cooperative Processing

### Community Wine Presses

Greek and Italian immigrant communities in Australia installed shared presses and vats for wine production. Participants weigh in their grapes and receive either juice back or a proportional share of the vat wines.

### Nut Processing Cooperatives

Throughout Australia, nut growers operate cooperatives with centralized cleaning and packaging machinery, reducing individual capital requirements while improving product quality.

### Municipal Distillation (Yugoslavia)

In Yugoslav towns, the foyer of every small town hall houses an enormous brass still (nearly two stories high). Residents bring produce for processing:
- Plums are weighed and converted to slivovitz (plum brandy) in proportion to input
- Fermenting crops like surplus potatoes can be converted to alcohol
- The municipality handles distillation, with all fuel costs covered

Mollison advocated that well-off individuals who can afford processing equipment should make it available on rental in their districts, extending this cooperative processing model to grain grinding, oil pressing, [[comparison-steam-distillation-vs-co2-extraction-for-essential-oils]], and fine oil separations.
