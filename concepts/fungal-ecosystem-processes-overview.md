# Fungal Ecosystem Processes Overview

Fungi are integral to virtually all ecosystem processes, playing central roles
in nutrient cycling, [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]]
support, population regulation, and environmental modification. The framework
presented by Dighton (2003) in "Fungi in Ecosystem Processes" provides a
comprehensive structure for understanding the multifaceted contributions of
fungi to terrestrial ecosystem functioning.

## The Dighton Framework

The [[biodiversity-of-fungi-ecosystem-services-agriculture]] provided by fungi
can be organized into five major categories: making nutrients available
(decomposition and mineralization), assisting primary production (mycorrhizal
symbiosis), participating in food webs (fungal-faunal interactions), regulating
populations and communities (pathogens and competition), and interacting with
human activities (bioremediation, pathogens, and biocontrol). Fungi are not
merely decomposers but are active participants at every trophic level in
terrestrial ecosystems.

## Nutrient Cycling: Making Nutrients Available

Fungi are primary agents of decomposition and nutrient mineralization in most
terrestrial ecosystems. Through extracellular enzymes, saprotrophic fungi break
down complex plant polymers including cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin,
and chitin, releasing nutrients in inorganic forms available for plant uptake.

The rate of decomposition is governed by the interplay between [[fungal-decomposition-resource-quality-determinants]], environmental conditions
(temperature, moisture, pH), and decomposer community composition. Fungi
dominate decomposition in acidic, organic-matter-rich soils, particularly in
forest and tundra ecosystems.

Fungi also contribute to soil formation through rock weathering, [[fungal-microaggregate-formation-and-soil-stability]], and production of soil-
stabilizing compounds (glomalin, extracellular polysaccharides).

## Assisting Primary Production

Mycorrhizal fungi are essential partners in terrestrial primary production.
Nearly all land plants depend on mycorrhizal associations for enhanced nutrient
uptake, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen. In exchange for plant-derived
carbon, mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root surface area and produce
enzymes that mobilize nutrients from organic matter and mineral sources.

Ectomycorrhizal fungi dominate in forest ecosystems, producing extensive
extramatrical hyphal networks. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are ubiquitous in
grasslands and agricultural systems and play critical roles in soil aggregation
through glomalin production. The discovery of carbon transfer between plants
through common mycorrhizal networks has fundamentally changed our understanding
of plant community dynamics.

## Fungi in Food Webs

Fungi serve as food resources for a diverse array of soil fauna and higher
trophic levels. Fungal hyphae and fruiting bodies are consumed by collembola,
mites, nematodes, insects, and mammals. The fungal energy channel represents a
major pathway of carbon and energy flow in soil food webs, complementing
bacterial-based and root-based channels.

Fungal-feeding fauna stimulate decomposition through grazing activities that
comminute organic matter, redistribute fungal propagules, and create fresh
surfaces for colonization. Changes in fungal [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] can cascade through soil food webs to affect
nutrient cycling and plant productivity.

## Population and Community Regulation

Fungi regulate populations of plants, animals, and other fungi through
pathogenic interactions. Plant pathogenic fungi influence vegetation
composition, from dramatic elimination of dominant tree species by introduced
pathogens (chestnut blight, Dutch elm disease) to subtle shifts in competitive
hierarchies mediated by root pathogens. Fungal pathogens of insects serve as
natural population regulators and have been investigated as biological control
agents.

## Fungal Interactions with Human Activities

Fungi interact with human activities in numerous ways. As decomposers, they are
essential for waste recycling and soil fertility. As mycorrhizal symbionts, they
enhance crop nutrition. As biocontrol agents, they offer alternatives to
chemical pesticides. Fungi also cause devastating plant diseases, threaten food
security, and produce mycotoxins. In environmental management, fungi play
important roles in bioremediation of contaminated soils, including accumulation
of heavy metals and radionuclides.
