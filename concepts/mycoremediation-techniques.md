# Mycoremediation Techniques

## Overview

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade, sequester, or remove environmental contaminants from soil, water, and other substrates. Pioneered primarily by Paul Stamets and expanded by researchers worldwide, mycoremediation [[mycoremediation-emerging-techniques]] harnesses the remarkable enzymatic capabilities of mycelium to break down pollutants that are resistant to conventional cleanup methods. This page covers the major mycoremediation techniques, their mechanisms, and practical field applications.

## Core Mechanisms

Fungi remediate contaminants through several primary mechanisms:

1. Enzymatic degradation: Fungi secrete extracellular enzymes (lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, laccases, cytochrome P450) that break down complex organic [[cotter-mycoremediation-techniques]] molecules
2. Biosorption: Mycelium binds contaminants to its cell walls through chelation, ion exchange, and surface adsorption
3. Bioaccumulation: Fungi absorb and concentrate contaminants within their tissues
4. Mineralization: Complete breakdown of organic [[cotter-mycoremediation-techniques]] contaminants into CO2, water, and basic elements
5. Cometabolism: Incidental degradation of contaminants while fungi metabolize other compounds

## Mycofiltration

### What It Is

Mycofiltration uses mycelial mats or fungal networks to filter contaminated water as it passes through a substrate inoculated with specific fungi.

### How It Works

- Mycelium is grown on a substrate (straw, wood chips, sawdust, coconut coir) in a contained system
- Contaminated water passes through the mycelial mat
- Fungal enzymes and biosorption processes remove or degrade contaminants
- Filtered water exits the system with reduced contaminant levels

### Applications

- Agricultural runoff: Filtering nutrients (nitrates, phosphates), pesticides, herbicides, and sediment from farm runoff before it reaches waterways
- Wastewater treatment: Secondary or tertiary treatment for municipal or industrial wastewater
- Stormwater management: Filtering road runoff containing petroleum hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and sediment
- Erosion control: Mycelial mats stabilize soil on slopes and stream banks while filtering water

### Effective Fungal Species

- Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom): Broad-spectrum degradation of organic pollutants, particularly effective against petroleum hydrocarbons
- Stropharia rugosoannulata (wine-cap stropharia): Excellent for filtering bacteria (E. coli) from water
- Trametes versicolor (turkey tail): Effective against a wide range of organic pollutants and dyes
- Ganoderma lucidum (reishi): Heavy metal biosorption

### Design Considerations

- Flow rate: Must be slow enough for adequate contact time (typically hours, not minutes)
- Substrate depth: 6-18 inches of colonized substrate for effective filtration
- Maintenance: Periodic replacement as mycelium exhausts substrate or becomes saturated
- Temperature: Most effective in moderate temperatures (15-30 C / 59-86 F)
- pH: Optimal range varies by species; most prefer slightly acidic conditions
- Pre-treatment: Large debris and sediment should be removed before mycofiltration

## Mycoaccumulation

### What It Is

Mycoaccumulation uses fungi to absorb and concentrate heavy metals and other inorganic contaminants from soil or water into their mycelial biomass.

### How It Works

- Fungal mycelium extends through contaminated substrate
- Metals bind to cell wall components (chitin, glucans, proteins) through chelation and ion exchange
- Some species actively transport metals into their cells (bioaccumulation)
- Contaminated fungal biomass is then harvested and disposed of as hazardous waste or processed for metal recovery

### Applications

- Heavy metal cleanup: Lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, chromium, copper, zinc
- Radioactive contamination: Some fungi accumulate cesium-137 and other radionuclides
- Mining waste: Treatment of acid mine drainage and tailings
- Industrial sites: Remediation of contaminated soils at manufacturing facilities

### Effective Fungal Species

- Pisolithus tinctorius: Accumulates copper, zinc, and aluminum
- Suillus luteus: Tolerant of and accumulates zinc and cadmium
- Pleurotus ostreatus: Accumulates lead and cadmium
- Trametes versicolor: Biosorbs heavy metals effectively
- Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi: Reduce metal uptake in crop plants while accumulating metals in their own networks

### Limitations

- Accumulated metals remain in fungal biomass and must be properly disposed of
- Not destruction — contaminants are concentrated, not eliminated
- Some metals are more readily accumulated than others
- Environmental conditions (pH, organic matter, competing ions) affect efficiency
- Field-scale applications require significant biomass and careful management

## Mycodegradation

### What It Is

Mycodegradation uses fungal enzymes to break down (degrade) complex organic contaminants into simpler, less harmful, or non-toxic compounds.

### Target Contaminants

#### Petroleum Hydrocarbons

- PAHs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons): A major class of petroleum-derived contaminants
- BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene): Common groundwater contaminants
- Oil and fuel spills: Crude oil, diesel, gasoline, jet fuel
- Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus) species are particularly effective

#### Pesticides and Herbicides

- Organophosphates, organochlorines, carbamates
- Atrazine, dioxins, DDT and metabolites
- White-rot fungi (Trametes, Phanerochaete) produce lignin-degrading enzymes that also break down many pesticides

#### Explosives

- TNT (trinitrotoluene): White-rot fungi can mineralize TNT
- RDX, HMX: Military explosives degraded by several fungal species
- Phanerochaete chrysosporium is extensively studied for explosive degradation

#### Dyes and Textile Chemicals

- Synthetic dyes from textile industry wastewater
- Laccase enzymes from white-rot fungi effectively decolorize dye wastewater
- Trametes versicolor and Phanerochaete are the most studied species

#### Polychlorinated Biphenyls (PCBs)

- Persistent organic pollutants once widely used in electrical equipment
- Extremely resistant to conventional degradation
- White-rot fungi show promise for PCB degradation through lignin-degrading enzyme systems

#### Plastics

- Emerging research area: Some fungi can degrade polyurethane, polyester, and other plastics
- Pestalotiopsis microspora: Isolated from Amazonian rainforest, can degrade polyurethane
- Aspergillus and other species show varying plastic degradation capabilities
- Research is early-stage; not yet practical at scale
