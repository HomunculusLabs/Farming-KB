# Mycoremediation Techniques

## Overview
Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade, sequester, or remove environmental contaminants from soil, water, and other substrates. Pioneered primarily by Paul Stamets and expanded by researchers worldwide, mycoremediation harnesses the remarkable enzymatic capabilities of fungi — particularly white-rot basidiomycetes — to break down or transform pollutants that are resistant to conventional cleanup methods. Techniques range from simple field applications using inoculated substrates to engineered bioreactor systems for controlled treatment.

## Mycofiltration
Mycofiltration uses fungal mycelium as a biological filter to capture and break down contaminants from water. Spawn or myceliated substrate is placed in contact with flowing water — through constructed beds, burlap sacks, or other containment structures. As water percolates through the fungal mat, mycelium captures particulates, absorbs dissolved metals, and enzymatically degrades organic pollutants including bacteria, protozoa, and chemical contaminants. [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] documents successful applications of mycofiltration for farm runoff treatment, where fungal beds downstream from animal operations significantly reduced pathogen and nutrient loads.

## Mycoaccumulation and Biosorption
Mycoaccumulation involves the uptake and storage of contaminants within fungal tissue. Heavy metals, radionuclides, and certain organic pollutants can be absorbed by fungal biomass through cell wall binding (biosorption) or active intracellular uptake. The fungal cell wall contains chitin, glucans, and proteins with functional groups (carboxyl, amino, hydroxyl, phosphate) that bind metal ions. [[fungal-biosorption-mechanisms]] details the chemistry of these binding processes. After accumulation, the fungal biomass can be harvested and safely disposed of or processed for metal recovery, concentrating contaminants from large volumes of substrate into manageable amounts.

## Enzymatic Degradation
White-rot fungi produce lignin-modifying enzymes — lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), and laccase — that nonspecifically oxidize a wide range of organic pollutants. These enzymes are nature's most powerful oxidative systems and can break down polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), explosives like TNT and RDX, chlorinated solvents, and many pesticides. The [[mycoremediation-white-rot-fungi-lignin-degradation-enzymes]] process is particularly effective because the enzymes' broad substrate specificity allows them to attack structurally diverse pollutants. See [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] for detailed enzyme mechanisms.

## Mycorestoration and Composting
Mycorestoration combines mycoremediation with ecological restoration, using fungi to rebuild healthy soil ecosystems after contamination. Inoculation with saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi helps re-establish nutrient cycling, improve soil structure, and support plant recolonization. Composting with fungal inoculants (particularly Pleurotus species) can degrade organic contaminants while producing usable compost. The [[cotter-mycoremediation-techniques]] approach emphasizes combining multiple fungal species to address complex contaminant mixtures, using oyster mushrooms for organic pollutant breakdown and mycorrhizal fungi for long-term soil health restoration.

## Field Application Methods
Common field application methods include: inoculated wood chip beds for water filtration; mushroom spawn mixed into contaminated soil; mycelial mats placed beneath contaminated sites; and fungal slurries sprayed onto polluted surfaces. The [[mycoremediation-site-assessment]] process is critical before application — understanding contaminant type, concentration, soil conditions, and hydrology determines which fungal species and application method will be most effective. Monitoring throughout the process ensures treatment goals are met.

## Practical Context
Mycoremediation Techniques is relevant to farming knowledge because it connects observation,
site conditions, and management choices. Treat it as a working topic rather
than a standalone label: the useful questions are where it appears, what
conditions make it stronger or weaker, and how it changes decisions in the

## Field Indicators
Look for repeated patterns across seasons instead of relying on a single
observation. Useful indicators include substrate or soil condition, moisture
availability, disturbance history, plant or fungal associates, and the timing
of visible changes. Recording these factors makes the page easier to connect
to local trials and future notes.

## Management Relevance
In a production or homestead setting, the topic matters when it affects yield,
resilience, ecological function, safety, or maintenance labor. Decisions should
be scaled to the site: small trials, careful monitoring, and comparison against
an untreated area are usually more reliable than immediate whole-system
changes.
