# Pulsing Model of Ecological Succession

The pulsing model of [[ecological-succession]] represents a paradigm shift from the classical climax model of ecosystems. Rather than progressing toward a single stable endpoint, many ecosystems are evolved to go through periodic disturbance as part of an overall dynamic stability. This concept, developed in systems ecology since the 1960s, provides a powerful framework for understanding land management, agriculture, and broader patterns of change.

## From Climax to Pulsing Paradigm

### The Old Climax Concept

The classical model of ecological succession held that disturbed landscapes progress through herbaceous weeds, grassland, pioneer shrubs, fast-growing trees, and finally reach a stable "climax" state dominated by long-lived species. This climax was seen as the endpoint — a steady-state equilibrium maintained indefinitely in the absence of further disturbance.

### The New Pulsing Concept

Ecological theory since the 1960s has recognized that many ecosystems undergo periodic disturbance as part of their overall stability. The disturbance may be external (bushfire, flood) or internal (defoliating insect plagues). While disturbance appears catastrophic at the organism level, for the ecosystem as a whole it is beneficial.

Pulsing ecosystems develop a pattern of long, slow **accumulation of biomass** (production) followed by a short, intense **pulse of consumption** where total biomass falls rapidly and nutrients are recycled.

## Four-Phase Model of Ecosystem Change

The two-phase model (production/consumption) can be elaborated into a more instructive four-phase model:

### 1. Conservation Phase

The long-lived, steady-state climax characterized by:
- High degree of interconnection between system elements
- Large amount of stored biological capital
- Little leakage of nutrients
- Maximum biomass accumulation

This phase makes the system vulnerable to disturbance precisely because so much capital is stored and available for release.

### 2. Release Phase

The pulse of disturbance, typically very short in duration. This could be fire, flood, grazing, cultivation, or any agent that rapidly converts stored biological capital. The release phase:
- Rapidly reduces biomass
- Makes stored nutrients available
- Creates open niches and opportunities

### 3. Reorganisation Phase

The most unstable phase, where:
- Open niches, soluble nutrients, and energy are available to be tapped or lost
- There is potential to flip to systems that are less, or more, productive and organized
- High risk of ecological drift to lower productivity (generally due to loss of mineral nutrients)
- Lower risk of major degradation (often due to coincidence of severe weather events)
- Potential for ecological invasion and possible shift to more productive systems

This phase is the critical juncture that determines the trajectory of the next cycle.

### 4. Exploitation Phase

Fast-growing pioneer species colonize opportunities, catch and store energy quickly, and cement patterns for the more gradual build-up of biomass and greater connectedness, leading toward a new conservation phase.

## Landscape Evolution and Catastrophism

The pulsing model was anticipated in geomorphology by the theory of **Catastrophism**, which suggests many landforms are primarily the result of very powerful forces acting for relatively short periods. Examples include:
- Mountain formation by volcanic activity at tectonic plate edges
- River courses set by catastrophic flood events rather than gradual erosion
- Erosion gullies formed during extreme run-off events (e.g., Victoria's gold rush period)
- Landslips and lake formation from earthquakes
- Massive dust storms moving more soil in one event than decades of normal wind erosion

The old theory of **Gradualism** still explains slow adjustments to forms created by catastrophic events — the gradual erosion of gully sidewalls, the slow weathering of landslips.

## Chaos Theory and Pulsing Systems

Chaos Theory provides a general framework for understanding the role of change and stability across scientific disciplines. It suggests events are not randomly distributed but occur in clusters, with clusters within clusters operating at multiple scales:

- The Big Bang → galaxy formation → star clusters → solar systems
- Geological catastrophism → gradual adjustment
- Ecosystem disturbance → succession → climax → disturbance

This hierarchical clustering of events connects the pulsing model to a universal pattern.

## Fire as a Pulse in Australian Ecosystems

Most Australian forests are adapted to periodic fire, which:
- Recycles nutrients
- Results in plant regeneration
- Provides flushes of nectar and seed for insects, birds, and animals
- Releases previously unavailable minerals in clays and rocks

Fire is a form of biomass consumption analogous to animal grazing but much more intense. Smaller, frequent fires reduce the likelihood of larger, more damaging fires. However, the optimal frequency and intensity for ecosystem health remains contentious.

Frequent fires can result in ecological drift due to loss of nitrogen and phosphorus. For any particular low-fertility ecosystem adapted to fire, a specific frequency and intensity provides optimal balance between stimulation and stability. The pulse of fire provides action and benefits, but the long slow phase of plant growth is equally essential.

## Applications in Permaculture Design

The pulsing model has several key implications for [[permaculture-design]]:

1. **Strategic use of change**: Using grazing, fire, or cultivation to provide a pulse of high yield between longer phases of biological rebuilding represents strategic use of change against a background of catching and storing energy.

2. **Frequency matters**: Pulsing the system too frequently leads to a downward spiral of land degradation. The benefits from the pulse are dependent on the slow accumulation of the building phase.

3. **The problem is the solution**: Understanding that destruction releases potential helps reframe disturbances as opportunities within the reorganisation phase.

4. **Design for the reorganisation phase**: The critical design challenge is what happens after release — ensuring the system moves toward a more productive and organized state rather than drifting toward degradation.
