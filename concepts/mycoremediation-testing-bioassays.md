# Mycoremediation Testing with Bioassays

## Overview

After deploying a [[mycoremediation]] or [[mycofiltration]] system, verifying that contaminants have been reduced to safe levels is essential. While professional [[solomon-soil-testing-laboratory-analysis-low-input-gardening]] provides the most precise results, low-cost bioassays using [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]] offer accessible, highly sensitive indicators of toxicity. These methods require no specialized equipment and are appropriate for everyone from students and home cultivators to commercial operators and researchers.

## Metabolic Product Harvesting

Fungi can be coaxed into producing novel antibiotics or enzymes when exposed to biological or chemical triggers. To harvest these metabolic products from a colonized mycelial block:

1. Insert small sterile, open-ended tubing into the biomass at an angle that can be capped, accessible from the exterior
2. Allow the tube to sit for one week so the mycelium heals and seals around it, making it watertight
3. Add biological or chemical contaminant solutions through the tube
4. If done correctly, the solution should not flow through but maintain a level throughout the experiment
5. The metabolic response (antibiotics or enzymes specific to the stimulus) can then be harvested

## Testing Biological Contaminants

### Cell Counting

Cell counting tracks bacterial growth rates and is critical for calculating the filtration efficiency of a mycoremediation system.

**Step 1 — Gram Stain:** Perform a gram stain on a small bacterial sample to validate identity and purity. Gram stain kits cost under $30 from science supply stores.

**Step 2 — Dilution and Plating:** Use the [[dilution-to-extinction]] method to prepare serial dilutions of the bacterial culture. Prepare three agar plates from each dilution and incubate overnight.

**Step 3 — Colony Selection:** Bacterial colonies appear as clear, yellow, or rose-colored dots on the agar surface. Select plates with thirty or fewer colonies for counting. Record the dilution rate for future direct counting.

**Step 4 — Calculation:** Count colonies on selected plates, average the three plates per dilution, and multiply by the inverse of the dilution to calculate bacteria cells per volume in the original culture.

**EasyGel Kits:** These [[easygel-coliform-detection]] kits don't require autoclaving and can be used anywhere. Coliforms are stained in different colors by type and are easily counted without microscopes.

**Sampling Schedule:** Since bacteria like E. coli can double every 20 minutes at optimal temperatures, pull samples at regular intervals (e.g., initiation, 30 minutes, 1 hour, 3 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, then every 6 hours). Dilute each sample, plate it, incubate overnight, and perform gram stain verification.

## Testing Chemical Contaminants

### The Bean Test

The bean test uses [[seed-germination-and-stratification]] seedling health as indicators of chemical contamination levels.

**Materials needed:**
- At least 70 bean seeds of known viability from the same stock
- 14 small jars or large test tubes
- Gloves and eye protection
- Distilled water
- Sterile pipettes

**Procedure:**

1. Label containers: one "control," one "0," and the rest "1" through "12"
2. Place five beans in each container
3. Add 10 ml distilled water to the control container
4. Add 9 ml distilled water to each remaining container
5. Prepare a 1 percent solution of the test chemical and add to container 0 (covering the beans)
6. Transfer 1 ml from container 0 to container 1, mix well; repeat sequentially through container 12
7. Each dilution magnifies by a factor of ten: container 1 = 1 part per thousand, container 2 = 1 part per ten thousand, etc.
8. Soak beans for 24 hours, then strain and save the water
9. Plant beans from each group separately in sterile soil, labeled by dilution number
10. Water seedlings with saved dilution solution, being consistent across all containers
11. Record sprouting rates, growth rates, and any abnormalities (leaf tissue damage, color variations, wilting) compared to the control group

**Interpretation:** The "safe" threshold is the highest concentration at which beans grow normally, matching the control group in all respects.

**Using with Mycofiltration:** Water harvested from a mycofiltration system can be run through the bean test without dilution and compared against the established dilution spectrum to estimate contaminant concentration and filtration efficiency.

**[[soil-testing]]:** For [[fungal-competition-survival-contaminated-soils]] or composts (especially those with persistent herbicides like clopyralid), plant herbicide-sensitive seeds directly into the sample. Many [[teaming-with-microbes-the-organic-gardeners-guide-to-the-soil-food-web]] have lost entire crops to persistent herbicides in composted manures, which can persist for 4–6 years after the original forage was treated.

### The Worm Assay

Worm assays determine soil toxicity by exposing earthworms to incrementally contaminated soil samples. Worms are highly sensitive to a broad range of hydrocarbons and other toxins that beans may tolerate.

**Materials needed:**
- Sanitized plastic cups (~250 ml) with perforated lids
- 20 worms per assay ([[eisenia-fetida]] or Lumbricus terrestris), cultivated (not wild-harvested), same species and approximate size
- Soil samples moistened with distilled or deionized water

**Procedure:**

1. Fill containers with soil samples hydrated with contaminant solutions at known ppm concentrations. Prepare five replicates per concentration plus controls
2. Inspect and record original worm color, reaction to light, and sensitivity to touch (healthy worms twitch when touched in the middle)
3. Add 20 worms per container, cover with perforated lids, and incubate at 65–75°F (18–24°C) with a small light above to discourage escape
4. Observe and record daily for 14 days, noting changes in behavior, color, or mortality
5. On the final day, sift through the soil to find and count all worms, recording final color, light reaction, and touch sensitivity

**Interpretation:** Compare results against controls and against manufacturer toxicity data or EPA registration documents for the chemical in question. Once baseline toxicity data is established, it serves as a reference for evaluating soil that has been through mycoremediation.

Worms can also be analyzed for coelomic fluids and blood to determine how and where bioremediation occurred.

## Follow-Up with Professional Testing

Initial bioassay results should be supplemented with professional laboratory analysis of the remediated water or soil. Key questions to answer:

- Did the mycoremediation achieve complete inactivation or degradation of the contaminant?
- Did the contaminant undergo biotransformation into different compounds?
- Is it possible the result is worse than the original contamination (biotransformation into a more toxic form)?

All data collected — whether from lab work, cell counting, bean tests, or worm assays — guides recalibration of subsequent trials. Good data drives fine-tuning of project parameters before committing to full-scale field installations where more is at stake. Success in mycoremediation depends on good data.
