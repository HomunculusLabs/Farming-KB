## Biosynthesis of Fungal VOCs

Fungal VOCs arise from multiple metabolic pathways.
Primary metabolism generates compounds like ethanol,
acetaldehyde, and acetone through glycolysis and amino
acid catabolism. Fatty acid degradation produces alkanes,
alkenes, and alcohols. The mevalonate pathway generates
terpenes and sesquiterpenes, while the shikimate pathway
contributes aromatic compounds. Sulfur-containing VOCs
such as dimethyl disulfide and dimethyl trisulfide derive
from methionine and cysteine catabolism.

The specific VOC profile of a fungus depends on species,
growth stage, substrate composition, environmental
conditions (temperature, pH, oxygen availability), and
interactions with other organisms. Stress conditions

Quorum sensing, typically associated with bacteria, also
occurs in fungi through volatile signals. Some fungi
alter their growth patterns and [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] in
response to VOCs produced by neighboring colonies of the
same species, coordinating collective behaviors such as
spore dispersal timing and resource allocation.

Sexual development in fungi can be triggered by volatile
pheromones. Trichoderma species produce 6-pentyl-α-pyrone
(6-PP), a coconut-scented compound that regulates both
antibiotic production and hyphal morphology. In
Aspergillus nidulans, volatile compounds mediate the
balance between asexual and [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]].

## Inter-Kingdom Interactions

Fungal VOCs mediate interactions with bacteria, plants,
and animals. In bacterial-fungal interactions, VOCs from
fungi can either stimulate or inhibit bacterial growth
and biofilm formation. The bacterium Pseudomonas
aeruginosa responds to fungal VOCs by altering virulence
factor production, while some soil bacteria migrate
toward or away from fungal VOC gradients.

industry uses VOC profiles as quality indicators—off-odors
signal contamination or improper storage. In biotechnology,
fungal VOCs serve as biocontrol agents against post-harvest
pathogens on fruits and vegetables.

VOC-mediated interactions are also relevant to indoor air
quality. Mold VOCs (MVOCs) produced by indoor fungi like
Aspergillus and Penicillium contribute to sick building
syndrome and serve as early warning biomarkers for moisture
problems. 1-octen-3-ol, at high concentrations, can cause
respiratory irritation and headaches.

## Detection and Analysis

Modern VOC analysis relies on gas chromatography-mass
spectrometry (GC-MS), proton transfer reaction-mass
spectrometry (PTR-MS), and electronic noses (e-noses).
Headspace sampling techniques capture VOCs from living
cultures without destructive sampling. Metabolomic
approaches now enable comprehensive VOC profiling
combined with transcriptomic analysis to identify the
genes and pathways responsible for specific compounds.
