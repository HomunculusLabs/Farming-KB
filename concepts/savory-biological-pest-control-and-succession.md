# Biological [[knf-pest-control-methods]] and Succession

Biological pest control uses living organisms to manage pest populations
rather than chemical poisons. While generally less damaging to ecosystem
processes than chemicals, Savory's framework emphasizes that biological
controls remain fundamentally symptomatic unless they address the
underlying conditions that allowed the pest to reach outbreak levels.
The relationship to plant succession and community dynamics is
nuanced and important.

## Examples of Biological Controls

Several biological control successes are documented:

- **Ladybugs bred to prey on aphids** in crop systems.
- **Cactus moth larvae** introduced to Australia to control prickly
  pear cactus, destroying the vast majority within five years.
- **Parasites attacking fly larvae** in feedyards and concentrated
  breeding sites.
- **Sterile male technique** for screw worm control, where released
  sterile males mate with females who die without reproducing.
- **Oxpecker birds** that control tick populations on African game and
  livestock.

## The Prickly Pear Success Story

Prickly pear cactus introduced to Australia in the 1920s thrived so
abundantly that vast areas became useless. Chemical or mechanical
removal cost more than the land's value. Entomologists found the larvae
of a small moth in the cactus's American homeland that proved to be a
voracious eater. Within five years of release, the moth destroyed the
vast majority of the infestation, reclaiming land at a fraction of
alternative costs.

## The Oxpecker and Tick Paradox

The African cattle dipping requirement illustrates how chemical
interventions destroy biological controls and create worse problems:

- Weekly chemical dips killed ticks but also virtually all oxpecker
  birds.
- Healthy oxpecker populations kept game animals remarkably clean.
- Without oxpeckers, tick problems escalated, requiring ever stronger
  chemicals.
- Nearby undipped game became badly infested once oxpeckers were gone.

Savory observed that the veterinary service was arguably the greatest
cause of tick-borne diseases in Africa -- a perfect illustration of the
cause and effect guideline.

## The Green Revolution and Succession

The Green Revolution foundered on its failure to respect succession.
High-yielding varieties required lavish chemical protection:

- Chemicals damaged living soil and polluted the environment.
- Insect damage actually increased as community complexity decreased.
- Resistant pests emerged, requiring stronger chemicals.
- Escalating costs forced thousands of farmers off their land.

The assumption that technology can supplant succession has distorted
scientific reasoning. An extension service booklet claimed Hall's panic
grass "can be maintained only on areas reseeded as pure stands" --
ignoring that the species maintained itself for millions of years as
part of a complex community.

## Species That Resist Succession

Some species actively maintain conditions favoring themselves against
succession: prairie dogs create open country for predator visibility;
harvester termites maintain open ground and reduced water cycles;
some grasses and woody plants exude allelopathic chemicals. These
behaviors mean succession is not always smooth forward progress.

## Genetic Engineering and Succession

Every organism must take its place in succession and cannot defy this
process. Genetic engineering shortens by thousands of years the
traditional breeding process, but the new forms remain subject to
succession's laws. Creating herbicide-resistant crops represents the
wrong kind of thinking -- treating soil as merely a medium for holding
plants upright. Genetic engineering will become a powerful tool for
good if handled with wisdom.

## The Prevention Paradigm

Savory argues science must shift from cure to prevention: study
function, community health, and underlying principles; focus on
preventing conditions that enable outbreaks; recognize that agriculture
suffers from the same myopia as medicine -- treating symptoms without
considering the environment that produces them.

## Biological Controls Within Holistic Management

Biological controls can play a role with important caveats:

1. Evaluate using all testing guidelines, including cause and effect   guideline and [[savory-weak-link-analysis]].
2. Do not rely on them as primary strategy -- address the ecosystem
   conditions enabling the outbreak.
3. They carry risks of affecting non-target species.
4. Generally less damaging than chemical poisons.
5. A step toward working with natural processes, even if not addressing
   root causes.
