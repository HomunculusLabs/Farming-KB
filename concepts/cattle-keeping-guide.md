Cattle are versatile large livestock providing milk, meat, draft power, and manure. For the small farmer, a single dairy cow or a small herd of dual-purpose cattle can provide significant food security.

## How Cattle Fit into a Permaculture System

- **Orchard grazing**: Can be allowed to graze in the orchard after at least 7 years (if sheep have not been harming the trees). They should graze to control grass, not clear all vegetation.
- **Manure production**: Large quantities of manure for composting and soil building
- **Milk**: A family cow provides a gallon or more per milking — enough for family needs and potentially a small surplus
- **Meat**: Steers and bulls provide beef; dual-purpose breeds work well for both milk and meat
- **Draft power**: Steers can be trained as oxen for farm work

## Feeding

### Ideal Pasture

- **Best dairy pasture**: Lush clover and alfalfa with grasses and herbs (lavender, mustard, rosemary, sage)
- **Winter hay**: Tender green hay cut just after blooming — 2 to 3 pounds per 100 pounds of body weight per day
- **Total winter hay**: About two tons of hay per cow over the winter
- **Feeding approach**: Don't give more than she can eat between milkings or she will not eat the old hay
- **Quality matters**: With good quality hay or grass alone, a cow will still give milk. If quality drops, milk supply drops.

### Grazing Without Fenced Pasture

- **Staking method**: Stake a milk cow with a large bucket of water and move her three times a day
- **Advantage**: Utilizes more pasture area efficiently

### Grain Supplementation

- **Purpose**: Increase milk production after calving
- **Ratio**: Maximum 1 pound (0.5 kg) of grain per 3 pounds (1.4 kg) of milk produced
- **With quality grass**: 0.5 pounds (0.23 kg) of grain gives about 90% of milk-producing capacity
- **Timing**: Slowly increase grain as milk supply grows, taper off near end of lactation
- **Processing**: Grains should be chopped or ground; mixing different types together is better
- **Supplements**: Cornstalks, washed and sliced [[growing-root-vegetables-guide]], whole sunflower heads
- **Alfalfa note**: Quality alfalfa pasture or hay may be rich enough to require no grain supplement

## Cattle Breeds

Breeds are divided into two groups:

### Dairy Breeds
- Higher milk production
- Generally gentler temperament

### Beef Breeds
- Bred for meat yield and quality

### Dual-Purpose Breeds
- **Shorthorn** and **Brown Swiss**: Bred to do both — most versatile for small farmers
- Young bulls can still be used for beef
- Steers can be trained as oxen

### Top Dairy Breeds
- Ayrshire, Brown Swiss, Guernsey, Holstein, Jersey, Red Poll, Devon

### Purchasing Advice

- Buy a 4 or 5-year-old cow instead of a heifer if inexperienced — she will be more experienced in giving birth and calving

## Cattle Behavior and Handling

- **Vision**: Cattle are red-green colorblind and have nearly 360-degree vision — they startle easily from peripheral movement
- **Blinders**: Put blinders on nervous cattle and they calm down
- **Routine-driven**: Their lives are based on habit — utilize herd instinct: get one cow to do something and they all will follow
- **Food motivation**: Call them with food as a reward and they will come willingly
- **Cows with calves**: Very protective — may need to be tied for safety
- **Cows in labor**: Can be violent — exercise caution
- **Bulls**: Never turn your back on a bull. Never let children around a bull. If a bull threatens you, eat it. Do not breed a bull younger than 18 months.

## Common Health Problems

- **Downed cow**: Cannot get up — may need help repositioning legs on a level surface; if injured, ensure she is fed and watered until she can be moved
- **Scours**: Severe diarrhea in young calves caused by gut pathogens. Treatment: Kaopectate (pectin and kaolin clay). Prevention: clean, dry birthing area; ensure calves get colostrum.
- **Heel fly**: Lays eggs in the heel; larvae crawl through blood vessels and bore through the skin. Prevention: reduce fly population with natural predators (frogs, lizards)

## Breeding

- **Breeding age**: After 1 year old (but beware — a 3-month-old can conceive)
- **Gestation**: 285 days (9.5 months)
- **Heat cycle**: Every 18–24 days; first heat appears 30–60 days after calving
- **Heat signs**: Restless, stamping feet, excessive tail twitching, making noise, red swollen vulva, other cows attempt to mount her, may try to mount you or run through fences
- **Timing example**: Breed in mid-May for early March calves
- **Dry period**: Dry up the cow 2–3 months before calving for a healthy pregnancy
- **Calving**: Bring her into the barn with thick clean bedding; she should be able to calve alone. Call a vet if no birth after 3–4 hours of active labor.
- **Reproductive life**: A cow can have a calf every year until 10–16 years old (rarely to 20)

## Milking

- **Frequency**: At least once per day or she will develop mastitis
- **Yield**: At least a gallon or more per milking
- **Colostrum**: First 4 days after calving — all goes to the calf
- **Calf sharing**: Let the calf run with its mother until weaned for easiest feeding
- **Family needs**: A family typically needs about a gallon of milk per day
- **Weaning**: Begin when the calf shows interest in other foods (grass, hay, creep feeding). Separate gradually. Wean by 4–5 months old.
- **Winter calf needs**: A weaned calf eats about 2 pounds of hay per 100 pounds of body weight per day. Three pounds of cornstalks and cobs correspond to one pound of hay.
