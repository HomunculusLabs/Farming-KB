## Overview

Mycoforestry is a set of practices developed by Paul Stamets that applies fungal ecology to sustainable forest management. The core principle is working with fungal networks to enhance forest health, accelerate reforestation, reduce reliance on slash-and-burn practices, and create long-term sustainable forest ecosystems.

Source: [[mycelium-running-techniques]]

***

## Core Principles

### 1. Wood Chips Are Ecological Currency

The central tenet of mycoforestry is that woody debris — branches, tops, and unmerchantable wood left after logging — should be returned to the forest floor rather than burned:

- Every ton of biomass removed from a forest represents a withdrawal from the forest's ecological bank
- Wood chips deposited on the forest floor feed saprophytic fungi
- Fungi decompose wood chips into soil, creating the foundation for forest regeneration
- Approximately 12 inches of wood chips become 1-2 inches of soil in 2-3 years with mycelial inoculation
- Without inoculation, the same process takes 4-5 years

### 2. Fungi Lead Ecological Succession

Mushroom mycelium is the first organism to enter and begin transforming damaged landscapes:
- Saprophytic fungi break down complex organic materials into simpler compounds
- Their activity creates conditions for bacteria, insects, and eventually plants
- This natural succession can be accelerated and directed through strategic mycelial introduction

### 3. Avoid Slash and Burn

Traditional forestry practice involves piling logging debris and burning it. Stamets argues this is ecologically destructive:
- The original tree removal represents a massive carbon and nutrient withdrawal
- Burning adds a second carbon withdrawal
- Combined, these withdrawals impair long-term ecological recovery
- 40-year tree crop rotations thin soils faster than they can be rebuilt, with diminishing returns
- Dense, even-aged replanted forests pack enormous fuel loads from dead branches

### 4. Inoculate for Resilience

Tree seedlings inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi at planting show:
- Better survival rates
- Faster growth (8% height increase, 7% girth increase in the [[cortes-island-mycoforestry-project]])
- Greater drought tolerance
- Enhanced disease resistance
- More efficient nutrient uptake

## Mycorrhizal Partnerships in Forestry

### Ectomycorrhizal Trees

Most commercially important timber trees form ectomycorrhizae:
- **Pines** (Pinus spp.) — require specific mycorrhizal fungi for survival
- **Douglas fir** (Pseudotsuga menziesii) — Rhizopogon species are key partners
- **Spruces** (Picea spp.) — diverse ectomycorrhizal communities
- **Oaks** (Quercus spp.) — Pisolithus and Scleroderma associations
- **Birches** (Betula spp.) — Leccinum and Lactarius partnerships

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Trees

Some important forestry species form arbuscular mycorrhizae:
- Many tropical and subtropical timber species
- Some temperate species including ash and poplar
- Red cedar (Thuja plicata) — Rhizopogon parksii association

### The Importance of Fungal Diversity

A healthy forest typically has many tree species, each associated with many fungal species. This diversity:
- Provides insurance against pathogen outbreaks
- Ensures nutrient cycling across different substrate types
- Supports complex food webs
- Enhances ecosystem resilience to disturbance

## Forest Restoration Strategies

### After Clear-Cutting

After timber harvesting, mycoforestry approaches include:
1. Retain woody debris on site (chip rather than pile and burn)
2. Inoculate seedling root balls with appropriate mycorrhizal fungi
3. Apply wood chips as mulch around planted seedlings
4. Install [[mycofiltration-of-water]] barriers on logging roads
5. Allow natural succession supplemented by strategic fungal introductions

### After Fire

Post-fire restoration with fungi:
- Ectomycorrhizal spore banks can survive fires in the soil
- Shrub species that sprout after fire feed mycorrhizal populations until trees regrow
- Inoculate with fire-adapted fungal species when natural recovery is insufficient
- Use mycofiltration to prevent erosion from burned slopes

### Degraded Lands

For severely degraded land:
- Apply wood chips as a substrate for saprophytic colonization
- Use [[stropharia-rugoso-annulata-mycofiltration]] for soil building
- Introduce pioneer plant species with mycorrhizal inoculation
- Follow natural succession, accelerating each stage with strategic fungal introductions

## The Economics of Mycoforestry

### Cost Savings

- Logging road reclamation with mycofiltration: approximately **50%** of conventional cost
- Reduced need for chemical fertilizers (mycorrhizae provide nutrients)
- Reduced replanting costs (inoculated seedlings survive better)
- Long-term soil improvement reduces future management costs

### Value-Added Products

Mycoforestry can generate additional income streams:
- Edible mushroom cultivation in managed forests (chanterelles, porcini, shiitake)
- Medicinal mushroom harvesting (reishi, turkey tail, maitake)
- Carbon credits for enhanced soil carbon storage
- Ecotourism based on forest health and mushroom diversity
