## The Retina as a Light Sensor Array

The retina forms a layer at the back of the eyeball covered with
rows of photoreceptors, comparable in arrangement to the rows of
LEDs in flat-screen televisions or the sensors in digital cameras.
Each point on the retina contains two types of photoreceptor: rods,
which are sensitive to all visible light, and cones, which respond
to different colours. The human retina contains approximately 125
million rods and six million cones packed into an area roughly the
size of a passport photo, equivalent to a digital camera with a
resolution of 130 megapixels.

For comparison, the highest-resolution outdoor LED displays contain
only about ten thousand LEDs per square metre, and common digital
cameras offer around eight megapixels. This enormous density of
receptors in such a small area gives humans their high visual
resolution, enabling the brain to construct detailed representations
of position, shape, brightness, and colour of objects in space.

## Rods: Low-Light Vision

Rods are the more light-sensitive of the two photoreceptor types,
enabling humans to see at night and under low-light conditions.
However, rods do not support colour vision. They contain a single
visual pigment called rhodopsin, which absorbs light across the
entire visible spectrum. Because all rods use the same pigment,
they cannot distinguish between different wavelengths and therefore
provide only monochrome information to the brain.

Rhodopsin's broad sensitivity makes it ideally suited for detecting
the presence or absence of light rather than its colour. In
evolutionary terms, this represents the more primitive form of
visual perception, analogous to the most basic light-sensing

## The Visual Spectrum and Light as Electromagnetic Radiation

Light waves occupy a narrow band of the electromagnetic spectrum,
between 0.0000004 and 0.0000007 metre in wavelength. Blue light
has the shortest wavelength in the visible range, while red light
has the longest, with green, yellow, and orange in between. This
is why the colour pattern of rainbows always follows the same
orientation, from short-wave colours like blue to long-wave
colours like red.
