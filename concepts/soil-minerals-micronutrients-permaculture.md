## Soil Minerals and Micronutrients in Permaculture

Understanding the mineral composition of soils is central to [[permaculture-design]]. Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* devotes significant attention to the elements of the periodic table relevant to soil health and plant nutrition. Rather than treating minerals as isolated inputs for conventional fertiliser programmes, permaculture regards them as dynamic participants in living ecosystems — recycled by trees, fixed by microorganisms, and made available through biological processes. This page summarises the key minerals and micronutrients covered in the Manual from a permaculture perspective.

## Magnesium (Mg)

Magnesium is a critical plant nutrient, found in nature alongside calcium in **dolomite**. It serves as the central atom of the **chlorophyll molecule**, activates essential enzymes, and concentrates in seeds. It is highly mobile within plant tissue and plays a vital role in energy metabolism via mitochondria. Deficiencies are rare, occurring primarily on sandy or sandy-loam soils; the clay fraction of most soils retains adequate magnesium. Where correction is needed, dolomite application is the permaculture-preferred approach, as it simultaneously supplies calcium and magnesium carbonate, which buffers plant and soil acids. Mollison observes that mineral deficiencies generally do not occur where human populations are healthy, linking soil health directly to community nutrition.

## Aluminium (Al)

Mollison describes aluminium as a "**Jekyll and Hyde**" element. It forms a large proportion of many soils and red clays, yet becomes toxic when solubilised — particularly through acid rain and sulphur pollution. Aluminium sulphate is poisonous to plants and soil life. At soluble levels of 0.1–1.0 ppm, susceptible plants begin to decline; above 15 ppm, widespread plant death occurs. Calcium in the soil provides a partial defence against aluminium toxicity. In the kitchen, aluminium cookware should be avoided for salty, acidic, or sulphurous foods, as the metal is readily leached. There is also evidence linking heavy aluminium-silica uptake to senile dementia. Permaculture design mitigates aluminium risk by maintaining calcium levels, avoiding acidifying pollution, and buffering soil pH.

## Silicon (Si)

Silicon is a structural component of cell walls in grasses and bamboo, and is essential to the **[[green-manure-crops]]. **Earthworm castings concentrate potash at 11 times soil levels**, making earthworm husbandry a key potassium strategy. Excess potash fertiliser increases soil sodium and blocks calcium uptake — a reminder that balance, not maximum application, is the permaculture principle.

## Calcium (Ca)

Calcium is needed in all soils and is an essential component of cell walls, enzymes, and chromosome structure. In drylands, sodium displaces calcium; the remedy is gypsum applied at approximately 30 tonnes/ha, which exchanges calcium for sodium and flushes the sodium out as sodium sulphate. Mollison specifies ideal agricultural soil ion proportions as **Ca:Mg:K:Na = 50:35:6:5**. Potassium can increase sodium levels; sodium is antagonistic to calcium; and magnesium should be maintained below calcium levels. Calcium phosphate is the chief mineral in bones. Low calcium levels produce predominantly male offspring in farm animals and humans. Calcium is lost during stress, linking nutritional adequacy to resilience.

## Titanium (Ti)

Titanium is not a plant nutrient, but Mollison highlights a remarkable ecological function: in sandy soils with sunlight, titanium acts as a **catalyst to produce ammonia** for plants, often in combination with iron (TiFe). This process can supply 50–100 kg/ha/year of ammonia, activated by rain and moist soil conditions. This represents a genuinely useful desert strategy — a non-biological nitrogen fixation pathway that operates passively in arid environments, supplementing the work of nitrogen-fixing plants and microorganisms.

## Role in Permaculture Systems

Micronutrients play a critical role in permaculture design, supporting plant
health and [[soil-food-web]] function. Deficiencies in trace elements like zinc,
copper, manganese, and boron can limit productivity even in otherwise
well-managed systems.

## Key Micronutrient Interactions

Iron and manganese interact closely in soil chemistry, with excessive manganese
often inhibiting iron uptake. Copper and zinc compete for absorption sites on
plant roots. Boron is essential for calcium mobility within plant tissues and
affects pollen tube growth and fruit set.

## Practical Applications

Permaculture practitioners address micronutrient needs through compost
applications, rock dust amendments, and seaweed extracts. Cover cropping with
diverse species helps cycle micronutrients from deeper soil layers. Mycorrhizal
associations enhance plant access to zinc, copper, and other trace elements.

## Monitoring and Diagnosis

Leaf tissue analysis provides the most reliable micronutrient assessment.
Visual symptoms include chlorosis (iron), stunted growth (zinc), and hollow
stems (boron). Regular soil testing every 2-3 years tracks micronutrient trends.
