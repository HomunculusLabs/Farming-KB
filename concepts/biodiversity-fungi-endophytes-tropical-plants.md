## Definition and Scope

The broad circumscription of endophytic fungi includes all fungi that inhabit plant tissues asymptomatically for some portion of their life cycle. This definition encompasses both clavicipitaceous endophytes (systemic, grass-associated) and nonclavicipitaceous endophytes (asymptomatic colonizers of woody perennials, bark, roots, and xylotropic habitats). The boundary between endophytes and pathogens is uncertain; many endophytes are regarded as minor or secondary pathogens that remain quiescent until host stress triggers disease.

## Diversity in Tropical Hosts

Studies of tropical plants consistently reveal rich endophyte communities:

- **Euterpe oleracea** (tropical palm): yielded three new species of Idriella and a new genus of loculoascomycete, Letendraeopsis
- **Manilkara bidentata** (broad-leaved tropical tree): 23 endophyte species from just three leaves, most known from a broad host range; Lodge et al. estimated one leaf per tree from three trees captured more than 80% of the endophyte mycobiota
- **Tropical hosts generally**: Intensive surveys invariably yield new species, and genetic variation among isolates of widespread species like Xylaria cubensis is high, presumably due to sexual recombination

## Endophytes and Global Species Diversity

Endophytic fungi may account for a substantial proportion of Earth's undiscovered fungal species. If the total number of fungal species approaches 1.5 million (Hawksworth 1991), more than 1 million remain to be discovered. It is almost certain that a substantial proportion of these will be endophytes. The endophyte-to-host species ratio in temperate plants exceeds 1, and a ratio of 4.0 could account for the bulk of the "missing" 1.4 million species. Whether tropical forests show similar or higher ratios remains under investigation.

Genera such as Phyllosticta — widespread, highly speciose, and nearly exclusively endophytic — may be vastly underrepresented in current species lists. The magnitude of host-endemic species versus generalists is likely influenced by regional differences in host-species diversity patterns between temperate and tropical forests.

## Sampling Scale

The magnitude of sampling required is enormous. At a scale of square millimeters, a 50,000-hectare tropical rainforest site (leaf area index ~12) provides approximately 6 x 10^15 mm2 of leaf surface area for potential endophyte colonization. Infection densities can range from 0.2 to more than 20 per mm2. The total number of endophyte infections in 50,000 hectares of tropical forest could range between 10^14 and 10^16 or greater.

## Ecological Roles

Endophytic fungi may influence host plant ecology in several ways:

- **Herbivore defense**: A possible role as antagonists of insect herbivores is frequently proposed. Secondary invasion of leaf galls by foliar endophytes has been linked to larval mortality. A relationship between endophytic infection, leaf miner injury, and premature leaf abscission has been demonstrated in Quercus emoryi
- **Host specificity**: Some endophytes show narrow host ranges (e.g., Rhabdocline parkeri on Pseudotsuga menziesii), while others (e.g., Xylaria cubensis) are broadly distributed across tropical and subtropical hosts
- **Latent pathogens**: Many endophytes exist as latent pathogens, becoming aggressive when host conditions change

## Specialized Endophytes

- **Penicillium nodositatum**: Forms specialized root associations ("myconodules") with Alnus species, resembling actinorhizae but confined to the outer cortical root layer
- **Ingoldian hyphomycetes**: Aquatic hyphomycetes commonly occur as endophytes in submerged roots (e.g., Heliscus lugdunensis, Tricladium splendens, Lunulospora curvula found in Alnus roots)
- **Gall-associated endophytes**: Plant galls harbor distinct fungal populations, including coelomycetous fungi introduced by gall midges that serve as larval food sources

## Distribution and Host Range

Fungal surveys of various hosts during the past 20 years have demonstrated that endophytic colonization of land plants by fungi is ubiquitous. Endophytes are known from plants growing in tropical, temperate, and boreal forests; from herbaceous plants from various habitats, including extreme arctic, alpine, and xeric environments; and from mesic temperate and tropical forests. Endophytic fungi occur in mosses and hepatics, ferns and fern allies, numerous angiosperms and gymnosperms (including tropical palms and broad-leaved trees), estuarine plants such as *Salicornia perennis* and *Spartina alterniflora*, diverse herbaceous annuals, and many deciduous and evergreen perennials.

Detailed investigations of the internal mycobiota of plants frequently uncover novel taxa and reveal new distributions of known species. Because endophytic infections are inconspicuous, the species diversity of the internal mycobiota is relatively high (both within and among individual host species), and a relatively small proportion of potential hosts have been examined.

## Endophytes as Protective Mutualists vs Saprobes

Although the systemic, [[clavicipitaceous-grass-endophytes]] and the nonsystemic fungi of grasses and other hosts are both considered endophytes, they differ in important ways and should not be regarded as biologically or ecologically homologous:

- **Grass endophytes**: Few species (primarily Clavicipitaceae), extensive internal colonization, occurring in several host species, systemic and seed-transmitted. Each host is colonized by only one species.
- **Nongrass endophytes**: Many species, taxonomically diverse, restricted internal colonization, most with limited host species, nonsystemic and spore-transmitted. Hosts are infected by several species concurrently.

Much has been published on the highly specific nature of grass-endophyte symbiosis, the effects of fungal alkaloids in infected hosts on vertebrate and invertebrate herbivores, and on the apparently greater vigor of endophyte-infected grasses. Pervasive systemic colonization of host tissue with endophyte hyphae ensures that herbivores will encounter fungal metabolites in their meal.

## Secondary Metabolite Production

Endophytes produce a remarkable array of biologically active secondary metabolites:

- Fisher and colleagues (1984b) reported antibacterial or antifungal activity for more than 30% of endophytic isolates from ericaceous plants
- Strains of *Pezicula* species (and its anamorph *Cryptosporiopsis*) from several deciduous and coniferous tree hosts produce ensembles of bioactive secondary metabolites in culture
- Endophytic species of the Xylariaceae frequently produce compounds with high biological activity, including cytochalasins and indole diterpenes
- Nongrass endophytes produce antifungal or antibacterial substances, as well as insecticidal compounds, in vitro

However, it remains unclear whether these metabolites are produced in plants during the period of quiescent occupation by endophytes, or in sufficient concentrations to benefit the host in a protective mutualism. In vitro, many of these compounds are intracellular and so, although they may have survival value for the endophyte through interference competition, their general role in protection of living hosts has not yet been determined.

## Latent Pathogens and the Endophyte-Pathogen Continuum

Fungal pathogens of particular hosts also commonly are isolated as endophytes. Such fungi are not usually among the most abundant isolates from apparently healthy tissue, but they are consistent and recurrent components of a characteristic host mycobiota. Typical pathogens include:

- Anthracnoses such as *Apiognomonia venita* on *Platanus* species and *Apiognomonia errabunda* on *Fagus* species
- *Colletotrichum* species on numerous hosts
- The canker pathogens *Melanconis alni* and *Diplodina acerina*
- Conifer needle pathogens such as *Cyclaneusma minus*, *Lophodermium seditiosum*, and *Rhizosphaera kalkhoffii*, recurrently found in asymptomatic foliage

The occupation of host tissue prior to either natural senescence or induced necrosis gives endophytes an advantage over saprobes normally excluded from healthy tissue. Endophytes that are quiescent during the normal lifespan of deciduous host organs can immediately intercept and use host metabolites mobilized during early senescence.

## Endophyte Succession on Decomposing Litter

Species of endophytes inhabiting leaf and stem tissue in the canopy of forests also can be isolated during the early stages of litter decomposition. Many common endophytic fungi represent the earliest fungi to colonize tissue as latent invaders. They grow and sporulate rapidly in response to senescence and can be isolated from litter in the early stages of decomposition, but they gradually are replaced by saprobic fungi more typical of decomposer assemblages. This behavior has been examined in successional studies of both deciduous and evergreen hosts.

## Molecular Methods and Taxonomic Challenges

Identification of endophytic fungi usually involves microscopic examination of host tissue and often requires a high degree of taxonomic expertise. This is especially true for isolates in pure culture that fail to produce spores or identifiable structures; determination of growth conditions that induce sporulation is very important. Fungi that neither grow nor sporulate in culture must be detected and identified by other means, such as comparisons of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) gene sequences, which also can elucidate phylogenetic position.

The absence or deficiency of basic taxonomic information is a major obstacle to ecological studies of endophytic fungi. The problem can be overcome partially by integrating existing databases (host indices, nomenclature), but fundamental biological survey work also is needed. Future investigations should include studies aimed at detecting the production of antibiotics and pest deterrents in plants as a first step toward evaluating the ecological significance of secondary metabolite production by endophytes and the potential use of endophytes in biological control.
