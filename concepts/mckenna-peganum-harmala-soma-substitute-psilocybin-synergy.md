## Haoma and the Zoroastrian Complication

In the Iranian tradition, Soma is known as **Haoma**. The relationship between
Zoroaster (circa 575 BC) and the Haoma rite is ambiguous and deeply contested.
Zoroaster's approach to the ancient sacrament appears repressive: only two references
to Haoma appear in the Gathas, his oldest texts. One calls it "Duroaosa, averter of
death" — suggesting a grudging acknowledgment of its power — while the other condemns
"the filthiness of this intoxicant."

Yet in the later Avestan texts, particularly **Yasna IX–X**, Haoma reappears in nearly
every respect as the same entity as the Vedic Soma. R.C. Zahner has argued that
Zoroaster may never have actually abolished the Haoma rite at all, and that it remained
the central liturgical act of Zoroastrianism throughout its history. The prohibition
recorded in the Gathas may have been directed at **bull sacrifice** — a practice
associated with the older goddess-worshipping cults — rather than at the plant itself.

This ambiguity is critical to McKenna's argument. If the Haoma rite persisted even
under Zoroaster's reform, then the identity of the plant matters profoundly, and the
possibility that it changed over time — from mushroom to Syrian rue — becomes
historically plausible.

## Flattery and Schwartz: The Case for Syrian Rue

The strongest modern argument for Peganum harmala as the original Soma/Haoma was made
by David Flattery and Martin Schwartz in their scholarly work ***Haoma and Harmaline***
(1989). They contended that Sir William Jones's original 1794 identification of Vedic
Soma with Syrian rue was correct — a conclusion most subsequent scholars had dismissed.

Flattery and Schwartz drew on **Zend Avesta** and **Parsi religious materials** that
other researchers had passed over. Their reading of these texts led them to a plant
with yellowish coloration, mountain origin, and hanging branch morphology — all
consistent with Peganum harmala. The Soma ritual required large quantities prepared
through three daily pressings, which also aligns with the need to brew Syrian rue
seeds in volume to achieve psychoactive concentrations.

Flattery wrote that "the consumption of sauma [Soma] may have been the only means
recognized in Iranian religion of seeing into menog existence before death," and that
"the experience of the effects of sauma must have at one time been required of all
priests (or shamans antecedent to them)." This framing places Soma not as a casual
intoxicant but as a **mandatory visionary technology** for the religious elite.

## Harmaline vs. Harmine: Pharmacological Distinctions

A key point in McKenna's presentation is the pharmacological distinction between
**harmaline** and **harmine**, two closely related [[banisteriopsis-caapi]]*, the ayahuasca vine.

McKenna notes that harmaline is **more psychoactive and less toxic** than harmine. This
is significant because it suggests that Syrian rue, brewed to sufficient strength, can
produce a **reliable ecstatic hallucinogenic experience** on its own — without the
addition of any other psychoactive compound. This distinguishes it from the ayahuasca
paradigm, where Banisteriopsis caapi alone is not typically considered sufficiently
visionary without the addition of [[psilocybin-mushrooms]]. Just as harmine in
ayahuasca acts as a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) that allows DMT to become
orally active — transforming a compound normally broken down by the digestive system
into a potent visionary agent — harmaline from Syrian rue could potentiate and extend
the effects of psilocybin when the two are consumed together.

McKenna envisions a historical scenario in which:

1. **Psilocybin mushrooms** were the original Soma, consumed in elaborate Vedic
   and pre-Vedic rites.
2. As mushroom supplies dwindled due to ecological change, **Peganum harmala** was
   adopted as a partial substitute, sometimes combined with remaining mushroom
   supplies to stretch and amplify their effects.
3. Over generations, Syrian rue may have **supplanted mushrooms altogether**, becoming
   the primary sacrament while preserving the ritual form of the older mushroom
   ceremony.

This model elegantly explains why the Soma/Haoma rite persisted in form even as the
identity of the plant appears to have changed. The ritual structure — the pressing,
the three daily preparations, the hymns and invocations — would have survived the
transition from one active ingredient to another.

## Other Soma Candidates

McKenna acknowledges that many plants have been proposed as the identity of Soma,
though he finds them individually unconvincing:

- **Ephedra** — source of ephedrine; stimulating but not visionary
- **Sarcostemma** — a relative of American milkweeds
- **Cannabis** — psychoactive but not consistent with Vedic descriptions
- **Periploca** — a leafless climbing plant
- **Fermented mare's milk** or **fermented honey** — intoxicating but mundane
- Various **mixtures** of the above

McKenna's objection to these candidates is that none adequately account for the
overwhelmingly visionary, ecstastic qualities attributed to Soma in the [[rig-veda-soma-hymns-amanita-muscaria]],
nor do they fit the botanical descriptions as well as either psilocybin mushrooms
or Peganum harmala.

## Significance

McKenna's hypothesis about Peganum harmala as a Soma substitute is more than a
botanical speculation. It represents a model for how **entheogenic religions
transition** between plant sacraments — how the form of the rite persists even as
the substance changes, how pharmacological synergy facilitates this transition, and
how ecological pressures drive the search for substitutes. The Syrian rue–psilocybin
relationship mirrors the ayahuasca paradigm across continents, suggesting a deep
structural pattern in human relationships with psychoactive plants.
