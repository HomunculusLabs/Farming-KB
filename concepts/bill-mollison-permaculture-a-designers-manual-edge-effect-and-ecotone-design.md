# Edge Effect and Ecotone Design The edge effect is one of the most productive concepts in permaculture design. Bill Mollison observed that the boundary between two ecological communities, called an ecotone, is typically more diverse and productive than either community alone. Where forest meets meadow, where land meets water, where shade meets sun, species from both communities coexist along with specialists adapted to the boundary conditions. Mollison elevated this ecological observation to a design principle, arguing that permaculture designers should deliberately increase edge in their designs to maximize diversity and productivity. ### Forest Edge

The boundary between forest and open land is one of the most productive edges

## Limitations and Considerations

While maximizing edge is generally beneficial, Mollison acknowledged
situations where too much edge can be counterproductive. In very hot, dry
climates, excessive edge can increase moisture loss and soil desiccation.
In fire-prone areas, complex edges can create difficult-to-defend boundaries.
Some species require interior habitat conditions and are disadvantaged by
edge effects. The skilled designer balances edge maximization with these
considerations, creating edge where it adds value and maintaining interior
conditions where they are needed. This nuanced approach distinguishes
permaculture design from simple rule-following and reflects the deeper
ecological understanding that Mollison sought to cultivate.
## Spiral and Keyhole Edge Patterns

Mollison emphasized that edge is not merely a property of boundaries
between large zones but can be deliberately designed into individual
elements. The herb spiral is perhaps the most iconic example, creating
maximum edge between microclimates in a compact vertical structure. A
single spiral creates transitions from moist shade at the base to dry
sun at the top, from cool north-facing surfaces to warm south-facing
surfaces. Keyhole beds use a similar principle, creating a concave
access path that increases the usable edge of a raised bed while
reducing the path area needed. These patterns demonstrate how edge
design operates at every scale from the landscape down to the garden
