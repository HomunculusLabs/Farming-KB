# Vermicomposting

Vermicomposting is the process of using earthworms to decompose organic waste into vermicast (worm castings) — one of the finest soil amendments available. Worm castings are rich in plant-available nutrients, beneficial microorganisms, humic acids, and plant growth hormones. Vermicomposting can be done indoors or outdoors, in small apartments or on large homesteads, making it one of the most accessible composting methods.

## Benefits of Vermicomposting

- **Nutrient-rich castings**: Castings contain NPK roughly 1-1-1, plus a full spectrum of micronutrients, humic acids, and plant growth hormones. Nutrients are in plant-available forms.
- **Microbial diversity**: Worm guts and castings teem with beneficial bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms.
- **Year-round composting**: Can be done indoors in any climate.
- **Compact**: Requires very little space compared to traditional composting.
- **Odor-free when managed properly**: A healthy worm bin smells like earth, not rot.
- **Waste reduction**: Diverts kitchen scraps from the waste stream.
- [[composting-systems]] covers traditional thermophilic composting as a complementary approach.

## Worm Species

### Eisenia fetida (Red Wiggler)
The most common and versatile composting worm. Also called redworm, brandling worm, or manure worm.

- **Size**: 2-3 inches fully grown
- **Temperature range**: 40-90°F (ideal: 55-77°F)
- **Reproduction rate**: Very fast — doubles population every 2-3 months under ideal conditions
- **Appetite**: Consumes roughly half its body weight per day
- **Availability**: Widely available online, at garden centers, and through worm farms

### Eisenia hortensis (European Nightcrawler)
Larger worm that is both a composter and a bait worm.

- **Size**: 3-4 inches fully grown
- **Temperature range**: Similar to red wigglers but slightly more cold-tolerant
- **Reproduction rate**: Slower than E. fetida
- **Appetite**: Slightly less than red wigglers

### Perionyx excavatus (Indian Blue Worm)
Tropical species that processes waste quickly but is sensitive to temperature fluctuations. Not recommended for beginners or cooler climates.

**Recommendation**: Start with Eisenia fetida (red wigglers). They are the most forgiving, widely available, and efficient composters.

## Bin Setup

### Bin Types

**Plastic bin (stacking tray system)**:
- Commercial units like the Worm Factory or VermiHut use stacked trays.
- Worms migrate upward through mesh floors as food is added to upper trays.
- Convenient harvesting — bottom tray is finished castings.
- Good for indoor use; well-contained.

**Single plastic bin**:
- A 10-20 gallon plastic storage tote with drainage holes drilled in the bottom.
- Simple and inexpensive.
- Requires manual harvesting (screening or light migration method).

**Flow-through bin**:
- Commercial or DIY bins where material is added at the top and harvested from a grate at the bottom.
- Minimal disturbance to worms during harvesting.
- Best for larger-scale operations.

### Bedding

Bedding provides a moist, carbon-rich environment for worms. Materials:

- Shredded newspaper (black and white only — no glossy inserts)
- Shredded cardboard (no tape, labels, or glossy coatings)
- Shredded brown paper bags
- Aged leaves
- Coconut coir (peat-free alternative to peat moss)
- Straw or hay (chopped)

**Bedding preparation**: Soak bedding in water, then wring out thoroughly. Bedding should be as moist as a wrung-out sponge — damp but not dripping. Fill the bin 6-8 inches deep with fluffed, moist bedding.

### Bin Conditions

| Factor | Ideal Range | Notes |
|--------|-----------|-------|
| Temperature | 55-77°F (13-25°C) | Below 40°F worms slow; above 85°F they die |
| Moisture | 70-80% | Wrung-out sponge consistency |
| pH | 6.0-7.0 | Slightly acidic to neutral |
| Aeration | Aerobic | Bedding should be fluffy, not compacted |
| Light | Dark | Worms are photophobic — bins should be covered or opaque |

## Feeding

### What to Feed

- Fruit and vegetable scraps (most kitchen waste)
- Coffee grounds and filters
- Tea bags (remove staples)
- Crushed eggshells (provides grit and calcium)
- Grain products (bread, rice, pasta) — in moderation
- Shredded paper and cardboard
- Yard waste (leaves, grass clippings in small amounts)

### What NOT to Feed

- Meat, fish, bones, dairy (attracts pests, creates odors)
- Oils, grease, fatty foods
- Citrus (small amounts okay; large amounts are too acidic)
- Onion and garlic (small amounts okay; worms dislike large quantities)
- Spicy peppers (jalapenos, etc.)
- Pet waste (unless specifically running a hot composting system first)
- Glossy/coated paper or cardboard

### Feeding Guidelines

- **Pre-freeze scraps**: Freezing and thawing breaks down cell walls, making food easier for worms to consume.
- **Chop food**: Smaller pieces decompose faster.
- **Bury food**: Always bury food under bedding to prevent fruit flies and odors.
- **Feed in rotation**: Feed in different areas of the bin each time. This prevents overfeeding in one spot.
- **Amount**: Start with small amounts (1 cup per week per pound of worms). Increase as the population grows. A fully established bin can process about 1/2 pound of food per pound of worms per day.
- **Rule of thumb**: If food is still recognizable after 1 week, you're feeding too much. Wait until most food is consumed before adding more.

## Harvesting Castings

### Light Migration Method (single bin)
1. Dump the bin contents onto a tarp or plastic sheet in bright light.
2. Form the material into cone-shaped mounds.
3. Worms will migrate downward to escape the light. Wait 15-20 minutes.
4. Scrape off the top layer of castings from each mound.
5. Repeat until mostly worms remain at the bottom. Return worms and remaining material to the bin with fresh bedding.

### Screening Method
1. Shovel finished material through a 1/4 inch screen.
2. Castings fall through; worms and undecomposed material stay on top.
3. Return the screened material to the bin.

### Tray System
With stacking tray systems, simply remove the bottom tray (which contains the oldest, most finished material). The worms will have migrated upward through the mesh into the upper feeding trays over time.

## Vermicompost Tea

Worm casting tea is made by steeping finished vermicast in aerated water:

1. Add 1-2 cups of worm castings per 5 gallons of dechlorinated water.
2. Aerate with an air pump and stone for 18-24 hours.
3. Optionally add 1 tablespoon unsulfured molasses as microbial food.
4. Apply as a soil drench (undiluted or diluted 1:1) or foliar spray.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Foul odor | Overfeeding, anaerobic conditions | Stop feeding, add dry bedding, improve aeration, remove rotting material |
| Fruit flies | Exposed food, fruit scraps on surface | Bury food deeper, add a layer of dry newspaper on top, use a fruit fly trap |
| Worms escaping | Bin too acidic, too wet, too hot, or new worms adjusting | Check pH (add eggshells if acidic), improve drainage, reduce feeding, keep a light on above the bin for the first few days |
| Worms dying | Temperature extremes, toxins (pesticides, salt, citrus overload) | Ensure proper temperature range, review what was fed, remove contaminated material |
| Moldy food | Normal in small amounts | Bury food deeper; mold is part of the decomposition process and not harmful |
| Slow processing | Population too small, food too large, bedding too wet | Add more worms, chop food smaller, add dry bedding |
| Mites (tiny red/brown dots) | Overly wet, overfed bin | Reduce moisture and feeding, add dry bedding, leave bin open briefly |
