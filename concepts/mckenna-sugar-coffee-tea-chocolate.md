## Sugar, Coffee, Tea, and Chocolate

## Broadening Our Taste

Modern primates such as baboons still test unfamiliar food sources carefully — examining for visual appearance and odor, then tentatively placing it in the mouth and holding it, not swallowing. A balance must be struck between excluding injurious foods and including as many sources of nutrition as possible. Evolutionary logic dictates that animals able and willing to tolerate many marginal foods will be more evolutionarily successful.

## Spices as the Engine of Exploration

"Variety is the spice of life" is an adage familiar to us all. Yet when we examine the impact of plants and plant products on human history, it seems more true to say that "Spice is the variety of life." Medieval Europe was one of the most constipated, neurotic, and woman-hating societies ever to exist — a society dying to escape from itself, obsessed with moral rectitude and sexual repression.

Dyes and spices became an absolute mania for medieval Europe. The arts of shipbuilding and navigation and banking and trading all turned to serve the near-addiction that most Europeans felt for these things. The breakup of Christian medieval Europe occurred as a result of an epidemic obsession with the new, the exotic, and the delightful — in short, with consciousness-expanding substances.

Cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, mace, and cardamom arrived to brighten the palate and the wardrobe of a wool-swaddled, beer and bread culture. Social boundaries were dissolved; old problems were seen in a new light; new secular classes emerged to challenge the power monopoly of popes and kings.

## Sugar as Addiction

Sugar is culturally defined by us as a food. This definition denies that sugar can act as a highly addictive drug, yet the evidence is all around us. Many children and compulsive eaters live in a motivational environment primarily ruled by mood swings resulting from cravings for sugar.

Sugar, whose existence depended on a savage slave trade, deepened its claim on consumers throughout the sixteenth century. The cycles began with sugar and expanded through tea, coffee, opium, and cocaine.

## Coffee and Tea: New Alternatives to Alcohol

For all practical purposes, tea, coffee, and cocoa were introduced simultaneously into England in the 1650s. For the first time in its history, Christian Europe had an alternative to drinking alcohol. All three were stimulants; all were brewed with hot water rendered free from waterborne diseases; and all required copious amounts of sugar. The sugar craze promoted coffee, tea, and chocolate use, which in turn promoted sugar consumption.

Caffeine is the alkaloid at the basis of much human involvement with stimulating plants. Caffeine is a powerful stimulator well below the toxic dose. Its tendency to become addictive is reinforced when sugar is used to make the ingestion of caffeine more palatable.

## Tea Brews a Revolution

The British East India Company was founded in 1600 and controlled the tea trade to its great advantage until 1834. Toward the end of the eighteenth century, Lord North's strategy to sell tea at reduced prices in the colonies and place a small tax on tea led to the Boston Tea Party of December 16, 1773. The salty tea of revolution was brewed that night, and the Declaration of Independence became inevitable.

## Coffee and Revolution

Coffee was not brewed as a drink until around 1100 in Europe, and only in the thirteenth century did the modern practice of roasting coffee beans begin in Syria. Caffeine in coffee and theobromine in tea were the ideal drugs for the Industrial Revolution: they provided an energy lift, enabling people to keep working at repetitious tasks.

Coffee was introduced in Paris in 1643, and within thirty years there were over 250 coffee houses in the city. In the years immediately preceding the French Revolution there were nearly 2,000 coffee establishments operating. If wild talk is the mother of revolution, then coffee and coffee houses must be its midwife.

## Chocolate

Chocolate, made from the ground beans of Theobroma cacao, contains small amounts of caffeine but is rich in theobromine. Cacao trees had been introduced into central Mexico from tropical South America centuries before the Spanish conquistadores. They had a major sacramental role in Maya and Aztec religion. The Maya used cacao beans as money. Montezuma was seriously addicted to ground cacao, drinking his chocolate unsweetened in cold water.

A mixture of ground chocolate and psilocybin-containing mushrooms was served to guests at the coronation feast of Montezuma II in 1502. In the short span of two centuries, four stimulants — sugar, tea, coffee, and chocolate — emerged out of local obscurity to become a basis for vast mercantile empires, defended by the greatest military powers and supported by the newly reintroduced practice of slavery.

## Exploitation Cycles

The opium trade was nothing less than British terrorism waged against the population of China. There is a pattern repeated in our own century: just as tea dealers turned to opium when their market suffered, so Western intelligence groups turned to cocaine after losing a near monopoly on heroin. The history of commercial drug synergies over the past five hundred years is not pleasant to contemplate.

## The Triangular Trade and Sugar's Global Reach

The sugar economy that McKenna describes was not merely a commercial phenomenon but a transforming force in global demography and ecology. The Atlantic triangular trade — manufactured goods from Europe to Africa, enslaved people from Africa to the Americas, and sugar and other commodities from the Americas to Europe — created patterns of economic dependency and exploitation that persist to the present day. Between 1500 and 1870, an estimated 12 million Africans were transported across the Atlantic, the majority destined for sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean.

The environmental impact was equally profound. Sugar cultivation required the clearing of vast tracts of tropical forest, the draining of wetlands, and the construction of extensive irrigation systems. Caribbean islands like Barbados and Jamaica were almost entirely converted to sugar monoculture within decades of initial colonization, destroying native ecosystems and creating landscapes shaped entirely by the demands of a single crop. McKenna sees in this history an early example of the [[query-how-to-grow-chili-peppers-from-seed-to-harvest]], vanilla, annatto, and sometimes [[mckenna-food-gods-sugar-addiction-slavery]]" — characterized by cravings, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and compulsive consumption despite negative consequences — has been documented in both animal models and human clinical studies.

The health consequences of the sugar economy that McKenna described are now measured in epidemic proportions. The global rise in type 2 diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease, and [[cannabis-metabolic-syndrome-research]] is directly correlated with increased sugar consumption over the past century. The World Health Organization recommends limiting added sugar intake to less than 10% of total caloric intake, yet average consumption in many developed countries exceeds 15-20%. The food industry's systematic addition of sugar to processed foods — a practice that exploits the neurological reward mechanisms McKenna identified — has made it difficult for consumers to reduce their intake even when aware of the health risks.
