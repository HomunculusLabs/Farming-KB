# Endophytic Fungi in Grasslands

## Overview

Endophytic fungi live within plant tissues without causing visible disease symptoms. In grasslands, the most important group is the Clavicipitaceous endophytes (family Clavicipitaceae), which form systemic, asymptomatic infections in above-ground tissues of cool-season grasses. These fungi produce alkaloid compounds that profoundly affect herbivory, plant competition, and ecosystem dynamics. Based on *Symbiotic Fungi*.

## Types of Grass Endophytes

### Clavicipitaceous Endophytes (Class 1)
- **Systemic**: Colonize all above-ground plant parts (leaves, stems, inflorescences, seeds)
- **Vertically transmitted**: Passed from parent to offspring through seeds
- **Sexual stage**: Epichloe species (choke disease); asexual stage: Neotyphodium/Epichloe festucae
- **Host range**: Primarily cool-season grasses (Poaceae subfamilies Pooideae)

### Non-Clavicipitaceous Endophytes (Classes 2-3)
- **Localized**: Restricted to specific tissues
- **Horizontally transmitted**: Spread by spores between plants
- **More diverse**: Include many ascomycete genera
- **Broader host range**: Found in warm-season grasses, forbs, and woody plants

## The Symbiosis

### Benefits to the Fungus
- Sheltered environment within plant tissue
- Continuous supply of nutrients (carbon, nitrogen)
- Reliable transmission through seeds (for clavicipitaceous types)
- Dispersal via plant growth and seed distribution

### Benefits to the Host Grass
- **Herbivore deterrence**: Alkaloids produced by the fungus reduce insect and mammalian herbivory
- **Drought tolerance**: Enhanced water relations and osmotic adjustment
- **Disease resistance**: Reduced susceptibility to other fungal and bacterial pathogens
- **Nutrient effects**: Altered root exudates and mycorrhizal associations
- **Growth promotion**: Under stress conditions, endophyte-infected plants often outperform uninfected ones
- **Competitive ability**: Endophyte-mediated protection translates to greater competitive success in mixed communities

## Alkaloid Chemistry

The four major classes of alkaloids produced by clavicipitaceous endophytes:

### 1. Ergot Alkaloids
- **Compounds**: Ergovaline, lysergic acid amide, ergopeptine alkaloids
- **Effects**: Vasoconstriction, hyperthermia, reduced prolactin in mammals; toxic to insects
- **Livestock impact**: Fescue toxicosis -- reduced weight gain, reproductive problems, heat intolerance in cattle grazing endophyte-infected tall fescue

### 2. Lolines (Pyrrolizidines)
- **Compounds**: Loline, N-acetylloline, N-formylloline
- **Effects**: Toxic to insects; relatively non-toxic to mammals
- **Distribution**: Produced by endophytes in many grass species

### 3. Peramine
- **Effects**: Potent insect feeding deterrent
- **Mammalian toxicity**: None detected
- **Distribution**: Common in many Neotyphodium/Epichloe associations

### 4. Indole Diterpenes
- **Compounds**: Lolitrem B, paxilline, janthitrem
- **Effects**: Tremorgenic in mammals; insecticidal
- **Livestock impact**: Ryegrass staggers -- tremors and incoordination in sheep and cattle grazing perennial ryegrass

## Livestock Effects and Management

### Fescue Toxicosis
The most economically significant endophyte effect. Endophyte-infected tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea) dominates approximately 35 million acres in the United States. The wild-type endophyte (E. coenophiala) produces ergovaline and lolitrem B, causing:
- Reduced weight gain and milk production
- Elevated body temperature
- Reproductive failure
- Rough hair coats
- Foot and tail gangrene in severe cases

Estimated annual economic losses exceed $1 billion in the United States alone.

### Novel Endophytes
"Novel" or "safe" endophytes are selected or engineered strains that retain insect-deterrent alkaloids (lolines, peramine) but do not produce ergot alkaloids or indole diterpenes. These provide the agronomic benefits of endophyte infection without livestock toxicity.

- **MaxQ**: Novel endophyte for tall fescue; widely adopted in the US
- **AR1 and AR37**: Novel endophytes for perennial ryegrass in New Zealand and Australia
- **NEA2**: Novel endophyte for tall fescue with improved persistence

### Management Strategies
- **Replacing wild-type with novel endophytes**: Most effective long-term solution
- **Pasture renovation**: Killing infected stands and reseeding with endophyte-free or novel-endophyte varieties
- **Interseeding legumes**: Dilutes endophyte alkaloid concentration in the diet
- **Supplementation**: Managing nutrition to compensate for endophyte effects

## Ecological Consequences

### Plant Community Dynamics
Endophyte-infected grasses can dominate pastures, reducing plant diversity. The competitive advantage conferred by endophytes may lead to monoculture-like conditions.

### Food Web Effects
- **Insects**: Endophyte alkaloids reduce herbivorous insect populations, affecting insectivorous birds and other predators
- **Soil organisms**: Endophyte-mediated changes in root exudates and litter quality affect decomposer communities
- **Nutrient cycling**: Altered decomposition rates due to anti-fungal alkaloids in leaf litter

### Invasive Species
Endophyte infection may contribute to grass invasiveness. Some highly invasive grasses (e.g., tall fescue) owe their success partly to endophyte-conferred advantages.

## Evolutionary Considerations

The mutualism is not always mutualistic. Endophytes can impose fitness costs on host plants (reduced seed production, altered competitive ability) that are offset by herbivore protection only when herbivory pressure is high. This context-dependent fitness effect explains why endophyte-infected and uninfected grasses coexist in many populations.
