# Precipitation and Global Weather

Understanding global weather systems and precipitation patterns is essential
for [[permaculture-design]]. Water promises to be the main limiting factor for
survival and growth, and the major future expense of food gardens and
agriculture.

## The Engines of the Atmosphere

Dense cold air flows continually off the polar ice caps. This high-pressure
air spirals out as persistent easterlies affecting high latitudes (60-80
degrees). Long spokes of this air curve outward to Latitude 30 degrees. The
spiral drives a series of contra-rotating low-pressure cells (turning
clockwise in the southern hemisphere), which mesh with rotating spirals of
high-pressure air that have risen at the equator and are falling at
Latitudes 15-40 degrees.

From Latitude 50-20 degrees, about 15-18 alternating high-low pairs of
great cells circulate the earth. On westerly coasts, the alternation of
cold polar and warm high-pressure air arrives at about 10-day intervals.

## Disturbances in the System

These great processions are disturbed and deflected by continents, stubborn
high-pressure cells over cool land masses, and the relative intensity of the
air cells. Cold fronts pile up against each other and deflect polewards. A
sequence of warm- and cold-front rains (cyclonic or spiral rains) results.

All wind belts shift north or south with the sun annually, and to some
slower extent as a result of the 18.6-year moon cycle. Periods of drought
and excessive rain can result. The system appears chaotic but we are
learning to assess some long-term cycle effects.

## Spiral Circulation

About 12-18 cold fronts (cloud bands) circle from west to east around the
poles, arriving as "cyclonic fronts" every 10 or so days on coasts. They
affect areas up to 30 degrees latitude. Each cloud front is a result of the
meeting of cold polar and warm sub-polar air masses. It is now clear that
oceanic circulation drives the air masses, rather than the opposite.

## Precipitation Basics

There are two basic inputs to precipitation: water falling from clouds
(rainfall, snow, hail), and condensation (water condensed or trapped from
sometimes clear air or fogs by cool surfaces). World rainfall averages about
86 cm (34 inches). We take 50 cm (20 inches) or less as semi-arid, and 25
cm (10 inches) or less as arid and desert.

Longer periods of increased aridity can be caused by deforestation on a
broad or local scale. It is because of the potential for changes in
precipitation that so much emphasis is placed on water storage strategies
and the conservation of water.

## Distribution and Intensity

Climatic averages are at best a very general guide to precipitation effects.
Of far more use is the expected distribution of rainfall (including extremes
such as 100-year flood records) and data on the intensity of rains. These
factors are a limiting influence on:

- The size of road culverts and dam spillways
- Storage capacity needed for dry periods
- Placement of plant systems, fences, and buildings

Attention to flood records avoids future costs and disappointment. If flood
data is omitted, life itself can be at risk.

## Types of Rainfall

Rainfall is distinguished by the processes causing rain:

- **Orographic:** The cooling of air as it rises over mountains or hills
- **Cyclonic or frontal:** The over-riding of cool and warm air masses
  of the polar circulation
- **Convectional:** Columns of hot air rising from deserts or oceans into
  cooler air

## Dew

Dew is a common result of clear nights, rapid radiation loss, and a moist
air mass over coasts and hills. It occurs more frequently in clear-sky
deserts than in cloudy areas. A slight wind speed (1-5 km/h) assists the
quantity deposited. Intensity of deposition is greatest 3-100 cm above
ground level.

Only in deserts is the 4-5 cm (1-2 inches) of dew per year of any
significance. Dew may be captured by loosely-stacked stone piles, where
low night winds cool rock surfaces and dew accumulates to dampen ground
below. The most efficient dew-collectors are free-standing shrubs of 1-2 m
in height.

## Fog

Chang differentiates between three fog types:

1. **Radiation ground fog:** On clear nights, hollows and plateaus cool
   rapidly and fog forms, often in much the same pattern as winter frosts.

2. **Advection fog:** Where cold offshore currents condense moisture in
   warm sea airstreams. These are the coastal fogs that plague many coasts.

3. **Upslope or orographic fog:** Where warm, humid airstreams are carried
   up hill slopes and condense as the air cools.

Unlike dew, fogs can provide a great quantity of moisture. Cloud forests
intercept enormous volumes of fog-drip, making them critical water sources
in many regions. Any strategy to generate, conserve, or store water is
critical to design approach.
