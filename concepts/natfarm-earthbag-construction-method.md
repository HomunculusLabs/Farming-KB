# Earthbag Construction for Sustainable Homes

An alternative to [[underground-house-and-earth-berm-construction]] houses is the popular and extremely [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] house
earthbag method. Rather than building traditional framing to hold up dirt,
the house is built with stacked bags full of soil. This creates urban water catchment
and is incredibly strong and long lasting. The bags can be covered with
adobe or plaster. This [[query-how-do-i-choose-a-natural-building-method-for-my-climate]] is a practical expression of

## Bag Selection

The bags can be recycled sacks, misprinted brand new bags, or burlap, but
polypropylene is stronger and lasts longer. They should be able to hold 50
pounds, at least 17 inches (43 cm) wide and 30 inches (76 cm) long. If
using bags of that size, you need approximately 140 bags per 100 square feet
(9 square meters) of wall. A thousand bags might build 700 square feet (65
square meters) of wall space. The shape of the house can be almost anything
you can imagine, and many builders create spheres or even cones.

## Fill Material

The bags must be filled with a mixture of materials, ideally from the land
on which the house is built. The mixture should never have more than 30%
clay and should not have too much organic material in it. Generally whatever
dirt you have should work fine. You can even use waste rock or gravel,
volcanic rock, crushed shells, or any mineral that compacts tightly when
compressed. The best material is a mixture of sand and clay. Some people
try to use topsoil, but since you need this valuable humus for growing food,
it does not make sense to use it for building.

## Construction Steps

### Step 1: Foundation
An earthbag house does not need a traditional foundation, although you may
want a poured concrete floor. First excavate a level area, which you can use
to fill your dirt bags. You may also decide you want a basement, in which
case use reinforced concrete.

### Step 2: Footer Trench
Dig a trench the width of the base of your wall, following the outline of
the house. The base width must be at least a tenth of the wall height: if
your wall is 10 feet high (3 meters), the base must be 1 foot (30 cm)
thick. Trenches in wet and [[swale]] beyond that to force water into the soil to
be soaked up by trees rather than by the foundation.

### Step 5: Fill and Lay Bags
Fill the bags with your soil mixture. You do not need to seal them unless
their ends are exposed. Normally, just fold the top over when you lay the
bag down. If you do need to seal it, use a stapler.

### Step 6: Tamp Down
Tamp the bags down to compact the materials. This is the most labor-
intensive part of the process. A long-handled tool with a flat plate on the
bottom is used to smash each bag as flat as possible. Without tamping, the
bags might shift and settle unevenly.

### Step 7: Barbed Wire Reinforcement
On top of each row of bags, lay two strands of four-point barbed wire. The
wire embeds into the bags and holds them steady under tension or during an
earthquake. This simple reinforcement is remarkably effective at stabilizing
the wall structure.

### Step 8: Openings
Windows in earthbag homes are usually triangular because that is the easiest
shape to make without adding support. For square windows, the lintel above
must hold the massive weight of earth above it. Interior walls and hallways
are often built with archways to avoid using wood.

### Step 9: Plumbing and Electrical
Piping and electrical wiring can be installed inside wood framing in various
rooms. Place the bathroom, laundry, and kitchen back to back, with all
electrical components centrally placed so wiring does not have to go far.
