## Group 1 — Agaricini (Gilled Fungi)

**Key feature:** Under the cap, thin lines called *gills* radiate from a common
centre like the spokes of a wheel.

- This is by far the **largest class** of larger fungi.
- It includes the common cultivated mushroom of commerce.
- It **defies generalization** — one cannot safely forage this group on the
  basis of broad rules alone. Each species must be individually recognized and
  confirmed before consumption.
- The class contains the dangerous **Amanita family**, which includes several of
  the deadliest mushrooms known (e.g., *[[amanita-phalloides]]*, the death cap, and
  *A. muscaria*, the [[fly-agaric]]).

> **Warning:** Beginners should exercise extreme caution with any gilled
> mushroom. Misidentification within this group has been responsible for the
> vast majority of fatal mushroom poisonings.

---

## Group 2 — Polyporei / Boleti (Pored Fungi)

**Key feature:** Under the cap, instead of gills, there is a **sponge or mass of
tubes** through which spores are released.

- When the specimen has a **well-defined stem** and the **pores are distinct and
  separable** from the cap flesh, it is classified as a *Boletus*.
- This group is considered **probably free from danger to human life** — no
  Boletus is known to be lethally toxic, though some cause gastrointestinal
  upset.

### Foraging guidance for Boleti

| Characteristic | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Mild taste, modest color | Often **delicious** and safe |
| Highly colored specimens | Have a **reputation of being poisonous**; approach with caution |
| Bitter or slimy varieties | Many exist; generally best **avoided** |

> **Rule of thumb:** Favor mild-flavored, modestly colored Boleti with firm,
> non-slimy flesh.

## Group 3 — Puff-balls (Gasteromycetes)

**Key feature:** No stem or cap — the fungus is **globular** in shape.

- When **young and white inside**, all puff-balls may be **freely eaten**.
- Palmer reports having eaten every species of white puff-ball for years
  without finding a hurtful kind.
- As puff-balls mature, the interior turns yellow, then olive-brown, and finally
  releases a cloud of spore dust. Only firm, white-fleshed specimens should be
  consumed.

> **Caution:** Always slice a puff-ball open before cooking. If the interior
> shows any outline of gills or a mushroom structure, discard it — it may be an
> immature *Amanita* button rather than a true puff-ball.

## General Safety Principles

1. **Know exactly what you are using.** Positive identification of every
   specimen is non-negotiable. "It looks like it might be edible" is never
   sufficient grounds for consumption.
2. **The Amanita family demands special vigilance.** Several Amanita species are
   deadly, and their toxins are not destroyed by cooking, drying, or freezing.
   Learn to recognize the universal veil (cup or volva at the base), free gills,
   white [[spore-print]], and annulus (ring on the stem) that characterize this
   genus.
3. **The [[russula-genus]] offers a built-in safety signal.** Any Russula that
   tastes good is safe to eat, because the noxious element in poisonous
   Russulas is intensely **acrid and biting** — comparable to the heat of
   smartweed or horseradish — and is easily detected on the tongue. If it burns,
   spit it out.
4. **When in doubt, leave it out.** There is no single test, myth, or folk rule
   that reliably separates edible from poisonous mushrooms.

## Cultural and Historical Context

- In America at the time of Palmer's writing, mushrooms were widely considered
  **valueless** and even suspect. This stood in sharp contrast with cultures
  where mushroom foraging was (and remains) a celebrated tradition:
  **Chinese, Russian, German, French,** and **Italian** cuisines all make
  extensive use of wild fungi.
- In taste and nutritional character, mushrooms occupy a place **between animal
  and vegetable food**, approximating the former more closely. Like meat, most
  kinds decay rapidly from decomposition and are subject to maggot infestation.

---

## Growth and Seasonal Behavior

- Mushrooms grow **extremely quickly** — a specimen can push above the soil
  surface in a single night — but most of the organism's development occurs
  **subterraneously** over days or even weeks before any above-ground sign
  appears.
- Different species **follow one another through the season** in a pattern
  analogous to the successive blooming of flower families.
- Some species produce **more than one crop** in a year, typically from **May
  through November**.
- Fungi are often found in **the same spots year after year** — experienced
  foragers learn the "regular haunts" of their favorite varieties.

---

## North American Foraging Potential

Palmer considered the American landscape to be **very rich in esculent (edible)
fungi**, suggesting that foragers willing to invest in study and careful
identification would find abundant reward.
