## The Lundberg Family Farm

The Lundberg family farm in the Sacramento Valley of California represented one of
the most significant encounters between [[fukuoka-natural-farming]] philosophy and
American commercial agriculture. When Fukuoka visited the Lundbergs, he found a
family that had already made a radical break from conventional rice farming
practices. The Lundbergs had transitioned to organic methods, eliminating synthetic
fertilizers and pesticides from their rice paddies, and had built a successful
[[cotter-mushroom-business-marketing]] premium organic rice to health-conscious consumers across the
United States.

The Lundberg operation was impressive in scale, managing thousands of acres of rice
using organic methods that most conventional farmers considered impractical at that
scale. Their success demonstrated that organic rice farming was not merely a niche
hobby but a commercially viable alternative to chemically dependent agriculture. The
Lundbergs had developed expertise in composting, cover cropping, water management,
and [[cho-natural-pest-control-methods]] that allowed them to maintain yields competitive with
conventional farms while producing a product that commanded premium prices in the
marketplace.

Fukuoka was both encouraged by what the Lundbergs had accomplished and challenged to
push them further toward his ideal of truly [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]]. He acknowledged that
their organic methods represented a significant improvement over conventional
agriculture but pointed out that they still relied on many practices that his
philosophy rejected: plowing the soil, flooding paddies with pumped irrigation
water, applying prepared compost, and using mechanical cultivation to control weeds.
True natural farming, in Fukuoka view, would eliminate all of these interventions.

## Fukuoka Critique of Organic Farming

A central theme of Fukuoka interaction with the Lundbergs was his distinction
between organic farming [[fukuoka-textdoc-three-stages-of-human-collapse-and-natural-farming-future]], a distinction that many people failed
to grasp. Organic farming, as practiced by the Lundbergs and other organic pioneers,
still placed the human farmer at the center of the agricultural system. The farmer
decided what to plant, when to plant, how to prepare the soil, what amendments to
apply, and how to manage pests and weeds. The farmer was simply substituting natural
inputs for synthetic ones, but the fundamental paradigm of human control over nature
remained unchanged.

Natural farming, by contrast, sought to eliminate the farmer as a decision-maker
entirely. In Fukuoka ideal system, nature would make all the decisions about what
grows, when it grows, and how it interacts with other organisms. The farmer role
would be limited to scattering seeds and harvesting, with no plowing, no weeding, no
fertilizing, and no pest control. The rice would grow in a diverse ecosystem that
included clovers, weeds, insects, and other organisms, all of which would contribute
to the health and productivity of the system as a whole.

Fukuoka was not dismissive of the Lundbergs achievements. He recognized that organic
farming was a necessary stepping stone, a way for people to begin questioning the
assumptions of conventional agriculture and to develop the skills needed to work
with natural systems rather than against them. But he was adamant that organic
farming was not the destination, only a waypoint on the road back to nature. The
ultimate goal was a food production system so integrated with natural processes that
it was indistinguishable from the surrounding ecosystem.

## Rice Growing Without Plowing or Flooding

Fukuoka proposed a [[query-what-is-the-kratky-method-for-growing-cannabis]] rice in California that eliminated both
plowing and the continuous flooding that [[jadam-conventional-and-organic-pesticide-integration]] rice production
required. In his Japanese system, rice was direct-seeded into a field that also
supported a winter crop of clover and grains, with no plowing between seasons. The
clover fixed nitrogen and suppressed weeds, while the straw from the previous rice
crop served as mulch. Water management was minimal: paddies were flooded briefly
during the rice growing season but drained for most of the year.

Adapting this system to California presented significant challenges. The California
climate was drier than Japan, requiring more irrigation. The California rice-growing
season was shorter. And the scale of California rice farms was vastly larger than
the small fields Fukuoka managed in Japan. Nevertheless, Fukuoka believed the
principles were universal and could be adapted to any climate with sufficient
observation and experimentation. He encouraged the Lundbergs to try direct seeding
with [[fukuoka-ladino-clover-ground-cover-management]] and minimal water management as experimental plots
alongside their established organic practices.

The potential benefits of natural rice farming in California were enormous.
Eliminating continuous flooding would reduce water consumption dramatically, a
critical consideration in drought-prone California. Eliminating plowing would reduce
soil erosion, preserve soil structure, and save fuel and labor costs. The diverse
ecosystem of a natural rice field would support [[coleman-beneficial-insects-and-ecological-pest-control]] birds,
reducing pest pressure without any pesticide application. And the rice produced
would be nutritionally superior to rice grown with any degree of artificial
intervention.
