# Holistic Decision-Making Framework

The Holistic Management decision-making framework provides a structured process for making decisions that simultaneously consider economic, social, and ecological factors. At its heart is a "thought model" that enables practitioners to see the whole they are managing and test every action against a comprehensive goal.

## The Three-Part Goal

In managing holistically, a goal is defined in three essential parts, all interdependent:

### Quality of Life
A statement of the quality of life that the people involved desire from the ecosystem. This goes beyond material wants to encompass values, relationships, community, spiritual needs, and personal fulfillment. For a ranch family, this might include creating a stable environment for family and staff, ensuring education for children, revitalizing the community, and enjoying life in the process of achieving goals.

The quality of life goal is often merely assumed or taken for granted, which is a critical mistake. Without it, people risk pursuing production in ways that destroy the quality of life they mean to sustain.

### Forms of Production
A description of what must be produced from the ecosystem to deliver the desired quality of life. This may be profit, recreation, cultural preservation, aesthetics, or other products. If profit is part of the goal, the description states whether from crops, livestock, wildlife, timber, or other forms—but not the specific species, as these may change.

Multiple forms of production can coexist. A ranch might simultaneously pursue profitability from livestock, cultural preservation of historic sites, and aesthetic goals for riparian areas.

### Future Landscape Description
A broad description of the landscape needed to sustain the production forms, which in turn sustain the quality of life. This description focuses not on current conditions but on what the landscape must become. It specifies how the four ecosystem processes—succession, water cycle, mineral cycle, and energy flow—must function.

For example: "Open grassland at a high successional level with scattered trees and shrubs, a mosaic of brush thickets and grassland along river bottoms, providing high complexity and stability, good mineral and water cycles, and high energy flow."

## The Self-Regulating Nature of Three-Part Goals

The strength of the three-part goal derives from how the parts limit and complement each other. Where a particular quality of life implies great material demands, the discipline of describing a landscape that can meet those demands without sacrificing ecological health checks the possibility of exploitation. Some environments simply cannot produce enough to satisfy unlimited greed, and reconciling all three parts addresses that reality.

The three parts must be worked through in a specific order: first quality of life, then production, then landscape. This order often resolves long-standing conflicts over land management because people drawn to the same land often seek similar quality of life despite different approaches.

## Common Errors in Goal Setting

Three common errors produce what Savory calls "nongoals":

1. **Setting production goals without reference to ecosystem or quality of life**: This is mainstream agriculture's primary error—technologically forcing production at the expense of landscape and community.

2. **Confusing tools and goals**: Declaring a goal of removing livestock from public lands confuses a tool (rest) with a goal. The landscape goal should come first; then use whatever tools necessary to achieve it.

3. **Excessively narrow definitions**: Goals like "eradication of brush," "preservation of a rare species," or "flood control" address symptoms, not causes. Brush encroachment is often a symptom of rest and overrest in brittle environments. Addressing the symptom guarantees perpetual work.

## The Testing Guidelines

Before implementing any action, HRM practitioners run it through a series of testing questions. Each guideline addresses a different dimension of the decision:

**Cause and Effect**: Does the action address the root cause or merely treat a symptom? This guideline is first because addressing symptoms rather than causes is the most pervasive error in resource management.

**Weak Link**: Every situation has a weakest point. Will the action strengthen the weakest link, or will it fail because that link remains unaddressed? Weak links can be social (people-related), biological (nature-related), or financial (money-related).

**Marginal Reaction**: When comparing multiple options, which yields the greatest return per dollar or unit of effort invested? This guideline prevents spreading resources too thin across too many initiatives.

**Gross Margin Analysis**: A financial tool for comparing enterprise profitability by separating fixed from variable costs. This distinguishes truly profitable enterprises from those that merely appear profitable because fixed costs are hidden in overhead.

**Energy/Wealth Source and Use**: Is the action based on a sustainable energy source (primarily solar energy captured by green plants) or does it depend on nonrenewable energy (fossil fuels, mined minerals)? Does the wealth generated build lasting community infrastructure or merely consume resources?

**Society and Culture**: Will the action lead to the quality of life sought by the people involved? What will it do to the quality of life of others? Does it strengthen or undermine the social fabric, cultural values, and community institutions?

Technology must pass all these tests: it should benefit the whole ecosystem, be socially and culturally acceptable, strengthen the weakest link, address causes not symptoms, be economically sound, and reflect wise use of wealth and energy.

## Feedback and Adaptation

The testing guidelines are not a one-time checklist but a continuous feedback loop. After a decision passes the guidelines and is implemented, monitoring reveals whether the predicted effects materialize. If not, the decision is revisited and revised.

This adaptive approach stands in contrast to conventional management, which often implements decisions based on research or expert opinion and then persists with them even when results are poor. The holistic framework explicitly acknowledges that our understanding is always incomplete and that the ecosystem will respond in ways we cannot fully predict.

## Goal Ownership

Goal formation demands patience, sensitivity, and time—potentially a year or more. All people who make decisions and are held responsible should participate. Top leadership must create an atmosphere of trust and collaboration. When people who share responsibility for land formulate their own goals, they develop deeper commitment and a sense of ownership that helps ensure success.

A useful exercise for owners concerned about opening the goal-setting process: write down your own three-part goal and seal it in an envelope. Then conduct an open discussion including all levels of people involved. When the group combines all opinions into a shared goal, open the envelope. Chances are the original goals will not differ much from the group's, but the group's version will reflect deeper understanding and commitment.

## From Temporary to Permanent Goals

Events often demand initiating HRM with temporary goals—a general heading toward the action, like a military pilot heading roughly toward combat before knowing the exact target. Within a year, however, the goal-setting process must be reopened to formulate more permanent goals. This may not change the goals themselves, but it deepens commitment and fosters smoother, more creative management.

Goals are never set in concrete. They are continually under review and subject to alteration as understanding of the model and its possibilities deepens.
