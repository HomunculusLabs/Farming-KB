## Mechanism

Microwave sterilization works through the dielectric heating of water molecules:
- Microwaves (typically 2.45 GHz) cause polar molecules (primarily water) to rotate rapidly
- This molecular friction generates heat throughout the material
- Unlike conventional heating, heat is generated internally rather than conducted from the surface
- This can achieve more uniform heating in some substrate configurations
- The non-uniform heating pattern can also create hot spots exceeding 100°C and cold spots that remain below sterilization temperature

## Applications in Mushroom Cultivation

### Substrate Sterilization
- Microwave treatment can be used for small-scale substrate preparation
- Particularly useful for [[mushroom-substrates]] that have already been pasteurised
- Effective for sterilizing small amounts of supplemented sawdust in microwave-safe containers
- Can be used for sterilizing [[mushroom-grain-spawn-preparation]]
- Not recommended as a primary sterilization method for grain spawn itself

## Advantages

- **Speed**: Much faster than pressure cooker cycles (minutes vs hours)
- **Accessibility**: Most kitchens have a microwave oven -- no special equipment purchase
- **No special equipment**: No pressure cooker needed, reducing startup costs for new cultivators
- **Re-heating**: Agar media can be re-melted multiple times if capped with breathable material
- **Energy efficiency**: Lower energy consumption for small volumes compared to running a large pressure cooker
- **Convenience**: Can be done in any kitchen without dedicated lab space

## Limitations

- **Penetration depth**: Microwaves penetrate only a few centimeters into dense substrates, making them unsuitable for large volumes or dense materials
- **Uneven heating**: "Hot spots" and cold spots are common -- stirring or rotation is essential for uniform treatment
- **Scale**: Only practical for small volumes (typically under 500ml per container)
- **Moisture loss**: Extended microwaving can dry out substrates, requiring careful monitoring and potential rehydration
- **Not reliable for grain spawn**: Dense grain spawn requires the thorough, uniform heat that only a pressure cooker provides -- microwaving grain often leaves contaminated pockets
- **Bag sealing**: Microwave-safe containers needed -- standard polypropylene spawn bags may melt or deform
- **No pressure advantage**: Unlike a pressure cooker, microwaves cannot achieve temperatures above 100°C at atmospheric pressure, limiting effectiveness against heat-resistant endospores

## Best Practices

1. Use microwave-safe containers only (glass, certain plastics marked as microwave-safe)
2. Heat in short bursts (1-2 minutes) with stirring or rotation between bursts
3. Cover loosely to allow steam escape while preventing contamination entry
4. Monitor moisture content -- add sterile water if needed after treatment
5. For agar: cap flasks with polyfill (allows microwave reheating without pressure buildup)
6. Do not use for primary grain spawn sterilisation -- [[microwave-sterilization]]
