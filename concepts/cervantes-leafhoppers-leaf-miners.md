# Leafhoppers and Leaf Miners on Cannabis

## Leafhoppers

### Identification

Leafhoppers include many small, 0.125 inch (3 mm) long,
wedge-shaped insects that are usually green, white, or yellow.
Many species have minute stripes on wings and bodies. Their
wings peak like roof rafters when not in use. Leafhoppers suck
plant sap for food and exude sticky honeydew as a by-product.

Spittlebug and leafhopper larvae wrap themselves in foliage and
envelop themselves in a saliva-like liquid derived from plant
sap, creating a protective frothy spittle mass that shields
them from predators and environmental exposure.

### Damage

Leafhopper feeding produces stippling (spotting) similar to
that caused by [[cervantes-spider-mites-identification-control]] and [[cervantes-thrips-pest-control]] on foliage.
Leaves lose vigor as chlorophyll production diminishes. In
severe cases, the combined stress of feeding damage and honeydew
accumulation can lead to plant death.

### Control

- Cultural and physical: Cleanliness is the foundation of
  leafhopper control. Black light traps are attractive to some
  leafhopper species and related beetles
- Biological: The fungus Metarhizium anisopliae is
  commercially available under the trade name Metaquino and
  provides effective biological control
- Sprays: [[cervantes-pyrethrum-pyrethroid-insecticides]],
  rotenone, and sabadilla offer chemical control options
- [[cervantes-homemade-pest-disease-sprays]]
  containing garlic and hot pepper can deter leafhopper
  feeding

## Leaf Miners

### Identification

Adult leaf miner flies lay eggs that hatch into one-eighth-inch
(0.25 mm) long green or black maggots. The maggots are rarely
seen before the leaf damage they create becomes apparent. Leaf
miners are more common in [[query-how-do-i-choose-between-indoor-outdoor-and-greenhouse-cannabis-growing]]
and outdoor gardens than in indoor grow rooms.

The telltale sign of leaf miner activity is whitish tunnel
outlines visible on leaf surfaces as the maggots burrow
between the upper and lower leaf surfaces (leaf mesophyll
tissue). The damage usually occurs on or in young, supple
growth.

### Damage

The tiny maggots burrow between leaf surfaces, creating
characteristic winding tunnels. While seldom fatal, unchecked
infestations slow plant growth, prolong flowering, and produce
smaller buds. In rare cases the damage is fatal. Wound damage
from mining activity also encourages secondary disease entry
through the compromised leaf tissue.

### Control

Leaf miners cause little problem to indoor crops. The most
efficient and effective control is to remove and dispose of
damaged foliage, which includes the trapped maggot.

- Manual control: Smash the maggot trapped within the leaf
  tunnel using your fingers. If the infestation is severe,
  smash all visible larvae and remove severely infested leaves.
  Compost or burn infested leaves
- Yellow sticky traps: Install yellow sticky traps to capture
  adult flies before they lay eggs
- Biological: Braconid wasp (Dacnusa sibirica), chalcid wasp
  (Diglyphus isaea), and parasitic wasp (Opius pallipes) are
  natural enemies of leaf miners
- Sprays: Repel with
  [[cervantes-neem-oil-organic-pest-control]] and
  [[cervantes-pyrethrum-pyrethroid-insecticides]]
  sprays. However, maggots are protected within their leaf
  tunnels, making sprays often ineffective. Hemp Diseases
  and Pests suggests watering plants with a 0.5 percent
  solution of neem, which works fast and stays on plants
  for four weeks after application

### The Leaf Miner Challenge

The primary challenge with leaf miners is that the damaging
larval stage is protected inside leaf tissue, shielded from
sprays and contact insecticides. This makes prevention through
adult trapping and removal of infested foliage more effective
than chemical intervention. Systemic neem drenches that are
taken up by the plant offer a way to reach the protected
maggots within their tunnels.

## Comparative Identification

Distinguishing between leaf damage from different pests is
important for proper treatment:

- Spider mites: yellowish-white stippling on leaf tops, fine
  webbing visible when misted
- Thrips: whitish-yellowish specks, black fecal spots, and
  visible thrips on leaves
- Leafhoppers: stippling similar to mites and thrips, plus
  honeydew production
- Leaf miners: visible winding tunnels within leaf tissue,
  no external spotting pattern
