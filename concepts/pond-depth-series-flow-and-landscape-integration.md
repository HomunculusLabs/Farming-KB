## Pond Depth, Series, Flow, and Landscape Integration

## Overview

The productive capacity of ponds depends not only on their size but on their depth, their arrangement relative to one another, and their integration into the broader landscape mosaic. Mollison presents a framework for understanding how ponds of different depths serve different functions, how ponds can be connected in series for trophic cascades, and how pond complexes fit into total landscape design.

## Orders of Depth

Depth has its own orders and ecological effects, distinct from surface area and volume:

### Films and Mud (0–2 cm)
- Mere films of water or wet mud patches suffice to grow most productive green salad and root crops
- Allow bees to safely drink
- Revive frogs and breed mosquitoes
- Soil at field capacity is effectively a "pond in hiding"

### Shallow Water (2–6 cm)
- Smaller floating plants take root and form mats
- Scuds and small crustaceans flourish

### Medium Depth (10–100 cm)
- True floating and anchored plants separate out their niches
- Indian water chestnut (*[[trapa-natans]]*) and almost any fish species can be grown
- Even in rich algal ponds, some light penetrates

### Standard Culture Depth (up to 2 m)
- The maximum commonly used depth for fish culture
- No unstable thermoclines develop to create sudden overturns of oxygen and temperature
- Ponds are easily wind-aerated
- Deep enough to buffer air temperature changes

### Lake Depth (2–15 m)
- Lakes of clear water are potentially productive at this range

### Deep Water (15+ m)
- Less biological cycling takes place
- Cold, sometimes anaerobic depths develop, especially in V-shaped lakes with leaf-fall on margins
- Deeps of 4–5 m have limited use for fish escaping high surface temperatures or ice
- Caves under water serve the same purpose — trout commonly huddle in drainage pipes under dam walls on hot clear days

### Thermocline Dynamics

Thermoclines may develop above 15 m depth and are typical of lakes subject to sudden seasonal temperature changes. Overturns typically occur in autumn and spring:
- **Summer:** Surface waters remain consistently warmer than those at depth
- **Winter:** Surface ice forms, then warmer water at depth, and potentially ice deep in the lake where very cold streams pool up and freeze
- Fish may live in an "icy sandwich" or tucked away in modest caves or hollows in shallower water

Depth and plants together serve as a set of breeding refuges for the food organisms of omnivores and carnivores. The configuration of levels at different depths may have a profound effect on total yield or availability of such food organisms.

## Ponds in Series and Flow

Ponds can be arranged in multiple configurations — isolated, parallel, consecutive series, order sequences, or any combination. They can also be annidated (nested) within each other, and may take linear form as canals or waterways.

### Isolated Ponds

Familiar to every aquarium keeper. Used for:
- New stock quarantine
- Diseased stock separation
- Trial polycultures
- Insurance against general loss

A set of tanks above grade or still-ponds filled one at a time can be effectively isolated. Even treated identically, they manage to be different — one of the fascinations of aquaculture. Water systems are more connected within themselves and convey small differences more rapidly than land systems.

### Parallel Ponds

Perhaps the most common fish-culture system, analogous to irrigation bays on land:
- Possess a head canal (inflow system)
- Individual flow-through to each pond
- A tail canal (drainage system) that also handles surplus rainwater

**Advantages:** Ideal for valley floor ponds, single feeder pipes, and narrow tidal benches. **Disadvantages:** Cannot "feed the other" — a controlled trophic ladder cannot be set up where food is cultured and allowed to flow down to higher trophic levels (minnows to trout). All ponds may fail together if disease, pollutant, or qualitative change occurs in the common water supply, unless flow regimes are staggered.

### Consecutive Series

Can be arranged as **trophic levels**, with orders of ponds breeding forage fish or shrimp that spill over to ponds containing carnivorous fish. Marshy ponds of tadpoles, scuds, and *Daphnia* flow into ponds of omnivores. Primary sewage lagoons (anaerobic) feed secondary lagoons rich in insect fauna and zooplankton (facultative ponds), which cascade into aerobic ponds of useful fish, mussels, shrimp, or green vegetable crop.

**Size relationships in consecutive series:**
- Anaerobic ponds: approximately one-seventh to one-ninth the size of the next — best constructed as tunnels or canals from which methane gas can be collected
- Hatchery series: one-tenth to one-twentieth the size of the next — hatchery → fry pool → fingerling tank → large ponds

Eels and trout are reared in such increasing size order of ponds.

Consecutive series has essential function in terms of orders but may make little sense for equal-sized ponds of the same function. Consecutive series may also be forced by valley configuration or site limitations, requiring close monitoring for water quality risks.

### Evolutionary Pond Systems

Every old fish culture system or terrace complex has an intricate set of ponds and flows. Elements are added as needed — as money and time permit, as new information and needs arise, or as new species are incorporated. Although complex, these systems work well and are comfortable to work with. Successful modifications are preserved, mistakes rectified, catastrophes remedied.

However, many such systems are never intended for easy reading — a novice inheriting one might spend weeks or months working out how to control and manage the system. All are quite unique, often subtle in operation, with complex water control.

### Ruled-Up (Rectilinear) Systems

In contrast to evolutionary systems, the flatland rice farmer who converts to catfish may evolve a simple, standardized, monocultural rectilinear farm — visible from an airliner as a network of precise regularity imposed on the landscape. Anyone can understand it; the system is easy to control, but it costs in food and tends toward monotony. All that is needed to manage is power (energy).

## Ponds in the Landscape Mosaic

When creating ponds in barren or agricultural landscapes, Mollison advocates planning for a beneficial mosaic of forest, pond, marsh, and prairie or rangeland.

### Component Roles

- **Forest** (correctly chosen): Produces clean water of good nutrient quality, absorbs wastes from fish and their plant associates, and provides food either directly (fruit) or indirectly (insect bodies and frass) to the pond
- **Marsh**: Provides rich habitat for birds and crustacea; ponds and forest collect phosphates
- **Meadow**: Provides for some mammals and plants that interact with both forest and water

### Proportional Guidelines

For sub-humid or humid areas, approximate proportions:
- **15%** pond
- **15%** marsh (contiguous)
- **30–60%** forest
- **10–40%** meadow, crop, or pasture

### Spatial Arrangement

- Forest should be upstream of, downstream from, and **between** ponds
- Marshes upstream of and in the ponds
- Pasture or prairie downstream and in random patches where trees are difficult to grow
- The whole linked by permanent or intermittent drains, streams, canals, and swales

### Ideal Forest Species for Pond Edges

- **River red gums**, some eucalypts, and leguminous trees — provide enhanced phosphate drip from rain throughfall; belong close to waterways and water edges
- **Fibrous-rooted willow and Casuarina species** — need or fix phosphates; belong in downstream forests
- **Freshwater mussels** — confined to the pond

Past successes include the Chinese carp-pond complex with mulberries, ducks, and silkworms.

## Key Principles

1. **Vary depth intentionally** — different depths serve different functions from mud films to 2 m culture depth
2. **Avoid [[thermocline]] disruption** — keep culture ponds under 2 m for stable conditions
3. **Use consecutive series for trophic cascades** — waste from one pond becomes food for the next
4. **Isolate quarantine and trial ponds** — prevent disease spread through the system
5. **Design landscape mosaics** — integrate ponds with forest, marsh, and meadow at approximately 15/15/30-60/10-40% proportions
6. **Plan from the start** — accessory systems (cages, refuges, solar ponds) are far easier to integrate during initial design than to retrofit
