## Southern Hemisphere Origin Hypothesis

The strong concentration of species diversity in tropical and subtropical regions of the southern hemisphere supports the hypothesis that the genus *Psilocybe* originated in the southern hemisphere. This pattern is consistent with broader biogeographic trends observed in many fungal genera, where tropical humid forests serve as centers of diversification and evolutionary radiation.

The distribution data suggest that species subsequently radiated northward over geological time, with the genus reaching its northernmost limits in temperate Europe and North America, where far fewer species are found. The reduced diversity at higher latitudes may reflect both the recency of colonization and the less favorable climatic conditions for a predominantly tropical genus.

## Mexico as a Biotic Conjugation Point

Mexico occupies a unique biogeographic position as a conjugation point where northern and southern mycobiotas meet and overlap. Its complex topography, spanning from tropical lowlands to alpine peaks, creates numerous ecological niches that accommodate species from both faunal and floral realms. Two notable examples illustrate this pattern:

- *P. caerulipes* reaches northeastern Mexico by dispersing through *Fagus* (beech) mesophytic forests, representing a northern temperate element extending southward along corridor habitats.

## Anthropogenic Dispersal: The Case of *P. cubensis*

Not all *Psilocybe* distributions are natural. *P. cubensis* provides a clear and well-documented case of anthropogenic introduction to the Americas. This species grows on bovine dung, and its current pantropical distribution in the Americas is directly attributed to the introduction of cattle by Spanish colonists during the colonial era. Prior to the arrival of European livestock, the specific substrate required by *P. cubensis — fresh bovine dung in warm, humid environments — did not exist in the New World, as the Americas lacked domesticated cattle.

Further dispersal of the species has been linked to the transatlantic slave trade, which moved both people and livestock across tropical regions, inadvertently spreading the fungus to new locations throughout the Caribbean and Central and South America.

## Intercontinental Species Relationships

Phylogenetic relationships between geographically distant *Psilocybe* species suggest historical connections that challenge simple biogeographic models:

- *P. samuiensis* from Thailand and *P. makarorae* from New Zealand are both related to *P. mexicana* from Mexico, suggesting a shared ancestral lineage that dispersed across the Pacific basin.
- *P. aucklandii* from New Zealand is related to *P. zapotecorum* from Mexico, pointing to another possible trans-Pacific connection between South American/Mesoamerican and Southeast Asian/Australasian mycobiotas.

These relationships imply either ancient Gondwanan distributions or long-distance dispersal events that connected populations across vast oceanic barriers, a phenomenon increasingly recognized in fungal biogeography.
