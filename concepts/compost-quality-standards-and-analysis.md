# Compost Quality Standards and Analysis

Not all compost is created equal. The nutritional quality of finished compost varies enormously depending on the starting materials, the C:N ratio, the composting method, and the attention given to the process. If compost is going to supply the nutritional needs of most vegetable crops, it must meet specific minimum standards for nitrogen content, C:N ratio, and overall nutrient balance.

## The Minimum Standards

For compost to serve as the **sole source of fertility** for high-demand vegetables:

| Parameter | Minimum Requirement | Ideal Target |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------| 
| Nitrogen (N%) | >1.5% | >3% |
| C:N Ratio | <15:1 | <12:1 |
| Total N+P+K | >6% | >6% with fair P content |
| Micronutrients | Significant amounts | P, K, Ca, Mg, trace minerals in right proportions |

### Quality Tiers

| Quality | N% | C:N | N+P+K | Suitable For |
|---------|----|-----|-------|-------------|
| Low-grade | ~1.5% | ~20:1 | <3% | Low-demand vegetables only |
| Medium-grade | ~2% | ~15:1 | 3-4% | Medium-demand vegetables |
| High-grade | >3% | <12:1 | >6% | All vegetables including high-demand |

## Compost Types and Their Quality

### Municipal Compost

Municipal refuse compost is **not potent enough for vegetable growing**. The reasons:

- Much of the input material is paper, cardboard, chipped tree trimmings, and other woody waste
- Average starting C:N exceeds 50:1
- Even after extensive processing, the final C:N is still around 25:1
- Far more than half the carbon disappears — and most of the nitrogen with it
- The final weight is only a tiny fraction of starting weight

**Result**: Municipal composting is actually a huge contributor to greenhouse gases (methane, ammonia, nitrous oxide). The product is best used as mulch along roadsides and under ornamentals — not for growing vegetables.

### Bin Compost

Plastic compost bins (open-bottomed cylinders 2-3 feet across, 3-5 feet high) are the most common home composting method. The problems:

- Too much woody, decomposition-resistant material usually goes in
- The whole heap doesn't heat uniformly — only a temporary hot spot below where newer materials are added
- That hot spot cools too quickly to ferment properly
- Awkward to turn and aerate
- The final result lacks potency and may lack mineral balance

**Verdict**: Rarely effective enough to grow a great veggie garden by itself. Will grow nice flowers and shrubs.

### Tumbler Compost

Compost tumblers are marketed for their speed. The problem:

- Research shows conclusively that the more frequently a compost heap is turned, the lower its value as fertilizer
- Each turning releases more nitrogen to the atmosphere
- Tumblers promote over-turning

**Verdict**: A convenient high-speed recycling machine, not a producer of the finest garden fertilizer. Consider the environmental cost of manufacturing a short-lived plastic device.

### Shredder-Grinder Compost

Grinding organic material into smaller particles makes it decompose faster and get hotter. However:

- Research shows that heaps heated above 150°F (65°C) lose much of the bulk of their nutrients
- Above this temperature, the heap off-gasses methane, ammonia, and nitrous oxide
- What is left is essentially humus — depleted of the nutrients that would have fertilized plants

**Verdict**: Let the microbes in an ordinary heap do all the work at a slower pace, at a temperature that doesn't ruin the value of your product.

## The Soil C:N Standard

All soil contains humus — the stable residue of decomposed organic matter. Soil humus has a C:N ratio of around **12:1** in every climate. This is the target your compost should approach.

### When C:N Is Higher Than 12:1

Soil microbes "burn" the carbon for fuel while preserving the nitrogen. The result:
- Higher level of humus in the soil
- Healthier soil with better tilth
- Plant nutrients become available as decomposition completes

### When C:N Is Lower Than 12:1

Bacteria convert surplus nitrogen to ammonia gas, then to water-soluble nitrates. The result:
- Immediate plant growth (nitrates are available)
- BUT: the higher nitrate level encourages microbes to multiply and attack humus
- Slightly lower quantity of organic matter than before
- This is exactly what happens with chemical fertilizers — the reason organicists oppose them

## The Complete Organic Fertilizer Complement

For most gardeners, the practical approach is:

1. **Make medium-quality compost** using the annual heap method
2. **Supplement with COF or sacked chicken manure** for medium- and high-demand vegetables
3. **Don't expect compost alone** to supply all nutritional needs — especially in cool climates

### Climate Considerations

- **Cool summers** (maritime climates): Soils never get warm enough to fully release nutrients from compost. Use COF in addition for high-demand crops.
- **Hot climates**: Organic matter rots quickly. In warm soil, you can get acceptable results growing high-demand vegetables with only high-quality compost.
- **Temperate climates**: A balanced approach works best — compost for soil health and structure, COF for plant nutrition.

## Application Rates

### For Medium-Quality Compost

- **Low-demand vegetables**: 1/4 to 1/2 inch deep, raked into the surface
- **Medium-demand vegetables**: Same rate, plus COF or well-decomposed manure
- **High-demand vegetables**: Need high-quality compost (>3% N) or COF supplement

### For High-Quality Compost

- Can serve as the sole source of fertility for all vegetables
- Apply at 1/4 to 1/2 inch deep annually
- Still benefits from occasional COF application for maximum results

## Testing Your Compost

There is no simple home test for compost nutrient content. The practical indicators of good compost:

1. **Color**: Dark brown to black
2. **Texture**: Crumbly, loose, soil-like
3. **Odor**: Pleasant earthy smell (no ammonia, no sourness)
4. **Recognition**: Nothing recognizable from the original materials
5. **Temperature**: Room temperature (not hot — means decomposition is complete)
6. **Worms**: Presence of earthworms indicates good biological activity

## The Hard Truth

Many garden writers present every kind of compost as being inevitably successful and easy, and as an effective fertilizer. This is not actually the case. There are many composting pitfalls. If you can't afford to have your food gardening efforts fall far short of your hopes, you need the truth — and the truth is that most home compost is low-grade and needs supplementation.
