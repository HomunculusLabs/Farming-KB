# Mineral Deficiency Remedies and Garden Solutions

## General Garden Approaches by Soil Type

### Leached (Washed-Out) Sands
Leached sandy soils present the most challenging conditions for nutrient retention. The recommended treatment protocol:

1. Dig up garden beds and place a plastic sheet liner below to prevent deep leaching.
2. Add a bucket of clay and a handful of dolomite per square metre.
3. Add a soil gel to improve water retention.
4. Add compost and a complete fertiliser such as blood and bone.
5. Mulch thickly and replant.
6. Return to the diagnostic key if symptoms recur.

### Peaty Soils or Over-Composted Ground
Peats and soils with excessive compost accumulation need different management:

1. Add urea or blood and bone to supply nitrogen.
2. Raise garden beds to improve drainage.
3. Lime the area to balance pH.

### Lime-Rich Soils, Coral Sands, Desert Coasts with Calcrete
High-calcium soils lock up trace elements, making them unavailable to plants. Remedies include:

1. Spray weak zinc and copper sulphates on plants.
2. Apply iron sulphates in very dilute solutions (12 g per 10 square metres with lots of water).
3. Add trace elements and sulphur to compost pits.
4. Use sulphur at about a handful per square metre.
5. Alternatively, lay plastic sheeting on the ground, build up logs around it to 25 cm high, fill with humus (compost plus 50% sand), mulch heavily, and add blood and bone.
6. On atolls, dig down to near the water table and mulch thickly in large grow pits (3-6 m wide by 10-12 m long by 3 m deep), using any mulch especially Casuarina, palm, and household wastes.

## Specific Mineral Remedies

### Sulphur {1}
Add plain sulphur (not medical quality) at one handful per square metre. In areas near cities, atmospheric fallout may supply sufficient sulphur already.

### Copper {2}
Add as fine-crushed ore, or apply copper sulphate in water at 7 kg/hectare, spread at 1 g per square metre every 5-7 years. Copper is needed only in trace amounts and excess is toxic.

### Iron {3}
Try sulphur first to lower pH, then if necessary add iron sulphate or spray foliage with very dilute iron solution. An effective traditional approach is to bury old iron — pieces of galvanised iron, old wire, or scrap metal — in humus pits near trees, where it slowly releases iron as it corrodes.

### Manganese {4}
Try sulphur first to acidify, then use very dilute foliar spray of manganese sulphate. Manganese availability is strongly pH-dependent.

### Zinc {5}
Add zinc oxide in acid areas, zinc sulphate in alkaline areas, also sulphur in alkaline areas. Apply zinc at 7 kg/hectare (or equivalent) every 7-10 years. Zinc deficiency is common in alkaline soils.

### Boron {6}
**Caution: Boron is poisonous in large quantities.** First, lime acid areas and peats, and add sulphur to alkaline areas. If this does not resolve the deficiency, add borax (sodium borate) at 1 gram per square metre and test reaction with cabbages. Avoid detergents containing "borates" — they can poison soil over time.

Boron excess (poisoning) can occur on sea sediments and is common in reclaimed marine areas (e.g., Holland). Treatment: raise garden beds, lime, and flush out with fresh water.

### Calcium {7}
- Use limestone in areas where manganese is plentiful, dolomite if not.
- Use cement powder in deep red hot tropical soils, then continue adding mulch and use lime only if deficiencies occur.
- Use gypsum in alkaline salty soils, then flush with fresh tank water and continue using lime.
- Natural calcium sources include bone, bamboo mulch, and buckwheat straw.

### Nitrogen {8}
Nitrogen management requires attention to drainage and soil biology:

1. Ensure soil is well drained to 0.5 m for vegetables, 1-2 m for trees.
2. Check cobalt levels for deficiency.
3. If legumes are used, ensure they are inoculated and that manganese levels are not too high.
4. If all above is satisfactory, apply dilute urine (20 parts water : 1 part urine), ammonium sulphate in alkaline areas, legume mulches, or interplant nitrogen-fixing trees (about 48 small acacia or tagasaste trees per quarter acre).
5. Use compost, then surface mulch.
6. Build up worms and [[phosphorus-availability]] is highly pH-dependent. The strategy is multi-pronged:

1. Bring pH to 6.0-6.5 using lime in acid soils and humus in alkaline.
2. Use bone meal, bury bones, or use tested rock phosphate free of cadmium or uranium.
3. Stop deep digging and start mulching with minimal soil disturbance (build up narrow beds).
4. Encourage soil life, add mulch on top, water with comfrey "tea" and dilute bird manure on leaves.
5. If you have clay in the beds, symptoms will eventually disappear with persistent treatment.
6. If you have high-iron clays, use a lot of bone meal initially — it will slowly release over time.
7. If desperate, use a few handfuls of superphosphate per square metre, then continue with organic sources.
8. Feed a patch of comfrey with bird manure and make comfrey tea in a drum of cold water for watering plants.
9. All animal manures (including human) contain some phosphorus.
10. Calcined (roasted) rock phosphate is effective on acid soils in high-rainfall areas.

### Chlorine Toxicity {13}
Chlorine poisoning from water supplies is a real concern. Options: petition local council, or let tap water stand with a handful of lime in it for a day before using on the garden.

## Mineral Fertilisers and Soil Amendments: The Test Strip Method

The recommended approach for broader-scale applications (prairie re-establishment, erosion control, land reclamation, plantation):

1. Sow mixed legume (clover), Brassica, and grass crops.
2. Divide into **trial plots** treated at varying levels with soil or foliar spray amendments.
3. Test plant health and response based on soil test (pH, mineral availability) or leaf analysis.
4. Trial plots can be as little as 1% of total acreage.
5. Avoid areas under trees, old stockyards, hay-stacks, intense fire scars, watering points, gateways, and roads — these have special features needing separate assessment.

Observations from pioneer weed colonisation (e.g., chicory, thistles on compacted traffic areas) provide useful data about soil conditions but should not lead to deliberate compaction.

## Record-Keeping

Keep a fertiliser diary for your garden and leave it for the next person. Document what has been done for the soil — this continuity is essential for long-term [[soil-health-management]].
