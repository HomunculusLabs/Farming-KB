# Natural Fruit Tree Pest and Disease Management

Fukuoka's approach to managing [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]] in fruit orchards
demonstrates that chemical pesticides are not merely unnecessary but
actively counterproductive. Through decades of observation in his natural
orchards, he documented how [[fukuoka-ecological-balance-principles]] maintains tree health
without human intervention — and how pesticide use destroys that balance.

## The Fundamental Principle

In nature, trees are constantly attacked and parasitized by insects and
disease, but the belief that trees will succumb without spraying does not
hold under natural conditions. Crops become susceptible to attack because
they have been artificially improved (reducing innate resistance) and
because the [[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]] is unnatural. If varieties closer to their
natural ancestors are selected and grown properly, pesticides become
unnecessary.

Fukuoka categorizes fruit trees by their natural resistance levels. Trees
listed under "moderate" and "strong resistance" — including peach, pear,
grape, and Satsuma orange — can be grown without spraying, provided
attention is given to a few specific pests.

## Arrowhead Scale

Arrowhead scale infestation of citrus trees (Satsuma orange, Iyo orange,
and shaddock) has become severe in conventional orchards. Fukuoka observed
that in his [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-development]], parasitic wasps and several types of ladybugs
emerged to feast on the scales in large numbers. Even when natural enemies
are present, places where branches crisscross and foliage is congested
sustain considerable damage — not because of the pest, but because of the
tree form.

The solution is not stronger chemicals but protecting natural predators
and improving the microenvironment. If a grower is not concerned about a
minor loss in tree appearance, no spraying at all is needed.

## Mites: A Case Study in Pesticide-Induced Problems

The mite story is Fukuoka's most compelling illustration of how pesticides
create the very problems they claim to solve. Until twenty to thirty years
ago, a mixture of lime and sulfur effectively controlled fruit mites, and
mites were never a major pest. After World War II, orchardists switched to
powerful organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides, delighted that
these destroyed all insect pests. But soon, no matter how often they
sprayed, large outbreaks of mites recurred.

Researchers offered various explanations: resistance development, new
species emergence, or disappearance of natural enemies. New pesticides were
developed, but this only aggravated both pest control failure and pesticide
pollution. Fukuoka's conclusion was direct: "If man had left the mite
alone, it would never have become a major pest."

Mites are highly sensitive to microclimatic changes in the tree. When
black wattle ([[acacia-mearnsii]]) is used as a windbreak or shade tree, the
number of mites and scales may drop dramatically or almost entirely vanish
depending on sunlight and breeze exposure. The black wattle produces tannin
and excretes insect-repelling substances, but the most direct cause of
population changes is microclimate modification.

The interplanting of evergreen trees with deciduous trees is also an
effective preventive measure. Fukuoka considers it reckless to spray
potent pesticides without understanding the relationships between
pesticides, natural predators, and beneficial fungi that feed on mites.

## Cottony-Cushion Scale and Red Wax Scale

Two of the three major citrus pests in Japan were effectively controlled
by natural predators until pesticides disrupted the balance:

- **Cottony-cushion scale**: Controlled for forty years by the vedalia
  ladybug. After the war, organophosphate spraying caused serious
  outbreaks. In Fukuoka's unsprayed orchard, the pest continued as prey
  for several types of ladybugs with almost no damage.
- **Red wax scale**: Previously controlled by parasitic wasps that emerged
  when wartime pine rosin shortages stopped spraying. After the war,
  farmers adopted a fluorine pesticide against the scale, causing severe
  outbreaks. When the highly toxic agent was banned (after causing local
  deaths), infestation declined almost immediately — demonstrating that
  the most intelligent pest control is not to spray.
