## The Fundamental Principle

In nature, trees are constantly attacked and parasitized by insects and
disease, but the belief that trees will succumb without spraying does not
hold under natural conditions. Crops become susceptible to attack because
they have been artificially improved (reducing innate resistance) and
because the [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-development]], parasitic wasps and several types of ladybugs
emerged to feast on the scales in large numbers. Even when natural enemies
are present, places where branches crisscross and foliage is congested
sustain considerable damage — not because of the pest, but because of the
tree form.

The solution is not stronger chemicals but protecting natural predators
and improving the microenvironment. If a grower is not concerned about a
minor loss in tree appearance, no spraying at all is needed.

## Mites: A Case Study in Pesticide-Induced Problems

The mite story is Fukuoka's most compelling illustration of how pesticides
create the very problems they claim to solve. Until twenty to thirty years
ago, a mixture of lime and sulfur effectively controlled fruit mites, and
mites were never a major pest. After World War II, orchardists switched to
powerful organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides, delighted that
these destroyed all insect pests. But soon, no matter how often they
sprayed, large outbreaks of mites recurred.

Researchers offered various explanations: resistance development, new
species emergence, or disappearance of natural enemies. New pesticides were
developed, but this only aggravated both pest control failure and pesticide
pollution. Fukuoka's conclusion was direct: "If man had left the mite
alone, it would never have become a major pest."
