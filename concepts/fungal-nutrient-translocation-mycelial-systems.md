## Mechanisms of Translocation

The precise mechanisms underlying nutrient translocation in fungi are not yet fully
resolved but are thought to include mass flow, diffusion, generalized cytoplasmic
streaming, and specific vesicular transport (Cairney, 2005). The highly dynamic
pleiomorphic vacuolar system present in filamentous fungi may play a role in
long-distance translocation over millimetres or centimetres (Ashford and Allaway, 2002).

Olsson and Jennings (1991) demonstrated that translocation of carbon-14 and phosphorus-32
through hyphal systems of Rhizopus, Trichoderma, and Stemphylium occurred by diffusion.
The rate of carbon translocation within the fungal thallus reacts in real time to provide
directional flow to building phases of the hyphae (Olsson, 1995). In the face of high
demand for nutrients and carbon at advancing hyphal fronts, nutrients and carbon are
translocated acropetally through cytoplasmic flow and diffusion in the cytoplasm and
apoplasm.

## Cord Systems and Resource Connection

Hyphae of both saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes that ramify through soil
often aggregate to form rapidly extending, persistent, specialized high-conductivity
channels termed cords (Rayner et al., 1994, 1999; Boddy, 1999). These cords form complex
networks that can extend for metres or even hectares in natural environments.

Cord-forming fungi such as Phanerochaete velutina and Phallus impudicus use these
specialized structures to connect and exploit multiple resource patches simultaneously.
Wells and Boddy (1990) showed that 75% (Phanerochaete velutina) and 13% (Phallus
impudicus) of the phosphorus added to a decomposed wood resource was translocated to
newly colonized wood resources through mycelial cord systems. Maximum rates of phosphorus
translocation reached 7225 nmol P per square centimeter per day through cords.

## Translocation Over Ecologically Relevant Distances

In field experimental manipulations, Wells and Boddy (1995a) showed that phosphorus
translocation could be conducted over distances of up to 75 cm between decomposing
resources on the forest floor and into living wild strawberry and moss plants. This
capacity to move nutrients over significant distances connects otherwise isolated
nutrient pools and makes fungal networks central to
