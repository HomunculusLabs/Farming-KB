## Gordon Wasson and the Comparative Method in Ethnomycology

Gordon Wasson"s influential theories about mushroom symbolism and the universality of psychedelic mushroom cults were deeply rooted in the Frazerian comparative method — an approach that had already been rejected within professional anthropology by the time Wasson developed his mushroom thesis.

## The Frazerian Legacy

James George Frazer's The Golden Bough (twelve volumes, published in three editions) argued that all religions were expressions of a universal motif — that of the dying and resurrected vegetation god. Frazer's comparative methodology involved amassing evidence from as many different sources as possible, across vast distances of time and culture.

Frazer's legacy influenced writers including T.S. Eliot, W.B. Yeats, D.H. Lawrence, and Robert Graves. Far from ushering in a scientific age, The Golden Bough prompted people to revive supposed pagan religions, leading to the emergence of modern Druidry and Wicca.

The Frazerian approach was enormously seductive. By identifying patterns across cultures, it offered a seemingly scientific framework for understanding religious belief. The problem was that the patterns were often imposed rather than discovered — the result of selective evidence-gathering rather than genuine cross-cultural comparison.

## Wasson's Intellectual Lineage

Wasson was influenced by Frazer both directly and through his long correspondence and friendship with Robert Graves. From Frazer he borrowed:

1. **The importance of taboo** — which he thought a lasting and "deep-seated emotional attitude"
2. **The concept of survivals** — "fossil meanings, fossil sayings, fossilised folkloric bits of our various languages"
3. **The comparative methodology** — amassing evidence from many different sources

From Graves he took the belief that myths, however fantastical, have their origins in actual historical events: hence any myths or folktales involving mushrooms could be read as vestigial memory of a supposed mushroom cult.

This intellectual inheritance placed [Wasson firmly within a tradition that professional anthropologists had already moved beyond. His methodology was, in effect, a generation out of date by the time he applied it to mushrooms.

## The Rejection of Cultural Evolution

By the time Wasson was developing his theories, the arguments about cultural evolution and the Frazerian comparative method were long over. Pioneering anthropologist Franz Boas (1858-1942), preferring fieldwork to library-based research, could find no evidence for the evolutionary cultural progressions outlined by Spencer, Tylor, and others. Without the rationale of cultural evolution, searching for survivals was meaningless.

The principal objection to the Frazerian comparative method was that it attempts to make data fit theory rather than vice versa. The selection process inevitably reflected the class, gender, and cultural biases of the scholars — cherry-picking evidence from sources widely separated by distance and time stripped it of the all-important context in which it originally occurred.

Boas"s critique was devastating: without the framework of cultural evolution, the Frazerian search for "survivals" had no theoretical justification. Similar customs in different cultures did not necessarily share a common origin, and stripping customs of their cultural context to compare them across space and time produced misleading rather than illuminating results.

## The Mycophilia/Mycophobia Dichotomy

Wasson"s most influential contribution to ethnomycology was his proposed dichotomy between "mycophilic" (mushroom-loving) and "mycophobic" (mushroom-fearing) cultures. He argued that this division was ancient and deep-rooted, reflecting an original cultural split between those who venerated psychoactive mushrooms and those who tabooed them.

While intuitively appealing, the dichotomy oversimplifies a complex reality. Most cultures contain elements of both attitudes, and the binary classification often obscures more than it reveals about specific cultural relationships with fungi. ## Lasting Impact

Despite these methodological criticisms, Wasson's work had an enormous cultural impact. The enduring popularity of his theories speaks more to their psychological and cultural appeal than to their scholarly rigor.
