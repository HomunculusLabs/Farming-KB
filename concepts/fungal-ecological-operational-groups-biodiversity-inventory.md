# Fungal Ecological Operational Groups for Biodiversity Inventory

## Overview

Fungal biodiversity inventories are typically designed around **operational groups** based on habitat or substratum use, ecological habits, or functional role, rather than on strict taxonomic criteria. This approach is necessary because organisms covered in fungal biodiversity studies fall into ecological groups whose species have very different life histories and require very different sampling techniques, independent of their taxonomy. A single taxonomic approach would lead to significant duplication and would miss critical groups entirely.

## Why Ecological Groups Over Taxonomic Groups

### The Problem with Taxonomic Organization

If fungal biodiversity protocols were organized by taxonomic unit — treating all ascomycetes together, all basidiomycetes together — the following problems would arise:

1. **Massive duplication:** Protocols for sampling aquatic habitats, endophytes, leaf parasites, animal associates, wood inhabitants, and terrestrial forms would have to be presented in chapters on both ascomycetes and basidiomycetes
2. **Missed connections:** Fungi performing similar ecological roles but belonging to different phyla would be separated, obscuring ecological patterns
3. **Impractical fieldwork:** Field teams typically specialize in one ecological group, not one taxonomic group

### The Ecological Group Advantage

Organizing by ecological group recognizes that:
- Distinct protocols are necessary for sampling each group
- Different specialists are needed for different ecological groups
- Temporal and spatial scales appropriate for sampling differ among groups
- Life history information is most useful when grouped ecologically

## Major Fungal Operational Groups

### Aquatic Fungi

**Freshwater habitats:** Includes single-celled aquatic species and other aquatic fungi such as [[particle-filtration-dilution-plating-microfungal-isolation]], direct isolation, molecular methods, or baiting techniques. Soil fungi represent one of the most diverse and least characterized fungal groups.

### Wood-Inhabiting Fungi

**Macrofungi:** Fruiting bodies (sporocarps) that grow on dead wood, including polypores, agarics, and other forms. Surveyed through systematic plot-based searches of fallen logs, standing dead trees, and stumps.

**Microfungi:** Microscopic fungi colonizing wood surfaces and internal wood tissues, isolated through surface sterilization and plating techniques.

### Obligate Root Mutualists (Mycorrhizal Fungi)

**Ectomycorrhizal fungi:** Form sheaths around root tips of forest trees. Surveyed through both sporocarp surveys and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]:** Form arbuscules within root cortical cells. Surveyed primarily through root staining and microscopic examination, or molecular methods targeting spores in soil.

**Other types:** Ericoid, orchid, and ectendomycorrhizal fungi, each requiring specialized sampling approaches.

### Endophytes

Fungi living asymptomatically within plant tissues. Sampling involves surface sterilization of plant material (leaves, stems, roots) followed by isolation on agar media. Endophyte communities can be extremely diverse, with dozens of species recovered from a single leaf.

### Plant Pathogens

**Biotrophic pathogens:** Obligate parasites including rusts and [[bloomfields-orchard-spore-discharge-mechanisms]].
