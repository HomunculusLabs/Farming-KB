## Organic Farming and Ecology Are Self-Defeating

In a provocative passage, Fukuoka criticizes conventional approaches to African development:

- Even well-intentioned organic farming and ecological projects can be self-defeating
- Projects that require inputs, management, or technology cannot be sustained
- The very act of "trying to help" can introduce dependency and disruption
- True solutions must be so simple that they require no ongoing intervention

## Lessons from Africa

Africa taught Fukuoka important lessons:

- Natural farming cannot be imposed from outside — it must grow from within communities
- The scale of ecological destruction in Africa dwarfs what he encountered elsewhere

## The Savanna Landscape

From the air, a splendid panorama of vast savanna with scattered thorny bushes opened up, along with isolated round huts of nomads, camels and goats. Fukuoka was especially happy to note that wherever he looked, he could see stream and river beds. There was at least one live spring within any field of view, meaning there was a good deal of ground water even in the savanna. He later learned that two great rivers at least a thousand kilometers long flow around and through a desert with less than 300mm of annual precipitation. The desert even grew at the banks of these rivers.

## The Discovery of Clay

The desert sand contains clay. If there is clay, there is hope. Fukuoka told the young people of Somalia: "This earth is young; it is sleeping. The land must be awakened from its slumber with natural farming." This observation about clay content became the basis for [[fukuoka-desert-greening-plant-irrigation]].
