# Singh [[fungal-biodegradation-and-biodeterioration]] of Materials
#mycoremediation #biodegradation #biodeterioration #plastics #concrete #polyethylene #wood-decay

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Harbhajan Singh, Mycoremediation: [[fungal-bioremediation]] (2006), Chapter 1

## Overview

Fungi are capable of degrading or deteriorating a remarkably wide range of natural and synthetic materials. This biodeterioration capability, while sometimes problematic (library materials, concrete, cultural heritage), also underpins their potential for bioremediation of anthropogenic wastes. Fungi are "natural and true ecosystem engineers" (Lawton and Jones, 1995) whose mycelial networks may cover several hectares of forest floor. See also [[fungal-ecology-mycoremediation]], [[singh-key-fungal-genera-bioremediation]].

## Fungal Properties Relevant to Bioremediation

### Survival and Adaptation
- Fungi have survived ~5300 years (Haselwandter and Ebner, 1994)
- Armillaria bulbosa: among the largest and longest-living organisms
- Exist in almost every habitat; highly plastic; most cells totipotent
- Can regenerate from spores or hyphal fragments
- Many tolerate extreme pH (acidic or highly alkaline)
- Mycelial fungi reorient to grow upstream in continuous liquid flow (A. niger)

### Mycelial Structure
- Hyphae: threadlike tubular structures; septate with pores for cytoplasm/organelle movement
- Cell walls: rigid chitin matrix (glucosamine)
- Two vesicle types: macrovesicles (secrete wall polymers/enzymes) and microvesicles (chitin skeleton)
- Pellet formation: coagulating (spore aggregation) and noncoagulating (single spore) types
- P. chrysosporium: surface polysaccharide bridging during aggregation

### Morphological Analysis Methods
- Autoradiography (nonviability of significant hyphal tips in Trichoderma)
- Image analysis (automated and fluorescent staining)
- Hyphal growth unit (G): total hyphal length / number of tips
- Fractal analysis for colony growth studies
- Flow cytometry for Penicillium, Phoma, Trichoderma

## Plastic and Polymer Degradation

### Polyethylene
- [[penicillium-simplicissimum]] YK: degrades polyethylene MW 4000-28000 (bioremediation may be possible)
- Mucor rouxii NRRL 1835 and [[aspergillus-flavus]]: change mechanical properties and weight of disposable polyethylene bags
- White-rot fungi: efficient in polyethylene degradation (Iiyoshi et al., 1997)

### Chitosan-Graft Polymers
- Aspergillus flavus: colonized and degraded chitosan-graft polymethyl methacrylate film by 45% in 25 days

### Polyamide-6
- [[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]]: attached to fibers; reduced 50% polymer molar mass after 3 months

### Plasticized PVC (pPVC)
- [[aureobasidium-pullulans]]: colonizes within 25-40 weeks; grows on intact pPVC as sole carbon source; secretes extracellular esterase; causes weight loss

### Ground Waste Tire Rubber
- Resinicium bicolor: most effective of 15 white- and [[fungal-wood-decay-types-white-rot-brown-rot]] fungi for detoxification prior to devulcanization

## Concrete and Building Material Deterioration

### Mechanisms
- Fusarium spp.: loss of calcium and weight in concrete
- Aspergillus niger and Mycelia sterilia: increased cement porosity; loss of bending strength; low calcium leaching (acid production)
- Direct mycelial contact with cement surface NOT necessary

### Stone and Marble
- Black fungi (Phoma, Alternaria): interact with marble surfaces causing physical, chemical, and aesthetic damage

## Wool Degradation
- Trichophyton simii and A. niger: degrade wool in liquid culture
- Both cause weight loss and protein release

## Library and Cultural Heritage Biodeterioration

- 1919-1977: 234 species from 84 genera isolated from library materials (Zyska, 1997)
- Foxing: 25 years of research on fungal stains on paper (Arai, 2000)
- Wall paintings: fungal decay documented

## Wood Decay Fungi

### Classification by Decay Type

**White-Rot (Basidiomycetes primarily)**
- Most effective lignin degraders/mineralizers
- Several hundred species
- Hardwood > softwood degradation
- Simultaneous rot (T. versicolor) vs selective delignification (P. chrysosporium)
- Nonselective: [[trametes-versicolor]], [[ganoderma-applanatum]]
- Selective: Ceriporiopsis subvermispora, Phellinus nigrolimitatus

**Brown-Rot (Basidiomycetes)**
- Remove cellulose/hemicellulose; leave modified lignin as brown residue
- Common in softwood
- Fenton-type catalytic system (hydroxyl radicals)
- [[gloeophyllum-trabeum]] group (oxalic acid accumulation) vs [[coniophora-puteana]]/Poria placenta group
- Oxalic acid up to 600 uM/g maintained

**Soft-Rot (Ascomycetes and Imperfect Fungi)**
- Two types: Type I (cavities in secondary walls) and Type II (erosion)
- Better hardwood degradation
- Species: Allescheria, Graphium, Monodictys, Paecilomyces, Papulospora, Thielavia, Daldinia, Hypoxylon, Xylaria
- Daldinia concentrica: 53% weight loss in birch wood in 2 months

## Oil Spill and Petroleum Remediation

Fungi contribute to petroleum hydrocarbon degradation in both soil and marine environments:
- Trichoderma sp. M2 and Penicillium sp.: modify petroleum hydrocarbons
- [[fusarium-oxysporum]] and Trichoderma atroviride: produce esterases for hydrocarbon degradation
- ECM fungi survive in petroleum-contaminated soils, utilizing crude oil as nutrient
- Long-term environmental consequences less severe in agricultural/forest areas than coastal ecosystems (due to soil fungi/bacteria)
