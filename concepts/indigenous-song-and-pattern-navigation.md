# Indigenous Song and Pattern Navigation

## Overview

Indigenous peoples worldwide developed sophisticated systems of encoding navigation, environmental knowledge, and temporal information into song, dance, pattern, and art. These mnemonic systems served as living encyclopedias of ecological and geographical knowledge, enabling accurate wayfinding, seasonal prediction, and interspecies communication without reliance on written records or numerical data. In permaculture design, understanding these pattern-based knowledge systems offers insights into how complex information can be encoded, transmitted, and applied in sustainable ways.

## Song as Navigation and Timing

### Pacific Northwest Song Navigation

Anne Cameron documented song navigation among the Nootka Indians of British Columbia. Navigators possessed specific songs for each destination: a song for China, a song for Japan, a song for the big island, and a song for the smaller one. The navigator needed only to know the song to know their position and course.

These navigation songs recorded what were called "the streams and creeks of the sea" — ocean currents, headlands, bays, and star constellations. From California to the Aleutians, sea currents were relatively constant in speed and direction, assisting canoe travel. Steerswomen used the precise rhythm of song duration to time both current speed and boat speed through water. Current speed was likely timed between headlands, while boat speed was measured against a log or float. Song duration thus functioned as an accurate, reproducible timing mechanism.

### Song Stanzas as Precision Tools

Song stanzas function as highly accurate timers, accurate over extended periods and reproducible at any time. Song content recorded observations from prior voyages and remained open to receiving new data. This system represents an elegant fusion of art and practical science — entertainment, education, and navigational precision combined in a single, memorable medium.

## Interspecies Communication Through Pattern Understanding

### Animal Calling

Several indigenous cultures demonstrated the ability to call specific animal species through sound and behavioral pattern understanding:

- **Paiute wise men** could call deer
- **Gilbert Islanders** could call dolphins
- **Tribal Tasmanians** could call kangaroos

These practices required profound understanding of interspecific "pulser" patterns — the rhythmic, behavioral signals that different species use to communicate. This knowledge extended to practical applications: Eskimo navigated through fog by listening to the quail dialects specific to certain headlands.

### Fibrillation and Physiological Influence

Some practitioners demonstrated the ability to influence physiology through pattern understanding. The ability to induce fibrillation in heart nerves represents practical insight into pulser stress induction. Many tribal peoples could induce such behavioral states in other animal species or in people, sometimes referred to as voodoo or "singing."

## Pattern-Based Temporal Systems

### Phenomenological Time

Australian Aboriginal concepts of time extend far beyond the linear concept used in Western societies. Of far greater everyday importance was phenomenological (or phenological) time — time as given not by clocks but by the life phenomena of flowers, birds, and weather.

A Pitjatjantjara woman, upon seeing a small desert flower coming into bloom, could determine that dingoes in distant hill ranges were rearing pups and that it was time for her group to travel to collect them. Thousands of such ecological relationships were known to tribal peoples. Some signals might not occur for 100 or 500 years (such as the flowering of certain bamboo species), yet when they did occur, the associated actions and ceremonies were known and the linked phenomena understood.

### The Anasazi Spiral Calendar

One of the most remarkable examples of pattern-encoded information is the Anasazi spiral petroglyph near the top of a mesa in the desert country of the southwestern United States. This apparently simple spiral form contains 19 intercepts on its "horizon" line.

Three carefully balanced and shaped rock slabs served as gnomons, casting moon-shadows or directing vertical daggers of sunlight to specific points on the spiral. The 19 points at which the spiral intersects the horizontal axis correspond to positions where the shadow of the moon is cast by a gnomon, indicating the moon elevation cycle of approximately 18.6 years caused by the sway of Earth's axis.

This single, simple spiral thus records:
- **Lunar cycles** for regulating planting
- **Solar cycles** for timing ceremonies
- **The 19-year (18.6 year) drought and flood prediction cycle**

The knowledge so presented would require a small library to encode using conventional alphabetical and numerical systems. The significance of this arrangement was revealed through persistent investigation by Anna Sofaer, an artist with intuitive observational skills.

## The Mnemonics of Meaning

### The Buddhist Chant: Om Mani Padme Hum

The Tibetan Buddhist chant "Om mani padme hum" (pronounced "Aum ma-ni pay-may hung") serves as a mnemonic reminder of the pattern of events:

- **Aum (Om)**: The awakening or beginning harmonic — the sound of all stillness and the sounds of all time; the fundamental harmonic recalling the universe itself
- **Ma-ni**: The unchanging essence or diamantine core of all phenomena — truth, represented as a diamond, jewel, or thunderbolt, sometimes depicted as a blue orb or radiant jewel, sometimes as a source of lightning or fire
- **Pay-may**: "Enfolded in the heart of the lotus" — the visible and everyday unfolding of events, petals, or patterns revealing the essential unchanged core to understanding. The core itself, or the realization of it, is nirvana
- **Hung**: "It is here, now" — a declamation of belief, also preface to the "Om" or beginning of the new chant cycle

### The Dorje Figure

Dorje, or Dorje-chang, is the Tibetan Buddha-figure who holds the dorje (thunderbolt), represented as a radiant stone symbolizing cosmic energy. Dorje is considered the primordial Buddha of Tibet, who began the great succession of reincarnations. The figure is blue for eternity and may carry a bell signifying the voiceless wisdom of the inanimate or the sound of the void.

Dorje parallels Thor of the Norsemen, Durga of the Hindu, and thunderbolt figures of other tribal peoples, demonstrating the universal nature of these pattern-concepts across cultures.

## Universal Pattern Understanding

Many world beliefs share an essential core derived from contemplation of natural events. These beliefs represent ways of life and philosophies rather than dogmas or sets of measures. They precede and transcend the emphasis on the individual or the division of life into disciplines and categories.

When searching for the roots of belief, or meaning itself, one returns repeatedly to the oneness underlying science, word, song, art, and pattern. This integrated world view, found in Amerindian and Australian tribal cultures, uses analogies of rainbows, serpents, and song cycles to relate aspects of the natural world.

## Age and Wisdom in Tribal Society

In tribal society, wisdom is measured not by years lived but by degree of revelation. Those who understand and embody advanced knowledge are the most intuitive and therefore most entitled to special veneration. Such knowledge is almost invariably based on pattern understanding and is independent of sex or even age. One is "aged" by degree of revelation, not time spent living — there are some very unrevealed "elders" in the world.

## Relevance to Permaculture Design

Permaculture draws heavily on these indigenous pattern-understanding traditions:

1. **Information encoding**: Complex site data can be encoded in memorable patterns rather than abstract tables
2. **Observation-based timing**: Phenological indicators (flowering, bird behavior, weather patterns) provide more locally relevant timing than calendar dates
3. **Pattern literacy**: Training the ear and eye to read natural patterns provides a rich encyclopedia of environmental information unavailable to those who lack pattern knowledge
4. **Design integration**: The integration of art, science, and practical function in indigenous systems models the permaculture approach to holistic design
5. **Accessible knowledge**: Pattern, song, and dance make complex information available to all members of society, not just specialists

The challenge for modern permaculture designers is to study and portray knowledge in compact, memorable, and transmissible forms — to research and recreate surviving art forms that retain their meaning, and to reintegrate art with science and society's functions and needs.
