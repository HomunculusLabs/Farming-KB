# Nutrient Mobility and Deficiency Symptoms

## Overview

In plant nutrition, nutrient mobility refers to the ability of a nutrient to move within plant tissues once it has been absorbed. This mobility determines where deficiency symptoms first appear and provides critical diagnostic information for gardeners and growers. Mobile nutrients show deficiency symptoms in older leaves first (as the plant relocates them to new growth), while immobile nutrients show symptoms in new growth (because they cannot be translocated from older tissues).

## Mobile Nutrients

Mobile nutrients can be redistributed from older tissues to areas of new growth when supply becomes limited. These include:

### Nitrogen (N)
- **Mobile in both soil and plant**
- Deficiency appears as yellowing (chlorosis) of older leaves, starting at the tips and progressing along the margins
- Plants require nitrogen for amino acid synthesis, enzyme production, and chlorophyll formation
- Available as both ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻), with form affecting [[soil-ph]]
- Atmospheric nitrogen (78% of air) is unavailable to plants without microbial fixation
- The nitrogen cycle involves microbial partners (Rhizobia in legumes, free-living bacteria)
- Excess nitrogen promotes lush vegetative growth at the expense of flowers and fruit

### Phosphorus (P)
- **Moderately mobile in plants, relatively immobile in most soils**
- Deficiency shows as purplish or bluish-green discoloration of older leaves
- Critical for root development, flower formation, seed production, and energy transfer (ATP)
- Readily fixed (bound) by aluminum and iron in acidic soils, by calcium in alkaline soils
- Mycorrhizal fungi dramatically increase phosphorus availability to plants
- The phosphorus cycle is relatively slow compared to the nitrogen cycle
- Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (biofertilizers) can help release bound phosphorus

### Potassium (K)
- **Highly mobile in plants**
- Deficiency appears as leaf tip burns, bronzing, and marginal scorching of older leaves
- Does not form structural compounds but activates over 60 enzymes
- Essential for stomatal regulation, water movement, and disease resistance
- Critical for sugar transport from leaves to other plant parts
- Moves as a cation (K⁺) and is held by [[cation-exchange-capacity]] in soils

### Magnesium (Mg)
- **Mobile in plants**
- Deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis (yellowing between leaf veins) in older leaves
- Central atom in the chlorophyll molecule — essential for photosynthesis
- Also activates enzymes involved in phosphorus transport
- Found in dolomitic limestone and Epsom salts as common garden sources

## Immobile (or Semi-Mobile) Nutrients

Immobile nutrients cannot be readily relocated from older tissues. Deficiency symptoms appear in new growth, growing tips, and young leaves.

### Calcium (Ca)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency causes malformation of new leaves (whiptail), curling, and growing tip death
- Essential for cell wall structure (calcium pectate in middle lamella)
- Moves through the plant primarily via the transpiration stream in xylem
- Meristematic tissue (growing tips) has high calcium requirements
- Soil calcium availability is strongly influenced by pH and cation exchange capacity
- Sources include limestone (calcium carbonate), gypsum (calcium sulfate), and bone meal

### Iron (Fe)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency appears as interveinal chlorosis in young leaves (new growth)
- Critical for chlorophyll synthesis and electron transport in photosynthesis
- Availability is highly pH-dependent — iron becomes unavailable in alkaline soils (above pH 7.5)
- Involved in oxidation-reduction reactions
- Siderophores (organic molecules) produced by microbes help solubilize iron
- Mycorrhizal fungi improve iron uptake

### Manganese (Mn)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency shows interveinal chlorosis similar to iron, but with brown spots on leaves
- Essential for photosynthesis (water-splitting enzyme in photosystem II)
- Involved in oxidation-reduction reactions like iron
- Availability decreases at high soil pH

### Zinc (Zn)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency causes shortened internodes (rosetting), small leaves, and reduced leaf size
- Required for auxin synthesis (a plant growth hormone) and enzyme activation
- Mycorrhizal fungi significantly improve zinc uptake
- Common in cold, wet soils and high-pH conditions

### Boron (B)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency causes growing tip death, flower and fruit formation problems
- Essential for sugar transport, cell wall formation, and pollen tube growth
- Works in conjunction with potassium for sugar movement
- Leaches easily from sandy soils

### Copper (Cu)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency causes wilting, excessive leaf branching, and growing tip damage
- Required for several enzymes including those involved in photosynthesis and lignin synthesis
- Deficiency is rare but can occur in organic (peaty) soils

### Molybdenum (Mo)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency resembles nitrogen deficiency (general yellowing) because molybdenum is needed for nitrogen fixation
- Required by the enzyme nitrogenase (used by Rhizobia to fix atmospheric nitrogen)
- Also needed for nitrate reductase enzyme
- Deficiency most common in acidic soils

### Nickel (Ni)
- **Immobile in plants**
- Deficiency causes leaf tip burns (necrosis)
- Required for the enzyme urease, which breaks down urea
- Essential element recognized relatively recently

## Micronutrients Overview

Micronutrients (trace minerals) are required in smaller quantities than macronutrients but are equally essential for plant health. The key micronutrients are:

| Nutrient | Mobility | Key Functions | Common Deficiency Signs |
|----------|----------|---------------|------------------------|
| Iron (Fe) | Immobile | Chlorophyll, redox reactions | Interveinal chlorosis (young leaves) |
| Manganese (Mn) | Immobile | Photosynthesis, enzymes | Interveinal chlorosis + brown spots |
| Zinc (Zn) | Immobile | Auxin synthesis, enzymes | Rosetting, small leaves |
| Copper (Cu) | Immobile | Photosynthesis, lignin | Wilting, tip dieback |
| Boron (B) | Immobile | Sugar transport, cell walls | Tip death, poor fruit set |
| Molybdenum (Mo) | Immobile | N fixation, nitrate reduction | Yellowing (like N deficiency) |
| Nickel (Ni) | Immobile | Urease enzyme | Leaf tip burns |
| Chlorine (Cl) | Mobile | Osmosis, photosynthesis | Wilting, chlorosis |

## Factors Influencing Nutrient Availability

Multiple soil and environmental factors affect whether nutrients are accessible to plant roots:

### Soil pH
- The single most important factor affecting nutrient availability
- Most nutrients are optimally available in the slightly acidic range (pH 6.0–7.0)
- Acidic soils (below pH 6.0): excess aluminum, manganese, and iron can become toxic; calcium, magnesium, and molybdenum become deficient
- Alkaline soils (above pH 7.5): iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and boron become unavailable
- Phosphorus is particularly sensitive to pH, becoming locked up at both extremes
- Soil testing for pH is fundamental to diagnosing nutrient problems

### Temperature
- Influences microbial activity that makes nutrients available
- Affects root growth and metabolic activity
- Cold soils slow nutrient uptake even when nutrients are present
- Nitrogen fixation by Rhizobia is temperature-dependent

### Soil Moisture
- Water is the primary solvent for nutrient transport to roots
- Both drought and waterlogging reduce nutrient availability
- Mass flow of dissolved nutrients to roots depends on water movement
- Excess water displaces oxygen, creating anaerobic conditions that change nutrient chemistry

### Soil Aeration
- Well-aerated soils support beneficial microbial activity
- Anaerobic conditions reduce nitrogen fixation and promote denitrification
- Compaction reduces root penetration and limits access to nutrients

### Organic Matter
- Increases cation exchange capacity, holding more nutrients
- Provides slow-release nutrients as it decomposes
- Supports microbial populations that solubilize nutrients
- Buffers soil pH changes
- Humus improves soil structure, water retention, and nutrient cycling

## Macronutrients Summary

The six macronutrients required in relatively large quantities:

| Nutrient | Symbol | Mobile? | Primary Functions |
|----------|--------|---------|-------------------|
| Nitrogen | N | Yes | Amino acids, chlorophyll, enzymes, growth |
| Phosphorus | P | Moderate | Roots, flowers, seeds, energy (ATP) |
| Potassium | K | Yes | Enzyme activation, stomatal control, disease resistance |
| Calcium | Ca | No | Cell walls, membrane integrity, signaling |
| Magnesium | Mg | Yes | Chlorophyll center, enzyme activation |
| Sulfur | S | Moderate | Amino acids (cysteine, methionine), proteins, enzymes |

## Diagnostic Approach

When diagnosing nutrient problems:

1. **Note which leaves show symptoms** — older leaves suggest mobile nutrient deficiency; new growth suggests immobile nutrient deficiency
2. **Check soil pH** — this single factor can explain multiple deficiency symptoms simultaneously
3. **Consider soil type** — sandy soils leach mobile nutrients; clay soils may bind nutrients
4. **Review recent practices** — over-fertilization of one nutrient can induce deficiency of another (antagonism)
5. **Get a soil test** — the only reliable way to confirm nutrient status and pH
6. **Consider microbial health** — mycorrhizal fungi, Rhizobia, and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria affect nutrient availability
