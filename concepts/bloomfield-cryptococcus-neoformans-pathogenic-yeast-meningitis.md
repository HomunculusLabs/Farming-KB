# Cryptococcus Neoformans, Pathogenic Yeast, and Meningitis

Cryptococcus neoformans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen that causes
life-threatening meningitis, most commonly in patients with compromised
immune systems. The fungus is ubiquitous in the environment, found in
soil contaminated with pigeon droppings and in association with certain
tree species, yet it possesses a remarkable capacity to colonize the
human central nervous system when immune defenses falter. It causes
meningitis in approximately 10 percent of AIDS patients and remains a
leading cause of mortality among HIV-positive individuals worldwide.

## Infection Process

Cryptococcus is very widespread, and humans probably encounter its cells
on a frequent basis. When yeast cells or spores of Cryptococcus are
inhaled, they are usually removed in the lifelong river of mucus that
cleanses the deepest recesses of our lungs and then, by way of the
throat, pours into the acid bath of the stomach. But when critical
defenses are damaged in the immune-compromised patient, Cryptococcus
travels from the lungs to the nervous system. In the brain it forms
abscesses called cryptococcomas that lead to debilitating headaches, and
eventually to blindness, dementia, and death.

## The Polysaccharide Capsule

A conspicuous virulence factor of Cryptococcus is the presence of a
capsule built from mixtures of sugar molecules that extends as a wide
halo around every cell. This polysaccharide structure helps the fungus
avoid detection and destruction by the immune system. The capsule acts
as a physical barrier that interferes with phagocytosis by macrophages
and can even cloak the yeast within the phagocyte, allowing the fungus
to survive and replicate inside the immune cell that was supposed to
destroy it.

## Diagnosis

Cryptococcosis is diagnosed by collecting samples of cerebrospinal
fluid from a lumbar puncture. The clear fluid in the spinal column
circulates all the way through the ventricles of the brain, and if a
few drops of the sample from an infected patient are spread on a culture
plate, colonies of Cryptococcus will appear in a day or two. Swifter
diagnosis can be performed by direct microscopic examination of the
cerebrospinal fluid or by using immunological tests that detect
polysaccharides produced by the fungus.

## Treatment

Cryptococcal infections are treated with high doses of amphotericin B,
delivered as an intravenous infusion, or in extreme cases injected
directly into the brain through intrathecal administration. Amphotericin
acts against fungal cells by binding to ergosterol in the plasma
membrane, causing the membrane to perforate so that the cell leaks
salts and sugars vital to its survival. Amphotericin is often prescribed
in conjunction with 5-fluorocytosine, which impairs RNA function and DNA
synthesis. The combination is synergistic, providing the same cessation
of fungal growth with lower doses of each drug.

Fluconazole, an azole antifungal agent, is a third effective drug. It
blocks ergosterol synthesis by inhibiting the enzyme 14-alpha-demethylase
and is effective as an oral medication, making it particularly valuable
in resource-limited settings. Before the 1950s, death was swift and
certain for a patient with cryptococcal meningitis. With prudent use of
modern drugs, many cases in otherwise healthy patients can be cured, and
the suffering of AIDS patients can usually be alleviated.

## Global Health Impact

The International AIDS Conference in Durban, South Africa in July 2000
provided a harrowing picture of cryptococcal meningitis in the
developing world. The tragedy of terminal AIDS in a hospital with clean
sheets and television cannot compare with the nightmare of the
poverty-stricken, HIV-positive African patient with an overpowering
headache. Fluconazole, patented by Pfizer under the brand name Diflucan,
cost about $10 per daily dose at that time, well beyond the means of
most people in countries devastated by AIDS. In 2001, Pfizer made the
groundbreaking decision to offer an unlimited free supply of fluconazole
to fifty of the world's poorest nations.

## Sexual Stage: Filobasidiella

Cryptococcus has a known sexual stage called Filobasidiella, a
basidiospore-producing fungus. The discovery that the yeast Cryptococcus
and the filamentous Filobasidiella were different manifestations of the
same organism was made by K. J. Kwon-Chung in 1976. She isolated the
sexual stage from pigeon droppings in which the yeast cells recognized
as Cryptococcus were growing alongside the basidiospore-producing phase.

The existence of two names for one organism reflects the historical
problem of pleomorphy in mycology. Cryptococcus and Filobasidiella are
different manifestations of the same species. Filobasidiella spores are
smaller than the yeast cells of Cryptococcus and are more likely to
enter deep into the lungs if inhaled, representing one possible
explanation for the pathogenicity of the organism.

## Melanin and Neurotropism

Cryptococcus can manufacture melanin from L-dopa, dopamine, or
epinephrine, all concentrated in the brain. The pigment is deposited on
the inner surface of the cell wall, creating a resistant barrier that
confers partial resistance to amphotericin B and to immune defenses.
Some mycologists believe that melanin production explains the fungus's
neurotropism: it may attempt to grow elsewhere in the body, but without
the melanin precursors available in the brain, the yeast cells cannot
become pigmented and remain vulnerable to immune clearance.

## Opportunism and Virulence

Medical mycologists do not regard Cryptococcus as a well-adapted
pathogen. It is rebuffed by most people unless their immune systems are
damaged. It is a perfect example of an opportunist, ordinarily living on
dead tissues or feces as a saprobe, but sufficiently robust to tolerate
occasional incarceration in a living host. The sexual stage of the
pathogen has been found in nature associated with pigeon droppings and
certain tree species, particularly eucalyptus. Cryptococcus can also
utilize creatinine as a nitrogen source, an unusual capability among
fungi that may contribute to its survival in pigeon guano.

## Mating Types

Two mating types of Cryptococcus are recognized, designated the a-strain
and the alpha-strain. Interestingly, the alpha-strain is vastly
overrepresented among clinical isolates, suggesting that it possesses
enhanced virulence. Understanding the relationship between mating type
and pathogenicity has been an important avenue of research for
understanding the biology of opportunistic fungal pathogens.
