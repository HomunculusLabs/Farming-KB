# Time and Timing Guideline

Time is one of the four "missing keys" that Allan Savory identified as
essential to understanding and managing land health. While time itself does
not rank as a tool in the [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]] Management model, it is a factor
that guides the use of all tools. Savory calls it the factor that was "staring
us in the face and always overlooked" throughout the history of range
management.

## Time as the Missing Key

The French scientist André Voisin was the first to recognize that overgrazing
had nothing to do with the number of animals in a pasture. He proved that
overgrazing is controlled by the time plants are exposed to animals and the
time allowed for recovery between exposures. If animals remain in any one
place too long, or if they return before plants have recovered, overgrazing
occurs regardless of how few animals are present.

Savory extended this insight to show that time is the determining factor not
only for grazing but also for trampling. The soil disturbance needed for
health becomes destructive if prolonged too much or repeated too soon. Time
also governs planting and harvesting, chemical applications, and all the
rhythms of agriculture.

## The Two Time Problems: Overgrazing and Overrest

Overgrazing occurs when a plant is bitten while it is regrowing on energy
from sacrificed roots. This happens in two ways: animals remain in an area
long enough to rebite a regrowing plant, or animals return before a severely
grazed plant has recovered. Both problems are fundamentally about time.

Overrest, the opposite problem, occurs when plants in brittle environments
go too long without either grazing or adequate animal impact. Old material
accumulates, blocking sunlight from reaching growing points and eventually
killing the plant. Conventional management, by reducing animal numbers to
address overgrazing, inadvertently worsens overrest across vast areas.

## Time in Practice: Grazing and Recovery Periods

The practical application of the time principle centers on two related
variables: grazing periods (how long animals remain in a paddock) and
recovery periods (how long plants have to recover before animals return).
These are linked by a simple formula: Grazing Period equals Recovery Period
divided by (Number of Paddocks minus one).

Planning must account for the fact that recovery periods need to change as
daily plant growth rates change during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. In brittle
environments, where ninety percent of a year's forage may grow in a few
weeks of prime conditions, growth rates fluctuate enormously. No single
recovery period can suit all conditions when paddock numbers are low.

Time management becomes even more critical during the dormant season.
Animals entering a paddock for a second or third time during dormancy
will find lower protein and energy and more fiber than on first selection.
By manipulating recovery periods and grazing periods, managers can control
the number of times animals select from each paddock, directly affecting
both [[savory-livestock-nutrition-and-supplementation]] and wildlife feed availability.

## Time Across the Ecosystem Processes

The time dimension affects all four [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]] blocks. For the
[[savory-mineral-cycle-management]],
the timing of grazing relative to plant growth stages affects the return of
nutrients to the soil surface.

For [[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]],
the timing of disturbance relative to rest governs whether plant communities
advance, stagnate, or deteriorate.
