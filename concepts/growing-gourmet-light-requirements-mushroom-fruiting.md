# Light Requirements for Mushroom Fruiting

## Overview

Light plays a critical role in mushroom fruiting, serving as an
environmental signal that alerts mycelium to the presence of an open-
air environment where spores can be effectively dispersed. Different
mushroom species have evolved highly specific light requirements,
ranging from near-darkness tolerance to precise photoperiod and
spectral wavelength needs. Understanding these requirements is
essential for successful [[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]] and healthy
fruitbody development.

## The Role of Light in Fruiting

In nature, light acts as a signal alerting the mycelium to an open-
air environment where, should mushrooms form, spores can be spread
into the air. Light controls stem elongation and cap development.
Ideal light conditions -- intensity and wavelength -- vary with each
species and strain. Indirect natural light, or the dappled light
filtering through a forest canopy, is considered ideal for woodland
mushrooms.

Modest light is not harmful to developing mushroom mycelium during
spawn run; it seems unaffected by its presence. However, bright,
unfiltered, direct sunlight is damaging. Light is especially harmful
when intensities exceed 10,000 lux. The fluorescent lights used in
indoor facilities do not inhibit mycelial growth, and in some
circumstances may stimulate early primordia formation.

## Light Intensity Requirements by Species

Species described in this source show a wide range of light needs
during primordia formation:

- **Shiitake:** 500-1000 lux
- **Oyster Mushrooms (P. ostreatus):** 500-1000 lux for initiation,
  with Kalberer (1974) showing total yield maximized at 300-430 lux
  at 12 hours per day
- **Enoki (Flammulina velutipes):** 100-200 lux (very low light)
- **Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus):** 200-500 lux
- **Nameko (Pholiota nameko):** 100-200 lux
- **Maitake (Grifola frondosa):** 200-500 lux
- **Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum):** 500-600 lux
- **Buna-shimeji (Hypsizygus marmoreus):** 400-600 lux
- **King Oyster (Pleurotus eryngii):** 500-1000 lux
- **Tree Oyster (P. ostreatus):** 1000-1500 (2000) lux

## Research on Oyster Mushroom Light Response

Eger et al. (1974) determined that P. ostreatus forms the most
primordia in response to a light intensity of 2000 lux, or about
185 foot candles. Light intensities exceeding 10,000 lux caused a
precipitous drop in primordia formation. At 10,000 lux, primordia
failed to form entirely. Continuous, optimal light stimulation during
primordia formation resulted in the largest population of primordia.

Kalberer (1974) showed that total yield was maximized (and stem mass
minimized) at 300-430 lux at 12 hours per day. The critical primordia
formation period extends for 6-7 days. If total light exposure,
measured in "lux hours" per day, fails to exceed 2400, equivalent to
100 lux of continuous lighting, P. ostreatus is triggered into "coral"
formation -- abnormal, elongated mushroom structures.

## Wavelength Specificity

For most species, light levels between 50-1000 lux and 380-480
nanometers (green to blue) seem most stimulatory to primordia
formation. Leatham and Stahlman (1987, 1989) conducted trials with
Shiitake on chemically defined media, showing that the wavelengths
most stimulatory for primordia formation were between 400-500
nanometers -- what we know as blue to ultraviolet light.

At low calcium levels, Shiitake mycelium formed mushrooms when
stimulated by light between 600-680 nanometers (red light). At high
calcium levels, blue light was most effective. This interplay between
light wavelength and calcium concentration continues to be a subject
of great research interest.

## Practical Lighting Recommendations

For indoor cultivation, Stamets recommends using six 8-foot-long
"Daylight" 6500 Kelvin fluorescent tubes to light each 1000 square
foot growing room, supplemented with natural light through diffusion
panels. This provides broad-spectrum illumination that meets the
needs of most gourmet mushroom species.

For species requiring very low light (like Enoki at 100-200 lux),
dim lighting or indirect ambient room light may suffice. For light-
hungry species, full-spectrum fluorescent or LED lighting at the
appropriate intensity and duration is necessary.

## Consequences of Improper Lighting

Insufficient light causes several problems:

- Elongated stems with small, underdeveloped caps
- "Coral" formations in Oyster mushrooms
- Failure to form primordia altogether
- Abnormal malformations similar to high CO2 environments

Excessive light (above 10,000 lux) can inhibit mycelial growth and
primordia formation entirely, and may alter pigmentation of
developing caps.
