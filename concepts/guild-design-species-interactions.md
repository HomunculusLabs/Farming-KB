## Guild Design and Species Interactions

Guild design is the methodology of assembling beneficial plant and animal communities — polycultures — that cooperate to enhance productivity, reduce inputs, and create self-managing ecosystems. Building on the spatial framework of [[permaculture-beginners-guide-zones-and-sectors]], guild design addresses the biological relationships between species, seeking to maximize mutual benefits while minimizing antagonism.

## Defining the Guild

A **guild** is a harmonious assembly of species clustered around a central element (plant or animal). This assembly acts in relation to the central element to:

- **Assist its health** — through nutrient provision, pest protection, and improved growing conditions
- **Aid management** — by reducing the work required from the human designer
- **Buffer adverse environmental effects** — through shelter, frost protection, wind reduction, and microclimate moderation

The concept draws from natural observation: in the wild, assemblies of plants or animals of different species are often found occurring together consistently over their range. Closer examination reveals sets of mutual benefits that arise from this convivial togetherness — one bird species acting as lookout for another, or defending others from hawks. Guild design attempts to replicate and enhance these natural beneficial assemblies in cultivated systems.

## Reasons for Species Association

### Root Competition Reduction

Almost all cultivated food trees thrive in herbal ground covers rather than grasses. Invasive grasses compete aggressively with tree roots for water and nutrients. Replacing grass with diverse herbaceous ground covers eliminates this competition and allows trees to access surface soil resources.

### Pest Control

Guilds assist pest control through multiple mechanisms:

- **Anti-feedants**: Species that produce bitter or unpalatable chemicals. Nasturtium roots, for example, provide root chemicals to tomatoes or gooseberries that deter whitefly. Many plants, when fermented or extracted in water, can be sprayed as pest deterrents on vulnerable crops.
- **Biological fumigation**: Some species kill root parasites or predators. *[[crotalaria]]* captures nematodes that damage citrus and solanaceous roots. *[[tagetes]]* (marigolds) fumigate soils against grasses and nematodes.
- **Predator hosting**: Almost all small-flowered plants — especially *Quillaja*, many *[[comfrey]] and globe artichokes allow tree roots to feed at the surface without competition. Spring bulbs (daffodils) and winter-grown wild *Allium* species die down in mid-spring and do not compete with deciduous tree roots during summer dry periods, nor do they intercept light rains.
- **Soil building**: High [[casuarina]]*, and *Pultenaea*
- Sugar-providing grasses (sugar cane)
- High humus producers (bananas)

Many can be slashed or trimmed to produce rich mulch below trees or between rows, cycling nutrients from the guild back to the central element.

### Physical Shelter

Hardy windbreak species provide frost protection, exclude salty or hot winds, supply mulch, and moderate the environment. Examples include borders of bamboo, cane grasses, *Casuarina*, hardy palms, and tamarisks. In-crop shade shelter from legumes is needed by avocado, citrus, cocoa, coffee, and other crops requiring partial shade. In-crop trees can eliminate frost effects in marginal frost areas.

### Culinary Associates

Keeping common culinary associates together — tomatoes with parsley and basil; potatoes with mint; dill with cucumbers — reduces work by allowing simultaneous harvest for cooking, salads, or processing. Dill and apples also pair well, raw or cooked, and dill (an Umbelliferae) hosts predatory wasps below apple trees.

## Vertebrate Guild Members

### Ground Foragers

Pigs or poultry can be used to clear fallen fruit that hosts fruit fly or larval pest forms. Foragers run in orchards during the relatively short period when fruit is falling and rotting, or used to eat reject fruit and deposit manure — closing the nutrient loop.

### Insectivores

Birds that search bark crevices — woodpeckers, honey-eaters — find resting larvae and egg masses. To encourage these, plant scattered flowering shrubs and herbaceous plants such as *Kniphofia*, *Banksia*, *Salvia*, *Buddleia*, and *Fuchsia*, all of which provide insect and nectar foods for insectivorous birds.

### Mollusc Control

Snails and slugs are almost totally controlled by a duck flock on range. Several large lizards (*Tiliqua* spp.) also feed primarily on snails. Ducks can be ranged seasonally (autumn to spring) in plant systems and in summer on marshlands, though appropriate scheduling is essential since ducks will eat seedlings.

### Guard Animals

A small number of guard dogs, fed and kennelled in orchards, provide sufficient control for fox predation on orchard poultry foragers. Dogs reared with domestic poultry do not attack the flocks themselves. Hawk kites suspended over berry crops, or flown as light model planes over extensive grain crops, deter flock-bird predators and are more dependable than natural hawks.

## The Analytic Approach to Guild Selection

Mollison proposes a formal analytic structure for evaluating species interactions:

### Interaction Classes

From study of complex home gardens and botanical gardens:

- **~80% of all plant species** can co-mingle without ill effect (neutral interaction)
- **~10–15%** greatly assist others in one or more ways (positive interaction)
- **~5%** show antagonistic behavior toward one or more other species (negative interaction)

### Symbolic Notation

- **+** : Beneficial interaction — yield above that of a monoculture control
- **0** : No change — neutral interaction
- **−** : Reduction in yield or vigor from interaction

For two useful species, the interaction matrix yields nine possible outcomes: three beneficial, three neutral, and three antagonistic.

### States of Action

A finer distinction is made between:

- **Co-action** (unilateral action): one species acts on the other without reciprocal effect
- **Interaction** (mutual action): both species act on each other reciprocally
- **Inaction** (neutrality): no detectable action between species

In the interaction matrix, **(++)** and **(−−)** likely represent mutual action (interaction). **(−0), (0−), (+0), (0+), (+−), (−+)** likely represent co-action (one species acting unilaterally). **(00)** represents inaction.

### Symbiosis (++)

The **(++)** interaction is called **symbiosis** — common in both nature and society, it is a "win-win" situation ideally suited to guild development. The mycorrhizal associates of higher plants exemplify this, where mutualism or fair trade occurs between a plant and its root fungal associate.

### Practical Guidance

Because of the vagaries of weather in any given year, many farmers accept a **(~+)** effect (neutral for one, positive for the other) to ensure at least getting a crop, even if the "losing" species underperforms. It is always safer to mix or complicate a crop than to depend entirely on a single main crop. The guiding principle: **be guided by analyses but study reality** — theoretical interaction matrices must be validated by actual field observation.

## Design Implications

Every designer and gardener can plan guilds for specific target species, specific pests [[jadam-soil-management-no-till-and-weed-control]] needs, and specific garden beds or orchards. The guild concept transforms agriculture from a battle against nature into a collaboration with it — assembling communities of species that work together to produce food, manage pests, build soil, and moderate climate, all while reducing the energy and material inputs required from the human manager.

The apple-centred guild illustrated in Mollison's text demonstrates the principle: a central apple tree surrounded by nitrogen-fixing legumes, pest-deterring companion plants, predator-hosting umbellifers, mulch-providing comfrey, wind-sheltering bamboo, and foraging poultry that clean up fallen fruit — each element serving multiple functions, each function served by multiple elements.
