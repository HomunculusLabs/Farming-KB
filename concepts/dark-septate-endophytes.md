# Dark Septate Endophytes

Dark septate endophytes (DSE) are a diverse group of root-colonizing fungi characterized by melanized, septate hyphae that form symbiotic associations with plant roots. They are among the most widespread and abundant root-associated fungi in many ecosystems, yet remain poorly studied compared to mycorrhizal fungi.

## Definition and Morphology

Dark septate endophytes are defined by their distinctive morphology within root tissues:
- **Melanized hyphae**: dark-pigmented (due to melanin in cell walls), regularly septate hyphae growing intercellularly and intracellularly within root cortical cells
- **Microsclerotia**: densely aggregated hyphal structures formed within root cells, serving as survival and propagation structures
- **Chlamydospores**: thick-walled resting spores produced in soil or on root surfaces
- In culture, DSE characteristically have thick-walled, dark-pigmented, septate hyphae and are usually sterile or very slow to sporulate

The dark pigmentation from melanin provides resistance to environmental stresses including UV radiation, temperature extremes, and microbial attack. This melanization distinguishes DSE from other root endophytes and contributes to their ecological success in [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] fungi may be limited
- Commonly found in Arctic and alpine plant communities, saline soils, heavy metal-contaminated sites, and dry habitats
- Frequently colonize non-mycorrhizal plant families (e.g., Brassicaceae, Caryophyllaceae)
- Often co-occur with mycorrhizal fungi in the same root systems

## Taxonomic Diversity

DSE represent a polyphyletic assemblage drawn from multiple fungal lineages:
- **Ascomycota**: the majority of described DSE species belong to the Ascomycota, particularly the orders Helotiales, Pleosporales, and Capnodiales
- Common genera include Phialocephala, Cadophora, Leptodontidium, Chloridium, and Acephala
- Many DSE isolates remain unidentified due to their sterility in culture and the difficulty of connecting anamorphic (asexual) and teleomorphic (sexual) stages

## Host Interactions

### Morphological Patterns

DSE exhibit two main types of root colonization:
- **Ectendophytic colonization**: hyphae grow both on the root surface and within cortical cells, sometimes forming intracellular coils or microsclerotia
- **Intercellular colonization**: hyphaes grow primarily between cortical cells, resembling the Hartig net of ectomycorrhizal associations (particularly in conifer hosts)

### Functional Roles

The functional significance of DSE associations remains debated but includes:
- **Nutrient acquisition**: some evidence suggests DSE enhance phosphorus and nitrogen uptake, though less efficiently than mycorrhizal fungi
- **[[query-how-do-phytohormones-regulate-plant-growth-and-stress-responses]] tolerance
- Others may be commensalists, colonizing roots without significant benefit or harm
- Under certain conditions, DSE may act as weak parasites, particularly under nutrient-rich conditions

## Ecology in Extreme Environments

DSE are particularly prominent in stressful habitats:
- **Arctic and alpine**: often the dominant root-associated fungi where cold temperatures limit mycorrhizal fungi
- **Saline soils**: melanized cell walls may provide osmotic protection
- **Heavy metal contamination**: some DSE can sequester or tolerate high metal concentrations
- **Dry habitats**: DSE may help maintain root function during drought

## Isolation and Study Methods

Studying DSE requires specific approaches:
- Root tissues are collected, washed, and surface-sterilized (serial washing often preferred over chemical sterilization)
- Segments are plated onto malt extract agar or potato dextrose agar, often with antibiotics
- DSE grow slowly, requiring prolonged incubation (weeks to months)
