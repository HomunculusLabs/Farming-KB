## Overview

The chemical stability of psilocybin and related indole compounds in
[[neurotropic-fungi]] is a critical but often overlooked factor that has led to
contradictory results in the scientific literature. Understanding the
degradation pathways of these compounds is essential for accurate chemical
analysis, reliable ethnobotanical research, and safe consumption practices.

## Age-Dependent Degradation

The age of fungal specimens is one of the most important variables affecting
the detectability and potency of psychoactive compounds. Repke et al. (1977)
demonstrated that the presence of baeocystin varies significantly according
to specimen age. In *[[psilocybe-baeocystis]]* and *P. cyanescens*, specimens
analyzed 20-60 days after collection showed no trace of indole compounds
whatsoever.

This age-related degradation aligns with [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] of *[[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]], not merely unstable
ones. This was dramatically demonstrated by Guzmán in 1958 while exploring
Oaxaca in search of neurotropic fungi. He experienced vivid colored
hallucinations without having consumed any fungi. He had been sleeping in a
small closed-door room filled with a large collection of fresh or
semi-fermented specimens of a mixture of *Psilocybe* species (*P.
caerulescens*, *P. cordispora*, *P. cubensis*, *P. mexicana* and *P.
zapotecorum*). The air was heavy with a strong acrid aroma, and his
prolonged exposure resulted in hallucinations that stopped immediately when
he left the room for fresh air.

This observation has significant implications for understanding both the
chemistry and the traditional ceremonial practices involving large quantities
of fresh mushrooms in enclosed spaces.

## Impact on Chemical Studies

The volatility and degradation of these compounds has caused numerous
problems in the scientific literature:

### Hofmann"s Negative Results

Hofmann, working with material gathered by Wasson in a Mexican market,
found no indoles in specimens of *[[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]]* across
multiple studies may partly reflect specimen age and handling differences:

**Studies finding psilocybin**: Robbers et al. (1969), Ola'h (1969),
Fiussello and Ceruti-Scurti (1972), Pollock (1976), Bresinsky and Besl
(1990).

**Studies finding no psilocybin**: Mantle and Waight (1969), Ott and
Guzmán (1976), Beug and Bigwood (1982), Stijve (1987), Stijve et al.
(1984), Stijve and Meijer (1993).

Allen and Merlin (1992) discussed the resulting doubts about the
psychoactive properties of this species. The current consensus leans toward
*P. foenisecii* being more toxic than neurotropic.

## Implications for Ethnobotanical Research

The age-dependent degradation of psilocybin has several important
implications:

1. **Fresh specimens are essential** for reliable chemical analysis and
   for ceremonial use. Indigenous practices of using fresh mushrooms are
   chemically well-founded.

2. **Herbarium specimens are unreliable** for quantitative or even
   qualitative assessment of psychoactive compound content. Chemical
   results from old herbarium material should be treated with extreme
   caution.

3. **Storage conditions matter**. Sealed, cool, dark storage slows but
   does not prevent degradation. The indigenous practice of discarding
   specimens older than one year reflects practical wisdom about the
   rate of compound loss.

4. **Environmental exposure during ceremonies** with large quantities of
   fresh mushrooms in enclosed spaces may contribute to the overall
   psychoactive experience through inhalation of volatile compounds.

## Recommendations for Research

Based on the evidence from Guzmán, Allen & Gartz and subsequent researchers:

- Always record the age and storage history of specimens used in chemical
  analysis
- Prioritize fresh or recently collected material for quantitative studies
- Include taxonomic verification by a qualified mycologist alongside
  chemical analysis
- Consider the volatile nature of these compounds when designing storage
  and transport protocols for research specimens
