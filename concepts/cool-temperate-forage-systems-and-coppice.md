# Cool Temperate Forage Systems and Coppice

## Overview

Cool temperate climates present distinct challenges: short growing seasons, frost risk, cold winds, and limited winter forage availability. Mollison's documentation of successful cool temperate systems reveals a toolbox of strategies centered on nitrogen-fixing forage trees, coppice systems, small livestock integration, and protected growing environments that extend seasons and multiply yields.

## Tagasaste: The Premier Cool Climate Forage Tree

Tagasaste (*Chamaecytisus palmensis*, syn. *Cytisus proliferus*) is a fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing leguminous shrub native to the Canary Islands that has proven exceptionally valuable in cool temperate regions including New Zealand, Tasmania, and southern Australia.

### Growth and Yield

- Trees yield up to 7 meters of mulch trimmings per annum
- Provides summer forage from coppice and short-period browsing by sheep
- At 1-meter in-row spacing with 2-meter between rows, tagasaste provides productive browse while grasses thrive between rows (verified in DSIR trials by Doug Davies at Banks Peninsula, New Zealand)

### Establishment Techniques

At Canterbury Plain, New Zealand, tagasaste has been successfully drilled with turnip for future sheep forage. Seedling trees establish through the brassica crop, using the turnip as a nurse crop that provides temporary ground cover and microclimate protection during establishment.

### Protection Function

At Taheke, New Zealand, avocado trees in hard frost areas thrive when planted within clumps of tagasaste. The tagasaste provides wind shelter and frost protection, creating a microclimate warm enough for subtropical species. Kikuyu grass slashed around the base provides additional mulch.

## Gorse as a Pioneer Nurse Crop

While gorse (*Ulex europaeus*) is widely regarded as a problematic invasive weed, Mollison documents its deliberate use as a pioneer nurse crop in New Zealand reforestation projects.

### The Gorse-to-Forest Succession

At Collingwood River, New Zealand, Dick Nichols manages 280 hectares of rampant gorse using a systematic succession strategy:

1. **Year 0-1**: "Zone 1" areas are fully cut and planted to pioneer trees. Lines of gorse are rolled down or slashed, and eucalyptus, pines, and acacia are planted
2. **Year 1-2**: Tamatillo cuttings are planted directly into the gorse slash
3. **Year 2-3**: Remaining gorse is slashed, and mature rainforest species are slit-seeded into the slash
4. **Year 5-6**: The gorse has rotted, and rainforest establishes under acacia (which is in turn felled). Native birds carry in rainforest seed

This approach converts gorse from a liability to an asset: it provides nitrogen fixation, wind protection, bird habitat, and organic matter as it dies back. The key is active management of the succession rather than passive control.

### Gorse as Rough Mulch

At Nelson, New Zealand, Dick Roberts uses gorse as "rough mulch" to protect fruit and forage trees in steep sheep pasture. The reduction of weed species protects the succeeding crop, and tree crops eventually shade out the gorse when thickly planted.

## Willow Coppice Systems

Willow (*Salix* species) coppice is one of the most versatile multi-yield systems in cool temperate permaculture, documented extensively near Christchurch, New Zealand.

### Yields from Willow Coppice

A single willow coppice field can produce:
- **Basketry material**: Traditional craft use
- **Medicinals**: Willow bark contains salicin, a precursor to aspirin
- **Livestock forage**: Fresh growth is palatable to many species
- **"Stickwood" for radiant (rocket mass) heaters**: High-density fuel
- **Active charcoal filters**: Water and air purification
- **Artist's charcoal**: High-quality drawing material
- **"Willow water"**: Fresh willow chips soaked in water provide gibberellic acid for promoting root growth in cuttings — a natural rooting hormone

### Pollarding for Propagation

At Perth, Tasmania, willows are pollarded (cut back to the trunk at head height) for propagation cuttings at a nursery. This regular cutting cycle stimulates vigorous regrowth and provides a sustainable supply of planting material.

### Windbreak Applications

Matsudana willow (*Salix matsudana*) provides strict (columnar) windbreaks for kiwifruit vine crops on the plains near Nelson, New Zealand. The narrow, upright form minimizes space loss on the ground while providing effective wind protection with little maintenance to control shape.

## Protected Growing Structures

### Keyhole Herb Spirals

At Stanley, Tasmania, a herb spiral in the form of a ziggurat provides ample culinary herbs at the kitchen door. The spiral design (2 meters across, 1 meter high) creates multiple microclimates:

- The top is drier and sunnier, suitable for Mediterranean herbs like rosemary and thyme
- The middle zones provide moderate conditions for a wide range of species
- The base is cooler and moister, suited to parsley, mint, and water-loving herbs
- The south side (in the Southern Hemisphere) is shaded and cooler

Drainage and aspect suit most cool area herb species within a compact footprint.

### Chicken-Heated Greenhouses

At Aspen, Colorado, USA, a chicken-heated greenhouse combined with a solar-heated seedling cloche enables early planting in short-season high mountain areas. The chickens provide body heat and CO₂ enrichment, while their manure contributes to the composting cycle within the greenhouse.

### Jean Pain Composting Method

At Tumbarumba, New South Wales, Neil Dauce and Jason Alexandra reduce forest fuel loads using the Jean Pain method. Wood chips are mulched, composted, and fermented to produce methane. This approach simultaneously:

- Reduces wildfire risk through mechanical fuel removal
- Enriches soils compared to "cool burns" (controlled burns that impoverish soils and often lead to wildfires)
- Produces renewable energy (methane)
- Generates heat for water heating and space heating
- Creates valuable compost as a byproduct

## Small Livestock Integration

### Rabbit Cage Tractors

At Forest, Tasmania, Michael Mangevelakis uses rabbit cage "tractors" that clean clover and grasses from mounded strawberry crops. The rabbits serve multiple functions:

- Weed control in strawberry beds
- Manure production for the strawberries
- Meat production as a yield of the "weed" system

This system transforms pest animals (rabbits) into productive contributors to the garden ecosystem.

### Chicken Tractors

At Lake Chelan, Okanogan, USA, Michael Pilarski (Friends of the Trees Coordinator) uses chicken tractors that:

- Control grass competition for small fruit crops
- "Weed" small fruits over the autumn period
- Provide manure for the system
- Produce eggs and meat

### Lycium Hedges for Poultry

At Stanley, Tasmania, a *Lycium ferocissimum* (boxthorn) hedge carries vines of hops and banana passionfruit while sheltering main crop potatoes. Its berries are relished by poultry, and as a sharp thorny shrub (used historically to keep out lions in Africa), it provides ideal shelter from predators.

### Autumn Olive and Siberian Pea Shrub

At Wilton, New Hampshire, turkeys forage below autumn olive (*Elaeagnus umbellata*) with its abundant berries. Siberian pea shrub (*Caragana arborescens*) and autumn olive are identified as the two most important tree forage legumes of cool and cold climates, providing:

- High-protein seed for poultry and livestock
- Nitrogen fixation through root nodules
- Wildlife habitat and food
- Windbreak and shelter functions
- Erosion control on slopes

## Forest Edge Berry Systems

### Fir Stump Microsites

At Cle Elum, Washington, USA, Salish Indians traditionally maintained high production of berry fruits, fungi, and edible shoots within 35-year-old fir forests. Stumps of firs support many berry species, and slabs from stumps, fibers, and fallen logs generate berry clumps on lawns or in clearings. Birds bring seed and manure to stumps, and species such as salmonberry, black raspberry, huckleberry, and wild strawberry thrive there, protected from deer browsing.

At Whidbey Island, Washington, this pattern is replicated deliberately: fir stumps and logs are positioned to create berry-producing microsites throughout the landscape.

### Bramble-Apple Guilds

At Kiewa, Victoria, Australia, Geoff Wallace observed free-seeded apple trees establishing in blackberry brambles — a traditional guild combination in England. Cattle demolish the brambles to reach the apples, and after the brambles are trodden down, the now-exposed apple trees prosper in their protected hole. Wallace now deliberately plants apples in brambles as a natural guild.

## Key Principles for Cool Temperate Systems

1. **Nitrogen-fixing trees are essential**: Tagasaste, autumn olive, Siberian pea shrub, and acacia provide forage, shelter, and soil fertility
2. **Use pioneer species deliberately**: Even "weeds" like gorse serve valuable functions during succession
3. **Coppice for multiple yields**: Willow and other coppiceable species produce food, fuel, medicine, and craft materials from the same plant
4. **Integrate small livestock**: Rabbit and chicken tractors provide manure, pest control, and food production simultaneously
5. **Create protected microclimates**: Herb spirals, chicken-heated greenhouses, and living windbreaks extend the effective growing season
6. **Leverage forest edges**: Stumps, logs, and brambles create productive microsites for berries and fruit trees
7. **Plan for winter forage**: Tagasaste, tree lucerne, and other evergreen forage species bridge the winter feed gap
