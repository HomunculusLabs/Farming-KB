# Mushroom Tissue Cloning

Tissue cloning is one of the most reliable methods for obtaining a pure culture of a specific mushroom strain. Rather than working with spores — which introduce genetic variation — tissue cloning captures the exact genetics of a verified mushroom, preserving desirable traits like fruiting speed, yield, flavor, and hardiness.

## Why Interior Tissue Is Used

The exterior surface of any collected mushroom is inevitably contaminated. It carries:

- **Spores** — from the mushroom itself and countless other fungal species
- **Mold spores** — ubiquitous in air and soil
- **Bacteria** — coating every exposed surface
- **Dust, insects, and debris** — additional contamination vectors

By extracting tissue from the **interior** — never exposed to the external environment — cultivators dramatically reduce the microbial load. Interior tissue is essentially sterile in a healthy mushroom, making it far more likely to produce a clean culture on agar.

## Selecting Clean Tissue

Proper tissue selection is the foundation of a successful clone. Target tissue that is:

- **Dry** — moisture promotes bacterial growth and makes tissue slimy
- **Fluffy** — indicates vigorous, healthy [[mycelium]]
- **Undamaged** — free from bruising, discoloration, or insect damage

The best tissue comes from the **inside of the cap**, near the gill/pore junction with the cap flesh. This area is densest and driest. Cap tissue is preferred over stem pith because it is less fibrous and colonizes agar more readily.

**Avoid:**

- **Wet or slimy tissue** — bacteria will outcompete mycelium on agar
- **Discolored areas** — brown, black, or off-color zones indicate decay
- **Insect-damaged regions** — galleries, holes, or frass are contaminated
- **Tissue near the base** (most species) — closest to substrate and soil organisms

## Dehydration Technique for Wet Specimens

Wild mushrooms collected after rain or from humid environments often have tissue too wet to clone directly. Excess moisture spreads bacteria across agar and causes the tissue to rot.

**Dehydration procedure:**

1. Harvest the mushroom and place it in a clean, dry container — never a sealed bag
2. **Air-dry** for several hours to overnight in a well-ventilated area (flow hood or still-air box preferred)
3. Surface should feel dry and leathery — not crispy — when ready
4. Interior tissue is then dry enough to work with directly

## Surface Sterilization with Hydrogen Peroxide

For visibly dirty specimens — caked with soil or from high-contamination environments — surface sterilization before opening the mushroom is essential.

**3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) protocol:**

1. Prepare a small container of 3% H₂O₂ (standard drugstore concentration)
2. Briefly dip or spray the exterior of the mushroom
3. Allow 30–60 seconds contact time — peroxide kills spores on contact but doesn't penetrate deeply enough to damage interior tissue
4. Blot dry with sterile paper towel or air-dry before opening

Hydrogen peroxide is preferred over alcohol because it is less likely to be drawn into tissue and kill target mycelium. It breaks down into water and oxygen, leaving no harmful residue.

## Special Handling for Polypores

Polypores (bracket and shelf fungi) have dense, woody flesh that is slow to colonize agar. Their growing-edge tissue is thin and often heavily contaminated.

For polypores, the best cloning tissue comes from the **base attachment point** — where the fruiting body connects to wood substrate. This tissue is the youngest and most metabolically active, has had less environmental exposure, and is more likely to be free of deep contamination.

Excise a small wedge from the base with a sharp sterile scalpel and transfer to agar. Be patient — polypore clones may take 2–3 weeks for visible growth, versus 3–7 days for [[oyster-mushrooms]].

## Still-Air Transfers and Contamination Control

Every time a petri dish is opened, the sterile agar is exposed to airborne contaminants. To minimize risk:

- **Work in still air** — [[laminar-flow-hood]] ideal; a still-air box (plastic tote with arm holes) works well
- **Minimize open time** — have tissue ready before opening the plate
- **Flame-sterilize your scalpel** between every cut and transfer
- **Keep hands and tools downstream** of the agar surface

Even a single mold spore can outgrow mushroom tissue and ruin a plate. Bench discipline matters more than any single technique.

## Transfer Technique

1. **Flame-sterilize** scalpel until glowing red, cool briefly on agar surface
2. **Open the mushroom** with a sterile tear or cut, exposing interior flesh
3. **Excise** a small piece — roughly 2–5 mm square — from clean interior
4. **Open the agar plate** and use scalpel tip to **make a small depression** in the surface
5. **Insert tissue** cut-side down into the depression for direct contact with nutrient medium
6. **Close and seal** with parafilm or micropore tape

The depression anchors the tissue and ensures good agar contact for mycelium to feed and grow outward.

## Multiple Samples Per Plate

Place **multiple tissue samples on a single plate** to increase efficiency and hedge against contamination:

- Space 4–6 samples evenly on a standard 100 mm plate
- Label with species, collection date, and specimen number
- If one sample contaminates, others may still succeed
- Transfer the healthiest growth to a fresh plate once clean growth is confirmed

## Special Techniques for Finicky Species

### Submersion Forcing Mycelium Upward

When interior tissue is too degraded or contaminated, place the entire mushroom (or a large section) on sterile agar or moistened sterile material. In high humidity, the mushroom attempts to grow new mycelium **outward and upward** from its surface. These new hyphal tips, emerging in a controlled environment, are excised and transferred to fresh agar.

### Colonized Wood Bits in Hydrogen Peroxide

For resistant wood-inhabiting fungi, small bits of **colonized wood** from the substrate interior or fruiting body base are soaked in 3% H₂O₂. The peroxide kills surface contaminants without penetrating the wood. After a brief soak, wood bits are transferred to agar where embedded mycelium emerges and colonizes the plate.

## Antibiotic Agar: The Blanchette Recipe

For field-collected specimens from high-contamination environments, **antibiotic agar** can rescue a culture that would otherwise be lost. The Blanchette recipe suppresses bacterial growth while allowing fungal mycelium to develop:

- Prepare standard nutrient agar (MEA, PDA, or CYA) and autoclave
- Cool to 50–55°C, then add:
  - **[[streptomycin-sulfate]]** — 30–50 mg/L
  - **Penicillin G** — 20–30 mg/L (optional, broader coverage)
- Pour plates and place contaminated or high-risk tissue samples

Antibiotics inhibit bacteria long enough for mycelium to establish. Once well-established, transfer to standard agar for isolation.

> **Note:** Use antibiotic agar sparingly. Overreliance masks contamination issues and selects for resistant bacteria.

## Sectoring: Separating Strains from Mixed Plates

Plates inoculated with spores often produce a **genetic mosaic** — multiple distinct strains growing together. This also occurs with tissue clones if a secondary fungal species infected the original mushroom.

Different strains appear as **sectors** — distinct zones differing in:

- Growth rate (fast vs. slow)
- Color (white, off-white, yellowish, bluish)
- Texture (fluffy, rhizomorphic, matted)
- Density (thick vs. thin)

**Isolation procedure:**

1. Identify the sector with the most desirable characteristics — typically fast, rhizomorphic growth
2. Excise a small piece from the **leading edge** (hyphal tips) of that sector with a sterile scalpel
3. Transfer to a fresh plate, placing at one edge to observe growth pattern
4. If sectoring persists, repeat — each transfer narrows the genetic population
5. After 2–3 transfers, a **single-strain isolate** with uniform growth should result

This technique is essential for strain development and ensuring genetic purity before [[spawn-production]].
