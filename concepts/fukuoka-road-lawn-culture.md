# What Is This "Lawn Culture"?

Fukuoka's critique of the American lawn is one of his most
incisive observations about the gap between the appearance
of nature and true nature. The American lawn, he argues,
represents the ultimate expression of a [[fukuoka-food-civilization-critique]] that
creates nature in its own image rather than living within
nature's embrace.

## The American Dream and the Lawn

The goal of the average American in life appears to be to
build up some savings, live in a large house in the country
surrounded by tall trees, and keep a well-manicured lawn.
An even greater source of pride is to own several horses.
The ideal of people in the West is an aristocratic life.
When they do things in country woods, their lifestyle
resembles that of an Oriental hermit. In fact, however, it
is just the opposite: an extension of aristocratic tastes
and nothing more.

## A Nature Designed for Man's Pleasure

At the Amherst College seminar, Fukuoka looked at the lawn
on campus and noted: "I didn't see a single butterfly about.
There were no worms or ants in sight. This was certainly not
the green of nature. Why, this was nothing other than a
nature designed for the pleasure and convenience of man."

People think that by protecting this they are protecting
nature. If what they wish to protect is just a pale
imitation of nature, then can their desire to preserve
nature indeed be genuine and true? The lawn looks unnatural
to a Japanese sensibility. There is no doubting its beauty,
but it cannot satisfy someone who is Japanese. It doesn't
put one in the mood to practice the tea ceremony or arrange
flowers; it leaves one unsettled instead.

## Where Emotions Really Come From

Fukuoka uses the lawn to make a deeper philosophical point.
We look at a green tree and think green trees are beautiful.
We go to the mountains and a sense of the mountains arises.
Travel to a lake, and one feels the spirit of the water.
These emotions all arise from nature. Go somewhere where
nature has been disturbed and nothing but disturbed emotions
will arise.

Americans seem to be much more advanced in their desire to
protect nature than the Japanese, but hasn't this arisen
from a keen sense of regret at their loss of nature? The
question is whether Americans who take such good care of
this spurious green growth are feeling the emotions that
arise from cherishing real nature.

## The Revolution of the Home Garden

Fukuoka proposed that various types of fruit trees be
interplanted with roadside trees in the cities and that
vegetables should be planted instead of lawns and
flowerbeds, so people walking by can pluck and eat roadside
fruit or pull up a couple of daikon and take them home.
This would create "home garden" boulevards everyone could
use.

When he talked about how clover and daikon seeds sown over a
lawn would cause the clover to win out over the grass in two
to three years, enabling the daikon to grow within green
manure, the people who got the biggest kick out of this
were Japanese wives, Chinese, and other Orientals living in
America. Americans laughed when he brought this up because
they knew just how great a revolution this would be, given
the close attachment of people to the serene and dignified
"lawn culture."

## Attacking the Lawn Culture

As long as this problem remains unresolved, the potential of
natural home gardens in America will be limited. Fukuoka
deliberately set out to attack or reject the culture of the
lawn, expecting full well to incur the hatred of Americans
in the process. His reason was that, short of doing this,
it is not possible to successfully promote the idea of home
gardens.

If Americans really wanted to bid their cherished "lawn
culture" goodbye and create natural home gardens, their home
life would benefit greatly. When he spoke one night at a
church on the University of California campus at Berkeley,
some of the people there decided to give this a try in
their own neighborhoods. He suspected that things would get
underway first in the corners of the city where Japanese
wives live.

## The Average American Home

The average American home has a spacious yard, natural
woods, and a lawn, so when people set up vegetable gardens
there, something more ideal than is possible in Japan may
result. Americans are not put off by the sight of a few
weeds. So what might look to Japanese people like an
unattractive, poorly tended garden gone to the weeds would
to many Westerners be a picture of natural harmony at its
best.

Natural farming appears to have made a solid start in
backyards and among the small farmers of Europe and America.
The challenge remains to transform the ornamental lawn into
a productive, living ecosystem that reconnects people with
the source of their food. See [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-principles]] and [[fukuoka-european-civilization-standstill]].

## The Fundamental Contradiction

The lawn culture reveals a deeper contradiction in Western
civilization: it creates a beautiful green surface while
destroying the [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] beneath. Lawns are maintained
through constant intervention -- mowing, watering, and
applying chemicals -- making them the most labor-intensive
and ecologically destructive form of "nature" imaginable.

Yet this artificial green is what Westerners associate with
nature and wish to protect. The tragedy is that genuine
nature -- with its weeds, insects, birds, and ecological
complexity -- is destroyed in order to create a sterile
imitation that satisfies only the human eye. True protection
of nature, Fukuoka suggests, means abandoning the illusion
of control and allowing nature to express itself fully, even
when the result looks messy to the conditioned eye.
