# Mushroom Laboratory Setup and Equipment Design

A comprehensive guide to constructing and equipping a sterile culture laboratory for
mushroom spawn production, tissue culture, and mycological research, from basic home
setups to small commercial operations.

## Why Build a Home Laboratory

A major milestone in any mushroom grower's development is the construction of a
sterile culture laboratory. Key benefits include:

- **Cost savings**: Commercial spawn is expensive; producing your own significantly
  reduces per-bag costs
- **Fresh supply**: Spawn made in small batches as needed is more vigorous than
  commercial spawn that has been overincubated or stored for months
- **Full control**: Taking control of every step from spore to fruiting block
- **Strain independence**: Freedom from commercial spawn producers' limited strain
  selections

A warning is in order: it can take years to develop the skills needed for
independent spawn production. The best strategy is to start small with trials before
scaling to full commercial operations.

## Choosing and Preparing Lab Space

### Space Requirements

You can convert just about any enclosed space into a basic lab:

- **Closet or spare room**: Ideal starting spaces
- **Partitioned section**: A portion of a larger room can be partitioned with metal
  or PVC frames enclosed in clear plastic (like greenhouse walls)
- **Spare bathroom**: Thoroughly sanitized, with a portable air filter running for
  an hour before entry

### Critical Design Features

**Entry**: Should be well-sealed to keep interior air clean. Zippered entries work
well for plastic partition walls.

**Floors**: Never carpeted or textured. Must be smooth concrete, tile, linoleum, or
any hard surface that can be swept and mopped daily.

**Walls**: Smooth materials that handle weekly wipe-downs. All surfaces must be
impervious to water and cleaning agents.

**Air quality**: Positive-pressure ventilation is ideal but not strictly necessary
at first. A small fan fitted with a HEPA filter pulls clean air in, creating
positive pressure so clean air pushes out through any leaks rather than contaminated
air being drawn in.

## Basic Equipment Checklist

### Essential Equipment

| Item | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Laminar flow hood with HEPA filter (2×3 ft recommended) | Sterile work surface for transfers |
| Pressure cooker or sterilizer | Substrate and agar sterilization |
| Electric impulse sealer (12–16 inch seal) | Sealing autoclavable spawn bags |
| Narrow-mouthed mason jars with metal lids | Grain spawn preparation |
| Glass Erlenmeyer flask (1,000 ml) | Agar preparation |
| Alcohol lamp (denatured alcohol) | Tool sterilization during transfers |
| Scalpels or X-Acto knives | Tissue cutting and transfer |
| Scissors | Various cutting tasks |
| Wooden clothespins | Clamping bags during sterilization |

### Advanced Equipment

| Item | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Blender with all-glass container | Liquid culture preparation |
| Laboratory refrigerator | Storing plates, cultures, and spawn masters |
| Microscope | Inspecting cultures for contamination and morphology |
| Plate stirrer | Liquid culture homogenization |
| Autoclavable jar lids with micron filter disks | Long-term culture storage |

### Consumable Supplies

- Petri plates (reusable glass or sterile plastic)
- Autoclavable bags with micron filter patches
- Nitrile or rubber gloves
- 70% isopropyl alcohol or half-strength bleach
- Paper towels
- Aluminum foil
- Surgical or dust masks
- Tape or Parafilm for sealing plates
- Ziplock bags (quart or sandwich size)
- Colored permanent markers for labeling
- Laboratory notebook for records and observations

## Constructing a Laminar Flow Hood

### How It Works

A laminar flow hood is a work surface inside an enclosed cabinet with its own HEPA-
filtered air supply. The unit pulls in ambient air at its side or base, filters it
through a HEPA filter, and blows clean air across the work surface in a continuous
stream. This constant outward flow of filtered air prevents ambient airborne
microorganisms from entering the work area.

### HEPA Filter Selection

Look for a **gasketed HEPA filter** with an efficiency rating of **99.997% at 0.3
micron**. Gaskets ensure a perfect seal in the box enclosure, eliminating leakage of
unfiltered air. A full-size hood uses a filter approximately **2 feet high and 3
feet wide**.

A separate smaller laminar flow hood is valuable for isolating wild specimens, molds,
bacteria, or sporulating cultures that could threaten the cleanliness of the main
work station.

### Blower/Fan Selection

The blower is typically a furnace-type "squirrel-cage" blower. Critical
specifications:

- **Proper electrical rating** for your workspace
- **Appropriate CFM (cubic feet per minute)**: Too little force means inadequate
  airflow; too much creates turbulence and blows contamination around
- **Target airflow**: Approximately 100–600 CFM, with 550 CFM being ideal for
  commercial units

### Box Construction

1. Measure the exact dimensions of the HEPA filter
2. Construct a box extending ~8–10 inches from the back of the filter and ~18–24
   inches from the front (the work space)
3. Mount the blower on top, typically in its own separate housing with an open top
   for a coarse prefilter
4. Use a stainless steel sheet or comparable surface for the work area that
   withstands disinfection without staining or rusting
5. Fasten with small bolts, wing nuts, and compression washers for easy filter
   replacement

### Sealing

Seal all joints and seams to create an airtight enclosure:

- **Aluminum tape** (HVAC ductwork tape) is preferred over silicone caulk for
  sealing joints and junctions
- Line all joints of the box and junctions between box and HEPA filter
- Extend tape up and over filter frame edges
- Line the entire inside perimeter where the filter housing connects to the work area

### Leak Testing

Use a **smoldering stick of incense** to check for air leaks:
- Hold the smoldering end close to each seam
- If the smoldering end brightens, air is being pulled in — seal the leak
- Check the entire construction for airtightness

### Cost Considerations

- **Purchased prefabricated kit**: Over $1,000 (screw together and plug in)
- **DIY construction**: ~$400 for a high-quality HEPA filter and blower unit, plus
  additional materials
- **Prefilter installation**: Extends HEPA filter life from 1 year to 2–3 years
- Scavenging materials (old tabletops, commercial kitchen components) can
  significantly reduce costs

## Air Management

### Positive Pressure Ventilation

A positive-pressure unit consists of a small fan with a HEPA filter that pulls clean
fresh air into the lab. Benefits include:

- Clean air pushes out through any leaks rather than contaminated air entering
- Provides fresh air for developing spawn (which generates CO₂)
- Ensures operator safety by preventing CO₂ buildup

Commercial laboratories often have positive-pressure air lock entries to ensure the
lab space is never open to the outside. A length of ribbon hanging from the door
serves as a visual cue confirming outward airflow direction.

### CO₂ Considerations

Spawn generates significant carbon dioxide during colonization. Fresh air exchange
is essential both for spawn health and operator safety. Never seal a laboratory
completely without adequate ventilation.

## Scale and Growth

### Starting Small

The author generated over one hundred 5-pound bags of spawn per week for five years
using two tabletop sterilizers and two laminar flow hoods side by side. Growth can
be gradual:

1. **Hobby level**: Single laminar hood, tabletop sterilizer, small batch trials
2. **Supplemental income**: Multiple hoods and sterilizers, consistent spawn
   production
3. **Commercial scale**: Autoclave with capacity for 1,000+ bags per week

### The Learning Curve

Success depends more on developing mastery with available equipment than on the size
or sophistication of the equipment itself. Many successful cultivators began with
minimal setups — Styrofoam coolers, alcohol lamps, and cardboard glove boxes in an
attic — before progressing to dedicated facilities.
