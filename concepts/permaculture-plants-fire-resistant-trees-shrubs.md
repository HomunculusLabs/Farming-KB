# Fire-Resistant Trees and Shrubs for [[permaculture-design]]

Jeff Nugent and Julia Boniface's *Permaculture Plants: A Selection* includes
comprehensive lists of trees and shrubs with low fire potential — an essential
design consideration for properties in fire-prone regions. Selecting
fire-resistant species for strategic placement around structures and along
access routes can significantly improve property survival during wildfire
events.

## Why Fire Resistance Matters in Permaculture

Fire is a natural ecological process, but in permaculture design, protecting
key infrastructure (homes, water systems, animal shelters) from wildfire is
critical. The concept of **fire-resistant landscaping** uses plant selection
and placement to create defensible space and slow fire spread.

Fire-resistant does not mean fireproof. All plants will burn under extreme
conditions. However, species with low fire potential have characteristics
that make them significantly less likely to ignite and carry fire:

- Low volatile oil content
- High moisture content in leaves
- Dense, compact growth habit (less air space for fire to spread)
- Minimal accumulation of dry, flammable litter
- Bark that does not easily peel or ignite

## Notable Fire-Resistant Trees

### Broadleaf Deciduous Trees
Deciduous trees generally have higher fire resistance than conifers because
they drop their leaves in winter, reducing fuel load, and their green leaves
contain more moisture:

- **Oak species** (*Quercus*) — Holm oak, cork oak, burr oak, English oak,
  and valley oak are all listed. Cork oak is particularly noted for its
  fire-resistant bark and post-fire recovery ability
- **Maple species** (*Acer*) — Norway maple, sycamore, Japanese maple, box
  elder maple, and common maple all have low fire potential
- **Elm species** (*Ulmus*) — Generally fire resistant
- **Linden** (*Tilia vulgaris*) — Low fire potential
- **Tulip tree** (*Liriodendron tulipifera*) — Fire resistant
- **Birch species** (*Betula*) — Paperbark birch, silver birch, Chinese
  birch (note: paperbark types can be fire prone due to peeling bark)

### Evergreen Fire-Resistant Trees
Some evergreen species are surprisingly fire resistant:

- **Holly** (*Ilex aquifolium*) — Thick, moist leaves resist ignition
- **Camphor laurel** (*[[cinnamomum-camphora]]*) — High moisture content
- **Ginkgo** (*[[ginkgo-biloba]]*) — Dense foliage with low flammability
- **[[chinese-tallow-tree]]** (*Sapium sebiferum*) — Listed as fire resistant
- **London plane** (*Platanus acerifolia*) — Thick bark resists fire
- **Chinese pistachio** (*Pistacia chinensis*) — Excellent fire resistance
- **Paulownia** (*[[paulownia-tomentosa]]*) — Fast-growing with fire resistance

### Conifers with Low Fire Potential
Most conifers are highly flammable, but a few exceptions exist:

- **Coast redwood** (*Sequoia sempervirens*) — Thick, fire-resistant bark
  (though young trees are vulnerable)
- **Swamp cypress** (*Taxodium distichum*) — Grows in wet areas; higher
  moisture content reduces flammability

## Notable Fire-Resistant Shrubs

### Useful Food-Producing Shrubs
Several edible and functional shrubs have low fire potential:

- **Carob** (*[[ceratonia-siliqua]]*) — Fire resistant, recovers after fire,
  produces edible pods
- **Olive** (*Olea europaea*) — Fire resistant and recovers after burning;
  also produces edible fruit and oil
- **Pecan** (*Carya illinoensis*) — Low fire potential, nut producer
- **Common hawthorn** (*[[crataegus-monogyna]]*) — Fire resistant, edible berries
- **Russian olive** (*[[elaeagnus-angustifolia]]*) — Nitrogen fixing, fire
  resistant
- **Japanese raisin tree** (*Hovenia dulcis*) — Edible fruit stalks

### Ornamental and Functional Shrubs
- **Privet** (*Ligustrum spp*) — Dense hedge-forming, fire resistant
- **Photinia** (*Photinia glabra*, *P. serrulata*) — Popular hedging plants
- **Pittosporum** (*Pittosporum spp*) — Dense, fire-resistant screening
- **Coprosma** (*Coprosma repens*) — Low spreading ground cover, fire
  resistant
- **Agave** (*Agave spp*) — Succulent leaves store water, highly fire
  resistant
- **Opuntia** (*Opuntia spp*) — Prickly pear cactus; succulent, fire
  resistant
- **Atriplex** (*Atriplex spp*) — Saltbush; fire resistant, edible for
  livestock

## Ground Covers with Low Fire Potential

Ground covers are critical in fire-resistant landscaping because they
replace flammable grass and create a living mulch layer:

- **Woolly yarrow** (*Achillea tomentosa*)
- **Bugle lily** (*Ajuga spp*)
- **Pigface** (*Carpobrotus spp*, *Arcotheca calendulaceum*)
- **Dwarf coyote bush** (*Baccharis pilularis*)
- **Gum rockrose** (*Cistus ladanifer*)
- **Coprosma** (*Coprosma kirkii*)

## Species That Recover After Fire

Some species are marked as recovering after fire, meaning they may burn
but will resprout or regenerate:

- Cork oak, carob, olive, Casuarina species, sycamore, white poplar,
  black poplar, peppercorn tree, oak species, Prunus species, willows,
  tagasaste (note: tagasaste is marked as NOT recovering)

## Design Application

### Zone 1 (Near Structures)
Use only the most fire-resistant species closest to buildings. Prioritize
deciduous trees and succulent ground covers. Maintain a clean zone of at
least 3-10 meters around structures.

### Zone 2 (Intermediate)
Mix fire-resistant trees and shrubs with careful spacing. Avoid continuous
canopy connections that could carry fire from ground to tree crowns.

### Access Routes
Plant fire-resistant species along access roads and evacuation routes to
maintain safe passage during fire events.

### Windbreaks
Use fire-resistant species for windbreaks in fire-prone areas. Avoid
highly flammable species like eucalypts and pines on the windward side.

## Important Caveats

- **No plant is fireproof** — Under extreme conditions, all plants burn
- **Maintenance matters** — Even fire-resistant species become hazards if
  allowed to accumulate dead material
- **Local conditions vary** — Drought-stressed plants are more flammable
- **Community context** — Fire-resistant planting is one component of a
  broader fire management strategy including access, water supply, and
  building materials
