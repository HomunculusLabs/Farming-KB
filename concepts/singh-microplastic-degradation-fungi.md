---# Microplastic Degradation by Fungi

Microplastic pollution represents one of the most pervasive and
persistent environmental challenges. Fungi, with their diverse enzymatic
arsenals and ability to colonize hydrophobic substrates, have emerged as
promising biological agents for microplastic degradation, complementing
existing mycoremediation strategies for organic and inorganic contaminants.

## The Microplastic Problem

Microplastics (particles smaller than 5 mm) originate from primary sources
(microbeads, pellets) and secondary fragmentation of larger debris. Key
polymers of concern include polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP),
polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polystyrene (PS), polyvinyl chloride
(PVC), and polyurethane (PUR). Conventional degradation of these polymers
takes decades to centuries.

## Fungal Mechanisms of Plastic Degradation

### Enzymatic Depolymerization

Fungi deploy several enzyme classes to attack synthetic polymers:

- **Cutinases and esterases**: Hydrolyze ester bonds in PET and PUR.
  Naturally evolved for degrading cutin (plant polyester), making them
  effective against similar synthetic bonds. *Fusarium solani* cutinase
  was among the first shown to degrade PET.
- **Laccases**: These multicopper oxidases, central to
  [[singh-fungal-biofilms-in-contaminated-environments]] create localized
  microenvironments with concentrated enzyme activity

## Challenges and Future Outlook

1. **Slow degradation rates**: Current rates too slow for stand-alone
   treatment; process intensification needed
2. **Incomplete mineralization**: Most studies show partial
   depolymerization rather than complete conversion
3. **Ecotoxicity of intermediates**: Partial degradation products may be
   more toxic than parent polymers
4. **Scale-up difficulties**: Laboratory conditions differ from field
   environments
5. **Nanoplastic formation**: Biodegradation may generate nanoplastics
   with unknown environmental behavior
