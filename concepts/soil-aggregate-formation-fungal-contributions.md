## Overview

Soil aggregates are clusters of sand, silt, clay, and organic
particles bound together into stable structures ranging from
microaggregates (<0.25 mm) to macroaggregates (>0.25 mm).
Their formation and stability are fundamental to soil health,
affecting water infiltration, erosion resistance, gas exchange,
and habitat for soil organisms. Fungi, particularly arbuscular
mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), play a central role in building and
maintaining soil aggregates through physical entanglement and
biochemical binding agents, most notably glomalin-related soil
protein (GRSP).

## The Hierarchy of Aggregate Formation

Tisdall and Oades (1982) proposed the hierarchical model of
aggregate formation, which remains widely accepted. According
to this model, different binding agents operate at different
scales. Persistent binding agents (plant roots and fungal
hyphae, humic substances) stabilize macroaggregates (>2 mm).
Transient binding agents (polysaccharides from microbial
activity) stabilize macroaggregates at smaller scales. Temporary

Hyphal turnover is continuous. As old hyphae die and decompose,
they leave behind channels that become preferential pathways
for water movement and root penetration. The residual organic
matter from decomposed hyphae serves as nucleation sites for
new aggregate formation. This cycle of growth, entanglement,
death, and reincorporation continuously rebuilds soil structure.

While AMF receive the most attention, saprophytic fungi also
contribute significantly to aggregate formation. Decomposer
fungi produce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) during
organic matter breakdown, including polysaccharides and
glycoproteins that bind soil particles. Their hyphal networks
stabilize organic matter in macroaggregates, and their
enzymatic activity creates binding sites through partial
decomposition of plant residues.

Basidiomycetes, which form extensive cord-forming mycelia in
forest soils, are particularly important aggregate builders.
Their rhizomorphs physically bind leaf litter to mineral soil,
creating distinct organic horizons with high aggregate
stability. In compost and mulch systems, fungal-dominated
communities produce more stable aggregates than bacterial-
dominated communities.

## Agricultural Implications

Conventional agricultural practices that reduce fungal
populations—tillage, fungicide application, monoculture,
and fallow periods—directly impair soil aggregate stability.
No-till farming, cover cropping, reduced fungicide use, and
diverse rotations maintain and restore fungal communities,
leading to improved soil structure.

Research consistently shows that soils with higher AMF
colonization have more water-stable aggregates, higher
infiltration rates, and greater resistance to erosion. In
no-till systems, macroaggregate formation is driven primarily
by fungal hyphae, while in tilled systems, aggregate formation
shifts to bacterial-driven processes that produce less stable
aggregates.

## Measurement and Assessment

Aggregate stability is typically measured using wet sieving
methods, where soil is submerged and mechanically agitated
to determine the proportion of aggregates that resist
disintegration. The mean weight diameter (MWD) of aggregates
after sieving is a standard index. GRSP is extracted using
citrate buffer at varying temperatures and quantified through
Bradford protein assays or immunological methods.

Visual assessment of mycelial networks in soil thin sections
under microscopy provides direct evidence of hyphal
contributions to aggregation. Modern techniques like X-ray
computed tomography (micro-CT) allow three-dimensional
visualization of pore networks within aggregates, revealing
how fungal hyphae create and maintain soil architecture.
