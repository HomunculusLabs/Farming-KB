## Nematodes: Biology and Classification

Nematodes are microscopic roundworms living in soil, decomposing
organic matter, fresh or salt water, or on living hosts. Water is
essential for locomotion and breeding. They swim in an eel-like fashion
and can live in the thinnest water films. In moist casing, large
numbers of nematodes are visible as a shimmering veneer caused by
"winking," where nematodes stand on their tails waving their bodies.
This adaptation facilitates dispersal as their sticky outer skin
adheres to flies, mites, hands, or clothing. When dried slowly,
nematodes enter a "cryptobiotic" or cyst state, preserved for years
until reactivated by water. In this cyst state they persist through
high temperatures normally lethal to active nematodes.

Parthenogenesis (asexual reproduction by females) is common among
nematodes and leads to rapid population expansion. A single nematode
can breed millions of descendants within weeks. Two feeding types are
recognized in mushroom culture: saprophagous and mycophagous.

Saprophagous nematodes (Rhabditus spp.) have tube-like mouths for
sucking nutrient particles suspended in water, feeding on organic
matter and accompanying microorganisms, particularly bacteria. Although
not primary pathogens, their presence indicates improper hygiene or
imbalanced conditions. In uneven substrates with overly wet areas,
their waste products from metabolic excretions foul the substrate,
rendering it unsuitable for mycelial growth. During Phase I composting
they migrate to cooler outer shell of the pile, but Phase II can
eliminate them only if the entire compost is subjected to pasteurization
temperatures.

## Mycophagous Nematodes: Direct Mycelial Damage

Mycophagous nematodes (Ditylenchus myceliophagus, Aphelenchiodes
composticola) feed directly on mushrooms using a mouth stylet or needle
to puncture hyphae, inject digestive juices, and suck out cellular
contents. The damaged cell soon dies. Feeding continuously and moving
from cell to cell, mycophagous nematodes can destroy whole mycelial
networks. In infected substrates, fine mycelial growth disappears
leaving only coarse strands giving a stringy appearance. The substrate
becomes soggy and foul smelling, promoted by anaerobic bacteria.
