# Fungal Roles in Aquatic Environments
Fungi are ubiquitous in aquatic environments, occurring as unicellular yeasts,
polymorphic and filamentous fungi, and as both free-living and symbiotic
organisms. While fungi are of fundamental importance in the terrestrial
biosphere as decomposer organisms, plant pathogens and [[mycelium-running-endophyte-mutualistic-symbionts]],
they also play essential roles in freshwater, estuarine, and marine ecosystems.
Fungi in [[biodiversity-fungi-freshwater-aquatic-habitats]] contribute to decomposition of organic matter,
nutrient cycling, food web dynamics, and the transformation of minerals and
pollutants.

## Saltmarsh Decomposition Systems

### The Spartina-Cordgrass Ecosystem

Saltmarshes are expansive and highly productive intertidal ecosystems. The
saltmarshes of the Georgia, USA, Atlantic coast form an intertidal ecosystem
5-10 km wide extending from the barrier-island chain to the mainland. The
predominant macrophyte is smooth cordgrass ([[spartina-alterniflora]] Loisel.),
with cross-marsh average annual shoot production of approximately 1.3 kg per
square metre of marsh (Newell, 2001a, from Dai and Wiegert, 1996). Unlike most
grasses, smooth cordgrass does not abscise its leaf blades; they remain
attached to the leaf sheath after senescence and death. A large crop of
standing-dead litter is available to microbes for decomposition for much of
the year — up to 538 g dry mass per square metre for leaf blades alone
(Newell et al., 1998).

### Wetting Dynamics and Fungal Adaptation

Because cordgrass shoots extend above the flooding-tidal level most of the
time, tidal flooding wets the dead-blade mass only about 10% of the time on an
annual-average basis. The most important wetting phenomenon is dewset, and the
combination of tidal, rain, and dew wetting results in the leaves being wet
roughly half of the time (Newell et al., 1989; Newell, 2007). Smooth-cordgrass
ascomycetes are well adapted to alternating wet and dry periods, losing little
or no biomass after aestivating and reviving (Newell, 1995).

### Fungal Decomposition of Standing-Dead Cordgrass

Cordgrass ascomycetes thoroughly integrate their mycelia into standing-dead
cordgrass using turgor-pressure-driven drilling and solid-resistance reduction
through external-enzymatic lysis. Inside the dead shoots, the ascomycetes
carry out their digestive work free from prokaryotic competition or pressure
from micropredators such as amoebae (Newell et al., 1996; Newell, 2001a,
2002). Additional factors favouring fungal production include potential
consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria, synergistic relationships with
green microalgae, nitrogen uptake from flooding-tidal waters, and synergistic
exchange of gases and water vapour between standing-decaying and living parts
of shoots connected by aerenchyma channels (Newell and Porter, 2000).

## Freshwater Fungi

### Stream and River Decomposition

Freshwater fungi, particularly [[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] (also called Ingoldian
fungi), are major decomposers of leaf litter in streams and rivers. These
fungi colonize submerged leaves, producing conidia that are adapted for
dispersal in flowing water — often with branched, tetraradiate, or sigmoid
shapes that enhance attachment to surfaces and entrapment in foam. Aquatic
hyphomycetes increase the nutritional quality of leaf litter for invertebrate
shredders, playing a central role in stream food webs.

### Molecular Community Analysis

DNA-based characterization techniques have significantly advanced the study of
fungal communities in aquatic environments. DGGE/TGGE fingerprinting of the
18S rRNA gene has been applied to decomposing leaf litter at different stages
of decay (Nikolcheva et al., 2003, 2005). T-RFLP combined with
ascomycete-selective primers revealed the same predominant species in
[[fungi-in-the-environment-saltmarsh-cordgrass-laccase-decomposition]] communities as direct microscopy and ascospore capture
(Buchan et al., 2002, 2003).

### Zoospore-Producing Fungi

Chytrid fungi and oomycetes produce motile zoospores that swim through water
films using flagella. These zoospores exhibit chemotaxis toward host surfaces
or nutrient sources, representing an important adaptation for aquatic and
semi-aquatic habitats. Chytrids are particularly important as parasites of
algae and other aquatic microorganisms, and their parasitism can significantly
alter algal community composition and primary production in freshwater
ecosystems.

## Marine Fungi

Marine fungi inhabit coastal and open-ocean environments, colonizing submerged
wood, driftwood, mangrove roots, and other substrates. The distinction between
truly marine fungi (obligate inhabitants of marine environments) and
facultative marine fungi (terrestrial species capable of growth in marine
conditions) is ecologically significant. Marine fungi contribute to the
decomposition of mangrove litter, wood degradation in coastal waters, and the
breakdown of macroalgal detritus. Many marine fungi are halotolerant,
accumulating compatible solutes such as glycerol, arabitol, and mannitol for
osmotic balance.

## Fungal Roles in Nutrient Cycling in Aquatic Systems

- **Carbon cycling**: Fungi decompose complex organic matter (lignocellulose,
  chitin) that bacteria cannot efficiently process, converting particulate
  organic carbon into dissolved and fungal biomass carbon
- **Nitrogen cycling**: Saltmarsh ascomycetes may benefit from potential
  consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria and potential uptake of nitrogen
  from flooding-tidal waters (White and Howes, 1994; Newell, 1996, 2001a,
  2002). See [[fungal-biogeochemistry]]

## Methodological Approaches

### Culture-Dependent Methods

Traditional culture-based methods for assessing the diversity of aquatic fungal
communities are now generally recognized as insufficient. However,
cultivation-dependent studies on sandstone identified members of Pleospora,
Acremonium, Engyodontium, Stemphylius, and Penicillium (Melville, Zhu, and
Gadd, unpublished). Microscopical analyses of phototrophic epilithic
[[soil-edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]] from the Roman Catacombs found
that the most frequently encountered fungal isolates were Sporotrichum and
Aspergillus spp. (Albertano and Urzi, 1999).

### Culture-Independent Methods

DNA-based characterization techniques are becoming increasingly important for
[[mycorrhiza-bioassays-ecological-studies]] of aquatic fungal communities.
These include:

- **rDNA/ITS PCR with ascomycete-selective primers**: Revealed the same
  predominant species of ascomycetes as direct microscopy in saltmarsh
  cordgrass communities (Buchan et al., 2002, 2003)
- **ARISA (Automated Ribosomal Intergenic Spacer Analysis)**: High-throughput
  automated technique exploiting length variations within the ITS region.
  Community complexity ranges from approximately 30 to over 200 fungal peaks
  per profile
