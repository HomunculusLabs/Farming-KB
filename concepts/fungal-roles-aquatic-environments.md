# Fungal Roles in Aquatic Environments

Fungi are ubiquitous in aquatic environments, occurring as unicellular yeasts, polymorphic and filamentous fungi, and as both free-living and symbiotic organisms. While fungi are of fundamental importance in the terrestrial biosphere as decomposer organisms, plant pathogens and mutualistic symbionts, they also play essential roles in freshwater, estuarine, and marine ecosystems. Fungi in aquatic habitats contribute to decomposition of organic matter, nutrient cycling, food web dynamics, and the transformation of minerals and pollutants.

## Saltmarsh Decomposition Systems

### The Spartina-Cordgrass Ecosystem

Saltmarshes are expansive and highly productive intertidal ecosystems. The saltmarshes of the Georgia, USA, Atlantic coast form an intertidal ecosystem 5-10 km wide extending from the barrier-island chain to the mainland. The predominant macrophyte is smooth cordgrass (Spartina alterniflora Loisel.), with cross-marsh average annual shoot production of approximately 1.3 kg per square metre of marsh (Newell, 2001a, from Dai and Wiegert, 1996). Unlike most grasses, smooth cordgrass does not abscise its leaf blades; they remain attached to the leaf sheath after senescence and death. A large crop of standing-dead litter is available to microbes for decomposition for much of the year -- up to 538 g dry mass per square metre for leaf blades alone (Newell et al., 1998).

### Wetting Dynamics and Fungal Adaptation

Because cordgrass shoots extend above the flooding-tidal level most of the time, tidal flooding wets the dead-blade mass only about 10% of the time on an annual-average basis. The most important wetting phenomenon is dewset, and the combination of tidal, rain, and dew wetting results in the leaves being wet roughly half of the time (Newell et al., 1989; Newell, 2007). Smooth-cordgrass ascomycetes are well adapted to alternating wet and dry periods, losing little or no biomass after aestivating and reviving (Newell, 1995).

### Fungal Decomposition of Standing-Dead Cordgrass

Cordgrass ascomycetes thoroughly integrate their mycelia into standing-dead cordgrass using turgor-pressure-driven drilling and solid-resistance reduction through external-enzymatic lysis. Inside the dead shoots, the ascomycetes carry out their digestive work free from prokaryotic competition or pressure from micropredators such as amoebae (Newell et al., 1996; Newell, 2001a, 2002). Additional factors favouring fungal production include potential consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria, synergistic relationships with green microalgae, nitrogen uptake from flooding-tidal waters, and synergistic exchange of gases and water vapour between standing-decaying and living parts of shoots connected by aerenchyma channels (Newell and Porter, 2000).

### The Cordgrass-Ascomycete Community

The major members of the smooth-cordgrass shoot-decomposer myco-community include Phaeosphaeria spartinicola, Phaeosphaeria halima, and Mycosphaerella sp.2. Percentage area of decaying blades occupied by at least one of the three predominant species was greater than 90% at sites with low grazing pressure from periwinkle snails (Newell, 2001b). A regularly occurring species encountered at 40% or more of blade area was Buergenerula spartinae. Other species observed at much lower percentages (less than 1%) included Hydropisphaera erubescens and Koorchaloma spartinicola, along with several species lacking published descriptions.

### Lignocellulose Degradation

Standing-dead smooth cordgrass is composed largely of lignocellulose (70-75%). While early testing suggested cordgrass ascomycetes were weak degraders, subsequent tests with static incubation and presence of non-LC carbohydrates revealed that Phaeosphaeria spartinicola was capable of mineralizing lignocellulose at more than 0.8% per day (Bergbauer and Newell, 1992). Combinations of the three predominant cordgrass-blade ascomycetes achieved more than 20% mineralization after 6 weeks at approximately 23 degrees C. Direct electron microscopic examination confirmed that cordgrass ascomycetes bring about both type 1 and type 2 soft rot -- type 2 being very similar to the white rot of basidiomycetes (Newell et al., 1996).

### Laccase Enzymes

All eight strains of the most common cordgrass ascomycetes tested positive for laccase activity using syringaldazine, and 13 distinct laccase gene sequence types were obtained from the standing-decay community (Lyons et al., 2003). Laccase-mediated degradation is supplemented by cellobiose dehydrogenase (CDH) interactions with laccase-mediator molecules. With redox-mediator molecules (either fungal-produced or added), laccase can oxidize non-phenolic compounds that would otherwise be inaccessible. This enzymatic capacity enables ascomycetes to drive significant decomposition in the absence of basidiomycete white-rot enzymes.

### Spore Production and Trophic Significance

A major fate of the large quantities of fungal mass produced within smooth-cordgrass marshes (rough estimate: over 0.5 kg per square metre per year) flows to litter-comminuting invertebrates at the base of the marsh food web (gastropods, arthropods) (Graca et al., 2000). Cordgrass ascomycetes are also prodigious expellers of ascospores: a conservative estimate is 5.9 billion spores per square metre per year (Newell, 2001b). This enormous spore flux represents significant carbon and nutrient transfer within the estuarine ecosystem.

## Freshwater Fungi

### Stream and River Decomposition

Freshwater fungi, particularly aquatic hyphomycetes (also called Ingoldian fungi), are major decomposers of leaf litter in streams and rivers. These fungi colonize submerged leaves, producing conidia that are adapted for dispersal in flowing water -- often with branched, tetraradiate, or sigmoid shapes that enhance attachment to surfaces and entrapment in foam. Aquatic hyphomycetes increase the nutritional quality of leaf litter for invertebrate shredders, playing a central role in stream food webs.

### Molecular Community Analysis

DNA-based characterization techniques have significantly advanced the study of fungal communities in aquatic environments. DGGE/TGGE fingerprinting of the 18S rRNA gene has been applied to decomposing leaf litter at different stages of decay (Nikolcheva et al., 2003, 2005). T-RFLP combined with ascomycete-selective primers revealed the same predominant species in saltmarsh cordgrass communities as direct microscopy and ascospore capture (Buchan et al., 2002, 2003).

### Zoospore-Producing Fungi

Chytrid fungi and oomycetes produce motile zoospores that swim through water films using flagella. These zoospores exhibit chemotaxis toward host surfaces or nutrient sources, representing an important adaptation for aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats. Chytrids are particularly important as parasites of algae and other aquatic microorganisms, and their parasitism can significantly alter algal community composition and primary production in freshwater ecosystems.

## Marine Fungi

Marine fungi inhabit coastal and open-ocean environments, colonizing submerged wood, driftwood, mangrove roots, and other substrates. The distinction between truly marine fungi (obligate inhabitants of marine environments) and facultative marine fungi (terrestrial species capable of growth in marine conditions) is ecologically significant. Marine fungi contribute to the decomposition of mangrove litter, wood degradation in coastal waters, and the breakdown of macroalgal detritus. Many marine fungi are halotolerant, accumulating compatible solutes such as glycerol, arabitol, and mannitol for osmotic balance.

## Fungal Roles in Nutrient Cycling in Aquatic Systems

- **Carbon cycling**: Fungi decompose complex organic matter (lignocellulose, chitin) that bacteria cannot efficiently process, converting particulate organic carbon into dissolved and fungal biomass carbon
- **Nitrogen cycling**: Saltmarsh ascomycetes may benefit from potential consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria and potential uptake of nitrogen from flooding-tidal waters (White and Howes, 1994; Newell, 1996, 2001a, 2002). See [[fungal-biogeochemistry]]

## Methodological Approaches

### Culture-Dependent Methods

Traditional culture-based methods for assessing the diversity of aquatic fungal communities are now generally recognized as insufficient. However, cultivation-dependent studies on sandstone identified members of Pleospora, Acremonium, Engyodontium, Stemphylius, and Penicillium (Melville, Zhu, and Gadd, unpublished). Microscopical analyses of phototrophic epilithic microbial communities from the Roman Catacombs found that the most frequently encountered fungal isolates were Sporotrichum and Aspergillus spp. (Albertano and Urzi, 1999).

### Culture-Independent Methods

DNA-based characterization techniques are becoming increasingly important for ecological studies of aquatic fungal communities. These include:

- **rDNA/ITS PCR with ascomycete-selective primers**: Revealed the same predominant species of ascomycetes as direct microscopy in saltmarsh cordgrass communities (Buchan et al., 2002, 2003)
- **ARISA (Automated Ribosomal Intergenic Spacer Analysis)**: High-throughput automated technique exploiting length variations within the ITS region. Community complexity ranges from approximately 30 to over 200 fungal peaks per profile
- **RISA and T-RFLP**: Electrophoretic fingerprinting techniques providing community-level resolution. A limitation is bias in nucleic acid extraction yields, as lysis efficiency may vary between different species and between spores and mycelia (Prosser, 2002)

## Ecological Interactions in Aquatic Systems

Fungi in aquatic environments engage in complex interactions with bacteria, algae, invertebrates, and other fungi. In saltmarsh systems, cordgrass ascomycetes may form consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria and green microalgae on blade surfaces. Fungal biomass serves as food for detritivorous invertebrates, linking microbial decomposition to higher trophic levels. Competition between fungi and bacteria for resources in aquatic sediments influences decomposition rates and nutrient release patterns.
