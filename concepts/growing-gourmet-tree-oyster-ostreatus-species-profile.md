---# Tree Oyster Mushroom ([[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] P. sapidus also shares
synonymy with P. ostreatus according to Vilgalys et al. (1993). P.
columbinus is also in doubt as a separately valid species.

## Distribution and Natural Habitat

Distributed throughout temperate and tropical forests of the world.
Common on broad-leaf hardwoods in spring and fall, especially
cottonwoods, oaks, alders, maples, aspens, ash, beech, birch, elm,
willows and poplars. Primarily a saprophyte but behaves as a
facultative parasite at the earliest opportunity. Occasionally on
composting bales of straw, and in Mexico on coffee production waste
pulp. The most abundant fruitings are in low valley riparian habitats.

## Microscopic Features

Spores white to slightly lilac to lilac grey, 7.5-9.5 x 3-4
micrometers. Clamp connections present. Context monomitic.

## Mycelial Characteristics

Whitish, longitudinally radial, soon becoming cottony, forming a thick,
tenacious mycelial mat. Aged mycelium often secretes yellowish to
orangish droplets of a metabolite which is a toxin to nematodes.
Fragrance is sweet, rich, pleasant, distinctly anise and almost
almond-like.

## CO2 and Light Sensitivity

P. ostreatus is extraordinarily interesting from many viewpoints. Highly
tolerant and responsive to carbon dioxide levels, Zadrazil (1974) noted
[[duggar-mushroom-growing-room-design]].

Super-sensitive to light levels. Eger et al. (1974) determined P.
ostreatus forms the most primordia at 2000 lux. Light exceeding 10,000
lux caused primordia to fail. Kalberer (1974) showed total yield was
maximized (and stem mass minimized) at 300-430 lux at 12 hours per day.
If total light exposure fails to exceed 2400 lux-hours per day, P.
ostreatus is triggered into "coral" formation.

## Nematode-Predatory Behavior

Thorn and Barron (1984) first noted that P. ostreatus exudes a
metabolite toxic to nematodes. As the nematode lies stunned, the
mycelium soon invades through one of its orifices, quickly consuming
the internal organs. From an evolutionary viewpoint, it is remarkable
that a saprophytic mushroom can become predatory to an animal in its
quest for new sources of nitrogen. This may explain why nematodes have
never been reported as a pathogen in [[oyster-mushroom]]
