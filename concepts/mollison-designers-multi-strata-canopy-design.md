---# Multi-Strata Canopy Design

Multi-strata canopy design is the art and science of stacking productive
plant layers vertically to maximize the yield from a given area of land. This
approach mimics the structure of natural forests, where light, water, and
nutrients are captured and recycled through multiple layers of vegetation.

6. **Root layer**: Edible tubers, bulbs, and root crops that occupy

   significant yield diversity.

7. **Vine layer**: Climbing plants that use the vertical space on trunks
   and branches. Kiwifruit, grapes, passionfruit, and climbing beans are
   classic examples.

Not all seven layers must be present in every design. The specific layers
included depend on climate, available species, and the designer's objectives.
However, the principle of filling every available niche with a productive
species remains constant.

## Light Penetration and Gap Dynamics

In a multi-strata system, light is the primary resource driving species
interactions. Only 1 to 5 percent of full sunlight reaches the forest floor
under a mature closed canopy. This creates intense competition for light
and requires careful species placement.

Mollison advises designers to think in terms of light gaps. In natural
forests, gaps created by fallen trees drive succession and allow understory
species to flourish. In designed systems, the designer can create permanent
gaps by spacing canopy trees to allow light shafts to reach lower layers.
A canopy cover of 50 to 70 percent typically provides enough light for
productive understory crops while maintaining the microclimate benefits of
tree cover.

The orientation of gaps matters. In temperate latitudes, east-west oriented
gaps allow maximum solar penetration to lower layers during the growing
season. In the tropics, where the sun is more directly overhead, circular
gaps of varying size are more appropriate.

## Root Stratification

Below ground, a parallel stratification occurs in the root zone. Different
species occupy different soil depths, reducing competition for water and
nutrients. Mollison describes this as the "root complementarity" principle.

Deep-rooted trees such as oak, walnut, and comfrey mine minerals and water
from depths of 2 metres or more. Shallow-rooted species such as berries and
