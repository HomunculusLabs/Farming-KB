# Permaculture Designers Manual - Grains and Cereals

updated: 2026-04-18

Grains and cereals occupy a complex position in permaculture design. While the Designers Manual does not devote a single chapter to grain production, references to cereals are scattered throughout Chapters 10 through 14, always framed within the context of integrated systems rather than monoculture. Mollison consistently argues that broadscale grain agriculture is one of the most destructive land uses, yet acknowledges that cereals remain dietary staples requiring intelligent integration into permaculture systems.

## Critique of Conventional Grain Production

Conventional grain monoculture depends on massive external inputs of fossil fuel energy, biocides, and synthetic fertilisers. Mollison notes that world grain systems are heavily controlled by a few agro-chemical multinationals through seed patents, seed trade restriction, and retail outlets -- part of a global power play to control nations by controlling food via "aid" allocation and market control.

The manual specifically critiques wheat production for its enormous water demands, and cereal monocultures generally for reducing net protein production and eroding soils compared to the diverse grasslands they replaced. The great prairies of North America, the pampas of Argentina, the Russian steppes, and the African veldts have all been converted to pastures or cropping -- and in every case, net protein production has been reduced and soils eroded.

## Grain Legumes

Grain legumes receive more attention than cereal grains in the manual. Species such as fava beans, peas, lentils, and chickpeas provide both protein-rich food and soil fertility through nitrogen fixation. In cool climate home gardens (Section 12.6), fava beans are a key over-winter crop, left in the field or stored. Grain legumes serve as critical poultry and livestock feed supplements.

Mollison emphasises that seed saving of the 20-30 basic food crop seeds of grains and grain legumes is now subjected to a concerted effort at total control by multinational corporations. In response, people everywhere have set up seed exchanges, seed libraries, open-pollinated non-patented seed companies, and hundreds of thousands of growers have studied home seed saving systems.

## Wild Rice (Zizania)

Wild rice receives the most detailed treatment of any grain in the manual (Section 13.10). A tall annual grass to 2.5 m, it has been gathered as a grain for centuries by Amerindian tribes from Minnesota to Florida. Of the 3 million kg produced, about 50% is still wild-gathered traditionally.

Wild rice is suited to cold swamps and shallows with intense summer heat. Commercial yields are low (50-100 kg/ha) but prices are high ($10/kg in 1984), giving fields of 100 hectares returns up to $100,000 annually. Home plots gathered over 3-4 weeks are very productive -- 200-800 plants supply a family. A self-generated polyculture of crayfish, mink, beaver, and waterfowl has evolved with wild rice culture in Minnesota.

## Rice in Integrated Systems

Rice (Oryza) is treated mainly within aquaculture contexts (Chapter 13). Rice padi culture serves much of Asia and Oceania, with fish as a secondary protein crop. In modified rice paddies, fish yields add 70-400 kg/ha/year. In Indonesia, fish are increasingly replacing rice as the yield over large areas of abandoned padi, for a great gain in protein yield and reduction in fuels needed for food preparation.

The Ifugao terrace culture of the Philippines has been sustainable for 3,000+ years, growing 0.5 ha of padi rice plus 0.25 ha sweet potato per family of 5, spending only 80 productive person-days per individual per year.

## Sorghum and Millet

Sorghum is referenced in tropical and arid climate sections as a drought-adapted grain crop. It is suited to areas where maize fails due to water stress. Millet species are referenced as dryland cereals for subsistence systems. These grains are noted as appropriate for small-scale, locally-adapted production in arid and semi-arid zones.
