## Organic Mulch as a Soil Food Web Tool

## Definition

A organic mulch soil food web is anything placed on top of soil to reduce evaporation, prevent weed
growth, and insulate plants. While many materials can serve this purpose, only
**organic mulches** — those derived from once-living things — are of interest for
[[soil-food-web]] management. Inorganic mulches (stone, plastic, rubber) provide
physical benefits but contribute nothing to soil biology.

## Common Organic Mulch Materials

Leaves, leaf mold, aged pine needles, grass clippings, aged bark, wood chips,
straw, well-rotted manure, seaweed, "almost compost," plant remnants, and paper
all qualify as organic mulches. Each differs in its carbon-to-nitrogen ratio,
lignin content, and tendency to favor either [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] or [[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]].

## Standard Mulch Benefits

Organic mulches provide a suite of well-known agronomic benefits:

- **Weed suppression**: Smother existing weeds by depriving them of sunlight and
  prevent weed seed germination.
- **Temperature moderation**: Cool soils during hot periods and insulate against
  cold, preventing premature growth during freeze-thaw cycles.
- **Soil protection**: Prevent compaction from heavy rain impact.
- **Moisture conservation**: Reduce evaporation from the soil surface.
- **Aesthetics**: Provide a neat, finished appearance to garden beds.

## Soil Food Web Benefits

The biological benefits of organic mulch are often overlooked but are perhaps
its greatest contribution to long-term soil health:

- **Habitat and food**: Mulch provides nutrients and shelter for a vast range of
  soil organisms, from bacteria and fungi to worms and arthropods.
- **Earthworm activity**: Worms pull mulch down into underground dens, producing
  nutrient-rich [[vermicompost-castings]], increasing worm populations, creating tunnels that
  improve water retention and aeration.
- **Arthropod communities**: Micro- and macroarthropods colonize mulch layers,
  accelerating decomposition, adding organic content, and attracting other
  [[soil-food-web]] members higher up the food chain.

## Mulch vs. Compost

[[organic-mulch]] is not as effective as [[compost]] for adding microbes quickly. Because
the decay process in mulch is not yet complete, it lacks the microbial diversity
and population density of finished compost. However, mulch can cause
[[soil-immobilization-mineralization]] if the bacterial and fungal feeding frenzy on fresh
mulch material is not matched by a corresponding predator feeding frenzy. This
tie-up effect is actually beneficial for weed control — nutrients are immobilized
at the surface where weed seeds germinate, while deeper-rooted crop plants in
the rhizosphere remain unaffected.

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Mulch Selection (Rule #6)

A key principle from soil food web science: **aged brown materials favor fungi;
fresh green materials favor bacteria.**

| Attribute | Fungal-Dominant Mulch | Bacterial-Dominant Mulch |
|---|---|---|
| Color | Brown, aged | Green, fresh |
| Examples | Autumn leaves, wood chips, bark | Grass clippings, fresh plant remnants |
| C:N ratio | Higher (more carbon) | Lower (more nitrogen) |
| Sugars | Low | High (necessary for bacteria) |

Both mulch types eventually attract arthropods, worms, and higher-level
predators, but they start the [[soil-food-web]] chain from different microbial
starting points.

## Green Mulches

Fresh grass clippings are the most readily available green mulch. They contain
the simple sugars that bacteria need to multiply rapidly.

**Cautions with green mulch:**
- Avoid clippings from lawns treated with weed killers or pesticides.
- Avoid clippings from areas where dogs frequent.
- Do not pile too thick — layers can go anaerobic, producing foul odors or
  excessive heat that damages soil life.

## Brown Mulches

Saved autumn leaves are an excellent brown mulch and strongly support [[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]]
unless they are ground extremely fine (which favors [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] instead).

Key considerations for specific brown materials:
- **Leaf mulch**: Grows more fungi, faster, than wood chips.
- **Peat moss**: Biologically sterile — always mix with other materials.
- **Pine needles**: Must be aged first; fresh needles contain terpenes toxic to
  many plants.
- **Cedar chips**: High in terpenes — best avoided as mulch.
- **Wood chips, bark, sawdust**: Work well, especially if aged or mixed with
  an organic nitrogen source such as grass clippings or alfalfa meal.

## Mulch Longevity and Decomposition

Mulch longevity depends on material composition. Bark chips (~2 inches thick)
can last 3–4 years because their lignin, cellulose, and wax content is difficult
for microbes to break down. [[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]] dominate the decomposition of bark chips
throughout their lifespan. Leaves, by contrast, decompose in roughly 6 months;
[[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]] dominate initially, but [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] populations increase as the
material breaks down and becomes more accessible from the inside.

## Placement Strategy (Rule #4)

**Where you place mulch determines which microbes dominate**, regardless of the
material itself:

- **Surface mulch** supports [[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]] — fungal hyphae can easily bridge upward
  from the soil into the mulch layer.
- **Mulch worked into the soil** supports [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] — incorporating material
  increases bacterial access and dominance.

The same material (e.g., tree leaves) can produce entirely different microbial
dominance patterns depending solely on whether it is left on the surface or
tilled in.

## Mulch Condition: Wet and Ground (Rule #8)

Wet, ground mulch accelerates bacterial colonization. [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] require
moisture to be active, and grinding mulch dramatically increases surface area
for colonization. Some bacteria even produce antibiotics that suppress fungal
competitors.

**To encourage bacterial dominance:**
- Use green mulches.
- Grind or chop material finely.
- Soak the mulch before or after application.
- If using only brown materials, chop into fine bits and mix into the top few
  inches of soil.

## Coarse Dry Mulches (Rule #9)

Mulches with less than 35% moisture are considered "dry mulches" and favor
fungal activity. [[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]] need some moisture but are far less water-dependent
than [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]], so they thrive in coarser, drier conditions where bacteria
struggle.

**To encourage fungal dominance:**
- Use brown leaves or wood chips.
- Do not pulverize the material.
- Do not saturate with water.
- Place on the soil surface rather than tilling in.
