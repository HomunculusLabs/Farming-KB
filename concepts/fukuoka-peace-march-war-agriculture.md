# The Peace March -- War, Agriculture, and Human Separation from Nature

[[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]] opens with [[natural-farming-fukuoka]]'s account of participating in an antinuclear peace march in Paris, and closes with "A Message for Peace" -- an open letter to world leaders. This framing device connects his agricultural philosophy to a broader critique of modern civilization, war, and the human condition.

## The Paris Peace March of 1983

During his 1983 [[fukuoka-european-travels-natural-farming]], Fukuoka arrived in Paris on the same day that an antinuclear peace march reached the city. The march had begun in Oslo when four women "deciding that they had to do something in the name of peace, simply started walking." As they proceeded south through Europe, people in each country joined them. By the time they arrived in Paris, the number of marchers had swollen to an estimated 8,000 people.

That evening, Fukuoka was invited to speak at a gathering of the marchers in what he described as "a large, renovated warehouse." A jazz performance was underway. His interpreter walked up to the conductor and whispered in his ear, and the music stopped. The crowd began to clap and yell "Speech, speech!" Fukuoka, who had come totally unprepared, spoke about "the roots of peace and war."

Because his words were being translated by three interpreters into English, German, and French -- "full of laughter-provoking blunders" -- he had time to think and felt able "to get across a more serious message." When he finished, "he was embraced by a delighted old man who was introduced to me as an Indian holy man." Fukuoka had forgotten what he spoke about, but he reflected: "It was enough merely that people of different countries should meet, embrace, and share with each other the joy of being alive."

## The Same Mindset Produces Both

Fukuoka argued that industrial agriculture and industrial warfare share the same root cause: the human desire to dominate and control nature. He saw a direct line from:

- The scientist who seeks to [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] to increase yields

- To the military engineer who seeks to build more destructive weapons

- To the politician who seeks to impose political systems on other cultures

All share the fundamental error of [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]] -- the belief that humans can and should control natural processes through knowledge and technology.

## Agriculture as the Foundation of Civilization's Errors

Fukuoka traced the origins of civilization's destructive tendencies to the invention of agriculture itself -- not all agriculture, but specifically the kind that seeks to control nature rather than work within it:

- Plowing the soil is the original act of violence against nature

- Selective breeding for yield rather than wholeness is the original reductionism

- Irrigation and water control are the original attempts to dominate natural systems

- Stored grain surpluses are the original basis for hierarchy, inequality, and eventually warfare

## The Green Revolution as Warfare

Fukuoka explicitly framed the [[fukuoka-critique-green-revolution]] as a form of warfare against nature and against traditional farming cultures:

- Chemical fertilizers and pesticides as "weapons" that kill soil life

- High-yield varieties as "colonizers" that replace diverse local crops with monocultures

- Agricultural aid programs as vehicles for spreading industrial dependency

- The promise of feeding the world as a justification for destroying self-sufficient farming communities

## Food as a Strategic Weapon

In his observations of American agriculture, Fukuoka saw food being used as an instrument of geopolitical power. "America is a powerful and affluent country, but it is also a country in great danger," he wrote. "Depending on how it uses the food it produces, such a large country producing so much food is capable of saving the world or of throwing the world into chaos and even destroying it."

He noted that the U.S. government pressured Japan to buy American oranges and wheat, and protested when Japan tried to ship surplus rice to Vietnam. "Food today is a strategic weapon of the U.S." This weaponization of food was the logical extension of the agricultural mindset that treats nature as a resource to be exploited.

## Natural Farming as Peace Practice

If the mindset that produces war also produces industrial agriculture, then [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] is inherently a peace practice:

- [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-and-spiritual-life]] means not imposing one's will on the land or on other people

- [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] are practices of non-interference and non-violence

- [[fukuoka-decentralized-self-sufficient-communities]] have no need for the resource competition that drives conflict

- [[fukuoka-desert-greening-techniques-detailed]] is literally making peace with barren land

Fukuoka argued that self-sufficient communities practicing natural farming would have no incentive for war because they would produce everything they need locally. The root cause of resource conflict -- scarcity created by mismanagement -- would be eliminated at its source.

## War as a Product of the Human Intellect

Fukuoka's most radical claim was that "war exists only in man's world and is alien to the rest of the biological kingdom; it does not belong to the natural world. War is an absurdity that arose from the human intellect." He rejected the social Darwinist justification for militarism: "Nature in its original form is neither a world of competition nor of cooperation." The survival of the fittest was a human projection, not a natural law.

He pointed out the escalation logic of weapons: "Defense and offense may appear unlike, the shield and the halberd may seem different things, but these are as joined in purpose and function as the right and left hands." The more powerful the arms, the more intense the wars. "Today weapons are in a race by themselves. Things have gotten to the point where weapons are moving people."

## The Letter to Roosevelt and the Message for Peace

Fukuoka revealed that in 1941, as a young man in his twenties, he had written a letter to Franklin D. Roosevelt arguing against the approaching war. The letter was never delivered. Nearly fifty years later, in *The Road Back to Nature*, he wrote a new "Message for Peace" addressed to the leaders of the United States, the Soviet Union, and other countries.

His argument was consistent across both letters: that human intellect cannot resolve the contradictions it creates, that the arms race is a spiral of madness, and that "the only road to salvation is by discarding the intellect." He appealed to leaders to become "saviors" rather than "merchants of death."

## Reflections on the Japanese

Fukuoka's experiences in Europe led him to reflect deeply on the Japanese character. He observed that "the Japanese are a good people who love chrysanthemums. But, depending on the leadership, they can be transformed with great ease into barbaric warriors." This malleability -- the tendency to follow the group unquestioningly -- was both a strength and a terrifying weakness.

After visiting Hiroshima with foreign guests for the peace memorial ceremony, he was struck by their disappointment: "We were totally disappointed. It wasn't at all what we had expected it to be." The ceremony had become a bureaucratic formality rather than a genuine reckoning with the horrors of nuclear war.

## The Spiritual Dimension

For Fukuoka, peace was not merely the absence of war but a fundamental state of alignment with nature. War was the ultimate expression of [[fukuoka-nature-transcends-human-knowledge]]. When humans see themselves as separate from and superior to the natural world, they will inevitably come into conflict -- with nature, with each other, and within themselves.

This connects to his [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]]: emptiness is not nothingness but the state of being free from the ego-driven desire to control. A farmer who practices mu is at peace with the land, and by extension, at peace with the world. "If everyone were to do nothing, the world would become a place of peace and plenty. Nothing more would have to be said."
