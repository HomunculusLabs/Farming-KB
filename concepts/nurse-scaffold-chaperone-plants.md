### Design Application

Chaperone plants are more critical in hot-sun climates toward the south. However, many plants, especially shrubs and small trees for the understory layers of the garden, benefit from sun protection during their early years regardless of climate. In practical design:
- Plant fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing shrubs as chaperones for slower-growing fruit or nut trees.
- Time the removal or reduction of chaperones to match the maturation of the target species.
- Select chaperones that provide multiple functions beyond protection, such as fodder, mulch, or wildlife habitat.

## Scaffold Plants

Scaffold plants provide temporary physical support for the establishment of desired vegetation. Like the temporary staging that workers use for erecting buildings, scaffold plants can be removed or repurposed once their structural mission is accomplished.

### Mechanism

Scaffold plants function through:
- **Wind protection**: Creating sheltered microclimates for young trees.
- **Soil stabilization**: Holding soil on eroding hillsides and gullies.
- **Physical barrier**: Dense or thorny planting that protects vulnerable species from deer and other animals.
- **Canopy closure**: Crowding out competitive grasses that would otherwise suppress tree seedlings.

### The Bermuda Cahow Restoration

The most documented example of scaffold plant use comes from ornithologist David Wingate's work in Bermuda. In the 1960s, Wingate attempted to restore the native cedar forest that had been destroyed by overgrazing and a subsequent blight. The cedar forest was the habitat of the endangered Bermuda petrel, or cahow. However, fierce ocean winds scoured down every seedling tree he planted.

Wingate's solution was to use non-native scaffold plants—tamarisk and the fast-growing nitrogen-fixer casuarina—to create an initial windbreak. He then replanted cedars in the shelter of the scaffold plants. The cedar forest quickly grew and was tough enough to withstand Hurricane Emily in 1987.

As the cedars matured, Wingate guided the vegetation closer to its native composition by girdling many of the scaffold plants (removing a ring of bark from around the trunk). This kills the tree but leaves it standing, reducing the ecological stress on the cedars that wholesale removal might cause. The standing dead wood provides habitat and slowly breaks down, returning nutrients to the soil.

### Design Application

Scaffold plants can be placed thickly in trouble spots—eroding hillsides, wind-exposed ridges, or areas of heavy animal browsing. Once permanent vegetation is established:
- Girdle scaffold trees to kill them standing, avoiding shock to the recovering ecosystem.
- Cut scaffold trees for mulch, timber, or firewood.
- Allow scaffold plants to remain as minor components of the mature system if they provide ongoing value.

### Nurse Crop Systems

In the Permaculture Plants catalog (Nugent and Boniface), nurse crops are described as species that simulate natural succession by planting pioneers on bare or pastured areas to streamline the reclamation process. Key characteristics of nurse species include:
- Usually nitrogen-fixing legumes.
- Usually fast-growing and often short-lived.
- Generally very variable, producing abundant seed.
- Seed often dispersed by animals and requiring scarification (passing through digestive tracts or exposure to fire) to germinate.
- Able to withstand hostile conditions imposed on young trees.
- Providing shade, shelter, weed suppression, and mulch from leaf litter.

### Tagasaste as Nurse Crop

Trials in New Zealand demonstrated that fruit and nut trees growing in close association with tagasaste (as close as 1 meter) consistently grew better than controls without tagasaste. When tagasaste is grown near timber trees, it forces them upward for light, producing straight, long trunks. For fruit or nut trees where a spreading habit is desired, tagasaste should be clipped and used as mulch around the nursed tree.
