## Slope Terracing and Earthworks

## Overview

Almost every slope benefits from earth-shaping for soil conservation. Terracing transforms sloped land into a series of level or gently inclined platforms, preventing erosion, retaining water, and creating productive growing space. Terraced systems are among the oldest and most proven land-management techniques in human agriculture, with examples in Asia and Oceania remaining in continuous production for up to 5,000 years — notably the rice terraces of the Ifugao people of the Philippines.

## Terrace Dimensions and Construction Methods

### Hand-Made vs. Machine-Made Terraces

- **Hand-made terraces** should be narrower, typically **3.5–6.5 metres** wide, to remain manageable for manual construction and maintenance.
- **Machine-made terraces** can be wider, as earth-moving equipment allows for larger-scale earthworks and more robust structural shaping.

### Garden Terraces on Steep Humid Slopes

On very steep humid slopes, garden terraces must be narrow and arranged in **sets of 6–8 terraces descending the hillside**. Staggered short sets are preferred for steep conditions rather than continuous long runs, which are harder to stabilize and drain.

## Dry vs. Wet Terrace Systems

### Dry Terrace Systems

Dry terraces rely on rainfall and mulch retention rather than continuous water flow. They are suited to arid, semi-arid, and seasonally dry climates. Key design principles include:

- Deep mulch layers to conserve soil moisture
- Contour planting and bund plantings to slow runoff
- Selection of drought-adapted crop species
- Fodder integration for ground cover and soil protection

**Dry terrace crops** include millet, dryland rice, barley, wheat, rye, amaranth, quinoa, rape/mustard, lentils, peas, grams, and other pulses.

### Wet Terrace Systems (Classical Paddy)

Classical wet rice and taro terraces use continuous water flow across the terrace surface, maintained by carefully engineered bunds and inlet/outlet channels. These systems:

- Maintain a constant shallow flood on each terrace
- Use the flat water surface for nutrient distribution and weed suppression
- Require robust bund construction to hold water at the edges
- Often incorporate aquaculture (fish) in deeper terrace pond areas

Wet terraces represent the peak of terracing engineering and have sustained dense populations across Southeast Asia for millennia.

## Slope Stability and Protective Planting

### The Upper Third Rule

The **upper one-third of any hill** and all slopes exceeding **30°** should be planted to mulch-producing trees rather than put into terrace cultivation. This critical zone intercepts rainfall, reduces runoff velocity, and protects lower terraces from excessive water and debris flow.

### Contour Stabilization Strips

On slopes and between terrace sets, establish contour strips of deeply rooted grasses and tree legumes:

- **[[leucaena-entity]], tagasaste) — fix nitrogen and provide mulch material

These strips act as living barriers that slow water flow, trap sediment, and reinforce terrace structure over time.

## Terrace Microzones and Growing Situations

Each terrace system contains multiple distinct growing microzones, each suited to different species and functions:

| Microzone | Description | Suitable Plants |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Banks / Bunds** | Raised earth edges of terraces | Tree legumes, Vetiver, lemongrass, comfrey |
| **Slope Faces** | Vertical or steep faces between levels | Chayote, cucurbits, beans, passionfruit, kiwifruit |
| **Trellis Areas** | Overhead structures on terraces | Vining crops, shade-tolerant understory plants |
| **Ditches / Drains** | Water channels between terraces | Aquatic plants, reeds, taro (in wet systems) |
| **Steep Unterraced Slopes** | Residual steep areas | Mulch-producing trees, fodder trees, bamboo |
| **Flat Terrace Area** | The primary level growing surface | Full range of crops (see below) |

## Crop Selection by Terrace Type

### Garden Terrace Crops

Garden terraces in humid and subtropical regions support a wide diversity:

- Banana, papaya, melons, chilies, peppers
- Cucurbits, maize, beans, sugar cane
- Brassicas, edible Chrysanthemum
- Moringa, coconut, mango

### Vine Crops from Bund Faces

Bund faces provide excellent vertical growing space for climbing and trailing species:

- Chayote, cucurbits, beans
- Passionfruit, kiwifruit

### Fodder and Mulch Crops

Dedicated mulch and fodder plantings are essential for terrace sustainability:

- **Tree legumes** — nitrogen fixation and coppiced mulch
- **[[banna-grass]], lemongrass, [[vetiver-grass]]** — erosion control and biomass
- **Comfrey** — deep nutrient mining and dynamic accumulator
- **Bamboo** — structural material and erosion control
- **Aleurites (candlenut), Cinnamomum** — multipurpose tree crops

### In-Crop Mulches

Living mulches grown within the terrace cropping area improve soil health:

- **Azolla** — aquatic nitrogen-fixing fern (for wet terraces)
- **Clovers** — nitrogen-fixing ground cover (for dry terraces)

## Design Essentials and Variations

### Structural Elements

- **Splash stones** — placed where water enters terrace channels to dissipate energy and prevent erosion
- **Border drains** — intercept runoff above the terrace system and direct it safely to discharge points
- **Bund plantings** — vegetate all bund surfaces immediately after construction to prevent weathering
- **Deep pond areas** — incorporate deeper zones within wet terrace systems for fish production and water storage

### Advanced Design Variations

- **Animal sheds over terraces** — integrate poultry or small livestock housing above terrace surfaces to provide manure directly to crops
- **Staggered short sets** — on very steep slopes, build short sets of 6–8 terraces with natural vegetated breaks between sets rather than continuous terracing
- **Vine integration** — use bund faces and trellis structures to maximize productive surface area
- **Trellis systems** — employ overhead trellising on garden terraces for vining crops while allowing light to reach understory plantings

## Key Design Principles

1. **Always protect the upper third** of hills with permanent tree cover before terracing lower slopes
2. **Narrow terraces on steep land** — wider terraces require steeper, less stable bund faces
3. **Vegetate immediately** — never leave bare earth on bunds or slope faces; plant mulch crops and grasses from day one
4. **Manage water carefully** — every terrace system needs planned inlet, overflow, and discharge paths
5. **Build in sets** — group terraces in manageable sets of 6–8 with drainage breaks between them
6. **Integrate multiple functions** — combine food production, fodder, mulch generation, and slope stabilization in every terrace zone
7. **Design for longevity** — well-built terraces with permanent vegetation can remain productive for thousands of years

## Historical Context

Terracing systems across Asia and Oceania represent some of the longest continuously productive agricultural landscapes on Earth. The Ifugao rice terraces of the Philippines have been maintained for approximately 5,000 years, demonstrating that terraced earthworks — when properly designed, vegetated, and managed — are among the most sustainable and enduring forms of land use available to human societies.
