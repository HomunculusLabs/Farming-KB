# Four Principles of Natural Farming

The four principles of natural farming are a set of foundational precepts formulated by Japanese farmer and philosopher Masanobu Fukuoka (1913-2008) as the basis of his "do-nothing" agricultural method. First articulated in his 1976 work *Shizen Noho* (*The Natural Way of Farming: The Theory and Practice of Green Philosophy*), the principles represent a radical departure from both conventional scientific agriculture and mainstream organic farming. The four principles are: **no cultivation** (no tillage), **no fertilizer**, **no weeding**, and **no pesticides**.

Together, they constitute an integrated philosophy in which farming proceeds by eliminating human intervention rather than by adding techniques or inputs. Fukuoka spent over forty years refining and demonstrating these principles on his farm on the island of Shikoku, Japan, achieving yields comparable to or exceeding those of scientifically managed farms while drastically reducing labor, costs, and environmental impact.

## Overview

Fukuoka's four principles emerged from decades of direct observation and experimentation, rooted in the conviction that nature is inherently self-sufficient and that human agricultural knowledge, far from improving upon nature, typically disrupts and degrades natural systems. He described natural farming as moving "centripetally inward toward a state of nothingness (*Mu*)" by the elimination of human labor, in contrast to scientific farming, which moves "centrifugally outward toward something-ness" through ever-increasing complexity and intervention.

The principles are not merely a set of prohibitions but form a coherent ecological logic in which each practice supports the others. Eliminating tillage preserves soil structure and microbial life; the absence of chemical fertilizers allows soil organisms to maintain balanced nutrient cycles; tolerating weeds provides ground cover, prevents erosion, and contributes organic matter; and forgoing pesticides permits natural predator-prey relationships to self-regulate. When one principle is violated, the others tend to break down as well, creating cascading dependencies on further human intervention.

Fukuoka distinguished his approach, which he termed "Mahayana natural farming," from what he called "Hinayana natural farming" (comparable to organic farming). While Hinayana farming earnestly seeks the blessings of nature but still relies on discriminating knowledge and active techniques, Mahayana natural farming represents a state in which the farmer becomes one with nature and subsists on its bounty without purposeful effort. Using an analogy from Japanese swordsmanship, he compared Mahayana farming to the "unmoving no-sword school," Hinayana farming to the "one-sword school directed toward the center," and scientific farming to the "two-sword school directed outward." Scientific farming increases the number of means at its disposal, while natural farming seeks the best results while rendering all means useless, or doing entirely without.

## Detailed Explanation

### No Cultivation (No Tillage)

The principle of no cultivation holds that plowing and tilling are unnecessary and actively harmful to soil health. Fukuoka argued that plowing appears to loosen and aerate soil but actually produces the opposite effect: by breaking soil into progressively finer particles with smaller interstitial spaces, tillage compacts the soil and reduces air porosity. He likened the effect to kneading bread dough, where repeated folding produces a denser, more uniform mass rather than a looser one.

In nature, soil is "plowed" by the roots of grasses and green manure crops, which penetrate to depths of twelve to fifteen inches or more, far deeper than the four to eight inches typically reached by a farmer's plow. As these roots grow, they carry air and water deep into the earth. When roots die and decompose, they feed proliferating microorganisms that generate humus, which in turn attracts earthworms. Moles subsequently burrow through the soil, further improving its structure and aeration at even greater depths.

Fukuoka observed that trees in mountain forests grow to magnificent heights without any tillage, demonstrating that nature is fully capable of maintaining and enriching soil without human assistance. He noted that plowing becomes necessary only after soil has been degraded by other interventions: clearing land removes deep humus sources and halts soil microbial proliferation, transplanting damages root systems and encourages shallow growth, and fertilizer application discourages deep rooting and halts natural topsoil aggregation.

Once these degradation cycles begin, the farmer is forced to till to compensate for damage caused by previous tilling. He cited the post-World War II experience in Japan, where the introduction of herbicides led farmers to reduce plowing frequency and subsequently discover that their yields improved, demonstrating that intertillage had been effective only as a weeding process and was worthless as a means of loosening soil.

### No Fertilizer

Fukuoka's no-fertilizer principle asserts that crops do not fundamentally require applied fertilizers because they grow of their own accord through the inherent fertility of living soil. He drew a critical distinction between the assumption that crops must "be grown" (leading to the conclusion that fertilizer causes growth) versus observing that plants grow naturally (in which case nutrient uptake by roots is merely one small effect within a much larger natural process). The buds on a tree exist for budding; the roots spread and extend throughout the earth with their powers of elongation. A tree has a shape perfectly adapted to the natural environment and grows in total harmony with the great cycles of nature.

He catalogued numerous harms caused by fertilizer use. Fertilizers speed up crop growth, but this is only a temporary and local effect that inevitably weakens the crops, analogous to the rapid acceleration of plant growth by hormones. Plants weakened by fertilizers have lowered resistance to diseases and pests. Much applied fertilizer is wasted: approximately thirty percent of nitrogen in ammonium sulfate applied to paddy fields is denitrified by soil microorganisms and lost to the atmosphere, and phosphate fertilizers often penetrate no more than two inches into the soil surface.

More than seventy percent of the three major fertilizer components, ammonium sulfate, superphosphate, and potassium sulfate, is concentrated sulfuric acid which acidifies the soil. Fukuoka estimated that some 1.8 million tons of sulfuric acid were dumped annually onto Japanese farmlands, suppressing soil microorganisms and potentially spelling disaster for long-term agricultural viability. Heavy reliance on a narrow range of macronutrients also produces progressive deficiency of trace elements such as zinc, manganese, boron, and iodine. Adding too much of one fertilizer renders another ineffective, and the resulting interactions are so complex that the full consequences may never be understood.

Fukuoka pointed to mountain forests as evidence that abundant plant growth occurs without any applied fertilizer. Reforested cedars on favorable sites produce approximately two tons of new lumber growth per quarter-acre annually, or roughly four tons when branches, leaves, and roots are included. This production continues year after year even though the entire above-ground biomass is removed at harvest. He argued that the problem is not that soil lacks nutrients, but that conventional farming practices destroy the living soil ecosystem that makes those nutrients naturally available.

### No Weeding

The no-weeding principle challenges the assumption that weeds compete harmfully with crops and must be eradicated. Fukuoka observed that in nature, plants live in layered communities: bushes and shrubs grow at the foot of large trees, grasses spread among the shrubs, and mosses flourish beneath the grasses. Rather than cut-throat competition for nutrients, he described this as a "peaceful world of coexistence" governed by ecological laws of succession, mutual benefit, and dynamic balance.

He argued that the moment a farmer decides to raise one particular crop, the attention focused on that crop gives birth to a complementary sense of repulsion that excludes all other plants as "weeds." But weeds serve essential ecological functions. Their deep roots penetrate and loosen soil, and when those roots die, they add humus that allows soil microbes to proliferate and enrich the earth. Rainwater percolates through the soil and air is carried deep down, supporting earthworms and moles.

Weeds prevent erosion by anchoring topsoil, preventing losses ranging from several tons to well over a hundred tons of soil per year on even gently sloping land. Without grasses growing over the ground surface, Fukuoka warned, the topsoil would wash entirely away in twenty to thirty years, reducing fertility to essentially zero.

Rather than pulling weeds, Fukuoka advocated managing them through strategic ground covers of clover, green manure crops, or other beneficial plants that suppress undesirable weeds while enriching the soil. He noted that the many different grasses and herbs in a natural meadow, though appearing to grow in total confusion, actually follow discernible laws and patterns of succession. Certain weeds grow as individuals, others in bunches, and yet others form colonies. Some rise up over their neighbors, some wrap themselves around others in symbiosis, and some die while others thrive as undergrowth. By studying and making use of these properties, one species can be used to drive out a large number of others. He referred to this approach as the principle of "weed utility" rather than merely "no weeding."

### No Pesticides

The no-pesticides principle holds that insects identified as "pests" do not truly exist as such in nature, and that disease and pest problems are symptoms of agricultural practices that have disrupted natural ecological balance. Fukuoka argued that the thousand plant diseases observed in nature exist in dynamic equilibrium; it is only when man identifies certain insects as pests and attempts to eradicate them that imbalance is created.

He was particularly critical of the pollution caused by modern pesticides, noting that there are no truly non-polluting pesticides, including those marketed as low-toxicity or derived from natural predators. He argued that manipulating predator-prey relationships through biological control still constitutes human meddling with the natural order and produces unpredictable cascading effects throughout the ecosystem. A pesticidal or bactericidal effect referred to as "efficacy" in the target organism is, in Fukuoka's view, phytotoxicity in plants and pollution in animals and man. Animal cells, plant cells, and microorganisms are basically all quite similar, and a substance effective on one will act to a greater or lesser degree on the others.

To someone who understands the chain of being that links together the world of living organisms, Fukuoka contended, there is no way to determine which organisms are beneficial predators and which are pests. Many insects that are harmless in a direct sense are harmful indirectly, and when the food web grows complex, as when one beneficial insect feeds on a pest that kills another beneficial insect which feeds on another pest, it becomes futile to draw sharp distinctions and apply pesticides selectively. By meddling with controls, all man accomplishes is the destruction of the natural order. In his own orchards, a diverse plant community supporting a full range of insects, including natural predators, achieved self-regulating pest control without any chemical or biological intervention.

## Practical Application

Fukuoka's four principles were validated through decades of practical farming on his own land. His signature method was the direct-seeding, no-tillage barley-rice succession with green manure cover, which he called the "clover revolution" in rice and barley cropping.

In this system, clover was sown over standing rice in early autumn, barley was drilled approximately two weeks before rice harvest, rice straw was returned to the field as uncut mulch, and rice seed enclosed in clay pellets was scattered in late autumn or early winter. The clover served as a living mulch and nitrogen fixer. In early summer, when the clover appeared about to choke out the young rice seedlings, temporary flooding for four to seven days weakened the clover sufficiently for rice to establish. After this, intermittent irrigation maintained soil moisture at approximately eighty percent.

This method eliminated plowing, chemical fertilizers, weeding, and pesticides while producing yields comparable to conventional methods. The system was studied at numerous agricultural testing stations throughout Japan, where researchers confirmed there was no basic problem with no-tillage succession cropping using straw mulch. For orchards, Fukuoka used ground covers of ladino clover, alfalfa, and black wattle to suppress weeds, fix nitrogen, prevent erosion, and support natural insect predator populations. Fruit trees were allowed to grow toward their natural form rather than being heavily pruned, on the grounds that pruning disrupts the tree's natural balance and reduces its bearing consistency.

## Key Considerations

Fukuoka acknowledged that transitioning to natural farming is not instantaneous. Fields degraded by years of plowing, fertilizing, and pesticide application require a recovery period during which the soil ecosystem must rebuild. He stressed that natural farming cannot be successfully practiced on soil killed by conventional methods without first restoring conditions closer to a natural state. He also noted that the principles demand precision in their application; while the overall method is simple, each operation must be performed with great care and at the right time. He described it as a "highly advanced technique, quite unlike extensive farming."

A critical distinction Fukuoka drew was between his approach and organic farming, which he considered "just another type of scientific farming." While organic farming substitutes natural inputs for synthetic ones, it still operates within the same paradigm of active human management and the belief that crops must be deliberately "grown." Natural farming seeks to reduce human action toward zero, trusting that nature will perform necessary functions when allowed to do so. The net effect of composting, for example, is to accelerate decomposition by at most ten to twenty percent, a marginal gain that Fukuoka considered fundamentally unnecessary when compared to the simplicity of returning organic matter directly to the soil surface as mulch.

The principles are universal in their philosophical foundation but local in their specific application. Fukuoka repeatedly emphasized that the forms these principles take will differ according to climate, soil, and ecosystem, but the underlying logic, that nature is self-sufficient when not disrupted by human intervention, applies everywhere. He framed natural farming not merely as an agricultural technique but as a path toward ecological restoration, proposing that the same principles could be applied to revegetate deserts by scattering clay pellets containing mixed seeds of green manure trees, legumes, grains, and vegetables, allowing natural selection to determine which species are suited to local conditions.
