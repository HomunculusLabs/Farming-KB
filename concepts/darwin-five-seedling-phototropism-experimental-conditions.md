## Historical Background

By the late 19th century, it was well known that growing plants bend toward light,
but the mechanism underlying this response remained unknown. The Darwins set out to
determine precisely *where* on the plant the light was sensed and *how* that signal
was translated into a bending response. Their design used five carefully controlled
conditions on young [[phototropism]], bending decisively toward the light. This confirmed the
baseline behavior. As Darwin wrote, "There are extremely few [plants], of which some
part… does not bend towards lateral light."

### Condition B — Tip Removed

The shoot tip was excised before light exposure. Without the tip, the seedling
continued to grow upward but **did not bend** toward the light. Darwin described
these plants as having gone "blind." The fact that growth continued without
directional bending showed that the tip detects the direction of incoming light
rather than generating growth itself.

### Condition C — Tip Covered with a Lightproof Cap

An opaque cap blocking all light was placed over the intact shoot tip. Despite the
tip being physically present, the seedling **did not bend**. The intact but darkened
tip was functionally blind, confirming that the tip's *light-sensing* ability — not
merely its physical presence — was required.

### Condition D — Tip Covered with a Clear Glass Cap

A transparent glass cap was placed over the shoot tip, allowing light to pass
through freely. These seedlings bent toward the light **normally**, just like the
controls. This distinguished between a mechanical and an optical effect: the
bending response depended on light reaching the tip, not on any physical
obstruction.

### Condition E — Middle Section Covered with a Lightproof Tube

A lightproof tube was wrapped around the middle section of the coleoptile while the
tip remained fully exposed. These seedlings **bent normally**, demonstrating that
covering the bending zone itself does not prevent the response, so long as the tip
can still "see" the light.
