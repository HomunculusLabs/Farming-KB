## Boletus Edulis and Pore Fungi (Polyporaceae)

## Overview

The Polyporaceae (pore fungi) differ from gill fungi in their [[mycology]]: boletes with red or reddish pore surfaces should be left alone.

## Fistulina hepatica — The Beefsteak Fungus

Sometimes called "tongue of liver," found on the trunks and stumps of oak trees. Widely distributed, but since the amount occurring in any locality at one time is ordinarily small, it is not a plant of much market interest. The flavor and texture of this fungus is said to be best when fully matured, as it is then less astringent. It should be served with meats — its name and recommended preparation both suggest its culinary affinity for beef dishes.

## Polyporus: The Bracket Fungi

Among the bracket fungi, the genus Polyporus furnishes a few edible species. For the most part these are edible only when young, and in no case are they particularly attractive or worthy of experimental cultivation.

### Notable Species

**Polyporus sulphureus** — [[chicken-of-the-woods]]. Common throughout a large part of the United States on a variety of trees, often attaining a weight of five pounds. Forms immense clusters of sulphur-yellow and orange bracket-like sporophores. One of the most conspicuous and easily recognized fungi.

**Polyporus tuberaster** — Cultivated in Italy, where it is known as "pietra fungaia." The spawn, in the form of a mass of clay held together by profusely interwoven mycelium, is brought in from the fields and forests. Masses of this spawn are found weighing many kilos. Placed in a cellar or other favorable situation and covered with fresh loam, the sporophores will often produce for several months. This represents one of the few examples of deliberate Polyporus cultivation.

**Polyporus squamosus** — In most countries a common parasite of various broad-leaved trees. Occasionally attains a diameter of a foot or more, perhaps the largest of the fleshy polypores.

## Identification Principles for Pore Fungi

Foragers should remember:

1. **Separable pore layer** — The tube layer should peel easily from the cap flesh in true boletes
2. **Color change on cutting** — Note whether and how quickly the flesh changes color when bruised
3. **Pore color** — Red or orange pores indicate potential toxicity
4. **Habitat** — Association with particular tree species can help narrow identification
5. **Age** — Most bracket fungi are edible only when young and tender; older specimens become tough and woody
