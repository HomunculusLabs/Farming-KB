### Grow Bag Media

Rodale experimented with mixtures for grow bags. Pure compost, pure sand, and perlite or vermiculite all supported mycorrhizae but were not successful when used alone—they were either too low in nutrients or, in the case of compost, too high in phosphorus and/or nitrogen.

Compost with high nitrogen, low phosphorus, and moderate potassium levels works best. Methods for producing ectomycorrhizal fungi:

- Collect fruiting bodies (mushrooms) of ectomycorrhizal fungi to use as propagules. Sporocarps are loaded with spores. Experienced mushroom hunters can make spore prints by removing the stem and placing the cap spore-side down on paper or glass for 24 hours.

- Add propagules directly adjacent to plant roots or spray seeds with spore suspensions.

- For home gardeners, the easiest method is to cut fruiting bodies into small pieces and mix them into potting soil. A few spores are enough to inoculate, and it is impossible to use too much.

## Staining for Verification

Viewing arbuscular mycorrhizae requires a dissecting microscope because the fungi are mostly transparent and obscured by dark plant root pigments. These pigments must be removed and a stain applied to reveal the fungi.

The standard laboratory procedure involves washing freshly collected roots, bathing them in a hot 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution to clear plant parts, acidifying with hydrochloric acid (HCl), and then staining. Traditional stains like trypan blue are highly alkaline and dangerous for untrained users.

A safer alternative method:

1. Soak root samples in water for at least 24 hours.

2. Soak in a 1:1 mixture of hydrogen peroxide to water.

3. Use diluted KOH at 2.5% (still hazardous—follow safety guidelines).

4. Stain with an India ink and vinegar mix.
