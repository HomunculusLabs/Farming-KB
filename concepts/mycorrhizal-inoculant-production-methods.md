# Mycorrhizal Inoculant Production Methods

Producing your own [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-inoculum-production-best-practices]] can be far more affordable than purchasing commercial formulations. Several methods exist, ranging from field-scale systems developed by research institutes to simple greenhouse pot cultures. See also [[rodale-mycorrhizal-inoculum]] for additional detail.

## Rodale Method

The Rodale Institute, based in Pennsylvania, partnered with the USDA Agricultural Research Service in 2002 to develop a system enabling farmers to produce [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]. After eight years of study, they published a description of their On-Farm Arbuscular Fungus Inoculum Production System in 2010. The system was designed to be simple, use readily available and inexpensive materials, and produce spore-rich inoculum less expensive than commercial brands.

A key finding: soil transfers run the risk of introducing organisms such as root pathogens. Using a host plant different from the crop plant prevents inadvertent growth of pathogens associated with the final crop.

### Grow Bag Media

Rodale experimented with mixtures for grow bags. Pure compost, pure sand, and perlite or vermiculite all supported mycorrhizae but were not successful when used alone—they were either too low in nutrients or, in the case of compost, too high in phosphorus and/or nitrogen.

Compost with high nitrogen, low phosphorus, and moderate potassium levels works best. The best dilution rate varied depending on the [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and diluting media, but a 1-to-4 mixture of compost to diluting media worked best.

A 1-to-4 mixture of yard-clipping compost to vermiculite produced an average of 30 spores per cubic centimeter. Additional propagules come from colonized roots and vesicles within them.

### Rodale Recipe

The Rodale recipe produces 16 bags of inoculum—enough for 200 to 400 cubic feet of inoculated potting media, depending on dilution ratio (1:9 or 1:19). The process takes four months starting in early spring. Host plants are frost-killed and fungal propagules overwinter in the mix.

Materials needed:

- Bahiagrass (*Paspalum notatum*) seed

- Field soil or commercial propagule mix

- Vermiculite

- Conical plastic pots

- Coarse-grained sand (swimming pool filter type)

- Ground cover fabric

- Black 7-gallon grow bags

- Compost

Steps:

1. Four months before the predicted last frost, germinate bahiagrass in vermiculite.

2. Three months before the predicted last frost, transplant seedlings into conical pots filled with a 1-to-3 mixture of soil to sand.

3. After the last frost, set up the inoculum production area with ground cover fabric and grow bags. Fill bags three-quarters full with a 1-to-4 compost-to-vermiculite mixture. Transplant five seedlings into each bag.

4. During the growing season, weed and water as needed. In winter, frost kills the bahiagrass and mycorrhizae overwinter naturally in the bags.

5. The following spring, harvest inoculum. Remove dead plant material, shake growing media from root balls into a bin. Cut roots into segments less than ½ inch. Mix into potting media at 1:9 for flats or 1:19 for larger pots.

This method can be adapted using smaller bags and different host plants. Rye, fescue, corn, and other grasses will support mycorrhizae for spore and propagule collection.

## Greenhouse Recipe

The greenhouse method uses sterile conditions and commercial inoculum to produce arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in pots.

Materials:

- Plastic pots

- Host plant seeds

- Paper towels and plastic bags

- Sterile growing media

- Commercial inoculum

- Low-phosphorus fertilizer

Steps:

1. Sterilize pots in a dishwasher with bleach-based detergent, or soak in 1 part bleach to 9 parts water for at least ten minutes, then wash and rinse.

2. Soak seeds for five minutes in 1:9 bleach solution (5% available chlorine) or in 3% hydrogen peroxide heated to 140°F (60°C), then cooled. Rinse with sterilized water.

3. Place seeds between damp paper towels in a plastic bag until germination.

4. Plant seeds in 6- to 10-inch sterile pots using pathogen-free media such as 1:3 peat-to-vermiculite, rockwool, coarse sand, or sterilized soil. The media must not contain phosphorus.

5. Mix commercial inoculum into the media at 1 part inoculum to 20 parts growing media. Roll seeds in the mix or sprinkle over them. Plant three to six seeds per pot.

6. Feed host plants periodically with low-phosphorus fertilizer (P number of 6 or less). Examples include alfalfa meal (2-1-3), fish emulsion (5-2-2), and soybean meal (7-2-1). Compost teas and diluted mixes also work.

7. After about six weeks, mycorrhizae begin to form. Root samples can be tested for colonization rates.

8. After about 14 weeks, stop watering to let media dry out.

9. At around week 16, gently pull plants, shake off media containing spores and fungal fragments, and store in a cool dry location. Cut roots into small pieces containing vesicles and hyphae. These propagules remain viable for about a year.

## Pot Culturing

Inoculant can be produced in greenhouse conditions using various host plants. Nursery growers and farmers have had success using corn, onion, strawberry, peanut, sorghum, and big bluestem as hosts.

Warm-weather plants can also serve as host plants, including pencilflower (*Stylosanthes* spp.), bahiagrass, and kudzu (*Pueraria phaseoloides*).

## Field Culturing

To grow mycorrhizal inoculum using field methods, start with any commercial soil mix that does not include added fertilizers or manures. Sterilize the mix in an autoclave or oven at 260°F (121°C) for about 90 minutes.

After a week or two, mix 1 part field-collected soil with 3 parts sterilized soil, or use commercially available fungal mixes instead of field soil to prevent pathogens.

The best collection site is a field currently or previously in production that offers soil rich in organic material. The better the crop grown in that soil, the more likely crops inoculated with the propagule mix will thrive. Higher diversity of mycorrhizae in the field soil is better. Field soil can be tested for propagules before use.

A strawberry plant method also works: transplant a strawberry from the field to a pot with sterile soil, or establish a runner in potted field soil then transplant to sterile soil. Nurture plants until mature, then remove plants and roots, save soil clinging to roots, and chop up roots for inoculum.

## Field Culture vs. Pot Culture

Comparisons have demonstrated that pot culturing arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi is usually far more successful than field culturing. The overcrowding of fungal hyphae in the pot encourages and increases spore production. As fungi and roots grow to fill the pot, the fungi can no longer expand by adding hyphal branches but are still receiving carbon, so they channel that energy into sporulation.

## Production of Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

[[ectomycorrhizal-fungal-ecology]] species are easy to produce provided you have an appropriate host plant. Studies have shown that soil containing ectomycorrhizal fungi from one location can successfully associate with the same host tree species at another location. Conifers and deciduous trees respond equally to these treatments.

Methods for producing ectomycorrhizal fungi:

- Collect fruiting bodies (mushrooms) of ectomycorrhizal fungi to use as propagules. Sporocarps are loaded with spores. Experienced mushroom hunters can make spore prints by removing the stem and placing the cap spore-side down on paper or glass for 24 hours.

- Add propagules directly adjacent to plant roots or spray seeds with spore suspensions.

- For home gardeners, the easiest method is to cut fruiting bodies into small pieces and mix them into potting soil. A few spores are enough to inoculate, and it is impossible to use too much.

## Staining for Verification

Viewing arbuscular mycorrhizae requires a dissecting microscope because the fungi are mostly transparent and obscured by dark plant root pigments. These pigments must be removed and a stain applied to reveal the fungi.

The standard laboratory procedure involves washing freshly collected roots, bathing them in a hot 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution to clear plant parts, acidifying with hydrochloric acid (HCl), and then staining. Traditional stains like trypan blue are highly alkaline and dangerous for untrained users.

A safer alternative method:

1. Soak root samples in water for at least 24 hours.

2. Soak in a 1:1 mixture of hydrogen peroxide to water.

3. Use diluted KOH at 2.5% (still hazardous—follow safety guidelines).

4. Stain with an India ink and vinegar mix.
