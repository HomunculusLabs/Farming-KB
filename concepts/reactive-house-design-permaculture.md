# Reactive House Design in Permaculture

updated: 2026-04-18

Permaculture Two (1979) Chapter 6 establishes the "reactive house" concept: buildings that supply their own heat and at least some food through passive design, biological aids, and integration with the surrounding environment.

## The Core Principle

The whole thrust of reactive house design is to reduce or eliminate the need for external energy input for climate control. Because sun heat is regulated and stored in the heat masses of floors, walls, and water tanks, and draughts are excluded, the very slight heat yield from body warmth, cooking, and perhaps a small pot-bellied stove is all that is needed.

Buildings are at the core of the [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]] system. Whether built for people or livestock, new buildings must be constructed to supply their own heat and at least some food. Most domestic energy is consumed in buildings; they are where we survive extremes of heat and cold, and where we supervise and plan life-support systems.

## Essentials of a Reactive House

- Sheltered from cold winds via designed windbreak planting
- Oriented on an east-west axis, facing the sun (attached glasshouse feasible)
- No windows or very small fixed windows in E and W walls (available for vine crop insulation, trellis, shrubbery)
- Few windows and doors in S wall (shadehouse attachment facilitated)
- Every opening very well sealed for draughts (only essential vents in toilet and bathroom)
- In hot summer areas, N aspect shaded by deciduous trees or vine crop
- Careful draught-proofing and reduced ventilation
- Insulation of ceilings; vines or trellis along E and W walls
- Heat mass as concrete slabs, tanks, and brick or stonework within glasshouse or insulated warm rooms

## Attached Glasshouse Design

Key features for an attached solar glasshouse:

- Oriented to within 60 degrees of due north (towards open mid sky)
- E and W walls insulated, solid construction
- Base insulated, especially around foundations
- Wooden frames (metal frames lose heat too quickly)
- Single glass panels are most durable and efficient
- Pit below grade as base reduces heat loss to outside ground
- Well-sealed top vent essential
- Water in small containers as best heat store; pools below benches
- Glass at about 45 degrees to ground for greatest efficiency
- Plain white paint on S wall reflects heat efficiently

## Ventilation System

Summer: Open upper vent at top of glasshouse. Air escapes, drawing cool air from lower vents over damp mulch and through vine-covered, ferny shadehouse where fine spray keeps air cool.

Winter: Close lower shadehouse vents and upper glasshouse vent. Open internal vents so warm air from glasshouse circulates in insulated rooms. Close at evening to trap warm air.

Both shadehouse (for small fruit and brassicas) and glasshouse (for spices and tropicals) yield food while cutting fuel costs.

## Solar Pond Heating

Solar ponds can be filled either outside or inside the glasshouse for hot water provision. Heated wastewater from sinks, showers, and baths can be released into a cooling-off tank inside the glasshouse, adding both heat and humidity.

## Wind Protection Structures

Structures breasting wind should be like a vessel breasting the sea: either "easy" in entry like a sharp-bowed boat, or permeable like a raft, or both. U-shaped suntraps should be curved and permeable to cold winds, facing north. Orientation is adjustable:

- Higher/longer wing toward strong prevailing winds (e.g., salty easterlies)
- Swing toward NE if mornings sunny and evenings cloudy
- On very hostile sites, a whole series of interlinked sun traps may be the only sensible approach

Wind-protection nets: Corner junctions give "squarer" fields than centre junctions. Sun screens may be curved and tapered to admit low and early light.

## Biological Insulation

Stephen Lesiuk (May 1978) measured heat gain/loss over bare and vine-covered brick walls in spring:

- Ivy on brick walls suppressed about 70% of summer heat excess
- Prevented about 30% of heat escape from the house at night

Dozens of brick-cavity buildings could benefit from this simple biological insulation. The loss of warmth and cool air in buildings is most affected by winds passing along walls. Still air or water is the best insulation.

## Fuel Conservation Through Food Choice

A diverse garden provides foods that do not need cooking: nuts, fruits, greens, and many root crops need no cooking and are of equal or superior food value. The old emphasis on grains and pulses created a demand for fuel that countries like India cannot afford. Strategies include cooking in one pot, foods needing only reheating, and cooking for larger community.

## Water Tank Integration

Water tanks, often regarded as old-fashioned, can be vine-covered in the shadehouse as a cool air/water block. In one Perth case, residents petitioned to have a farmer's tank removed as "unsightly" -- they lost; the farmer kept his tank while they endured water restrictions and salted soil.
