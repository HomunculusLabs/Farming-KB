# Straw Substrate Boil-a-Bag Cultivation Method

The boil-a-bag cultivation method, developed by D.H. Jarrold, is a technique
for growing mushrooms indoors using pasteurized straw as the primary
substrate, contained within heat-resistant plastic bags. The method is notable
for its use of a vermiculite filter can system for gas exchange during
colonization, two distinct inoculation approaches based on needle length, and
a simple aquarium-based humidity chamber for fruiting. It represents a
low-cost alternative to jar-based cultivation methods like the [[pf-tek]] for
growers working with straw substrates rather than grain-based formulations.
The entire cycle from inoculation to harvest is self-contained, with spore
printing at the end closing the loop for subsequent grows.

## Boil-a-Bag Containers

The technique requires plastic bags capable of withstanding pressure cooking
or microwaving without melting. Jarrold specified bags made by Lakeland
Plastics measuring 8 by 12 inches (20 by 30 cm), sold in packs of
approximately 50. The bags must be large enough to hold a packed straw
substrate mass while leaving room for the filter can assembly at the top. Any
heat-stable plastic bag of similar dimensions that can tolerate 15 psi
pressure for 60 minutes is suitable. The low cost of the bags makes this
method economically attractive for cultivators producing multiple grows
simultaneously.

## Essential Equipment

The method requires a specific set of tools and materials that can be
assembled from common household and hardware sources:

**Sterilization and inoculation tools:** A pressure cooker capable of
maintaining 15 psi, a spirit burner for flame sterilization, propyl alcohol
for surface disinfection, and a glass Pyrex container for sterile water
preparation. Metal tweezers, scissors, and a flat-edged metal scraper are
needed for handling spores and mushroom tissue during printing and transfer.

**Syringe and needle:** A standard syringe with either a standard 4 cm needle
or a custom long needle made from thin stainless steel tubing. The long needle
is fabricated by removing the original needle from its plastic holder using
grips and replacing it with stainless steel tubing obtainable from model shops
or engineering suppliers. Heating the tube tip before insertion into the
holder makes assembly easier.

**Spore print jar:** A small clean glass jar with a metal screw lid for
collecting spore prints. Jars are sterilized in an oven before use by placing
lids and jars separately on a metal baking tray under the grill at half
maximum heat for 30 minutes.

**Humidity chamber:** A 4 by 2 foot aquarium serves as the fruiting chamber.
Soaked hydro pellets (expanded clay aggregate) are placed on the bottom to
maintain humidity. A plastic sheet seals the top to retain moisture and
exclude flies. Fresh air exchange is provided by fanning the chamber twice
daily.

## Filter Can Construction

The filter can is a critical innovation that allows gas exchange during
mycelial colonization while preventing contamination. It is constructed from a
standard 3 to 4 inch round food can prepared through several steps:

**Can preparation:** The can is opened leaving the lid attached by
approximately one inch of metal, creating a hinged lid that can be opened and
closed repeatedly. Multiple holes are punched through the bottom of the can
using a hammer and nail, creating a network of breath holes for gas exchange
between the substrate and the external environment.

**Filtration layers:** The bottom is lined with approximately 1 cm of rock
wool or ordinary loft insulation fiberglass. The can is then filled with
[[vermiculite-cannabis]] to within 1 cm of the top. Alternatively, small pieces of rock
wool can be used instead of vermiculite as the primary filter medium. A final
thin layer of rock wool is placed on top to hold the vermiculite in place and
prevent it from spilling during handling.

**Assembly with bag:** The filter can sits inside the open neck of the bag,
secured with masking tape wrapped three times around the bag neck and pressed
firmly onto both the can and bag surfaces. This creates an airtight seal
around the can while allowing gas exchange through the filter medium. The can
can be opened for long-needle inoculation and then closed again, or left in
place throughout colonization.

## Straw Substrate Preparation

### Chopping and Soaking

Straw is chopped to lengths of 3 to 10 cm. Pre-cut straw is available from
many pet stores as animal bedding, which saves preparation time and ensures
consistent piece size. The chopped straw is placed in a large watertight
container for hydration and supplementation.

A heaped tablespoon of Marmite, Vegemite, or yeast extract is dissolved in
boiling water and poured over the straw along with approximately 6 liters of
additional boiling water. The yeast extract serves as a nutrient supplement
intended to accelerate spore germination and mycelial growth. The straw is
weighed down with a second container filled with water to keep it fully
submerged, and soaked for approximately one hour with periodic checking to
ensure the water level remains above the straw surface.

### Draining and Supplementation

After soaking, the straw is turned out into a bathtub and left for one hour to
drain thoroughly. If the straw is still too wet after draining, excess water
is squeezed out by hand until the straw reaches the correct moisture content,
which Jarrold describes as having surplus water removable by hand squeezing.

A few handfuls of brown rice flour are sprinkled over the straw as additional
food for the mycelium and mixed in thoroughly by hand. For six bags worth of
substrate, approximately three to four handfuls of brown rice flour is
recommended. The brown rice flour supplementation parallels the role of brown
rice powder in the
[[pf-tek-substrate-jar-preparation-and-steam-sterilization]], providing
readily available carbohydrates that support rapid mycelial colonization.

## Bag Filling and Sterilization

The straw mixture is firmly packed into the bags until almost full, leaving
enough room to insert the filter can into the top of the bag. Firm packing
eliminates large air pockets that could harbor contaminants and ensures good
contact between the substrate and the incoming mycelium.

The filter can is fitted into the bag neck and secured with wide masking tape
wrapped three times around and pressed firmly onto both the can and bag
surfaces. Two pieces of support tape are applied from the can to the bag base
on each side to stabilize the assembly during handling and inoculation.

Sterilization is performed in a pressure cooker at 15 psi for 60 minutes.
Jarrold recommended processing two bags at a time depending on cooker size,
and suggested splitting the 60 minutes into two 30-minute stages if preferred.
The pressure sterilization, rather than simple pasteurization, is necessary
because the brown rice flour supplement creates a nutrient-rich environment
that is more vulnerable to bacterial contamination than plain straw alone.
