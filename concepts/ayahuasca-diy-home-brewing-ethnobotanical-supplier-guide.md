# Ayahuasca DIY Home-Brewing: Ethnobotanical Supplier Guide

## Overview

R. Stuart's 2002 reporting on [[ayahuasca-tourism]] recommends home-brewing
using ethnobotanical supplier ingredients combined with an experienced
sitter as a practical alternative to commercial ceremony tourism. This
recommendation emerges from a critical assessment of the industry's
exploitation, variable quality, and cost — but raises serious questions
about safety, legality, [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]].

## The Source Recommendation

Stuart's recommendation follows a systematic critique of commercial
ayahuasca tourism: practitioners range from honorable healers to
exploitative scammers; pricing is inconsistent and often exploitative
(15x markup for tourists); commercial ceremonies may use milder doses
and err on caution; linguistic and cultural barriers limit depth; and
[[ayahuasca-tourism-medical-risks-prescription-interactions]] are poorly managed in remote settings. Home-brewing offers
control, cost-effectiveness, and a familiar, safe environment.

## Core Ingredients

Traditional ayahuasca requires two primary plant components:

### Banisteriopsis caapi (Ayahuasca Vine)

The vine provides beta-carboline alkaloids — harmine, harmaline, and
tetrahydroharmine (THH) — which function as monoamine oxidase
inhibitors (MAOIs). These compounds inhibit the body's breakdown of DMT
in the gut, making orally administered DMT psychoactive. Without the
MAOI component, consuming DMT orally produces no effect. The vine is
typically the bulk ingredient, contributing most of the liquid volume
and the characteristic bitter flavor.

### Psychotria viridis (Chacruna) or Diplopterys cabrerana (Chaliponga)

These leafy plants provide N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), the primary
psychedelic compound. Psychotria viridis is most common in Peruvian
preparations. Diplopterys cabrerana is used more in Ecuadorian and
Colombian traditions and contains higher DMT concentrations along with
additional alkaloids.

## Ethnobotanical Suppliers

Both ingredients and many traditional admixture plants are available
from ethnobotanical suppliers — businesses specializing in psychoactive
and medicinal plant materials. These operate in a legal gray area.

Key sourcing considerations:

- **Species identification**: Verify correct botanical species.
  [[banisteriopsis-caapi]] has several varieties ("Red," "Yellow," "Black")
  with differing alkaloid profiles. Psychotria viridis must be
  correctly identified among many Psychotria species.
- **Material quality**: Dried vine should be whole or coarsely cut,
  not powdered. Leaves should be green to dark green, not brown.
- **Sustainability**: Ayahuasca vine takes years to mature. Reputable
  suppliers disclose cultivation versus wild harvesting practices.
- **[[ayahuasca-tourism-legal-status-by-country]]**: DMT is a Schedule I substance in the US, but the
  plants containing it occupy a complex legal gray area. Purchasers
  assume legal responsibility.

## Preparation Basics

[[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] involves boiling plant materials in water for
extended periods (6–24 hours), with periodic water additions. The
process repeats multiple times (typically 3+ pulls), and liquids are
combined and reduced to drinkable concentration.
