# Lichen Water Relations

[[lichens]] are symbiotic associations between a fungal partner (mycobiont) and a
photosynthetic partner (photobiont), typically a green alga or cyanobacterium.
As poikilohydric organisms, lichens lack internal mechanisms for controlling
water content and instead equilibrate their water status with the surrounding
environment. This fundamental characteristic shapes their physiology, ecology,
distribution, and survival strategies. Understanding lichen water relations is
essential for comprehending their role in nutrient cycling, gadd fungal    18, and adaptation to extreme environments.

## Poikilohydry and Water Sources

Lichens are poikilohydrous: they have no ability to control their internal
water content. Water can be taken up from atmospheric water vapour, rain,
dew, and by capillarity when lichens are in contact with moist substrates.
Their water content varies passively with the water potential of the
environment. This contrasts sharply with homoiohydrous vascular plants, which
maintain relatively constant tissue water potentials through stomatal control
and root-mediated water uptake.

The poikilohydric nature of lichens means that their metabolic activity is
intermittent and closely coupled to periods of favourable water status. During
dry periods, lichens enter a state of desiccation-induced dormancy, suspending
photosynthesis and respiration. Upon rehydration, metabolic activity resumes
rapidly, though full recovery may require time depending on the severity and
duration of desiccation.

## Water Content and Water Potential

Lichen water content is expressed as percentage dry weight. Maximum water
content varies greatly among species, from approximately 100% in some
leprose lichens to over 1000% in some gelatinous and foliose species. At
saturation, lichens may hold 2 to 10 times their dry weight in water. The
lichen thallus acts as a sponge, with the fungal medulla and photobiont
layer serving as the primary water-holding tissues.

Water potential describes the energy status of water in a system. Fully
hydrated lichens typically have water potentials between 0 and -0.5 MPa.
As lichens dry, their water potential declines, eventually reaching
equilibrium with the dry atmosphere. Below approximately -10 MPa, most
metabolic activity ceases. The relationship between water content and water
potential is complex and varies with lichen morphology and surface
characteristics.

## Vapour Uptake and Hygroscopicity

Many lichens can absorb water vapour directly from the atmosphere, a capacity
that depends on their thallus morphology and the hygroscopic properties of
cell walls and extracellular polysaccharides. Vapour uptake is particularly
important for lichens in arid and semi-arid environments, where liquid water
is episodic. Species with well-developed cortical layers may have reduced
vapour exchange, while those with exposed photobiont layers can take up water
more readily.

The minimum relative humidity at which a lichen can absorb sufficient water
for photosynthesis varies among species. Some crustose lichens in deserts can
photosynthesize at relative humidities as low as 60%, while other species
require liquid water. This variability in water-uptake capacity is a major
factor determining lichen distribution patterns across moisture gradients.

## Effects of Thallus Morphology

Thallus morphology profoundly influences water relations. Foliose and
fruticose lichens, with their loosely organized internal structure and
relatively large surface area, can absorb and lose water rapidly. Crustose
lichens, closely appressed to their substrate, tend to have slower water
exchange but may retain water for longer periods.

The presence of specialized structures such as rhizines, cilia, and
cyphellae can enhance water uptake and retention. Gelatinous lichens,
containing cyanobacteria as the photobiont, can swell dramatically when
wetted due to the water-absorbing properties of the cyanobacterial sheath
polysaccharides.

## Photosynthetic Response to Rehydration

Upon rehydration, lichen photosynthesis resumes within minutes. However, the
pattern of photosynthetic recovery varies with the type of water supplied.
When wetted with liquid water, photosynthetic CO2 exchange typically shows
an initial burst of CO2 release (respiratory burst), followed by a decline
in respiration and eventual positive net photosynthesis. When hydrated by
water vapour alone, the respiratory burst may be absent and photosynthesis
may commence more rapidly.

The initial respiratory burst upon liquid-water rehydration is attributed to
the rapid activation of mitochondrial respiration and the consumption of
carbohydrate reserves accumulated during previous photosynthetic periods.
The intensity and duration of this burst increase with the duration of
preceding desiccation.

## Desiccation Tolerance

Lichens are among the most desiccation-tolerant organisms known. They can
survive repeated cycles of drying and rehydration without apparent damage.
Tolerance mechanisms include the accumulation of compatible solutes
(sugar alcohols such as ribitol, arabitol, and erythritol), the
stabilization of membranes and proteins by trehalose, and the capacity
for rapid repair of cellular damage upon rehydration.

The photobiont partner is generally more sensitive to desiccation than the
mycobiont. Fungal mechanisms for protecting the photobiont include the
production of cortical pigments that screen excess radiation during periods
when the photobiont cannot dissipate energy through photosynthetic pathways.

## Gas Exchange and Diffusion Limitation

In lichens, CO2 and water vapour diffuse through the same pathway. When the
thallus is saturated with water, the diffusion pathway for CO2 is essentially
blocked by a film of water, and photosynthesis may become severely limited.
This phenomenon explains the depression of net photosynthesis at high thallus
water contents. Optimal photosynthetic rates typically occur at intermediate
water contents where CO2 diffusion is not inhibited.

This diffusion limitation creates a fundamental trade-off: sufficient water
is needed for metabolic activity, but excess water restricts CO2 supply to
the photobiont. Lichens have evolved various structural adaptations to
mitigate this conflict, including the development of porous cortical layers
and anatomical features that promote water loss and gas exchange.

## Ecological Implications

Water relations strongly influence lichen distribution, productivity, and
competitive interactions. In humid environments, lichens face competition
from faster-growing vascular plants. Their poikilohydric strategy, while
effective for survival, limits sustained productivity compared with
homoiohydrous organisms. In arid and extreme environments such as polar
regions and deserts, lichens become dominant primary producers because of
their superior stress tolerance.

Lichens contribute significantly to ecosystem processes including nitrogen
fixation (when cyanobacterial photobionts are present), carbon fixation,
rock weathering, and soil formation. Their ability to resume metabolic
activity rapidly after brief periods of hydration makes them effective
exploiters of ephemeral moisture in otherwise dry environments.
