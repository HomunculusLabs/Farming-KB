# Three Sisters Guild

The gaias garden three sisters guild is one of the oldest and most well-known examples of [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]], originating from Native American agriculture. Familiar to many gardeners, it consists of the triad of corn, beans, and squash -- a combination that illustrates essential [[gaias-garden-ecology-for-gardeners]] of mutual support and interconnected plant communities. The Three Sisters demonstrates how [[polyculture-design-hemenway]] can outperform monoculture in yield, resilience, and resource efficiency.

## How the Three Sisters Work Together

Each of the three plants supports and benefits the others in specific, measurable ways:

- **Corn** (Zea mays) forms a living trellis for the bean vines to climb, eliminating the need for external supports. Corn roots secrete special sugars that feed the nitrogen-fixing bacteria living on bean roots, creating a direct nutrient exchange between the two species. The tall corn stalks also provide a windbreak that protects the shorter squash from damage.
- **Beans** (Phaseolus vulgaris) draw nitrogen from the air and, via symbiotic bacteria (Rhizobium species), convert it to plant-available form, fertilizing both the corn and the squash. The bacterial nodules on bean roots are fed by sugars exuded from corn roots, creating a two-way exchange. Beans also help stabilize the corn against wind through their climbing habit.
- **Squash** (Cucurbita spp.) ramble across the ground with broad leaves that form a living parasol, densely covering the soil to inhibit weeds and keep the soil cool and moist. The large squash leaves create a [[gaias-garden-net-and-pan-water-harvesting]] of higher humidity and lower soil temperature that benefits all three plants. The prickly squash vines also deter raccoons and other pests from approaching the corn.

## Yield Advantages

Together, the Three Sisters produce more food, with less water and fertilizer, than a similar area planted to any one of these three crops in isolation. Research by agronomist Jane Mt. Pleasant at Cornell University, who blended her Iroquois heritage with her research, has shown that yields of this polyculture, measured in calories, are about 20 percent higher than corn grown alone in an equal-sized plot. This calorie advantage comes from the complementary nutritional profiles: corn provides carbohydrates, beans supply protein, and squash adds vitamins and oils.

## The Fourth Sister

In the Southwest, a fourth "sister" is traditionally found in the guild: Rocky Mountain bee plant (Cleome serrulata). Often growing near former Anasazi settlements, it is virtually an indicator plant for ancient ruins. This 2- to 5-foot-tall, pink-flowered annual is a powerful attractant for beneficial insects that pollinate beans and squash. The young leaves, flowers, and seedpods are edible, and native people boiled and ate them or made a paste for later use. Bee plant also accumulates iron and is the source of a deep-hued paint used to create the characteristic black designs on Anasazi pottery. Songs and blessings of New Mexico's Tewa people mention corn, beans, squash, and bee plant, indicating this multifunctional flower is an integral member of a sacred plant pantheon.

By extending the Three Sisters with bee plant, the guild's connections strengthen further, drawing insects into the network. This moves the guild into three kingdoms: animal, plant, and bacterial -- creating connectedness that draws upon billions of years of life's wisdom.

## Interconnections

The Three Sisters guild weaves at least eight connections among three plants: beans furnish nitrogen for all three; squash shades soil for all three; corn feeds the bacteria and creates a trellis for beans. The lesson is that by hooking into the cyclical rhythms of many-kingdomed nature, a guild can capitalize on enormous sources of energy and experience. Focusing only on food plants limits this potential; including [[gaias-garden-living-plant-systems-insectary]], [[gaias-garden-beneficial-birds-wildlife]], and soil-building [[hemenway-cover-crops-guide]] dramatically increases the web of beneficial interactions.

## How to Grow the Three Sisters Guild

1. Mark out planting holes about 3 feet apart (figure about four or five ears of corn per hole)
2. Poke three or four kernels of corn into each hole, cover with an inch of soil. Many-stalked varieties work best (Black Aztec, Hopi White, or Tarahumara sweet corn were developed specifically for guild growing)
3. When corn sprouts, mound soil up around the young stalks to warm them and improve drainage. Do not thin -- you want two or three stalks per mound, hence the greater-than-usual distance between mounds
4. About two weeks after planting corn, plant pole beans (not bush varieties). Coat seeds with legume inoculant specific for beans to ensure nitrogen-fixing bacteria colonize the roots. Plant two or three bean seeds per mound
5. At the same time, plant squash or pumpkins between each mound. Use a vining variety, not zucchini, so the squash will sprawl over the soil
6. After harvest, leave stalks, vines, and debris on the ground to [[gaias-garden-bringing-the-soil-to-life]], returning extracted fertility to the soil and protecting the ground from erosion

## Variations and Adaptations

The basic Three Sisters pattern can be adapted to many climates and conditions:

- In shorter growing seasons, start corn indoors or use faster-maturing varieties
- Replace corn with sunflowers in some designs; sunflowers also serve as trellises and their seeds feed birds
- Substitute runner beans (Phaseolus coccineus) in cooler climates, as they tolerate more shade
- Add [[comparison-perennial-vegetables-vs-cover-crops]] like groundnuts (Apios americana) at the base of the corn for additional root-crop yield
- Include [[gaias-garden-living-plant-systems-insectary]] like calendula or borage at the edges for additional pollinator attraction

## Design Principles Illustrated

The Three Sisters demonstrates several core [[gaias-garden-white-oak-hazelnut-guild-design]] principles:

- **[[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]]**: Each plant performs multiple roles -- structural support, nutrient cycling, pest deterrence, soil protection, and food production
- **[[gaias-garden-interplanting-polyculture]]**: Multiple species share the same space productively without competing for the same resources
- **Nutrient cycling**: Beans fix atmospheric nitrogen; squash mulches the soil with its leaves; corn provides structure and root exudates
- **Cross-kingdom connections**: Plant, animal, and bacterial realms are linked into a cooperative network
- **[[gaias-garden-mature-garden-creation]]**: The combined canopy creates a distinct microclimate that benefits all members

## Historical and Cultural Significance

The Three Sisters is not merely a gardening technique but a cultural institution among many Native American peoples. The Iroquois consider corn, beans, and squash to be three sisters who can only thrive together, and their creation story features the Three Sisters as gifts from the Creator. Traditional planting ceremonies and harvest festivals celebrate the guild. The Hidatsa, Mandan, and Arikara peoples of the Upper Missouri River developed sophisticated variations that included sunflowers and other species. This cultural depth reflects the deep ecological understanding that the Three Sisters embodies -- an understanding that [[polyculture-design-hemenway]] brings to bear on all of his guild designs. Modern scientific research has validated what indigenous peoples knew for millennia: polycultures outperform monocultures in total productivity and resilience.

## Nutritional Synergy

Beyond yield advantages, the Three Sisters provide complementary nutrition:

- **Corn** supplies carbohydrates and calories in abundance, forming the energy base of the diet
- **Beans** provide complete protein, supplementing corn's protein with essential amino acids (notably lysine and tryptophan) that corn lacks. The combination of corn and beans creates a complete protein profile comparable to meat.
- **Squash** adds vitamins A and C, minerals, and healthy oils from its seeds. Squash seeds are rich in zinc, magnesium, and omega-3 fatty acids.

This nutritional synergy mirrors the ecological synergy between the plants -- each fills a gap left by the others, creating a complete system greater than the sum of its parts.

## Soil Health Benefits

The Three Sisters guild builds soil health over time in ways that monoculture cannot:

- Bean root nodules add fixed nitrogen to the soil, reducing or eliminating the need for external fertilizer
- Corn's extensive root system aerates the soil and creates channels for water infiltration
- Squash's dense leaf cover prevents soil erosion from rain and wind
- The diverse root architectures of three different species explore different soil depths and zones, accessing nutrients that a single crop would miss
- The mulch from squash leaves and corn stalks, left in place after harvest, feeds soil organisms and builds organic matter

## From Three Sisters to Super-Guilds

The Three Sisters is an entry point into guild thinking. Once gardeners understand how three plants can support each other through complementary functions, they can begin designing more complex guilds like the [[apple-centered-guild-design]] and eventually [[gaias-garden-super-guild-design]] that connect multiple tree-based guilds into landscape-scale communities. The principles are the same at every scale: identify niches, stack functions, and create beneficial connections between species.
