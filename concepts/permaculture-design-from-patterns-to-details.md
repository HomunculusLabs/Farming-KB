# Design from Patterns to Details

## Permaculture Design Principle #7 — "Can't See the Wood for the Trees"

[[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]], we must first understand the
broad patterns — of landscape, climate, culture, and energy flow — before filling
in specific details of plantings, structures, and management.

## The Hierarchy of Systems

Holmgren draws on H.T. Odum's concept of systems hierarchy: larger, slower systems
set the context for smaller, faster ones. The climate sets the context for the
bioregion, the bioregion for the watershed, the watershed for the local landscape,
the landscape for the individual site, and the site for specific elements.

Understanding this hierarchy prevents the common error of designing details without
regard to their context. A beautiful garden that ignores watershed dynamics, solar
access, or local wind patterns will fail despite its detailed perfection.

## Zone and Sector Analysis

The most widely applied pattern-based design tool in permaculture is zone and sector
analysis. Zones describe concentric areas of decreasing intensity of management
around a central point (usually the home):

- **Zone 0**: The home itself — intensive management, highest frequency of visits
- **Zone 1**: [[permaculture-designers-manual-wildlife-corridors]], noise, and views. Design
works to channel beneficial energies (winter sun, summer breezes) and block or
deflect harmful ones (cold winds, fire, flood).

## The Dendritic Pattern of Catchments

One of nature's most fundamental patterns is the dendritic (branching) form of
catchments and drainage systems. From the smallest rill to the largest river
basin, water organizes itself in branching patterns that maximize the collection
and distribution of water across landscapes.

Holmgren shows how this pattern can inform road placement, swale design, and the
layout of paths and access ways. Roads that follow ridge lines minimize disruption
of water flow. Swales placed along contours follow the natural pattern of water
collection and distribution.

## Patterns of Vegetation and Soil Fertility

In temperate mid-rainfall regions, there is a clear gradient from low-fertility
grasslands to high-fertility forests, reflected in canopy height, species
composition, and characteristic herbivores. This gradient is driven by soil
fertility, rainfall, and fire frequency.

Understanding these patterns allows designers to work with natural succession
rather than against it. Where soil is poor, grassland systems with appropriate
grazing may be more productive than forcing tree establishment. Where fertility
is high, forest systems naturally emerge and should be encouraged.

## Scale in Human Settlements

Holmgren extends the patterns principle to human [[holmgren-optimum-scale-speed-balance-industrial-post-industrial]]. Small-scale [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] reaches a productivity peak
that cannot be exceeded without mechanization. But mechanization brings its own
costs and a new development hill must be climbed to reach the next optimum.

## Melliodora as Pattern Example

Holmgren uses his own property, Melliodora, to illustrate pattern-based design.
The site uses slope for gravity-fed water systems, places the most intensive
elements (garden, poultry) closest to the house, and arranges tree systems to
moderate climate and cycle nutrients. The house itself is designed as a passive
solar system, working with the pattern of seasonal sun angles.
