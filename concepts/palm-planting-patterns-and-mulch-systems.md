# Palm Planting Patterns and Mulch Systems

The design of palm polycultures—particularly coconut-based systems—is a central topic in tropical [[permaculture]]. The choice between clump and grid planting, combined with strategic mulch provision and earth shaping, determines both yield and the amount of ground area available for intercropping, livestock, and water harvesting.

## Village Zonation Around Palm Polyculture

Mollison outlines an appropriate village zonation model (Figure 10.32) that arranges land uses in concentric or sequential zones around the village centre:

- **Zone A**: Livestock housing and yards
- **Zone B**: Palm polyculture (coconuts with mixed understory)
- **Zone C**: Forage production for larger animals
- **Zone D**: Padi (rice) crop
- **Zone E**: Header dam and aquaculture
- **Zone F**: Commercial crops within or alongside palm
- **Zone G1–G3**: Progressive reduction in species diversity and increase in wild gathering—fuelwoods and native trees

This zonation recognises that palms form the backbone of a productive village landscape, with diminishing management intensity as distance from the settlement increases.

## Land Patterning for Maximum Water Absorption

Before planting palms and trees, the land should be patterned to maximise water absorption (Figure 10.33). Cross-slope hollows cut into the hillside hold mulch and capture runoff. Broad swales serve the dual purpose of water harvesting and providing access routes at harvest time. Trees in these patterned landscapes benefit from the improved groundwater reserves that develop over time.

A hillside patterned with cross-slope hollows and swales is particularly well suited to clump plantings of palms, as the circular planting geometry aligns naturally with the circular [[swale]] form.

## Clump vs Grid Planting

### The Linear (Grid) Pattern

Conventional coconut plantations plant trees on a square or triangular grid—typically 6 × 6 m on new sites. This is the "normal" layout (Figure 10.34A–B), but it has significant drawbacks:

- Nuts fall unpredictably and may be damaged by adjacent trees
- Mulching with fronds and husks is labour-intensive because material must be gathered from many scattered trees and redistributed
- Only about **30% of the ground area** is freed for intercrop or grazing
- Linear plantings cause mechanical damage to intercrop from falling fronds by years 2–4

### The Clump Pattern

Ten to twelve coconuts planted in a tight circle (each only a few feet apart) quickly adopt a **divergent growth habit** (Figure 10.35), with fronds spreading outward like spokes of a wheel. This pattern has multiple advantages:

- **Nut counts** compare favourably with square-grid plantings
- **Nuts drop cleanly** to the ground within the circle, simplifying harvest
- **Mulching is far easier**: fronds and husks are gathered and processed in one place, then applied to the base of all trees in the clump. Turning husks face-down prevents mosquito breeding in the mulch heap
- **Fertiliser application** (manure, blood and bone) is equally simple—applied to one concentrated zone
- **Trellis support**: clumps form more suitable structures for vanilla, black pepper, and other vine crops
- **Watering is economical**—irrigation targets a single concentrated root zone
- **Replanting** can proceed in discrete sets of 10–12 palms without gross linear disturbance to the whole system
- **Up to 60% of the ground area** is freed for intercrop and grazing, versus only 30% for linear planting

## Spacing Rules

A reliable rule for palm spacing is: **twice the frond length for that site, plus two feet**. This allows full crown development without abrasion damage to fronds from wind-sway. On new sites, coconuts are normally planted at **6 × 6 m**.

## Intercrop Spacing

Standard intercrop spacings within the freed ground between palm clumps:

| Crop | Spacing |
|------|---------|
| Cacao | 2 × 3 m |
| Banana | 3 × 3 m |
| Cassava | 1 × 1 m |
| Velvet beans | 0.5 × 0.5 m |
| Maize | 1 × 0.5 m (in rows) |
| Citrus | 9 × 9 m |

Local agricultural advisors should be consulted for site-specific adjustments.

## Access Roads and Grassed Ways

Sensible roading or grassed access ways are essential for gathering and handling heavy crop. Provision for these must be made even where horses or donkeys with panniers are used. Broad swales in the land patterning can double as harvest access routes, reducing the total area devoted to roads. Access design should also account for the transport of mulch materials—hedge foliage, intercrop leaf, and palm by-products—between mulch source zones and crop beds.

## Mulch Layout Systems

A carefully designed plantation layout (Figure 10.36) ensures mulch is both produced and distributed efficiently:

### Linear Plantation Mulch

Natural fall from palm fronds, husks, and nut shells will **line-mulch about 1 in every 8–12 rows** of palms, creating mulch beds approximately **2 m wide × 0.5 m high**.

### Clump Plantation Mulch

Ten to twelve palms in a clump will provide about **0.5 m deep** of mulch for the inner circle. This concentrated mulch is easier to gather and keep in place, especially in windy areas.

### Design Benefits of Mulch-Oriented Layout

- Reduces the labour of harvest by providing regular access
- Provides sources of mulch for short-term crops (dryland taro, ginger, yams)
- Enables mulch accumulation by long-term crop as interplant

## Bananas as Pioneer Mulch

The addition of bananas, especially with avocado, has become standard practice in many plantations. Banana plants at harvest (including root mass) provide approximately **25 tonnes per hectare of organic matter**—a key resource for healthy fruit and palm crops (Penn, J., *New Scientist*, 20 May 1985).

Bananas interplanted with legume crops may be regarded as **"pioneer" mulch** in grassland reclamation, establishing the organic matter base that subsequent perennial crops depend on.
