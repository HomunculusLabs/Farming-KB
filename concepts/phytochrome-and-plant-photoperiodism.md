# Phytochrome and Plant Photoperiodism

## Overview

Phytochrome is the primary photoreceptor protein responsible for mediating
plant responses to red and far-red light. Discovered through the study of
photoperiodism — the ability of plants to measure day/night length —
phytochrome functions as a molecular light-activated switch that controls
flowering, germination, [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]], and numerous other developmental
processes. [[daniel-chamovitz]], in *What a Plant Knows* (2012), traces the
discovery from [[chamovitz-maryland-mammoth-photoperiodism-discovery]] tobacco through to modern molecular
understanding.

## Discovery Through Photoperiodism

### Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

The story of phytochrome begins with a remarkable tobacco strain discovered
in southern Maryland around 1906. Maryland Mammoth tobacco could reach 4.5
metres in height and produce nearly 100 leaves, but rarely flowered. This
presented a serious problem for farmers who needed seed for the next crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of
Agriculture investigated. They planted Maryland Mammoth in two groups: one
left in the field, and another moved to a dark shed each afternoon. Simply
reducing the hours of light was sufficient to trigger flowering. This
established the concept of **photoperiodism** — plants measure the duration
of light to regulate their life cycle.

### Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Photoperiodism research revealed three categories:

- **Short-day plants**: Flower when nights exceed a critical length
  (e.g., chrysanthemums, soybeans, and critically, Cannabis)
- **Long-day plants**: Flower when nights are shorter than a critical
  length (e.g., irises, barley)
- **Day-neutral plants**: Flowering is independent of day length

### Night Length, Not Day Length

A crucial discovery was that plants measure the length of continuous
**darkness**, not the length of daylight. Scientists found that:

- A brief flash of light in the middle of a long night prevents short-day
  plants from flowering
- A brief flash of light in the middle of a short night triggers flowering
  in long-day plants
- This means the "dark period" is what plants actually measure

### Agricultural Application

Chrysanthemum farmers exploit this biology commercially. Chrysanthemums
normally flower in autumn (short days), but Mother's Day demand requires
spring flowering. By turning on greenhouse lights for a few minutes each
night through autumn and winter, flowering is suppressed. Two weeks before
Mother's Day, the lights are turned off, and all plants flower
simultaneously.

## The Red/Far-Red Reversible Switch

### Red Light Discovery

Scientists discovered that the colour of light used in night-break
experiments mattered enormously:

- **Red light** (~660 nm): Effective at interrupting the dark period
- **Blue or green light**: No effect on flowering
- This demonstrated that plants use red light specifically to measure
  night length

### Far-Red Reversal

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made the remarkable
discovery that **far-red light** (~730 nm, wavelengths just longer than
bright red, barely visible at dusk) could completely cancel the effect of
red light:

- Red light → plant flowers (short-day plants won't flower; long-day
  plants will)
- Far-red light immediately after → cancels the red light effect
- Red after far-red → [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] restored
- The system acts as a **reversible molecular switch**

A few seconds of either colour is sufficient, and the plant effectively
"remembers" the last colour it saw.

## Phytochrome Molecular Mechanism

### Two Forms of Phytochrome

Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single photoreceptor
protein mediates both effects. Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible
forms:

- **Pr (P660)**: Absorbs red light (~660 nm). Inactive form.
  When red light strikes Pr, it converts to Pfr.
- **Pfr (P730)**: Absorbs far-red light (~730 nm). Active form.
  When far-red light strikes Pfr, it converts to Pr.

### Ecological Logic

This molecular switch makes ecological sense:

- **Morning**: Sunlight contains abundant red light → Pr converts to Pfr
  → plant "wakes up"
- **Evening/dusk**: Light shifts toward far-red → Pfr converts to Pr
  → plant prepares for night
- **Under canopy**: Leaves above absorb red light but transmit far-red
  → high far-red/red ratio signals shade → triggers shade avoidance

### Where Phytochrome Senses Light

Unlike phototropism (where the tip of the plant is the "eye"), phytochrome
for photoperiodism is located primarily in the **leaves**. A beam of red
light on a single leaf is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire
plant. If all leaves are removed and only the stem tip remains, the plant
is blind to red/far-red light cues. Phytochrome in the leaves generates a
mobile signal (later identified as **florigen**, a protein called FT) that
travels to the shoot apex to induce flowering.

## Relevance to Cannabis

Cannabis is a short-day plant, and phytochrome-mediated photoperiodism
directly controls flowering:

- **[[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]]**: Maintained under 18+ hours of light to keep
  Pfr levels elevated
- **Flowering induction**: Triggered by reducing to 12/12 light,
  allowing Pfr→Pr conversion during extended darkness
- **Light quality matters**: High far-red ratios promote stretching;
  high red ratios promote [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]]
