# The Kingdom of Fungi — Third Kingdom of Life

Terence McKenna's interest in fungi extended beyond their psychoactive
properties to encompass their evolutionary significance and ecological
importance. In *Food of the Gods*, he framed fungi as a third kingdom
of life — distinct from plants and animals, ancient beyond reckoning,
and possessing characteristics that challenge the fundamental categories
by which Western biology has organized the living world.

## Ecological Dominance

Fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin and cellulose on Earth.
Without fungal decomposition, the carbon cycle would collapse and the
planet's forests would become burial grounds for undecayed wood. Fungi
also form [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] and improving plant health.
Some fungi are parasites of plants, animals, and other fungi; some are
predators that trap nematodes and other soil organisms.

The total biomass of fungi on Earth is estimated to exceed that of
all animals combined, and the diversity of fungal species — estimated
at 2.2 to 3.8 million, of which only about 150,000 have been formally
described — rivals that of any other kingdom of life. Despite this
ecological dominance and diversity, mycology remains one of the most
underfunded and understudied branches of biology.

## McKenna's Evolutionary Hypothesis

McKenna proposed that the evolutionary history of fungi intersects
with the evolutionary history of human consciousness in a way that
has been systematically ignored by mainstream science. He suggested
that early hominids encountered psilocybin-containing mushrooms on
the African savanna and that the psychoactive effects of these
mushrooms — particularly the dose-dependent effects of stimulation,
vision, and ego-dissolution — played a catalytic role in the
development of language, social behavior, and religious experience.

The "stoned ape" hypothesis, as it has been called, is speculative
and not widely accepted by anthropologists. But McKenna used it to
illustrate a broader point: that the relationship between humans and
fungi is far older and more consequential than the conventional
narrative of agriculture, fermentation, and antibiotic discovery
suggests.

## Ergot and the Eleusinian Mysteries

McKenna drew connections between fungal alkaloids and some of the
most significant cultural developments in Western history. The
ergot fungus, [[morning-glory-lsa-and-ergoline-alkaloids]] from which LSD was eventually
synthesized, may have been the active ingredient in the kykeon —
the sacred potion consumed at the Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient
Greece.

The Eleusinian Mysteries were the most important religious initiation
ceremony in the ancient Greek world, conducted annually for nearly
two thousand years. Participants reported profound transformative
experiences that they were forbidden to describe. The secrecy
surrounding the ceremony has preserved the mystery, but the
pharmacological properties of ergot alkaloids — which include both
psychedelic and entheogenic effects — make them plausible candidates
for the kykeon's active component.

## Fungal Intelligence

McKenna was among the first popular writers to discuss the concept of
fungal intelligence — the capacity of mycelial networks to process
information, make decisions, and solve problems. Slime molds, though
not true fungi, have been shown to solve mazes and optimize
nutrient-transport networks with an efficiency that rivals engineered
systems. True fungi exhibit similar behaviors, redirecting growth
toward nutrient sources and away from toxins, and coordinating the
development of complex fruiting body structures across millions of
undifferentiated cells.

The question of whether this constitutes "intelligence" in any
meaningful sense remains contentious. But the capacity of mycelial
networks to integrate information from their environment and produce
coordinated responses is undeniable, and it challenges the
anthropocentric assumption that intelligence requires a nervous
system.
