# Maitake (Grifola frondosa) — Timely Harvest Criteria and Post-Harvest Handling

## Overview

Maitake (*Grifola frondosa*), the "hen of the woods," has a narrow harvest
window. Missing it causes rapid deterioration — softening, spore release, and
off-odors. This page covers the visual, tactile, and olfactory indicators for
optimal harvest timing, plus harvest technique and post-harvest handling
(Stamets 1993).

## 1. Cluster Morphology — Full Formation and ~80 % Unfolding

The primary indicator is overall cluster morphology. A harvest-ready maitake
shows a fully formed structure of **fan-shaped or semicircular petals** (caps
plus lateral stems) extending outward from a central base like a cluster
flower in full bloom. Key milestones:
- **~80 % unfolding** — petals have largely opened but are still actively growing.
- **Petals growing longer and thicker** compared to earlier stages.
- **Pileal margins curling slightly inward** — thin edges show subtle inward
  curl, indicating turgor and that petals have not yet begun to flatten or
  reflex downward.

Monitor daily once petals begin to unfurl; the 70→80 % transition can be
rapid under warm or humid conditions.

## 2. Color Transition from Dark Gray-Brown to Lighter Tones

Immature fruiting bodies are distinctly **dark gray-brown**. At harvest
readiness, pigmentation shifts to lighter gray, grayish white, or light
yellow-brownish. A uniform shift across the entire cluster confirms even
maturity. Persisting dark gray-brown coloration suggests the mushroom is
still too young.

## 3. No Undifferentiated Growth at Pileal Margins

Actively growing maitake displays **whitish or grayish-white bands or spots**
of undifferentiated new tissue at the outer cap margins. At harvest maturity
these marginal bands must be **no longer present** — the entire cap surface
should appear uniformly differentiated with mature coloration. Their
disappearance signals the mushroom has ceased rapid vegetative expansion and
is entering peak culinary quality.

## 4. Hymenial Tube (Pore) Differentiation — Onset Only

**Hymenial tubes** are the fertile pore structures on the cap underside where
basidiospores form. The harvest window corresponds to their very onset:
- **Minute pores appear** at the back/underside of petals (caps + lateral
  stems) — small, just beginning to differentiate.
- **NO pores within 1 cm of the cap margin** — marginal pore development
  indicates overmaturity.
- **NO pores at the base of the cluster stem** — pore formation should be
  restricted to upper and lateral petal surfaces.

Gently tilt petals to inspect the underside; a hand lens helps detect early
pores. The ideal moment is when minute pores are visible centrally but have
not extended marginally.

## 5–7. Aroma, Petal Posture, and Spore Release — Critical Negative Indicators

Three negative indicators must be avoided at all costs:
- **Pleasant mushroom aroma** confirms harvest readiness; a **foul fishy
  smell** (developing within 24–48 hours past the window) signals tissue
  breakdown from proteolysis and microbial activity.
- **Petals must NOT droop or curve downward** — drooping signals loss of
  turgor, declining texture, and imminent spore release. Petals should
  remain upright or gently outward-curving.
- **White basidiospores must NOT be released** — visible white spore
  deposits (fine powder beneath the cluster) mean the mushroom is well past
  its prime. Spore release accelerates senescence, shortens shelf life, and
  degrades flavor and texture. Always harvest before any spore release.

## 8. Consequences of Delayed Harvest

If left unattended past the optimal window, a cascade of quality loss occurs:
1. **Tissue softens** — flesh becomes mushy, loses firm crisp texture.
2. **Foul fishy odor** — proteolysis and microbial activity.
3. **White basidiospores released** — reproductive maturity at the expense
   of edibility.
4. **Reduced shelf life** — overmature mushrooms deteriorate rapidly.
5. **Market rejection** — soft, off-smelling fruiting bodies are unsaleable.

## Harvest Technique

Proper technique preserves quality and protects the substrate/casing for
subsequent flushes:
1. **Stop misting 24 hours before harvest** — reduces surface moisture,
   minimizes microbial contamination of cut surfaces.
2. **One hand supports the base** — holds the cluster's full weight during
   cutting to prevent tearing the base or damaging adjacent pins.
3. **Cut the thick base with a small sharp knife** — sever cleanly; avoid
   tearing which damages mycelium at the substrate interface.
4. **Rotate gently and pull upward** — especially important with soil casing
   substrates; gentle rotation breaks the mycelial connection without
   disturbing the casing layer or unharvested primordia.
5. **Trim to remove debris** — clean away substrate, casing soil, or woody
   material for market-quality presentation.

## Soil Casing Harvest Considerations

When cultivated with a soil casing layer, additional care is required:
- The casing may adhere firmly to the cluster base; use gentle rotation, not
  brute-force pulling, to prevent disruption.
- Avoid disturbing the casing surface, which may harbor developing pins for
  subsequent flushes.
- After harvest, inspect for damage; repair divots or exposed substrate with
  fresh casing material.
- Maintain casing moisture post-harvest to support remaining flushes.

## Post-Harvest Handling

Maitake is highly perishable (Stamets 1993):
- **Wrap in rice paper** — breathable protection reducing moisture loss while
  allowing gas exchange; avoid sealed plastic which traps moisture.
- **Store at 12 °C (54 °F) refrigeration** — slows metabolism and microbial
  growth without causing chilling injury.
- **Expected shelf life: ~2 weeks** under optimal conditions.

---

## Yield Expectations

Maitake yields are **generally low**:
- **Typical:** 1.1–2.2 kg per bag/container.
- **Maximum observed:** ~4.4 kg per bag under optimal conditions.
- **Market form:** Virtually all sold **fresh** — the delicate layered
  morphology does not suit drying or processing well.
