# Natural Growth Regulators Produced by Soil Microbes

## The Microbial Basis of [[tompkins-electroculture-plant-growth]] Regulation

It is necessary to have the correct set of bacteria and fungi in the
root system to make natural growth regulators because bacteria and
fungi make the root growth hormones. Any excess hormones are used by
other organisms in the root system. Commercial root hormone products
are microbial in origin. The bacteria and fungi make the growth
hormones, the plant promoters, and other growth-regulating compounds.

Since it is the bacteria and fungi that make the growth hormones and
plant promoters, everyone should focus on having the right set of
organisms to make these compounds. That is what the Beneficial
Organism assay does for you: it allows you to determine if the soil,
compost, or compost tea have the organisms which make the plant
growth promoters, hormones, and related materials.

## Microbial Products Uptake by Plants

Microbes can live between the cells of the plant, but not inside the
cells. The products of microbial growth can be taken up by plants.
These include enzymes, hormones, fats, amino-sugars, and various
other [[cordyceps-cancer-bioactive-compounds]]. When organisms are applied to soil or
plant surfaces, they produce these materials as part of their normal
metabolic activity. The plant then benefits from these compounds as
they become available in the root zone or on leaf surfaces.

## What Kinds of Growth Regulators Are Produced

Soil microorganisms produce a wide range of growth-regulating
substances including root growth hormones, plant growth promoters,
enzymes, and various bioactive compounds. These are produced as
normal metabolic byproducts when the right set of organisms is
present and active in the soil or compost tea. The specific
compounds produced depend on the species of bacteria and fungi
present, the food resources available to them, and the environmental
conditions under which they are growing.

## The Role of the Beneficial Organism Assay

The Beneficial Organism assay developed by SFI and Holmes Enviro
uses selective media, enzyme analysis, and molecular approaches to
identify beneficial bacteria in soil, compost, or compost tea. See [[ingham-field-guide-soil-food-web-assessment]] for assessment methodology. The
results tell you about four functional groups of bacteria and 20
specific beneficial bacterial species. This assay is critical for
determining whether your soil, compost, or tea contains the
organisms capable of producing growth regulators your plants need.

When other labs say they assay "Pseudomonas," they do not tell you
if the bacteria are beneficial or disease species. The SFI assay
differentiates beneficial from pathogenic strains. The same applies
to Bacillus species: you need to know whether you have the Bacillus
species that taste bad to aphids, white flies, or other pests, not
just whether you have a few bacterial species that might be good
or bad.

## Importance of Active Organisms

The point of compost tea is to have the bacteria, fungi, and protozoa
active when you add them to soil or plant surfaces. When organisms
are in a product that has to sit on the shelf for days, weeks, or
months, those organisms must be dormant, or the product would spoil.
Therefore, no product that can sit for days or weeks can function
as a true compost tea in the same way as freshly brewed material.

Fresh-made tea gives you benefits you cannot get from dormant tea.
A dormant tea is much better than nothing, but a truly active tea
with living, reproducing organisms will produce growth regulators,
enzymes, and other bioactive compounds at the time of application.

## Repairing Preservative Damage

If you choose to use a commercial hormone product, then you should
repair the preservative damage by using compost tea to replace
organisms impacted by the preservatives in the commercial product.
Grow the right organisms and then inoculate your potting mix or soil
with them. The commercial products contain microbial-derived hormones
but also contain preservatives that can harm the very organisms in
your soil that would naturally produce those hormones.

## Compost Tea as a Source of Growth Regulators

Compost tea provides not only the organisms that produce growth
regulators but also the food resources they need to continue
producing them after application. The organisms in tea produce
spores, growth hormones, and enzymes during the brewing process.
These are all delivered to plant surfaces when the tea is applied.
This is why overnight improvement in plant color and disease
prevention are commonly observed when well-made tea is applied.

## Enzymes and Bioactive Compounds

Along with growth hormones, microorganisms produce a wide range of
enzymes that help plants access nutrients. These enzymes break down
complex organic materials into forms that plants can use. The
combination of enzymes, hormones, and other bioactive compounds
produced by a diverse community of soil microorganisms provides
a more complete and balanced growth-promoting effect than any
single commercial product can achieve.

## Root Zone Interactions

The root zone is where much of the growth regulator production
occurs. Bacteria and fungi colonize the root surface and the
rhizosphere, the narrow region of soil directly influenced by root
secretions. [[root-exudates]] provide food for these microorganisms,
and in return, the organisms produce growth regulators that
influence root development, branching, and nutrient uptake capacity.
This mutualistic exchange is fundamental to plant health and is why
building the right biology around roots is so important.

## Commercial Products vs. Living Systems

Commercial root hormone products are microbial in origin, meaning
they were originally produced by bacteria or fungi. However, these
products are stabilized with preservatives and do not contain
living organisms. They provide a one-time dose of growth regulators
but cannot sustain production over time. A healthy [[soil-food-web]], by
contrast, produces growth regulators continuously as long as the
organisms remain active and have adequate food resources.

## Why Diversity Matters for Growth Regulation

Different species of bacteria and fungi produce different growth
regulators. A diverse community produces a wider array of compounds
than a limited community. This is why monoculture approaches or
single-species inoculants cannot replace the full spectrum of
growth regulation provided by a complete soil food web. The
interaction between multiple species also produces emergent effects
that no single organism can achieve alone. Building diversity is
building plant health.

## Foliar Application of Growth Regulators

When compost tea is applied as a foliar spray, the organisms on the
leaf surface produce growth regulators locally. This can explain the
rapid improvements in plant appearance often observed after foliar
tea applications. The organisms arrive active and begin producing
beneficial compounds immediately, providing the plant with a boost
