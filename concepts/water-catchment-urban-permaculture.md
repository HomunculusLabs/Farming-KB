# Water Catchment in Urban Permaculture

## Overview

Water catchment in urban permaculture focuses on capturing, storing, and directing rainwater, greywater, and street runoff within the built environment. Urban areas generate enormous volumes of water that typically run off into sewers — permaculture design intercepts this flow to irrigate gardens, recharge groundwater, and create productive landscapes.

## The Urban Water Problem

- Millions of gallons fall on city roofs and streets and run off into sewers
- Municipal water often travels hundreds of miles through aqueducts, dams, tunnels, and treatment plants
- The natural water cycle (evaporation from oceans, condensation in clouds, rainfall) is replaced by a destructive system that depletes aquifers and kills river ecosystems
- "Unlike the natural water cycle, the system of aqueducts and reservoirs benefits only ourselves"

## Roof Water Harvesting

### Container Systems

- **55-gallon drums** from bakeries (free source) linked with PVC pipe for gravity-fed irrigation
- Can provide hundreds of gallons of storage when linked
- **Ferrocement tanks**: 1200-gallon cistern for under $20 using remesh, plastic, and carpet
- Tanks elevated 2+ feet above highest garden point for gravity feed — "no pumps, no maintenance, no moving parts"
- **Thermal mass bonus**: Water tanks trap solar heat by day, radiate at night — protects citrus and tomatoes from frost

### Design Principles

- Site tanks at top of slope in sloping yards
- In flat areas, elevate on pad of rock or broken concrete
- Harvest neighbor's roof runoff too: "Maybe they'll realize what a resource they're wasting"
- Use gravity exclusively — no pumps needed
- Solar batch water heating tanks for domestic hot water

### Bathtub Water Recovery

- 1/2-inch vinyl tube through window jam, suction-cupped to tub bottom
- Hand pump starts the siphon
- Route to deciduous fruit trees (fig, loquat, Asian pears) — trees that shade in summer but lose leaves for winter sun
- Can connect to old irrigation lines to reach multiple trees

## Greywater Systems

### Pond-and-Slope Filtration

- Natural pond systems filter greywater through biological processes
- Slope of yard used to move water through successive filtration stages
- Ponds attract birds, insects, and create habitat
- Water loop: home → ponds → garden
- If combined with cistern catchment, the water loop becomes complete

### Principles

- "Greywater cycling create opportunities for growth in barren places"
- Design for gravity flow through the system
- Use biological filtration (plants, gravel, microorganisms) rather than mechanical filters

## Street and Parking Lot Runoff

### Contour-Based Capture

- Mark contour lines on sloping parking lots
- Remove 3-foot wide asphalt strips along contour lines
- Plant native trees in the removed strips
- Runoff from remaining pavement waters the trees passively
- Works in asphalt or concrete-covered lots: remove pavement from low spots

### Driveway and Street Capture

- "Bust up your driveway and divert the water from the street to the street trees"
- Curb cuts or broken pavement sections to allow street runoff into planted areas

### Swales in Urban Settings

- **On gentle slopes (<15 degrees)**: Broad, shallow contour swales to hold storm runoff and infiltrate it
- **On steep slopes (>15 degrees)**: V-shaped "boomerang" swales forming basins directing water to individual trees
- Swales fill with silt over time and form terraces
- Mulch swales to reduce evaporation
- Plant trees on swales — long after swales silts up, they're visible as lines of trees on hillsides

## Broadscale Watershed Restoration

### Check Dams

- Build low, leaky check dams from rocks or brush in urban streams
- Plug small flows with straw bales
- Key dams into stream banks to prevent water from eating around them
- Broader in center (strongest flow), lowest at center with rock splash pad below
- Maximum height: ~18 inches
- Design so silt in one dam reaches the bottom of the next
- Once filled with silt, build them higher

### Microcatchments

- Pit the land with a shovel on slopes or flat areas
- Pits collect seeds, water, animal droppings, and mulch
- Creates rich, sheltered microclimates for plant establishment
- "Just laying down mulch on bare spots will conserve enough water to allow many seeds to germinate"
- Works in first and second order streams; if structures blow out, move higher in watershed

## Trees and Precipitation

### How Trees Create Water

- Trees act as condensers: leaves provide surfaces for atmospheric moisture to condense
- A single giant tree (e.g., Ocotea foetens) may present 16 hectares of leaf surface to moist air
- Condensation drip can be 80-86% of total precipitation on sea-facing slopes
- Fog drip measured at 130-230 cm on Table Mountain (South Africa) and Lanai (Hawaii)
- Forests return 75% of water to air (25% evaporated, 50% transpired)
- Forested areas return 10x more moisture than bare ground, 2x more than grasslands

### Ekman Spirals

- Wind deflected over forest edges creates compression spirals
- These add ~40% to rainfall in bands parallel to tree lines
- Even a single hedgerow of 40% permeability causes compression effects
- A 12m tree line compresses air to 244m above

### Urban Implications

- Plant crosswind belts of tall trees for maximum precipitation enhancement
- Urban woodlots around industrial zones filter pollution and create moisture
- Tree planting in cities literally increases local rainfall through condensation and transpiration

## Soil as Water Storage

- "The cheapest and easiest place to store water is in the soil"
- Soil storage types: retention (bound to particles), interstitial (between particles), humus (organic sponge)
- 2.5-7 cm of rain stored per 30 cm soil depth (retention storage)
- Fine-textured, high-organic soils store 10-30 cm per 30 cm depth
- Forest litter absorbs 1 cm rain per 3 cm depth
- Trees roots create channels for water infiltration to depth
