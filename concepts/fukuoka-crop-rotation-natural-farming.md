# Crop Rotation in Natural Farming

Crop rotation is one of the oldest and most fundamental practices in agriculture. Masanobu Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-natural-vs-scientific-farming]] approach to crop rotation was distinctive in that it emerged from observation of natural plant succession rather than from scientific experimentation or economic calculation.

## The Natural Rotation

In nature, plants do not grow in neat rows or follow prescribed rotations. They grow in complex, ever-changing communities shaped by soil conditions, climate, competition, and cooperation. Fukuoka sought to understand and work with these natural patterns rather than impose artificial rotation schemes.

His rice-barley rotation is the most famous example. Rice and winter grains have been grown in continuous succession in Japan for centuries -- a practice that Fukuoka called "an extraordinary method of farming that has taken hold nowhere else in the world."

The reason it works is that rice (grown in summer paddies) and barley (grown as a winter crop) occupy the same land at different times of year, each preparing the soil for the other. This is [[fukuoka-natures-self-organizing-principles]] at work.

## The Course of Rice Cultivation in Japan

Fukuoka placed crop rotation in its historical and cultural context. In the Land of Ripening Grain, as the Japanese people long called their country, rice cultivation held a deeper meaning for farmers than simply the growing of a staple food crop.

"The farmer did not grow the rice, nature did; and the people born to this land partook of its blessings." The words "bountiful Land of Ripening Grain" expressed the joy of the Yamato people, who were able to receive the rich blessings of heaven and earth with a grateful heart.

However, once man began to think that he grew the rice, scientific discrimination arose, creating a rift between the rice and the land. People lost a sense of unity with nature, leaving in its place only man's relationship with rice cultivation and his relationship with the soil. Modern thinking reduced rice to just another foodstuff.

More than just the staff of life, the rice grown on Japanese soil was the very soul of the Yamato people. But as the activities of the farmer have been lowered in the common perception to the production of rice as another foodstuff, a commercial article, the original purpose of rice production has gradually been corrupted.

## Changes in Rice Cultivation Methods

Fukuoka traced the deterioration of Japanese rice farming through four stages:

1. 1940 -- Primitive farming (improvements in tilling methods)

2. 1950 -- Animal-powered agriculture (increased fertilizer production)

3. 1960 -- Scientific farming (mechanization)

4. 1970 -- Agribusiness (energy-intensive systemized agriculture)

Prior to the development of scientific agriculture, rice farmers devoted themselves entirely to serving the land that grows the crops. But they gradually turned their attention from the land to the problem of boosting soil fertility. Soil scientists showed that tilling the soil to a depth of one inch can yield five bushels of rice per quarter-acre, and from this concluded that working the soil down to five inches would yield 25 bushels.

Animal-powered agriculture was later pushed because heavy applications of manure and prepared compost were known to help achieve high yields. But farmers learned that preparing compost is not easy work. Yields failed to improve enough to justify the heavy labor required, peaking at about 22 bushels per quarter-acre.

Although Japanese agricultural technology appears to have progressed rapidly over the last fifty years, the productivity of the land has declined. In terms of quality, this period has been one of retreat rather than advance.

## The Three-Dimensional Orchard

Fukuoka's orchard was a living example of natural crop rotation in three dimensions. Fruit trees (citrus, loquat, persimmon, peach, plum, pear, apple, cherry) occupied the canopy layer. Green manure crops (clover, vetch, alfalfa) covered the ground. Vegetables grew in the spaces between. Protected trees (acacia, wax myrtle, Japanese alder) served as windbreaks and nitrogen fixers.

This multi-layered system naturally rotated crops through space as well as time. Different plants occupied different niches, competed minimally with each other, and contributed to the overall health of the ecosystem. This approach is detailed in Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-management]].

## The Weed Succession Principle

Fukuoka discovered a powerful principle for natural vegetable farming: observe the weeds growing in a field and plant vegetables from the same botanical family. Weeds and crops within the same family share similar soil preferences and growing conditions.

His detailed weed succession table mapped the relationships between weed families and their cultivated cousins:

- Grass family weeds (crabgrass, barnyard grass) indicated conditions favorable for barnyard millet, foxtail millet, wheat, barley, and rice

- Mustard family weeds (shepherd's purse) indicated good conditions for daikon, turnip, Chinese cabbage, and leaf mustard

- Legume weeds (clover, vetch, kudzu) indicated good conditions for soybeans, adzuki beans, and garden peas

- Composite family weeds (dandelion, fleabane, thistle) indicated conditions for garland chrysanthemum, lettuce, burdock, and mugwort

## The Problem with Scientific Rotation

Scientific agriculture typically designs rotation schemes based on economic considerations and controlled experiments. These rotations are often rigid, requiring specific sequences and timing that may not suit local conditions.

Much research is being done today on the morphology of rice at various stages of growth. Scientists are attempting also to achieve high yields through detailed comparative studies on the planting period, quantity of seed sown, number and spacing of transplanted seedlings, and depth of transplantation. However, because none of the resulting techniques has more than about a five percent effect on yields, efforts are underway to combine and consolidate these into one unified high-yielding technology.

Yet such efforts have failed to make any notable gains. Fukuoka argued that nature's rotations are more complex and more effective than any human-designed scheme. When vegetables are grown beneath fruit trees in a semi-wild state, "they appear at first glance to grow without order, but these develop into splendid plants while nature solves the problems of continuous cropping, space, disease and pest damage, and the recovery of soil fertility."

## Green Manure Crops

Green manure crops -- nitrogen-fixing legumes like clover, vetch, and alfalfa -- played a central role in Fukuoka's rotation system. These crops enrich the soil, suppress weeds, and provide habitat for beneficial insects.

In the rice field, white clover served as a permanent ground cover, providing nitrogen for both the rice and the barley. The key was to grow green manure crops not as separate rotation phases but as continuous companions to food crops. This eliminated the need for fallow periods and maintained soil fertility year-round.

This practice is closely related to Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-barley-clover-cultivation]] and [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]].

## Self-Sustaining Fertility

The ultimate goal of natural crop rotation was self-sustaining fertility. When crops are rotated according to natural principles, the soil becomes more fertile over time rather than less. Organic matter accumulates, soil structure improves, and the microbial community becomes more diverse and active.

"After thirty years of natural farming, my soil is deeper, darker, and richer than the soil of neighboring farms that have been chemically fertilized for decades."

The only course available today for successfully rejecting scientific farming and halting its rampant growth is the establishment of a natural way of farming the agricultural mainstays: rice, barley, and wheat.

## The Organic Farming Limitation

Much has been made of the organic farming methods taken up by a small number of farmers out of concern over the polluting effects of scientific farming, but organic farming too is an outgrowth of scientific farming that is oriented toward petroleum energy-intensive commercial agribusiness. True natural crop rotation goes beyond organic farming to work within the [[fukuoka-living-soil]] framework that nature itself provides.
