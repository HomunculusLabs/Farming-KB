# Snow, Ice, and Avalanche Management

In cold-climate [[permaculture-design]], snow and ice are not merely hazards to endure — they are resources to be managed, harvested, and strategically deployed. Understanding the physical properties of frozen water enables designers to protect infrastructure, store precipitation for dry-season use, concentrate solar energy, and safeguard human settlements from [[avalanche-phenomenon]] risk. This page synthesizes the key principles from [[bill-mollison]]'s treatment of ice management, snow physics, and avalanche dynamics.

## Ice Management

### Ice Depth and Water Supply

Ice formation in ponds and water bodies directly reduces available water storage. The depth of ice is a critical factor:

- Cold-climate ponds freeze to a depth of **15–90 cm**, rendering that volume temporarily unavailable for use.
- Supply pipes must be buried at least **1 metre** below the surface to remain functional through winter.
- Where buried pipes are impractical, maintaining a trickle flow of at least **45 L/day** can prevent freezing in vulnerable sections.

### Water Storage as Thermal Mass

Water stored within a house cellar serves a dual purpose:

- **Heat store**: The high specific heat capacity of water buffers indoor temperatures, absorbing warmth during the day and releasing it at night.
- **Emergency supply**: Cellar-stored water remains accessible even when surface sources are frozen.

### Ice as a Building and Optical Material

Ice possesses unique properties that make it useful far beyond simple thermal applications:

- **Ice lenses**: Transparent, refracting medium suitable for lens construction. Lenses are poured into moulds, shaped for solar concentration, and individual components can be glued together using water itself as the adhesive (which freezes to bond them).
- **Ice buggies**: Ice can be moulded into virtually any shape around axles, creating transport devices for towing materials to summer storage locations.
- These applications exploit ice's transparency and ease of shaping — properties that are unavailable in most other naturally occurring materials.

## Snow as a Resource

### Precipitation Capture and Storage

Snow is a form of delayed precipitation that can be actively managed within a [[permaculture]] design:

- **Compacted snow** endures for more than one season, making it a viable medium-term water storage mechanism.
- Snow is caught on **[[living-fences-and-hedgerows]]**, which act as windbreaks that cause snow to deposit in useful locations rather than being blown away.
- Deposited snow can be **directed into mounds** for controlled meltdown into swales and cisterns during spring thaw, effectively time-shifting precipitation to the growing season.

### Snow Albedo and Passive Solar Heating

The high reflectivity (albedo) of snow surfaces can be harnessed for heating:

- **Reflective heating**: Sunglasses are necessary in highly reflective snow landscapes — a testament to the intensity of reflected solar radiation.
- **Earth-formed reflectors**: Structures shaped from earth and covered with snow create effective winter heat concentrators. The snow surface reflects and directs low-angle winter sunlight onto buildings, growing areas, or thermal mass.
- **Summer reflectors**: When snow is absent, the same earth-formed reflectors can be covered with white gravel, sand, or clay to maintain reflective properties.

## Snow Physics

Understanding the thermal and radiative properties of snow is essential for effective design in cold climates.

### Thermal Radiation Properties

| Property | Value / Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Emissivity | 0.962–0.986 (near "black body" for thermal radiation) |
| Night-time cooling | Cools very rapidly due to high emissivity |
| Thin snow effect on soil | Chills soil to **35°C below** ambient air temperature |
| Insulation threshold | Snow depths **>15 cm** provide effective insulation |

The paradox of snow is that while it is an excellent reflector of visible light, it is nearly a perfect black body for thermal radiation — meaning it radiates heat away very efficiently at night. Thin snow layers are particularly dangerous because they cool the soil dramatically without providing insulation. Only when snow accumulates beyond 15 cm does the insulating air-trapping structure develop sufficiently to protect the ground beneath.

### Light Penetration

- **Normal snow**: Light penetrates **10–15 cm** below the surface.
- **Hoarfrost / dry snow**: Light penetrates significantly deeper, reaching **30–60 cm**.

This subsurface light penetration can affect soil biology and shallow-rooted plants, and is a factor in basal melting dynamics.

### Basal Melting and Cohesion Loss

Earth heat conducts upward through the soil and melts snow from below (basal melting). This process:

- Creates a lubricating water layer at the snow-ground interface.
- Severely reduces snowpack cohesion.
- Is a primary contributor to avalanche initiation on slopes.

## Avalanche Dynamics and Defense

### Conditions for Avalanche Risk

Avalanche potential exists when the following conditions coincide:

- **Slope angle**: Slopes steeper than **6°** with **15 cm or more** of accumulated snow are at risk.
- **Triggering events** include:
  - Strong winds exceeding **13–18 km/sec** (wind-loading vulnerable slopes)
  - Sudden freeze-thaw cycles
  - Warm winds introducing rapid temperature change
  - Spring thaw and progressive warming
  - Meltwater saturation over hoarfrost or ice layers
  - Gunshots and explosive vibrations
  - Cornice collapse from ridge crests
  - Skiers, animals, or any surface loading

### The Avalanche Air Wave

A critical and often underestimated hazard is the **avalanche air wave**:

- This compressed air shockwave **precedes the sliding snow mass**.
- It travels at approximately **330 m/sec** (near the speed of sound).
- The air wave can **destroy structures well beyond the actual slide path**, meaning buildings outside the debris zone are still vulnerable.

### Avalanche Defense Strategies

| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| **Preserve high-slope forests** | Intact forest cover anchors snow, breaks wind loading, and disrupts slab formation |
| **Tunnels for services** | Route roads, utilities, and pathways through tunnels rather than across exposed avalanche terrain |
| **V-shaped barriers** | Diverting structures that split and redirect avalanche flows away from settlements |
| **Prevent settlement** | The most effective defense — avoid building or occupying high-risk avalanche zones entirely |
| **Snow barriers on plateaus** | Install barriers on plateaus above slopes to prevent wind-deposited snow from accumulating on start zones |

### Design Implications

In [[permaculture-site-design-and-zones]] for mountainous or snowy terrain:

1. **Zone placement**: Never site dwellings, animal shelters, or critical infrastructure in identified avalanche runout zones.
2. **Forest management**: High-altitude forests are the most cost-effective avalanche defense — protect them as a priority.
3. **Access planning**: Use tunneled routes or protected corridors for essential winter access; avoid ridge-top travel where cornices form.
4. **Snow fencing**: Strategic placement of fences and hedgerows captures and controls snow deposition, simultaneously reducing avalanche hazard and building water reserves.
5. **Monitoring**: Understanding the trigger conditions allows communities to maintain watch during critical periods (rapid warming, high winds, spring thaw).
