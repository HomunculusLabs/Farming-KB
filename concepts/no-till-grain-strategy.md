# No-Till Grain Strategy

The no-till grain strategy adapts Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming method for
growing staple grains without plowing, synthetic fertilizer, or pesticides.
Often called the "no-work method," this approach follows four core principles:
no cultivation, no chemical fertilizer, no weeding, and no chemical pesticides.
The system grows rice, barley, wheat, rye, millet, and oats together with
white clover in continuous rotation on the same land, eliminating fallow periods
and building soil fertility through straw mulch, living mulch systems,
and biological nitrogen fixation.

## Energy Efficiency and Land Requirements

The no-till grain system is remarkably energy-efficient compared to conventional
and even traditional animal-powered farming. A farmer eating an average 2,000
calorie per day diet can produce 1,300 pounds (590 kg) of rice, equivalent to
22 bushels, on just a quarter acre. Using animals for labor requires at least
five times as many calories for the same yield, while tractor-based farming
requires at least ten times as much energy input.

The land efficiency of grain production compared to other food sources
demonstrates why Fukuoka advocated plant-based diets:
- 1,800 square feet of grain to feed one person
- 5,400 square feet of potatoes alone for one person
- 13,500 square feet of dairy farming for one person
- 36,000 square feet of pigs alone for one person
- 90,000 square feet of beef alone for one person

## Paddy Setup and Initial Preparation

A rice paddy for no-till cultivation requires initial site preparation, but
this disturbance happens only once. The process involves leveling the ground
and building a low mud retaining wall around the plot capable of holding 2
inches (5 cm) of water. If the soil is extremely compacted, a chisel plow may
be used the first year only. Lime or dolomite and a thin layer of chicken
manure are spread over the area and watered in. After this single treatment,
the soil is managed entirely through natural means.

On the outer perimeter of grain fields, a band of weed control plants
should be grown such as comfrey, lemongrass, or citrus. These should be
mulched with sawdust for additional protection. If a paddy that fills with
water cannot be built, dry rice species exist that only need watering and have
the additional benefit of surviving on monsoon rains alone.

## Cold Climate Adaptations

In areas too cold for rice cultivation, a system with shorter cycles can be
used. Spring wheat is planted in spring with oats, barley, or winter wheat as
the winter crop. Experimental no-till crops for cold climates include squash,
melons, tomato, cotton, vetch, and sunflowers. The key principle remains the
same: continuous ground cover with minimal soil disturbance.

## Step-by-Step No-Till Grain Process

### Seeding Rates and Crop Combinations

A variety of plants are grown together in polyculture. Each plot always grows
rice and white clover as the foundation, with additional grains such as rye,
barley, millet, winter wheat, or oats. Seeding rates per acre are:
- Clover: 1 pound (0.5 kg)
- Companion grains (rye, barley, oats): 6 to 14 pounds (2.7 to 6.3 kg)
- Rice: 5 to 10 pounds (2.3 to 4.5 kg)

### Seasonal Management Cycle

The annual cycle follows this sequence:

1. **Early fall**: Rice seed is sown, either broadcast and covered with straw
   or made into [[fukuoka-seed-ball-one-seed-technique]]. Seed balls can be
   made by mixing seeds with mud and pressing through wire mesh, or by wetting
   seeds and rolling in fine clay dust. The clay mixture combines potter's
   clay, compost, and sometimes paper mush.

2. **Mid-fall**: Last year's rice is harvested and laid on rice racks to dry
   for a couple of weeks. Husks and straw are threshed off and saved.

3. **One month after rice harvest**: Unhusked rice is sown in the field and
   saved husks and straw are spread over it as mulch.

4. **Winter**: If rice has grown to 6 inches (15 cm), allow 40 ducks per acre
   to graze the field. Check for thin spots and plant more seed. Keep the rice
   drained to prevent freezing. The ducks provide integrated pest control
   by eating insects and weeds while fertilizing the soil.

5. **Spring**: Check again for thin spots and overseed as needed.

6. **Late spring**: Harvest the rye, barley, or other winter grain. Walking
   on the rice during harvest does not damage it significantly. Stack grain
   to dry for about a week, then thresh.

7. **Post-harvest**: Spread threshed straw and husks on the field. If
   multiple plots exist, cross-spread straw between plots for disease
   prevention (oat straw on the rye field and vice versa).

8. **Early summer**: Only rice and managed weeds remain growing. Flood the
   paddy for about a week until the clover turns yellow (without killing it).

9. **Summer**: The field should always be at least half rice. Prepare seeds
   of different grains for fall sowing, rotating to a different grain than
   the previous year.

## Duck Integration in Rice Paddies

Ducks play a multifunctional role in the no-till rice system. At a stocking
rate of 40 ducks per acre during winter, they graze on weeds and insects,
stir the soil with their foraging activity, and deposit manure that
fertilizes the rice. This integration exemplifies [[animal-tractoring]] and
[[aquatic-polyculture-design-mollison]] principles, where multiple yields
are obtained from the same land area simultaneously.

## Soil Building Through Continuous Cover

Unlike conventional grain farming that leaves soil bare between crops, the
no-till strategy maintains continuous living and dead ground cover. White
clover fixes nitrogen and suppresses weeds while straw mulch prevents
erosion, retains moisture, and feeds soil organisms. Over time, the soil
structure improves as earthworms and other organisms create channels for
water infiltration and root penetration, eliminating the perceived need for
tillage.
