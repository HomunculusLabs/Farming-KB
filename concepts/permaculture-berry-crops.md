# Permaculture Berry Crops

Berry crops occupy the shrub and ground layers of [[permaculture-energy-systems]], providing high-value nutrition in compact spaces. Many berry species are shade-tolerant and suited to the understory beneath fruit and nut trees.

## Blueberries (Vaccinium spp)
Family: ERICACEAE. Small bush to small tree (0.3-6 m depending on species and variety). Deciduous, native to North America. Require well-drained, very acid soil (pH 4.0-5.5) with plenty of humus. Propagate from seed or cuttings. Excellent bee forage.

Design notes: Plant in the understory of larger fruit trees. Mulch heavily with acidic materials (pine needles, peat moss, [[permaculture-plants-ground-covers-and-accumulators]]). Group with companion ericaceous species. High in antioxidants and vitamin C. Self-fertile varieties available but cross-pollination improves yield.

## Fuchsia (Fuchsia spp)
Family: ONAGRACEAE. Over 100 species native to South America and New Zealand. Range from small shrubs to small trees (up to 6 m). Easily grown in tubs. F. exorticata (konini) and F. magellanica are particularly frost hardy. Prefer well-drained, moist garden soil.

Fruit eaten raw or in jellies, best 1-2 days after flower drops. Darker varieties taste better. Flowers are also edible. Useful for container gardens and shaded areas in [[permaculture-climatic-zones-design]].

## Strawberry Tree (Arbutus unedo)
Family: ERICACEAE. Attractive evergreen tree/shrub to 7 m. Native to Mediterranean, Asia Minor, and Ireland. Frost and drought tolerant, moderately salt-spray tolerant. Tolerates most conditions except tropical climates and alkaline/waterlogged soils.

Fruit takes up to 12 months to ripen -- trees often display flowers and green/white/yellow/red fruit simultaneously. Eaten raw, preserved, made into syrup, wine, brandy, liqueurs, or cider-like drink. Bark, leaves, and fruit used for tanning. Plant densely as a hedge. Good bee forage. Propagation from seed; pollinator desirable.

## Hackberry (Celtis australis)
Family: CELTIDACEAE. Small to medium tree to 25 m. Same family as elm. Fruits purple-black when ripe with sweet flavour. Eaten raw, in jams, jellies, or wines. Good addition to chicken forage systems. Serrated leaves turn pale yellow in autumn. Reasonably frost hardy. Useful shade tree.

## Japanese Raisin Tree (Hovenia dulcis)
Family: RHAMNACEAE. Deciduous tree to 18 m, native to Japan, China, and Himalayas. Very hardy to cold. The swollen fruit stalk (not the fruit itself) is the edible part -- tastes like raisins. In China, used to counteract the effects of wine. Seeds, boughs, and young leaves used to prepare a honey substitute. Good spring honey plant. Attracts [[insectary-plants-for-pest-control]].

## Ugni (Ugni molinaea)
Family: MYRTACEAE. Also known as Chilean guava. Small evergreen shrub producing aromatic red berries with a strawberry-like flavour. Excellent for hedging. Tolerates moderate frost. Fruit eaten fresh or made into preserves.

## Lillipillies (Eugenia/Acmena/Syzigium spp)
Family: MYRTACEAE. A large group of Australian rainforest trees and shrubs producing edible berry-like fruits. Range from small shrubs to tall trees. Many produce crisp, slightly tart fruits good for fresh eating, jams, and jellies. Useful as screen plants, windbreaks, and habitat. Frost tolerance varies by species.

## Guava (Psidium guajava)
Family: MYRTACEAE. Small evergreen native to Mexico, reaching 2-10 m. Susceptible to frost (tolerant to -2°C) but tolerates drought and periodic flooding. Fruit is highly scented, yellow when ripe, with 2-5 times the Vitamin C of fresh orange juice. Tannin-rich leaves and bark are used for dyeing and leather tanning. Heavy wood suitable for tool handles, firewood, and charcoal. Roots, bark, and leaves are used medicinally for gastroenteritis and diarrhoea. Propagated from seed (70% true to type), cuttings, and suckers. Minimum spacing 3 m.

## Strawberry Guava (Psidium cattleianum)
Family: MYRTACEAE. Compact evergreen shrub 3-6 m. Small round fruit (~2 cm), deep wine-red (a yellow variety also exists). Flesh is juicy with a distinctive strawberry-like flavour. Frost hardier than P. guajava, tolerating down to -5°C. Bears fruit in the 3rd-4th year, often producing two crops per year in warm climates. In Brazil, leaves spread on floors release an aroma that repels flies. Bark is rich in tannin for leather tanning.

## Brazil Cherry / Surinam Cherry (Eugenia uniflora)
Family: MYRTACEAE. South American evergreen shrub to small tree (~3.5 m). Makes an excellent fruiting hedge. Tolerant of drought, shade, wind, salt spray, and frosts to -2°C. Bears fruit within a few weeks of flowering, often producing two crops per year. In Brazil, the leaves release a fly-repelling aroma when walked on. High in Vitamin A (1500 IU per 100 g). Propagated from seed and cuttings.

## Grumichama (Eugenia dombeyi)
Family: MYRTACEAE. Evergreen tree to 10.5 m, native to coastal southern Brazil. Withstands temperatures to -3°C. Produces purple-black, cherry-like fruit. Can be used as a productive hedge or specimen tree in [[food-forest-layers-and-species]] plantings.

## Berry Crop Nutritional Comparison

| Species | Key Nutrient Highlight | Notes |
|---------|----------------------|-------|
| Blueberry | Vitamin C ~14 mg/100 g | High in antioxidants, especially anthocyanins |
| Strawberry Guava | Vitamin C ~228 mg/100 g | Also high in dietary fibre |
| Common Guava (P. guajava) | **Vitamin C 242 mg/100 g** | One of the highest Vitamin C fruits available |
| Brazil Cherry | Vitamin A 1500 IU/100 g | Good source of provitamin A |
| Ugni (Chilean Guava) | Vitamin C ~70 mg/100 g | Concentrated flavour, aromatic |

Common guava stands out with a remarkable Vitamin C content of 242 mg per 100 g -- roughly 17 times that of blueberry and 4-5 times that of orange juice. Guavas are consistently ranked among the highest Vitamin C fruits available, making them exceptionally valuable for permaculture systems focused on nutritional resilience. See [[permaculture-fruit-tree-guide]] for complementary fruit tree species and their nutritional profiles.

## Design Principles for Berry Systems

- Stack berry crops in the understory and shrub layers of the [[permaculture-fruit-tree-guide]]
- Use shade-tolerant species (fuchsia, lillipillies) beneath taller canopy trees
- Blueberries and strawberry tree need acidic soil -- group with ericaceous companions
- Fuchsia species work well in container gardens and shaded areas
- Berries are high-value, perishable crops -- plant near the house in [[permaculture-climatic-zones-design]]
- Mulch berry plantings with nitrogen-rich material from [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]] cuttings
- Many berry flowers are excellent [[insectary-plants-for-pest-control]]
- Use berry hedges (strawberry tree, ugni, lillipillies) as boundary plantings with productive function
- Select species for sequential ripening to extend the harvest season across months
- Practice [[chop-and-drop-mulching]] with prunings from berry shrubs to recycle nutrients
- Integrate berry crops with [[bee-forage-systems-design]] to boost pollination across the site
- Layer Myrtaceae species (guava, strawberry guava, ugni, lillipillies) together to share pollinators and microclimate benefits
- Consider salt-tolerant species like strawberry tree and lillipillies for [[salt-tolerant-plant-guide]]
