# Rhizodeposition and the Two-Step Microbial Filtering Model

## Overview

Plant roots are colonized by microbial communities that differ dramatically from those
in surrounding bulk soil. This selectivity arises through a two-step filtering process:
first, rhizodeposition from roots enriches a subset of soil microbes in the
rhizosphere; second, host genotype-dependent mechanisms further filter this enriched
pool into the root interior (endorhiza). The [[winston-cannabis-microbiome-study-design]] by Winston et
al. (2014) provided empirical evidence for this model in Cannabis sativa, demonstrating
that both soil origin and cultivar identity shape root-associated [[cannabis-endorhiza-bacterial-communities]].

## What Is Rhizodeposition?

Rhizodeposition encompasses all organic compounds released by plant roots into the
surrounding soil. These compounds serve as the primary carbon and energy source for
rhizosphere microorganisms and drive the dramatic shift in microbial community
composition observed at the root-soil interface. Major classes include:

- **Exudates:** Low-molecular-weight compounds actively secreted by root cells,
  including sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose), amino acids (glutamate, serine),
  organic acids (citrate, malate, oxalate), and phenolics. These are released
  continuously and constitute the largest fraction of rhizodeposited carbon.

- **Mucilage:** High-molecular-weight polysaccharides secreted by root cap and
  epidermal cells that form a gelatinous sheath around the root tip. Mucilage
  lubricates root penetration through soil, maintains root-soil contact for water
  and nutrient uptake, and provides a labile carbon source for microbes.

- **Lysates:** Cellular contents released through root cell turnover and lysis,
  including membrane lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and cell wall fragments.
  These provide a more complex and varied nutrient source than exudates alone.

- **Gases and volatiles:** Root-emitted [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds]] (VOCs) such as
  sesquiterpenes and other terpenoids serve as signaling molecules that attract or
  repel specific microbial taxa.

Collectively, rhizodeposition can allocate 10–40% of total plant photosynthate to the
belowground environment, creating a carbon-rich hotspot that selects for copiotrophic
(fast-growing, nutrient-responsive) bacteria over the oligotrophic (slow-growing)
taxa dominant in bulk soil.

## Step One: Edaphic Filtering and Rhizosphere Enrichment

The first filtering step occurs as soil microbes respond to rhizodeposition in the
rhizosphere — the narrow zone of soil directly influenced by root activity. This step
is primarily driven by abiotic and nutritional factors rather than host genetics:

- **Carbon enrichment:** The sudden influx of labile carbon from root exudates
  selects for bacteria capable of rapid growth on simple sugars and organic acids.
  Proteobacteria (especially Alpha- and Gammaproteobacteria) typically increase in
  relative abundance, while Acidobacteria and other oligotrophs decline.

- **[[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]]:** Soil texture, pH, moisture content, [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]]
  determine which subset of the total soil community can respond to rhizodeposition.
  Sandy soils may support different rhizosphere communities than clay soils due to
  differences in water retention, gas diffusion, and root-soil contact area.

- **Root architecture:** Branching density, rooting depth, and lateral root formation
  determine the volume of soil influenced by rhizodeposition and create heterogeneity
  in rhizosphere conditions across the root system.

The key characteristic of this first filtering step is that it is primarily
environmental. Different plant species growing in the same soil may share similar
rhizosphere communities because the edaphic filter dominates over species-specific
effects at this stage.

## Step Two: Host Genotype-Dependent Selection

The second filtering step occurs at the root surface and during entry into the
endorhiza (root interior). Here, host genetic factors become the primary determinant
of which rhizosphere microbes successfully colonize internal root tissues:

- **Immune recognition:** Plant pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) detect
  microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) such as flagellin and EF-Tu.
  Microbes triggering strong immune responses are excluded, while those that evade
  or suppress immunity gain entry. Immune receptor repertoires vary between
  cultivars, creating genotype-specific selection.

- **Root exudate specificity:** Different genotypes produce distinct exudate profiles
  — varying in sugar composition, organic acid ratios, and [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]]
  concentrations — selectively attracting compatible symbionts.

- **Root barrier properties:** Cell wall composition (pectin methylation, suberin
  deposition, lignin content) creates differential barriers to microbial entry
  between genotypes.
