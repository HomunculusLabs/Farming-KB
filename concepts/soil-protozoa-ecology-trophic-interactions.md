## Overview

Soil protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that occupy a critical position
in
the soil food web as primary consumers of bacteria and fungi. Despite their
microscopic
size (typically 2–200 μm), protozoa are among the most abundant and metabolically
active
organisms in soil, with populations ranging from 10,000 to over 1 million individuals
per
gram of soil. Their feeding activities regulate [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]]. Three major
groups
dominate soil protozoan communities: flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates, each
occupying
distinct ecological niches defined by their morphology, motility, and feeding
strategies.
Understanding protozoan ecology is fundamental to managing soil biological fertility
and
optimizing [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] inputs. In aquatic microhabitats such
as
water-filled macropores and the rhizosphere, flagellate populations can exceed
100,000
cells per gram of soil during peak activity periods.

### Naked Amoebae

Naked amoebae (Gymnamoebia) are the most diverse and morphologically plastic soil
protozoa, ranging from 10–50 μm. They move using temporary cytoplasmic projections
called
pseudopodia and can squeeze through soil pores smaller than their resting body
diameter
by flowing as a viscous liquid. This ability to access small pores makes amoebae
particularly effective [[agaricus-campestris-biology-and-life-cycle]] (e.g., Naegleria), demonstrating the
evolutionary plasticity within this group. Amoebae are particularly important in
structured soils with stable aggregates, where they graze bacteria protected within
aggregate interiors.

### Testate Amoebae

Testate amoebae are distinguished from naked amoebae by their shell-like covering
(test),
which they either secrete (proteinaceous or siliceous) or construct from ingested
mineral
particles, sand grains, or diatom frustules. The test provides protection from
desiccation and predation, allowing testate amoebae to survive in drier microhabitats
than naked amoebae. They range from 15–250 μm in size and are common in moist organic
soils, [[aact-forest-litter-fungi-mycorrhizal-ingham]] layers, and Sphagnum moss. Common genera include Euglypha,
Trinema,
Assulina, and Centropyxis. Testate amoebae are valuable bioindicators of soil
moisture
regimes and are widely used in paleoecological studies because their empty tests
preserve
in peat deposits and lake sediments, providing records of past soil and environmental
conditions spanning thousands of years. The species composition of testate amoeba
communities in peat cores has been used to reconstruct historical water table levels
and
climate changes with remarkable precision.
