# Seed Quality and Selection (Solomon)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Gardening When It Counts, [[steve-solomon]], Chapters 4-6

Solomon founded Territorial Seed Company and brings decades of experience to the subject of seed quality. His central argument: most home gardeners don't realize how much seed quality matters, and the seed rack at the garden center is often the worst possible source.

## Why Seed Quality Matters

Poor seed causes three types of failure:
1. **Germination failure**: Seeds that don't sprout at all
2. **Weak emergence**: Seeds that sprout but grow poorly due to lack of vigor
3. **Poor performance**: Seeds that grow adequately but yield little or nothing at harvest

The home gardener rarely blames the seed. They assume the problem was their soil preparation, sowing depth, watering, or timing. But seed quality is often the real culprit.

## Germination Standards

Commercial farmers require minimum germination percentages before accepting seed. Solomon's company wouldn't buy cabbage seed below 85% germination, and preferred lots exceeding 90%.

### The USDA Minimum Standard Problem

In the US, seeds sold in packets under one pound are not required to show germination test results. Instead, they fall under "USDA Minimum Standard Germination" — a set of floor-level minimums that permit quite poor seed to be sold legally.

Typical USDA minimums: Cabbage 75%, Lettuce 80%, Muskmelon 65%, Leek 60%.

The problem: A seed packer can sell seed right at the minimum standard. By the time the home gardener plants it (6-12 months after purchase), germination may have dropped below useful levels.

Quality mail-order companies like Johnny's Selected Seeds maintain germination levels well above the minimums — buying seed at levels 5% or more above their sell-at minimum to ensure it's still good when planted.

## Laboratory Germination vs. Field Emergence

Lab germination is tested under ideal conditions: sterile media, controlled temperature, optimal moisture. Field conditions are never ideal.

Relationship between lab and field germination:
- 95% lab germination typically produces about 78% field emergence
- 85% lab germination typically produces about 65% field emergence
- 75% lab germination typically produces about 50% field emergence
- Below 75% lab germination: field results become unreliable

The seeds that germinate quickest in the lab also produce the highest percentage in the field. Vigor and speed of germination are closely linked.

## How Cheap Seed Is Made

It is easy to produce cheap seed:
- Do not bother eradicating off-types from [[cervantes-seed-production-storage]] fields
- Do not hire skilled plant breeders to maintain pure lines
- Do not patrol surrounding areas to prevent cross-pollination
- Do not hand-select the best plants for seed production
- Let the field grow unsupervised, harvest, and re-use seed from the same bag year after year

Each generation grown this way becomes increasingly variable and less productive. If a variety degrades too badly, the cheap producer simply buys a small quantity of expensive commercial-quality seed of a similar-looking variety and uses it to start their production field — then mislabels the harvest with the heirloom name.

This is why many "heirlooms" from cheap producers bear no resemblance to the original variety.

## Regionality

No single variety performs well everywhere. A seed company serving the entire English-speaking world would need trial grounds in every broad climatic zone. Most don't.

Solomon's [[mollison-climate-zones-permaculture-strategies]]:
- **Short-season**: Northern tier of US states, southern Canada within a few hundred miles of the US border
- **Moderate**: Middle American states, roughly from northern Pennsylvania to southern [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]], [[fukuoka-east-coast-forest-livestock-soil-damage]] of Australia south of Sydney
- **Warm**: Southern US states, Australia from Sydney north to Bundaberg

The likelihood of success is far higher when the seed supplier's trial grounds are in roughly the same climatic zone as your garden.

## Evaluating Seed Companies

A responsible seed company:
1. Conducts [[coleman-seed-selection-and-variety-trials]] in conditions relevant to their customers
2. Maintains germination levels well above minimum standards
3. Tests seed inventory twice yearly
4. Offers varieties adapted to the regions they serve
5. Sources from quality primary growers who maintain genetic purity

Warning signs of a poor seed company:
- Varieties that "work everywhere" (impossible)
- [[cannabis-landrace-and-heirloom-varieties]] at suspiciously low prices
- No information about germination percentages
