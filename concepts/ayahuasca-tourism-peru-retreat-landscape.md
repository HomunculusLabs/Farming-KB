## Geographic Centers

### Iquitos

Iquitos has served as the most popular destination for ayahuasca tourists in Peru for over a decade (as of the early 2000s).

As the largest city in the Peruvian Amazon accessible only by air or river, Iquitos functions as the primary gateway for international visitors seeking ayahuasca experiences.

The city's tourism infrastructure has developed substantially around this demand, with numerous lodges, guides, and facilitators catering specifically to foreign visitors.

Its relative accessibility compared to deeper jungle locations makes it the default entry point for most first-time ayahuasca tourists.

The concentration of services in Iquitos also means intense competition among providers, which can affect both quality and pricing.

### Pucallpa and Yarina

Pucallpa, a city in the Ucayali region on the Ucayali River, serves as another significant base for ayahuasca tourism.

It is less developed as a tourist destination than Iquitos, offering fewer amenities but potentially more direct contact with indigenous practitioners.

The nearby town of Yarina features local shamans who conduct nightly ceremonies in backyard ceremonial huts, attracting up to a couple dozen visitors per session.

These operations are more informal than those in Iquitos, with fewer amenities but a stronger sense of participating in genuine community practice.

### San Francisco

San Francisco is a Shipibo town reachable by a taxi ride from Yarina.

The enormous sign at its entrance proclaims it the "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca," explicitly branding the community as a ceremonial destination for both domestic and international visitors.

Ayahuasca use is deeply embedded in Shipibo family life here -- the article notes that "there are at least a couple of [[icaros]] (sacred chants), tobacco purging, and dietary protocols that are central to the indigenous tradition.

Prices for Peruvians run around two dollars, while tourists typically pay ten to thirty dollars depending on the practitioner, location, and duration of the experience.

This tier offers the most direct contact with indigenous practice but also carries the greatest variability in quality and the highest risk of encountering exploitative practitioners.
