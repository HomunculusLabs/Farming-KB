### Design Priorities

Priority placement decisions relate to slope, soil suitability, and existing landforms. Some priorities are immediately obvious ([[monsoon-sprinkler-fire-control-mollison]], access, [[dighton-fungal-roles-soil-erosion-prevention]]), while others are tackled in stages as time, money, and species permit.

At the end of each stage, both past performance and future evolution should be assessed, building a guide for future adjustments, additions, or extensions.

### The Unique Aspect

Every design should identify and develop a **unique aspect** — a distinctive combination that gives the property its identity and competitive advantage. This might be:

- Unique energy system combinations with surplus for sale
- Social income from recreational or accommodation uses
- Special conditions of existing buildings, vegetation, or soil type
- Social and market contacts of the region
- Special occupant skills (e.g., a chemist who can process plant oils)

A design is a marriage of landscape, people, and skills in the context of a regional society.

## Tests of Good Design

Over a three to six year evolution, a sound [[permaculture]] design should achieve:

- **Reduced need to earn** through conservation of food and energy costs
- **Repair and conservation** of degraded landscapes, buildings, soils, and species at risk
- **Sustainable products** in short-, medium-, and long-term timeframes
- **A unique, preferably essential, service or product** for the region
- **[[effective-working-groups-right-livelihood]]** — good work for occupants in services or goods
- **Sound and safe [[ayahuasca-tourism-legal-status-by-country]]** for the occupiers
- **Harmonious and productive landscape** without wastes or poisons
- **Cooperative and information-rich participation** in regional society

## The Owner-Designer Model

Mollison strongly advocates training **owner-designers** who can apply long-term residential management rather than relying on roving designers. The rationale is clear:

- The success of any design depends on how it is accepted and implemented by people on the ground
- Grand centralized schemes more often result in ruins than in stable ecologies
- It is more effective to educate people to plan for themselves than to maintain expensive permanent planning corps
- Long-term residential observation reveals site dynamics no visiting consultant can detect

A designer or consultant serves best as an aide to initial placements, procedures, and resource listings — not as the primary decision-maker.

## Resource Consciousness

The planning phase emphasizes treating everything as a potential resource:

- **On-site resources:** Clay, rock, weeds, animals, insects, water, slope
- **Interchangeable energies:** Labor, skill, time, cash, and site resources can substitute for each other through exchange
- **Local sourcing:** Best sources of seed and plants are neighbors, public nurseries, and forestry departments
- **Foraged materials:** Second-hand bricks, wood, and roofing reduce costs significantly

Nearby towns, gardens, and parks often reveal rich plant resources. There is no better guide to plant selection than noting district successes — native species and exotics that accompany a recommended plant indicate its likely compatibility.

## Commercial Considerations

Even in commercial plantings, Mollison advises restricting total commercial species to 3–10 reliable plants and trees for easier harvesting and marketing, while the home garden and orchard can maintain far greater diversity of 25–75 species or more.

The progression toward commercial viability follows the nucleus development model: establish self-reliance first, then look for exportable yields or commercial gain. This conservative approach prevents the common failure of over-extending before the productive base is secure.

## Flexibility and Evolution

Design methodologies account for all known intervening factors, but ultimately success comes down to:

- **[[savory-flexibility-in-management]]** — adapting to what actually happens
- **Steering based on trial results** — using real data to adjust course
- **Acting on new information** — remaining open to better approaches
- **Continuing observation** — the designer as perpetual student of the site

Truth, like the world, changes in response to information. The designer who stops observing stops designing effectively.
