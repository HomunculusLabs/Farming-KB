**Financing Public Services Through Land Trusts** — When all residential titles
sell, the monies generated by the sale of the 30 percent of titles vested in the
village trust produce a substantial annual interest yield for [[permaculture-village-development]]. This revenue effectively builds fences, terraces, and
eventually schools, workshops, and alternative energy systems. At approximately
year five of development, when residents have a clear understanding of future
needs, the capital itself can be deployed to install wealth-producing assets for
village employment, including glasshouses, computers, and machinery. By
allotting 30 percent of sites to the body corporate (Trust A), developers ensure
three outcomes: all necessary public services are funded; residents have
effectively paid nothing for land over a 20-year period; and once sales exceed
60 percent of total titles, either capital or interest becomes available for
public services.

**Trust Capital Deployment** — The trust can use its assets in many ways but is
most effective when ensuring either conservation of energy or business
development on site, and when using common wealth to increase local productive
assets. If a credit union has been established, much of the trust capital can be
transferred to a loans account so that residents can draw on it at low interest
for local occupational development. Conscientious use of a credit union by
village residents greatly increases capital flow to [[mollison-village-enterprises-occupations]]. Some capital may be available as housing loans,
though a building cooperative is more effective in this instance. Well managed,
the capital should actually increase over time, yielding a large annual fund for
village use.

**Trust Income and Maintenance** — The Trust always needs income for maintenance
of roads, fences, water supply, fire control, and other site factors. This can
be raised through small charges for leases and loans to residents, by charging
an hourly rate for lease, use, or hire of machines and facilities, and by a 10
percent levy on net profits of locally-funded cooperatives. This levy mirrors
the Mondragon cooperative system in Spain, which funds its banks, schools, and
research facilities through the same mechanism, and applies only to net profits
of trust-funded cooperatives.

**Community Services Council** — A Community Services Council at any new
community or village with common lands may be elected to administer policy on
publicly owned or common assets, collect lease monies on utility plots,
administer funds for schools and medical services, and ensure that rates are
used in maintaining roads, water supplies, and other public services. Community
services in any village can encompass fire control, land leasing of common
properties for business use, public reserves and sports grounds, ambulance and
health services including cemetery administration, education and educational
facilities, community centre use and hire, power and water supply, forest
management, and tourism promotion if desired. Some of these are income-
producing, some subject to state aid or tax immunity, and some are income-
consuming. The Council needs to balance costs, allot land and assets for income
to service groups, and ensure common property and rights are fairly assigned and
well managed.

**Council Composition and Governance** — Council should be comprised of a
selection of those actively involved in the service areas, not an uninvolved
group. Each area of action can have a basically independent management sub-
group, reporting to Council regularly. These sub-groups can appoint one of their
number to represent that group on Council. Council needs to meet monthly or even
less frequently, with sub-groups handling everyday business within their budget
and allotted areas of operation. A Council can call for and act on submissions
or reports relating to specific policies and strategies. Sub-groups can raise
their own funds as well as access public funds, may have an active business
management role in income-producing areas, and supply workers to carry out those
businesses.

**Models for Community Income** — Income is needed for continued upgrading of
public services. Several models exist: a yearly levy on each family or property
collected by the Community Council, which would supplement normal Shire Council
rates but may create hardship; a tithe system where individuals, groups, and
businesses tithe 10 percent of net profit to public services, as all
[[mondragon-cooperatives]] do, meaning rates would be on a sliding scale rather
than flat; a local currency printed by the Community Services Council and backed
by a valuable community resource such as firewood or clean bottled water; and
user-pays charges by schools, community centres, and health services, offset
against costs on a per-use basis, which should be used in conjunction with
another model since it covers only facilities not universally used.

**Local Currency Systems** — The Community Services Council can print its own
currency backed by a valuable community resource to fund projects, new
buildings, and other needs. A timber forest planted early on and harvested at
various stages of maturity can produce most income needed for subsequent years,
as demonstrated by some modern towns which have developed social forestry. Local
currency operations are detailed in Mollison's broader treatment of [[informal-and-formal-community-economics]].

**Potential Enterprises and Occupations** — In a 50-household village of
approximately 100 adults, residents can self-employ in service to the village
and nearby districts. Costs are highest in three areas: food, energy, and
transport. Approximately 20 adults can support one adult providing food supply,
so five to seven families can earn a living from food provision including open-
air market gardens, glasshouse crops, a cooperative store, livestock and fishery
enterprises, and part-time livelihoods from cafés, food processing, and baked
goods. Two to five families can earn livings from energy system design and
retrofit, hardware and appliance sales, and installation and maintenance of
[[village-energy-systems-and-conversion-efficiency]]. Two to four livings are
indicated in vehicle service, repairs, maintenance, bulk fuel supply, fuel
production, and fleet leasing. Two people can handle financing, accounting, and
credit union operations. One or two can offer medical and pharmaceutical
services. A plumber-builder-mechanic can serve village establishment and
maintenance needs. These essential occupations initially fund about 20 of 50
households, with more as manufactures develop.

**[[enterprise-development-mushroom-growers]] Beyond Essentials** — Beyond
essential occupations, potential village enterprises include glasshouse and
special crops, cut flowers, herbs, pharmaceutical production, processed dairy,
fish and aquaculture; teaching for children, adult education, and applied
workshops; craft, medical, or design workshops; a small business service centre
employing three to four people; consultancy in architecture, landscape, and
design; nursery and plant material provision; animal breeding programmes;
computer services, programming, and databases; publishing; trade distribution,
wholesale, and import-export; craft products from metal, wood, pottery, and art;
income from guests, visitors, and site tours; and physical therapy and
paramedical services.

**Capital and Management Requirements** — All enterprises depend on two basic
factors: sufficient capital for startup and development, and careful management
including accounting, forward planning, market research, product development,
sensible costing and staffing, correct lease and rental agreements, and
appropriate legal structures. Many small enterprises can pre-sell their wares
for initial capital, and many others need no capital to start but may need time
to develop. A very reliable early income can be made from information through
workshops and classes, although workshop income fades over time as students gain
skills themselves.

**Job Sharing and Polycultural Livelihoods** — It should be the long-term aim of
any village to own and operate its own employment enterprises. In past times, a
villager rarely held just one job — the banker was also a part-time barber,
trader, and perhaps gardener. Sharing even simple occupations is wise so that
individuals have occupational shares in two to three enterprises. In this way,
total failure is unlikely, as is unemployment. Holidays can be taken, and wet
days spent on indoor work. Job-sharing should be the rule, not the exception.
Although the total village structure is complex, the work of any individual is
simple, much like a plant in a polyculture.
