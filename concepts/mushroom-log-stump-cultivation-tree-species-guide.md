## The General Principle

The ideal trees for stump or log cultivation are those that the mushroom species grows upon naturally. The simplest way to determine a mushroom's natural host is to consult a reputable field guide's description of the preferred habitat.

However, many saprophytic mushrooms are opportunistic — given the opportunity, they can adapt to wood types not ordinarily found in their natural domain. This flexibility allows cultivators to work with locally available trees rather than importing specific species.

## Spawn Types for Log and Stump Inoculation

Three primary spawn types are used for wood inoculation, each with advantages and disadvantages.

Dowel (plug) spawn consists of wooden dowels impregnated with mycelium. Its advantage is staying power — the dowels survive longer than sawdust spawn, which is quickly consumed by mycelium or munched by insects. The dowel's longer survival time gives mycelium a better chance of infecting the log. For homeowners, gardeners, and small-scale operations, dowel spawn is recommended.

Sawdust spawn is less expensive by weight but less dense and prone to falling out of inoculation holes unless packed tightly. In large commercial operations, automated sawdust inoculation equipment makes this the preferred choice for speed and economy. Some shiitake growers in Korea use equipment that automatically inserts sawdust spawn and caps the hole with Styrofoam.

Wafer spawn, developed by innovative Japanese growers, consists of thin cellulose wafers grown through with mycelium that are inserted into cuts in the log or stump. Equipment and wafer dimensions are sized for single-pass insertion.

After inoculation, most log growers cover holes with melted wax — cheese wax, beeswax, or less preferably paraffin — to prevent desiccation and contamination.

## Experimental Approach

Stamets strongly encourages experimentation with local tree species but recommends conducting mini-trials before investing significant time and money. Inoculate a few logs of each candidate species and observe colonization speed, contamination rates, and fruiting success before scaling up.

If cultivators have successes with tree species not commonly listed in references, documenting and sharing those experiences contributes to the collective knowledge of the mycological community.
