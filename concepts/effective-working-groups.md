## Effective Working Groups

> Large boards and assemblies are the refuge of the incompetent; small, responsible groups are the vehicle of the effective.

## The Problem with Large Groups

One of the most persistent failures in organizational design is the assumption that large
gatherings produce better decisions. [[fukuoka-philosophy-in-practice]], groups of seven or more people become
impractical and deeply frustrating for everyone involved. Boards, assemblies, and
consensus-based groups at this scale suffer from chronic inefficiency: meetings stretch
on for hours, decisions are postponed or watered down, and individual responsibility
is diffused to the point of meaninglessness.

The frustration is not merely logistical — it is structural. As group size grows, the
number of interpersonal relationships increases exponentially, and the cost of
coordination overwhelms the capacity for productive action. People disengage, dominant
personalities take over, and the group's output degrades despite the time invested.

## Two Forms of Tyranny

[[bill-mollison]] identified two fundamental failures that plague group decision-making,
each arising from a different democratic ideal taken to its pathological extreme:

### The Tyranny of Majority (Voting)

When decisions are made by majority vote, the minority is governed by the preferences
of the majority. This sounds reasonable in principle, but in practice it means that
a significant portion of the group is compelled to act against its own judgment. The
majority may be uninformed, hasty, or driven by social dynamics [[permaculture-integrate-rather-than-segregate]] merit.
Voting rewards persuasion and coalition-building over competence and produces decisions
that satisfy no one fully while alienating many.

### The Tyranny of Minority (Consensus)

Consensus decision-making appears more inclusive — no action proceeds without universal
agreement. However, this grants effective veto power to any single dissenter. A single
individual can block an entire group from acting, regardless of the merits of the
proposal. The result is either paralysis or a culture of [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] coercion where
dissenters are socially punished until they comply. Consensus rewards obstruction and
produces the least common denominator of every decision.

## The Solution: Abolish Decision Meetings

Mollison's radical but practical proposal is to **abolish decision meetings entirely**.
Instead of gathering people to debate and vote, replace the meeting structure with a
task-allocation system based on two categories:

### Volunteer Allotment (Creative & Constructive Work)

Tasks that require creativity, design, construction, or innovation are offered to the
group, and individuals volunteer based on their interest and competence. No one is
assigned creative work against their will — motivation comes from genuine engagement
with the task. Volunteers take full responsibility for their allotment and report
results rather than seeking permission.

### Roster System (Maintenance Work)

Recurring maintenance tasks — the essential work that keeps systems running — are
organized on a roster. People take turns performing these duties on a scheduled basis.
The roster is published in advance, is fair and transparent, and requires no meetings
to administer. Everyone contributes to maintenance, and no one is burdened
disproportionately.

## The [[troika-approach-mollison]]

The core organizational unit in Mollison's framework is the **troika** — a group of
one to three people assigned to a specific function. This tiny group has full
executive authority over its domain and can act immediately without calling meetings,
seeking consensus, or waiting for approval.

The troika approach works because:

- **Speed**: One to three people can make decisions and act on them within minutes.
- **Clarity**: Responsibility is unambiguous — everyone knows who is accountable.
- **Commitment**: People who choose a function own it completely.
- **Parallelism**: Multiple troikas operate simultaneously on different functions.

Each troika manages independently within the bounds of a general plan agreed upon by
the broader community. The plan sets direction and constraints; the troikas determine
the specifics of execution.

## Group Size Guidelines

Mollison provides clear guidance on the functional limits of group size at different
scales of human organization:

| Group Size | Function |
|---|---|
| **1–3** | Executive decisions; the troika for fast, accountable action |
| **4–6** | Cooperative work groups; ideal for hands-on collaboration |
| **7–20** | Social and recreational gatherings; upper limit for informal cohesion |
| **30–40** | Minimal viable community covering most [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]] |
| **200–300** | Population needed for genetic [[grass-seed-substrate-water-absorption-variability-and-calibration]] resilience |
| **300–600** | Upper limit of group identity; beyond this, people become strangers |
| **1,000–5,000** | Bioregional groups; multiple communities sharing a landscape |
| **7,000–40,000** | Towns; the upper practical limit for [[village-infrastructure-and-integrated-settlement-design]] |

These numbers reflect the scale at which human social [[mckenna-human-cognition-and-the-stoned-age]] organizational
capacity naturally function. Exceeding them requires bureaucracy, hierarchy, and
formal systems that undermine the direct, personal accountability that makes small
groups effective.

## Work-Netting vs. Networking

Mollison draws a critical distinction between two models of human connection:

- **Networking** is social — it builds relationships, shares information, and creates
  awareness. It is valuable but does not produce work.

- **Work-netting** is productive — it connects people through shared tasks,
  mutual obligations, and reciprocal labor exchange. People in a work-net are linked
  by what they do for each other, not merely by what they know about each other.

Effective communities rely on work-netting as their primary organizing principle. The
social bonds that sustain the community emerge naturally from productive collaboration
rather than being maintained as a separate activity. When people work together on real
tasks, trust and affection follow. When people socialize without working together,
the relationships remain shallow and fragile.

## Key Principles

1. **Small groups act; large groups talk.** Keep executive functions in troikas of
   1–3 people.
2. **Replace meetings with task allocation.** Volunteer creative work; roster
   maintenance work.
3. **Distribute authority, not responsibility.** Each group owns its function
   completely.
4. **Plan broadly, execute locally.** The community sets direction; troikas determine
   method.
5. **Build work-nets, not just networks.** Connect people through productive exchange,
   not just social contact.
6. **Respect scale limits.** Organizational structures must match the scale at which
   human relationships actually function.
