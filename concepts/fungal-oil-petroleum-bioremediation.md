# Mycoremediation of Oil and Petroleum Hydrocarbons

Oil spills and petroleum contamination represent some of the most widespread environmental pollution challenges globally. While bacterial biodegradation of petroleum hydrocarbons has been extensively studied, fungi play important complementary roles through their ability to degrade both aliphatic and aromatic fractions, including the most recalcitrant high-molecular-weight components.

## Composition of Petroleum Pollutants

Petroleum-contaminated sites contain a complex mixture of hydrocarbon classes:
- **Aliphatic hydrocarbons** -- straight-chain and branched alkanes (relatively easily degraded)
- **Cycloalkanes** -- cycloparaffins of varying ring sizes
- **Monocyclic aromatic hydrocarbons** (MAHs) -- BTEX compounds (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylenes)
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons** (PAHs) -- the most persistent fraction (2-6 fused rings)
- **Resins and asphaltenes** -- polar, high-molecular-weight components (most recalcitrant)

The relative proportions affect remediation strategy. Alkanes are most biodegradable; asphaltenes are least.

## Fungal Degradation of Aliphatic Hydrocarbons

### Alkane-Degrading Fungi
Numerous fungi can utilize aliphatic hydrocarbons as sole carbon sources:
- **Cunninghamella elegans** -- degrades n-alkanes from C10 to C32; optimal chain length C14-C16
- **Aspergillus niger, A. fumigatus, A. terreus** -- alkane oxidation capabilities
- **Cladosporium, Penicillium, Fusarium** -- common soil isolates with alkane-degrading ability
- **Paecilomyces lilacinus** -- shows activity against long-chain alkanes

### Mechanisms of Alkane Degradation
- **Cytochrome P450 monooxygenase** -- introduces oxygen at terminal or subterminal positions
- **Secondary alcohol dehydrogenase** -- converts alcohols to ketones
- Further oxidation yields fatty acids that enter beta-oxidation
- Terminal oxidation yields primary alcohols, aldehydes, then fatty acids
- Subterminal oxidation yields secondary alcohols and ketones

## Fungal Degradation of Aromatic Components

The aromatic fraction (BTEX and PAHs) is the most environmentally concerning due to toxicity and persistence. Fungi degrade these through:

- **Cytochrome P450 oxidation** -- converts BTEX and PAHs to epoxides, dihydrodiols, and phenols
- **Ligninolytic enzymes** -- [[white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization]] use peroxidases and laccases for non-specific aromatic oxidation
- **Ring cleavage** -- some fungi can open aromatic rings and ultimately mineralize PAHs to CO2

The BTEX fraction is discussed in detail in [[fungal-degradation-chlorophenols-btex]].

## Oil Spill Case Studies

### Exxon Valdez (1989)
The most studied oil spill bioremediation effort provided key lessons:
- Biostimulation with fertilizer nutrients enhanced biodegradation of oil on shorelines
- Indigenous microbial populations (both bacterial and fungal) responded to nutrient addition
- The experience established that nutrient delivery to intertidal zones was feasible
- Oil composition changes over time favored more resistant (aromatic) fractions

### General Field Experience
Field applications have demonstrated:
- Nutrient addition (biostimulation) is often more effective than organism addition (bioaugmentation)
- [[fungal-bioremediation-case-studies]] of fungal inoculation have shown mixed results
- Environmental variables (temperature, oxygen, moisture) are critical controlling factors
- Oil type and weathering state significantly affect biodegradability

## Role of Fungi in Petroleum-Contaminated Soils

### Advantages Over Bacteria
- **Enzymatic versatility** -- fungi produce both intracellular (P450) and extracellular (ligninolytic) enzyme systems
- **Hyphal exploration** -- mycelia can penetrate soil aggregates and reach trapped oil
- **Tolerance of low water activity** -- many hydrocarbonoclastic fungi tolerate dry conditions
- **Production of biosurfactants** -- some fungi produce compounds that enhance hydrocarbon solubility

### Fungal-Bacterial Consortia
In natural petroleum-contaminated environments, fungi and bacteria function as part of complex consortia:
- Bacteria often dominate early-stage degradation of alkanes
- Fungi contribute more to later-stage degradation of aromatic and polar fractions
- Synergistic interactions may improve overall degradation rates

## Bioremediation Strategies for Petroleum Sites

### Land Treatment (Biopiling)
- Contaminated soil is placed in piles or windrows
- Nutrients, moisture, and oxygen are managed through aeration and irrigation
- Composting amendments (straw, sawdust) support fungal growth
- Temperature management promotes thermophilic degradation

### Bioaugmentation Approaches
- [[formulation-and-delivery-of-fungal-inocula]] can be added to contaminated soils
- Lignocellulosic carriers (sawdust, wood chips) serve dual purposes as inoculum carrier and fungal substrate
- [[singh-bioaugmentation-vs-biostimulation-fungal-strategies]] must account for competition with indigenous organisms

## Monitoring Petroleum Bioremediation

Effective monitoring tracks:
- Total petroleum hydrocarbon (TPH) concentration changes
- Individual hydrocarbon fraction analysis (alkanes, aromatics, resins, asphaltenes)
- Toxicity assays (Microtox, seed germination tests)
- Microbial community analysis (fungal biomass, enzyme activity measurements)
- Oxygen consumption and CO2 evolution rates
