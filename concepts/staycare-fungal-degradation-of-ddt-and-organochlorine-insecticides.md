# Fungal Degradation of DDT and Organochlorine Insecticides

Organochlorine insecticides represent some of the most persistent and
environmentally problematic synthetic chemicals ever produced. Dichloro-
diphenyl-trichloroethane (DDT) and related compounds were widely used
as agricultural and public health insecticides from the 1940s until
their ban in most countries during the 1970s and 1980s. Their extreme
persistence, lipophilicity, and tendency to biomagnify through food
chains have created a legacy of contaminated soils and sediments that
remain in need of remediation.

## Properties of Organochlorine Insecticides

The organochlorine insecticides share several characteristics that make
them particularly challenging for bioremediation:

- **High chemical stability**: The carbon-chlorine bonds in these
  compounds are resistant to hydrolysis, photolysis, and biological
  degradation.
- **Low water solubility and high lipophilicity**: These properties
  promote bioaccumulation in fatty tissues and strong adsorption to
  soil organic matter, reducing bioavailability for microbial attack.
- **Long environmental half-lives**: DDT has an estimated soil
  half-life of 3-15 years; aldrin approximately 3 years; and
  dieldrin approximately 8 years under field conditions.
- **Biomagnification potential**: Organochlorines concentrate
  progressively through food chains, reaching highest levels in
  top predators.

## DDT Degradation by fungal-bioremediation systems.

Optimal DDT degradation by P. chrysosporium occurs on cellulose or
starch as the primary carbon source. In soil system experiments, a
mixture of corn cobs and silt loam (4:1 ratio) at 40% moisture content
achieved significant DDT degradation over approximately 90 days.
The fungus can also degrade DDE (dichloro-diphenyl-dichloroethylene),
a major persistent metabolite of DDT found in aged contaminated soils.

## Degradation of Other Organochlorine Insecticides

Several other organochlorine insecticides have been investigated for
fungal degradation:

- **Lindane (gamma-hexachlorocyclohexane)**: Trametes hirsutus
  demonstrated faster degradation of lindane than P. chrysosporium.
  Lindane is one of the more water-soluble organochlorines, which
  may enhance its bioavailability for fungal enzymatic attack.
- **Aldrin and dieldrin**: These cyclodiene insecticides are extremely
  persistent, with field half-lives of approximately 3 and 8 years
  respectively. [[aspergillus-niger]] has demonstrated the ability to
  metabolize endosulfan isomers, representing a potential route for
  detoxification of this commonly used organochlorine insecticide.
- **Methoxychlor, chlordane, heptachlor, and mirex**: These
  additional organochlorine insecticides have received limited
  study with respect to fungal degradation but are potential targets
  for white rot [[staycare-composting-as-a-fungal-bioremediation-strategy]] given the non-specific
  oxidative mechanisms involved.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Organochlorine Degradation

White rot fungi degrade organochlorine insecticides primarily through
their ligninolytic enzyme systems. The non-specific free-radical
mechanism of [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]] and Pollutant Degradation and
[[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] in Fungal Bioremediation enables
oxidation of the [[fungal-chlorinated-aromatic-degradation]] structures without requiring
specific enzyme-substrate recognition. This non-specific mechanism is
particularly valuable for organochlorine mixtures commonly found at
contaminated sites.

Dechlorination is a critical step in organochlorine degradation, and
fungal ligninolytic peroxidases have been shown to catalyze oxidative
dechlorination reactions. The removal of chlorine atoms from the
molecular structure reduces both the toxicity and persistence of these
compounds.
