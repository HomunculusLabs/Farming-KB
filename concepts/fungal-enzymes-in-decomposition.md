# Fungal Enzymes in Decomposition

Fungi produce a remarkable arsenal of extracellular enzymes that enable them
to decompose complex plant polymers, including cellulose, hemicellulose, and
lignin. These fungal enzymes in decomposition are central to global
carbon cycling, forest ecosystem functioning, and the applied fields of
[[bioremediation-fungal-vs-bacterial]] and mushroom cultivation. Understanding these
enzyme systems is essential for improving both [[mycoremediation-techniques]]
and [[mushroom-substrate-preparation]] for commercial production.

## Overview of Depolymerizing Enzymes

The biochemistry of enzymes that depolymerize plant cell wall polymers is
well characterized for hydrolytic enzymes that attack carbohydrates. These
enzymes tend to be specific to particular bond chemistries; for instance,
beta-1,4 specificity is required to hydrolyze cellulose, and alpha-1,4
specificity for starch hydrolysis. In contrast, enzymes that attack the
irregular lignin polymer function through non-specific free radical mechanisms
rather than targeted bond cleavage.

## Cellulase Complex

Knowledge of cellulose-degrading enzymes is based mainly on studies of
Trichoderma spp., the most prolific sources of cellulases known. When
enzymes from wood-rotting basidiomycetes have been screened for cellulases,
their composition has closely resembled those of Trichoderma reesei, with
five endoglucanases, one exoglucanase, and two beta-1,4-glucosidases
identified.

Cellulase is a complex of enzyme activities that includes:
- **Exoglucanases (cellobiohydrolases):** Composed of a cellulose-binding
  domain linked to a catalytic domain through a proline-rich linker region.
- **Endocellulases:** Act on internal bonds of the cellulose chain.
- **Beta-glucosidases:** Convert cellobiose to glucose.

These act in synergy to depolymerize cellulose fibrils, releasing glucose
and cellobiose. Glucose is readily taken up by the fungus, providing carbon
for energy and growth. Feedback control is exerted by glucose and sucrose
through catabolite repression at concentrations of 1 g/L, while induction
occurs with 1 mg/L cellobiose or cellulose.

## Hemicellulases

Enzymes for hydrolysis of hemicellulose have been identified in many
wood-rotting fungi. Hardwoods contain xylan, and fungal species colonizing
them produce xylanases. Other hemicelluloses are hydrolyzed by mannanases,
galactosidases, and glucosidases. These enzymes have very similar
characteristics to the cellulase complex in that different enzymes attack
exo- and endo-hemicellulose.

## Ligninolytic Enzymes

Unlike cellulose, lignin is not a symmetrical, well-ordered polymer with a
single repeating bond. It is difficult to envision a single enzyme capable
of depolymerizing such an irregular structure. White rot fungi therefore
employ non-specific enzymes that function mainly through production of free
radicals capable of attacking a wide range of organic molecules.

### Lignin Peroxidases (LiP)

The first enzyme shown to attack lignin-type compounds was lignin peroxidase,
isolated from Phanerochaete Chrysosporium. LiP is a haem-containing
peroxidase with unusually high redox potential. It is distinctive in its
ability to oxidize methoxyl substituents on non-phenolic aromatic rings by
generating cation radicals. The pH optimum is below 3.0, and veratryl
alcohol, produced as a secondary metabolite by many white rot fungi, enhances
LiP activity by protecting it from inactivation by excess hydrogen peroxide.

### Manganese-Dependent Peroxidases (MnP)

MnP is generally considered essential for lignin degradation in vivo. In
addition to the peroxidase catalytic cycle, Mn(II) is converted to Mn(III)
during the reaction. Mn(III)-chelates with organic acids such as oxalate are
small molecules able to diffuse into wood cell wall pores inaccessible to
enzymes. The rate of lignin degradation in vivo is controlled by the
slowest reaction step, which may be availability of hydrogen peroxide or
Mn(II) rather than the catalytic rate of MnP.

### Laccases

Laccase is a copper-containing enzyme found in several isoforms, including
blue and yellow laccases. The majority of white rot fungi produce laccase,
frequently as the dominant extracellular enzyme. In the presence of oxygen,
laccase converts phenolic groups to quinone radicals then quinones in a
multistep oxidation process. In the presence of low-molecular-mass mediators,
laccase can also react with non-phenolic substituents at a higher redox level.

## Mechanisms of Action In Vivo

Immunogold-cytochemical labelling studies have shown that enzymes such as
LiP, MnP, laccases, and cellulases are too large to penetrate intact,
secondary wood cell walls. They remain close to the surface of fungal hyphae
or adhere to the inner surface of the wood cell wall. Degradation occurs by
surface interaction, but initiation of decay can occur at a distance from the
hyphae, probably involving diffusible small molecular mass molecules such as
oxalate, hydrogen peroxide, iron, and manganese ions.

Brown rot fungi, which do not degrade lignin, use a fundamentally different
mechanism for cellulose hydrolysis. They produce complete breakage of
amorphous cellulose fibrils by generating hydroxyl radicals from the Fenton
reaction of hydrogen peroxide with iron. White rots, in contrast, cause a
progressive decay from the fibril surfaces.

## Applications in Bioremediation

The [[enzyme-based-bioremediation]] potential of fungal enzymes extends well
beyond natural decomposition. The free radical mechanism of ligninolytic
enzymes enables them to transform structurally diverse pollutants including
polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, chlorophenols, polychlorinated biphenyls,
dyes, and explosives. The non-specific nature of these reactions means that
enzymes do not need to be adapted to particular contaminants, making them
powerful tools for environmental cleanup.
