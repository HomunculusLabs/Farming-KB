## Mycorrhizal Types and Their Role in Plant Nutrient Uptake

## Overview

Mycorrhizae are symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots that are found in approximately **95% of all vascular plants** (Brundrett, 1991). These associations have co-evolved with their host plants and represent one of the most important mutualisms in terrestrial ecosystems. By extending the effective absorptive surface area of root systems, [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] dramatically enhance plant access to mineral nutrients, particularly phosphorus, from soil.

## Mycorrhizal Association and Primary Production

Mycorrhizal fungi contribute disproportionately to ecosystem primary production relative to their biomass:

- Vogt et al. (1982) showed that mycorrhizal fungi represent only **~1% of total ecosystem biomass** but account for **14–15% of net primary production**
- When combined with fine root biomass: **45% of NPP in young forests** and **75% in mature stands**
- Pankow et al. (1991) suggested that mycorrhizal importance increases during the protective/mature phase of forest succession, when they control nutrient cycling from decomposing organic matter back into plants and reduce nutrient loss from the ecosystem

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM)

- **Host plants**: Herbaceous plants, grasses, and some trees (especially tropical)
- **Fungal associates**: Primarily zygomycetes (Glomeromycota)
- **Structural features**: Formation of **arbuscules** — tree-like branching hyphal structures within root cortical cells that maximize the surface area for nutrient exchange between plant and fungus. Some species also form **vesicles** — swollen hyphal storage structures in cortical cells
- **Biodiversity pattern**: A **large number of plant species** associate with a **relatively small diversity of fungal species**
- **Identification**: Based on spore anatomy (produced within or outside roots), as these fungi do not produce large fruiting bodies

### Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)

- **Host plants**: Coniferous and deciduous trees
- **Fungal associates**: Primarily basidiomycetes and ascomycetes, some zygomycetes
- **Structural features**:
  - **Hartig net**: Fungal hyphae penetrate between (not into) cortical cells, extending to the endodermis
  - **Sheath/mantle**: A covering of fungal tissue on the root surface, ranging from loose hyphal wefts to highly organized pseudoparenchymatous structures
  - **Extraradical hyphae and rhizomorphs**: Mycelial strands extending into soil, sometimes with conductive elements analogous to plant vascular tissue
- **Biodiversity pattern**: A **limited number of plant species** associate with a **huge diversity of fungal species** (thousands of species)
- **Identification**: Based on mantle structure, branching patterns, and emanating hyphal characteristics

### Ectendomycorrhizae

- **Host plants**: Certain tree species
- **Features**: Combine characteristics of both ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal structures

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae

- **Host plants**: Ericaceae, Empetraceae, Epicaridaceae, Diapensiaceae, and Prionotocaceae
- **Habitat**: Sclerophyllous evergreens in nitrogen- and phosphorus-poor environments (bogs, heathlands)
- **Structural features**: Hyphal **coils** within cortical cells (not arbuscules). Host roots have very fine roots with a single cortical cell layer
- **Fungal associates**: Few genera, including *Hymenoscyphus* and *Oidiodendron*
- **Ecological role**: Enable plants to access nitrogen and phosphorus in extremely nutrient-poor acidic soils

### Arbutoid Mycorrhizae

- **Host plants**: *Arbutus* and related genera
- **Features**: Similar to ericoid mycorrhizae with hyphal coils in cortical cells
- **Closely related** to both ericoid and ectomycorrhizal types

### Orchidaceous Mycorrhizae

- **Host plants**: Orchids
- **Key feature**: **Obligate** association — the fungal partner (usually *Rhizoctonia*) is transported within the seed and is essential for seed germination and initial plant establishment
- **Represents the most co-evolved** mycorrhizal relationship

## Mechanisms of Enhanced Nutrient Uptake

### Replacement of Root Hairs by Fungal Hyphae

During mycorrhizal development, root hair development is typically suppressed. Fungal hyphae replace root hairs with two major advantages:

1. **Smaller diameter**: Fungal hyphae are thinner than root hairs, allowing them to penetrate smaller soil pores and access nutrients in microsites unavailable to roots
2. **Greater exploration distance**: Hyphae extend farther from the root surface, exploring a larger soil volume

### Quantitative Evidence of Enhanced Absorptive Capacity

Rousseau et al. (1994) provided compelling data for ectomycorrhizal pine seedlings:

| Parameter | *Pisolithus tinctorius* | *Cenococcum geophilum* | Non-mycorrhizal |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Mycorrhizal infection (%) | 69.5 | 66.5 | 0 |
| Hyphal area (mm² g⁻¹ soil) | 33.8 | 28.1 | 1.5 |
| Total fungal area (mm² g⁻¹ soil) | 47.4 | 28.1 | 1.5 |
| Total fungal length (m g⁻¹ soil) | 6.78 | 2.8 | 0.28 |

The extraradical mycelium accounted for only **5% of the absorbing system dry weight** but provided **75% of the potential absorbing area** and over **99% of the absorbing length**.

### Extended Phosphorus Depletion Zones

Using radiotracer phosphate techniques, Nye and Tinker (1977) and Owusu-Bennoah and Wild (1979) demonstrated that:

- Mycorrhizal roots create **larger phosphorus depletion zones** than non-mycorrhizal roots
- Arbuscular mycorrhizae extend clover root depletion zones from **10 mm to 20 mm**
- In some cases, depletion zones can extend up to **110 mm** from the root surface
- Hyphal extension rates vary by species: *Acaulospora laevis* ~20 mm/week vs. *Glomus* spp. <10 mm/week

### Root Architecture Modification

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] alter host root system architecture. Berta et al. (1993) showed that mycorrhizal plants produce significantly more lateral roots than non-mycorrhizal plants, providing dual benefits:

1. **Increased root branching** expands the root system within the soil
2. **Fungal hyphal exploration** extends nutrient access beyond the root zone

## Cost-Benefit Analysis of Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

The nutritional benefit of mycorrhizae is not always worth the carbon investment. Fitter (1991) demonstrated:

| Cost Component | Value |
|---------------|-------|
| Mycorrhizal fungus biomass | 10–20% of root biomass |
| Growth/maintenance cost of fungus | 0.1–1% of root biomass per day |
| Root maintenance cost | ~1.5% of root biomass per day |

The benefit is primarily realized when **plant phosphorus demand exceeds readily available soil supplies**. During periods of adequate soil nutrient availability, the maintenance cost of the mycorrhizal symbiont approaches the cost of root maintenance alone.

## Ectomycorrhizal Rhizomorphs

Some ectomycorrhizal fungi develop specialized **rhizomorphs** or **mycelial strands** — differentiated hyphal structures with conductive elements analogous to plant vascular tissue. These structures (Duddridge et al., 1980) enable:

- Long-distance transport of nutrients and water
- Connection between multiple root tips
- Resource sharing across the mycelial network
- Extended influence of the fungal partner far from the root surface
