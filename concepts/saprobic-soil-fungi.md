# Saprobic Soil Fungi

## Overview

Soil fungi are among the most diverse and ecologically important groups of microorganisms. They drive decomposition, nutrient cycling, and soil formation processes. Despite their importance, comprehensive inventories of soil fungal diversity remain challenging due to methodological limitations, enormous species richness, and the difficulty of detecting cryptic species. This page synthesizes principles from Chapter 13 of "Biodiversity of Fungi" (Bills, Christensen, and Frisvad).

## Distribution Patterns

### Geographic and Vegetation Associations

Certain vegetation types are characterized by distinctive assemblages of soil fungi:

- **Cold regions** (Antarctic, arctic, alpine, boreal): Cylindrocarpon, Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, certain Penicillium species, and sterile strains are common
- **Cold bogs and coniferous swamps**: Yeasts, yeastlike forms, Tolypocladium, Trichoderma, sterile dematiaceous forms, certain penicillia and eurotialean fungi prevail
- **Beech forests** (North America and Europe): Striking similarities in principal saprobic fungal species
- **Desert soils**: Low propagule densities but high species diversity; Aspergillus species may account for up to 20% of total species isolated
- **Tropical forest soils**: Distinct mycotas with often abrupt litter-soil interface transitions

### Soil Horizons

- **A1 horizon**: Highest water content, organic matter, fungal abundance, and diversity in areas of moderate to abundant precipitation
- **Arid/semiarid areas**: Subsurface moist horizons harbor substantially different assemblages from surface soil
- **Litter layer**: Transition between living plant community and soil; composition tied closely to plant community
- **Humus-soil interface**: Plant-associated species intergrade with soil species; exceptionally species-rich
- **Mineral soil depth**: In humid tropics, species richness declines rapidly with increasing depth
- **Vertical succession**: Fungi exhibit succession downward through litter and soil horizons
- **Pleistocene relicts**: Fungi characteristic of tundra communities found at 1m depth in Wyoming intermountain basin

### Seasonal Variation

- Relative abundances of common species in forest and grassland soils vary seasonally
- Seasonal variation less conspicuous in agricultural soils and some boreal conifer forests
- Soil mycobiota change over successional time
- Intensive and extensive sampling necessary for both spatial and temporal inventory

### Chytridiomycetes in Soil

- Distributed widely from Arctic to tropics; abundant when specialized enrichment methods used
- Species diversity increases from higher latitudes toward equatorial regions
- Distributions sensitive to moisture, ion content, pH, and temperature
- Typically extracted from top 5 cm; vertical zonation not established
- Different soil types harbor totally different assemblages despite sharing common species
- Number of species detected increases with number of samples

### Marine Sediments

- Filamentous fungi isolated from estuarine, mangrove, and marine sediments
- Almost all taxa (except a few facultative marine species) appear to be terrestrial soil fungi
- Terrestrial propagules accumulate in inactive spore bank due to mycostatic effect of seawater
- No data available on fungal communities of "aerial soils" in forest canopies

## Sampling and Isolation

### Preliminary Planning

- Clear definition of habitats
- Efficient sampling design
- Exploration of magnitude of species diversity
- Determination of minimum sample sizes
- Scale of sampling determined by inventory goals

### Sample Collection

- Collect from upper surface of mineral soil (A1 horizon) or equivalent
- Remove litter and humus from ~20 cm2 area
- Sterilize digging instrument in 70% ethanol before collecting 6-10 plugs (10-30g)
- Place in labeled sterile bags
- Use cooler (0-10C) for transport in warm weather
- Process within hours or few days; cold storage or freezing if necessary

### Isolation Capacity

- Maximum manageable isolates: ~2,000-3,000 by two full-time experienced researchers
- Based on familiarity with high proportion of genera and species
- New species continue to be encountered indefinitely regardless of effort
- Determination of all taxa in a given soil type seems impossible

### Species-Isolate Curves

- Asymptotes range from 60-150 species for 600-1,000 isolates (temperate or tropical soils)
- Minimum of 600-1,000 isolates per site recommended for comprehensive species lists
- For quantitative comparative studies: 180-250 isolates per site sufficient for reliable data on principal species
- Common taxa of homogeneous vegetation type determinable with 200-250 strains from 6-10 samples

### Principal Isolation Methods

Three standard soil plating methods for comparability:

1. **Warcup soil plates**: Soil particles placed directly on agar surface
2. **Suspension plating**: Soil suspended in solution, dilutions plated
3. **Particle filtration**: Soil particles filtered and placed on media

Key principles:
- Adequate separation of propagules from soil and each other
- Physical, nutritional, and chemical means to limit colony expansion
- Sublethal doses of fungitoxic agents to restrict hyphal growth without eliminating slow-growing species
- Low-nutrient media and moderate temperatures to discourage weedy mold contaminants

### Selective and Exploratory Techniques

General isolation approach can be complemented by:

- Selective media targeting specific taxonomic groups
- Baiting techniques for zoosporic fungi and root pathogens
- Specialized enrichment methods for chytrids
- Temperature manipulation (thermophilic, psychrophilic)
- Selective nutritional baits

## Life-Form Analysis

Woody plants offer wider range of microhabitats than herbaceous species. Dead plant parts to sample include:

- **Leaves**: Fallen, buried, dead attached, suspended; various surfaces and parts
- **Twigs/stems/branches/trunks**: Fallen, buried, dead attached; bark surfaces, cut surfaces, cavities
- **Roots**: Cortex, exposed or covered, physically damaged sections
- **Flowers**: Fallen or attached damaged organs; nectar, pollen
- **Fruits and seeds**: Various states of decay; animal-damaged, unfertilized ovules

## Ecological Significance

Soil fungi are fundamental to:

- Decomposition of plant litter and organic matter
- Nutrient mineralization and cycling
- Soil aggregation and structure formation
- Plant nutrition (mycorrhizae, decomposition)
- Suppression of plant pathogens (antagonism)
- Carbon sequestration in soil
- Detoxification of pollutants
