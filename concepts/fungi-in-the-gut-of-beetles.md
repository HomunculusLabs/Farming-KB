# Fungi in the Gut of Beetles

## Overview

The insect gut represents a hidden but remarkably rich ecological niche for fungi. Research by Blackwell, Suh, and Nardi, as presented in "Fungi in the Environment" (Gadd, Watkinson, and Dyer, eds.), has revealed that the digestive tracts of beetles harbor extraordinary fungal diversity, including hundreds of previously undescribed yeast species. These gut fungi engage in relationships with their beetle hosts that range from casual commensalism to tightly coevolved mutualism, influencing insect nutrition, detoxification, speciation, and [[dighton-mycorrhizal-diversity-ecosystem-function]]. The study of insect gut mycobiota has become a frontier in [[fukuoka-microbial-ecology-decomposition]], challenging previous assumptions about fungal diversity and host-microbe interactions.

## Historical Context

The recognition of endosymbionts in arthropods extends back over a century (Buchner, 1965). Early research focused heavily on bacterial symbionts, particularly the rickettsial endosymbionts widespread among insects, which were discovered to induce sterility and potentially increase rates of speciation. Bacterial gut symbionts have long been recognized for their nutritional contributions to insects, and more recently, indigenous gut bacteria have been shown to prevent colonization by non-indigenous microbes. The insect gut is now considered a "hot spot" of bacterial gene exchange and bacterial adaptation (Dillon and Dillon, 2004).

By contrast, although there were numerous early reports of fungal endosymbionts in insects, fewer were substantiated after initial publication. More recent [[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]], however, have revealed that insect-yeast interactions are far more abundant and diverse than previously appreciated, though the exact nature of many of these interactions remains incompletely understood.

## Types of Fungi in Beetle Guts

The fungal inhabitants of beetle guts include several distinct groups:

**True yeasts (Saccharomycotina):**

- Single-celled fungi that reproduce asexually by budding, the hallmark of the "yeast habit."
- Constitute a monophyletic group within the Ascomycota.
- Many exhibit both yeast and filamentous growth forms.
- Represent the most commonly isolated fungi from beetle digestive tracts.

**Yeast-like fungi (Pezizomycotina):**

- Ascomycetes that have secondarily assumed the yeast growth form.
- Include Symbiotaphrina species associated with anobiid beetles.
- Include unnamed yeast-like endosymbionts (YLSs) of planthoppers.
- May perform essential services for their insect hosts, including detoxification.

**Basidiomycete yeasts:**

- Malassezia species have been detected in beetle guts through gene cloning.
- These were not recovered in cultural studies, likely because they require special media.

**Other microorganisms:**

- Cloning studies have also revealed protists, other insects, and microsporidia in beetle guts.
- The gut ecosystem is more complex than culture-dependent studies alone would suggest.

## Key Findings from Mushroom-Feeding Beetles

Research on beetles that feed on basidiomata (mushrooms) has yielded remarkable discoveries:

**Scale of diversity:**

- 650 yeasts were isolated from the digestive tracts of more than 90% of all beetles dissected.
- 290 yeast genotypes representing more than 200 undescribed taxa were discovered.
- The discovery of almost 200 undescribed yeasts gains greater significance considering that fewer than 700 species of ascomycete yeast had been described from all of Earth's habitats previously.
- Several previously unknown, entirely insect-associated yeast clades have been discovered, including the Candida tanzawaensis clade, which contained 30% of all gut yeasts collected.

**Specificity and host fidelity:**

- One dominant yeast species is usually present in a beetle gut, based on cloning methods.
- Yeast-beetle specificity has been observed between certain yeasts and beetles across broad geographical ranges.
- Specific associations persist through multiple developmental stages of some beetle species.
- Because almost all dissected beetles in Cucujoidea and Tenebrionoidea bore yeasts, untapped beetle diversity supports high estimates of undiscovered yeast species.

**Distribution across the phylogenetic tree:**

- Insect gut yeasts are distributed in clusters throughout the yeast phylogenetic tree.
- This suggests that yeast-beetle associations have evolved independently multiple times rather than representing a single coevolutionary event.

## Functional Roles of Gut Yeasts

Gut yeasts perform several functions that benefit their beetle hosts:

**Nutritional supplementation:**

- Yeasts provide vitamins, amino acids, and sterols that may be limiting in the beetle diet.
- Symbiotaphrina species in anobiid beetles contribute B vitamins and other nutrients.
- Yeast-like endosymbionts (YLSs) of planthoppers contribute essential amino acids.

**Detoxification:**

- Symbiotaphrina species associated with anobiid beetles (death-watch beetles and drugstore beetles) detoxify food resources.
- Anobiid beetles feed on dry, seasoned wood containing toxic secondary compounds.
- The fungal symbionts metabolize these toxins, making the wood digestible.
- Dowd (1989, 1991) demonstrated this detoxification function experimentally.

**Digestive enzyme production:**

- Lawrence (1973, 1989) observed that certain ciid beetles utilized a restricted selection of basidiomata, correlating mushroom groups by "hardness" and texture.
- It has been hypothesized that gut yeasts may provide digestive enzymes that enable beetles to utilize specific fungal substrates.
- Specialized mouthparts alone may not account for the observed specificity of host fungus use.

**Xylose fermentation in wood-boring beetles:**

- The wood-ingesting passalid beetle Odontotaenius disjunctus consistently harbors a yeast similar to [[pichia-stipitis]].
- P. stipitis is notable for its ability to ferment and assimilate xylose, a rare attribute among yeasts.
- Xylose forms the backbone of hemicellulose, a major component of plant cell walls in wood.
- This correlation between xylose-processing yeasts and wood-boring beetles has been observed across multiple distantly related beetle families.

## Compartmentalization of Microbes in the Hindgut

The insect gut comprises three main divisions: foregut, midgut, and hindgut. The distribution of gut yeasts varies across these compartments:

- In many insects, both the foregut and midgut contain yeasts.
- Green lacewings (Chrysoperla spp.) harbor several closely related yeast taxa in the crop, a foregut chamber.
- The hindgut of O. disjunctus (passalid beetle) contains a more diverse [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] than that of fungus-feeding beetles, including protists in addition to yeasts.
- The gut of fungus-feeding beetles, while yeast-rich, shows low diversity of other organisms.

## Molecular Approaches to Studying Gut Mycobiota

Gene cloning and microscopy have expanded understanding of beetle gut fungi beyond what culture-dependent methods reveal:

- LSU and SSU rDNA genes were cloned from beetle guts and compared with cultured isolates.
- [[stamets-outdoor-natural-culture-methods]] recovered most ascomycete yeasts, but some unculturable organisms were detected only through [[ingham-soil-food-web-diversity-assessment-dna-molecular-methods]].
- Malassezia species (basidiomycete yeasts) were common in cloned sequences but absent from cultures.
- No cloned sequences were identical to known sequences in GenBank, indicating substantial undiscovered microbial diversity.
- DNA databases remain low in microbial sequences from insect-associated habitats.

## Implications for Fungal Diversity Estimates

The beetle gut findings have significant implications for our understanding of global fungal diversity:

- Beetles represent the most species-rich order of insects (Coleoptera), with over 400,000 described species and an estimated total exceeding 1 million.
- If a significant proportion of beetle species harbor unique gut yeasts, the total number of undescribed yeast species could be enormous.
- The discovery of more than 200 undescribed yeasts from a relatively small sampling effort suggests that current estimates of fungal diversity are substantially underestimated.
- Insect guts may represent one of the largest reservoirs of undocumented [[fungal-biodiversity]] on Earth.
