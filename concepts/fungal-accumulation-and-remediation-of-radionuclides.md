## Fungal Accumulation and Remediation of Radionuclides

## Overview

Fungi play a significant role in the accumulation, redistribution, and potential remediation of radionuclides in terrestrial ecosystems. Their extensive hyphal networks, ability to accumulate metals and radionuclides in their tissue, and production of harvestable fruiting bodies make them important agents in both natural radionuclide cycling and potential bioremediation strategies. This is particularly relevant for radiocesium (¹³⁷Cs) and radiostrontium (⁹⁰Sr), the two most environmentally significant radionuclides from nuclear events like Chernobyl.

## Uptake Mechanisms

### Ion Exchange and Potassium Replacement

Radionuclide uptake by fungi occurs primarily through two mechanisms:

1. **Cell-wall ion exchange sites**: Fungal hyphal walls possess charged sites that can bind cations including radionuclides. This passive adsorption mechanism contributes to immobilization of radionuclides in soil.
2. **Potassium replacement**: Radiocesium (Cs⁺) can substitute for potassium (K⁺) in fungal biochemical pathways due to their similar ionic radii and charge. This replacement is species-specific:
   - In the filamentous fungus *Fusarium solani*, both Rb and Cs can replace K
   - In the yeast *Candida utilis*, only Rb (not Li, Na, or Cs) could replace K

When uptake is expressed on a hyphal surface area basis, values range from 0.1 to 2.5 nmol m⁻² hyphal area per hour, depending on species.

### Strontium Uptake

Strontium behaves similarly to calcium in fungal metabolism. Connolly et al. (1998) demonstrated that the wood decay fungus *Resinicium bicolor* could utilize strontium from strontianite sand, translocate it through mycelial cord systems, and deposit it in [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]]. This suggests a pathway for translocation of ⁹⁰Sr within decomposer fungi.

## Mycorrhizal Involvement

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

Ectomycorrhizal fungi are particularly effective radionuclide accumulators. In boreal coniferous ecosystems in Sweden, they were shown to be major components of radiocesium accumulation:

| Ecological Component | ¹³⁷Cs Content (kBq kg⁻¹ dry weight) |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Facultative mycorrhizal species | 230 |
| Obligate mycorrhizal species | 120 |
| Saprotrophic fungi | 140 |
| Leaf litter horizon | 26 |
| Angiosperms | 8 |

Dighton and Horrill (1988) found that up to 92% of radiocesium in mycorrhizal basidiocarps in the UK was derived from pre-Chernobyl fallout sources, demonstrating long-term retention. The distribution of fungal mycelia in upper soil horizons correlates with radiocesium accumulation and reduced downward movement through immobilization in fungal tissue.

Ertel and Ziegler (1991) observed that tree species differ in radionuclide uptake, with spruce and larch (ectomycorrhizal) accumulating more than sycamore (arbuscular mycorrhizal). This difference may be partly attributable to mycorrhizal type rather than just bark roughness.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi

The role of arbuscular mycorrhizae in radionuclide uptake is more complex and sometimes conflicting:

- Jackson et al. (1973): Arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization of soybeans by *Glomus mosseae* significantly increased ⁹⁰Sr uptake
- Berreck and Hasselwandter (2001): [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] in *Festuca ovina* reduced Cs uptake into shoots; mycorrhizal development reduced Cs uptake at moderate nutrient levels
- The mechanism of protection appears to be sequestration of Cs in extraradical hyphae and reduced translocation into the host plant
- Potassium fertilization did not reduce Cs uptake in mycorrhizal plants

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae

Clint and Dighton (1992) showed that radiocesium influx into mycorrhizal heather (*Calluna vulgaris*) with ericoid mycorrhizae was lower than into nonmycorrhizal plants. However, internal redistribution allowed greater translocation of Cs to shoots in mycorrhizal plants, especially when pre-incubated in high potassium environments.

## Saprotrophic Fungi

Saprotrophic fungi have great potential for radiocesium immobilization in grassland soils. Dighton et al. (1991) estimated, based on an average influx rate of 134 nmol Cs g⁻¹ dry weight of mycelium and hyphal biomass of 6 g dry weight cm⁻³ soil, that the fungal component of upland grass ecosystems in northern England could take up between 350 and 804 nmol Cs m⁻² h⁻¹. Given micromolar radiocesium concentrations in soil pore water, these fungi have the potential to accumulate a large percentage of total fallout.

## Translocation Within Fungal Thalli

Translocation patterns differ among elements:

- **Carbon and phosphorus**: Translocate through hyphal systems of *Rhizopus*, *Trichoderma*, and *Stemphylium* by diffusion (Olsson and Jennings, 1991)
- **Radiocesium**: Translocation through *[[schizophyllum-commune]]* hyphae is slower than diffusion, suggesting an active accumulation mechanism (Gray et al., 1995)
- There is evidence for preferential transport of radiocesium to developing fruit bodies (nutrient sinks)

## Bioremediation Potential

### Mycoremediation of Radionuclides

The concept of using fungi for radionuclide cleanup has several advantages:

1. **Harvestable fruit bodies**: Ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes produce large fruiting structures (mushrooms) that can be harvested to physically remove accumulated radionuclides
2. **Soil immobilization**: Fungal mycelia in upper soil horizons prevent leaching of radionuclides to deeper layers
3. **Industrial biosorption**: *[[rhizopus-arrhizus]]* and *Aspergillus niger* can be pelletized for use in air-lift bioreactor systems, functioning similarly to commercial ion-exchange materials

### Mycorrhizal-Enhanced Phytoremediation

Entry et al. (1994) demonstrated that ectomycorrhizal symbionts can enhance radionuclide uptake by tree seedlings. Inoculation with species such as *[[pisolithus-tinctorius]]*, *Laccaria laccata*, *[[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]]*, and *[[cenococcum-geophilum]]* resulted in 3–5 times more ⁹⁰Sr uptake compared to uninoculated controls in ponderosa and Monterey pine seedlings. The combination of mycorrhizal-enhanced uptake into trees plus harvestable fruit body production represents a potentially effective soil remediation technique.

## Food Chain Transfer

Fungi serve as vectors for radionuclide transfer to higher trophic levels:

- **Reindeer**: Lichens (dominated by their fungal component) form ~40% of annual food intake; mean ¹³⁷Cs activity in Norwegian lichens ranged from 6,700 to 24,000 Bq kg⁻¹ (1986–1988)
- **Roe deer**: High radiocesium intake during autumn in Sweden associated with fungal fruit bodies in diet (3,000 Bq d⁻¹ in August–September)
- **Human consumers**: Barnett et al. (2001) estimated radiocesium transfer to UK consumers via fungi at less than one microsievert—likely higher in cultures with greater wild mushroom consumption
- **Fungal-feeding beetles**: Show mercury accumulation of 17.5 mg kg⁻¹ dry weight vs. 2–2.5 mg kg⁻¹ for other feeding guilds

## Summary

Fungi demonstrate remarkable resistance to radionuclides and efficient absorption from the environment. Key attributes include cell-wall ion exchange, potassium-replacement pathways, and the ability to translocate and sequester radionuclides within long-lived hyphal networks. Their production of harvestable fruit bodies, capacity for soil immobilization, and potential for mycorrhizal-enhanced phytoremediation make them promising agents for environmental remediation of radionuclide-contaminated sites.
