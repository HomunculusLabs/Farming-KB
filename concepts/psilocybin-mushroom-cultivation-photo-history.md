# Psilocybin Mushroom Cultivation Photo History

The Psilocybin Mushroom Image Guide, archived by Erowid, preserves one of the earliest
comprehensive photographic records of psilocybin mushroom cultivation. Compiled from
contributions by mycologists including John W. Allen, Georg Mueller, Christopher B., and
anonymous contributors from the Altered Consciousness BBS era, the collection documents
the visual history of indoor and outdoor psilocybin mushroom growing from the 1980s
through the early 2000s. The archive serves as both a taxonomic reference and a cultural
document of the underground mycology community's transition from print to digital
knowledge sharing.

## The PF Tek Era and Grow Chamber Photography

The guide contains extensive documentation of the PF Tek (Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique)
method, which revolutionized home mushroom growing by using vermiculite-brown rice flour
substrate cakes rather than traditional grain or compost methods. Key photographic
documentation includes colonization jars at 21 days post-inoculation showing various
stages of mycelial progression across six jars, fruiting mycelium cakes at 41 days in
styrofoam coolers with perlite humidification and saran wrap lids under manual ventilation,
and dedicated grow chambers with integrated thermometer/hygrometer monitoring representing
the state of the art in affordable home cultivation setups.

Photographs also document contaminated cakes showing brown mold on PF-style substrate,
serving as identification references for cultivators learning to distinguish healthy
mycelium from competitor organisms. The PF Tek represented a fundamental democratization
of cultivation knowledge — shifting from complex laboratory methods requiring pressure
cookers and laminar flow hoods to simplified techniques accessible to beginners using
half-pint wide-mouth mason jars, brown rice flour, and vermiculite.

## Species Documentation Through Photography

### Psilocybe cubensis — The Most Photographed Species

P. cubensis dominates the guide with the largest number of photographs, reflecting both
its global distribution and its status as the most commonly cultivated psychoactive
mushroom. Photos document wild specimens from Florida (caps up to 5.5 inches across on
field edges), Thailand (Koh Samui strains including the Ban Hua Thanon variety from
Southern Thailand), and dung-inhabiting natural growth. Thai mushroom commerce is
documented with a vendor holding giant P. cubensis specimens at Koh Samui. Dried
specimens range from individual connected pairs to ten-pound bags. Strain diversity is
visible through morphological variation in cap shape, stem thickness, and coloration
across geographical origins.

### Psilocybe azurescens — The Pacific Northwest Woodlover

Photographs by Georg Mueller document P. azurescens growing on alder wood chips near
Stuttgart, Germany (October 2000), showing the complete developmental sequence from
young specimens through mature fruiting bodies. Additional photographs show large
fruiting rings, illustrating the species' tendency to form extensive mycelial networks
producing synchronized flushes. The German specimens demonstrate the species' capacity
for naturalization far beyond its native Pacific Northwest range through human-mediated
dispersal.

### Psilocybe cyanescens — Wavy Caps in Two Continents

Wavy caps are documented across two continents: the Black Forest region of southwestern
Germany (extensive series by Mueller in early November showing clusters in dry leaf
litter) and northern California (anonymous contributions from 1997-1998 showing
specimens in grass clearings and on alder chips, referenced from The Mushroom Cultivator).
The geographic range illustrates cosmopolitan distribution achieved through wood chip
landscaping practices.

### Psilocybe semilanceata — The Liberty Cap

Liberty cap photographs span multiple European locations: the Black Forest (Mueller,
early November), Southern Norway (specimen growing near a village town hall, September
2000, locally called "spiss fleinsopp"), and Italian collections from "Funghetti" by
Lucio Pagani showing liberty caps in grass clumps, documenting pan-European temperate
distribution in pastures and grasslands.

## Lesser-Documented Species

The guide preserves rare photographic records of species with limited documentation
elsewhere in the mycological literature:

- **Psilocybe samuiensis**: Rice paddy specimens from Koh Samui, Thailand, by Allen
- **Psilocybe pelliculosa** (Conifer Psilocybe): Cluster specimens from the BBS archive
- **Psilocybe subcubensis**: Four Koh Samui specimens by Allen showing morphological
  differences from P. cubensis
- **Psilocybe hoogshagenii** var. hoogshagenii: Mexican specimens from the Jacobs
  collection
- **Copelandia cambodgeniensis**: Most potent of the Copelandia species, common on
  Oahu Island, Hawaii
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis** (Big Laughing Gym): Specimens from Mendocino, California
  growing on stumps and tree bark, with views of deformed caps and cap undersides

## Copelandia and Panaeolus Documentation

The guide includes photographs of several species from the Panaeolus and Copelandia
genera, tropical and subtropical psychoactive mushrooms less frequently documented
than Psilocybe species. Panaeolus subbalteatus (=cinctulus) is shown in multiple
specimens including specimens growing on horse dung in the Black Forest, Germany
(October 2000). Panaeolus campanulatus (bell-capped panaeolus) is photographed
growing in grass and clover habitats. Three Copelandia species are represented:
C. bispora (rare, four specimens from an unnamed location), C. cambodgeniensis
(the most potent Copelandia, common on Oahu), and C. tropicalis (uncommon, from
Hawaii). These photographs provide valuable morphological reference material for
field identification of these genera, which are frequently confused with inactive
Panaeolina species by amateur mushroom hunters.

## Mushroom Art and Cultural Artifacts

Beyond taxonomic documentation, the guide preserves cultural artifacts connecting
mycological photography to millennia of human-fungi relationships: West African mushroom
figurines from private collections and Joseph Campbell's "The Way of the Animal Powers";
Mexican terra cotta figurines from A.D. 100 discovered by Peter T. Furst; mushroom stones
from 1000 B.C.–500 A.D. Mexico from Schultes and Hofmann's "Food of the Gods"; and
Balinese handmade silk mushroom batik textiles photographed by John W. Allen.
