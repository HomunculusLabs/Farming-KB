# Fungal Relationships with Insects

The interrelationships between fungi and insects are among the most complex in biology. Practically all insects engage, consume, or succumb to fungi. From *Mycelium Running* by [[stamets-mycoremediation-principles]].

## Overview

Fungi and insects share an ancient coevolutionary history spanning hundreds of millions of years. Thousands of species of entomopathogenic fungi attack thousands of insect species, while many insects actively cultivate fungi for food, protection, and medicine. This dance between predator and prey has led to survival strategies for both, some bordering on the bizarre.

## Insects That Farm Fungi

### Termites and Termitomyces
The mushroom genus *Termitomyces* includes several species associated with terrestrial termite colonies, especially in the tropics. Termites construct their nests with organic matter and cultivate mycelium. When abandoned, these mycelium-rich nests sprout delicious mushrooms highly favored as edibles in central Africa. The primordium begins several feet underground deep in a termite nest, extending upward as a long "taproot," and then forming a mushroom on the surface. How termites cultivate this mushroom has baffled mycological minds.

### Attine Ants (Leafcutters)
Studies at Oregon State University (Currie et al. 2003) show that attine ants grow *Lepiota* mycelium as a host for a benign bacterium (*Pseudonocardia*) that produces an antibiotic against destructive microfungal parasites (*Escovopsis*). They also feed *Lepiota* mycelium to their larvae. This complex tripartite partnership (ant-fungus-bacterium) has allowed lineages of attine ants to survive for more than 50 million years, establishing massive colonies numbering in the millions.

The mutualism between ants, mushrooms, and bacteria is a model for how humans can live in closer harmony with the environment. Both ants and people benefit from the guardianship of mycelium — by partnering with fungi, many organisms can resist disease.

### Snails as Fungus Farmers
The marsh periwinkle (*Littoraria irrorata*) damages and then defecates on certain grass (*Spartina alternifolia*), where a particular fungus grows. Days later, the snails return and consume both fungus and plant. Grasses without the snail-enabled fungus grew 50 percent faster but were less appealing to feeding snails. The snail-fungus relationship affects other species in the marsh environment.

## Entomopathogenic Fungi — Fungi That Kill Insects

Thousands of species of fungi attack insects. The key genera include:

| Genus | Type | Target Insects |
|---|---|---|
| *Metarhizium* (esp. *M. anisopliae*) | Green mold | Ants, termites, locusts, mosquitoes, mites |
| *Beauveria* (esp. *B. bassiana*) | White mold | Wide range including beetles, ants |
| *Paecilomyces* | White mold | Various insect pests |
| *Hirsutella* | Mold | Mites, aphids |
| *Cordyceps* | Mushroom-forming | Ants, caterpillars, beetles |

### Mechanism of Infection
When insects contact spores of entomopathogenic fungi, the spores attach to the insect and germinate, boring hyphal pegs through the exoskeleton using chitin-dissolving enzymes. Other portals of entry include the respiratory tract, anus, and mouth. Once inside, the mycelium forks and runs through the internal organs, interfering with metabolism and causing malaise, necrosis, and death in a few days. The insects, looking mummified with fuzzy mycelium, then become launching platforms for further sporulation.

### The Cordyceps Zombie Ant Phenomenon
*Cordyceps lloydii*, when it infects a carpenter ant, compels the insect to climb to the canopy of the Costa Rican cloud forest. Once it has ascended, the ant locks its mandible into a leaf and dies. A *Cordyceps* mushroom erupts from its head. By releasing chemical compounds that compel the insect to ascend, the mushroom ensures its spores are spread far and wide by winds. Many such fungi use insects as vehicles for distribution and their carcasses as spore production centers.

### Social Insect Defenses
Social insects — those with a queen, such as ants and termites — set up sentry systems to protect the colony. If an infected individual is recognized, it is promptly killed and placed in a graveyard away from the nest. The insects sense or smell the plague and immediately mobilize to prevent infection. Millions of years of coevolution have allowed fungi and insects to recognize each other and engage in constant biochemical dialogue.

## Economic Impact of Insect Pests

- Termite species number at least 2,500 worldwide; about 150 attack buildings
- Nearly 600 species of carpenter ants exist; about a dozen attack buildings
- Damage from subterranean termites in the US exceeds $10 billion per year
- Formosan termite damage exceeds $1 billion annually
- New Orleans loses $300 million per year to termites
- The US pesticide industry is valued at $9 billion; biopesticides represent $450 million (5%) and growing 15% per year

## Mycopesticides — Using Fungi Against Pest Insects

The search for ecologically rational methods to control insects has focused on biopesticides — nature-based remedies that cause negligible collateral damage. Conventional chemical pesticides, especially organophosphates, harm nontargeted organisms, pollute water, and impair human health.

### The Stamets Discovery: Pre-sporulating Mycelium as a Trojan Horse
While growing *Metarhizium anisopliae* for experiments, Paul Stamets selected white-wedge sectors of mycelium that lacked or had delayed spore formation. Through successive transfers, he isolated a nonsporulating mycelial phenotype. The sporeless mycelium, still lethal to insects, emits attractants and feeding stimulants. This is the opposite of the sporulating state, which insects actively avoid.

Carpenter ants were observed picking up kernels of pre-sporulating myceliated rice and carrying them back to their nests, becoming unwitting distribution vectors. Within weeks, the treated structure was free of carpenter ants and was never reinvaded — the parasitized insect carcasses became moldy from repellent spores, creating a resident protective shield.

### Advantages of Mycopesticides over Chemical Pesticides
- Target-specific: fungi can be matched to specific pest insects
- Self-sustaining: infected insects spread the fungus throughout the colony
- No persistent environmental toxicity after use
- Insects cannot easily evolve resistance to a living organism
- The presporulating mycelium acts as a "Trojan horse" — attractive rather than repellent
- Multiple strains can be rotated to prevent insect recognition

### Key Species for Mycopesticide Development
- *Metarhizium anisopliae*: highly prevalent in soils; pathogenic to ants, termites, locusts, mosquitoes
- *Beauveria bassiana*: wide host range; effective against beetle pests
- *Cordyceps* spp.: dimorphic — can express as mold or mushroom

## Endophytes and Insect Defense

Endophytic fungi living inside plant tissues can protect their host plants from insect predation by producing specialized mycotoxins (often alkaloids). Endophytes hosted by grasses are similar to ergot fungi whose alkaloids prevent insect attack. Endophytes in large crabgrass appear to produce toxins that kill fire ants. Those in darnel weed (*Lolium temulentum*) cause sleepiness in cattle and horses.
