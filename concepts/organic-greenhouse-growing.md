# Organic Greenhouse Growing

Updated: 2026-04-18

A greenhouse is a very useful addition to any garden. The major advantage of owning one is that it enables you to sow and harvest crops months earlier than would otherwise be possible in temperate or cold areas. The growing season is therefore considerably extended, making it possible to increase annual yields substantially. With careful planning, certain crops can be harvested continuously, and many frost-tender plants can be "overwintered" -- kept in the greenhouse during winter months to protect them from cold.

## Choosing a Greenhouse

Greenhouses come in an assortment of shapes, sizes, and materials. Practicality should be the first consideration over appearance. Even a tiny greenhouse can accommodate a large quantity of plants and produce, especially if used in conjunction with a [[cold-frame-gardening]] or cloche. Buy the biggest you can afford, or consider one designed to take additional sections at a later date -- in practice, a greenhouse of any size will always be full.

### Greenhouse Shapes

- **Traditional rectangular "barn" shape** -- four vertical sides and a span roof with a central ridge. Very popular and practical, enables best use of available space.
- **Circular or lantern-shaped** -- usually six, nine, or twelve sides. Very attractive designs but working space inside is usually limited. Useful for small gardens.
- **Sunrooms (lean-to)** -- also serve as an additional room in the house. Convenient for heating and regular plant care, but light levels are reduced because light can only enter from three sides.

### Materials

Greenhouse frames are usually made of aluminium or wood. Aluminium houses let in more light because the glazing bars can be thinner. Wooden houses are slightly cheaper to heat (the wood itself is warmer) and look more attractive, but must be painted regularly unless you choose cedar. Never use creosote as a preservative -- it gives off fumes toxic to plants.

Glass is the most common glazing material, but plastic sheeting stretched over metal hoops is considerably cheaper, though the plastic must be replaced every couple of years. Plastic [[greenhouse-growing-guide]] structures have excellent light transmission but can only be used for crops that need no supplementary heating.

### Ventilation

Adequate ventilation is vital for temperature control. A 6 x 8ft (2 x 2.5m) greenhouse should have at least two roof vents, with proportionally more in larger houses.

## Heating a Greenhouse

The amount of heat you provide determines the range of plants you can grow. Even in an unheated greenhouse, yields will always be considerably earlier and heavier than from plants grown outside.

### Controlling Temperatures

The important statistics are the minimum temperature at night and the maximum during the day. To grow plants well, the variation between the two should be no more than about 50 degrees F (10 degrees C). Use a maximum/minimum thermometer to record daily extremes, and try to even out the differences through careful ventilation and heating adjustment.

### Methods of Heating

- **Solid fuel** (coal) -- cheap and efficient but requires considerable capital outlay and frequent refueling
- **Gas** -- more expensive initially but very convenient; can be controlled with thermostat
- **Oil and paraffin** -- lower cost but controlling the thermostat is difficult; heaters can emit fumes harmful to plants
- **Electricity** -- one of the cheapest options and certainly the most convenient; temperature control is very accurate and cost-effective

### Reducing Heating Costs

- Locate the greenhouse in a bright place to benefit from free solar heat
- Provide shelter from strong winds
- Insulate inside with insulated packaging (bubble-wrap material) -- can cut heat loss by 40-50 percent
- Use a [[organic-propagation-methods]] to restrict the area requiring heat
- Consider a thermal screen that can be drawn at night and pulled back during the day
- Use a [[comparison-bsf-composting-vs-hot-composting]] (horse manure mixture) as a cheap organic way to heat the soil

## Caring for Greenhouse Plants

Plants grown in the greenhouse need much more attention than those cultivated outside. As well as providing adequate ventilation and heating, you must feed and water regularly, decide on the most suitable growing medium, and keep the house clean.

### Potting Composts

A number of organic potting or seed composts are commercially available. For a peat-free compost, mix green compost with coir and add Dolomite lime to balance acidity. Add pelleted chicken manure and kelp liquid feed. Alternatively, make a soil-based compost using loam from stacked turf mixed with coir and coarse sand, enriched with [[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]], blood, fish, and bone meal.

### Feeding

The base fertilizer in composts will be used up within about eight weeks. Greenhouse plants must therefore be provided with additional food. Use homemade animal-manure tea or commercial liquid kelp or animal-manure feed. Plants that are expected to grow and produce fruit at the same time -- like tomatoes and cucumbers -- will need feeding every time you water them at the height of the growing season.

### Watering

Install a hose that reaches inside the greenhouse. In early spring, fill the watering can after use and leave it inside so the water warms to greenhouse temperature by the next day. Keep a tank inside the greenhouse and top it off after each watering. Water in the morning rather than later in the day. Most greenhouse plants do better with a thorough soaking rather than frequent light watering.

Automatic watering options include [[compost-container-types]] on the staging and trickle irrigation systems with narrow-gauge tubing fixed into each pot.

### Growing in Greenhouse Borders

Provided the soil is well prepared with plenty of [[mollison-designers-humus-building-and-soil-organic-matter]], the greenhouse border can be used productively. Avoid growing the same crop year after year (especially tomatoes, which are at risk from root rot). Grow disease-resistant varieties or replace border soil annually. Alternatively, use homemade growing bags filled with worm-worked compost.

## Maintaining a Greenhouse

### Keeping Clean

By keeping the greenhouse spotlessly clean, you discourage pests and diseases. Remove any plant debris, wash the house thoroughly with warm soapy water at the end of the season, and leave it open for frost to help sterilize it. Always keep the glass clean -- dirty panes reduce available light. Wash pots and seed trays in boiling water before reuse.

### Providing Humidity

Most plants grown under glass require a moist, humid atmosphere. Achieve this by wetting the paths, the staging, and often the plants themselves early in the morning ("damping down") using a fine mist sprayer.

### Shading

In summer, shade the greenhouse to reduce high light levels that would scorch plants and produce unacceptably high temperatures. Roller blinds are the best method. A cheaper alternative is to paint the exterior glass with special shading compound that reflects light and wipes off easily when no longer needed.

## Deciding What to Grow

To make the best use of your greenhouse, keep it full for the major part of the year. Most vegetables are grown in unheated or slightly heated houses during spring and summer for early yields.

- **Vegetables** -- raise early crops of radishes, lettuce, and other salad vegetables, starting in midwinter if the house is heated
- **Fruit** -- grape vines, peaches, and other tender fruits grown as fans on walls or at the end of the house produce superior quality
- **Decorative plants** -- flowering and foliage plants raised from seed, used as houseplants or to decorate the greenhouse itself

## Greenhouse Pests and Diseases

Greenhouse pests can build up rapidly because the warmth and humidity ideal for plant growth are also ideal for pests. Prevent attacks by vigilance and scrupulous cleanliness.

- **Vine weevils** -- white-bodied grubs with brown heads that eat roots. Use nematode-management|nematodes or knock off all compost and repot
- **Aphids** -- small green or black flies that suck sap and transmit virus diseases. Encourage natural predators or spray with insecticidal soap
- **Red spider mites** -- invisible to the naked eye; cause fine mottling on leaves. Increase humidity; introduce the parasitic mite Phytoseiulus persimilis
- **Scale insects** -- small disc-like insects clinging to stems. Remove manually or use insecticidal soap
- **Mildew** -- white powdery coating on leaves. Remove affected leaves immediately; ensure plants never go short of water; spray with copper fungicide or dispersible sulfur
- **Blight** -- brown marks on leaves and sometimes fruit. Remove infected leaves immediately; spray with copper fungicide

## Cold Frames

A cold frame is an important piece of equipment, especially if you use the greenhouse to raise seedlings for planting outside. Plants raised in warm, humid greenhouse conditions need to be gradually acclimatized to lower temperatures and increased exposure -- a process called "hardening off."

Most frames consist of a wooden, metal, or brick box with a glass lid. The simplest is a wooden box with a sheet of rigid plastic over the top. You can build one yourself. For a large garden, frames with larger glass panes supported on old railroad ties are possible. Because all plants in a cold frame must be at the same stage of hardening off, it may be more convenient to have two small frames than one large one.

### Using the Cold Frame

1. A week or so before seedlings are due to be replanted, bring them outside and put them into a closed cold frame for 24 hours
2. During the second day, open the top slightly to begin acclimatizing the plants, but close it again at night
3. Gradually increase the opening until the top is off completely during the day but still closed at night
4. Start opening the frame more at night until the top is removed completely -- the plants are then fully acclimatized and ready to be planted outside

Cold frames can also be used in spring to grow early vegetables of all kinds and crops such as melons. They are useful in winter for growing vegetables that mature in spring, like lettuce.
