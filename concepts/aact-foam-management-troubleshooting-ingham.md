## Understanding Foam in AACT

Foam is a natural byproduct of healthy [[ingham-aact-brewing-equipment-reliability-machine-testing-standards]]. The presence of foam
generally indicates active microbial metabolism and the production of
surfactants by bacteria and other microorganisms. However, the character,
quantity, and behavior of foam provides important diagnostic information about
brew health.

### What Causes Foam

- Bacterial production of **surfactants** (surface-active compounds)
- Proteins and other metabolic byproducts from active microbial growth
- [[fungal-hyphae]] can contribute to stable foam structures
- Excessive food sources (particularly molasses) can cause over-foaming
- The interaction between microbial metabolites and air bubbles from aeration

### Healthy vs. Problematic Foam

**Healthy foam characteristics:**
- Light, bubbly foam that dissipates somewhat over time
- Off-white to light tan color
- Moderate amount — does not overflow the brewer
- Accompanied by pleasant earthy or yeasty smell

**Problematic foam indicators:**
- Thick, stable foam that does not [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]]
- Foam overflowing the brewing vessel
- Discolored foam (dark brown, green, or black)
- Foul or putrid odors accompanying the foam
- Foam appearing very early or very late in the brew cycle

## Foam Management Techniques

### Reducing Food Source

If foam is excessive, the most common cause is too much food:
- Reduce molasses or other carbohydrate additives
- Cut food sources by 25–50% for the next brew
- Excess nutrients drive explosive bacterial growth and surfactant
  production

### Adjusting Aeration

- [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] creates more foam than coarse bubble systems
- If using fine bubble diffusers and foam is problematic, consider
  switching to coarser bubbles or reducing air flow rate
- Ensure the air stone or diffuser is not positioned too close to the
  surface, which throws bubbles directly into foam-producing zone

### Mechanical Foam Control

- Use a nylon stocking or mesh bag over the compost basket to contain
  fine particulates that contribute to foam stabilization
- Gently stirring the foam back into the brew can break [[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]]
- Some brewers use a foam control ring or baffle at the top of the vessel
- Do NOT add anti-foaming chemicals — these kill beneficial organisms

### Compost Selection

The compost used in brewing affects foam:
- Well-made, mature compost with diverse biology produces moderate foam
- Compost high in actinobacteria can produce more persistent foam
- Compost that is too young or not fully decomposed may foam excessively
  due to unfinished [[fungal-decomposition-processes]]
- Ensure compost has been properly cured (minimum 3–6 months)

## Using Your Nose as a Diagnostic Tool

Your nose is one of the best tools for monitoring AACT:

### Good Smells
- Earthy, forest-floor aroma
- Slightly sweet or yeasty
