# AACT Foam Management and Troubleshooting

Dr. Elaine Ingham's guidance on foam formation, management, and what foam
indicates about the biology in Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) from the
Field Guide I for AACT.

## Understanding Foam in AACT

Foam is a natural byproduct of healthy AACT brewing. The presence of foam
generally indicates active microbial metabolism and the production of
surfactants by bacteria and other microorganisms. However, the character,
quantity, and behavior of foam provides important diagnostic information about
brew health.

### What Causes Foam

- Bacterial production of **surfactants** (surface-active compounds)
- Proteins and other metabolic byproducts from active microbial growth
- Fungal hyphae can contribute to stable foam structures
- Excessive food sources (particularly molasses) can cause over-foaming
- The interaction between microbial metabolites and air bubbles from aeration

### Healthy vs. Problematic Foam

**Healthy foam characteristics:**
- Light, bubbly foam that dissipates somewhat over time
- Off-white to light tan color
- Moderate amount — does not overflow the brewer
- Accompanied by pleasant earthy or yeasty smell

**Problematic foam indicators:**
- Thick, stable foam that does not break down
- Foam overflowing the brewing vessel
- Discolored foam (dark brown, green, or black)
- Foul or putrid odors accompanying the foam
- Foam appearing very early or very late in the brew cycle

## Foam Management Techniques

### Reducing Food Source

If foam is excessive, the most common cause is too much food:
- Reduce molasses or other carbohydrate additives
- Cut food sources by 25–50% for the next brew
- Excess nutrients drive explosive bacterial growth and surfactant
  production

### Adjusting Aeration

- Fine bubble aeration creates more foam than coarse bubble systems
- If using fine bubble diffusers and foam is problematic, consider
  switching to coarser bubbles or reducing air flow rate
- Ensure the air stone or diffuser is not positioned too close to the
  surface, which throws bubbles directly into foam-producing zone

### Mechanical Foam Control

- Use a nylon stocking or mesh bag over the compost basket to contain
  fine particulates that contribute to foam stabilization
- Gently stirring the foam back into the brew can break surface tension
- Some brewers use a foam control ring or baffle at the top of the vessel
- Do NOT add anti-foaming chemicals — these kill beneficial organisms

### Compost Selection

The compost used in brewing affects foam:
- Well-made, mature compost with diverse biology produces moderate foam
- Compost high in actinobacteria can produce more persistent foam
- Compost that is too young or not fully decomposed may foam excessively
  due to unfinished decomposition processes
- Ensure compost has been properly cured (minimum 3–6 months)

## Using Your Nose as a Diagnostic Tool

Your nose is one of the best tools for monitoring AACT:

### Good Smells
- Earthy, forest-floor aroma
- Slightly sweet or yeasty
- Fresh bread-like quality
- Mushrooms or rich soil

### Bad Smells (Indicates Problems)
- Sulfur, rotten egg, or septic odor
- Vomit or sour milk smell
- Putrid or decayed organic matter
- Ammonia or sharp chemical smell

If the tea smells bad at any point during the brew, the beneficial organisms
have been lost or outcompeted by anaerobic organisms. The tea should be
discarded or applied only to non-food areas. Bad-smelling tea can actually
harm plants by introducing pathogens or phytotoxic compounds.

## Slime and Biofilm Management

### Wipe Slime Protocol

Slime on the inside of the brewing vessel or on equipment indicates biofilm
buildup:
- After each brew, wipe down the interior with a clean cloth
- Use dilute hydrogen peroxide (3%) or baking soda solution for cleaning
- Rinse thoroughly with clean water before the next brew
- Do NOT use bleach or harsh chemicals — residues kill beneficial organisms

### Filter Maintenance

Filters in the brewing system can accumulate slime and biofilm:
- Clean or replace filters between brews
- Nylon stockings used as compost baskets should be rinsed and dried
  between uses
- Replace nylon stockings periodically as they degrade

## The Relationship Between Foam and Dissolved Oxygen

Foam formation and dissolved oxygen (DO) levels are interconnected:
- Active microbial growth consumes oxygen, creating demand
- Adequate aeration must maintain DO above 6 ppm (mg/L)
- When DO drops below 5.5–6 ppm, anaerobic organisms begin to proliferate
- If foam is collapsing and the tea smells off, check DO levels immediately
- Foam collapse combined with odor change = emergency — the brew is going
  anaerobic

## pH Monitoring During the Brew

pH provides additional diagnostic information alongside foam observation:
- AACT pH should generally remain between 6.0 and 7.5
- pH dropping below 5.5 can indicate anaerobic conditions
- pH rising above 8.0 may indicate ammonium accumulation
- Fungi prefer slightly lower pH (5.5–7.0) than bacteria (6.0–7.5)
- Monitor pH at the start, middle, and end of each brew cycle
