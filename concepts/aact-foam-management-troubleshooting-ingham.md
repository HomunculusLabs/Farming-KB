# AACT Foam Management and Troubleshooting

Dr. [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] Tea (AACT) from the
[[ingham-aact-brewing-equipment-reliability-machine-testing-standards]]. The presence of foam
generally indicates active microbial metabolism and the production of
surfactants by bacteria and other microorganisms. However, the character,
quantity, and behavior of foam provides important diagnostic information about
brew health.

### What Causes Foam

- Bacterial production of **surfactants** (surface-active compounds)
- Proteins and other metabolic byproducts from active microbial growth
- [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]]
- Foam overflowing the brewing vessel
- Discolored foam (dark brown, green, or black)
- Foul or putrid odors accompanying the foam
- Foam appearing very early or very late in the brew cycle

## Foam Management Techniques

### Reducing Food Source

If foam is excessive, the most common cause is too much food:
- Reduce molasses or other carbohydrate additives
- Cut food sources by 25–50% for the next brew
- Excess nutrients drive explosive bacterial growth and surfactant
  production

### Adjusting Aeration

- [[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]]
- Some brewers use a foam control ring or baffle at the top of the vessel
- Do NOT add anti-foaming chemicals — these kill beneficial organisms

### Compost Selection

The compost used in brewing affects foam:
- Well-made, mature compost with diverse biology produces moderate foam
- Compost high in actinobacteria can produce more persistent foam
- Compost that is too young or not fully decomposed may foam excessively
