# Rock-Eating Fungi and Mineral Weathering

Certain fungi possess the ability to actively degrade and solubilize rock minerals, a process sometimes called "rock-eating." This biological weathering has profound implications for soil formation, nutrient cycling, and mycorrhizal plant nutrition. The phenomenon was dramatically documented by Jongmans et al. (1997) in Nature, where ectomycorrhizal fungi were shown tunnelling through mineral grains.

Fungal mineral weathering represents a critical intersection between geology and biology, demonstrating that living organisms can fundamentally alter the mineralogical composition of the Earth's surface. This process operates over timescales from days to millennia and contributes significantly to the global biogeochemical cycles of major elements including silicon, aluminum, iron, calcium, potassium, and phosphorus. See [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] for background on soil-forming processes.

## Evidence of Mineral Tunnelling

Jongmans et al. (1997) observed ectomycorrhizal hyphae penetrating feldspar and hornblende mineral grains in podzol soils under boreal forest in Sweden. The tunnels:

- Had diameters of 3-10 um, matching hyphal dimensions

- Were filled with organic material

- Extended millimeters into mineral interiors

- Were morphologically distinct from cracks produced by physical weathering

- Were particularly associated with mycorrhizal root tips

This demonstrated that fungi were actively dissolving silicate minerals from the inside, not merely exploiting pre-existing cracks. Subsequent studies have confirmed fungal tunneling in diverse mineral types and ecosystem contexts, from tropical forests to arctic tundra.

## Mechanisms of Mineral Dissolution

### Organic Acid Production

Fungi secrete low-molecular-weight organic acids that attack mineral structures:

- Oxalic acid (HOOC-COOH): Most common, produced by Aspergillus niger, Paxillus involutus, and many ectomycorrhizal fungi

- Citric acid, malic acid, and other di- and tri-carboxylic acids

- Oxalate forms stable complexes with metal cations (Ca, Fe, Al) in mineral structures

- Aspergillus niger produces high yields of oxalic acid for metal leaching purposes

The importance of oxalic acid in mineral weathering extends beyond simple acid dissolution. Oxalate can form insoluble metal-oxalate complexes (such as calcium oxalate crystals) that precipitate at the hyphal surface, creating a microenvironment with altered chemistry that further promotes mineral dissolution. This biomineralization process is a hallmark of fungal weathering.

### Proton Extrusion

Active proton pumping into the hyphal environment lowers pH and promotes dissolution of oxide and silicate minerals. Proton extrusion is linked to nutrient uptake and ammonium assimilation. When fungi absorb ammonium ions from soil solution, they release protons to maintain charge balance, creating localized acidification that can dissolve adjacent mineral surfaces.

### Siderophore Production

Fungal siderophores chelate iron and other metals from mineral structures, increasing solubility and bioavailability. Ectomycorrhizal fungi produce hydroxamate siderophores in response to iron limitation. These high-affinity iron-chelating compounds can extract iron from insoluble oxide and silicate minerals, making it available for both fungal and plant nutrition.

### Extracellular Enzyme Activity

Some fungi produce extracellular enzymes that directly attack mineral structures:

- **Oxidoreductases**: Can reduce structural Fe(III) to Fe(II), destabilizing iron-bearing silicate minerals

- **Lithotrophic metabolism**: Certain fungi can oxidize reduced mineral components (e.g., Mn(II) to Mn(IV)) as an energy source, indirectly promoting mineral dissolution

- **Phosphatases**: Release phosphate from mineral phosphates, enhancing plant phosphorus nutrition

### Specific Mineral Transformations

- **Feldspar weathering**: Releases K, Al, and Si -- critical for plant nutrition in acidic soils

- **Calcite dissolution**: Lapeyrie and colleagues documented calcium oxalate crystal formation on mantle hyphae of hybrid larch roots

- **Lead mineral transformation**: Fungi transform lead phosphate minerals (Sayer et al., 1999)

- **Metal-bearing mineral solubilization**: Screening methods developed for metal tolerance and solubilizing ability

- **Mica weathering**: Fungi can interlayer expand and degrade mica minerals, releasing interlayer potassium

- **Apatite dissolution**: Mycorrhizal fungi solubilize calcium phosphate minerals, a major source of phosphorus in many soils

## Ecological and Environmental Significance

1. **Soil formation**: Fungal mineral weathering is a primary agent of pedogenesis, particularly in boreal and temperate forest soils

2. **Plant nutrition**: Mycorrhizal fungi enhance access to mineral-bound phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients

3. **Toxic metal mobilization**: Can also release toxic metals from mineral matrices, with implications for contaminated site management

4. **Carbon sequestration**: Organic acids deposited in mineral tunnels contribute to long-term carbon storage

5. **Geomicrobiology**: Fungi are increasingly recognized as significant geological agents

6. **Global silicon cycle**: Fungal weathering of silicate minerals is a major pathway for silicon release from rocks to the biosphere

7. **Critical zone research**: Fungal weathering is now recognized as a key process in the Earth's critical zone where rock meets life

## Ectomycorrhizal Fungi as Weathering Agents

Paxillus involutus synthesizes oxalic acid and solubilizes calcium from mineral matrices. Leyval et al. (1993) demonstrated mobilization of phosphorus and cadmium from rock phosphates by rhizosphere microorganisms including ectomycorrhizal fungi. The intimate contact between hyphae and mineral surfaces maximizes weathering efficiency.

The weathering capacity of ectomycorrhizal fungi is particularly important in forest ecosystems, where these fungi form dense hyphal mats that explore vast volumes of soil. The [[the-soil-food-web]] page describes the forest floor context where this weathering occurs.

## Saprotrophic and Rock-Inhabiting Fungi

Beyond mycorrhizal fungi, free-living saprotrophic and rock-inhabiting fungi contribute to mineral weathering:

- **Aspergillus niger**: Used industrially for bioleaching of metals from ores; produces exceptionally high concentrations of citric and oxalic acids

- **Penicillium species**: Common in rock surfaces and soil, contribute to silicate dissolution in diverse environments

- **Black meristematic fungi**: Found on rock surfaces in extreme environments (deserts, arctic), slow-growing but persistent weathering agents

- **Lichenized fungi**: Lichen symbioses combine fungal acid production with algal photosynthesis

## Applications and Implications

Fungal mineral weathering has practical applications:

- **Bioleaching**: Industrial recovery of metals from low-grade ores using fungal acids

- **Soil restoration**: Accelerating soil formation on mine tailings through fungal inoculation

- **Enhanced weathering for carbon capture**: Fungal-accelerated silicate weathering draws down atmospheric CO2
