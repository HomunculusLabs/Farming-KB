# Fungi and Climate Change — Mycelium Running

In "Mycelium Running," Paul Stamets frames fungi as central players in climate change -- both as organisms threatened by global warming and as tools for mitigating its effects. He argues that human-induced ecological disruption destabilizes the fungal recycling systems that maintain the planet's carbon and nutrient cycles, and that mycotechnologies offer practical solutions for habitat restoration and climate resilience.

## The Threat: Ecological Collapse and Global Warming

Stamets cites the 2003 Pentagon report "An Abrupt Climate Change Scenario and Its Implications for United States National Security" (Schwartz and Randall 2003), warning that the biosphere may undergo rapid destabilization from pollution and global warming. He connects this to a broader pattern: the cascade of toxins and debris generated by humans destabilizes nutrient return cycles, causing crop failure, global warming, climate change, and potentially eco-catastrophes.

The interconnectedness of life, he argues, is a truth we ignore at our peril. When humans exceed the carrying capacity of their host environment, food chains collapse and diseases emerge to cull the threatening population. Stamets believes partnering with mycelium can help restore balance. He envisions a hypothetical "United Organization of Organisms" where each species gets one vote -- and humans would likely be voted off the planet unless we act as a responsible species. Nature, he says, will not evict us if we act responsibly; our fungal friends equip us with tools to act responsibly and repair our shared environment.

## Carbon Dioxide: Fungal Decomposition vs. Combustion

One of Stamets's most pointed arguments addresses the claim that growing mycelium on wood chips increases global warming. He counters that while carbon dioxide does cause global warming, fungal output is negligible compared to fire. Combustion of a tree releases vast quantities of hot carbon dioxide in about an hour. Fungal decomposition of that same tree releases cool carbon dioxide gradually over years.

Since carbon dioxide is heavier than air, it permeates soils where plant cells absorb it as raw material for creating cellulose, lignin, carbohydrates, proteins, and sterols, while outgassing oxygen. Burning sends carbon dioxide high into the atmosphere beyond the reach of plant communities and destroys the understory plants that would recapture it. Stamets concludes: decomposition by fungi buffers carbon dioxide emissions and cycles much of the gas back into the flourishing ecosystem.

## Calcium Oxalate and Carbon Sequestration

Stamets describes how mycelia form oxalic acid crystals, which combine with calcium and other minerals to form oxalates such as calcium oxalate (CaC2O4). Notably, calcium oxalate sequesters two carbon dioxide molecules. Carbon-rich mushroom mycelia unfold into complex food webs, crumbling rocks as they grow and creating dynamic soils that support diverse populations. This mineralization process represents a direct mechanism of geological carbon sequestration driven by fungal metabolism.

## Mycoforestry as Climate Strategy

In the mycoforestry framework, Stamets recommends chipping excess wood in replanted forests and leaving chips on the forest floor rather than burning or hauling away debris. Mycoforesters spread mulch around young trees, along trails, and in mycofiltration buffer zones near watersheds to fortify forests with beneficial fungi. Where wood chips touch the ground, fungi grow into them and transport moisture with threadlike mycelium. The myceliated wood chips act like a sponge, retaining water for neighboring plants and reducing fire danger.

He argues that the current practice of burning or hauling wood debris robs biomass from the carbon cycle. The argument that wood debris fuels forest fires does not hold if debris is brought into contact with decomposing mycelium already residing in the forest floor. Moist wood does not easily burn. Chipping wood and leaving it in place may increase fire risk for the first few years, but thereafter fire potential is drastically reduced as the wood decomposes. This approach maintains carbon in the biological cycle rather than releasing it rapidly through combustion.

## Forest Floor Benefits

Leaving wood on the forest floor provides multiple climate-relevant benefits:

- Delayed release of nutrients to build soils
- Supporting mycofiltration membranes that reduce erosion and siltation
- Providing cavity habitats for diverse populations of bacteria, fungi, plants, insects, and animals
- Moisture retention that buffers against drought
- Protection against forest fires by keeping ground moist
- Substrates for decommissioned logging roads

In planted second- and third-growth forests, high tree density results in numerous dead side branches that become brittle and susceptible to fire. The current method of dealing with this is selective thinning, but Stamets argues that if debris is burned or exported, the environment loses a nutritional layer. Instead, mycoforestry can convert this debris into soil-building substrate through fungal decomposition.

## Antifreeze Compounds and Thermogenesis

Mushrooms produce antifreeze glycoproteins that protect mycelium from ice crystallization. These antifreezing agents also help prevent soils from freezing, conferring protection to plants during extreme cold -- a potentially significant buffer as climate change increases weather volatility. Hoshino et al. (2003) filed a patent on antifreezing polypeptides from several mushrooms.

Soils infused with actively growing mycelium also benefit from thermogenesis -- the natural escalation of temperature as mycelium decomposes organic matter, releasing heat, water, and carbon dioxide. These dual properties suggest fungi help buffer ecosystems against both freezing and extreme temperature fluctuations driven by climate instability.

## No-Till Agriculture and Carbon Retention

Stamets links farming practices to climate outcomes. Conventional tilling releases more carbon dioxide into the atmosphere than no-till methods, which keep carbon dioxide in the soil. Fungal allies in no-till agriculture extend fine cellular filaments between plant fibers, breaking down plant cells into nutrients and synthesizing moisture-retaining polysaccharides. A 21-year study in Germany found that no-till organic farming methods were superior to conventional methods in energy use and effects on wildlife. These same polysaccharides that build soil carbon also boost the human immune system, connecting agricultural sustainability directly to human health.

## Logging Roads and Watershed Protection

Stamets addresses the climate and ecological impact of logging roads, which he identifies as the primary vector of watershed siltation and pollution. In Washington State, for every mile of paved road there are more than 7 miles of unpaved roads, many from logging operations. Decommissioning costs range from $4,100 to $105,600 per mile.

Mycofiltration offers a biological solution: placing layers of bark, wood chips, and mycelium on abandoned logging roads to catch silt and prevent erosion. This approach uses fungal networks to stabilize soils, reduce runoff pollution into watersheds, and eventually return road surfaces to near-natural states at a fraction of conventional costs. The problem is worldwide, from Brazil to Bosnia.

## Biodiversity Loss and Ecological Collapse

Stamets warns that we are losing many species before we can even identify them. In effect, as we lose species, we are experiencing devolution -- turning back the clock on biodiversity, which is a slippery slope toward massive ecological collapse. Half of the current mushroom species could disappear in the next hundred years if current trends continue. The surprising diversity of fungi speaks to the complexity needed for a healthy environment.

Toxic fungicides like methyl bromide, once touted, not only harm targeted species but also non-target organisms and their food chains. When the natural benefits of fungi are repressed, the perceived need for artificial fertilizers increases, creating a cycle of chemical dependence that ultimately erodes sustainability. However, mycologically sustainable environments can be created by introducing plant-partnering fungi in combination with mulching using saprophytic mushroom mycelia.

## Mycorestoration as Climate Response

Stamets proposes mycorestoration -- using fungi to restore damaged habitats -- as essential for climate response. He envisions mycoremediation, mycofiltration, and mycoforestry as interconnected strategies that, combined with permaculture and living machine systems, form a new model of habitat restoration. The 21st century, he argues, may be remembered as the Biotech Age, when mycotechnologies play a prominent role in strengthening habitat health. Knowing how to work with fungi -- by custom pairing fungal species with plant communities -- is critical for our survival.
