# Mucormycosis (Zygomycete Infections)

Mucormycosis (also called zygomycosis) is a severe, often fatal fungal infection caused by
molds in the order Mucorales. These rapidly growing fungi are common environmental organisms
that can cause devastating disease in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with
diabetic ketoacidosis, neutropenia, or iron overload.

## Overview

The term "mucormycosis" has largely replaced "zygomycosis" because not all Zygomycota cause
disease, and the causative agents belong specifically to the order Mucorales. These include
genera such as Rhizopus, Mucor, Lichtheimia (formerly Absidia), Rhizomucor, Cunninghamella,
and Apophysomyces.

## Causative Organisms

### Rhizopus Species
- R. arrhizomus (oryzae) is the most common cause of mucormycosis
- Found worldwide in soil, decaying vegetation, bread, and fruit
- Characterized by rhizoids (root-like structures) and stolons

### Mucor Species
- M. circinelloides and M. racemosus are occasional pathogens
- Common in stored food and environmental samples

### Lichtheimia Species
- Important causes of mucormycosis in Europe
- Formerly classified in the genus Absidia

### Cunninghamella
- C. bertholletiae causes severe infections in immunocompromised patients
- Particularly associated with neutropenic patients

### Apophysomyces
- A. elegans and A. trapeziformis cause infections primarily in immunocompetent individuals
- Associated with traumatic inoculation (injuries, surgery, natural disasters)

## Clinical Forms

### Rhinocerebral Mucormycosis
- Most common form in diabetic patients
- Begins in the paranasal sinuses, spreads to orbit and brain
- Symptoms: facial pain, headache, nasal congestion, black eschar on palate or nasal mucosa
- Can progress to cranial nerve palsies, blindness, and death within days
- Mortality: 50-80% even with treatment

### Pulmonary Mucormycosis
- Most common form in neutropenic patients (leukemia, transplant recipients)
- Symptoms: fever, cough, chest pain, hemoptysis
- Angioinvasion can cause pulmonary infarction and fatal hemoptysis
- Often indistinguishable from aspergillosis on imaging

### Cutaneous Mucormycosis
- Can occur via traumatic inoculation (soil contamination of wounds)
- Also seen in burn patients and premature infants
- May present as necrotic eschars with surrounding erythema

### Gastrointestinal Mucormycosis
- Rare; associated with malnutrition, premature infants, and iron overload
- Can cause bowel infarction and perforation

### Disseminated Mucormycosis
- Hematogenous spread from any primary site
- Most common in profoundly neutropenic patients
- Can affect virtually any organ

## Pathogenesis

Mucormycetes are angioinvasive fungi — they invade and destroy blood vessels:
1. Spores are inhaled or introduced through broken skin
2. Hyphae germinate and invade tissue rapidly
3. The fungi have a predilection for blood vessels
4. Angioinvasion causes thrombosis, tissue necrosis, and infarction
5. Dissemination occurs via hematogenous spread

Key virulence factors:
- **Ketone reductase:** Allows growth in high-glucose, acidic (ketoacidotic) environments
- **Iron acquisition:** Produces siderophores and uses host iron; iron overload is a major
  risk factor
- **Rhizopus oryzae uses a fungal CotH protein** that binds to host GRP78 receptor on
  endothelial cells, facilitating invasion

## Risk Factors

- **Diabetic ketoacidosis:** The single most important risk factor for rhinocerebral disease
- **Neutropenia:** Prolonged low neutrophil count (cancer chemotherapy, leukemia)
- **Iron overload:** Deferoxamine therapy, hemochromatosis
- **Transplant recipients:** Especially hematopoietic stem cell transplants
- **Corticosteroid therapy:** Immunosuppression
- **Trauma and burns:** For cutaneous disease
- **Premature birth:** Gastrointestinal and cutaneous forms

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is notoriously difficult:
- **Tissue biopsy** is essential — demonstrates broad, ribbon-like, pauciseptate hyphae
  with right-angle branching
- **Culture:** Requires specific media; growth is rapid (2-5 days) but contamination is common
- **No serological tests** available (unlike aspergillosis galactomannan testing)
- **PCR:** Not yet standardized for clinical use
- Must distinguish from Aspergillus hyphae (narrower, septate, regular branching)

## Treatment

- **Control underlying risk factors:** Reverse ketoacidosis, reduce immunosuppression, discontinue
  deferoxamine
- **Surgical debridement:** Essential — necrotic tissue must be removed; repeat surgeries often
  needed
- **Liposomal amphotericin B:** Primary antifungal therapy ([[amphotericin-b-antifungal]])
- **Posaconazole and isavuconazole:** Step-down or alternative therapies
- **Combination therapy:** Uncertain benefit; some studies suggest amphotericin B plus
  echinocandin
- **Hyperbaric oxygen:** Investigational adjunctive therapy

## Epidemiology

- Increasing incidence globally, likely due to rising diabetes and immunosuppressed populations
- Particularly common in India (where diabetes prevalence is high)
- Outbreaks associated with contaminated medical supplies (linen, adhesive bandages)
- Environmental exposure to spores is universal — disease reflects host vulnerability
