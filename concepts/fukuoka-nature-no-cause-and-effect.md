# Nature Has No Cause and Effect

One of Masanobu Fukuoka's most radical philosophical claims is that
nature operates without cause and effect. This assertion, which seems
to contradict the foundations of Western science, is central to his
critique [[fukuoka-critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] and his argument for leaving the
management of ecosystems to nature itself.

## The Claim in Context

Fukuoka developed this idea while considering the problem of
desertification. Scientists, he observed, cannot determine why deserts
are spreading because the causes are preceded by other causes in an
endless chain of interwoven events and factors beyond human
comprehension. Suppose researchers could identify which plant was the
first to die off in a land turning to desert — they would still not
know whether to begin by planting the first vegetation to disappear or
the last to survive. The reason: in nature, there is no cause and
effect.

## Against Reductionist Causality

Modern science is built on the principle of causality: every effect has
a cause, and understanding causes allows us to control effects. This
approach has been extraordinarily successful in physics and engineering,
where systems can be isolated and variables controlled. Fukuoka argued
that this approach fails catastrophically when applied to living
ecosystems.

### The Problem of Infinite Regression

In a living system, every identified cause is itself the effect of
prior causes. A plant dies because of drought; the drought occurred
because vegetation disappeared; vegetation disappeared because of
overgrazing; overgrazing happened because nomadic herds expanded; the
herds expanded because of climatic shifts; the climate shifted because
of ocean current changes — and so on without end. Any attempt to trace
a single causal chain through this web is arbitrary and misleading.

### The Problem of Simultaneity

In ecosystems, causes and effects are not sequential but simultaneous.
The soil feeds the plants that feed the soil. The predator controls
the prey that sustains the predator. The microorganism decomposes the
organic matter that feeds the microorganism. These are not linear
causal chains but circular, mutual, reciprocal relationships. Assigning
causality to any single element is a distortion imposed by the human
mind, not a feature of the system itself.

### The Problem of Emergence

Living systems exhibit properties that none of their components possess
individually. Soil fertility is not caused by any single organism or
mineral but emerges from the interactions of thousands of species.
Plant health is not caused by any single nutrient but emerges from the
dynamic balance of many factors. Attempts to identify "the" cause of
any ecological phenomenon necessarily miss the emergent quality that
makes it what it is.

## Scientific Myopia: Specialists and Blind Spots

Fukuoka observed that science rarely looks to microorganisms for an
understanding of large causal relationships. Botanists study plants but
not the microorganisms that live in and around them. Zoologists study
animals but not the fungi that sicken them. Each specialist sees only a
fragment of the whole.

He wrote: "We have gathered together such a diverse collection of
specialists that we have lost sight of both the starting line and the
finish line." The specialist approach, by isolating variables for
study, destroys the very context that gives those variables meaning.

## Implications for Revegetation

Fukuoka's epistemology has direct practical consequences. If nature
has no simple cause-and-effect relationships, then scientific attempts
to revegetate barren land by identifying and addressing "root causes"
are doomed. No amount of study can determine the correct sequence of
planting, the optimal species mix, or the ideal soil amendments,
because the system is too complex and too interconnected for such
deterministic planning.

The alternative is to trust nature's own restorative capacity. By
scattering clay pellets containing seeds and microorganisms over
barren land, one provides the raw materials for natural succession
without attempting to direct the outcome. Nature will sort out which
species are suited to the conditions. Some will survive and create the
conditions for others, in a process that no human planner could
replicate.

## One Gram of Soil

Fukuoka grounded his epistemological argument in a concrete observation:
one gram of soil on his farm contains about 100 million nitrogen-fixing
bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes. This single gram represents
an ecosystem of staggering complexity, with more interactions per
second than a human mind could comprehend in a lifetime. How can any
scientist claim to understand the "causes" operating within such a
system, let alone improve upon them?

The implication is that human knowledge, however sophisticated, is
always a vast simplification of reality. The farmer who acts on this
simplified understanding inevitably disrupts relationships that the
simplification has rendered invisible.

## Parallels in Systems Theory

Fukuoka's position anticipates several concepts from modern systems
theory and complexity science:

- **Nonlinear dynamics**: Small inputs can have disproportionate
  effects; large inputs can have negligible effects
- **Feedback loops**: Positive and negative feedback create behaviors
  that cannot be predicted from studying individual components
- **Self-organization**: Complex patterns emerge from simple rules
  followed by many agents without central direction
- **Sensitive dependence**: Initial conditions that differ
  imperceptibly can lead to radically different outcomes

Fukuoka arrived at these insights not through [[davidson-mathematical-modeling-fungal-mycelia]] but
through decades of [[fukuoka-direct-observation-method]] of natural and agricultural
ecosystems.

## The Farmer's Humility

The recognition that nature has no cause and effect is ultimately an
argument for humility. The farmer who accepts this truth stops trying
to impose human designs on the land and instead learns to work within
the patterns that nature already establishes. This does not mean doing
nothing — Fukuoka was emphatic that [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]] requires careful
observation and timely action. But the actions are guided by nature's
rhythms rather than by human theories about what should work.

"All begins by relinquishing human knowledge," Fukuoka wrote. Not
because knowledge is worthless, but because the knowledge we have is
always incomplete, and acting on incomplete knowledge in complex living
systems causes more harm than good.
