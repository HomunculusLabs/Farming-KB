## Four Mechanisms of Foliar Pest Deterrence

### 1. Surface Repellency
The biology applied to leaf surfaces may be directly repugnant to insect pests, causing them to avoid landing on treated leaves. The diverse community of bacteria, fungi, and their metabolites creates a surface environment that insects recognize as unfavorable.

### 2. Elevated CO2 Signal
The applied microorganisms elevate CO2 levels in the leaf microenvironment. Many insect pests are attracted to plants under stress by detecting specific [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] that serve as attractants for insect pests. Tea organisms consume these alcohols, removing the chemical signal that attracts insects to stressed plants. The attractant is no longer present, so insects do not target the plant.

### 4. Restored Plant Health
Perhaps most importantly, tea organisms cause the plant to become less stressed and return to health. Healthy plants are inherently less susceptible to insect attack because:
- Their cell walls are stronger and more resistant to penetration
- Their immune responses are fully functional
- They produce appropriate defensive compounds
- Their nutritional profile is balanced (not high in free amino acids that attract pests)

## Free Amino Acids and Pest Attraction

Ingham draws a connection between plant nutrition and pest susceptibility:
- Free amino acids stuck in non-protein form attract pests
- These amino acids need to move into protein synthesis
- Too much protein sitting idle can also kill plants
- Protein must be mobilized into structure and function
- The soil food web controls the form and availability of nutrients that determine this balance

Plants grown with healthy soil foodwebs contain 3–10 times more protein than conventionally grown plants, but this protein is properly incorporated into cellular structures rather than accumulating as free amino acids.

## Scale Insect Control

Ingham reports success controlling scale insects on rhododendrons and apples using tea with excellent fungal biomass. The fungal component appears critical for this application. This suggests that different pest problems may require different organism profiles in the tea.

## Disease Suppression Mechanisms (Foliar)

The foliar disease suppression mechanisms overlap with pest deterrence:
1. **Out-competition**: Beneficial organisms consume the food resources disease organisms need
2. **Space occupation**: All infection sites are occupied by beneficial organisms, leaving no room for pathogens
3. **Predation**: Protozoa and nematodes consume disease-causing organisms
4. **Enzyme production**: Beneficial organisms produce enzymes that inhibit pathogens

These are mechanical/competitive interactions — "cat and mouse" mechanisms — not toxic chemical effects.

## Specific Crop Applications

Ingham"s recommendations vary by crop type and growth stage, reflecting different leaf surface characteristics and disease profiles:

### Brassicas and Leafy Greens
These crops benefit most from bacterial-dominated teas applied at 7-14 day intervals. The large, thin leaves provide extensive surface area for bacterial colonization. Application should begin at transplant or first true leaf stage and continue through harvest. AACT has shown particular effectiveness against downy mildew and Alternaria leaf spot on brassicas when applied before infection periods (typically during cool, moist conditions in spring and autumn).

### Fruit Trees and Perennials
Woody perennials benefit from fungal-dominated teas that provide longer-lasting colonization of bark, buds, and leaf surfaces. Fungal hyphae can persist on woody surfaces for weeks, providing sustained competitive exclusion of pathogens. Apple scab, powdery mildew, and brown rot have all been reduced through regular applications of fungal-dominated AACT to fruit trees. Applications should target the canopy during bud break and continue through the growing season, with particular attention to periods of rapid new growth.

### Tomatoes and Solanaceous Crops
Tomatoes are susceptible to both foliar diseases (early blight, late blight, septoria leaf spot) and insect pests (aphids, whiteflies, tomato hornworm). AACT applications combining both [[query-how-to-make-a-balanced-compost-recipe]] source — provide broad-spectrum protection. Application should begin at transplanting and continue weekly through the growing season, with increased frequency during humid conditions that favor disease development.

## Application Timing

- Apply before disease arrives — AACT is a protectant
- If disease is already present, effects should be visible within one day
- If no impact after one application, reapply
- For overwhelming disease loads, a fungicide may be needed initially, followed by AACT for protection
- Even without disease, nutrient uptake improvements may be visible within days

## Regulatory Considerations

Ingham emphasizes that no disease "control" claims should be made for compost tea in commercial contexts. The mechanisms described are competitive interactions, not pesticide effects. No toxic chemicals are involved.

## Key Takeaways

The effectiveness of AACT for pest deterrence depends on maintaining diverse microbial communities on leaf surfaces. Unlike chemical pesticides, these mechanisms work by strengthening the plant's natural defenses and altering the chemical signals that attract pests. The approach requires regular application but avoids the resistance buildup and environmental harm associated with synthetic treatments.
