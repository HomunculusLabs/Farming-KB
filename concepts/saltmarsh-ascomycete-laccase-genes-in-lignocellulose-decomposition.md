# Saltmarsh Ascomycete Laccase Genes in Lignocellulose Decomposition

The saltmarshes of the Georgia, USA, Atlantic coast are expansive and
highly productive intertidal ecosystems. The predominant macrophyte is
smooth cordgrass (Spartina alterniflora), with cross-marsh average annual
production of approximately 1.3 kg per square metre of marsh. A
specialized community of ascomycete fungi drives the decomposition of this
vast standing-dead litter resource.

## The Saltmarsh Decomposition System

Smooth cordgrass does not abscise its leaf blades; they remain attached to
the leaf sheath after senescence and death. A large crop of standing-dead
litter is available to microbes for much of the year, with up to 538 g dry
mass per square metre for leaf blades alone. The most important wetting
phenomenon is dewset, combined with tidal and rain wetting, resulting in
the leaves being wet roughly half of the time.

Smooth-cordgrass ascomycetes are well adapted to alternating wet and dry
periods, losing little or no biomass after aestivating and reviving. Inside
the dead shoots, the ascomycetes carry out their digestive work free from
prokaryotic competition or pressure from micropredators such as amoebae.
Other factors favouring fungal production include potential formation of
consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria, synergistic relationships with
green microalgae, and potential uptake of nitrogen from flooding-tidal
waters.

## The Ascomycete Decay Community

The major members of the smooth-cordgrass shoot-decomposer myco-community
are Phaeosphaeria spartinicola, Phaeosphaeria halima, and Mycosphaerella
sp.2. A regularly occurring species encountered at 40% or more of blade
area is Buergenerula spartinae. These ascomycetes produce prodigious
quantities of fungal mass (over 0.5 kg per square metre per year) and
expel approximately 5.9 billion ascospores per square metre per year.

## Lignocellulose Challenge

Standing-dead smooth cordgrass is composed largely of lignocellulose
(70-75%). Early testing suggested cordgrass ascomycetes were weak degraders
of lignocellulose, but this was likely due to unfavourable microcosm
conditions. Subsequent tests with static incubation and presence of malt
and yeast extract revealed that Phaeosphaeria spartinicola was capable of
mineralization of lignocellulose at more than 0.8% per day.

A combination of the three predominant ascomycetes, incubated statically
with wet but not submerged radiolabelled cordgrass lignocellulose, showed
mineralization of more than 20% after 6 weeks at about 23 degrees C.
Direct electron microscopic examination confirmed that cordgrass
ascomycetes brought about both type 1 and type 2 soft rot. Type 2 soft rot
is very similar to the white rot of basidiomycetes.

## Laccase-Mediated Lignocellulolysis

Ascomycetes are not expected to possess lignin or manganese peroxidases,
the enzymes best known for lignolytic capabilities. However, they possess
laccase, a copper-containing enzyme capable of oxidation of phenolic
molecules. A key discovery altered understanding of ascomycete
lignocellulolysis: if a redox-mediator
molecule is present along with laccase, non-phenolic portions of lignin
can be oxidized and lignin can therefore be extensively broken down.

Eggert et al. (1997) discovered that a basidiomycete with no manganese
or lignin peroxidase could carry out strong lignolysis because of its
ability to manufacture its own redox-mediator molecules to laccase
(3-hydroxyanthranilate). Furthermore, interactions of cellobiose
dehydrogenases with laccase-mediator molecules can allow cellulose lysis
to proceed alongside lignin oxidation.

## Detection of Laccase Genes

All eight strains of the most common cordgrass ascomycetes grown on
cordgrass agar and flooded with syringaldazine solution immediately
presented a pink colour, indicating laccase activity. Researchers then
designed degenerate primers targeting conserved sequences within
copper-binding sites II and III of fungal laccase genes.

PCR with these primers produced amplification products of approximately
900 base pairs in all positive controls (Aspergillus nidulans,
Cryphonectria parasitica, Podospora sp., and Saccharomyces cerevisiae),
confirming their identities as portions of laccase genes.

## Diversity of Laccase Genes in Decay Fungi

All species of the smooth-cordgrass ascomycete community possessed laccase
genes in at least one strain. Between the two major Phaeosphaeria species,
five different types of partial laccase gene were identified (less than
96% similar in amino acid sequences), with as many as three different
types within one strain. For all strains tested, 13 distinct sequence
types were obtained.

When laccase-gene primers were applied to DNA extracted from decaying
blades, 26 partial laccase genes were cloned and sequenced. Only two were
not identifiable with the gene sequences from the three major members of
the ascomycetous decay community. Thus the major species identified by
direct microscopy were the same species producing potentially lignolytic
laccase genes within the standing-decaying cordgrass blades.

## Ecological Significance

The findings demonstrate a broad spectrum of laccase-activity potential
among the cordgrass-decay ascomycetes, with multiple laccase gene types
per species suggesting functional redundancy and adaptability. This
genetic diversity may allow ascomycetes to effectively decompose
lignocellulose in the challenging saltmarsh environment without
basidiomycete enzymes, challenging traditional assumptions about the
division of decomposition labour between fungal
groups.

### Broader Implications for Decomposition Ecology
The discovery that ascomycetes possess multiple laccase genes capable of
lignocellulose degradation has implications beyond saltmarshes. In many
terrestrial ecosystems, ascomycetes dominate the early stages of
litter decomposition before basidiomycetes colonize. The laccase gene
diversity observed in saltmarsh fungi suggests that ascomycetes in other
habitats may possess similar capabilities. Future research using
metagenomic approaches could reveal the full extent of laccase gene
diversity in environmental fungal communities and clarify the relative
contributions of ascomycete and basidiomycete enzymes to global
lignocellulose turnover.
