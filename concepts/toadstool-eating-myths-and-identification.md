# Toadstool-Eating Myths and Mushroom Identification

In his 1877 article "Toadstool-Eating" for the *Popular Science Monthly*, Julius
A. Palmer launched a systematic assault on the folklore, newspaper
misinformation, and folk tests that surrounded [[mushroom-identification]] in 19th-
century America. His central argument was that no simple rule or magical test
could distinguish edible from poisonous fungi — only trained observation,
analogous to the discernment of an art connoisseur, could do so.

## The Mushroom-Toadstool False Dichotomy

Palmer opened with a fundamental correction that challenged the most basic
assumption of his readers: "Every mushroom is a toadstool, and every toadstool
is a mushroom." The popular distinction, which held that "mushrooms" were edible
and "toadstools" were poisonous, was as illogical as calling a beet a
"vegetable" while classifying every other garden plant as a mere "plant." In
botanical terminology, both terms refer to the same group of organisms — the
fleshy fungi. This false dichotomy had led to dangerous complacency, as people
believed that identifying a "mushroom" meant they were safe, ignoring the
thousand species that might spring up on American hills.

## The State of American Mycological Knowledge

Palmer painted a bleak picture of American mycological literature. Excepting the
writings of Dr. Curtis of South Carolina, he had found no original American
contribution to the subject. The ideas of the four leading British mycologists —
Berkeley, Cooke, Smith, and Badham — were occasionally reprinted in American
magazines, "written up" by professional writers who often introduced errors.
Even Dr. Curtis's correspondence with [[charles-james-sprague]], deposited at the
Boston Society of Natural History, focused on microscopic revelations rather
than practical identification guidance for the amateur mushroom hunter.

Palmer described how he had procured the leading British works from London and
begun the study of fungology, only to find that "discrepancies and obscurities
will confront the student." The descriptions were by no means exact. The
classification was not even harmonious, as generic names varied with leading
authorities from the time of Sowerby to the present. When you had placed a
specimen in its proper family, "you have not in any way defined its quality,
since one mushroom may be esculent, and its twin brother poisonous."

## Debunking Folk Tests: Peel Like a Potato

Palmer methodically dismantled the most common identification rules. The first
myth: "Every mushroom should peel like a potato." Palmer agreed that many edible
mushrooms do peel readily — but noted that nearly all fungi peel off readily and
handsomely, regardless of whether they are edible or poisonous. Peeling is
simply not a characteristic of edible species alone.

## The Umbrella Handle Test

Another folk rule held that the stem of a safe mushroom "ought to come out like
an umbrella-handle." Palmer explained that this property depends entirely on
whether the gills are free from the stem, not on whether the mushroom is edible.
Whole classes of fungi — many of them noxious — have free gills, meaning the
stem separates easily from the cap. The test is therefore meaningless as a
safety indicator.

## Newspaper Misinformation

Palmer quoted a widely-circulated weekly that offered eight rules for
[[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]], then demolished each one with specific counter-
examples drawn from his own experience:

- **"Found in damp, dark places"**: The very best mushrooms grow in damp, dark
  places, Palmer noted. This rule would exclude some of the finest edibles.
- **"Very thin cap"**: The edible fairy-ring champignon (*[[marasmius-oreades]]*) has
  a very thin cap and is universally prized.
- **"Stem growing from the side"**: The elm-tree mushroom (*[[agaricus-ulmarius]]*)
  has an eccentric stem and is an excellent edible.
- **"Milky mushrooms are dangerous"**: The brown milk-mushroom (*Lactarius
  volemus*) is unquestionably edible, and several others are doubtless so.
- **"Gills of equal length"**: An entire family, the Russulae, have equal gills,
  and many members are edible.
- **"Runs into dark, watery liquid"**: The maned mushroom (*[[coprinus-comatus]]*)
  turns to ink and is "most delicious" when fresh.
- **"Tastes bitter or burns the mouth"**: The honey-colored mushroom (*Agaricus
  melleus*) is very acrid raw but harmless when cooked.
- **"Turns colors when bruised"**: Several edible Agarics turn red when bruised,
  and many edible Boleti change color on cutting.
- **"Marked by the trail of a snail"**: Palmer had never seen a snail on a
  mushroom, and suggested the writer meant slugs, which he had often cut out of
  edible specimens, rejecting only the part they had spoiled.

## Charms and Superstitions

Palmer also dismissed magical tests that he had encountered: dropping salt on
the mushroom to see if it turned black or yellow, stirring with a silver spoon
to watch for discoloration, and rejecting all fungi growing from wood. He
declared emphatically: "No test of any kind, in form, shape, color, basis of
growth, or experimental action, will distinguish healthful from harmful fungi."

## The Art Connoisseurship Model

Having demolished the folk tests, Palmer offered a positive model for mushroom
identification. He compared it to art connoisseurship: how does an expert tell a
Leonardo da Vinci painting from one by Guido Reni? Or distinguish a hyacinth
bulb from an onion? These distinctions, impossible to communicate in words to
someone who has never seen either, become intuitive through experience. "It is
surprising how quickly the hand and eye acquire a delicacy of analysis,
impossible to communicate in words," Palmer wrote. "This talent is essential."

## Practical Testing Method

Palmer advocated a systematic method for the cautious amateur: first, trace the
specimen to its family using structural characteristics; second, broil a small
piece with salt and butter (no pepper) on an empty stomach; third, increase
quantities in successive experiments until perfectly safe. Non-edible fungi
typically revealed themselves during cooking — nauseous slime weeping from the
stem, a grassy disagreeable odor, or a taste that destroyed all desire for
another mouthful.

## Legacy

Palmer's insistence on systematic study over folklore was a watershed moment in
[[early-american-mycology-and-palmer]]. His debunking of folk tests remains relevant today, as many
of the same myths persist in popular culture. The core principle — that mushroom
identification requires genuine knowledge and experience, not simple rules — is
now universally accepted by mycologists and forms the foundation of modern
mushroom safety education worldwide.
