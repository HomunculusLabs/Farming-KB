# Nature, God, and the Transcendent World

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s philosophy extends far beyond agriculture into a
profound metaphysical framework that unifies God, nature, and human
understanding. His concept of the transcendent world, accessible through
the abandonment of discriminating knowledge, offers a radical critique of
both Western science and conventional Eastern religion.

## God and Nature as One

Fukuoka repeatedly asserts that nature is God and God is nature. The
soul of nature does not seek decay. When humans cause a hillside to
collapse, this appears to be destruction. But from a macroscopic vantage
point, nature does not destroy itself; it merely adopts many different
forms. True natural destruction occurs only when man has upset the course
of nature at some earlier point.

Nature is always absolutely perfect; it flows constantly from perfection
to perfection. The external form changes over time, but nature itself is
immutable and unmoving. There is no superior or inferior in God and
nature because the opposites of perfection and imperfection do not
exist. Viewed scientifically, relatively, myopically, nature may appear
to progress from imperfection toward perfection, but this is only its
outward form.

## The Mountain of Truth

Fukuoka uses the metaphor of a large mountain to explain the unity of
religious traditions. People from the West climb from the left, people
from the East climb from the right, and others choose the center. Many
different paths exist, along each of which people climb toward the summit.

If a drop of rain falls onto the mountain and flows to the left, it
becomes Western philosophy. If it flows to the right, it becomes Eastern
philosophy. When seen from the left, the individual sitting on the summit
may appear as the face of Christ. Seen from the right, he may appear as
the Japanese Buddha. And seen from the south, perhaps he has the
countenance of Gautama. All are viewing the same entity from different
positions.

Fukuoka believes there is only one truth, past, present, and future.
Christians may say there is no god but the Christian God. The Buddhist
may insist that Buddha is the Supreme Being. But just as there is only one
mountain, there is only one God. Muhammad too said, "There is only one
God." The differences are in perspective, not in reality.

## The Limits of Words and Knowledge

When saints who have reached the summit sit in a circle to speak of God,
they do not need spoken words. Since they are looking up from the summit
at the same heavens, they would agree even were they to remain silent.
However, these saints have no choice but to rely on spoken words to
explain the God they have seen to those still climbing. These words
cannot serve as a means for transmitting the vision, for they inevitably
give rise to numerous misconceptions.

The lecturing, preaching, and writing of books on God and the Buddha by
religious leaders who neither know nor have ever personally experienced
God only deepens confusion. The greater the number of holy books and
scriptures, the stronger they are thought to make the vine along which
people climb to approach God. Rather than climbing this vine, people read
scriptures and deepen their knowledge, which misleads them and causes them
to descend lower.

Knowledge does not uplift people; it ensnares them in confusion.
Intellectual discrimination merely fogs our understanding of God.
Synthesizing knowledge may be helpful in obtaining a concept of God, but
this only deepens knowledge and corrupts people, preventing them from
ever approaching God.

## The Transcendent World in Daily Life

People appear to know that God and nature are independent existences when
in fact they know nothing of the matter. When asked about the transcendent
world, the level of conversation immediately falls back down to worldly
matters. People carry in their heads the notion of things as being
mineral, animal, or whatever. They are unable to extricate themselves
from the ideas that rely on mineral, botanical, and naturalist categories,
and this shows they have no understanding of the word "transcendence."

God, or nature itself, lies above. God lies in the direct path one walks,
one step at a time, by renouncing knowledge and the self. But people
choose to go in the opposite direction, trying to possess God by resorting
to all means and knowledge at their disposal. Although the true way lies
along the road of renunciation, they believe it lies through possession.

## Touching the Great Spirit

Fukuoka recounts an encounter with a Native American woman who had
walked across Japan as part of a "March for Survival." When asked why she
had come so far, she replied, "I've been searching for myself." Fukuoka
responded simply, "But you're right here, aren't you?" The woman stared
in surprise, then suddenly cried out, "Great Spirit!"

This phrase struck Fukuoka deeply. He came to understand that coming into
contact with true nature can be an overwhelming experience, a world of
inspiration that can justly be called the "Great Spirit." Nature is an
astounding reality. One must constantly keep in mind that this contact
with the breadth and grandeur of the soul of Mother Nature is the
essence of the spiritual path.

## The Absolute Nothingness of Mu

Fukuoka connects his understanding to the Buddhist concept of absolute
nothingness (Mu), the nothingness taught by Lao Tzu, and the God of
Christian mystics. Regardless of who speaks or what is said, these all
refer to the same reality. Only the names by which they are called and
the manner in which they are described differ.

"Absolute nothingness" is a term understood only by those who live in a
state free of mundane desires and attachment; it cannot be understood by
those who inhabit the secular world. To use the word is already obsolete.
Fukuoka dreads our casual, unknowing use of such profound terms. The
direct experience of nature and God requires not intellectual
understanding but the abandonment of understanding itself.
