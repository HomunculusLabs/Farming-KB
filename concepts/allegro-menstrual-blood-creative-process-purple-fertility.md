# Menstrual Blood in Creative Process and Purple Fertility Symbolism

In [[allegro-fertility-philosophy-of-the-ancient-near-east]] Eastern fertility philosophy, menstrual blood
occupies a central position as the fundamental creative substance—the raw material from
which new life is formed. Its dual nature as both life-giving and destructive mirrors
the broader fertility worldview where creation and destruction are two faces of the same power.

## Menstrual Blood as Embryo Material

The ancient understanding of conception placed menstrual blood at the centre of the
generative process. Pliny records: "(menses is) the material for human generation, as
semen from the male acting like rennet collects this substance within it." The male
contribution was a coagulating agent that gathered and shaped the female's blood into
an embryo.

This framework explained infertility plainly: women who do not menstruate cannot bear
children, because they lack the essential creative substance. Menstruation during
pregnancy produced sickly or still-born children—the generative blood was spent
prematurely rather than nourishing the foetus.

The optimal moment for conception was at the beginning or end of the menstrual period,
when the blood was freshly available or newly ceasing. The biblical story of David and
Bathsheba (II Samuel 11:4) gains significance in this light: Bathsheba was purifying
herself after her period, placing her at the fertile threshold the ancients recognised.

Galen elaborated this model: semen draws blood to itself and uses it as food to build
the foetus. Menstrual blood provides the initial material and sustains the growing
child throughout gestation, transformed by the male seed into flesh, bone, and being.

## The Sacred Taboo of Menstruation

The profound generative power attributed to menstrual blood produced a corresponding
system of religious taboo. Leviticus 15:19-25 prescribes that everything a menstruating
woman touches becomes "unclean," and that intercourse during this period incurs a
seven-day disqualification from religious life.

As Allegro emphasises, this "uncleanness" has nothing to do with morals or hygiene. It
is a religious state of taboo—a recognition that the woman at this time is charged with
creative power so potent that ordinary human contact becomes dangerous.

The narrative utility of this taboo appears in Genesis 31:34, when Rachel conceals her
father Laban's stolen household gods by sitting upon them, claiming she cannot rise
because she is in "the manner of women." Laban's reluctance to search further
demonstrates the taboo's depth—no man would violate the menstrual prohibition even
to recover stolen property.

## Healing Powers of Menses

Paradoxically, the same blood that could destroy was also credited with remarkable
healing properties. Ancient tradition attributed to menstrual blood the power to cure
gout, scrofula, parotid tumours, abscesses, erysipelas, boils, eye-fluxes,
hydrophobia, and epilepsy—part of the serious ancient pharmacopoeia.

Even more striking is the prescribed countermeasure for quartan fever: intercourse with
a menstruating woman. The therapeutic and the taboo intersect—the most dangerous state
of female creative power becomes, under controlled circumstances, a cure for one of the
most persistent fevers of [[comparison-grafting-vs-budding-fruit-trees]], dry up seed, blight crops, turn wine sour,
send dogs mad, rust metals, and dull mirrors.

These effects operate on the principle of "like repelling like." The creative power of
menstrual blood, when misdirected, reverses its generative function—becoming antithetical
to growth and life. A substance that creates embryos can destroy them; a blood that
nourishes can poison.

## Purple Colors from Menses: The Colour of Fertility

The dark colour of menstrual blood was its distinguishing visual feature, and this
darkness carried profound symbolic weight. Purple and violet became the colours of
fertility significance—hues associated with the creative power of the womb-blood itself.

Kings wore purple as a mark of their generative authority. The Latin purpura denoted
both royal robes and high dignity, linking political power to the same creative force
that produced new life.

Tyrian purple, the most prized dye of the [[acacia]] tree produced a "purple gum" that was used medicinally to check excessive
menstruation. Amulets made from this gum were understood to carry the "tree's menstrual
blood"—the plant kingdom's own generative substance, paralleling the human and earthly
cycles.
