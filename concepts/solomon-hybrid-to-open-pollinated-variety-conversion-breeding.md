# Hybrid to Open-Pollinated Variety Conversion Breeding

## Overview

Converting a hybrid vegetable variety into a stable open-pollinated (OP)
variety is a multi-generation breeding process that home gardeners can
undertake with patience and adequate plant populations. The economic
superiority of hybrids has caused interest in OP varieties to virtually
disappear within the commercial trade, leaving home gardeners with
degenerated, ragged, low-quality OP choices. Converting desirable hybrids
to OP form preserves their best traits for home-scale [[biointensive-seed-saving]].

## Why Hybrids Dominate Commercial Trade

Commercial growers prefer [[comparison-heirloom-vs-hybrid-seeds]] because they are more vigorous and
perfectly uniform. Despite higher seed prices, the greater yields make
hybrids more profitable for market farming. The mass hybridization process
works by:

1. Breeding self-sterility into a parent line so it cannot self-pollinate
2. Growing the self-sterile variety alongside a genetically uniform second
   variety
3. Ensuring enough rows of the second variety for bees to fully pollinate
   the self-sterile rows
4. Harvesting seed from the self-sterile parent — nearly all resulting from
   crossing with the second variety

## The Conversion Process

### First Generation: The Bottleneck

When a hybrid is first allowed to open-pollinate, it typically produces very
few seeds. For example, Green Valiant hybrid broccoli produced only a few
hundred seeds total from half a dozen plants in the first generation. This
scarcity occurs because mass hybridization often breeds in self-sterility.

### Second Generation and Beyond

The second generation typically sets seed normally and abundantly. From there,
it takes a few generations to achieve a reasonably uniform OP variety that
closely resembles the original hybrid, though not as perfectly uniform or
vigorous. This lack of uniformity and vigor is the inherent nature of OP
varieties.

### Solomon's Carrot Conversion Experience

Solomon attempted to create an OP from an overwintered hybrid carrot with
unusual traits. The first spring, the flowers produced not a single viable
seed — the anti-hybridist suggestion seemed correct. At Carol Deppe's
suggestion, he planted the hybrid again alongside a row of an OP variety the
following year. Both rows set abundant good seed. The cross-pollination
between the hybrid and OP provided the genetic bridge needed for fertility.

### Tim Peters' Broccoli Conversion

Breeder Tim Peters converted Green Valiant hybrid broccoli into Umpqua, an
OP variety. After the scant first-generation seed, subsequent generations
produced uniform broccoli quite similar to Green Valiant, though not as
uniform or vigorous as the original hybrid.

## When "Hybrids" Are Already OP

Some seed sold as hybrid and priced accordingly may actually behave as OP
varieties. Solomon grew 100 plants of Kolibri F1 purple kohlrabi and found
it produced seed abundantly. That seed grew kohlrabi as uniform, productive,
and tasty as Kolibri F1 had been. Similar stories exist about other so-called
[[cervantes-hybrid-varieties-types]]. Hybrid tomato seeds may cost 20 times more than OP
varieties, creating strong economic incentive for ethical shortcuts.

## The Decline of OP Variety Quality

Alan Kapuler, an American biophysicist and seed breeder, explained the
historical decline in OP variety nutrition and flavor:

### Traditional Selection (Pre-1870)

For thousands of years, each family raised seed for unique varieties selected
over generations to suit their needs, tastes, and soils. Before antibiotics
and modern sanitation, children faced a gauntlet of childhood diseases.
Families with more nutritious [[heirloom-vegetable-varieties]] tended to have children who
survived to carry on those varieties. Less nutritious varieties disappeared
through natural selection. Good taste served as an indicator of high nutrition,
and humans naturally selected for flavor.
