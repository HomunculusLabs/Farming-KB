# Forest Rehumidification and Snow Meltwater Effects

## Forests as Cloud Makers

### The Water Recycling Power of Forests

As rain falls again and again across a landscape, the clouds moving inland carry water mostly evaporated from forests, and less and less from the sea. Forests are cloud-makers through two processes: water vapour evaporated from leaves by day, and water transpired as part of life processes. On high islands, standing clouds cap forested peaks but disappear if the forests are cut.

A large evergreen tree such as *[[eucalyptus-globulus]]* may pump out 3,600-4,500 litres of water per day — this is how Mussolini pumped dry the Pontine marshes of Italy. With sixty or more such trees per hectare, many tens of thousands of litres of water are returned to the air to become clouds.

### The Amazon Findings

Research documented in the New York Times (5 July 1983) revealed critical findings about forest-weather relationships:

- **A forest can return 75% of its water to air** — in large enough amounts to form new rain clouds
- **Forested areas return ten times as much moisture** as bare ground, and twice as much as grasslands
- **Transpiration from trees and other plants accounts for half, or even more, of all moisture returned to air**
- **Forests produce 50% of their own rain** — over the Amazon, twice as much rain falls than is available from incoming air, so the forest continually recycles water

Of the 75% of water returned by trees to air, 25% is evaporated from leaf surfaces and 50% is transpired. The remaining 25% of rainfall infiltrates the soil and eventually reaches streams. The Amazon discharges 44% of all rain falling, meaning the remainder is locked into forest tissue or returns to air.

### Cloud Composition

Clouds forming above forests are now known to be mixtures of oceanic and forest water vapour, clearly distinguishable by careful isotope analysis. The water vapour from forests contains more organic nuclei and plant nutrients than "pure" oceanic water. Oxygen isotopes are measured to determine a forest's contribution — this analysis can be performed for any cloud system.

### Sap Flow Dynamics

Vogel (1981), applying the "principle of continuity" of fluids to a tree, calculated that sap may rise in a young oak fifty times as fast as the leaves transpire. This requires only 7% of the total trunk area as conductive tissue, with an actual sap speed of 1 cm/sec. Only about one-fiftieth of the xylem is conducting sap upwards at any one time — most xylem cells contain either air or sap at standstill. The tree may move water upward in pulsed stages rather than as a continuous streamflow.

## The Hawaiian Cloud Bridge: A Cautionary Tale

The great bridging cloud that once reached from the forests of Maui to the island of Kahoolawe was remembered by the fathers of present Hawaiian settlers. As cutting and cattle destroyed the upper forests on Maui, the cloud cap lifted from Kahoolawe. With cloud forests gone and rivers dry, Kahoolawe became a true desert island, now used as a bombing range for the US Air Force.

This example illustrates a principle of critical importance: **drought in one area may relate directly to deforestation in an upwind direction.** As Thomas E. Lovejoy, Vice-president of Science at the World Wildlife Fund, stated: "This clearly shows that natural vegetation must play an important role in the forming of weather patterns."

## Effects on Snow and Meltwater

### Snow Entrapment by Trees

Although trees intercept some snow, the overall effect of shrubs and trees is to entrap snow at the edges of clumps and hold 75-95% of snowfall in shade. Melting is delayed for 2-10 days compared with bare ground, resulting in a more gradual release of meltwater.

Of the trapped snow within trees, most is melted and infiltrates into the soil. On open ground, snow may sublime directly to air without productive effect. The beneficial effects of trees on high slopes extend well beyond humid coasts.

### The Problem of Bald Uplands

On high cold uplands such as the continental interiors of the USA or the area near Mt. Ararat in Turkey, the thin skeins of winter snow either blow off the bald uplands (to disappear in warmer air) or sublime directly to water vapour in the bright winter sun. In neither case does the snow melt to groundwater, and no streams result on the lower slopes.

### The Pyramid Lake Case Study

When forests were cleared for mine timber in 1846 at Pyramid Lake, Nevada, the streams ceased to flow and lake levels fell. Combined with river diversion and irrigation, whole lakes rich with fish and waterfowl became dustbowls — as happened to Lake Winnemucca. The Cuiuidika'a Indians (Paiute) who lived there lost their fish, waterfowl, and freshwater in less than 100 years.

### The Value of Tree Belts on Uplands

Even a thin belt of trees on high slopes entraps large quantities of driven snow in drifts. The result is a protracted release of meltwater to river sources in the highlands and sustained stream-flow at lower altitudes. This is perhaps the most critical function of mountain forests in continental interiors.

## Design Strategies for Water Capture

Given the evidence that local precipitation can be significantly influenced by design, several basic strategies for water capture from air emerge:

### Cooling the Air
- Shade air using trees and shrubs
- Provide cold surfaces for air to flow over (metals, glass, rocks, or thin leaf laminae)

### Forcing Air to Higher Altitudes
- Provide windbreaks that deflect air upward
- Create updraughts from heated or bare surfaces (large concreted areas)
- Use mechanical means (industrial fans)

### Providing Condensation Nuclei
- Plant diverse vegetation that releases pollen, bacteria, and organic particles
- Select species whose leaf litter provides effective ice nucleation

### Compressing Air
- Force streamlines to converge over trees and objects
- Create turbulent flow in airstreams through strategic placement of windbreaks (Ekman spirals)

## The Integrated Role of Trees

Trees — especially crosswind belts of tall trees — meet all of the above criteria in one integrated system. They also store water for local climatic modification. The evidence is clear: we do not need to accept "rainfall" as the sole determinant of local precipitation, especially within 30-100 km of coasts (where much of the world's population lives), and we do not need to accept that total precipitation cannot be changed by our designs on site.

As Mollison observes, these findings forever put an end to the fallacy that trees and weather are unrelated. The design strategies are obvious and urgent: save all remaining forest, and plant trees for increased condensation on hills that face the sea.
