# Impoverished National Agricultural Policy and the Farmer's Decline



## Overview

Fukuoka delivers a scathing critique of Japan's postwar agricultural policy, arguing that the Basic Agriculture Law of 1961, far from serving as a foundation for farmers, established controls that passed power to the financial community. He traces how the farmer's effort to raise food production backfired, how food self-sufficiency dropped below thirty percent, and how farmers lost both their land and the freedom to choose their crops. This analysis reveals the structural forces that transformed Japanese agriculture from a self-sufficient peasant economy into a dependent, energy-intensive industry.



## The Turning Point: Mechanization Arrives

The first step in Japan's agricultural transformation was the arrival of the motorized transport-tiller in the farming village — a major turning point. This was rapidly followed by three-wheeled vehicles and trucks. Before long, ropeways, monorails, and paved roads stretched to the furthest corners of the village, completely altering the farmer's notions of time and space.

With the wave of change from labor-intensive to capital-intensive farming came the replacement of the horse-drawn plow with tillers, and later, tractors. Methods of pesticide and fertilizer application underwent major revisions, with motorized hand sprayers abandoned in favor of helicopter spraying. Traditional farming with draft animals was abandoned in favor of heavy chemical fertilizers and pesticides.

The rapid mechanization lit the fires for the revival and precipitous growth of the machine industry, while the adoption of pesticides, chemical fertilizers, and petroleum-based farming materials laid the foundation for development of the chemical industry. What began as a movement to assure adequate food supplies in times of acute shortage grew into a drive to increase food production, the momentum of which carried over into the industrial world.



## The Basic Agriculture Law Betrayal

The situation changed completely in the late sixties and early seventies. Stability of food supply had been achieved for the most part and the economy was overflowing with vigor. Politicians and businessmen started thinking of how to bring the large number of farmers and their land into the picture.

Once food surpluses started to arise, the farmers became a weight around the government's neck. The food control system set up to ensure adequate food supply began to be regarded as a burden on the nation. The Basic Agriculture Law was established in 1961 to define the role and direction of Japanese agriculture. But instead of serving as a foundation for farmers, it established controls over the farmer and passed the reins of control to the financial community.

The general public started thinking that agricultural land could be put to better use in industry and housing than for food production. City dwellers even began to see farmers, reluctant to part with their land, as selfish monopolizers. Laborers and office workers joined the effort to drive farmers off their land, and taxes as high as those on housing land were levied on farmland.

The farmer lost both his land and the freedom to choose his crops. Farmers simply went with the flow of the times. Most lamented that they could not make a decent living off farming. Even though Japan's food self-sufficiency dropped below thirty percent, farmers were unable to speak up because the people were under the illusion that the farmland reduction policy was in the interest of the consumer.



## The Demonstration Farm as Weapon

Behind the claim that today's farming youth is being carefully trained as agricultural specialists and model farmers lie plans to wipe out small farms and proposals for a euthanasia of farming. Underlying the spectacular programs for modernizing agriculture and increasing productivity, and the calls to expand the scale of farming operations, lies a thinly-disguised contempt for the farmer.

While the one-acre farmer was doing all he could to work his way up to three or even five acres, policy leaders were saying that ten acres was not large enough, and were running demonstration farms of 150 acres. Clearly, farmers were pitted one against another in a fratricidal process of natural selection. To economists who supported the doctrine of international division of labor, the farmer's insistence on producing food was evidence of the obstinate farming temperament which they despised.



## The Price of Rice Is Not Set by Farmers

People talk of "cheap rice," but it has never been the farmer who sets the price of rice or other farm produce. Nor is it the farmer who determines production costs. The price of rice nowadays is the price calculated to support the manufacturers of agricultural equipment; it is the price needed for the production of new farm implements; it is the price at which fuel can be bought.

When Fukuoka visited the United States in the summer of 1979, the price of rice on the U.S. market was about 50 cents per pound — roughly the same as economy rice in Japan. Since gasoline was about one dollar per gallon, reports that rice could easily be imported at one-quarter to one-third the local price seemed incredible.

In natural farming, the cost of producing rice is essentially the same as wheat production, and both can be produced more cheaply than buying imported grain. The mechanism by which the market price of rice is set has nothing to do with farmers. Distribution costs in Japan were five times those in the United States and twice as high as in West Germany.



## Farmers Besieged from All Sides

Today's farmers are besieged from all sides. Angry voices rise from the cities crying that farmers are overprotected, over-subsidized, producing too much rice, and raising taxes. But these are superficial views of people who don't see the whole picture. At one time, six farming households supported one official. Today, there is reportedly one agriculture or forestry official for every full-time farmer.

Statistics tell us that the average American farmer feeds one hundred people and the average Japanese farmer only ten, but Japanese farmers actually have higher productivity than American farmers. It just appears the other way around because Americans farm under much better conditions. U.S. farmers working 500 to 700 acres had smaller net incomes than Japanese farmers on 3 to 5 acres.



## The Farmer-Nature Connection Severed

Today's farmers are in love with money. They no longer have time or affection for nature or their crops. All they have time for is to blindly follow the figures spit out by distribution industry computers and the plans of agricultural administrators. They do not talk with the land or converse with the crops; they are interested only in money crops. They grow produce without choosing the time or place, without giving a thought to the suitability of the land or crop.

When the farmer forgets the land to which he owes his existence and becomes concerned only with his own self-interest, when the consumer can no longer distinguish between food as the staff of life and food as merely nutrition, when the administrator looks down his nose at farmers and the industrialist scoffs at nature, then the land will answer with its death. Nature is not so kind as to forewarn a humanity so foolish as this.
