## Catch and Store Energy

## Permaculture Design Principle #2 — "Make Hay While the Sun Shines"

[[permaculture-principle-2-catch-store-energy]] addresses the fundamental challenge
of terrestrial life: energy arrives in pulses (sunlight, rainfall, seasonal growth)
but needs to be available continuously. Natural systems have evolved sophisticated
mechanisms for catching and storing energy; permaculture design aims to understand
and replicate these patterns in human-managed landscapes.

## The Energy Challenge of Land-Based Life

Holmgren identifies four fundamental constraints on terrestrial life that do not
apply to oceanic ecologies: gravity limits water storage, seasonal variation creates
pulses of abundance and scarcity, mineral nutrients are finite and leachable, and
carbon must be stored against constant oxidative loss. Understanding these constraints
is essential to designing productive, resilient landscapes.

## Water Storage in Landscape

Water is the primary energy carrier in terrestrial ecosystems. Natural landscapes
have evolved as efficient rainwater storages at multiple scales:

- **Vegetation** holds water in tissues, [[aact-forest-litter-fungi-mycorrhizal-ingham]] and mulch** act as an open sponge, absorbing and holding water
- **Soil chemistry and humus** provide stable moisture supply for plants
- **Deep subsoils**, especially clay, provide very stable moisture storage
- **Springs and soaks** release water slowly from subsoil storage
- **Wetlands and swamps** serve as temporary storages and filters — "the kidneys
  of catchment landscapes"
- **Lakes and pools** provide larger-scale storage in suitable geologies

The pattern moves from ephemeral to permanent storage as water moves downward under
gravity. Swamps and wetlands ameliorate destructive flood forces while capturing
energy as new alluvial soils deposited by slow-moving floodwater.

## The Shift in Catchment Management

Holmgren documents a radical shift in catchment management thinking: from the old
plumbing drainage model (draining water away fast) to a hydrological model (slowing
and filtering flow from mountains to sea) to an ecographic model (understanding
rivers as productive, self-renewing ecosystems).

The old model used large dams low in catchments with fast, constant flows —
destructive of natural resources. The new model recognizes sinuous, complex natural
floodplains that slow and divert flow optimally for maintaining productivity.
