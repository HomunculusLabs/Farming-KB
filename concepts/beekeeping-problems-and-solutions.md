## Beekeeping Problems and Solutions

Beekeeping involves constant problem-solving. This guide covers the most
common and serious issues beekeepers face, from parasitic mites to queen
failure, with practical solutions for each.

## Varroa Destructor Mites

Varroa is the single greatest threat to managed honey bees worldwide. These
parasitic mites feed on fat bodies of adult bees and developing brood,
transmitting deformed wing virus and other pathogens.

### Monitoring Methods
- **Alcohol wash** — most accurate; shake 300 bees (half cup) in alcohol,
  count mites. Threshold: 3% or 9+ mites
- **Sugar roll** — similar accuracy without killing bees
- **Sticky board** — natural mite drop over 24-72 hours; less precise but
  non-invasive
- **Powdered sugar** — dust bees, mites fall through screen; less accurate

### Treatment Options
- **Oxalic acid vapor** — effective during broodless periods (late winter,
  after a flow)
- **Formic acid (MAQS)** — can be used with honey supers on; effective but
  requires temperature management (50-85F)
- **Amitraz (Apivar)** — chemical strip treatment; 42-day treatment window;
  check for resistance in your area
- **Apiguard ([[thymol]])** — natural miticide; temperature-sensitive (above
  60F); cannot use with supers on
- **Drone brood trapping** — management technique using drone comb to attract
  mites, then removing and freezing the frame

### Integrated Strategy
Combine monitoring with rotating treatment chemicals to avoid resistance.
Never rely on a single method. See [[beekeeping-seasonal-calendar]] for
timing recommendations.

## Small Hive Beetle (Aethina tumida)

Small dark beetles that infest weak colonies, lay eggs in comb, and produce
slimy larvae that ruin honey.

### Prevention
- Maintain strong colonies — beetles prefer weak hives
- Keep apiary in full sun; beetles prefer shaded locations
- Reduce entrance size to limit beetle access
- Use beetle blaster traps with oil or [[diatomaceous-earth]] in corners of
  super boxes

### Treatment
- **Beetle traps** — insert between frames; fill with vegetable oil
- **Ground drench** — permethrin around the hive stand (beetles pupate in
  soil beneath hives)
- **Hive management** — keep colonies populous and honey supers harvested
  promptly
- **In-Hive Beetle Ejector** — screen inserts that force beetles into oil
  traps

## Nosema (Nosema apis and Nosema ceranae)

Microsporidian gut parasite causing dysentery, reduced lifespan, and colony
decline. More common in winter and spring.

### Symptoms
- Feces streaks on the hive exterior and frames
- Bees crawling on the ground, unable to fly (K-wing)
- Reduced brood area and sluggish colony growth

### Treatment
- **Fumidil-B (fumagillin)** — fed in sugar syrup in fall or early spring
- **Nutrition** — ensure adequate pollen availability; feed pollen patties
  if needed
- **Hive management** — replace old dark comb (nosema spores persist in wax);
  provide good ventilation

## Queen Issues

### Queenlessness
- **Signs** — no eggs or brood, drone-laying pattern (eggs on sides of cells),
  agitated or irritable bees, queen cells present
- **Diagnosis** — find the queen or look for fresh eggs (standing upright)
- **Solution** — introduce a new mated queen or combine with a strong colony;
  see [[queen-rearing-basics]] for raising replacements

### Failing Queen
- **Signs** — spotty brood pattern, supersedure cells on frame faces,
  dwindling population, drone-heavy brood
- **Solution** — requeen immediately; old queens produce poorly and the
  colony will decline rapidly

### Laying Workers
- **Signs** — multiple eggs per cell, eggs on cell walls (not centered),
  all drone brood, no queen present for 3+ weeks
- **Solution** — difficult to fix; best approach is to shake all bees onto
  a new stand several feet away and introduce a queen in a cage, or combine
  with a queenright colony

## Swarming

Swarming is natural colony reproduction but represents lost honey production
for the beekeeper.

### Prevention
- **Provide space** — add supers before the colony is crowded
- **Requeen annually** — younger queens are less likely to swarm
- **Remove swarm cells** — during weekly inspections, cut out queen cells
- **Perform splits** — pre-emptively divide colonies to simulate the
  effect of swarming
- **Checkerboarding** — alternate honey and empty frames in the top super
  to disrupt congestion signals

### When Prevention Fails
- Capture swarms using techniques in [[swarm-capture-guide]]
- Rehive swarms promptly — a feral swarm in a wall or tree is lost

## Chalkbrood

Fungal brood disease causing mummified larvae that look like white chalk
lumps.

### Causes
- Cool, damp conditions; poor ventilation; poor nutrition; weak colony
### Solution
- Improve ventilation, requeen with hygienic stock, ensure adequate nutrition,
  replace old comb. No chemical treatment needed.

## Wax Moth

Greater and lesser wax moth larvae destroy comb in weak or stored equipment.

### Prevention
- Store drawn comb with paradichlorobenzene (PDB) crystals or in freezers
- Keep colonies strong enough to defend against invasion
- Freeze infested equipment for 48 hours before reuse

## Robbing

Strong colonies raid weak ones, especially during dearths.

### Prevention
- Reduce entrances of weak colonies
