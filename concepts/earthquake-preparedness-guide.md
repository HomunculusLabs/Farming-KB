# Earthquake Preparedness Guide

Earthquakes strike without warning and can cause catastrophic damage to structures, infrastructure, and utilities. Unlike hurricanes or floods, there is no advance notice and no evacuation window. Preparation must be done in advance. This guide covers structural assessment, non-structural hazard mitigation, emergency supplies, and post-earthquake safety procedures.

## Understanding Earthquake Risk

Earthquakes occur along fault lines, but damaging shaking can be felt hundreds of miles from the epicenter. Risk factors include:

- **Proximity to known fault lines** — USGS seismic hazard maps (earthquake.usgs.gov) show risk by region
- **Soil type** — Soft soils (alluvium, landfill, liquefaction-prone soils) amplify shaking significantly
- **Building age and construction type** — Unreinforced masonry, pre-1970s homes with cripple walls, and soft-story buildings are most vulnerable
- **Topography** — Steep slopes are prone to earthquake-triggered landslides
- **Historical seismicity** — Regions with past earthquakes are likely to experience future ones

### Earthquake Magnitude and Shaking Intensity

- **Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source (Richter scale, now Moment Magnitude)
- **Intensity** measures the shaking experienced at a specific location (Modified Mercalli Intensity scale, I-XII)
- A magnitude 6.0 at 10 miles depth can cause more damage than a 7.0 at 50 miles depth
- Each whole number increase in magnitude represents approximately 32 times more energy release

## Structural Assessment and Strengthening

### Foundation Inspection

The most critical structural element. A compromised foundation during an earthquake leads to partial or total building collapse.

**Check for:**
- Foundation bolts — Is the house bolted to its foundation? (Homes built before 1940 often are not)
- Cripple walls — The short wood-framed walls between the foundation and the first floor. These are extremely vulnerable. Brace them with structural plywood and blocking.
- Cracks in the foundation — Horizontal cracks are more serious than vertical cracks
- Bolting schedule — Foundation bolts should be spaced no more than 6 feet apart, within 12 inches of each end

**Retrofit measures:**
- Install foundation anchor bolts (1/2-inch diameter, minimum) if not present
- Brace cripple walls with 1/2-inch structural plywood and metal framing clips
- Install earthquake-resistant gas shut-off valve on the gas meter
- Reinforce the connection between the house frame and foundation with steel plate washers

### Chimney Safety

Unreinforced masonry chimneys are one of the most dangerous structural elements during an earthquake.

- Have the chimney inspected by a structural engineer or masonry contractor
- Brace the chimney to the roof framing with steel strapping
- Consider replacing unreinforced masonry chimneys with a metal flue or reinforced chimney
- Install a chimney brace kit (available at hardware stores)

### Water Heater Strapping

Water heaters are top-heavy and can topple during strong shaking, breaking gas lines and causing fires or flooding.

**Strapping requirements:**
- Use two metal straps (plumber's tape or earthquake straps) — one in the upper third, one in the lower third
- Wrap completely around the water heater
- Secure to wall studs with lag screws and washers
- Straps must be tight enough to prevent the water heater from shifting more than 2 inches
- Flexible gas and water connections provide additional protection
- This is a simple, inexpensive retrofit (under $30 in materials) that can prevent a house fire

### Structural Hazards

- Unreinforced masonry (brick, cinder block) walls: Brace or replace
- Garage with living space above (soft story): Consult an engineer
- Homes on stilts or pier foundations: May need cross-bracing
- Hillside homes: Assess slope stability and foundation adequacy

## Non-Structural Hazard Mitigation

Most earthquake injuries are caused by falling objects, not building collapse. Secure everything that could fall, slide, or tip over.

### Heavy Furniture and Appliances
- Strap water heaters, refrigerators, freezers, and ranges to wall studs using appliance straps
- Secure bookshelves, cabinets, and tall furniture to walls with L-brackets or nylon straps
- Place heavy items on lower shelves
- Install latches on all cabinet doors (especially upper kitchen cabinets)
- Use museum wax or putty to secure decorative items and electronics

### Overhead Hazards
- Do not hang heavy objects (pictures, mirrors, chandeliers) over beds or sitting areas
- Use closed hooks and picture wire rated for the weight
- Brace light fixtures to ceiling framing
- Install safety film on windows to prevent glass shattering

### Hazardous Materials
- Secure propane tanks with chains to a solid structure
- Store pesticides, herbicides, fuels, and chemicals in locked, latched cabinets
- Store incompatible chemicals separately to prevent mixing during shaking
- Know how to shut off gas, water, and electricity (keep wrench near gas meter)

## Emergency Supplies for Earthquakes

Earthquakes can disrupt utilities (water, gas, electricity, sewage) for days to weeks. Maintain supplies for minimum 14 days.

### Water
- 1 gallon per person per day (drinking and hygiene)
- Additional water for pets and livestock
- Water purification supplies (bleach, tablets, filter)
- Store water in clean, food-grade containers, rotated every 6 months

### Food
- Non-perishable food requiring no cooking (canned goods, dried fruit, granola, crackers)
- Manual can opener
- Camp stove with fuel for outdoor cooking
- Comfort foods (stress management)

### Shelter and Warmth
- Emergency thermal blankets (Mylar)
- Sleeping bags
- Tent or tarp for shelter if building is damaged
- Warm clothing and sturdy shoes (keep by bed)
- Work gloves, dust masks, safety glasses

### Tools and Supplies
- Crowbar and pry bar (for debris removal and exiting damaged buildings)
- Wrench (for shutting off gas and water)
- Axe or saw
- Flashlights and headlamps (one per person)
- Batteries
- Fire extinguisher (ABC type) — secured to a wall, accessible
- Heavy-duty plastic sheeting and duct tape

### Communication and Documents
- Battery or hand-crank NOAA weather radio
- Charged phone with backup battery
- Ham radio (see [[homestead-communication]])
- Important documents in waterproof bag (insurance, ID, deeds, medical records)
- Cash (ATMs and card readers will not work without power)

### First Aid
- Comprehensive first aid kit (see [[homestead-first-aid-guide]])
- Include extra trauma supplies: splints, tourniquet, hemostatic gauze
- Prescription medications (30-day minimum supply)
