## False Peyotes — Mexican Cactus Ethnobotany

"False peyotes" are a group of cactus species used by the Tarahumare (Rarámuri) and Huichol (Wixárika) Indians of northern and central Mexico as substitutes for or complements to true peyote (ethnomycology). These species belong to several genera within the Cactaceae family, most notably Ariocarpus, Epithelantha, and Pelecyphora. While they are not botanically related to peyote, they occupy important roles in the indigenous pharmacopeia and ceremonial systems of the region.

The term "false peyote" is a Western ethnobotanical classification. In indigenous understanding, these plants are all legitimate members of the broader hikuli complex — the Tarahumare category of sacred cacti — each with its own character, spiritual power, and specific ceremonial purpose. The distinction between "true" and "false" peyote reflects Western scientific priorities rather than indigenous categories of understanding.

## Hikuli Mulato — Epithelantha micromeris

The Tarahumare call Epithelantha micromeris "hikuli mulato," believing that consuming this small cactus makes the eyes so large and clear that the user can see sorcerers. This tiny button cactus, rarely exceeding 5 cm in diameter, is densely covered with white spines that give it a silvery appearance. It grows in limestone rock crevices throughout the Chihuahuan Desert, often partially hidden among rocks and gravel. The genus name Epithelantha means "flower upon the tubercle," referring to the small pink or white flowers that emerge from the apex of each tubercle, often followed by bright red berries.

The Tarahumare classify it among their "hikuli" plants — the broad category of cacti believed to possess spiritual power. Unlike peyote, which is the primary hikuli, hikuli mulato is considered a secondary plant used for specific divinatory purposes. The belief in its ability to reveal hidden sorcerers reflects a broader Mesoamerican pattern of using psychoactive plants as tools for detecting deception and hidden threats. This divinatory function parallels the use of other psychoactive plants across cultures for uncovering truth and revealing the unseen.

## Hikuli Sunami — Ariocarpus fissuratus

Known as "hikuli sunami" to the Tarahumare, Ariocarpus fissuratus is reputed to be more powerful than peyote itself. The cactus is called "sunami" and the Tarahumare believe that robbers are powerless to steal when this cactus calls soldiers to its aid — a belief that underscores its role as a protective plant in the indigenous worldview.

Ariocarpus fissuratus is a geophytic cactus, with much of its body buried below the soil surface. Only the flattened, triangular tubercles protrude above ground, making it extremely difficult to locate in its natural habitat. This subterranean growth habit may contribute to its reputation as a hidden, powerful plant whose effects are slow but profound. The species is found in the Chihuahuan Desert of northern Mexico and southern Texas, where it grows on limestone plains and rocky hillsides. It is sometimes called the "living rock cactus" due to its remarkable resemblance to the surrounding stones, a camouflage that has likely protected it from over-collection by all but the most knowledgeable harvesters.

The Tarahumare"s belief that sunami can summon supernatural protection against thieves reflects a worldview in which sacred plants are understood as sentient beings with their own agency and powers. This animistic understanding of plant consciousness is widespread among indigenous cultures of the Americas and stands in contrast to the Western scientific framework that views plants as passive chemical factories.

## Hikuli Walula Saeliami — The Unidentified Sacred Cactus

Perhaps the most mysterious of the false peyotes is "hikuli walula saeliami," meaning "hikuli of greatest authority." This cactus is so rare that it has not yet been identified by botanists, but it is reputedly the most powerful of all hallucinogenic cacti according to Tarahumare tradition. Its identity remains one of the unresolved questions in Mesoamerican ethnobotany.

Several candidates have been proposed by researchers, including rare Ariocarpus species such as A. agavoides, unusual forms of peyote-cultivation|Lophophora, and possibly Aztekium ritteri. Whether this cactus represents a genuine botanical species or a cultural memory of a now-extinct plant remains an open question.

## Tsuwiri — Ariocarpus retusus

Among the Huichol people, Ariocarpus retusus is called "tsuwiri" and is considered dangerous to eat. It is believed capable of sorcery and deception, driving a man mad in the desert if he has not been properly instructed by the marakame (shaman) or is not in a state of ritual purity that allows him to find the true peyote plant.

This belief reflects a broader pattern in Huichol ethnobotany where certain plants serve as "test" or "decoy" species — their negative effects are understood as consequences of improper preparation, wrong intent, or lack of ritual context rather than inherent toxicity. The Huichol peyote pilgrimage to Wirikuta is a carefully structured journey where the shaman guides participants to correctly identify and harvest true peyote, and tsuwiri serves as a cautionary element in this elaborate ceremonial process.

## Pelecyphora aselliformis

Pelecyphora aselliformis, another cactus classified among the "false peyotes," is a small, globe-shaped cactus bearing distinctive comb-like spines arranged in rows resembling the segments of a woodlouse (the origin of its species epithet). Native to the state of San Luis Potosí in central Mexico, it grows in semi-desert grasslands on limestone soils at elevations between 1,600 and 2,200 meters.
