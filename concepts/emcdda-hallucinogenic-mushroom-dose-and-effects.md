# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Dose and Effects

## Factors Influencing Effects

Mushroom effects are dependent on dose, individual reaction and
sensitivity to [[psilocybin]], previous experiences, and the setting. The
major effects are related to the central nervous system, with some
sympathomimetic effects. Subjective effects vary greatly between
individuals and from one episode of use to the next within the same
person.

## Dosage Guidelines

The dose "recommended" for recreational use is reported to be between 1
and 3.5 to 5 grams of dried mushrooms. The dosage for fresh, wet
mushrooms is believed to be approximately 10 times higher (10-50
grams). These ranges should be interpreted with caution, as it is
difficult to transpose the maximum dose of the active substance into
mushroom weight or number, given that concentration may vary and there
is usually more than one active substance present.

Short-term tolerance may develop rapidly to both physical and
psychological effects, meaning dosages may increase over repeated use
within a short period.

## Route of Administration

Users ingest mushrooms raw or dried, or brew them into tea. Because
hallucinogenic mushrooms generally do not taste good, users typically
chop them into smaller pieces. Eating them mixed or cooked into food may
cause nausea and vomiting. Mushrooms can also be dried for later use.

User reports on the internet suggest dried mushrooms are also smoked,
said to induce faster but milder effects. However, it is not clear
whether smoking psilocybin and [[psilocin]]-containing mushrooms can produce
psychoactive effects. Literature reports also refer to mushroom extract
being intravenously injected, which results in more severe acute
effects.

## Onset and Duration

Users report that the onset of effects is between 10 to 60 minutes,
depending on the modality of ingestion, physical condition, food
intake, and user experience. As with other hallucinogens, the experience
is most often described as "tripping." The duration of a trip is usually
2 to 6 hours, with after-effects (e.g. difficulties sleeping) lasting
an additional 2-6 hours.

## Acute Psychological Effects

Subjective effects described by users range widely:

**Mild/positive effects:**
- Relaxation (comparable to cannabis)
- Giddiness and uncontrollable laughter
- Energy, joy, euphoria
- Visual enhancement (seeing colours brighter)
- Mental stimulation and new insights
- Quickly changing emotions

**Intense perceptual effects:**
- Visual disturbances (moving surfaces, waves)
- Delusions and altered perception of real events
- Altered perception of images and faces
- Real hallucinations
- Impaired judgement of time or distance

**Negative effects ("bad trips"):**
- Restlessness and incoordination
- Anxiety and panic reactions
- Sense of unreality or depersonalisation
- Psychosis-like states
- Paranoia and total loss of reality

"Good trips" are associated with relaxation, mental stimulation, new
insights, and quickly changing emotions. However, users report that the
effects are not viewed as positively as the entactogenic effects of
ecstasy. Negative effects may outweigh the positive effects in social
situations, limiting repeat use.

## Acute Physiological Effects

General physiological effects are not significant and may include:
- Dizziness and nausea
- Weakness and muscle aching
- Shivering and abdominal pain
- Dilation of pupils (mydriasis)
- Mild-to-moderate increase in heart rate (tachycardia)
- Mild-to-moderate increase in breathing rate (tachypnea)
- Elevation of blood pressure
- Body temperature generally remains normal

More pronounced symptoms such as severe stomach pain, persistent
vomiting, and diarrhoea have been recorded. A UK Mixmag survey (2005)
found that over a quarter of those who had used hallucinogenic mushrooms
in the last year experienced nausea or vomiting. The tendency for
temporarily increased blood pressure may be a risk factor for users with
cardiovascular conditions, especially untreated hypertension.

## Comparison with Other Hallucinogens

Unlike ecstasy, which is purchased and taken in a convenient tablet form,
mushrooms are usually chewed or brewed in hot water. The cumbersome
route of administration, combined with an unfamiliar and unpleasant
taste, is likely to serve as a barrier to widespread or frequent use.
Opportunities for young people to witness others using mushrooms are
therefore limited, reducing the potential for diffusion compared to
ecstasy.

Qualitative research in the Netherlands reports that users do not find
mushroom use a particularly pleasant experience, as it lacks the
positive mood-altering (entactogenic) effects of ecstasy. Hallucinogenic
mushrooms tend to be taken as an experimental drug, with people
generally discontinuing use after a few times.
