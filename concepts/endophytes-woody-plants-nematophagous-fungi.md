## Overview

[[fungal-endophytes]] are not restricted to grass species and agricultural crops. Wilson (2000) reviewed endophytes of woody plants and demonstrated similarities in their effects on host plants to those documented in grasses. Beyond endophytes, nematophagous fungi represent another class of fungi that regulate animal populations through more subtle interactions than outright pathogenicity — by trapping and killing free-living nematodes in soil, including those with pathogenic stages as intestinal parasites of ruminant animals.

## Endophytes in Woody Plants

### Effects on Insect Herbivores

The major grazers of woody plant leaves are insect larvae, and endophyte presence has been shown to reduce grazing intensity. This can lead to both spatial and temporal alteration of grazing insect abundance within the canopy of the host plant. Wilson (2000) notes that much of the evidence for this is correlatory, but the impacts on insect populations could affect predator avoidance and maturation time.

### The Emory Oak Leaf Miner Case

Faeth and Hammon (1997a,b) documented a counterexample: a positive association between populations of the Emory oak leaf miner (Cameraria spp.) and infection by endophytes Ophiognomonia cryptica, Plectohomella, and Asteromella. Key findings include:

- The incidence of endophyte infection was not affected by the population of insects
- The survival and larval growth of leaf miners was not related to the degree of infection
- However, Wilson and Faeth (2001) later showed that endophyte presence significantly reduced oviposition by adult leaf miners

This suggests the endophyte's effect is not primarily for plant protection against the leaf miner itself, but the potential detrimental effect of the endophyte (as a habitat for the insect) is partially mitigated by reduced colonization by the insect that acts as its vector. The interaction is thus more complex than a simple defense mutualism.

### Leaf Abscission and Nutrient Cycling

Wilson (1993) and Faeth and Hammon (1997b) showed that endophyte-infected leaves of trees were more likely to abscise earlier than uninfected leaves. The implications for nutrient cycling are significant but poorly understood:

- If nutrient resorption is restricted in endophyte-infected leaves, higher-resource leaf litter would result
- This could considerably affect rates of leaf litter breakdown, nutrient mineralization, and soil fertility under endophyte-infected trees
- Endophytes may affect leaf litter decay in early stages of decomposition
- Petrini et al. (1992) showed that "typical" saprotrophic fungi in decaying leaf litter usually rapidly replace endophytes

## The Mutualism-Parasitism Continuum

### Pathogenic Fungi Mimicking Endophyte Effects

Kruess (2002) demonstrated that the plant pathogenic fungus Phoma destructiva acted similarly to endophytic fungi known to reduce herbivory. Infection of the thistle Cirsium arvense with P. destructiva resulted in:

- Infected plants being less palatable to larvae of the beetle Cassidia rubiginosa
- Beetle development and pupal survival being retarded on infected plants

This finding blurs the distinction between mutualism and parasitism. Known plant pathogenic fungi can have similar antiherbivore effects to leaf endophytes considered to be mutualists, suggesting a continuum of degrees of magnitude of observed effect on the host plant rather than discrete categories.

### Physiological Basis of the Continuum

Evidence supporting the continuum model includes:

- Endophytic fungi increase apoplastic carbohydrate concentrations in leaves (e.g., big bluegrass Poa ampla: glucose 94.5 vs 51.1, fructose 205.5 vs 132.2 μg/g in infected vs. endophyte-free plants)
- Alkaloid production is nitrogen-demanding, and these fungi can alter the nitrogen balance within the host plant
- The fungal pathogen Cercospora produces cercosporin, which kills host plant cells to make more nutrients available — an extreme form of the same physiological strategy

## Ancient Associations

The body of evidence cited by Clay (1997) shows strong inference from controlled greenhouse studies and limited field studies that fungal endophytes can play a significant role in determining community composition of plants by selectively altering competition and fitness of infected species. The presence of fungi within host plant tissues is an ancient association dating back to the Silurian period (Kidston and Lang, 1921; Pirozynski and Malloch, 1975).

## Nematophagous Fungi

### Trapping Mechanisms

Nematophagous fungi have adopted a variety of strategies for capturing free-living nematodes:

- **Sticky secretions** from hyphae
- **Specialized structures** derived from modified hyphae (sticky knobs of Nematoctonus sp.)
- **Constricting rings or nets** produced by Arthrobotrys and Dactylella species — these close around nematodes by an almost instantaneous tactile-induced change in turgor pressure, with hyphae cells creating the noose

After trapping, the fungus secretes enzymes and digests the nematode, using it as a nitrogen source.

### Biological Control of Ruminant Parasites

Many free-living nematodes in soil have a pathogenic stage as gut intestinal parasites of ruminant animals, such as Trichostrongylus colubriformis and other strongyles. Key findings on the use of nematophagous fungi as biocontrol agents:

- Conidia and chlamydospores of Duddingtonia and Arthrobotrys survive passage through the gut of ruminants (Faedo et al., 1997)
- Duddingtonia flagrans spores consistently survived better than Arthrobotrys spp. spores
- Spores germinate in dung where free-living nematode stages develop high population densities
- By trapping and killing nematodes, these fungi significantly reduce the infective potential of the nematode population (Faedo et al., 1997; Hay et al., 1998)

### Limitations as Biocontrol Agents

Kerry and Jafee (1997) reviewed nematophagous fungi as biocontrol agents and highlighted two limiting factors:

1. **Survival of propagules**: Little information exists on the survival of conidia and trapping structures, or their role as food for other soil-inhabiting fauna
2. **Density-dependent parasitism**: Nematode trapping efficiency is closely correlated to the density of nematode-trapping fungal structures in soil, which relates to conidial density — providing adequate inoculum density may be a limiting factor

The reduced ability to recover fungi from feces of animals in the tropics (Manueli et al., 1999) suggests the use of these fungi as biocontrol agents may be geographically restricted.

## Fungal Parasites of Plant-Parasitic Nematodes

Beyond trapping fungi, other fungi parasitize nematode eggs and females:

- **Viaene and Abawi (1998)**: Arthrobotrys and Monacrosporium reduced populations of juvenile Meloidogyne nematodes; Fusarium, Verticillium, and Alternaria parasitized nematode egg masses
- **Kerry (1988)**: Approximately 150 fungal species were found to parasitize female nematodes and eggs, including Nematophthora gynophila and Verticillium chlamydosporium controlling cereal cyst nematode populations
- **Elsen et al. (2001)**: [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] significantly reduced populations of the burrowing nematode Radopholus similis, with the major effect on females — suggesting potential long-term population regulation through reduced fecundity

## Nematicidal Basidiomycetes

Barron and Thorn (1987) found that basidiomycete fungi of the genus Pleurotus (P. ostreatus, P. strigosus, P. subareolatus, P. cornucopiae) produced minute spathulate secretory cells that generated droplets of toxins killing nematodes on contact within 30 seconds. Subsequent to nematode death, fungal hyphae penetrated orifices and destroyed the nematode. This represents a fundamentally different mechanism from trapping — direct chemical toxicity rather than physical capture.
