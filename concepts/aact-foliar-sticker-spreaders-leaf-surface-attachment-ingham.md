# AACT Foliar Sticker-Spreaders and Leaf Surface Attachment

## Overview

When applying Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) as a foliar spray, one of the most critical challenges is ensuring that the beneficial microorganisms actually adhere to and colonize the leaf surface (phyllosphere). Without proper attachment, the organisms can be washed away by rain, blown off by wind, or simply fail to establish on the waxy, hydrophobic leaf cuticle. Elaine Ingham's field guide addresses this extensively, noting that sticker-spreaders are essential adjuvants that improve the efficacy of foliar AACT applications.

## The Leaf Surface Challenge

Plant leaves present a hostile environment for microbial colonization. The cuticle is covered with epicuticular waxes that are inherently hydrophobic, meaning water-based compost tea tends to bead up and run off rather than spread evenly across the leaf surface. Additionally, many leaves have trichomes, hairs, and complex surface topography that can trap or repel organisms depending on their size and motility.

Ingham emphasizes that simply spraying AACT onto foliage is insufficient if the organisms cannot attach. The beneficial bacteria and fungi need to physically contact the leaf surface, find or create suitable microhabitats, and begin reproducing. Without sticker-spreaders, a significant portion of the applied biology may be lost.

## Sticker-Spreader Options for AACT

### Aloe Vera

Aloe vera gel is one of the most recommended natural sticker-spreaders for AACT applications. It contains polysaccharides (acemannan) that create a thin film on leaf surfaces, helping microorganisms adhere. Aloe also provides additional benefits:

- Contains natural saponins that help wet the leaf surface
- Provides simple sugars that serve as a food source for beneficial microbes
- Is biologically compatible with the aerobic organisms in AACT
- Does not harm or kill beneficial bacteria and fungi

The recommended approach is to use pure, food-grade aloe vera gel or juice added to the tea just before application. Ingham notes that aloe should be mixed at appropriate rates to avoid creating anaerobic conditions.

### Saponin-Based Spreaders

Saponins are natural surfactants found in many plants (such as yucca and quillaja). They reduce surface tension, allowing the tea to spread more evenly across leaf surfaces. Ingham mentions saponin-containing products in the context of Nu-Film and similar commercial adjuvants.

Key considerations for saponin use:
- Must be derived from natural sources to avoid harming beneficial biology
- Should be used at recommended concentrations to avoid phytotoxicity
- Help break the surface tension of water, improving coverage on waxy leaves

### Fish Hydrolysates as Wetting Agents

Fish hydrolysates can serve dual purposes as both microbial food sources and mild wetting agents. However, Ingham cautions that they must be used carefully:

- Some fish products contain compounds that can be antagonistic to certain beneficial organisms
- The protein content can vary widely between products
- Should be tested for compatibility with the specific AACT biology being applied
- Over-application can lead to nitrogen burn on sensitive leaf tissue

### Unsulphured Molasses

Molasses is commonly used in AACT brewing as a bacterial food source, but it also has mild sticker properties when included in the final application. Important distinctions:

- Must be unsulphured (blackstrap molasses without sulfur dioxide)
- Sulfured molasses can harm beneficial aerobic organisms
- Provides a food source that helps organisms establish on leaf surfaces
- Should not be over-applied as the sugar concentration can create osmotic stress

## What NOT to Use

Ingham provides clear warnings about products that should never be combined with AACT foliar applications:

### Dormant Oils

Dormant oils (horticultural oils) are explicitly NOT recommended for use with AACT. These petroleum-based or vegetable-based oils:
- Coat and smother beneficial microorganisms on contact
- Create anaerobic conditions on the leaf surface
- Defeat the entire purpose of applying aerobic biology
- Are fundamentally incompatible with the living organisms in compost tea

This is a critical mistake that many growers make, combining a biological approach with a chemical eradication approach that work at cross-purposes.

## Rain and Reapplication

Rain presents a significant challenge to foliar AACT programs. Ingham addresses several key points:

- Heavy rain can wash off beneficial organisms before they establish
- The organisms need time (typically 12-24 hours) to attach and begin colonizing
- Some rain sticker-spreaders can provide limited protection against light rainfall
- Reapplication may be necessary after significant rain events
- Application timing should consider weather forecasts

The concept of "minimum coverage" is important: the goal is not to coat every square millimeter of leaf surface but to achieve sufficient coverage that beneficial organisms can establish and spread on their own. Ingham notes that beneficial organisms, once established, can reproduce on the leaf surface and spread to areas not directly sprayed.

## Application Timing and Temperature

Temperature significantly affects both application efficacy and organism survival:

- Organisms reproduce more slowly at colder temperatures
- Foliar applications should be made during mild conditions
- Avoid spraying in extreme heat (above 85°F/29°C) which can stress organisms
- Early morning or late afternoon applications are preferred
- Freezing temperatures will kill many beneficial organisms on the leaf surface
- Fungi are particularly sensitive to temperature extremes on leaf surfaces

## Nutrient Considerations for Foliar AACT

Ingham addresses the question of whether additional nutrients should be added to AACT for foliar applications:

- The primary purpose of foliar AACT is biological, not nutritional
- However, small amounts of additional nutrients can help organisms establish
- Nitrogen should ideally be in protein or amino acid form, not as nitrate
- Nitrate forms of nitrogen can be taken up directly by leaves but don't support the biology
- Protein forms feed the microbial community on the leaf surface

## Key Principles

1. **Test compatibility**: Always test sticker-spreaders with your specific AACT before large-scale application
2. **Less is more**: Excessive sticker-spreader concentration can create problems
3. **Timing matters**: Apply when conditions favor organism survival and attachment
4. **Avoid conflicts**: Never combine AACT with products that kill biology (dormant oils, copper sprays, etc.)
5. **Coverage over concentration**: Better to achieve thorough coverage at appropriate rates than to concentrate organisms in small areas
6. **Monitor results**: Use a microscope to verify that organisms are present and active on leaf surfaces after application

## Practical Application Rates

While specific rates vary by crop and conditions, Ingham's field guide includes one-sheet application rates for various industries. General guidelines for foliar AACT:

- Apply sufficient volume to achieve thorough coverage of both upper and lower leaf surfaces
- Sticker-spreader concentration should follow product-specific recommendations
- AACT should be applied within hours of brewing completion for maximum organism viability
- Typical foliar rates range from 10-30 gallons per acre depending on crop canopy
