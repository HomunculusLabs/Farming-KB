# Fukuoka's Natural Rice and Barley Cultivation

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

[[fukuoka-living-soil]] developed a complete system for growing rice and winter grains (barley and wheat) using no-tillage, no-fertilizer, no-pesticide, and no-weeding methods. Over 40 years of experimentation, he evolved from conventional transplanting to a fully natural system of succession cropping that produces yields equal to or better than scientific [[farming-with-air-water-and-sunlight]] a fraction of the labor.

## The Three Stages of Development

Fukuoka passed through three stages in developing his method for barley and wheat:

1. **Tillage, ridging, and drilling:** Conventional ridge cultivation with various seeding width experiments
2. **Light-tillage, low-ridge or level-row cultivation:** [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], narrow row drilling
3. **No-tillage, direct-seeding cultivation:** Broadcasting seed on unplowed fields with straw mulch

## The Discovery of Natural Seeding

The breakthrough came during Fukuoka's work on the yellow rice borer eradication program in Kochi Prefecture. While inspecting fields to ensure no rice remained standing (to starve the borers), he discovered volunteer rice seedlings sprouting from unhulled seed spilled where farmers had threshed rice the previous year. This observation of "planting without sowing" led to his method of biennial or overwintering cultivation.

He later noticed overwintered rice germinating from seed in many locations -- in drainage channels, along field margins, and in footpaths between paddies. This demonstrated that rice seed could survive winter and germinate naturally without nursery beds, transplanting, or any human intervention.

## The Merged Rice-Barley Method

After years of experimentation, Fukuoka found that rice and barley -- both members of the grass family -- could be grown in essentially the same way through direct seeding on level fields. The key was succession cropping with clover as a living ground cover.

### The Annual Cycle

**Autumn (after rice harvest):**
1. While rice is still standing, broadcast barley/wheat seed over the field
2. Simultaneously broadcast clover seed
3. Scatter rice straw over the entire field as mulch

**Winter:**
- Barley/wheat grows through the straw mulch
- Clover establishes as ground cover
- No tillage, no fertilizer, no weeding

**Spring:**
- Barley/wheat matures
- Clover grows vigorously, suppressing weeds
- Harvest barley/wheat

**Early Summer:**
- Broadcast rice seed over the standing barley
- Or broadcast rice seed immediately after barley harvest
- The clover suppresses weeds and fixes nitrogen

**Summer:**
- Rice grows among the clover
- Clover may be temporarily suppressed by rice shade but recovers
- No irrigation pumping is needed if [[water-management]] is natural

**Autumn:**
- Harvest rice
- Begin the cycle again

### Key Innovations

**Straw mulch:** Fukuoka was the first to spread fresh rice straw on fields in autumn, defying agricultural experts who forbade this practice for fear of disease. He confirmed through experiment that rice straw decomposes entirely by spring, leaving no trace of pathogenic microbes. The straw mulch provides:

- Weed suppression (the primary function)
- Moisture retention
- Organic matter as it decomposes
- Microbial habitat

**Clover ground cover:** Ladino clover serves multiple functions:
- Nitrogen fixation (though Fukuoka downplays this as the primary benefit)
- Weed suppression (clover outcompetes most weeds once established)
- Soil protection from erosion
- Moisture retention
- Habitat for beneficial insects

**No nursery beds:** Rice is direct-seeded, eliminating the entire process of seedling raising and transplanting.

**No ridges:** Both rice and barley grow on level fields, the most natural arrangement for grass family crops.

## Results of No-Tillage Cultivation

Fukuoka documented the following results from more than ten years of continuous no-tillage rice/winter grain succession cropping:

1. **Soil improvement:** No-tillage cultivation does not degrade the land; it actually improves and enriches it. The soil becomes darker, richer, and more aggregated each year.
2. **Complete germination:** Broadcasting achieves total germination when combined with proper timing and mulch.
3. **Weed control:** The combination of straw mulch and clover ground cover provides effective weed suppression.
4. **Higher yields:** The method is higher-yielding than conventional tillage-based methods.
5. **Minimal labor:** The labor requirement is a tiny fraction of conventional methods.

## Clay Pellet Seed Coating

For natural rice cultivation to work, the seed must survive on the soil surface through winter until conditions favor germination. Fukuoka developed a method of coating rice seeds in clay pellets:

- Mix rice seed with clay and water to form small pellets (approximately 1 cm diameter)
- The clay coating protects the seed from:
  - Being eaten by birds and rodents
  - Desiccation during dry periods
  - Rotting during wet periods
  - Freezing during cold weather
- The clay pellet eliminates the need for chemical pesticide protectants that conventional agriculture uses on pre-germinated seed
- When conditions are right (adequate moisture and temperature), the seed germinates through the clay coating

This clay pellet technique makes winter sowing possible and is one of the key innovations that enables Fukuoka's rice cultivation system.

## Rice as a Potential Perennial

Fukuoka observed that in warm climates, rice can behave as a perennial. Rice plants that are not harvested at the end of the season can regrow from the base the following year, producing a second (or third, or fourth) crop without replanting. In tropical and subtropical regions, rice may be capable of continuous, indefinite production from a single planting.

This observation challenges the fundamental assumption that rice is strictly an annual crop. Fukuoka's ratooning observations suggest that rice shares characteristics with perennial grasses, and that natural selection in the right environment could produce rice varieties that reliably regrow each season.

## The Barley Cultivation Journey

Fukuoka's path to natural barley cultivation illustrates his experimental method:

**Seeding width experiments:** He tried increasing sowing area by 25%, 30%, and 40%. Broad seeding reduced stability. Multiple rows per ridge caused excessive vegetative growth with few heads. Narrower seeding with wider spacing between rows improved yields.

**Ridge height reduction:** Moving from high ridges to low ridges to level rows progressively simplified cultivation while maintaining or improving yields.

**The straw breakthrough:** When he scattered fresh rice straw over planted barley fields, weed control was dramatically improved. This contradicted the expert prohibition against leaving straw on paddies.

**The clover addition:** Sowing ladino clover as ground cover together with barley provided permanent weed suppression and soil improvement.

## Water Management

Fukuoka's approach to water management for rice differs from conventional flooding:

- Continuous flooding is not practiced; instead, the field follows a more natural wet-dry cycle
- The clover ground cover and straw mulch help retain soil moisture
- Water is introduced when needed but not maintained at constant depth
- The goal is to work with the natural water cycle rather than fighting it
- In some years, the clover provides enough ground cover that the rice can grow with minimal irrigation

## Comparison with Conventional Rice Farming

### Japan's Rice Farming Stages

Fukuoka traces four stages of Japanese rice farming:
1. **1940:** Primitive farming (improvements in tilling methods)
2. **1950:** Animal-powered agriculture (increased fertilizer production)
3. **1960:** Scientific farming (mechanization)
4. **1970:** Agribusiness (energy-intensive systemized agriculture)
