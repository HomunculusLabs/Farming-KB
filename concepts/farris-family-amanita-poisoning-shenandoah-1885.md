## Farris Family Amanita Poisoning, Shenandoah, 1885

## Overview

The Farris family poisoning of 1885 was a significant incident of fatal mushroom
poisoning in Shenandoah, Pennsylvania, involving five victims who consumed toxic
*[[amanita]]* specimens gathered in the wild. The case is historically notable for
Dr. Shadle's pioneering use of **[[atropine]] sulphate** as an antidote, which resulted
in the recovery of three of the five patients (60% survival). The case was reported
by Charles McIlvaine in his 1885 article "Amanitine and Its Antidote," published in
the *Philadelphia Medical and Surgical Reporter*, and later discussed by Julius
Auboineau Palmer in *About Mushrooms* (1894).

## Circumstances of Poisoning

On **Sunday, August 30, 1885**, members of the Farris family and a neighbor (Mrs. B.)
gathered what were described as "beautiful toadstools" found growing under chestnut
trees in wooded areas near Shenandoah, Pennsylvania. The mushrooms were almost
certainly a deadly *Amanita* species containing amatoxins, most likely *Amanita
phalloides* or a closely related species.

Mrs. Farris prepared approximately **three pints** of the mushrooms, stewing them
in milk with butter, pepper, and salt. The family consumed the dish heartily as
their Sunday supper. Mrs. B., the neighbor who had helped collect the mushrooms,
also partook.

## Clinical Course

### Onset of Symptoms (5–8 Hours Post-Ingestion)

Symptoms did not manifest immediately, consistent with the well-known delayed onset
characteristic of amatoxin poisoning. The first signs appeared towards daybreak on
Monday morning, approximately 5 to 8 hours after the meal: foul breath, coated
tongue, stomach pain, nausea, and epigastric sickness.

### Progression (12 Hours Post-Ingestion)

When Dr. Shadle made his first visit, approximately 12 hours after ingestion, four
patients were experiencing intense, uncontrollable vomiting, while Mrs. R. (likely
Mrs. B., the neighbor) suffered violent retching. Gastrointestinal distress was
severe and unremitting.

### Advanced Phase (30+ Hours After Onset)

By approximately 30 hours after symptom onset, the clinical picture had worsened
dramatically: persistent gastro-intestinal irritation, relaxed bowels (diarrhea),
and insufferable tenesmus (painful straining).

### Critical Phase and Fatalities

The poisoning progressed to extreme systemic toxicity. Patients exhibited extreme
prostration, stupor, cold extremities, and complete indifference to surroundings.
Thomas R. advanced to full coma; Mrs. Farris progressed to coma-vigil (unresponsive
with open eyes), approximately 12 hours before death. Physical findings included
shrunken face and sunken eyes (severe dehydration), dusky skin, dry cold body,
pulse elevated to 120–140 bpm (soft, compressible, intermittent), temperature up
to 104°F, mild delirium, and peculiar sensations of blindness.

**Thomas R. died 56 hours** after ingestion. **Mrs. Farris died 63 hours** after
ingestion. Both deaths were attributed to progressive asthenia — profound weakness
and circulatory collapse caused by the amatoxin's destruction of hepatic tissue.

## Treatment

### Initial Measures

Dr. Shadle employed a range of supportive treatments appropriate to late-19th-century
medical practice: an emetic for Mrs. R., mild purges for the others, cracked ice
for burning sensations, bismuth/creosote/acacia/peppermint preparations for GI
irritation, morphine sulphate hypodermically for abdominal pain, alcohol and
digitalis for exhaustion and failing heart, and hot bricks/bottles for cold
extremities.

### Atropine Therapy — The Antidote

Dr. Shadle's critical insight was his conclusion that the toxin acted as a **narcotic
poison affecting the nerve centres**, particularly those governing respiration and
cardiac function. On this basis, he initiated treatment with **sub-cutaneous
injections of sulphate of atropia (atropine)** in doses ranging from **1/60 to 1/30
grain**.

The response to atropine was consistently positive: stronger cardiac action after
each injection, previously absent pulse returning at the wrist, deeper and more
regular respiration, and temporary but marked improvement in consciousness.

### Outcome

Of the five patients poisoned, **two died** (Thomas R. at 56 hours; Mrs. Farris
at 63 hours post-ingestion) and **three recovered** (60% survival rate). The three
survivors owed their recovery to the atropine injections, supported by other
symptomatic measures. The two patients who died likely sustained irreversible organ
damage before atropine therapy could take full effect.

## Dr. Shadle's Clinical Lessons

Based on his experience, Dr. Shadle drew three key conclusions:

1. **Slow onset of poisoning:** The toxin does not produce immediate symptoms,
   creating a dangerous false sense of security. The delay of 5–8 hours is
   characteristic and clinically significant.

2. **Death by asthenia:** The poison destroys life not through violent acute
   effects but through progressive weakness and circulatory collapse. The patient
   is gradually overwhelmed as vital functions fail.

3. **Atropine as the antidote:** Atropine sulphate is the specific antidote and
   should be **"pushed heroically"** — administered aggressively and in sufficient
   doses to counteract the narcotic effects on the cardiac and respiratory centres.

## Historical Significance

The Farris family case is one of the earliest well-documented instances of atropine
being used successfully as a treatment for *Amanita* poisoning in the United States.
Dr. Shadle's report, as relayed by McIlvaine and Palmer, contributed to the evolving
understanding of the delayed onset of amatoxin poisoning, the mechanism of death in
fatal *Amanita* poisoning (asthenia and organ failure), and the potential role of
atropine in supporting cardiovascular and respiratory function during the critical
phase.

Modern toxicology has since identified specific antidotes for amatoxin poisoning
(including intravenous silymarin, penicillin G, and liver transplantation), and
atropine is no longer considered a primary specific antidote. However, in the context
of late-19th-century medicine, Dr. Shadle's aggressive use of atropine represented
a reasoned and innovative approach based on his understanding of the toxin's action
on the central nervous system.
