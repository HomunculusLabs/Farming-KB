# Permanent Agriculture: Feudal, Baronial, and Communal Permanence

## The Search for Permanence in Land Use

He categorizes these pathways into three distinct models — feudal, baronial, and communal permanence — each with fundamentally different relationships between people, land, and energy systems. Understanding these models is essential for grasping why permaculture advocates for a particular kind of permanence and rejects the others.

## Feudal Permanence

The first model Mollison describes is the **peasant approach**, well documented by F.H. King for old China. In this system, people hauled nutrients from canals, cesspits, pathways, and forests to maintain an annual grain culture. Mollison characterizes this as "feudal permanence" because of its methods, its historical period, and its politics.

The defining features of feudal permanence include:

- **Intensive human labor**: People were bound to the landscape by unrelenting toil, working constantly to move nutrients from where they accumulated to where crops needed them
- **Service to external power**: Labor was performed in service to a state or landlord, not for the benefit of the workers themselves
- **Annual cropping**: The system relied on annual grains rather than perennial or tree-based systems, requiring constant replanting and soil disturbance
- **External energy dependency**: While the nutrients were local, the social organization was not self-sustaining — it depended on hierarchical power structures

Mollison's verdict on this model is stark: it leads eventually to famine and revolution. The unrelenting labor combined with subservience to external power creates inherently unstable conditions. When drought, political disruption, or ecological exhaustion occurs, the system has no resilience — disaster follows directly.

## Baronial Permanence

The second model operates on **permanent pasture** — the prairie, pampas, and modern Western farms characterized by large holdings, few people, and vast grazing leases typically for a single species of animal. Mollison calls this "baronial permanence" for its near-regal properties of immense extent.

This model's characteristics include:

- **Lowest possible level of land use**: Pasture or monoculture cropland represents the least productive use of land that can be devised, in terms of biomass generated per hectare
- **Mechanization and scale**: Once mechanized, these systems destroy whole landscapes and soil complexes
- **Single-species dependency**: Grazing a single animal species over large areas eliminates biodiversity and creates fragile monocultures
- **External energy dependence**: Modern versions require massive inputs of fossil fuels for machinery, fertilizers, and transport

Mollison typifies such systems, once mechanized, as **agricultural deserts** — landscapes that have been stripped of their ecological complexity and reduced to the bare minimum of production for a single purpose. The baronial model appears permanent only because its scale masks the rate of degradation; in reality, it is steadily consuming the soil and biodiversity capital that makes it possible.

## Communal Permanence

The third model — and the one Mollison advocates — is **communal permanence**, exemplified by forests. Forests represent permanent agriculture, but they require generations of care and knowledge, and hence the tribal or communal reverence found only in stable communities.

The essence of communal permanence is captured in Mollison's evocative image: the ability to plant a pecan or citrus tree when old, knowing it will not be cut down by one's children's children. This requires:

- **Intergenerational continuity**: Communities must be stable enough that long-term investments in tree crops and soil building make sense
- **Shared stewardship**: Land held in common rather than as commodity, managed for the benefit of all inhabitants
- **Self-sufficiency in energy**: The characteristic that typifies all permanent agricultures is that the needs of the system for energy are provided by that system itself
- **High productivity through diversity**: Selected forests yield more total biomass than annual crops while providing diverse nutrient and fuel resources

## The Energy Distinction

Mollison draws a sharp line between these models based on their energy relationships. Modern crop agriculture is **totally dependent on external energies** — hence the oil problem and its associated pollution. In contrast, truly permanent systems generate their own energy. Figure 1.1 in Mollison's text illustrates this dramatically: conservation farming and permaculture can reach a 1:120 energy gain ratio, while conventional farming starts at 10:1 against (a net energy loss).

The transition from productive permanent systems — where land is held in common — to annual, commercial agricultures where land is regarded as a commodity involves three interconnected shifts:

1. **Low to high energy society**: Moving from self-sustaining to externally powered systems
2. **Stewardship to exploitation**: Land use shifts from caretaking to extraction
3. **Local to global resource dependency**: Energy and nutrients must be imported from increasingly distant sources, often from the third world

## The Transition Model

Mollison proposes an 8-year transition from contemporary agriculture to permaculture, with intermediate stages:

- **Years 1–3** (Transitional/Conservation Farming): Replacing animal forage grains with tree crops, increasing forest cover, adopting low-to-no tillage on remaining croplands, retrofitting houses for energy conservation
- **Years 4–7**: 70% of cropland devoted to forage farming systems that integrate trees, animals, and crops
- **Year 8+**: Full permaculture with the majority of land in perennial, self-sustaining systems

The accounting across this transition covers multiple dimensions:

- **Economic**: Farm income is achieved by reducing production costs rather than increasing gross output; dollar costs don't balance until organic farming is achieved
- **Energy**: Oil dependence drops from a 10:1 loss ratio to a 1:120 gain through firewood and fuels produced on-farm
- **Environmental**: Soil loss is reversed (soils are created rather than destroyed), water is conserved, and pollutants are removed
- **Social**: Employment increases as human design and skills replace machine systems; food quality improves; life quality emerges as "right livelihood"

## The Risk of Departure

Mollison warns that the further society departs from communal permanence, the greater the risk of tyranny, feudalism, and revolution — and the more work for less yield. Any error or disturbance can bring disaster: a drought year in a desert grain crop or a distant political decision on tariffs. The real risk, he argues, is that the needs of people working "on the ground" — the inhabitants — are overthrown by the needs or greeds of commerce and centralized power.

This has been the historical fate of peasant Europe, Ireland, and much of the third world: the forest is cut for warships or newspaper, and people are reduced to serfs in a barren landscape. The lesson is clear — permanence without communal stewardship and self-sufficient energy is not true permanence at all, but merely the appearance of stability masking inevitable collapse.
