# Compacted Soil and Tree Health

## Overview

Compacted soil is one of the most significant threats to tree, shrub, and perennial health
in managed landscapes. Woody perennials have deep, extensive root systems and long lifespans
that depend on stable, well-structured soil. When soil becomes compacted—whether from foot
traffic, heavy equipment, construction, or lawn maintenance—the consequences cascade through
the entire [[soil-food-web]], ultimately starving and killing the plants above.

## What Trees, Shrubs, and Perennials Need

### Fungal Dominance (Rule #3)

According to Rule #3, trees, shrubs, and perennials prefer **fungally dominated soils**.
This is fundamentally different from lawn grasses, which thrive in bacterially dominated
soils. Successional plants (weeds, annuals, grasses) evolved with bacterial soils, while
late-succession woody plants evolved with fungal communities.

This creates a core conflict in suburban landscapes: **trees surrounded by lawns are forced
into soil conditions that work against them**. Lawns are maintained with nitrate-heavy
fertilizers (which feed bacteria), while trees require ammonium (which feeds fungi). The
lawn management regime actively suppresses the fungal communities that trees depend on.

### Mycorrhizal Relationships

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] form the foundation of tree nutrition. Two major types exist:

- **Ectomycorrhizae (Ecto):** Form a sheath around root tips, common on temperate forest
  trees (oak, pine, birch, spruce). They extend hyphal networks far into soil to mine
  minerals and deliver water.
- **Endomycorrhizae (Endo):** Penetrate root cells directly, associated with most
  deciduous hardwoods, shrubs, and perennials.

Rules #16 and #17 establish that mycorrhizal relationships are essential for woody plant
health and that disrupting them (through compaction, chemicals, or tillage) directly harms
the plant.

**Critical timing constraint:** Mycorrhizal spores must contact live roots within
approximately **24 hours of moisture exposure** to germinate and colonize successfully.
This narrow window makes establishing mycorrhizae in disturbed soils particularly
challenging.

### Colonizing Existing Trees

Establishing mycorrhizae on mature trees is harder than on young plantings. Observing
**mushroom associations** near trees provides clues about existing fungal partnerships.
A classic example: **birch trees frequently associate with *[[amanita-muscaria]]*** (fly
agaric), signaling an active ectomycorrhizal relationship.

## How Compaction Damages the Soil Food Web

### Physical Destruction of Soil Structure

Healthy soil is a living architecture—aggregates bound by fungal hyphae, bacterial glues
(glomalin), and organic matter create pore spaces for air and water. Compaction crushes
this structure: large pores collapse, water infiltration drops, root channels compress,
and the physical habitat for soil organisms is destroyed.

### Killing Larger Soil Organisms

Compaction disproportionately harms larger organisms:

1. **Mycorrhizal fungi:** Hyphal networks are physically crushed and severed, destroying
   mycelial mats that may extend far beyond the drip line and connect multiple trees.
2. **Protozoa and nematodes:** Need pore spaces large enough to move through soil in
   pursuit of bacterial prey. Compaction traps them in confined spaces.
3. **Earthworms and arthropods:** Cannot move through compacted soil; they migrate away
   or die in place.
4. **Beneficial bacteria:** Even bacteria suffer as air-filled pores shrink.

### Anaerobic Conditions and Root Death

When compaction eliminates air spaces, soil shifts from **aerobic to anaerobic**—perhaps
the most insidious consequence. Aerobic bacteria decline; anaerobic bacteria proliferate.
Anaerobic metabolism produces toxic byproducts: **alcohols, aldehydes, organic acids,
hydrogen sulfide, and methane**. These are directly **phytotoxic**—they kill fine root
tips and root hairs, the structures responsible for water and nutrient uptake. The root
system retreats, reducing capacity to support the canopy.

Visible symptoms appear months or years later: chlorosis, canopy dieback, pest and disease
susceptibility, and eventual decline.

## Remediation Strategies

### Step 1: Mechanical Aeration (First Step Only)

**Plug aeration** relieves surface compaction by removing soil cylinders, creating channels
for air and water. However, aeration alone is **only a first step**. Without reintroducing
soil food web organisms, soil will re-compact. The pores serve as **entry points for
biological amendments**.

### Step 2: Fungal Compost

Apply **1–2 inches of fungal compost** under trees and shrubs, extending to the **drip
line**. The compost should be fungally dominated—produced with wood chips, leaves, and
longer composting times. It introduces diverse bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes.
Apply as top-dressing only; do not mix in to avoid damaging existing roots.

### Step 3: Brown Mulches

**Nature places mulch over tree roots**—fallen leaves, twigs, and decomposing wood create
the ideal fungal environment. Use **brown (carbon-rich) mulches**: wood chips, shredded
bark, fallen leaves. Apply 2–4 inches deep, pulled back from the trunk (avoid volcano
mulching). Brown mulches selectively feed fungi, supporting the dominance woody plants
require.

### Compost Tea Applications

**Compost tea** delivers living microorganisms directly into the soil and onto root
surfaces. Apply at **beginning of growing season** (spring) to jumpstart biological
activity and at **end of growing season** (fall) to support root growth before dormancy.
Use **fungally dominated compost tea** for trees—brew with fungal compost and extend
aeration time. Apply as soil drench or foliar spray; additional applications during
stress (drought, construction damage) aid recovery.

## Integrating All Three Tools

The three soil food web tools—**brown mulches, fungal compost, and compost tea**—work
synergistically:

| Tool | Primary Function | Application |
|------|-----------------|-------------|
| Fungal compost | Introduce diverse biology, improve chemistry | 1–2" top-dress to drip line |
| Brown mulches | Feed fungi long-term, protect surface | 2–4" layer, away from trunk |
| Compost tea | Rapidly inoculate soil and plant surfaces | Spring and fall soil drench |

## Practical Considerations

- **Trees in lawns:** Create mulched tree rings to the drip line; stop nitrate lawn
  fertilizers in the root zone; transition away from synthetic chemicals.
- **Patience:** Visible canopy improvement may take 1–3 seasons; full soil food web
  recovery takes 3–5 years. Monitor for mushroom emergence as a positive sign.
- **Prevention:** Fence tree root zones during construction. If damage occurs, begin
  remediation immediately rather than waiting for decline symptoms.

## Key Rules Referenced

- **Rule #3:** Trees, shrubs, and perennials prefer fungally dominated soils
- **Rule #16:** Mycorrhizal relationships are essential for woody plant health
- **Rule #17:** Disrupting mycorrhizal relationships directly harms the plant
