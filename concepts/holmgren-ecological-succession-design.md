# Ecological Succession in Permaculture Design

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: David Holmgren, Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability (Principle 12: Creatively Use and Respond to Change)

## Classic Succession Model

The classic model of ecological succession was based on observation of patterns of change in moist, fertile and temperate North American and European landscapes. Following disturbance, the colonisation of bare ground by herbaceous weeds leads to grassland and then pioneer shrubs and brambles, followed by fast-growing forest trees; finally, slow-growing, long-lived trees form a stable climax ecology.

In this model there is generally change from:
- low biomass to high biomass
- low soil humus to high soil humus
- low resilience to disturbance to high resilience
- low diversity to high diversity
- predominance of competitive relationships to predominance of co-operative and symbiotic relationships

This model has been useful in understanding agriculture and other human land uses:
- Most crop agriculture is analogous to the herbaceous weed phase
- Pastures grazed by animals are a managed version of the grassland
- Many marginal and abandoned patches of land are covered by vigorous pioneer shrubs and fast-growing forest trees
- Traditional pre-industrial European royal forests of valuable long-lived timber trees were close to a climax state

## Initial Floristic Composition

This alternative model suggests that disturbance leads to a simultaneous regeneration of all species; over time, the short-lived species die out, leaving the long-lived ones. Diversity is greatest following regeneration and lowest at maturity. This model describes most Australian ecosystems. For example, in a dry sclerophyll eucalypt forest, a hot fire produces a thick regeneration of seedling eucalypts and wattles, along with many flowering understorey species; after 30 years the wattles may be gone and the understorey diversity reduced to the most shade-tolerant species.

## Permaculture Use of Plant and Animal Succession

Both models influenced the ideas proposed in Permaculture One, where fast-growing pioneer plants provide some yields and improve the environment for more long-lived and valuable plants. More broadly, if we could provide more of our needs from later successional stages dominated by perennial plants, especially trees, then our cultivated ecologies would be more ecologically balanced and resilient.

Some of the ways succession has been used at Melliodora include:
- prevention of burning to allow accumulation of organic matter and nitrogen fertility
- rabbit-proof and stock-proof fencing to exclude animals in the early stages, allowing a wider range of more palatable pasture species
- slashing grass and weeds to mulch tree plantings
- mixed shelter plantings of fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing acacias, tagasaste and casuarinas with slower-growing, longer-lived, shade-tolerant shrubs and trees
- interplanting of widely spaced walnut trees with hazelnut hedgerows to provide nut yields in the decades before the walnuts fully occupy the site
- planting of dense canopied deciduous and evergreen trees along the gully to shade out blackberry
- use of goats to eat blackberry, coarse pasture grasses and weeds, to encourage richer pasture species suitable for grazing geese

## Common Problems from Ignoring Succession

- Most people tend to design gardens and are disappointed that there isn't enough space for everything and that some things die out or grow poorly as the system changes
- Planting trees at wide spacings in shelterbelts, where they struggle with competing grass and damaging wind
- Giving up growing some species because frost, wind and sun damage the plant, when a more mature system with more shade and shelter would allow these delicate species to thrive
- Assuming that large numbers of new weeds herald an invasive takeover, when disturbance has allowed growth of weeds like thistle which decline dramatically as perennial grass species become established

## The Role of Animals in Succession

The role of animals in preventing or steering succession in a particular direction is often ignored or misunderstood. In the case of thistles, close grazing, especially by sheep or rabbits in autumn, is a recipe for maintaining the thistles. Even botanists sometimes fail to recognise the signs that grazing by wild animals is often the dominant factor determining what regenerates and persists in natural systems.

## Plants Come Before Animals

The basic fact of ecological succession is that plants provide the resources to support animals. This reminds us that we should establish pastures and fodder crops before we get animals. A small rural property with too many livestock, fed out of purchased bags and bales, accumulating manure that cannot be absorbed by the overgrazed pasture, is an example of the contradiction of succession order. The commercial feedlot illustrates this contradiction on a grand scale: it is financially viable only because of cheap energy, externalised environmental costs, and agricultural subsidies.

## Collingwood Children's Farm Example

When Holmgren worked with Collingwood Children's Farm in Melbourne, they proposed fodder trees and shrubs that could be lopped by children to feed the goats, as an alternative to the then current practice of providing stale bread. The bread was bad for the goats' digestion and gave children the message that goats eat "sponge white" out of a plastic bag from the shop. The tagasaste and willow tree fodder has been a great success with the staff, the children and the goats.

## Four-Phase Model of Ecosystem Change

A more elaborate four-phase model is also instructive:
- Conservation: the long-lived, steady-state climax, with high interconnection between elements and large stored biological capital
- Release: the pulse of disturbance, typically very short in duration
- Reorganisation: the most unstable phase, with open niches, soluble nutrients and energy available. Potential to flip to more or less productive systems
- Exploitation: fast-growing pioneer species colonise opportunities, catch and store energy quickly, and cement patterns for gradual build-up toward a new conservation phase

This model focuses attention on the conditions within the climax that make the system vulnerable to disturbance, and on the complex and uncertain dynamics that follow the release phase. The reorganisation phase incorporates:
- the high risk of ecological drift to lower productivity (generally due to loss of mineral nutrients)
- the lower risk of major degradation (often due to coincidence of severe weather events)
- the potential for ecological invasion and possible shift to more productive systems better able to use available resources

## Sheet Mulching and Successional Alternatives

It is annoying that Permaculture has become equated with the sheet-mulch gardening technique as if it were the pinnacle of sustainable land use. For Holmgren it has always been an easy way to convert lawn and pasture into productive food gardens without the hard work of digging and weeding. In a lower-energy future such materials will not be available cheaply. Used as a maintenance technique, sheet-mulch creates dependence on a constant supply of mulch.

Successional alternatives to continuous sheet-mulch include:
- reversion to a grass and weed fallow, and moving the garden to a fresh location
- gradual dominance by fruit trees and other shade-tolerant perennials, with natural litter fall and prunings providing adequate mulch
- more intensive methods of soil management, including application of high-quality compost, carefully selected rock minerals, and modest use of mulching, combined with light surface cultivation and deep aeration forking

At Melliodora a combination of the second and third strategies has been pursued, but the first has also been experienced where creeping bent grass has prevailed.
