# Wildfire Management in Permaculture

## Fire as a Sector

[[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]], wildfire is treated as one of the "wild energies" arriving from outside the system — alongside sun, wind, rain, and water flow. [[mollison-designers-sector-analysis-wind-sun-fire]] maps the compass directions from which fire threat originates, and zone-sector overlay determines where to place protective elements. The fundamental choice for every sector is to block, channel, or open it to advantage. For fire, the strategy is always to block and deflect.

Fire provides a specific problem for designers progressing from grassland to forest operations. From 3-5 years until [[food-forest-establishment]], the system carries high fire risk, requiring programmed planting mosaics to reduce district-wide danger. Fire recurs on a predictable schedule in specific vegetation types: approximately 30 years in wet sclerophyll forest, 8-10 years in dry savannah, and annually in unbrowsed grassland.

## Firestorm Conditions

The critical factors for firestorm development are:

**Fuel supply**: Dryness, distribution, and quantity of available fuels. Loose fuels of more than 6 cm diameter are particularly dangerous. Pine woods erupt at less than 30% humidity due to high resin content.

**Oxygen supply**: Winds that fan the flames, especially hot winds. Fire spread increases as the square of wind velocity — at 20 km/h the spread might be 2 km²/h, at 30 km/h it becomes 4 km²/h. At 80 km/h, ground fires may self-extinguish.

**Preheating**: Upslope or radiant heat in front of flames, or advected desert winds in unprotected forests. For every 10° increase in upslope angle, fire speed and intensity double (16 km/h at 0° → 32 km/h at 10° → 64 km/h at 20° → 128 km/h at 30°).

**Unstable air masses**: Wind shear, dust devils, scattered cumulus clouds, and shifting winds all presage fire danger when dry fuels drop below 35% moisture. In unstable air, smoke ascends rather than leveling out. Volatile oils, terpenes, and resins may be smelled, and a light-blue haze develops over resinous forests.

Fire tornadoes can develop on the lee side of hills with ascent velocities up to 250 km/h, lifting large logs and building debris. Ground winds at the column base (tens of metres across) reach 100 km/h. Incandescent material is carried 1-30 km downwind to start fresh fire sequences.

## Firebreak Design

Firebreaks decrease fire intensity. They include roads, ponds, marshes, rivers, stony areas, and summer-green or sappy crop hedgerows. Two types are distinguished:

**Horizontal firebreak**: Weakens or reduces the fire front energy. Roads, swales, and water bodies create gaps in fuel continuity.

**Vertical firebreak**: Prevents fire "crowning" in trees by removing lower branches, dead material, and planting sappy ground cover under the forest canopy.

No firebreak — even 10 km of water — is effective in full firestorm conditions. The strategy must therefore focus on prevention and structural survival rather than attempting to stop an established firestorm.

## Vegetation Management for Fire Resistance

The choice of tree species is fundamental to fire management. Pines accumulate fuel and "burn like a tar barrel" — they belong in Zone 4, away from the fire danger sector and towards the cold wind sector where they serve as hardy windbreaks. Eucalypts are similarly dangerous; funnel-shaped plantings of eucalypts or pampas grass along driveways create blowtorch effects.

**Fire-resistant tree species**: Deciduous fruits, figs, willows, poplars, Coprosma. These are summer-green with low leaf oil content and sappy tissues that resist ignition.

**Sacrificial hedges**: Downslope lines of willows and soft-leaved Acacia act as fire barriers. They are fire-killed but rejuvenate from roots when cut down. Willow leaves do not combust but shrivel and emit steamy ash, dampening ground fires.

**Ground covers**: Lilies (Agapanthus, spring bulbs, arum, Canna), succulent covers (comfrey, iceplants, Tradescantia, Impatiens), and shortgrass sward reduce flame and radiation effects.

**Avoid near structures**: Olives, pines, eucalypts, and any resinous or oily-leaved species.

## House and Building Design

In fire-prone areas, structures require specific fire-resistant features:

- **Simple roof and wall outline**: No internal roof valleys or re-entrant corners that accumulate incandescent ash
- **Gutter management**: Leaf-free profiles, or pluggable gutters that can be water-filled during fire events (plugs chained near downpipes)
- **Screened openings**: Fine-mesh metal screens on all windows, underfloor vents, and wall cavities to reduce spark entry
- **Clear perimeter**: No wood piles, shrubs, or inflammable mats against walls; fuel and chemicals stored in separate sheds
- **White-painted walls**: Reflect radiant heat
- **Underfloor protection**: No open underfloor spaces; excavated or slab construction preferred

## Site Selection

**Dangerous sites**: Hilltops, sharp hilltops, hill saddles with diverging ridgelines (wind funnel effect), and any location with upslope forest of resinous species.

**Safe sites**: Damp valley mouths, well-tended built-up areas, farms with flood-flow or Keyline irrigation, irrigated areas, peninsulas in dams and lakes, and downslope plateaus with rigorously applied maintenance criteria.

**The nested house**: Excavate into a hill shelf rather than building on stilts. This provides protection from radiation, eliminates open underfloor areas, and allows rimwalls, ponds, or earthbanks on the plateau edge. A dugout refuge behind the house — fully earthed over with wet blanket door and curved entrance — provides absolute fire insurance with a 270-liter permanent water tank, old blankets, and buckets.

## Radiation Shields

Most human fire casualties are not initially burned but smothered by smoke or killed by radiant heat, which kills plants and animals at a distance from the flames. Radiation shields are solid or reflective objects that absorb or deflect radiant heat from the fire front. Stone walls, earth banks, water bodies, and metal-roofed structures all serve as radiation shields. The 30-meter fuel reduction zone around structures acts as a combined fuel break and radiation buffer.

Keyline-designed earthworks — swales, berms, and dams — function as both water harvesting systems and fire defense infrastructure when positioned on the fire-facing sector of a property. This dual-function principle is central to permaculture: every element serves multiple purposes. Fire dams that also irrigate pasture, windbreaks that also resist fire, and deciduous orchards that also provide food and shade exemplify this approach.

## The 30-Meter Fuel Zone

Around every house and building, reduce forest and grass fuel to 30 meters (100 feet). This does not mean tree removal — it means replacing fire-prone species with fire-resistant plantings of deciduous fruits, figs, willows, poplars, lily clumps, and summer-green ground covers. Combined with structural fire-resistant design and adequate water storage, this zone is the single most effective fire protection measure available to landowners.

The 30-meter zone creates an area where radiant heat is insufficient to ignite structures, where firefighters can safely operate, and where spot fires from airborne embers can be rapidly suppressed. In practice, this zone is maintained through regular pruning, grazing, and species management rather than one-time clearing.

## Fuel Management Strategies

Beyond species selection, active fuel management is essential. Mechanical or grazing removal of fuels just before fire-danger periods includes dead brush, long dry grasses, and the dead lower branches of trees. Livestock can be rotated through forest margins to consume grasses and low vegetation. In non-inhabited areas, both "cool" fires (damp weather) and "hot" fires (dry periods) are sometimes lit as management mosaics to preserve fire-dependent flora and fauna. However, this is unsafe and difficult to control, and often causes the very fires it aims to prevent.

Grass fires typically spread after 10-11 a.m., once dew has dried. Forest fires spread from midday to 3 p.m. After an initial flareup, approximately one hour suffices to develop firestorm conditions. Wind direction shifts late in the day may blow fire back on itself and make it safer, but backing winds are unpredictable. The safest strategy is always early evacuation of a broad area, except for teams in safe refuges whose job is to extinguish minor fires in the first half-hour after the main front has passed.

For this reason, forested suburbs need local refuges — gravelled areas with underground shelters — as do isolated homesteads and long stretches of road through inflammable forests. Every refuge needs a permanent water supply, fire blankets, and protected entrance.

## Water Systems for Fire Defense

Dams designed specifically to flood-flow over hillsides subject to fire serve dual purposes: landscape hydration and emergency fire suppression. Keyline irrigation systems, when properly designed, can be activated to wet downslope forests and paddocks before fire arrives. Even modest irrigation capacity can damp wildfires when directed at the right time and place.

A complete wildfire control system includes permanent water storage at high points (gravity-fed distribution), pumps with reliable power sources (diesel or manual backup), and hydrants or hose connections positioned to cover all structures. The 270-liter tank incorporated into each fire refuge is a minimum; larger properties should plan for thousands of liters of dedicated fire-fighting water storage.

## Long-Term Design Strategy

Wildfire will always occur on arid borders; the permaculture approach is to first live with fire, and secondarily (over years) design to exclude it from settled areas. The progression involves:

1. **Immediate**: Create the 30-meter fuel reduction zone around all structures
2. **Short-term**: Replace fire-prone species with deciduous and succulent plantings
3. **Medium-term**: Establish sacrificial hedges and water-based firebreaks
4. **Long-term**: Convert grassland to forest with fire-resistant canopy species, programming planting mosaics to reduce district-wide risk during the vulnerable 3-5 year establishment period

The transition from grassland to forest is the period of highest fire risk. Designers must plan planting sequences that minimize continuous fuel loads during this phase, using interspersed firebreaks, grazing animals, and diverse plantings to prevent the accumulation of uniform fuel that drives catastrophic wildfire.

## Mollison's Personal Experience

During the 1967 Hobart firestorm, Mollison's family survived in a dense eucalypt forest through rigorous preparation: annual removal of all lower limbs, loose bark, twigs, fallen leaves, dry brush, and dead stumps. Material was chopped into swales or burned as cooking fuel. Tall tree stems survived and regenerated. A downslope willow hedge was sacrificially fire-killed but dampened the ground fire. The house was blistered but intact. In the same street, 70 houses burned to the ground; 1,100 houses burned across the area; 90 people were killed.

This account demonstrates that survival is possible even in extreme firestorm conditions, provided that fuel reduction, structural design, water storage, and personal preparation are all rigorously maintained. The permaculture approach to wildfire is not about eliminating fire from the landscape — an impossible goal in fire-prone regions — but about designing systems and structures that can coexist with periodic fire events while protecting human life and property.
