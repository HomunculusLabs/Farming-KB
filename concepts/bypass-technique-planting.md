# Bypass Technique for Balcony Gardens

## What It Is

The bypass technique is a vertical permaculture method where climbing plants grow from one balcony to the next, creating a communal garden that spans an entire block of flats.
Each storey can be overseen and harvested by the people living there.
The concept transforms a conventional apartment building into a vertical growing system, exploiting the vertical dimension that urban environments offer in abundance.
The technique represents one of the most creative applications of permaculture zone and sector analysis to the dense urban environment, turning a passive building facade into a productive agricultural surface.

The bypass technique demonstrates Holzer's broader design philosophy: that productive food systems can be integrated into any environment, no matter how constrained, if the designer thinks laterally and works creatively with the available elements.
Where conventional urban agriculture focuses on ground-level gardens or rooftop plots, the bypass technique exploits the vertical surfaces of buildings — a resource that exists in vast quantities in every city but is almost never used for food production.

## How It Works

1. Neighbours interested in permaculture create a terrace system reaching from storey to storey
2. Climbers like grapes and kiwi plants grow up the front of the building, using each balcony as a climbing aid
3. On the balconies, troughs with soil are prepared so the plants can put down new roots
4. Lead the plant into the trough, heap soil over it, and possibly weigh it down with a stone
5. The plants draw fresh strength and nutrients from each trough's soil and grow from one storey to the next
6. Each resident oversees and harvests from their level

The system is both vertical and horizontal — it can travel in any direction.
Creative thinking is essential, as there are many possible ways to design and plant such a system.
The technique is not limited to upward growth; plants can be trained laterally along a row of balconies, or even guided around corners of buildings.
The key insight is that each balcony becomes a node in a continuous growing system, with the plant rooting repeatedly at each level and thereby accessing fresh water and nutrients at regular intervals.
This repeated rooting also provides redundancy — if one trough dries out or is neglected, the plant's other root zones keep it alive.

The layering technique used to establish new root zones is simple: a growing shoot is placed in a trough of soil, covered, and held down with a stone or wire peg.
Within a few weeks, roots develop at the buried node, and the shoot can continue growing upward from the new root zone.
This process can be repeated at every balcony level until the entire building facade is covered with a single connected plant.
For kiwi vines, which are particularly vigorous, a single plant could theoretically span 10 or more storeys.

## Suitable Plant Species

The choice of climbing plants is critical for the bypass technique.
Holzer specifically recommends grapes and kiwi plants as primary species.
Both are vigorous climbers that readily produce aerial roots or can be easily layered into soil.
Other suitable species include a range of annual and perennial climbers suited to different climates:

- **Hops (Humulus lupulus)**: Extremely fast-growing perennial climber that dies back in winter, reducing wind loading on the structure. The young shoots are edible and the flowers are used in brewing.
- **Climbing beans (Phaseolus coccineus)**: Runner beans and scarlet runner beans are productive annual climbers that fix nitrogen in the trough soil. They provide both food and ornamental value with their red flowers.
- **Cucumbers and squashes**: Trailing varieties can be trained along balconies, though they require more regular watering than grapes or kiwi.
- **Passionfruit (Passiflora edulis)**: In warmer climates, passionfruit vines are vigorous and productive climbers that attract pollinators.
- **Malabar spinach (Basella alba)**: A heat-tolerant climbing leaf vegetable suitable for tropical and subtropical balconies.
- **Chayote (Sechium edule)**: A vigorous perennial vine that produces edible fruits and tuberous roots, well-suited to mild climates.

For permanent installations, a mix of perennial and annual climbers provides year-round interest.
Perennials like grapes and kiwi provide the permanent structural framework, while annuals like beans and cucumbers can be interplanted for seasonal production.
In colder climates, kiwi varieties such as 'Issai' or 'Ken's Red' offer greater cold hardiness than standard fuzzy kiwi.

## Trough and Container Design

The troughs at each balcony level serve as both rooting points and growing containers.
They should be deep enough (minimum 20-30cm) to support root development and wide enough to allow the plant to be layered in without cramping.
Drainage is essential — each trough needs drainage holes to prevent waterlogging during heavy rain.
The growing medium should be a rich, moisture-retentive mix: a combination of garden soil, compost, and well-rotted manure works well.
Adding perlite or vermiculite improves aeration without significantly increasing weight.

Self-watering containers can reduce maintenance requirements, particularly on upper floors where carrying water is burdensome.
A simple wicking system — where a water reservoir below the soil is connected by capillary action — can keep troughs moist for several days between waterings.
Mulching the surface with straw or leaf mould reduces evaporation and suppresses weeds.
The weight of each container when fully watered should be calculated in advance: a 100cm x 30cm x 25cm trough filled with wet soil can weigh 50-75kg.

## Structural Considerations

Attention must be given to the structural integrity of the building with the additional weight of the planters.
A fully watered trough measuring 100cm x 30cm x 25cm can weigh 50-75kg, so it is important to distribute weight evenly and avoid concentrating heavy planters at a single point.
Professional structural advice should be sought for installations involving large containers on older buildings or where the weight of mature vines could add significant wind loading to railings and facades.
The root systems of vigorous climbers like kiwi can also potentially damage masonry and pointing over time, so physical barriers or regular root pruning may be necessary.

Wind exposure increases with height and must be accounted for.
Upper-floor balconies experience stronger and more turbulent winds than those at ground level.
Windbreaks in the form of trellis panels, mesh screens, or the climbing plants themselves can reduce wind damage.
Securing troughs with brackets or rails is essential to prevent them being dislodged in storms.
Building regulations and lease terms should also be consulted before installing permanent fixtures to balconies.

## Social Benefits

- The system is self-sustaining: if one resident goes on holiday, the plants are rooted in containers on different storeys with their own water and nutrients
- With increasing access to nature, people's empathy for each other grows
- Urban permaculture improves the climate of the town literally and figuratively by promoting interpersonal relationships
- Plants can be a bridge between people

The bypass technique inherently requires cooperation between neighbours, which makes it a powerful community-building tool.
The shared responsibility of maintaining a vertical garden creates regular informal contact between residents who might otherwise never interact.
Food sharing becomes a natural extension — when one resident's balcony produces a glut of grapes or beans, it flows naturally to neighbouring apartments.
Children in the building gain hands-on experience of food growing, and the visible greening of the building facade can improve the entire street's atmosphere.
Research in urban planning has consistently shown that green building facades reduce the urban heat island effect, improve air quality by trapping particulate matter, and increase property values in surrounding areas.

## Broader Vision

If politicians and businesses put the philosophy of sustainability into practice, towns and cities could become green oases.
All courtyards, car parks, playgrounds, open spaces, walls, and roofs could support luxuriant green vegetation, reducing dust contamination and harmful substances.
Permaculture systems demand cooperation — they do not function in isolation from outside influences.

The bypass technique is one element in a larger vision of the productive city.
Combined with [[green-roof-and-living-walls]], rooftop gardens, community orchards, and productive street landscaping, it contributes to a distributed urban food system that reduces transport emissions, improves air quality, enhances biodiversity, and strengthens community resilience.
Urban food production also has documented mental health benefits: access to growing spaces in cities is associated with reduced stress, improved mood, and increased physical activity.
The bypass technique demonstrates that even the most constrained urban environments — apartment building facades — can be integrated into a productive food system with creativity and cooperation.
Case studies from European cities have shown that building-scale vertical gardens can produce significant quantities of food, with a single well-established bypass system potentially yielding tens of kilograms of grapes or beans per growing season across all its storeys.

## Implementation Challenges

Getting started with the bypass technique requires finding a group of willing neighbours, which can be the greatest practical barrier.
Not all building residents will be interested, and disagreements about maintenance responsibilities, plant choices, or aesthetic preferences can arise.
A written agreement covering shared responsibilities, costs, and harvest distribution can prevent conflicts.
Permission from the building owner or management company may also be required, and some buildings may have structural or insurance restrictions that complicate installation.
Despite these challenges, successful bypass installations have been established in apartment buildings across Europe, demonstrating that the concept works in practice as well as in theory.
