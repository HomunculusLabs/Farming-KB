## Critique of Large-Scale Irrigation and Desert Engineering

Fukuoka was sharply critical of the large-scale irrigation and desert engineering projects that 
had been promoted by governments and international development agencies as solutions to 
[[desertification]]. These projects, he argued, treated the symptoms rather than the cause of 
desertification. By bringing in water from external sources, they created temporary oases of 
green that depended on continued human intervention and often collapsed when that intervention 
was withdrawn. More fundamentally, irrigation-based approaches disrupted the natural water 
cycle by extracting groundwater or diverting surface water faster than it could be naturally 
replenished. This led to salinization, waterlogging, and the eventual failure of the irrigation 
system itself—a pattern that had been repeated across the arid regions of the world with 
depressing regularity. Fukuoka also criticized the reliance on exotic, fast-growing tree 
species in afforestation projects. Eucalyptus, mesquite, and other non-native species were 
often planted because of their rapid growth and apparent adaptability, but Fukuoka argued that 
these species frequently created more problems than they solved—depleting groundwater, 
suppressing native vegetation, and creating monocultures vulnerable to [[critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] and environmental management, which assumed 
that natural systems could and should be engineered to serve human purposes. Fukuoka argued 
that this engineering mindset was itself a major cause of environmental degradation—that by 
trying to control and optimize natural processes, human beings had disrupted the 
self-regulating mechanisms that maintain ecosystem health. The revegetation of deserts, in 
Fukuoka's view, was not primarily a technical problem but a philosophical one, requiring a 
fundamental shift in the relationship between human beings and the natural world—from one of 
domination and control to one of humility, observation, and cooperation.

## Practical Observations from Field Work

Fukuoka's [[desert-revegetation]] efforts took him to India, Africa, the Middle East, and other 
arid regions of the world. In India, he worked in the arid landscapes of Rajasthan and the 
Deccan Plateau, where he demonstrated that seed balls containing native grass and legume 
species could establish viable plant cover on severely degraded land. His approach attracted 
attention from local farmers and environmental organizations, who saw in it a low-cost, 
low-technology alternative to expensive irrigation and afforestation projects. In Africa, 
Fukuoka observed that many of the continent's deserts were relatively recent phenomena, caused 
by overgrazing, deforestation, and agricultural expansion rather than by permanent climatic 
change. This observation reinforced his conviction that desertification was reversible: if the 
deserts had been created by human activity, they could be restored by human activity that 
worked with, rather than against, natural processes. Fukuoka's most ambitious proposal was the 
"Green Belt Sahara" project, in which he envisioned a vast band of revegetated land stretching 
across the southern edge of the Sahara Desert, created through the aerial broadcasting of 
millions of seed balls. While this project was never realized at the scale Fukuoka envisioned, 
it inspired similar initiatives and demonstrated the potential for low-cost, nature-based 
approaches to desert restoration.
