# Free-Range Forage Design Principles

Mollison identifies three critical factors in designing free-range forage
systems for domestic animals: social behaviour, year-round nutrition, and
interactions with crops and forest systems. These principles apply across
species but require species-specific implementation.

## Factor One: Social Behaviour

Every species has innate social structures that design must accommodate:

- **Chickens** organise in pecking orders of 12-30 birds. Flocks larger than
  this fragment into subgroups with territorial conflicts. Mollison recommends
  designing poultry systems around family groups of 15-25 hens with one rooster,
  each with its own shelter and forage range.

- **Pigs** are highly social and intelligent. They form matriarchal family
  groups and establish clear territories. Sows kept in family groups farrow
  more successfully than isolated sows. Design must provide wallowing areas,
  rooting zones, and shelter that pigs can modify themselves.

- **Bees** operate as superorganisms with complex communication. Hive
  placement must consider forage range (typically 2-3 km radius), orientation
  (entrances facing morning sun), and spacing (hives too close compete for
  resources; too far apart waste forage).

Social stress increases disease susceptibility and reduces productivity.
Design for the animal's natural social unit, not for human convenience.

## Factor Two: Year-Round Nutrition

The critical challenge in [[greenhouse-cool-climates-mollison]] is bridging the winter feed gap:

- **Spring:** Early forage species (chicory, dandelion, chickweed, clover)
  provide essential nutrition as animals transition off stored feed.
  [[mollison-designers-cold-caves-root-cellar-storage]].
  Mollison stresses that sprouting grain dramatically improves its nutritional
  value for confined poultry over winter.

## Factor Three: Interactions with Crops and Forests

Animals in integrated systems provide services beyond meat and eggs:

- **Poultry** in orchards consume codling moth larvae, curculio, and other
  pests while depositing manure. Their scratching behaviour breaks down fallen
  leaves and fruit, accelerating [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]. However, they also damage
  young tree roots -- exclude them from newly planted areas for the first 2-3
  years.

- **Pigs** in forest systems root for grubs and mast, breaking up compacted
  soil and controlling invasive root suckers. Their wallows create temporary
  wetlands that benefit amphibians. However, uncontrolled rooting destroys
  [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-ground-cover-cultivation]] and can cause erosion -- manage timing and density carefully.

- **Bees** provide pollination services that increase fruit set in orchards
  and seed set in pastures. Mollison values bees primarily as pollinators
  rather than honey producers, though honey is a valuable secondary yield.

- **Geese** in orchards control grass competition around young trees without
  damaging bark (unlike sheep or cattle). See
