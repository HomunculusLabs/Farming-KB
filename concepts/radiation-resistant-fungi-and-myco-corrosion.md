# Radiation-Resistant Fungi and Myco-Corrosion

Certain fungi demonstrate remarkable tolerance to ionizing radiation and
[[bacillus-pumilus-radiation-resistance-surface-decontamination]] has important implications for
[[fungal-biodiversity-in-extreme-environments]].

## Fungal Colonization of Radioactive Environments

Studies of the walls of the 'Shelter' over Reactor No. 4 of the Chernobyl
nuclear power plant demonstrated that microfungi can survive and colonize
highly radioactive concrete. The radiation levels at the site were
extraordinarily high: alpha radiation at 500 Bq/cm2, beta at 20,000 Bq/cm2,
and gamma at 700 mR/h. Despite these extreme conditions, melanized strains
of Alternaria, Cladosporium, and Aureobasidium were able to colonize the
radioactive concrete surfaces.

These findings indicate that certain fungal species possess inherent
mechanisms for tolerating or resisting the damaging effects of ionizing
radiation, particularly when protected by melanin pigmentation in their cell
walls. Melanin is known to absorb a broad spectrum of electromagnetic
radiation and may provide shielding against ionizing radiation, as well as
scavenging free radicals generated by radiation exposure.

## Myco-Corrosion of Concrete and Building Materials

The ability of fungi to colonize and deteriorate mineral-based building
materials extends beyond radioactive environments. Any type of building or
ceramic material, concrete, and cement can be degraded by fungi. Under
certain conditions, deterioration of concrete by fungi may be more efficient
than that caused by bacteria.

Fungal attack on concrete arises through mechanisms similar to those involved
in bioweathering of natural rocks:

- **Acidolysis**: excretion of protons and organic acids that dissolve
  mineral components of concrete.
- **Complexolysis**: secretion of metal-chelating metabolites that mobilize
  cations from the cement matrix.
- **Biomechanical action**: hyphal penetration along crystal planes,
  cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries within the material.

An experimental study of the effect of microfungi on barrier concrete showed
that fungi were able to colonize and corrode the surface of concrete,
selectively avoiding areas filled with granite aggregate. Species including

## Implications for Nuclear Waste Storage

The microbial ability to corrode concrete barrier materials raises serious
concerns regarding both existing and future nuclear waste storage
facilities. Concrete is widely used as an engineered barrier in nuclear waste
containment systems, designed to provide physical isolation and chemical
stability for thousands of years.

Myco-corrosion may reduce the theoretically calculated service life of
concrete barriers, causing a potential risk from the release of radionuclides
into the environment. This concern is amplified by the observation that
radiation-resistant fungi can actually thrive in the presence of radioactive
contamination, potentially creating a self-reinforcing cycle of colonization
and deterioration.

## Metal Transformations in Radioactive Contexts

The ability of fungi to transform toxic [[staycare-fungal-biomethylation-of-metals-and-metalloids]] has potential
relevance for treatment of contaminated land and substrates in radioactive
environments. Fungi with Cr(VI)-reducing activity may be useful for treatment
of chromium-polluted soils, and similar mechanisms could potentially be
applied to radioactive isotopes.

Fungi can also oxidize manganese and iron in metal-bearing minerals such as
siderite (FeCO3) and rhodochrosite (MnCO3), precipitating them as oxides.
