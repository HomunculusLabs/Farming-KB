## Yeast Taxonomy, Distribution, and Biodiversity

## Overview

Yeasts are a polyphyletic group of ascomycetous and basidiomycetous fungi characterized by having a unicellular growth phase and sexual stages that are not enclosed in fruiting bodies. Approximately 100 genera comprising more than 700 species have been described (Kurtzman and Fell 1998), but current evidence suggests these represent less than 1% of the species occurring in nature. Yeasts are ubiquitous, metabolically diverse, and play important roles in ecosystems, industrial processes, and as pathogens.

## Taxonomic Status and Challenges

### The Taxonomic Resolution Problem

One of the major impediments to determining yeast distributions in nature and their ecosystem functions is the poor taxonomic resolution provided by currently used identification methods. Species and genera have traditionally been defined on the basis of phenotypic characters such as cell morphology and responses on various growth tests. However, research with genetic crosses and molecular comparisons has shown that many phenotypic characters considered taxonomically definitive vary among strains of the same species.

Consequences of relying on phenotypic identification:
- Divergent strains of known species may be mistaken for novel taxa
- New species may go unrecognized
- Inaccurate estimates of biodiversity in particular habitats

Molecular genetic methods, particularly DNA nucleotide sequence comparisons, are now recognized as essential for accurate yeast identification. However, more rapid molecular methods are needed for processing large numbers of isolates.

### Evolution of the Yeast Concept

The definition of "yeast" has changed markedly over recent decades:

- *[[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]]* and related ascomycetous species were once considered the "typical" yeast
- Banno (1967) demonstrated a basidiomycetous life cycle for *Rhodotorula*
- Subsequent discoveries of basidiomycetous life cycles in numerous other species (Fell et al. 1969, 1973; Kwon-Chung 1975)

Molecular evolution analyses using ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal DNA (rDNA) have further refined yeast taxonomy:
- Ascomycetous yeasts (including most hemiascomycetes such as *Cephaloascus*, *Ascoidea*, and *Dipodascus*) form a sister group to the euascomycetes
- *Schizosaccharomyces*, *Taphrina*, *Protomyces*, *Pneumocystis*, and *Saitoella* comprise a divergent group phylogenetically basal to both ascomycetous yeasts and euascomycetes
- Basidiomycetous yeasts are a distinct group including *Rhodosporidium*, *Filobasidiella*, and the morphologically more complex *Tremella*

## Distribution in Nature

### General Patterns

Yeasts are commonly distributed throughout the biosphere (Phaff et al. 1966). Their distribution follows [[nutrient-availability]]:

- **Soils**: Occasional populations of 10⁵–10⁶ colony-forming units per gram suggest actively growing cells. Some species are exclusive soil inhabitants: *Debaryomyces (Schwanniomyces) occidentalis*, *[[lipomyces]]* species, *Schizoblastosporion starkeyi-henricii*, and certain *Cryptococcus* species
- **Aquatic environments**: Populations highest in fresh waters, decreasing in marine waters with increased depth and distance from land. Open ocean densities often as low as 10 cells/g, increasing to 10³ cells/g near plankton blooms, current boundaries, surface slicks, thermoclines, or pollutants (Fell 1976)
- **Plant materials**: The majority of yeast species are collected from fallen plant materials and other organic matter. The highest densities are associated with assimilatable sugars and carbon sources

### Plant-Associated Yeasts

Leaf surface tissues and plant exudates sustain large numbers of yeasts:
- di Menna (1959) reported 10⁵–10⁷ viable cells/g of fresh foliage
- Flowers and decaying fruits support a wide spectrum of species at densities up to 10⁶ cells/g of tissue
- Decaying cladodes of some cacti reach densities of 10⁶ cells/g (Phaff and Starmer 1987)

### Insect-Associated Yeasts

Insects represent rich sources of yeasts, especially wood-borers and species of *Drosophila*. Phaff and Starmer (1987) provided comprehensive summaries of yeasts associated with different insect types.

## Ecological Roles

### Metabolic Diversity

Yeasts are primarily saprotrophs that assimilate plant- or animal-derived organic compounds. Their metabolic versatility underpins their ecological importance:

- **Decomposition**: Primary decomposers of plant materials, fruits, and organic matter
- **Industrial applications**: Production of ethanol (beverage, industrial, and fuel), vitamins, organic acids, carotenoids, and enzymes
- **Bioremediation**: Many yeasts catabolize benzene compounds, potentially useful for cleaning industrial chemical spills
- **Pharmaceutical synthesis**: Advances in molecular techniques make yeasts attractive for synthesizing pharmacologically active proteins

### Pathogenic Species

Although usually decomposers, some yeast species are pathogens of plants and animals. Notable pathogens include *[[candida-albicans]]* (human opportunistic pathogen), *[[cryptococcus-neoformans]]* (human pathogen), and various *Malassezia* species (skin-associated commensals and pathogens).

## Sampling and Collection Protocols

### Sample Collection

Most yeasts are obligate aerobes requiring oxygen, so they are usually found in upper layers of soil and sediment (exceptions include yeasts preserved in deep ice layers).

Collection methods by habitat:
- **Terrestrial/shallow water**: Sterile jars, vials, reclosable plastic bags, coring devices
- **Deep-water sediments**: Grabs, gravity and piston cores, submersibles
- **Deep-sea water**: Water column samplers such as the Sterile Bag Sampler

For water samples: 50-ml samples are usually adequate. Minimal water should be included to prevent anaerobic conditions. Temperature and salinity must be recorded simultaneously.

### Sample Handling

- Process immediately if possible, or hold on ice
- Low-temperature maintenance is critical for samples from cold habitats
- Containers should not be airtight (to avoid anaerobic conditions) but should be adequately closed to prevent contamination
- Processing should occur in a contaminant-free environment (culture room or hood)
- Utensils sterilized by flaming with alcohol; workspace kept clean with disinfectant

## Isolation Protocols

### Direct Plating

Soil particles placed directly on agar medium and incubated ≥24 hours. Particles examined microscopically in situ under low power to locate yeast growth at the particle/medium interface. Confirmed colonies transferred to microscope slide, then to growth medium. Streak cultures made for purification; colonies from single cells transferred to slant tubes.

### Water Filtration

Water samples plated directly or passed through 0.45-μm nitrocellulose filters. Filter placed face-up on solid nutrient agar with bacterial inhibitors. Quantity filtered depends on population density (few milliliters to 2–3 liters).

### Soil Dilution

Known quantity of soil mixed with sterile water (sea water for marine samples) in a test tube, followed by a dilution series. Suspensions spread onto isolation media; colonies recorded and enumerated.
