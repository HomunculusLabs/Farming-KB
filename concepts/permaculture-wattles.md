A large genus of ~850 species native to Australia. Family: Mimosaceae. Key [[permaculture-pioneer-species]] with diverse uses.

## General Characteristics

- Seed of some species edible; traditional food of Australian Aboriginal people
- Seed generally good poultry fodder
- Usually good source of pollen but not nectar
- Most species smaller plants; wood used for fuel, craft, fence posts, mine timbers
- Some species are prime furniture timbers
- Many are nitrogen-fixing, fast colonisers of damaged areas

## Selected Species

### Acacia aneura (Mulga)
- Outstanding fodder tree; native to all Australian states except Victoria and Tasmania
- Rainfall 200-500mm; some trees straight-trunked to 9m, shrubs in extreme conditions
- 1.4kg daily ration of mulga leaf supplies sheep with sufficient protein (12%), calcium, vitamin A
- Diet needs supplementing with sulphur and phosphorous
- One of the hardest woods in the world; firewood, fence posts, mine timbers, craft wood
- Companions: Eucalyptus spp, other Acacia spp, Atriplex spp, Maireana spp

### Acacia ampliceps (Salt Wattle)
- Large dense shrub or small tree, 2-8m
- Warm to hot, semi-arid to arid tropical areas; rainfall 250-700mm
- Fast growing; tolerates alkaline, highly saline, waterlogged conditions
- Good fuel wood; heavy cattle grazing

### Acacia baileyana (Cootamundra Wattle)
- Shrub or small tree to 5m and as wide; fine misty blue foliage
- Flowering June-September; frost and drought tolerant; prefers higher rainfall
- Shade/shelter tree; seed is good poultry feed
- Burns readily but usually recovers from fire
- Shortening branches after flowering prolongs tree life

### Acacia bivenosa (Two-veined Acacia)
- Moderately fast, spreading shrub 1.5-2.5m (up to 4m)
- Hot arid zones of NW Australia; altitude 0-500m, rainfall 60-650mm
- Neutral to alkaline, less fertile soils; sand stabilisation potential
- Low palatability to cattle and sheep; coppices well

### Acacia cyclops (Coastal Wattle)
- Dense bushy rounded shrub 2-4m; native SW Australia
- Flowers in summer; rainfall 200-800mm; shallow calcareous soils
- Tolerant of saline and waterlogged soils; wind and salt spray resistant
- Fire retardant properties; soil stabilisation
- Popular firewood in South Africa (dense, high quality)
- Used for sand dune stabilisation (became invasive in South Africa)

### Acacia dealbata (Silver Wattle)
- Fast growing nitrogen fixer, 25-28m
- Rainfall 600-1000mm; altitude 50-1000m; SE Australia
- Regenerates quickly after clearing or fire; suckers profusely
- Wood used for pulp, clothes pegs, wood wool, shoe heels; good glueing properties
- Not good firewood

### Acacia decurrens (Green Wattle)
- Fast coloniser of damaged areas; to 9m x 5m
- Rainfall 700-2600mm; altitude up to 2500m
- Spreads by seed and root suckers; forms dense stands
- Regenerates after coppicing; bark used in tanning
- Wood: building poles, mine timbers, fence posts, hardboard, fine crafts
- Good windbreak and excellent pioneer into pastured land

### Acacia cuspidifolia (Spine-tipped Wattle)
- Dense bushy large shrub to small tree, 2.5-4m x 4-6m
- Hot arid central-western WA; rainfall 75-235mm
- Tolerates saline, alkaline, stony, compacted soils
- Strong regrowth after heavy grazing; firewood and charcoal potential
- Excellent potential for shelter and soil protection

### Propagation and Establishment

Acacia species are generally straightforward to propagate from seed, but their hard seed coats require specific pre-treatment for successful germination. The most reliable method is boiling water treatment: pour boiling water over seeds and allow them to soak for 12-24 hours. Seeds that swell have absorbed water and are viable; those that remain hard can be re-treated or scarified mechanically with sandpaper or a small crack made with a file. Some species, particularly those from fire-adapted ecosystems, respond well to smoke treatment — exposing seeds to smoke or soaking them in smoke-water solution for 24 hours can dramatically improve germination rates by simulating the post-fire conditions that trigger germination in the wild. Direct seeding is possible for larger-scale plantings but requires careful site preparation and weed control during establishment. Nursery propagation in tubes or pots for 3-6 months before outplanting gives higher survival rates, especially in arid or degraded sites. Seedling wattles develop deep taproots quickly, making them sensitive to root disturbance — deep planting tubes (supertubes) that encourage downward root growth are recommended over shallow containers. Mulching at planting reduces competition from weeds and conserves soil moisture during the critical establishment period. Most wattles grow rapidly once established, with many species reaching 1-2 metres in their first year under favorable conditions. See [[permaculture-nitrogen-fixing-trees-guide]] for establishment techniques and [[fukuoka-black-wattle-fertilizer-tree-method]] for Fukuoka's approach to integrating wattles into orchard systems.

### Ecological Considerations and Invasive Potential

Several Acacia species have become significant invasive problems outside their native Australia, and any permaculture planting of wattles requires careful species selection. Acacia dealbata (silver wattle) is invasive in parts of Europe, South America, and southern Africa, forming dense monocultures that exclude native vegetation. Acacia cyclops (coastal wattle) has become one of the most problematic invasive plants in South Africa's fynbos ecosystem, transforming nutrient-poor native shrubland into dense thickets that alter fire regimes and soil chemistry. The invasive success of wattles is partly due to their nitrogen-fixing ability — they enrich soils in ecosystems adapted to low-nutrient conditions, giving them a competitive advantage over native species. In permaculture design, this nitrogen-fixing capacity is a benefit when wattles are used as nurse trees or [[permaculture-pioneer-species]] in degraded landscapes, but it requires management to prevent unwanted spread. Seed production is typically abundant, and seeds can persist in the soil seed bank for decades. Management strategies include planting sterile or low-seeding cultivars where available, locating wattles away from sensitive natural areas, and regular monitoring for seedling spread. Within their native Australian range, wattles are keystone species providing food and habitat for native birds, insects, and mammals, and their ecological value in Australian permaculture systems is well-established. See [[salt-tolerant-plant-guide]] for wattles suited to difficult sites and [[permaculture-salt-tolerant-fodder-plants]] for dual-purpose fodder and land rehabilitation species.

### Acacia Wood and Non-Timber Products

Beyond their ecological functions as [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]] and pioneer species, wattles produce a remarkable range of useful materials. Acacia wood varies enormously in quality and workability between species. Acacia melanoxylon (blackwood) produces a premium furniture timber with excellent woodworking properties, a rich dark color, and dimensional stability that makes it sought after for fine cabinetry, musical instruments, and boat building. Acacia koa from Hawaii, though not an Australian species, is one of the world's most valued tonewoods used in premium ukuleles and guitars. Many Acacia species produce excellent firewood — Acacia aneura (mulga) is among the hardest woods known, with a density that produces intense, long-lasting heat and a bed of coals that sustains overnight cooking fires. The bark of several wattles, particularly Acacia decurrens and Acacia mearnsii (black wattle), is one of the world's most important commercial sources of tannin, used in leather production, adhesives, and traditional medicine. Wattles also produce edible seeds: Aboriginal Australians traditionally harvested and ground Acacia seeds into a nutritious flour high in protein and complex carbohydrates — a practice being revived by contemporary bush food producers. The flowers of many species are prolific nectar producers for honey, and acacia honey is commercially valued for its light color and mild flavor. Gum arabic, used in food processing, pharmaceuticals, and art, is harvested from Acacia senegal and related African species.
