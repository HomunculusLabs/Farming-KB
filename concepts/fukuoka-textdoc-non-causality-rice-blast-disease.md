# Non-Causality and Rice Blast Disease

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] uses rice blast disease as a central case study to
illustrate his principle of non-causality, arguing that the scientific
practice of identifying single causes for crop diseases is fundamentally
misguided and that the chain of causation in nature is infinite and
ultimately unresolvable.

## The Scientific Approach to Rice Blast

The conventional agricultural scientist identifies rice blast as a
disease caused by the infiltration of rice blast bacteria (Pyricularia
oryzae). The logical response, following this diagnosis, is to spray
copper or mercury agents to dispel the disease. Over time, this has
escalated to the application of pesticides several times a year as a
panacea. Steady improvement in pesticides has led directly to the
present state of affairs where these chemicals are applied routinely
throughout the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]], transforming what was once a sporadic
intervention into a permanent feature of [[jadam-rice-cultivation-methods]].

## The Infinite Chain of Causation

Fukuoka demonstrates that behind every apparent cause lie countless
other causes. When soil acidity becomes a problem, one might immediately
conclude that lime is deficient. However, this deficiency may stem not
from the soil itself but from erosion caused by repeated cultivation on
ground exposed by weeding, or from rainfall and temperature patterns
that have shifted due to broader environmental changes. Applying lime to
treat soil acidity thought to result from insufficient lime may actually
increase acidity further, confusing cause with effect in a feedback loop
that spirals further from resolution.

## Multiple Interrelated Contributing Factors

- **High temperatures and heavy rainfall** may contribute to
outbreaks by creating conditions favorable to the pathogen while
simultaneously weakening the host plant through [[query-how-do-i-manage-heat-stress-in-my-cannabis-garden]] and
waterlogging
- **Over-application of nitrogenous fertilizers** may weaken plant
resistance by promoting excessive soft [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] that is more
vulnerable to infection, creating lush tissue that the fungus easily
colonizes
- **Flooding of the paddy during high temperature periods** may
weaken roots and reduce the plant's ability to absorb nutrients
essential for disease resistance, while also creating an anaerobic root
zone that compromises overall plant vigor
- **The variety of rice being grown** may have low inherent
resistance, having been selected primarily for yield rather than
resilience, trading defensive capacity for productive capacity
- **Insufficient sunlight during heading** may slow maturation and
leave the plant in a vulnerable state for longer, extending the window
during which infection can take hold
- **Excess nitrogenous components** may remain unassimilated in
leaves due to poor photosynthesis, creating conditions that the blast
fungus exploits as a nutrient source

## The Relativity of Causal Attribution

The attribution of causality depends entirely on the observer's
perspective and the time frame being considered. What appears to be a
cause when viewed at one scale may appear as an effect when viewed at
another. The scientist who isolates a bacterium and demonstrates its
pathogenicity in a petri dish has established a valid observation within
the narrow confines of the laboratory, but this observation does not
capture the broader ecological reality in which the disease occurs in
the field.
