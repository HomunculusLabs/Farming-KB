# Fungal Decomposition of Wood and Litter

## Overview

Wood and litter decomposition by fungi is a foundational ecological process that drives nutrient cycling, soil formation, and carbon flux in terrestrial ecosystems. The diversity of decomposer fungi and the complexity of their interactions with substrata make decomposition one of the most important and least understood aspects of [[fungal-ecosystem-services]]. Wood-inhabiting fungi follow organized successional patterns as substrata deteriorate, with distinct communities occupying different decay stages. Up to 80% of net primary production in forests enters the decomposition pathway via dead organic matter, processed largely by fungi.

Three nutritional strategies — saprotrophy, biotrophy, and necrotrophy — have been recognized for fungi (Cooke and Rayner 1984). These are not necessarily mutually exclusive; individual species can manifest all three in different circumstances, and fungi frequently shift their nutritional strategies in the course of their life cycles. Saprotrophy is the most frequent nutritional habit of microfungi colonizing plant debris.

## White Rot

White rot is the most efficient form of wood decay, capable of degrading all major structural components of wood including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. White-rot fungi produce ligninolytic enzyme systems (lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, laccase) that oxidatively break down lignin. The name "white rot" refers to the bleached appearance of decayed wood resulting from lignin removal — the wood becomes softened and punky, light weight, and fibrous or spongy.

White rot is primarily caused by basidiomycetes, though some ascomycetes also exhibit white-rot capabilities. White-rot fungi are essential for recycling the carbon in woody plant tissue, as no other organism group possesses comparable lignin-degrading ability. In forest ecosystems, white-rot fungi are the primary agents responsible for the complete breakdown of woody debris and its incorporation into soil organic matter.

## Brown Rot

Brown rot fungi selectively degrade cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving lignin largely intact. The decayed wood appears brown and cracks into small hard blocks as the remaining wood is primarily lignin — this is termed brown rot. Brown-rot fungi employ a non-enzymatic Fenton chemistry mechanism: they generate hydroxyl radicals through iron reduction that depolymerize cellulose. This chemical (rather than enzymatic) approach makes brown rot unique among biological decay systems.

Brown-rot residues can be a major soil component in coniferous forests and serve as moisture sinks for maintaining [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] in seasonally dry forests. The lignin-rich residues from brown-rot decay contribute to long-term carbon storage, as the modified lignin resists further microbial degradation for extended periods.

## Soft Rot

Soft rot fungi (primarily ascomycetes) cause a slower, less aggressive decay characterized by cavity formation within the secondary cell wall. Soft rot is particularly important in environments where basidiomycete activity is limited: waterlogged wood, tropical conditions, and wood exposed to extreme temperatures. Soft rot is the dominant decay type in many aquatic and semi-aquatic environments. Some cellulolytic molds associated with soft rot are also important in the early stages of leaf litter decomposition.

## Successional Patterns on Wood

Wood-inhabiting fungi follow predictable successional sequences as substrata deteriorate:

1. **Pioneer colonizers**: Non-pathogenic [[endophytic-fungal-diversity]] species already present in living tissue begin active growth when the tree dies or wood is wounded. A large proportion of endophytic fungal species become saprobic on the death of their host, an advantageous strategy because their intimate association with the plant allows immediate utilization of the dead tissue. Staining fungi and soft-rot fungi colonize exposed surfaces through ray cells and wounds. Bark of living trees, which is dead, often supports growth of microfungi.

2. **Competitive phase**: White-rot and brown-rot basidiomycetes establish territories within the wood. Competition between individuals and species determines which fungi dominate. Some species produce antibiotic compounds to suppress competitors. Different groups of wood-inhabiting fungi use quite different strategies for resource capture.

3. **Climax communities**: As easily degradable components are exhausted, stress-tolerant decomposers dominate. The community shifts toward species capable of degrading the remaining recalcitrant material. High numbers of species may be characteristic of fungal communities occurring in complex mixtures of resources.

The correlated factors of age, decay stage, and contact with soil, together with microenvironmental variables such as exposure to sun, moss cover, and soil moisture, are the most strongly correlated with community structure. Fungal succession varies even between upper and lower internodes of grass stems, and between standing and uprooted culms, demonstrating the fine-scale complexity of successional dynamics.

## Life Strategies in Decomposer Fungi

Concepts of life strategies in fungi have been derived from the work of Grime (1977, 1979) on higher plants, subsequently modified and extended by Cooke and Rayner (1984) and Andrews (1992):

- **Ruderal**: Short life spans, high reproductive potential, rapid growth devoted to reproduction. Many Mucorales are ruderals, abundant in soil. Ruderal fungi appear particularly sensitive to transient factors such as water stress but play an insignificant role in litter decomposition (Webster 1957).

- **Competitive**: High competitive ability, persistent and long-living, capable of defending captured resources, with rapid spore germination and reproduction.

- **Stress-tolerant**: Endurance of environmental stress or resource depletion. Lack noticeably rapid growth, spore germination, or reproduction, but are enzymatically competent. Most known stress-tolerant fungi can be grouped in well-defined ecological niches, each adapted to a particular type of stress.

## Host Specificity and Substrate Preferences

In general, gymnosperm hosts/substrata support a more restricted mycota than angiosperm hosts/substrata. The numbers of species of wood-inhabiting fungi typically increase with the range of decay classes sampled rather than the number of tree species — suggesting that decay stage diversity is more important than host species diversity for supporting fungal species richness.

Emerging evidence suggests that many saprobic fungi are specialized to colonize substrata with particular physical characteristics rather than to particular host taxa. Features such as substratum longevity, topography (e.g., spininess, venation patterns), rigidity, and water retention capability must have encouraged the evolution of species guilds. Some substratum-specific saprobes may be present as endophytes in unrelated hosts but do not develop after the host material dies.

## Dead Wood as Habitat

Dead wood of various sizes and decay stages provides critical habitat for a wide range of organisms beyond fungi: invertebrates, birds, and other organisms use dead wood for nesting, feeding, and shelter. The concept of "coarse woody debris" as a structural and functional component of forest ecosystems emerged from recognition of the ecological importance of dead wood. Standing dead trees (snags) support distinct fungal communities from fallen logs.

## Forest Management Implications

Logging and wood removal dramatically reduce wood-inhabiting fungal diversity. The species-area relationship for wood-inhabiting fungi is strong: more wood (larger area, more pieces, more decay stages) means more species. Small woody debris is encountered more frequently than large-diameter wood, so surveys must sample both. Old-growth forests with accumulated dead wood of various decay stages harbor significantly more species than managed stands.

## Litter Decomposition

Fungal succession on leaf litter follows a similar pattern: sugar fungi exploit soluble carbohydrates first, followed by cellulolytic molds, then lignin-modifying fungi. In humid tropical ecosystems, the process takes months; in temperate systems, years. It may be years before plant debris decays fully and its residues are incorporated into the soil. The temporal interface between plant debris and soil is critical for community analysis.

Primary colonizers of newly fallen leaves differ between upper and lower leaf surfaces. Litter decomposition rate depends on litter quality (C:N ratio, lignin content), moisture, temperature, and fungal community composition. Stable organic compounds such as waxes, lignins, and phenolics may persist for hundreds of years, are incorporated into mineral soil horizons, and are colonized by characteristic groups of fungal species.
