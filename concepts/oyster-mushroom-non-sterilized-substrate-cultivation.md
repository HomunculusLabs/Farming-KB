# Oyster Mushroom Non-Sterilized Substrate Cultivation

Growing oyster mushrooms without conventional pasteurization or sterilization represents one of the most accessible and low-cost approaches to mushroom cultivation. This method, documented extensively by Pakistani grower Ahklaq Khan, uses lime (calcium carbonate) and pulse powder as natural antimicrobial agents to prepare wheat straw substrate without any heat treatment. The technique eliminates the most expensive and labor-intensive step in conventional mushroom growing, making it particularly valuable for resource-limited growers in developing countries.

## Rationale for Non-Sterilized Cultivation

Conventional oyster mushroom cultivation requires pasteurization or sterilization of substrate before inoculation. This step is typically the most expensive component of the process because it demands fuel consumption for steaming or boiling, specialized equipment such as drums or autoclaves, and significant labor input. Pasteurization at 60-65°C for 2-8 hours or sterilization at 121°C for 60-90 minutes are the standard approaches, both requiring sustained heat energy.

The appeal of non-sterilized cultivation lies in its simplicity: substrate preparation costs drop dramatically, the process becomes faster, and no specialized heating equipment is needed. This opens mushroom cultivation to the poorest farmers who may not have access to fuel or pressure vessels. The key insight is that certain substrate materials, particularly wheat straw, can be rendered safe for inoculation through chemical rather than thermal treatment.

## Substrate Selection: Why Wheat Straw

Among all tested non-sterilized substrate materials, wheat straw produced the most consistent and successful results. Ahklaq Khan experimented with cotton waste, rice straw, sawdust with poultry manure, dried grass, waste cloth, and oak, but all required sterilization for acceptable yields. Wheat straw alone achieved nearly 100% success without any heat treatment.

The scientific basis for wheat straw's suitability is not fully understood, but several factors likely contribute. Wheat straw has a relatively balanced lignocellulosic composition with moderate nitrogen content. Its physical structure allows adequate aeration even when packed densely. The straw's natural chemistry may also create conditions less favorable for contaminant organisms compared to other materials. The exact mechanisms remain an area for further research.

## Lime as an Antimicrobial Agent

The cornerstone of this method is the use of limestone (calcium carbonate, CaCO3) as a chemical pasteurization agent. Lime serves multiple critical functions:

- **Antibacterial action**: The alkaline environment created by lime kills viruses, bacteria, and many competitor fungi harmful to initial mycelial growth. Most contaminant organisms cannot survive at the elevated pH levels lime creates.
- **Acid neutralization**: Wheat straw and other agricultural residues tend toward acidity as they decompose. Lime raises the substrate pH into the range favorable for oyster mushroom mycelial growth (pH 6-8).
- **Heat generation**: When lime reacts with water, an exothermic chemical reaction produces significant heat. This self-generated heat provides supplementary pasteurization, raising substrate temperature to levels that further suppress contaminants.

The method uses 20kg of limestone for 40kg of wheat straw, a 1:2 ratio. This high lime concentration creates a strongly alkaline environment that persists through the 24-hour treatment period.

## Pulse Powder as a Nitrogen Supplement

Yellow pulse (legume) powder serves as an organic nitrogen supplement added at the spawning stage. While wheat straw provides the primary carbon source, supplementation with a small amount of nitrogen-rich material supports more vigorous mycelial growth and higher yields. The pulse powder is added at a rate of 1kg per 40kg of wheat straw.

The use of locally available legume powder rather than commercial nitrogen supplements keeps input costs minimal. Pulses are widely grown and available in agricultural communities, making this ingredient accessible to subsistence farmers.

## Detailed Step-by-Step Protocol

### Step 1: Soaking and Liming

Two containers are needed. The first is filled with 100L of water, and 40kg of chopped wheat straw is added and allowed to soak. In the second container, 100L of water is combined with 5kg of limestone (half the total lime). The lime dissolves with an exothermic reaction that generates heat and gases. The soaked wheat straw is then transferred into the lime solution, where it absorbs the alkaline treatment.

### Step 2: Bran Addition

The lime-treated straw is removed from the solution and mixed thoroughly with 4kg of wheat bran or rice bran on a clean surface covered with a polythene sheet. This mixing must be thorough and even to ensure consistent nutrient distribution throughout the substrate.

### Step 3: Lime Layer Assembly

A circular tray pattern is constructed on the floor using bricks. The remaining 10kg of dry limestone is spread inside the brick circle. The wheat straw and bran mixture is poured over this dry lime layer. Water is added until the base becomes wet, initiating a chemical reaction in the dry lime that produces additional heat and gases. This self-generated heat provides further pasteurization of the substrate mass.

### Step 4: Sealed Incubation

The entire substrate mass is quickly covered with a polythene sheet, sealed tightly to retain heat and humidity. This sealed incubation proceeds for exactly 24 hours. The covering must be airtight to maintain the chemical pasteurization environment. Extended coverage beyond 24 hours is not recommended as the conditions may become unfavorable.

### Step 5: Spawning

After 24 hours, the treated substrate is moved to shelves, trays, or bags for spawning. The substrate is spread evenly, and spawn is sprinkled uniformly across the surface. Yellow pulse powder (1kg) is then distributed evenly over the spawned substrate as a nitrogen supplement.

### Step 6: Sealed Colonization

All shelves are covered tightly with polythene sheeting to exclude air and maintain the high humidity required for mycelial colonization. Temperature is maintained at 10-15°C for optimal results during this phase.

### Step 7-8: Mycelial Growth and Fruiting Induction

Mycelium begins spreading within 7-8 days. Full colonization of the shelf typically requires about 50 days. At this point the polythene covering is removed and pinning is induced through increased humidity, light exposure, and ventilation.

### Step 9-10: Watering and Harvest

Water is sprayed 3-4 times daily to maintain 90-100% humidity at 15-20°C. Mushrooms appear within 8-10 days of pinning induction and are ready for harvest 2-3 days later. Continuous harvesting can proceed for 3-4 months, potentially extending to 1-2 years under favorable conditions. Typical yields are 1-2kg of fresh mushroom per kilogram of substrate.

## Comparison with Conventional Methods

Jozef Poppe's forty years of experience have confirmed that even minimal heat treatment, specifically pasteurization of wheat straw at 65-70°C for just 2 minutes, achieves complete incubation in 3 weeks and harvest 3 weeks later. This suggests that while the non-sterilized method works, conventional pasteurization achieves faster results with less total production time. However, the non-sterilized approach eliminates fuel costs entirely and requires no specialized equipment.

The 50-day colonization period for non-sterilized substrate is considerably longer than the 20-25 days typical for pasteurized or sterilized wheat straw. This extended timeline must be factored into production planning, particularly for commercial operations.

## Limitations and Considerations

- The method has been validated primarily with wheat straw. Other substrates tested (cotton waste, rice straw, sawdust, dried grass) did not achieve acceptable contamination rates without sterilization.
- The long colonization period of approximately 50 days means slower returns compared to conventional methods.
- Environmental conditions must be favorable. The recommended 10-15°C colonization temperature limits this method to cooler seasons or climates in many regions.
- Large quantities of lime are required (20kg per 40kg straw), which may represent a significant cost in some areas.
- Success depends heavily on proper execution of the chemical treatment protocol. Insufficient liming or inadequate sealing leads to contamination.

## Practical Applications

This method is most suited for small-scale farmers in rural areas of developing countries who have access to wheat straw and lime but lack the infrastructure for conventional pasteurization. The extremely low startup costs and minimal equipment requirements make it an ideal entry point for first-time mushroom growers. The technique demonstrates that mushroom cultivation can be accessible even to the most resource-constrained farmers, supporting broader goals of poverty alleviation through mushroom growing.

The technique is particularly relevant for community-based mushroom projects where training farmers in complex pasteurization procedures may be impractical. Its simplicity means that once learned, the method can be replicated independently without ongoing technical support.
