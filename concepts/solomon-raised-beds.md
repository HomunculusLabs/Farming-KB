# [[steve-solomon]] directly challenges the intensive gardening orthodoxy that
advocates closely spaced, deeply double-dug [[comparison-raised-beds-vs-hugelkultur]] with vegetables planted
in precise hexagonal patterns. He states flatly that claims of much higher
yields and less water use per unit of production from intensive beds "are not
true." He has used those difficult [[row-gardening-vs-intensive-methods-solomon]] himself and found easier
approaches that produce higher quality from a given area, with one quarter the
watering effort -- or none at all.

## Three Planting Arrangements

Solomon identifies three main planting arrangements, each suited to different
crops and conditions:

**Raised beds:** Slightly elevated growing areas, typically four feet (120 cm)
wide with one-foot (30 cm) paths between them. Best for small-sized vegetables
like beets, carrots, lettuce, bush beans, and bush peas when irrigation is
available. Also suitable for larger plants like Swiss chard, some cucumbers,
cabbage, kale, and Brussels sprouts.

**Raised wide rows:** Narrow raised beds about 20-24 inches (50-60 cm) wide with
one-foot paths between, making row centers about 36 inches (90 cm) apart. Better
suited to gardening without irrigation. In dry climates like western Oregon,
Solomon used row centers of four to five feet (120-150 cm).

**Hills:** Small, highly fertilized mounds for large, widely spaced plants like
kale, Brussels sprouts, broccoli, squash, melons, corn, and some tomatoes. Sweet
corn, which needs a large cluster for wind pollination, does better on the flat
or in hills.

## Making Slightly Raised Beds

The beds should not be raised more than two to three inches (5-8 cm) -- there is
no point in going higher unless you cannot squat or bend over. This slight
elevation is accomplished by scooping soil from what will become the paths and
tossing it onto the growing bed.

**Width guidelines:** Four feet (120 cm) is about the limit for most people to
reach the center while bending over. Narrower beds (down to three feet) are
needed for those with limited flexibility but reduce overall space efficiency.
Wider beds (up to 4.5 feet) work for flexible people with long arms.

## Advantages of Slight Elevation

**Seed-sprouting ease:** Tiny seeds like lettuce and carrots need a fine seedbed
to emerge. On flat ground, raking creates a valley of fine soil edged by ridges
of clods that smother seedlings. When beds are raised a few inches, clods and
lumps can be rapidly raked down into the paths, creating a uniform fine surface.

**[[fukuoka-textdoc-weed-control-with-weeds-rice-barley-clover-discovery]]:** Raised beds remind people to stay off the growing areas.
Plants grow better because the soil stays looser -- walking on loosened soil
compacts it, making it difficult for vegetables to develop root systems.

**No retaining walls needed:** Solomon advises against wooden, stone, or
concrete retaining walls around beds. These create edges where weeds and grasses
get a grip that makes them nearly impossible to remove with a hoe. The beds are
perfectly functional with simple earth sides.

## Restoring Raised Beds After a Crop

After a raised bed or wide row has grown a crop, it needs restoration for the
next planting. This is far less work than initial preparation. For a
100-square-foot (10-square-meter) bed, the process takes about 30 minutes. A
quarter-inch (6 mm) layer of manure or compost spread over the surface and
lightly dug in is usually sufficient. For high-demand vegetables, a half-inch
layer or an application of Complete [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]] should be added.

## Hills for Large Plants

Hills are particularly frugal with fertility and well-suited to survival
gardening. To prepare hills:

1. Spread lime, compost, or manure and COF over the whole area.  2. At each hill
location, place a shovelful of low-potency manure or compost in a small pile.
3. Using a combination shovel/fork, dig about 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) deep,
working up a circular spot about 24 inches (60 cm) in diameter, blending the
amendments.  4. End with a mound of highly fertile soil about 10-24 inches
across, depending on the crop.
