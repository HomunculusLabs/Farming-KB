# Resource Categories and Management

In [[permaculture]], resources are defined as useful reserves created when living components and technological units translate incoming energies (sun, wind, rain) into stored forms. Understanding the nature of different resources is essential to designing systems that are truly sustainable.

## Definition of Resources

The energies coming into a system — natural forces such as sun, wind, and rain — are translated by living components and built technological units into useful reserves called **resources**. Some of these resources must be used by the system for its own purposes (stocks of fish must be maintained to produce more fish). An ideal technology should at least fuel itself.

**Yield** is the surplus over and above system needs — available for use, export, or trade. The way to obtain yield is to be conservative in resource use, for energy, like money, is much more easily saved than generated. Resource saving involves recycling waste, insulating against heat loss, and routing resources to their next use point.

## Two Broad Categories of Resources

### Common Heritage Resources

Forests, soils, air, water, sunlight, and seeds are resources regarded as part of a common heritage — belonging to everyone and affected by how they are used or abused.

### Personal or Group Resources

Fabricated, ordered, or otherwise developed resources that people create through their work, and whose presence or absence does not apparently affect the common resource. However, what we create is always made from the common resource, so drawing a sharp line between categories is ultimately impossible.

## The Five Resource Categories (Use-and-Results Approach)

When resources are examined by what happens when they are used, five distinct categories emerge:

### 1. Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources benefit from being used and deteriorate if neglected.

- **Green browse**: If deer do not browse shrubs, the latter become woody and unpalatable. A browsed biennial, unable to flower, may tiller out and become perennial (e.g., fireweed nibbled by wallaby in Tasmania).
- **Seedling trees** can be maintained at browse height; if ungrazed, they "escape" to unbrowsable height and shade out other palatable plants.
- **Information** is another resource that increases with use. It withers or becomes outdated if not used. Too little impoverishes a system, but when freely used and exchanged, it flourishes and increases.

**Caution**: Both overgrazing (through damage) and underbrowsing can cause extinction of palatable species and browsers.

### 2. Resources Unaffected by Use

These resources remain intact regardless of how they are used.

- **Impalpable examples**: A view or a good climate is unaffected by use.
- **Palpable examples**: Diversion of part of a river for hydroelectric generation or irrigation (water returned after use); a stone pile used as mulch, heat store, or water run-off collector.
- **A well-managed ecosystem** is itself a resource unaffected by use.

### 3. Resources That Disappear or Degrade If Not Used

These are perishable or time-sensitive resources that must be captured or they are lost.

- Unharvested crops of annuals
- Grass that could be stored for winter
- Irruptions of oceanic fish
- Swarms of bees or grasshoppers
- Ripe fruit
- Water run-off during rains

### 4. Resources Reduced by Use

These resources are finite and diminish with extraction.

- Fish or game stocks unwisely used
- Clay deposits
- Mature forests
- Coal and oil

### 5. Resources That Pollute or Destroy Other Resources If Used

These are inherently destructive resources whose use causes systemic harm.

- Residual poisons in ecosystems
- Radioactives
- Super-highways and large areas of concrete
- Large buildings
- Sewers running pollutants to the sea

## Sustainability Implications

Categories 1 through 3 are those most commonly produced in natural systems and rural living situations, and are the **only sustainable basis of society**. Categories 4 and 5 result from urban and industrial development and, if not used to produce permanent beneficial changes, become pollutants — some are permanent pollutants within human lifetimes.

## Policy of Resource Management

A responsible human society:

1. **Bans the use of resource category 5** — pollutants, persistent poisons, radioactives, large areas of concrete and highways, sewers from city to sea.
2. **Manages categories 1 through 4 wisely** with regulation to produce sustainable yield.
3. **Applies these criteria at all levels** — national, community, and household investment priorities.

## The Principle of "Enough"

The key principle to wise resource use is the **principle of "enough"** — basic to understanding societies in chaos or systems in disorder. Any system or organism can accept only that quantity of a resource which can be used productively. Any resource input beyond that point throws the system into disorder; oversupply is a form of **chronic pollution**.

Both over- and undersupply have much the same destructive effects, though oversupply produces more grotesque results in life systems. Undersupply can be coped with through reduced growth and wider spacing of organisms, but oversupply causes inflated growth, crowding, and sociopathy in social organisms. In people, both gross over-nutrition and under-nutrition are common, requiring ethical resource management to balance the pathologies of famine and obesity.

## Consequences of Ignoring Resource Ethics

Failure to ban category 5 resources and manage categories 1–4 wisely will cause society itself to fail. Programs of highway building and city expansion, release of persistent biocides, and loss of soil bring societies down more surely and permanently than war itself.

Mollison argued that governments that tolerate [[desertification]], land salting, concreted highways, and city sprawl take more good land permanently out of life production than the loss of territory to a conqueror. This immorality is termed "progress" and "growth" to confuse the ignorant and supplant local self-reliance for the temporary ends of centralized power.

As evidence: superhighways and overpasses in Massachusetts alone need approximately 400 billion dollars to repair, and the collapsing sewer systems of London and New York some 80 billion — amounts neither jurisdiction can raise. This demonstrates that unthinking historical development strategies can cripple future societies. **Today's luxuries are tomorrow's disasters.**
