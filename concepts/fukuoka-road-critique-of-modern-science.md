# Fukuoka's Critique of Modern Science and Reductionism

In [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]], [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] delivers one of his most sustained critiques of modern science, arguing that the scientific method itself is fundamentally incapable of understanding nature. This position, developed across his world travels and decades of farming, distinguishes his [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]] from mere organic agriculture or environmentalism.

## Science Cannot Know Nature's Soul

Fukuoka argues that scientists can only gaze upon nature's "outer shell" — they "will never have access to its true inner heart and soul." The soul of nature, which he identifies with the will of God dwelling in nature, "is not something that scientists can find by dissecting nature." Geneticists who extract and synthesize genes are mistaken: "nature's soul does not lie hidden within DNA. Nor is this where God resides."

This is not merely a theological claim but an epistemological one. Scientists "are incapable even of knowing that they are in no position to understand the soul of a flower in the meadow." The soul of nature, and the will of God who dwells in nature, exist beyond the reach of any method that dissects, isolates, or analyzes. No matter how much man "dissects nature or with his own mind denies the existence of human intelligence and calls everything 'Mu,'" he only "moves further and further away from both nature and God."

## Fragmented Knowledge and Global Disruption

The core problem, as Fukuoka sees it, is that science develops "fragmented and diffusive," expanding "outward without aim or direction." This has "brought human thought to the extremes of confusion, recklessly splitting apart God, nature, and man — originally one indivisible — and leaving only a legacy of incoherent chaos." Science, in his view, "has gone on a wild rampage, and the global disruption this has caused is only now becoming clear."

The fragmentation is not merely intellectual but ontological. By splitting what was originally one — God, nature, and man — science creates a worldview in which each element is studied in isolation, losing the relationships that give them meaning. The result is "incoherent chaos" that masquerades as progress. This critique connects directly to his [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]] practice, which begins from wholeness rather than fragmentation.

## Science Doesn't Know What Real Food Is

In a striking interview passage, Fukuoka states flatly that "science doesn't have the vaguest idea what real food is." Science cannot tell us "why, how, and in what way man eats and should eat," nor can it explain "the basis and cause for being alive." He contrasts this with the confidence of animals: "The foxes, the badgers, and the birds haven't the faintest idea what to eat or grow; to man, theirs is a very fragile, marginal existence. Well actually, it's just the other way around. These animals are the ones with the most reliable wisdom and the most dependable way of life."

Science's inability to understand food extends to its inability to understand life itself. When asked whether man knows more about living than any other animal, Fukuoka reverses the assumption: animals "live each day fully without the least hesitation or uncertainty," while man lives in dread of death, regretting each day's passing. This connects to his [[fukuoka-road-natural-diet-health]] argument that true nourishment comes from alignment with nature, not from nutritional science.

## The Illusion of Understanding Life

Scientists studying nucleic acids "have no idea what the first cause of life is." Nucleic acids are "not the cause or the source of life, but just a manifestation of the result." Fukuoka insists that "the root of life lies outside the scope of science." He compares the genetic material scientists study to a "relay station for transmitted waves" — important as a carrier, but not the source itself. The first cause of cosmic life "exists in a higher plane of reality" that he calls the "Holy Spirit" or "God."

Physicists may claim that the Eastern concept of nothingness (Mu) is close to resolution within quantum theory, and astronauts floating in space may sense God's presence in a weightless state. But "God and nature lie beyond the grasp of the human intellect." No matter how much man dissects nature, he cannot reach the source.

This means that all of biotechnology's claims to understand and manipulate life are built on a fundamental error. Scientists confuse the carrier with the source, the manifestation with the cause. In doing so, they mistake scientific truth for absolute truth — a confusion that leads them deeper into error with each new "advance."

## Biotechnology and the Illusion of Creation

Fukuoka was an early critic of genetic engineering. He warns that "scientists who haven't the slightest idea of the true meaning and purpose of human life are busy devising ways to program life." He argues that new life forms "created with computerized data and genetic engineering will become a source of human calamity" because they are "created with efficiency foremost in mind" and can never "go beyond the vast and infinite standpoint of God." Scientists are "under the illusion that they can replace God as the Lord of all creation."

He grounds this argument in philosophy: since computers and human thought alike are "constructed on the basis of the concepts of space and time," any organisms they create "shall forever be imperfect creatures caught within the domain of the concepts of space and time." They can never match the perfection of organisms created from the standpoint that transcends space and time — the standpoint of God. This critique anticipates many later concerns about genetic modification and connects to his broader [[fukuoka-road-spiritual-aspects-farming]] worldview.

## Scientists as "Palanquin Bearers"

In one of his most vivid passages, Fukuoka describes today's scientists as "the palanquin bearers of rampaging science" who "dance about wildly, drunk on the sound of the words 'high technology.'" He worries that people are "metamorphosing into sophisticated computers" and that the day will come when "stacks of data processed and output by computers will be transmitted directly into the human brain."

This image of scientists as bearers carrying a runaway palanquin captures Fukuoka's view that science has outpaced wisdom. The bearers serve something they cannot control, celebrating its momentum even as it carries them toward disaster. He fears a future where "the human brain will have become a mere data receiving unit," and humanity will have "deserted God" with no possibility of return — becoming "an orphan of the universe."

The palanquin metaphor also captures the complicity of individual scientists. Each bearer is merely doing his job — carrying his corner of the litter — without grasping where the procession is headed. The specialist who develops a new pesticide, the geneticist who engineers a new crop, the engineer who builds a larger dam: each acts within his narrow domain, unable or unwilling to see the cumulative effect. This fragmentation of responsibility mirrors the fragmentation of knowledge that Fukuoka identifies as science's deepest structural flaw.

## Against Darwinian Reductionism

Fukuoka challenges Darwin's theory of evolution directly, noting that "fossils of the 'missing link' species have never been found. The lack of any evidence in support of evolution virtually negates Darwin's theory." He proposes instead that nature is "always absolutely perfect" and flows "constantly from perfection to perfection" — a view incompatible with the progressive narrative of evolutionary biology.

This is not merely an anti-evolutionary stance but part of a larger critique of progressive narratives in science. Just as biotechnology assumes it can improve upon nature's designs, evolutionary biology assumes nature is a work in progress rather than already complete. Both assumptions, in Fukuoka's view, flow from the same error: the belief that human intelligence can comprehend and improve upon what is already perfect. This connects to his [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]], which treats nature's existing patterns as the model rather than as raw material for human improvement.

## Human Intelligence as Nature's Enemy

At the core of Fukuoka's critique is his conviction that "intelligence and reason were nothing more than perverse pranks." The human intellect and what we call reason are "the greatest enemies responsible for man's loss of his native aesthetic sense and of the understanding inherent to man." Reason "appeared capable of becoming the means necessary for conversing with God, but instead it turned out to be a dangerous weapon that strips man of wisdom and brutalizes God."

The practical implication is devastating: "The flower perceived innocently is itself divine nature, but when examined with the intellect, this is transformed into the cold flower of reason, the heart of which nature shuts out." The intellect "attempts to open up nature, but succeeds only in closing it down because human knowledge is in fact nothing more than a cumulation of judgment by the human intellect." This is why [[fukuoka-road-observation-natures-patterns]] — innocent, pre-intellectual observation — is the only valid way to know nature.

## The Road Back to Nature

Despite his withering critique, Fukuoka does not despair entirely. He recalls receiving "a revelation of God one moment fifty years ago" — a vision so overwhelming that he was "taken aback" and failed to advance along the road he should have. Instead, he "turned my back on God and tried to follow quietly the path of a solitary farmer." He came to call this path "the road back to nature."

He acknowledges that his own natural farming is "haphazardly conceived" and incomplete, yet his orchard — where cherry, plum, peach, and pear trees bloom alongside semi-wild vegetables — is called "an Eden, a paradise on Earth" by visitors. The flowers of nature "bloom of their own accord and scatter without care or concern," embodying the perfection that science can never grasp but that anyone can witness if they simply look.

Yet even as visitors call his farm beautiful, Fukuoka notes that "as soon as they finish clicking the shutters on their cameras, they hurry off home to the towns and cities." People today "no longer have the time or ease of mind to immerse themselves in such sensibility." Rather than "indulging directly in the raw, unrefined beauty of nature," they return home with photographs and "are content to enjoy the natural beauty captured in their photographs." Science has taught them to consume nature as an object rather than to dwell within it as participants — a habit of mind that his [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]] practice seeks to undo.

## The Underground Pit of Knowledge

Fukuoka's metaphor for the accumulation of scientific knowledge: "It is as if man is digging and enlarging an underground pit. All that we have to do is look at the rice growing here under the sun. But scientists examine it under the microscope; they take it back to their dark laboratories and study it there." As the pit grows, "the darkness of the unknown surrounding it grows. One bit of knowledge creates two new queries in our minds." The result: "To say 'I understand' is to not understand. At the universities, 'I understand' means nothing more than to have broken what we are examining down and wandered into the obscure darkness of ignorance."

## The Flowers No One Knows

Fukuoka challenged the fundamental assumption of scientific knowing: "No one — not a single human being — knows what that flower is saying, what sort of will it has, whether it is speaking to man, whether it is thinking of something or of nothing at all, what sort of existence it has with respect to man — and whether that is real or fictional." This radical epistemological humility extends to all of nature: "Man is incapable of knowing what this thing we call nature is." Those who claim to understand nature "don't understand what it means to understand."

## The Chemical Industry's Grip on Agriculture

Fukuoka identified the economic reason scientific agriculture persists despite its failures: "If natural farming were to spread widely throughout Japan, there would no longer be any need for agricultural chemicals, chemical fertilizers, and pesticides. The key sectors of Japan's chemical industry will all collapse. No wonder then that no one is willing to believe such a method of farming is possible." The "boom period in agricultural technology" was really "producing chemicals and fertilizers which are wrecking agriculture."
