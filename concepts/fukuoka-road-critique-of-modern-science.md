# Fukuoka's Critique of Modern Science and Reductionism

In [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] delivers one of his most sustained critiques of modern science, arguing that the scientific method itself is fundamentally incapable of understanding nature. This position, developed across his [[fukuoka-world-travels-global-influence]] and decades of farming, distinguishes his [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]] practice, which begins from wholeness rather than fragmentation.

## Science Doesn't Know What Real Food Is

In a striking interview passage, Fukuoka states flatly that "science doesn't have the vaguest idea what real food is." Science cannot tell us "why, how, and in what way man eats and should eat," nor can it explain "the basis and cause for being alive." He contrasts this with the confidence of animals: "The foxes, the badgers, and the birds haven't the faintest idea what to eat or grow; to man, theirs is a very fragile, marginal existence. Well actually, it's just the other way around. These animals are the ones with the most reliable wisdom and the most dependable way of life."

Science's inability to understand food extends to its inability to understand life itself. When asked whether man knows more about living than any other animal, Fukuoka reverses the assumption: animals "live each day fully without the least hesitation or uncertainty," while man lives in dread of death, regretting each day's passing. This connects to his [[fukuoka-road-spiritual-aspects-farming]] worldview.

## Scientists as "Palanquin Bearers"

In one of his most vivid passages, Fukuoka describes today's scientists as "the palanquin bearers of rampaging science" who "dance about wildly, drunk on the sound of the words 'high technology.'" He worries that people are "metamorphosing into sophisticated computers" and that the day will come when "stacks of data processed and output by computers will be transmitted directly into the human brain."

This image of scientists as bearers carrying a runaway palanquin captures Fukuoka's view that science has outpaced wisdom. The bearers serve something they cannot control, celebrating its momentum even as it carries them toward disaster. He fears a future where "the human brain will have become a mere data receiving unit," and humanity will have "deserted God" with no possibility of return — becoming "an orphan of the universe."

The palanquin metaphor also captures the complicity of individual scientists. Each bearer is merely doing his job — carrying his corner of the litter — without grasping where the procession is headed. The specialist who develops a new pesticide, the geneticist who engineers a new crop, the engineer who builds a larger dam: each acts within his narrow domain, unable or unwilling to see the cumulative effect. This fragmentation of responsibility mirrors the fragmentation of knowledge that Fukuoka identifies as science's deepest structural flaw.

## Against Darwinian Reductionism

Fukuoka challenges Darwin's theory of evolution directly, noting that "fossils of the 'missing link' species have never been found. The lack of any evidence in support of evolution virtually negates Darwin's theory." He proposes instead that nature is "always absolutely perfect" and flows "constantly from perfection to perfection" — a view incompatible with the progressive narrative of evolutionary biology.

This is not merely an anti-evolutionary stance but part of a larger critique of progressive narratives in science. Just as biotechnology assumes it can improve upon nature's designs, evolutionary biology assumes nature is a work in progress rather than already complete. Both assumptions, in Fukuoka's view, flow from the same error: the belief that human intelligence can comprehend and improve upon what is already perfect. This connects to his [[fukuoka-road-observation-natures-patterns]] — innocent, pre-intellectual observation — is the only valid way to know nature.

## The Road Back to Nature

Despite his withering critique, Fukuoka does not despair entirely. He recalls receiving "a revelation of God one moment fifty years ago" — a vision so overwhelming that he was "taken aback" and failed to advance along the road he should have. Instead, he "turned my back on God and tried to follow quietly the path of a solitary farmer." He came to call this path "the road back to nature."

He acknowledges that his own [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] is "haphazardly conceived" and incomplete, yet his orchard — where cherry, plum, peach, and pear trees bloom alongside semi-wild vegetables — is called "an Eden, a paradise on Earth" by visitors. The flowers of nature "bloom of their own accord and scatter without care or concern," embodying the perfection that science can never grasp but that anyone can witness if they simply look.

Yet even as visitors call his farm beautiful, Fukuoka notes that "as soon as they finish clicking the shutters on their cameras, they hurry off home to the towns and cities." People today "no longer have the time or ease of mind to immerse themselves in such sensibility." Rather than "indulging directly in the raw, unrefined beauty of nature," they return home with photographs and "are content to enjoy the natural beauty captured in their photographs." ## The Underground Pit of Knowledge

Fukuoka's metaphor for the accumulation of scientific knowledge: "It is as if man is digging and enlarging an underground pit. All that we have to do is look at the rice growing here under the sun. But scientists examine it under the microscope; they take it back to their dark laboratories and study it there." As the pit grows, "the darkness of the unknown surrounding it grows. One bit of knowledge creates two new queries in our minds." The result: "To say 'I understand' is to not understand. At the universities, 'I understand' means nothing more than to have broken what we are examining down and wandered into the obscure darkness of ignorance."

## The Flowers No One Knows

Fukuoka challenged the fundamental assumption of scientific knowing: "No one — not a single human being — knows what that flower is saying, what sort of will it has, whether it is speaking to man, whether it is thinking of something or of nothing at all, what sort of existence it has with respect to man — and whether that is real or fictional." This radical epistemological humility extends to all of nature: "Man is incapable of knowing what this thing we call nature is." Those who claim to understand nature "don't understand what it means to understand."

## The Chemical Industry's Grip on Agriculture

Fukuoka identified the economic reason [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] persists despite its failures: "If natural farming were to spread widely throughout Japan, there would no longer be any need for agricultural chemicals, [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], and pesticides. The key sectors of Japan's chemical industry will all collapse. No wonder then that no one is willing to believe such a method of farming is possible." The "boom period in agricultural technology" was really "producing chemicals and fertilizers which are wrecking agriculture."
