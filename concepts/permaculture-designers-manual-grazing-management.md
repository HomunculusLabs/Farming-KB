# Grazing Management

> From: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 2, 10, 11, 12

Grazing management is central to permaculture design for broadacre and farm-scale systems. Mollison demonstrates that careful grazing rotation, combined with water management and pasture species selection, can dramatically increase production while conserving soil and water.

## Rotational Grazing Systems

On a practical farm (Norm Sims, Weekly Times, 5 Jan 1983), grazing rotation of 21 days across 26 paddocks demonstrated significant benefits. Each paddock averaged 1.6 hectares with a trough water-point for cattle. The system resulted in doubled production using half the irrigation water, reduced salinity, and pasture production that "never looked better" even in severe drought.

Key strategies in use:
- Laser levelling of fields for even irrigation.
- Water reticulation, storage, and recycling.
- Grazing rotation of 21 days.
- Central access road.
- Crop for concentrated rations grown on-site.
- Pasture area reduced to give best watering regime.

## Number of Paddocks

For effective rotational grazing, about 15-18 fields are necessary for rotation. On irrigated areas, some strip grazing is possible using electric fences. Guinea grass suits rotational grazing with 12-18 fields, interspersed with sugar-pod trees and tree fruit forages.

## Establishing Pasture Systems

Establishing perennial grass swards on weedy or eroded areas is a one to three year process. The best sequence is:

1. Choose a land-forming system such as swaling, interception banks, or pitting. Try to establish some dams for irrigation above good soil types.
2. Sow a pioneer grass such as molasses grass, Sorghum almum, or silk sorghum mixed with sawdust into slashed weeds, or drill selected grass and legumes after slashing.
3. Burn molasses grass, or drill selected perennials and broadcast Sorghum almum.
4. Concurrently with earth-forming, plant a mixture of leguminous trees along swales at 30-100 metre strip spacing. Allow 2-3 years to grow with light grazing to year 3.
5. Commence managed rotational grazing, and drill or broadcast forage legumes into established grasses.

## Tropical Pasture Grass Species

**Guinea grass (Panicum maximum):** A bunch grass for warm areas of more than 90-300 cm rain. Drought resistant but yields best in humid areas. Shade tolerant, suits thin-crowned tree crop. Combines well with twining legumes. Drilled to 6 mm to establish. A first choice for sub-tropic and tropic pastures.

**Kikuyu grass (Pennisetum clandestinum):** Cold tolerant, grown from cool to tropical areas. Valuable for cooler uplands, thinner soils, and good autumn growth if nitrogen-fixing trees are established. Excellent for water spillways and erosion control.

**Makarikari grass (Panicum coloratum):** For 40-90 cm rainfall. Tolerates alluvial fans, flood plains, black clay soils, red earths, and semi-caked salted soils. Valuable for winter-green feed and drought resistance. Suits long rotation grazing in open savannah of Acacia.

**Para grass (Brachiaria mutica):** For warm areas, valuable for swampy soils and at dams. Provides good soil structure. Ideal as a fenced-out reserve food for drought.

**Sorghums (Sorghum almum, silk sorghum):** Annual, biennial, or persistent from seed. Used as broadcast-sown pioneers in slashed mulch at 50-90 cm rainfall. Also as emergency dry-season fodder, garden mulch source, and weed control.

## Forage Legumes

**Lucerne (Medicago sativa):** Grown from cool temperate to tropics, usually as a pure sward cut to baled hay, but also in well-managed pasture under rotational grazing.

**Silverleaf desmodium (Desmodium uncinatum):** Trailing legume combining well with grass pastures.

**Cowpea (Vigna spp.):** Vigorous annual or short-lived perennial useful for soil-building and weed control. Good silage, compost, mulch, pioneer crop.

Many forage legumes need rotational grazing. Seeds need inoculant. About 15-18 fields are necessary for proper rotation cycles.

## Integrated Animal Systems

Large animals such as pigs and cows need fenced tree strips, tree guards, and border hedgerow to supply tree forage crop. Intensive animal tractor systems can be a phase followed by tree crop, an accessory to tree crop, or a permanent part of the grazing system.

Mollison advocates for multi-species animal systems wherever possible. Cattle, sheep, and goats grazing together utilize different pasture layers — cattle graze tall grasses, sheep prefer shorter species, and goats browse shrubs and weeds. This complementary grazing pattern improves overall pasture utilization and reduces the selective grazing pressure that degrades pasture composition when a single species grazes alone. Poultry following larger livestock (often in mobile coops or free-range systems) add another layer of nutrient cycling by breaking up and distributing dung pats while consuming insect pests and weed seeds.

The design of animal shelters within grazing systems should incorporate multiple functions. Mollison recommends positioning animal housing so that manure and bedding can be directly transferred to composting systems or garden areas via gravity or short cart runs. Shelterbelts of fodder trees around animal yards provide both shade, wind protection, and dry-season browse supplement.

## Drought Reserve

Para grass is ideal as a fenced-out reserve food for drought, to finish off animals for sale in poor seasons. Such reserves are essential in [[permaculture-designers-manual-arid-climates]] where rainfall is unreliable.

## Mollison's Stocking Density and Grazing Calculations

The Designers Manual provides practical guidelines for calculating stocking rates based on available pasture and rotational frequency. For the 21-day rotation system on 26 paddocks (each approximately 1.6 ha), cattle graze each paddock for less than one day before being moved, giving each paddock a 20+ day recovery period. Mollison cites the Norm Sims case study where this density — roughly 40-50 head rotating across 42 total hectares — doubled production compared to set-stocking while halving irrigation water use.

Stocking density is calculated as: total number of animals × average animal weight ÷ total grazable hectares. High-density, short-duration grazing (sometimes called cell grazing or holistic planned grazing) concentrates animal impact in brief pulses, stimulating root exudation and pasture regrowth. Mollison's approach anticipates what Allan Savory later formalized as holistic management, though Mollison's recommendations remain grounded in the Australian and tropical pasture research of his era.

For tropical systems, Mollison recommends lower stocking rates during dry periods and higher rates during the wet season when pasture growth is rapid. Guinea grass pastures under irrigation in the subtropics can support approximately 2-3 head per hectare with rotational management, compared to 0.5-1 head per hectare under continuous grazing. The critical factor is recovery time — grasses must be allowed to reach full canopy before re-grazing to maintain root carbohydrate reserves.

## Relationship Between Grazing Animals and Pasture Health

Mollison explains the symbiotic relationship between grazing pressure and pasture vitality. Moderate grazing stimulates grass tillering (the production of new shoots from the base), maintains pasture in a vegetative (growing) rather than reproductive (flowering/seeding) state, and returns nutrients to the soil through manure and urine. The key insight is that grasses co-evolved with large herbivores and actually depend on periodic defoliation for optimal health.

Overgrazing, in Mollison's framework, is defined not by too many animals but by animals remaining too long in one area. A large herd moved frequently causes less damage than a small herd left in place indefinitely, because frequent movement prevents re-browsing of regrowth. The 21-day rotation interval allows grasses to rebuild carbohydrate reserves stored in roots — a process that takes approximately 15-20 days in warm climates and 25-35 days in cooler zones.

Grazing animals also cycle nutrients rapidly. Cattle convert standing dry matter into dung pats that concentrate organic matter and microbial activity. In well-managed rotations, dung is distributed evenly across paddocks by animal movement. Mollison recommends running poultry behind cattle in some systems to break up dung pats, control fly larvae, and spread manure more evenly — an early description of what is now called multi-species grazing.

## Electric Fencing and Paddock Design

Mollison emphasizes that effective rotational grazing depends on flexible subdivision using electric fencing. Single-wire electric fences, powered by solar energizers, allow rapid paddock creation and adjustment. For irrigated areas, strip grazing using portable electric fences enables intensive management of high-quality pasture. Fence lines should follow contours where possible to minimize erosion along fence trails.

Water points are central to paddock design. Mollison recommends central water with radiating paddocks or laneways connecting paddocks to a shared water point. In the Norm Sims system, each paddock had its own trough connected to a reticulated water supply, reducing the distance cattle travel for water and distributing nutrient deposition (manure and urine) more evenly across the pasture.

## Seasonal Grazing Strategies

Mollison describes seasonal adjustments to grazing management. In the wet season, rapid pasture growth may require shorter rotation intervals or larger herd sizes to keep pace with growth. In the dry season, rotation intervals lengthen as growth slows, and drought reserves such as para grass paddocks become critical. The establishment of leguminous tree strips along swales provides both dry-season fodder (tree leaf) and nitrogen fixation for surrounding pasture.

Integration with crop production is another seasonal strategy. Animal tractor systems — confining livestock to small areas with portable fencing to clear, manure, and prepare ground — can be used as a rotation phase before planting annual crops. After the crop is harvested, livestock return to graze crop residues and fertilize the soil for the next planting cycle.

## Pasture Renovation and Recovery

Mollison addresses the question of how to restore degraded pastures that have suffered from overgrazing or neglect. The first step is to reduce or eliminate grazing pressure entirely while implementing earth-forming measures — swales, keyline plowing, or contour ripping — to improve water infiltration and retention. Pioneer grass species such as molasses grass (Melinis minutiflora) are broadcast into slashed weed cover to establish a protective ground layer. Once the pioneer sward is established, leguminous trees are planted along contour lines at 30-100 metre intervals, and rotational grazing is gradually reintroduced in the third year as the tree system matures.

Pasture recovery is accelerated by the incorporation of [[permaculture-designers-manual-cover-crops-detailed]] and green manures during the rest period. Cowpeas and other vigorous legumes can be drill-sown into existing grass stands to add nitrogen and organic matter. Mollison notes that pastures which have been rotationally grazed for several years develop increasingly diverse botanical composition as legumes and broadleaf species naturally colonize the improved soil conditions.
