# Hydnum Tooth Fungi and Clavaria Coral Fungi

## Overview

The Hydnaceae (tooth-bearing or hedgehog fungi) and Clavariaceae (club and coral fungi) represent two families of fleshy fungi with distinctive and easily recognized morphologies. The Hydnaceae bear their spores on spines, teeth, or similar structures, while the Clavariaceae may have the [[spore]]-bearing surface covering practically the entire sporophore. Both families are notable for their safety: no poisonous species of the Hydnaceae have been reported, and all sufficiently fleshy Clavariaceae are considered edible.

## Hydnaceae: The Tooth-Bearing Fungi

### General Characteristics

In the Hydnaceae, the hymenium or spore-bearing surface is confined to **spines, teeth, or similar structures**. These teeth may arise from a true cap or from a tubercular or much-branched structure. In form and texture the members differ greatly, and very few possess a central stem. The majority are tough or woody, but some edible species are extremely soft and delicate.

### Edible Hydnum Species

**Hydnum imbricatum** — The most economically valuable member of the genus, possessing a more or less central stalk and a true pileus. A form of considerable size, with a cap frequently irregular and measuring eight to ten inches in diameter in extreme forms. The upper surface is sooty brown or mouse color, and as it expands it differentiates or tears into scale-like areas, sometimes with lighter markings or striations where the flesh shows through. The lower surface is densely beset with teeth, white to buff in color, matching the stem. Particularly abundant in mountain woods, growing on the ground in damp places among mosses and liverworts — as abundant in the foothills of the Rockies as farther east.

### The Coral-like Hydnums

Three species of peculiar form are especially noteworthy:

**Hydnum erinaceus** — The Satyr's Beard. Consists of a fleshy unbranched basal mass from which long straight spines grow downward in a solid phalanx, sometimes attaining a length of an inch or more. Specimens have been collected along the Missouri River weighing fourteen ounces and measuring seven inches across. Common in moist woods and meadows throughout the country, growing upon decaying trees and logs.

**Hydnum coralloides** — The Coral Hydnum. When grown quickly in response to a favorable environment, forms **elaborate tufts of pure white, branching sporophores, icicled by their pendant spine-like but delicate appendages**. A common stem grows from the wood and becomes variously and gracefully branched to form the tufts. The teeth clothe the under surfaces of all branches and branchlets, maintaining a certain delicacy and simplicity of each part. Has been found in clusters which could barely be placed in a peck measure.

**Hydnum caput-ursi** — The Bear's Head. Similar branching habit to the Coral Hydnum but distinguished by a massing of the drooping spines at the ends of the branchlets, with a certain mark of shagginess due to a wavy or sinuous form assumed by some specimens.

### Culinary Qualities

These coral-like hydnums are relished by many, but those to whom a woody flavor is not attractive — although relieved by a certain spiciness — will not seek them as diligently. The woody flavor is characteristic and must be accepted or rejected according to individual preference.

## Clavariaceae: The Club and Coral Fungi

### General Characteristics

The Clavariaceae are characterized by a spore-bearing surface which may cover **practically the entire sporophore**. The plants are upright, and most members are rather fleshy, often much branched or coralloid, though sometimes club-like in habit.

### Edibility

All species which are sufficiently fleshy or delicate are considered edible, and certainly those that are sufficiently attractive to be collected for the table are non-injurious. This makes the Clavariaceae one of the safest families for foraging — their distinctive coralloid or club-like form is virtually impossible to confuse with the dangerous gilled mushrooms.

### Notable Species

**Clavaria aurea** — The golden coral fungus. Yellowish in color, among the more fragile and tender species.

**Clavaria flava** — Also yellowish in color. Like C. aurea, one of the more fragile and delicate members of the genus.

**Clavaria formosa** — Somewhat ochraceous in color. A conspicuous form that attracts attention in the field.

**Clavaria cristata** — Whitish to buff in color. Among the commoner conspicuous forms.

### Foraging Considerations

The coral fungi offer several advantages to the amateur collector:

- **Distinctive morphology** — The coralloid or club-like growth form is unique and easily recognized
- **No poisonous species** — Among fleshy forms suitable for the table
- **Visual appeal** — The branching, often colorful sporophores are attractive and interesting
- **Multiple species** — Several common species provide foraging opportunities across different habitats and seasons

The more fragile and tender species (C. aurea, C. flava) require gentle handling and quick preparation, while the sturdier species can withstand more robust cooking methods. Their delicate texture and mild flavors make them best suited for preparations that highlight rather than mask their natural characteristics.
