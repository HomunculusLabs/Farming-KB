# Phytochromes and Photoperiodism

Phytochromes are a family of photoreceptor proteins that enable plants to
detect red and far-red light, forming the molecular basis for some of the most
critical adaptive behaviors in the plant kingdom. In "What a Plant Knows,"
Daniel Chamovitz describes phytochrome as a biological toggle switch that
allows plants to measure day length, detect the proximity of neighboring
vegetation, regulate seed germination, and control the timing of flowering.
The discovery of phytochrome by Warren Butler and colleagues in the 1950s
revolutionized our understanding of how plants perceive and respond to their
light environment.

## Phytochrome Molecular Biology: Pr and Pfr Switching

Phytochrome is a biliprotein consisting of a protein covalently bound to a
light-absorbing chromophore (phytochromobilin). It exists in two
interconvertible forms that are stable in darkness but reversibly convert
when exposed to light of the appropriate wavelength. The red-light-absorbing
form, called Pr (for "P red"), absorbs light maximally at about 660 nanometers
(red light). When Pr absorbs red light, it undergoes a conformational change
to become Pfr (for "P far-red"), which absorbs maximally at about 730
nanometers (far-red light).

The Pfr form is considered the biologically active form of phytochrome. When
Pfr absorbs far-red light, it converts back to Pr. This red/far-red reversibility
acts as a molecular switch: red light activates phytochrome signaling, while
far-red light deactivates it. Only seconds of illumination at either wavelength
are sufficient to drive the conversion. This switching is completely reversible
and can be cycled many times, as demonstrated by the classic iris flowering
experiments where repeated red/far-red pulses alternately induced and
suppressed flowering.

In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through a thermal process called dark
reversion. The rate of dark reversion varies among phytochrome isoforms and
provides a biological timer that helps plants measure the duration of darkness.
Plants that have experienced a long period of darkness will have most of their
phytochrome in the inactive Pr form, signaling that the night is long and, for
short-day plants, that conditions are right for flowering.

## Photoperiodic Control of Flowering

Photoperiodism is the physiological response of organisms to the length of day
or night. Plants use phytochrome (in combination with other photoreceptors like
cryptochromes) to measure the duration of continuous darkness and determine
whether the current season is appropriate for flowering. As discussed in
"What a Plant Knows," the critical discovery was that plants measure the length
of the night, not the day -- a few minutes of light in the middle of a long
night can completely disrupt flowering responses.

Plants are classified by their flowering requirements into three categories.
Short-day plants (SDPs) such as chrysanthemums, soybeans, and Maryland Mammoth
tobacco flower when the night length exceeds a critical threshold. Long-day
plants (LDPs) such as irises, barley, and spinach flower when the night length
falls below a critical threshold. Day-neutral plants flower regardless of
photoperiod, responding instead to other cues such as plant age or temperature.

The molecular mechanism involves the interaction between phytochrome signals
from the leaves and the CONSTANS (CO)/FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) regulatory module
in the shoot apex. In long-day plants, phytochrome signaling promotes the
stabilization of the CO protein during long days, leading to FT expression and
floral induction. In short-day plants, the regulatory logic is inverted: FT
expression is permitted only when CO is unstable, which occurs during short
days. The FT protein, sometimes called "florigen," travels from the leaves to
the shoot apex through the phloem, where it triggers the transition from
vegetative to floral development.

## Shade Avoidance Syndrome

When a plant is shaded by neighboring vegetation, the light environment
changes in a predictable way: chlorophyll in the canopy leaves absorbs most
red and blue light while transmitting or reflecting far-red light. This shifts
the red-to-far-red ratio (R:FR) from approximately 1.2 in full sunlight to
well below 0.1 in dense shade. Phytochrome is exquisitely sensitive to this
ratio, and low R:FR triggers the shade avoidance syndrome (SAS).

Shade avoidance is a dramatic developmental reprogramming that includes
accelerated stem elongation, upward leaf movement (hyponasty), reduced branch
formation, and early flowering. The plant essentially reallocates resources
from leaf expansion and storage to stem elongation in a race to escape the
shade. In agricultural settings, shade avoidance can reduce yields
significantly: densely planted crops that sense each other's presence elongate
at the expense of grain or fruit production.

The phytochrome family in Arabidopsis includes five members (phyA through
phyE), each with distinct roles. PhyB is the primary sensor of R:FR ratio and
mediates shade avoidance under continuous light. PhyA specializes in detecting
far-red light and is particularly important for germination and de-etiolation
under canopy-filtered light. PhyC, phyD, and phyE have overlapping and
redundant functions that fine-tune the shade avoidance response.

## Seed Germination and Phytochrome

Seed germination in many species is regulated by phytochrome, particularly in
small-seeded species whose seeds are buried at shallow depths where light can
penetrate. The phytochrome system acts as a "canopy detector" for seeds: if
a seed is buried under leaf litter that filters out red light but transmits
far-red, phytochrome remains in the inactive Pr form and germination is
suppressed. If the seed is exposed to direct sunlight rich in red light,
phytochrome converts to the active Pfr form and triggers germination.

This mechanism has profound ecological significance. Seeds that germinate only
when exposed to direct sunlight are more likely to be in gaps in the canopy
where light is available for photosynthesis. Seeds buried under dense
vegetation or deep leaf litter remain dormant until disturbance exposes them
to appropriate light conditions. Some weedy species exploit this system
opportunistically: their seeds can persist in soil seed banks for decades,
germinating rapidly whenever soil disturbance brings them close enough to the
surface to perceive red light.

Phytochrome A plays a unique role in germination. Unlike other phytochromes,
phyA is extremely sensitive to very low fluence rates of far-red light and
can mediate germination responses to light that has passed through substantial
canopy filtering. This makes phyA important for seedlings germinating in
marginal light environments where other phytochromes cannot function.

## Seasonal Adaptation

Beyond flowering and germination, phytochrome coordinates broader seasonal
adaptations including bud dormancy, autumn leaf senescence, and cold
acclimation. As days shorten in autumn, the decreasing R:FR ratio and reduced
day length signal the onset of winter through phytochrome-mediated pathways.
Deciduous trees initiate leaf senescence and bud set in response to these
signals, while herbaceous perennials prepare for dormancy.

Phytochrome also modulates cold acclimation -- the process by which plants
increase freezing tolerance in response to gradually lowering temperatures and
shorter days. The interaction between phytochrome signaling and the C-repeat
binding factor (CBF) cold response pathway integrates photoperiodic sensing
with temperature adaptation, ensuring plants prepare for winter before freezing
temperatures arrive.
