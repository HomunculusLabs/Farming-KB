# Mushroom Growth Hormone: Decapitation Experiments and Gill Diffusate Evidence

## Overview

The concept of a mushroom-specific growth hormone has been investigated
since the 1960s through decapitation experiments and gill diffusate studies.
Badham's 1982 tropism study on *[[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]* references this body of
work, noting that the second phase of directed growth ([[coprophilous-habitat-negative-geotropism-spore-dispersal-adaptation]])
appears to involve a substance produced in the gills that modifies tropic
behavior. This page reviews the historical evidence for mushroom growth
hormones and their proposed role in tropic regulation.

## Historical Background

### Gruen (1963) — Endogenous Growth Regulation

H.E. Gruen demonstrated endogenous growth regulation in carpophores of
*[[agaricus-bisporus]]*, showing that [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] is controlled
by internally produced growth factors. This work established that mushroom
growth is not simply a response to external stimuli but is modulated by
hormone-like substances produced within the fruiting body itself.

### Hagimoto (1963) — The Mushroom Growth Hormone

H. Hagimoto coined the term "mushroom growth hormone" and demonstrated its
connection to geotropic responses in *Agaricus bisporus*. Decapitation
experiments (removal of the pileus) showed that the stipe lost its
gravitropic sensitivity after the cap was removed, suggesting the hormone
originates in or near the pileus.

### Hagimoto and Konishi (1960) — Gill Diffusate Activity

Hagimoto and Konishi showed that agar diffusates from gills could partially
restore pileus influence in decapitated fruiting bodies, strongly suggesting
the active substance is produced in the lamellae and diffuses downward to
regulate stipe growth.

## The Decapitation Experimental Paradigm

Decapitation involves removing the pileus (cap) from a developing
basidiocarp and observing the effect on stipe growth and tropic responses:

**Normal (intact) basidiocarp**: Stipe exhibits phototropic and geotropic
responses; negative geotropism produces upright growth and horizontal
pileus orientation at maturity.

**Decapitated basidiocarp**: Stipe continues elongating but loses
directional control; [[light-role-spore-formation-geotropic-sensitivity-psilocybe-cubensis]] reduced or eliminated; growth
becomes disorganized without proper orientation.

**Decapitated + gill diffusate application**:
- Partial restoration of tropic behavior and geotropic sensitivity
- Growth pattern more closely resembles intact basidiocarps

This paradigm has been repeated across multiple species with consistent
results, supporting a gill-produced tropic regulator.

## Connection to Higher Plant Hormones

Several researchers investigated whether known plant hormones regulate
mushroom growth:

### Indole Acetic Acid (IAA)

Konishi and Hagimoto (1961) detected IAA in *Agaricus bisporus*
fruiting bodies. Jefferys and Greulach (1956) found no evidence that
IAA regulates growth in *Coprinus sterquilinus*. IAA presence appears
incidental rather than regulatory.

### Gibberellin-like Substances

Pegg (1973) found gibberellin-like substances in basidiomycete
sporophores, but no evidence suggests they regulate [[adaptive-significance-mushroom-tropisms-spore-dispersal]].
As with IAA, these compounds appear to be present without functional
role in tropic regulation.
