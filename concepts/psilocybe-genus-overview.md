# Psilocybe Genus Overview

## Introduction

Psilocybe is the largest and most diverse genus of neurotropic fungi, with
116 recognized species worldwide according to Guzman, Allen and Gartz (1998).
The genus accounts for the majority of all known hallucinogenic mushrooms and
contains the species with the highest concentrations of psilocybin and related
tryptamine alkaloids. The name derives from the Greek "psilos" (bare) and
"kube" (head), referring to the typically smooth, hemispheric cap.

## Taxonomic Framework

The taxonomic concept followed is that of Guzman (1983, 1995), a modification
of Singer (1986) excluding Section Chrysocystidiatae. The genus is divided
into multiple sections based on morphological, ecological, and chemical
characteristics. The neurotropic bluing species belong to sections Aztecorum,
Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae,
Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum. The Kuhner and Romagnesi concept
that merges Psilocybe, Hypholoma, and Stropharia into a single genus is not
followed.

## Key Species and Sections

### Section Zapotecorum

This section contains species primarily distributed in subtropical and
tropical forests of the Americas, including P. zapotecorum, P. cubensis,
P. subcubensis, and P. yungensis. P. zapotecorum is one of the most widely
distributed species, found from Mexico to South America and Australasia.
P. cubensis, originally described from Cuba, is a pantropical coprophilous
species and the most commonly cultivated hallucinogenic mushroom worldwide.

### Section Mexicanae

Species in this section are predominantly Mexican, including P. mexicana
(the type species used by Hofmann to first isolate psilocybin in 1958),
P. caerulescens (known locally as "derrumbes" or landslides), and P.
hoogshagenii. These species are closely associated with the indigenous
cultures of central and southern Mexico and have a long history of
ceremonial use among Mazatec, Zapotec, Mixtec, and Chinantec peoples.

### Section Semilanceatae

This temperate section includes P. semilanceata (liberty cap), one of the
most widely distributed neurotropic species, found across Europe, North
America, and parts of Asia and the Southern Hemisphere. P. cyanescens,
known for its potent bluing reaction, is distributed in the Pacific
Northwest of the United States and has been introduced to Western Europe.

### Section Aztecorum

Species of this section grow at high elevations in Mexico. P. aztecorum
occurs at up to 4,000 meters in the mountains of central Mexico, associated
with Abies (fir) forests. P. bonetii is recognized as a variety of P.
aztecorum. These species were historically used by the Aztec and
contemporary Nahua peoples.

## Distribution Patterns

The genus Psilocybe shows its greatest diversity in or near the Austral
hemisphere, mainly in subtropical humid forests. Mexico has the highest
number of species with 44 (39% of the world total), followed by South
America. The genus is distributed on all continents, from Alaska and
Siberia in the north to Chile, Australia, and New Zealand in the south,
and from sea level to elevations exceeding 4,000 meters.

## Ecological Diversity

Psilocybe species occupy diverse ecological niches. Many are saprotrophic,
growing on decaying wood, forest litter, or dung (especially the
coprophilous species). Some species appear to be at least facultatively
mycorrhizal. P. semilanceata is a grassland species, while P. cubensis
grows primarily on bovine dung in tropical and subtropical pastures.
Species of Section Zapotecorum often grow in cloud forests on decaying
wood or among mosses.

## Bluing Reaction as Diagnostic Feature

The characteristic bluing reaction upon bruising is caused by the oxidation
of psilocin, a dephosphorylated derivative of psilocybin. This blue-green
color change is one of the most important field identification features
for neurotropic Psilocybe species. However, not all bluing Psilocybe species
have been chemically confirmed to contain psilocybin, and the intensity of
bluing does not necessarily correlate with alkaloid concentration.

## Species of Particular Note

- P. mexicana: Source of the original psilocybin isolation by Hofmann
- P. semilanceata: Most widely distributed temperate species
- P. cubensis: Most commonly cultivated species worldwide
- P. azurescens: Among the most potent species, from the Pacific coast
- P. samuiensis: Described from Thailand, related to P. mexicana
- P. makarorae: Recently described from New Zealand
- P. natalensis: First indigenous bluing species from South Africa
- P. weilii: Recently described from Georgia (USA)
- P. guilartensis: Recently described from Puerto Rico
- P. portoricensis: Recently described from Puerto Rico

## Non-Bluing Psilocybe Species

Many Psilocybe species are not known to be neurotropic and do not exhibit
bluing. These include species in sections Psilocybe and Hispididae, which
are small, brown, inconspicuous mushrooms often overlooked by collectors.
The paper by Guzman, Allen and Gartz focuses specifically on the 116
neurotropic species. The total number of species in the genus is
considerably larger when non-neurotropic taxa are included, with many
awaiting description, particularly from tropical regions of Asia, Africa,
and South America.

## Ongoing Taxonomic Work

New species continue to be described from various regions. Recent additions
include P. chiapanensis, P. isabelae, P. jaliscana, P. laurae, and P.
villarrealii from Mexico; P. antioquensis, P. guatapensis, and P. heliconiae
from Colombia; and P. subzapotecorum from Mexico. The authors note that
several species described by other researchers require further taxonomic
revision, particularly within the Subaeruginosae and Zapotecorum complexes.
