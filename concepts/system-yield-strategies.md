# System Yield and Yield Strategies

In [[permaculture]], yield is not a fixed quantity determined by external conditions. It is the sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by design. Understanding the full scope of what constitutes yield — and the strategies available to increase it — is central to effective permaculture practice.

## Defining System Yield

System yield is defined as the sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by the design, once the system itself has available all its needs for growth, reproduction, and maintenance. Energy becomes surplus only after the system's own requirements are fully met.

This definition is deliberately broader than conventional measures. Agricultural economists typically regard a single product (such as peaches per hectare) as the yield — an approach that Mollison argues is itself the true limit to yield. A true accounting must consider both upstream costs (energy inputs) and downstream costs (health impacts, pollution, soil mineral loss). A "product yield" that creates more problems than it solves may actually represent a net loss.

## Types of Yield

Yields can be understood in several dimensions:

### Product Yield
The sum of primary and derived products available from, or surplus to, the system. Some products are intrinsic (existing before design intervention), while others are created by design — new connections, new species, new processing pathways.

### Energy Yield
The sum of conserved, stored, and generated energy surplus to the system. This includes thermal energy, chemical energy, biological energy stored in soils and biomass, and mechanical energy captured through design.

### Impalpable Yield
These are the invisible yields related to health, nutrition, security, and a satisfactory social context and lifestyle. They are often the primary motivation driving people toward good design or life on the land. Impalpable yields govern our concept of needs and set the limits of "enough," providing an ethical foundation for the entire concept of yield.

## The Role of Life in Yield

Living things, including people, are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources on this planet and produce yield. Life intercepts cosmic and solar energies, geothermal energy, tidal forces, and electromagnetic and gravitational forces, converting diffuse flows into concentrated biological resources.

This has profound implications: it is the sum and capacity of life forms that decide total system yield and surplus. Technology depends on the continuation of life, not the reverse. Coal and rock do not have yield in the sense of continual production — only life and flow can yield persistently.

## Yield Is Not Fixed

Perhaps the most radical insight in permaculture is that yield is not some external, immutable quantity limited by pre-existing circumstances. It results from human behavior, knowledge, and the application of intellect, skill, and comprehension. These factors can either limit or liberate yield. The profound difference between [[permaculture-design]] and passive nature is that in permaculture, we actively intervene to supply missing elements and guide system evolution.

## Strategies That Create Yield

Permaculture organizes yield-enhancing strategies into several broad categories:

### Foundational Strategies
- **Creation of niches in space** and provision of critical resources
- **Rehabilitation and creation of soils** as the basis of all production
- **Diversion of water and water recycling** to maximize availability
- **Integration of structures and landscape** for mutual benefit
- **Selection of low-maintenance cultivars and species** suited to the site
- **Investigation of other species** for usable yields
- **Supplying key nutrients** through biological waste recycling (mulch, manure)
- **Assembly of beneficial guilds** of cooperative plants and animals

### Spatial and Pattern Strategies
- **Annidation:** Nesting or stacking one thing within another — a vine in a tree, a pond within a [[swale]], a cold frame within a greenhouse
- **Tessellation:** Arranging a mosaic of complementary parts that fit together without gaps
- **Edge and harmonics:** Using innovative spatial geometry to maximize productive edges between different elements
- **Routing materials or energy** to their next best use point

### Conservation Strategies
- **Routing resources to next best use** at every stage
- **Recycling at the highest level** — processing resources to their most valuable form before reuse
- **Safe storage of food products** for extended availability
- **No-tillage or low-tillage cropping** to preserve soil structure and biology
- **Creation of very durable systems and objects** that reduce replacement costs
- **Storage of run-off water for extended use**

### Cultural Strategies
- Removing cultural barriers to resource use
- Making unusual resources acceptable and expanding choices
- Breaking down taboos that limit potential yields

### Administrative Strategies
- Removing socio-legal impediments to resource use
- Creating effective structures to aid resource management
- Costing and adjusting systems for all energy inputs and outputs
- Cooperative endeavors, pooling of resources, sharing within communities
- Financial recycling within the community to keep capital productive locally
- Positive action to remove impeding systems and replace them with functional alternatives

## Increasing Yield Through Design

Yield increases when more strategies are applied, more connections are made between elements, and more information is brought to bear on a design. There is always room for another plant, another cycle, another route, another arrangement, another technique. The theoretical potential of yield is unlimited — what limits it is the comprehension, understanding, and ability of the designers and managers.

Conversely, yield can be decreased by perverse social forces. Politicians, developers, and even religious dogmatists limit yield by disallowing certain products — refusing snails while eating lettuce, refusing blackbirds while eating strawberries, restricting types of toilets or plants permitted in gardens. Cultural biases, not ecological realities, are often the primary impediment to realizing potential yields.
