## The Sawdust Experiment

Right after the war, Fukuoka used large quantities of sawdust and wood chips in his orchard. Soil experts opposed this, saying that the organic acids produced when the wood rots would most likely make the soil acidic and that to neutralize it he would have to apply large quantities of lime.

Yet the soil did not turn acid, so lime was not needed. The experts' prediction was not wrong in a narrow sense, but it captured only one moment in a dynamic process and prescribed a fix for that moment without understanding the full sequence of transformations that would follow.

## Phase Three: Fungal Maturation

Third, when the soil is left to itself, the molds are eventually replaced by mushrooms and other fungi, which break the sawdust down to cellulose and lignin. At this point, the soil is neither acidic nor basic, but hovers about a point of equilibrium.

This natural progression from bacteria to molds to fungi represents a self-regulating system that moves toward balance without any human intervention. Each stage of decomposition creates conditions favorable for the next stage, and the final result is soil that has been enriched without ever requiring chemical correction.

## The Folly of Lime Application

The decision to counteract the acidity of rotting wood by applying lime only addresses the situation at a particular moment in time and under certain assumed conditions, without a full understanding of the causal relationships involved.

By applying lime at the moment when bacteria are producing acids, the farmer interrupts the natural succession. He may prevent temporary acidity but also prevents the molds and fungi from performing their role in breaking down cellulose and lignin into stable humus.

## Confusing Cause with Effect

The same logic applies more broadly to all soil acidity control measures. Applying lime to treat soil acidity thought to result from insufficient lime may bring about excessive plant growth and increase acidity even further, in which case one ends up confusing cause with effect.

Soil acidity control measures taken without understanding why the soil became acidic in the first place may be just as likely to prolong acidity as to reduce it. The intervention creates the very problem it was meant to solve.

## Nonintervention as the Wisest Course

The lesson of the sawdust experiment extends far beyond that single practice. Fukuoka concludes that nonintervention is the wisest course of action when it comes to soil management.

The soil has its own mechanisms for maintaining balance, and these mechanisms are more sophisticated and more reliable than anything human science can devise. This principle stands in direct opposition to conventional [[fukuoka-critique-agricultural-science-laws-liebig]], which treats soil as an inert medium requiring constant chemical adjustment.

## The Scientist Sees a Problem, the Farmer Sees a Process

In the scientific view, soil pH is a number to be corrected. In Fukuoka's view, soil acidity is a transient phase in a dynamic biological process that moves toward equilibrium on its own. The scientist sees a problem; the natural farmer sees a process.

This distinction has profound implications for how agriculture should be practiced. If the soil is self-regulating, then the farmer's role is not to correct imbalances but to avoid creating them in the first place.
