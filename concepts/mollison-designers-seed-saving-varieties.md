# Seed Saving and Open-Pollinated Varieties

Seed saving is one of the most fundamental skills in [[bill-mollison]] viewed the ability to save, select, and exchange seeds as
essential to community self-reliance and ecological resilience. The
concentration of the [[fukuoka-second-genesis-theory-global-seed-scattering]] supply into the hands of a few corporations
producing hybrid and genetically modified varieties represents, in Mollison's
view, a dangerous erosion of food sovereignty and genetic diversity.

## Open-Pollinated vs. Hybrid Varieties

The distinction between open-pollinated and [[heirloom-vs-hybrid-seeds-comparison]] is critical for any
seed saver. Open-pollinated (OP) varieties are genetically stable populations
that breed true from seed. When you save seed from an OP variety and plant it
the following season, the offspring will closely resemble the parent plants.
This stability results from many generations of selection within a relatively
uniform population.

Hybrid varieties, by contrast, are produced by crossing two distinct inbred
parent lines. The first generation (F1) hybrid exhibits hybrid vigour:
superior yield, uniformity, and disease resistance. However, seed saved from
F1 hybrids does not breed true. The second generation (F2) segregates into a
wide range of types, most of which are inferior to the F1 parent. Saving seed
from hybrids is therefore futile for maintaining variety characteristics.

Mollison strongly advocates for the exclusive use of open-pollinated
varieties in [[seed-saving-techniques]]

Seed [[seed-saving-methods-home-garden]] vary with the botanical family and pollination mechanism
of the crop. Mollison outlines the fundamental techniques:

**Dry-seeded crops** (beans, peas, grains, lettuce, onions, brassicas):
Allow seed pods or seed heads to mature fully on the plant. The plant will
often yellow and begin to dry. Harvest before seed shatters, then dry
further in a well-ventilated, rodent-free area. Thresh to separate seed from
chaff. Winnow using wind or a fan to remove lightweight debris.

**Wet-seeded crops** (tomatoes, cucumbers, squash, melons, peppers):
Extract seeds from ripe fruit. For tomatoes and cucumbers, fermentation for
2 to 3 days in water helps remove the gelatinous coating that inhibits
germination. Rinse seeds thoroughly and dry on a non-stick surface. Seeds
must be completely dry before storage to prevent mould and fungal damage.

**Biennial crops** (carrots, beets, onions, cabbage, leeks):
These require two growing seasons to produce seed. In the first year, select
the best roots or plants and store them through winter. Replant in early
spring. The plants will bolt, flower, and set seed in the second year.
Biennial seed saving requires planning and commitment but is essential for
maintaining these important food crops.

## Isolation Distances

Cross-pollination between varieties of the same species produces offspring
that are genetic mixtures of both parents. To maintain varietal purity, seed
savers must prevent unwanted cross-pollination through isolation.

Isolation can be achieved by distance, time, or physical barriers. Distance
isolation requires separating varieties by a minimum distance that varies
enormously by crop and pollination mechanism:

- **Self-pollinating crops** (tomatoes, beans, peas, lettuce): 3 to 6 metres
  is usually sufficient. These crops have flowers that pollinate themselves
  before opening, making cross-pollination rare.
- **Insect-pollinated crops** (squash, cucumbers, brassicas, onions):
  Isolation distances range from 400 metres to 1600 metres depending on the
  crop. Squash and brassicas are particularly promiscuous.
- **Wind-pollinated crops** (corn, spinach, beets): Require isolation of
 400 metres to 2000 metres. Corn pollen is light and can travel great
  distances on wind.
