# Mushroom Growing Structures

## Overview

B.M. Duggar's *Mushroom Growing* (1915) describes several types of structures
and environments suitable [[blesching-cannabis-fever-temperature-regulation]] a central
concern across all facility types.

## The Mushroom House

### Temporary Structures

For temporary or small-scale use, a mushroom house may be nothing more than a
simple boarded shed or an unused barn. A heater is essential since winter is
the critical season for mushroom work. Duggar notes that many commercial
houses which pay well are not particularly elaborate in their construction.

### Permanent Structures

As a permanent investment, the house should be built with careful regard for
convenience, durability, and economy. Anyone undertaking the work on an
extensive scale should inspect a successful plant before building. Perfect
drainage is described as a factor of the first importance.

It is often convenient to construct the house over an excavation at least
several feet below the natural surface. This partial below-grade placement
aids in heating efficiency and promotes more uniform temperatures during
warmer seasons. However, provision must be made for convenient unloading of
spent compost and cleaning out old beds.

## Construction Materials and Insulation

The better class of mushroom houses feature double walls packed with fresh
shavings for insulation. More expensive options include hollow tile or
concrete construction. Hollow tile has much to recommend it where the
outlay is justified by the scale of the operation.

The interior of a mushroom house always carries a moist atmosphere. Contact
between moisture-laden air and cold exterior walls produces condensation
known as "sweating." A sloping roof with an intervening air space between
the inner and outer surfaces corrects this problem.

## Bed Construction and Tier Systems

The greatest economy of space is achieved by constructing houses tall enough
to accommodate beds arranged in tiers of two to five levels. Approximately
two to two and a half feet of free space should be allowed between tiers,
with beds at least eight to ten inches deep and more than three feet of
total height allocated per bed level.

Upright supports for beds arranged in four or five tiers should consist of
scantling not less than two by six inches, placed about four feet apart.
Experienced commercial growers often use large gas piping for supports,
which offers the advantages of great permanency and superior sanitation.

Wall beds are seldom more than three feet across, while beds approachable
from either side may be up to six feet across. Ample aisle space of about
three feet is usually sufficient, with one or more wider aisles provided
in very large houses to facilitate handling materials.

## Caves and Cellars

When beds are prepared in caves, cellars, or simply on the floors of
existing buildings, preparation is relatively simple. Caves are particularly
well-suited to mushroom growing because they naturally maintain uniform
[[pf-tek-dual-chambered-terrarium-design-humidity-management]].

## Open Air Culture

### English Practice

In Southern England, winter temperature extremes are moderate enough to
permit [[cotter-outdoor-mushroom-garden-mulch-beds]] culture in modified hot beds or cold frames. Simple
culture frames of the usual type are employed, except that glass is omitted
— the beds are covered instead with sheet iron or boards, with straw added
for additional insulation when necessary. Fresh manure placed beneath the
compost supplies bottom heat to force early spawn growth.

Duggar observed successful open-air beds in late autumn and winter in
Southern England. These likely required deeper beds with fresh manure at the
bottom for sustained bottom heat, mulching with clean straw, and cloth
covers for protection.

### Limitations and Regional Suitability

Open-air culture cannot be regarded as having great commercial possibilities
in most regions. The difficulties are considerable: [[agaricus-arvensis]]* have been found in California during
winter months, suggesting natural suitability.

## Temperature Requirements

The ideal mean temperature for mushroom growing is rather below than above
the optimal range, at approximately 48 to 56 degrees Fahrenheit. This
preference for cooler conditions makes winter the primary [[extending-growing-season]]
and informs the design of all mushroom structures, from heated houses to
insulated caves. Open-air beds must be carefully protected from drenching
rains that could flood beds and lower temperatures below productive levels.

## Ventilation and Moisture Control

Proper ventilation is essential in enclosed mushroom houses. It is usually
advisable to place ventilators in the roof, as this arrangement
accomplishes the necessary air exchange with less inconvenience from direct
drafts that could harm the developing mushrooms or dry out the beds.

Moisture control is intimately tied to ventilation and insulation. The
constant moist interior atmosphere, combined with cold exterior walls,
creates the condensation problem known as sweating. Proper wall insulation
with materials such as fresh shavings, combined with a sloping roof design
featuring an air gap, mitigates this issue while maintaining the high
humidity that mushrooms require.
