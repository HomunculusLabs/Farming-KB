## Hallucinogenic Plants Overview: Golden Guide

"A Golden Guide to Hallucinogenic Plants" (1976) by Richard Evans Schultes is a compact but encyclopedic survey of psychoactive flora used by indigenous peoples worldwide. The guide, illustrated with botanical drawings, covers over 100 species across five continents and traces the history of hallucinogenic plant use from prehistoric times through the 20th century. It remains one of the most accessible introductions to ethnopharmacology.

## Defining Hallucinogens

Schultes defines hallucinogens as **plant substances that produce sensory distortions -- visual, auditory, tactile, or olfactory -- often accompanied by alterations in mood, thought patterns, and sense of self**. Key distinctions made in the guide:

- **True hallucinations** are perceptions without external stimulus, directly caused by chemical action on the central nervous system
- **Pseudohallucinations** may result from non-chemical causes: fever, fasting, dehydration, sensory deprivation
- Effects are usually short-lived, lasting only as long as the active compound remains at its site of action

The guide distinguishes hallucinogens from other psychoactives: stimulants (caffeine, cocaine, amphetamines), depressants (alcohol, barbiturates), and narcotics (opium, morphine) -- though noting that many hallucinogenic plants have secondary stimulant or narcotic properties.

## Chemical Foundations

Hallucinogenic plants contain active principles from many chemical classes. The guide emphasizes that all share one property: they are **biodynamic** -- affecting normal metabolism of the animal body. Major chemical classes include:

- **Indole alkaloids**: Found in [[purple-ohms-argyreia-nervosa-legal-mushroom-substitutes]] (Hawaiian wood rose), [[agara-galbulimima-belgraveana-new-guinea-tree]], Gymnocranthera
- Africa: Tabernanthe iboga (ibogaine), Erythrina, Argyreia
- Europe: Amanita muscaria, Atropa belladonna, Hyoscyamus niger (henbane), [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]] (ergot), Peganum harmala

## Historical Overview

The guide traces hallucinogen use across millennia:

**Prehistoric**: Cave paintings suggest mushroom use 7,000-9,000 years ago. Shamanic use likely extends far back into prehistory.

**Ancient civilizations**: Soma in Vedic India; kykeon in Greek Eleusinian mysteries; peyote in North American prehistory; ergot in ancient Greece; Cannabis in Central Asia and China; Datura in both hemispheres.

**Medieval period**: European use of belladonna, henbane, mandrake, and ergot (St. Anthony's Fire); Islamic use of Cannabis (hashish); continued indigenous use in the Americas.

**Colonial era**: Spanish suppression of indigenous sacraments in Mexico; ethnobotanical documentation by early chroniclers.

**Modern era**: Wasson's 1955-1957 discovery of Mazatec mushroom use; Hofmann's identification of psilocybin (1958) and morning glory alkaloids (1960s); the psychedelic revolution; ethnopharmacological research.

## Patterns of Indigenous Use

Schultes identifies consistent patterns across cultures:

- **Divination**: Communicating with spirits, diagnosing illness, predicting the future (e.g., Virola snuff among Amazonian tribes, Calea zacatechichi among Chontal)
- **Healing and curing rituals**: Shamanic diagnosis through visions (e.g., Ayahuasca among Amazonian peoples)
- **Initiation rites**: Passage to adulthood (e.g., Datura/wysoccon among Algonquin)
- **Recreational/social**: Less common in traditional societies; more prevalent in acculturated contexts
- **Witchcraft and sorcery**: Both beneficial (healing) and harmful (causing illness) applications

## The Sibundoy Valley

Schultes gives special attention to the Sibundoy Valley in the Colombian Andes, calling it **"the most narcotic-conscious area of the New World."** This single valley is home to cultivated clones of highly atrophied Datura species (possibly virus-induced mutants), Methysticodendron amesianum (a tree unique to the valley, 80% scopolamine), and numerous other hallucinogenic species maintained by Kamsá and Inga medicine men.

## Psychopharmacology

The guide concludes with a section on psychopharmacology, noting:

- Some psychiatrists believe mental disorders result from chemical imbalance: "For every twisted thought, there is a twisted molecule"
- The concept of **model psychoses** -- artificially induced states similar to schizophrenia -- has historical precedent in indigenous healing practices
- Hallucinogens may have medical value far beyond their psychoactivity (e.g., scopolamine's antispasmodic properties)
- Alteration of CNS function by chemicals is older than written history

## Schultes's Legacy

Richard Evans Schultes (1915-2001) was the foremost ethnobotanist of the 20th century, spending over a decade in the Amazon studying indigenous plant use. The Golden Guide represents his effort to make this knowledge accessible to a general audience while maintaining scientific rigor. The bibliography reflects Schultes's encyclopedic knowledge, citing works from 1911 through 1973, including Louis Lewin's "Phantastica," Wasson's "Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality," Harner's "Hallucinogens and Shamanism," and Schultes's own "The Botany and Chemistry of Hallucinogens" with Albert Hofmann.
