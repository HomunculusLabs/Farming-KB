# Water Spreading and Infiltration Techniques

## Overview

In thinly-vegetated or overgrazed desert areas, the vast majority of rainwater runs off over the land rather than infiltrating into the soil. On bare hill country, less than 12% of rainfall is absorbed. Even modest rains create sudden, turbulent flood flows that sweep debris, silt, seed, and organic matter out onto the plains, where waters become increasingly saline as they travel. Water spreading techniques aim to halt these flows, divert them, and create conditions for infiltration while the water is still fresh enough to support plant growth.

## The Problem of Runoff in Drylands

### Water Loss Pathways

In Australia, 88% of rainfall runs off or is evaporated from land and water surfaces. Other hot deserts show similar losses, with only 12% of rainfall available to plants. The fate of rainwater in a degraded desert area follows a predictable and destructive pattern:

1. Rain falls (often as intense storms of 2.5 cm/day over several days)
2. Very little is absorbed on bare hillsides
3. Sudden turbulent floods develop in dry streambeds (wadis)
4. Large boulders, silt, sand, leaf litter, manures, seeds, and organic debris are swept onto plains
5. Waters spread across plains, becoming increasingly saline
6. Headwater rains eventually reach salt-pans where they may pool for months or years
7. Evaporation leaves salt-encrusted flats
8. Very little but ephemeral plant growth results

### The Value of Infiltration

If fresh water is infiltrated into the soil, it forms a soil reserve or shallow aquifer that travels very slowly underground. This effectively desalts the shallow water reserves, even though very salty deep leads flow below. Trees play a critical role by recycling infiltrated water through growth and transpiration, keeping deeper salty water below root level. When forests are cut, burned, and overgrazed, infiltration decreases and deep saline leads rise to the surface through flooding and bare-soil evaporation.

## Infiltration Structures

A range of structures help halt runoff and promote infiltration:

- **Tree lines**: Rows of trees that slow overland flow and transpire infiltrated water
- **Swales**: Dead-level contour hollows that create temporary pools for infiltration
- **Deep-ripped contours**: Mechanical breaking of compacted layers along contour to increase permeability
- **Silt dams**: Structures in valley floors that trap sediment and create growing media
- **Dams**: Larger water storage structures that also retard flood peaks
- **Stone lines**: Lines of stones placed across slope to slow and spread runoff
- **Rock palisades**: Natural rock arrangements that act as permeable barriers to flow

### The Critical Role of Trees

Trees are central to the infiltration system. They:

- Recycle water through transpiration, keeping salty deep water at bay
- Create soil structure through root penetration and organic matter addition
- Intercept rainfall in their canopy, reducing the kinetic energy of impacting drops
- Provide leaf litter that improves soil surface infiltration capacity
- Create microclimates that reduce evaporation

When desert trees are removed, the entire infiltration cycle is disrupted: less water enters the soil, more runs off, deep saline water rises, and the landscape progressively degrades.

## Runoff Percentages by Substrate

Understanding expected runoff from different surface conditions is essential for designing appropriate infiltration systems. At a typical rainy period of 10 mm/day in arid lands:

| % Runoff | Substrate |
|----------|-----------|
| 0% | Coarse sands, active dunes, fixed dunes in dune seas |
| 5% | Forested areas |
| 10% | Wooded and grassed areas, tussock grassland |
| 15% | Cultivated sands and sandy loams |
| 20% | Approximate average for semi-arid and arid areas |
| 25% | Ungrazed steppe of mixed shrubs, forbs, grasses |

Higher values occur on compacted, concreted, or clay-sealed bare sites (up to 80%).

## Factors Affecting Water Runoff

Beyond surface conditions, several important determinants affect how much water runs off versus infiltrates:

### Catchment Size

Small catchments discharge a greater peak flood flow than large catchments. Foothill areas peak to floods more rapidly than plains.

### Stream Gradient

Steep streams discharge more rapidly than downstream areas of gentle gradient. Headwater streams are usually steeper than the larger streams in plains, which are often sand-clogged with wide flood plains.

### Storm Size

Large storms may totally wet a small catchment, causing local floods, whereas the same storm has little downstream effect. In deserts, floods commonly disappear a few kilometres from the headwaters except during widespread rains.

### Storages and Retardation Basins

Dams, retention banks, interceptor banks, swales, natural swamps, and created wetlands all reduce or delay flood peaks. Local soil absorption is much more efficient in small streams of high density, but large catchments have greater dampening capacity when retardation areas are preserved.

### Catchment Form

Normal dendritic (tree-like) drainage patterns, where branches join at small angles, are subject to faster flooding than fold-mountain or fault-area grid catchments with their abrupt turns, constricted valleys, and relatively long runs of middle-order streams.

### Drainage Density

The actual length of stream per square kilometre determines how efficiently water is removed. Very dense streamlines create higher and faster flood peaks. Sheet flow across flat interfluves, or braided broad streams of flat desert areas, retards floodwaters.

### Vegetation Cover

Runoff increases as vegetation is cleared. Less crown interception, [[humus]] storage, and ground interference by rain means more water runs off. The transition from shrub cover to tussock grass, and from grass to bare soil, progressively increases runoff percentages.

## Precipitation Intensity Categories

| mm/day | Phenomena |
|--------|-----------|
| 0–10 | Light dews, light semi-arid rains, prairie rains. Smallest rills begin flowing at 6–10 mm |
| 11–20 | All-day drizzle, sheet erosion can occur at 13 mm, gully erosion develops at 15 mm |
| 21–50 | Monsoon cloud streets on coasts, light rain all day in maritime climates, convectional desert rains |
| 51–100 | Condensation drip from onshore fogs (~60 mm), tropical rains exceed 100 mm once yearly |
| 100–200+ | Heavy rain all day, exceeded once yearly on monsoon coasts, once per century in temperate maritime areas |

## Design Implications for Settlement

### Flash Flood Risk

Rain in deserts (falling as 2.5 cm/day for 2–3 days) produces severe local flooding. This becomes critical when settlements are located in wadis, upstream of restricted gaps in hills, or in hollows near dry watercourses. Oversize channels, drains, infiltration, and diversion systems are needed to cope with high-intensity storms.

### Freshwater-Saltwater Interface

A general profile of water depth and quality from hill intake to salt pan decides the strategies for settlement placement, water systems, water quality, and water disposal. Wells and windmills should be located close to hills in the loose sediments of alluvial fans, where freshwater is most accessible.

### Strategic Water Management

The proportion of water directed to different uses should be planned:

- **Drinking water**: Roof catchment to sealed tanks (highest quality)
- **Garden water**: Tank overflow to swales, supplemented by household greywater
- **Landscape water**: Interceptor banks harvesting overland flow from surrounding areas
- **Aquifer recharge**: Widespread infiltration structures maintaining soil water reserves
