## Permaculture Design Methods

Permaculture offers several structured design frameworks that guide practitioners from initial observation through to implementation and ongoing management. Each method has its strengths, and many designers combine elements from multiple approaches.

## SADIM

SADIM (Survey, Analyse, Design, Implement, Maintain) is one of the most widely taught permaculture design frameworks. It provides a linear yet iterative process.

### Survey
Gather all relevant information about the site and the client. This includes:
- Physical site characteristics (see [[permaculture-site-analysis]])
- Climate data, historical weather patterns
- Existing infrastructure, access, utilities
- Client goals, values, budget, timeline
- Legal constraints (zoning, easements, water rights)

### Analyse
Interpret the survey data to identify opportunities and constraints. Create maps, diagrams, and lists. Identify:
- Sector energies (sun, wind, water, fire)
- Zone placement logic
- Soil and water conditions
- Existing resources to work with

### Design
Create the design using the analysis. Develop concept plans, detailed planting plans, and implementation schedules. Apply permaculture principles from [[permaculture-ethics-and-principles]] throughout.

### Implement
Carry out the design in phases. Prioritize earthworks and water systems first, then infrastructure, then planting. Work within available resources and adjust as conditions change.

### Maintain
Monitor the system over time. Observe how elements interact, note successes and problems, and adapt. Maintenance is ongoing — permaculture systems are dynamic, not static.

## OBREDIMET

OBREDIMET (Observe, Boundaries, Resources, Evaluate, Design, Implement, Maintain, Evaluate and Tweak) is a more detailed framework developed by some UK-based permaculture teachers.

### Observe
Extended observation of the site across seasons. Minimum one full year of observation is ideal before major earthworks or planting.

### Boundaries
Define physical, legal, financial, temporal, and personal boundaries. What are the limits of the project? What cannot be changed?

### Resources
Inventory all available resources: on-site materials, skills, community connections, financial resources, time, and energy. Distinguish between existing, available, and needed resources.

### Design
Develop multiple design options. Evaluate each against the ethics and principles. Select the best fit and develop detailed plans.

### Implement
Execute the design in priority order. Document the process — what was planted where, when, and why. Keep records for future evaluation.

### Maintain
Regular care of the system: pruning, mulching, harvesting, feeding animals, managing water systems.

### Evaluate and Tweak
Periodically review the system's performance. Are yields meeting expectations? Are problems emerging? Adjust the design based on feedback. This closes the loop and connects back to observation.

## Random Assembly

Random assembly is a creative design tool rather than a full design process. It helps break out of conventional thinking and discover unexpected but functional combinations.

### Process
1. Create a comprehensive list of all elements needed or desired in the design (plants, animals, structures, functions)
2. Write each element on a card or slip of paper
3. Randomly draw groups of 3-5 elements
4. For each random combination, brainstorm how they could be connected beneficially
5. Record promising connections and integrate them into the design

### Example
Random draw: apple tree, chickens, comfrey, rain tank, clover. The designer might realize that chickens can manage the orchard floor, comfrey accumulates potassium for the apples, the rain tank irrigates the clover understory, and clover fixes nitrogen for the whole guild. This becomes a coherent [[permaculture-guild-design]].

## Flow Diagrams

Flow diagrams map the movement of energy, materials, and resources through a system. They reveal waste streams, missing connections, and opportunities for integration.

### Creating Flow Diagrams
1. List all elements in the system
2. Identify outputs (yields, waste, byproducts) of each element
3. Identify inputs (needs, resources consumed) of each element
4. Connect outputs to inputs wherever possible — "every output is an input"
5. Identify unconnected outputs (waste) and unmet inputs (needs requiring external resources)

### Value in Design
Flow diagrams expose inefficiencies and integration opportunities. For example, connecting chicken manure output to compost input, or connecting [[permaculture-food-waste-cycling]] outputs to poultry feed inputs. They also reveal over-reliance on external inputs that could be replaced by on-site cycling.

## Concept Mapping

Concept maps (or mind maps) are visual tools for organizing ideas and relationships. Start with a central concept and branch out into subtopics, connecting related ideas with lines. Useful for:
- Brainstorming early in the design process
- Organizing research and observations
- Communicating design logic to clients or collaborators

## Design Process Tips

### Start with Water
Water is almost always the first priority. Get water management right and the rest of the design follows more easily. See [[water-management]] for detailed strategies.

### Earthworks Before Plants
Install swales, ponds, terraces, and access roads before planting. Moving earth around established root systems damages plants and soil.

### Design for Evolution
Permaculture systems mature over time. Design the initial layout to support the desired future state. Plan succession pathways so pioneer species prepare conditions for climax communities.

### Document Everything
Keep a design journal, photograph progress, and record observations. Documentation enables evaluation, adaptation, and sharing with others. It also helps with [[permaculture-seed-saving-networks]] and community knowledge exchange.

### Use Multiple Methods
No single framework covers every situation. Use SADIM for structure, random assembly for creativity, flow diagrams for integration analysis, and concept mapping for organizing complex ideas. The best design process is the one that works for the specific site, client, and context.
