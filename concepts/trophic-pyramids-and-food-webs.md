# [[mollison-trophic-pyramids-food-webs-and-energy-cycling]] [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] Webs

The **trophic pyramid** is a conceptual model used to illustrate energy flow [[query-how-do-i-use-knf-inputs-through-the-plant-growth-cycle]] ecosystems, showing how much forage is needed to sustain animals at each successive feeding level. While the pyramid is a useful teaching tool, [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]] argues that it is overly simplistic and largely inapplicable to real-world field conditions, where complex food webs and feedback loops dominate.

## The Pyramid Model

In its standard form, the trophic pyramid depicts a hierarchy: vegetation at the base (trophic level 1) supports herbivores (level 2), which support carnivores (level 3), which in turn support top predators (level 4). At each level, roughly 90% of energy is "lost" to metabolism and waste, leaving only about 10% available to the next level.

This model is often invoked to argue that vegetarian diets are inherently more efficient — a claim Mollison examines critically.

## Why Pyramids Fail to Describe Reality

### 1. Nature Is Far More Complex

Real ecosystems do not operate in simple "trophic levels." They exhibit complex interactions among species, governed not merely by food habits but by pasture management, seasonal availability, and spatial dynamics. This complex reality is better described as a **food web** — the normal condition in field situations.

### 2. Pyramids Ignore Feedback

Vegetation eventually "eats" grasshoppers, frogs, fish, and people. As animals grow, they return nutrients to the soil through excreted, moulted, or discarded body wastes. Even if a frog eats 10 kg of grasshoppers to make 1 kg of frog, it excretes 9 kg or more back to earth as manure, stimulating more vegetation growth and producing more grasshoppers.

The manure from insect "pests" may be the basis of regenerative future evolution. With these feedbacks, the web resembles less a one-way staircase than a series of **cyclic events** — less a ziggurat and more a spider's web.

### 3. The Question of Maturity

A mature organism no longer uses food for growth but for maintenance. An 80-year-old carp at 10 kg has eaten vast quantities over its lifetime but now returns digested material to the pond rather than converting it to biomass. Old organisms become **constant recyclers** (food in, waste out) and cease to grow, or even begin to lose weight.

This is why sustainable harvesting targets young, growing plants and animals when food is scarce — with the exception of fruit and [[query-what-are-the-best-nut-trees-for-a-permaculture-food-forest]], where we consume seed or fruit (an immature tree).

### 4. Food Chains Are Not Simple

People normally eat vegetation, but also eat grasshoppers, frogs, fish, and other animals. Even a cow eats grasshoppers incidentally while grazing, and every eater ingests large quantities of bacteria and small animals living on vegetation. Strict trophic separation does not exist in nature.

## The Vegetarianism Debate

The pyramid is often used to argue that everyone should become vegetarian. Mollison acknowledges this has merit but identifies real-world complications:

### Efficient Vegetarianism

Vegetarian diets are genuinely efficient **only when**:
- Based on easily cooked or processed crops grown in home gardens
- Wastes — especially body wastes — are returned to garden soils
- People eat from where they live, avoiding exploitation and transport costs

### Problems with Industrial Vegetarianism

In the urban Western world, vegetarianism relies heavily on grains and grain legumes (especially soybeans), which:
- Require enormous quantities of wood and fossil fuels to cook
- Are controlled by a few multinational corporations owning patent rights
- Are grown in large monocultural operations on rich bottomland soils
- Caused more deforestation in the USA and Brazil (1980–82) than any other crop
- Resulted in about 70% of beans being fed to pigs or used in industrial paint production

Grains and grain legumes account for most soil erosion in agricultural regions worldwide, and much of what is eaten in the West is grown in areas where real famine threatens (mung beans from India, chickpeas from Ethiopia, soybeans from Africa).

## The Role of Animals in Sustainable Systems

Animals serve a critical function in converting otherwise unusable vegetation into acceptable human food. Deer, rabbits, sheep, and herbivorous fish transform inedible herbage into edible protein. Animals represent a valid method of **storing inedible vegetation as food**.

Old farmers had a saying: "We will sell nothing from our farm that will not walk or fly off." Animals were the only traditional recycling strategy for a sustainable export market — returning nutrients to the soil while producing saleable products.

## Dietary Guidelines from Permaculture

Mollison offers four principles for responsible diets:

1. **Vegetarian diets** are very efficient when based on home-grown produce with waste returned to soil
2. **Omnivorous diets** make the best use of complex natural systems — eating from whatever is edible at any level
3. **Carnivorous diets** have valid places in cold [[query-best-nut-trees-for-a-permaculture-food-forest-by-climate-zone]]s, coastal communities, and where animals convert waste products
4. **Energy budgets** are essential — those using flush toilets and importing all food have already broken the nutrient cycle

## Toxin Concentration

The trophic pyramid remains valid in one important respect: poisons at the base concentrate at the top. The highest levels of some radioactives and DDT have been measured in mothers' milk. This underscores why eating lower on the food chain can reduce toxin exposure.

However, the omnivorous (non-selective) feeder is buffered from catastrophic famine by a complex web of trophic connections — some losses and some gains accrue, providing resilience that strict dietary specialisation cannot match.

## Design Implications

For [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]]ers, these insights suggest:

- **Think in webs and cycles**, not pyramids — design for feedback loops, not linear flows
- **Value animals as nutrient recyclers**, not just protein producers
- **Close nutrient loops** by returning all wastes to productive soil
- **Grow food locally** rather than importing from regions facing famine
- **Maintain dietary flexibility** — omnivorism adapted to local conditions is more resilient than strict dietary ideology
- **Consider toxin accumulation** when designing food systems, especially in polluted environments
