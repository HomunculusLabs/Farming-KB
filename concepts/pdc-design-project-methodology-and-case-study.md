# PDC Design Project Methodology and Case Study Approach

The Williams PDC culminates in a comprehensive design project that serves as the capstone assessment. This project requires students to apply every element of the course to a real site with a real client, moving from analysis through design to presentation. The methodology mirrors professional [[permaculture-design]] practice.

## The Design Project as Capstone

Williams structures the PDC so that all learning exercises, readings, and discussions build toward the final design project. The project is not theoretical -- it requires an actual client and an actual site. The best way to find clients, Williams advises, is to be part of a community and join your local permaculture guild.

The design project demonstrates that the student can:
- Perform comprehensive site analysis
- Conduct an effective client interview
- Apply [[permaculture-principles]] to site-specific conditions
- Integrate multiple design elements into a coherent whole
- Communicate complex design ideas clearly to a client and audience

## Team Assembly and Organization

Design projects are conducted in teams, mirroring professional design practice. [[composting]]utlines specific guidance for team composition and organization.

### Team Composition

Teams should include people with expertise or interest in:
- Animal systems
- [[appropriate-technology]]
- Community organization
- Composting
- Earthworks
- Electrical systems
- Food systems and gardening
- Forestry and trees
- Hydrology and water
- Invisible structures (economics, governance)
- Passive design
- Plant knowledge
- Soils
- Structures and building
- Water treatment

Not every team member needs a PDC, but they should have passion and willingness to research. Each member becomes a "Design Steward" responsible for specific elements.

### Organizational Structure

The team uses the Organizational Mandala from the community section, adapted for design work:
- Each team member is a "lobe" of the mandala
- Sub-circles within each lobe represent specific tasks
- All tasks must be assigned -- nothing can be left uncovered
- A central coordinator may track progress, or the team may use self-organizing trust

Each Design Steward receives the working base map and client interview, then takes responsibility for both the analytical research and the design development of their assigned elements.

## Case Study Method: Learning from Existing Sites

Williams integrates case studies throughout the PDC, drawing from real projects to illustrate design principles. The case study approach follows a specific methodology:

### Site Selection for Case Studies

Effective case studies should represent diverse conditions:
- **Climatic diversity**: arid, temperate, and tropical examples
- **Scale diversity**: urban lots, suburban yards, rural acreage, and community-scale projects
- **Stage diversity**: new installations, established systems, and mature food forests

Williams references several real-world examples:
- **Ecosa Institute student projects** in Prescott, Arizona, including the "Living Cities: Prescott 2035" design competition entry featuring sustainable human-made hydro-cycles for homes
- **Cougar Mountain** student design by Inger-Lise Burns
- **Lost Valley Educational Center** and its land steward Rick Valley's approach to design
- **Cody Lundin's property** near Ashfork, Arizona, demonstrating integrated appropriate technology

### Case Study Analysis Framework

When analyzing any case study, students should examine:
1. **Context**: What climate, watershed, bioregion, and community does the site exist within?
2. **History**: What patterns of human use and ecological succession preceded the current design?
3. **Analysis**: What sectors, zones, soil conditions, water flows, and microclimates were identified?
4. **Design decisions**: What elements were chosen, where were they placed, and why?
5. **Integration**: How do the design elements connect to and support each other?
6. **Results**: What has worked well? What has needed adaptation? What would the designer change?

### Student Project as Case Study

Williams treats each student design project itself as a future case study. He advises students that doing a full permaculture analysis of their county produces reusable data for dozens of future projects. Similarly, the detailed analysis done for the capstone project becomes reference material for subsequent professional work.

## The Analysis Phase: Building the Design Foundation

The analysis phase is the most critical part of the design project. Williams emphasizes that no design decisions should be made before analysis is complete. The analytical tools used in the capstone project are:

### Base Map
Obtained from county/city offices or created via Google Earth. An original is kept clean, with working copies for overlays. Analytical data goes on tracing paper (single sheet preferred) or transparencies (organized by related data categories).

### Broad Scale Site Analysis
Students analyze from global to site-specific scale, examining the site's position within its hemisphere, continent, country, region, bioregion, watershed, county, and immediate neighborhood.

### Client Interview
A structured questionnaire covering: permaculture knowledge level, succession preferences, health needs, dietary preferences, physical/mental limitations, budget and timeline, vision and mission statement, hands-on involvement desire, number of people served, social habits, and any additional considerations.

### General Climate Data
Solar altitude and azimuth, prevailing seasonal winds, flood plains, average temperatures, average annual rainfall, and microclimate identification.

### Site History
Research into historical land use, previous crops, design precedents, and major climactic events. The goal is to tell "the story of the site" and understand temporal patterns.

### Indicator Species
Observation of existing plant and animal indicator species that reveal soil conditions, water availability, and ecological health.

### Inventories
Comprehensive lists of existing resources: plants, animals, building materials, salvageable materials, water sources, available labor, and community resources.

### Observation and Interaction
Multiple site visits under different conditions -- different times of day, weather conditions, and seasons. Camping on site is recommended. Small experimental interventions are encouraged to test site responses.

### Sectors
Graphical analysis of forces flowing across the site: fire hazard direction, summer/winter wind sectors, noise sources, summer/winter solar azimuth, desirable and undesirable views.

### Soil Samples
Systematic sampling across the site using the jar test method, with infiltration rate testing at each sample location. Results are mapped onto the base map.

### Surface Water Flow
Charting of water movement across the site based on topography, determining earthworks needed to slow, spread, and sink all rainfall on-site.

### Zones
Mapping of human impact intensity across the site from Zone 0 (permanent structures) through Zone 5 (true wilderness), recognizing that urban sites may only contain Zones 0-3.

### Zoning Ordinances
Checking with city/county for legal restrictions on what can be done on the site.

## The Charette Process: Collaborative Design Development

The charette is the brainstorming and design development phase. Williams outlines a multi-round charette process:

### First Charette
- All team members share analysis results
- Team produces an agreed-upon composite base map
- Each member receives copies of the base map and client interview
- Group brainstorming on overall design direction

### Independent Research Phase
- Each Design Steward researches their specific elements
- Finding appropriate plants, researching design precedents, locating analogous structures
- Team reconvenes to share findings and align on element choices
