# Fodder Pond Sequences and Polyculture Chains

[[fodder-pond-sequences]] are cascading series of interconnected ponds designed so that organisms and nutrients flow naturally from one pond to the next, with upstream ponds producing food for downstream ponds through trophic transfer. This design eliminates the need for purchased feed in extensive aquaculture systems by creating self-feeding polyculture chains where each pond stage supports the next.

## The Sequential Flow Principle

As water falls through a pipe from each small pond to the next, upstream migration of species is prevented while downstream migration is possible or even aided. When ponds are arranged along spoon or V-drains, multiple origins and destinations can be achieved. Forage fish and invertebrates always migrate downstream, and a perched pond above the trout or predator system can maintain a trout-free forage-fish polyculture.

The criteria for selecting upstream species are simple: plants must be non-invasive, and animals must be low on the trophic ladder. This ensures that food organisms flow toward predators without competitive displacement.

## The Four-Stage Pond Sequence

### Stage A — Origin Pond
The intake pond establishes the base of the food chain:

- **Animals:** Manurial species such as ducks (which add nutrient-rich manure), freshwater mussels, amphipods, mud shrimp (*phreatocids*), small freshwater crabs (*Halicarcinus*), snails, shrimp (*Atya*, *Macrobrachium*), and frog larvae (*Hyla*, *Rana*, *Crinea*).
- **Plants:** Non-flowering or non-invasive species such as taro, plus manurial species like *[[azolla]]* (a nitrogen-fixing floating fern that provides both food and surface cover).
- **Structures:** Rotted logs, reed beds, brush, and small cover elements. Taro or other useful crops can be planted in any downstream pond as well.
- **Edges:** [[comfrey]], vining legumes, and fruit trees.

The duck manure stimulates algal and plankton growth, which feeds shrimp. Some shrimp larvae escape to the next pond, establishing the downstream food chain.

### Stage B — Secondary Forage Pond
The next pond downstream receives organisms and nutrients from Stage A:

- **Animals:** All Stage A species plus more predatory invertebrates and very small planktonic-feeding fish such as minnows, *Paragalaxids*, and bottom-dwelling *Saxilaga*.
- **Plants:** Useful edible species such as kangkong (water spinach) grown on mounds.
- **Structures:** Reed beds, small pipes, and logs for habitat complexity.
- **Edges:** Mulberry, berry fruit, legumes, and comfrey.

### Stages C and D — Predator Ponds
These follow the same general sequence as Stage B but culminate in the final outlet pond where trout, perch, or other predatory fish consume the forage organisms that have migrated or been washed downstream. Products can be harvested at any level — shrimp, snails, ducks, frogs, taro, [[tilapia]], perch, or trout.

## Yield Potential by System Intensity

| kg/ha/year | Conditions |
|---|---|
| 10–50 | Deep, cold waters with few shallows |
| 30–60 | Deep artificial reservoirs |
| 60–90 | Reservoirs modified for fish and forage culture |
| 80–150 | Unfertilised brackish lagoon cultures; modified rice paddy |
| 200–500 | Fertilised extensive cultures of 1–3 species (fertiliser 25–50% of costs) |
| 500–1,000 | Upper range of totally natural systems |
| 1,000–2,000 | Well-chosen unfed polycultures |
| 2,000–5,000 | Intensively fertilised and fed polycultures (food 60%+ of costs) |
| 5,000–20,000 | Intensive cage cultures or small aerated ponds (food 96% of costs) |

Well-chosen and unfed polycultures can achieve yields of 1,000–2,000 kg/ha/year, representing the upper range of natural systems without any purchased feed input.

## Strategies for Increasing Water Diversity

Three strategies increase the diversity of small still-pond, marsh, or perched pond systems:

1. **Locate ponds at headwater and ridge positions** to create small ecological islands that attract colonising species.
2. **Salt small ponds** to develop semi-estuarine species assemblages that would not otherwise be present inland.
3. **Manure small ponds and marshes** with [[soil-micronutrients-sulphur-potassium-calcium-trace-elements]], animal manures, and phosphatic or nitrogenous fertilisers to produce large quantities of forage fish, algae, or crustaceans for downstream predator species.

Such systems can accept water polluted by phosphates and nitrates as part of their intake, provided plants and organisms can be found to tolerate that pollutant level — effectively turning nutrient pollution into productive aquaculture.

## Polyculture Synergies

### Grass Carp and Prawns
Grass carp neatly trim fringing vegetation such as *Dolichos* species, preventing weed encroachment into taro. They mix well with *Macrobrachium* prawns and actually increase prawn yields without artificial feeding. The prawns grow as well on grass carp fecal detritus as on chicken pellets in nearby monoculture ponds, while the grass carp maintain neatly-trimmed pond edges — eliminating the need for mechanical mowing.

### Tilapia and Garden Integration
Tilapia eat many fruits and edible leaves from [[gaias-garden-weeds-pioneer-plants]] and vines, making market gardens and fish ponds natural companions. Pond water is nutrient-rich and benefits gardens as irrigation, doubling the relationship's productivity. [[latitude-effects-on-photosynthesis-and-crop-production]] from well-fertilised ponds may be twice that from reservoir irrigation alone.

### Edge Planting for Fish Fodder
Semi-aquatic and bank plants including Nasturtium, Tradescantia, *Dolichos*, and comfrey supply hardy, palatable food to herbivorous fish. In channel cultures, banks planted with these species become a complete food supply. Creating the conditions for yield at the pond itself — through edge planting, marginal vegetation, and invertebrate culture — is always preferable to importing food from elsewhere at great energy cost.

## The Shallow Water Proportion

Research (Swingle, 1966) proposes that up to 50% of a catfish pond can be devoted to shallows. These shallow areas provide food (primarily shrimp and invertebrates) for the main fish species, not at a cost of reducing fish numbers, but rather at a gain in the [[aquaculture-design-principles-and-energy-efficiency]] needed to feed them. This principle generalises across species and is a key insight for designing productive polyculture systems.
