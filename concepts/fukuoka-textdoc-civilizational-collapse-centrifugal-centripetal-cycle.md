# Fukuoka's Theory of Civilizational Collapse and the Centrifugal-Centripetal Cycle

In the closing sections of *The Natural Way of Farming*, Masanobu Fukuoka presents a sweeping theory of civilizational development and collapse, rooted in his observations of natural cycles. He argues that human civilization follows a destructive pattern of outward expansion that must inevitably rupture, reverse, and contract, and that natural farming offers the only viable path through the coming crisis.

## The Linear Illusion

Fukuoka begins by challenging the common assumption that humanity will continue advancing without end. People seem to think that, although reality is rife with contradiction, development will continue forever in a process of sublation, wandering between thesis, antithesis, and synthesis.

Yet the universe does not advance along a linear or planar path. It expands and grows volumetrically outward and must, at the furthest limit, rupture, split, collapse, and disappear. But at a point beyond this limit, what should have vanished reverses its course and reappears, now moving centripetally inward, contracting and condensing. What has form vaporizes at the limits of development to a void, and the void condenses into a form and reappears, in a never-ending cycle of contraction and expansion.

Fukuoka likens this pattern of development to the Wheel of Dharma or a cyclone because it is a pattern of circular, three-dimensional motion rather than linear progress.

## The Three-Stage Collapse Pattern

Fukuoka describes a three-stage pattern of civilizational development and decline:

### Stage One: Centrifugal Expansion
Civilization expands outward like a cyclone. Science, technology, industry, and agriculture all grow in scale and complexity. Populations increase, cities expand, and human activity spreads across the globe. This is the age of outward motion, where humanity moves away from nature and toward increasing artificiality. Everything human endeavor produces grows larger, faster, and more powerful.

### Stage Two: Rupture and Collapse
At the furthest limit of expansion, what should have vanished reverses its course. What has form vaporizes at the limits of development to a void. The economic system falters, institutions break down, and the social order fragments. Fukuoka sees this as already beginning in his time, with the pollution crisis, food quality decline, and spiritual emptiness of modern society serving as early symptoms of the larger collapse.

### Stage Three: Centripetal Contraction
The void condenses into form and reappears, now moving centripetally inward, contracting and condensing. Humanity must return to simpler, more natural ways of living. This contraction is not regression but a necessary return to the source. The pattern repeats eternally, expansion giving way to contraction which gives way to expansion once more.

## Agriculture as the Primary Vector of Collapse

Fukuoka identifies agriculture as the primary driver of the civilizational expansion that leads to collapse. The moment humans began cultivating crops, they separated themselves from nature. This separation expanded through:

- The development of fertilizers and tillage, which destroyed soil ecology and set in motion processes of erosion and depletion.
- The application of pesticides, which devastated insect populations and microbial communities that had maintained natural balance for millennia.
- The mechanization of farming, which consumed vast amounts of energy and alienated farmers from the land they worked.
- The industrialization of food processing, which stripped food of its nutritional and spiritual value, replacing it with artificial products that merely converted input materials into saleable goods.

Each of these developments represents a centrifugal movement away from the natural order, and each carries the seeds of its own reversal.

## The Pollution Crisis as Symptom

Fukuoka interprets environmental pollution not as a problem to be solved through better technology but as a symptom of the collapse phase. The fact that there are no non-polluting pesticides is not a failure of chemistry but a fundamental impossibility. Any substance that acts on one organism also acts, to a greater or lesser degree, on all others. Animal cells, plant cells, and microorganisms are basically all quite similar. When a pesticide that works on some insect is said to be harmless to plants and animals, this is merely a word game that plays on a very minor difference in resistance.

The attitude of ready acceptance toward technologies that pose no immediate threat but may have greater repercussions complicates the problem and aggravates the dangers. People are satisfied if a substance poses no immediate threat and do not bother to consider the greater repercussions of its effects. This pattern repeats across all domains of [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]]. The aerial spraying of mountain forests with herbicides, pesticides, and [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] is considered a success if a given weed or insect pest is selectively killed, but this is a grave error that can prove most dangerous. Natural conservationists have already recognized such practices as polluting.

## The Problem of Scale

Fukuoka notes that [[fukuoka-illusions-scientific-farming-centrifugal-centripetal-model]]'s achievements are always temporary because they operate on a limited spatial and temporal frame of reference. High-volume [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-livestock-farming-open-grazing]] appears efficient, but this is a nearsighted assessment. The pitiful sight of fowl, pigs, and cattle confined to cages and unable even to move bears witness to the loss of nature of these animals and points also to man's alienation from nature.

Both the farm worker engaged in raising livestock and the city dweller who consumes these products lose their health and humanity as they turn away from nature. Livestock production under factory-like conditions is generally thought to be efficient, but this is a nearsighted assessment based on a limited spatial and temporal frame of reference. Today's livestock, reduced to standardized machinery, no longer receives the strength and grace of nature.

## Natural Farming as the Path Through Collapse

Fukuoka proposes natural farming not as a reform of modern agriculture but as an alternative civilization. He distinguishes between:

**Laissez-faire**, which is the abandoning of nature by man after he has altered it. Leaving a pine tree untended after transplanting and pruning, or suddenly letting a calf out to pasture after raising it on formula milk, is not natural farming but abandonment.

**Hinayana natural farming**, which approaches closest to nature. It begins by examining and learning from a laissez-faire situation, observing nature that has been abandoned by man to make out the true form of nature revealed when the effects of human action are removed.

**Mahayana natural farming**, which is true natural farming. It requires tearing the robes of human action from nature and removing the innermost garments of subjectivity. One must beware also of arbitrarily settling upon causal relationships on the basis of subjective human notions. This is nearly impossible to achieve with crops and domestic animals that have already been altered by human hands.

## The Quarter-Acre Vision

Fukuoka's practical response to civilizational collapse is the quarter-acre self-sufficient farm. On a small plot of land, a family can grow rice, barley, vegetables, and fruit while raising chickens and livestock. This is not a retreat from civilization but a model for a decentralized, sustainable society that works with nature rather than against it.

One could fence off the entrance to one of those depopulated mountain villages that have lost their inhabitants to the cities and create a large, open grazing range for animals. All sorts of domestic animals could be placed inside the enclosure and left to themselves for a number of years, after which we could go in and see what had happened.

## The Difference Between Natural Farming and Retreat

Fukuoka was careful to distinguish his vision from simple nostalgia or anti-technology sentiment. He was not advocating a return to primitive conditions or the rejection of all knowledge. Rather, he argued that the direction of civilization must shift from centrifugal expansion to centripetal contraction. This does not mean abandoning all technology but reorienting it toward harmony with nature.

The key insight is that the same knowledge that enables centrifugal expansion, when applied with a different consciousness, can support centripetal contraction. Understanding soil ecology, for example, can be used either to exploit the soil more intensively or to restore its [[fukuoka-natural-fertility-cycle]]. The difference lies not in the knowledge itself but in the intention and consciousness behind its application.
