# Fungal Biosorption of Metals and Bioremediation Potential

## Fungi as Geomicrobiological Agents

Fungi are significant [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-aerobic-and-anaerobic-composting]] conditions — there is increasing evidence that fungi outperform bacteria in mineral dissolution under many environmental conditions.

Their filamentous growth habit, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, and ability to translocate nutrients within mycelial networks make them well suited to geomicrobiological roles. Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the Ordovician period (~480–460 MYBP), inhabiting deserts, metal-rich habitats, and hypersaline environments. They exhibit considerable tolerance of extreme conditions — metal toxicity, desiccation, UV radiation — and can adopt diverse growth, metabolic, and morphological strategies.

Some fungi are polymorphic, occurring as filamentous mycelium and unicellular yeasts or yeast-like cells, as seen in the black meristematic rock-dwelling fungi (microcolonial fungi). Oligotrophic fungi can scavenge nutrients from air and rainwater, enabling growth on rock surfaces using organic and inorganic residues, waste products of other microorganisms, dust particles, and aerosols.

Rock-dwelling microorganisms are classified as epilithic (on surfaces), hypolithic (associated with pebbles), endolithic (within rock subsurface), chasmolithic (in pre-existing cracks), cryptoendolithic (inside cavities), or euendolithic (actively boring into rock). Fungi have been found in many rock types — limestone, soapstone, marble, granite, sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite, and quartz — and are likely ubiquitous components of the microflora of all rocks across a wide range of geographical and climatic zones.

Key traits enabling fungal geomicrobiological activity include exudation of protons and metal-complexing metabolites, formation of hydrated mucilaginous sheaths around hyphae (providing an interconnected microenvironment within the fungal network), and the capacity to form mutualistic symbiotic associations with plants, algae, and cyanobacteria. Most fungi are filamentous, granting the ability to increase or decrease surface area, switch between exploration and exploitation strategies, and form linear organs of aggregated hyphae (rhizomorphs) for protected translocation across heterogeneous environments. This ability to translocate nutrients is critical for exploiting spatially separated resources in soil.

Fungi are also well suited as weathering agents because they can exude protons and metal-complexing metabolites, and because their biomechanical and biochemical weathering mechanisms operate synergistically. Biomechanical weathering includes direct penetration of hyphae into decayed rocks and tunnelling into otherwise intact mineral matter along crystal planes, cleavages, cracks, and grain boundaries. Indirect biomechanical effects arise from shrinking and swelling of hydrated mucilage produced by many fungi.

## Metal Immobilization Mechanisms

Fungal metal immobilization reduces external free metal activity and may shift dissolution equilibria to release additional metal into solution (Gadd, 1993, 2000; Sterflinger, 2000). Metals can exert toxic effects in many ways: blocking functional groups of enzymes, displacing essential metal ions, disrupting cellular and organellar membranes, and interfering with free-radical protection systems. However, fungi employ a variety of mechanisms, both active and incidental, for tolerance and survival.

Three primary immobilization mechanisms operate:

1. **[[gadd-mycogenic-mineral-precipitation-secondary-minerals]] outside the cell through both metabolism-dependent and metabolism-independent processes, including redox transformations.

Fungi are highly efficient bioaccumulators of Ni, Zn, Ag, Cu, Cd, and Pb, particularly from dilute external concentrations (Gadd, 1993, 2000; Baldrian, 2003). Metal binding is an important passive process occurring in both living and dead fungal biomass (Gadd, 1990, 1993). Non-viable biomass retains biosorptive capacity — a property relevant to biotechnological metal recovery and wastewater treatment applications.

The main mechanisms of solubilization of minerals and insoluble metal compounds by fungi are **acidolysis**, **complexolysis**, and **redoxolysis**, all of which may be enhanced by metal accumulation in and around the fungal biomass. The primary fungal impact on biogeochemical cycling results from acidolysis (proton-promoted dissolution via excreted protons, organic acids, and respiratory CO₂) and complexolysis (ligand-promoted dissolution via carboxylic acids, amino acids, siderophores, and phenolic compounds). Organic acid excretion by fungi is inter- and intraspecific and can be strongly influenced by the presence of toxic metal minerals. Fungal-derived carboxylic acids with strong chelating properties (e.g. oxalic and citric acid) perform aggressive attack on mineral surfaces.

## Precipitation of Secondary Mycogenic Minerals

The formation of secondary organic and inorganic minerals by fungi occurs through metabolism-independent and metabolism-dependent processes. Precipitation, nucleation, and deposition of crystalline material on and within cell walls are influenced by environmental pH and cell wall composition (Gadd, 1990; Ferris et al., 1994; Sterflinger, 2000; Verrecchia, 2000).

This process has important consequences for soil structure. Precipitation of carbonates, phosphates, and hydroxides increases soil aggregation. Cations such as Si⁴⁺, Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, and Ca²⁺ — released through fungal dissolution mechanisms — stimulate precipitation of compounds that act as bonding agents for soil particles.

Hyphae contribute to soil physical structure in complementary ways: they enmesh soil particles, alter particle alignment, and release organic metabolites that enhance aggregate stability (Bronick & Lal, 2005). Biomechanical penetration into mineral matter is facilitated by thigmotropic reactions (contact guidance) and lubrication with mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating metabolites. Fungal hyphae can exert considerable mechanical force derived from osmotically generated turgor pressure.

### Carbonate Precipitation

Microbial carbonate precipitation coupled with silicate weathering represents an important CO₂ sink in terrestrial environments (Verrecchia et al., 1990; Rivadeneyra et al., 1993; Riding, 2000). Fungal filaments mineralized with calcite (CaCO₃), together with calcium oxalate, are common in limestone and calcareous soils across a range of localities (Kahle, 1977; Callot et al., 1985; Verrecchia, 2000).

During decomposition of fungal hyphae, calcite crystals act as nucleation sites for further secondary calcite precipitation, reinforcing soil structure over time. Chitin — the major component of fungal cell walls — is a substrate on which calcite readily nucleates (Manoli et al., 1997). Near-surface limestones, calcic and petrocalcic horizons are often secondarily cemented with calcite and whewellite (calcium oxalate monohydrate). Calcium oxalate can also be degraded to calcium carbonate in semi-arid environments, acting to cement pre-existing limestones (Verrecchia et al., 1990).

### Metal [[fungal-biosorption-of-heavy-metals-by-filamentous-fungi|toxic metals]] by precipitating them as insoluble oxalates.

The formation of toxic metal oxalates enables fungi to tolerate environments containing high metal concentrations. *Beauveria caledonica*, an oxalate-over-excreting fungus, transforms Cd, Cu, Pb, and Zn from a variety of toxic metal minerals into corresponding oxalates and is tolerant to all tested minerals (Fomina et al., 2005a). Oxalate excretion is enhanced by NO₃⁻ as a nitrogen source (versus NH₄⁺), and by the presence of HCO₃⁻, Ca²⁺, and certain toxic metals or minerals (e.g. pyromorphite, zinc phosphate). If a fungal culture is capable of excreting large amounts of a strong chelator such as oxalate, the solubilization mechanism shifts from proton-promoted to ligand-promoted dissolution.

### Other Mycogenic Minerals

A specific combination of biotic and abiotic factors leads to deposition of various other secondary minerals associated with fungi, including birnessite, ferrihydrite, goethite, montmorillonite, moolooite (copper oxalate), glushkinskite (magnesium oxalate), and calcium formate. Precipitation immobilizes metals in the soil environment and limits bioavailability (Gadd, 2000).

An oxidized metal layer (desert varnish) found on rocks in arid and semi-arid regions is also believed to be of microbial origin with some proposed fungal involvement.
