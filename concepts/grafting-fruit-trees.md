# Grafting Fruit Trees

Grafting is the art of joining two plants together so they grow as one. In fruit tree [[coleman-cool-house-vs-cold-house-economics-winter-production]], the scion (top portion) provides the desired fruit variety while the rootstock (bottom portion) provides disease resistance, size control, and soil adaptation. Grafting is essential for reproducing named fruit varieties that do not come true from seed.

## Grafting Basics

### Terminology

- **Scion**: The variety you want to propagate. A dormant, 1-year-old shoot with 2-3 buds. This becomes the fruiting portion of the tree.
- **Rootstock**: The bottom portion that provides the root system. Chosen for disease resistance, dwarfing characteristics, and soil compatibility.
- **Graft union**: The point where scion and rootstock are joined. This must remain above the soil line for the life of the tree.
- **Cambium**: The thin green layer of actively dividing cells just beneath the bark. Successful grafting requires aligning the cambium layers of scion and rootstock.
- **Topworking**: Grafting new scion varieties onto the existing framework of an established tree.

### Compatibility

Scion and rootstock must be closely related for a successful graft. Generally, species within the same genus graft successfully:

| Rootstock Group | Compatible Scions |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Apple (Malus) | Apple, crabapple |
| Pear (Pyrus) | Pear, quince (with interstem) |
| Cherry (Prunus avium) | Sweet cherry |
| Plum (Prunus domestica) | Plum, apricot (sometimes), peach (sometimes) |
| Peach (Prunus persica) | Peach, nectarine, almond |
| Citrus (Citrus) | Most citrus species graft together |
- Quince is semi-compatible with pear and is used as a dwarfing rootstock
- Some stone fruits can cross-graft but compatibility varies by variety

### Tools and Supplies

- **Grafting knife**: Thin, sharp blade (razor-sharp). A single-bevel grafting knife is ideal.
- **Pruning shears**: For collecting scion wood and preparing rootstocks.
- **Grafting wax or tape**: Seals the graft union to prevent desiccation. Parafilm is excellent and biodegradable.
- **Rubber budding bands or grafting rubber**: Holds scion and rootstock tightly together during healing.
- **Hand pruners and saw**: For preparing rootstocks and topworking.
- **Rubbing alcohol**: For sterilizing tools between cuts.

## Whip-and-Tongue Graft

The whip-and-tongue is the strongest graft for joining scions and rootstocks of similar diameter (1/4 to 1/2 inch). It provides maximum cambium contact and is the standard method for bench grafting dormant trees in late winter.

### Steps

1. **Collect scion wood** in late winter (January-February) while dormant. Select healthy, 1-year-old shoots, pencil-thickness, with 2-3 visible buds. Store in sealed plastic bags with moist paper towel in the refrigerator (34-38°F) until grafting.
2. **Prepare rootstock**: Cut the top off at a clean, straight angle. The diameter should match the scion.
3. **Cut the scion**: Make a smooth, slanting cut about 1.5-2 inches long on the scion base. The cut surface should be flat and smooth — a single pass with a sharp knife.
4. **Cut the tongue on the scion**: Starting 1/3 from the tip of the slant, make a downward cut into the scion about 1/2 inch deep, parallel to the wood grain.
5. **Cut the matching tongue on the rootstock**: Make identical cuts on the rootstock top — the same slant angle and matching tongue.
6. **Join the pieces**: Slide the scion tongue into the rootstock tongue so they interlock. The cambium layers must align on at least one side (preferably both).
7. **Wrap tightly**: Begin wrapping below the graft union, working upward. Overlap each wrap by half. Cover the entire cut surface. Use parafilm or grafting tape.
8. **Seal**: Apply grafting wax to any exposed cut surfaces. If using parafilm, the wrap alone is often sufficient.
9. **Heal**: Keep grafted trees at 50-65°F in a humid environment (callus formation). [[query-how-do-i-choose-between-a-cold-frame-row-cover-and-low-tunnel]], unheated greenhouse, or refrigerator drawer works. Graft union heals in 2-4 weeks.

### Best Timing

- Late January through March, while both scion and rootstock are still dormant
- The ideal window is 4-6 weeks before the average last frost date
- Graft before bud break on the rootstock but after the risk of severe freezing has passed

## Cleft Graft

Cleft grafting is used to topwork established trees (changing the variety) or when the rootstock is significantly thicker than the scion. The scion is inserted into a cleft (split) in the rootstock.

### Steps

1. **Prepare the rootstock**: Cut the trunk or branch off square with a pruning saw. The cut should be clean and smooth.
2. **Make the cleft**: Using a cleft grafting tool (or a heavy knife and wedge), split the rootstock center downward about 2-3 inches.
3. **Prepare two scions**: Cut each scion into a long, tapering wedge — about 2 inches long. The outer side should be slightly thicker than the inner side (the "shoulder" prevents the scion from being pushed too deep).
4. **Insert the scions**: Insert one scion on each side of the cleft, with the cambium layers aligned. The wedge shape of the scion should fit snugly in the cleft.
5. **Remove the wedge**: Once scions are in place, remove the cleft tool or wedge. The natural spring of the rootstock wood holds the scions tightly.
6. **Seal completely**: Cover all exposed cut surfaces with grafting wax, especially the top of the rootstock and the cleft opening. This prevents drying.
7. **Wrap**: Wrap with parafilm or grafting tape for additional security.

### Best Timing

- Late winter to early spring, just before bud break
- The rootstock must be dormant enough to split cleanly but the bark should be beginning to slip (separate easily from wood)
- Can also be done in late summer as a "late cleft" for evergreen fruit trees

## Bud Grafting (T-Budding)

T-budding uses a single bud from the scion variety, inserted under the bark of the rootstock. It is the most efficient method for large-scale nursery production and works well during the active growing season.

### Steps

1. **Collect budwood**: Cut current-season shoots from the desired variety. Remove leaves but keep the leaf petiole (it becomes the handle for the bud). Use immediately or store moist and cool for up to a few days.
2. **Prepare the rootstock**: On a smooth section of the rootstock stem, make a vertical cut about 1 inch long through the bark (do not cut into the wood). At the top of the vertical cut, make a horizontal cut to form a "T."
3. **Remove a bud from the scion**: Starting about 1/2 inch below the bud, slice upward under the bud, removing a thin shield of bark with the bud in the center. The shield should be about 1 inch long.
4. **Insert the bud**: Open the "T" on the rootstock by gently twisting the knife blade. Slide the bud shield down into the T cut, positioning the bud in the center. The top of the shield should align with the horizontal cut of the T.
5. **Wrap**: Wrap with rubber budding bands or parafilm, covering the entire bud shield but leaving the bud itself exposed. The wrap holds the bud in tight contact with the rootstock cambium.
6. **Aftercare**: The bud heals in 2-3 weeks. The following spring, cut the rootstock off just above the bud union. The bud will grow into the new tree.

### Best Timing

- **Late summer (August-September)**: Most common timing. Bark slips easily on rootstock. Buds remain dormant until the following spring.
- **Late spring (May-June)**: "June budding" for fast-growing rootstocks. The bud pushes immediately and grows through the remainder of the season.

## Aftercare for Grafted Trees

### First Year

- **Remove wrapping**: After the graft has healed (4-8 weeks), remove rubber bands or tape to prevent girdling. Parafilm degrades naturally and usually does not need removal.
- **Remove suckers**: Rootstocks send up shoots from below the graft union. Remove these immediately — they compete with the scion and can overtake it.
- **Stake**: Grafted trees, especially on dwarfing rootstocks, need staking for the first 1-3 years to prevent wind damage at the graft union.
- **Water**: Keep soil consistently moist but not waterlogged. Grafted trees have reduced root systems initially.
- **Protect**: Wrap the trunk with tree guards or hardware cloth to prevent rodent damage and sunscald.

### Long-Term

- **Keep the graft union above ground**: If soil covers the graft union, the scion may root, negating the rootstock's benefits.
- **Monitor for incompatibility**: Some graft combinations show delayed incompatibility (breaking at the union after several years). Look for swollen, cracked, or weeping areas at the union.
- **Prune for structure**: See [[pruning-fruit-trees]] for training grafted trees to the desired form.

## Common Problems

| Problem | Cause | Prevention |
|---------|-------|------------|
| Graft fails to take | Cambium misalignment, drying out, wrong timing | Align cambium carefully, seal all cuts, graft at correct season |
| Suckers from rootstock | Rootstock is more vigorous than scion | Remove suckers immediately, choose compatible rootstock |
| Graft union breakage | Wind, inadequate staking, weak union | Stake trees, choose strong grafting method |
- **Delayed incompatibility** | Poor species/genetic match | Research proven rootstock-scion combinations |
