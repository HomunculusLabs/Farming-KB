# Fruiting Conditions After full substrate colonization, mushrooms require a shift in environmental conditions to trigger the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting. This shift is callfruiting-initiationtion" and involves changes to temperature, humidity, fresh air exchange, and light. ## The Fruiting Trigger Mushroom formation is triggered by a combination of environmental cues that sigmyceliummycelium that conditions are favorable for reproduction: 1. **Drop in temperature**: A 5-10°C reduction from colonization temperature simulates seasonal change. 2. **Introduction of fresh air**: Lower CO2 levels signal that the mycelium has reached the substrate surface and is exposed to open air. 3. **High humidity (85-95%)**: Prevents the developing fruiting bodies from drying out before maturity. 4. **Light**: Most cultivated mushrooms require at least some light to initiate and guide fruiting. Indirect ambient light or a 12-hour light cycle with 5000-6500K spectrum is typical. ## Pinning (Primordia Formation) "Pinning" is the formation of tiny mushroom initials called primordia. These are the first visible stage of mushroom development, appearing as small white bumps on the substrate surface. The pinning stage is the most critical and delicate phase of cultivation: - Pins are extremely sensitive to drying. A drop in humidity can kill an entire pin set within hours. - Temperature fluctuations during pinning can cause aborts (pins that stop growing and rot). - CO2 levels must be lowered but not so drastically that humidity drops. - The goal is a dense, even pin set across the entire substrate surface, which leads to uniform mushroom size at harvest. ## Key Environmental Parameters ### Fresh Air Exchange (FAE) During colonization, high CO2 encourages mycelial growth. For fruiting, CO2 must be reduced to below 800 ppm (compared to 5000-10000 ppm during colonization). In practice, this means providing regular air exchange: - Passive exchange through filtered holes or gaps - Active exchange using fans (with care not to blow directly on substrates) - Mist and fan cyclshotgun-fruiting-chamberting Chamber) setups Insufficient FAE causes elongated, thin-stemmed mushrooms with small caps ("leggy" growth). Excessive FAE can dry out substrates and pins. ### Humidity Maintaining 85-95% relative humidity during fruiting is essential: - **Humidifiers**: Ultrasonic or evaporative, often on timers or humidity controllers. - **Perlite**: Expanded volcanic rock that holds water, providing passive humidity in fruiting chambers. - **Misting**: Hand-misting with a spray bottle 2-4 times daily, combined with fanning for air exchange. - **Martha tent**: A modified greenhouse enclosure with automated humidity and fresh air exchange for larger-scale cultivation. ### Temperature Most gourmet mushrooms fruit between 15-22°C (59-72°F). ## Common Fruiting Problems

### Aborts (Primordia That Stop Growing)
Aborts are pins that cease development and begin to rot. They are caused by:
- Sudden humidity drops during the critical pinning phase
- Temperature fluctuations exceeding 5°C in either direction
- Insufficient fresh air exchange causing CO2 buildup
- Physical disturbance of developing pins from handling or spraying
- Bacterial contamination spreading from one pin to neighboring pins
Aborts should be removed promptly to prevent contamination of healthy
fruiting bodies. Affected areas can be patched with sterile salt to
limit bacterial spread across the substrate surface.

### Fuzzy Stems (High CO2 Response)
When CO2 levels remain too high during fruiting, mushrooms develop
elongated stems with small or absent caps. This is the mycelium
reaching upward searching for fresh air. The condition is reversible
if FAE is increased before the mushrooms mature, but fully elongated
fruiting bodies cannot revert to normal morphology once expanded.

### Splitting Caps
Rapid changes in humidity cause caps to crack or split as tissue
expands unevenly. Maintaining consistent humidity within the 85-95%
range prevents this problem. Over-misting mature mushrooms also causes
splitting as the tissue absorbs excess water faster than it can expand.

### Slow Growth or Stalled Pins
Stalled pins often indicate suboptimal conditions in one or more
parameters. Common causes include temperatures too low for the species,
insufficient humidity causing desiccation, or exhausted substrate that
cannot supply adequate moisture to developing fruiting bodies.

## Species-Specific Fruiting Parameters

Different mushroom species have distinct optimal fruiting conditions:
- **Oyster mushrooms** (Pleurotus spp.): 15-24°C, very high FAE
  requirements, tolerate a wide humidity range, fruit prolifically
  on straw and agricultural waste substrates
- **Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes): 12-18°C, requires a cold shock
  for reliable fruiting initiation, lower FAE needs than oysters
- **Lion's mane** (Hericium erinaceus): 16-21°C, sensitive to low
  humidity which causes browning of the spiny teeth structures
- **Button mushrooms** (Agaricus bisporus): 13-18°C, requires casing
  layer for fruiting, moderate FAE, no light required for initiation
- **King oyster** (Pleurotus eryngii): 12-18°C, low FAE produces
  thicker stems which are commercially desirable, longer fruiting
  cycle than other oyster species

## Harvesting Timing

Proper harvest timing affects both yield and quality:
- Harvest before the veil tears for species with partial veils
  (e.g., Psilocybe, Agaricus) to maximize potency and shelf life
- Oyster mushrooms should be harvested just as the cap margins begin
  to flatten but before they become deeply concave and release spores
- Shiitake is best harvested when the cap is 70-80% expanded with a
  thick, firm texture and visible cracking on the cap surface
- Regular harvesting encourages subsequent flushes; leaving overmature
  mushrooms on the substrate depletes resources and reduces total yield
