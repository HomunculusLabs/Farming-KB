# Keyline Design

Keyline design is a landscape management system developed by Australian farmer P.A. Yeomans in the 1950s. It uses the natural topography of land to optimize water distribution, increase soil fertility, and prevent erosion. Keyline pattern cultivation is a specific technique that spreads water from wet valleys to dry ridges using the contour of the land.

## P.A. Yeomans and Origins

Percival Alfred Yeomans (1904-1984) developed the Keyline System while managing "Nevallan" and "Yobarnie," two properties in New South Wales, Australia. Facing severe drought and erosion on degraded land, Yeomans observed that conventional contour plowing concentrated water in valleys, causing erosion and leaving ridges parched. His insight was to cultivate parallel to the "keyline" — a specific contour line that naturally redistributes water from wet areas to dry areas.

Yeomans outlined his system in several books, most notably "Water for Every Farm" (1954). The system integrates water management, soil development, tree planting, and pasture improvement into a unified landscape approach. It remains one of the most influential frameworks in [[permaculture-design-methods]] design and regenerative agriculture.

## Understanding Keylines

### The Keyline Concept

In any valley, there is a point where the valley floor ceases to be convex and becomes concave — where the slope changes from the valley walls to the valley floor. The contour line that passes through this inflection point is the "keyline."

### Key Features of a Valley

- **Keypoint**: The point in a valley where the slope changes from convex to concave. This is the highest point in the valley where water would naturally begin to pool or slow down.
- **Keyline**: The contour line that passes through the keypoint, extending across the full width of the valley and up the adjacent ridges.
- **Keyline valley**: The area below the keyline, where water naturally concentrates.
- **Keyline ridge**: The area above the keyline, where water naturally sheds.

## Keyline Pattern Cultivation

The core technique of keyline design is a specific cultivation pattern that moves water from the wetter valleys to the drier ridges without earthworks.

### How It Works

1. Identify the keyline for each valley on the property
2. Begin cultivation parallel to the keyline, following its contour across the valley and up the ridges
3. As cultivation continues uphill from the keyline, the parallel lines drift slightly off-contour — the upper end of each line is slightly higher than the lower end
4. This slight slope (typically 1-3%) causes water that falls on the cultivation lines to flow from the wet valley toward the dry ridge
5. Below the keyline, cultivation lines follow the keyline contour exactly, keeping water in the valleys where it naturally collects

### The Cultivation Pattern

**Above the Keyline (Toward Ridges)**
- Cultivation lines are parallel to the keyline but drift uphill as they extend up the ridges
- Water flows along these lines from valley to ridge
- This irrigation effect spreads moisture to areas that would otherwise be dry
- Gradually reduces the moisture differential between valleys and ridges

**Below the Keyline (In the Valley)**
- Cultivation lines are parallel to the keyline and remain on contour
- Water stays in the valley, spreading across the full width of the valley floor
- Prevents concentrated water flow that causes erosion
- Slows water movement, increasing infiltration

### Cultivation Depth

Keyline cultivation is done with a chisel plow or subsoiler (Yeomans' original "Keyline Plow") that fractures the soil without inverting it. This is a form of deep ripping:

- Cultivate to a depth of 4-8 inches on first pass
- Subsequent passes can go deeper (up to 12-16 inches) as soil structure improves
- The shanks fracture compacted subsoil, improving root penetration and water infiltration
- Ground cover should always be maintained — never cultivate bare soil
- See [[no-till-methods]] for minimizing soil disturbance

## Water Distribution Systems

Keyline design includes several water management features that work together:

### Keypoint Dam

A dam constructed at or near the keypoint of the primary valley. Water collected here can be gravity-fed to lower areas of the property. Because the keypoint is high in the landscape, the dam water can irrigate a large area below without pumping.

- The dam is small relative to the landscape area it serves
- Gravity-feed pipelines or channels distribute water from the keypoint dam
- The dam fills from valley runoff above the keypoint
- Spillways direct excess water safely along contour

### Contour Channels

Shallow channels or swales cut on contour (or slightly off-contour per keyline principles) to slow, spread, and sink water. These are less dramatic than full [[swale-design-construction]] but serve a similar function on gentler slopes.

### Irrigation Channels

Gravity-fed channels that distribute water from keypoint dams across the property. These follow keyline contours and can irrigate pasture, cropland, and tree systems.

## Tree Planting in Keyline Design

Trees are integral to the keyline system. Yeomans specified tree belts planted along the upper contours of valleys and ridge lines:

- **Valley tree belts**: Planted along the upper edges of valleys, parallel to keylines. These stabilize valley walls, intercept runoff, and provide windbreaks.
- **Ridge tree belts**: Planted along ridge contours to slow wind, reduce evaporation, and provide shelter for livestock and crops.
- **Dam tree belts**: Trees planted around dams to reduce evaporation and stabilize banks.

Tree species selection depends on climate and purpose — timber, fodder, wildlife habitat, or wind protection. See [[food-forest-design]] for integrated tree-crop systems.

## Integration with Other Systems

### Keyline + Swales

On steeper terrain, keyline design can be combined with full [[swale-design-construction]]. Keyline principles inform the layout and spacing of swales to optimize water distribution.

### Keyline + Earthworks

Larger [[earthworks-for-water-management]] projects (ponds, diversion swales) should follow keyline contours to ensure gravity-fed water distribution and prevent erosion.

### Keyline + Grazing

Keyline-cultivated pastures recover faster and support higher stocking densities. The improved water distribution promotes more even forage growth. Combine with rotational grazing for maximum soil improvement.

### Keyline + No-Till

After initial keyline cultivation to break compaction and establish water distribution patterns, the land can transition to [[no-till-methods]]. Keyline cultivation is a one-time or infrequent intervention, not an annual practice.

## Application Scale

Keyline design works at multiple scales:

- **Small gardens (0.1-1 acre)**: Hand-dug swales and contour planting on keyline principles
- **Hobby farms (1-50 acres)**: Keyline cultivation with walk-behind tractor, small keypoint dams
- **Large farms (50+ acres)**: Tractor-mounted keyline plow, keypoint dams with gravity irrigation, comprehensive tree belt system

## Limitations

- Keyline design requires understanding of contour and landform — surveying tools or a contour map are needed
- On very flat land, the keyline concept is less applicable (water distribution is less of an issue)
- On very steep land (over 15% slope), keyline cultivation alone is insufficient — terracing or other earthworks are needed
- The system was developed for the specific climate and geology of eastern Australia — adaptations may be needed for other regions
- Initial implementation requires investment in earthworks, trees, and cultivation equipment
