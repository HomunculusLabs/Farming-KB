# McKenna on Ergot and the Witches' Sabbath Theory

In [[psychedelic-mushroom-set-and-setting]]
examines the dual role of ergot-fungus|ergot ([[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]) in
Western history: as the likely visionary catalyst of the [[kykeon-composition-and-ergot-alkaloid-hypothesis]] was an ergotized beer brewed from barley infected with Claviceps
purpurea. The argument draws on several lines of evidence: grain was central
to the Eleusinian cult as a harvest festival celebrating the Great Mother
Goddess and Dionysus; the purple traditionally associated with Demeter's robe
may signify the distinctive purple of ergot sclerotia; and Sir Arthur Evans
found vessels adorned with barley ears at Knossos, suggesting a special
barley drink preceded wine on Crete. Kerenyi believed the small size of these
vessels indicated they were used for a visionary sacrament.

Claviceps purpurea, a small fungus that infects edible grains, produces ergot
-- a source of powerful alkaloids capable of causing hallucination, triggering
labor, and producing strong vasoconstrictive effects. Wasson and colleagues
argued that grain and ergot were joined in a "bisexual union as siblings"
symbolizing the mystery of Demeter and Kore. The scandal of 415 B.C., in
which Alcibiades was fined for possessing the Eleusinian sacrament for
entertaining friends, demonstrates that whatever the catalyst was, it was
tangible and powerful.

## The Toxicity Problem

McKenna acknowledges a serious difficulty with the ergot theory. In
documented instances of mass ergot consumption, the results have been horrific.
In A.D. 994, an outbreak of ergotism killed nearly 40,000 people in France; an
outbreak in 1129 killed about 1,200. Two clinical types of ergotism are
recognized: gangrenous (starting with tingling in the fingers, progressing
through vomiting and diarrhea to dry gangrene and limb separation) and
convulsive (painful spasms of limb muscles culminating in epileptic-like
convulsions and delirium).

Mary Kilbourne Matossian has argued that La Grande Peur of 1789, a peasant
uprising pivotal in the French Revolution, had its roots in ergot-infected
rye bread. Ergot-infected flour has also been proposed as a factor in the
decline of the Roman Empire and in the Salem witch burnings.

McKenna suggests two possible resolutions: Claviceps paspali, which
preferentially infects barley instead of rye, may contain higher proportions
of psychoactive but less toxic "simple" ergot alkaloids and lower proportions
of toxic peptide-containing alkaloids. Additionally, macerating ergotized
grain in water could effectively separate water-soluble psychoactive
alkaloids from fat-soluble toxic alkaloids. As McKenna dryly notes, "there
are old mycologists, and there are bold mycologists, but there are no old,
bold mycologists."

## Graves's Alternative: Psilocybin at Eleusis

Should ergot prove unrelated to Eleusis, Robert Graves's theory that
[[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] constituted the mystery deserves careful reconsideration.
Graves proposed that the Eleusinian priesthood had discovered an alternative
[[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-amanita-muscaria-emerging-alternative]] easier to handle than [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]] -- one that
could be baked in sacrificial cakes shaped like pigs or phalloi without
losing its hallucinogenic powers. Recipes in classical sources for the ritual
Eleusinian beverage contained ingredients whose first letters could be
arranged to spell "mushroom" in an ogham-like cipher.

## The Witches' Sabbath and Plant Suppression

McKenna connects psychoactive plants more broadly to the medieval witch
hysteria. The late medieval Church that conducted the great witch burnings
wanted all magic and derangement attributed to the Devil. It deliberately
suppressed all knowledge of plants such as thorn apple (Datura), deadly
nightshade, and monkshood, and of the role these plants played in nocturnal
"witchcraft." Flying ointments and magical salves were compounded from Datura
roots and seeds, rich in delirium-producing tropane alkaloids. When applied
to the body, these produced states of extraordinary derangement and delusion
that were interpreted as demonic possession.

The Inquisition deliberately suppressed the botanical dimension of these
practices. A natural explanation for "witch flights" would have diminished the
Devil to a "carnival conjurer" who caused "mere illusions" -- incompatible
with his assigned role as the mighty enemy and seducer of Christendom. As
late as the Middle Ages the witch was still the *ha-gazussa*, a being who sat
on the Hag -- the fence that separated the village from the wilderness --
participating in both worlds.
