## The Healer's Continuous Singing

The Mazatec healer, who may be a woman or a man, sings almost
uninterruptedly throughout the night. This continuous singing is not
background music but is understood as the primary vehicle through which
healing occurs. The healer weaves into their chants the names of
Christian saints, spirit allies, and the spirits of the Earth, the
elements, animals and plants, the sky, the waters, and the fire. This
comprehensive invocation of spiritual entities across multiple domains
reflects a cosmology in which all aspects of the natural world are
inhabited by sentient beings with whom the healer can communicate.

The integration of Christian saints alongside indigenous spirit allies
illustrates the syncretic character of Mazatec practice. Following
centuries of Spanish colonial rule and Catholic missionization, the
Mazatec incorporated Christian elements into their pre-Columbian
entheogenic tradition without abandoning its core shamanic structure.
This pattern of syncretism is common across indigenous entheogenic
practices in Latin America, where Christian and indigenous spiritual
frameworks have merged into hybrid forms that serve both traditional
healing functions and community religious needs.

## Gender and Healing Authority

Metzner specifically notes that the Mazatec healer "may be a woman or a
man," highlighting the gender-egalitarian character of Mazatec shamanic
practice. This is significant because the most famous Mazatec curandera,
[[mazatec-mushroom-tradition]] was brought to international attention by
R. gordon-wasson's 1957 article describing his experiences with Maria
Sabina. Metzner references Wasson's broader body of work, including his
