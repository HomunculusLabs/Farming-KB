# Outdoor Mushroom Garden Mulch Beds

[[outdoor-mushroom-cultivation]] has a long patent history, dating back
thousands of years. It can be applied just about anywhere in the world
using local trees and wood debris, making it an excellent starting
point for beginning cultivators. Compared to [[cannabis-sativa-indoor-cultivation-techniques]],
outdoor patches produce somewhat varied yields since you are at the
mercy of fluctuating temperature and rainfall. However, outdoor beds
can fruit several times a year for many years, require minimal labour
and cost, and integrate beautifully into existing gardens and
landscapes.

The primary methods include wood chip bed cultivation, log and stump
cultivation, and trenched or raft log cultivation.

## Wood Chip Bed Cultivation

Wood chip beds are among the most accessible methods for outdoor
mushroom cultivation. Many popular edible mushrooms thrive on wood
chips and incorporate naturally into fruit and [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]].

[[cotter-king-stropharia-cultivation]] (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) is
particularly suitable to most climates and loves fresh-cut hardwood
chips, preferably heartwood or sapwood rather than outer bark layers
commonly sold as commercial mulch.

Wood chip beds can be spawned year-round except in extremely cold
climates, where spring spawning allows mycelium to establish before
winter mulching adds insulation. Wood chip beds generally fruit for one
to two years. Adding fresh wood chips at the end of the year can extend
the life span for several more years, though worms often infiltrate
beds and consume the mycelium in the second year.

If no visible mycelium is detected with light digging, the beds should
be completely refurbished. Alternatively, you can harvest the
best-looking mycelium from select regions and move it into fresh chips,
or add cardboard on top of the existing bed to restart the process,
building soil depth by inches per year of rich compost. Never add fresh
chips when mushrooms are about to fruit, as the mycelium will
prioritise colonising the new substrate and delay fruiting.

## Log and Stump Cultivation

[[falconer-growing-mushrooms-in-sheds]] on logs and stumps has been practised for hundreds of
years and is well-suited for shiitakes, maitakes, and oysters. Hardwood
species [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]] are generally suitable, including invasive
and non-native trees that can be simultaneously managed through
harvest.

Logs must have bark intact all around, as mushrooms form between the
inner and outer bark layers, using this space as a nursery where
primordia develop before breaking through. Avoid trees that drop their
bark when cut, such as elms. Mushrooms can exert pressures greater than
80 pounds per square inch to push through bark.

Inoculation involves drilling holes 1 to 1.5 inches deep with a
5/16-inch bit, inserting plug or sawdust spawn, and covering with wax
to preserve moisture and exclude contamination. Logs should colonise
for three to four months aboveground with weekly watering before being
placed in their fruiting position. Stumps can take many years to fruit
but may produce for a decade or more once established.
