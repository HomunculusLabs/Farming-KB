# Rangelands and Natural Productivity

In Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Mollison uses the example of
natural rangelands to challenge the assumptions underlying conventional
livestock agriculture. He demonstrates that natural ecosystems far
out-produce managed monocultures, and argues that our excuses for
destroying such systems rest not on health, productive capacity, or
soil conservation, but on the sequestration of land for private misuse.

## The Savannah Benchmark

Brown (1972) estimated the standing crop of large herbivores on the
African savannah to be about 500 kg/ha, or about the same as on
improved pastures in humid temperate areas. However, the comparison is
stark: instead of one or two species sustained by external oil energy
input, the savannah supports guilds of browsers (five species), grazers
(eleven species), and four species that alternately graze or browse,
each having specific food preferences.

This crop estimate specifically excludes the far greater biomass of
smaller mammals, birds, insects, burrowers, reptiles, other lower
vertebrates, and plant products, so it is a minimal yield figure for a
natural system. No known cultivated system of livestock can surpass this
productivity without external inputs, nor are similar yields achieved
without stressing soils, plants, or animals.

## The Stock Unit System

In estimating the effects of animals on browse or grazing, agriculturists
have used a "stock unit" system of equivalents based on the weights of
the animal. Mollison notes that this system needs more work and
modification, as sheer size also affects metabolic efficiency. A second
modifier is that of individual metabolic efficiency: even within a
species, certain individuals convert food to energy or growth much more
efficiently than others. Such differences within species may be greater
than differences between species.

The concept of the stock unit remains useful so that available food in
season or in a particular type of pasture can be assessed over the year
in "cow-days" of fodder, and approximate stocking rates for other
species, or complex species mixes, can be estimated. The standard stock
unit is a European cow and calf, about 450 kg liveweight, or the
equivalent of 2 African cows, 5 European sheep, 20 African sheep, 25
Thompson gazelles, or 125 dikdik antelope. Ten standard stock units are
equivalent to 1 elephant, 4 black rhino, 15 zebras, or 27 hartebeest.

## Range Plant Categories

Animals on managed range are a valid sustainable system only if the
balance of plant and animal species is carefully maintained. In broad
groups, range plants fall into these categories:

- Forbs: annual and perennial herbs and bulbous species
- Grasses and other monocotyledons, annual and perennial
- Trees and shrubs
- Mosses and lichens
- Fungi and saprophytes

The first two groups are eaten by grazing species, the third by browsers,
and the last two by small herbivores and cold-area species. Animals
balance their range diets in very different ways, so that a variety of
animal species is needed to utilise the available forage. Further, these
are not constant proportions but dynamic seasonal changes.

## Animal Effects on Landscape

Mollison describes how animals actively shape their environments. In
swamps, savannahs, and semiarid or arid plains, trees may occur in
discrete groups related to natural conditions or to the effects of other
animal species working on the environment.

Species such as alligators in swamps create wallows for their own
comfort, as do pigs, buffaloes, elephants, and warthogs. A wallow
presents a new clump plant site by altering drainage (as both hole and
bank), nutrient status, and soil structure. These disturbances create
niches for new plant establishment, demonstrating that animal activity
is integral to landscape productivity.

## The Critique of Single-Species Grazing

Mollison limits yields by not considering the abundant soil life, and by
not devising methods to harvest some of this product either directly or
indirectly. In a clearance of rabbits where some 1,100 were caught or
dug out per acre (Tasmania, 1954), Mollison and others estimated yields
of close to 700 kg/ha. Such degraded pastures also contain enormous
numbers of insect larvae in excess of this figure.

He argues that we need to assess yields more closely, and to question
our direction in trying to raise a few species of animals on lands that
will support many more species and much higher yields of protein from
hitherto unconsidered species.

## The Fencing Problem

The second factor (after cropping) that has reduced the production of
natural grasslands, with their gallery forests in valleys and islands of
tree species, is the seemingly intractable and modern concepts of private
land ownership, hence fencing and the prevention of migratory movements
of herds. Mollison suggests that while we may never live to see such
errors totally reversed, it is a very worthwhile design enterprise to
both recreate and to experiment with new forms of perennial and
productive grasslands and their associated tree species in livestock
management, and for meadow species preservation.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

This analysis has profound implications for permaculture design. Rather
than attempting to maximise the yield of a single livestock species,
designers should aim for diverse animal guilds that mirror natural
ecosystems. This connects directly to [[mollison-mixed-livestock-rotation-systems]]
and [[mollison-forage-trees-in-livestock-systems]] as practical
approaches to achieving this diversity.
