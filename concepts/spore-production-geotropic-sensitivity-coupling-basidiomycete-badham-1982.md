## Evidence from Sporeless Mutants

The most compelling evidence for coupling between spore production and geotropic sensitivity comes from experiments with sterile (sporeless) mutants. When sporeless basidiocarps were placed in the wind tunnel under identical conditions to fertile ones:

- **Fertile basidiocarps** at Stage 2 showed clear negative geotropic recurvature—the pileus turned upward and opened with gills facing down.
- **Sporeless mutants** continued to grow into the wind throughout development and never exhibited the negative geotropic response.
- **Darkness conditions** in fertile mushrooms resulted in few spores forming and a correspondingly weak or absent geotropic response.

This triad of observations—sporeless mutants, dark-grown fertile mushrooms, and normally sporulating mushrooms—establishes a clear correlation between spore formation and the capacity for geotropic sensing. The fact that the geotropic response was absent in sporeless mutants despite all other conditions being identical strongly suggests that a substance produced during sporogenesis is necessary for the gravity-sensing mechanism.

## The Gill Hormone Hypothesis

The most parsimonious explanation for the coupling is that a substance produced in the gills during spore formation acts as a signal that modifies the tropic response of the stipe. This hypothesis is supported by a substantial body of prior work:

- **Decapitation experiments** (removal of the pileus) have been shown to affect stipe growth and orientation, linking tropic behavior to cap/gill tissue.
- **Agar diffusates from gills** can partially replace the influence of the pileus on stipe growth (Hagimoto and Konishi, 1960), demonstrating that a diffusible substance from the gills affects stipe elongation.
- **Gill hormones** have been postulated by multiple researchers (Gruen, 1963; Hagimoto, 1963) to regulate various aspects of mushroom development.

The proposed mechanism is that as basidia mature and spores begin to form on the gill surfaces, a hormone or signaling molecule is produced that either: (a) sensitizes stipe cells to gravity, or (b) directly overrides the evaporative demand signal that was driving anemotropic growth during the first phase. This substance would diffuse from the gills down through the stipe tissue, modifying the growth response of stipe cells.

## Light Requirement and Spore Formation

The negative geotropic response requires at least some light, but Badham suggested this light requirement may be indirect—light may simply be necessary for spore production rather than for the geotropic mechanism itself. This interpretation is supported by the observation that:

- Fertile basidiocarps grown in a 12-hour photoperiod showed strong negative geotropism at Stage 2.
- Fertile basidiocarps grown in constant light from above, when rotated parallel to the airflow, showed stipe elongation and pileus opening into the wind (not upward), because the gravity stimulus was equalized by rotation.
- Dark-grown cultures produced few spores and showed minimal geotropic response.

If light is required only for sporulation, and sporulation is required for geotropic sensitivity, then the apparent light requirement for geotropism is explained without invoking a separate photoreceptor for gravity sensing. However, this does not rule out the possibility that light has additional direct effects on geotropic sensitivity.

## Comparison with Coprinus and Polyporus

The phenomenon of tropism changing with developmental stage has been documented in other mushroom species. Plunkett (1961) described a change in tropism in *Polyporus brumalis* stipes, noting that the effect of directional stimuli on pileus differentiation changes as the fruiting body matures. Schwalb and Shanler (1974) observed phototropic and geotropic responses during development of normal and mutant fruiting bodies of *[[cespitose-fruiting-body-spacing-and-tropism]] bodies. Only after this spacing function has been served does the mushroom redirect its growth upward for the final purpose of spore release.

## Unresolved Questions

Several questions remain about the spore production-geotropism coupling:

- What is the molecular identity of the gill-produced substance that enables geotropic sensing?
- Is the substance a dedicated "geotropic hormone" or a metabolic byproduct of sporulation that incidentally affects stipe growth?
- Do all agarics show this coupling, or is it specific to certain genera or ecological groups?
- What is the sensory mechanism by which stipe cells detect gravity? Are there homologs of plant statoliths (amyloplasts) in fungal stipe cells?
- Can the geotropic response be induced artificially by applying gill extracts to sporeless mutants?
