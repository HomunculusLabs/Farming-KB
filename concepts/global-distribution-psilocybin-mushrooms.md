## Mexico as Center of Diversity

Mexico's preeminence in neurotropic fungal diversity is attributable to several factors:
its position at the intersection of Nearctic and Neotropical biogeographic zones creates
exceptional habitat diversity; its varied topography, from coastal plains to 4,000-meter
volcanoes, provides numerous ecological niches; and its long history of indigenous mushroom
use has motivated extensive mycological research focused specifically on psychoactive species.

The correlation between fungal diversity and indigenous ceremonial use is significant.
Mexico has more than 200 documented common names for neurotropic fungi, many in indigenous
languages. The most famous is *teonanácatl* (Nahuatl for "divine mushroom"), first
reported by the 16th-century Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún. Other names include
*apipiltzin* ("kid or little boy"), *pajaritos* ("little birds"), *san isidros* (after
Saint Isidore, patron of agriculture), and *derrumbes* ("landslides"), each typically
referring to specific species or groups of species used by particular indigenous
communities.

The concentration of both biological diversity and cultural knowledge in Mexico suggests
a long co-evolutionary history between human communities and neurotropic fungi, though
establishing the antiquity of this relationship with precision remains difficult given
the limited archaeological preservation of fungal materials.
