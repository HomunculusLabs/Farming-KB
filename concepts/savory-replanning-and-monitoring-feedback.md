# Replanning and Monitoring Feedback

Replanning and monitoring feedback form the operational backbone of the
[[savory-holistic-goal-definition]]. Deviations are
identified early and corrected through control measures. When events exceed our
control, such as natural disasters, drought, or fire, full replanning becomes
necessary. Savory illustrates this with a driving analogy: just as a driver must
constantly monitor the road ahead and adjust the steering wheel, a land manager
must continuously observe conditions and correct course.

The cycle operates at three levels within the model: [[permaculture-designers-manual-strategic-planning]] sets
the long-term direction, tactical planning addresses the annual or seasonal
work, and operational planning guides daily actions. At each level, monitoring
provides the feedback that triggers either minor course corrections or
fundamental replanning.

## Monitoring Individual Conditions

Effective monitoring requires close attention to actual conditions on the
ground, not general assumptions. Savory stresses that managers should not
assume growth rates simply because rain has fallen. Individual plants must be
checked to determine actual recovery and growth. Soils, slopes, and plants
vary too much across a landscape to fit a single formula. On brittle rangelands,
judging from actual plant daily growth rates is essential, as soil capping and
poor aeration can suppress growth even after rainfall.

New Zealand sheep farmers who rely on broadcast daily growth rates may find
this adequate in their relatively uniform environments, but on brittle ranges,
general information or area rainfall reports are inadequate. The wrong
assumption about growth rates can lead to plans that put cattle at quadruple
normal density on paddocks with only a fraction of the required recovery time,
which Savory identifies as the most rapid path to disaster during drought.

Savory recommends penciling out up to six or more alternatives before
selecting one to follow through a problem period, because drought raises very
complex questions. The worse the drought and the less forage standing at the
outset of the dormant season, the more important the planning becomes. Ranchers
who follow conventional grazing practices without biological planning and time
reserves routinely lose many hundreds of thousands of dollars in a drought
needlessly.
