# Food Preservation

Preserving a homestead harvest extends self-reliance year-round. Multiple methods — each with different strengths — ensure food security and reduce waste. See [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] for the biology behind fermentation preservation.

## Canning

### Water Bath Canning
For high-acid foods only (pH below 4.6): fruits, pickles, jams, tomatoes (with added acid), salsas with sufficient vinegar/lemon juice.
- Jars submerged in boiling water (212°F) for specified time.
- Acid prevents Clostridium botulinum growth.
- Equipment: large canning pot with rack, Mason jars, new lids, bands, jar lifter, headspace tool.

### Pressure Canning
Required for low-acid foods (pH above 4.6): vegetables, meats, soups, beans, broths.
- Must reach 240°F (at 10-15 PSI depending on elevation) to destroy botulinum spores.
- Equipment: pressure canner (not pressure cooker — must be gauge-tested), Mason jars, lids.
- Follow USDA processing times exactly. Altitude adjustments are critical.

### Key Canning Principles
- Always use tested recipes from USDA, NCHFP, or university extensions — do not improvise.
- Adjust processing time/pressure for altitude (+1 minute per 1000 ft for water bath; increase PSI for pressure canning).
- Headspace: 1/4 inch for jams/jellies, 1/2 inch for fruits and tomatoes, 1 inch for low-acid foods.
- Check jar seals after cooling (lid should not flex). Remove bands, clean jars, store in cool dark place.

## Fermentation

Lacto-fermentation preserves food through beneficial bacteria producing lactic acid. See [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] for the science.

- **Sauerkraut:** Shredded cabbage + 2% salt by weight. Pack tightly, submerge under brine, ferment 1-4 weeks at 60-70°F.
- **Kimchi:** Cabbage, radish, scallions, garlic, ginger, chili flakes (gochugaru) + 3% brine. Ferment 1-2 weeks.
- **Pickles (fermented):** Cucumbers in 3-5% brine with dill, garlic, spices. Ferment 1-3 weeks. Crunch preserved by tannins (grape or oak leaves) and avoiding softening enzymes (cut 1/16 inch off blossom end).
- **General principles:** Use non-iodized salt, keep vegetables submerged, ferment at 60-70°F, burp vessels if not using airlocks, move to cold storage when desired flavor is reached.

## Dehydrating

Removes moisture to inhibit microbial growth. Dried foods are lightweight and space-efficient.

### Methods
- **Electric dehydrator:** Consistent temperature control (125-145°F for most foods). Best for reliable results. Excalibur and Nesco are common models.
- **Solar dehydrator:** Passive or active solar collectors. Free energy but weather-dependent. Build with screen trays, venting, and glass cover.

### Preparation by Food
| Food | Prep | Temp | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Herbs | Rinse, pat dry | 95-110°F | 2-4 hrs (until brittle) |
| Fruit (apples, berries) | Slice 1/4 inch, pretreat (lemon juice) | 135°F | 6-12 hrs |
| Tomatoes | Slice 1/4 inch, salt optional | 135°F | 8-14 hrs |
| Vegetables | Blanch 2-3 min, slice | 125-135°F | 4-12 hrs |
| Jerky | Slice 1/8-1/4 inch, marinate | 160°F | 4-8 hrs |

### Storage
- Cool completely, condition (rest in jars for 1 week, shake daily to distribute remaining moisture).
- Store in airtight glass jars or vacuum-sealed bags. Cool, dark, dry location.
- Check periodically for moisture or mold.

## Freezing

Fastest preservation method with best nutrient retention:

- **Blanching:** Required for most vegetables. Boil 2-3 minutes, immediately ice bath. Stops enzyme action that degrades quality. Leafy greens: 1-2 min. Root vegetables: 3-5 min.
- **Fruits:** Freeze on trays first (individually quick frozen), then transfer to bags to prevent clumping. Add ascorbic acid to prevent browning.
- **Container types:** Freezer bags (remove air), vacuum-seal bags, rigid plastic containers, glass (leave 1 inch headspace). Avoid thin plastic wrap alone.
- **Freezer inventory:** Label with contents, date, and quantity. Track with spreadsheet or hanging list. First in, first out (FIFO).
- **Shelf life:** Most frozen vegetables 8-12 months; fruits 6-12 months; cooked dishes 2-3 months; meat 6-12 months.

## Root Cellaring

Storing crops in cool, humid, ventilated conditions without processing:

| Crop | Temp (°F) | Humidity (%) | Storage Life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potatoes | 38-40 | 85-90 | 4-6 months |
| Carrots, beets, turnips | 32-35 | 90-95 | 4-6 months |
| Onions, garlic | 32-40 | 60-70 | 6-8 months |
| Winter squash | 50-55 | 50-70 | 2-6 months |
| Apples | 30-35 | 85-90 | 3-6 months |
| Cabbage | 32-35 | 90-95 | 3-4 months |

- **Ventilation:** Essential to prevent ethylene buildup and CO2 accumulation. Passive venting or small fan.
- **Separate ethylene producers:** Apples and some root crops produce ethylene gas that causes premature sprouting in potatoes and onions. Store separately or ensure ventilation.
- **Moisture management:** Use damp sand, sawdust, or leaves around root vegetables. Onions/garlic need dry conditions.
- **Check regularly:** Remove any rotting produce immediately to prevent spread.

## Smoking and Curing

Traditional preservation for meats and fish:

- **Curing:** Salt (dry rub or brine) draws moisture and inhibits bacteria. Nitrites/nitrates (pink curing salt #1 or #2) prevent botulism in smoked meats and develop color/flavor. Critical to follow tested ratios (typically 0.25% sodium nitrite by meat weight).
- **Cold smoking:** 70-90°F smoke for flavor with extended curing time. Used for bacon, salami, ham. Requires careful temperature control and food safety knowledge.
- **Hot smoking:** 140-180°F, cooking and preserving simultaneously. Fish (150°F for 30 min minimum), poultry, ribs. Internal temperature must reach safe minimums (165°F for poultry, 145°F for fish).
- **Safety:** Use a proper smoker, monitor temperatures with calibrated thermometers, follow USDA guidelines. Improperly cured/smoked meats are a serious botulism risk.

## Cold Storage Without Electricity

- **Root cellar or basement:** Insulated, below-ground space with passive temperature regulation.
- **Earth berms:** Bury barrels or boxes of root crops in the ground, insulate with straw.
- **Cool pantry:** North-facing room or unheated closet. Less precise but adequate for squash, onions, garlic.
- **Clamp/pit storage:** Traditional method — dig a trench, layer crops with straw and soil. Works for potatoes, carrots, and root vegetables through winter in cold climates.
- **Evaporative cooling:** Zeer pot or similar evaporative cooler for short-term storage of produce in warm climates.

## Shelf Life Guidelines

| Method | Typical Shelf Life | Key Factor |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure-canned low-acid | 2-5 years | Seal integrity |
| Water-bath high-acid | 1-2 years | Acid level, seal |
| Dehydrated (properly stored) | 1-5 years | Moisture exclusion |
| Frozen | 6-12 months | Temperature consistency (0°F) |
| Fermented (refrigerated) | 6-12 months | Brine coverage |
| Root cellar | 3-8 months | Temperature/humidity control |
| Smoked/cured | Varies widely | Salt concentration, storage temp |

When in doubt about safety — throw it out. Botulism toxin is invisible, odorless, and tasteless.
