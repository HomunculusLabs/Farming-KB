# Salt-Tolerant Plants in Permaculture

Salt-tolerant (halophytic) plants are essential for permaculture systems in coastal areas, saline soils, and arid regions where salt accumulation is a problem. These plants enable productive land use where conventional crops would fail, and many serve additional functions including forage, timber, honey production, [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], and soil rehabilitation.

## Saltbushes (Atriplex spp.)

Saltbushes are among the most important halophytic plants for permaculture, valued for their ability to remove salt from soil while providing livestock forage.

### Old Man Saltbush (*Atriplex nummularia*)
A widely planted species that serves as both a salt-remediation plant and a fodder crop. The leaves and could be used for removing salt from the soil. Yields of over 10 tonnes per hectare per year are possible for some species. Some Atriplex species are Australian natives and can exist in rainfalls less than 300 mm. Better growth rates are experienced in non-saline soils.

### Related Atriplex Species
- *Atriplex canescens* — four-wing saltbush
- *[[atriplex-halimus]]* — tree saltbush
- *Atriplex rhagodioides*
- *Atriplex undulata*
- *Atriplex vesicaria* — bladder saltbush

## Bluebushes (Maireana spp.)

### Cotton Bush (*Maireana aphylia*)
A much-branched perennial shrub reaching 1 m in height and width. It prefers clay and clay loam soils. The cotton bush is an Australian native adapted to arid and semi-arid conditions.

### Black Bluebush / Shrubby Bluebush (*Maireana pyramidata*)
Growing to a height of nearly 2 m, this species is confined to calcareous red and red-brown soils. It provides valuable forage with crude protein content of 15.1–22.0% and digestibility of 58%.

### Pearl Bluebush (*Maireana sedifolia*)
Another valuable forage species adapted to alkaline, saline conditions in arid Australia.

## Samphires (*Halosarcia* spp.)

Native to waterlogged saltlands of Western Australia, samphires are succulent halophytes that thrive in conditions lethal to most plants. They are important components of saltmarsh ecosystems and can be used for land rehabilitation.

## Salt-Tolerant Trees

### Athel Tamarisk (*Tamarix articulata*, syn. *Tamarix aphylia*)
A vigorous, bushy, pine-like tree that tolerates most soils in frost-free districts with a minimum of 350 mm rainfall. **WARNING**: most tamarisks cause salinisation of the upper soil. Their extensive roots extract all soluble salts from the soil, excrete the salts on the leaves, and return them to the topsoil with litter fall. They reduce crop yields for up to 50 m. This is an important consideration when siting tamarisk in permaculture designs.

### Paperbarks (*Melaleuca* spp.)

Many Melaleuca species are valuable for honey and essential oils, and several tolerate saline conditions:

- **Prickly Paperbark** (*Melaleuca stypheloides*) — A fast-growing tree (6–18 m) found in swampy coastal sites in eastern Australia. Tolerant of saline, sandy, wet, and heavy soils. Drought and frost tolerant.
- **White Cloud Tree** (*Melaleuca bracteata*) — A small tree (to 7 m) native to all mainland states except Victoria. Drought and frost tolerant, prefers heavy to medium soils.
- *Melaleuca cymbifolia*
- *Melaleuca thyroides* — An evergreen shrub to 2 m tall, native to Western Australia. Frost and [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]] with good tolerance to waterlogging.
- *Melaleuca glomerata*
- *Melaleuca nervosa*
- *Melaleuca pauperiflora*
- *Melaleuca subtrigona*
- **Broom Honey Myrtle** (*Melaleuca uncinata*)
- *Melaleuca acuminata*
- **Mallee Honey Myrtle** (*Melaleuca brevifolia*)
- *Melaleuca hamulosa*
- *Melaleuca preissiana*
- **Moonah / Rottnest Island Pine** (*Melaleuca lanceolata*) — produces light amber honey in summer

### Casuarina spp.
Important nitrogen-fixing trees tolerant of saline conditions:
- *Casuarina obesa*
- **Swamp Sheoak** (*Casuarina glauca*)
- **Black Sheoak** (*Casuarina cristata*)

## Sissoo (*Dalbergia sissoo*)

A fast-growing leguminous tree reaching 30 m, which adapts well to semi-arid conditions (may only reach 10–15 m without irrigation). Key characteristics:

- **Temperature range**: Below 0°C to nearly 50°C; considered frost hardy
- **Rainfall**: 500–2,000 mm; tolerates arid conditions
- **Altitude**: Sea level to 1,500 m
- **Soils**: Alluvial soils along river banks and stream beds; does not grow well on heavy clay
- **Roots**: Long superficial roots that send up suckers when damaged — excellent [[mollison-designers-earthworks-for-erosion-control]]

The wood is hard, elastic, and close-grained. White sapwood is durable and valued for furniture, carving, bending, veneer, handles, boat building, and structural work. It is a good firewood (specific gravity 0.64–0.7) and can be managed in coppice rotation. Young branches and leaves serve as fodder for deer and cattle.

## Mopane (*Colophospermum mopane*)

A moderately tall (to 23 m) leguminous tree native to central and southern Africa. Remarkable characteristics:

- Grows on poor soils with bad drainage where not even useful grasses will grow
- Withstands high summer temperatures but only mild frosts
- Rainfall range: 125–800 mm
- Generally grows in shallow compacted clay, alkaline and badly drained soils; best growth on rich deep alluvial soils
- Amazing ability to withstand dry, saline sites
- Best below 900 m altitude
- Young plants produce root suckers; seedlings may be suppressed by grasses

**Uses**: Leaves are readily eaten by stock and retain nutrient values even when fallen. The durable wood is used for pylons in bridge construction, mine timbers, railroad ties, and fence posts. Also used for parquet and carving wood. Because the wood is so hard and heavy (specific gravity > 1.0), heavy saws are required; it should be cut while green. Said to be the best firewood in Africa — burns slowly and quietly while giving off great heat. Trees coppice vigorously after fire, frost, or felling.

## Hop Bush (*Dodonea viscosa*)

Grows in areas of annual rainfall less than 250 mm, making it one of the most drought-tolerant shrubs available for permaculture systems.

## Design Considerations for Salt-Tolerant Plantings

1. **Site assessment**: Determine the type and severity of salinity before selecting species
2. **Avoid salt-accumulating species near crops**: Tamarisks, while salt-tolerant, can worsen surrounding soil conditions
3. **Use nitrogen fixers**: Combine salt-tolerant Acacia, Casuarina, and Dalbergia species to [[query-how-does-rock-dust-improve-soil-fertility]]
4. **Plan for coppice**: Many salt-tolerant trees (sissoo, mopane) respond well to coppice management
5. **Multi-function stacking**: Select species that provide forage, honey, timber, or erosion control alongside salt tolerance
6. **Gradual remediation**: Saltbushes and other halophytes can gradually improve soil conditions, allowing less tolerant species to be introduced over time
