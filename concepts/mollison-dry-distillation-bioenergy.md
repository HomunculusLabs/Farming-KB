## Dry Distillation System Design

### Construction

- Brick-lined container loaded with wood

- Fire lit underneath; wood inside cannot combust (steam-distilled)

- All juices are steamed out and cooked to charcoal

### Products and Yields

1. **Methane gas**: Comes off during process; use 4% of methane to pump the rest to bottles or gasometer

2. **Creosote**: After CO2 is absorbed by passing gases through lime water or ponds; used for painting and proofing planks; converts birch to non-rot product

3. **Methanol**: From methane, cooled rapidly in water; stored in steel drum; runs tractors

4. **Charcoal**: The cooked wood; fuel for heating

5. **Heat**: Hot mass below house provides space heating; unlimited hot water off that

### Integration

- System supplies all cooking gas and great hot mass for heating living area

- "I would like to see somebody set it up. Perhaps we have the practical situation for it right here."

- French historically used double-brick areas -- one for fire, one that cooked timber

## Forest Management for Bioenergy

- Forest should be managed for multiple end uses: bark, leaf, oil, and medicine products

- Inner zones need far more productivity in human and animal forages

- Trim from white oak management must be used (fire hazard if left between trees)

- Brush piles provide winter shelter for animals; cordwood left in woods is full of lizards and salamanders

- Hollow logs from tree cutting should be saved as nest boxes for birds and squirrels

- A large forested area should take five families in forest product

## Modern Bioenergy Comparisons

- Mollison compares his dry distillation to modern approaches

- Critical of both old method (covered with mud, wasted gases) and some modern systems

- Emphasizes that his system captures ALL products -- no waste

## Design Principles and Permaculture Integration

The dry distillation system embodies several core [[permaculture-zones-system]] 1 and 2. The hot mass from the unit doubles as a heating system, placing energy production in the most intensively used zone.

- **Waste as resource:** Tree trimmings, damaged wood, and forest management byproducts that would otherwise be burned in open piles or left as fire hazards become the feedstock for the distillation system. This converts a liability (fire hazard) into a suite of assets.

## Historical and Cross-Cultural Context

- **French double-brick systems:** Historical French charcoal production used adjacent brick chambers -- one for the fire and one for the wood being cooked. This design separated combustion from pyrolysis, allowing the wood to be heated in an oxygen-free environment.

- **Traditional charcoal burning:** Many cultures practiced charcoal production in earth-covered mounds, but these primitive methods wasted gases and oils. Mollison's system captures these byproducts through sealed brick construction and gas collection apparatus.

- **Wood gasification during wartime:** During World War II, millions of vehicles in Europe were converted to run on wood gas (producer gas), demonstrating that biomass-derived fuels can power internal combustion engines at practical scales.

- **Modern biochar movement:** The charcoal produced by dry distillation is essentially biochar, which when added to soil provides long-term carbon sequestration, improved water retention, and enhanced microbial habitat -- connecting energy production to [[mollison-sewage-biogas-systems]] for comprehensive community energy and waste management.

## Practical Implementation Notes

- **Safety considerations:** Methane is explosive and must be stored and handled carefully. The system requires pressure relief valves, proper gas storage vessels, and adequate ventilation. Methanol is toxic if ingested and flammable.

- **Feedstock selection:** Hardwoods produce more charcoal and creosote than softwoods. Mixed feedstocks provide a balanced output profile. The system works best with wood that is too small, crooked, or damaged for timber use.

- **Gas collection infrastructure:** Piping, storage tanks, and gasometers must be properly sized and maintained. The 4% self-powering requirement means the system can bootstrap itself once initial firing is achieved.

- **Creosote handling:** Wood creosote is a natural preservative but can be irritating to skin and lungs. Proper handling, storage, and application equipment is needed for using creosote as a wood treatment product.
