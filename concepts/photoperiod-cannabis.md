# Photoperiod Cannabis

The photoperiod is the relationship between the duration of the light period and dark
period in a 24-hour cycle. Cannabis is a photoperiodic-reactive plant: the length of
daily darkness controls whether the plant remains in [[vegetative-phase-cannabis]] or
enters [[flowering-phase-cannabis]]. Understanding photoperiodism is fundamental to
successful cannabis cultivation.

## Biological Mechanism

[[cannabis-plant-anatomy]] contains photoreceptor proteins (phytochromes) that detect the ratio of red to
far-red light. During daylight, phytochromes exist primarily in the active (Pfr) form.
In darkness, they slowly revert to the inactive (Pr) form. When the dark period exceeds
a critical threshold (approximately 12 hours), the accumulated Pr form triggers the
hormonal cascade that initiates flowering. This mechanism evolved to ensure cannabis
flowers in autumn when days naturally shorten.

## Vegetative Photoperiod

Most strains of cannabis will remain in vegetative growth as long as 18-24 hours of
light and 0-6 hours of dark are maintained. Eighteen hours of light per day provides all
the light cannabis needs to sustain vegetative growth. Research shows plants grow faster
under 24 hours of continuous light. [[cannabis-cloning]], transplanting, pruning, and training are all
initiated during the vegetative stage under a long-day photoperiod.

## Flowering Photoperiod

Flowering is most efficiently induced with 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness. When
plants are at least two months old and have developed sexual characteristics, switching
to a 12/12 day/night schedule induces visible signs of flowering in one to three weeks.
Older plants tend to show signs of flowering sooner. The 12-hour photoperiod represents
the autumnal equinox and is the optimum daylight-to-dark relationship.

## Critical Dark Period

The dark period must be total and uninterrupted. Light interruption during the critical
dark period can revert plants to vegetative growth. This is why [[light-deprivation-cannabis]] covers must be completely light-tight, and indoor grow rooms must be sealed
against light leaks during the dark cycle. Even a brief flash of light during the
critical dark period can disrupt the phytochrome cycle.

## Less Than 12 Hours of Light

Research has proven that less than 12 hours of light will not induce flowering any faster
and actually reduces flower formation and yield. More than 12 hours of light often
prolongs flowering. The 12/12 schedule is the optimal balance for most strains. Giving
any cannabis variety less than 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness will not make it
flower faster -- instead, the plant will take longer to mature, buds will be smaller,
and the overall harvest will be lessened.

## Genetic Variation in Photoperiod Response

A relationship exists between photoperiod response and genetics. Sativa-dominant plants
from tropical regions near the equator (where day length varies little year-round) may
respond differently to photoperiod changes. The pure sativa 'Haze' flowers slowly for
three months or longer, even under 12/12. Indica-dominant strains typically flower faster
and more reliably under 12/12. Many indica varieties will flower under a 14/10 or
13/11 day/night photoperiod. On the equator, days and nights are almost the same length
year-round, and plants tend to bloom when they are chronologically ready.

More hours of light during flowering can cause some strains to produce bigger plants
with reduced flowering time, but some growers have reported looser, leafier buds as a
result.

## Exceptions: Ruderalis and Autoflowers

Photoperiod does not induce flowering in Cannabis ruderalis. This subspecies evolved in
northern latitudes with short summers and flowers based on age rather than day length.
Modern [[autoflowering-cannabis]] varieties inherit this trait and ignore photoperiod
cues entirely. Ruderalis crosses bloom in the middle of summer when the photoperiod
does not induce flowering in standard cannabis.

## Pre-Flowering Under Long Days

Plants grown from seed under 18/6 will show pre-flowers (indicating sex) around the
fourth week of vegetative growth, even though they are not receiving a flowering
photoperiod. This allows growers to identify and remove males before switching to 12/12.
Pre-flowers develop after the plant has completed its initial vegetative growth stage,
providing a window for sex identification.

## Photoperiod and Outdoor Growing

[[outdoor-cannabis-growing]], the natural photoperiod controls flowering. Plants begin flowering as days
shorten in late summer. In tropical regions near the equator, the near-equal day length
allows year-round flowering. [[light-deprivation-cannabis]] techniques allow outdoor and
greenhouse growers to manipulate the natural photoperiod.

## Photoperiod in Greenhouses

[[greenhouse-cannabis-growing]] operations can control photoperiod by covering the
greenhouse with light-blocking material for 12 hours daily. This enables multiple
harvests per year that would not be possible relying on natural day length alone. Cold
frames can also be blacked out to induce early flowering.

## Photoperiod and Latitude

The effectiveness of photoperiod manipulation varies with latitude. Near the equator,
day length changes very little throughout the year, which is why equatorial sativa
strains have a less pronounced flowering response. At higher latitudes, the dramatic
seasonal change in day length makes photoperiod control very effective. This is why
northern-indica strains respond more quickly and reliably to 12/12 than tropical
sativas.

## Photoperiod and Plant Age

Plants must be at least two months old and have developed sexual characteristics before
they will respond reliably to a flowering photoperiod. Younger plants may flower under
12/12 but will produce very small yields because they have not had sufficient
vegetative growth. Older, more mature plants show signs of flowering sooner when
switched to 12/12.
