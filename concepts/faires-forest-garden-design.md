# Forest Garden Design

The forest garden mimics natural forest ecosystems where plants grow at different heights, forming self-sufficient communities that maintain themselves with minimal human effort. Big trees form a canopy over smaller trees, which cover shade-loving shrubs, which shade groundcover herbs. On the edges, sun-loving edible plants grow. This approach is central to [[mollison-designers-temperate-zone-food-forest-design]] and [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]].

## Why Forest Gardens Work

A tree is massive biomass that affects everything around it. It provides homes for creatures and insects, its roots have beneficial fungi, and it changes temperature and microclimate. A large oak transpires 40,000 gallons of water per year, cooling surrounding air and creating precipitation. Trees store water in canopy and bark, which runs down to plants below. Roots hold moisture in the soil and moderate temperature extremes. The forest model is self-fertilizing, self-watering, and self-maintaining when properly established.

## The Seven Layers

Forest gardens are organized into seven vertical layers, each supporting different plant types:

| Layer | Description | Key Species |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| **Canopy** | Tall fruit and nut trees (40-100+ ft) | Oak, chestnut, walnut, hickory, beech |
| **Understory** | Smaller fruit trees (15-40 ft) | Apple, pear, plum, cherry, persimmon |
| **Shrubs** | Berry bushes and woody plants (6-15 ft) | Blueberry, currant, elderberry, hazelnut, raspberry |
| **Herbaceous** | Perennial herbs and vegetables | [[comfrey-multifunctional-guild-plant]], rhubarb, asparagus, artichoke, herbs |
| **Groundcover** | Low spreading plants | Strawberry, mint, creeping thyme, clover |
| **Root** | Root vegetables and tubers | Jerusalem artichoke, horseradish, garlic, potatoes |
| **Vine** | Climbing plants on trees and structures | Grape, kiwi, hops, passionflower, climbing beans |

See [[forest-garden-seven-layers]] and [[food-forest-layers-and-species]] for comprehensive guidance.

## Key Principles

1. **Plant by height**: Choose plants based on height, climbing ability, shade tolerance, and water needs
2. **Spacing by climate**: Wider spacing in dry/cool climates; closer in warm/humid (but not too close -- fungus risk)
3. **All-at-once planting** in fertile areas: walnuts (long-lived), fruit trees (shorter-lived), legumes (mulch), perennials (weed control)
4. **Total yield exceeds monoculture**: Individual plants may be smaller, but combined yield is greater with more security
5. **No rotation needed**: When many species grow together mimicking a forest, crop rotation is unnecessary
6. **Create relationships**: Arrange plants and animals so they form as many beneficial connections as possible

## Plant Selection Criteria

When choosing species for a forest garden, evaluate:
- Succession stage (pioneer, Stage 2, or Stage 3)
- Deciduous or evergreen
- Mature height and root invasiveness
- Growth rate and lifespan
- Leaf density (shade or light)
- Disease resistance and pruning sensitivity
- Climate and soil compatibility
- Spread tendency and rarity
- Yield timing and productivity duration
- Multiple products (leaves, roots, seeds, fruit)
- Self-storing ability (nuts, roots)

Record all answers on 3x5 index cards with growing instructions. See [[food-forest-plant-palette]] for curated species lists.

## Establishment Sequence

1. **Pioneer layer**: Existing "weeds" become mulch. Cut woody weeds flat, leave to decompose
2. **Sheet mulch**: Lay cardboard or old carpet to kill weeds (dead mulch) — see [[faires-hugelkultur-and-soil-building]]
3. **Soil building**: Add mulch, green manure, [[faires-composting-and-vermiculture]]. Allow geese/ducks to forage
4. **Nucleus planting**: Plant a group of trees as the center. Allow chickens to forage
5. **Management phase**: Once established, pigs and other animals may forage; trees produce fruit and mulch
6. **Cover cropping**: After main harvest, plant rye, clover, buckwheat, barley, oats, vetch for winter protection

Start with windbreaks and canopy trees. Plant in phases. Sheet mulch heavily. Full establishment can take 5-10 years. Pioneer species help build soil. [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]] should be distributed throughout. See [[permaculture-forest-garden-establishment]] for detailed timelines.

## Plant Communities (Guilds)

Organize plants around a central element based on [[permaculture-companion-planting]] and [[permaculture-design-course-methodology]]:

**Apple tree guild example**: Fava beans at base (nitrogen fixer), clover groundcover with dandelions and chicory, ring of [[comfrey-multifunctional-guild-plant]]/artichoke/yarrow/nasturtiums/dill, daffodils at perimeter (stop grass).

**Strategic plant functions**:
- **Attract predators**: Provide food/shelter to pest-eating insects
- **Sacrifice plants**: Attract pests away from main crops
- **Trap pests**: Attract and kill or trap pests
- **Provide nutrients**: Fix nitrogen, create bacteria, mulch when cut
- **Create shelter**: Prevent frost, block wind, create microclimates

## Animal Integration

Animals play key roles in a productive forest garden:

- **Chickens**: Forage fallen fruit, control pests, fertilize soil — see [[chicken-integration-permaculture-guide]]
- **Ducks**: Eat slugs and snails in the understory — see [[comparison-chicken-keeping-vs-duck-keeping]]
- **Geese**: Graze grass between trees
- **Bees**: Pollinate fruit trees and other crops — see beekeeping-basics|beekeeping
- **Rabbits**: In movable pens, they fertilize and mow — see [[comparison-quail-husbandry-vs-rabbit-husbandry]]

## Edge Effect and Microclimate

Forest gardens maximize the [[edge-effect-in-permaculture]] — the productive zone where two ecosystems meet. The transition from forest edge to open ground creates diverse microclimates supporting sun-loving and shade-tolerant species simultaneously. [[urban-permaculture-edge-effect-and-design-principles]] increases total yield.

## Coppicing and Pruning

[[faires-coppicing-and-woodlot-management]] and [[fruit-tree-propagation]] provide firewood, mulch, and basket material. Regular cutting of certain trees (hazel, willow, chestnut) stimulates regrowth and produces useful woody material without killing the tree.

## Polyculture vs. Monoculture

Monoculture maximizes individual plant yield. Polyculture maximizes total system yield. A forest garden takes 10+ years to establish and has no strict formula -- every climate and microclimate is different. The goal is many eggs in many baskets rather than one crop that can fail, requiring only a few people working part-time versus 80+ hours per week for a conventional organic farm. See succession-in-permaculture|succession and [[succession-in-permaculture]] for understanding how the system matures over time.
