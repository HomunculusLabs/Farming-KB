Indigenous and traditional agricultural societies have developed
sophisticated systems for classifying soils based on practical
observation and use. These systems often rival or exceed modern
scientific classifications in their relevance to local food
production. Understanding both traditional knowledge and physical soil
structure is essential for permaculture designers.

## Traditional Soil Classification Systems

Tribal and traditional people classify their soils on a great variety
of characteristics that reflect deep observational knowledge built
over generations:

### Classification by Observable Properties

- Colour, which indicates humus content and organic matter levels
- Taste, which agrees with modern pH measurements
- Moisture capacity and water retention characteristics
- Sand content and general texture
- Firmness and compaction resistance
- Structure in both dry and wet seasons
- Organic content, assessed apart from colour alone

### Classification by Biological Indicators

- Vegetative indicators, reflecting the health of a specific crop
- Plant indicators for acidity, drainage, and fertility
- Animal indicators, such as termites, where the shape and size of
  their mounds reveal soil characteristics

### Classification by Position and Use

- Drainage characteristics and natural water movement
- Slope, elevation, and catena, the sequence of soil types based on
  slope relationships from ridge to valley
- Relative fertility described as hot and cold soils, indicating
  not temperature but nutrient availability and water retention
- Usage for specific purposes such as pigments, pottery, and salt
  extraction from reeds
- Work needed for crop, an energy input classification
- Suitability for specific crops, with soils ranked for up to twelve
  crop types, giving a complex multi-dimensional classification

## Modern Soil Classifications

Modern classifications are more complex in terms of nomenclature and
physical categories, using standard colour charts such as the Munsell
system, standard comparisons, and standard sieves. However, the
sophisticated assessments available from most agricultural societies
demonstrate that practical soil knowledge long predates formal
scientific systems.

## The Structure of Soils

Soil is a complex material. If it has enough plasticity, usually from
clay content, or glue or fibre from organic sources, it can be pressed
or compacted into mudbricks, hard pise, or baked to clay or stoneware.
In any of these forms, it is of little use to plants.

Uncompacted soils are open, crumbly, or soft unless concreted by
chemical solutes or compacted by ploughs, hooves, or traffic. Crumbly
soils have a definite structure. The soil particles are in nodules or
clumps held together by roots, clay minerals, and chemical bonds. If
we speed a plough or drag harrows through these fragile assemblies,
they may powder up as they do in a potter's ballmill.

Dryland soils with a high salt content are particularly susceptible to
loss of [[crumb-structure]], only partly relieved by application of gypsum.

## The Five Components of Soil

The mantle of soil and subsoil that covers the earth is as thin as
the shine on the skin of an orange, and this mantle extends as living
mud below the waters of earth as well as on land. It is composed of:

- Minerals, mainly silica, oxides of iron and aluminium, and complex
  mineral compounds
- Soil waters, fresh, saline, with differing pH, and dissolved
  minerals and gases
- Gases, some from the atmosphere, others emitted by the breakdown of
  rocks and the earth's interior
- Life forms, from fungal spores and bacteria to wombats and ground
  squirrels, from massive roots to minute motile algae
- Once-living remains, the humus of the earth, decayed, compressed,
  and fossil organic material

Soils rarely extend much below 1 to 2 metres, and are more often a
living system only 6 to 12 cm deep. Subsoils, lacking the life
components, and buried soils or deep washed silts are rare and
confined to valley floors, deltas, and glacial mounds.

## Particle Size and the Jar Test

To estimate the proportion of clay, silt, sand, and coarse particles
in soil, a sufficient first test is to mix a sample of soil from a
few typical sites, pour a cup of soil in a tall jar filled almost
with water, shake vigorously, and let the soil fractions settle out
over a day or a week. Clay can remain in suspension for up to a week.

For practical purposes, 40% or so clay is needed for dam walls, and
less than that for good mud bricks without lime or cement added. Of
these fractions, coarse particles are inert although useful in fine
soils as a wind-erosion deterrent. Sands are 0.05 to 2 mm, silt is
0.02 to 0.05 mm, and clay particles are less than 0.002 mm. One gram
of clay has a surface area of up to 1000 times that of one gram of
sand.

## Crumb Structure and Pore Space

Soil crumb structure, aided by lime as a calcium source, helps bond
particles together and creates 20 to 60% pore space. The organic
materials and gels hold the structure open in rain, and enable plant
nutrients to become soluble for absorption by roots. This pore space
is critical for both water infiltration and gas exchange, making crumb
structure one of the most important properties for plant growth.

The USDA classification of soil types by particle size provides a
standard reference, but the jar test remains one of the most
accessible and practical field methods for assessing soil composition
for specific uses in permaculture design.
