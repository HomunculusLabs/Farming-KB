# Fire Effect on Forest Soils and Nutrient Loss

## Overview

Fire has profound and long-lasting effects on forest soils and their nutrient status. While fire sharply reduces litter and leaves a nutrient-rich light ash, the net effect on soil health is overwhelmingly negative. Understanding these impacts is essential for land managers seeking to maintain soil fertility and ecosystem health in fire-prone landscapes.

## Fire Temperature and Nutrient Volatilisation

Fires "glow" at approximately 650°C and burn fiercely at 1,100–1,400°C when strong winds blow. At these temperatures, various soil nutrients are volatilised and lost to the atmosphere:

| Element | Volatilisation Temperature |
|---------|--------------------------|
| Sulphur and Nitrogen | Volatilised during glow phase (~650°C) |
| Carbon | Volatilised during fierce burning |
| Phosphorus | 774°C |
| Potassium | 774°C |
| Calcium | 1,484°C (cement structures powder at this temperature) |

Organic compounds containing these elements may volatilise more easily than soil elements, meaning the nutrient loss from burnt foliage and organic matter is often greater than from mineral soil alone.

## Nutrient Losses from Low-Intensity Burns

Even in low-intensity burns, significant nutrient losses occur from the soil:

| Nutrient | Percentage Lost | Amount (kg/ha) | Recovery Time |
|----------|----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Nitrogen | 54–75% | 109 kg/ha | ~11 years (via legumes and rain) |
| Phosphorus | 37–50% | 3.0 kg/ha | 20+ years (via rain) |
| Potassium | 43–66% | — | — |
| Calcium | 31–34% | — | — |
| Magnesium | 25–43% | — | — |
| Boron | 35–54% | — | — |

*(Figures sourced from Ecos, 42, Summer 84/85)*

## Additional Foliage Losses

Beyond soil nutrient loss, fires burning shrub and tree foliage cause additional nutrient depletion. This is particularly severe when fires occur early in the growth season, when plants have invested nutrients in new growth that is then entirely lost.

## Ash and Erosion

The nutrient-rich light ash left after fire can:
- Wash or blow away from the site
- Wash into lakes and streams as a clear or cloudy nutrient load
- Cause nutrient pollution in waterways

This means the apparent "fertilising" effect of ash is often temporary and translocated away from where it is most needed — the forest floor where it was generated.

## Soil Structure Damage

### Humus Loss
[[humus]] loss of 10–12 cm occurs in forest soils after fire. This represents centuries of organic matter accumulation destroyed in hours. Humus is critical for:
- Water retention and infiltration
- Soil aggregation and structure
- [[cation-exchange-capacity]]
- Microbial habitat and activity
- Root penetration

### Peat Combustion
Peat soils often combust to depths well below the surface, destroying the entire organic soil profile. Peat fires can smoulder underground for months, creating a persistent and difficult-to-extinguish fire hazard.

### Clay Structure Degradation
Clays lose their structure at high fire temperatures. The collapse of clay structure reduces:
- Water-holding capacity
- [[soil-aeration]]
- Root penetration ability
- Nutrient retention

### Mud Flows
The combination of humus loss, clay structure collapse, and vegetation destruction can result in mud flows after rain — a secondary hazard that can cause significant damage downslope of burnt areas.

## Recovery Dynamics

### Slow Nutrient Recovery
Soil nutrient recovery after fire is very slow and depends on:
- Trace elements brought in by rain (atmospheric deposition)
- Trace elements brought in by birds (guano, carcasses)
- Minerals recycled to topsoil by deep-rooted vegetation
- Nitrogen fixation by leguminous plants (primary nitrogen recovery pathway)

### The Recovery Curve
The recovery of arboreal animal populations after fire follows a sigmoid curve — slow initial recovery followed by accelerating return. This same curve applies to the slow increase of fire-removed nutrients in soils and leaf litter. Full recovery to pre-fire nutrient levels can take decades.

### Key Principle
Fires never improve soil status. This is a fundamental principle that contradicts the common misconception that fire "fertilises" the soil with ash. While ash does contain readily available nutrients, the total nutrient loss far exceeds the temporary availability of ash minerals, and the destruction of soil structure and organic matter creates long-term deficits.

## Implications for Land Management

### Prevention Over Remediation
The extremely slow recovery of soil nutrients after fire — particularly nitrogen (11 years) and phosphorus (20+ years) — strongly argues for fire prevention rather than reliance on post-fire recovery. Every fire event sets back soil development by decades.

### Fuel Reduction Methods
The safest fuel reduction methods (as opposed to "cool burning") are:
- Grazing off excess vegetation
- Slashing and [[composting]] in swales
- Using material as firewood
- Replacing tinder-dry material with sappy green plants

### The Ida and Jean Pain System
Ida and Jean Pain developed a broadscale, beneficial fire-reduction system that addresses soil health while reducing fire risk:
1. Chip all dry forest fuels
2. [[compost]] the chips or use them for biogas production
3. The biogas fuels the chipping and carting operations
4. Compost and sludge are used to grow gardens and improve soils
5. Improved soils further reduce litter accumulation

This closed-loop system is recommended for every bioregion where forests are to be preserved and eventually made fireproof.

### Bioregional Planning
Part of bioregional planning must include keeping monocultures of inflammable trees to uninhabited ridgetops, or better, scattering such stands throughout grazed or wet-forested areas where they can be managed with fuel reduction programs.
