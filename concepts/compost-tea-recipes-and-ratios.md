## Compost Tea Recipes and Ratios

Developing an effective [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] tea (AACT) recipe requires understanding that
these are living brews — the biology dictates the ratios, not the other way around. Dr. Elaine
Ingham emphasizes that a recipe is only as good as the testing that confirms it actually
produces the desired [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]]. Following someone else's recipe without understanding
the underlying mechanisms can lead to failed brews, crop damage, or worse.

## Compost-to-Water Ratios

The relationship between compost amount and brew volume is **not linear**. The ratio depends on
compost quality, target organism densities, and — critically — the machine design.

- **Small scale (5-gallon brews):** Start at approximately **1 pound of compost per 5 gallons**
  of water.
- **Commercial brewers (500 gallons):** Ratios of **15–20 pounds of compost per 500 gallons**
  are common — far less than a linear extrapolation would suggest.

The biology in compost provides inoculum, and given adequate food and oxygen, those organisms
multiply during brewing. At larger volumes, extraction efficiency improves because compost
remains suspended longer. Overloading compost in a large brewer can create anaerobic pockets.
Different machines extract organisms at different rates, so recipes must be tuned to your
specific brewer's oxygen transfer, agitation, and diffuser design.

## Compost Composition

The starting compost is the most critical ingredient. Ingham recommends a blend of:

- **50–50 blend:** Equal parts aerobic thermal compost and aerobic worm castings for a broad
  spectrum of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes.
- **25–75 blend:** 25% aerobic thermal compost and 75% aerobic worm castings for bacterial
  dominance (worm castings are bacterially dominated).

The word **aerobic** is non-negotiable. [[anaerobic-compost-brews]] or castings introduce undesirable
organisms and metabolites.

### Cedar Grove Warning

**Cedar Grove compost products should be avoided.** Their [[enriched-sawdust-cultivation-and-spawn-production-methods]] do not maintain
adequate aerobic conditions, resulting in unsuitable organism profiles for tea brewing. Always
source compost from verified aerobic operations.

## Molasses: Quantity and Aeration Quality

Molasses is the most common bacterial food additive, but the amount must be calibrated to the
machine's aeration capacity. Well-aerated machines can handle **1–4 tablespoons per 5 gallons**;
poorly aerated machines should use molasses sparingly or not at all.

### Growing Solutions Machine Warning

The Growing Solutions machine has a documented risk of **E. coli** proliferation when molasses
is added at standard rates. Its aeration design cannot maintain adequate dissolved oxygen with
excess sugar. Either eliminate molasses or reduce it dramatically and verify with lab testing.
This illustrates why you cannot transfer recipes between machines — aeration capacity varies
enormously between commercial units.

## Sugar Addition and Root-Feeding Nematodes

A persistent claim holds that adding sugar to compost tea controls root-feeding nematodes via
alcohol production. Ingham addresses this with scientific rigor.
The proposed mechanism: sugar causes bacteria to produce alcohol, which then kills nematodes.
This pathway has significant gaps:

1. **Dilution:** Alcohol concentration in tea is extremely low, further diluted in soil.
2. **Aerobic conditions:** Proper aeration minimizes alcohol production; aerobic metabolism
   favors CO₂ and water.
3. **Soil fate:** Any alcohol is quickly metabolized by soil organisms or volatilizes.

### When It Might Work (Indirectly)

Sugar additions *can* indirectly suppress root-feeding nematodes through a different mechanism:
sugar fuels bacterial growth, which attracts bacterial-feeding nematodes and protozoa that
compete with or consume root-feeding species. The key is building a balanced soil food web,
not a direct chemical kill. If soil already has adequate bacteria, or if soil is compacted,
no suppression occurs.

Ingham's core point: **understand the mechanism before making claims.** Saying "molasses kills
nematodes" is misleading when the actual mechanism is competitive exclusion. Misunderstanding
leads to incorrect modifications, failed expectations, and discredit for compost tea as a
legitimate practice.

## Kelp: Amount and Rationale

Kelp provides micronutrients, growth hormones (cytokinins and auxins), and fungal food. The
quantity depends on two factors:

1. **Plant nutrient needs:** Heavy-feeding crops in rapid growth benefit from more kelp;
   established perennials need less.
2. **Fungal surface area requirements:** For fungal-dominated teas (perennials, trees, pastures),
   enough kelp must support extensive hyphal growth — typically **1–4 tablespoons per 5 gallons**.

## Fungal Food Preferences

Fungi have different food preferences than bacteria. While molasses is excellent bacterial food,
it is poor for fungal growth.

- **Humic acid:** The premier fungal food. Provides complex carbon molecules that fungi decompose.
- **Oatmeal:** Finely ground oatmeal contains complex carbohydrates, proteins, and fibers
  requiring fungal enzymes, selectively feeding fungi over bacteria.

Molasses is simple sugar — bacteria metabolize it far faster. When molasses dominates, bacteria
outcompete fungi. For fungal teas, minimize molasses and use humic acid, oatmeal, fish
hydrolysate, and kelp instead.

## Testing Protocols

Every tea operation should implement at minimum these verification steps:

### 1. The Smell Test

Finished tea should smell **earthy and sweet**. Problem odors: sour/vinegar (anaerobic), rotten
egg (severe oxygen deficiency), septic/sewage (pathogens — discard immediately), or no smell
(insufficient biology).

### 2. The Sealed Container Test

Fill a sealed jar with finished tea, leave at room temperature 24–48 hours. Good tea forms a
thin foam and stays earthy; poor tea develops foul odors within hours as hidden anaerobes take
over. This reveals anaerobes the smell test misses in fresh brews.

### 3. Laboratory Analysis

Periodic lab analysis is essential for commercial operations. [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] at 400×
magnification reveals organism types, diversity, and activity. Soil food web laboratories
provide exact counts of total and active bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Use lab
analysis when developing recipes, changing inputs, and for ongoing quality assurance.

## Recipe Development Approach

Building an effective recipe is iterative:

1. **Start with proven aerobic compost** — non-negotiable foundation.
2. **Choose target biology** — bacterial dominance (annuals), fungal dominance (perennials,
   trees), or balanced.
3. **Select foods accordingly** — molasses for bacterial teas; humic acid, oatmeal, fish
   hydrolysate for fungal teas.
4. **Brew, test, adjust** — run smell and sealed container tests after each batch.
5. **Lab verification** — confirm organism profiles match targets.
6. **Document everything** — compost source, weights, amendments, brew time, temperature,
   aeration settings, and all test results.
