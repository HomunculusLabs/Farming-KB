# Dryland Settlement Vegetation and Fuel Forests

Rigorous vegetative design of settlement calls for adapted perennials for both food and shade, hardy and preferably local species in shrubberies, large areas of overhead vine trellis, and only a strictly regulated amount of lawn.

## Lawn and Water Policy

Lawns can be of drought-resistant groundcovers such as **Lippia** or other carpeting plants. However, golf courses, extensive grass lawns, car washes, and large open pools or ponds supplied at public cost are identified as **dryland disasters** which need to be taxed out of existence in arid lands.

## Water-Dependent Trees in Drylands

Many very hardy and useful trees need only a **[[prosopis]]* or similar high-shade species to mitigate this.

### National-Scale Requirements

On a national scale, **tree belts 1-5 km wide** on the savannah edges of deserts are imperative. Without them, advected hot winds affect crops for hundreds of kilometres into humid areas, and bushfire frequency is steeply increased.

Very large **buffer zones of ungrazed or lightly grazed desert** can be developed around villages. Pitted or swaled, these areas completely defend against dust, preserving both public and environmental health.

## Fuel Forests

The last area of settlement vegetation to plan is that of **fuel forests** for cooking and (if necessary) power supply, essential oils, mulch, and other tree products such as honey.

### Sewage-Fed Fuel Forests

If the settlement is sewered, surplus greywater and sewage can be processed through a sophisticated system:

1. **Collection ponds** — deep (to 3 m), narrow, preferably roofed or trellised, and well-sealed
2. **Primary digester and settling ponds** — chipper for organic wastes, alcohol ferment digester, and methane engine house
3. **Secondary settling** — clear water routed to fuel forests
4. **Dripline distribution** — carefully selected and designed fuel forest receives processed water

This system is in effect at **Yulara, near Uluru (Ayers Rock)** in Central Australia, where river red gum (*[[casuarina]] cunninghamii*, and trees adapted to root water in drylands form fuel forests surplus to settlement needs.

### Two-Element Fuel Forest Design

A fuel plantation needs two elements:

1. **Perpetual long-term forest matrix** — a grid of permanent forest no more than 300 m apart, nor less than 8 trees wide, initially established by trickle irrigation but selected to be very hardy on rainfall alone once established. These provide permanent windbreak and microclimate.

2. **Coppiced rotation blocks** — in the sheltered spaces, a grid of close-spaced trees (2-3 m) on trickle irrigation pipes (preferably automatic) which are harvested in a **4-6 year rotation as coppice**.

### Coppice Management

All fuel tree species should initially be selected as **coppice types**. Continuing field selection of seed can then proceed for varieties, sub-species, or provenance (locality) types that respond very well to this form of cultivation.

Key management principles:

- **Cut one-fourth to one-sixth of the crop annually** for sustainable harvest
- **Assess solid fuel need per household** and govern population level accordingly
- Such coppiced forest areas can provide all essential cooking and public energy needs

### Biogas Integration

If the settling pond or tank is **anaerobic** and harvested as a methane source, and if all organic wastes are shredded and added to this tank, energy production is maximized. Much of the shredded organic waste will support **ferment to alcohol fuels** before being routed to biogas production.

> No organic matter can be wasted in deserts, and all can be turned to productive use.

### Swale-Based Fuel Forests

Where sewage is not available, a series of **close-spaced swales** (4-8 m wide) with banks between them only 3-5 m wide, supplied by extensive interceptor banks to harvest broadscale runoff, will also support a fuel forest.

## Settlement Layout Principles

A carefully designed dryland settlement incorporates these vegetative elements in a specific layout:

- **Entry roads** sited to enter downslope and crosswind, with swales leading off to forests so no dust blows to housing
- **Narrow east-west through-roads** tree-shaded and swaled, accepting town water
- **Commerce centres** with trellised lanes and shaded parks
- **Sewage or swale-fed fuel forests** on 6-year rotations, surrounded by belts of perpetual forests, using gravity flow
- **Primary digester and settling ponds** with chipper, alcohol ferment digester, and methane engine house
- **Windbreak forests 400 m deep** sheltering settlement and preventing dust and desiccating winds
- **Planted and maintained rangelands** fenced out from settlement for essential animal products on long-term rotation

## The Essential Principle

Wherever water is harvested, it should be to **create forests**. Not to plant trees may mean creating waterlogged and anaerobic desert soils, perpetuating or extending salt problems. Trees are the **groundwater moderators of the drylands**—just as they are the nutrient storages of the humid tropics.

The historical error has been to develop grain foods at the expense of trees, extending grasslands and crops until desert is created, or destroying trees until salts in desert soils create surface crusts. A gradation from crops and grazing to tree products and forages is essential, and if enough trees are planted, modest grain and pasture strips can also be developed within the protected zone.
