# Brassinosteroid Effects on Fungal Growth

Brassinosteroids are a class of naturally occurring steroidal plant-growth regulators first isolated from brassica pollen in 1979. They are structurally similar to animal steroid hormones and are found throughout the plant kingdom, where they regulate cell elongation, cell division, vascular differentiation, and reproductive development. While their role in plant physiology has been extensively documented, evidence suggests that these compounds may also influence the growth and development of higher fungi, a domain in which they have received comparatively little attention.




## The Gartz et al. Study (1990)

The most significant investigation into brassinosteroid effects on fungi was conducted by [[Gartz-Jörg]], [[Adam-L]], and [[Vorbrodt-HM]] in 1990. Their study examined the impact of [[22S-23S-homobrassinolide]] on the cultivation of [[psilocybe-cubensis]], a basidiomycete mushroom widely used as a model organism in mycological research. The researchers sought to determine whether exogenous application of brassinosteroids could accelerate mycelial growth and improve fruiting body yield.




## Cultivation Methods

The study employed two distinct cultivation systems to assess brassinosteroid effects:

- **Agar medium:** A 6% [[malt-extract-agar]] substrate was used to measure radial mycelial growth rates. Brassinosteroid solutions were applied to the agar surface at controlled concentrations.
- **Compost substrate:** A bulk substrate composed of horse dung and rice grain, supplemented with [[KH2PO4]] (potassium dihydrogen phosphate) as a buffer and nutrient source, was used for fruiting trials. This substrate was sterilized and inoculated with mycelial cultures before being placed in fruiting conditions with controlled humidity and temperature.




## Mycelial Growth Enhancement

On 6% malt agar, the application of 22S,23S-homobrassinolide produced a striking acceleration in mycelial extension. Treated cultures exhibited growth rates approximately two to three times faster than untreated controls. This dose-dependent response demonstrated that brassinosteroids can directly stimulate vegetative growth in fungal mycelium, paralleling their well-established role in promoting cell expansion and division in plant tissues.




## Fruiting Body Development

The most practically significant findings emerged from the compost substrate trials. When Psilocybe cubensis was cultivated on horse dung and rice grain substrate treated with brassinosteroids:

- **Time to fruiting was reduced** from the typical 4 to 5 weeks down to 3 to 3.5 weeks, representing a 25 to 30% reduction in the cultivation cycle.
- **First flush yields increased substantially**, with treated cultures producing 4 to 7 mushrooms compared to only 1 to 3 mushrooms in untreated control groups.
- **Dry mass per 10 grams of substrate** rose to 3.4 to 3.9 grams in treated cultures, compared to 2.3 to 2.9 grams in controls, reflecting improved biological efficiency.
- **Incomplete fruit body formation was suppressed.** Control cultures frequently produced abortive or incompletely developed primordia that failed to mature, whereas brassinosteroid-treated cultures showed a marked reduction in this phenomenon.




## Morphological Changes

Beyond quantitative yield improvements, brassinosteroid treatment induced notable morphological alterations in the fruiting bodies. Treated specimens displayed stems that lacked the typical membranous annulus (the ring or skirt remnant of the partial veil) normally present in Psilocybe cubensis. This deviation from the standard morphological pattern suggests that brassinosteroids may interfere with normal developmental pathways governing veil formation and remnant retention, potentially by modulating the timing or expression of genes involved in tissue differentiation.




## Biological Implications

The responsiveness of Psilocybe cubensis to brassinosteroids raises an important question: do these steroidal compounds play an endogenous biological role in higher fungi? While brassinosteroids have not been conclusively identified as native metabolites in fungal organisms, their pronounced effects on fungal growth and development suggest that fungi may possess receptor systems or signaling pathways capable of recognizing and responding to steroidal signals. This possibility has parallels in the animal kingdom, where fungal sterols such as [[ergosterol]] serve fundamental membrane and signaling functions. If brassinosteroids or analogous steroidal compounds prove to be endogenous fungal regulators, it would significantly expand our understanding of fungal developmental biology and the evolutionary conservation of steroid-based signaling mechanisms.




## Broader Implications for Mushroom Cultivation

The results demonstrated by Gartz and colleagues point to practical applications in both commercial and experimental mushroom cultivation. The ability to reduce cultivation cycle times by nearly a third while simultaneously increasing yields and reducing abortive fruiting represents a compelling improvement in cultivation efficiency. These benefits are relevant to:

- **Gourmet mushroom production**, where shorter turnaround times translate directly into increased throughput and profitability for cultivators of species such as [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] and [[agaricus-bisporus]].
- **Medicinal mushroom cultivation**, particularly for species like [[ganoderma-lucidum]] where biological efficiency and consistent fruiting are commercially important.
- **Conservation mycology**, where accelerated cultivation could aid in the ex-situ propagation of rare or threatened fungal species.
- **Research applications**, where the ability to manipulate developmental timing provides a tool for studying fungal life cycles and reproductive biology.

However, it is important to note that the use of synthetic brassinosteroids as cultivation additives may face regulatory hurdles in food and pharmaceutical contexts, and further research would be needed to assess any effects on the chemical profile of fruiting bodies intended for consumption.
