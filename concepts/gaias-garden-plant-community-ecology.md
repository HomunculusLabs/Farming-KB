## What Are Plant Communities?

Certain species seem always to show up with the same companions.
In the arid West, pinon pine and juniper appear together, and
with them frequently are Gambel's oak and mountain mahogany. In
the East, a common community is the oak/hickory forest, with
mapleleaf viburnum and dogwoods often filling the understory.

There are hundreds of plant communities, and each contains a
recognizable array of trees, shrubs, and flowers whose
composition varies from one community to the next. These
communities can include specific animals, too. Oak/hickory
forests are home specifically to blue jays, tanagers, and
grosbeaks. Different environments favor different communities.

## Do Plant Communities Really Exist?

Ecologists have argued for decades about whether plant
communities are real entities or merely random assemblies of
species that happen to like the same climate, soil, and other
environmental conditions. The evidence cuts both ways.

In support of the random-grouping argument, any two examples
of the same community always contain different species and
numbers of plants. No two communities are alike. As the climate
changes, species drop out here and new ones move in there,
rather than changing abruptly.

On the other hand, a plant community has a definite structure.
If it lacks certain members, the community as a whole suffers.
Douglas fir forests that lack a particular truffle fungus are
not as healthy as those that do. Without the truffle, the fir
forest will not have many red-backed voles. Lacking voles to
eat, the spotted owl population will diminish. This poverty
ripples through many species, and the whole community is
diminished.

## From Natural Communities to Garden Guilds

In the ecological garden, we steal a page from nature's book
and often group plants in communities. Some gardeners have
re-created the natural plant communities that occur in their
region, while others have tinkered with plant groupings,
swapping some plants with human uses or other functions in
place of natives.

A natural community of plants has evolved guilds. A healthy
plant community recycles its own nutrients, requires minimal
input from outside, and provides habitat for wildlife. A guild,
on the other hand, is a human-made assemblage that mimics a
natural community.

The difference between natural gardens and ecological gardens
is instructive. Natural gardens consist almost exclusively of
native plants and attempt to mimic native plant communities.
Ecological gardens combine natives, food plants, medicinal and
culinary herbs, insect- and bird-attracting species, and plants
that build soil into synergistic, mutually beneficial groupings.

## Designing Guilds from Plant Communities

The process of creating a guild from a native plant community
involves several steps:

1. **Identify a suitable native plant community** for your
   region and site conditions
2. **List all community members** from botanical references
   or field guides
3. **Scan for useful species** that yield food, habitat, or
   other gifts for people and wildlife
4. **Substitute domesticated varieties** where appropriate,
   replacing wild relatives with improved cultivars
5. **Replace undesirable members** with functional equivalents
   that fill the same ecological role

## Example: The White Oak/Hazelnut Community

Hemenway works through the Oregon white oak community in detail.
This community contains white oak, California hazelnut, Pacific
madrone, mazzard cherry, black hawthorn, serviceberry,
oceanspray, snowberry, thimbleberry, trailing blackberry,
sweetbriar rose, strawberry, poison oak, yerba buena, sweet
cicely, and American vetch.

With exploration and fine-tuning, this community can be
transformed into a useful guild. White oak serves as the canopy
tree. Hazelnut can be upgraded to domesticated filberts with
heavier nut production. Wild cherries and hawthorns become
grafted fruit-bearing varieties. Thornless blackberries replace
the wild types. The nitrogen-fixing vetch supports fertility.

## How Ecosystems Support Themselves

A natural plant community supports itself through several
mechanisms that ecological gardeners seek to replicate:

- **Nutrient cycling** where decomposition returns minerals
  to plant-available forms
- **Nitrogen fixation** by leguminous community members
- **Pest regulation** through predator-prey relationships
  among community inhabitants
- **Mulch generation** from leaf drop and plant decomposition
- **Microclimate creation** through layered canopy structure
- **Water management** through root systems and ground cover
