# Growing Leeks Organically

Leeks are a superb winter vegetable, hardy in all but the very coldest
climates, and a valuable source of fresh greens when little else is
available in the garden. They are easy to grow, relatively undemanding,
and store well in the ground throughout winter. As members of the
[[hamilton-bulb-vegetables-onion-family-organic]], leeks
share many soil preferences with onions and garlic but have their own
distinct planting and cultivation methods.

## Soil and Site Preparation

Leeks prefer an open, sunny position and a soil rich in organic matter.
For [[hamilton-vegetable-bed-preparation]], dig in
plenty of well-rotted compost or manure. If the soil is acidic, apply
lime to raise the pH above 6.5, as leeks do best in neutral to slightly
alkaline conditions. A dressing of blood, fish, and bone meal worked
into the top few inches of soil provides a good foundation for growth.

In the [[hamilton-deep-bed-system]], leeks can be
planted in blocks of staggered rows, which makes efficient use of space
and simplifies the earthing-up process later in the season.

## Sowing and Planting

Leeks can be raised in one of several ways. The traditional method is
to sow seeds in a seedbed outdoors in shallow rows about six inches
(15cm) apart during mid-spring. The seedlings grow on in the seedbed
until early summer, when they are transplanted to their permanent
positions.

Alternatively, leeks can be multiple sown — three or four seeds placed
together in modules — and planted out as small clumps at twelve-inch
(30cm) spacing each way in the deep bed. This method eliminates the
need for a separate seedbed and simplifies planting.

Leeks can also be started in seed boxes or trays in the greenhouse at
about 60 degrees Fahrenheit (15 degrees Celsius) in mid-spring, giving
them a head start before planting out in early summer.

### The Dibber Method

The classic technique for planting leeks uses a dibber. Make holes
about six to eight inches (15 to 20cm) deep and six inches (15cm)
apart, with twelve inches (30cm) between rows. Trim the roots of the
leek seedlings by about two-thirds and the leaf tips by about half,
then drop one plant into each hole.

Do not refill the holes with soil. Instead, pour a little water into
each hole to wash enough soil over the roots to settle them. The
holes will gradually fill in naturally with rain and subsequent
cultivation. This method produces leeks with well-blanched, long white
stems.

## Maintenance and Care

Keep the rows weed free by regular hoeing. As the leeks grow, pull a
little soil around the base of each stem to blanch them. Take care to
prevent soil from getting between the leaves, as soil trapped inside
the leek is unpleasant to eat. Earthing up can be done gradually over
several weeks during late summer and autumn.

In the [[hamilton-crop-rotation-and-vegetable-planning]],
leeks follow in the potato and root vegetable group alongside onions,
shallots, and garlic. They are not particularly demanding feeders but
benefit from the organic matter incorporated during bed preparation.

## Varieties

Musselburgh is a popular and very reliable variety, producing thick
stems and excellent hardiness. Titan has a very long stem with a good
blanched base. The Lyon Prizetaker is valued for its excellent flavour
and good winter hardiness. For exhibition or particularly long stems,
choose varieties specifically bred for length.

## Harvesting

Leeks are quite hardy and can usually be left in the ground until
required throughout winter. Harvest by lifting carefully with a fork,
starting with the largest plants first and leaving smaller ones to
continue growing. If there is a danger of severe frost making the soil
too hard to dig, lift a few plants and store them in a box of moist
material in a cool, frost-free place.

## Pests and Diseases

Leeks are generally trouble-free compared with many vegetables. They
can be affected by the general range of garden pests but are notably
resistant to most serious problems. Good [[hamilton-organic-soil-management]] and crop rotation are the best preventive measures.

Occasional attacks by leek moth may occur — the caterpillars feed
inside the leaves, creating visible damage and brown patches. Inspect
plants regularly and remove any affected foliage. Allium leaf miner is
a more recent pest that can cause significant damage; covering crops
with fine mesh netting provides effective protection. Onion fly may
also attack leeks, though they are generally less susceptible than
onions.

Rust can appear as orange pustules on the foliage, particularly in
damp autumn weather. While unsightly, it rarely causes serious harm
to the plants and can be managed by removing affected leaves and
ensuring good air circulation between plants. Avoid overhead watering
which can spread fungal spores.

## Storing and Using Leeks

While leeks are best used fresh from the garden, they can be stored
for short periods. Lifted leeks keep well in a box of moist peat
substitute in a cool, frost-free place for several weeks. For longer
storage, leeks can be sliced and frozen — blanch briefly in boiling
water, cool rapidly, and freeze in sealed bags.

In the kitchen, leeks are enormously versatile. They form the base
of soups, stews, and stocks, can be braised or roasted as a vegetable
side dish, and pair beautifully with cheese in pies and tarts. The
milder flavour compared with onions makes them popular in delicate
dishes where a subtler allium taste is desired.

## Summary

Leeks are one of the most rewarding and trouble-free vegetables for
the organic garden. Their value as a winter crop makes them essential
for maintaining year-round vegetable production. The dibber planting
method is simple and effective, producing beautifully blanched stems.
Combine leeks with other [[hamilton-bulb-vegetables-onion-family-organic]] in your rotation plan, give them a sunny position with rich
soil, and enjoy fresh greens from the garden throughout the coldest
months of the year.
