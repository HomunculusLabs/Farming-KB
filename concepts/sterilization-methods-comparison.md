## Ultraviolet (UV) Irradiation

### Mechanism
UV-C light (254 nm) damages microbial DNA by forming thymine dimers,
preventing replication and causing [[microwave-reflective-transparent-materials-surface-sterilization]]
- UV lamps degrade over time, requiring regular replacement and intensity
  monitoring
- Some organisms have DNA repair mechanisms that can reverse UV damage
- Ozone generation as a byproduct requires ventilation
- Material degradation of exposed plastics and rubbers over time

## Gamma Irradiation

### Mechanism
High-energy photons (gamma rays, typically from cobalt-60 or cesium-137
sources) ionize molecules and break chemical bonds, destroying
microorganisms through direct DNA damage and indirect free radical damage.

### Advantages
- Excellent penetration through materials
- No chemical residues
- Can sterilize sealed packages
- Highly reliable and well-validated for medical devices

### Limitations
- Requires a radiation source facility — extremely expensive infrastructure
- Regulatory complexity — licensing, shielding, and safety requirements
- Potential material degradation (embrittlement of polymers, color changes)
- Not suitable for in-situ or point-of-use sterilization
- Long turn-around times when outsourced to a irradiation facility

## Chemical Disinfectants

### Common Agents

- **Ethylene oxide (EtO)**: Alkylates DNA and proteins. Effective against
  all organisms but highly toxic and carcinogenic. Requires lengthy
  aeration periods (12–48 hours) to remove residues.
