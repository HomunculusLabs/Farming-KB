# Comparison of Surface Sterilization Methods

Surface sterilization is a critical requirement across fields including
aerospace, medicine, pharmaceutical manufacturing, food safety, and
[[mycology]]. Multiple methods exist, each with distinct advantages and
limitations. Understanding these tradeoffs is essential for selecting the
appropriate method for a given application.

## Autoclaving (Steam Under Pressure)

### Mechanism
Moist heat under pressure (typically 121°C at 15 PSI) denatures proteins
and destroys cell membranes. The high temperature and steam penetration
provide thorough sterilization.

### Advantages
- Gold standard for reliability — well-understood and validated
- Effective against all microorganisms including bacterial spores
- Penetrates fabrics, porous materials, and some sealed containers
- Low per-cycle cost after equipment purchase
- Leaves no [[ingham-manure-antibiotics-chemical-residues-composting]]

### Limitations
- High thermal impact — can damage heat-sensitive materials and adjacent
  systems
- Requires significant time for heating, sterilizing, and cooling
- Cannot be used for sterilizing complex sealed fixtures without
  disassembly
- Requires a pressure vessel, which adds complexity and safety concerns

## Ultraviolet (UV) Irradiation

### Mechanism
UV-C light (254 nm) damages microbial DNA by forming thymine dimers,
preventing replication and causing [[blesching-cannabis-apoptosis-and-cancer-cell-death]].

### Advantages
- No heat generation — safe for heat-sensitive materials
- No chemical residues
- Rapid treatment cycles
- Simple equipment — UV lamps are inexpensive

### Limitations
- Line-of-sight only — cannot reach shadowed or recessed surfaces
- Limited penetration depth even in [[microwave-reflective-transparent-materials-surface-sterilization]]
- UV lamps degrade over time, requiring regular replacement and intensity
  monitoring
- Some organisms have DNA repair mechanisms that can reverse UV damage
- Ozone generation as a byproduct requires ventilation
- Material degradation of exposed plastics and rubbers over time

## Gamma Irradiation

### Mechanism
High-energy photons (gamma rays, typically from cobalt-60 or cesium-137
sources) ionize molecules and break chemical bonds, destroying
microorganisms through direct DNA damage and indirect free radical damage.

### Advantages
- Excellent penetration through materials
- No chemical residues
- Can sterilize sealed packages
- Highly reliable and well-validated for medical devices

### Limitations
- Requires a radiation source facility — extremely expensive infrastructure
- Regulatory complexity — licensing, shielding, and safety requirements
- Potential material degradation (embrittlement of polymers, color changes)
- Not suitable for in-situ or point-of-use sterilization
- Long turn-around times when outsourced to a irradiation facility

## Chemical Disinfectants
