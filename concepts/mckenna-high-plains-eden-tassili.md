# The High Plains of Eden

In Chapter 6 of *Food of the Gods*, McKenna presents archaeological
and ethnobotanical evidence for a lost psychedelic paradise culture
that once thrived in the Sahara Desert during the African Humid Period.
He argues that the [[mckenna-tassili-plateau-round-head-civilization]] rock art documents a sophisticated
mushroom-using civilization that may represent the historical reality
behind the [[allegro-garden-of-eden-and-delight]] myth.

## The Tassili Plateau

The Tassili-n-Ajjer plateau in southeastern Algeria contains one of
the world's most important collections of prehistoric rock art. This
UNESCO World Heritage site features thousands of paintings and
engravings spanning roughly 12,000 years of human habitation. During
the African Humid Period (approximately 9,000 to 5,000 BCE), the
Tassili was a lush savanna with rivers, lakes, and abundant wildlife
— a far cry from today's hyper-arid desert.

The plateau rises to over 2,000 meters, creating an elevated ecosystem
that supported diverse plant and animal life. McKenna argues that this
environment would have been ideal for the growth of psilocybin-
[[psilocybin-containing-mushrooms-in-us]], particularly *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* and related
species that thrive in warm, humid grasslands grazed by herbivores.

## The Round Head Civilization

The earliest rock art at Tassili, dating to roughly 9,000-7,000 BCE,
is attributed to the so-called "Round Head" culture, named for the
characteristic circular or dome-shaped heads of the human figures
depicted. McKenna finds these depictions extraordinary because:

- The figures frequently appear to be in states of ecstatic trance
- Many hold or are surrounded by mushroom-like objects
- The figures exhibit morphological features suggesting altered
  perception — enlarged eyes, distorted proportions, floating postures
- Ritual scenes show what appear to be communal psychoactive ceremonies
- Dancing and music-making scenes predominate

The Round Head people appear to McKenna to depict a culture organized
around the ritual use of [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]]. The art suggests a
society with a rich internal imaginative life, in which the
boundaries between the human and spirit worlds were permeable and
actively explored.

## Paradise Found?

McKenna asks whether the Tassili plateau may represent the historical
kernel of the Paradise or Eden myth. The parallels are striking:

- A lush garden environment in a now-barren landscape
- A culture organized around a "forbidden" plant (the mushroom)
- A sudden fall from grace (the desertification of the Sahara)
- Depictions of a harmonious, non-hierarchical social organization

The desertification of the Sahara, beginning around 5,000 BCE, would
have destroyed the ecological basis of this mushroom-using culture,
forcing its survivors to migrate to more marginal environments. This
ecological catastrophe, McKenna suggests, may be the historical event
behind humanity's expulsion from the Garden.

## African Genesis

McKenna uses the Tassili evidence to support a broader argument about
the African origins of human culture and consciousness. He connects
the Round Head civilization to:

- The emergence of fully modern human behavior in Africa
- The development of symbolic thought and representational art
- The spread of pastoralism and agriculture from Africa to Eurasia
- The possible dissemination of mushroom-using religious practices

He argues that the cultural innovations usually associated with the
Near Eastern Neolithic — agriculture, pottery, monumental architecture
— may have their origins in earlier [[iboga-and-the-bwiti-cult-initiation]] cultures.
