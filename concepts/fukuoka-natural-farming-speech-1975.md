## Natural Farming Speech: Principles and Practice

updated: 2026-04-18

In January 1975, Masanobu Fukuoka delivered a speech before members of
the Sekai Kyusei Kyo (The Religion for the Salvation of the World)
outlining his experiences and approach to natural farming. This speech
provides one of the most comprehensive summaries of both the
philosophical foundations and practical results of his decades of work.

## Naturally Farmed Rice: The Results

Fukuoka brought samples of his rice to demonstrate the results. The
rice was entirely free of disease -- no rice blast disease, no
sclerotic disease, and no leaf blotch. All one could find were a few
marks left by locusts.

He showed winter-sown rice planted before New Year's alongside the
same variety sown in June. The two were slightly different: one had
12-13 stalks and the other 17-18. The rice was sown at an interval of
about five inches. There was good variation in the number of grains per
head, from about 120 to 260-270 grains. The rice generally produced up
to 12 tillers with an average of 250 and a maximum of 300 or more
grains per head.

## The Origin of Natural Farming

Fukuoka graduated from Gifu Agricultural College and at age twenty-five
joined the plant inspection division at the Yokohama Customs Bureau.
There he did research in plant pathology and worked as a plant customs
inspector. While looking through the eyepiece of his microscope, he
noticed that the tiny worlds of the fungi and bacteria have something
in common with the vast universe of heavenly bodies.

While pursuing these thoughts, filled with doubts and wonder, he fell
ill. Prompted by a chance incident, he underwent what he supposed could
be called a conversion of faith -- a turning point. He had the feeling
then that science was some sort of outrageous monster. Sensing that
everything is utterly meaningless, he quit his post and headed back to
Shikoku.

## The War Years

During the war, a life of leisurely isolation in the hills became
impossible, so Fukuoka joined the agricultural [[natural-farming-methods-comparison]] and provided
guidance to local farmers on growing rice and barley and encouraging
[[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-establishment-bare-hillside]] on a hilltop overlooking the Inland Sea.
In addition to his 7-8 acres of mandarin grove, he also had an acre
and a half of paddy field on which he grew rice and barley. He placed
the greatest importance on rice and considered himself above all a rice
farmer.

The farm was called the "natural farm" but had no sign to that effect.
A constantly changing group of about six to ten young people lived in
huts in the orchard, helping with the field work. They were generally
