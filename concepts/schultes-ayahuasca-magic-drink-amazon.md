# Ayahuasca Magic Drink of the Amazon

Ayahuasca is the most important hallucinogenic plant complex of the
Amazon basin. Prepared from the bark of *Banisteriopsis caapi* (Malpighiaceae),
a giant forest liana, often in combination with the leaves of *Psychotria
viridis* (Chacruna) or *Diplopterys cabrerana*, the bitter and nauseating
drink produces visions in brilliant color, deep spiritual introspection,
and the sensation of the soul separating from the body. Ayahuasca means
"vine of the soul" in Kechwa and "tendril of the soul" in Quechua,
reflecting the central experience of soul-flight during intoxication.

## Banisteriopsis caapi: The Vine of the Soul

*Banisteriopsis caapi* is the primary ingredient of the ayahuasca brew.
These giant forest lianas form the basis of an important hallucinogenic
drink ritually consumed in the western half of the Amazon Valley and by
isolated tribes on the Pacific slopes of the Colombian and Ecuadorean
Andes. The bark of *B. caapi* and *B. inebrians*, prepared in cold or
boiling water, may be taken alone or with additives that significantly
alter the effects. The bark can also be chewed, and recent evidence from
the northwestern Amazon suggests the plants are also used in the form of
a snuff.

Banisteriopsis holds a place of cultural primacy throughout the western
Amazon. It allows the soul to leave the body and wander freely,
communicating with the spirit world. Its psychoactive principles are
beta-carbolines and tryptamines.

## Psychotria viridis: Chacruna

*Psychotria viridis* (Chacruna) is a bush of great cultural significance
as a DMT-providing ingredient of the hallucinogen Ayahuasca, which holds a
central place in the shamanic tradition of the Amazon. Fresh or dried
leaves of Chacruna are mixed with the bark or husk of *Banisteriopsis
caapi* and cooked to prepare the drink. *Diplopterys cabrerana* (syn.
*Banisteriopsis rusbyana*) serves a similar function as an additive. Used
for ages in the Amazon region, Chacruna is the plant that supplies the
dimethyltryptamine (DMT) responsible for the vivid visionary content of
the ayahuasca experience.

## Chemistry: Beta-Carbolines and DMT

The hallucinogenic activity of *Banisteriopsis caapi* is primarily due to
harmine, the major beta-carboline alkaloid in the plant. Harmine and
related beta-carboline alkaloids (harmaline, tetrahydroharmine) act as
monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), preventing the enzymatic
destruction of DMT in the gastrointestinal tract. This pharmacological
synergy between the beta-carboline-containing vine and the DMT-containing
leaves of Chacruna is what makes oral ayahuasca possible. Without the
MAOI action of the vine, DMT would be broken down before reaching the
bloodstream and would produce no psychoactive effects when taken orally.

The psychoactive principles of the complete ayahuasca preparation are
thus beta-carbolines (from *Banisteriopsis*) and tryptamines (from
*Psychotria* or *Diplopterys*). *Tetrapteris methystica*, another
Malpighiaceous vine used as an ayahuasca substitute by the Maku Indians
of the Rio Tikie, likely contains the same or similar beta-carboline
alkaloids.

## Amazonian Cultural Context

In the worldview of many Amazonian societies, ayahuasca reveals the real
world, while daily living is considered an illusion. The drinking of
Caapi represents a return "to the maternal womb, to the source and origin
of all things." Under its influence, participants see "all the tribal
divinities, the creation of the universe, the first human beings and
animals and even the establishment of the social order," as described by
the anthropologist Reichel-Dolmatoff.

It is not always the shaman or medicine man who administers these sacred
plants. The general population, usually the adult male portion, often
shares in the use of hallucinogens. This communal aspect distinguishes
ayahuasca ceremonies from the more private, individualized use of plants
like ololiuqui.

## Ceremonial Use and Purpose

Ayahuasca is usually drunk in religious ceremonies. In the famous
Tukanoan Yurupari ceremony in Colombia, it serves as an adolescent
initiation ritual for boys. The Jivaro believe that ayahuasca makes
possible communication with ancestors and that, under its influence, a
man's soul may leave the body and wander free. The drink is employed for
divination, diagnosis of disease, and as a potent medicine greatly feared
and respected by indigenous peoples throughout the Amazon.

## Preparation Methods

The preparation of ayahuasca involves boiling the bark of *Banisteriopsis
caapi*, sometimes in combination with the leaves of *Psychotria viridis*
(Chacruna) or *Diplopterys cabrerana*, in water for several hours. The
resulting beverage is bitter and often nauseating, which is considered an
essential part of the purgative and cleansing experience. The preparation
may be taken cold or hot, and various other plants are sometimes added to
modify the effects or address specific ceremonial purposes.

## Effects and Experience

Effects of taking ayahuasca range from pleasant intoxication with no
hangover to violent reactions with sickening aftereffects. Usually, visual
hallucinations in brilliant color occur. The intoxication ends with a deep
sleep and dreams. The experience is fundamentally one of visionary
ecstasy, in which the boundary between the individual self and the
cosmic order dissolves, revealing what indigenous peoples consider to be
the true underlying reality.

## Ayahuasca Analogs and Global Reach

The discovery that beta-carboline-containing plants like *Peganum
harmala* (Syrian Rue) could substitute for *Banisteriopsis caapi* in
activating oral DMT has given rise to the study of ayahuasca analogs
worldwide. This pharmacological bridge between continents demonstrates
that the chemistry underlying visionary plant use is universal, even as
the cultural frameworks surrounding it remain profoundly local and
specific.
