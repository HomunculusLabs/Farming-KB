# Fungal Allergies

## Overview

Fungal allergies represent a significant and often underdiagnosed category of allergic disease. Fungal spores and fragments are ubiquitous in both indoor and outdoor environments, and sensitization to fungal allergens can cause a range of conditions from mild rhinitis to severe, progressive lung disease. An estimated 3-10% of the general population is sensitized to fungal allergens, with higher rates among atopic individuals.

## Fungal Allergens and Sources

### Major Allergenic Fungi

The fungi most commonly implicated in human allergic disease:

- **[[alternaria-alternata]]**: One of the most important outdoor fungal allergens. A dematiaceous (darkly pigmented) mold found on plants, soil, and decaying organic matter. Spores are large (20-60 microns) and tend to settle quickly, causing higher exposure near the source
- **[[cladosporium-cladosporioides]] and C. herbarum**: The most common airborne fungi worldwide. Found on dead plant material, soil, and food. Extremely abundant in temperate climates during summer months
- **[[aspergillus-fumigatus]]**: Ubiquitous soil fungus; important both as an allergen and a pathogen. Spores are small (2-3 microns) and can reach deep into the lungs. See [[fungal-infections-human]] for pathogenic effects
- **Penicillium species**: Common indoor molds. Found in water-damaged buildings, on food, and in soil. [[penicillium-chrysogenum]] and P. citrinum are the most commonly implicated in allergy
- **[[trichoderma-viride]]**: Common in damp indoor environments; significant sensitization rates among occupants of water-damaged buildings
- **[[epicoccum-nigrum]]**: Outdoor fungus common on decaying vegetation and grasses
- **Curvularia lunata**: Common in tropical and subtropical regions; associated with allergic fungal sinusitis
- **Stemphylium botryosum**: Plant pathogen and outdoor allergen
- **Basidiomycete spores**: Mushrooms, rusts, and smuts produce allergenic spores. The significance of basidiospore allergy is increasingly recognized but historically underappreciated

### Indoor Fungal Exposure

Indoor mold growth is a major source of fungal allergen exposure:

- **Water-damaged buildings**: Chronic moisture problems (leaks, flooding, condensation) create conditions for mold growth on drywall, wood, ceiling tiles, carpet, and other building materials
- **HVAC systems**: Air conditioning ducts and components can harbor fungal growth, distributing spores throughout a building
- **Bathrooms and kitchens**: High-humidity areas are common sites for mold colonization
- **Basements and crawlspaces**: Poor ventilation and moisture intrusion promote fungal growth
- **House dust**: Settled spores and hyphal fragments accumulate in house dust and become resuspended during cleaning or activity

## Spore Seasons and Environmental Patterns

### Outdoor Spore Dynamics

Fungal spore concentrations in outdoor air follow seasonal patterns, though mold spores are present year-round in most climates:

- **Spring (March-May)**: Increasing spore counts as temperatures warm. Cladosporium and Alternaria begin rising
- **Summer (June-August)**: Peak outdoor spore season. Cladosporium reaches highest counts (can exceed 50,000 spores/m³ on warm, humid days). Alternaria peaks in mid-to-late summer. Highest overall fungal allergen exposure
- **Autumn (September-November)**: Declining spore counts, with secondary peaks during harvest season and leaf decomposition. Elevated spore counts near decaying vegetation and compost
- **Winter (December-February)**: Lowest outdoor spore counts in temperate regions. Indoor spore exposure may increase as windows are closed and heating systems distribute settled spores

### Meteorological Factors

- **Temperature**: Most fungi grow optimally at 20-30°C; spore release increases with warmth
- **Humidity**: Relative humidity above 60% promotes both fungal growth and spore release. Rain can initially wash spores from the air (decreasing counts) but subsequently triggers spore release (increasing counts)
- **Wind**: Spore dispersal is wind-dependent; counts are typically highest during dry, windy conditions following rain
- **Time of day**: Many fungi release spores in the afternoon when conditions are warmest and driest. Nighttime spore counts are typically lower

## Clinical Manifestations

### Allergic Rhinitis

Fungal spores are a significant trigger of allergic rhinitis (hay fever):

- Symptoms: sneezing, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, nasal itching, postnasal drip
- Often perennial (year-round) rather than purely seasonal due to indoor exposure
- Frequently co-occurs with sensitization to other allergens (pollen, dust mites, pet dander)
- May be underdiagnosed because fungal allergens are less commonly tested in routine allergy panels

### Allergic Conjunctivitis

- Itchy, red, watery eyes
- Often accompanies allergic rhinitis
- Cladosporium and Alternaria are common triggers

### Allergic Asthma

Fungal sensitization is a risk factor for severe, poorly controlled asthma:

- **Fungi are associated with severe asthma**: Patients with fungal sensitization tend to have more severe asthma, more frequent exacerbations, and poorer lung function
- **Severe Asthma with Fungal Sensitization (SAFS)**: Defined as severe asthma requiring high-dose inhaled corticosteroids plus positive skin prick or IgE test to fungal allergens. Prevalence is estimated at 3-11% of adults with severe asthma
- **Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis (ABPA)**: A hypersensitivity reaction to Aspergillus antigens occurring primarily in patients with asthma or cystic fibrosis. Characterized by wheezing, pulmonary infiltrates, eosinophilia, elevated total IgE, and central bronchiectasis. See [[fungal-infections-human]] for ABPA details

### Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), also called extrinsic allergic alveolitis, is an inflammatory lung disease caused by repeated inhalation of organic antigens, including fungal spores:

**Acute HP**:
- Fever, chills, cough, dyspnea 4-8 hours after heavy exposure
- Resolves within 24-48 hours if exposure ceases
- Often mistaken for viral respiratory infection

**Subacute HP**:
- Insidious onset over weeks to months
- Progressive cough, dyspnea, fatigue, weight loss
- May develop into chronic disease if exposure continues

**Chronic HP**:
- Develops after months to years of continuous or intermittent low-level exposure
- Progressive dyspnea, cough, digital clubbing
- Can lead to pulmonary fibrosis, respiratory failure, and death
- Often irreversible by the time of diagnosis

**Fungal causes of HP**:
- **Farmer's lung**: Caused by thermophilic actinomycetes and Saccharopolyspora rectivirgula (often found in moldy hay)
- **Hot tub lung**: Mycobacterium avium complex (bacterial) and various fungi in contaminated hot tub aerosols
- **Woodworker's lung**: Various fungi contaminating wood dust
- **Maple bark stripper's lung**: Cryptostroma corticale (maple bark fungus)
- **Cheese worker's lung**: Penicillium casei, P. roqueforti
- **Mushroom worker's lung**: Spores from commercial mushroom cultivation
- **Suberosis**: Cork dust contaminated with Penicillium frequentans

### Allergic Fungal Sinusitis

- Chronic sinusitis with nasal polyposis and allergic mucin (thick, eosinophil-rich mucus)
- Most commonly caused by Aspergillus, Alternaria, Curvularia, or Bipolaris
- Often requires surgical debridement combined with systemic corticosteroids and antifungal therapy
- Tends to recur without ongoing management

### Atopic Dermatitis

- Fungal sensitization may exacerbate atopic dermatitis, particularly in humid environments
- Malassezia species (lipophilic yeasts normally found on skin) are implicated in head and neck dermatitis
- Some patients benefit from antifungal treatment of the skin

### Skin Reactions

- Contact dermatitis from direct skin contact with fungi or fungal spores
- Urticaria (hives) from systemic exposure to fungal allergens

## Diagnosis

### Allergy Testing

- **Skin prick testing**: The primary method for detecting fungal sensitization. Commercial extracts are available for Alternaria, Cladosporium, Aspergillus, Penicillium, and others. Sensitivity varies by extract quality and standardization
- **Serum-specific IgE**: ImmunoCAP and other assays measure IgE antibodies to specific fungal allergens. Less sensitive than skin testing but useful when skin testing is not possible
- **Component-resolved diagnostics**: Molecular allergology using recombinant individual allergen components (e.g., Alt a 1 from Alternaria, Asp f 1 from Aspergillus) provides more precise sensitization profiles

### Environmental Assessment

- **Spore counts**: Air sampling using Burkard or Rotorod samplers provides quantitative spore counts. National Allergy Bureau (NAA) reports daily spore counts in many areas
- **Indoor air quality testing**: Surface sampling, air sampling, and bulk material sampling to identify and quantify indoor fungal contamination
- **Visual inspection**: Identification of moisture problems and visible mold growth

## Management

### Avoidance

- **Indoor moisture control**: Fix leaks, improve ventilation, maintain humidity below 50%, use dehumidifiers
