# Maryland Mammoth Tobacco and the Discovery of Photoperiodism

The discovery of photoperiodism — the ability of plants to measure [[coleman-winter-day-length-and-persephone-months]] use it as a cue for developmental timing — ranks among the most important findings in 20th-century plant biology. It originated not from a theoretical hypothesis, but from a practical agricultural problem involving a peculiar tobacco strain that refused to flower.

## The Maryland Mammoth Mystery

In 1906, tobacco farmers in the valleys of southern Maryland noticed something extraordinary. A new strain of tobacco had appeared — whether through mutation, hybridization, or introduction is unclear — that grew with unprecedented vigor. It could reach four and a half metres in height and produce almost a hundred leaves. But unlike normal tobacco, which flowered and set seed by late summer, this strain seemed incapable of flowering at all under Maryland field conditions.

The farmers named it Maryland Mammoth, and initially it seemed like a dream crop. More leaves meant more harvestable material. However, tobacco farmers need seed from flowering plants to produce the next year's crop. A tobacco that never flowers is ultimately useless for sustained agriculture.

The Mammoth's behavior was paradoxical. It grew vegetatively without limit throughout the summer and only stopped when autumn frosts killed it. Something about the Maryland environment was preventing this plant from completing its life cycle. The question was: what?

## Garner and Allard's Experiments

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard, two scientists at the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] Department of Agriculture, took up the challenge. They planted Maryland Mammoth tobacco in pots and designed a simple but elegant experiment.

They divided their plants into two groups. Both groups spent their days in the field, receiving identical sunlight. But every afternoon, one group was moved into a dark shed, artificially shortening their day. The control group remained outside, experiencing the natural long days of a Maryland summer.

The results were dramatic. The plants left outside continued their relentless [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]], producing leaf after leaf without flowering. But the plants moved to the dark shed — those experiencing artificially shortened days — stopped growing and began producing flowers. By simply reducing the amount of light the plants received each day, Garner and Allard had solved the mystery.

## The Concept of Photoperiodism

Garner and Allard named this phenomenon "photoperiodism" — the response of organisms to the relative length of day and night. Their 1920 paper, published in the Journal of [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]], established several key principles:

1. Plants can measure the duration of light and darkness
2. This measurement controls the transition from vegetative growth to flowering
3. Different plant species respond differently to day length

They classified plants into categories based on their photoperiodic response:

- **Short-day plants** — flower when days are shorter than a critical length (e.g., chrysanthemums, soybeans, Maryland Mammoth tobacco)
- **Long-day plants** — flower when days exceed a critical length (e.g., irises, barley, spinach)
- **Day-neutral plants** — flower regardless of day length (e.g., tomatoes, rice, corn)

The Maryland Mammoth was a short-day plant, and the natural long days of a Maryland summer kept it in a vegetative state indefinitely.

## Day or Night: Which Matters More?

A critical follow-up question emerged: do plants measure the length of the day or the length of the night? Around the time of World War II, researchers discovered that the answer was surprising.

By briefly turning lights on in the middle of the night — a "[[night-break-experiments-and-agricultural-light-manipulation]]" — scientists could prevent short-day plants from flowering, even though the total day length remained short. Conversely, a brief flash of light during the night could induce flowering in long-day plants that would not normally flower under short-day conditions.

This demonstrated that plants actually measure the length of the **continuous dark period**, not the light period.

The night break experiment worked even when the light flash lasted only a few seconds.

The plant's light-sensing system was remarkably sensitive to interruptions in the dark period.

This finding overturned the intuitive assumption that it was the duration of daylight that mattered, and established the primacy of darkness in photoperiodic regulation.

## The Red Light Key

Scientists then investigated which color of light was most effective at disrupting the night period. They discovered that only red light (approximately 660 nm) was effective. Blue light, green light, and most other colors had no effect on photoperiodic responses. This was the same red light that Darwin's [[phototropism]] experiments had shown was important for [[phototropism-and-plant-light-responses]], but here it served a different function.

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the same USDA laboratory where Maryland Mammoth was first studied made another remarkable discovery. Far-red light (approximately 730 nm, with wavelengths just longer than visible red) could completely reverse the effect of red light. If a red flash was followed immediately by a far-red flash, it was as if the red flash had never occurred.

This red/far-red reversibility became the key to understanding photoperiodism at the molecular level. The photoreceptor responsible — phytochrome — was soon identified as a biological light switch that could be toggled between two forms: an active form (Pfr, promoted by red light) and an inactive form (Pr, promoted by far-red light).

## Agricultural Impact

The discovery of photoperiodism transformed agriculture. Flower farmers could now control the timing of crops by manipulating light exposure:

- Chrysanthemum growers keep lights on briefly each night through autumn and winter, preventing flowering. Two weeks before market day, the lights are turned off, and the entire crop flowers simultaneously.
- Poinsettia producers use the reverse strategy, providing short days to trigger the characteristic red bract development in time for Christmas.
- Strawberry growers use day-length manipulation to extend or restrict the fruiting season.

The understanding of photoperiodism also enabled plant breeders to develop varieties adapted to different latitudes. A soybean variety bred for Minnesota (long summer days) would flower too early if planted in Texas, while a Texas variety might not flower at all in Minnesota. Breeding programs could now select for appropriate photoperiodic sensitivity based on the target growing region.

## Molecular Mechanisms

Modern molecular biology has revealed that photoperiodic sensing ultimately controls the expression of flowering genes such as FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) in Arabidopsis. The phytochrome system measures [[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]] and, through a complex signaling cascade involving CONSTANS (CO) and other proteins, activates or represses FT expression at the appropriate time. The FT protein then travels from the leaf to the shoot apex, where it triggers the floral transition.

This molecular understanding has confirmed what Garner and Allard discovered with their simple shed experiment: plants are exquisitely sensitive timekeepers, using light as their primary reference signal to coordinate their life cycle with the seasons.

## Phytochrome's Dual Role

It is important to distinguish photoperiodism from the shade-avoidance response, even though both involve phytochrome. In [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]], plants detect the ratio of red to far-red light to determine whether neighboring vegetation is blocking sunlight. In photoperiodism, plants use phytochrome to measure the absolute duration of uninterrupted darkness. The same pigment performs two fundamentally different measurements: spectral quality in one case, temporal duration in the other.

Phytochrome exists in multiple forms (phyA through phyE in Arabidopsis), each with distinct roles. PhyA is particularly important for photoperiodic sensing because it remains active during extended dark periods. PhyB, by contrast, is more rapidly degraded in darkness and plays a larger role in [[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]]. This division of labor allows a single class of photoreceptors to simultaneously process information about both the spectral environment and the passage of time.

The red/far-red reversibility that was so critical to identifying phytochrome also means that natural light environments can modulate photoperiodic responses. Twilight, which contains a higher proportion of far-red light, can act as a natural signal that effectively "lengthens" the perceived night for some photoperiodic responses. Moonlight, by contrast, contains very little far-red radiation and does not disrupt the night period, allowing plants to distinguish between the dim light of the moon and the biologically significant light of dawn and dusk.
