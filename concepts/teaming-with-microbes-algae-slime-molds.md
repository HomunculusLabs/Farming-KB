# Teaming with Microbes: Algae, Slime Molds, and Their Roles

**Source:** Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis, *Teaming with Microbes*

**Updated:** 2026-04-21

Though often overlooked, algae and slime molds play supporting roles in the [[composting-and-soil-biology]]. They are not related to each other -- algae are photosynthetic organisms while slime molds are heterotrophic consumers -- but both contribute to nutrient cycling and soil formation in ways that affect gardeners and the broader ecosystem.

## Algae

### Definition and Types

Algae are broadly defined as single-celled or thread-like photosynthetic organisms, including seaweeds and giant kelp.

Three kinds exist: marine, freshwater, and terrestrial. The latter -- terrestrial algae -- live in soil, on or near the surface where sunlight is available, not near roots.

Most algae require very moist conditions, but some grow in hot deserts and at the frozen poles, though even these need a film of water to survive. A teaspoon of soil may contain 10,000 to 100,000 cells of green algae (phylum Chlorophyta), yellow-green algae (Xanthophyta), and diatoms (Bacillariophyta).

### Photosynthetic Primary Producers

Algae are closely related to bacteria on the tree of life but are often thought of as primitive plants because they are photoautotrophic -- they take energy from the sun and produce their own food.

Like plants, algae are primary producers, not dependent on the soil's organic matter or other members of the [[composting-and-soil-biology]] for food. However, they lack the specialization of higher plants: no true roots, leaves, stems, or vascular system.

Their role in the [[teaming-with-microbes-photosynthesis-exudates-carbon-cycle]] is distinct from that of higher plants. While vascular plants dominate terrestrial photosynthesis, algae contribute significantly in aquatic systems and on exposed soil surfaces where their productivity can be surprisingly high.

### Diatoms and Diatomaceous Earth

The cell walls of diatoms are composed of silica covered with an organic skin that decays after the organism dies, leaving behind silica skeletons in huge numbers.

These skeletons make up diatomaceous earth, a product familiar to many gardeners for its pest-control properties. The sharp edges of diatom frustules damage the exoskeletons of insects and other arthropods, making diatomaceous earth a popular mechanical insecticide.

### Role in Soil Formation

Algae were among the pioneer organisms that helped create soil.

Growing on moist rock surfaces, they died and combined with weathered rock, air, and water to form early soils -- providing necessary organic matter when no other source existed. Algae help create soil by forming carbonic acids as part of their metabolic functions, causing rock to weather through chemical weathering brought about by biological activity.

This pioneering role connects algae to the broader story of [[fungal-fungal-community-assembly]] on bare substrates. After algae establish the first thin soil layer, fungi and other organisms follow, building increasingly complex soil ecosystems over time.

### Lichens: Algae-Fungi Symbiosis

Lichens represent the symbiotic relationship between certain algae and fungi.

The fungus provides a humid, protected environment for the alga; the alga provides photosynthesized food to the fungus. Together they accelerate rock weathering considerably. Lichens also contribute nitrogen to soil through their cyanobacterial partners.

Lichens are among the most resilient organisms on Earth, capable of surviving extreme temperatures, desiccation, and UV radiation. Their role in primary succession -- colonizing bare rock and beginning the process of soil formation -- makes them essential pioneers in [[the-soil-food-web]].

### Blue-Green Algae and Nitrogen Fixation

Blue-green algae (Cyanophyta) use the enzyme nitrogenase to fix nitrogen, either in a symbiotic relationship or nonsymbiotically, similar to nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

This is how rice plants can get nitrogen from the water in which they grow. The [[composting-and-soil-biology]] benefits significantly from cyanobacterial activity, particularly in flooded agricultural systems and in soils where other nitrogen-fixing organisms are limited.

### Role in Soil Structure

Where algae exist in soil, they excrete polysaccharides, mucilage, and slimes -- all sticky substances that help bind and aggregate soil particles.

Their presence helps form air passageways in otherwise compacted soil. Algae also fit into some soil food webs as primary producers eaten by certain nematodes and protozoa, connecting them to the [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]].

### Limitations for Gardeners

The role of algae in gardening is minor because they need sunlight, which penetrates only a short distance into soil.

They are most visible on soil surfaces, in ponds, and on tree bark. However, their contribution to the earliest stages of soil formation should not be underestimated -- without algae, the process of converting bare rock to productive soil would be vastly slower.

## Slime Molds

### What Are Slime Molds?

Slime molds are unusual-looking, amoeba-like organisms that inhabit damp, rotting wood, leaves, manure, lawn thatch, rotting mushrooms, and other organic material.

They spend most of their lives pursuing bacteria and yeast in the soil, contributing to nutrient cycling as they consume and release nutrients.

The few hundred different kinds are in many ways like fungi but differentiated by how they eat: fungi digest food externally then absorb nutrients, while slime molds engulf food and digest it internally.

### Two Groups

- **Dictyosteliomycota** (cellular slime molds): Individual cells retain their walls and operate independently for most of their life cycle.

- **Myxomycota** (plasmodial slime molds): Individual cells lose their walls, forming a multinucleated mass of cytoplasm that moves as a single organism.

Both start as spores that germinate into myxamoebae -- amoeboid organisms that live in soil and ingest bacteria, fungal spores, and small protozoa, locking up nutrients and preventing leaching.

### The Swarm

For reasons not fully understood, individual myxamoebae swarm together -- up to 125,000 forming a mass that looks like a big slug, dollop of jelly, or vomit.

These masses range from tan to yellow, pink, or red. Each myxamoeba leaves a chemical attractant trail; others follow, adding their exudates until attraction increases and swarms congregate.

The resulting plasmodium emerges from soil and slowly moves (about 1mm per hour) over leaves, grass, driveways, logs, and mulch, engulfing food as it goes. Remarkably, if cut in half or quarters, the parts will rejoin -- demonstrating a primitive form of collective intelligence.

### Sporangia

Eventually the plasmodium forms a fruiting structure (sporangium) of distinctive shape for each species -- tiny towers, nets, or other forms in yellow, blue, red, brown, and white.

Spores form at the top and are dispersed to start the cycle again. These spores can survive for extended periods in the soil, waiting for favorable conditions before germinating.

### Role in the Soil Food Web

- **Nutrient cycling**: Slime molds help cycle nutrients by consuming bacteria and fungi, releasing nutrients in forms available to plants.

- **Soil binding**: The slime each myxamoeba creates helps bind soil particles, contributing to soil aggregation and structure.

- **Food source**: Slime molds are food for insect larvae, worms, and specialized beetles with mandibles designed to scoop them up, connecting them to [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] in soil.

When conditions become unfavorable, plasmodia dry up and turn to powdery dust. While they don't play a major role in most gardens, they are memorable when encountered and serve as indicators of active soil biology.
