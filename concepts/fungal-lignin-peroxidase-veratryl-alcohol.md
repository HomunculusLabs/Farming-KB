# Fungal Lignin Peroxidase and Veratryl Alcohol Mediation

## Overview

Lignin peroxidase (LiP, EC 1.11.1.7) is a water-soluble, glycosylated
heme enzyme secreted by white rot fungi. Unique among peroxidases, LiP
can produce radical cations from non-phenolic aromatic compounds with
redox potentials beyond the reach of manganese peroxidase or laccase.
Veratryl alcohol (VA), a fungal metabolite co-secreted with LiP, serves
as a crucial redox mediator that extends the catalytic range of LiP.
The LiP-veratryl alcohol system is central to fungal degradation of
recalcitrant pollutants including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons
(PAHs), chlorinated aromatics, and diverse xenobiotic compounds.

## LiP Structure and Properties

LiP comprises a family of isozymes with major isozymes crystallized
and their genes identified. LiP has the same heme and similar active
site residues as manganese peroxidase, with histidine as the proximal
ligand accepting a proton from hydrogen peroxide and distal arginine
facilitating oxygen-oxygen bond cleavage. LiP has four disulfide bonds
(compared with five in MnP) and shares high sequence homology with
MnP. The major structural distinction is that neutral or positive
residues replace the acidic manganese-binding residues of MnP,
precluding manganese(II) binding while enabling oxidation of
non-phenolic aromatics with high redox potentials.

## Catalytic Cycle and Mechanism

LiP follows a standard peroxidase catalytic cycle. Reaction of native
enzyme with hydrogen peroxide yields Compound I, containing an
oxyferryl heme with a porphyrin cation radical. Two sequential
single-electron reductions restore the native enzyme through Compound
II. Redox potential largely determines whether an aromatic is a LiP
substrate. Strong electron-withdrawing groups (alpha-carbonyl, nitro)
deactivate aromatic nuclei, while alkoxy groups activate them. LiP is
relatively non-specific towards reductant nature and will accept
electrons from a broad range of substrates including sulfides.

## Veratryl Alcohol as Redox Mediator

Veratryl alcohol (3,4-dimethoxybenzyl alcohol, VA) is produced by
white rot fungi simultaneously with LiP secretion. VA has a standard
redox potential of 1.36 V/NHE, placing it beyond the direct oxidation
range of laccase but within LiP's capability. When oxidized by LiP,
VA forms a radical cation (VA+.) that can act as a diffusible redox
mediator. The VA+. radical cation can oxidize secondary substrates
through charge transfer, creating reactive radical cations in lignin
or pollutant molecules. On completing the catalytic cycle, two VA
radical cations are released, with the primary oxidant restored
following oxidation of the secondary substrate.

## Diffusible Mediator Debate

Whether VA+. can act as a diffusible mediator was debated for years.
Aust reported a VA+. decay rate of 1.2 times 10 to the 12th power
per second, suggesting it could not diffuse. However, Candeias and
Harvey measured a rate of 17 plus or minus 1 per second at pH below
5, confirmed by Bietti and colleagues. At this rate, VA+. would
indeed function as a diffusible redox mediator. This understanding
means that, according to the Nernst equation, compounds with higher
redox potentials than the VA+./VA couple could be oxidized by the
LiP-VA system, provided VA+. is generated in sufficiently high local
concentration.

## Mediated Oxidation of Pollutants

The LiP-VA system oxidizes diverse pollutants. VA+. mediated the
oxidation of benzo[a]pyrene, lignin depolymerization, aromatic ring
opening, and ring hydroxylation reactions. The dye poly R and
isoeugenol (with ionization potential 9.0 eV, compared with VA at
8.67 eV) were oxidized through mixed-substrate mechanisms. This
demonstrates that the LiP-VA system can oxidize compounds with
ionization potentials higher than VA itself, significantly extending
the practical substrate range for bioremediation applications.

## pH Gradient Requirement

Optimal LiP-VA catalysis requires a specific pH gradient. The
deprotonation of VA+. is induced by hydroxide ions. For optimal
polymer oxidation, the enzyme environment should be maintained as
acidic as possible while keeping the local pH near the substrate
high to favour base-induced carbon-carbon fragmentation. LiP
catalysis operates up to pH 4.5 with an optimum around pH 2.75.
White rot fungi secrete oxalate during secondary metabolism, which
may create a pH gradient extending from the fungus (acid) as the
enzyme source to the polymeric substrate (alkaline). This natural
pH gradient is essential for efficient bioremediation.

## Oxygen Activation and Radical Propagation

Activated oxygen species including superoxide anion, perhydroxyl
radical, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radical may participate in
LiP-catalyzed oxidation. Carbon-centred radicals from radical
cation fragmentation react with oxygen at diffusion-limited rates.
During fungal growth, acid conditions favour perhydroxyl radical
(pKa 4.8) appearance. The perhydroxyl radical has a high redox
potential (1.5 V/NHE) and can perpetuate oxidative attack. It also
dismutates with superoxide to provide hydrogen peroxide for further
peroxidative reactions. Superstoichiometric oxidation with respect
to initial hydrogen peroxide supply has been observed.

## Phenolic Compound Effects

Phenolic compounds can both serve as LiP substrates and cause enzyme
inactivation. Phenolics are oxidized by LiP directly or by VA+.
radical cations, with reaction products strongly favouring oxidized
phenolics. In the absence of VA, phenolic reduction of Compound I
yields an enzyme-associated phenoxy radical with Compound II, with
no effective kinetic pathway to restore the native state. Reaction
with hydrogen peroxide then forms catalytically inactive Compound
III. In vivo feedback control by phenolics in conjunction with
other phenol-oxidizing enzymes may be biologically relevant but
presents challenges for enzyme-based remediation technologies.

## Implications for Enzyme-Based Bioremediation

The complexity of the LiP-VA system creates significant challenges
for enzyme-based bioremediation technology. Requirements include
cofactors (VA as redox mediator), a carefully regulated pH gradient,
controlled hydrogen peroxide supply, avoidance of phenolic compound
inactivation, and management of competing oxygen-consuming reactions.
These constraints make whole organism-based bioremediation approaches
more practical than isolated enzyme systems. Biofarming procedures,
where contaminated soil is mixed with white rot fungi on lignocellulose
substrates, represent the most promising strategy for LiP-mediated
pollutant degradation.
