# Mushroom spore syringe Preparation

## Overview

Spore syringe preparation is the process of creating a sterile liquid suspension of mushroom spores that can be used to inoculate substrate. D.H. Jarrold's method describes a practical approach using spore prints as the starting material. The resulting spore syringe is the primary inoculation tool for the [[straw-based-mushroom-cultivation-boil-bag-method]] and other indoor growing techniques.

The process involves harvesting spores from a print, suspending them in sterile water, and loading the solution into a syringe — all performed under carefully controlled sterile conditions to prevent contamination. A well-prepared spore syringe can inoculate multiple substrate bags, making it an efficient means of propagating mushroom cultures.

## Key Details

### Equipment Required

Jarrold specifies the following equipment for spore syringe preparation:

- **Syringe**: A standard medical-grade syringe (typically 10-20ml capacity) with a Luer lock fitting. The syringe must be clean and capable of withstanding pressure cooker sterilization temperatures.
- **Long needle**: Fabricated from stainless steel tubing, the long needle allows deep injection into substrate bags. The tubing is cut to the desired length and one end is filed or ground to create a sharp point. This needle is essential for the through-filter-can [[pf-tek-inoculation-technique-and-syringe-management]].
- **Short needle**: A standard hypodermic needle for through-bag inoculation and general spore solution handling.
- **Pyrex container**: A heat-resistant glass container (such as a Pyrex measuring jug or beaker) used to hold sterile water during the spore harvesting and syringe loading process. Pyrex withstands rapid temperature changes without cracking.
- **Spore jar**: A small glass jar with a lid, used to receive spores scraped from the spore print. The jar and lid must be sterilizable. Some practitioners use a small drinking glass covered with aluminum foil as a simpler alternative.
- **Scraper**: A flat, sterilizable tool used to scrape spores from the spore print surface into the spore jar. A scalpel blade, flat screwdriver tip, or dedicated metal scraper can serve this purpose.
- **Tweezers**: For handling the spore print and manipulating small items during the procedure. Metal tweezers can be flame-sterilized between uses.
- **Scissors**: For cutting tape, foil, or other materials as needed during the preparation process.
- **Spirit burner**: A small alcohol lamp or spirit burner providing a consistent flame for sterilizing needles, scrapers, tweezers, and other metal tools. The flame is the primary sterilization method during the procedure.
- **Propyl alcohol (isopropyl alcohol)**: Used for surface disinfection of equipment, work surfaces, and the exterior of containers. A 70% concentration is optimal for microbial killing — higher concentrations evaporate too quickly to be effective.
- **Cotton wool buds**: Used for applying alcohol to surfaces and for cleaning the needle between inoculation points. Cotton buds dampened with alcohol are also useful for wiping the injection sites on substrate bags.

### Sterilization of Water and Syringe

Before any spore handling begins, the water and syringe must be thoroughly sterilized:

1. **Water preparation**: Fill the Pyrex container with clean water (distilled or boiled tap water). The water should be at room temperature — hot water in a sealed syringe can create a vacuum as it cools, potentially drawing contaminants inward.
2. **Syringe preparation**: Draw the water into the syringe. Expel any air bubbles by pointing the needle upward and gently depressing the plunger until a small bead of water forms at the tip.
3. **Packaging**: Place the loaded syringe (needle attached) and any additional water in the Pyrex container. Cover the container with aluminum foil to prevent contamination during and after sterilization.
4. **Pressure cooking**: Place the covered Pyrex container in the pressure cooker and sterilize at 15psi for 20 minutes. This is shorter than the [[mushroom-substrate-sterilization-and-pasteurization]] cycle because water has fewer hiding places for contaminants than straw substrate.
5. **Cooling**: Allow the pressure cooker to cool and depressurize naturally. Remove the container and set it aside in the clean workspace. The sterile water and syringe are now ready for use.

### Scraping Spores from the Print

The spore print provides the raw material for the syringe. Jarrold's technique for harvesting spores:

1. **Workspace preparation**: Clean the work surface thoroughly with 70% isopropyl alcohol. Ensure good lighting so that the spore deposit (typically a dark pattern on the print surface) is clearly visible.
2. **Spore jar setup**: Place the sterilized spore jar (or glass with foil cover) on the cleaned surface. Remove the lid or foil cover briefly to expose the sterile interior.
3. **Spore print handling**: Using flame-sterilized tweezers, pick up the spore print by its edges. Hold it over the open mouth of the spore jar, print side facing downward.
4. **Scraping**: Using the flame-sterilized scraper, gently scrape the spore deposit from the print surface. The spores will fall into the jar below. Apply light, consistent pressure — heavy scraping may also dislodge pieces of the print medium itself, which should be avoided.
5. **Coverage**: Scrape the entire visible spore deposit. A dark, well-made spore print from a mature mushroom cap can contain millions of spores, far more than needed for a single syringe.
6. **Sealing**: Once scraping is complete, replace the lid or foil cover on the spore jar to prevent airborne contamination from entering.

### Loading the Syringe

With sterile water and harvested spores prepared, the syringe is loaded under sterile conditions:

1. **Water transfer**: Carefully uncover the Pyrex container with the sterile water. Using the sterile syringe (still sealed from the pressure cooker cycle), draw a small amount of sterile water into the barrel. Expel this water into the spore jar to rinse the spores into suspension. Repeat this rinse step 2-3 times to ensure all spores are loosened from the jar walls.
2. **Spore suspension**: Draw the spore-water mixture back into the syringe. Gently agitate the syringe by flicking the barrel or slowly inverting it several times to distribute the spores evenly throughout the water. The solution should appear slightly cloudy or tinted, depending on spore density.
3. **Final volume**: The syringe should be filled to its marked capacity. If multiple syringes are being prepared from a single spore print, divide the water evenly among them.
4. **Needle attachment**: If the needle was removed during the process, reattach it now. Ensure the Luer lock is secure and that the needle is clean.

### Sterile Technique and Contamination Control

Maintaining sterile conditions throughout the process is critical. Jarrold emphasizes several key practices:

- **Breathing mask**: The operator must wear a breathing mask throughout the spore handling and syringe loading procedure. Human breath carries bacteria, yeasts, and mold spores that can easily contaminate the work area. A simple surgical mask or dust mask is sufficient.
- **Flame sterilization between inoculations**: Before each individual inoculation of a substrate bag, the needle tip must be flame-sterilized until glowing red-hot, then allowed to cool for several seconds. This prevents cross-contamination between bags and kills any organisms that may have contacted the needle since the previous inoculation.
- **Alcohol swabbing**: After flame sterilization and cooling, the needle tip can be wiped with an alcohol-dampened cotton bud as an additional precaution before piercing the substrate bag.
- **Minimal exposure**: Keep containers covered whenever possible. The spore jar, Pyrex container, and syringe tip should only be exposed to open air for the minimum time necessary to complete each step.
- **Draft-free environment**: Perform all work in a room with still air. Close windows, turn off fans, and avoid moving through the workspace during the procedure.

### Spore Solution Dispersal

The prepared spore syringe is used to inoculate substrate bags as described in [[straw-based-mushroom-cultivation-boil-bag-method]]. Key points for effective dispersal:

- **Agitation**: Before each inoculation, shake the syringe vigorously to resuspend spores that may have settled to the bottom. Spores are microscopic and will settle quickly in still water.
- **Distribution**: Inject spore solution at multiple points within the substrate bag to maximize the chances of successful colonization. Space injection sites evenly throughout the substrate.
- **Volume**: Use approximately 1-2ml of spore solution per injection point. A single 10ml syringe can inoculate 5-10 bags, depending on the number of injection points per bag.
- **Storage**: Unused spore solution can be stored in the syringe at cool room temperature or refrigerated for several weeks to months. Refrigeration extends viability significantly but is not strictly necessary for short-term storage.

### Troubleshooting

Common issues encountered during spore syringe preparation include:

- **Contaminated syringes**: Indicated by cloudiness, unusual colors, or growth within the syringe after storage. This usually results from insufficient sterilization or poor sterile technique during loading. Discard contaminated syringes — they cannot be rescued.
- **No visible spores**: If the spore print was light or old, spore density may be very low. The solution may appear clear even when viable spores are present. Proceed with inoculation but expect slower colonization.
- **Clogged needle**: Spore clumps or debris can block the needle. Flame-sterilize and clear the needle by expelling a small amount of solution. If the blockage persists, replace the needle.
- **Syringe won't draw water**: This can occur if the syringe plunger is too tight after sterilization. Lubricating the rubber plunger with a tiny amount of sterile silicone spray or water can help. Ensure the needle is not clogged before assuming a plunger issue.

### Spore Print Quality Considerations

The quality of the starting spore print directly affects the reliability of the resulting syringe:

- **Print freshness**: Fresh prints (made within the past few months) have higher spore viability than older prints. Spore germination rates decline over time, though properly stored prints can remain viable for a year or more.
- **Print density**: A dark, heavily deposited print indicates a large number of spores and typically produces a more reliable syringe. Light prints may still be usable but will contain fewer viable spores per milliliter of solution.
- **Print cleanliness**: Prints made in open air are more likely to contain contaminant spores from the environment. Prints made in a still-air box or glove box are cleaner and produce more reliable results.
- **[[vegetable-storage-conditions-by-temperature-and-humidity]]**: Store spore prints in a cool, dry, dark environment. Sealing them in ziplock bags with a desiccant packet extends shelf life significantly.

### Storage and Longevity

Properly prepared spore syringes can remain viable for extended periods when stored correctly:

- **Short-term storage**: At room temperature (18-22°C), spore syringes remain viable for 1-3 months. Avoid direct sunlight and heat sources.
- **Long-term storage**: Refrigeration at 2-4°C extends viability to 6-12 months or longer. Place the syringe in a sealed bag to prevent condensation and contamination during refrigeration.
- **Freezing**: Spore syringes can be frozen for long-term preservation, though some practitioners report reduced germination rates after freezing. Adding a small amount of glycerol (5-10%) to the water can protect spores during freezing.
- **Dark storage**: Keep syringes in a dark location regardless of temperature. Prolonged exposure to ultraviolet light degrades spore DNA and reduces germination rates substantially.
- **Viability testing**: If there is doubt about syringe viability, test by inoculating a small sample of sterile substrate (such as a jar of brown rice) and observing for [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] within 7-14 days. No growth after this period suggests the spores are non-viable.
