# PDC Arid Landscape Design

In the 1981 Permaculture Design Course, Bill Mollison devotes extensive attention to arid landscape design in Pamphlet III, presenting a comprehensive system for working with desert environments. This material complements the existing [[pdc-climate-design-strategies]] page with detailed desert-specific techniques.

## Understanding Desert Geomorphology

Mollison begins with the classic arid landscape profile: a receding scarp traveling geologically backwards, with a down-drop, back slope, and then a slope to the back-slope. Out in the desert, buttes -- residual bits of scarp -- stand alone on the plain, rising 40 to 400 feet high. These scarps and remains occur in sequences across the desert.

Scarps in deserts usually consist of fairly soft rock capped with about six inches of hard red laterite (aluminum oxides, ferric oxides, iron crust). The rest of the scarp is soft material. In Anatolia, Turkey, southern Iran, and Egypt, people have carved their way into desert rock using primitive tools.

## Where to Live: The Notch

The most satisfactory place to locate a dwelling in the desert is at the base of the scarp, where a notch is cut by water splashing off the top. Once you are 16 feet into the rock, temperature variation is practically nil. In hot deserts, dwellings sit on the shade side; in cold deserts, they are placed where summer sun misses but winter sun enters.

The American Indians of the southwest have long made homes in these scarps and niches. Box canyons, 300-400 yards wide with abrupt cliffs, offer natural defense. A single fence across the canyon entrance keeps out large feral animals.

## Water in the Desert

The limiting factor in the desert is not food but water. Mollison emphasizes that "desert rainfall" is a meaningless statistic. An area with a "10 inch average rainfall" may have had no rain for three years, then receive 27 inches in one season.

Key water strategies include:

- Water holes form up on the scarp just before the drop-off, not in the wadi floor
- Sand stores water about 50% of its bulk -- you can fill a tank with sand and the water between grains will not evaporate
- Windmills (20-25 foot diameter) can return 25,000 gallons per day, supplying about 500 people
- You need a run-off area 20 times the area cultivated -- for one acre of garden, you need 20 acres of run-off

## The Desert Garden System

Mollison outlines a three-part desert garden system:

1. **Barrier around**: Moisture barrier (vertical plastic sheets or clay tamped barrier, penetrating at least three feet) preventing lateral water transfer
2. **Mulch within**: All organic matter conserved; without mulch, soil pH rapidly rises and becomes toxic to plants
3. **Trellis over**: High trellises using termite-proof wood (black locust, honey locust) for grapes, melons, and vine crops, with normal vegetables growing underneath

Drip irrigation is essential, ranging from primitive (ostrich egg with a single hole, inverted one-gallon wine flagon) to sophisticated modern Israeli systems.

## Stone Mulch

A key technique is stone mulch. Three or four stones around a tree can make the difference between survival and non-survival. Rocks gain heat rapidly by day, drawing up water from surrounding soil and evaporating it. At night they cool faster than surrounding sand, so any moisture condenses within the rocks and drips into the sand. Desert figs and citrus in their natural habitat are always found in loose rock or boulder piles.

## Broad-Scale Desert Reclamation

Mollison identifies three primary attack systems for broad-scale desert reclamation:

1. **Up-wind approach**: Start from the up-wind sector, planting tree belts that moisturize air downwind. Even 500 yards of tree belt, if established, will have amazing down-wind effects.
2. **Headwater approach**: Seize the headwaters and stop water from running off into evaporation pans. Reafforest the watershed and follow the water generated out into the desert.
3. **Nucleation approach**: Use existing oases and scattered settlements as nucleated areas, zoning outward while controlling feral and hoofed animals.

Additional strategies include:
- **Papanek seed spirals**: Plastic spiral with encapsulated seed, dropped from airplanes to imitate desert seed boring into sand
- **Chinese rice mat method**: Cover desert with rice mats, plant tamarisks and Australian acacias in humus-filled baskets
- **Stick fences**: 60% penetrable fences on 50-100 foot squares to stabilize sand around settlements

## Desert Ecologies

Mollison describes several functioning desert ecologies:

- **North African**: Date, melon, goat, and coffee -- a six-species ecology that runs for thousands of years
- **Aboriginal**: Seed eating birds, enormous tubers (legumes weighing 300-400 pounds), reptiles as protein source
- **American southwest**: Pinion pines providing staple food (60 bushels of pine nuts per day in a good year)

The desert produces abundant food -- you never run short. The essential scarce ingredient is water.

## Domestic Animals in the Desert

Pigeons are the number one desert domestic livestock. They are almost predator-free in their nesting habits, produce the highest market-value manure, and self-clean their nests (one egg pushed out, the other grows). Reptiles serve the role that fish serve for coastal dwellers.

Chickens help control termites by kicking apart their mud tunnels. Termites are the desert equivalent of earthworms -- primary decomposers -- and must be managed carefully as they attack living trees like carob.

## Desert Encroachment and Social Collapse

Mollison traces a clear pattern of desert-driven social collapse visible from Yugoslavia through Thailand and southward through Africa: as the desert encroaches, the farmer starts to fail; poorer people who cannot fence lose ground to animals; herdsmen increase; the herding economy becomes dominant; then comes "long migration and extinction and thin animals, and dying herdsmen staring towards the sunset."

He concludes that "the biggest job we have as a group of environmental designers is to start to decrease that effect" -- to replant deserts and reverse the largest area of global degradation that occurs each year.
