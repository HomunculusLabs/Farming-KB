## What Is a Swale

A swale is a critical technology for winter-wet climates that is not widely
used. It is also very useful when laying out forests. Swales are shallow
blade trenches cut on true contours with no movement of water along the
trenches. They are quite broad, hardly ever less than four feet wide, and
often much wider. Swales should not be used on steep slopes, only on
moderate to shallow slope systems. The trench captures rainwater and runoff,
holding it long enough to soak into the soil rather than running off the
property.

## Swale Construction

### Basic Design

Swales are cut along true contour lines using an A-frame level, bunyip
level, or laser level for accuracy. In clay soils, deepen the swale in
profile to increase water holding. In sandy and gravelly soils, widen the
swale to increase the surface area for infiltration. Along the swale, small
ponds are created at clay pockets where water will naturally collect, while
wider sections are dug where soil is more permeable.

### Construction Methods

Swales can be dug by hand with shovels, by small crawler tractors, or with
larger [[advanced-earthmoving-equipment]] for broadscale applications. The most efficient
hand-digging method uses two people: one with a broad shovel placed in the
ground, the other pulling with a rope attached to the shovel neck. This
rocking motion allows two people to throw up earth banks as fast as they can
walk. This is the traditional method used in Turkey and Afghanistan to
contour enormous acreages of very shallow country.

### Anchoring the Ends

Swales work best when their ends are anchored by a tree or bush, so water
is less likely to erode around the ends. A native tree or shrub should be
planted at both ends of each swale section. This prevents the water from
finding a path around the swale ends and causing concentrated erosion.

## Swale-Based Orchard Irrigation

Mollison describes an elegant system for irrigating orchards using swales
combined with reverse siphons. The system works as follows:

### The Terrace Swale System

Grade little shelves on approximately true contour at about 40-foot spacing,
which is correct for orchard trees. Down the slope, grade these platforms and
leave the area between them in grass. Plant trees in the outer edge of each
swale. A pipe from the water source (uphill) is connected through reverse
siphons going all the way down the slope.

### Reverse Siphon Irrigation

Bury a pipe that comes up in the next system below for reverse siphoning.
One person can water hundreds of trees in about an hour using this system.
The side channel has a slight fall. Water is blocked at intervals with short
pieces of four to six inch pipe fitted with plugs. When the upper section
saturates, the plugs are moved down to the next area, flooding each section
in sequence.
