# Water Runoff Factors and Estimation

Water runoff is a critical factor in landscape design, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where the balance between water capture and erosion determines the viability of agricultural and ecological systems. Understanding the factors that influence runoff allows designers to estimate water availability, plan storage systems, and implement strategies that convert destructive overland flow into productive infiltration.

## The Grassland Runoff Scale

Mollison provides a numerical scale (10–88) for estimating runoff based on vegetation type and land condition:

- **10**: Deep forest and tall grass prairie — minimal runoff, maximum infiltration
- **20**: Tall grass and forb communities grazed by livestock
- **30**: Drained and cultivated clays and clay loams
- **40**: Steppe and shrubland grazed by hoofed animals
- **50**: Undrained clays, claypans, urban roaded areas
- **60**: Skeletal soils and scattered vegetation
- **70–88**: Compacted soils, solonetic soils, non-wetting sands, extensive paved areas, full water storages

As vegetation degrades from perennial prairie to ephemeral annual grasses (through burning or overgrazing), runoff rises rapidly. Burning of clump grasses has a particularly sudden and dramatic effect on increasing water runoff.

## Seasonal and Fire Effects

Runoff is most destructive after prolonged dry periods that reduce vegetative cover, including:

- Long dry summers followed by thunderstorms
- Broadscale fires that strip ground cover
- Extensive autumn or spring cultivation
- Any period of landscape stress that exposes soil

These are precisely the conditions under which thunderstorms create gullies, flood thin soils, and trigger [[humus]] content
- **Low infiltration**: Non-wetting sands, sealed clay soils, compacted soils, sheet rock, massive rock domes

Gravels, boulder-banks, shattered rock ridges, unconsolidated pediment wastes, limestone outcrops, and eroded fold crests all serve as intake areas for aquifers. These remain open and reduce runoff unless fine blown dusts and sands bury crevices, or wash down into rock fissures to seal aquifer intakes — at which point springs cease flowing and overland flow increases.

## Time Required for Infiltration

Water soaks into soils without runoff when dispersed by tussock, litter, pebble, artificial pitting, or contour banking. The time required varies dramatically:

- **Sandy soils**: 30 minutes
- **Clay-fraction soils**: 3–4 days

Most plants can withstand 2–3 days of flooding. Best plant growth results from 0.5–1.2 metres of saturated soil. Infiltration rates should be timed in a test pit (pre-wetted, then filled) to determine how quickly this optimal depth is achieved. This data allows calculation of [[swale]] width, depth, and retardation basin sizing.

The target is wetting 0.5–1.2 m of soil in less than 48 hours. Given this data, the total interception dimensions of swales or banks can be calculated, and retardation basins designed to reduce water loss, increase vegetation, and control floods.

## Water Viscosity and Temperature Effects

Water density is minimally affected by temperature, but viscosity changes significantly with warmth. This has practical consequences:

- Day-time rains on warm surfaces run off faster than night rains or cool-period precipitation
- Desert monsoon regimes produce earlier flood peaks than winter-rain areas
- Both flow velocity (the primary erosive factor) and turbulence are accentuated by warmth and lower viscosity
- Viscous (cold) water may cause local water level rise in low-order streams at night

## Estimating Desert Runoff

Runoff in deserts ranges from 5–80% of rainfall depending on surface permeability. While averages may be low, flash floods (not uncommon in total precipitation) achieve much higher percentages. Key guidelines:

- **Average runoff**: approximately 20% of rainfall
- **Safe design ratio**: 20 hectares of catchment to 1 hectare of crop or cultivated vegetation
- **Reduced ratio** (reliable rain areas only): 15:1 catchment-to-field

Non-wetting dunes can flow as small streams in just 1 cm of rain, filling large storages in a single day.

## Design Implications

For designers working in water-limited environments, the key principles are:

1. Map all catchment factors — vegetation condition, soil type, slope, sealed surfaces
2. Time infiltration rates in test pits to size earthworks appropriately
3. Design for worst-case seasonal conditions (post-dry, post-fire)
4. Account for temperature effects on flow velocity and erosion
5. Use conservative catchment-to-crop ratios (20:1 minimum in unreliable rainfall zones)
6. Plan artificial storage and infiltration capacity proportionally to soil clay content
