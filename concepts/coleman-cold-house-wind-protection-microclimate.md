The key insight is that cold winter winds cause plant damage
primarily through two mechanisms:

1. **Desiccation**: Cold, dry winds strip moisture from plant
   tissues faster than the plants can replace it. This desiccating
   effect is often more damaging than the absolute temperature.

2. **Wind-chill**: Moving air removes the thin layer of warm air
   that naturally surrounds plant surfaces, effectively lowering
   the temperature the plant experiences below the ambient air
   temperature.

## The Two-Layer Protection Model

### The Outer Layer (Greenhouse Plastic)

The greenhouse structure itself provides the primary windbreak.

### The Inner Layer (Row Cover)

The floating row cover provides a second, smaller zone of wind
protection immediately around the plants. It also reduces the
rate of heat loss through radiation and convection from the plant
canopy and soil surface to the greenhouse air above.

The combined effect of the two layers creates what Coleman calls
a "twice-tempered" climate. Outdoors in Zone 5 Maine, the climate
under the inner row cover layer is effectively equivalent to
**Zone 8** — a shift of three hardiness zones.

## Evidence from Crop Performance

### Freezing Without Wind Damage

Coleman has observed that many cold-hardy vegetables can survive
being frozen solid, as long as they are protected from wind. When
temperatures drop well below freezing inside the cold house, the
crops may appear limp and frozen in the morning. But as the sun
warms the greenhouse, the plants thaw and recover completely. This
recovery from freezing is only possible because the plants have not
been simultaneously desiccated by wind.

### The "Miraculous Contrast"

Coleman describes the experience of visiting the cold houses in
midwinter: before entering, the frozen landscape outside gives no
hint that anything could be alive. But upon lifting the row cover
inside the greenhouse, the contrast is described as miraculous.
Rows of vigorous, healthy leaves in various shades of green, red,
maroon, and yellow stretch the length of the house — a "perpetual
spring" beneath the snow.
