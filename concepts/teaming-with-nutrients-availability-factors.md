## Moisture

Soil moisture levels directly influence nutrient availability and uptake. Water is the solvent that dissolves mineral nutrients and carries them into plant roots. Too little water means nutrients remain locked in soil particles despite being present; too much water displaces oxygen from pore spaces and creates anaerobic conditions that suppress [[ozier-pdc-composting-and-soil-building]] is the primary mechanism by which nutrients become available to plants in organic systems. Bacteria and fungi decompose organic matter, releasing nutrients in plant-available forms. Protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria and fungi, excreting excess nutrients at the root zone where plants can absorb them.

Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root surface area by hundreds of times, accessing phosphorus and micronutrients that plant roots alone cannot reach. These fungal networks also transport water and nutrients between plants, creating shared resource pools within plant communities.

The rhizosphere — the narrow zone of soil directly influenced by root secretions — hosts microbial populations 10-100 times denser than bulk soil. Plants actively shape this microbiome by exuding sugars, amino acids, and organic acids that feed specific microbial communities. In return, these microbes solubilize phosphorus, fix atmospheric nitrogen, produce growth hormones, and suppress pathogens.

## Soil Type and Nutrient Retention

Different soil types retain and release nutrients at vastly different rates:

- **Sandy soils:** Large particles with low surface area result in low CEC and rapid nutrient leaching. Nutrients must be applied in small, frequent doses. Adding organic matter dramatically improves retention.
- **Clay soils:** Tiny particles with enormous surface area create high CEC, holding nutrients tightly but sometimes too tightly for immediate plant uptake. Phosphorus fixation is a common problem in clay soils.
- **Loam soils:** A balanced mixture of sand, silt, and clay provides moderate CEC with good drainage and aeration — generally the most favorable for nutrient availability.
- **Organic soils:** Very high CEC from humic and fulvic acids. Nutrient retention is excellent but these soils can be challenging to manage due to shrink-swell behavior and subsidence.

## Redox Potential

Redox (reduction-oxidation) potential measures the electron availability in soil, which determines whether nutrients are in oxidized or reduced forms. Well-aerated soils have high redox potential, favoring oxidized forms like nitrate (NO3-) and sulfate (SO4 2-). Waterlogged soils have low redox potential, favoring reduced forms like ammonium (NH4+) and sulfide (S 2-).

In flooded or waterlogged conditions, manganese and iron become more soluble and can reach toxic concentrations, while nitrogen is lost through denitrification. This explains why plants in poorly drained soils sometimes show iron toxicity symptoms even when iron levels in the soil are not excessive.

## Practical Implications for Gardeners

The interaction of all these factors means that simply adding fertilizer is never sufficient. Gardeners must manage the whole system:

- Test soil regularly to know baseline nutrient levels and pH
- Maintain high organic matter to improve CEC, moisture retention, and biological activity
- Use mulches to regulate soil temperature and moisture
