# Intuitive Reasoning Versus Inductive Experimentation in Agriculture

## Overview

Intuitive reasoning, by contrast, begins with a vision of the ideal and works backward to discover how nature might achieve it without human interference.

## The Inductive Method in Scientific Farming

Scientific agriculture proceeds primarily through inductive experimentation: conducting many small tests under controlled conditions, collecting data, and gradually building generalizations. Scientists isolate individual factors such as [[fukuoka-textdoc-critique-inductive-deductive-reasoning-agriculture]] in agriculture begins with an ideal vision of how cultivation should proceed, infers the environmental conditions under which this ideal might arise, and devises a means of achieving it. This method is historically the driving force behind major leaps in development.

However, Fukuoka distinguishes between ordinary deduction and what he calls "true deduction." Ordinary deduction is merely the inverse of induction, defined in relation to it. True deduction originates "at a point beyond the world of phenomena" and arises from philosophical understanding of the natural world.

The creative roots of natural farming lie in true intuitive understanding. The departure point must be a grasp of nature gained by fixing one's gaze on the natural world that extends beyond immediate surroundings. An infinitude of yield-improving possibilities lie hidden in this broader view.

## The Mountain Climbing Analogy

Fukuoka compares induction and deduction to two climbers ascending a rock face:

- The **lower climber** checks his footing before giving the lead climber a boost, playing an inductive role.
- The **lead climber** lets down a rope and pulls the lower climber up, playing a deductive role.

Together they form a whole, making slow progress and never going beyond the peak they have already sighted. But to reach the summit by a fundamentally different route, one must go beyond this binary. True intuitive reasoning is like descending onto the peak from a helicopter, bypassing the incremental climb entirely.

This analogy illustrates that while induction and deduction are complementary, both are constrained to a single path up the mountain. Dramatic improvement requires an entirely different approach that transcends both methods.

## Intuitive Reasoning as a Third Way

Intuitive reasoning transcends both induction and deduction. It does not proceed from data to theory or from theory to data, but from direct perception of nature's wholeness. Rather than asking "what small improvement can I make to this factor?", the intuitive farmer asks "what does the land want to become, and how can I assist without interfering?"

Intuitive reasoning requires a fundamental shift in perspective. One must distance oneself from the immediate situation and observe it at a remove, from the standpoint of Mu, seeking to return to Mu nature. This may seem difficult, but the world beyond immediate reality is actually nothing more than the world as it was prior to human awareness.

Fukuoka describes this undivided and inseparable unity as the "nothingness" that must be understood as it is. To start from Mu and return to Mu, that is natural farming. The starting point of natural farming is also its destination, and the journey in-between.

## The Hotel Analogy for Yield

To illustrate why inductive analysis of individual factors fails, Fukuoka uses the analogy of a hotel:

- The building sits on a rock foundation representing nature. Floors and rooms represent cultivation conditions and factors.
- **Yield** is determined by the building's total capacity and how fully each room is occupied.
- The **upper limit** of yield is set by the natural environment, the rock foundation and the building site. This maximum yield may be called the natural yield and is the best and highest yield attainable.
- **Actual harvest** is lower because some rooms remain vacant, representing weaknesses in cultivation factors.

Scientific farming attempts to fill rooms one by one, treating each factor independently. But the rooms are interconnected; renovating one room affects all others. The only true way to increase yield is to enlarge the building itself, to work with nature's full capacity rather than trying to patch individual deficiencies.

Fukuoka extends the analogy further: one cannot know how business is faring at a hotel just by looking at the number of guest rooms or the number of vacancies. There may be many empty rooms, but other rooms may be packed full. Just as a new company president can dramatically change morale within the company, the entire yield of a crop may turn on a change in a single factor. All the rooms and floors of the building are separate and distinct, yet all are intimately linked together into one organic whole.

This analogy directly challenges Liebig's law of the minimum, which holds that yield is dominated by whichever nutrient is in shortest supply. Fukuoka argues that factors vary in a continuously fluid relationship with those of other elements; at times these work together, at other times they cancel each other out. There are no absolute criteria with which to judge whether one element or condition is good or bad, excessive or insufficient.

## Photosynthesis Research as Case Study

Fukuoka examines the scientific study of photosynthesis as a detailed case study in the futility of inductive approaches. Scientists studied how to maximize starch production by increasing photosynthetic activity while minimizing respiration. They identified numerous factors: carbon dioxide, stomata closure, water uptake, water temperature, and sunlight affecting photosynthesis; and sugar, oxygen, wind strength, nutrients, and humidity affecting respiration.

The critical discovery was that the same conditions favoring photosynthesis also promote respiration. High temperatures around 40 degrees Celsius and abundant sunlight increase starch production but also increase starch consumption through respiration. At 30 degrees Celsius, photosynthetic activity is high and remains in balance with respiration, giving the highest yield. Under low temperatures with fair conditions, normal yields are maintained because good root activity supplies ample nutrients.

The combinations of production factors and elements that can occur in nature are limitless and may lead to any number of different yields. Scientists were unable to design a course that assembled the best conditions because the best conditions cannot be combined under natural circumstances. And maximizing photosynthesis does not guarantee maximum yields; nor do yields necessarily increase when respiration is minimized.

There is no standard by which to judge what "maximum" and "minimum" are. An optimal temperature that is workable under the greatest range of conditions will help raise normal yields but is not the temperature required for high yields. Even sunlight, which increases photosynthesis, is not necessarily accompanied by a rise in yield. In Japan, yields are higher in the northern part of Honshu than in sunny Kyushu to the south.

Fukuoka concludes that scientific visions of high yields based on rice plant physiology amount to empty theorizing. The numbers may add up on paper, but no one can build a theory like this and get it to work in practice. The rice scientist well-versed in his particular specialty is like the sports commentator who can give a good rundown of a match and may even make a respectable coach, but is not himself a top-notch athlete.
