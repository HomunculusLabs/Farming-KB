# Coastal Permaculture Design

updated: 2026-18

Permaculture Two (1979) Section 5.3 addresses the special challenges of coastal permaculture: salt winds, sand blast, exposure, and the opportunities presented by mild climate, night dews, and tidal resources.

## The Coastal Environment

Across the unmodifiable plain of water, winds arrive at gale force, carrying salt and abrasive sand grains. Similarities to both desert and "altiplano" or high plateau country are obvious. Birds, plants, and other species demonstrate this by common occurrence in desert, coastal, and montane regions. Waders, choughs, currajongs, crows, starlings, certain berry plants and insects show the same distribution, and some frogs share coast and high plateau or coast and desert.

## Plant Defences for Coastal Sites

All tough, woolly, thick-leaved, waxy, shiny, and needle-leaved trees serve the same function: resistance to wind, drying out, and salt or sand. Useful categories:

- "Gorky" barks and fibrous-stemmed species resist sand blast
- Self-mulching species like tamarisk and casuarina
- Fibrous palm and yucca stems
- Plant-water reservoirs: baobab, bottle trees, fleshy ice-plants, memembryantheum, saltbushes
- New Zealand coprosmas and hardy coastal pines (Araucaria, Callitris)

## Front-Line Windbreak Species

Tall, graceful windbreaks that stand against the first onslaught:

- Coconut Palm (Cocos nucifera)
- Cotton Palm (Washingtonia filifera)
- Canary Palm (Phoenix canariensis)
- Date Palm (Phoenix dactylus)
- Norfolk Island Pine (Araucaria excelsa, A. heterophylla)
- Macrocarpa Pine (Cedrus macrocarpa)
- Rottnest Island Pine (Callitris robusta, C. tambrica)
- Oyster Bay Pine (C. tasmanica)

## Second-Rank Hedge Species

Lower, bushy species that suppress ground wind and form thickets:

- Boobyalla and Acacia (A. sophorea, A. cyclops, A. myopororum)
- Banksia (B. marginata, B. serrata, B. attenuata, B. menziesii)
- Tamarisk (Tamarix aphylla, T. parviflora)
- Carob (Ceratonia siliqua)
- Cape thorn (Lycium ferocissimum)
- Crested Wattle (Albizia lophantha)
- Coprosma (C. repens, C. retusa, C. kirkii)
- Buddleia (B. salvifolia, B. globosa)
- Metrosideros (NZ Christmas tree, M. excelsa)

## Inner Garden Hedges

Lower hedges for garden microclimate:

- Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis)
- Wormwood (Artemisia)
- Euonymus (E. japonica)
- Chilean barberry (Berberis darwinii)
- Pampas grass

## Damp-Area Coastal Species

Coastal ti-tree (Melaleuca) hedgerows for stakes and poles: M. pubescens, M. hypericifolia, Leptospermum laevigatum, M. flavescens.

## Inner Productive Species

As front ranks decrease wind, a complexity of more useful species follow:

- Olive (Olea europaea)
- Carob Bean (Ceratonia siliqua)
- Kaffir Plum (Harpephyllum caffrum)
- Bamboo species
- Quinces and stone fruits, citrus
- Nectarines appreciate mild winters
- Needle-leaved pines (P. pinea, P. pineaster, P. radiata) provide acid mulch offsetting alkalinity

## Salt-Burn: The Real Enemy

Salt death of leaves rather than wind-pruning accounts for the streamlined shape of trees near the sea (Lillian Callow, Trees for the Sea Coast, The Tree Society, Perth, W.A.). Wind protection reduces salt deposition on leaves more than it reduces wind per se.

## Ground Covers and Creepers

- Kennedia prostrata -- Good leguminous mulch (Ruth Geneff, Perth)
- Mesembryanthemum, Dolichos, Tecoma, Tetrogonia prevent sand drift
- Annual and perennial lupins thrive as coastal scrubs, binding loose sand
- On rocky headlands: Quercus ilex, Casuarina equisetifolia, Callitris spp.

## Trellis for Early Wind Protection

In new gardens, the great lack is wind shelter. Species like citrus, avocado, and macadamia struggle. The fastest possible assistance is building trellis at near right-angles to E, W, and N walls. Trellis has multiple effects:

- Separates functional space into recreational, garden, or service area
- Prevents cold wind flow along walls, acting as a sun trap
- Provides basic structure for vine crop

## Horizontal Trellis Uses

- Shade windows from full summer sun
- Create overhead vine trellis to shelter tender crops in extreme summer climates
- Increase solar radiation on crops

## The Shadehouse Effect

The refreshing coolness of a shadehouse in hot Australian summer is remarkable. Even tiny pools, a few ferns, and a spray or drip of water increase the effect both physically (evaporation) and psychologically. Air in such places has a different quality, an aliveness normally missing from still areas.
