Windbreaks are a fundamental element in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-windbreak-design-and-shelter|shelter]] for crops,
livestock, and soil by modifying wind flow across a site. Their effectiveness depends
primarily on two critical factors: **height** and **density (permeability)**. Properly
designed windbreaks can dramatically increase crop yields, conserve soil and water, and
create microclimates that extend the productive capacity of a landscape.

## Height and Permeability: The Two Critical Factors

- **Height** governs the extent of the sheltered area downwind. Windbreak height should
  ideally be **one-fifth of the space between windbreaks**. Taller windbreaks protect a
  proportionally larger area, making height the single most important design variable.
- **Permeability** determines the quality of wind reduction. Species should permit
  **40–70% of wind to pass through** the barrier. Solid barriers create severe
  turbulence on the lee side, reducing effective shelter distance. Moderately permeable
  barriers produce a more gradual and extensive wind reduction zone.

## Spacing Guidelines

- In areas subject to **severe wind**, windbreaks should be spaced at **no more than
  20 times the hedgerow height** apart.
- This ensures continuous overlap of sheltered zones, preventing exposed gaps where wind
  can accelerate and cause damage.
- Proper spacing maximizes the cumulative shelter effect while using the minimum number
  of plantings necessary.

## Frost Pockets

[[mollison-designers-windbreak-configurations-for-crops-and-orchards]] can inadvertently create **frost pockets** in sheltered hollows.
Cold air drains downhill under gravity, collecting in low-lying areas behind windbelts
where it pools and causes frost damage. Designers must account for cold air drainage
patterns and avoid placing frost-sensitive crops in these depressions. Where unavoidable,
additional ventilation channels or upslope planting gaps may allow cold air to drain.

## Windbreak Configurations (A through E2)

Mollison outlines five principal windbreak configurations suited to different climates,
sites, and purposes.

### Configuration A — Dense Windbreak with Bare Stem

A tall, dense windbreak with a bare trunk below the canopy, allowing air flow near ground
level while blocking wind at higher elevations. Suitable for general wind protection
without impeding low-level drainage or access.

- **Example species:** Cupressus, Pinus, [[casuarina]]

### Configuration B — Alternate Zig-Zag Permeable Planting

Staggered rows in a zig-zag pattern, well-suited to **seafronts** and coastal exposures.
Gaps between individual trees are covered by trees in the adjacent row, providing
filtered airflow that reduces salt-laden wind damage while maintaining ventilation.

- **Example species:** Araucaria, Pinus, Casuarina

### Configuration C — Compound High-Density Windbreak

A multi-layered, high-density windbreak for **eroding beaches** and severely exposed
sites. Ground covers grow through and beneath trees, creating a dense vegetative matrix
from soil surface to canopy that stabilizes sand and deflects wind at multiple levels.

### Configuration D — Permeable Low Hedgerow of Legumes

A low, permeable hedgerow of nitrogen-fixing leguminous species providing moderate wind
protection while improving soil fertility. Suited to broadacre landscapes where hedgerow
height must be managed and soil enrichment is a parallel objective.

- **Example species:** Acacia, Leucaena, Prosopis, Albizia, Glyricidia, tagasaste

### Configuration E1 — Savannah-Style Open-Spaced Trees

Widely spaced trees scattered through crops or pasture, mimicking a savannah structure.
Best suited to **arid areas** where dense plantings would compete excessively for water.
The scattered trees provide shade, reduce ground-level wind speed, and contribute leaf
litter to soil organic matter.

### Configuration E2 — Complete Crown Cover

Interplanted fruit trees combined with leguminous shelter trees forming a continuous
crown cover. The leguminous component provides [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-and-green-manure]] wind shelter while
fruit trees produce a food yield, creating a multi-functional protective and productive
planting.

## Windbreaks Integrated with Crops

Windbreaks are integral to productive agricultural [[soil-biota]]
  that depend on it.

## Water Conservation Benefits

By reducing wind speed, windbreaks dramatically decrease water loss through evaporation:

- In **strong wind**, water evaporation is **halved** for distances up to **10 times
  the windbreak height**.
- **Snow moisture** is increased by windbreaks — snow deposits within the sheltered
  zone rather than being blown away, contributing valuable spring soil moisture recharge
  in colder climates and at higher elevations.

## Crop Yield Increases

The shelter effect of windbreaks produces substantial, well-documented yield improvements:

| Crop | Yield Increase |
|------|---------------|
| Avocado | 100% |
| Corn | 45% |
| Alfalfa | 60–70% |
| Wheat | 30% |
| Lettuce | 7–18% |

These figures underscore the economic justification for [[windbreak-establishment-and-benefits]]. The
returns in [[fukuoka-textdoc-increased-production-not-increased-yields]] frequently outweigh the costs of planting and
maintaining the windbreak itself.

## Spiral Windbreak Pattern for Garden Vegetables

For **garden-scale** [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]], a **spiral windbreak pattern** provides
shelter from multiple wind directions while creating a range of microclimates within a
compact area. The spiral form allows diverse vegetable species with differing wind and
exposure tolerances to be grown in close proximity, each finding its preferred position
along the gradient from exposed outer edge to sheltered interior.
