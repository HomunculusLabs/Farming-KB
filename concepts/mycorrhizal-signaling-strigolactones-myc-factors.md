# Mycorrhizal Signaling Molecules: Strigolactones and Myc Factors

The establishment of mycorrhizal symbiosis requires a sophisticated molecular dialogue between plant host and fungal partner. The discovery of strigolactones as plant signals perceived by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and the identification of fungal Myc factors (Mycorrhizal factors) that trigger plant symbiotic responses, represent landmark advances in understanding how these ancient partnerships are initiated and regulated.

## Strigolactones: Plant-to-Fungus Signals

Strigolactones are sesquiterpene lactones originally identified as seed germination stimulants for root parasitic plants of the Striga and Orobanche genera. The groundbreaking discovery by Akiyama et al. (2005) that these same compounds act as hyphal branching factors for arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi transformed our understanding of mycorrhizal signaling:

- **Hyphal branching induction**: At extremely low concentrations (10-13 M), strigolactones stimulate extensive hyphal branching in AM fungi, increasing the probability of contact with the host root. This branching response is one of the earliest detectable fungal responses to the host plant.

- **Dual role as plant hormones**: Strigolactones have since been recognized as a novel class of endogenous plant hormones present in a wide range of angiosperms from Arabidopsis thaliana to rice (Gomez-Roldan et al. 2008; Umehara et al. 2008). They inhibit shoot branching, regulate root architecture, and mediate plant responses to phosphate deficiency.

- **Nutrient stress signaling**: Strigolactone production by roots is upregulated under phosphate deficiency, creating a physiological link between the plant's nutrient status and its receptivity to mycorrhizal colonization. When phosphorus is abundant, reduced strigolactone exudation signals lower demand for mycorrhizal partnership.

- **Evolutionary significance**: The dual function of strigolactones -- as signals to beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and as germination stimulants for parasitic plants -- suggests that parasitic plants have co-opted an ancient plant-fungal communication pathway.

## Fungal Myc Factors: Fungus-to-Plant Signals

The fungal partners in arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis secrete signaling molecules collectively termed Myc factors that are perceived by the host plant and trigger the downstream genetic program required for successful colonization:

- **Perception by plant receptors**: Plant receptors (including the SYM pathway components) detect Myc factors in the rhizosphere, initiating a signal transduction cascade that leads to calcium spiking and activation of symbiosis-related genes.

- **Common SYM pathway**: The signaling pathway activated by Myc factors shares components with the Nod factor signaling pathway used in legume-rhizobium symbiosis. This shared genetic machinery (the common SYM pathway) reflects the evolutionary conservation of endosymbiotic signaling mechanisms.

- **Activation of symbiosis genes**: Myc factor perception activates plant genes involved in cortical cell preparation, prepenetration apparatus formation, and the accommodation of fungal structures within root cells.

- **Chemical nature**: Myc factors are identified among the fungal phytotoxins and other exudates. While their precise chemical structures are still being characterized, they are thought to include lipochitooligosaccharides or related compounds that share structural features with rhizobial Nod factors.

## Signal Exchange Sequence

The molecular dialogue between AM fungus and plant host follows a coordinated sequence:

1. **Root exudation**: Under phosphorus limitation, the host root releases strigolactones and other compounds into the rhizosphere.

2. **Fungal activation**: AM fungal hyphae detect strigolactones and undergo extensive branching, increasing exploration of the root vicinity.

3. **Hyphopodium formation**: Upon root contact, the fungus differentiates an appressorium-like structure (hyphopodium) on the root surface.

4. **Myc factor release**: The fungus secretes Myc factors that are perceived by root epidermal cells.

5. **SYM pathway activation**: Plant receptors trigger the common SYM signaling cascade, involving nuclear calcium spiking and activation of transcription factors such as CYCLOPS, NSP1, and NSP2.

6. **Prepenetration apparatus formation**: The plant cell assembles a specialized cytoplasmic bridge (the prepenetration apparatus) that guides the fungal hyphae through the epidermis.

7. **Cortical colonization**: The fungus spreads through the cortex, forming arbuscules within cortical cells through a second round of Myc factor signaling and SYM pathway activation.

## Integration with Plant Hormone Networks

Mycorrhizal signaling intersects with multiple plant hormone pathways:

- **Auxin**: Mycorrhizal colonization modifies root auxin transport and accumulation, affecting lateral root formation and root architecture in ways that may promote further colonization.

- **Ethylene**: This hormone can act as a negative regulator of mycorrhizal colonization, and the plant may modulate ethylene levels to control the extent of fungal spread.

- **Abscisic acid**: ABA influences mycorrhizal colonization, particularly under drought stress, potentially coordinating the plant's stress responses with its symbiotic relationships.

- **Jasmonic acid**: JA signaling interacts with mycorrhizal establishment, potentially mediating the plant's defense responses to prevent over-colonization while allowing symbiotic development.

## Future Directions

Key unresolved questions in mycorrhizal signaling include the precise chemical identity of Myc factors, the quantitative relationship between strigolactone concentration and fungal response specificity, and the mechanisms by which plants discriminate between beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and pathogenic organisms using overlapping signaling pathways.

## Crosstalk with Pathogen Signaling

The SYM pathway for mycorrhizal signaling shares components with plant defense signaling pathways, creating potential conflicts between symbiotic acceptance and pathogen rejection. Plants must discriminate between beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and pathogenic organisms using overlapping signaling molecules.

This crosstalk may explain why some pathogens can suppress mycorrhizal colonization by activating defense pathways, and conversely why mycorrhizal colonization can induce systemic resistance against certain pathogens. Understanding this signaling crosstalk is crucial for managing both beneficial and harmful plant-fungal interactions in agricultural systems.

## Exudate Composition and Signaling

Beyond strigolactones, plant root exudates contain a complex mixture of secondary metabolites, amino acids, organic acids, and sugars that influence mycorrhizal signaling and fungal behavior. Flavonoids, previously recognized primarily for their role in rhizobium-legume signaling, have been shown to affect AM fungal hyphal growth and branching.

The qualitative and quantitative composition of root exudates varies with plant species, genotype, developmental stage, and environmental conditions, creating a dynamic signaling landscape that modulates mycorrhizal interactions.

## Downstream Genetic Responses

The perception of Myc factors by plant receptors triggers a cascade of gene expression changes that prepare the root for fungal colonization. Key downstream genes include those encoding for:

- **EXO70i subunits**: Components of the exocyst complex involved in vesicle trafficking during periarbuscular membrane formation.

- **MtBCP1 and related genes**: Encoding proteins that may regulate membrane dynamics during fungal accommodation.

- **AM-specific transporters**: Including phosphate and ammonium transporters that are specifically expressed in arbusculated cells, enabling nutrient exchange at the fungal-plant interface.

- **RAM2 and RAM1**: Genes required for the production of cutin monomers that form the intraradical hyphal interface, essential for proper arbuscule development.

These genetic responses are largely shared with nodulation signaling, reinforcing the concept of a common symbiosis signaling pathway that evolved before the divergence of these two plant-microbe symbioses.
