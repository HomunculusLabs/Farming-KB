# Yeast Biodiversity and Inventory Protocols

Yeasts are a polyphyletic group of ascomycetous and basidiomycetous fungi characterized by a unicellular growth phase and sexual stages not enclosed in fruiting bodies. Approximately 100 genera comprising more than 700 described species are known (Kurtzman and Fell 1998), but current evidence suggests these represent less than 1% of the species that occur in nature. This page outlines the taxonomic framework, global distribution patterns, and standardized protocols for inventorying yeast biodiversity, based on the work of Cletus P. Kurtzman and Jack W. Fell.

## Taxonomic Status and the Evolving Definition of Yeasts

The definition of "yeast" has changed markedly over recent decades. Originally centered on *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* and related ascomycetous species as the "typical" yeast, the concept expanded dramatically when Banno (1967) demonstrated a basidiomycetous life cycle for a species of *Rhodotorula*. This was followed quickly by demonstrations of basidiomycetous life cycles in numerous other species (Fell et al. 1969, 1973; Kwon-Chung 1975).

Molecular evolution studies further broadened and refined the definition. Analyses of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal DNA (rDNA) have shown:

- **Ascomycetous yeasts** (including most hemiascomycetes such as Cephaloascus, Ascoidea, and Dipodascus) form a sister group to the euascomycetes rather than a more primitive group (Kurtzman 1994; Kurtzman and Robnett 1998).
- **Schizosaccharomyces, Taphrina, Protomyces, Pneumocystis,** and **Saitoella** comprise a divergent group phylogenetically basal to both ascomycetous yeasts and euascomycetes.
- **Basidiomycetous yeasts** are a distinct group including common genera such as *Rhodosporidium* and *Filobasidiella*, as well as morphologically more complex *Tremella*.

### The Identification Challenge

One of the major impediments to determining yeast distributions and ecosystem functions is the poor taxonomic resolution provided by conventional identification methods. Species and genera defined by phenotypic characters such as cell morphology and responses on various growth tests are increasingly recognized as inadequate. Many phenotypic characters considered taxonomically definitive vary among strains of the same species. As a consequence, divergent strains of known species may be mistaken for novel taxa, whereas new species may go unrecognized.

Currently, DNA nucleotide sequences of unknown yeast strains are compared with those of authentic reference strains for species identification. It is clear that molecular genetic methods must be used for accurate biodiversity appraisal. However, more rapid molecular methods will be needed if large numbers of isolates are to be examined. A database of 26S 5'-end base sequences is adequate for identification of most species, while species-specific PCR primers allow faster screening of large isolate numbers.

## Global Distribution Patterns

Yeasts are ubiquitous throughout the biosphere (Phaff et al. 1966). Most yeasts are saprotrophs that assimilate plant- or animal-derived organic compounds. Although usually decomposers, some species are pathogens of plants and animals. Their distribution follows available nutrient gradients:

### Soil Habitats

Occasional soil populations of 10⁵–10⁶ colony-forming units (cells) per gram of soil suggest the presence of actively growing cells. Some species are isolated exclusively from soils: *Debaryomyces (Schwanniomyces) occidentalis*, *Lipomyces* species, *Schizoblastosporion starkeyi-henricii*, and certain *Cryptococcus* species.

### Aquatic Environments

Population levels of aquatic yeasts are usually highest in fresh waters and decrease in marine waters with increased depth and distance from land (Hagler and Ahearn 1987). Open ocean populations occur at densities as low as 10 cells/g but may increase to 10³ cells/g in waters associated with plankton blooms, current boundaries, surface slicks, thermoclines, or pollutants (Fell 1976).

### Plant Materials

The majority of yeast species are collected from fallen plant materials and other organic matter (Phaff and Starmer 1987). The highest yeast densities are associated with concentrations of assimilatable sugars and other carbon sources:

- **Leaf surfaces**: di Menna (1959) reported 10⁵–10⁷ viable cells/g of fresh foliage.
- **Flowers and decaying fruits**: Support wide spectra of species in high numbers.
- **Cactus cladodes**: Densities as great as 10⁶ cells/g of tissue from decaying cladodes (Phaff and Starmer 1987).

### Insect Associations

Insects represent rich sources of yeasts, especially wood-borers and species of *Drosophila*. Phaff and Starmer (1987) summarized yeasts found with different types of insects. These associations are often highly specific.

## Protocols for Inventories

### Collecting Samples

Most yeasts are obligate aerobes and therefore require oxygen for growth and reproduction; consequently, they are usually found in upper layers of soil and sediment. Exceptions include yeasts preserved in deep layers of ice.

**Terrestrial and shallow-water samples**: Collected manually using sterile jars, vials, reclosable plastic bags with zippers, and coring devices (Parkinson et al. 1971).

**Deep-water sediments**: Collected remotely using grabs, gravity and piston cores, or submersibles.

**Deep-sea water**: Collected through the water column with samplers such as the Sterile Bag Sampler. A 50-ml sample is usually adequate; multiple samples can be taken from a single location. Minimal water should be included to avoid anaerobic conditions.

Temperature and salinity must be obtained simultaneously with yeast collection. Samples should be processed immediately or held on ice. Containers should not be airtight (to avoid anaerobic conditions) but adequately closed to prevent contamination. Processing should occur in a contaminant-free environment, preferably a culture room or hood. Air drafts are the primary contamination source.

### Isolation Strategies

**Direct plating**: Soil particles are placed directly on agar medium and incubated for at least 24 hours. Particles are examined microscopically in situ under low power to locate yeast growth at the particle/medium interface. Suspected colonies are transferred to a microscope slide for closer inspection, confirmed colonies are streaked for purification, and single-cell colonies are moved to slant culture tubes.

**Soil dilution**: A known quantity of soil is thoroughly mixed with sterile water (seawater for marine samples) in a test tube, and a dilution series is prepared. Suspensions are spread onto isolation media, and colonies are recorded and enumerated.

**Water filtration**: Water is passed through 0.45-μm nitrocellulose filters. The filter is placed face-up on solid nutrient agar containing bacterial inhibitors. Volume filtered ranges from milliliters to 2–3 liters depending on population density. Colonies are enumerated after appropriate incubation.

**Enrichment**: Samples are placed in flasks with nutrient medium tailored to the target yeast's specific preferences—customized by adding specific carbon and nitrogen compounds, vitamins, growth factors, manipulating salinity (for marine species), or substratum levels (for osmophilic species). For example, *Cyniclomyces guttulatus* requires amino acids, 35–40°C incubation, and elevated CO₂. *Rhodosporidium lusitaniae* is isolated from forest soil using lignin-related phenolic compounds as sole carbon source. Preparations are incubated 12–24 hours with continuous shaking. Filamentous fungi grow in pellets while yeasts remain free cells. The enrichment suspension is then streaked onto isolation plates.

### Isolation Media

Yeast Isolation Medium (PYG; Appendix II) is commonly used. Wickerham's YM medium is an alternative. Various carbon sources can substitute for glucose. Fresh or filtered seawater is used depending on habitat—37‰ for oceanic sampling, 15‰ for brackish/estuarine samples. Media are solidified with 2–3% agar. Chloramphenicol (200 mg/L) or penicillin G + streptomycin sulfate (each 150–500 mg/L) are added as antibacterial agents.

### Overgrowth Problems and Solutions

Filamentous fungal overgrowth is a constant problem. Solutions include: shake culture (pelletizes filamentous fungi), lower incubation temperatures (12°C for temperate/tropical samples), daily plate examination for early isolation, and pH manipulation (most yeasts tolerate pH 3.5–3.8 while many bacteria do not). Incubation conditions should mimic ambient environmental conditions: cold habitats sampled at ~5°C, thermophilic species at elevated temperatures.

## Abundance, Sampling Bias, and Limitations

Several important limitations affect yeast biodiversity estimates:

- **Patchy distribution**: Results from one time and place cannot be easily extrapolated even a few centimeters away.
- **Media selectivity**: All isolation media are selective and artificial; prevalent soil strains may not grow when removed from natural substrata.
- **Weed species**: Dominant species rapidly overgrow other strains on artificial media.
- **Plate capacity**: Maximum ~300 colonies per plate means rare species (≤1% of community) may be missed.
- **Standing crop vs. dynamics**: Cell counts do not reflect turnover rates, hyphal fragmentation, spore release, or consumption by invertebrates.

Multiple collections at each site are required to delineate spatial and temporal patterns. GPS coordinates and detailed environmental data (climate, soil, vegetation) should be recorded. Collection protocols must integrate with other biological disciplines for optimal understanding of ecological interactions.

## Characterization of Organisms

Standard yeast description includes fermentation rates on 7–8 sugars, growth on ~40 carbon sources and 1–2 nitrogen compounds (nitrate, lysine), growth at various temperatures, vegetative growth morphology, and any sexual state. The diazonium-blue B (DBB) test discriminates ascomycetes from basidiomycetes.

Despite extensive phenotypic work, misidentifications remain common, driving the shift to molecular characterization. Two common methods are nuclear DNA reassociation and comparison of nucleotide sequences from the 5'-end of large (26S) rRNA and rDNA. A database of 26S D1/D2 sequences enables identification by BLAST search and is necessary for discovery of new species. For large-scale screening, species-specific PCR primers designed from GenBank data provide faster throughput.

## Reference Culture Collections

Major yeast culture collections worldwide include CBS (Netherlands), DBVPG (Italy), IFO/JCM (Japan), CCGMC (China), PYCC (Portugal), DSMZ (Germany), VKM (Russia), NRRL/ATCC/FST-UCD (USA), NCYC (UK), and CCY/RIVE (Slovakia). Reference cultures are essential for correct species identification.
