# Orders of Magnitude and Hierarchical Design

## Overview

In [[permaculture-design]], the concept of "orders"—borrowed from stream hydrology and fractal geometry—provides a powerful framework for understanding how size, function, and behavior change systematically across scales. Orders of magnitude govern everything from the species found in different stream reaches to the appropriate size of human settlements, from trophic levels in food webs to the stable functioning of social hierarchies. Understanding these orders allows designers to work with natural scaling laws rather than against them.

## The Order Principle

At each point of branching (or size and volume change), everything else changes: pressures, flows, velocities, gaseous exchange rates, and the life forms that associate with the specific size of branches. This is how we make sense of:

- **Fish species** distributed across stream orders (from turbulent headwaters to quiet estuaries)
- **Bird species** distributed across tree branching (from trunk to leaf laminae)
- **Human settlements** distributed across size hierarchies (from hamlet to metropolis)

The key insight is that order determines not just physical scale but **behavior, function, and associated life forms**.

## Physical and Biological Scaling Laws

### Stream Orders as a Universal Model

Stream branching reveals remarkably consistent ratios that apply broadly:

| Parameter | Ratio |
|-----------|-------|
| Bifurcation ratio (number of branches per order) | ~3:1 |
| Length ratio (branch length increase per order) | ~2:1 |
| Meander ratio (stream width to meander wavelength) | ~1:3.6 |

### The Scaling Factor Changes with Dimension

The scaling factor itself varies with the type of system:

- **Stream branches**: Factor of ~3
- **Trophic pyramids** (food webs): Factor of ~10
- **Physical entities** (protons to universes): Increasing ratios with size

Giant and dwarf stars behave very differently despite being the same class of object—their behavior is determined by their order of magnitude. Similarly, a village and a city function according to fundamentally different rules.

## Orders Across Natural Phenomena

Orders of magnitude are observed in a wide range of phenomena:

- **Human settlement size**: Hamlets, villages, towns, cities—each with characteristic functions and populations
- **Social hierarchies**: Numbers in functional groups follow predictable scaling
- **Trophic levels**: Energy decreases by roughly a factor of 10 at each level in food pyramids
- **Zoological families**: Size distributions within allied animal families follow ordered patterns
- **Physical entities**: From subatomic particles to galaxies, each scale has distinct behaviors

### Behavioral Thresholds

The concept of behavioral thresholds is critical. For example:
- A riot cannot be created by one person
- Fewer than 15 people rarely clap as an audience
- Villages are conservative at about the 1,000-people order and become unstable much below or above that number

These thresholds suggest that there are stable and unstable "attractor" sizes for different types of systems, and designers should aim to work within stable ranges.

## Design Implications

### Roads and Access Networks

When we alter one aspect of a branching system, the whole regime changes. Increasing the size of a highway inevitably alters all connected roads—the branching ratios demand compensatory changes throughout the network. Designers must study and apply appropriate and stable size and factor classes for specific constructs.

### Settlement Design

When designing a village, the process should be:

1. **Study the orders of size** of settlements in the region
2. **Choose an appropriate stable order** (e.g., the ~1,000-person village)
3. **Derive the number and types of services** needed for that size
4. **Determine occupations** required by the service profile
5. **Design space and types of shops and offices** appropriate to the population
6. **Plan the access network** following branching principles

### Dam Spillways and Drainage

The sequence of dam spillways should respect the stable orders of branching. Each spillway in a cascade operates at a different order, with different flow velocities, turbulence characteristics, and erosion patterns.

### Functional Hierarchies

In organizations where information passes in both directions, the numbers of people at each level should follow the branching ratio (~3:1). Hierarchies that deviate too far from natural ratios become dysfunctional—either too flat (information overload at the top) or too steep (information loss between levels).

## Refuting Status: Function Over Hierarchy

Mollison makes a crucial philosophical point about how we interpret orders. In human systems, we have confused the order of hierarchical function with status and power, "as though a tree stem were less important than the leaves in total" or as though "higher" meant more desirable, "as though the fingers were less to be desired than the palm of the hand."

### The Principle of Functional Reciprocity

The correct understanding is that **each part needs the other, and none functions without the others**:

- When a dominant animal is removed from a behavioral hierarchy, another is created from lower orders
- When subordinates are removed, others are created from within the dominants
- The same principle applies to streams: altering one aspect changes the whole regime

The [[permaculture]] position is to **refute the concept of status and assert that of function**: "It is not what you are; it is what you do in relation to the society you choose to live in. We need each other, and it is a reciprocal need wherever we have a function in relation to each other."

This reframing has profound implications for how we design human systems. Rather than creating hierarchies of status, permaculture seeks to create networks of function where every element serves and is served by the whole—exactly as a tree's roots, trunk, branches, and leaves each serve essential functions without any one being "higher" or "more important" than the others.

## Building Appropriate Systems

Designers can build either appropriate or inappropriate systems by choosing particular orders. The key is to:

1. **Study existing stable orders** in natural and human systems
2. **Apply appropriate size and factor classes** for specific constructs
3. **Respect behavioral thresholds** that define stable vs. unstable configurations
4. **Design for functional reciprocity** rather than status hierarchy
5. **Recognize that water, nutrients, information, and energy are of the whole**—not to be thought of in terms of their parts alone
