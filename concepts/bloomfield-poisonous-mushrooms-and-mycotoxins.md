## Why Do Mushrooms Produce Toxins?

Several hypotheses have been proposed:

1. **Secondary metabolite byproducts**: Toxins may be unavoidable biochemical trash with no function — the default hypothesis when no selective advantage is apparent.

2. **Defense against insect larvae**: This is the most compelling explanation. Insect eggs deposited in fruiting body tissues hatch into grubs that can sabotage the stem or cap structure. Amatoxins block protein synthesis, and developing embryos are highly prone to genetic damage. Phallotoxins provide an additional weapon by inhibiting growth. Insects and fungi have interacted for 400+ million years — ample time for defense/counter-defense evolution.

3. **Anti-herbivore defense for extinct animals**: Like the Osage orange (dispersed by now-extinct mammoths), amatoxins might be "ghosts of evolution." This theory is weak because amatoxins act too slowly to deter immediate consumption, and some mammals eat Amanita with impunity.

4. **Developmental regulation**: By limiting protein synthesis in certain tissues, toxins could affect fruiting body structure. Disproven by the existence of toxin-free Amanita species (like Caesar's mushroom) that develop normally.

## Psychoactive and Other Poisonous Mushrooms

### Fly Agaric (Amanita muscaria)
- Produces muscimol (excites nervous system) and ibotenic acid (sedates nervous system)
- Both bind to brain receptors causing visual hallucinations
- Associated with Lewis Carroll's Alice in Wonderland (influenced by contemporary mycologist Mordecai Cubitt Cooke's accounts)
- Rich history of ritual use and abuse

### Coprine (Coprinus atramentarius)
- Contains coprine, which blocks the enzyme that metabolizes acetaldehyde (the byproduct of alcohol breakdown)
- If alcohol is consumed with the mushroom: tingling in arms and legs, nausea, vomiting, palpitations, racing heart
- Identical mechanism to the prescription drug disulfiram (Antabuse) used to treat alcoholism

### Webcaps (Cortinarius species)
- Contain compounds that destroy kidney cells, inducing renal failure
- Delay between ingestion and symptoms: up to 3 weeks
- One of the longest latency periods of any mushroom toxin

### False Morels
- Ascomycete fruiting bodies containing the volatile toxin gyromitrin
- Medical histories parallel amatoxin poisoning
- Can theoretically be eaten safely if cooked thoroughly to evaporate gyromitrin — described as "mushroom roulette"

### Satan's Bolete (Boletus satanus)
- Cannot kill but causes severe gastrointestinal distress
- The author survived eating it, possibly detoxified by boiling or chili ingredients

## Mycotoxins: Poisons from Mycelia

### Aflatoxins
- Produced by [[stachybotrys-chartarum]]** gained notoriety from the 1993-94 Cleveland incident:
- Macrocyclic trichothecenes caused blood vessel hemorrhaging in infant lungs
- 10 infants affected, 1 died; 6 additional infant deaths reclassified from SIDS
- Associated with water-damaged homes
- Only some strains produce trichothecenes
- Many other black molds (Cladosporium, Penicillium, Aspergillus, Alternaria) are far more prevalent but non-toxic

**Trichothecene biological warfare potential**:
- Act immediately on contact (unlike most fungal toxins)
- Skin: burning and blistering, tissue shedding
- Inhalation: coughing, breathing difficulty, burning lungs
- Eyes: tearing, blurred vision
- Ingestion: nausea, vomiting, bloody diarrhea
- Lethal dose: a few milligrams of T-2 toxin or diacetoxyscirpenol
- Lower doses cause anemia, platelet loss, white blood cell crash over days
- No specific antidote exists
- Soap and water decontamination works only if applied immediately
- Allegedly used as "yellow rain" by Soviet Union in Laos, Cambodia, and Afghanistan (1970s-80s)

## Indoor Air Quality and Fungal Spores

- Summer indoor air: 500+ spores per cubic meter (about 2 bathtub volumes)
- Recommended maximum: 50 spores per cubic meter (200 for offices)
- Mold-damaged homes: thousands of spores per cubic meter
- Culture plate methods can underrate total irritating particles by 100x (dead hyphal fragments are equally allergenic)
- We inhale and exhale fungi and their cellular debris from birth to death

## Fungal Nitrogen Acquisition

Fungi face serious nitrogen shortages when consuming plant tissues (plenty of sugar, negligible protein). Solutions include:
- **Antibiotic predation**: Secreted antibiotics kill soil bacteria; nutrients absorbed from leaking cells. Ink-caps, puffballs, and bird's nest fungi are all adept bacterial predators.
- **Nematode trapping**: A single gram of soil can house 1,900 nematodes and 1,000+ meters of hyphae. Traps range from simple adhesive branches to elaborate explosive rings that grip with up to 18 atmospheres of pressure. Nematodes are lured by chemical attractants (siren songs).

## Lycoperdonosis

A rare respiratory illness caused by inhaling puffball (Lycoperdon) spores:
- 1994 Wisconsin case: teenagers snorted puffball spores seeking hallucinogenic effects
- Millions of spiny spores entered alveoli
- Symptoms: breathing difficulty, high fever, myalgia
- Lung biopsies revealed acute inflammation around basidiospore masses
- Macrophages engulf spores but cannot digest them (chitinous walls); become "hunchbacked" with conspicuous bulges
- Recovery: macrophages migrate to ciliated lung regions, sacrifice themselves to mucus conveyor belt; steroid therapy controls inflammation
