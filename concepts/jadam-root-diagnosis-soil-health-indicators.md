# JADAM Root Diagnosis and [[soil-health-indicators]]

JADAM organic farming teaches that the condition of crop roots directly
reveals the condition of the soil. Rather than relying on complex soil tests,
farmers can diagnose soil health by observing root growth patterns. The
fundamental principle is simple: what is visible above ground (the plant)
represents what is invisible below ground (the root system).

## The Root-to-Shoot Ratio Principle

The essence of soil management is making the ratio of underground to
aboveground plant parts greater than one. If the aboveground part is one, the
underground part must be larger, wider, deeper, and more vigorous. The total
area of root extension decides crop health and is the basic condition for high
yield and good quality. In JADAM's direct formulation: root area equals money.

Soil management and ensuring good root growth is the best strategy for
farming, particularly in the era of climate change with its super-high
temperatures and abnormal rainfall.

## Visual Root Diagnosis Method

### The 40-Centimeter Dig Test

To assess soil health, dig about 40 centimeters (16 inches) vertically into
the soil. Remove a chunk from 20 centimeters (8 inches) depth and examine the
roots. If the chunk contains abundant hairy roots, the soil is free from
compaction. If no roots appear below 20 centimeters, check several more spots.
If all spots return the same result, a compacted soil layer covers the entire
field.

### The Iron Rod Test

An alternative method uses a sharp iron rod pushed into the soil. If you
cannot push it in, soil compaction is likely present. This quick diagnostic
can be performed across multiple locations rapidly.

## Signs of Healthy vs. Unhealthy Root Systems

### Healthy Roots

In good soil, roots extend wide and deep, guaranteeing high yield and high
quality. Historical observations show that decades ago, pepper plants would
extend roots 1.5 meters (5 feet) into the soil, growing so robustly that
farmers needed sickles to chop them down. A small tug would not dislodge them.

### Unhealthy Roots

Modern crops show coiled, stunted root systems. Pepper roots that once
reached deep now come out with a small tug, remaining coiled in the nursery
soil shape. Chinese cabbage and other crops show the same pattern. These
coiled roots indicate soil degradation from compaction, chemical
contamination, and biological damage.

## The Building Foundation Analogy

JADAM uses a construction analogy: attempting to grow abundant fruit without
proper root development is like preparing only a 2-meter deep foundation and
trying to raise a 10-story building. Plants are smart; they assess their
growing conditions and grow only to the extent possible given root capacity.

For a plant to produce abundant fruit, its nodes should be short with new
shoots emerging continuously, providing many flower buds and fruits. This can
produce more than a ten-fold difference in yield. The root must extend wide
and deep for continuous new shoot production.

## High Yield Equals Root Contact Area

High yield is directly related to the total area of root contact with
nutrients. Many farmers believe that applying abundant base fertilizer and
additional fertilizer will produce more shoots. JADAM argues this is wrong:
no amount of surface fertilization compensates for restricted root systems.

Farmers who have done everything right but still suffer disease and low yield
should suspect soil compaction as the underlying problem. Roots will not grow
properly in compacted soil regardless of surface inputs.

## Historical Decline in Root Health

Forty years ago, peppers were among the easiest crops to grow. Today, many
farmers consider pepper the most difficult open-field crop. Modern farmers
have abundant products, education, and technology, yet cannot match the
performance of ancestors who grew wonderful peppers without microorganism
products, synthetic fertilizers, or scientific cultivation education.

The root cause is literally in the roots. Soil-borne diseases like canker
have become rampant because roots cannot settle properly into degraded soil.

## The JADAM Three Tenets of Soil Management

JADAM believes leaf mold from nearby mountains represents the ideal soil.
The three tenets for achieving this ideal in agricultural fields are:

1. The microorganism composition of the field should be identical to the leaf
   mold in the surrounding mountains
2. Organic matter in the field should be abundant like the mountain leaf mold
3. Minerals in the field should be diverse like the mountain leaf mold

### Microorganism Diversity

JADAM rejects the "good microbe versus bad microbe" framework as both
scientifically premature and commercially motivated. Less than one percent of
estimated one million soil microorganism species have been studied. Even
well-known pathogens like Bacillus anthracis serve beneficial roles in certain
contexts. Diversity, not selection, is the foremost value.

One gram of mountain leaf mold contains 2 to 10 billion microorganisms across
over one million species: approximately 30,000 protozoa, 50,000 algae,
400,000 fungi, and billions of bacteria. Factory-cultured products contain
only a fraction of this diversity from distant, non-native origins.

### Indigenous Microorganisms

JADAM calls microorganisms from nearby forests "indigenous microorganisms"
(IMO). These are already adapted to local conditions. Bringing them home,
cultivating them, and applying them restores the natural balance and diversity
that once existed in farm soils. The key is harmony between producers,
predators, and decomposers in the soil ecosystem.
