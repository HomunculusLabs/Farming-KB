# Entheogenic Fungi in Mexico

Mexico is the global center of both diversity and traditional use of
neurotropic fungi. With **76 documented species** of neurotropic fungi — 44
of which are Psilocybe — Mexico accounts for roughly 35% of all known
neurotropic species worldwide. This extraordinary concentration is matched
by an equally rich ethnobotanical heritage: Mexico is home to the most
extensively documented indigenous mushroom cults on Earth.

## Indigenous Groups and Mushroom Use

Multiple indigenous peoples of central and southern Mexico maintain
traditional relationships with psychoactive fungi. The most well-documented
groups include:

- **Mazatec** (Oaxaca) — Perhaps the most famous practitioners. The Mazatec
  use several species in healing and divination ceremonies, particularly
  [[psilocybe-mexicana-cultivation]] and [[psilocybe-caerulescens-profile]]. Maria Sabina, the Mazatec curandera who introduced
  Wasson to the velada ceremony, became iconic in the global psychedelic
  movement.

- **Mixtec** (Oaxaca) — Known for their use of [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]], called "derrumbes" (landslides) in Spanish,
  a name evoking the disorienting experience. The Mixtec also used puffball
  species (gasteromycetes) as "narcotic" fungi, though chemical analysis
  later showed most of these were inactive.

- **Nahua** — Various Nahua-speaking communities across central Mexico
  maintain traditions involving [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] and other
  species.

- **Chinantec** (Oaxaca) — Reported to use Dictyophora indusiata (the
  "veiled lady" mushroom) and its forms for divination purposes, despite
  lack of confirmed neurotropic chemistry in this species.

- **Zapotec** — Associated with [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]]
  and other species in the region of Oaxaca.

- **Chatino, Zapotec, and other Oaxacan groups** — Additional communities
  with documented or reported use.

## Historical Discovery

The formal "discovery" of Mexican entheogenic mushroom use unfolded in stages:

**Early accounts** trace back to the Spanish colonial period. Bernardino de
Sahagún (1569-1582) documented the Aztec use of "teonanácatl" (divine
mushroom) in his *Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España*. However,
these accounts were largely ignored for centuries and were sometimes dismissed
as figurative descriptions rather than literal mushroom use.

**Modern rediscovery** began with Richard Evans Schultes (1939), who traced
the identity of teonanácatl to actual mushroom species used by contemporary
indigenous peoples. This was followed by the fieldwork of R. Gordon Wasson,
his wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson, and the French mycologist Roger Heim
(1953-1958). Wasson and Wasson's 1957 *Life* magazine article "Seeking the
Magic Mushroom" brought the practice to global attention, for better and for
worse.

**Taxonomic work** by Gastón Guzmán, Rolf Singer, and others in the following
decades systematically documented the species involved, culminating in
Guzmán's comprehensive monographs of Mexican Psilocybe species (1977-1997).

## Common Names

Guzmán (1997) catalogued more than **200 common names** for neurotropic
fungi in Mexico, many in indigenous languages. Notable examples include:

| Name | Language | Meaning | Species |
|---|---|---|---|
| teonanácatl | Nahuatl | divine mushroom | various Psilocybe |
| apipiltzin | Nahuatl | kid / little boy | [[psilocybe-mexicana]] |
| atkad | Zapotec | mayor / leader | [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]] |
| di nizé taaya | Chatino | fungus of the genius | unknown |
| shi thó | Mazatec | that eruptions thing | [[psilocybe-caerulescens-entity]] |
| pajaritos | Spanish | little birds | [[psilocybe-mexicana]] |
| san isidros | Spanish | St. Isidore (patron of agriculture) | [[psilocybe-cubensis]] |
| derrumbes | Spanish | landslides | [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]] |

The word "teonanácatl" is now indiscriminately applied to any Mexican
hallucinogenic fungus, though its original Nahuatl meaning was more specific.

## Impact on Mycology

Guzmán (1990) identified the Wasson-Heim-Singer studies (1954-1958) as a
watershed dividing Mexican mycology into two eras. Before this period,
mycology was a neglected field in Mexico. The international attention
generated by the mushroom ceremonies attracted funding, students, and
specialists to the study of all fungi in the country, not just the
neurotropic ones.

## Sacred Fungi of Uncertain Chemistry

Beyond the well-documented [[psilocybin]]-containing species, several fungi
are used as "sacred" mushrooms in Mexico despite lacking confirmed neurotropic
compounds. These include boletes used in New Guinea ceremonies (reported by
Heim), gasteromycetes (puffballs) used by the Mixtec, and Dictyophora
indusiata used by the Chinantec. In some cases, their sacred status may
derive from confusion with genuinely active species found in the same areas,
or from symbolic rather than pharmacological significance.

The Mixtec puffball tradition, extensively studied by Ott et al. (1975),
revealed that at least nine different puffball species were used
interchangeably as "narcotic" fungi. Chemical analysis confirmed that none
contained psilocybin, and most were common edible species. This finding
illustrates the complexity of interpreting indigenous mushroom classifications
through a purely pharmacological lens.
