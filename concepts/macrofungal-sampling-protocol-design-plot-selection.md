# Macrofungal Sampling Protocol Design and Plot Selection

Designing effective [[lichen-biodiversity-sampling-protocols-data-analysis]] for macrofungi requires
careful consideration of plot size, substratum type, and
sampling frequency.

The edited volume "Biodiversity of Fungi" provides detailed
guidance on approaches to sampling macrofungi for biodiversity
inventory and monitoring.

## Substratum and Area Considerations

The number of fungal species on wood increases with the
amount of substratum sampled.

Small [[fungal-succession-woody-debris-ecology]] is encountered far more frequently than
large-diameter wood, so the area searched depends on the
distribution and frequency of different diameter classes.

Discovery of cryptic fungi often requires more intensive
searching of designated subplots.

Canopy inspection in tropical forests may reveal additional
species, though most canopy fungi also occur on fallen
branches or in gaps.

## Plot Design for Small Woody Debris

Small to medium woody debris (1.0 to 15.0 cm diameter) is
sampled using randomly or regularly spaced subplots.

Each piece of debris within the plot is examined for
macrofungi producing recognisable fruiting structures.

One specimen of every species is collected from each piece
of substratum within the plot.

Portions of hosts that protrude into a subplot are also
sampled to avoid edge effects.

After sampling, substrata are returned to their original
positions for future resampling.

For inconspicuous species like small ascomycetes, all woody
debris should be collected and examined under a dissecting
microscope in the laboratory.

## Plot Size Selection

Circular plots are usually the most efficient to set up and
sample, requiring only a premeasured string and center pole.

For small ascomycetes on branches and twigs, 0.5 square metre
circular plots positioned within larger plots have proven
effective.

Plots 10 metres in diameter work for medium-size wood pieces.

The practical upper limit for circular plot diameter is 10 to
20 metres (78.5 to 314 square metres).

If subplots will be resampled repeatedly, they should be
divided into halves or quadrants, with debris collected from
each sector only once to allow recovery.

## Large Woody Debris and Standing Trees

For fungi fruiting on large logs and standing trees, the
entire tree or log serves as the sample unit rather than
a plot.

Each visible fruiting structure is recorded from base to top,
with the type and decay class of the wood noted.

Collection sites can be tagged or mapped and resampled
periodically to track species turnover over time.

The point quarter method can estimate density of fallen or
standing dead trees along transect lines.

GPS coordinates and GIS mapping are recommended for large
survey areas to facilitate specimen relocation.

## Sampling Frequency

How often plots should be sampled depends on species turnover
between sampling dates.

For Xylariaceae and small ascomycetes in the tropics,
3-month intervals provide an optimal balance between species
discovery and sampling effort.

Fruiting agarics show high turnover in both temperate and
tropical forests, requiring weekly or biweekly sampling.

Corticoid and polypore fungi can be sampled at approximately
2-month intervals due to their longer fruiting persistence.

## Data Collection Parameters

For each specimen collected, the following data should be
recorded.

- Date and collector information
- Macrohabitat (forest type, plant association, climatic zone)
- Substratum/host class and diameter
- Substratum preference and size class
- Decay class of wood (hard and sound, hard and decayed,
  softened and punky, well decayed and humus-like)
- Condition of bark (tight, loose, or absent)
- GPS coordinates and elevation

Detailed notes on fresh specimen appearance should be
recorded at the time of collection since colour and
texture change dramatically during drying.

## Inconspicuous and Indistinguishable Species

Two main challenges complicate macrofungal sampling on wood.

Many resupinate basidiomycetes and small ascomycetes are not
detectable or recognisable in the field.

Removing all [[macrofungi-woody-substrata-survey-design]] to the laboratory for microscopic
examination resolves this but precludes resampling.

It is also frequently difficult to determine whether a species
has already been collected within a subplot.

Different maturity stages of some species are hard to
recognise, and similar-looking species may be confused.

No assumptions should be made about the identity of
inconspicuous fungi — all separate patches should be collected.
