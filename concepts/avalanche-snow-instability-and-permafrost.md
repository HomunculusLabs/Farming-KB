# Avalanche, Snow Instability, and Permafrost

Understanding snow dynamics and [[permafrost]] behaviour is essential for safe settlement
and productive land use in cold climates. Snow movement occurs across a spectrum from
imperceptible microcreep to catastrophic avalanches, while permafrost governs the
stability of vast northern landscapes. Both phenomena demand specific design responses.

## Avalanche and Snow Instability

### Conditions for Avalanche

On slopes of more than **6°** and in valleys of steep foothills, wherever snow builds
up to **15 cm or more** in depth, [[avalanche]] risk exists. Snow will avalanche wherever:

- The crust is broken by strong winds (more than **13–18 km/sec**)
- Sudden freeze is followed by thaw
- Warm winds cause snowmelt
- Normal spring thaw occurs
- Deeper unstable snow layers become saturated by meltwater over buried hoarfrost, ice,
  or earth layers

At critically unstable points, a gunshot, the collapse of a snow cornice (overhang), a
strong wind, or even a skier, antelope, or rabbit can trigger an avalanche.

### Modes of Snow Movement

Snow moves in a graduated spectrum of modes, from slow to catastrophic:

1. **Microcreep** — under freeze/thaw and gravity, advances downslope a few centimetres
   per day
2. **Slip (snow boards)** — thick snow masses cover metres per day
3. **Snow mound** — advances from rocky uplands as a streamflow effect
4. **Snow bodies** — move en masse down short slopes
5. **Snow slides** — velocities of **4–5 m/sec**, can be arrested by terraces
6. **Avalanches** — increase in speed down long slopes, reaching up to **6.5 m/sec**
   (note: modern measurements reach far higher speeds); usually episodic (every ~35
   years), though systematic (annual) avalanches also occur

### Impact and Blast Effects

The impact of a snow mass ranges from **4 to 54 tonnes per square metre**, depending
on total mass and debris carried. However, the **compressed air blast** in front of the
mass travels at up to **330 m/sec**, producing a severe shockwave. Even snow-dust
avalanches produce waves of **10–100 m/sec** and develop pressures of **35
atmospheres** — enough to knock down trees and buildings. It is often this blast wave,
not the snow itself, that causes destruction.

### Avalanche Triggers and Indicators

**Eighty percent** of all avalanches occur where old, compact, or dry snows are
dislodged by the condensation of moisture and rain from air of more than **70%
humidity**. Many of the remainder are triggered by gusty winds or where meltwater
lubricates the mass during thaw periods (usually between midday and 3 p.m. in fine
weather). Sudden falls of **50–69 cm** of snow followed by long periods of cloud-free
weather also cause unstable snow conditions.

**Tree indicators** are valuable for identifying avalanche-prone terrain:
- **Downslope lean** of tree trunks
- **Lack of lower limbs** and understory
- Bare "trains" of different age classes or pioneer species (especially aspen) in
  downslope strips
- **Blown-down forests** radiating from avalanche snouts

These indicators persist for at least **20–30 years** in coniferous forests. Rock
avalanches produce many of the same effects.

### Avalanche Prevention

The best defense is prevention through a set of strategies:
- **Preservation of high-slope forests** — the most effective single measure
- **Tunnel placement** of essential services through hazardous zones
- **V-shaped barriers** to split up the avalanche front
- **Prevention of settlement** in high-risk areas
- **Snow barriers on plateaus** above slopes — hold snow above the slope and lessen
  the load
- **Solar radiation crust** on snow preserves slope stability (seasonal protection
  only; breaks up if iced or shattered by wind)

Cross-slope terraces, walls, and barriers have generally **not proved effective**.
A new threat follows the death of steep-slope forests by acid rain damage, which
removes the primary natural defense.

Avalanche areas are often mapped, and avalanche warnings issued when conditions can be
predicted locally. The pioneer species indicator — aspen colonising avalanche chutes —
reliably delineates these zones.

## Permafrost

### Distribution and Characteristics

Permafrost (permanently frozen ground) occupies **47–50%** of Canada, Russia, and
Greenland. Much permafrost is fossil, at times to **400 metres thick**, so normal
melting under peat is slow — earth heat from below reduces thickness by only about
**1 cm per year**.

Peaty surfaces, common over permafrost, both prevent melting and rapidly admit cold,
thus preserving frozen area stability. However, peaty layers can be eroded or removed
by clearing, fire, overgrazing, or ice particle scour (ice particles at −40°C are as
hard and abrasive as sand). Once peat is removed, **solar thaw is about 5,000 times
more effective** than earth heat.

### Thaw Consequences

When peat cover is lost, thaw may occur rapidly. Bare sunken areas of clayey soils
(mollisols) develop, and these thawed areas can reach **2–45 metres deep** within a
few years. The erosion effects — plus **9% volume loss of ice** — can be severe, and
has destroyed many upland grazing areas in Greenland.

### Soil Formation in Permafrost Regions

In such harsh areas, soils are formed only as a result of **frost shattering of rock**.
Both soils and rocks are sorted by ice action and thaw — a process called
**gelifluction**. This slow but relentless sorting creates patterned ground features
characteristic of permafrost landscapes.

## Loess Deposits

[[loess]] (windblown soils) are **50–80% silica** and **20–25% felspar**, low in calcium.
Deep loess fields occur throughout Europe, Russia, China, and near continental
glaciers elsewhere. They support excellent forests but dry out quickly and have high
porosity. Loess deposits are remarkably stable as **steep walls to 300 metres high**,
making them much used for underground housing in China — occupied for millennia.
