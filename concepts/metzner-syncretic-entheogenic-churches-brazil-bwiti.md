# Syncretic Entheogenic Churches — Brazil, Bwiti, and Native American Church

## Overview

Syncretic entheogenic churches represent a distinct category of
psychoactive plant use that occupies a middle ground between traditional
[[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]] rituals and modern therapeutic practice.

Ralph Metzner distinguished these from shamanic ceremonies by their
emphasis on group worship and celebration rather than individual healing
or divination.

They typically involve larger groups of 20 to several hundred
participants, use standardized ceremonial formats, and are organized
into legally recognized religious institutions.

The most significant examples include the [[metzner-barquinha-umbanda-and-afro-brazilian-ayahuasca-syncretism]] churches,
the Native American Church, and the Bwiti cult of Central Africa.

## Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches

Brazil has produced no fewer than three major organized churches in which
ayahuasca serves as the principal sacrament: Santo Daime, Uniao do
Vegetal (UDV), and Barquinha.

Each was founded in the 1950s by rubber tappers working in the Amazon who
came into contact with the hallucinogenic vine through mestizo or Indian
ayahuasceros.

The founders of each reported a significant vision instructing them to
organize a church using the brew as sacrament.

The UDV, the largest, follows the most formal structure: participants sit
in rows listening to sermons and songs from maestres, with a question-
and-answer period. Santo Daime involves congregational hymn-singing led
by women singers, sometimes with dancing in simple rhythmic steps.

Barquinha, the least known outside Brazil, has incorporated elements of
Umbanda, an Afro-Brazilian religion involving contact with ancestors and
deities called orixas.

Research by Charles Grob, Dennis McKenna, and others confirmed that
long-term membership is associated with no adverse health effects and
with reduced addictions and psychopathologies.

## The Native American Church

The Native American Church (NAC) represents the most legally established
entheogenic religious practice in North America.

Participants sit on the ground in a circle around a blazing central fire
inside a tipi, with a "roadman" presiding. Ceremonies last all night and
involve almost constant singing of traditional peyote songs with a rapid,
rhythmic beat. There is virtually no discussion of healing processes or
visions during or after the ceremony.

For most of the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]], the NAC enjoyed legal access to peyote
in most Western states, though this protection has been eroded by recent
court decisions. Membership is legally limited to persons with 25% or more
Native American ancestry. NAC participation is associated with reduced
incidence of alcoholism, a significant social benefit given the devastating
impact of alcohol on Native American communities.

## The Bwiti Cult — Gabon and Zaire

The Bwiti cult among the Fang people of Gabon and Zaire involves the use
of iboga ([[plants-of-the-gods-tabernanthe-iboga-chemistry]]), from which ibogaine is derived.

The Fang, who are village dwellers, say they learned about iboga from the
Pygmies, the deep forest dwellers.

Initiates undergo a powerful death-rebirth experience in which their
"head is opened up," after which they can converse with ancestor spirits.

Bwiti ceremonies are held in temples with an altar and officiating
priests. Initiates sit and sing in lengthy ceremonies, daubed with white
mud. A mirror in the assembly room allows initiates to "see" their
ancestral spirits.

Pharmacological research has indicated that ibogaine may have a specific
action on cocaine receptor sites in the brain, raising the possibility of
a chemical antidote to addiction.

Metzner argued that all consciousness-expanding hallucinogens, including
LSD, peyote, and ayahuasca, can serve to counteract the "consciousness-
contracting" effect of addictive narcotics and stimulants.

## Social Functions of Syncretic Churches

An important social function of syncretic entheogenic churches is the
strengthening of community bonds and the provision of a sense of
belonging.

As organized religions, they provide members with social cohesion and a
degree of legal protection.

Anthropologists have noted that these churches also function as a
protective shield of traditional lore against the encroachments of
Christian missionaries and Western consumer culture.

The syncretic form has been remarkably successful at making profoundly
spiritual experiences with entheogenic plants accessible to a broad
spectrum of people in all walks of life.

## Distinction from Shamanic Practice

Metzner emphasized the key distinction: in shamanic ceremonies, the
primary focus is healing and divination, with the shaman as the central
figure who takes the medicine to "see" on behalf of others.

In syncretic churches, the focus shifts to group worship and celebration,
with priests or officiants rather than healers.

There is very little discussion or sharing of visions, and individual
healing experiences, while they occur, are not the primary purpose of the
gathering.
