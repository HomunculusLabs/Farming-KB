## Ayahuasca Urban Healing in Iquitos

## Overview

The use of the plant hallucinogen ayahuasca (*Banisteriopsis caapi*) as an adjunct to folk healing has received its greatest cultural elaboration in urban settings of the Peruvian Amazon. Marlene Dobkin de Rios' year-long fieldwork (June 1968–May 1969) in the urban slum of Belén, Iquitos, documented how mestizo healers (ayahuasqueros) conduct group healing sessions using the powerful psychoactive brew in the context of widespread magical illness beliefs and extreme socioeconomic deprivation.

## Cultural Variables in Hallucinogenic Use

Dobkin de Rios argued that the study of plant hallucinogens in non-Western societies had been scattered and incomplete, particularly regarding the role of **cultural variables** — belief systems, attitudes, and values — in determining and structuring one of the most subjective experiences available to anthropological inquiry.

While clinical psychologists focus on antecedent variables surrounding drug administration (emotional atmosphere, subject attitudes and motivations, personality characteristics), the anthropologist working in a natural field setting cannot control variables or adequately measure dependent variables (somatic effects, reduced intellectual-motor proficiency, changes in visual perception). What anthropological analysis can offer is focus upon:

- The **cognitive system** dealing with belief in the drug's efficacy
- The **shared expectations** of community members who expect to see certain visions and indeed report them with great frequency
- The actual **nature and utilization** of the insights and visions obtained during hallucinogenically induced experiences

## The Healing Session

### Setting and Preparation

Mestizo healers in urban centers such as Iquitos and Pucallpa gather groups of patients ranging from five or six to as many as twenty-five in forest clearings at the edge of these Amazonian cities. Healing sessions always occur at night, typically beginning around 10:00 p.m. and lasting until 2:00 or 3:00 a.m. — four to five hours of strong drug intoxication.

### Patient Selection

Healers carefully select their patients, tending to **eliminate individuals who suffer from severe psychotic disorders**. Simple illnesses are rarely treated with ayahuasca; instead, herbs, plants, and store-bought medicines are prescribed for these types of affliction.

### The Brew and Administration

The ayahuasca potion is made by cutting and boiling the woody vine for several hours. The brew often contains additives beyond various *Banisteriopsis* species, including:

- *Toé* — identified as *Datura suaveolens*
- *Chacruna* — *B. rusbyana*, whose leaves contain dimethyltryptamine (DMT)

The healer takes out a communal cup and distributes the potion while reciting orations and whistling to protect each person who drinks. He varies the amount according to factors including the patient's body weight and physical strength. The optimal dose found by Ríos Reátegui (1962) was 7 mg/kg of body weight. Healers appeared to give larger doses to persons suffering from psychosomatic illness — diseases with physical symptoms believed to originate from magical harm.

Healers generally drink last, and an attempt is made by those present to reach a climax in their visions as close together as possible.

### Session Activities

After drinking, people sit around smoking and sometimes chatting for about half an hour, waiting for effects to manifest. The healer uses this time to stress the effectiveness of the **purge** (ayahuasca is called this because of side effects of vomiting or diarrhea) and to tell patients how important it is that each person try to keep the potion in their stomach as long as possible.

During the ceremony, healers employ multiple techniques:

- Extensive use of **Quechua** in special drug songs
- **Whistling** interspersed throughout the evening's activities
- Movement around the circle contacting each person with a **schacapa** (rattle made by tying together dried leaves of a forest plant)
- Blowing **cigarette smoke** over the body of sick persons
- **Sucking** at painful or afflicted regions of the body, bringing forth a spine or thistle believed to have been magically introduced by an enemy or evil spirit
- **Counseling** and **ritual exorcism** of each person throughout the session

### Dietary Proscriptions

Dietary restrictions are integral to ayahuasca healing because of a belief that the vine possesses a **jealous guardian spirit**. To propitiate this spirit, patients refrain from eating salt, lard, or sweets for at least 24 hours before and after using the purge. Sexual abstinence may also be demanded prior to the session.

## The Belén Slum: Research Setting

### Demographics and Conditions

Belén is a community of approximately 12,000 people dwelling in houses built on rafts along the Amazon River. It serves as a receiving area for vast numbers of forest migrants who come to Iquitos in search of work and better schooling. The slum exhibits:

- Exceedingly high unemployment rates
- Excessive malnutrition
- Family breakdown and fragile unions
- Prostitution and vandalism
- Chronic illness and social pathologies
- Extreme economic insecurity

### Economic Activities

Men and women work mainly at commercial activities tied to the movement of forest produce to the city market. The largest occupational categories include:

- **Rematistas**: Small-scale wholesalers who buy and resell vegetable or forest produce
- **Regatones**: More capitalized entrepreneurs who own motor-powered boats loaded with staples (rice, sugar, coffee, gasoline), plying river inlets and selling at considerable markups to one-crop river hamlets

The area is flooded at least four months each year. Householders must use canoes to reach the market or city proper. Fishing trips today often take men away for four to six weeks at a time.

## Field Methodology

Dobkin de Rios employed several innovative ethnographic methods:

- **Fortune-telling with naipes** (cards) — she established a small consultation center as a *curiosa* (fortuneteller), charging a small culturally-appropriate fee to make the service valued
- **Participant observation** — including personal experience with 100 micrograms of Sandoz LSD and later ayahuasca in ritual context
- **Thematic Apperception Test** administration, compared with over 200 naipes readings
- **Cross-checking** with previously published sociological surveys and vital statistics

The subjective experience with ayahuasca proved essential for understanding informants' reports — culturally reported visions seemed difficult to comprehend until firsthand experience clarified the veracity of reports about seeing unidentified persons appear in living color, etc.

## Magical Illness Categories

Among Belén residents, most illnesses are attributed to magical causes — particularly the willing of evil by others. The major categories include:

- **Susto**: Profound alteration of metabolism or nervous disorder caused by loss of the soul through intense psychic trauma provoked by fear
- **Daño**: Caused by envy (*envidia*) or vengeance (*despecho*), associated with hemorrhaging, muscular pain, tumors, and consistent bad luck (*saladera*)
- **Pulsario**: Marked by restlessness, hyperactivity, and free-floating anxiety; described as a painful ball at the mouth of the stomach; attacking mainly women
- **Mal de ojo**: The evil eye — caused by the magical action of one person's glance, frequently affecting children
