# Jivaro Bewitching and Tsentsak Magical Darts

Among the [[jivaro]] people of the Ecuadorian Amazon, the *[[tsentsak]]* — magical darts — form the central instrument of both shamanic power and sorcery. These objects exist in a dual state: their natural aspect is that of ordinary material objects (insects, thorns, bones), while their supernatural aspect, revealed only under the influence of *[[natem]]* ([[ayahuasca]]), transforms them into spirit helpers — demons in the forms of giant butterflies, jaguars, or monkeys who actively assist the shaman in his tasks.

## Becoming a Shaman: Receiving Tsentsak

The path to shamanhood begins with the transfer of tsentsak from an established shaman to a novice. The donor shaman cuts a piece from one of his own magical darts with a machete and gives it to the novice to swallow. Upon taking it into his stomach, the recipient experiences intense pain and must remain on his bed for ten days, drinking natem every evening.

The Jivaro believe that tsentsak can be kept indefinitely in the stomach and regurgitated at will. During the transfer period, the donor shaman periodically blows and rubs all over the novice's body, apparently to increase the power of the transfer.

## The Critical Choice: Bewitcher or Curer

After approximately one month, a tsentsak emerges from the novice's mouth. At this pivotal moment, the new shaman experiences a tremendous desire to bewitch — to use his newly acquired power to cause harm. Two paths diverge:

- **Bewitching shaman**: If the novice casts his tsentsak to fulfill this urge, he becomes a *bewitcher*
- **Curing shaman**: If he can control his impulse and reswallow the first tsentsak, he becomes a *curer*

The character of the donor shaman heavily influences this outcome. If the donor was primarily a bewitcher, the novice's magical darts will possess such a strong desire to kill that their new owner will be powerfully inclined to adopt their attitude. One informant described the urge to kill felt by bewitching shamans as arriving "with a strength and frequency similar to that of hunger."

## Sexual Abstinence and Power Accumulation

The new shaman must remain sexually inactive for at least three months. Failure in self-discipline means he will not become a successful shaman. Progressive levels of power are tied to the duration of abstinence:

| Abstinence Period | Power Attained |
|---|---|
| 3 months | Minimum threshold for any shamanic ability |
| 5 months | Power to kill (bewitcher) or cure a victim (curer) |
| 1 year | Status of a truly effective practitioner |

During this period, the shaman collects all kinds of insects, plants, and other objects, which he now has the power to convert into tsentsak. Almost any small enough object can become a magical dart — including living insects and worms. Different types of tsentsak cause different kinds and degrees of illness, and the greater the variety a shaman possesses, the greater his ability.

## The Bewitching Process

Bewitching is directed at specific, known individuals — typically neighbors or fellow tribesmen — almost always to avenge a particular offense against family or friends. This contrasts with the large-scale headhunting raids conducted against entire enemy tribe neighborhoods.

The procedure follows a precise sequence:

1. The shaman drinks *natem* and secretly approaches the victim's house
2. Just out of sight in the forest, he drinks green tobacco juice, enabling him to regurgitate a tsentsak
3. He throws the magical dart at his victim as they exit the house
4. A strong, well-aimed tsentsak may pass entirely through the body, causing death within days to weeks
5. More commonly, the dart lodges within the body, causing illness

Crucially, the victim receives no indication that someone is bewitching them. The bewitcher actively avoids detection lest the victim procure a curing shaman. If the intended victim does not appear, the shaman may bewitch any family member who emerges — usually a wife or child.

## Spirit Helpers in Bewitching

### The Pasuk

The *pasuk* is the chief spirit helper, specialized for bewitching service. Each shaman possesses only one pasuk, and each pasuk serves only a single shaman. The pasuk has a protective shield — under natem, curing shamans see it in human form and size, "covered with iron except for its eyes." To the uninitiated, it appears as a simple tarantula. Only a direct tsentsak shot to its eyes — the sole vulnerable point in its armor — can kill it.

The pasuk can remain near the victim after the bewitcher departs, disguised as an insect or forest animal. It carries its own supply of darts to shoot into the victim should a curing shaman succeed in extracting the original tsentsak.

### Wakani Birds

*Wakani* ("soul" or "spirit") birds are supernatural servants available to any shaman. The shaman blows on them and sends them to the victim's house, where they fly around the person repeatedly, causing fear, fever, insanity, and eventually death.

The wakani bird also serves as a resupply mechanism: the bewitcher can send it to perch near the victim's house, then continually send new tsentsak for the bird to throw from its beak. This makes it effectively impossible for a curing shaman to permanently rid the patient of magical darts.

## Anamuk: Creating Supernatural Animals

Shamans can also kill or injure through *anamuk* — using magical darts to create supernatural animals that attack victims. Specific examples include:

- An **armadillo bone** tsentsak shot into a river manifests underwater as an *anaconda*, overturning the victim's raft or canoe
- A **snake's tooth** creates a *poisonous serpent* to bite the victim
- Similar methods produce *jaguars* and *pumas* to kill their targets

## The Five-Year Power Test

Approximately five years after receiving tsentsak, a bewitching shaman must undergo a test to verify he still retains enough power. Under the influence of natem, he attempts to throw a tsentsak through a tree at the junction of its two main branches. If successful, the tree appears to split — though this splitting is invisible to anyone not under the hallucinogen's influence.

If the shaman fails, he knows he cannot kill human victims and is in grave danger from other shamans. He must urgently purchase a new supply of tsentsak. Until then, he drinks large quantities of natem, tobacco juice, and *piripiri* extract while concealing his weakened condition from enemies.

## The Weakening Cycle

Shamans gradually weaken over time and must repeatedly purchase tsentsak. The degree of illness produced in a victim depends on both the force of the throw and the character of the magical dart itself. If a tsentsak passes completely through the body, "there is nothing for a curing shaman to suck out," and the patient dies. If it lodges within, extraction is theoretically possible but not always successful in practice. The most powerful shamans are those who can afford to repeatedly purchase fresh tsentsak supplies from other practitioners.

## Tsentsak Economy and Social Relations

The tsentsak system creates a complex economy among Jivaro shamans. Since magical darts can be purchased, gifted, and forcibly recovered, they function as both spiritual capital and social currency. The transfer of tsentsak creates an enduring bond between donor and recipient — the donor's character (bewitcher or curer) shapes the novice's tendencies, and the recipient's initial supply comes entirely from another shaman's stock.

This economy means that power is never truly self-generated. A shaman's strength is always dependent on his ability to acquire tsentsak from others, creating networks of obligation and competition. The constant need to replenish supplies ensures that shamans remain embedded in a web of social and economic relationships with their peers.

## The Role of Natem in Revealing Supernatural Reality

Central to the entire tsentsak system is *natem* — the Banisteriopsis-based hallucinogenic brew. Without natem, the supernatural aspects of tsentsak remain invisible. Under its influence, ordinary objects transform into spirit helpers, the pasuk's iron armor becomes visible, and the rainbow bridge between shamans can be perceived. Natem functions as what Harner calls "the chemical door to the otherwise invisible world of the Jivaro shaman."

The shaman's dependence on natem is absolute. It is required not only for bewitching and curing operations but also for diagnosing the location of intruding darts, seeing spirit helpers, communicating with the supernatural realm, and maintaining the protective relationship with his own tsentsak. Without regular natem consumption, a shaman loses his connection to the supernatural reality that defines his power.

## Tobacco Juice as a Supportive Enabler

While natem opens the door to the supernatural world, tobacco juice (*[[nicotiana-rustica]]*) serves as the shaman's constant companion. Green tobacco juice is consumed throughout the day and night to keep tsentsak "fed" and alert. Although not truly hallucinogenic, tobacco juice produces a narcotized state that is believed necessary to maintain one's magical darts in a state of readiness to repel enemy attacks.

A shaman does not dare go for a walk without carrying green tobacco leaves. The juice enables regurgitation of tsentsak for both offensive (bewitching) and defensive purposes. In conjunction with natem and piripiri, tobacco juice forms a triad of psychoactive substances essential to the Jivaro shaman's pharmacopoeia.

## Comparison with Other Amazonian Sorcery Systems

The Jivaro tsentsak system shares features with other Amazonian shamanic traditions while remaining distinctive. Like the ayahuasca-using traditions of Peru and Brazil, the Jivaro employ plant hallucinogens to perceive and interact with a hidden supernatural reality. However, the Jivaro system is notably more individualistic and adversarial — their shamanism centers on a framework of attack and defense between individual practitioners rather than communal healing or worship.

The concept of magical projectiles is widespread in South American and global shamanic traditions, but the Jivaro elaboration — with its detailed taxonomy of dart types, its spirit helper hierarchy (pasuk and wakani birds), its elaborate rules of engagement, and its power-testing rituals — represents one of the most fully developed sorcery systems documented in the ethnographic literature.

## The Supernatural Hierarchy of Spirit Helpers

The Jivaro spirit helper system forms a structured hierarchy that parallels the social organization of the human world. At the apex stands the *pasuk* — the chief spirit helper, exclusive to a single shaman, armored and nearly invulnerable. Below the pasuk are the wakani birds, which are more widely available but serve as extensions of the bewitcher's will rather than independent agents. The numerous tsentsak themselves occupy the base of this hierarchy, functioning as both weapons and defensive armor.

This hierarchy reflects a fundamental Jivaro cosmological principle: the supernatural world mirrors and extends the human world in complexity and interpersonal dynamics. Just as human Jivaro society is organized around personal alliances, rivalries, and debts, so too is the shaman's supernatural realm structured by relationships between individual spirit beings, each with their own capacities, loyalties, and vulnerabilities.

## The Ethics of Bewitching

Within Jivaro culture, bewitching is not categorically condemned but is governed by social norms. Bewitching is carried out against specific, known individuals to avenge particular offenses against one's family or friends. This targeted, retaliatory framework distinguishes shamanic bewitching from random or gratuitous violence and embeds it within the Jivaro system of justice and social regulation.

However, the urge to bewitch is described as arriving with compulsive force — "with a strength and frequency similar to that of hunger." This characterization suggests that the Jivaro recognize bewitching as a potentially addictive or overwhelming drive that must be actively resisted by those who choose the curing path. The initial choice to swallow or cast the first tsentsak thus takes on the character of a moral decision that defines the shaman's entire career.

## Tsentsak and the Jivaro Worldview

The tsentsak system reveals a Jivaro epistemology in which the material and supernatural worlds are not separate realms but coexisting aspects of a single reality. Ordinary objects are always potentially more than they appear; their "true" nature is supernatural, accessible only through the hallucinogenic doorway of natem. This worldview inverts Western assumptions about the relationship between appearance and reality — for the Jivaro, the visible, material world is the superficial aspect, while the invisible, supernatural world revealed by natem is the deeper truth.

This epistemological framework has practical consequences. Because the supernatural reality is always present but ordinarily invisible, the Jivaro shaman's role is not that of a magician who performs impossible feats, but of a seer who perceives what is already there. The tsentsak that he regurgitates and throws are not created by his will but are spirit helpers that exist independently, requiring only his agency to be deployed in the physical world.
