Coleman's baby-leaf salad mix relies on 'Bull's Blood' beet
leaves as the primary source of red color. The mix is composed
exclusively of whole baby leaves no more than three inches in
length, carefully chosen from natu [[psilocybin-in-the-counter-culture]] in the mix.

The cool conditions of winter greenhouses intensify the beautiful
deep maroon of 'Bull's Blood' leaves, and the flavor does not
carry radicchio's bitter edge, which under poor growing
conditions can become so bitter as to ruin an entire salad.
This makes beet leaves a superior choice for the salad mix,
providing vibrant color without the risk of bitterness that
accompanies radicchio in cold growing conditions.

The beet leaves also appear in Coleman's braising mix, which
is made up of hardy Asian greens cut slightly larger than the
baby-leaf salad but never more than four inches long. The mix
includes spinach and chard along with mizuna, mibuna, tatsoi,
pak choi, and tokyo bekana. Coleman tried red-leaved mustards
but found none had sufficient color in winter, so 'Bull's Blood'
beet leaves serve as the red component there as well.

## 'Bull's Blood' as a Winter Crop

'Bull's Blood' is listed in the cold-house harvest chart as a
crop available from October through April. In the cool house
(with minimal supplementary heat), the harvest window extends
even further, from October through May. This long harvest season
makes it one of the most productive winter crops in the system,
providing continuous harvestable material for nearly eight months
under minimal protection.

The variety is valued not only for its color but also for its
reliability under cold conditions. Unlike some other salad mix
components that suffer quality declines during the coldest parts
of winter, 'Bull's Blood' maintains consistent quality and color
throughout the season. This reliability is essential for a salad
mix that aims to contain consistent proportions of each
ingredient in every batch.

## Growing Beets for Fall and Spring Harvest

Coleman has been successful growing exceptional quality beets
and greens in a cold house both late into the fall and very
early in spring. The two harvest windows require somewhat
different approaches and target different market opportunities.

### Fall Crop: Golden Beets

For the fall crop, since outdoor field beets can be sold quite
late from storage, Coleman has found that a gourmet item like
golden beets gives the best return on greenhouse space. The fall
cold-house beet crop must compete with inexpensive stored field
beets, so differentiation through variety selection is key to
commanding premium prices at market.

### Spring Crop: Baby Beets

For the spring crop, Coleman chooses baby beet varieties because
they offer such a refined product when greenhouse grown. Baby
beets from the cold house reach market six to eight weeks ahead
of the outdoor spring crop, providing a significant early-season
advantage. The tender, small roots are prized by chefs and
farmers market customers alike.
