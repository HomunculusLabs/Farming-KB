# Fungal Biodiversity Discovery

The scale of undiscovered fungal diversity represents one of the most significant gaps in our understanding of life on Earth. Current estimates suggest that only 5–10% of extant fungal species have been discovered and formally described, making fungi among the least documented major groups of organisms.

## Scale of Undiscovered Diversity

The ALL Species Summit, convened by Edward O. Wilson at Harvard University in October 2001, identified the discovery and description of unknown species as the major challenge facing science — comparable in scale to the Human Genome Project. Fungi constitute a significant proportion of this undiscovered biota.

Key figures on fungal diversity:

- **74,000–120,000** fungal species have been named to date
- **1,500,000** species hypothesized to exist on Earth (Hawksworth 1991), a figure widely cited and generally accepted
- Evidence accumulated since 1990 — especially from tropical plant studies and molecular investigations — suggests this estimate may be **too low**
- At most, only about **5%** of fungal species on Earth are currently known

In temperate areas, approximately **six times as many fungi as native plants** can be expected to occur at a given site. A thoroughly studied 200-hectare area can yield 2,500–3,000 fungal species when examined by multiple specialists over more than 25 years.

The gap between described and estimated species is further explored in [[biodiversity-of-fungi-species-estimates-discovery-gap]], which examines how molecular methods continue to reveal previously hidden diversity across all fungal groups.

## Challenges in Discovering Fungi

Fungi present unique challenges for inventorying that differ fundamentally from those faced when surveying animals or plants:

### Temporal Availability
- Some fungi produce fruit bodies only at intervals of **many years**
- Those fruiting structures may persist for only a **few hours** once they appear
- Other fungi are perennial and can be found at any time (e.g., lichen-forming fungi, many polypores)
- The [[fungal-fruiting-body]] of many species appears only under highly specific environmental triggers

### Detection Difficulties
- Fungi **cannot be trapped or fogged** and collected en masse like insects
- Many fungi live **inside plants or arthropods** as endophytes or parasites
- The same plant may support different fungi during different life stages (flowering to decay)
- Some fungi are found only by **microscopic examination** of leaves and woody surfaces
- Some can be detected only by **isolating and culturing** until they sporulate

### Pleomorphism
- Some fungi have **more than one sporing stage** in their life cycles (pleomorphic fungi)
- Only one stage may be found during a survey
- Historically, different stages were given **separate scientific names**, creating confusion
- Modern molecular methods and the [[biodiversity-of-fungi-fungal-phylogeny-kingdom-classification]] framework have helped link anamorph and teleomorph stages

### Quantification Problems
- A single underground [[mycelium]] (one individual) may produce numerous above-ground fruit bodies over a **wide area**
- Multiple fruit bodies on a single twig may arise from discrete adjacent individuals or from a **single spreading mycelium**
- Culture isolation yields species dependent on the **methods used**, not necessarily the species actually present
- Many soil fungi may be present only as **resting spores**, not actively growing
- Major discrepancies can occur between fungi isolated in culture and those known to be present via molecular methods or fruit body observation

### Group-Specific Methods
Different kinds of fungi demand **different detection and identification procedures**. The techniques used by specialists in different fungal groups can appear mysterious to other biologists and are rarely brought together in one place. This fragmentation has historically impeded comprehensive fungal inventorying.

## Ecological Importance of Fungi

Fungi are crucial to the maintenance of ecological processes and human well-being:

- **Nutrient cycling**: Without fungi, major problems in nutrient cycling would occur
- **Plant nutrition**: Plants would suffer without nutrients that fungi secure from soil through [[mycorrhiza]]l associations
- **Food webs**: Many animals depend on fungi for food
- **Decomposition**: Woody materials would not be broken down without fungal decomposers
- **Digestion**: Some insects cannot digest plant materials without fungal symbionts
- **Soil structure**: Soil structures themselves would differ without fungal activity

Fungi also have direct benefits for humankind as sources of **antibiotics, pharmaceuticals, food, industrial chemicals, enzymes, and biological control agents**. They have particular promise as components of sustainable development. Conversely, some fungi are detrimental as plant pathogens, toxin producers, causes of disease, food-spoilage agents, and biodeteriogens.

## The Need for Standardized Methods

Because of these multifarious complications, the development of **standardized approaches** to fungal inventorying and the selection of groups that might serve as **bioindicators** (surrogates) for fungal biodiversity are critical to facilitate comparisons between different sites. The desirability of standard methods has been discussed but no broad consensus has been reached.

New methods for describing and communicating information on newly discovered fungi will need to be developed. The volume of new species to be described is such that **traditional publication methods will be unable to cope** — Internet publishing linked to an internationally recognized registration and archival service may be the way forward.

## Role of Institutions and Collections

[[mycological-research-institutions]] worldwide maintain the herbarium collections, culture collections, and taxonomic expertise necessary for documenting fungal diversity. These institutions are essential partners in the discovery process—providing reference collections, identification services, and long-term specimen storage. The work documented by [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]] and [[biodiversity-of-fungi-herbarium-specimen-curation-best-practices]] ensures that newly discovered species are properly documented and accessible to future researchers.

## Implications for Cultivation

The vast undiscovered fungal diversity represents enormous untapped potential for mushroom cultivation. Many undescribed species may have desirable culinary, medicinal, or bioremediation properties. Understanding the scope of [[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]] helps guide exploration efforts toward habitats and taxonomic groups most likely to yield cultivatable species.
