## Renewable Services in Permaculture Design
A key distinction in Holmgren's framework is between renewable resources (which
are consumed when used) and renewable services (which provide ongoing benefits
without being consumed). This distinction has profound design implications. A
chicken provides both a renewable resource (eggs, meat, feathers) and renewable
services (pest control, manure production, scratching and tilling soil, breaking
down compost). Designing to maximize services while still harvesting resources
creates more efficient and resilient systems. A shade tree provides the service
of cooling a building (reducing energy consumption for air conditioning), the
service of intercepting rainfall (reducing stormwater runoff), and the service of
providing habitat — all while also being a renewable resource for timber,
firewood, or fruit. The most valuable elements in a permaculture design are often
those that provide the widest range of non-consuming services alongside their
consumable yields.
## EMERGY Analysis and Renewable Resource Accounting
Holmgren references EMERGY (embodied energy) analysis, developed by Howard T.
Odum, as a tool for evaluating the true cost of resource use. EMERGY analysis
accounts for all the energy that has gone into producing a resource, including
the environmental work of photosynthesis, geological processes, and ecological
interactions — not just the direct energy inputs of human labor and fossil fuels.
This analysis reveals that many supposedly "cheap" resources are actually
enormously expensive when their full EMERGY cost is calculated. A barrel of oil,
for example, represents millions of years of solar energy captured through
photosynthesis and concentrated through geological processes. Timber from a
200-year-old forest represents two centuries of solar energy capture, water
cycling, soil building, and ecological interaction. Using either resource for
ephemeral purposes (disposable packaging, single-use products) wastes vast
quantities of embodied energy. EMERGY analysis supports Holmgren's argument that
the product half-life should be at least as long as the resource replacement
time — using centuries-old timber for paper products is an egregious violation of
this principle.
## Case Study: Biomass Energy in Permaculture
Biomass energy illustrates the complexity of applying this principle. On the
surface, burning wood for fuel appears to be using a renewable resource
sustainably — trees regrow. But the EMERGY analysis reveals important
distinctions. Fallen branches, pruning waste, and forest thinnings represent a
yield that is already available without additional energy investment — the forest
has already done the work of growing the wood through photosynthesis. These
materials are excellent candidates for fuel, compost, or mulch because their
EMERGY cost to the human system is very low. By contrast, purpose-grown biomass
monocultures (willow coppice, miscanthus, corn ethanol) require significant
inputs of fossil energy for planting, fertilizing, harvesting, and processing.
The net EMERGY yield of these systems may be marginal or even negative, depending
on the specific practices used. Holmgren's principle pushes designers toward
using existing biomass flows (waste wood, crop residues, animal manures) before
investing energy in creating new biomass production systems.
## Water as a Renewable Resource
Water is perhaps the most important renewable resource in any permaculture
design, and Holmgren's principle applies to it with particular urgency. Rainwater
falling on a site is a daily or seasonally renewable resource that should be
captured, stored, and used as close to where it falls as possible, following the
principle of [[permaculture-retrofit-suburbia]]:
converting energy-consuming, import-dependent suburban properties into
productive, self-reliant households that derive most of their needs from on-site
renewable resources and services. The transition from non-renewable to renewable
