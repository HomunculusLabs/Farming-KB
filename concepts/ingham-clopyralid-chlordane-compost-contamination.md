# Clopyralid, Chlordane, and Compost Contamination

## The Contamination Problem

Compost can be contaminated by persistent chemicals that survive
the composting process and persist in the finished product. When
contaminated compost is used in gardens, landscapes, or agriculture,
these chemicals can damage sensitive plants. The two most discussed
contaminants in the composting world are clopyralid, a broadleaf
herbicide, and chlordane, a formerly used insecticide. Both present
serious challenges for compost producers and users.

## Clopyralid Persistence

Clopyralid is a synthetic auxin herbicide used on broadleaf weeds in
agricultural and turf settings. The critical problem with clopyralid
is that there is no knowledge of any [[aact-fungal-dominant-tea]] that
decompose it. While such organisms may exist, they clearly do not
occur routinely on grass clippings or other materials commonly
composted. This means clopyralid can survive the entire composting
process and remain active in finished compost at concentrations
sufficient to damage sensitive plants.

## Sources of Clopyralid Contamination

The most common source of clopyralid in compost is grass clippings
from lawns treated with clopyralid-containing herbicides. Hay and
straw from treated fields can also carry residues. Because clopyralid
breaks down very slowly through microbial activity, it persists
through the composting process. Compost made from contaminated
feedstocks will carry the herbicide into whatever soil or growing
media it is applied to.

## Prevention Strategies

The only reliable strategy for clopyralid is prevention. Know your
feedstock sources and avoid materials from treated areas. Grass
clippings should not be accepted for composting unless you can
verify they have not been treated with clopyralid or related
compounds. Many commercial composting operations now require
documentation from suppliers confirming that feedstocks are free of
persistent herbicides.

## Chlordane in Soil and Compost

Chlordane is an organochlorine insecticide that was widely used for
termite control and agricultural [[cannabis-pest-management-guide]] before being banned
in the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] in 1988. Despite the ban, chlordane persists
in soil for decades because it degrades very slowly. Unlike
clopyralid, there are bacteria and fungi that can decompose
chlordane, but they are not always found in compost starting
materials.

## Biological Remediation of Chlordane

While chlordane is more amenable to biological decomposition than
clopyralid, the organisms capable of breaking it down may not be
present in all compost piles. Adding diverse inoculums and ensuring
active aerobic conditions can help build the microbial communities
needed to decompose chlordane over time. This is a slow process,
however, and may require months to years of active composting.

## Manure Antibiotics and Their Impact

Antibiotics from manure present another contamination concern for
compost. Livestock treated with fungal-antibiotics|antibiotics, de-wormers, or other
pharmaceuticals pass residues in their manure. The biggest problems
in composting are strongylid medicine (de-wormer), followed by
herbicide residues, insecticides, fungicides, then copper and
sulfur. Antibiotics are generally easier to decompose than
persistent herbicides, as long as the system is not overwhelmed.

## Detecting Contamination During Composting

The way to know there is a contamination problem is when temperature
does not respond normally during composting. If temperatures stay
low for too long after starting a windrow or pile, something is
inhibiting the [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]]. In this case, add more nitrogen
as [[comparison-fish-hydrolysate-vs-fish-emulsion]] (especially if you suspect de-wormer problems)
along with molasses. If temperature still stays down, add commercial
compost starter inoculums containing bacteria that decompose these
resistant materials.

## Priority of Contaminant Concerns

Based on field experience, the hierarchy of contaminant problems in
composting is: strongylid de-wormer medicine (most difficult),
herbicide residues, insecticides, fungicides, copper and sulfur, and
then antibiotics (generally easiest to address). Grass clippings are
particularly problematic because they often contain chemical residues.
Pay close attention to monitoring temperature at the start of the
composting process to detect contamination early.

## Testing Compost for Contaminants

If you suspect your compost may be contaminated with persistent
herbicides, a simple bioassay can be performed. Grow sensitive
plants such as tomatoes, peas, or beans in a mix of your compost
and a clean potting medium. If the plants show symptoms of
herbicide damage (cupping, curling, or stunted growth), the compost
is likely contaminated. Laboratory testing for specific herbicides
like clopyralid is available but can be expensive. The bioassay
approach is affordable and directly measures the biological impact.

## Grass Clipping Warnings

Grass clippings are one of the most common sources of compost
contamination. Lawns treated with broadleaf herbicides often
contain residues of clopyralid or related compounds. These residues
survive composting and can damage garden plants when the finished
compost is used. Always verify that grass clippings have not been
treated with persistent herbicides before accepting them for
composting. This is especially important for municipal composting
operations that accept materials from multiple sources.
