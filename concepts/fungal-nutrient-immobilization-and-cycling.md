# Fungal Nutrient Immobilization and Cycling

## Overview

Fungi play a central role in nutrient cycling by decomposing organic matter, immobilizing mineral nutrients into their biomass, and regulating the spatial and temporal release of those nutrients back into the soil. This process of immobilization and subsequent mineralization is fundamental to ecosystem fertility, as it determines when and where nutrients become available for plant uptake.

## The Immobilization Process

During decomposition, mineral nutrients are sequestered by decomposer organisms and incorporated into their biomass. While in this immobilized state, nutrients are not in soluble form in the soil solution but are held within microbial tissue. The residence time of immobilized elements generally corresponds to the turnover time (lifespan) of the organism.

Fungi are particularly important immobilizers because:

- **Larger organism size**: [[fungal-hyphae]] are considerably larger than bacteria, allowing greater biomass accumulation per individual.
- **Lower turnover rates**: Fungal biomass, especially that of basidiomycetes, turns over more slowly than bacterial biomass. Marumoto et al. (1982) demonstrated that nitrogen mineralization was significantly slower from decomposing fungal cells than from bacterial cells.
- **Greater biomass investment**: Long-lived basidiomycetes growing on woody resources accumulate substantial biomass, creating large nutrient reservoirs. Shorter-lived molds utilizing simple carbohydrates have much lower biomass investment.

## Nutrient Accumulation in Fungal Biomass

The degree of nutrient immobilization varies with ecosystem type, the chemical composition of available plant parts, and the dominant fungal groups involved.

### Evidence from Forest Ecosystems

Stark (1972) demonstrated that fungal hyphae growing on pine needle litter contained 193–272% greater nitrogen and 104–223% greater phosphorus than the underlying litter, illustrating the capacity of fungi to concentrate nutrients from their substrate into their tissue.

Fogel and Hunt (1983) quantified the standing stock of organic matter in a second-growth [[douglas-fir]] forest and found that fungal biomass accounted for approximately 2% of the total ecosystem standing stock — comparable to the forest floor litter layer (4%). [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] alone contained 6% of total tree standing stock. For nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, mycorrhizal return to soil represented approximately 83–87% of total tree return.

### Fungal Fruit Body Nutrient Concentrations

Clinton et al. (1999) measured nutrient concentrations in fungal fruit bodies and underlying forest floor substrate in a Nothofagus forest. Fungal tissue was dramatically enriched in most elements compared to the forest floor substrate:

| Element | Mushrooms (mg/kg) | Substrate (mg/kg) |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| N       | 35               | 8.7               |
| P       | 4                | 1.0               |
| K       | 22               | 3.5               |
| Mg      | 0.8              | 0.1               |
| Ca      | 0.5              | 7.0               |
| C:N     | 13               | —                 |

Calcium was the only element more concentrated in the substrate than in fungal tissue, likely due to calcium's structural role in plant cell walls.

## Nutrient Immobilization in Tropical Forests

Lodge (1993) highlighted the critical role of fungal nutrient immobilization in [[dighton-fungi-tropical-forest-ecosystems]], where large nutrient capitals are stored in plant biomass but nutrient supply from soil is frequently limited:

- **Phosphorus limitation**: Phosphorus binds to aluminum and iron oxides in tropical soils, reducing plant availability. Fungal tissues can contain 5–36 mg/g phosphorus, and leaf litter phosphorus content can increase 10-fold due to fungal immobilization.
- **Leaching prevention**: Due to high rainfall, nitrogen and potassium are prone to leaching from the rooting zone. Fungi immobilize these nutrients at times of maximum leaching risk.
- **Fungal biomass as nutrient reservoir**: Fungal biomass in wet tropical soils contains significant proportions of total ecosystem nutrients — up to 22.2% of leaf litter phosphorus and 10.5% of soil extractable phosphorus.

Cord-forming basidiomycetes translocate nutrients from decomposed leaf litter to freshly fallen litter, improving resource quality and enhancing decomposition rates. Lodge demonstrated that phosphorus content of recently fallen leaf litter could increase by 120–140% during the first 6 weeks of decomposition, and nitrogen by 110–160%.

## Fungal-Faunal Interactions in Nutrient Cycling

The accumulation of nutrients into fungal biomass makes fungi an important food source for soil animals, creating trophic interactions that influence nutrient cycling rates.

### Grazing Effects on Fungal Communities

Newell (1984a,b) demonstrated that selective grazing by collembola altered the vertical distribution of competing mycelia of *[[mycena-galopus]]* and *Marasmius androcaceous* in a spruce forest floor. Preferential grazing of *Marasmius* restricted its growth to lower depths, allowing *Mycena* to dominate in the A soil horizon.

### Top-Down Control of Decomposition

Coleman et al. (1990) showed that in forests (high soil fauna density), reducing microbial predators increased decomposition (relief of grazing pressure), while in agricultural soils (low soil fauna density), reducing fauna decreased decomposition (suggesting synergistic fungal-faunal interactions).

Beare et al. (1992) found that excluding fungivorous [[soil-arthropods]] from no-till agricultural soils reduced litter dry mass loss by only 5%, but significantly altered nitrogen dynamics. Saprotrophic fungi were responsible for up to 86% of net nitrogen immobilization (1.8 g/m²) into surface litters when fungivores were excluded.

## Nutrient Translocation Through Fungal Networks

Fungi are unique among soil organisms in their ability to translocate nutrients through their hyphal networks.

### Mechanisms of Translocation

- **Carbon and phosphorus**: Translocate through hyphae primarily by diffusion (Olsson and Jennings, 1991). Carbon flow responds in real-time to demands at advancing hyphal fronts via cytoplasmic streaming.
- **Cesium and heavy metals**: Translocate more slowly than diffusion rates, suggesting incorporation into structural components of the cytoplasm or hyphal wall, accounting for bioaccumulation of radiocesium in basidiomycete fungi.

### Translocation in Wood-Decomposing Fungi

Wells and Boddy (1990, 1995a,b) demonstrated significant phosphorus translocation through mycelial cord systems:

- *Phanerochaete velutina* translocated 75% of added phosphorus from decomposed wood to newly colonized wood.
- *Phallus impudicus* translocated 13% of added phosphorus.
- Maximum translocation rates reached approximately 7,225 nmol P cm⁻² day⁻¹ through cords.
- Translocation was demonstrated over distances up to 75 cm between decomposing resources.
- Cord formation was only triggered in unsterile soil, suggesting other organisms provide the signal for cord development.

### Environmental Effects on Translocation

- **Temperature**: Higher temperatures increase translocation rates.
- **Soil moisture**: Drying induces cord thickening and reduces phosphorus translocation; wetting has no effect on cord structure or P movement.

## Competition Between Fungi and Trees for Nutrients

In practical forestry, nutrient competition between decomposer fungi and second-rotation tree crops can be significant. Logging residues support fungal communities that immobilize substantial nutrient quantities. Current practice often involves burning to remove residues and release nutrients, but this results in nutrient loss from the ecosystem. A better understanding of fungal nutrient dynamics could enable more sustainable residue management.

## Bioaccumulation of Metals

Fungi can immobilize nutrient elements and metal ions for extended periods through bioaccumulation. Because fungi are non-discrete organisms with extending hyphal networks, they can continuously translocate elements within their thallus. If elements are consistently translocated away from dying regions, immobilization time is extended. This mechanism explains the well-documented accumulation of radiocesium (¹³⁷Cs) in basidiomycete fruit bodies.
