# Schizophyllum commune and the Mazatec "Nis" Naming Confusion

## Overview

[[schizophyllum-commune]] is a globally distributed basidiomycete fungus that has been
the subject of a significant ethnobotanical confusion in Oaxaca, Mexico. The
Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca use the word "nise" (meaning "little bird") to refer to
both Psilocybe mexicana, a well-documented neurotropic mushroom, and Schizophyllum
commune, a common edible fungus with no psychoactive properties whatsoever.
This linguistic overlap has contributed to misunderstandings in the mycological
literature regarding which species were actually used in traditional Mazatec
ceremonies.

## Species Profile: Schizophyllum commune

Schizophyllum commune (commonly known as the split gill) is one of the most
widespread fungi on Earth, found on every continent except Antarctica. It is a
saprotrophic white-rot fungus that decomposes dead wood. Key morphological
features include:

- Small, fan-shaped fruiting bodies typically 1-4 cm across
- Hairy upper surface, white to grayish in color
- Characteristic split gills that radiate from the point of attachment
- These gills can close and reopen in response to humidity changes, an
  adaptation for spore dispersal in varying conditions
- Tough, leathery texture that persists through drying and rewetting cycles

## The "Nise" Problem: Linguistic Overlap

Among the Mazatec people of Oaxaca, the term "nise" translates to "little bird."
This name is applied to at least two entirely different fungi:

1. **Psilocybe mexicana** — a small, psychoactive mushroom containing
   [[richard-evans-schultes]]** (1939), who initiated modern ethnobotanical study
  of Mexican hallucinogenic fungi
- **R. Gordon Wasson** (1957), whose Life magazine article brought Mazatec
  mushroom ceremonies to worldwide attention
- **Roger Heim** (1956-1958), who provided the first taxonomic and chemical
  studies of the sacred mushrooms
- **Gastón Guzmán** (1959, 1983, 1997), whose monographic work on Psilocybe
  established the modern taxonomic framework

The Mazatec use multiple common names for sacred mushrooms, including:
- "Apipiltzin" — kid or little boy
- "Atkad" — mayor or leader
- "Di nizé taaya" — fungus of the genius
- "Shi thó" — that eruptions thing
- "Teotlaquilnanácatl" — divine fungus
- "Nise" — little bird (shared with S. commune)

## Broader Pattern of Naming Confusion

The Schizophyllum-Psilocybe naming confusion is not an isolated case. Several
other examples of vernacular name overlap between edible and psychoactive
species have been documented in the neurotropic fungi literature:

- **[[panaeolus-antillarum-misidentification-copelandia-confusion]]**, an edible tropical fungus, is frequently confused
  with Copelandia species by recreational users because both present white
  fruit bodies and grow on cow dung. P. antillarum does not bruise blue and
  lacks psychoactive compounds.

- **[[guzman-chlorophyllum-molybdites-toxic-edible-paradox-yoruba-divinatory-use]]** has been attributed neurotropic properties
  based on its Yoruba name "a jegba ariwo-orun" (eat and hear voices from
  heaven), yet most mycological authorities consider it poisonous rather
  than psychoactive.

## Significance for Ethnobotanical Research

This pattern of naming confusion has several important implications for
ethnobotanical and mycological research:

1. **Chemical verification is essential** — Reliance on indigenous names alone
   is insufficient to determine the psychoactive properties of a fungus.
   Chemical analysis must always accompany ethnobotanical reports.

2. **Specimen preservation matters** — Misidentified voucher specimens can
   propagate errors through the literature for decades, as seen with several
   Panaeolus and Psilocybe species reported to contain psilocybin based on
   mixed or misidentified collections.

3. **Contextual understanding is required** — The cultural context in which a
   fungus is named and used must be thoroughly understood before drawing
   conclusions about its pharmacological properties. A name like "nise" may
   describe a morphological feature (resemblance to a small bird) rather than
   indicating a specific chemical property.

4. **Market surveys can be misleading** — Fungi collected from indigenous
   markets may not accurately represent the species used in actual ceremonies,
   as market vendors and ceremony practitioners may have different levels of
   taxonomic knowledge.
