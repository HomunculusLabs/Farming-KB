# Fukuoka Seed Selection Methods

Masanobu Fukuoka's approach to [[home-garden-seed-saving]] and seed selection was integral to his [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]] system. Rather than breeding "improved" varieties optimized for high-input agriculture, he worked with traditional and local varieties that thrived under natural conditions -- without plowing, fertilizer, or chemicals.

## Improved vs. Natural Varieties

Fukuoka drew a sharp distinction between modern "improved" seed varieties and traditional ones:

**Improved varieties** are bred for performance within industrial agriculture:
- Selected for response to chemical fertilizer and irrigation
- Bred for uniform appearance, large size, and market appeal
- Often weak and disease-prone without chemical support
- Require a full program of chemical inputs to perform well
- The "Green Revolution" and "Good Rice Movement" varieties depend on eight to ten chemical sprayings per growing season

**Traditional/natural varieties** are adapted to local conditions over generations:
- Develop strong root systems and compact, sturdy growth
- Naturally resistant to local diseases and insects
- Thrive without chemical inputs
- May produce less vegetative growth but proportionally more grain
- Evolved through "dwelling together with human beings" over thousands of years

Fukuoka stated: "If farmers would stop using weak, 'improved' seed varieties, stop adding too much nitrogen to the soil, and reduce the amount of irrigation water so that strong roots could develop, these diseases would all but disappear."

## The Ideal Plant Form

Central to Fukuoka's seed selection was the concept of the natural form. For rice:

- **Compact plants** -- short, sturdy, with small heads and few leaves
- **Strong roots** -- developed in non-flooded, well-aerated soil
- **High grain-to-straw ratio** -- small plants produce grain equal to or heavier than their straw weight (2,000 lbs straw yielding 2,000+ lbs rice)
- **Natural seedling establishment** -- sprouted in place, not transplanted
- **Disease resistance** -- inherent, not chemical-dependent

He noted: "If you show a rice plant from my field to a farmer, he will know immediately that it looks as a rice plant should." Any experienced farmer can tell by the overall form, root shape, and joint spacing whether a plant was grown naturally or with chemicals.

## His Experiment with Traditional Rice

Fukuoka experimented with an old variety of glutinous rice from southern Japan. Results:
- Each seed produced an average of 12 stalks
- About 250 grains per head
- Some areas yielded 27.5 bushels (1,650 lbs) per quarter acre
- He believed this variety could approach "the greatest theoretically obtainable harvest from the solar energy reaching the field"

## Selection Criteria

Fukuoka's seed selection was informal and observational rather than systematic:

1. **Grow in natural conditions** -- seeds are evaluated in the actual environment of no-till, no-chemical, no-fertilizer farming
2. **Observe natural vigor** -- plants that thrive without support are selected
3. **Save seed from the best performers** -- [[home-garden-seed-saving]] from naturally healthy, vigorous plants
4. **Local adaptation** -- varieties that have evolved in the local climate and soil are preferred
5. **Taste and nutrition** -- seeds producing flavorful, nutritious food are valued over market appearance

## The Winter Grain Controversy

Fukuoka's stubborn defense of traditional Japanese rye and barley against government pressure to abandon them illustrates his seed philosophy. The government promoted American wheat varieties (which required a long growing season and often rotted during Japan's rainy season harvest) and later pressured farmers to abandon all winter grains in favor of imported wheat. Fukuoka refused, arguing that traditional rye and barley could be harvested in May before the rainy season and were "easy crops to raise" that "produce the greatest number of calories from Japan's fields."

## Vegetable Seed Selection

For semi-wild vegetable growing, Fukuoka valued:
- **Resilience** -- plants strong enough to compete with weeds
- **Self-seeding** -- varieties that reseed themselves year after year (daikon, turnips, burdock, garlic, perennial onions)
- **Wild flavor** -- vegetables closest to their wild ancestors have the richest flavor and highest nutritional value
- **Perennials where possible** -- potatoes, taros, Chinese leeks, and garlic come back year after year

## Seeds Evolved with Humans

Fukuoka made an important philosophical point about seeds: "Crops which have evolved over thousands and tens of thousands of years by dwelling together with human beings are not products born entirely from the discriminating knowledge of the farmer, and can be thought of as naturally occurring foods." But "instantly altered varieties which have been developed by an agricultural science which has drawn far away from nature" fall outside this category of natural food.
