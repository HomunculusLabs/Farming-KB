# Compost Tea Recipe Ratios and Ingredients

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT) requires careful selection of ingredients in the right proportions to grow the desired [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]]. The goal is not to add huge amounts of compost but to extract a good inoculum and feed it with targeted foods.

## Compost to Water Ratio

The relationship between compost amount and water volume is non-linear. More water requires proportionally less compost. For well-designed actively aerated machines:

| Brew Volume | Compost Needed |
|-------------|---------------|
| 5 gallons   | ~1 pound       |
| 25 gallons  | ~7 pounds      |
| 50 gallons  | ~10 pounds     |
| 100 gallons | ~12 pounds     |
| 500 gallons | 15-20 pounds   |

A machine requiring 100 lbs of compost for 100 gallons indicates poor design. Compost volume relates to machine design quality: poorly designed machines need far more compost to achieve the same extraction.

If you have enough compost, put it directly on the field. Make tea only when compost is limited or when you need specific organism groups extracted and multiplied.

## Compost Selection

Use a mix of good aerobic [[ingham-thermal-compost-versus-worm-compost]] with good aerobic worm castings, in roughly a 50-50 or 25-75 ratio. The compost must contain the full range of beneficial organisms (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes) to be extracted into the tea.

Thermal compost requirements: temperature must reach 55°C for at least 3 days in all parts, never exceeding 70°C. Worm compost (vermicompost): at least 75-80% of material must pass through worm digestive systems. Both must have adequate oxygen (above 5.5 ppm) and moisture (45-75%).

## Fungal Tea Recipe (for 500 gallons)

- **[[bacterial-vs-fungal-compost-teas]]**: Above 150 micrograms [[bioremediation-fungal-biomass-biosorbent-material]], preferably 300+ micrograms, with 10+ micrograms of active fungi
- **Molasses**: 0.1-0.5% of water volume (0.5 gallon summer to 2.5 gallons spring in 500 gal)
- **Kelp**: 0.5 pound (provides surface area for fungi and micronutrients)
- **Rock dust**: As needed based on plant nutrient requirements
- **Humic acid or fish hydrolysate**: About 0.1%; increase if fungal component needs improvement

## For a 50-Gallon Brew

- **Humic acid**: 1-4 ounces (50-200 ml). Check SFI website for tested brands
- **Fish hydrolysate**: 2-10 ounces (100-500 ml). Organic Gem and Neptune's Harvest are recommended brands. Beware of hydrolysate made from anaerobic fish scraps. Do not exceed half liter per 50 gallons to avoid [[ingham-antibiotics-osmotic-shock-compost-tea]]
- **Protein meals**: Corn, oat, soy, vegemite, barley, feather meal all work well
- **Molasses**: At low amounts (under 1%) helps bacteria. At 5% concentration, it helps fungi significantly more than bacteria, though bacterial biomass remains high

## Ingredient Functions

### Bacterial Foods (Simple Sugars)
- **Molasses**: Contains multiple sugar types and some humic material; broad bacterial food. Amount relates to machine aeration capacity, not brew volume
- **Honey**: Helps bacteria, not fungi; contains no known fungal foods
- **Simple sugars**: Feed bacteria exclusively. Too much drives soil anaerobic

### Fungal Foods (Complex Materials)
- **Fish hydrolysate** (enzymatically hydrolyzed): Contains bone, cartilage, and scale material; excellent fungal food. Comes with mineral elements attached
- **Humic acids** (enzymatic hydrolysis or hot water extraction from leonardite): Complex fungal food, thousands of types exist
- **Oatmeal, cornmeal, wheat bran**: Moderate fungal foods
- **Wood chips** (1/8 to 1/2 inch): Added to compost, not tea; wide C:N ratio feeds fungi
- **Shredded paper and cardboard**: Fungal food for compost production

### Micronutrients
- **Kelp**: Provides micronutrients and plant growth factors; also provides surface area for fungal growth. About 1 ounce per 5 gallons is sufficient for fungal surface area. When fish hydrolysate is used, minimize additional kelp since hydrolysate already provides minerals

### What Not to Add
- **White sugar**: Too limited a food resource (only sucrose)
- **Plant materials** (milk, yogurt, tofu, soy sauce, peanut butter, eggs): Can drive tea anaerobic during warm periods. May work in spring or fall when temperatures are lower

## Key Principles

1. The amount of molasses relates to the machine's aeration capacity, not the brew volume
2. Add foods that grow beneficial organisms, not "bad guys"
3. Diversity of foods matters more than quantity of any single ingredient
4. Fish hydrolysate comes with mineral elements attached; minimize additional rock dust or kelp when using it
5. Start with a simple baseline recipe (just molasses and kelp) for troubleshooting
6. If you have enough compost, put it directly on the field; make tea only when compost is limited or when you need specific organism groups

## Troubleshooting

If tea smells bad after brewing, too much food was added for the organisms to process aerobically. Reduce food amounts. Put brewed tea in a sealed container half-full for 24 hours in a warm place. If it still smells OK, send a sample for testing.

Bad odors indicate anaerobic conditions: rotten egg, sulfur, ammonia, vinegar, sour milk, or vomit smells mean the tea should not be used. Pleasant earthy smell indicates healthy aerobic conditions.
