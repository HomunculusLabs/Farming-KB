## Minor Poisonous and Irritant Fungi of Britain

## Lepiota Helveola — Poisonous Lepiota

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 1¼ inches; rounded then flattened, sometimes with slight central prominence; initially covered with ochre-brown cuticle becoming flushed with pink or lilac in damp weather; tears into numerous scales showing white or pinkish surface between |
| **Gills** | White, then cream-colour, crowded, fimbriate (fringed) edge |
| **Stem** | Height ~1½ inches, width ~¼ inch; cylindrical, hollow, concolorous with cap, covered with silky fibrils |
| **Ring** | Annular bracelet form |
| **Flesh** | White, often becoming slightly pinkish when cut |
| **Smell** | Pleasant |
| **Taste** | Sourish |
| **Habitat** | Amongst grass; autumn |
| **Status** | Rare in Britain |

*Lepiota helveola* belongs to the group of small brownish-pink *Lepiota* species whose specific differences remain uncertain. It is significant because it causes symptoms resembling mild *Amanita phalloides* poisoning — placing it in the cell-degenerating toxin category rather than the less dangerous irritant group. Despite its small size, it should be treated with genuine caution.

## Lepiota Cristata — Crested Lepiota

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 1½ inches; campanulate (bell-shaped) at first, later convex; brownish or reddish-brown cuticle breaks up except at the central boss, showing as concentric circles of small scales encrusted in a white satiny surface |
| **Gills** | White or whitish, crowded, thin; attached to a collarette separating them from the stem |
| **Stem** | Height ~5½ inches, width ~3/16 inch; slender, hollow, white and silky, often tinged pink or yellow toward base; white branching mycelial fibres spread in soil |
| **Ring** | Membranous, white, fugaceous (evanescent) |
| **Flesh** | White |
| **Smell** | Strong and disagreeable |
| **Taste** | Unpleasant |
| **Habitat** | Grassy places; spring to autumn |

Because of its disagreeable smell and unpleasant taste, *Lepiota cristata* is usually considered suspect but is **not actually poisonous**. This is an instructive case: an unpleasant-smelling, bad-tasting fungus is harmless, while the deadly *Amanita* species are tasteless and odourless.

## Lactarius Torminosus — Woolly Milk Cap

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 6 inches; convex at first with strongly inrolled margin hiding gills, covered with whitish woolly felt; later depressed in centre; viscid when moist; usually rosy pink or pale orange-red (may be almost white) with darker concentric zones and paler margin |
| **Gills** | Thin, decurrent, yellowish pink |
| **Stem** | Height ~2 inches, width ~1 inch; coloured like cap but paler, sometimes with small superficial pits; firm and brittle, finally hollow |
| **Flesh** | Pinkish |
| **Milk** | White, very acrid taste |
| **Habitat** | Woods and heaths, frequently under birches; summer and autumn |

*Lactarius torminosus* is **not truly poisonous**, though like all acrid *Lactarius* species it acts as an irritant when eaten raw. The specific epithet *torminosus* means "griping" — a direct reference to its effects when uncooked. Cooking destroys the acrid principle.

This species illustrates the enormous cultural variation in fungal consumption: it is the **favourite toadstool in Finland**, where it is boiled in water and eaten fresh or salted down. It is also eaten in neighbouring Scandinavian countries. In Norway, it is strongly roasted and added to coffee. A fungus that British mycological texts describe as "irritant" is a prized food elsewhere — demonstrating that edibility judgments are often cultural rather than strictly chemical.

## Russula Emetica — The Sickener

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 4 inches; convex then flattened or depressed; initially bright shiny red, readily losing colour to pink, yellow, or white; edge smooth at first, later furrowed; skin easily removed |
| **Gills** | Shining white, more or less free, somewhat crowded |
| **Stem** | Height ~3 inches, width ~1 inch; rigid, white or pink-tinged |
| **Flesh** | Firm, white, pink under cap skin |
| **Taste** | Very acrid after a few seconds, persistent |
| **Smell** | Pleasant |
| **Habitat** | Woods; summer and autumn; different forms associated with beeches and conifers |

The specific epithet *emetica* means "causing vomiting." The taste is characteristic — very acrid, developing after a few seconds and persisting. Despite the acridity, the smell is pleasant, which makes it particularly treacherous for those who might identify fungi by smell alone.

## Russula Fragilis — Fragile Russula

Much resembles *R. emetica* but with a wider colour range (typically bright red). Usually smaller with a striate cap edge. The stem is spongy then hollow, and very fragile — true to its name. Unlike *R. emetica*, the flesh is not pink under the cap skin. The taste is immediately very acrid (not delayed like *R. emetica*) but less durable. Commonest form has a red cap; some colour variants have received varietal names.

## Russula Fellea — Bitter Russula

Entirely pale ochraceous. The cap is convex then flattened, viscid, with darker centre and thin striate margin. Gills are adnate, thin, somewhat crowded, white at first and exuding watery drops. The taste is very acrid and bitter. Grows in beech woods in late summer and autumn. The specific epithet *fellea* refers to bile — reflecting its bitter taste.

## Russula Drimeia — Pungent Russula

Bright purple or brown-violet cap, darker in the middle, decoloured by rain. Convex with incurved margin, then flat or depressed, slightly viscid. Gills are adnate and somewhat crowded, citron-yellow then sulphur-yellow. Stem is solid and firm, paler than the cap. Flesh is compact, yellowish then white, purplish under the skin. Very acrid taste with pleasant smell. Grows in coniferous woods in autumn. A variety (*Queletii*) has white gills.

### The Russula Genus Generally

The genus *Russula* much resembles *Lactarius* but exudes no milk when the brittle flesh is broken. Many species are brightly coloured. Most have a mild taste and are edible; others have an acrid taste and are usually considered poisonous. Whatever irritant substance the acrid species contain is dispersed in cooking, but they are best avoided. Taste is such a reliable character in *Russula* that some classifications make it a primary division.

## Entoloma Lividum — Leaden Entoloma

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 5 inches; tawny or greyish, often turning white here and there; hard and compact, roundish at first with thin mealy strongly inrolled edge, then flattened except for central fleshy disk; edge irregularly raised; skin finely silky |
| **Gills** | Yellowish then pink, often with yellow edge |
| **Stem** | Shining white, firm, somewhat swollen above or below; mealy apex with fine striations |
| **Flesh** | White, fragile |
| **Smell** | At first agreeable meal-like, but not lasting |
| **Taste** | At first agreeable meal-like, but not lasting |
| **Habitat** | Grassy places in autumn, especially deciduous woods |

The genus *Entoloma* has pink spores and typically sinuate gills. *Entoloma lividum* often causes violent sickness and diarrhoea, and occasional death. Around Dijon it is known as *le grand empoisonneur de la Côte d"Or* — "the great poisoner of the Côte d"Or." Its initially pleasant meal-like smell and taste are deceptive, making it one of the more dangerous species for inexperienced collectors.

## Hebeloma Crustuliniforme — Crustlike Hebeloma

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 4¼ inches; convex then spread out, often with flattened central boss and thin undulating edge; pale yellow, brown, or reddish, darker in centre; viscid at first |
| **Gills** | White then clay-coloured then date-brown, crowded, uneven edge; drops of liquid exuded from gill edges in wet weather, showing as spots on drying |
| **Stem** | Height ~2¼ inches, width ~1 inch; short, stuffed then hollow, often thickened at base; white squamules at apex becoming brownish from falling spores |
| **Flesh** | White, firm |
| **Smell** | Radish |
| **Taste** | Radish |
| **Habitat** | Often in rings in grassy woods; autumn |

The genus *Hebeloma* has brown spores and typically sinuate gills. The species name *crustuliniforme* refers to its habit of growing in rings suggesting "a row of rolls coming from a baker"s oven." It is very indigestible, and the persistent radish taste after cooking makes it objectionable. The exudation of liquid droplets from gill edges is a distinctive character.

## Volvaria Gloiocephala — Sticky Volvaria

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 4½ inches; campanulate then spread out with central prominence; glutinous, entirely smoky grey or white with greyish disk, striate edge |
| **Gills** | Crowded, white then salmon-pink |
| **Stem** | Height ~7 inches, width ~½ inch; white, later grey or tawny; mealy at first, cylindrical but usually narrowed above, swollen at base surrounded by white or grey persistent volva |
| **Flesh** | Soft, white |
| **Smell** | Unpleasant |
| **Taste** | Unpleasant |
| **Habitat** | Grass, usually on heaps of rubbish, straw or manure; summer and autumn; occasionally on thatched roofs and vegetable remains in sand dunes |

The genus *Volvaria* has pink spores, free gills, and a membranous volva. Formerly regarded as deadly poisonous, *Volvaria gloiocephala* is now known to be **harmless** and is much eaten in Portugal and Algeria. This is another case of a species once feared as deadly that has been rehabilitated — similar to *Amanita mappa*. The lesson is that mycological knowledge of edibility has been built slowly and sometimes reversed completely.

## Key Lessons from Minor Poisonous Fungi

1. **Acridity is not equivalent to lethality**. Species like *Lactarius torminosus* and the acrid *Russula* species cause discomfort when raw but are rendered safe by cooking. They belong to a fundamentally different category from the cell-destroying *Amanita* toxins.

2. **Pleasant smell and taste are not safety guarantees**. *Entoloma lividum* and *L. helveola* have initially pleasant sensory qualities despite being genuinely dangerous.

3. **Cultural variation in consumption is enormous**. The Finnish enthusiasm for *Lactarius torminosus* and the Portuguese consumption of *Volvaria gloiocephala* demonstrate that "edibility" has a large cultural component beyond pure chemistry.

4. **Species once thought deadly may prove harmless**. Both *Amanita mappa* and *Volvaria gloiocephala* underwent complete reassessment, illustrating the provisional nature of mycological knowledge.
