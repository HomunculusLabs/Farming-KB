# Ingham — AACT Disease Suppression Mechanisms

In the *Field Guide for AACT*, Dr. Elaine Ingham details the specific mechanisms by which [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] suppresses plant diseases. The fundamental principle is that AACT does not kill pathogens — it outcompetes them.

## The Five Mechanisms

### 1. Outcompete Disease Organisms
Beneficial bacteria and fungi compete with pathogens for:
- Space on root and leaf surfaces
- Food resources (exudates, organic matter)
- Oxygen and moisture
When beneficial populations are adequate, pathogens cannot establish.

### 2. Food Resource Competition
The second control point is food:
- If beneficial organisms consume available food resources first, pathogens starve
- This is why adding too much food to AACT can backfire — it feeds everyone, including potential pathogens
- Proper food resource management means beneficials get established before pathogens can exploit resources

### 3. Physical Occupation of Space
Beneficial organisms physically occupy infection sites:
- Bacterial biofilms coat root surfaces, leaving no attachment points for pathogens
- Fungal networks form physical barriers around roots
- On leaf surfaces (the phyllosphere), beneficial microbes occupy the same niches pathogens need

### 4. Production of Inhibitory Compounds
Some beneficial organisms produce compounds that inhibit pathogens:
- Certain bacteria produce antibiotics
- Some fungi produce antifungal compounds
- These are secondary mechanisms — competition and occupation of space are primary

### 5. Systemic Induced Resistance
Healthy microbial communities can trigger plant immune responses:
- Plants exposed to beneficial microbes develop stronger defense systems
- This "priming" effect makes plants more resistant to subsequent pathogen attack

## AACT Does Not Kill Anything

Ingham emphasizes this critical distinction:
- AACT is not a pesticide or fungicide
- It works by establishing beneficial organisms that prevent problems
- The goal is a balanced, diverse microbial community
- If the biology is adequate, disease organisms are naturally suppressed

## Specific Disease Protocols

The Guide provides protocols for specific diseases:
- **Fusarium** — improve fungal biomass, ensure aerobic conditions
- **Rhizoctonia** — fungal-dominated teas, adequate coverage
- **Powdery Mildew** — foliar application of fungal-dominated teas
- **Late Blight** — early application before infection, fungal teas
- **Verticillium Wilt** — root-zone applications to improve beneficial fungal competition
- **Brown Rot** — foliar and soil applications during critical periods

## Pathogenic vs. Saprophytic Fungi

Ingham clarifies the fungal categories:
- **Saprophytic** — use dead organic matter; most fungi are in this category
- **Pathogenic** — cause disease in living tissue
- **Parasitic** — use living tissue but don't necessarily kill the host (like mycorrhizae)
- Some fungi can be saprophytic at one time and pathogenic at another, depending on conditions

## The Oxygen Factor

Pathogenic fungi tend to do better in reduced oxygen conditions:
- Aerobic conditions favor beneficial fungi that outcompete pathogens
- "The worst fungal pathogens tend to do better in slightly oxygen-reduced to anaerobic conditions"
- This is why maintaining aeration in both soil and compost tea is critical

## Practical Applications

The soil food web approach has practical applications in agriculture,
landscaping, composting, and ecological restoration. By understanding which
organisms are present and what their populations indicate about soil health,
practitioners can make informed management decisions. Microscope analysis of
compost and compost tea provides direct feedback on biological activity and
helps refine amendment strategies for specific soil conditions and crop needs.

## Monitoring and Assessment

Regular monitoring of soil biology through microscope analysis provides
valuable feedback on the effectiveness of management practices. Key indicators
include bacterial biomass, fungal biomass, protozoan populations, and
nematode community structure. Changes in these populations over time reveal
trends in soil health that may not be visible through chemical testing alone.
This biological perspective complements conventional soil analysis and provides
a more complete picture of soil function.
