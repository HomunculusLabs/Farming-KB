### Costantin and Matruchot's Secret Method

Some years before the early 1900s, the French mycologists **Costantin and Matruchot** reported that they had been able to produce [[mushroom-spawn]] from spores. Their work was reported in such a manner that only the results—not the methods—were made known, and it remained a secret. Since they distinctly referred to the use of spores, it was assumed they found a practicable method of stimulating spore germination, though even this was not certain.

Their method found practical application for a time in a department of the **Pasteur Institute** in Paris, and later appeared to have been taken over by a corporation in Paris interested in mushroom growing. Under their system, spore culture tubes of spawn were grown and sold to growers who were expected to multiply the volume by preparing beds yielding the crop spawn required.

### Repin's Method

Another secret method of effecting spore germination was referred to by **Repin** in a circular on the subject. His method appeared to have been practically applied in the making of **"plate" spawn** by one of the large seed firms of Paris. As far as could be ascertained, this spawn had not come into very general use.

### Ferguson's Investigations

Miss **Ferguson's** investigations on spore germination were reported in **Bulletin 16** of the Bureau of Plant Industry, U.S. Department of Agriculture. The author's own studies were reported in **Bulletin 85** of the same series.

### Future Prospects

The author had not abandoned the hope that a practical spore method might be developed, but even should such a method be found, it was not certain that it would be more practicable than the tissue culture method, which had already been the primary cause of the rapid development of mushroom growing in the United States.

The tissue culture method with its approach might also develop further in the United States—as it had in France, where growers purchase spore cultures and make their own general supply of spawn.

## Commercial Brick Spawn

### Advantages

The compact commercial [[brick-spawn]] exhibits two significant advantages:

1. **Better keeping quality** than any other product on the market—the compressed, dried form resists deterioration during storage and shipment
2. **Easily handled**—standardized dimensions and compact form facilitate packaging, shipping, and use in the field

### Dimensions

When dry, a commercial brick spawn ordinarily measures about **5½ × 8¼ × 1½ inches**. Moulds must be made somewhat larger than these dimensions to allow for shrinkage during drying.

### Materials

The compost used for brick spawn is customarily a **mixture** of:

- **Stable manure** (horse manure with bedding): Should constitute at least two-thirds of the material, free of heavy straw
- **Cow manure**: A considerable admixture gives a more compact brick and encourages a mould-like rather than thread-like texture of the mycelium
- **Loam**: Sometimes a small quantity is added

Good success for home use has been obtained when stable manure was used alone, without admixture.

## Brick Manufacturing Methods

### Hand Moulds

Ordinary hand moulds may be made of iron or in the form of a **strong oak frame**, optionally lined with steel plates. Two methods of hand-moulding were described:

#### Method 1: Puddled Compost

1. Thoroughly wet or puddle the compost
2. Place the mold on a board of suitable width
3. Compress the manure into the mold
4. Remove the mold from the brick formed
5. Push the board along for a succession of impressions
6. Place boards supporting the bricks in racks
7. Dry bricks for a few days until they may be turned on edge for further drying

#### Method 2: Moist but Not Puddled Compost

1. Use compost in a condition that is quite moist but not puddled
2. Compress into the brick with some force, often employing a mallet
3. The brick thus obtained is sufficiently rigid to be immediately handled if necessary

### Mechanical Presses

Several machines have been successfully used in making brick spawn. Hand moulds are generally employed first, with subsequent repressing given with a brick machine if one is available. The use of machines allowed more consistent density and dimensions.

## Inoculation, Growth, and Drying

### Two Methods of Inoculation

#### Method 1: Post-Drying Stack Inoculation

1. Dry bricks on racks, **not too quickly**, to a point when they are but slightly moist throughout
2. Moisture content must be determined by the **internal** condition, not the exterior
3. Pile or stack bricks in a situation favorable for mushroom growing
4. Between each pair of bricks in successive layers, distribute **four or five bits** of spawn from pure cultures or other sources
5. Allow the mycelium to grow through the bricks over a period of weeks

#### Method 2: Pre-Drying Insertion Inoculation

1. While bricks are still fairly moist, insert pieces of spawn into the brick—**one or two towards each end**
2. Make a cut in the soft brick, insert the spawn, and nearly close the slit with a stroke of the knife
3. Continue drying slowly until bricks are about in the condition described under Method 1
4. Stack bricks in piles in a well-drained, ventilated room or cellar for mycelial growth

### Drying Principles

In both methods, the key principle is **slow, controlled drying**. Rapid drying damages the viability of both the compost medium and any mycelium already present. The bricks must be dried sufficiently for storage and shipment while retaining enough internal moisture to support mycelial growth during the inoculation phase.

### Growth Period

After inoculation and stacking, the bricks are left in favorable conditions (proper temperature, moisture, and ventilation) for the mycelium to colonize the entire brick. The time required depends on temperature, moisture, and the vitality of the inoculum, but typically ranges from several weeks to a month or more. Once thoroughly "run" (fully colonized), the bricks are dried to final storage moisture and prepared for market.

## Quality Assurance

The reliability of the final product depends on every step of the process:

- **Quality of starting materials**: Good stable manure, free from contamination
- **Proper composting**: Adequate fermentation of the manure before brick-making
- **Thorough sterilization**: Elimination of competing organisms from the medium
- **Vigorous inoculum**: Pure culture spawn of known, tested variety
- **Controlled drying**: Neither too rapid (which damages viability) nor too slow (which allows contamination)
- **Proper storage**: Cool, dry conditions until use

The commercial brick spawn industry, built upon these principles, became the backbone of mushroom cultivation in the English-speaking world and laid the foundation for the modern spawn industry.
