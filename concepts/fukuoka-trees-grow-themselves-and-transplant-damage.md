    10|# Trees Grow Themselves and the Damage of Transplanting
    11|
    12|[[masanobu-fukuoka]] argued that trees in mountain forests grow to magnificent
    13|heights without any human help, and that the fundamental error of modern
    14|orcharding lies in preventing trees from growing according to their own nature.
    15|Central to this critique is the damage caused by transplanting, root pruning,
    16|and the artificial conditions imposed on [[mollison-designers-grafting-and-budding-fruit-trees]] from the moment they are
    17|planted in prepared ground.
    18|
    19|## Forest Trees vs. Orchard Trees
    20|
    21|In the mountain forests, trees grow under nearly natural conditions without
    22|fertilizer, irrigation, [[coleman-beneficial-insects-and-ecological-pest-control]], or pruning. Reforested cedars in a
    23|favorable area produce about forty tons per quarter-acre over twenty years,
    24|roughly two tons of new growth annually. When branches, leaves, and roots are
    25|included, annual production approaches four tons per acre, all without any
    26|fertilizer input.
    27|
    28|This is essentially slash-and-burn agriculture carried out by [[mollison-sewage-treatment-natural-processes]],
    29|yet the trees grow on and on year after year. The forest creates its own
    30|fertility through the continuous cycling of [[solomon-compost-organic-matter]]. Fallen leaves and
    31|dead branches decompose on the forest floor, feeding soil organisms that in
    32|turn make nutrients available to living roots. No external inputs are needed
    33|because the system is self-contained and self-renewing.
    34|
    35|In orchards, by contrast, farmers fell existing trees and carry them off as
    36|logs, pull up the roots of trees and grasses, burn everything, and then turn
    37|the soil over and over again to loosen it. In doing so, they destroy the
    38|physical structure of the soil. After pounding and kneading the soil like bread
    39|dough, they drive out air and the humus essential to microorganisms, reducing
    40|it to lifeless yellow mineral matter.
    41|
    42|Then they plant fruit saplings in this dead soil, add fertilizer, and attempt
    43|to grow fruit trees entirely through human forces. The poor farmer, in
    44|Fukuoka's view, has taken the long way around. Nature provides all the
    45|fertilizer that is needed. Crops grow very well without chemical fertilizers
    46|when the natural soil ecosystem is intact.
    47|
    48|## How Transplanting Damages Root Systems
    49|
    50|When people transplant saplings, they generally give no thought to the
    51|long-term consequences of what they are doing. They graft a scion to the stock
    52|of another species or clip the roots of a fruit sapling before transplanting.
    53|From this point on, several critical problems develop that permanently
    54|compromise the tree.
    55|
    56|The roots cease to grow straight and lose the ability to penetrate hard rock.
    57|Even a slight entanglement of the tree roots during transplanting interferes
    58|with normal growth of the first generation of roots and weakens the tree's
    59|ability to send roots deep into the soil. The tree becomes dependent on the
    60|shallow topsoil for its nutrition and water supply, making it vulnerable to
    61|drought and requiring constant irrigation.
    62|
    63|Applying chemical fertilizers compounds the damage by encouraging the tree to
    64|develop a shallow root structure that extends along the topsoil rather than
    65|penetrating deep into the subsoil. Fertilizer application and weeding halt the
    66|normal aggregation and enrichment of topsoil. Clearing new land for agriculture
    67|by pulling up trees and bushes robs the deeper layers of the soil of humus,
    68|halting the active proliferation of soil microbes.
    69|
    70|These very actions are what make plowing and turning the soil necessary in the
    71|first place. The farmer creates a problem through intervention and then must
    72|intervene further to solve it, creating an endless cycle of dependency on
    73|human management.
    74|
    75|## The Soil Works Itself
    76|
    77|Fukuoka observed that the soil lives of its own accord and plows itself. It
    78|needs no help from man. In nature, grasses and trees, along with earthworms
    79|and moles in the ground, act as the plowhorse and oxen, completely rearranging
    80|and renewing the soil.
    81|
    82|Grass roots work the soil down to twelve, fifteen inches or more, while tree
    83|roots penetrate far deeper. As these roots grow and die, many types of
    84|microorganisms proliferate. These organisms die and are replaced by others,
    85|increasing the amount of humus and softening the soil. Earthworms eventually
    86|appear where there is humus, and as the number of earthworms increases, moles
    87|begin burrowing through the soil.
    88|
    89|Farmers typically plow the soil to a depth of about four to eight inches,
    90|whereas the roots of grasses and green manure crops work the soil down to
    91|twelve inches, fifteen inches, or more. When these roots reach down deep into
    92|the earth, air and water penetrate into the soil together with the roots. The
    93|only effective way to soften up the soil is through natural biological
    94|processes. When man leaves the soil to itself, the forces of nature enrich
    95|and loosen it far more effectively than any plow.
    96|
    97|## Natural Root Development vs. Transplanted Trees
    98|
    99|Trees that grow from seed in place develop root systems perfectly adapted to
   100|the local soil conditions. Cedar grows where it can thrive to great size,
   101|mixed woods rise where conditions suit them, and pine trees germinate in places
   102|suited to pine. One does not see pines growing at the bottom of a valley or
   103|cedar seedlings taking root on mountain tops.
   104|
   105|One type of fern grows on infertile land and another in areas of deep soil.
   106|Plants that normally grow along the water's edge are not found on mountain
   107|tops, and terrestrial plants do not thrive in the water. Although apparently
   108|without intent or purpose, these plants know exactly where they can and should
   109|grow.
   110|
   111|When seedlings are transplanted, this natural root-soil relationship is
   112|severed. The taproot, which in nature would grow straight down through cracks
   113|in rocks and deep into subsoil layers, is cut or bent during the transplanting
   114|process. The fine feeder roots that would develop in harmony with specific soil
   115|microorganisms in their native location are instead placed in foreign soil that
   116|lacks the biological community they evolved with.
   117|
   118|The result is a tree that is permanently dependent on human intervention for
   119|its survival. It needs irrigation because its roots cannot reach deep moisture,
   120|it needs fertilizer because the soil biological community was destroyed, and it
   121|needs pest control because it is growing in a weakened state that makes it
   122|susceptible to attack.
   123|
   124|## The Role of Black Wattle and Nitrogen-Fixing Trees
   125|
   126|Fukuoka observed that black wattle planted in depleted soil on a mountainside
   127|would be succeeded a number of years later by giant cedars many times its size.
   128|The black wattle, a nitrogen-fixing leguminous tree, creates conditions
   129|favorable for other species through its root-associated microbes.
   130|
   131|When black wattle is planted among cedar or cypress, these latter thrive with
   132|the help of the microbes present on the roots of the black wattle. This
   133|observation suggests a natural succession model for orchard establishment:
   134|rather than clearing all existing vegetation and planting fruit trees into bare
   135|soil, the farmer could work with natural succession processes, using pioneer
   136|and nitrogen-fixing species to prepare the ground for productive fruit trees.
   137|
