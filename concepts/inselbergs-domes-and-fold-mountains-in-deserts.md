## Inselbergs, Domes, and Fold Mountains in Deserts

The elevated landforms of arid regions—inselbergs, rock domes, and fold mountain systems—represent the most significant water-harvesting opportunities in desert landscapes. Their geological characteristics combine reliable run-off generation with microclimatic diversity, making them priority sites for [[wadi]] and scarp systems, they are straightforward:

- Rarely develop deep caves (but do have some rock shelter).
- Rarely have pronounced valley entries.
- Usually dip steeply into loose sandy soils around the base.
- May occur in series, separated by great fissures.

### Water Harvesting from Domes

Despite their simplicity, inselbergs are very solid run-off areas, some covering many tens of hectares. The basic design principle is straightforward: allow one acre of cultivated "field" for every 20 hectares of rock run-off. Base floods are led via fluming (constructed channels) to walled fields, supporting both crops and fruit trees or palms.

A few shallow mulch-filled valleys exist on these massifs where soil is mainly [[dam-construction]] in desert environments:

**Large dams at range exits**: Where a river exits these ranges, a dam can create a large ovoid freshwater lake. The permanence of this water depends on the ratio of run-off area to storage area—this must be 20-30:1 in deserts. Modest, deep storages are more likely to hold water year-round than large shallow ones. Wherever massive ranges back the fold mountains (as in Colorado, Nevada, and near Mt. Ararat in Turkey), foothill dams hold water from a combination of snow melt and rain run-off.

**Critical safety note**: Settlements and roads must be well off any flood path should dams burst. As few settlements exist in deserts, this is an open option—unlike large dams in well-settled areas, which tend to be built above existing valley settlements with frequently disastrous consequences in war or earthquake.

### Modest Dam Systems

A great many smaller dams are available in fold systems:

- Narrow rock-walled exit valleys.
- Foothill dams where exit waters are led to diversion dams.
- Complex freshwater dam systems led off low-slope wadi-like streams flowing to the plains.
- A characteristic of fold systems is that they occur in large series with correspondingly large catchments, so even modest dam sites receive substantial inflow.

### Palisade Rock Formations

All fold mountains of sedimentary or metamorphic rock show more or less marked palisades of harder rock series, usually running along slopes like sub-contours. These palisades have significant ecological value:

- Vegetation is far taller, greener, and denser on the upslope side, where run-off is slowed and forced to infiltrate.
- While not as effective as constructed swales, they assist water retention.
- Some are wide-spaced, fairly well on contour, project well above the surface, and develop striking tree-lines.

### Horizontal Bores

Wherever series of palisades are a feature, a horizontal bore will often tap sweet water trapped in the rock strata. Unlike qanats, such water can be sealed off and used only as needed, and can be regulated to control the amount taken from the aquifer.

### Headwater Stream Management

A shallow, fast dry-stream system usually develops from the upper foothill regions of folds—typically stream sequences only 4-15 m wide with banks 0.5-2 m high. The bed is usually rock-filled, and detritus along the banks shows the extent of flood levels. These violent, sudden floods carry boulders and smash the lower branches of large trees.

Direct damming of these flows is impractical—dams would fill with boulder and silt within a few rains. Instead, the strategy is to **bleed off** the torrent and divert it to a more peaceful contour flow, and thence to a dam. Leading flow along an oversized, wide, shallow contour trench causes silt to settle in the trench before reaching the dam. The trench itself becomes a planting site and a silt trap that is easily cleaned out, whereas a dam is difficult to de-silt.

## Sandstone Valley Scarps

A variation on the scarp theme occurs in incised sandstone valleys in semi-arid older landscapes, where rivers have cut down as the land rises. Here the cliff is not a simple scarp but a set of complex smaller scarps and caves in bedded sandstones or mudstones. Caves occur at the base of cliffs or in the cliff face itself. Side valleys may resemble box canyons but are less regular than fault-scarp valleys, and waterfalls may appear as series of cascades. Wet seepages are common on lower ledges, making these more complex sites than true desert scarps, open to innovative design.
