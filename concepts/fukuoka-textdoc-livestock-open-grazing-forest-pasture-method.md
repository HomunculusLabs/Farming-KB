# Livestock Open Grazing and Forest Pasture Method

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] envisioned a fundamentally different approach to
[[fukuoka-natural-livestock-farming]] — one based on open grazing in forests and mountains
rather than confinement in factory-like facilities. His forest pasture
method integrates multiple animal species into orchards and wooded
mountain areas, allowing natural ecological relationships to replace
artificial feed regimens, antibiotics, and intensive management. This
approach stands in direct opposition to the industrial livestock systems
that Fukuoka saw as both ecologically destructive and spiritually
degrading.

## Critique of Factory Livestock Farming

Modern livestock raised in large numbers and reduced to standardized
machinery no longer receive the strength and grace of nature. As products
of human endeavor achieved through the power of science alone, they differ
fundamentally from nature — which creates something from nothing — because
they are merely processed goods, the transformation of one thing into
another.

The pitiful sight of fowl, pigs, and cattle confined to cages and unable
even to move bears witness to the loss of nature in these animals and
points also to humanity's [[mu|alienation from nature]]. Both the farm
worker directly engaged in raising livestock and the city dweller who
consumes these food products lose their health and humanity as they turn
away from nature. The energy economics are equally damning: the ratio of
energy output to input is 50 percent for broilers, 20 percent for pork, 15
percent for milk, and only 8 percent for beef.

Fukuoka observed that the larger and more highly improved the breed of
animal being raised, the greater the energy input required and the greater
the effort and pains taken by the farmer. Livestock bred by artificial
insemination and raised on artificial milk formulas show poor viability.
Breeding programs opposed to nature often result in unnaturally deformed
creatures that man deludes himself into thinking are superior.

## The Integrated Forest Pasture Vision

Fukuoka's idealized vision of livestock farming depicts a landscape of
remarkable ecological integration. Bees busily make rounds of clover and
vegetable blossoms thickly flowering beneath trees laden heavy with fruit.
Semi-wild chickens and rabbits frolic with dogs in fields of growing
wheat, and great numbers of ducks and mallards play in the rice paddy. At
the foot of the hills and in the valleys, black pigs and boars grow fat on
worms and crayfish, and goats peer out from the thickets and trees.

This is not a primitive scene of economic disadvantage but what Fukuoka
described as an organic partnership between man, animal, and nature. An
environment comfortable for small animals is also an ideal setting for
human beings. The scene could be taken from an out-of-the-way hamlet in a
country untarnished by modern civilization.

## The Multi-Species Orchard System

The practical method involves allowing pigs, chickens, and rabbits to
graze together with cattle in an orchard or reforested area. Each species
performs a distinct ecological function within the system.

Pigs like to root up the ground looking for insects and earthworms in
valleys and damp areas. They act as small tractors that dig up the soil.
In this turned soil, clover and grain are sown. Combined with cow and pig
droppings as [[natural-fertilizer-sources-and-application]], a fine growth of pasturage is
established. Once this pasture grass begins to nourish, chickens, goats,
and rabbits can be raised in the same way.

This system creates a self-reinforcing cycle: pigs till and fertilize,
clover fixes nitrogen and provides forage, chickens control insects, and
all animals contribute manure that builds soil fertility. No external
feed, no [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], and no mechanized soil preparation are
required.

## Adaptation Through Natural Selection

It would be unreasonable to take modern, genetically upgraded livestock,
release them suddenly in a forest, and expect immediate improvement in
results. But if the possibilities are studied with patience, a path opens
up. After habituating animals to open grazing in forests over the course
of two or three generations, natural selection takes over and those
animals adapted to nature survive.

Fukuoka emphasized selecting breeds that can graze mountain pastures
rather than raising genetically improved Holstein cows and beef cattle in
indoor pens on concentrated feed. Native breeds and varieties such as
Jersey cattle, which are thought to be of lower productivity, actually
have higher feed efficiency and do not lead to depletion of the land. The
goat — called the poor man's cattle because it takes care of itself and
also provides milk — is inexpensive to raise and does not weaken the
productivity of the land.

## Poisonous Plants and Animal Wisdom

In allowing animals to graze in the fields and mountains, some people
worry about poisonous plants, but animals have an innate ability to tell
these apart from other plants. Bracken, for example, may be a poisonous
herb under certain conditions, but it grows in clusters. If a cow eats
too much and suffers, something is probably wrong with the cow — not with
the pasture. Healthy animals in natural settings possess wisdom about what
to eat and what to avoid.

## Japan's Mountainous Terrain as Opportunity

Some eighty percent of Japan consists of mountains and valleys. Fukuoka
proposed fencing off the entrance to one of the depopulated mountain
villages that have lost their inhabitants to the cities, thereby creating
a large, open grazing range for animals. All sorts of [[permaculture-domestic-animals]]
could be placed inside the enclosure and left to themselves for a number
of years, after which the results could be evaluated.

Ticks and mites do present a challenge, but infestation varies
considerably with conditions — generally limited in cool, breezy areas and
closely related to humidity and temperature. The problem can be prevented
by providing the right environment and raising hardier cattle, while
giving some consideration to the protection of [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] that
help control tick populations.

The very notion of "raising" livestock should not even exist in natural
farming. Nature is the one that raises and grows. Man follows nature; all
he needs to know is with what and in what manner cattle live. The barn
becomes unnecessary — all that is needed, for the convenience of man, is
the smallest of sheds: perhaps a milking shed for the cows and a tiny
chicken shed in which hens can lay their eggs. The animals themselves
scratch and forage freely for food night and day under the open sky, find
themselves a place to roost, and grow up strong and healthy.
