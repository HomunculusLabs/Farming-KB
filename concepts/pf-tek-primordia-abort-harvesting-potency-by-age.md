## Stages of Fruiting Body Development

### Colonization to Pinning

After the substrate cake is fully colonized with white mycelium (2-3 weeks
post-inoculation), jars are left in indirect light. The mycelium continues
to infiltrate the substrate until sufficient nutrients are accumulated to
trigger the fruiting cycle. Within one week to several weeks after surface
colonization, tiny white pin-like structures appear — this is called
"pinning" and marks the beginning of the fruiting cycle.

### Primordia Formation

Soon after pinning, small round fungus growths appear that begin to turn
yellow. These develop into primordia — tiny worm-like structures with tiny
reddish heads. These are the first recognizable mushrooms, and they represent
the earliest stage of actual [[fruiting-body-development]]. PF considers
primordia to be the optimal stage for harvesting in terms of potency.

### Normal Fruiting Bodies

If left to develop, primordia grow into normal mushrooms with defined caps
and stems. The cap initially remains conical and attached to the stem by a
veil (partial veil). As the mushroom matures, the cap expands, the veil
stretches, and eventually breaks. After veil break, the cap opens fully,
gills become exposed, and sporulation (spore release) begins.

## Abortive Mushrooms

Over half of the primordia that first form will abort — they cease growing,
become convoluted, and deform. PF describes aborts as having convoluted
caps, gnarly stems, and stunted growth. Despite their unappealing appearance,
abortive mushrooms are among the most potent material the cake produces.

Identification of aborts:
- Growth stops while other mushrooms on the same cake continue
- Cap becomes convoluted, wrinkled, or gnarly
- Stem may be twisted or disproportionately thick
- Head darkens and eventually turns black if not harvested

PF also notes blob mutants — amorphous masses of fungus with little or no
cap. These are also potent and suitable for harvesting.

## The PF Verdict on P. cubensis Potency

PF pushed back against the characterization of Psilocybe cubensis as a "weak"
mushroom, arguing that it all depends on cultivation method, growing medium,
and harvesting/preservation technique. When grown on brown rice, harvested
young, and cool-dried with desiccant, PF maintained that P. cubensis produces
material of the highest entheogenic quality. This perspective highlights the
often-underappreciated role of post-harvest handling in determining the final
potency of cultivated material.
