## Overview

Wicking beds offer a unique growing environment characterized by consistent bottom-up moisture delivery, reduced surface wetness, and limited root zone depth compared to in-ground gardens. These characteristics make wicking beds ideal for certain crops while poorly suited to others. Selecting the right crops and adapting management practices to seasonal conditions are critical to maximizing productivity and avoiding common problems such as waterlogging, nutrient deficiency, and root constriction.

## Best Crops for Wicking Beds

### Leafy Greens and Herbs

Leafy vegetables and culinary herbs are the most reliable performers in wicking beds. Lettuce (all types), spinach, rocket, Asian greens (bok choy, mizuna, tatsoi), Swiss chard, kale, and coriander all thrive in the consistently moist but well-aerated root zone. These crops have relatively shallow root systems (15–25 cm) that remain within the capillary fringe, and they benefit from the dry surface layer that reduces fungal disease pressure on foliage. Basil, parsley, chives, mint (in contained beds), and thyme also perform well, though Mediterranean herbs like rosemary and lavender prefer drier conditions and may suffer in the humid root zone.

### Root Vegetables with Shallow Taproots

Radishes, turnips, beetroot, and spring onions grow well in wicking beds because their edible roots develop within the upper 15–20 cm of the growing medium where moisture is available. Carrots can be successful but require a deep growing medium (minimum 35 cm above the reservoir) and a mix with low nitrogen to prevent forking. Potatoes are generally not recommended because the tubers can rot if they extend into the saturated zone near the reservoir, and hilling operations are difficult in a contained bed.

### Brassicas

Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, and kohlrabi perform well in wicking beds, particularly during cooler seasons. Their moderate water requirements align well with the capillary delivery rate, and the consistent moisture helps prevent buttoning (premature bolting) in broccoli and cabbage. Provide adequate spacing (40–50 cm for large brassicas) to ensure each plant has sufficient root access to the wicking zone.

## Crops Requiring Adapted Management

### Fruiting Vegetables

Tomatoes, capsicums, eggplants, and cucumbers can produce excellent yields in wicking beds, but they present two challenges. First, their high water demand during fruiting may exceed the wicking rate, requiring supplemental surface watering during peak summer. Second, tomatoes in particular develop extensive root systems that may grow downward into the reservoir itself. Solutions include using indeterminate varieties trained vertically, increasing the growing medium depth to 35 cm or more, and selecting drought-tolerant rootstock varieties. Cherry tomatoes and determinate bush types are generally easier to manage than large indeterminate cultivars.

### Legumes

Bush beans, climbing beans, and peas grow well in wicking beds and have the added benefit of nitrogen fixation. However, excess moisture in the root zone can promote root rot diseases in beans, particularly Pythium and Rhizoctonia species. Ensure the overflow drain is functioning properly and consider adding extra perlite or pumice to the growing medium to improve drainage. Peas perform best in the cooler seasons and benefit from a trellis attached to the bed frame.

### Alliums

Garlic, onions, and leeks can be grown in wicking beds but require attention to the moisture gradient. These crops prefer drier conditions as they mature, and excessive moisture near bulb formation can promote fungal rots. Plant them in the upper portion of the bed where the moisture gradient is drier, and reduce reservoir refill frequency during the final 4–6 weeks of growth to allow bulbs to cure in drier soil.

## Crops to Avoid

### Deep Taproot Species

Plants with deep taproots that extend beyond the growing medium into the reservoir layer will suffer root rot in the anaerobic water zone. Avoid parsnips, daikon radish, salsify, and scorzonera unless the growing medium is very deep (40+ cm). Even then, some portion of the taproot may reach the saturated zone.

### Waterlogging-Sensitive Species

Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, lavender, oregano, sage), succulents, and plants adapted to arid conditions generally perform poorly in the consistently moist environment of a wicking bed. These species prefer well-drained soil with dry periods between watering and are better suited to conventional raised beds with drip irrigation or hand watering.

### Autumn Transition

As temperatures cool and day length decreases, reduce reservoir refill frequency as plant water demand drops. Plant cool-season crops (brassicas, Asian greens, broad beans, garlic) to take advantage of the wicking bed's consistent moisture during autumn rains. Remove spent summer crops and add their residue to the compost pile rather than incorporating it directly into the bed, to avoid introducing disease pathogens. Top-dress with 2–3 cm of mature compost and a dusting of rock phosphate or bone meal for phosphorus-heavy crops.

### Winter Dormancy

In frost-prone climates, wicking beds can continue producing cold-hardy crops (kale, spinach, winter lettuce, mizuna) through the winter with minimal maintenance. The water reservoir provides thermal mass that moderates soil temperature fluctuations, and the soil stays 2–4°C warmer than surrounding ground. In areas with hard freezes, drain the reservoir and inlet pipes to prevent ice damage, or insulate the bed with bubble wrap or hessian. Avoid walking on frozen growing medium, as the ice crystals can destroy soil structure. A row cover or low tunnel over the bed extends the growing season by 4–6 weeks in cold climates.

## Water Savings and Efficiency Metrics

Wicking beds typically use 50–80% less water than equivalent in-ground gardens with overhead irrigation, and 30–50% less than drip-irrigated raised beds. The water savings come from three mechanisms: elimination of surface runoff and deep percolation losses, reduction of soil surface evaporation through the dry mulch layer, and delivery of water directly to the root zone where it is most efficiently absorbed. A standard wicking bed measuring 2 m × 1 m with a 20 cm reservoir holds approximately 200–250 liters of water, which can sustain a full planting of leafy greens for 2–3 weeks in summer without any supplemental watering.
