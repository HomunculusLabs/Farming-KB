# Fungal Herbarium Collection — Specimen Management Standards

## Overview

Fungal herbaria are repositories of preserved fungal specimens that serve as permanent physical records of fungal diversity, distribution, and taxonomy. Proper specimen management is essential for maintaining the scientific integrity of these collections, which underpin [[fungal-research-compound-reference]], taxonomic studies, and ecological investigations. Herbarium standards ensure that specimens remain accessible and useful for researchers across generations.

## Label Requirements

Every fungal herbarium specimen must carry a label with **eight essential elements**:

1. **Title** — The collection title or project name
2. **Scientific name** — Current accepted name of the fungus
3. **Authority / Bibliography** — Author citation and any relevant taxonomic references
4. **Locality** — Geographic location including country, state/province, and coordinates
5. **Substratum / Host / Vegetation / Habitat** — Ecological context of the collection
6. **Collector name and number** — Unique identifier assigned by the collector
7. **Date** — Date of collection
8. **Herbarium name and specimen number** — Institutional acronym and accession number

Complete, accurate labeling is non-negotiable. Specimens without adequate locality data or collector information have severely diminished scientific value.

## Materials and Preservation

### Paper and Mounting

- Use **archival-quality paper** — cotton rag or acid-free paper only
- **Avoid** rubber cement, adhesive tape, and adhesive-backed papers, as these degrade over time and can damage specimens
- Specimens should be securely attached using appropriate archival methods

### Specimen Preparation

- **Air-drying** is the standard method for macrofungal specimens
- **Silica gel** is effective for rapid field drying, particularly in humid environments
- **Freeze-drying** (lyophilization) is an option for preserving delicate morphology and biochemical compounds

### Ancillary Materials

Supplementary materials should be stored separately and cross-referenced to the specimen:

- **Field notes** — Detailed ecological and observational data
- **Spore prints** — Deposits of spores for color and microscopic analysis
- **Photographs** — *In situ* images showing habitat, fresh morphology, and diagnostic features

All ancillary materials must carry the same specimen number as the herbarium packet for unambiguous cross-referencing.

## Collection Acceptance Criteria

Herbaria typically evaluate incoming collections against the following criteria:

- **Preservation quality** — Specimen must be well-preserved with identifiable features intact
- **Identification level** — Should be identified to species level where possible
- **Geographic relevance** — Collections from undercollected areas are prioritized
- **Scope alignment** — Relevant to the herbarium's taxonomic or geographic focus
- **Expert identification** — Specimens identified by recognized specialists are preferred
- **Legal compliance** — Must be accompanied by appropriate collection permits

## Herbarium Facilities

Proper herbarium infrastructure is critical for long-term specimen preservation:

- **Insect-resistant screens** — Prevent infestation by dermestid beetles and other pests
- **Climate control** — Temperature and humidity regulation to slow deterioration
- **Vertical storage** — Specimens stored in pullout trays for efficient access and space utilization
- **Security** — Restricted access to prevent theft or damage

## Electronic Cataloguing and Data Access

Modern herbarium management increasingly relies on electronic databases:

- **Interoperability** — Electronic catalogues should be compatible across herbaria to facilitate data sharing and collaboration
- **Online querying** — Web-accessible databases dramatically increase the utility of collections, enabling researchers worldwide to search holdings without physical visits
- **Digitization** — High-resolution imaging of specimens complements database records

Integration of herbarium data with [[dna-barcoding-fungal-identification]] and [[fungal-species-estimates]] maximizes the research value of collections.

## Destructive Sampling

Physical alteration or destruction of herbarium specimens requires:

- **Explicit permission** from the curating institution
- **Thorough documentation** of what was removed, by whom, and for what purpose
- **Voucher retention** — All remaining material must be retained and cross-referenced

Destructive sampling is often necessary for DNA extraction, chemical analysis, or detailed microscopic study, but must always be conducted with the goal of preserving the specimen's ongoing utility.
