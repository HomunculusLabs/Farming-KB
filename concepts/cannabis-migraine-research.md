# Cannabis and Migraine

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

Migraines are recurring moderate to severe headaches that can be debilitating for hours or days. Cannabis has a long history of use for migraine treatment, and modern research suggests that an underlying endocannabinoid deficiency may contribute to migraine pathology.

## Condition Overview

### Symptoms
- Recurring headaches ranging from moderate to severe
- Pain can induce nausea and vomiting
- Severe sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sounds (phonophobia)
- Paranormal flashes of light (auras) signaling arrival
- Prodromes: craving for chocolate, aversion to noise, mood shifts, sluggishness, stiff neck, gastrointestinal problems

### Epidemiology
- About 75% of adult migraine sufferers in the United States are women
- Estrogen is considered a culprit in the gender discrepancy
- Migraine sufferers may be at increased risk for stroke
- Menstruation increases the hazard of occurrence by up to 96%

### Orthodox Understanding
Within allopathic medicine, the cause and cure for migraines remain unknown. Physical causes considered include abnormalities in blood vessels of the scalp or brain. Hormonal change, stressors, weather changes, and food sources may play roles as triggers, but evidence remains inconclusive.

Treatment consists of numerous oral and injectable pharmaceuticals. Sometimes blood vessel surgery provides relief. However, overuse of pharmaceuticals (especially tricyclics and opiates) and their adverse effects have posed problems.

### Diagnosis
Doctors diagnose through interview: specific pain history, frequency of attacks, duration, location, presence of aura, nausea/vomiting, and impact on daily routines.

## Cannabis Research for Migraine

### Evidence Summary
| Substance | Study Type | Key Finding | CHI |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|-----|
| Cannabis and Dronabinol | One human patient (2006, New York) | Reduced all symptoms: pain, photophobia, transient blindness, enlarged blind spots, and tinnitus | 3 |
| Cannabinoids (meta-analysis) | Review of available studies (2004, GW Pharmaceuticals, Montana) | Endocannabinoid deficiency may underlie migraines; multiple mechanisms of action identified | 4 |
| THC and CBD | Human patients during migraine episode (1985, Israel) | THC, but not CBD, inhibited serotonin release from platelets during migraine pains | 3 |
| **Total CHI Value** | | | **10** |

### Key Research Findings

1. **Endocannabinoid deficiency hypothesis:** Ethan Russo's in-depth review suggests that migraines and other conditions "display common clinical, biochemical and pathophysiological patterns that suggest an underlying clinical endocannabinoid deficiency that may be suitably treated with cannabinoid medicines"

2. **Multiple mechanisms of action:** Cannabis constituents work through anti-inflammatory, serotonergic, and dopaminergic mechanisms, as well as interaction with NMDA and endogenous opioid systems

3. **Serotonin modulation:** THC (but not CBD) inhibited serotonin release from blood platelets during migraine pain, providing a possible mechanism for pain relief

4. **Non-psychoactive option:** Oral Dronabinol (5 mg twice daily) relieved all symptoms without reported weight gain, suggesting non-smoked options are effective

5. **Historical validation:** Cannabis's effectiveness is recorded throughout ancient medical literature, providing a long track record of use

## Strain-Specific Considerations

The endogenous cannabinoid anandamide binds with both CB1 and CB2 relatively equally. THC also binds relatively equally with CB1 and CB2 receptors. Sativas and sativa-dominant strains generally contain higher THC:CBD ratios.

Given that the endocannabinoid deficiency hypothesis involves both CB1 and CB2 pathways, balanced cannabinoid profiles may be most appropriate.

## Mind-Body Medicine

Research reveals that migraines often follow a specific psychological pattern: "Migraine headaches occurred when an individual had been making an intense effort to carry out a definite planned program, or to achieve some definite objective. The headache occurred when the effort had ceased, no matter whether the activity had been associated with success or failure. The essential features were striving and subsequent relaxation."

Aggravating factors:
- Perception of being forced, pushed, or pressured
- Two days before menstruation
- Muscle tension in the neck
- Psychic tension, tiredness
- Noise and odors on days before headache

Protective factors:
- Days off
- Relaxation after stress
- Regular sexual activity (some women reported relief)
