# Mushroom Environmental Factors: Temperature, Moisture, and Light

## Overview

Benjamin Minge Duggar's 1915 mushroom cultivation manual provided one of the first systematic scientific treatments of the environmental factors governing mushroom production. Rather than offering simple rules of thumb, Duggar explored the complex interactions between temperature, moisture, and light, revealing that these factors could not be evaluated in isolation from one another or from the biological community sharing the mushroom bed. His work at Cornell University and elsewhere represented a departure from the purely empirical tradition of mushroom growing, introducing experimental methodology into what had been a craft passed down through generations of practical cultivators.

## Temperature Requirements

### The Optimum Range

The optimum air temperature for mushroom growing under commercial conditions was judged to be 54°F to 56°F (12°C to 13°C). When considering a practical working range rather than the strict optimum, Duggar extended acceptable limits from 50°F to 65°F (10°C to 18°C). Cultivation that maintained temperatures more or less continuously above 60°F or below 50°F was not commercially successful, though much depended on other environmental factors.

### Heat Tolerance in Pure Culture vs. Practice

One of Duggar's most significant findings was the dramatic difference between the mushroom's thermal tolerance in pure culture and its apparent tolerance in commercial beds. In pure cultures, Agaricus campestris mycelium grew well up to 85°F (29°C) provided sufficient moisture was maintained. Sporophores were produced in Mason jar cultures at temperatures constantly at or above 70°F, and in pot cultures under practically sterile conditions at 60°F to 70°F.

Yet in commercial beds, temperatures of 72°F sustained for just 24 hours proved fatal to pin heads and injurious to established spawn. This discrepancy was not due to direct thermal damage but to the stimulation of competing organisms—bacteria, fungi, and insects—that either crowded out or directly attacked the mushroom mycelium at elevated temperatures. The high temperature operated by making some other environmental condition injurious, not by killing the mushroom itself.

### The Freezing Experiment

Duggar described a remarkable natural experiment with beds spawned in November. The spawn developed well for a month, but from that time until March the temperature never exceeded 52°F. On several occasions it reached 32°F or below, and the beds froze solid. When warmer conditions resumed in March, the beds bore abundantly and produced mushrooms of unusual size. Although the warm weather shortened the productive life of the beds, the heavy mushrooms appeared much later into the warm season than beds spawned in January.

This finding led to a practical recommendation: growers seeking heavy mushrooms should avoid "pushing" beds by maintaining the highest permissible temperature. If there was no urgency, a temperature below 50°F for a week or two before buttons began to form was advantageous for developing maximum size.

The freezing experience also demonstrated the remarkable resilience of mushroom mycelium. Although the beds appeared completely dormant during the cold months, the spawn was not killed. As soon as temperatures rose into the productive range, the mycelium resumed active growth and fruiting. This hardiness explained why mushrooms could be found in pastures after spring thaws, the spawn having survived winter conditions in the soil.

### Temperature Monitoring Practices

Duggar recommended careful temperature monitoring using ground thermometers plunged into the beds. A useful supplementary technique involved placing straight, smooth stakes (like short walking canes) into the bed at twelve to twenty foot intervals; by pulling them out and feeling them with the hand, a grower could estimate the bed's internal temperature. Duggar noted that if the temperature at seven inches depth was 100°F, the surface inch would be approximately 95°F indoors and 85°F to 90°F outdoors, providing a practical gradient for assessment.

When the heat of the manure was on the decline, it fell rapidly—five, often ten degrees per day—until it reached about 75°F. Between 75°F and 65°F the temperature might rest for weeks. This plateau period was critical for spawn establishment, as it provided a window during which the mycelium could colonize the bed before temperatures dropped too low for active growth.

### The Summer Cultivation Problem

Duggar noted that higher temperatures not only harmed production through biological competition but also affected mushroom quality. Sporophores expanded and elongated too rapidly at elevated temperatures, preventing the formation of large, heavy buttons that commanded the highest market prices. This effect was partially attributable to decreased atmospheric humidity at higher temperatures. Duggar proposed that refrigeration or complete compost fumigation might enable summer production, though he doubted large-scale compost sterilization was a practical possibility at the time. The vision of year-round mushroom production through environmental control would eventually be realized in the mid-twentieth century with the development of climate-controlled growing rooms, validating Duggar's fundamental insight that the biological limitations were environmental rather than physiological.

## Moisture Requirements

### Atmospheric Humidity

A relative atmospheric humidity of 60 to 75 percent was considered satisfactory for mushroom cultivation. Duggar emphasized that both extremes were detrimental: an atmosphere saturated with moisture, with water dripping from walls, was as unfavorable as excessively dry conditions. The key was maintaining a gradual but slow evaporation from the bed surface, which could be regulated through controlled ventilation.

### The Undesirable Extremes

Duggar identified two contrasting moisture extremes that represented the worst possible environments for mushroom cultivation. On one extreme were sealed caves with no air circulation, where stagnant moisture accumulated without evaporation. On the other extreme was exposure to drying winds that stripped moisture from bed surfaces. Proper mushroom cultivation required the middle path between these poles, with enough moisture to sustain mycelial growth and enough air exchange to prevent stagnation.

### Bed Moisture Assessment

Proper compost moisture was assessed at approximately 65 percent water content by weight. The practical field test involved compressing compost in the hand: properly prepared material would not easily yield drops of water under pressure, yet the hand would emerge distinctly moistened. This test provided a reliable indicator that could be applied in the field without instrumentation.

### Watering Challenges

Duggar identified watering as one of the chief difficulties in mushroom growing. Amateurs tended toward two opposite errors: some drenched beds with water and destroyed the spawn, while others were afraid to water even when rapid surface drying was evident. When spawn was not watered during surface drying, it would not grow near the bed surface and consequently would not bear mushrooms there. The casing material required careful moistening by repeated sprinkling before application, and subsequent watering demanded light, frequent applications rather than heavy soakings.

The timing of watering was as important as the quantity. Water applied too soon after casing could seal the surface and prevent proper mycelial colonization. Water applied too late, after the surface had dried and cracked, could channel through fissures and pool around spawn, causing local drowning. Duggar recommended using a fine-rose watering can and applying water at approximately 90°F to avoid thermal shock to the mycelium, a technique that also helped reduce the risk of introducing cold-water contamination.

### Ventilation and Moisture Balance

The question of ventilation was inseparable from moisture management. Proper ventilation removed excess carbon dioxide and replenished oxygen, but also accelerated moisture loss from bed surfaces. Duggar advised that ventilation should be regulated to maintain gradual evaporation without allowing the beds to dry out. In poorly ventilated spaces, condensation on walls and ceilings created conditions favorable to mold growth and bacterial contamination. The ideal environment maintained a steady state where the rate of evaporation from the bed surface exactly matched the capacity of the air to carry away moisture without becoming saturated.

## Light Requirements

### Dispelling the Darkness Myth

Many cultivators assumed that light was injurious to mushrooms because they were ordinarily grown in cellars or light-proof houses. Duggar definitively refuted this assumption. In pastures and fields where mushrooms were naturally found, young buttons were exposed to direct sunlight as soon as they emerged through the sod. Light had no directly injurious effect upon the quality or productiveness of cultivated mushrooms.

### The Temperature Confound

The perceived harmfulness of light was actually a temperature effect. Sunlight admitted through cellar windows raised the air temperature appreciably. In cellars with many windows, the temperature could become too high for successful work during bright weather. If temperature could be regulated, light could do no harm. Mushrooms were successfully grown in greenhouses both under benches and on benches, provided proper temperature and moisture conditions were maintained.

### Practical Implications for House Design

This finding had important implications for mushroom house construction. There was no biological need for complete darkness, and well-ventilated, naturally lit spaces could be used successfully if temperature was controlled. The preference for cellars was driven by their natural temperature stability rather than any requirement for darkness. Growers who had access to north-facing rooms, basement areas with windows, or even greenhouse underspaces could cultivate mushrooms successfully provided they managed the temperature. This insight broadened the range of structures that could be adapted for mushroom cultivation beyond the traditional underground cellar.

### Caves as Natural Growing Environments

The Paris cave system, where mushrooms had been cultivated for centuries, represented the extreme of a light-free growing environment. The caves maintained remarkably constant temperature and humidity without artificial intervention, and the complete absence of light was incidental rather than essential. The success of cave cultivation derived from the environmental stability of underground quarries, not from darkness per se. In fact, the cave environment occasionally suffered from the opposite problem: insufficient air circulation in the deepest galleries, which could lead to stagnant conditions and CO2 accumulation that retarded fruiting.

## The Interaction of Environmental Factors

Duggar emphasized that the effects of temperature, moisture, and light could not be evaluated independently. Severe changes in any factor either retarded growth or acted injuriously through their influence on the other factors. A rise in temperature decreased atmospheric humidity, accelerating surface drying. Increased ventilation improved air quality but accelerated moisture loss. The cultivator had to balance these competing demands continuously throughout the growing season. What appeared to be a temperature problem might actually be a ventilation problem, and what seemed like a moisture deficit could be caused by excessive heat driving evaporation beyond what the watering schedule could replace. The skilled cultivator learned to read the bed conditions holistically rather than adjusting single variables in isolation. This integrated understanding of environmental management distinguished the scientific approach from the rule-of-thumb methods that had previously dominated the field.

## Spawn-Run Temperature and Timing

Duggar provided detailed guidance on the timing of spawning relative to bed temperature. Spawning should not be carried out until the bed temperature fell to 70°F to 75°F, and even 75°F was permissible only when the temperature was unquestionably declining. Recent tests indicated that 65°F to 70°F was generally preferable when the grower intended to maintain 55°F in the growing house during the fruiting period. It did no harm to spawn at 50°F to 65°F, but below 50°F growth would be very slow.

Some earlier guides had advised spawning at 85°F to 90°F. Duggar warned emphatically that this temperature was fatal if maintained for any significant time, noting that spawn planted when bed heat was still rising, or while above 100°F, yielded no mushrooms because the mycelium was killed. Beds spawned at temperatures that subsequently peaked at 123°F were completely destroyed. These warnings reflected practical experience that contradicted older written authorities, illustrating the gap between published advice and field reality.

## Species Considerations

Duggar noted that unquestionably there existed species of edible mushrooms which matured readily at temperatures unfavorable for Agaricus campestris—some at higher temperatures, some at lower. Whether any of these species were capable of being grown economically remained an open question. This observation foreshadowed the later diversification of cultivated mushroom species beyond the common button mushroom. The environmental requirements of different Agaricus species varied considerably: A. campestris and A. fabaceus had different needs, as did A. arvensis and A. silvicola, and much experimental work was required before any definite rules could be laid down governing practices with these diverse forms.

## Bed Type and Environmental Considerations

Duggar described two fundamental bed types that influenced environmental management. The flat bed (English type) was more commonly employed in America and required less labor. The ridge bed (French type) was practiced in the relatively low-roofed Paris caves, where it offered distinct advantages including more effective sanitation and slightly increased floor space. However, the ridge bed showed a tendency to run lighter in weight at the beginning and end of the crop compared with the continuous flat bed. The choice between bed types affected how temperature and moisture distributed through the growing medium, with ridge beds offering better drainage but greater surface area exposed to evaporation.

For the flat bed, Duggar recommended a depth of 8 to 10 inches after suitable compression. The bed should be firm enough to support a person without sinking more than 1 or 2 inches. Each ridge bed should be about 20 to 24 inches wide at the base, tapering to a top no more than 6 inches across, and standing 12 to 14 inches high. These dimensions produced a gentle slope from crest to floor that encouraged the formation of heavy clusters of mushrooms. A steeper slope led to smaller mushrooms because of more rapid drying and the tendency of clusters to pull away from their attachment to the spawn below.

## Practical Rules for Environmental Management

Based on his extensive experimentation and field experience, Duggar distilled several practical rules that any cultivator could follow. First, never spawn a bed when the heat is rising or when warmer than 100°F; always spawn when the temperature is declining and under 90°F. Second, maintain the growing house at 54°F to 56°F for best results, extending the acceptable range to 50°F to 65°F if necessary. Third, keep atmospheric humidity between 60 and 75 percent, ensuring gradual surface evaporation without dripping or drying. Fourth, do not fear light, but be aware that sunlight brings heat that must be managed. Fifth, use fresh spawn rather than old, as vitality degrades over time regardless of storage conditions. These rules, grounded in experimental evidence rather than tradition, represented a modernizing influence on the ancient art of mushroom cultivation.
