## AACT Ingredients and Bacterial vs. Fungal Teas

The composition of [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) determines which organisms will proliferate during the brewing process, which in turn determines the tea's suitability for different plant types and growing conditions. Understanding the relationship between tea ingredients and the resulting microbial community is essential for producing effective compost tea.

## Extract vs. AACT

A critical distinction that Ingham emphasizes is the difference between a simple **compost extract** and a true **[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]]**:

- **Compost extract**: Compost mixed with water, with or without minimal aeration. Organisms are simply washed off the compost particles. No multiplication occurs during the process. Useful for quick applications but limited in organism numbers.

- **[[actively-aerated-compost-tea-introduction]] Tea (AACT)**: Compost is placed in a brewing vessel with water, food sources, and continuous aeration. Beneficial aerobic organisms multiply dramatically (10–100× or more) during the 24–48 hour brewing period. The result is a concentrated, diverse community of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes.

AACT produces significantly higher organism biomass and diversity than simple extracts, making it the preferred method for disease suppression and soil biology restoration.

## Worm Leachate

Worm leachate (or vermicompost leachate) is distinct from compost tea. It is the liquid that drains through a worm bin, containing dissolved nutrients and some microorganisms. While it has value as a liquid fertilizer, it should not be confused with AACT:

- Lower organism diversity than properly made AACT
- May contain [[anaerobic-organisms-compost]] if the worm bin has stagnant areas
- Nutrient content is good but biological content is unpredictable
- Best used as a supplement to, not a replacement for, properly brewed AACT

## AACT Core Ingredients

### 1. High-Quality Compost
The compost is the **inoculum**—the source of all the beneficial organisms. Quality is paramount:

- Must be aerobic throughout (no anaerobic pockets)
- Should contain diverse bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]]
- Thermal compost and vermicompost each have different organism profiles
- Must be tested under a microscope before use to confirm organism presence and diversity

### 2. Dechlorinated Water
Chlorine and chloramine in municipal water supplies kill beneficial organisms:
- Let water sit for 24 hours to off-gas chlorine (but not chloramine)
- Use aeration to drive off chlorine faster
- Fish tank dechlorinators or ascorbic acid can neutralize chloramine
- Rainwater, well water, or reverse-osmosis water are ideal alternatives

### 3. Food Sources
Food sources drive organism multiplication during brewing. The **type** of food determines whether the tea will be bacterial- or fungal-dominated.

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Teas

### Bacterial-Dominated Tea

Used for annual crops, vegetables, lawns, and early-succession plant communities. These plants evolved in disturbed environments where bacteria are the primary decomposers.

**Bacterial foods:**
- **Simple sugars**: Molasses (blackstrap, unsulfured), honey
- **Protein meals**: [[fish-hydrolysate]], blood meal, feather meal
- **Plant meals**: Alfalfa meal, soybean meal
- **Carbohydrates**: Malt, corn syrup, fruit juices

Bacterial teas are typically ready in 18–24 hours. The tea should smell sweet or earthy. A sour, putrid, or rotten smell indicates [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] and potential pathogen growth.

### Fungal-Dominated Tea

Used for perennials, trees, shrubs, orchards, and late-succession plant communities. These plants evolved in stable, undisturbed environments where fungi are the primary decomposers and nutrient cyclers.

**Fungal foods:**
- **Humic acids**: Extracted from humus, leonardite, or oxidized lignite
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Particularly fish hydrolysate with high oil content
- **Seaweed/kelp**: Cold-water kelp meal or liquid kelp
- **Rock dust**: Provides minerals and surface area for [[fungal-hyphae]]
- **Plant materials**: Composted leaves, wood chips, straw
- **Molasses**: In limited quantities (excess sugar favors bacteria over fungi)

Fungal teas require longer brewing times (24–48 hours) and more careful management. The tea should smell earthy and slightly mushroomy.

## Detailed Ingredient Profiles

### Cold Water Kelp
- Rich in cytokinins, auxins, and trace minerals
- Contains alginates that benefit both bacteria and fungi
- Cold-water processing preserves heat-sensitive growth hormones
- Usage: 1–2 tablespoons per 5 gallons of tea

### Crab Waste (Granulated)
- High in chitin, which feeds chitin-decomposing bacteria and fungi
- These chitin-decomposers also attack chitin-based pathogens and insect pests
- Provides calcium and other minerals
- Usage: 1–2 tablespoons per 5 gallons

### Flaked Alfalfa Meal
- Contains triacontanol, a natural plant growth regulator
- Rich in protein (nitrogen source for bacterial growth)
- Feeds both bacteria and fungi moderately
- Usage: 1–4 tablespoons per 5 gallons

### Blood Meal
- Very high nitrogen (12–14%)—strong bacterial food
- Use sparingly; excess nitrogen can favor anaerobic conditions
- Usage: ½–1 teaspoon per 5 gallons (less is more)

### Fish Waste (Granulated)
- Excellent bacterial food with balanced nutrients
- Contains amino acids, oils, and minerals
- Supports diverse [[cannabis-endorhiza-bacterial-communities]]
- Usage: 1–2 tablespoons per 5 gallons

### Fish Hydrolysate
- Fish processed with enzymes (not heat) preserving proteins and oils
- Superior to fish emulsion (which is heat-processed)
- Feeds both bacteria and fungi; high oil content favors fungi
- Usage: 1–4 tablespoons per 5 gallons

### Minerals
- Rock dust, Azomite, glacial moraine, or sea minerals
- Provide trace elements needed for microbial metabolism
- Rock dust also provides surface area for fungal attachment
- Usage: 1–4 tablespoons per 5 gallons

## Compost Selection: Thermal vs. Vermicompost

### Thermal Compost
- Made through heat-generating microbial decomposition (55–65°C)
- Bacterial diversity is typically very high
- Fungal diversity depends on the starting materials (more woody = more fungi)
- [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] may be reduced by high temperatures
- Best for bacterial-dominated teas

### Vermicompost (Worm Compost)
- Produced by earthworms at ambient temperatures
- Higher fungal diversity than thermal compost
- Contains more protozoa and nematodes (worms don't kill them)
- Contains humic acids and plant growth factors from worm gut processing
- Excellent for fungal teas and general-purpose teas

### Worm Castings vs. Worm Compost
- **Worm castings**: Pure material passed through worm gut; concentrated biology and nutrients
- **Worm compost**: Mixture of castings and partially decomposed organic matter; more diverse food resources for organisms during brewing

## Variable Conditions Affect Results

AACT brewing results vary based on:
- **Water temperature**: Optimal range 18–25°C (65–77°F). Too cold slows growth; too hot selects for thermophiles and may kill beneficial organisms.
- **Brewing time**: Bacterial teas 18–24 hours; fungal teas 24–48 hours. Over-brewing leads to organism die-off and anaerobic conditions.
- **Aeration**: Must be sufficient to maintain dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brew.
- **Food concentration**: Too little food limits growth; too much food can cause oxygen depletion and anaerobic conditions.
- **Compost quality**: The single most important variable—garbage in, garbage out.
