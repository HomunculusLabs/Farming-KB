# Heavy Metal Fungal Interactions

Fungi interact with heavy metals through a remarkable range of mechanisms including biosorption, bioaccumulation, sequestration, transformation, and detoxification. These interactions have profound ecological significance [[singh-fungal-biofilms-in-contaminated-environments]] and offer promising avenues for bioremediation of metal-polluted soils and waters. The capacity of fungi to tolerate, accumulate, and sometimes transform toxic metals is mediated by both physiological adaptations and community-level processes.

## Heavy Metal Tolerance in Mycorrhizal Fungi

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] can confer significant heavy metal tolerance to their host plants, a phenomenon with major implications for the restoration of contaminated sites. Bradley et al. (1981, 1982) provided early evidence that mycorrhizal infection in *Calluna vulgaris* (heather) provided resistance to [[heavy-metal-toxicity-and-biological-element-transport]], and that this amelioration was due to the fungal symbiont rather than the plant itself.

Colpaert and Van Assche (1992) demonstrated zinc toxicity effects on ectomycorrhizal *Pinus sylvestris*, and Colpaert and Van Assche (1993) studied cadmium effects, finding that ectomycorrhizal fungi could protect their host from metal toxicity through mechanisms including sequestration within the fungal tissue. Colpaert and Van Tichelen (1996) reviewed mycorrhizas and environmental stress more broadly.

Hartley et al. (1997a) asked whether ectomycorrhizal fungi exhibit adaptive tolerance to potentially toxic metals in the environment, finding evidence that populations from contaminated sites had evolved enhanced tolerance. Hartley et al. (1997b) studied toxic interactions of metal ions (Cd²⁺, Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺, and Sb³⁺) on in vitro biomass production of ectomycorrhizal fungi, revealing complex multi-metal interactions. Hartley et al. (1999a) demonstrated cross-colonization of Scots pine by the ectomycorrhizal fungus *[[paxillus-involutus]]* in the presence of inhibitory levels of cadmium and zinc.

Hartley-Whitaker et al. (2000a, 2000b) investigated the sensitivity to cadmium and zinc of both host and symbiont in ectomycorrhizal Scots pine, finding that the host, the fungus, and the symbiosis as a whole each had different tolerance thresholds. This work highlighted the importance of considering the mycorrhizal partnership as a functional unit rather than studying host and fungus in isolation.

Tam (1995) studied heavy metal tolerance by ectomycorrhizal fungi and metal amelioration by *Pisolithus tinctorius*, one of the most widely studied ECM species for metal tolerance. Turnau et al. (1993) described *Paxillus involutus*–*Pinus sylvestris* mycorrhizae from heavily polluted forests, documenting naturally occurring metal-tolerant mycorrhizal associations. Turnau et al. (1994) studied element distribution in *Pisolithus tinctorius* mycelium treated with cadmium dust, showing where metals accumulate within fungal tissues.

Jones and Hutchinson (1986) demonstrated that mycorrhizal infection modified the response of paper birch to nickel and copper, and Jones et al. (1988) studied the effects of infection by *Lactarius rufus* or *Scleroderma flavidum* on nickel uptake by paper birch. Hashem (1995) found that mycorrhizal infection contributed to resistance of cranberry (*Vaccinium macrocarpon*) to manganese toxicity.

## Biosorption of Metals by Fungal Biomass

Fungal biomass can adsorb metal ions from solution through a process called biosorption, which involves binding to cell wall components including chitin, glucans, and proteins. This process has attracted significant interest for bioremediation applications.

Rizzo et al. (1992) studied biosorption of metals by *Armillaria* rhizomorphs, the rope-like structures that allow this pathogenic and saprotrophic fungus to spread through soil. They found that rhizomorphs were effective at adsorbing metals from solution. Tobin et al. (1984) measured uptake of metal ions by *Rhizopus arrhizus* biomass, demonstrating the general applicability of fungal biosorption across diverse fungal taxa.

Singleton and Tobin (1996) reviewed fungal interactions with metals and radionuclides for environmental bioremediation, synthesizing the growing body of evidence that fungi could be deployed for cleanup of contaminated sites. Morley et al. (1996) reviewed fungal sequestration, mobilization, and transformation of metals and metalloids, providing a comprehensive framework for understanding the diverse ways fungi interact with metals.

White and Gadd (1990) demonstrated biosorption of radionuclides by fungal biomass, extending the biosorption concept to radioactive contaminants. Starling and Ross (1991) studied uptake of zinc by *Penicillium notatum*, providing species-specific biosorption data.

## Heavy Metal Effects on Soil Fungal Communities

Heavy metal contamination from smelters, mining, and industrial processes dramatically alters soil fungal communities. Rühling et al. (1984) documented fungi in metal-contaminated soil near the Gusum brass mill in Sweden, showing reduced species diversity and altered community composition. Fritze et al. (1989) measured soil microbial effects of a copper-nickel smelter in southwestern Finland, finding significant negative effects on fungal biomass and activity.

Nordgren et al. (1986) compared soil microbial activity, mycelial lengths, and physiological groups of bacteria in a heavy metal polluted area, finding that fungal hyphal length was particularly sensitive to metal contamination. Jordan and Lechevalier (1975) had earlier demonstrated effects of zinc smelter emissions on forest soil microflora.

Rühling and Söderström (1990) documented changes in fruit body production of mycorrhizal and litter-decomposing macromycetes in heavy metal polluted coniferous forests in northern Sweden, showing that aboveground fungal communities were also affected by soil contamination. Kuperman and Carreiro (1997) measured soil heavy metal concentrations, microbial biomass, and enzyme activities in a contaminated grassland ecosystem, finding correlations between metal levels and reduced microbial function.

Bardgett et al. (1994) assessed the impact of pasture contamination by copper, chromium, and arsenic timber preservative on soil microbial properties and nematodes, demonstrating that organic contaminants containing heavy metals also affect fungal communities.

## Metal Mobility and Soil Chemistry

Fungi can alter the mobility and bioavailability of heavy metals in soil through various mechanisms including acidification, chelation, and mineral weathering. Tyler et al. (1987) studied soil acidification and metal solubility in forests of southern Sweden, showing that anthropogenic acidification increases the availability of toxic metals to soil organisms.

Berthelin et al. (1995) reviewed the effects of microorganisms on mobility of heavy metals in soils, including fungal contributions through [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]] [[fungal-oxalate-secretion-and-mineral-dissolution]]. Dighton and Terry (1996) noted that fungal uptake and immobilization of metals can reduce metal mobility and bioavailability in soil.

Newell and Wall (1998) studied the response of saltmarsh fungi to the presence of mercury and polychlorinated biphenyls at a Superfund site, extending metal-fungal interaction studies to coastal wetland ecosystems. Necker and Kunze (1986) conducted incubation experiments on nitrogen mineralization by fungi and bacteria in metal-amended soil, finding that metal contamination disrupts fundamental nutrient cycling processes.

## Arsenic Accumulation and Speciation

Arsenic represents a special case in metalloid-fungal interactions due to its complex chemistry and speciation. Byrne and Tusek-Znidaric (1983) documented arsenic accumulation in the mushroom *Laccaria amethystina*, finding remarkably high concentrations. Byrne et al. (1979) had earlier demonstrated silver accumulation by fungi, showing that metalloid and metal accumulation are widespread fungal capabilities.

Byrne et al. (1995, 1997) identified arsenic compounds in mushrooms and provided evidence for mycelial methylation, showing that fungi can transform inorganic arsenic into organoarsenic species including arsenobetaine. Slejkovec et al. (1997) further characterized arsenic compounds in higher fungi, revealing a diverse array of arsenic species produced by fungal metabolism.

Fischer et al. (1995) studied bioaccumulation of methylmercury and transformation of inorganic mercury by macrofungi, demonstrating that fungi can both accumulate and transform toxic metal species, sometimes producing more toxic methylated forms.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Metal-Contaminated Soils

The response of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi to heavy metal contamination differs from that of ectomycorrhizal fungi but is equally important. Joner and Leyval (1997) measured uptake of ¹⁰⁹Cd by roots and hyphae of a *Glomus mosseae*/*Trifolium subterraneum* mycorrhiza from soil amended with cadmium, finding that AM hyphae could take up cadmium and potentially shield the host from exposure.

Tonin et al. (2001) assessed [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]] in the rhizosphere of zinc violet (*Viola calaminaria*) and the effect of these fungi on heavy metal uptake by clover, finding that AM fungi from metal-contaminated sites had distinct community composition. Weissenhorn et al. (1995a, 1995b) studied the bioavailability of heavy metals and arbuscular mycorrhiza in soils polluted by atmospheric deposition from a smelter and in sewage sludge-amended soils.

Koomen and McGrath (1990) found that mycorrhizal infection of clover was delayed in soils contaminated with heavy metals from past sewage sludge applications, suggesting that metal contamination can disrupt the normal development of mycorrhizal symbioses. Leyval et al. (1997) provided a comprehensive review of the effects of heavy metal pollution on mycorrhizal colonization and function, covering physiological, ecological, and applied aspects.

Vodnik et al. (1998) studied the uptake and transport of lead in some ectomycorrhizal fungi in culture, providing in vitro evidence for metal transport through fungal hyphae. Blaudez et al. (2000) demonstrated differential responses of ectomycorrhizal fungi to heavy metals in vitro, showing that species vary enormously in their metal tolerance.

## Metal-Containing Soil Amendments and Fungi

Dixon-Hardy et al. (1998) investigated the influence of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus source on the solubilization of insoluble metal compounds by *Aspergillus niger*, showing that fungal metabolism can mobilize metals from insoluble forms. Cromack et al. (1979) documented calcium oxalate accumulation and soil weathering in mats of the hypogeous fungus *Hysterangium crassum*, demonstrating how fungal activity can physically and chemically alter soil mineralogy.

## Bioremediation Applications

The ability of fungi to interact with heavy metals has been harnessed for bioremediation purposes. Donnelley and Fletcher (1994) discussed the potential use of mycorrhizal fungi as bioremediation agents. Skladany and Metting (1992) reviewed bioremediation of contaminated soil more broadly. Senior et al. (1993) and Tosh et al. (1993) developed criteria for ectomycorrhizal selection in landfill site reclamation programs, recognizing the role of mycorrhizal fungi in restoring contaminated land.

Martino et al. (2000a, 2000b) studied [[singh-ericoid-mycorrhizal-fungi-remediation]] from heavy metal polluted soils, finding that these fungi could grow in the presence of zinc and that heavy metals influenced their production of pectolytic enzymes. Marx (1975, 1980) pioneered the use of mycorrhizal fungi in establishing trees on strip-mined land, one of the earliest applications of mycorrhizal technology for site restoration.
