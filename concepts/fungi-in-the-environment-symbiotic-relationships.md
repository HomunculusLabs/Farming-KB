# Symbiotic Relationships of Fungi

Fungi form some of the most important and widespread [[fungal-moisture-relationships]] in nature. Their ability to form mutualistic partnerships with plants, algae, cyanobacteria, and even other fungi underpins many ecosystem processes. These [[symbiosis-art-of-living]] range from intimate cellular associations to loose ecological partnerships and play crucial roles in nutrient cycling, soil formation, and ecosystem productivity.

## Overview of Fungal Symbioses

One of the most remarkable adaptations of fungi for exploitation of soil and rock environments is their ability to form mutualistic partnerships with land plants ([[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]) and algae or cyanobacteria ([[lichens]]). In these symbioses, the fungal partner is provided with carbon by photosynthetic partners while the fungi protect the association from harsh environmental conditions, increase the absorptive area, and provide increased access to mineral nutrients.

## Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

### The Mutualistic Exchange

The [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] is characterized by a reciprocal exchange: plants provide carbohydrates (up to 30% of net photosynthate in forest ecosystems) to the fungal partner, while the fungus supplies soil nutrients, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, that would otherwise be inaccessible to the plant. This mutualism is one of the most widespread and ecologically important symbioses on Earth, involving approximately 80-90% of all terrestrial plant species.

### Ectomycorrhizal Symbiosis

[[symbiotic-fungi-ectomycorrhizal-network-carbon-trading]] form sheaths around root tips and Hartig nets between cortical cells. They are particularly important in forest ecosystems, especially boreal and temperate forests dominated by Pinaceae, Fagaceae, and Betulaceae. EM fungi:
- Enhance mineral weathering and nutrient acquisition
- Provide protection against root pathogens
- Connect individual trees through [[mycorrhizal-networks-common-mycelial-network]]
- Contribute significantly to soil carbon storage through production of recalcitrant mycelial compounds

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

[[symbiotic-fungi-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-mechanisms]] form arbuscules within root cortical cells -- finely branched hyphal structures that are the primary sites of nutrient exchange. The AM symbiosis is ancient, dating back over 400 million years. AM fungi:
- Are obligate biotrophs, entirely dependent on their plant host for carbon
- Form associations with the majority of herbaceous plants and many tropical trees
- Play key roles in phosphorus uptake and plant community diversity
- Influence soil aggregation through glomalin production

### Ericoid Mycorrhizas

[[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-and-mycorrhizal-relationships]] associate with plants in the Ericaceae family, which dominate heathland and boreal ecosystems. These fungi are particularly important for nitrogen acquisition from organic sources and enable their host plants to thrive in nutrient-poor, acidic soils.

### Orchid Mycorrhizas

[[orchid-mycorrhiza-protocorm-development]] are essential for orchid seed germination and early development. Some orchids remain entirely dependent on their fungal partners for carbon throughout their lives (mycoheterotrophic orchids). Certain fungi can form associations with both wood-decay and orchid mycorrhizal lifestyles, including Armillaria species and Mycena osmundicola.

## Lichen Symbiosis

lichens|Lichen-forming fungi are nutritional specialists that derive fixed carbon from populations of living cyanobacteria and/or green algal cells. Every fifth fungus (approximately 14,000 species), or every second ascomycete, is a lichen. The lichen symbiosis is one of the most successful means for fungi to survive in extreme sub-aerial environments.

In the lichen partnership, the fungal partner (mycobiont) builds the thallus structure -- a sophisticated culturing chamber for photobiont cells. Approximately 85% of lichen-forming ascomycetes have green algal photobionts (chlorolichens), 10% have cyanobacterial photobionts (cyanolichens), and about 4% have both simultaneously.

### Triple Symbioses

In cephalodiate lichens, the fungal partner creates microaerobic conditions around cyanobacterial colonies, resulting in increased heterocyst frequencies and thus enhanced nitrogen fixation compared with the free-living state. These [[plant-cyanobacteria-symbiosis]] are important contributors to nitrogen input in many ecosystems.

## Fungal-Fungal Interactions

### Mycoheterotrophy

Some plants exploit mycorrhizal networks to obtain carbon from other plants -- a phenomenon called mycoheterotrophy or "carbon stealing." These [[mycoheterotrophic-plants-mycorrhizal-carbon-theft]] connect to common mycorrhizal networks and draw carbohydrates away from photosynthetic plants. This occurs to varying degrees in natural ecosystems and has important implications for plant community composition.

### Antagonistic Interactions

[[fungal-competition-antagonism-soil]] in soil and on wood resources involves dramatic interactions including combative zones, pigment production, and territorial behaviour. Interactions can result in deadlock (both species maintain territories), replacement (one species overruns the other), or mutual replacement. These competitive interactions influence decomposition rates and fungal community structure.

## Insect-Fungal Symbioses

[[ant-termite-fungal-symbiosis]] (leaf-cutter ants, termites, beetles) cultivate fungi for food. These mutualisms represent some of the most complex symbiotic systems in nature, involving co-evolved partnerships where insects provide substrate and protection while fungi provide enzymes for digesting plant material.

## Farming Relevance

Understanding fungal symbioses is essential for sustainable farming:
- [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-methods-agriculture]] improves crop establishment and nutrient uptake
- [[mycorrhizal-networks-common-mycelial-network]] facilitate nutrient sharing between plants
- [[lichen-ecology-and-soil-formation]] contribute to soil crust formation and nitrogen fixation in arid lands
- Managing for beneficial [[fungal-symbiosis-with-plants-beyond-mycorrhizae]] reduces input requirements
- Endophytic fungal associations can enhance crop stress tolerance and disease resistance
- Fungus-growing insect symbioses demonstrate the potential for fungal enzymes in feed and biofuel applications

## Evolutionary Perspectives on Fungal Symbiosis

The transition from aquatic to terrestrial life likely depended on fungal symbiotic partnerships. Fossil evidence suggests that early land plants were already associated with arbuscular mycorrhizal-like fungi over 400 million years ago. The lichen symbiosis may be equally ancient, with lichen-like structures found in early terrestrial fossils. The evolution of these partnerships has been a driving force in the colonization of land by both plants and fungi.

## Cost-Benefit Dynamics of Symbiosis

While fungal symbioses are often described as mutualistic, the relationship is better understood as a continuum from mutualism through commensalism to parasitism, with the outcome depending on environmental conditions:

- **Carbon drain**: Mycorrhizal fungi can demand up to 30% of host plant photosynthate, representing a significant carbon cost that may reduce growth under certain conditions
- **Conditional mutualism**: The net benefit to the host depends on nutrient availability, light conditions, and community context
- **Sanction and reward**: Host plants may preferentially allocate carbon to more cooperative fungal partners while sanctioning less beneficial ones
- **Cheating**: Some mycorrhizal fungi receive carbon without providing proportional nutrient benefits, particularly in nutrient-rich environments

Understanding these dynamics is important for predicting how symbioses will function under changing environmental conditions, including elevated CO2 and nitrogen deposition.
