# Mushroom Gill Anatomy, Spore Discharge, and Buller's Drop

The gilled mushroom represents one of the most elegant solutions to the
problem of spore dispersal in the fungal kingdom. Beneath the cap of a
typical agaric, thin parallel plates called gills (or lamellae) radiate
from the central stem, each covered on both surfaces with a microscopic
spore-producing layer called the hymenium. The mechanics of spore
discharge from these gills involve a remarkably precise interaction
between fluid physics, surface tension, and cellular architecture that
has fascinated mycologists for over a century.

## Hymenium and Basidia

The spore-producing surface of a gilled mushroom is called the hymenium.
This tightly packed mat covers the gills and consists primarily of
basidia, the spore-bearing cells that define the Basidiomycota. Basidia
appear as projections from the hymenium, shaped like miniature
four-pronged crowns, each bearing a single basidiospore on its spike.
One after the other, the four spores on each basidium are shot into the
air. The mechanism that catapults spores from the hymenium was solved
by A. H. R. Buller in the early twentieth century.

## Buller's Drop Mechanism

The spore discharge mechanism depends upon the condensation of a tiny
droplet of water, called Buller's drop, at the base of each spore. As
the droplet expands, it contacts a second drop on the adjacent surface
of the spore. When the two drops merge, the sudden redistribution of
water creates a microscopic catapult that flings the spore from the
gill surface. The spore accelerates so fast that it pulls thousands of
g's when launched. The entire discharge event occurs in a millionth of
a second.

The fluid between the gills is saturated with water vapor that
evaporates from the mushroom tissues. Water condenses on the spore
surface because the spore is hygroscopic, meaning its sugary surface
grabs water molecules from the surrounding air. The spore is comparable
to a cube of bath salts in a steamy bathroom: steam fills the room when
the shower is running, and water vapor condenses into liquid when it
contacts the cube.

## Evaporative Cooling of Mushrooms

Contrary to the expectation that mushrooms might be warmer than the air,
measurements with thermocouples inserted between gills revealed that
mushrooms are actually colder than the surrounding atmosphere during
periods of spore release. Cooling is most extreme on warm days, when
gills can chill as much as 5 degrees Celsius below ambient temperature.
This evaporative cooling is stimulated by the passage of air around the
fruiting body, analogous to the chill experienced after swimming.

Mushroom cooling is essential for spore discharge because it promotes
the buildup of water on the spore surface by slowing the movement of
water molecules, encouraging them to occupy a more condensed liquid
state rather than remaining as water vapor. If the mushroom warmed,
even slightly, water would evaporate from the spore surface, Buller's
drops would never expand, and the spores would remain trapped on the
gills.

The cooling phenomenon was first recognized historically: John Gerard
noted in The Herball or Generall Historie of Plantes (1636) that
mushrooms "are all very cold and moist." Richard Falck had reported in
the early 1900s that some mushrooms were up to 9 degrees warmer than
the air, but this was likely due to decomposition of mushrooms he had
plucked and brought to the laboratory. You can test fungal frigidity
yourself: pluck a fresh-looking fruiting body and press its cap against
your cheek. Xerula radicata, the rooting shank, can feel positively
frigid on a warm summer day.

## Gill Spacing and Architecture

The gills of a mushroom are spaced with extraordinary precision. In the
fly agaric (Amanita muscaria), hundreds of white gills are spaced as
evenly as spokes on a bicycle wheel and all of their edges hang flush
with the bottom of the cap. Small errors in the vertical orientation of
the gills or in their spacing would prevent spore dispersal, because
spores shot at the wrong angle would impact on an adjacent gill rather
than falling freely through the air spaces between them.

The catapult mechanism does not impart enough momentum to propel spores
onto an adjacent gill or spine, or onto the opposite side of a tube.
This precision is the result of the fluid physics of the discharge: the
spore turns abruptly and accelerates straight down between the gills
after the initial launch, traveling only a short distance so that it
does not hit the neighboring gill.

## Aerodynamics of the Mushroom Cap

The mushroom stem supports the cap above the boundary layer of still air
on the ground, so that released spores will be swept away by wind. But
if spores were exposed to wind immediately after falling from the cap,
many would be blown straight back into the cap and become stuck on the
gills. Wind tunnel experiments have shown that when mushrooms are
exposed to wind, the airstream divides at the leading edge of the cap
and accelerates above and below, producing lift much like an aircraft
wing.

This interruption of airflow also slows air movement immediately beneath
the fertile tissues, so that spores fall through calm air for a few
tenths of a second before encountering wind. This brief period of calm
reduces the number of spores blown back into the cap and promotes
effective dispersal.

Mushrooms with campanulate or bell-shaped caps are particularly
effective at slowing airflow. Coprinus comatus, the lawyer's wig or
shaggy mane, is an impressive example. Young specimens are white and
stand taller than stinkhorns, resembling ballistic missiles. As the
mushroom ages, it blackens at the bottom edges of its gills and the
elegant bell begins to resemble a ragged flag.

## The Umbrella Form

The ubiquitous umbrella form of the mushroom serves multiple functions.
It maintains a humid atmosphere around the hymenium, which is essential
for Buller's drop mechanism to operate. It shields the spores from rain:
any fluid running over the gills would wash away the fluid drops that
develop on the spores and spoil the discharge mechanism. And it supports
the gills in a geometry that provides unimpeded downward pathways for
the free fall of discharged spores.

## Historical Research

A. H. R. Buller's seven-volume Researches on Fungi (1909-1934) remains
the foundational work on spore discharge mechanisms. Buller described
the phenomenon of mushroom heating reported by Richard Falck and
contested Falck's conclusions about convection currents assisting spore
dispal. Modern temperature measurements confirmed that mushrooms cool
rather than warm during spore release, vindicating the evaporative
cooling model. Buller's work on the drop mechanism itself, observed
through the then-novel technique of high-speed microscopy, established
the principles that govern ballistospore discharge in all basidiomycete
fungi.
