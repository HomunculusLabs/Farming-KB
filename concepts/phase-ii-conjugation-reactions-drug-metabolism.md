## Overview

Phase II metabolism (conjugation reactions) encompasses enzymatic processes that attach hydrophilic endogenous molecules to drugs, xenobiotics, or Phase I metabolites, dramatically increasing water solubility and promoting elimination via urine or bile. These reactions are catalyzed primarily by transferase enzymes that move a functional group from an activated cofactor to the substrate. Phase II occurs predominantly in hepatocytes but also takes place in kidney, intestine, lung, brain, skin, and placenta.

Unlike Phase I (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis), Phase II reactions are generally considered detoxification steps, though important exceptions exist where conjugation creates reactive or more toxic species. Some drugs with native hydroxyl, amine, carboxyl, or thiol groups undergo direct Phase II conjugation without any Phase I modification — morphine, acetaminophen, and lorazepam are classic examples.

Phase I and Phase II are not strictly sequential. Some drugs undergo direct Phase II without Phase I; others undergo Phase II followed by Phase I; and the relative contribution of each pathway varies with dose, age, genetics, and disease state. The interplay between these phases is central to understanding drug clearance, toxicity, and interindividual variability in drug response.

## Major Enzyme Families

### UDP-Glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs)

UGTs transfer glucuronic acid from UDP-glucuronic acid (UDPGA) to nucleophilic functional groups, forming a β-glycosidic bond (β-D-glucuronide). O-glucuronidation at phenols, alcohols, and carboxylic acids is most common; N-glucuronidation occurs with amines and amides; S-glucuronidation with thiols. C-glucuronidation at activated carbon atoms is rare. The reaction increases molecular weight by approximately 176 Da and markedly increases hydrophilicity.

The UGT superfamily comprises four families. The **UGT1 family** (UGT1A1, UGT1A3, UGT1A4, UGT1A6, UGT1A9, UGT1A10) resides on chromosome 2q37 and is generated by alternative splicing of a single gene locus with shared exons 2-5 and unique first exons. The **UGT2 family** (UGT2B4, UGT2B7, UGT2B10, UGT2B15, UGT2B17) is on chromosome 4q13 with each isoform encoded by a separate gene. UGT3 (using UDP-N-acetylglucosamine) and UGT8 (galactose, sphingolipid metabolism) have minor roles in [[cytochrome-p450-enzymes-and-xenobiotic-metabolism]].

Key isoforms and their clinical relevance:

- **UGT1A1** — Bilirubin glucuronidation, SN-38 (irinotecan active metabolite), ethinyl estradiol, raloxifene. The UGT1A1*28 polymorphism (TA₇ promoter repeat, ~40-50% of Caucasians carry at least one allele) reduces expression by 30-50%. Homozygotes face increased risk of severe neutropenia with irinotecan; FDA recommends dose reduction. Gilbert syndrome (TA₇/TA₇) causes mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia (~5% of population). Crigler-Najjar type I represents complete deficiency (fatal without liver transplant); type II is partial (~10% activity, responds to phenobarbital).
- **UGT2B7** — Morphine to morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G, 40-100× more potent than morphine at μ-opioid receptor) and morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G, neuroexcitatory). Also metabolizes codeine, buprenorphine, zidovudine, lorazepam, and diclofenac. The most important UGT for opioid metabolism.
- **UGT1A4** — Lamotrigine, olanzapine, trifluoperazine, midazolam. Valproic acid inhibits this isoform, doubling lamotrigine levels and increasing Stevens-Johnson syndrome risk.
- **UGT1A6** — Acetaminophen, serotonin, 4-nitrophenol, NSAIDs.
- **UGT1A9** — Propofol, mycophenolic acid, furosemide, carvedilol.
- **UGT2B15** — S-oxazepam, lorazepam, testosterone, androstane diol. D85Y polymorphism affects steroid metabolism.
- **UGT2B17** — Testosterone, androgens. Gene deletion polymorphism common in Asians (~70%).

Neonatal UGT1A1 activity is approximately 1% of adult levels at birth, maturing over 3-4 months. This immaturity causes physiologic jaundice and explains chloramphenicol toxicity in newborns (gray baby syndrome). Cats possess a defective UGT1A6 gene, rendering them exquisitely sensitive to acetaminophen and [[ingham-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds]] — a critical veterinary toxicology consideration.

### Sulfotransferases (SULTs)

SULTs transfer a sulfonyl group (SO₃⁻) from 3'-phosphoadenosine-5'-phosphosulfate (PAPS) to hydroxyl or amine groups, forming sulfate esters or sulfamates. The reaction proceeds through SN2 displacement at sulfur. Sulfation is saturable at low substrate concentrations; when overwhelmed, metabolism shifts to glucuronidation or Phase I oxidation. Unlike glucuronidation, sulfation is sometimes reversible due to tissue arylsulfatases.

PAPS is synthesized from ATP and inorganic sulfate in two steps: ATP sulfurylase (ATP + SO₄²⁻ → APS + PPi) followed by APS kinase (APS + ATP → PAPS + ADP). Dietary inorganic sulfate and cysteine catabolism supply the sulfate pool. PAPS is primarily synthesized in the liver and exported to other tissues.

Human SULTs comprise two families. **SULT1** (phenol sulfotransferases) includes SULT1A1 (major hepatic phenol SULT; acetaminophen, estradiol, iodothyronines, minoxidil), SULT1A3 (monoamine-preferring; dopamine, norepinephrine; primarily intestinal), SULT1B1 (thyroid hormone sulfation), and SULT1E1 (estrogen sulfotransferase; critical hormone regulator, [[blesching-cannabis-breast-cancer]] relevance). **SULT2** (hydroxysteroid sulfotransferases) includes SULT2A1 (DHEA sulfation, bile acids, androgens, progestins) and SULT2B1 (cholesterol sulfation in brain and skin, with two isoforms SULT2B1a and SULT2B1b).

For acetaminophen specifically, sulfation predominates at therapeutic doses (40-60%), with glucuronidation accounting for 30-40%. As dose increases, both saturate and CYP2E1-mediated oxidation to NAPQI increases, producing hepatotoxicity when glutathione is depleted. This dose-dependent shift from Phase II to Phase I is a paradigmatic example of metabolic saturation leading to toxicity.

### Glutathione S-Transferases (GSTs)

GSTs catalyze nucleophilic attack of the glutathione thiolate anion (GSH) on electrophilic centers, forming glutathione conjugates (GS-X). GSTs activate GSH by lowering its pKa from ~9.2 to ~6.5, generating the reactive thiolate at physiological pH. Downstream, γ-glutamyltranspeptidase removes the glutamyl residue, dipeptidase removes glycine, and N-acetyltransferase acetylates the cysteine to form mercapturic acids (N-acetylcysteine conjugates) excreted in urine. This multi-step pathway is called the mercapturic acid pathway. GS-X conjugates are exported from cells by MRP (multidrug resistance-associated protein) transporters.

Human cytosolic GSTs include alpha (GSTA1-5, liver and intestine), mu (GSTM1-5), pi (GSTP1), theta (GSTT1-2), omega (GSTO1-2, thioltransferase activity), sigma (GSTS1, prostaglandin synthase), and zeta (GSTZ1, maleylacetoacetate isomerase in tyrosine catabolism) classes. Microsomal GSTs (MGST1-3) and mitochondrial GSTK1 also contribute.

The **GSTM1 null genotype** (gene deletion, ~50% of Caucasians, ~60% Asians) and **GSTT1 null genotype** (~20% of Caucasians) alter cancer susceptibility and drug response. **GSTP1 Ile105Val** (~50% heterozygous or homozygous for Val) affects cisplatin resistance and cyclophosphamide bioactivation. GSTO1 polymorphisms influence arsenic metabolism.

GSTs are critical in acetaminophen overdose — GSH conjugation of NAPQI is the primary detoxification route. N-acetylcysteine therapy works by replenishing GSH stores. GSTM1 and GSTT1 also detoxify aflatoxin B₁ epoxides and benzo[a]pyrene diol epoxides, linking these enzymes to [[cancer-prevention-dietary-mushroom-intake]] in exposed populations.

### N-Acetyltransferases (NATs)

NATs transfer an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to aromatic amines and hydrazines. Humans have two functional genes: **NAT1** (8p22, widely expressed in liver, bladder, breast, lung, colon, placenta, leukocytes; relatively monomorphic) and **NAT2** (8p22, hepatic and intestinal, highly polymorphic). Both N-acetylation (arylamine → arylacetamide) and O-acetylation (arylhydroxylamine → N-acetoxyarylamine) are catalyzed.

NAT2 polymorphism defines the acetylator phenotype. Slow acetylators (NAT2*5/341T>C, *6/590G>A, *7/857G>A alleles) comprise 40-70% of populations depending on ethnicity (50-60% Northern Europeans, 10-20% Asians, 70-80% North Africans, 5-10% Inuit). Clinical consequences include: increased isoniazid hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy in slow acetylators; higher incidence of procainamide-induced lupus erythematosus (the parent drug causes lupus, while N-acetylprocainamide does not); elevated dapsone methemoglobinemia and hemolysis risk; and altered hydralazine and sulfasalazine metabolism.

NAT2 acetylator status also modulates cancer risk differentially: slow acetylators have increased bladder cancer risk (decreased detoxification of aromatic amine bladder carcinogens) but decreased colorectal cancer risk. The NAT1*10 (R64Q) allele shows slightly increased activity and has been associated with elevated colorectal and lung cancer risk.

Critically, O-acetylation of N-hydroxylarylamines by NATs produces highly reactive N-acetoxyarylamines that form DNA adducts — this is a bioactivation pathway contributing to aromatic amine carcinogenesis from industrial chemicals and heterocyclic amines in cooked meat.

### Methyltransferases

Methyltransferases transfer a methyl group from S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) to substrate oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur atoms, producing S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). Key enzymes include:

- **COMT (Catechol-O-methyltransferase, chromosome 22q11.21)** — Inactivates catecholamines and catechol drugs by methylating the meta-hydroxyl group. Two isoforms from alternative promoters: soluble (S-COMT, predominates in most tissues) and membrane-bound (MB-COMT). The Val158Met polymorphism (rs4680) produces three activity phenotypes: Val/Val (high), Val/Met (intermediate), Met/Met (low, 3-4 fold reduction with thermal instability). Met/Met is associated with better prefrontal cognitive function but increased anxiety, pain sensitivity, and altered L-DOPA pharmacokinetics. COMT inhibitors (entacapone, tolcapone, opicapone) are used as L-DOPA adjuncts in Parkinson's disease.
- **TPMT (Thiopurine S-methyltransferase, chromosome 6p22.3)** — Inactivates 6-mercaptopurine and azathioprine by S-methylation, competing with the HGPRT activation pathway to cytotoxic thioguanine nucleotides. ~89% of Caucasians have high activity, ~11% intermediate, ~0.3% deficient (autosomal codominant). Key alleles: TPMT*3A (G460A + A719G, most common deficient allele), TPMT*3C (A719G, common in African and Asian populations). TPMT-deficient patients accumulate cytotoxic thioguanine nucleotides causing fatal myelosuppression. FDA/CPIC recommend genotyping or phenotyping before therapy; deficient patients receive 10% of standard dose.
- **HNMT (Histamine N-methyltransferase)** — Inactivates histamine in liver, kidney, bronchi, and CNS. Thr105Ile polymorphism affects activity and may influence asthma susceptibility.
- **INMT (Indolethylamine N-methyltransferase)** — N-methylates tryptamine and related compounds; may participate in endogenous DMT biosynthesis.
- **NNMT (Nicotinamide N-methyltransferase)** — Methylates nicotinamide and pyridines; emerging roles in metabolic disease and cancer.

### Amino Acid Conjugation

Amino acid conjugation proceeds in two steps: acyl-CoA synthetase activates the carboxylic acid substrate (ATP-dependent: R-COOH + ATP + CoA-SH → R-CO-SCoA + AMP + PPi), then N-acyltransferase transfers the acyl group to the amino acid (R-CO-SCoA + H₂N-CHR'-COOH → R-CO-NH-CHR'-COOH + CoA-SH). Humans primarily use glycine (via GLYAT on chromosome 11q12.1) and glutamine. Benzoic acid forms hippuric acid; salicylic acid forms salicyluric acid; phenylacetic acid forms phenylacetylglutamine. Sodium phenylbutyrate (used in urea cycle disorders) is metabolized via glutamine conjugation to enable nitrogen excretion.

Species differences are pronounced: cats conjugate bile acids exclusively with taurine via BAAT (chromosome 9q22.3); humans use glycine (~75%) and taurine (~25%); pigeons uniquely use ornithine. Dogs, rabbits, and some primates use both glycine and glutamine. These differences significantly complicate preclinical toxicology extrapolation.

## Phase II in Detoxification vs. Bioactivation

Most Phase II reactions are detoxifying — increasing water solubility, masking reactive functional groups, and enabling elimination. However, several important bioactivation pathways exist where conjugation creates more toxic or reactive species:

- **Acyl glucuronides** (diclofenac, valproic acid, zomepirac) undergo intramolecular acyl migration from the 1-O-β position to 2-, 3-, or 4-O-iso positions, then covalently bind to cellular proteins, triggering immune-mediated hepatotoxicity.
- **Morphine-6-glucuronide** is 40-100× more potent than morphine at μ-opioid receptors, causing prolonged respiratory depression in renal impairment.
- **NAT-mediated O-acetylation** of N-hydroxyarylamines produces DNA-reactive nitrenium ions, initiating carcinogenesis from aromatic amine exposure.
- **SULT-mediated sulfation** of N-hydroxyarylamines similarly generates reactive N-sulfooxyarylamines that form DNA adducts. SULT1A1 is a key enzyme in this activation in human liver.
- **GST conjugation** of dihaloalkanes (e.g., 1,2-dichloroethane) produces episulfonium ion intermediates causing DNA crosslinking.
- **GSH conjugates** of hexachlorobutadiene are processed to cysteine conjugates that renal β-lyase bioactivates to nephrotoxic thiols.

## Factors Affecting Phase II Metabolism

**Age:** Neonates have markedly reduced capacity across all Phase II pathways. UGT1A1 at 1% of adult levels causes physiologic jaundice peaking at days 3-5 of life. UGT2B7 matures by ~6 months. NAT2 matures by 1-2 years. Elderly patients show variable reductions in UGT and SULT activities due to reduced hepatic mass and blood flow.

**Genetics:** Beyond the polymorphisms detailed above, UGT2B17 gene deletion (~70% of Asians) alters testosterone metabolism and bone density. UGT2B7*2 (H268Y) affects morphine and zidovudine clearance. SULT1A1*2 (R213H) reduces enzyme thermal stability and is associated with altered cancer risk. GLYAT polymorphisms affect benzoic acid and valproic acid conjugation capacity.

**Disease:** Liver disease (cirrhosis, hepatitis) reduces UGT, SULT, and GST activities while decreasing cofactor synthesis (UDPGA, PAPS, GSH). Hyperbilirubinemia competitively inhibits UGTs. Inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) downregulate Phase II enzyme expression via nuclear receptor signaling. Renal disease reduces some UGT activities; uremic toxins may inhibit enzymes. Cancer alters GST, UGT, and SULT expression with implications for chemotherapy response.

**Drug interactions:** Valproic acid inhibits UGT1A6, UGT1A9, and UGT2B7 (clinically significant with lamotrigine). Probenecid inhibits UGT2B7 (affects morphine and zidovudine). Diclofenac and mefenamic acid inhibit specific UGTs. Rifampicin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine induce UGTs via PXR and CAR nuclear receptors. St. John's Wort induces UGTs via PXR. Fluconazole inhibits UGT2B7 and some SULTs. Quercetin inhibits SULT1A1. Oltipraz and 3-methylcholanthrene induce GSTs via Nrf2/ARE pathway.

## Drugs Metabolized Primarily by Phase II

Morphine (UGT2B7 → M6G + M3G), codeine (UGT2B7), acetaminophen (UGT1A6 + SULT1A1 + GST for NAPQI), lorazepam (UGT2B7/2B15, no Phase I required), oxazepam (UGT2B15 for S-enantiomer, UGT2B7 for R-enantiomer — enantioselective), temazepam (UGT2B15/2B7), propofol (UGT1A9, also UGT1A8 extrahepatically), lamotrigine (UGT1A4), zidovudine (UGT2B7), ethinyl estradiol (UGT1A1), mycophenolic acid (UGT1A9 → MPAG; also AcMPAG via UGT2B7), isoniazid (NAT2), dapsone (NAT2 + UGT2B10), L-DOPA (COMT → 3-OMD), 6-mercaptopurine (TPMT inactivation), azathioprine (→ 6-MP → TPMT), irinotecan/SN-38 (UGT1A1), salicylic acid (GLYAT glycine conjugation → salicyluric acid), chloramphenicol (UGT2B7), benzoic acid (GLYAT → hippuric acid, historically used as a liver function test), and cyclophosphamide (bioactivation via CYP2B6; detoxification via GSTM1, GSTP1, GSTT1).

## Analytical Methods

Phase II metabolites are characterized using ultra-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Glucuronides show characteristic neutral loss of 176 Da (C₆H₈O₆); sulfates lose 80 Da (SO₃); GSH conjugates lose 129 Da (pyroglutamic acid) or 307 Da (complete GSH). High-resolution mass spectrometry (Orbitrap, Q-TOF) enables metabolite identification through accurate mass measurement. Nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H-NMR, ¹³C-NMR, 2D COSY/HSQC/HMBC) provides definitive structural identification of novel conjugates.

In vitro systems include human liver microsomes (for membrane-bound UGTs, supplemented with UDPGA cofactor and alamethicin to permeabilize the ER), human liver cytosol (for SULTs, GSTs, NATs, COMT, supplemented with PAPS, GSH, acetyl-CoA, or SAM), human liver S9 fraction (comprehensive Phase I + Phase II screening), cryopreserved hepatocytes (most physiologically relevant with intact cofactor synthesis and transporters), and recombinant enzyme systems (isoform-specific reaction phenotyping in baculovirus-infected insect cells or HEK293 cells).

Probe substrates for clinical enzyme assessment include acetaminophen (UGT1A6/SULT1A1), morphine (UGT2B7), lamotrigine (UGT1A4), and caffeine metabolite AFMU/1X ratio in urine (NAT2 phenotype). Emerging approaches include hepatocyte organoids, liver-on-a-chip microfluidic systems, urine metabolomics for mercapturic acid biomarkers, and machine learning models (MetaSite, GLORYx) for in silico Phase II metabolism prediction.

Phase II metabolism is indispensable for rational drug design, personalized medicine through pharmacogenomics, and understanding interindividual variability in drug response [[cannabis-nutrient-burn-and-toxicity]]. The growing recognition of Phase II drug-drug interactions and the clinical implementation of UGT, NAT2, and TPMT genotyping reflect the maturation of this field from biochemical curiosity to clinical necessity.
