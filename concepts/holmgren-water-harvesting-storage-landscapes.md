# Water Harvesting and Storage in Permaculture Landscapes

Water is one of the four key energy storages that [[david-holmgren]] identifies for
rebuilding natural capital in managed landscapes. The value of catching and storing
water is well understood, especially in Australia, but recent concerns about the
adverse environmental impacts of catching and storing too much water in the wrong
landscapes emphasise the importance of focusing on the greatest overall benefit.

## Built Water Storage Structures

By building reservoirs, dams, swales, tanks, cisterns, and other structures,
people increase the biological potential of landscapes to store water and therefore
to support other biological processes. If done in the right location and
proportions, the environmental benefits of water storage are greater than the
impacts.

For a given quantity of stored water, small catchment dams have more benefits and
less adverse environmental effects than large reservoirs in major river valleys.
Large reservoirs in suitable environments, such as steep mountain valleys, are
less environmentally damaging and are analogous to lakes naturally created by
glaciers and landslips.

## Potential Energy of Stored Water

High water (in tanks, dams, and reservoirs) has potential energy to drive
mechanical processes such as high-pressure irrigation, fire fighting, and
generation of mechanical and electric power. Opportunities for micro-hydropower,
especially in mountainous tropical countries, are probably collectively greater
than the hydropower from damming large rivers, and the adverse environmental
impacts of the micro solutions are much less.

The higher the water source, the greater the pressure and its flexibility in use.
Cheap hoses of 12 millimetres diameter are adequate for garden watering from a
high-pressure source; more expensive hoses of 18 or 25 millimetres are needed
for low-pressure hand watering.

## Nutrient-Rich Water for Aquaculture

Nutrient-rich, relatively shallow water in dams, wetlands, ponds, swales, and
paddies provides storage of chemical energy to support highly productive
aquaculture systems. In fact, shallow aquaculture systems on fertile lowlands
always produce more protein than grazing or even dairying.

This ecological efficiency of aquaculture for protein production was the reason
for promoting aquaculture in early Permaculture literature. In the wet tropics,
growing rice in paddies integrated with fish and duck production is one of the
most highly productive and sustainable agricultures yet developed.

## Keyline Water Harvesting

Keyline and other catchment-based Permaculture strategies reflect the broad
design rules of natural catchments when they seek to slow the flow of both water
and nutrients down the landscape and cycle them through soil and plants as many
times as possible. The primary function of surplus run-off water stored in keyline
dams is to irrigate pasture to rapidly build soil fertility and structure (which
can then store more water).

This approach integrates water storage with the broader
[[holmgren-soil-humus-carbon-storage-rebuilding]] strategy, using water as the
vehicle for building the productive capacity of land.

## Urban Catchment Management

Strategies to catch and use water run-off from buildings and hard surfaces for
household use and garden irrigation reflect progressive approaches to urban
catchment management. These aim to absorb and use stormwater as close to the
source as possible, reversing the conventional approach of rapid drainage that
degrades streams and wastes a valuable resource.

## Use of Slope in Water Design

On a slope, even a very gentle one, part of the gravitational force acts to move
things downhill. By siting tanks and dams high on hills, water can be
gravity-fed without needing to pump. Terraces, swales, contour access tracks, and
other structures slow the movement of water and soil downslope, maximising
retention and infiltration.

At Melliodora, all these examples of slope use have been applied: tanks sited
high for gravity feed, swales to slow and spread water, and terraces to create
productive growing space on sloping land. These earthworks represent the
appropriate use of non-renewable resources (fossil fuels powering bulldozers) to
create infrastructure that can be maintained indefinitely, by human labour if
necessary.

## Catchment Concepts for Planning

Catchment landscapes are self-organising systems for catching and storing water,
nutrients, and carbon. Key insights include:

- River catchments cover the whole landscape and generally include several
  different ecosystem types
- Their general patterns reflect the flow and form of energy, especially in
  rainfall and water flow
- Rivers are the lifeblood of landscapes; their waters and associated fertile
  alluvial flats have been the focus for human development through the ages
- What happens at the head of a catchment can affect the whole river system
- The health of river systems reflects the state of the whole catchment
- Headwaters are pure but infertile; estuaries are fertile but accumulate toxins

## The Dendritic Pattern

Catchment drainage patterns tend to be dendritic, or tree-like: the river is the
trunk, the tributaries are branches and twigs, and the permanent forests of the
headwaters are the canopy. This pattern is a revegetation metaphor, giving us
priority locations for trees and forest within a catchment landscape.

The headwater regions should be forested to protect steeper areas from erosion,
while simultaneously protecting valuable agricultural lowlands from flooding,
siltation, and salinity. This catchment pattern of land use has been promoted by
both the Landcare movement and the earlier Soil Conservation movement.
