# Cob Construction

Cob construction is a building technique using a mixture of clay subsoil, sand, straw, and water to create monolithic load-bearing walls. The word cob comes from Old English, meaning lump or rounded mass. Cob is one of the oldest building methods known, with examples dating back thousands of years across Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. In permaculture design, cob is valued for its extremely low embodied energy, local material sourcing, thermal mass properties, sculptural versatility, and the fact that anyone can learn to build with it.

## History and Distribution

Cob building has a long history across many cultures. In Britain, cob houses have been continuously occupied for over 500 years, with some examples dating to the 14th century. Devon and Cornwall are particularly known for cob construction, where the technique was the predominant [[query-how-do-i-choose-a-natural-building-method-for-my-climate]] rural housing until the 19th century.

Similar techniques exist worldwide under different names: bousillage in France, quincha in South America, zalan in Hungary, and various forms of mud and straw construction across Africa and Asia. The technique persists wherever suitable clay soils are found and communities maintain the building knowledge.

## Materials and Soil Selection

Cob requires a soil with sufficient clay content to act as a binder. The ideal soil mixture contains approximately 15-25 percent clay, with the balance being sand and silt. Too much clay causes excessive shrinkage and cracking; too little clay results in weak walls that crumble. Fibre, typically long straw, is added at approximately 10-15 percent by volume to provide tensile strength and control cracking.

Field soil testing is essential. A simple ball test involves compressing moist soil into a ball in the hand. If it holds together when dropped from waist height without crumbling, it has adequate clay content. A more precise test uses a glass jar filled with soil and water, shaken and allowed to settle for 24 hours, which reveals the proportions of sand, silt, and clay.

Sand can be added to clay-rich soils, and clay can be added to sandy soils, to achieve the ideal mixture. The quality of straw matters: long, clean barley or wheat straw works best. Straw should be dry and free from mould.

## Mixing Techniques

Cob can be mixed by several methods. The traditional and most accessible method is mixing by foot on a tarpaulin. Sand and clay soil are spread on the tarp, watered, and stomped by foot while the mixture is turned by lifting the tarp edges. Straw is added gradually and incorporated by continued stomping and turning.

Mechanical mixing using a tractor or mortar mixer can speed up production for larger projects. Some builders use livestock such as oxen or horses to tread the mixture. Regardless of method, the goal is a homogeneous mixture where clay coats each sand grain and straw fibres are evenly distributed.

The correct moisture content is critical. The mixture should be wet enough to be workable but dry enough that it does not slump when built up on the wall. A common test is that a ball of the mixture holds its shape when tossed in the air but does not stick to the hands excessively.

## Wall Building

Cob walls are built without formwork, by depositing lobs of the mixture directly onto a stone or concrete foundation. Each course or lift is typically 30-60 cm high. The builder shapes and compacts each lift by hand, using a process called cobbing or cobbing up. The surface of each lift is roughened before the next lift is added to ensure good bonding between layers.

Walls taper from thick at the base to thinner at the top, typically 60-90 cm wide at the base and 40-50 cm at the top. This taper improves structural stability and reduces the weight on lower courses. Openings for doors and windows are formed by embedding wooden frames directly into the cob as the wall is built up.

The building process is inherently slow, allowing each lift to partially dry before the next is added. This drying time varies with weather conditions, from a few days in hot dry climates to several weeks in cool humid conditions. The slow building process is an advantage, as it allows design modifications as the building takes shape.

## Structural Considerations

Cob walls are load-bearing, supporting the roof directly without a separate structural frame. The compressive strength of well-made cob is sufficient for single and two-storey buildings. Wall thickness provides both structural stability and thermal mass.

Cob is strong in compression but weak in tension. Long walls need buttresses or piers at regular intervals for lateral stability. Building corners and junctions with good interlocking between walls provides additional stability. The roof should be well-anchored to the walls with a ring beam or plate.

## Thermal Performance

The high density of cob gives it exceptional thermal mass. Walls absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night, moderating indoor temperature swings. In hot climates, cob buildings stay cool during the day. In cold climates, cob walls combined with passive solar design capture and store winter sun heat.

The thermal lag of a typical cob wall is 8-12 hours, meaning peak indoor temperature occurs well after peak outdoor temperature. Combined with appropriate insulation strategies, cob can create comfortable interiors with minimal mechanical heating and cooling.

## Weather Protection

Despite myths to the contrary, cob buildings are durable in wet climates when properly detailed. The key protection strategies include a good foundation that raises walls above ground moisture, a roof with generous overhangs of at least 60 cm on all sides, and a breathable lime or earth render on exterior surfaces.

The foundation should be stone or concrete, extending at least 30-45 cm above ground level. This prevents rising damp from wicking up into the cob walls. The roof overhang protects walls from direct rain exposure, which is the primary cause of erosion.
