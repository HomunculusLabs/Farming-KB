# PF Tek Substrate Formula and Jar Preparation Method

## Overview

The PF TEK ([[pf-tek-psilocybe-fanaticus]] Technique), introduced in 1991–1992, revolutionized amateur mycology by providing an accessible method for cultivating *[[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]* using common household materials. The core innovation was the **PF substrate formula** — a simple mixture of **brown rice powder**, **vermiculite**, and **water** — which eliminated the need for expensive laboratory equipment and specialized knowledge. The technique was widely copied and became the foundation for modern home [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] worldwide.

## The PF Substrate Formula

### Ingredients

The PF substrate requires only three ingredients:

- **Brown rice powder** (BRP): Finely ground brown rice, providing complex carbohydrates, proteins, and micronutrients for mycelial growth. Brown rice is preferred over white rice because the retained bran layer provides additional nutrients.
- **Vermiculite**: A naturally occurring mineral (hydrated magnesium-aluminum-iron silicate) that is expanded by heating. It serves as the structural matrix of the substrate, providing water retention, aeration, and a physical scaffold for [[mycelial-colonization]].
- **Distilled water**: Provides moisture for both [[mycelial-growth-and-colonization]] steam sterilization. Distilled water is preferred to avoid introducing mineral contaminants or chlorine.

### The Role of Vermiculite

The original PF TEK described vermiculite as the "magic ingredient" and the "secret" of the technique. Its importance cannot be overstated:

- **Water reservoir**: Vermiculite can absorb and retain 3–4 times its weight in water, providing a sustained moisture supply throughout the colonization period
- **Aeration**: The exfoliated (expanded) structure creates air pockets within the substrate, allowing gas exchange for the mycelium
- **Structural scaffold**: Mycelial threads stretch across the vermiculite particles, creating a three-dimensional colonization network
- **Contamination resistance**: The loose, well-aerated structure is less favorable for anaerobic contaminants than dense grain substrates
- **Fruiting surface**: The exposed vermiculite layer at the top of the jar provides an ideal microclimate for pin formation

### Comparison with Traditional Grain Methods

Traditional mycological cultivation used whole grain (rye, wheat, millet) as substrate. The PF TEK offered several advantages:

| Property | PF Substrate | Whole Grain |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| Preparation | Simple mixing | Requires soaking, simmering, draining |
| Sterilization | Steam (no pressure cooker) | Pressure canner typically required |
| Aeration | Excellent (vermiculite) | Poor (grains pack tightly) |
| Water retention | High | Moderate |
| Contamination rate | Low | Higher (grain bacteria) |
| Cost | Very low | Moderate |

The key innovation was that the vermiculite-based substrate could be **steam sterilized** (boiling water bath) rather than requiring a **pressure canner** (autoclave), dramatically lowering the barrier to entry for home cultivators.

## Jar Preparation

### Container Selection

The PF TEK uses standard **1/2 pint (approximately 240 mL) canning jars**:

- **Wide-mouth** jars are preferred for easier removal of the colonized substrate cake
- Regular-mouth jars can be used but make cake extraction more difficult
- The small jar size ensures thorough steam penetration during sterilization
- Jars must have metal lids with a central hole for inoculation

### Substrate Mixing Ratios

The standard PF substrate ratio (per 1/2 pint jar):

- 1/2 cup vermiculite
- 1/4 cup brown rice powder
- Approximately 1/4 cup water (adjusted for moisture content)

The mixing procedure is:

1. **Vermiculite first**: Place vermiculite in a bowl and add water, mixing until the vermiculite is evenly moistened but not dripping
2. **Add brown rice powder**: Sprinkle the brown rice powder over the moistened vermiculite and mix thoroughly
3. **Load jars**: Fill each jar to within approximately 1/2 inch of the top with the substrate mixture
4. **Clean the rim**: Wipe the inside of the jar rim clean of any substrate material
5. **Dry vermiculite layer**: Add a 1/4 to 1/2 inch layer of dry, uncontaminated vermiculite on top of the substrate. This barrier layer serves as a **contamination filter**, preventing airborne contaminants from reaching the nutritious substrate during and after inoculation

### Lid Preparation

The canning jar lids are modified for the PF TEK:

- A small hole (approximately 1/8 inch or 3 mm diameter) is punched or drilled through the center of each lid
- This hole serves as the **inoculation port** for introducing the spore syringe needle
- Some practitioners cover the hole with micropore tape or polyfill for additional contamination protection
- The metal lid ring holds the modified lid in place during sterilization and colonization

### Moisture Content

Achieving the correct moisture content is critical:

- **Too dry**: Mycelium colonizes slowly or fails to grow; substrate cracks and pulls away from jar walls
- **Too wet**: Substrate becomes waterlogged, anaerobic conditions favor bacterial contamination, mycelium growth is slowed
- **Correct**: Substrate should hold together when squeezed but release a few drops of water — the consistency of a damp sponge
- The dry vermiculite barrier layer at the top absorbs excess moisture rising from the substrate during colonization

## Steam Sterilization

### The Boiling Water Bath Method

The PF TEK's major accessibility innovation was that it **did not require a pressure canner**. Instead, jars are sterilized using a simple boiling water bath:

1. Place a washcloth, jar lid rings, or a rack at the bottom of a large cooking pot
2. Stand the prepared jars on this base (prevents direct contact with the pot bottom, which can cause thermal shock)
3. Fill the pot with water to approximately halfway up the jar sides
4. Cover the pot with a tight-fitting lid
5. Bring to a rolling boil and maintain for **90 minutes**
6. Remove jars and allow to cool completely (4–6 hours) before inoculation

### Why Steam Sterilization Works for PF Substrate

Steam sterilization at atmospheric pressure reaches approximately 100°C (212°F), which is insufficient to kill all bacterial endospores (which require 121°C under pressure). The PF substrate works despite this limitation because:

- Brown rice powder has a relatively low initial bacterial spore load compared to whole grains
- The vermiculite matrix provides excellent steam penetration
- The 90-minute exposure time is sufficient to kill vegetative bacteria and fungal spores
- The dry vermiculite barrier layer prevents post-sterilization contamination
- The small jar size ensures the core temperature reaches sterilizing temperatures throughout

### Pressure Canner Option

The PF TEK notes that a pressure canner can be used as an alternative to the boiling water bath:

- At 15 PSI, the autoclave temperature reaches 121°C (250°F)
- Sterilization time can be reduced to approximately 30–45 minutes
- This method provides more reliable sterilization but adds equipment cost
- Recommended for cultivators who already own a pressure canner or who experience high contamination rates with steam sterilization

## The Mass Spore Inoculation Approach

### A Fundamental Departure from Professional Mycology

The PF TEK employed **mass spore inoculation** directly onto the [[pf-tek-maximum-fruiting-substrate-formula]], rather than the professional approach of cloning mushroom tissue and growing on grain:

- Spore syringes are used to inoculate the substrate directly, bypassing the agar culture and grain spawn stages
- This means each jar receives a **multispore inoculation** containing thousands of individual spores, each potentially representing a unique genotype
- The resulting mycelium is **heterokaryotic** (multiple genetic individuals), producing mushrooms with variable characteristics

### Advantages of Multispore Inoculation

- **Simplicity**: Eliminates the need for agar work, laminar flow hoods, and tissue culture skills
- **Genetic diversity**: The complete genotype remains intact, preserving the reproductive capacity of the species
- **No senescence**: Tissue-cultured mushrooms eventually suffer senescence (genetic degradation and ceased fruiting) after multiple generations of cloning. Spore inoculation resets this clock with each generation
- **Never-ending succession**: As the PF TEK states, "The spores insure a never ending succession of fungus, with all the power of the spores' reproductive ability intact"

### Disadvantages

- **Variable results**: Each grow produces mushrooms with different characteristics (potency, size, speed, appearance)
- **No isolation**: Desirable traits cannot be reliably reproduced from grow to grow
- **Contamination risk**: Spore syringes are more likely to carry contaminants than clean agar cultures
