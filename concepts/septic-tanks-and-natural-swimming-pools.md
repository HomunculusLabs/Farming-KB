# Septic Tank Systems and Natural Swimming Pools

## Overview

In [[permaculture-design]], waste is never truly waste — it is an underutilised resource. Conventional septic systems treat effluent as a problem to be disposed of, but with thoughtful design, septic tank effluent can become a productive input for tree crops, aquatic systems, and even energy generation. Similarly, the conventional approach to swimming pools relies on toxic chemicals that undermine the very health they purport to support. Permaculture offers integrated, biological alternatives that turn both sewage and recreation into productive, regenerative systems.

This concept covers two broad strategies for handling water and waste:

1. **Productive septic disposal systems** — including underground leach fields planted to trees, and biogas conversion systems that feed into aquatic crop ponds.
2. **Natural swimming pools** — chemical-free alternatives that use biological filtration, reed beds, and flowform aeration to maintain clean, safe water for human bathing.

## Productive Disposal of Septic Tank Effluent

### Philosophy

The permaculture approach to septic effluent rejects the notion of "disposal" in favour of "productive use." Effluent is rich in nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and organic matter. Rather than allowing these nutrients to pollute groundwater or waterways, designed systems route effluent through biological filters and into productive landscapes where trees and aquatic crops can absorb and cycle the nutrients.

Two primary strategies are described: direct leach field systems with tree planting, and biogas conversion systems with integrated aquaculture.

## Leach Field Design with Tree Planting

### Trench Leach Fields

The trench leach field is the most common productive disposal system. Effluent from the septic tank flows by gravity into a series of subsurface trenches where it gradually percolates into the surrounding soil, feeding planted trees.

**Construction specifications:**

- The trench base is sloped at **1:12** to ensure even distribution of effluent along the length of the field.
- An **18 cm half-pipe** (split drain pipe) is laid in the trench bottom to carry and distribute effluent.
- The pipe is surrounded by **coarse gravel** to provide drainage and prevent clogging.
- A **plastic or tar-paper layer** is placed over the gravel to prevent soil from infiltrating and blocking the system.
- The trench is then **backfilled** with soil.

**Tree planting around leach fields:**

- Trees are planted **1–2 metres off the sides** of the trench, not directly over it, to prevent root intrusion into the distribution pipes.
- Tree spacing along the trench is **2–6 metres**, depending on the mature canopy size of the species selected.
- **Fruit and nut trees** are ideal candidates, as they benefit enormously from the consistent moisture and nutrient supply.
- In clay soils, the zone of benefit extends **20 metres or more** downslope from the leach field, creating a broad productive zone.

This system transforms what is normally a hidden, unproductive part of the property into a productive [[food-forest]] edge, with the septic system acting as a permanent fertigation source.

### Square Pit Leach Fields

An alternative to the trench system is the square pit, which is simpler to construct in some soil conditions.

**Construction specifications:**

- The pit is approximately **25 m²** in area.
- The base is filled with **graded stone**, from 6 cm diameter stones at the bottom to 2 cm stones at the top. This grading prevents fine material from clogging the system while allowing effluent to spread laterally.
- A layer of **cardboard** is placed over the stone to act as a biodegradable barrier.
- Over the cardboard, a **straw layer** is applied and **sown to oats**, which help stabilise the surface and begin the biological cycling process.

The oats germinate in the straw and root into the upper layers, creating a living mulch that further processes nutrients and prevents erosion. Over time, the entire surface can be planted to productive trees or shrubs.

## Biogas Conversion from Septic Tanks

A more sophisticated approach integrates biogas production, aquaculture, and tree planting into a single sequential system. The septic tank itself becomes an anaerobic digester, producing methane gas for household energy use before the effluent moves on to further biological treatment.

### System Components

**The biogas tank:**

- The tank is **1.5–2 metres deep** and **3–4 metres in diameter**, providing sufficient volume for anaerobic digestion.
- A **loading chute** allows weeds, green waste, and additional organic material to be fed into the tank, supplementing the sewage and increasing gas production.
- A **perforated pipe** connected to a gas compressor bubbles biogas back through the liquid for **1–3 hours daily**, which aids mixing and improves bacterial activity and digestion efficiency.
- **Gas** collects in an **inverted tank** (a floating gas holder) and can be piped to the house for **cooking, lighting, and refrigeration**.

**Aquatic crop pond:**

- Effluent overflows from the biogas tank into a **baffled pond** designed to slow water flow and maximise biological treatment.
- The pond is planted with aquatic species such as **Pistia (water lettuce)** and **watercress**, which absorb nutrients from the effluent and produce harvestable biomass.
- Aquatic plants are **harvested weekly** and returned to the biogas tank as additional feedstock, closing the nutrient loop.
- After passing through the aquatic pond, the now partially treated effluent can flow to a conventional leach field planted with trees, completing the system.

This integrated approach yields multiple products: energy (biogas), food (watercress), fodder (Pistia for livestock or compost), and tree crops (from the final leach field). It is a classic example of permaculture's principle of each element performing multiple functions.

## Natural Swimming Pools

### The Problem with Chemical Pools

> *"No man is an island, entire of itself; every man is a piece of the continent, a part of the main."*
> — John Donne

Conventional swimming pools are isolated from the natural world by chemistry. They are kept "clean" through the application of powerful biocides — primarily chlorine and its compounds — that kill virtually all biological organisms in the water. This approach has several serious drawbacks:

- **Pool chemicals are biocides.** By definition, they are designed to destroy life. Chlorine, algaecides, and other pool treatments do not discriminate between harmful bacteria and beneficial organisms.
- **Biocides are harmful to biological organisms** — including the swimmers themselves. The skin is the body's largest organ and readily absorbs chemicals from the water.
- **Chlorine reacts with organic matter** in pool water to form **chloroforms and other trihalomethanes**, many of which are known carcinogens. These byproducts are inhaled as vapour and absorbed through the skin.
- The guiding principle is simple and stark: **if fish cannot live in a pool, neither should humans.** A healthy aquatic environment supports life; a chlorinated pool destroys it.

### Natural Pool Design

Natural swimming pools eliminate chemicals entirely, relying instead on biological processes to maintain water quality. They function as constructed wetlands, using plants, gravel, and aeration to filter and purify the water.

**Key design elements:**

- **Reed beds and planted filtration zones.** A portion of the pool area (typically one-third to one-half) is designated as a shallow planted zone. Reed beds and other aquatic plants provide the primary biological filtration, absorbing nutrients and hosting beneficial bacteria that break down organic matter.
- **Gravel bases.** The planted filtration zones are built over graded gravel beds that provide surface area for bacterial colonisation and support root growth of the aquatic plants.
- **Flowform aeration.** Flowforms are sculpted water features that create cascading, vortexing flow patterns. As water passes through a flowform, it is aerated and energised, supporting aerobic bacterial activity and keeping the water oxygenated and fresh.
- **Regular testing.** Natural pools are monitored with **E. coli testing** to ensure bacterial levels remain safe for swimming. Unlike chlorinated pools, which rely on chemical kill to manage pathogens, natural pools maintain water quality through biological balance and competition.

Natural swimming pools represent a return to the principle that clean water is living water. They support biodiversity, provide habitat for amphibians and insects, eliminate exposure to carcinogenic chlorination byproducts, and create beautiful, ecologically integrated recreational spaces. They are more expensive to establish than conventional pools but far cheaper to maintain, and they improve rather than degrade the surrounding ecosystem.

## Design Principles

Several permaculture principles are embodied in these systems:

- **Every element should perform multiple functions.** A septic tank that also produces biogas, feeds aquatic crops, and irrigates fruit trees is far more valuable than one that merely disposes of waste.
- **Use biological resources before technological solutions.** Reed beds and gravel filters replace chemical treatments; tree roots replace mechanical aeration of drainfields.
- **Produce no waste.** Effluent is a nutrient resource, not a pollutant. The goal is to cycle all nutrients back into productive use.
- **Integrate rather than segregate.** Natural swimming pools bring recreation into the ecological web rather than isolating it behind chemical barriers. Septic systems are connected to food production rather than hidden and forgotten.
