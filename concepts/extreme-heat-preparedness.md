# Extreme Heat Preparedness

Extreme heat is the deadliest weather-related hazard in the United States, causing more fatalities than hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, and earthquakes combined. Homesteaders face unique heat risks due to outdoor physical labor, limited access to cooling infrastructure, and the need to care for livestock and crops.

## Understanding Heat Risk

**Heat Advisory:** Heat index of 100-104°F for 2+ hours
**Excessive Heat Warning:** Heat index of 105°F+ for 2+ hours

The heat index accounts for humidity. High humidity prevents sweat from evaporating, which is the body's primary cooling mechanism. A temperature of 90°F with 70% humidity feels like 106°F.

Key terms:
- **Heat exhaustion:** Heavy sweating, weakness, cold/clammy skin, fast/weak pulse, nausea. Can progress to heat stroke.
- **Heat stroke:** Body temperature above 103°F, hot/red/dry skin, rapid pulse, confusion. MEDICAL EMERGENCY.
- **Heat cramps:** Painful muscle spasms from exertion in heat.

## Cooling Strategies for Humans

### Passive Cooling (No Energy Required)

- Close windows, blinds, and curtains during the day to block solar heat gain
- Open windows at night when temperatures drop for cross-ventilation
- Plant deciduous trees on the south and west sides for summer shade
- Insulate attic spaces — hot attics radiate heat into living spaces
- Wet bandanas or towels on the neck, wrists, and forehead
- Cool water foot baths — feet have many blood vessels near the surface
- Wear loose, light-colored, lightweight clothing (cotton or linen)
- Minimize use of ovens, stoves, and any heat-generating appliances

### Active Cooling (Energy Required)

- Even a small window AC unit in one room can be life-saving
- Box fans and ceiling fans provide significant comfort
- Place a bowl of ice in front of a fan for improvised AC
- Fans are potentially dangerous above 95°F if air is above body temperature
- Evaporative coolers work well in dry climates (below 40% humidity)

### Outdoor Work Strategies

- Schedule all outdoor physical labor before 10 AM or after 6 PM
- Take breaks in shade every 20-30 minutes during any outdoor work
- Work in pairs — monitor each other for signs of heat illness
- Reduce pace and intensity significantly
- Pre-hydrate before starting work and hydrate continuously throughout

## Hydration

Dehydration is the primary driver of heat illness. Thirst is not a reliable indicator.

**Hydration guidelines:**
- Drink water before, during, and after outdoor activity
- Target: 1/2 to 1 gallon for most adults per day during extreme heat
- For heavy outdoor labor: 16-32 ounces of water per hour
- Include electrolyte replacements — plain water alone can cause hyponatremia
- Oral rehydration solution: 1 liter water + 1/2 tsp salt + 6 tsp sugar
- Avoid alcohol and caffeine — both are diuretics
- Monitor urine color: pale yellow = well hydrated; dark = dehydrated

## Vulnerable Populations

**Children:** Bodies heat up 3-5 times faster than adults. Never leave children in vehicles — interior temperature can reach 120°F+ within minutes.

**Elderly:** Reduced ability to sense heat and may not feel thirsty. Many medications impair heat regulation. Check on elderly neighbors daily during heat waves.

**Pregnant women:** Core temperature is naturally elevated. Dehydration increases risk of preterm labor.

**People with chronic conditions:** Heart disease, diabetes, obesity, and respiratory conditions increase heat vulnerability.

## Livestock Care During Extreme Heat

Livestock suffer severely during heat waves because they cannot escape conditions and many species cannot sweat effectively.

### General Principles
- Provide continuous access to shade — natural or constructed
- Ensure unlimited access to cool, clean water — consumption doubles or triples
- Check water sources multiple times daily
- Add electrolytes to water if animals show heat stress
- Avoid handling, transporting, or working livestock during peak heat
- Ensure adequate ventilation in all animal shelters

### Species-Specific Guidance

**Goats:** Tolerate heat moderately well but need shade and ventilation. Monitor for panting, drooling, reluctance to move.

**Ducks:** Surprisingly heat-sensitive. Provide a kiddie pool or shallow water feature — ducks cool through feet and bill. Reduce feed protein slightly during heat waves. Watch for panting, wings held away from body.

**Chickens:** Cannot sweat — cool only by panting. Shade the entire run, maximize coop ventilation, frozen water bottles in coop, add electrolytes to water. Frostbite risk is not a concern in heat but egg spoilage is — collect frequently.

**Cattle, Horses, Pigs:** Shade structures and misting systems. Pigs lack sweat glands — extremely heat-sensitive, provide wallowing areas. Cattle can drink 20+ gallons per day in extreme heat.

### Heat Stress Signs in Livestock
- Rapid, open-mouth breathing or panting
- Excessive drooling, elevated respiratory rate
- Reluctance to move or eat
- Staggering or collapse — if this occurs, move to shade, apply cool water, contact a vet

## Crops and Gardens

- Mulch heavily around all plants to retain moisture and reduce soil temperature
- Water deeply in early morning — avoid midday watering
- Use shade cloth (30-50% block) over sensitive crops
- Harvest ripe produce promptly — heat causes rapid spoilage

## Power Outage Considerations

- Generator or battery backup for fans and small AC
- Know location of public cooling centers before a heat wave
- Close off unused rooms to concentrate cooling in one area
