# Plant Olfaction and Volatile Organic Compound Signaling

## Overview

Plants possess a sophisticated olfactory system that enables them to detect and respond to a wide array of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in their environment.

Unlike animals, which possess dedicated olfactory organs, plants detect chemical signals through receptors distributed across their tissues, particularly in leaves and roots.

This chemical sensitivity allows plants to perceive the presence of neighboring organisms, coordinate defense responses, and even "eavesdrop" on the communication of other plants.

## How Plants Smell

Plants do not have a nose or olfactory epithelium. Instead, their ability to detect airborne chemicals relies on molecular receptors embedded in cell membranes, primarily in leaf tissue.

When a volatile compound binds to these receptors, it triggers intracellular signaling cascades that lead to physiological changes.

The process is analogous to human olfaction at the molecular level: both systems rely on specific protein receptors that recognize particular chemical structures and convert that recognition into a biological signal.

The volatile organic compounds that plants detect include ethylene (a plant hormone gas), methyl jasmonate (a wound signal), methyl salicylate (a systemic acquired resistance signal), and various terpenoids and green leaf volatiles released during herbivory.

Each of these compounds carries specific information that the receiving plant can decode.

## Ethylene: The Ripening Hormone

Ethylene (C2H4) is the simplest plant hormone and perhaps the most well-studied volatile signal in plant biology. It is a gas at room temperature, which means it can diffuse freely through the air and affect not only the plant that produced it but also neighboring plants.

Ethylene plays a central role in fruit ripening, leaf abscission, flower senescence, and stress responses.

The discovery of ethylene's role in ripening dates back to ancient times when it was observed that fruits ripened faster near burning incense or in enclosed spaces with ripening fruit.

In modern agriculture, ethylene is used commercially to control the ripening of bananas, tomatoes, and other climacteric fruits.

The ability to detect and respond to ethylene is fundamental to a plant's olfactory capacity.

Ethylene receptors in plants are similar to the two-component histidine kinase receptors found in bacteria.

When ethylene binds to these receptors, it inactivates them, releasing a downstream signaling cascade that activates ethylene-responsive genes.

This negative regulation system means that ethylene perception is essentially the removal of a brake on the ripening process.

## Interspecific Chemical Eavesdropping

One of the most remarkable aspects of plant olfaction is the ability to detect chemicals produced by other species.

When a plant is attacked by herbivores, it releases a complex blend of volatile organic compounds that serve multiple functions: attracting natural enemies of the herbivore (parasitic wasps, predatory mites), warning neighboring plants of the attack, and directly deterring the herbivore.

The classic example involves sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata) and wild tobacco (Nicotiana attenuata).

When sagebrush is damaged by herbivores, it releases methyl jasmonate and other volatiles.

Neighboring tobacco plants detect these compounds and preemptively activate their own defense mechanisms, producing nicotine and other toxic compounds before any herbivore reaches them.

This eavesdropping provides a significant fitness advantage, as the receiving plant can mount defenses before actual damage occurs.

Similarly, when a lima bean plant is infested with spider mites, it releases volatiles that attract predatory mites that feed on the spider mites.

Neighboring uninfested lima bean plants that detect these volatiles also begin producing their own attractant compounds, effectively recruiting bodyguards pre-emptively.

## Intraspecific Plant Communication

Plants can also distinguish between volatile signals from kin versus non-kin individuals.

Research on sagebrush has shown that plants respond more strongly to volatiles from genetically related individuals than from unrelated conspecifics.

This kin recognition through volatile cues suggests a level of social complexity in plant communities that was previously unappreciated.

The mechanisms behind kin-specific responses are not fully understood but may involve quantitative or qualitative differences in the volatile blends released by different genotypes.

Plants may have evolved to be more responsive to cues from kin because shared genetic interests make cooperative defense more evolutionarily stable.

## Root Exudate Detection

Below ground, plants engage in chemical communication through root exudates.

Roots release a complex mixture of organic acids, sugars, amino acids, phenolics, and secondary metabolites into the rhizosphere.

These compounds serve as signals to soil microorganisms, mycorrhizal fungi, and neighboring roots.

Plants can detect allelopathic compounds released by competitors and respond by altering their root growth patterns or increasing their own production of defensive chemicals.

The detection of strigolactones in root exudates, for example, serves as a signal for both mycorrhizal fungal symbiosis and, unfortunately for the plant, germination cues for parasitic plants like Striga.

## Implications for Agriculture

Understanding plant olfaction has significant agricultural applications.

Push-pull pest management strategies exploit plant volatile signaling by planting companion crops that emit repellent volatiles around the main crop (push) while planting attractive trap crops on the periphery (pull).

Intercropping systems leverage natural volatile-mediated defense priming to reduce pesticide dependence.

Synthetic volatile applications are being explored as alternatives to chemical pesticides.

Spraying methyl jasmonate or other elicitor compounds onto crops can prime their defenses without the environmental costs of broad-spectrum pesticides.

The challenge lies in timing, dosage, and the specificity of plant responses to different volatile blends.

## Key Molecular Players

- **Ethylene receptors (ETR1, EIN4, ERS1, ERS2):** Histidine kinase-family receptors that detect ethylene gas and initiate ripening and stress responses.

- **JAZ proteins:** Jasmonate ZIM-domain proteins that repress jasmonate signaling; degraded upon methyl jasmonate perception to activate defense genes.

- **NPR1:** Non-expressor of PR genes 1, a key regulator of systemic acquired resistance activated by salicylic acid and methyl salicylate signals.

- **TPS genes:** Terpene synthase genes responsible for producing the diverse terpenoid volatiles involved in plant communication and defense.
