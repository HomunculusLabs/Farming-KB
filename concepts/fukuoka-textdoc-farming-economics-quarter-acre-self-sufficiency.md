## Farming Economics and the Quarter-Acre Self-Sufficiency Model

## Overview

Fukuoka presents a radical critique of conventional agricultural economics, arguing that farming has nothing fundamentally to do with profitability and that the true measure of agricultural success is the ability to bring out the full forces of nature from the smallest possible piece of land. He proposes that a quarter-acre is sufficient to support a family and that the traditional Japanese "hundred-crop" farm represents the original and ideal form of agriculture, superior in every meaningful way to the large-scale monoculture operations that economists champion.

## The Question of Viability on Small Land

Even if farming aspirants are able to purchase land, what are the chances that they will be able to support themselves? Up until a few decades ago, seventy to eighty percent of the Japanese people were small farmers. Poor landed peasants were called "one-acre farmers."

If peasants were barely able to eke out a living on one acre, then what hope is there for someone planning to live off a quarter-acre? This question strikes at the heart of modern assumptions about agricultural economics.

The assumption that larger farms are more viable, more productive, and more efficient is so deeply embedded in both popular and expert thinking that the idea of a quarter-acre farm seems laughable. Yet Fukuoka argues that this assumption is not merely wrong but precisely backwards.

## Poverty Was Not the Land's Fault

The reason the farmers of the past were poor and hungry was not that their land was too small to support them. Their poverty was not of their own making. They were the victims of outside forces: an oppressive social system and political and economic mechanisms beyond their control.

Land taxes, feudal obligations, and the extraction of agricultural surplus to support urban populations and military adventures kept farmers in poverty regardless of how much they produced.

## A Quarter-Acre Is Enough

A quarter-acre of land is enough to supply the food needed for supporting a family. If anything, a full acre is too large. Had the peasants been exuberant at heart and ruled over by a benevolent government, instead of living in mean poverty, they could have lived like princes on their acre of land.

The land itself was never the constraint; the social system was.

## The Proof: Bigger Farms, Greater Instability

The proof is there before us. Following the war, farms increased steadily in size as the method of cultivation changed, going from one, to two, to four acres. Although the amount of farmland increased, more and more peasants abandoned farming and left the land.

Today, full-time farm households in Japan have grown to 15 and even 25 acres in size, as large as farms in many Western countries. At the same time, they have become increasingly unstable and even run the danger of collapse.

## The Predicted Benefits of Scale Failed

The predicted benefits of scale have failed to materialize. Larger farms require more machinery, more chemicals, more debt, and more dependence on volatile markets.

The farmer who once grew a hundred crops and fed his family from his own land now grows one or two cash crops and is at the mercy of prices set by distant commodities exchanges.
