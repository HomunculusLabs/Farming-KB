## Homestead Tool Maintenance

Well-maintained tools work better, last longer, and are safer to use. A [[homesteading|homestead]] relies heavily on hand tools — shovels, axes, saws, pruners, hoes, knives, and more. Regular maintenance including cleaning, sharpening, oiling, and proper storage extends tool life by years or decades and reduces replacement costs significantly.

## Basic Tool Care Principles

### Clean After Every Use

- Remove soil, sap, and debris with a stiff brush or putty knife
- Wash metal parts with water and dry immediately (never let tools air-dry — causes rust)
- Remove sticky residue with rubbing alcohol, mineral spirits, or citrus solvent
- Clean wooden handles with a damp cloth and dry thoroughly

### Inspect Regularly

- Check for loose handles, cracked wood, bent metal, and dull edges
- Tighten loose screws, bolts, and ferrules
- Replace cracked or splintering handles before they fail in use
- Check power tool cords for damage, fraying, or exposed conductors

### Store Properly

- Hang tools or store them off the ground
- Keep in a dry location (humidity causes rust and wood rot)
- Organize so tools do not bang against each other (prevents edge damage)
- Use tool racks, pegboards, or shadow boards for organization

## Sharpening Tools

### Sharpening Stones

Sharpening stones (whetstones) are the foundation of tool sharpening. Every homestead should have a basic set.

**Stone Types:**

**Arkansas Stones (Natural)**
- Soft Arkansas (novaculite): Coarse grit (400-600), for reshaping and heavy sharpening
- Hard Arkansas: Medium grit (800-1200), for general sharpening
- Black Arkansas (translucent): Fine grit (3000+), for honing and polishing
- Use with oil as lubricant

**Synthetic Water Stones**
- King, Norton, Shapton are reputable brands
- Available in wide grit range (220-8000+)
- Cut faster than natural stones
- Must be soaked in water before use (5-15 minutes)
- Require periodic flattening on a lapping plate

**Diamond Stones**
- DMT and Eze-Lap are common brands
- Available in coarse (325), fine (600), extra-fine (1200)
- Cut very fast, excellent for hard steels
- Do not require soaking or oiling
- Can be used dry or with water
- Eventually wear out (diamond particles detach)

**Ceramic Stones**
- Very hard, maintain flatness well
- Fine grits only (good for honing, not for reshaping)
- Use with water as lubricant
- Spyderco Sharpmaker is a popular ceramic sharpening system

### Sharpening Techniques

**Angle Consistency**
Maintain a consistent angle between the blade and the stone. Common angles:
- Pocket knives: 20 degrees
- Kitchen knives: 15-20 degrees
- Axes and hatchets: 25-30 degrees
- Chisels: 25-30 degrees
- Scissors: Match the existing bevel angle
- Pruning shears: Match the existing bevel angle

**Basic Sharpening Steps:**
1. Lubricate the stone (oil for oil stones, water for water stones)
2. Place the blade on the stone at the correct angle
3. Push the blade forward across the stone, maintaining angle
4. Lift the blade and return to the starting position (do not drag back on oil stones)
5. Alternate sides every 3-5 strokes
6. Repeat until a burr forms on the opposite edge
7. Switch to a finer stone and repeat
8. Remove the burr with light alternating strokes on the fine stone
9. Strop on leather for a polished edge

**Stropping**
- Leather strop with polishing compound removes the burr and polishes the edge
- Green chromium oxide compound on leather is excellent for final honing
- Stropping extends the time between full sharpenings significantly
- Strop after every few uses to maintain a razor edge

### Sharpening Specific Tools

**Shovels and Spades**
- Use a mill file (bastard cut) to sharpen the digging edge
- File from the inside of the blade toward the outside (follow the factory bevel)
- A sharp shovel cuts through soil with much less effort

**Axes and Hatchets**
- Use a mill file for the main edge, then a stone for honing
- File in one direction only — away from the cutting edge
- Maintain the factory bevel angle
- Do not make the edge too thin — it will chip
- A file guide helps maintain consistent angle

**Pruners, Loppers, and Shears**
- Disassemble if possible for easier sharpening
- Use a small diamond stone or fine file
- Follow the existing bevel angle on each blade
- Only sharpen the beveled side — flatten the flat side lightly
- Apply a drop of oil to the pivot point after reassembly

**Chisels**
- Flatten the back (flat side) first on a stone until it is perfectly flat
- Then sharpen the bevel on stones of increasing fineness
- A hollow-grind bevel is acceptable for light work
- A flat bevel is stronger for heavy chopping

**Saws**
- Hand saws: Use a saw set to set teeth, then file each tooth individually
- Chainsaws: Use a round file with a filing guide; file each cutter at the correct angle
- Japanese pull saws: Cannot be resharpened easily — replace when dull
- Bow saws: Replace blades when dull (inexpensive)

## Oiling Handles

Wooden handles require periodic oiling to prevent drying, cracking, and splintering.

### Linseed Oil (Best Overall)

- Boiled linseed oil penetrates wood and hardens
- Apply 2-3 coats, allowing each to soak in before wiping excess
- Reapply annually or when wood looks dry
- Food-safe when fully cured
