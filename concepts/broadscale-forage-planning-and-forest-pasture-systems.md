## Broadscale Forage Planning and Forest-Pasture Systems

Even forage distribution throughout the year eliminates the "feast and famine" regime that plagues pasture-only farming. By integrating tree crops with pasture, farmers can level out seasonal productivity, reduce animal stress, diversify income, and double yields on the same land area.

## The Seasonal Fodder Gap

In temperate winter-rainfall zones, both annual and perennial pastures reach peak productivity in spring, with a lesser autumn flush. Two critical shortfalls exist:
- **Midsummer**: Due to summer drought
- **Midwinter**: Due to cold and slow plant growth

Flock management (sale of young stock or culling after breeding) reduces summer feed requirements but cannot eliminate the fundamental gap.

## Tree-Crop Solutions to the Fodder Gap

### Midsummer Feed
- Carob pods
- Honey locust pods
- Foliage of Coprosma, pampas, and Chaetomys

### Autumn/Winter Feed
- The same foliage plants as summer
- Plus oaks, chestnut, and black walnut

Both types are concentrated, high-energy foods that enable more efficient use of dry pasture or rank grasses.

## Traditional Fodder Trees

Historically, in drought-prone areas, the foliage of kurrajong, willow, and poplar has been slash-felled to tide herds over drought. Mollison advocates replacing this laborious approach with self-feeding systems under forage forest, planting strips of low forage foliage where herds can be turned in for short periods.

## The Forest-Pasture Advantage

### Animal Welfare Benefits
- Animals can range into forest during extremes of heat and cold
- They occupy pastures in tolerable spring and autumn periods
- An estimated 15% of beef yield is lost due to lack of shelter alone
- This was "the normal habit of cattle and other large herbivores before we clear-felled farms for pasture"

### Yield Benefits
St. Barbe-Baker asserts that **where 22% of the land is planted to productive trees, yields double on the remaining 78% of the land surface**. No yields are lost by farm forestry; gains depend on design planning.

### Regional Benefits
If such systems evolved on a broad scale, the extremes of drought and flood would also be modified by the forests, benefiting the whole region through pasture/forest polyculture.

### Diversification Benefits
Tree products provide alternatives when markets for wool, hides, and meat fluctuate:
- Carob and chestnut can be converted to sugars, fuels, glues, food additives, flours
- The forest farmer gains great advantage over the "pasture only addict" tied to a single market

## Rolling Permaculture Development

All large properties (20 ha or more) have areas suitable for initial forest development with minimal productivity loss: steep, stony, eroded, or problem soils, awkward corners, cold or windswept valleys and rises.

### Seven-Step Establishment Process

1. **Reduce pests** by broadscale control or netted fencing
2. **Prepare land** by soil rehabilitation and liming
3. **Accumulate seed supplies** and plant many species for later assessment (select good seed, pre-treat by soaking/boiling)
4. **Mark strong seedlings** with pegs for later mulching and experimental treatment with fertilizers (seaweed solution, blood and bone, manure). Excellent ploy: mulch within empty tyres around trees for wind, rabbit, and drought protection. Thorn or thistle mulch in tyres discourages small browsers
5. **Gradually introduce** poultry or light livestock, watching for damage
6. **Assess and shift/add fences** as the system proves itself
7. **Cull poorer specimens** for pole timber, leaving selected high-yielding or strong [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]]

### Beneficial Effects of Rolling Permaculture
- Provides sheltered nesting, lambing, or calving place; increases meat production
- Enables early diversification into honey and pollen production
- Enables later diversification into nut crops and wide range of animal and plant products

## Orchard as Poultry Range

Orchards parallel the legume/grass mix of permanent pastures. The [[permaculture]] orchard is planned as a poultry range:

### Design Process
1. Prepare the whole site by soil conditioning
2. Set out leguminous species (small: white clover, lab-lab, lucerne; large: acacias, albizias, locusts; plus scattered leguminous shrubs)
3. Interplant selected orchard trees
4. Allow small animals to forage below as [[knf-pest-control-and-soap-water]] manurial elements

### Suitable Livestock
- Pigs (autumn)
- Geese (winter)
- Ducks and poultry (all year)

### Key Species
- Tree lucerne (top-trimmed as goose forage in winter, adds nitrogen, provides bee fodder, controls ground pasture)
- Hazel as edge species
- Small fruit understorey
- Perennial flower or [[vegetable-crop-demand-classification-fertility-matching-solomon]] for "in-line" plantings

### Evidence of Benefit
Fruit and nut species under which poultry or small livestock range are:
- More vigorous
- Healthier (less lichen, fewer dead branches)
- Show very little insect attack on fruits

Conversely, orchards where cattle or horses browse show severe damage and disease. Litter under poultry-run trees is more plentiful (natural mulches thicker), leaf mould is constantly turned over by hens, suckers are fewer, water absorption is better, grass mats are rare, and persistent weeds are absent.

### Remedial Measures for Monocultural Orchards
- Add 3–4 hens, a pig, and 5–6 large wattles per 1,000 m²
- Many smaller legumes
- Plant fuchsias, banksias, and [[kniphofia]] for insectivorous birds
- Borage and white clover for bees
- Keep evolving by adding more species

### Marketing Advantage
Planned variety gives good display at wayside stalls and enables [[coleman-direct-marketing-and-csa-models]] of varied products from flowers to fruit and nuts. The same number of fruit trees can be grown commercially; savings in pest infestation and fertilizer use more than compensate for expanded acreage.

## Yield Distribution Strategy

"The concentration of yields into one short period is a fiscal, not an environmental strategy." Permaculture aims to disperse yield over time through:

- Selection of early, mid, and late season varieties
- Planting the same variety in early or late-ripening situations
- Selection of long-yielding species
- General increase in diversity (leaf, fruit, seed, and root all as product yields)
- Self-storing species (tubers, hard seeds, nuts, rhizomes) dug on demand
- Preserving, drying, pitting, and cool [[hashish-binding-pressing-and-storage-techniques]]
- Regional trade between communities, or purchasing land at different altitudes or latitudes

## Pruning: Necessity or Habit?

Mollison questions whether conventional pruning serves permaculture goals:

**Reasons for pruning** (commercial orientation):
- Ease of spraying and harvesting
- Maximum size of fruit
- Reduction of leaf and increase of fruit spurs
- Even ripening from even light penetration
- Removal of diseased parts
- Small tree size for density

**Features of unpruned trees** (permaculture orientation):
- Less risk of disease from cut surfaces
- Smaller but more numerous fruit; greater yield per unit
- Stronger frames
- Fit into mixed forest, crop, [[holzer-natural-animal-husbandry]]
- Uneven ripening, more difficult harvesting or spraying

"Ladder and windfall harvest, self-harvest, and far less work offset most of the latter setbacks."
