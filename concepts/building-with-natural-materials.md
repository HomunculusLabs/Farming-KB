# Building with Natural Materials

Natural building uses locally sourced, minimally processed materials [[natural-building-materials-guide]] to create structures that are healthy, energy-efficient, and low in embodied energy. For the homesteader, natural building reduces costs, eliminates dependency on industrial supply chains, and creates buildings that are uniquely suited to their climate and landscape.

## Cob

Cob is a mixture of clay subsoil, sand, and straw, hand-sculpted into monolithic walls. It is one of the oldest building [[natural-building-materials-guide]] methods in the world and requires no power tools, no forms, and no special skills to begin learning.

### Cob Mix Recipe

The ideal cob mix is approximately:
- 1 part clay subsoil
- 2 parts sand
- 1 part straw (by volume)

Test your subsoil by doing a simple shake test: fill a jar 1/3 with subsoil, add water, shake vigorously, and let settle for 24 hours. Sand settles to the bottom, silt in the middle, clay on top. You want roughly 15-25% clay content. If your soil is too clay-rich, add sand. If too sandy, add clay.

### Mixing Process

1. Spread a tarp on flat ground
2. Add subsoil and sand, mix dry with feet
3. Add water gradually while mixing to a dough-like consistency
4. Add straw and fold/tread until straw is evenly distributed and the mix holds together when dropped from waist height
5. The mix should not be sticky (too much clay) or crumbly (too much sand)

### Building with Cob

- Build walls on a stone or concrete stem wall (prevents moisture wicking from the ground)
- Build up in courses (layers) no more than 12-18 inches tall per day
- Allow each course to firm up before adding the next
- Sculpt niches, shelves, and furniture directly into the walls
- Embed windows and doors with wooden frames as you build
- Walls should be 18-24 inches thick for structural strength and thermal mass
- Install a roof overhang of at least 2 feet to protect walls from rain

### Pros and Cons

Pros: Extremely low cost, sculptural freedom, excellent thermal mass, non-toxic, fire resistant, durable (centuries-long lifespan when properly maintained).

Cons: Labor intensive, slow building process, vulnerable to prolonged moisture exposure without proper roof and foundation, not code-compliant in many jurisdictions.

## Straw Bale

Straw bale construction uses baled straw (not hay) as either infill insulation within a structural frame (post-and-beam) or as the structural wall itself (load-bearing, also called Nebraska style).

### Straw vs Hay

Straw is the hollow stem of grain crops (wheat, oats, barley) after the grain is harvested. Hay is the whole plant including seed heads and leaves. Always use straw -- hay contains seeds that can sprout and organic matter that attracts pests.

### Building Methods

Post-and-beam (non-load-bearing): A timber frame provides structural support. Straw bales fill the spaces between posts as insulation. This is the most common approach and easiest to get permitted.

Load-bearing (Nebraska style): Bales carry the weight of the roof. Requires careful bale selection, compression, and pinning. Limited to single-story construction.

### Key Details

- Stack bales on edge for maximum strength (strings face up and down)
- Pin bales together with rebar or bamboo stakes
- Plaster both sides with earth or lime plaster for protection
- Keep bales dry during construction -- cover with tarps every night
- Minimum wall thickness: 18 inches (standard bale width)
- R-value approximately R-30 to R-50 depending on orientation and plaster

## Timber Frame

Timber frame construction uses large timber posts and beams connected by traditional joinery (mortise and tenon, dovetails) secured with wooden pegs rather than nails. The frame carries all structural loads, with infill panels (cob, straw bale, light clay straw) providing insulation.

### Timber Selection

- Oak: Traditional, strongest, most rot-resistant. Difficult to work.
- Pine: Easier to work, lighter, less rot-resistant. Treat or keep dry.
- Douglas fir: Strong, available in large dimensions in western North America.
- Locally milled timber reduces cost and transport. A portable sawmill can process trees from the [[woodlot-management]].

### Joinery

Basic joints every timber framer learns:
- Mortise and tenon: The fundamental joint. A rectangular hole (mortise) receives a protruding tongue (tenon).
- Bridle joint: Open mortise and tenon. Good for tension connections.
- Scarf joint: Joins two timbers end-to-end for long spans.
- Pegs: 1-inch oak or hardwood pegs driven through offset holes to lock joints.

## Earthship

Earthships are passive solar buildings developed by Michael Reynolds that use recycled materials and earth-rammed tires as the primary wall system. They are designed to be self-sufficient in water, food, and energy.

### Core Principles

- Thermal mass: Tire walls filled with rammed earth provide massive thermal storage
- Passive solar: South-facing glass captures winter sun
- Natural ventilation: Operable windows and skylights create cross-ventilation
- Rainwater catchment: Roof water collection for all water needs
- Greywater treatment: Interior planters filter and reuse greywater
- Solar and wind power: On-site electricity generation
- Food production: Interior and exterior growing spaces

### Tire Walls

- Pack used car tires with earth using a sledgehammer
- Each tire takes approximately 30 minutes to pack
- A typical Earthship uses 500-1000 tires
- Tires are laid like bricks in a running bond pattern
- Walls are then plastered over with adobe, cement, or earthen plaster

### Considerations

Earthships are labor-intensive (hundreds of hours of tire pounding) but use free materials. They perform well in arid and temperate climates but can have moisture issues in humid climates. Not permitted in all areas.

## Natural Plasters

All natural wall systems need breathable plaster for weather protection:

- Earthen plaster: Clay, sand, and straw fiber. Most common, easiest to make, fully breathable.
- Lime plaster: Lime, sand, and fiber. More water-resistant than earth plaster. Best for exterior in wet climates.
- Tadelakt: A Moroccan lime plaster technique that creates a waterproof, polished surface. Labor-intensive but beautiful.
- Alis: A clay-based paint with pigment. Used for interior wall finishes.

## Foundation and Roof

- Stone foundations: Traditional, excellent drainage, heavy labor
- Rubble trench with concrete footing: Modern approach, good drainage
- Living roof: Layered waterproof membrane, drainage mat, soil, and plants. Excellent insulation but heavy -- requires adequate structural support.
- Metal roof: Long-lasting, fire-resistant, good for rainwater catchment
- Thatched roof: Traditional, excellent insulation, requires skilled labor
