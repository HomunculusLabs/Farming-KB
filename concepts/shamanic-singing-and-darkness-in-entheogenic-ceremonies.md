# Shamanic Singing and Darkness in Entheogenic Ceremonies

## Three Distinctive Features of Shamanic Ceremony

Ralph Metzner identified three features that consistently distinguish traditional shamanic
entheogenic ceremonies from Western psychedelic psychotherapy sessions. These three elements
— minimal talking, essential singing, and darkness or low light — represent a convergent
cultural solution to the practical challenges of navigating expanded states of consciousness.
They have been independently arrived at by cultures separated by continents and millennia,
suggesting they address fundamental neurophysiological and psychological needs of the human
organism in altered states.

### Minimal Talking Among Participants

Traditional shamanic rituals involve very little or no talking among participants, except
during preparatory phases or after the experience to clarify teachings and visions received.
This contrasts sharply with Western psychotherapy, where verbal interaction between therapist
and patient is the primary medium of treatment. In the shamanic context, silence is not
absence but a container — a deliberate emptying of linguistic noise to make space for the
nonverbal communications of the medicine. The silence serves a paradoxical function: by
reducing external verbal input, it increases the vividness and clarity of the internal
communications that the entheogenic experience produces. Participants in shamanic ceremonies
frequently report receiving detailed information — diagnostic insights, instructions for
healing, prophetic visions — through nonverbal channels that would be drowned out by
conversation. The silence protects these delicate internal signals from the noise of social
interaction.

### The Centrality of Singing

Singing, or more specifically the shaman's singing, is "invariably considered essential to
the success of the healing or divinatory process" across all documented shamanic traditions
that use entheogenic plants. This universality is remarkable: from the Amazon to the Andes,
from Mesoamerica to Central Africa, the shaman sings. The shamanic songs serve multiple
simultaneous functions. The rhythmic pulse of the singing provides a "support for moving
through the flow of visions," preventing the voyager from becoming stuck in frightening or
seductive experiences. The shaman's singing "profoundly shapes the quality and content of
the experience" — this is not mere accompaniment but active co-creation of the visionary
landscape. In Amazonian ayahuasca ceremonies, the shaman's songs are called *icaros*, and
through them the actual healing and/or diagnosis takes place. The singing typically has a
"fairly rapid beat, similar to the rhythmic pulse in shamanic drumming journeys" found
throughout Northern Hemisphere shamanic traditions, suggesting it addresses a fundamental
neurophysiological need during altered states.

### Darkness or Low Light

Traditional ceremonies are "almost always done in darkness or low light, which facilitates
the emergence of visions." This practice recognizes that external visual stimuli compete with
and can override the internal visionary content that the medicine produces. Darkness is not
symbolic but practical — it removes sensory noise to allow the interior imagery to emerge
with maximum vividness and resolution. The peyote ceremony of the Native American Church is
the primary exception: conducted around a central fire at night, where participants may see
visions as they stare into the flames. Even here, the fire serves a functionally similar
role — providing a single, steady visual anchor rather than the chaotic, multi-directional
visual input of ordinary daylight.

## Mazatec Velada

The mushroom ceremonies (*velada*) of the Mazatec Indians of Mexico involve participants
sitting or lying in a very dark room with only a small candle. The healer — who may be a
woman or a man — sings almost uninterruptedly throughout the night, weaving into the chants
the names of Christian saints, spirit allies, and the spirits of the Earth, the elements,
animals and plants, the sky, the waters, and the fire. This synthesis of indigenous and
Catholic elements typifies the syncretic quality of post-contact Mesoamerican shamanism.
The near-total darkness combined with continuous singing creates optimal conditions for the
mushroom visions to emerge with maximum clarity.

## Amazonian Ayahuasca Ceremony

Traditional Amazonian ceremonies involve a small group sitting in a circle in semidarkness
while initiated healers sing the *icaros* through which healing and diagnosis occur. The
songs have a rapid rhythmic pulse that keeps the flow of the experience moving along,
preventing stagnation or fixation. Shamanic "sucking" methods of extracting toxic psychic
residues or sorcerous implants are sometimes employed in conjunction with the singing, adding
a physical dimension to the healing work. The semidarkness reflects the jungle environment
where these ceremonies evolved.

## San Pedro Ceremony

The ceremonies of the San Pedro (*Echinopsis pachanoi*) cactus in Andean regions show the
most structural variation. Some are conducted around a fire with singing. Others feature the
curandero setting up an altar on which are placed different symbolic figurines and objects
representing the light and dark spirits which one is likely to encounter. This altar serves
as a visual map of the visionary territory, preparing participants for what they may meet.
The presence of an altar introduces a visual element absent from the more minimalist Mazatec
and Amazonian formats.

## Native American Church Peyote Ceremony

Participants sit on the ground in a circle around a blazing central fire in a tipi. The
ceremony lasts all night, conducted by a "roadman" with the assistance of a drummer, a
firekeeper, and a cedar-man for purification. A staff and rattle are passed around and
participants sing the peyote songs, which involve a rapid, rhythmic beat. The fire replaces
the darkness of other traditions as the primary visual element. Despite the firelight, the
overall illumination level is low, and the circular arrangement creates a focused, contained
visual environment.

## Bwiti Iboga Ceremony

The Bwiti cult among the Fang people in Gabon and Zaire involves the use of the powerful
hallucinogen iboga. Ceremonies are held in temples with an altar and officiating priests.
The initiate men and women, daubed with white mud, sit and sing in lengthy ceremonies. A
mirror is sometimes placed in the assembly room, in which the initiates may "see" their
ancestral spirits. The mirror introduces a unique visual technology: instead of darkness or
fire, the reflective surface becomes a portal to the spirit world.

## Functional Analysis: Why These Three Features Converge

These three features address a common set of challenges arising in expanded states of
consciousness. The rapid rhythmic singing provides a stable auditory anchor that prevents
the voyager from being overwhelmed by the intensity of the experience. The combination of
darkness, minimal talking, and continuous singing works synergistically to prevent the
common problem of becoming "stuck" in a particular vision, emotion, or thought loop. The
presence of continuous singing allows the participant to surrender without the anxiety of
having nothing to hold onto. Darkness removes external light competition, allowing internally
generated visionary imagery to achieve maximum resolution. Together, these features create a
sensory environment optimized for the entheogenic experience — one that supports rather than
interferes with the medicine's work.

## Adoption in Hybrid Western Ceremonies

Metzner notes that the hybrid therapeutic shamanic rituals developing in the modern Western
world have "incorporated these three features from the shamanic model." Modern neo-shamanic
medicine circles typically use eye-shades (functionally equivalent to darkness), recorded or
live music with rapid rhythmic elements (equivalent to singing), and minimal verbal
interaction during the expanded state. This cross-cultural adoption suggests that these
features represent genuine best practices for entheogenic work that have been independently
validated across widely separated traditions over millennia.
