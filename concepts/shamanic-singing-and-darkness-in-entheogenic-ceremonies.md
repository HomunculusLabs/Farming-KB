### The Centrality of Singing

Singing, or more specifically the shaman"s singing, is "invariably considered essential to
the success of the healing or divinatory process" across all documented shamanic traditions
that use entheogenic plants. This universality is remarkable: from the Amazon to the Andes,
from Mesoamerica to Central Africa, the shaman sings. The shamanic songs serve multiple
simultaneous functions. The rhythmic pulse of the singing provides a "support for moving
through the flow of visions," preventing the voyager from becoming stuck in frightening or
seductive experiences. The shaman"s singing "profoundly shapes the quality and content of
the experience" — this is not mere accompaniment but active co-creation of the visionary
landscape. In [[harner-shamanic-journey-and-altered-states]].

### Darkness or Low Light

Traditional ceremonies are "almost always done in darkness or low light, which facilitates
the emergence of visions." This practice recognizes that external visual stimuli compete with
and can override the internal visionary content that the medicine produces. Darkness is not
symbolic but practical — it removes sensory noise to allow the interior imagery to emerge
with maximum vividness and resolution. The peyote ceremony of the [[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]] (*velada*) of the Mazatec Indians of Mexico involve participants
sitting or lying in a very dark room with only a small candle. The healer — who may be a
woman or a man — sings almost uninterruptedly throughout the night, weaving into the chants
the names of Christian saints, spirit allies, and the spirits of the Earth, the elements,
animals and plants, the sky, the waters, and the fire. This synthesis of indigenous and
Catholic elements typifies the syncretic quality of post-contact Mesoamerican shamanism.
