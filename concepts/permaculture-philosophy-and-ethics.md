# Permaculture Philosophy and Ethics

## Overview

Permaculture is not merely a set of agricultural techniques — it is a comprehensive philosophy rooted in ethics, personal responsibility, and a deep understanding of natural systems. [[bill-mollison]] framed permaculture as both a design science and a worldview that challenges the destructive trajectory of modern industrial civilization.

## The Design Philosophy

The philosophy behind permaculture is one of:

- **Working with, rather than against, nature**
- **Protracted and thoughtful observation rather than protracted and thoughtless action**
- **Looking at systems in all their functions, rather than asking only one yield of them**
- **Allowing systems to demonstrate their own evolutions**

This philosophy stands in direct opposition to the dominant industrial paradigm, which Mollison describes as one of conquest and extraction. The shift from "What can I get from this land?" to "What does this land have to give if I cooperate with it?" represents the fundamental philosophical reorientation that permaculture demands.

## The Prime Directive

> The only ethical decision is to take responsibility for our own existence and that of our children. Make it now.

This directive establishes the foundational ethical stance of permaculture: that individuals must take personal responsibility for their survival and well-being, and for that of future generations. Mollison argues that most thinking people would agree humanity has arrived at final and irrevocable decisions that will either abolish or sustain life on Earth.

## The Principle of Cooperation

> Cooperation, not competition, is the very basis of existing life systems and of future survival.

Mollison argues that life is fundamentally cooperative rather than competitive. Life forms of very different qualities interact beneficially with one another and with their physical environment. Even bacteria, he notes citing lewis thomas (1974), "live by collaboration, accommodation, exchange, and barter."

The competitive paradigm, according to Mollison, has led humanity to the brink of ecological disaster. The shift toward understanding and designing cooperative systems is not merely ideological but is grounded in the observable reality of how natural ecosystems function.

## The Three Ethics of Permaculture

Mollison distilled the ethical foundation of permaculture into three principles:

### 1. Care of the Earth
Provision for all life systems to continue and multiply. This means designing human settlements and activities that support rather than degrade the natural world. It encompasses soil health, water quality, biodiversity, and the integrity of ecological processes.

### 2. Care of People
Provision for people to access those resources necessary to their existence. This ethic ensures that the design system addresses human needs — food, shelter, energy, community, and meaningful activity — in ways that are sustainable and equitable.

### 3. Setting Limits to Population and Consumption
By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles. This ethic acknowledges that the Earth has finite resources and that both population growth and excessive consumption threaten the viability of life on the planet. It calls for voluntary self-regulation rather than external imposition.

Mollison notes that even the second and third ethics arise from the first — care of the Earth is the foundation from which all other ethical considerations flow.

## The Rainbow Serpent Worldview

The cover story of the manual presents the Aboriginal [[rainbow-serpent]] legend as a metaphor for the creative forces that shape the Earth. In this worldview:

- The Earth was originally flat, and the Rainbow Serpent shaped it — its body heaping up mountains and digging troughs for rivers
- The great oval represents the "egg of life" — that quantity of life which cannot be created or destroyed, from which all living things are expressed
- Within this egg is contained the tree of life, whose roots are in earth and whose crown is in rain, sunlight, and wind
- The ideal way to spend one's time is in the perfection of the expression of life

This worldview stands in stark contrast to the Western scientific materialism that Mollison critiques. For Aboriginal peoples, life is a totality neither created nor destroyed, and the aim of existence is to develop a conceptual and spiritual life while assisting in and celebrating the existence of all life forms.

## The Gaia Hypothesis

Mollison draws on [[james-lovelock]]'s [[gaia-hypothesis]] (1979), which sees the Earth and the universe as a thought process — a self-regulating, self-constructed, and reactive system that creates and preserves the conditions that make life possible, actively adjusting to regulate disturbances.

Lovelock described Gaia as "an alternative to that pessimistic view which sees nature as a primitive force to be subdued and conquered" and "an alternative to that equally depressing picture of our planet as a demented spaceship, forever travelling, driverless and purposeless."

Mollison warns, however, that humanity in its present mindlessness may be the one disturbance that the Earth cannot tolerate.

## Science, Mysticism, and Tribal Knowledge

Mollison observes that science and mysticism are coming closer together as science approaches its conceptual limits. He draws parallels between scientific statements on energy and Aboriginal statements on life, noting that for every scientific principle, tribal peoples have an equivalent understanding expressed through their own conceptual frameworks.

He argues that the experience of the natural world and its laws has been almost abandoned for "closed, artificial, and meaningless lives, perhaps best typified by the dreams of those who would live in space satellites and abandon a dying earth."

The manual advocates for adopting "sophisticated aboriginal belief systems" and learning respect for all life, warning that without an ethic or philosophy, humanity continues "like abandoned and orphaned children" rather than creating opportunities to achieve maturity, balance, and harmony.

## The Global Nation

Those who agree on [[permaculture-ethics]], philosophies, and goals form what Mollison calls a "global nation" — not a political entity but a community of people united by shared values and principles. This global nation has no central structure but rather a strong sense of shared work. Members are free to act as individuals, to form small groups, or to work within any other organization.

The manual emphasizes that this is a cooperative society which applies its knowledge. The aim is "to empower the powerless and create a million villages to replace nation-states" as the only safe future for the preservation of the biosphere, with interdependence and personal responsibility as the guiding aims.
