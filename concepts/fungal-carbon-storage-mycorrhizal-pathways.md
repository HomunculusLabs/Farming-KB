# Mycorrhizal Pathways of Soil Carbon Storage

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi make significant direct and indirect contributions to soil carbon
stores and are implicated in the processes of carbon sequestration in boreal forest and
peatland ecosystems that represent major terrestrial carbon sinks. The three dominant
types of mycorrhizal association (ericoid, ectomycorrhizal, and arbuscular) drive carbon
storage through fundamentally different mechanisms that are closely linked to
[[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] and
fungal-biogeochemistry|biogeochemistry in the major biomes.

## Biome-Scale Patterns of Soil Carbon

The extent of soil carbon sequestration varies widely and systematically across major
biomes, being closely correlated with vegetation type, dominant mycorrhizal association,
soil characteristics, and climate. The most important biomes for soil carbon
sequestration are the heathland and boreal forest regions that together cover
approximately 70% of the vegetated land mass of the Northern Hemisphere and contain the
majority of the world soil carbon stocks, mainly in peat and raw humus (Read et al.,
2004).

Mycorrhizal fungi consume 10-30% of net photosynthesis in some grassland and forest
ecosystems, representing a massive below-ground carbon flux. This flux makes mycorrhizal
mycelia a primary source of carbon input into soil, one that is chemically distinct from
that of their host plants: cell walls of chitin rather than cellulose, melanin rather
than lignin.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizal Pathway

Plants with [[ericoid-mycorrhiza-heathland-ecosystems]] produce
litter that is among the most extremely recalcitrant, being highly enriched in
polyphenolic compounds, low-molecular-mass phenolics, and aliphatic acids that are
fungistatic and fungitoxic (Jalal et al., 1982). This litter has extremely high C:N
ratios and decomposes very slowly.

ERM fungi are highly adapted to this litter, not only in tolerating and metabolizing some
of the most toxic phenolic compounds but also in the range of oxidative and hydrolytic
enzymes they express to mobilize nitrogen and phosphorus from litter (Leake and Read,
1997). Key features include selective and highly effective exploitation of both nitrogen
and phosphorus with very incomplete decomposition of lignocellulose, resulting in the
accumulation of recalcitrant humic compounds.

The effectiveness of nitrogen exploitation by ERM is often reflected in an absence of
detectable mineral nitrogen (nitrate or ammonium) in soils under ericaceous plants
(Adams, 1986). By these mechanisms, ERM contribute significantly to soil carbon
sequestration in heathland and tundra environments.

## Ectomycorrhizal Pathway

Ectomycorrhizal (EM) mycelia provide intensive, spatially precise nutrient foraging
through decaying litter that is rich in polyphenolics and tannins in boreal and temperate
forest ecosystems. Through use of organic nitrogen such as amino acids and proteins, EM
can short-circuit ammonification and nitrification pathways of the nitrogen cycle,
resulting in very low rates of inorganic nitrogen release into soil.

In competing with saprotrophs for limiting labile nutrient sources, their large supply of
host photosynthate gives EM fungi a major advantage in both biomass and absorptive
surface area. By selective nutrient removal and their limited abilities to degrade
lignocellulose or accumulating soil humus, they increase the C:N and C:P ratios of
remaining residues and further promote their recalcitrance. These positive feedback
mechanisms contribute significantly to soil carbon sequestration in boreal forest
ecosystems.

In addition to nutrient competition, the drying of soil under trees by water extraction
through EM networks has been implicated in reduced litter decomposition, further
enhancing carbon retention (Koide and Wu, 2003).

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Pathway

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]] fungi affect soil carbon
sequestration through a different mechanism. Although there is currently no evidence that
AM fungi directly affect decomposer organisms, their effects on stabilization of soil
macroaggregates (more than 2 mm in diameter) through both the activities of hyphae and
their glomalin secretions is implicated in increased soil carbon storage.

Glomalin is a hydrophobic protein secreted by AM hyphae into soil that has a major role
in [[fungi-in-the-environment-fungal-soil-aggregation-structure]]. This protein strongly accumulates in
soil with a residence time of 6-42 years, contributing as much as 15% of the total stable
soil organic carbon pool in grasslands (Miller and Kling, 2000) and 4-5% of that in
tropical rainforest soils (Rillig et al., 2001; Zhu and Miller, 2003).

Organic matter entrapped inside macroaggregates appears to be biologically and chemically
stabilized to a greater degree than that in the bulk soil (Jones and Donnelly, 2004). The
host plants of AM associations typically produce litter of low recalcitrance and
comparatively high nitrogen content that decomposes relatively quickly, so AM-dominated
ecosystems generally show less soil carbon accumulation than ERM or EM-dominated systems.

## Positive Feedback Mechanisms

Together, the observations on the three main types of mycorrhiza suggest strong
functional interdependencies between plant litter properties, adaptive features of the
mycorrhizal fungi, and the nature of soils that develop in different bioclimatic regions.
Mycorrhizas are doubly implicated in soil carbon storage through both direct inputs of
carbon and indirect effects on decomposition processes (ERM and EM) and soil aggregation
(AM).

These feedback mechanisms also have important implications for
[[dighton-fungal-ecosystem-services-framework]] and
[[mycorrhizal-carbon-phosphorus-exchange]], as the below-ground
carbon energy flow from recent photosynthate through mycorrhizal networks is so large
that it has driven the evolution of many plant species that exploit this carbon source.
More than 10% of plant species depend upon cheating mycorrhizal fungal partners out of
carbon to establish from seed.
