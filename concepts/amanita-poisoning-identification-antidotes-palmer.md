# Amanita Poisoning — Identification, Symptoms, and Antidotes (Palmer)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Julius A. Palmer, Jr., *About Mushrooms* (1894). Chapters on [[allegro-the-sacred-mushroom-amanita-identification]], [[palmer-toadstool-poisoning-cases]], and antidote discovery.

## The Amanita Family — The Only Deadly Mushroom Group

Palmer made the critical observation that the deadly poisonous properties of mushrooms are concentrated almost entirely in the Amanita family. His work was among the first American attempts to segregate the Amanitae from other non-esculent mushrooms, define the action of their poison, show the uselessness of ordinary remedies, and suggest effective treatment. This segregation, published in the *Monitor Scientifique* (Paris, February 1879), was the first systematic treatment of Amanita poisoning for a general audience.

## Amanita Identification — Three Diagnostic Marks

Palmer identified three consistent features that distinguish the Amanita from all other mushrooms:

1. **The Volva** — A white wrapper or cup at the base of the stem, often partially or wholly buried in soil. This is the remnant of the universal veil that enclosed the young mushroom. It may appear as a loose sac, a scaly ring around the stem base, or concentric rings of tissue.

2. **The Ring (Annulus)** — A membranous ring or skirt around the stem, typically located about two-thirds up from the base. This is the remnant of the [[partial-veil-tropism-protection-before-spore-release]] that protected the gills during development.

3. **The Cap (Pileus)** — Scurfy, warty, or friable surface texture. The cap of the deadly Amanita is NOT smooth and shiny like many edible species.

**Additional features**: White gills and white stem. Cap colors vary from pure white to drab, golden, red, or greenish. The most common deadly species has a yellow center shading to white.

The key principle: if ALL THREE marks are present, the specimen is an Amanita and potentially deadly. No other mushroom family has this combination of features.

## The Mushroom vs Toadstool Myth

Palmer emphatically rejected the popular distinction between "mushrooms" and "toadstools":

"Every mushroom is a toadstool and every toadstool is a mushroom, exactly as every plant is a vegetable and every vegetable growth is a plant."

The only reason one cultivated variety (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) is called a "mushroom" while all others are called "toadstools" is that this single variety has a world-wide reputation and commercial value. There is no reliable folk test to distinguish edible from poisonous species.

### Worthless Folk Tests
Palmer debunked all common folk tests for edibility:
- **Peeling test** — whether the cap skin peels easily
- **Silver spoon test** — whether a silver spoon turns black when cooking with the mushroom
- **Salt test** — sprinkling salt on the flesh
- **Litmus paper test** — testing color change
- **Onion/garlic test** — cooking with onion to detect discoloration
- **Animal feeding test** — whether animals eat the mushroom (squirrels and snakes eat Amanita)

All of these tests have been demonstrated to fail with deadly Amanita species.

## Amanitine Poisoning — Symptoms and Course

### Timeline of Poisoning
The poison (amanitine) is characterized by a delayed onset of 8 to 15 hours after ingestion. This delay is extremely dangerous because victims may believe the mushrooms were safe after no immediate ill effects.

### Symptom Progression
1. **Initial phase** (8-15 hours after ingestion): dizziness, nausea, abdominal pain
2. **Active phase** (12-24 hours): intense vomiting, purging (diarrhea), foul breath, coated tongue, burning sensation in mouth and throat
3. **Cardiovascular phase**: pulse racing at 120-140 beats per minute, soft and compressible, intermittent at intervals; temperature rising to 104°F
4. **Neurological phase**: mild delirium, peculiar sensation of blindness, stupor
5. **Terminal phase** (50-63 hours): coma or coma-vigil, shrunken/wrinkled face, sunken eyes, dusky skin, imperceptible pulse, death by asthenia (progressive weakness and loss of vitality)

### Why Treatment Historically Failed
Palmer documented that traditional medical treatments were almost universally ineffective against Amanita poisoning:

**Failed treatments**: coffee, tea, antimony, ether, whites of eggs, laudanum, milk, leeches, ice, bismuth subnitrate, creosote, acacia mucilage, peppermint, morphine sulfate, digitalis tincture, brandy and other alcoholic stimulants.

The symptoms resemble Asiatic cholera, leading physicians to treat for gastrointestinal distress rather than addressing the underlying nerve poison. The toxic element acts as a narcotic poison that spends its force on nerve centers, especially those governing respiration and heart action.

## The Atropine Antidote Discovery

### Early Research
- **Letellier** isolated the Amanita poison (amanitine) and attempted precipitation with tannin
- **Schmiedeberg and Koppe** isolated muscarine, which entered the pharmacopoeia
- **Boudier** isolated bulbosine from *Amanita bulbosa*
- These may be the same or similar alkaloids from different Amanita species

### Muscarine Mechanism
Dr. Ott's frog experiments demonstrated that muscarine stops the heart in diastole (relaxed state). Atropine reverses this effect. This established the specific pharmacological antagonism between muscarine and atropine.

### Solanaceous Plant Connection
Palmer recognized that all effective antidotes come from the Solanaceae (nightshade) family:
- **Atropine** — from *[[belladonna-atropa-belladonna-ethnobotany]]* ([[plants-of-the-gods-atropa-belladonna-deadly-nightshade]])
- **Daturine** — from *[[datura-stramonium]]* (jimsonweed)
- **Hyoscyamine** — from *[[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]]* (henbane)
- **Stramonium** — from *Datura stramonium*
