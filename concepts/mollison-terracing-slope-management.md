# Mollison: Terracing and Slope Management

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 9, Section 9.6)

## The Role of Terraces in Permaculture

Mollison describes terracing as a critical strategy for productive use of sloping land, but emphasises that terraces must be carefully designed and limited in their extent relative to the overall landscape.

Terraces are appropriate agricultural features on slopes of 10-18 degrees, converting land that would otherwise be subject to erosion into productive growing space.

## Proportional Limits for Terracing

Mollison establishes clear proportional limits for terracing systems.

All terrace systems should aim to occupy no more than 30% of tropical or 5% of dryland areas.

In the tropics, tree crop should be developed to maintain fertility of the terrace areas.

Water catchment areas should be developed to bring leaf mulch to terraced slopes in arid areas.

These limits reflect the recognition that over-terracing removes too much tree cover, leading to the very erosion problems terraces are meant to solve.

## Wet Terraces for Aquaculture

In humid cool or tropical areas, wet terraces comprising 10-20% of all terrace areas can be devoted to a fish-plant polyculture.

These yield fish, shellfish, and water plant products.

Yields of protein from water cultures can exceed all land-based systems if managed at the same levels of husbandry and care.

This integration of aquaculture with terracing dramatically increases overall system productivity.

## Steep Slope Strategies

Wherever people occupy very steep sites (slopes of 20 degrees or more), especially in areas of high rainfall, Mollison recommends abandoning broad terracing in favour of a series of 4-6 narrow production terraces.

Each series must be carefully drained to spill excess rain down permanently vegetated slopes.

The maximum recommended slope between terraces is 34 degrees (approximately 37 degrees).

The maximum height between terraces should not exceed 2 metres.

## Construction Techniques

By ridging the terrace tips, mulching paths, and staggering path spills in short series, the advantages of terrace cultivation can be achieved on quite steep slopes without risking erosion and soil loss.

Machines are inappropriate for steep slope terracing construction, which is always hand-cut.

This hand-cut approach creates more stable and longer-lasting terraces than machine construction on steep ground.

## Drainage of Terrace Systems

Proper drainage is essential for terrace stability.

Each terrace must have a defined spillway to prevent water accumulation that could breach the terrace wall.

Spills should be staggered between terraces so that water does not concentrate in one channel down the slope.

Permanently vegetated slopes between terraces absorb and slow water flow.

## Integration with Tree Systems

Terraces work best when integrated with tree crop systems on the slopes above and below.

Tree roots anchor slopes, leaf mulch from trees above feeds terrace soils, and terraces provide level growing space for intensive crops.

The combination of terraces and tree crops creates a stable, productive slope system that resists erosion while maximising yield.

## Slope Analysis for Terrace Placement

Before designing terraces, Mollison recommends careful analysis of slope angle, soil type, rainfall patterns, and existing vegetation.

Slopes under 10 degrees generally do not require terracing and can be managed with contour planting and swales.

Slopes from 10-18 degrees are ideal for conventional terrace systems.

Slopes from 18-20 degrees require careful engineering and narrow terraces.

Slopes above 20 degrees require the narrow production terrace approach with vegetated slopes between.

## Climate-Specific Considerations

In dryland areas, terraces must be designed to maximise water retention and minimise evaporation.

Run-on from slopes above should be directed to terraces, and deep mulching of terrace surfaces reduces water loss.

In humid tropical areas, terraces must handle high rainfall volumes, requiring robust drainage systems and consideration of wet terrace aquaculture zones.

In cool climates, terracing can create warmer microclimates on south-facing slopes (in the northern hemisphere), extending the growing season.

## Historical Context

Mollison acknowledges that terracing is an ancient technology, perfected over millennia in Asia, the Middle East, and the Americas.

Traditional terrace systems demonstrate the long-term sustainability of the approach when properly maintained.

Modern failures of terracing usually result from over-terracing, inadequate drainage, or removal of the tree cover that sustains terrace soil fertility.
