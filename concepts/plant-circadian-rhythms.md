# Plant Circadian Rhythms

Circadian rhythms are approximately 24-hour oscillations in physiological
processes that persist even [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]] absence of external time cues. [[xylem-and-phloem-transport-systems-in-plants]],
the circadian clock regulates a vast array of processes including leaf
movement, photosynthetic capacity, stomatal opening, hormone biosynthesis,
flowering time, and stress resistance. The clock allows plants to anticipate
daily environmental cycles and coordinate their physiology accordingly.

## Core Clock Architecture

The plant circadian oscillator is built on interconnected transcription-
translation feedback loops. In Arabidopsis, the morning-expressed genes CCA1
and LHY encode MYB transcription factors that repress expression [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]]
evening gene TOC1 (Timing of CAB Expression 1). TOC1 protein, in turn,
accumulates during the evening and represses CCA1 and LHY expression,
completing a negative feedback loop.

A second feedback loop involves the evening complex, composed of ELF3, ELF4,
and LUX proteins. This complex represses morning genes during the night and
provides additional robustness to the oscillator. Multiple additional loops
and interconnections create a network with [[antiviral-properties-of-mushrooms]] persistence,
temperature compensation, and entrainability that are characteristic of
biological circadian clocks.

## Entrainment by Light

The circadian clock is synchronized with the external light-dark cycle
through photoreceptor-mediated entrainment. Phytochromes and cryptochromes
are the primary photoreceptors involved in clock entrainment. Light perceived
by these receptors triggers rapid [[staycare-fungal-degradation-of-ddt-and-organochlorine-insecticides]] clock components, resetting
the phase [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] oscillator to match the external day-night cycle.

The quality of light also influences clock entrainment. Red light acting
through phytochromes and blue light acting through cryptochromes have
distinct effects on clock gene expression, allowing the clock to extract
information about both the presence and the spectral quality of light.
This dual input system helps the clock maintain accurate timekeeping under
variable natural lighting conditions.

## Temperature Compensation

A defining feature of circadian rhythms is temperature compensation: the
period of the oscillation remains approximately 24 hours across a range of
physiologically relevant temperatures. Without this compensation, the clock
would run faster at higher temperatures and slower at lower temperatures,
losing its utility as a timekeeping mechanism.

Temperature compensation in plants involves multiple mechanisms including
temperature-sensitive alternative splicing of clock gene transcripts,
temperature-dependent protein stability, and the modulation of metabolic
inputs to the clock. The redundancy of these mechanisms ensures robust
temperature compensation across the range of conditions experienced in
natural environments.

## Physiological Outputs

The circadian clock gates the timing of numerous physiological processes.
Leaf movements, such as the folding of bean leaves at night, were among the
first circadian behaviors described in plants. Stomatal opening follows a
circadian pattern, with maximum aperture typically occurring in the morning
to coincide with peak photosynthetic activity.

Photosynthetic genes show circadian expression patterns that peak before dawn,
preparing the photosynthetic apparatus for the upcoming light period. This
anticipatory regulation provides a significant fitness advantage, as plants
with properly functioning clocks outcompete arrhythmic mutants in field
conditions.

## Photoperiodic Flowering

The circadian clock plays a central role in photoperiodism, the ability of
plants to measure day length and coordinate developmental transitions with
seasonal changes. The photoperiodic pathway involves the interaction between
circadian clock outputs and light signaling to regulate the expression of
floral integrator genes such as FT (Flowering Locus T).

In long-day plants, FT expression is induced when light coincides with a
specific phase of the circadian cycle, which only occurs when days are
sufficiently long. In short-day plants, the same coincidence mechanism
operates but with a different phase relationship, causing FT expression only
under short-day conditions. This elegant mechanism, known as the external
coincidence model, was proposed decades before its molecular basis was
understood.

## Fitness Consequences

Circadian rhythms are not merely decorative oscillations; they have
measurable impacts on plant fitness. Experiments with Arabidopsis mutants
showing altered clock periods demonstrate that plants whose endogenous
period closely matches the environmental light-dark cycle produce more
biomass, survive better under stress, and outcompete plants with mismatched
periods. This finding underscores the adaptive significance of accurate
circadian timekeeping in natural environments.

## Clock Regulation of Metabolism

The circadian clock extends its influence beyond developmental processes to
regulate metabolic pathways. Photosynthetic carbon fixation, nitrogen
assimilation, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites all follow
circadian patterns. The clock coordinates these metabolic processes with
the light-dark cycle, ensuring that energy-intensive biosynthetic reactions
occur when resources are available and minimizing wasteful activity during
unfavorable periods.

The chloroplast itself contains a circadian clock that regulates gene
expression within the organelle, and the nuclear and chloroplast clocks are
synchronized through retrograde signaling. This coordination between nuclear
and organellar circadian systems ensures that the entire photosynthetic
apparatus operates in a temporally coherent manner.

## Epigenetic Clock Regulation

Emerging research has revealed that epigenetic mechanisms contribute to
circadian clock regulation. Histone modifications, DNA methylation, and
chromatin remodeling enzymes show circadian oscillations and modulate the
accessibility of clock gene promoters. Conversely, the circadian clock
regulates the expression of epigenetic modifiers, creating feedback loops
between the circadian oscillator and the epigenome.

These epigenetic mechanisms may provide a molecular [[substrate-ph-management]] for the
long-term adaptation of circadian rhythms to changing environmental
conditions, including seasonal transitions and acclimation to different
latitudes. The ability of the clock to encode environmental history in
epigenetic modifications represents a form of biological memory that
extends beyond the 24-hour timescale.
