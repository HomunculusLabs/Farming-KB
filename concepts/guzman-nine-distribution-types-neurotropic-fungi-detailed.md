# Nine Distribution Types of Neurotropic Fungi

Guzmán applied Hongo's (1978) classification of nine fungal distribution
types to the world's neurotropic fungi, providing a systematic framework for
understanding how psychedelic mushrooms are distributed across the globe.
This typology reveals that neurotropic fungi occupy virtually every
biogeographic niche, from cosmopolitan weeds to hyper-endemic specialists.

## Type 1: Cosmopolitan Species

Cosmopolitan neurotropic fungi are found worldwide, often associated with
human-modified environments. The primary examples are in the genus
Panaeolus and Panaeolina:

- *Panaeolina foenisecii* — the "mower's mushroom," common in lawns and
  pastures globally
- *Panaeolus fimicola* — found on dung across temperate and tropical regions
- *Panaeolus sphinctrinus* — widespread coprophilous species
- *Panaeolus papilionaceus* — common on dung in many countries

These species benefit from human agriculture and animal husbandry, which
have created vast areas of suitable substrate worldwide. Their distribution
tracks the spread of grazing mammals and the expansion of grasslands.

## Type 2: Northern Hemisphere Species

Species restricted to the northern temperate zone. The most prominent
examples include *Amanita muscaria* and *Claviceps purpurea*, both of which
have circumboreal distributions across Europe, Asia, and North America.

*A. muscaria* is perhaps the most widely recognized mushroom on Earth, yet
its distribution is strictly northern. It forms mycorrhizal associations
with Pinus and Betula, limiting it to forests where these hosts occur. It
has been introduced to southern hemisphere pine plantations in Brazil,
Colombia, Tanzania, Australia, and New Zealand, but these represent
anthropogenic extensions of its natural range.

## Type 3: Eurasian Species

Species found across Europe and Asia but absent from the Americas. Examples
include *Psilocybe bohemica* (Czech Republic, Slovakia, possibly extending
eastward) and *Psilocybe serbica* (Southeastern Europe). These species
appear to have evolved in Eurasian forest ecosystems and never crossed the
Bering land bridge or transatlantic corridors.

## Type 4: North American-Eastern Asiatic Species

This disjunct distribution type links species found in eastern North America
with those in eastern Asia, reflecting ancient connections across Beringia
or the North Pacific. The classic example is *Psilocybe cyanescens*, known
from the Pacific Northwest of North America and also reported from parts of
Western Europe (where it may be introduced). The pattern is clearer in other
fungal genera documented by Horak.

## Type 5: Far Eastern Endemics

Species known only from Japan and immediate surroundings. Several
Psilocybe species described from Japan by Hongo and others fall into this
category. Japan's complex topography, distinct climate zones, and island
biogeography have produced numerous endemic species with restricted ranges.

## Type 6: Southeastern Asiatic Species

Species from the tropical and subtropical forests of Southeast Asia and the
Pacific. *Psilocybe samuiensis* from Thailand is a key example, closely
related to *P. mexicana* from Mexico (Section Mexicanae), suggesting an
ancient Gondwanan connection rather than recent long-distance dispersal.

## Type 7: Tropical and Subtropical Species

The largest group by species count. These include *Psilocybe cubensis*, *P.
subcubensis*, and most *Copelandia* species. They thrive in warm, humid
conditions across the tropics and subtropics, often in association with
cattle dung or disturbed habitats.

*Copelandia cyanescens* ranges from Hawaii at 3,000m altitude to tropical
lowlands, and even extends into temperate regions in disturbed sites such
as the Valley of Mexico (2,220m) and parts of Central Europe.

## Type 8: Arctic and Alpine Species

High-elevation specialists adapted to cold, exposed habitats. *Psilocybe
aztecorum* fruits at high altitudes in Mexican volcanic mountains. Other
alpine species occupy similar niches in Himalayan and Andean environments.
These species are among the most range-restricted and vulnerable to climate
change.

## Type 9: Endemic Species

Species restricted to single countries, regions, or even specific
localities. Mexico has the highest number of endemic neurotropic fungi,
with many species known only from the states of Oaxaca, Puebla, or
Veracruz. Colombia has at least 12 neurotropic Psilocybe species, many
endemic. New Zealand's *P. makarorae* and *P. aucklandii* are endemic to
that archipelago.

Endemism is highest in regions with complex topography and diverse
microclimates — volcanic Mexico, the Andes, the islands of Southeast Asia
and the South Pacific. These regions serve as refugia where species evolve
in isolation.

## Distribution Patterns and Exploration Bias

A critical caveat: the apparent distribution of neurotropic fungi is heavily
biased toward well-explored regions. Europe, North America, and Japan have
been intensively surveyed, while vast areas of Africa, Southeast Asia, and
South America remain mycologically unknown. The concentration of locality
records in the northern hemisphere (visible in Guzmán's Figure 19) contrasts
with the higher species diversity in southern regions.

Stamets' (1996) world distribution map was criticized by Guzmán as
exaggerated, with dots filling all of the USA, Mexico, South America,
central Africa, central Asia, and southern Australia — implying continuous
distribution where actual records are sparse or absent.
