# Desert Garden Design

## Overview

The desert garden represents one of the most critical applications of [[rammed-earth]]) and mud brick courtyard walls can be built over time. Rock is often plentiful for protective walls.

### Raised Beds

Inside the protective enclosure, beds are raised using stones, pise, or logs. These beds can be almost totally mulch-filled as mulch sources are developed or gathered. Individual beds should be shaded to approximately 75% using shade cloth, palm fronds, or brush supported on frames 1–1.5 metres high.

### Permanent Trellis Structures

Solid pillars of concrete block and mud brick to 2.5 metres can be constructed to support permanent vine trellis over much of the garden area.

## Soil and Water Analysis

A careful soil and water analysis is essential before planting. Common deficiencies and problems include:

- **Phosphorus and zinc deficiency** — very common in desert soils
- **Iron and manganese deficiency** — probable in most dryland soils
- **Excess boron** — from detergents, causes plant failure; use common soaps where waste water irrigates beds
- **Excessive nitrates and fluorine** — common dryland water pollutants; water must be tested

All rain run-off and roof water must be carefully harvested and used to flush salts if local water exceeds 800 ppm or the garden is not free-drained.

## Plant Selection and Sequencing

### Perennial Staples

Deep-rooting and high-yielding perennials serve as standby crops:

- Asparagus
- Globe artichoke
- Sweet potato
- Most cucurbits and melon family species

### Tree Emphasis

The desert garden needs a strong emphasis on staple trees adapted to dry periods or able to survive on minimal water. A recommended planting includes:

- 5–6 date palms
- 4–5 olives
- 1 [[helianthus-tuberosus]])
- Potatoes (in deep 0.5–1 m boxes filled with mulch, pine needles, and household scraps)
- Watermelon, melons, and climbing or vine cucurbits

Surplus vegetables can be dried for storage.

## Corridor Farming

Sand-filled waterways always contain some moisture — these are corridors of moisture and soil. **Corridor farming** is a fundamentally different approach from area cropping. It involves choosing and placing a complex assembly of native and introduced species to provide:

- A basic food reserve
- Fuel resources
- Selected structural timbers

Corridors can take the form of:
- Rivers and riverbeds
- Dune series on hostile salted or stony pavement
- Floodplains of exotic rivers

These serve as sites for "semi-wild" plant and animal reserves. Provision can be made for good rain years by broadcast sowing into areas already prepared for infiltration, producing surplus crops of sunflower, millet, sorghum, panicgrass, or melons for long-term storage or trade.

## Water Management

The garden surrounds the house, using all wastewater either as soakage fields or as underground plastic-lined swales in coarser material. Vegetable beds are carefully constructed — they can be built of compacted mud, filled with better soil, and mulched. Every vegetable grown in temperate or tropical lands will grow well in small beds (usually 3 × 1 m) flooded every 3–10 days, mulched, and part-shaded by slats, vines, or the canopy of a light-crowned leguminous tree.

## Bed-by-Bed Planning

Home gardens return so much in health, cash, and quality of life that they deserve intensive, bed-by-bed planning. Each bed should have:

- Designed companion plants
- Planned seasonal succession
- Specific bed soil treatments
- A permanent watering method

Depending on available resources, beds can be raised by digging and edged with pise (rammed damp clay sands), bricks, logs, or stone walls. Trellis can be of metal mesh, bamboo, hardy termite-resistant wood, cane grasses, or living leguminous trees. Deserts typically have supplies of clays, sands, rubble, and sometimes lime or gypsum for garden use.

## Earth Shaping Techniques

A variety of earth-shaping methods are available for desert gardens:

- **Cool mulched pits** for trees
- **Circle gardens** around mulch holes
- **N–S ridges** to shade seedlings
- **Mulch "baskets"** at clump plantings
- **Log boxes of mulch** over alkaline sands
- **Broad flood bays** for carrot crops
- **Top-watered raised beds** for salty water supply (up to 1,200 ppm)

## Essentials Summary

The essentials of a successful desert garden are:

1. Small raised flooded beds, thickly mulched
2. Permanent hardy trees on leach fields or in swales
3. Semi-wild very hardy bulbs, tubers, and yams in selected sites
