# JADAM Soil Foundation Work

## Overview

Soil foundation work is described as the single most important technology in the JADAM organic farming system. It refers to the intensive application of [[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms]], minerals, and [[jadam-liquid-fertilizer]] before transplanting of crop plants and before the sprouting or flowering of fruit trees. This pre-planting investment creates the underground conditions that determine harvest success.

## The Core Principle

"What is visible (plant) represents what is invisible (root)." A plant can only grow to the extent that its foundation allows. Just as you cannot raise a 10-storey building on a 2-meter deep foundation, you cannot achieve high yield without first establishing deep, extensive root systems.

High yield equals the total area of the root that comes in contact with plant food (fertilizer). Many farmers think that simply applying abundant base fertilizer and additional fertilizer will produce more shoots. This is incorrect. Plants are smart; they look at where they are growing and grow only to the extent that is possible.

## Timing Is Critical

### For Fruit Trees
Apply intensively after leaves fall until sprouts or flowers appear the next year. In this period, unlimited water can be given so microbes can swim deep and thoroughly into the soil.

### For Field Crops
Apply from after-harvest to before transplant. Same principle -- without growing crops limiting water input, microbial penetration reaches deeper.

### For Greenhouses
Since rainwater does not fall, repeat applying JMS with irrigation water, wetting the soil fully every time.

## Application Method

Use a motored sprayer, sprinkler, or drip irrigation to apply a mixture of:

- **JMS**: 500 L (132 gal) can be used for an area of 0.25 to 8 acres
- **Sea salt**: 500 g (1.1 lb) per 0.25 acre
- **Phyllite solution**: 500 g (1.1 lb) per 0.25 acre
- **GC-JLF** (wild grass plus crop residues [[jadam-liquid-fertilizer]]): diluted about 100 times

Standard dilution: 1 ton (2205 lb) of water for 3558 sq ft (0.08 acres). For areas smaller than 0.08 acres, do not add sea salt separately (it is already added when making JMS).

This liquid fertilizer made from various wild grass and crop residues (leaves, branches, fruits) is described as the most optimized fertilizer available.

## Results

The greatest benefit of JADAM soil foundation work is that root settlement becomes extremely strong. "Roots will be almost dancing inside the soil." This leads directly to high yield because:

- Microbial population and diversity increase
- Chemicals trapped in compacted soil layers start to dissolve
- The compacted layer gradually disappears
- Decomposition of organic matter accelerates, reducing need for chemical fertilizer
- Nematodes, wilt, and virus diseases -- all notoriously hard to cure -- are suppressed
- Active soil microorganisms raise soil temperature, reducing chill and freezing damage
- Increased diversity in microbes means increased diversity in nutrients for plants
- Sea salt (with 83 identified minerals) supplements the mineral spectrum
- Contaminants in the soil are broken down by microbes

## Why Microbes Cannot Travel Alone

Microorganisms cannot travel without the help of water. They are somewhat like fish -- over 95% of their body is water, and they are like aquatic animals. They cannot simply move around inside dry soil. Microbes must be applied with water. If microbes are to reach the ends of the roots, water must reach there; microbes must "swim" with the water to reach that destination.

Early root settlement determines the success of harvest. Early root settlement is very, very important, along with water (irrigation) and nutrient (fertilizer).

## Solving the Transition Problem

Some farmers complain that crop yield decreases after switching to organic farming from conventional farming. This occurs because soil microbial activity has not yet been fully normalized to the original natural level. Chemical pesticide, fertilizer, and herbicide have decreased the biodiversity, population, and vigor of the microbial ecology. Even if organic matter is added to this depleted soil, it is not broken down rapidly due to weak microorganism activity, causing nutrient deficiency for crops.

If farmers make efforts to restore soil microbial abundance in advance, cutting off chemical fertilizer and replacing them with organic matter does not significantly reduce yield -- in many cases, yield actually increases.

## JADAM Soil Science Principles

JADAM soil science is built on the observation that healthy soil in its natural state never requires external fertilizer inputs. In undisturbed forests, grasslands, and natural ecosystems, plants thrive without amendment because the soil microbial ecosystem is intact and fully functional. The JADAM approach seeks to restore this natural soil condition through microbial inoculation and mineral supplementation rather than through imported organic matter or chemical fertilizers. The key insight is that soil microbes are the intermediaries between soil minerals and plant roots: they solubilize locked minerals, fix atmospheric nitrogen, decompose organic residues, and create the humic compounds that give soil its structure and water-holding capacity. When microbial diversity and abundance are restored, the soil essentially fertilizes itself.

## Microbial Inoculation Strategy

JADAM uses [[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms]] as the primary microbial inoculant for soil foundation work. JMS is produced by collecting leaf mold from a mature forest, where the natural microbial community is at its most diverse and vigorous, and culturing these native microorganisms in a solution of boiled potato water and sea salt. The resulting liquid contains billions of diverse bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, and other microorganisms per milliliter. When applied to soil through the foundation work process, these microbes colonize the root zone and begin the work of restructuring the soil. The microbial inoculation is most effective when the soil is thoroughly wetted, because microbes require a water film to move through soil pores. This is why the foundation work period, when no crops are present and unlimited water can be applied, is so critical for reaching deep soil layers that would otherwise remain biologically inactive.

## Soil Testing and Assessment Methods

JADAM advocates for simple, direct soil assessment methods that any farmer can perform without laboratory equipment. The primary indicator of soil health is root development: pulling up a plant and examining the root system reveals whether the soil foundation is adequate. Healthy roots are extensive, branching, and surrounded by a visible rhizosphere of microbial activity. Compact, stubby, or discolored roots indicate problems with soil structure, mineral availability, or microbial suppression. Beyond root observation, JADAM practitioners assess soil by its texture (crumb structure versus hard clods), its smell (earthy and sweet versus sour or chemical), its water infiltration rate, and the diversity of plants that volunteer in the field. Earthworm presence and abundance is another practical indicator of soil biological health. These observational methods are considered more relevant to farming outcomes than laboratory nutrient analysis, because they reflect the living condition of the soil rather than its static chemical composition.

## Mineral Balance and Sea Salt Supplementation

The JADAM system uses sea salt as the primary mineral supplement, based on the principle that seawater contains all 83 naturally occurring elements in proportions that approximate the mineral needs of living organisms. Sea salt is added to JMS during production and can also be applied separately during soil foundation work at a rate of approximately 500 grams per 0.25 acre. The phyllite solution ([[jadam-phyllite-solution]]), a rock mineral extract, provides additional silicate minerals that contribute to soil structure and plant cell wall strength. The combination of sea salt and phyllite solution supplies a broad spectrum of macro- and micronutrients without the risk of imbalance that can occur with formulated NPK fertilizers. JADAM argues that plants given access to the full mineral spectrum will select what they need and that deficiencies arise not from absolute absence of minerals in soil but from microbial inability to solubilize and deliver them to roots.

## Comparison with Korean Natural Farming Soil Approach

While both JADAM and [[korean-natural-farming-imo-recipes]] share the philosophy of working with natural biological processes, their approaches to soil management differ significantly. KNF, developed by Master Cho Han-Kyu, relies heavily on a suite of fermented plant and animal inputs including IMO (Indigenous Microorganism) collected from forest soils, fish amino acids, fermented fruit juice, and Oriental Herbal Nutrients. These inputs are applied throughout the growing season as both soil drenches and foliar sprays. JADAM, developed by Young-sang Cho, prioritizes ultra-low-cost methods and uses only three primary inputs: JMS (microbes), sea salt (minerals), and GC-JLF (liquid fertilizer from wild grass and crop residues). KNF involves more complex preparation of multiple fermented inputs, while JADAM simplifies to a single microbial solution, a single mineral source, and a single fertilizer. Both systems emphasize pre-planting soil preparation, but JADAM places greater emphasis on the intensive foundation work period when unlimited water can move microbes deep into the soil profile. KNF soil management tends to be more input-intensive and recipe-driven, while JADAM emphasizes that the farmer should develop intuition about soil conditions through direct observation.

## Practical Tips for Beginning Farmers

Farmers new to the JADAM system should start with soil foundation work on a small area, such as a single greenhouse bed or a quarter-acre field section, to build confidence in the method before scaling up. The most common mistake is applying JMS without sufficient water: the soil must be thoroughly saturated to allow microbes to penetrate deeply. Observing root development after the first crop cycle provides the clearest evidence of whether the foundation work was adequate. If roots are deep, branching, and surrounded by crumbly soil, the treatment was successful. If roots are shallow and the soil remains compacted, additional applications during the next fallow period are needed. Patience is essential: restoring soil biology after years of chemical farming may take two to three growing seasons of consistent foundation work before results become dramatic.

## Integration with Crop Rotation and Cover Cropping

Soil foundation work is most effective when integrated into a broader management system that includes crop rotation and cover cropping. After the initial foundation work period, maintaining microbial populations requires continuous feeding through organic matter returned to the soil. Cover crops provide this organic matter while simultaneously protecting the soil surface from erosion and suppressing weeds. The JADAM [[jadam-cover-crop-method]] recommends using a mixture of leguminous and non-leguminous species to maximize both nitrogen fixation and biomass production. Crop rotation prevents the buildup of crop-specific pathogens and breaks pest cycles, reducing the need for the chemical interventions that degrade soil microbial diversity. The combination of foundation work, cover cropping, and rotation creates a self-reinforcing cycle: better soil biology leads to better crop residues, which feed better soil biology. Over several seasons, the need for external inputs diminishes as the soil ecosystem becomes increasingly self-sufficient and productive.
